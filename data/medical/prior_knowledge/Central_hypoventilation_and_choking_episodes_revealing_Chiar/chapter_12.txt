# Introduction

Dysphagia, or difficulty swallowing, is a common and potentially serious symptom in neurological disease. Its consequences include malnutrition, dehydration, aspiration pneumonia, and reduced quality of life. Neurologic disorders produce dysphagia through disruption of the complex, coordinated sensorimotor events required for safe and efficient transfer of a bolus from the oral cavity to the stomach. Understanding the distinction between oropharyngeal and esophageal dysphagia, the patterns that arise from cranial nerve and brainstem lesions, and the appropriate use and interpretation of diagnostic tools is essential for acute management and long-term rehabilitation planning. This chapter focuses on neurogenic mechanisms of oropharyngeal dysphagia and evidence-based approaches to evaluation and initial management.

# Distinction between oropharyngeal and esophageal dysphagia

Accurate localization of the level of dysfunction—whether oral, pharyngeal, or esophageal—is the first step in assessment. Oropharyngeal dysphagia refers to impaired transfer of food from the mouth through the pharynx into the esophagus. It typically presents with immediate difficulty initiating a swallow, coughing or choking during or shortly after swallowing, nasal regurgitation, a wet or gurgly voice, and multiple swallows for a single bolus. Patients may report pooling of saliva or food in the mouth, drooling, or choking on liquids more than solids. Symptoms often occur at the start of the swallow and are associated with airway compromise (aspiration).

Esophageal dysphagia refers to perceived obstruction or delay in passage of a bolus through the esophagus. Clinical features include sensation of food "sticking" in the chest or throat after swallowing, retrosternal discomfort, regurgitation of undigested food, and symptoms that occur after a variable delay following the act of swallowing rather than immediately. Esophageal dysphagia may be progressive (mechanical obstruction like stricture or malignancy) or intermittent (motility disorders such as achalasia, diffuse esophageal spasm).

Distinguishing features useful at the bedside include timing of symptoms relative to swallowing (immediate suggests oropharyngeal), association with coughing/aspiration (common in oropharyngeal), and whether both solids and liquids are affected (neurogenic or motility disorders often affect both; mechanical obstruction usually begins with solids). Examination findings supportive of oropharyngeal dysphagia include drooling, poor lip closure, impaired tongue movement, nasal regurgitation, and a wet voice on phonation or after swallowing. It is important to remember that mixed presentations occur, and patients may have both oropharyngeal and esophageal components; appropriate imaging and endoscopic evaluation are required when the clinical picture is unclear.

# Neurogenic dysphagia patterns related to cranial nerve and brainstem lesions

Swallowing is controlled by a distributed network that includes cortical and subcortical regions, the brainstem central pattern generator (CPG) for swallowing, cranial nerve nuclei, peripheral cranial nerves, and the muscles of the oropharynx and upper esophageal sphincter (UES). Neurogenic dysphagia may result from lesions at any level. Recognizing pattern-recognition clusters helps localize pathology and tailor management.

Cortical and corticobulbar pathway lesions, such as unilateral or bilateral strokes in the sensorimotor cortex or internal capsule, commonly produce supranuclear dysphagia. These lesions often cause deficits in voluntary initiation of swallowing, delayed pharyngeal swallow trigger, reduced bolus control, and impaired coordination, particularly of the oral and pharyngeal phases. Because reflexive components mediated by the brainstem may be partially preserved, signs can include inefficient mastication, pocketing of food in the buccal sulcus, and delayed laryngeal elevation leading to aspiration, often with thin liquids. Cortical lesions typically cause contralateral weakness but bilateral involvement is common and increases severity. Speech deficits (dysarthria, apraxia of speech) often coexist and aid localization.

Brainstem lesions, particularly those affecting the medulla and lower pons, can directly disrupt the swallowing CPG and cranial nerve nuclei (nucleus ambiguus, nucleus tractus solitarius). Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) infarcts characteristically produce severe pharyngeal dysphagia with diminished pharyngeal constriction, reduced laryngeal elevation, absent gag reflex, and high risk of silent aspiration due to impaired laryngeal closure. These patients frequently have sensory loss in the pharynx and face, resulting in reduced swallow reflex. Bilateral lesions or lesions involving both sensory and motor nuclei can cause profound dysphagia requiring alternative feeding.

Cranial nerve palsies produce predictable deficits according to the nerve(s) involved. Peripheral lesions of the trigeminal nerve (CN V) impair mastication and bolus formation due to weakened jaw closure and lateral movement. Facial nerve (CN VII) lesions cause poor lip seal and buccal incompetence, resulting in anterior spillage and drooling. Glossopharyngeal (CN IX) dysfunction reduces pharyngeal sensation and contributes to diminished swallow trigger and impaired pharyngeal constriction. Vagus nerve (CN X) lesions affect palatal elevation, pharyngeal constriction, laryngeal elevation and closure, and UES relaxation through impairment of pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles, leading to nasal regurgitation, hoarseness, and aspiration. Hypoglossal nerve (CN XII) palsy results in tongue weakness and atrophy, causing inefficient bolus control, oral residue, and difficulty in propelling the bolus posteriorly.

Subcortical structures and basal ganglia involvement, as in Parkinson disease, progressive supranuclear palsy, or Huntington disease, produce characteristic dysphagia patterns. Parkinsonian dysphagia often manifests with bradykinesia and rigidity of orofacial and pharyngeal muscles, delayed swallow initiation, reduced tongue base retraction and pharyngeal contraction, decreased hyolaryngeal excursion, and impaired UES opening. These changes predispose to both oropharyngeal residue and aspiration, particularly with secretions and liquids. Response to dopaminergic therapy is variable, and disease progression results in increasing aspiration risk.

Neuromuscular junction disorders (myasthenia gravis) and primary muscle diseases (inflammatory myopathies, muscular dystrophies) produce fatigable or progressive weakness of the muscles required for swallowing. In myasthenia gravis, dysphagia may fluctuate, worsen with fatigue, and involve both bulbar and proximal limb muscles. Aspiration may follow prolonged chewing and repetitive swallows. Myopathies often cause weakness of tongue and pharyngeal muscles with associated drooling, dysarthria, and risk of aspiration of solids and liquids.

Peripheral neuropathies, such as those seen in motor neuron disease (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis), produce combined upper and lower motor neuron signs. Bulbar-onset ALS typically causes progressive dysphagia with tongue fasciculations, atrophy, reduced palatal movement, and impaired pharyngeal contraction. The risk of aspiration increases steadily, and loss of effective cough contributes to severe pulmonary complications.

Clinical reasoning requires mapping observed swallowing deficits to underlying anatomy and physiology: problems with bolus formation and containment point to oral phase and CN V/VII/CN XII dysfunction; delayed swallow initiation and pharyngeal residue imply sensory or motor impairment of CN IX/X or brainstem CPG; impaired laryngeal elevation and UES opening indicate dysfunction of suprahyoid muscles and central coordination. Recognizing whether aspiration is “pre-swallow” (due to poor airway protection before the swallow), “during swallow” (due to incomplete laryngeal closure), or “post-swallow” (due to pharyngeal residue) helps identify the lesion and relates directly to management choices.

# Modified barium swallow (videofluoroscopy): indications and interpretation (aspiration, penetration, pharyngeal transit)

The modified barium swallow (MBS), commonly referred to as videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS), is the gold-standard dynamic radiographic assessment of the oral and pharyngeal phases of swallowing. It provides real-time visualization of bolus flow, tongue and pharyngeal movements, laryngeal elevation, UES opening, and airway protection mechanisms.

Indications for VFSS include unexplained cough or choking with swallowing, suspected aspiration (including silent aspiration), progressive dysphagia where localization is uncertain, preoperative assessment when upper airway or pharyngeal anatomy will be altered, failure to progress with therapy, assessment of response to compensatory maneuvers and postural adjustments, and medico-legal documentation. VFSS is particularly useful when clinical bedside assessments are equivocal, when aspiration risk must be quantified, or when the clinician needs to evaluate the effectiveness of rehabilitation strategies.

Technical aspects: VFSS requires a radiology suite with fluoroscopy and a speech-language pathologist or swallowing-trained clinician present. The study should include a range of bolus consistencies and volumes (thin liquids, nectar-thick, honey-thick, puree/pudding, soft solids, and regular solids) as tolerated, with lateral and antero-posterior views to assess symmetry and identify unilateral pharyngeal weakness. Standardized protocols, such as the Modified Barium Swallow Impairment Profile (MBSImP), improve reliability of interpretation.

Interpretation centers on identifying and quantifying three major phenomena: penetration/aspiration, pharyngeal transit and clearance, and structural/mechanical impairment.

- Penetration and aspiration: Penetration refers to entry of material into the laryngeal vestibule above the level of the vocal folds; aspiration is passage of material below the vocal folds into the trachea. The Penetration-Aspiration Scale (PAS) is commonly used; it is an 8-point ordinal scale where 1 indicates no material enters the airway and 8 indicates material enters the airway, passes below the vocal folds, and no effort is made to eject. Silent aspiration is defined by lack of cough or throat-clear response despite material entering the airway. VFSS allows determination of when aspiration occurs (pre-, intra-, or post-swallow), which is crucial for mechanistic interpretation and management.

- Pharyngeal transit and clearance: Normal pharyngeal transit is rapid, with pharyngeal phase typically lasting less than 1 second for a single trial of thin liquids. VFSS evaluates bolus propulsion from the valleculae and pyriform sinuses, pharyngeal stripping wave (pharyngeal constriction), tongue base retraction, and the degree of bolus residue. Quantification of residue can be subjective, but scales and pixel-based measures exist. Significant residue in the valleculae or pyriform sinuses increases the risk of post-swallow aspiration.

- Upper esophageal sphincter (UES) opening: VFSS demonstrates the degree and duration of UES opening, which depends on hyolaryngeal excursion and cricopharyngeal relaxation. Reduced UES opening results in bolus stasis at the level of the pyriform sinuses and may lead to post-swallow aspiration. VFSS also allows visualization of pharyngeal diverticula, Zenker’s diverticulum, and overt structural lesions.

Interpreting VFSS requires integrating observations: delayed swallow trigger with preserved pharyngeal contraction points to sensory impairment; poor tongue base retraction and pharyngeal constriction imply weakness of pharyngeal musculature; incomplete laryngeal elevation with early airway invasion indicates risk during the pharyngeal phase. Compensatory strategies (e.g., chin-tuck, head turn) can be tested in real time to determine whether they reduce aspiration or improve bolus flow. The diagnostic output guides diet recommendations, rehabilitation strategies, and decisions about further investigations or interventions.

# FEES methodology and complementary role to videofluoroscopy

Fibreoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) is an instrumental assessment performed at bedside or clinic that uses a flexible nasendoscope to visualize pharyngeal and laryngeal structures before and after the swallow. FEES provides high-resolution views of mucosal anatomy, secretions, pooling, residue, and laryngeal penetration/aspiration. It is particularly valuable for repeated assessments, biofeedback during therapy, and evaluation of secretion management.

Methodology: FEES is performed by passing a transnasal flexible endoscope to a position above the epiglottis and close to the tip of the epiglottis or in the hypopharynx, allowing visualization of the velopharyngeal port, pharyngeal walls, laryngeal vestibule, and vocal folds. Topical nasal anesthesia may be used sparingly. The patient is given food and liquid boluses of varying consistencies mixed with food coloring (commonly blue or green) to improve contrast. The clinician observes baseline anatomy, secretions and pooling, pharyngeal squeeze on phonation, swallow initiation (white-out due to epiglottic inversion occurs during the swallow), and post-swallow residue and penetration/aspiration events.

Strengths and limitations: FEES excels at identifying structural abnormalities, mucosal lesions, and secretion management issues. It detects aspiration and penetration when occurring before and after the swallow and is well-suited for patients who cannot be transferred to radiology or are medically unstable. FEES allows prolonged observation and repeated trials, enabling assessment of fatigue effects and response to therapeutic maneuvers, making it ideal for real-time treatment trials and biofeedback.

FEES limitations include inability to directly visualize the pharyngeal and laryngeal structures during the actual swallow due to the brief "white-out" period when the pharynx closes around the endoscope. Therefore, aspiration that occurs strictly during the swallow (intra-swallow) can be inferred only by pre- and post-swallow findings and clinical correlation. FEES also gives no information on oral transit or UES opening and cannot assess esophageal clearance beyond the immediate post-swallow. Additionally, a small percentage of patients cannot tolerate nasal endoscopy.

Complementary roles: VFSS and FEES are complementary rather than competing modalities. VFSS provides dynamic visualization of oral and pharyngeal phases including UES opening and direct assessment during the swallow, which is essential for identifying timing of aspiration and bolus transit. FEES provides superior mucosal detail, secretion assessment, and detailed observation of pharyngeal residue and airway protective reflexes. Use FEES for bedside, serial monitoring, and therapeutic biofeedback; use VFSS when assessment of oral phase, transit times, UES function, or suspected structural abnormalities above the UES are required. Many centers employ both: FEES for initial bedside screening and serial therapy, VFSS for comprehensive physiologic analysis and surgical planning.

# Clinical bedside swallowing assessments and aspiration risk stratification

Bedside clinical assessment is the initial step in evaluating swallowing function and aspiration risk. It informs decisions about immediate management and need for instrumental testing. A thorough bedside assessment integrates history, cranial nerve and oromotor examination, observation of speech and secretions, simple swallowing trials, and validated screening tools.

History should elicit onset, progression, food consistencies provoking symptoms, associated symptoms (coughing, choking, voice change, drooling, nasal regurgitation), prior strokes or neurologic disease, and ability to manage secretions. Cognitive status and level of alertness are crucial since attention and cooperation affect swallowing safety.

Oromotor examination evaluates lip closure, lingual strength and range, palatal elevation, gag reflex (limited sensitivity), symmetry of facial movements, and voluntary cough strength. Assessing respiratory function and cough is essential because an effective cough protects the airway; peak cough flow below certain thresholds correlates with aspiration risk (e.g., peak cough flow < 160 L/min has been associated with poor airway clearance). Voice quality is assessed for wet/gurgly quality, hoarseness, or breathiness, which may indicate laryngeal dysfunction or aspiration.

Simple swallowing trials begin with small amounts of water or other liquids and progress through thicker consistencies and solids if safe. The 3-ounce water swallow challenge is a commonly used screening test: patients drink 90 mL (3 ounces) continuously from a cup. Failure is any coughing, throat clearing, or voice change during or immediately after drinking. The test has reasonable sensitivity but limited specificity, and positive findings should prompt instrumental assessment. Other validated bedside tools include the Gugging Swallowing Screen (GUSS) and the Mann Assessment of Swallowing Ability (MASA), which stratify risk and recommend diet modifications.

Aspiration risk stratification considers multiple domains: alertness and cognition, oromotor function, respiratory status, voluntary cough, presence of secretions, voice quality, and performance on water swallow trials. High-risk features include reduced consciousness, inability to manage secretions, absent or severely weak cough, wet voice after swallowing, and documented or suspected silent aspiration. In such patients, oral intake should be suspended until instrumental evaluation clarifies safety. Intermediate risk patients with mild cough or voice change may be allowed limited oral trials with supervised therapy and expedited instrumental testing. Low-risk patients with normal oromotor function and negative screening tests may begin graded diet advancement.

Clinical bedside assessment has limitations: it cannot reliably detect silent aspiration and may underestimate pharyngeal residue or UES dysfunction. Therefore, any uncertainty or high pre-test probability of aspiration mandates instrumental evaluation with VFSS or FEES. Documenting the details of bedside tests and clear rationale for subsequent steps is important for continuity of care and medico-legal reasons.

# Management principles: dietary modification, rehabilitative therapy, and indications for enteral feeding

Management of neurogenic oropharyngeal dysphagia aims to prevent aspiration and malnutrition, optimize safe oral intake, and rehabilitate swallowing function where possible. Strategies include compensatory measures and diet modification, rehabilitative exercises and maneuvers, medical and surgical interventions in selected cases, and enteral feeding for severe or refractory dysphagia.

Dietary modification and compensatory strategies form the first-line approach. Modifying bolus volume and consistency (thickened liquids, pureed solids) can reduce aspiration risk by improving swallow timing and bolus control. Texture classification systems (e.g., International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative, IDDSI) recommend specific levels; clinicians should ensure recommendations align with local dietetics. Postural adjustments (chin-tuck/neck flexion, head-turn/head rotation toward a weaker side, chin-up for improved oral bolus transfer) alter oropharyngeal geometry to reduce aspiration risk and can be tested during VFSS or FEES for efficacy. Maneuvers such as the supraglottic or super-supraglottic swallow, effortful swallow, Mendelsohn maneuver, and tongue-hold (Masako) target specific physiologic deficits: supraglottic maneuvers enhance airway protection during swallow; effortful swallows increase pharyngeal pressure generation; Mendelsohn prolongs hyolaryngeal elevation and UES opening. The choice of strategy is individualized based on instrumental assessment findings.

Rehabilitative therapy encompasses exercises to strengthen impaired muscles, improve coordination, and retrain sensory awareness. Progressive resistance exercises (isometric/isokinetic exercises for suprahyoid and tongue muscles), neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) in select settings, and sensory enhancement techniques (thermal-tactile stimulation) are often used. Evidence supports targeted exercise programs—e.g., tongue resistance training, Shaker head lift exercise to improve UES opening, and expiratory muscle strength training to augment cough strength and airway clearance—in improving physiologic measures and functional outcomes for some patients. Neuroplasticity principles guide therapy intensity and frequency: repetitive, task-specific practice yields better outcomes. In progressive neurologic conditions, rehabilitative therapy focuses both on maintenance of function and safe swallowing strategies as the disease advances.

Medical and surgical options are considered for refractory or specific anatomic/physiologic deficits. Botulinum toxin injection into the cricopharyngeus can be effective for cricopharyngeal dysfunction with impaired UES relaxation. Cricopharyngeal myotomy is a surgical option for select patients with structural or neuromuscular dysfunction of the UES when conservative measures fail and the patient’s overall condition supports surgery. Laryngeal elevation procedures, vocal fold medialization (for unilateral vocal fold paralysis causing aspiration), and other targeted interventions are reserved for carefully selected cases.

Indications for enteral feeding (nasoenteric tube or percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy, PEG) include inability to protect the airway from aspiration despite compensatory measures, inability to meet nutritional and hydration needs by oral intake, anticipated prolonged dysphagia with poor swallowing recovery (e.g., persistent severe dysphagia after large brainstem stroke), and recurrent aspiration pneumonia despite appropriate therapy. Decision-making must balance risks and benefits, taking into account prognosis, patient preferences, cognitive status, and goals of care. Short-term nasogastric tubes are appropriate for temporary support; PEG placement is considered when long-term enteral feeding is anticipated (typically beyond 4–6 weeks) and the patient’s condition and prognosis justify the procedure. Enteral feeding reduces the risk of malnutrition and dehydration but does not eliminate pulmonary aspiration risk because aspiration of oropharyngeal secretions can still occur; therefore, aspiration precautions and continued swallowing therapy remain relevant after tube placement.

Nutritional assessment and monitoring are integral. Baseline weight, body mass index, serum albumin and prealbumin can guide nutritional status assessment, with recognition that serum proteins are influenced by inflammation and not solely by nutrition. Target caloric and protein needs depend on medical condition; dietitian involvement is essential for tailored plans and monitoring.

Finally, clinical care must integrate multidisciplinary collaboration: neurologists, speech-language pathologists, dietitians, otolaryngologists, gastroenterologists, and nursing staff coordinate to assess risk, perform instrumental evaluations, implement therapy, and make shared decisions regarding feeding routes. Regular reassessment is important because swallowing function can change over time with recovery, progression, or response to therapy.

# Summary

Neurogenic oropharyngeal dysphagia arises from diverse lesions affecting cortical, subcortical, brainstem, cranial nerve, neuromuscular junction, and muscle targets. Distinguishing oropharyngeal from esophageal dysphagia is primarily a clinical task, but instrumental studies are often required. Videofluoroscopy provides dynamic assessment of oral and pharyngeal physiology and is the gold standard for evaluating aspiration, penetration, transit, and UES function. FEES offers complementary strengths in bedside applicability, mucosal visualization, and repeated therapeutic trials. Bedside screening and clinical assessment stratify aspiration risk and guide urgency of instrumental studies. Management is multidisciplinary and tailored: compensatory diet and postural strategies, rehabilitative exercises targeting specific physiologic deficits, selective medical or surgical interventions, and enteral feeding when aspiration risk or nutritional compromise cannot be managed orally. Understanding the physiologic basis of observed swallowing deficits enables rational selection of diagnostic tests and individualized therapeutic strategies.
