Introduction

The kidneys are highly vascular, structurally complex organs whose primary roles include regulation of extracellular fluid volume, electrolyte composition, acid–base balance, and waste excretion. Understanding renal anatomy and physiology at macroscopic and microscopic scales is essential to interpret how acute kidney injury (AKI) develops and what features predict recovery. This chapter links gross and microanatomy with the physiological processes—glomerular filtration, tubular transport, renal microcirculation, and oxygen handling—that determine how the kidney forms urine and responds to insults. Emphasis is placed on the features that render particular nephron segments vulnerable to ischemia or toxin and on the tissue responses (inflammation, cell death, repair, fibrosis) that underlie acute tubular necrosis (ATN) and the evolution of AKI. Clinical correlates—serum markers, urine indices, urine output, and their limitations—are integrated throughout.

# Gross renal anatomy and blood supply (renal artery, interlobar, arcuate, cortical and medullary microcirculation)

Grossly each kidney is a bean-shaped organ consisting of an outer cortex and inner medulla. The cortex contains glomeruli and the convoluted tubular segments; the medulla contains the straight tubular segments and the collecting ducts that establish the urine concentration gradient. Renal blood flow (RBF) is high relative to organ mass: approximately 20–25% of cardiac output in the resting adult, roughly 1–1.2 L/min in a 70 kg adult. Renal blood flow supports filtration and metabolic demand but produces unique microcirculatory patterns that influence susceptibility to injury.

Arterial supply begins with the renal artery, which branches into segmental arteries and then interlobar arteries that run between the renal pyramids. Interlobar arteries give rise to arcuate arteries that arch along the corticomedullary junction; arcuate arteries produce interlobular (cortical radial) arteries that supply the afferent arterioles feeding individual glomeruli. Each afferent arteriole supplies a glomerular capillary tuft; blood leaves the glomerulus via an efferent arteriole. The efferent arteriole divides into either peritubular capillaries (in cortical nephrons) or the vasa recta (in juxtamedullary nephrons) that descend into the medulla.

Cortical and medullary microcirculation differ functionally. Cortical blood flow is high, supporting active solute transport and filtration; peritubular capillaries provide oxygen and recover reabsorbed solutes and water. Medullary blood flow is relatively low (only 5–10% of total RBF) and organized into countercurrent vasa recta, which preserve the osmotic gradient but limit oxygen delivery. This microcirculatory architecture—high-flow cortex, low-flow hypoxic-prone medulla, countercurrent exchange—underlies the medulla’s susceptibility to ischemia and contributes to the pathophysiology of AKI.

# Glomerular structure: endothelium, basement membrane, podocytes and mesangium

The glomerulus is a selectively permeable filtration barrier composed of three principal layers arranged in series: the fenestrated endothelium, the glomerular basement membrane (GBM), and the podocyte layer with slit diaphragms. Beneath and between these are mesangial cells and extracellular matrix providing structural support and regulating filtration surface area.

Endothelial cells are fenestrated (70–100 nm pores) that allow free passage of plasma water and small solutes but restrict cells. Endothelial glycocalyx—a negatively charged, carbohydrate-rich coat—adds a charge-selective barrier and modulates hydraulic permeability and leukocyte interactions. Damage to the glycocalyx (in sepsis, ischemia, hyperglycemia) increases permeability and predisposes to proteinuria and inflammatory cell adhesion.

The GBM is an extracellular matrix rich in type IV collagen (alpha chains), laminin, heparan sulfate proteoglycans, and other glycoproteins. It contributes size-selective and charge-selective properties; its integrity is essential to prevent albumin and larger proteins from passing into Bowman’s space. Genetic defects (e.g., Alport syndrome) or immune-mediated damage (e.g., anti-GBM disease) alter GBM structure and filtration.

Podocytes are specialized epithelial cells with cell bodies and foot processes that interdigitate to form slit diaphragms—complexes of nephrin, podocin, and other proteins that regulate size-selectivity. Podocyte detachment or effacement (flattening of foot processes) reduces the filtration barrier, causing proteinuria. Podocytes are terminally differentiated; loss of podocytes can lead to glomerulosclerosis.

Mesangial cells occupy the central tuft between capillaries. They produce matrix, provide structural support, and contract in response to vasoactive stimuli (angiotensin II, endothelin), thereby modulating tuft surface area and ultrafiltration coefficient (K_f). Mesangial cells can phagocytose macromolecules and participate in local inflammatory responses.

The overall glomerular filtration rate (GFR) from a single kidney depends on net filtration pressure, glomerular capillary surface area, and hydraulic permeability—parameters affected by the structural integrity and the active responses of these cellular elements.

# Peritubular capillaries and vasa recta

After passage through the glomerulus, blood exits via the efferent arteriole to form either peritubular capillaries (cortical nephrons) or the vasa recta (juxtamedullary nephrons). Peritubular capillaries wrap the proximal and distal convoluted tubules, providing a high-perfusion network where reabsorbed solutes and water are returned to the circulation. The peritubular network has relatively high blood flow and capillary oncotic pressure (because much plasma was filtered in the glomerulus), which favors reabsorption from the interstitium. Peritubular capillary flow and pressure change with systemic hemodynamics, affecting reabsorption.

The vasa recta are long, hairpin-shaped capillaries that extend into the medulla and form a countercurrent exchange system that minimizes washout of the cortico-medullary osmotic gradient. As descending vasa recta move into the hyperosmolar medulla, they pick up solute and lose water; ascending vasa recta reverse this. This countercurrent arrangement allows solute exchange between ascending and descending limbs, preserving the gradient required for urine concentration. However, the low perfusion and countercurrent exchange also limit oxygen delivery to the medulla; oxygen carried into the medulla can be lost before reaching the deepest segments, contributing to relative hypoxia of the outer medulla.

Peritubular and vasa recta endothelial cells also participate in inflammatory processes. In AKI, endothelial activation increases leukocyte adhesion, microvascular congestion, and vascular dysfunction, which amplify injury and reduce oxygen delivery.

# Mechanisms of glomerular filtration and autoregulation

Glomerular filtration depends on Starling forces across glomerular capillaries: the balance between hydrostatic and oncotic pressures and the hydraulic permeability and surface area of the glomerular capillary bed. Net filtration pressure (NFP) is approximated as

NFP = P_gc − P_bs − π_gc

where P_gc is glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure, P_bs is Bowman’s space hydrostatic pressure, and π_gc is glomerular capillary oncotic pressure. The filtration rate (single nephron GFR) is the product of NFP and the ultrafiltration coefficient K_f, which reflects surface area and permeability:

SNGFR = K_f × NFP.

Physiologically, K_f is modulated by mesangial cell contraction and by changes in capillary permeability from endothelial or GBM injury.

Renal autoregulation maintains relatively stable RBF and GFR across a broad mean arterial pressure (MAP) range (approximately 80–180 mmHg in healthy adults) via two principal mechanisms: the myogenic response and tubuloglomerular feedback (TGF). The myogenic response is an immediate constriction of afferent arteriolar smooth muscle in response to increased pressure, maintaining constant blood flow despite systemic changes. Tubuloglomerular feedback is mediated by the juxtaglomerular apparatus, particularly the macula densa cells in the distal tubule that sense changes in tubular NaCl delivery. Increased NaCl delivery to the macula densa triggers ATP/adenosine-mediated afferent arteriolar constriction (and sometimes reduction in renin release), lowering GFR; decreased NaCl triggers afferent dilation and increased renin release.

Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) activity also regulates GFR. Angiotensin II preferentially constricts the efferent arteriole more than the afferent at physiological concentrations, increasing glomerular capillary pressure and helping preserve GFR when renal perfusion pressure is low. Excessive angiotensin II (or use of vasoconstrictors) can alter filtration dynamics and renal oxygen consumption.

In pathologic states—sepsis, hypovolemia, use of ACE inhibitors/ARBs or NSAIDs—these autoregulatory mechanisms may fail or be therapeutically manipulated, changing GFR and predisposing to AKI. For example, NSAID-induced inhibition of prostaglandins impairs afferent dilation, while ACE inhibitors reduce efferent tone, both potentially lowering GFR in states of decreased renal perfusion.

# Tubular epithelial cell physiology and solute transport

The nephron is subdivided into segments with specialized transport functions determined by epithelial cell polarity, transporter protein expression, and energy requirements. Each segment’s physiology explains typical patterns of injury and dysfunction in AKI.

Proximal tubule: The proximal tubule (PT) reabsorbs approximately 60–70% of filtered sodium, water, and nearly all filtered bicarbonate, glucose, amino acids, and low-molecular-weight proteins. PT epithelial cells are highly metabolic, rich in mitochondria, and have dense brush borders (apical microvilli) to increase surface area for reabsorption. Transport is driven primarily by basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase that maintains a low intracellular Na+ concentration, providing the driving force for apical Na+-dependent cotransporters: SGLT2 (early PT) and SGLT1 (late PT) for glucose, Na+/H+ exchangers (NHE3) for sodium and bicarbonate reabsorption, and various amino acid transporters. The PT also handles organic anions and cations via OATs and OCTs that participate in secretion. Because of active transport, the PT has a high oxygen demand and is particularly susceptible to ischemic injury.

Loop of Henle: The thin descending limb is water permeable and facilitates passive water extraction; the thick ascending limb (TAL) is impermeable to water and uses the Na+/K+/2Cl− cotransporter (NKCC2) on the apical membrane and Na+/K+-ATPase basolaterally to reabsorb NaCl without water, generating dilution of tubular fluid and contributing to the cortico-medullary osmotic gradient. The TAL also secretes potassium back into the lumen via ROMK channels, creating a lumen-positive potential that drives paracellular reabsorption of divalent cations (Ca2+, Mg2+). Loop diuretics block NKCC2, disrupting concentrating ability and divalent cation reabsorption.

Distal convoluted tubule: The early DCT uses the Na+/Cl− cotransporter (NCC) for sodium chloride reabsorption and contributes to fine-tuning of electrolytes. The late DCT and connecting tubule express epithelial sodium channels (ENaC) under aldosterone regulation, integrating sodium reabsorption with potassium secretion. Collecting ducts: Principal cells in the cortical collecting duct respond to vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone, ADH) by inserting aquaporin-2 water channels on the apical membrane, permitting water reabsorption and concentrating urine. Intercalated cells handle acid–base balance via H+-ATPase and H+/K+-ATPase and bicarbonate transporters.

Each transport process has characteristic energy requirements: PT and TAL are highly oxidative due to abundant Na+/K+-ATPase activity. Therefore, these segments are the most vulnerable to hypoxic or toxic injury. Injury to specific transporters produces recognizable syndromes (e.g., Fanconi syndrome from generalized PT dysfunction, Bartter-like syndromes from TAL injury).

# Oxygen delivery, susceptibility to ischemia, and mechanisms of acute tubular necrosis (ATN)

Renal oxygen delivery depends on RBF, oxygen content of blood, and microvascular distribution. Although RBF is high, the renal oxygen consumption is also high because active transport of solutes (especially sodium reabsorption via Na+/K+-ATPase) accounts for most renal metabolic demand. The outer medulla—particularly the S3 segment of the proximal tubule and the straight portion of the proximal tubule and TAL—has a high oxygen demand because of intense active transport and relatively low blood flow, making it uniquely susceptible to ischemia.

Ischemic ATN arises when a critical reduction in renal perfusion or oxygen delivery causes tubular epithelial cell injury and death. Mechanisms include ATP depletion, loss of cytoskeletal integrity, disruption of cell polarity, impaired tight junctions, and altered transporter localization. ATP depletion causes failure of Na+/K+-ATPase, leading to intracellular Na+ and Ca2+ accumulation, cell swelling, and activation of degradative enzymes (e.g., proteases, phospholipases). Cytoskeletal breakdown causes loss of brush border in proximal tubular cells, detachment from the basement membrane, luminal obstruction by sloughed cells and casts, and tubular backleak of filtrate—collectively reducing effective GFR.

Endothelial injury and microvascular dysfunction amplify ischemic damage. Endothelial cell swelling, leukocyte adhesion, and microthrombosis reduce capillary perfusion and prolong hypoxia. The inflammatory response—mediated by cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6), complement activation, and recruited neutrophils and macrophages—causes further injury. Mitochondrial injury is central: mitochondrial permeability transition leads to loss of membrane potential, release of pro-apoptotic factors, and cell death via apoptosis or necrosis.

Toxic ATN results from nephrotoxins (aminoglycosides, radiographic contrast, myoglobin, hemoglobin, cisplatin) that either are taken up by proximal tubule cells and disrupt cellular metabolism, or cause direct oxidative stress and mitochondrial injury. Some toxins target specific transporters or organelles; for example, aminoglycosides are endocytosed and concentrate in lysosomes, causing phospholipidosis and proximal tubular dysfunction.

The morphological hallmark of ATN is tubular epithelial cell necrosis, attenuation or loss of brush border, and luminal debris with casts. Clinically, ATN typically follows an inciting event (hypotension, sepsis, nephrotoxin) by days and is associated with oliguria in severe cases, rising serum creatinine, electrolyte disturbances (hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis), and impaired concentrating ability. Recovery involves dedifferentiation, proliferation, and migration of surviving tubular epithelial cells to repopulate denuded basement membranes, but extensive necrosis or persistent microvascular injury can lead to fibrosis and chronic kidney disease.

# Renal handling of water, electrolytes and acid–base

Water handling: Water filtration at the glomerulus is isotonic to plasma. Proximal tubule reabsorbs approximately 65–70% of filtered water iso-osmotically, tied to solute reabsorption. Loop of Henle and collecting ducts enable generation of hypotonic or hypertonic urine by separating solute and water reabsorption: the TAL reabsorbs NaCl without water to dilute tubular fluid; the countercurrent multiplier establishes a corticomedullary osmotic gradient. Vasopressin acts on V2 receptors in principal cells to insert aquaporin-2 channels, permitting facultative water reabsorption and concentration of urine. In absence of ADH, water is not reabsorbed in collecting ducts, resulting in dilute urine (diabetes insipidus).

Sodium: Filtered load of sodium equals plasma sodium concentration times GFR; daily reabsorption is tightly regulated to maintain volume and blood pressure. Using approximate percentages: proximal tubule reabsorbs ~65–70% of filtered Na+, TAL ~25%, DCT ~5–10%, and collecting duct fine-tunes reabsorption under aldosterone. Hormonal regulators: aldosterone increases ENaC expression and Na+/K+-ATPase activity to increase Na+ reabsorption and K+ secretion; natriuretic peptides (ANP, BNP) antagonize Na+ reabsorption and dilate afferent arterioles to promote natriuresis.

Potassium: The kidneys excrete the majority of daily potassium intake. PT and TAL reabsorb most filtered K+; secretion in the cortical collecting duct via principal cells is the main regulated process. K+ secretion depends on distal tubular flow rate, luminal sodium delivery, and aldosterone. In AKI, reduced GFR and impaired secretion lead to hyperkalemia, particularly dangerous when oliguria is present.

Calcium, phosphorus, magnesium: Paracellular reabsorption in the PT and TAL handles much of filtered calcium and magnesium. TAL lumen-positive transepithelial voltage drives paracellular reabsorption of divalent cations; loop diuretics disrupt this. Distal fine-tuning of calcium reabsorption occurs under hormonal control by PTH, which increases Ca2+ reabsorption in the DCT.

Acid–base: Kidneys maintain acid–base via bicarbonate reclamation, generation of new bicarbonate (via ammoniagenesis and titratable acid), and net H+ excretion. PT reclaims filtered bicarbonate using NHE3 and carbonic anhydrase. α-Intercalated cells secrete H+ actively and generate new bicarbonate via H+-ATPase and H+/K+-ATPase; NH4+ production in the PT and TAL and its handling by the collecting duct is a major component of net acid excretion. Disturbance of proximal bicarbonate reclamation leads to proximal (type 2) renal tubular acidosis; distal tubular defects produce distal (type 1) RTA.

In AKI, these functions are impaired: impaired water handling leads to inability to concentrate or dilute urine, impaired acid excretion produces metabolic acidosis, and impaired K+ excretion produces hyperkalemia.

# Pathophysiologic responses to injury: inflammation, fibrosis and repair

The kidney responds to injury through intertwined processes of inflammation, cell death, regeneration, and fibrosis. Initial injury—ischemic, toxic, or immune-mediated—elicits an innate immune response. Injured tubular epithelial cells release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as HMGB1 and heat-shock proteins that activate Toll-like receptors on resident and infiltrating immune cells. Endothelial activation increases adhesion molecule expression (ICAM-1, VCAM), recruiting neutrophils and monocytes. Cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), chemokines (MCP-1), and complement amplify inflammation, increase vascular permeability, and can directly injure renal cells.

Cell death occurs via necrosis, necroptosis, and apoptosis. Necrotic cells provokes more inflammation through release of intracellular contents; apoptosis is a more regulated, less inflammatory process. The balance of programmed cell death vs. necrosis influences recovery.

Repair begins with dedifferentiation of surviving tubular epithelial cells, upregulation of developmental pathways (e.g., Wnt/β-catenin), and proliferation to replace lost epithelial cells. These processes can restore tubular architecture and function if basement membranes are intact and microvascular perfusion is reconstituted. However, maladaptive repair—characterized by persistent epithelial phenotypic change, chronic inflammation, pericyte activation, and fibroblast proliferation—leads to interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (IFTA), the hallmark of progression to chronic kidney disease. Myofibroblasts (derived from resident fibroblasts, pericytes, and possibly epithelial-mesenchymal transition) lay down excess extracellular matrix (collagens I and III, fibronectin). TGF-β is a central pro-fibrotic mediator that stimulates matrix synthesis and inhibits matrix degradation.

Microvascular rarefaction—loss of peritubular capillaries following injury—reduces oxygen delivery chronically, promotes hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) signaling and further fibrogenesis. Thus, initial AKI episodes can set in motion processes that lead to chronic structural damage and progressive renal dysfunction.

# Clinical correlates: markers of kidney function (creatinine, BUN), urine output interpretation

Serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) are the most commonly used markers of kidney function, but both have important limitations. Creatinine is produced at a nearly steady rate from muscle creatine metabolism and excreted primarily by glomerular filtration, with some tubular secretion. Normal serum creatinine varies with age, sex, and muscle mass; typical adult reference ranges are approximately 0.6–1.2 mg/dL for adult males and 0.5–1.1 mg/dL for adult females, but these are population references rather than absolute normals. Estimated GFR (eGFR) derived from serum creatinine (using MDRD or CKD-EPI equations) provides a normalized estimate of GFR per 1.73 m^2 body surface area; normal GFR in healthy young adults is approximately 120 mL/min/1.73 m^2 and declines with age (e.g., ~1 mL/min/1.73 m^2 per year after the 4th decade).

BUN (normal ~7–20 mg/dL) reflects urea generation (protein intake and catabolism) and renal excretion; it is reabsorbed passively in the proximal tubule and collecting duct depending on volume status. Therefore, BUN is less specific for GFR and increases with dehydration, catabolic states, or GI bleeding.

AKI is defined clinically by dynamic changes in serum creatinine and urine output. The KDIGO criteria define AKI as any of:

- Increase in serum creatinine by ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours; or
- Increase in serum creatinine to ≥1.5 times baseline, known or presumed to have occurred within prior 7 days; or
- Urine volume <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours.

AKI is staged by severity (KDIGO stages 1–3) using creatinine and urine output thresholds; these stages have prognostic significance for morbidity and mortality.

Urine output is a sensitive early marker of AKI, often preceding rises in creatinine. Oliguria is commonly defined as <400 mL/day or <0.5 mL/kg/h. However, urine output interpretation requires context: transient oliguria may result from pre-renal azotemia (volume depletion, hypotension) and often reverses with restoration of perfusion; persistent oliguria suggests intrinsic renal damage (e.g., ATN). Polyuria may occur in recovery phase or due to osmotic diuresis.

Urine studies aid diagnosis and distinction of pre-renal azotemia from intrinsic renal causes. Urinalysis with microscopy can reveal muddy brown granular casts and epithelial cells characteristic of ATN, or red blood cell casts of glomerulonephritis. Urine chemistries include fractional excretion of sodium (FeNa) and fractional excretion of urea (FeUrea), recognizing limitations (diuretics increase FeNa). FeNa is calculated as

FeNa = (Urine Na × Plasma Cr) / (Plasma Na × Urine Cr) × 100%

FeNa <1% classically suggests pre-renal azotemia (appropriate tubular sodium reabsorption); FeNa >2% suggests intrinsic tubular injury. FeUrea may be used in diuretic use; FeUrea <35% favors pre-renal states. Urine osmolality: in pre-renal azotemia urine is typically concentrated (>500 mOsm/kg), whereas in ATN urine is often isosthenuric (~300 mOsm/kg). However, these cutoffs are imperfect and should be interpreted in clinical context.

Creatinine kinetics: Serum creatinine rises only after an accumulation due to reduced GFR; there is a lag between GFR fall and detectable creatinine increase. Small reductions in GFR can produce modest creatinine increases that hide substantial loss of renal function, particularly in patients with low muscle mass.

Non-renal factors affect these markers: muscle wasting lowers creatinine production; high protein diet or catabolism raises BUN; medications (cimetidine, trimethoprim) reduce tubular secretion of creatinine and increase measured serum creatinine without true GFR change.

Biomarkers: Emerging biomarkers (e.g., NGAL, KIM-1, IL-18, TIMP-2 and IGFBP7) aim to detect tubular injury earlier than creatinine, but are still being integrated into clinical practice and have variable specificity.

Clinical decision points: In a patient with suspected AKI, assess hemodynamics and volume status, review nephrotoxic exposures and medications that alter renal autoregulation (NSAIDs, ACEi/ARB), order urinalysis and urine chemistries, monitor urine output closely, and decide on need for renal replacement therapy based on refractory hyperkalemia, severe acidosis, volume overload with compromised oxygenation, uremic complications (pericarditis, encephalopathy), or progressive azotemia with oliguria despite optimization.

Conclusion

An integrated understanding of renal anatomy—from macrovascular supply to the molecular components of the glomerular filtration barrier—and of physiological processes—autoregulation, segment-specific transport, and oxygen handling—provides the conceptual framework to understand why AKI occurs, how it manifests clinically, and what influences recovery. The unique microcirculation and high metabolic demand of specific nephron segments create hotspots of vulnerability (outer medulla, proximal tubule, TAL) that explain the pathologic patterns observed in ischemic and toxic ATN. Recognition of the limitations of serum creatinine and BUN, combined with careful assessment of urine output, urinalysis, and clinical context, is essential for timely diagnosis and management of AKI. Finally, the balance between reparative regeneration and maladaptive fibrogenesis determines whether AKI resolves fully or leads to chronic structural kidney disease.

# Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. It represents a spectrum of host–pathogen interactions in which an initially localized infection triggers systemic innate and adaptive immune responses, endothelial activation, coagulation perturbations, and profound metabolic and hemodynamic alterations. These processes can progress to septic shock—a form of distributive shock with persistent hypotension and cellular metabolic abnormalities—and to multiorgan dysfunction and death. Understanding the pathophysiologic mechanisms that link microbial recognition to microcirculatory failure, immune dysregulation, and metabolic collapse is essential for clinical reasoning about diagnosis, prognostication, and supportive management.

This chapter reviews foundational concepts needed to interpret the clinical course of patients with sepsis and septic shock. We begin by defining key terms according to contemporary consensus, and then examine the innate immune activation that initiates the inflammatory cascade. We describe microcirculatory and endothelial injury, coagulation–inflammation cross-talk, cardiovascular consequences including vasodilatation and septic cardiomyopathy, and metabolic derangements such as lactic acidosis and hypoglycemia. We also cover late-phase immunoparalysis and susceptibility to secondary infections, clinical scoring systems used for recognition and prognostication, and general principles of supportive care.

# Definitions: infection, sepsis, severe sepsis, septic shock (Sepsis-3 criteria)

Clinically, infection denotes the presence of a pathogen within a normally sterile body site or colonization with a microorganism that invokes an inflammatory response. Sepsis is not merely infection plus systemic inflammatory response; rather, contemporary definitions focus on organ dysfunction attributable to a dysregulated host response.

The Sepsis-3 consensus defines sepsis as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Operationally, organ dysfunction is identified as an acute increase in the Sequential [Sepsis-related] Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of 2 points or more consequent to the infection. This change correlates with an in-hospital mortality risk of approximately 10% or greater.

Severe sepsis as a separate term was removed in Sepsis-3; previously it designated sepsis with signs of organ hypoperfusion or dysfunction. Septic shock is defined more precisely: it is a subset of sepsis in which circulatory, cellular, and metabolic abnormalities are profound enough to substantially increase mortality. Clinically, septic shock is present when a patient with sepsis requires vasopressors to maintain a mean arterial pressure (MAP) of at least 65 mmHg and has a serum lactate level greater than $2$ mmol/L despite adequate volume resuscitation. This combination—vasopressor-dependent hypotension plus persistent hyperlactatemia—is associated with hospital mortality exceeding 40%.

Important laboratory and physiologic thresholds that appear in these definitions include MAP $\ge 65$ mmHg (goal for vasopressor support) and lactate $\le 2$ mmol/L (often used as a marker of tissue perfusion/metabolic stress). The SOFA score, which we review below, quantifies the extent of organ dysfunction across six systems and forms the basis for the operational sepsis definition.

# Innate immune activation: pattern-recognition receptors, cytokine storm

Recognition of pathogens by the host is mediated primarily through pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) expressed on innate immune cells (neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, dendritic cells), endothelial and epithelial cells, and some parenchymal cells. PRRs detect conserved microbial-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs; often called pathogen-associated molecular patterns or PAMPs) such as bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS), peptidoglycan, lipoteichoic acid, flagellin, and viral RNA. PRRs also recognize damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released by injured host cells (e.g., HMGB1, mitochondrial DNA, heat-shock proteins), so sterile injuries can trigger similar pathways.

Toll-like receptors (TLRs), nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain–like receptors (NLRs), C-type lectin receptors, RIG-I–like receptors, and cytosolic DNA sensors are key PRR families. For example, TLR4 recognizes LPS in concert with MD-2 and CD14, leading via MyD88-dependent and TRIF-dependent pathways to activation of NF-κB and IRF transcription factors. This results in rapid upregulation and release of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., tumor necrosis factor-alpha [TNF-α], interleukin-1β [IL-1β], IL-6), chemokines (e.g., IL-8), and type I interferons for viral responses.

The "cytokine storm" refers to an early, large-amplitude release of proinflammatory mediators that amplifies the local response and recruits leukocytes systemically. TNF-α and IL-1β increase vascular permeability and induce endothelial activation; IL-6 drives acute-phase protein synthesis (e.g., C-reactive protein) and contributes to fever and hemodynamic perturbations. Cytokine and chemokine gradients direct neutrophil migration, but excessive or dysregulated activation leads to tissue injury through release of reactive oxygen species, proteases, and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). Complement activation (C3a, C5a) further augments inflammation and can cause neutrophil paralysis at high concentrations.

While early hyperinflammation has classically dominated thinking about sepsis, it is now clear that proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses occur simultaneously. Anti-inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-10, transforming growth factor-beta) and regulatory pathways (e.g., glucocorticoid response, PD-1/PD-L1 signaling) are engaged early, and in some patients an immunosuppressive phenotype predominates, predisposing to secondary infections. The balance, timing, and cell-specific signaling determine whether the response is adaptive (pathogen control and resolution) or maladaptive (organ dysfunction).

# Microcirculatory dysfunction, endothelial activation and glycocalyx shedding

Microcirculatory failure is central to sepsis pathophysiology and explains how systemic vascular parameters (e.g., blood pressure, cardiac output) can appear restored despite persistent tissue hypoxia and organ dysfunction. The microcirculation—arterioles, capillaries, and venules—becomes heterogeneous in flow distribution: some capillaries are underperfused or non-perfused while others have normal or increased flow, producing functional shunting and impaired oxygen extraction.

Endothelial activation is a hallmark. Inflammatory mediators and shear stress alter endothelial phenotype: expression of adhesion molecules (P-selectin, E-selectin, ICAM-1, VCAM-1) increases leukocyte and platelet adhesion; tight and adherens junctions are disrupted; angiopoietin-Tie2 signaling is perturbed (e.g., increased angiopoietin-2 relative to angiopoietin-1), fostering barrier dysfunction. Endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) activity may be uncoupled under oxidative stress, while inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) is upregulated, increasing NO production and contributing to vasodilatation.

The endothelial glycocalyx—a carbohydrate-rich layer of proteoglycans, glycosaminoglycans (e.g., heparan sulfate), and associated plasma proteins—serves as a barrier to fluid and protein extravasation and modulates cell–cell interactions and mechanotransduction. In sepsis, enzymatic degradation and shedding of the glycocalyx occur under inflammatory stimuli, releasing syndecan-1 and heparan sulfate fragments into the circulation. Glycocalyx loss exacerbates capillary leak, edema, and microvascular thrombosis, and correlates with worse outcomes. Elevated plasma syndecan-1 levels are an experimental and sometimes clinical marker of glycocalyx degradation.

Consequences of microvascular and endothelial dysfunction include increased vascular permeability (capillary leak), tissue edema that worsens oxygen diffusion, abnormal vasomotor responses at the arteriolar level, impaired autoregulation, and propensity to microthrombi. These processes reduce effective tissue oxygen delivery and can cause cellular bioenergetic failure even when macrocirculatory targets are achieved.

# Coagulation–inflammation cross-talk and propagation of organ injury

Sepsis couples inflammation to coagulation through multiple, reciprocal mechanisms. Inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and endothelial injury upregulate tissue factor expression on monocytes and damaged endothelium, initiating the extrinsic coagulation cascade and thrombin generation. Thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin and activates platelets, promoting microvascular clot formation. Simultaneously, endogenous anticoagulant systems fail: antithrombin levels drop due to consumption and decreased synthesis, the protein C system is impaired (reduced thrombomodulin expression and decreased activated protein C generation), and tissue factor pathway inhibitor may be overwhelmed. Fibrinolysis is often suppressed via upregulation of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), promoting fibrin persistence.

This procoagulant shift can progress to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), characterized by widespread microthrombi, consumption of clotting factors and platelets, and a paradoxical bleeding tendency. Microvascular thrombosis compromises tissue perfusion and contributes to organ dysfunction independent of hypotension. In addition, components of the coagulation cascade (e.g., thrombin, fibrin degradation products) stimulate innate immune receptors and amplify inflammation, establishing a feed-forward loop.

Platelet activation contributes to endothelial injury and microvascular plugs; neutrophils and platelets interact to form NETs, which trap microbes but also provide a scaffold for thrombosis. Complement-coagulation interactions further intensify the response; for instance, complement activation fragments increase tissue factor expression and leukocyte recruitment.

Laboratory markers that suggest coagulopathy in sepsis include thrombocytopenia (platelet count may drop below normal range 150–450 x10^9/L), prolonged prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), elevated D-dimer, decreased fibrinogen when consumption is advanced (normal fibrinogen: 1.8–4.0 g/L), and falling antithrombin levels. The ISTH DIC score can be used to assess overt DIC. Coagulopathy is both a marker and mediator of disease severity.

# Cardiovascular effects: vasodilatation, capillary leak, septic cardiomyopathy

Sepsis-induced cardiovascular dysfunction arises from combined effects on vascular tone, preload, afterload, myocardial function, and microcirculatory distribution.

Vasodilatation is driven by inflammatory mediators (NO via iNOS, prostacyclin), endothelial-derived hyperpolarizing factors, and altered adrenergic receptor signaling. Elevated NO causes smooth muscle relaxation and systemic vasoplegia; iNOS expression is upregulated in vascular smooth muscle and cardiac tissue. Vasodilatation reduces systemic vascular resistance and contributes to hypotension. Endothelial dysfunction and capillary leak cause intravascular volume depletion and decreased effective circulating volume despite normal or increased total body fluid.

Capillary leak leads to extravasation of plasma proteins and fluid into the interstitium, reducing preload and further compromising organ perfusion. Hypoalbuminemia from capillary leakage and acute-phase responses lowers oncotic pressure and worsens edema.

Septic cardiomyopathy is a reversible myocardial depression observed in a subset of patients. It is characterized by biventricular dilatation, reduced ejection fraction, and impaired contractile reserve; however, preserved or increased cardiac index may coexist because of reduced afterload. Mechanisms include circulating myocardial depressant substances (TNF-α, IL-1, nitric oxide), mitochondrial dysfunction in cardiomyocytes, impaired intracellular calcium handling, and altered β-adrenergic receptor signaling. Myocardial ischemia from microthrombi and supply–demand mismatch may also contribute. Cardiac biomarkers (troponin I/T) are often elevated, reflecting myocardial injury, but are not specific for ischemic coronary events.

Clinically, the circulatory phenotype in sepsis may range from "warm" shock—characterized by low systemic vascular resistance, high cardiac output, warm peripheries, and bounding pulses—to "cold" shock with low output and cool extremities. The initial phase frequently shows high cardiac output; progressive myocardial depression or exhaustion of compensatory mechanisms can transition patients to a low-output state. Hemodynamic management targets restoration of effective circulating volume and vascular tone; vasopressor therapy is guided by a MAP target (commonly MAP $\ge 65$ mmHg), though individualization (e.g., chronic hypertension) may require higher targets.

# Metabolic consequences: lactic acidosis, hypoglycaemia in sepsis

Sepsis profoundly disturbs cellular and systemic metabolism. Lactic acidosis is a key metabolic marker and prognostic indicator. Elevated blood lactate may arise from traditional anaerobic metabolism due to hypoperfusion and tissue hypoxia, but in sepsis additional mechanisms contribute: adrenergic-driven aerobic glycolysis (beta-2 adrenergic stimulation increases Na+/K+ ATPase activity and glycolytic flux), impaired pyruvate dehydrogenase activity, mitochondrial dysfunction that limits oxidative phosphorylation, and decreased clearance of lactate by the liver and kidneys. Thus, lactate should be interpreted as a marker of altered cellular metabolism and clearance rather than a direct measure of regional hypoxia alone. A lactate concentration above $2$ mmol/L denotes abnormal metabolism; higher values correlate with increased mortality, and lactate clearance over time has prognostic and therapeutic significance.

Acidosis in sepsis is typically mixed (lactic plus metabolic and sometimes respiratory contributions). Acidemia can impair myocardial contractility and vascular responsiveness to catecholamines, creating a vicious cycle that worsens shock.

Hypoglycemia is less common than hyperglycemia in sepsis but occurs, particularly in vulnerable populations (severe liver failure, adrenal insufficiency, infants, malnourished patients). Mechanisms include increased glucose utilization by activated leukocytes, impaired hepatic gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis due to hepatic dysfunction, adrenal insufficiency or impaired counterregulatory hormone responses, and increased insulin levels (either endogenous or exogenous). Hypoglycemia in sepsis is ominous because it reflects severe metabolic decompensation and is associated with higher mortality. Clinicians should monitor glucose frequently; common ICU practice targets moderate glycemic control rather than tight control to avoid hypoglycemia (guidelines often recommend initiating insulin therapy when glucose persistently exceeds $10$ mmol/L and targeting a range of about $6$–$10$ mmol/L, recognizing variation by protocol and patient).

Sepsis also causes alterations in lipid metabolism, protein catabolism, and micronutrient handling. Catabolism provides substrates for gluconeogenesis but contributes to muscle wasting. Mitochondrial dysfunction—impaired electron transport chain activity, oxidative damage, and uncoupling—plays a central role in the bioenergetic failure of organs in sepsis and helps explain why restoring macrocirculatory parameters may not reverse cellular metabolic insufficiency.

# Immunoparalysis and secondary infections

After or even concurrent with the initial hyperinflammatory phase, many patients develop features of immune suppression—often termed immunoparalysis. This state is characterized by apoptosis and depletion of immune effector cells (particularly lymphocytes), functional impairment of antigen-presenting cells, and reduced expression of activation markers such as HLA-DR on monocytes. Lymphocyte apoptosis reduces numbers of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and B cells; surviving cells may exhibit exhaustion phenotypes with upregulation of inhibitory receptors (e.g., PD-1). Monocyte and macrophage dysfunction manifests as decreased cytokine production to secondary stimuli and impaired microbial killing.

The net effect is increased susceptibility to opportunistic and nosocomial infections, reactivation of latent viruses (e.g., cytomegalovirus, herpesviruses), and poor vaccine responsiveness. Biomarkers associated with immunoparalysis include low monocyte HLA-DR expression (values below commonly cited thresholds such as $<30\%$ HLA-DR+ monocytes or absolute measures vary by assay), lymphopenia, and persistently suppressed cytokine production upon ex vivo stimulation.

Clinically, immunoparalysis may be suspected in patients with prolonged ICU courses, recurrent or new infections, impaired wound healing, or colonization with multidrug-resistant organisms. Therapeutic strategies under investigation include immunostimulatory approaches (e.g., interferon-gamma, granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor, IL-7, anti–PD-1/PD-L1 blockade) but are not yet standard care. Recognition of immunosuppression impacts decisions about prophylaxis, surveillance, de-escalation of antibiotics, and infection control.

# Clinical markers and prognostic scoring (qSOFA, SOFA)

Recognizing sepsis early and estimating prognosis are essential. The Sepsis-3 working group proposed two pragmatic tools: qSOFA for rapid bedside screening and SOFA for quantifying organ dysfunction.

qSOFA (quick SOFA) uses three bedside criteria: respiratory rate $\ge 22$ breaths per minute, altered mentation (Glasgow Coma Scale <15 or clinician assessment of new mental status change), and systolic blood pressure $\le 100$ mmHg. The presence of two or more qSOFA criteria in a patient with suspected infection identifies those at greater risk of poor outcomes and who may require escalation of care. qSOFA is a screening tool and not diagnostic for sepsis.

The full SOFA score evaluates six organ systems—respiratory, coagulation, liver, cardiovascular, central nervous system, and renal—scored from 0 to 4 points each, with higher scores indicating worse dysfunction. Components typically include:

- Respiratory: PaO2/FiO2 ratio (with thresholds such as $<400$, $<300$, $<200$, $<100$ mmHg corresponding to increasing points).
- Coagulation: platelet count, with lower counts receiving higher points (e.g., platelets $<150$, $<100$, $<50$, $<20$ x10^9/L).
- Liver: bilirubin concentration, increasing points with higher bilirubin (normal total bilirubin $\sim0.1$–1.2 mg/dL or $<20$ µmol/L).
- Cardiovascular: MAP and vasopressor use (e.g., use of vasopressors and doses translate to higher points).
- Central nervous system: Glasgow Coma Scale score.
- Renal: creatinine or urine output (e.g., creatinine thresholds or oliguria criteria).

An acute increase in SOFA score of $\ge 2$ points in the setting of infection is consistent with sepsis per Sepsis-3. SOFA is widely used in research and critical care to track organ dysfunction. Several other scoring systems (APACHE II, SAPS II) exist for severity of illness and mortality prediction, but SOFA has the advantage of being organ-focused and dynamic.

Laboratory and physiologic markers that are commonly tracked in sepsis include lactate (as above), white blood cell count (normal range $\sim4$–11 x10^9/L), C-reactive protein, procalcitonin (as a marker of bacterial infection and to support stewardship decisions), blood oxygenation measures, electrolytes, renal function tests (creatinine), and coagulation tests. No single biomarker is definitive; trends and clinical context are paramount.

# General principles of supportive care in sepsis

Supportive care in sepsis aims to treat the infection (source control, timely antibiotics) and to stabilize host physiology while minimizing iatrogenic harm. While antimicrobial choices and source control are central, the following general supportive principles are fundamental.

Early recognition and prompt antimicrobial therapy: Administer empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics as soon as sepsis is recognized and within the first hour if septic shock is suspected, after obtaining appropriate cultures when feasible. Therapy should be de-escalated based on culture data and clinical response.

Hemodynamic resuscitation: Initial fluid resuscitation with crystalloids is recommended to restore intravascular volume and improve tissue perfusion. Common practice begins with a bolus of 30 mL/kg of isotonic crystalloid for patients with sepsis-induced hypoperfusion, while recognizing that individual responses vary and repeated assessments are required. Dynamic measures of fluid responsiveness (e.g., passive leg raise, stroke volume variation) are preferred over static measures. Vasopressors are indicated when hypotension persists after initial fluid resuscitation; norepinephrine is the first-line vasopressor to achieve MAP targets (commonly MAP $\ge 65$ mmHg). Vasopressin or epinephrine may be added as second-line agents. Titrate to individual patient needs; consider higher MAP targets in patients with chronic hypertension.

Monitoring and targets: Monitor hemodynamics, urine output (target urine output $\ge 0.5$ mL/kg/hr), lactate trends, and oxygenation. Lactate clearance is used as a surrogate for improving perfusion; failure to clear lactate predicts worse outcomes. Oxygen delivery and hemoglobin targets should be individualized, with transfusion considered for hemoglobin typically below $70$ g/L (7 g/dL) in most patients, except those with active bleeding or ischemic heart disease.

Respiratory support: Provide supplemental oxygen to maintain adequate oxygenation; institute mechanical ventilation when indicated, using lung-protective strategies (low tidal volumes, plateau pressures $<$30 cm H2O). Consider conservative fluid strategies in patients with established acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

Renal support: Provide renal replacement therapy for severe acute kidney injury with refractory fluid overload, severe electrolyte disturbances (e.g., hyperkalemia), severe acidosis, or uremia. Timing and modality should be individualized.

Glucose and nutrition: Avoid both severe hyperglycemia and iatrogenic hypoglycemia. Current practice generally targets a glucose range to avoid levels above $10$ mmol/L and to aim for minimum necessary insulin dosing to avoid hypoglycemia. Begin enteral nutrition when feasible; parenteral nutrition is reserved for those who cannot receive enteral feeds.

Steroids: Low-dose corticosteroids (e.g., hydrocortisone) are considered for patients with septic shock refractory to adequate fluid resuscitation and vasopressor therapy; they may hasten vasopressor weaning though mortality benefits are inconsistent across trials. Evaluate for adrenal insufficiency in clinical context.

Infection source control: Drainage of abscesses, removal of infected devices, debridement, or definitive surgical source control are often necessary and should not be delayed once the patient is stabilized.

Preventing complications: Implement measures to prevent ICU complications—venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, stress ulcer prophylaxis when indicated, early mobilization, and strict infection control practices. Limit unnecessary transfusions and invasive procedures.

Anticipate and manage organ dysfunctions: Provide specific organ support as needed—mechanical ventilation for respiratory failure, vasopressors and inotropes for circulatory failure, renal replacement for kidney failure, and tailored neurocritical care for neurologic dysfunction.

Follow-up and de-escalation: Reassess antibiotic therapy daily with culture results and clinical response, de-escalate appropriately, and narrow-spectrum therapy when feasible. Monitor for secondary infections that may arise due to immune suppression.

Palliative care: Recognize when the goals of care shift; involve palliative care for patients with refractory multiorgan failure or poor prognosis and for shared decision-making with families.

In all interventions, continuous reassessment, attention to physiologic response, and balancing risks and benefits are essential. Many aspects of sepsis care remain guided by evolving evidence; clinicians should integrate current guidelines with individualized patient considerations.

# Conclusion

Sepsis and septic shock arise from a complex interplay between host defenses and invading pathogens. Early innate immune activation and cytokine release, endothelial and glycocalyx injury with microcirculatory dysfunction, coagulation activation, cardiovascular collapse, metabolic derangements, and subsequent immunosuppression together drive organ failure and mortality. Contemporary definitions emphasize organ dysfunction (SOFA) and the hemodynamic-metabolic phenotype of septic shock (vasopressor dependence and hyperlactatemia). Clinical management combines rapid antimicrobial therapy and source control with careful hemodynamic, respiratory, renal, and metabolic support. Understanding the mechanisms discussed in this chapter helps clinicians interpret clinical findings, use scoring systems appropriately, and apply supportive measures in a way that addresses both macrocirculatory targets and the cellular dysfunction that underlies sepsis.

Introduction

Hemostasis is the physiologic process that maintains blood in a fluid state in the vasculature while permitting rapid and localized clot formation after vascular injury. It represents the integrated activity of the vessel wall, platelets, plasma coagulation factors, and the fibrinolytic system, balanced by multiple regulatory mechanisms. Disruption of this balance can result in either pathologic bleeding or thrombosis. Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is a paradigmatic dysregulation in which widespread activation of coagulation leads to microvascular thrombosis, consumption of platelets and coagulation factors, and secondary activation of fibrinolysis, producing a clinical spectrum that ranges from subtle laboratory abnormalities to life-threatening hemorrhage and multi‑organ failure. This chapter reviews the physiologic pathways of coagulation and fibrinolysis, platelet function in primary hemostasis, the pathogenesis of DIC and related coagulopathies, laboratory tests used to evaluate coagulation, interpretation patterns that help distinguish DIC from other disorders, clinical manifestations and complications, and general management principles including indications for blood product support.

# Overview of coagulation pathways (intrinsic, extrinsic, common) and regulators

The coagulation cascade is often presented as two initiation pathways — extrinsic and intrinsic — converging on a common pathway that culminates in thrombin generation and fibrin clot formation. This model is a conceptual framework; in vivo coagulation is a cell‑based process that emphasizes the roles of tissue factor–bearing cells and platelets as surfaces for complex assembly.

The extrinsic pathway is initiated when tissue factor (TF), a transmembrane glycoprotein not normally in contact with blood, becomes exposed to plasma after vascular injury, endothelial disruption, or expression by activated monocytes. TF binds factor VII (FVII), and the TF–FVIIa complex activates both factor X (FX) and factor IX (FIX). Activation of FX marks entry into the common pathway.

The intrinsic pathway describes activation of coagulation by contact and amplification steps. Factor XII (FXII) can be activated by negatively charged surfaces, leading to a cascade activating factor XI (FXI) and then FIX. Activated FIX (FIXa), together with its cofactor FVIIIa on phospholipid surfaces, activates FX. In clinical practice, deficiencies of FXII do not typically cause bleeding, and the intrinsic pathway concept is best appreciated as part of the amplification phase in vivo where FIX and FVIII are crucial for sustained thrombin generation.

The common pathway begins with activation of FX to FXa. FXa, complexed with FVa on phospholipid surfaces and in the presence of calcium, converts prothrombin (FII) to thrombin (FIIa). Thrombin has multiple central roles: it converts soluble fibrinogen to insoluble fibrin, activates FXIII to FXIIIa to stabilize fibrin polymers, and provides potent positive feedback by activating FV, FVIII, and FXI, enhancing further thrombin generation. Thrombin is also a potent platelet activator.

Several physiologic regulators restrain coagulation and maintain balance. Antithrombin (AT) inactivates thrombin and FXa (and to some extent FIXa and FXIa); its activity is accelerated by heparan sulfate on endothelial surfaces and by pharmacologic heparins. The protein C pathway provides an important endothelial cell–dependent anticoagulant mechanism: thrombin bound to thrombomodulin on endothelial cells activates protein C, and activated protein C (with its cofactor protein S) proteolytically inactivates FVa and FVIIIa, reducing thrombin generation. Tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) primarily inhibits the TF–FVIIa complex and FXa. These checks limit excessive clot propagation to sites of vascular injury.

Clinically relevant laboratory correlates appear in coagulation tests: the prothrombin time (PT) principally reflects extrinsic pathway activity (sensitive to FVII, FX, FV, prothrombin, and fibrinogen), while the activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) reflects the intrinsic and common pathways (sensitive to deficiencies of FVIII, FIX, FXI, FXII, FX, FV, prothrombin, and fibrinogen). Understanding these relationships guides interpretation of abnormal coagulation tests in clinical coagulopathies.

# Platelet function and primary hemostasis

Primary hemostasis refers to the initial vascular response to injury: vasoconstriction, platelet adhesion, activation, aggregation, and formation of a platelet plug. Platelets are small anucleate cell fragments derived from megakaryocytes that play a central role in arrest of bleeding and as a procoagulant surface for secondary hemostasis.

Endothelial disruption exposes subendothelial matrix proteins such as collagen and von Willebrand factor (vWF). vWF binds to platelet glycoprotein Ib-IX-V complex (GPIb) under high shear conditions and to collagen via platelet receptors, mediating initial tethering. Adhesion is followed by platelet activation, signaled by engagement of collagen receptors (GPVI), thrombin receptors (PAR-1, PAR-4), ADP receptors (P2Y1 and P2Y12), and thromboxane A2 production. Activation triggers intracellular calcium fluxes, shape change, granule secretion (alpha and dense granules), and upregulation of the platelet integrin GPIIb/IIIa (also called αIIbβ3), which binds fibrinogen and vWF to cross-link platelets and form aggregates.

Platelet granules release important mediators: dense granules contain ADP, calcium, and serotonin that amplify recruitment and activation; alpha granules release fibrinogen, vWF, platelet factor 4, and growth factors. The platelets’ negatively charged phospholipid surface exposes phosphatidylserine, providing a catalytic membrane for assembly of coagulation factor complexes (tenase and prothrombinase), which accelerate thrombin generation. Thus platelets are essential not only for forming the primary plug but also as a scaffold for the secondary fibrin clot.

Quantitative platelet defects (thrombocytopenia) and qualitative platelet disorders (e.g., inherited GPIIb/IIIa defects, uremic platelet dysfunction, antiplatelet drugs such as aspirin and P2Y12 inhibitors) impair primary hemostasis and cause mucocutaneous bleeding (epistaxis, gingival bleeding, petechiae, menorrhagia). The peripheral blood smear and platelet function testing (e.g., bleeding time historically, platelet function assays, aggregometry, PFA-100/200, and point-of-care tests) help evaluate platelet disorders. Normal platelet count ranges are typically 150–450 x 10^9/L; bleeding risk rises with counts below approximately $50 \times 10^9$/L and becomes high for spontaneous bleeding when counts fall below $20 \times 10^9$/L, though clinical context and platelet function modify these thresholds.

# Fibrinolysis and its regulation

Fibrinolysis is the physiologic process that degrades fibrin clots, restoring vascular patency after repair. The key effector is plasmin, a serine protease formed by proteolytic cleavage of plasminogen. Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA), produced by endothelial cells, and urokinase‑type plasminogen activator (uPA) catalyze plasminogen activation. tPA preferentially activates plasminogen bound to fibrin, providing localized fibrinolysis.

Regulators tightly control fibrinolysis. Plasminogen activator inhibitor‑1 (PAI‑1), produced by endothelium and adipocytes, inhibits tPA (and uPA), reducing plasmin formation. Alpha-2‑antiplasmin is the major circulating inhibitor of plasmin, rapidly neutralizing free plasmin to limit systemic fibrinolysis. Thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI) removes C‑terminal lysine residues from partially degraded fibrin, reducing plasminogen and tPA binding and thereby slowing fibrinolysis.

An imbalance can lead to either hypofibrinolysis, contributing to persistent thrombosis, or hyperfibrinolysis, producing severe bleeding with low fibrinogen and elevated fibrin/fibrinogen degradation products. Hyperfibrinolysis is classically seen in trauma, certain malignancies, and some liver transplant settings. Laboratory assessment of fibrinolysis includes tests for fibrin degradation products and D-dimer (a specific degradation product of cross-linked fibrin) and global assays such as thromboelastography (TEG) or rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) which can demonstrate hyperfibrinolytic profiles. Normal plasma fibrinogen concentration is roughly 200–400 mg/dL (2.0–4.0 g/L), and D-dimer is usually low; however, D-dimer reference ranges vary by assay—values above the assay‑specific cutoff (often >0.5 μg/mL FEU) are considered elevated but must be interpreted in clinical context.

# Pathogenesis of DIC: causes, microthrombosis and bleeding

Disseminated intravascular coagulation is not a primary disease but a systemic, maladaptive activation of coagulation secondary to various underlying disorders. The core pathophysiology involves widespread generation of thrombin, leading to fibrin deposition within the microcirculation, consumption of platelets and coagulation factors, and activation of fibrinolysis. The net clinical phenotype depends on the balance and tempo between coagulation activation, consumption, and fibrinolysis: some patients present primarily with thrombosis and organ ischemia, others with bleeding, and many with both.

Common triggers of DIC include severe sepsis (especially gram-negative sepsis with high levels of endotoxin and cytokines), severe trauma and massive tissue injury, obstetric complications (placental abruption, amniotic fluid embolism, retained dead fetus), malignancy (especially acute promyelocytic leukemia and mucinous adenocarcinomas), severe transfusion reactions, severe hemolysis, and major vascular events such as aortic aneurysm rupture. In sepsis, inflammatory cytokines (e.g., tumor necrosis factor, interleukin‑6) and endotoxin upregulate tissue factor expression on monocytes and endothelial cells, downregulate anticoagulant mechanisms (e.g., thrombomodulin and the protein C pathway), and increase PAI‑1, favoring thrombosis and impaired fibrinolysis. In obstetric DIC or acute promyelocytic leukemia, release of procoagulant factors and annexin II–mediated plasminogen activation can drive simultaneous coagulation and hyperfibrinolysis.

Microvascular thrombosis is a central feature: widespread fibrin deposition in capillaries and arterioles can compromise organ perfusion, leading to ischemic organ dysfunction — commonly renal failure, lung injury (manifesting as hypoxemia or acute respiratory distress syndrome), and hepatic compromise. Concurrent consumptive coagulopathy depletes platelets and clotting factors; coupled with secondary fibrinolysis, this yields bleeding from invasive procedures, mucocutaneous sites, and potential catastrophic hemorrhages (e.g., intracranial bleeding, massive gastrointestinal bleeding). The relative contribution of fibrinolysis varies: in “consumptive” or thrombotic DIC, fibrinolysis may be suppressed (high PAI‑1) leading to predominant thrombosis, while in “hyperfibrinolytic” DIC, excessive plasmin activity produces profound hypofibrinogenemia and bleeding.

Pathologically, DIC demonstrates deposition of fibrin in small vessels, microangiopathic hemolytic changes (schistocytes on peripheral smear) from mechanical shear stress across fibrin strands, and evidence of organ ischemia on histology. Biochemically, laboratory markers reflect coagulation activation (elevated thrombin generation markers), consumption (low fibrinogen, prolonged clotting times, thrombocytopenia), and fibrinolysis (elevated fibrin degradation products including D‑dimer).

# Laboratory tests: PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer, platelet count, peripheral smear

Laboratory evaluation of suspected DIC relies on a panel of tests that reflect different facets of hemostasis. No single test is diagnostic; instead, trends and patterns across multiple assays and clinical context determine the diagnosis.

Prothrombin time (PT) and international normalized ratio (INR): PT measures the time for plasma to clot after addition of tissue factor and calcium and is sensitive to deficiencies or consumption of FVII, FX, FV, prothrombin, and fibrinogen. Normal PT varies with reagent and laboratory, but typical values are around 11–13.5 seconds, with an INR standardized to 0.8–1.2 for a healthy person not receiving warfarin. In DIC, PT/INR is often prolonged due to consumption of vitamin K–dependent factors and other common pathway factors.

Activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT): aPTT measures the intrinsic and common pathways. Normal aPTT is assay-dependent, typically 25–35 seconds. In DIC, aPTT may be normal or prolonged; prolonged values reflect factor consumption. Isolated prolongation may also result from heparin exposure or specific factor deficiencies.

Fibrinogen: Fibrinogen concentration is both a substrate for clot formation and an acute‑phase reactant. Normal plasma fibrinogen is about 200–400 mg/dL (2.0–4.0 g/L). In DIC, fibrinogen is often decreased due to consumption, and very low levels (e.g., <100 mg/dL or <1.0 g/L) strongly suggest significant consumptive coagulopathy and risk of bleeding. However, in inflammatory states, fibrinogen may be elevated, potentially masking consumption in early or low-grade DIC.

D‑dimer and fibrin degradation products (FDPs): D‑dimer is a degradation product specific to cross-linked fibrin and reflects clot formation and subsequent fibrinolysis. FDP assays detect breakdown products from both fibrinogen and fibrin. D‑dimer is highly sensitive but nonspecific; elevated levels occur in DIC, thrombosis, infection, inflammation, pregnancy, recent surgery, and cancer. There is no universal cutoff; many assays consider >0.5 μg/mL FEU as elevated. In DIC, markedly elevated D‑dimer supports the diagnosis but must be interpreted with other tests.

Platelet count: Thrombocytopenia is a common finding in DIC as platelets are consumed in ongoing thrombus formation. Normal platelet count is 150–450 x 10^9/L. Counts may fall progressively, and trends are more informative than single values.

Peripheral blood smear: The smear may show schistocytes (fragmented red cells), reflecting mechanical damage from microvascular fibrin strands; schistocytes support a microangiopathic process but are not specific for DIC. The smear also allows assessment of platelet number and morphology and detection of platelet clumping (which can cause falsely low automated counts).

Other tests that can be informative include assays of coagulation factor levels, antithrombin activity (normally 80–120%), protein C and S levels, and markers of thrombin generation (e.g., prothrombin fragment 1+2, thrombin–antithrombin complexes) when available. Global assays such as thromboelastography (TEG) and rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) provide dynamic assessment of clot formation, strength, and lysis and can be helpful in guiding transfusion in complex cases.

Scoring systems integrate laboratory data and clinical context. The International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) established a DIC scoring algorithm that uses platelet count, elevated fibrin markers (e.g., D‑dimer), prolonged PT, and fibrinogen level. An ISTH overt DIC score ≥5 supports a diagnosis of overt DIC in the appropriate clinical setting.

# Interpretation patterns in DIC versus other coagulopathies

Interpreting coagulation tests requires synthesizing patterns across assays and considering the clinical context. DIC produces a characteristic but not pathognomonic pattern: thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT and aPTT, low fibrinogen, and markedly elevated D‑dimer/FDPs. The combination of consumptive coagulopathy with elevated fibrin degradation products distinguishes DIC from isolated platelet disorders or single-factor deficiencies.

Comparison with specific coagulopathies:

- Liver disease: The liver synthesizes most coagulation factors, fibrinogen, and regulatory proteins. Chronic liver disease often causes prolonged PT/INR and aPTT and thrombocytopenia (from splenic sequestration and decreased thrombopoietin), but D‑dimer may be less markedly elevated. Fibrinogen can be normal or low. Distinguishing features include a history of chronic hepatic dysfunction, absence of a precipitous consumptive process, and sometimes preserved thrombin generation despite abnormal tests due to decreased levels of both procoagulant and anticoagulant factors. Protein C and antithrombin levels may also be low. In acute liver failure, acute consumption and bleeding can mimic DIC.

- Vitamin K deficiency or warfarin therapy: Vitamin K–dependent factor deficiency (II, VII, IX, X) prolongs PT/INR preferentially, with lesser effects on aPTT depending on the factors involved. Platelet count and fibrinogen are usually normal, and D‑dimer is not elevated unless there is concurrent thrombosis or fibrinolysis. Correction with vitamin K or reversal agents normalizes the PT/INR over time.

- Heparin therapy: Heparin prolongs the aPTT (and anti‑Xa assays are used to monitor low molecular weight heparins). Heparin-induced prolonged aPTT may be confused with consumptive coagulopathy, but platelets may fall in heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) with thrombosis rather than bleeding; D‑dimer may be elevated in HIT-associated thrombosis. Heparin effect can be neutralized for diagnostic clarification.

- Thrombotic microangiopathies (TMA) — thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS): TMAs present with thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (schistocytes), and organ dysfunction. PT and aPTT are usually normal because TMAs are platelet‑driven disorders without systemic activation of plasma coagulation. Fibrinogen and D‑dimer are generally not markedly abnormal, distinguishing TMAs from DIC. ADAMTS13 activity assay and clinical features help differentiate TTP (very low ADAMTS13 activity, neurological signs) from DIC.

- Primary fibrinolysis: Conditions of primary hyperfibrinolysis (e.g., some liver disease states, trauma, or certain malignancies) cause bleeding with low fibrinogen and elevated FDPs, but D‑dimer may be relatively lower compared with DIC (because D‑dimer comes specifically from cross-linked fibrin). Elevated plasmin activity with decreased alpha‑2‑antiplasmin and rapid fibrinogen degradation favor primary hyperfibrinolysis.

- Inherited single-factor deficiencies: Hemophilia A (FVIII deficiency) and hemophilia B (FIX deficiency) present with prolonged aPTT, normal PT, normal platelet count, normal fibrinogen, and normal D‑dimer. Clinical bleeding is typically deep tissue, joint, and muscle bleeding rather than mucocutaneous.

Careful attention to the clinical context (infection, obstetric complication, malignancy, trauma, liver disease), serial laboratory trends (falling platelets, rising PT/aPTT, declining fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer), and use of scoring systems such as ISTH DIC score help distinguish DIC from these other disorders.

# Clinical manifestations and complications (bleeding, organ ischemia)

Clinical manifestations of DIC reflect the dual processes of thrombosis and hemorrhage. Presentations range from subclinical laboratory abnormalities to fulminant DIC with diffuse bleeding and multi‑organ failure.

Bleeding: Consumptive depletion of platelets and clotting factors and enhanced fibrinolysis produce bleeding that is often diffuse and mucocutaneous. Common bleeding manifestations include oozing from venipuncture or catheter sites, bleeding from mucous membranes (epistaxis, gingival bleeding), hematuria, gastrointestinal bleeding, and heavy uterine bleeding in obstetric DIC. In severe cases, life‑threatening hemorrhages such as intracranial hemorrhage or massive gastrointestinal bleeding may occur. Bleeding severity correlates with the degree of thrombocytopenia, hypofibrinogenemia, and prolonged clotting times.

Thrombosis and organ ischemia: Microvascular fibrin deposition can impair tissue perfusion and lead to ischemic dysfunction of organs. Acute renal failure can result from microthrombi in renal microvasculature; pulmonary microthrombi contribute to hypoxemia and acute respiratory distress; hepatic ischemia compounds coagulopathy; and ischemic limb complications may occur. Macrovascular thromboses (venous thromboembolism) can also occur in the prothrombotic milieu. Organ dysfunction in DIC is often a secondary contributor to mortality.

Other complications: Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia may produce schistocytes, elevated lactate dehydrogenase, and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. Secondary infections, transfusion‑related complications, and metabolic derangements (acid–base disturbances, electrolyte abnormalities) may complicate the clinical course. Chronic low‑grade DIC, sometimes seen in malignancy, can produce recurrent thromboembolic events rather than major bleeding.

Recognizing early signs—new thrombocytopenia, unexplained bleeding, worsening organ function, and abnormal coagulation tests—in the context of a precipitating illness is critical to timely diagnosis and management.

# General management principles and indications for blood product support

Management of DIC rests on two fundamental principles: treat the underlying cause and provide supportive therapy for the coagulopathy and its clinical consequences. Specific therapy directed at the trigger (e.g., prompt antibiotics and source control for sepsis, delivery or evacuation for obstetric causes, induction therapy for acute promyelocytic leukemia) is essential and often determinant of outcome.

Supportive hemostatic therapy is tailored to clinical status and laboratory results. Replacement of consumed components is generally indicated when there is clinically significant bleeding or when invasive procedures are necessary and laboratory values are at critical thresholds. Common transfusion thresholds and practices include transfusion of platelets for active bleeding or when invasive procedures are required; commonly used thresholds are platelet transfusion when count is <50 x 10^9/L for active bleeding or major procedures, and consideration of transfusion when count is <20 x 10^9/L even without bleeding. For central nervous system bleeding or imminent invasive procedures, higher platelet thresholds (e.g., >100 x 10^9/L) are often targeted.

Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) supplies clotting factors and is used to correct prolonged PT/aPTT in bleeding patients or those undergoing invasive procedures. Typical dosing is 10–20 mL/kg. Indications include significant bleeding with coagulopathy (e.g., PT/INR elevation, aPTT prolongation) or need for urgent invasive intervention. Cryoprecipitate is used to replace fibrinogen when levels are low and bleeding is present. Indications often include fibrinogen <100 mg/dL ($<1.0$ g/L) in bleeding patients, with typical cryoprecipitate dosing aiming to raise fibrinogen by about $50–100$ mg/dL per unit per 10 kg body weight; alternatives include fibrinogen concentrate where available.

Anticoagulation with heparin: In selected cases with predominant thrombotic manifestations (e.g., chronic compensated DIC with recurrent clotting) or in some early phases of severe DIC where microvascular thrombi predominate and bleeding is not a major problem, low‑dose unfractionated heparin may be considered to limit ongoing coagulation activation. However, in the presence of active bleeding or marked consumption, anticoagulation is generally contraindicated. Clinical judgment is required, and heparin should not be used indiscriminately.

Adjunctive therapies: Recombinant human activated protein C was studied for severe sepsis-associated DIC but is no longer in clinical use after trials failed to show consistent benefit and raised safety concerns. Antifibrinolytic agents (e.g., tranexamic acid, aminocaproic acid) are effective in primary hyperfibrinolysis or surgical bleeding but are contraindicated if there is ongoing microvascular thrombosis without clear hyperfibrinolysis because they may worsen thrombosis. In hyperfibrinolytic states with severe bleeding (e.g., some obstetric DIC scenarios), tranexamic acid can be life‑saving if used judiciously. Replacement of antithrombin concentrates is not routinely recommended but may be considered in experimental or severe cases with low antithrombin activity, particularly in some settings such as sepsis-related DIC, though evidence of benefit is limited.

Monitoring and serial testing: Repeated assessment of platelet count, PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, and D‑dimer guides therapy. Trends are more informative than static single values. Use of point-of-care viscoelastic testing (TEG/ROTEM) can provide faster assessment of coagulation dynamics and guide targeted transfusions (e.g., fibrinogen concentrate vs platelet transfusion vs FFP) particularly in massive bleeding or perioperative settings.

Practical decision points: Treat the underlying cause promptly. Provide blood products when there is active bleeding or when laboratory thresholds for safe procedures are not met (platelets, FFP, cryoprecipitate). Avoid routine use of antifibrinolytics without clear evidence of hyperfibrinolysis. Consider heparin only in selected thrombotic-dominant scenarios. Engage multidisciplinary teams (hematology, critical care, transfusion medicine) for complex cases.

Conclusion

Understanding the integrated physiology of platelets, coagulation pathways, and fibrinolysis is essential for diagnosing and managing DIC and related coagulopathies. DIC reflects systemic dysregulation with clinical manifestations spanning bleeding and thrombosis, and laboratory evaluation relies on patterns across PT/INR, aPTT, platelet count, fibrinogen, D‑dimer, and peripheral smear findings. Accurate interpretation of these tests in clinical context, prompt treatment of the underlying cause, and judicious supportive therapy with blood products and targeted interventions are the cornerstones of care.

Introduction

Thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs) are a group of clinicopathologic syndromes defined by endothelial injury in small vessels that produces microvascular thrombosis, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and organ ischemia. The unifying pathologic substrate is damage to arterioles and capillaries with platelet-rich thrombi that shear erythrocytes and occlude blood flow to target organs. Clinically TMAs form a spectrum: some disorders have a single clear cause (for example, Shiga toxin–associated hemolytic uremic syndrome), whereas others are defined by a shared mechanism (severe ADAMTS13 deficiency causing thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura). Rapid recognition and early management are critical because untreated disease carries high mortality and morbidity; conversely, prompt, targeted therapy (e.g., plasma exchange for TTP, supportive care and renal replacement for STEC-HUS, complement blockade for complement-mediated HUS) can dramatically change outcome.

This chapter presents the defining features and common presentations of TMA, an operational classification with pathophysiologic emphasis, an approach to the diagnostic workup (what to order and why), practical triage tools used while awaiting definitive testing, organ-specific manifestations to anticipate and monitor, prognostic factors, and general therapeutic principles with an emphasis on urgency and initial management.

# Defining features of TMA and common clinical presentations

The triad that defines TMA in clinical practice is microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and evidence of organ ischemia. MAHA is characterized by a normocytic, often normochromic anemia with reticulocytosis, elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), indirect hyperbilirubinemia, low or absent haptoglobin, and the presence of fragmented red cells (schistocytes) on peripheral smear. Thrombocytopenia results from platelet consumption in microvascular thrombi; platelet counts vary widely but are often moderately to severely reduced in primary TMAs. Organ dysfunction reflects ischemia from microvascular occlusion and may present in any organ system; kidneys and brain are most commonly involved, but the heart, lungs, gastrointestinal tract, and skin can also be affected.

Presentations vary by etiology and severity. Many patients present with nonspecific complaints—fatigue, pallor, and petechiae or bleeding related to thrombocytopenia—alongside signs or symptoms of organ dysfunction, such as oliguria or rising creatinine (renal involvement), altered mental status or focal neurologic deficits (central nervous system involvement), chest pain or troponin elevation (cardiac involvement), or abdominal pain and bloody diarrhea in infections like Shiga toxin–associated HUS. Fever and systemic inflammatory signs are possible but not required. Importantly, the classic “pentad” historically ascribed to TTP—fever, MAHA, thrombocytopenia, neurologic signs, and renal dysfunction—is now recognized as an unreliable diagnostic requirement; many patients have only the core features of MAHA and thrombocytopenia with variable organ involvement. Timely detection depends on a high index of suspicion in any patient with new anemia plus thrombocytopenia and signs of hemolysis.

# Classification: TTP, Shiga toxin–associated HUS, complement-mediated HUS, and secondary TMAs

A practical classification organizes TMAs into disorders driven by distinct mechanisms, because etiology guides therapy.

Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)
TTP is defined pathophysiologically by severe deficiency of the von Willebrand factor (vWF)-cleaving protease ADAMTS13, typically due to autoantibodies that inhibit ADAMTS13 activity or accelerate its clearance. Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency leads to accumulation of ultra-large vWF multimers that promote platelet aggregation in the microcirculation, producing disseminated microthrombi. TTP classically causes profound thrombocytopenia and neurologic manifestations, though renal involvement can occur. The onset may be abrupt, and mortality is high without treatment. ADAMTS13 activity below 10% of normal is the accepted threshold for “severe” deficiency that supports the diagnosis of TTP. ADAMTS13 testing distinguishes TTP from other TMAs and indicates need for plasma-based therapy and immunosuppression.

Shiga toxin–associated HUS (typical HUS, STEC-HUS)
Typical HUS follows infection with Shiga toxin–producing bacteria, most commonly enterohemorrhagic Escherichia coli O157:H7 or other serotypes and Shigella dysenteriae type 1. The Shiga toxin injures endothelial cells—especially in the kidney—by inhibiting protein synthesis and triggering a prothrombotic and inflammatory microenvironment. Clinical presentation usually includes prodromal bloody diarrhea followed by MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury. Children are disproportionately affected, but adults may be affected as well. Management is primarily supportive; antibiotics are controversial because some may increase toxin release and worsen outcome. ADAMTS13 activity in STEC-HUS is typically normal or only modestly decreased.

Complement-mediated HUS (atypical HUS; complement dysregulation)
Complement-mediated HUS (often termed atypical HUS or aHUS) results from dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway, due to inherited mutations in complement regulators (e.g., factor H, factor I, membrane cofactor protein/CD46) or acquired autoantibodies (e.g., anti–factor H). Uncontrolled activation of complement causes endothelial injury, platelet activation, and thrombotic microangiopathy, frequently with prominent renal involvement and variable extra-renal features. Complement-mediated HUS may be triggered by infection, pregnancy, medications, or transplantation in genetically susceptible individuals. Complement blockade with C5 inhibitors (e.g., eculizumab) is an effective targeted therapy.

Secondary TMAs
Secondary TMAs are heterogeneous and occur when a distinct process causes endothelial injury and TMA. Causes include drugs (e.g., quinine, calcineurin inhibitors, gemcitabine, some antiplatelet agents), pregnancy-related TMAs (pregnancy-associated severe preeclampsia, HELLP syndrome, and postpartum microangiopathies), malignancy-associated TMAs (particularly adenocarcinomas and metastatic disease), infections (disseminated infections, rickettsial disease), hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, severe systemic autoimmune disease (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus), and severe hypertension. Some of these entities have overlapping mechanisms with complement-mediated HUS or TTP; others are distinct and require removal of the inciting factor (e.g., stopping a causative drug, treating the underlying malignancy). ADAMTS13 activity in secondary TMAs is typically >10%, helping to distinguish them from classic TTP, but clinical judgment is required because mixed or sequential mechanisms can occur.

# Diagnostic workup: peripheral smear, schistocytes, hemolysis labs, ADAMTS13 activity, stool tests for Shiga toxin, complement studies

A stepwise diagnostic workup balances the need for immediate empiric therapy in life-threatening situations with the goal of identifying the underlying cause to guide specific treatment.

Initial bedside and laboratory tests
When TMA is suspected, order a complete blood count with platelet count, peripheral blood smear, reticulocyte count, serum LDH, indirect bilirubin, haptoglobin, coagulation studies (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen), renal function tests (serum creatinine, BUN), electrolytes, urinalysis, and markers of organ injury as clinically indicated (e.g., troponin, liver enzymes). Typical laboratory patterns of hemolysis include elevated LDH (often markedly elevated), elevated indirect bilirubin, decreased or undetectable haptoglobin, increased reticulocyte count, and negative or only mildly prolonged coagulation tests (distinguishing TMA from disseminated intravascular coagulation, which produces prominent coagulopathy and low fibrinogen). Expected normal ranges (approximate; institution-specific ranges vary) include hemoglobin roughly 120–170 g/L in men and 120–150 g/L in women, platelet count 150–450 × 10^9/L, serum creatinine ~0.6–1.2 mg/dL, LDH ~120–240 U/L, and haptoglobin 0.3–2.0 g/L. In TMA, platelet counts are decreased and hemoglobin decreased relative to baseline.

Peripheral smear and schistocytes
A careful peripheral smear is essential. Schistocytes (fragmented red cells) are a hallmark of MAHA. Quantification is semi-quantitative; many labs report percent schistocytes or grade them qualitatively. A commonly used diagnostic cutoff is the presence of schistocytes greater than 1% of red cells or the presence of clinically significant fragmentation correlated with other hemolytic indices. Important caveats: schistocytes are neither perfectly sensitive nor specific and can arise in prosthetic heart valves, severe burns, or other mechanical hemolysis. Correlate smear findings with hemolysis markers and clinical context.

ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor testing
Measuring ADAMTS13 activity (and anti-ADAMTS13 autoantibodies) is the single most important test to distinguish TTP from other TMAs. An ADAMTS13 activity level <10% strongly supports the diagnosis of TTP, particularly when accompanied by an ADAMTS13 inhibitor or anti-ADAMTS13 autoantibodies. Activity levels ≥10% argue against classic TTP; intermediate levels may require repeat testing and careful interpretation. Because ADAMTS13 assays are not available everywhere and may take days to result, empiric therapy (e.g., plasma exchange) may be started based on clinical suspicion and triage tools while awaiting results. Remember that some secondary TMAs can coexist with modest reductions in ADAMTS13, but severe deficiency is essentially diagnostic of TTP.

Microbial testing for STEC and Shiga toxin
In suspected STEC-HUS, obtain stool cultures for Shiga toxin–producing E. coli and perform assays for Shiga toxin (PCR or enzyme immunoassay). A history of recent bloody diarrhea, especially in children or during known outbreaks, heightens suspicion. A positive stool assay for Shiga toxin or EHEC indicates STEC-HUS and typically obviates the need for plasma exchange and points toward supportive care. Rapid multiplex PCR panels may expedite diagnosis.

Complement studies and genetic testing
When complement-mediated HUS is suspected (atypical HUS), initial studies include complement levels (C3 and C4), factor H and factor I levels if available, and testing for anti–complement factor H autoantibodies. Reduced C3 with normal C4 suggests alternative pathway activation but is neither sensitive nor specific. Genetic testing for mutations in complement regulatory genes (CFH, CFI, CD46/MCP, C3, CFB, THBD, etc.) helps confirm a predisposition and impacts long-term therapy decisions; however, results take weeks and do not guide acute management. Complement testing should be coordinated with reference laboratories and genetics counseling, and positive results must be interpreted with clinical context since many variants are of uncertain significance.

Other studies
Additional studies include pregnancy tests in women of childbearing age, hepatitis and HIV screening, blood cultures if infection suspected, testing for autoimmune disease markers (ANA, anti-dsDNA, anti-phospholipid antibodies) when indicated, and drug history review with consideration of drug levels (e.g., calcineurin inhibitors). Bone marrow biopsy is rarely indicated for initial diagnosis but may be needed if malignant infiltration or marrow failure is suspected.

# Clinical scoring tools (PLASMIC score) and triage while awaiting tests

Because ADAMTS13 assays are often delayed, clinical prediction tools have been developed to estimate the probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and to guide empiric therapy. The PLASMIC score is widely used and validated for adult patients. It assigns one point for each of the following seven variables:

1. Platelet count < \(30 \times 10^9/L\)
2. Hemolysis evidence (elevated indirect bilirubin or undetectable haptoglobin)
3. Absence of active cancer
4. Absence of a solid-organ or stem cell transplant
5. Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) < 90 fL
6. INR < 1.5
7. Creatinine < 2.0 mg/dL

Scores of 6–7 indicate a high probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (TTP), scores of 5 moderate probability, and scores ≤4 low probability. The PLASMIC score helps decide whether to initiate emergent plasma exchange and immunosuppression while awaiting ADAMTS13 results. It is most applicable to adult patients and less validated in children, pregnant patients, and those with complex comorbidities.

Triage principles while awaiting confirmatory testing
When TTP is strongly suspected (e.g., high PLASMIC score, severe thrombocytopenia, MAHA, and neurologic features), do not delay plasma exchange; start plasma therapy emergently and arrange for ADAMTS13 testing. Plasma exchange (PEX) should be initiated within hours if possible because delays increase mortality. In contrast, if STEC-HUS is likely (history of bloody diarrhea, positive stool toxin or culture), supportive care is appropriate and plasma exchange is not routinely helpful. If complement-mediated HUS is suspected (family history, recurrent episodes, low C3, lack of diarrhea, prominent renal failure), early discussion with nephrology and hematology and consideration of complement blockade should be undertaken; empiric complement inhibition may be considered in severe disease while awaiting confirmatory testing in consultation with specialists. For secondary TMAs, focus on treating the underlying cause (stop the offending drug, control malignancy or infection) while providing supportive care and considering targeted therapy in specific contexts.

# Organ-specific manifestations (renal, neurologic, cardiac, skin)

Renal manifestations
The kidney is a frequent target in TMAs, especially in STEC-HUS and complement-mediated HUS. Renal findings range from mild proteinuria and hematuria to severe acute kidney injury (AKI) with oliguria and the need for renal replacement therapy. Histologically, glomerular capillary and arteriolar thrombi are typical, with endothelial swelling and mesangiolysis. Clinically, monitor serum creatinine, urine output, urine sediment, electrolyte disturbances (particularly hyperkalemia), and acid-base status. Indications for dialysis are the usual ones (refractory hyperkalemia, severe acidosis, volume overload, uremic symptoms). Trajectories of renal recovery differ by cause: STEC-HUS in children often recovers renal function over weeks, whereas complement-mediated HUS may cause progressive or recurrent renal impairment requiring chronic dialysis or transplantation if not controlled.

Neurologic manifestations
Neurologic involvement is a hallmark of TTP but can occur in any TMA. Symptoms range from headache, confusion, and lethargy to focal deficits, seizures, visual changes, and coma. Neurologic signs may fluctuate rapidly. Pathophysiology involves microvascular occlusion and ischemia in the central nervous system and may not correlate directly with platelet count or creatinine. Neuroimaging (CT, MRI) can show ischemic lesions, but a normal scan does not exclude significant microvascular disease. Neurologic deterioration is an indication for immediate plasma exchange in suspected TTP and merits intensive monitoring, seizure prophylaxis as indicated, and avoidance of sedatives that complicate neurologic assessment.

Cardiac manifestations
Cardiac involvement is increasingly recognized and may manifest as myocardial ischemia, heart failure, arrhythmias, or sudden cardiac death. Troponin elevation is common in severe TMAs and is an independent prognostic marker. Microthrombi in coronary microvasculature, demand ischemia from anemia, and complement-mediated endothelial injury contribute to myocardial injury. ECG changes and echocardiography may reveal ischemia, reduced ejection fraction, or wall motion abnormalities. Management includes cardiology consultation, hemodynamic support, and treatment of the underlying TMA; antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapies are usually not helpful for microvascular thrombosis and may be contraindicated when thrombocytopenia is severe.

Skin and peripheral manifestations
Cutaneous findings include petechiae, purpura, and livedo reticularis due to thrombocytopenia and microvascular occlusion. Digital ischemia and necrosis can occur in severe cases. Skin lesions may be an early sign of systemic microvascular thrombosis. Biopsy of skin lesions can show microthrombi but is rarely required for diagnosis and may risk bleeding.

Other organ systems
Gastrointestinal manifestations, including abdominal pain, ileus, or bloody stools, are common in STEC-HUS and may occur in other TMAs. Pulmonary involvement can present as hypoxemia or pulmonary hemorrhage in severe disease. Pregnancy-associated TMAs may involve the placenta and cause fetal compromise. Always evaluate for multiorgan dysfunction and coordinate multidisciplinary care (nephrology, hematology, intensive care, neurology, cardiology) as appropriate.

# Prognostic factors

Prognosis in TMA varies by etiology, severity at presentation, and timeliness of therapy. Key prognostic factors include the underlying cause (TTP, STEC-HUS, complement-mediated HUS, or secondary TMA), degree of organ dysfunction at presentation, age, and comorbid conditions.

In TTP, early initiation of plasma exchange reduces mortality dramatically compared with historical untreated cohorts. Important adverse prognostic indicators include older age, severe neurologic involvement, delayed initiation of therapy, and high LDH. Relapse risk in immune-mediated TTP is variable and associated with persistent ADAMTS13 deficiency or incomplete immunologic remission; monitoring ADAMTS13 activity during remission helps stratify relapse risk.

In STEC-HUS, children generally have a favorable renal recovery rate, but long-term sequelae (hypertension, chronic kidney disease) can occur. Adults infected with STEC have higher mortality and more severe renal outcomes. In complement-mediated HUS, prognosis depends on genetic background and response to complement blockade; without targeted therapy many patients progress to end-stage renal disease. Detection of complement regulatory gene mutations correlates with higher risk of recurrence post-transplantation and informs prophylactic and therapeutic decisions.

Other prognostic markers include elevated troponin (cardiac involvement), need for renal replacement therapy at presentation, very low platelet counts, and persistent hemolysis despite therapy. Mortality remains substantial in delayed or untreated disease; therefore, rapid recognition and initiation of appropriate therapy are the dominant modifiable prognostic factors.

# General therapeutic principles and urgency of interventions

The management of TMA follows two parallel principles: 1) urgent, often empiric interventions to stabilize the patient and treat immediately life-threatening mechanisms (particularly TTP), and 2) cause-specific therapy once the underlying diagnosis is clarified.

Urgency and initial stabilization
Patients suspected of having TMA require admission, usually to an intensive care unit if there is organ dysfunction. Monitor hemodynamics, urine output, neurologic status, and laboratory parameters (CBC, hemolysis indices, renal function, electrolytes). Transfusion decisions are nuanced: red blood cell transfusion is indicated for symptomatic anemia or hemodynamic instability, but transfuse cautiously because raising hematocrit may increase viscosity; platelet transfusions are generally avoided in TTP unless there is life-threatening bleeding or an invasive procedure is required, because they may exacerbate microvascular thrombosis. Treat seizures and other acute complications per standard protocols.

Plasma exchange for suspected TTP
When clinical suspicion for TTP is high (e.g., high PLASMIC score or severe thrombocytopenia with MAHA and neurologic signs), start therapeutic plasma exchange (PEX) emergently—ideally within hours. PEX removes autoantibodies and supplies functional ADAMTS13 through plasma replacement, and is strongly associated with reduced mortality. Typical initial daily PEX uses plasma volumes of 1–1.5 plasma volumes exchanged with fresh frozen plasma or solvent/detergent-treated plasma until platelet count and hemolysis parameters normalize. Simultaneously start immunosuppression: corticosteroids are commonly given (e.g., prednisone 1 mg/kg or equivalent), and rituximab is often added early for immune-mediated TTP to decrease relapse risk and accelerate recovery. Caplacizumab, an anti–vWF nanobody that inhibits platelet–vWF interaction, shortens time to platelet normalization in immune TTP and is indicated in many settings but requires careful bleeding risk assessment and specialist input.

Supportive care for STEC-HUS and other TMAs
For STEC-HUS, management is largely supportive: aggressive fluid and electrolyte management, avoidance of antibiotics (controversial—many guidelines caution against antibiotics that increase toxin release), and dialysis as needed. Platelet transfusions are reserved for active bleeding or invasive procedures. Early pediatric nephrology involvement is essential, and long-term follow-up for blood pressure and renal function is recommended.

Complement blockade for complement-mediated HUS
In complement-mediated HUS, eculizumab (a monoclonal antibody to complement protein C5) is highly effective in halting microangiopathy and preserving renal function when initiated promptly. Vaccination against Neisseria meningitidis and appropriate prophylactic antibiotics are required prior to eculizumab initiation because of increased susceptibility to encapsulated organisms. Duration of therapy is individualized—some patients require lifelong therapy, while others may be able to discontinue after sustained remission with close monitoring; genetic findings and clinical course inform these decisions. Newer complement inhibitors targeting different pathway components are under investigation or approved in specific contexts.

Management of secondary TMAs
Treat underlying triggers: discontinue iron agents, implicated drugs, or immunogenic exposures; treat infections; control severe hypertension; and manage malignancy-related processes. In some secondary TMAs with severe disease, empiric PEX or complement blockade may be considered while the cause is clarified, but decisions should be individualized and made with specialty input.

Procedural considerations and monitoring
When PEX is used, central venous access and careful fluid, electrolyte, and calcium monitoring are necessary due to citrate anticoagulation. Monitor platelet count, LDH, bilirubin, and hemoglobin daily to guide response. For TTP, continue daily PEX until platelet count has normalized for at least two consecutive days and hemolysis markers have improved, then consider tapering; immunosuppression should be continued and adjusted based on clinical response and ADAMTS13 monitoring.

Summary and key decision points
Rapid recognition of TMA and early differentiation among TTP, STEC-HUS, complement-mediated HUS, and secondary TMAs determine therapy and outcome. Obtain the basic hemolysis laboratory panel and peripheral smear urgently, draw ADAMTS13 samples before starting plasma if possible, and use clinical tools (e.g., PLASMIC score) to estimate the probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency while awaiting results. Initiate plasma exchange and immunosuppression without delay when TTP is likely. In suspected complement-mediated HUS or severe, progressive renal disease without diarrhea, involve nephrology and consider early complement blockade. For STEC-HUS, focus on supportive care and renal replacement therapy when indicated. Throughout, multidisciplinary coordination, frequent reassessment, and attention to organ-specific complications are essential.

Concluding remarks

Understanding the pathophysiology that underlies each TMA subtype guides targeted therapy. Because presentations overlap and diagnostic tests are delayed, clinicians must balance empiric life-saving interventions with efforts to identify the precise etiology. Familiarity with the characteristic laboratory patterns, scoring systems such as PLASMIC, organ-specific manifestations, and the urgent indications for plasma exchange or complement inhibition will permit timely, evidence-informed care and improve patient outcomes.

Introduction

The complement system is a central component of innate immunity that links recognition of pathogens and altered self to effector mechanisms including opsonization, recruitment of inflammatory cells, and direct lysis through the membrane attack complex (MAC). Complement activation is tightly regulated because uncontrolled activation injures host tissues, particularly the vascular endothelium and glomerular capillaries. Understanding the molecular architecture of the classical, lectin and alternative pathways, the enzymology of C3 and C5 convertases, the terminal MAC, and the regulatory proteins that constrain activation is essential for interpreting clinical syndromes involving complement dysregulation and for proper use of laboratory tests that evaluate complement activity.

This chapter reviews the three canonical activation pathways, the steps that generate enzymatic convertases and the MAC, the principal fluid-phase and membrane-associated regulators (including factor H, factor I, factor B, membrane cofactor protein/MCP (CD46), decay-accelerating factor/DAF (CD55), and complement receptor 1/CR1), the pathophysiologic consequences of uncontrolled complement activation on endothelium, and practical considerations for laboratory measurement. Emphasis is placed on connecting basic mechanisms to clinical interpretation of complement assays, pre-analytical and treatment-related artifacts, and common pitfalls.

# Components and activation cascades of the classical, lectin and alternative pathways

Complement activation proceeds through three linked pathways—classical, lectin, and alternative—each with distinct recognition mechanisms but converging on proteolytic generation of C3 convertases that amplify the response.

The classical pathway is initiated when the C1 complex (comprised of C1q, two C1r and two C1s molecules) binds to immune complexes, specifically to Fc regions of IgG or IgM bound to antigen, or to certain pentraxins (e.g., C-reactive protein, serum amyloid P). C1q binding triggers conformational activation of C1r, which autoactivates and cleaves C1s. Activated C1s is a serine protease that cleaves C4 into C4a and C4b and C2 into C2a and C2b (historical naming varies; modern convention often designates the larger fragment as C2b). C4b covalently attaches to nearby surfaces via a thioester and then binds C2a to assemble the classical C3 convertase, C4b2a. This enzyme cleaves C3 into C3a (an anaphylatoxin) and C3b (an opsonin bearing a reactive thioester).

The lectin pathway uses pattern recognition molecules—mannose-binding lectin (MBL), ficolins or collectins—that bind carbohydrate or acetylated structures on microbial surfaces. Associated MBL-associated serine proteases (MASP-1 and MASP-2) are activated upon ligand binding. MASP-2 cleaves C4 and C2 in a manner analogous to C1s, generating the same C4b2a classical/lectin C3 convertase.

The alternative pathway provides a constitutive amplification loop and can be activated without antibodies. Spontaneous low-rate hydrolysis of the thioester on native C3 produces C3(H2O), which can bind factor B. Factor D cleaves factor B into Ba and Bb, generating a fluid-phase C3 convertase C3(H2O)Bb that cleaves additional C3 to produce C3b. Surface-deposited C3b can bind factor B; subsequent cleavage by factor D forms the surface-bound alternative pathway C3 convertase, C3bBb. Properdin stabilizes C3bBb, prolonging its half-life. The alternative pathway amplifies C3b deposition initiated by any pathway and is critical for opsonization and progression toward terminal pathway activation.

All three pathways converge at C3 cleavage and feed into a common terminal pathway in which formation of C5 convertases leads to MAC assembly. The interdependence is clinically important: deficiency or inhibition in one pathway can shift the balance of activation and produce distinctive complement profiles.

# Formation and function of C3 convertases, C5 convertase and the membrane attack complex (MAC)

C3 convertases are multiprotein serine protease complexes responsible for the proteolysis of C3, creating the major effector fragments C3a and C3b. There are two principal C3 convertases: the classical/lectin pathway convertase C4b2a and the alternative pathway convertase C3bBb.

C4b2a forms when C4b covalently binds a surface and associates with C2a. Its activity is regulated by decay and by cofactors that enable factor I-mediated cleavage of C4b. C3b generated by C3 convertases can covalently attach to target surfaces, creating opsonic tags for phagocytes via complement receptors CR1 (CD35), CR3, and CR4.

The alternative pathway convertase C3bBb assembles on surfaces in which C3b is deposited. Bb is the protease subunit; factor D cleaves factor B bound to C3b to release Ba and yield Bb. Properdin (factor P) binds and stabilizes this complex, markedly increasing its half-life and activity.

C5 convertases arise when an additional C3b molecule associates with a C3 convertase complex. In the classical/lectin pathway, C4b2a3b serves as a C5 convertase; in the alternative pathway, C3bBbC3b (often written C3b2Bb) is the C5 convertase. These complexes cleave C5 into C5a and C5b. C5a is a potent chemoattractant and proinflammatory anaphylatoxin that activates neutrophils and endothelial cells. C5b initiates MAC assembly by sequentially recruiting C6, C7, C8, and multiple C9 molecules to form the C5b-9 complex.

The MAC (C5b-9) forms a pore within lipid membranes, allowing uncontrolled ion fluxes, osmotic lysis, and cell death. Sublytic quantities of MAC can instead induce cell activation, procoagulant changes, and cytokine release, particularly in endothelial cells. The balance between sufficient complement to kill pathogens and excessive MAC formation on host cells is maintained by regulators.

Kinetics and stability differ among convertases: C3bBb is intrinsically short-lived (half-life seconds to minutes) unless stabilized by properdin; C4b2a is more stable but also subject to regulatory decay. The enzymatic activity and surface localization of convertases underlie amplification and target discrimination: covalent C3b deposition restricts activity to surfaces where it was generated, yet dysregulation allows spread and tissue injury.

# Regulatory proteins: factor H, factor I, factor B, membrane cofactor protein (MCP/CD46), decay-accelerating factor (DAF/CD55), complement receptor 1

To prevent complement-mediated injury to host tissues, a set of soluble and membrane-bound regulators act at multiple steps: they block convertase formation, accelerate decay of convertases, act as cofactors for proteolytic inactivation of C3b and C4b, and inhibit MAC formation.

Factor H is a major soluble regulator of the alternative pathway. It binds C3b and polyanionic host surface markers (e.g., sialic acid and heparan sulfate) to distinguish self from non-self. Factor H exerts two main functions: it competes with factor B for binding to C3b, thereby preventing formation of C3bBb, and it acts as a cofactor for factor I-mediated cleavage of C3b into inactive iC3b. Factor H also accelerates decay of the alternative pathway C3 convertase. Genetic deficiency, functional defects, or autoantibodies against factor H predispose to diseases characterized by excessive alternative pathway activation, such as atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) and some forms of dense deposit disease.

Factor I is a serine protease in plasma that irreversibly cleaves C3b and C4b into inactive fragments, but it requires cofactors: factor H in the fluid phase and on surfaces for C3b; membrane cofactor protein (MCP/CD46), complement receptor 1 (CR1/CD35) and C4b-binding protein for surface-associated C3b and C4b. Factor I deficiency causes uncontrolled complement activation and consumption of C3, often resulting in recurrent pyogenic infections.

Factor B is a component of the alternative pathway; it is cleaved by factor D to yield Bb (the enzymatic subunit) and Ba. While factor B is considered part of the activation machinery rather than a regulator, its genetic variants can alter alternative pathway activity. For example, gain-of-function mutations in factor B increase convertase formation and stability, promoting complement-mediated endothelial injury.

Membrane cofactor protein (MCP/CD46) is a widely expressed membrane-bound regulator that serves as a cofactor for factor I-mediated cleavage of C3b and C4b on host cell surfaces. MCP protects self-cells by promoting degradation of surface-bound C3b, thereby preventing formation and persistence of C3 and C5 convertases locally. Mutations in MCP are implicated in aHUS and other thrombotic microangiopathies.

Decay-accelerating factor (DAF/CD55) and complement receptor 1 (CR1/CD35) accelerate the dissociation (decay) of both classical/lectin pathway and alternative pathway C3 convertases. DAF is anchored to the cell membrane by glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) and prevents assembly and promotes dissociation of C4b2a and C3bBb complexes. Loss of DAF (for example, in paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria where GPI anchors are deficient) permits complement-mediated lysis of erythrocytes. CR1 is expressed on erythrocytes, leukocytes and podocytes and combines decay-accelerating activity with cofactor activity for factor I-mediated cleavage of C3b and C4b; erythrocyte CR1 also mediates immune complex clearance.

Other regulators include vitronectin and clusterin which inhibit MAC formation and insertion, and C1-inhibitor (C1-INH) which controls activation of C1r and C1s (classical pathway) and MASPs (lectin pathway). Deficiency of C1-INH causes hereditary angioedema due to uninhibited kallikrein-bradykinin generation but also affects complement.

The interplay of these regulators defines the threshold and location of complement activation; genetic mutations, autoantibodies, and acquired functional inhibition of these proteins disrupt homeostasis and are central to complement-mediated diseases.

# Consequences of uncontrolled complement activation on endothelium

Endothelial cells are prime targets and effectors in complement-mediated injury. Uncontrolled complement activation produces several harmful effects that converge on microvascular thrombosis, inflammation, and organ dysfunction.

Direct membrane attack by MAC (C5b-9) creates pores in endothelial cell membranes. High amounts cause cell lysis; sublytic amounts trigger intracellular signaling pathways involving calcium influx, activation of kinases and transcription factors (e.g., NF-κB), and expression of proinflammatory and prothrombotic molecules. Sublytic MAC deposition promotes upregulation of tissue factor, downregulation of thrombomodulin, release of von Willebrand factor, and expression of adhesion molecules (VCAM-1, ICAM-1), thereby promoting leukocyte adhesion and coagulation.

Anaphylatoxins C3a and especially C5a potently activate neutrophils and monocytes, increase endothelial permeability, and promote chemotaxis. C5a induces upregulation of integrins on leukocytes, enhances degranulation and oxidative burst, and stimulates the release of proteases—all contributing to endothelial injury. Endothelial activation further exposes procoagulant surfaces and extracellular matrix, facilitating platelet adhesion and thrombus formation.

Complement-mediated endothelial injury underlies thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). In aHUS, genetic or acquired defects in regulators (factor H, MCP, factor I, complement factor B gain-of-function, C3 mutations, or factor H autoantibodies) lead to uncontrolled alternative pathway activity with resultant complement deposition on endothelium, microvascular thrombosis, hemolytic anemia from mechanical red cell fragmentation, thrombocytopenia, and organ ischemia—classically affecting the kidneys. Similar mechanisms contribute to transplant-associated TMA, catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome contexts, and some complications of infections.

Complement dysregulation also contributes to atherogenesis, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and inflammatory conditions such as atypical membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN) and C3 glomerulopathy, where persistent alternative pathway activation drives C3 deposition in glomeruli with progressive renal damage.

Cell-surface loss or dysfunction of complement regulators exacerbates endothelial vulnerability: e.g., reduced expression of MCP or CD55 on endothelial cells diminishes local protection. Additionally, systemic factors—high levels of activated complement fragments, immune complexes, or autoantibodies—overwhelm regulatory capacity and provoke widespread endotheliopathy. Clinically, these processes manifest as microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, elevated LDH, schistocytes on peripheral smear, acute kidney injury, and organ ischemia.

# Laboratory tests: C3, C4, CH50, AH50, functional pathway assays, detection of factor H autoantibodies

Complement testing includes measurements of individual component concentrations, global functional assays, and specific tests for regulators and autoantibodies. Understanding which tests reflect activation versus deficiency is critical.

C3 and C4 quantitation: Serum C3 and C4 are acute-phase proteins but are consumed during complement activation. Typical laboratory reference ranges (vary among labs): C3 approximately 90–180 mg/dL (0.9–1.8 g/L) and C4 approximately 10–40 mg/dL (0.1–0.4 g/L). Low C4 with normal C3 suggests classical/lectin pathway activation or congenital C4 deficiency. Low C3 with normal C4 points toward isolated alternative pathway activation. Low C3 and low C4 suggest classical or lectin pathway consumption. Note that values must be interpreted relative to clinical context and timing.

Total hemolytic complement (CH50) measures classical pathway functional activity by assessing serum-mediated lysis of antibody-sensitized sheep red blood cells. Results are reported as CH50 units (e.g., normal range roughly 60–144 U/mL depending on assay); an absent or very low CH50 indicates deficiency of one or more components of the classical pathway (C1q, C1r/s, C4, C2, C3, C5–C9) or the presence of an inhibitor. CH50 is a global readout and does not localize the defect. Repeated low CH50 with normal component levels suggests terminal pathway deficiency (C5–C9).

Alternative pathway hemolytic assay (AH50) assesses functional activity of the alternative pathway, typically using rabbit red blood cells in Mg2+-EGTA buffer to chelate Ca2+ (blocking classical/lectin pathways) and measure alternative pathway-mediated lysis. Normal AH50 ranges vary; a decreased AH50 indicates defects in alternative pathway components (factor B, factor D, properdin, C3) or presence of inhibitory substances.

Pathway-specific functional assays are commercially available that selectively test classical, lectin, and alternative pathway activities using ELISA-based platforms where deposition of C5b-9 or generation of C3 fragments on immobilized pathway-specific activators is quantified. These assays can define which pathway is deficient or inhibited. For example, classical pathway ELISAs use IgM or IgG ligands; lectin pathway assays use mannan; alternative pathway assays use LPS or zymosan in Mg-EGTA buffer.

Measurement of individual regulators: Quantitation of factor H, factor I, factor B, properdin, MCP, DAF and CR1 may be done by immunoassays. Genetic testing for mutations in complement genes (CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, MCP/CD46, THBD) is often performed when hereditary complement-mediated disease is suspected, e.g., atypical HUS or C3 glomerulopathy.

Detection of factor H autoantibodies: Autoantibodies to factor H (anti-FH) are detected by ELISA or western blot using purified factor H as antigen. Anti-FH antibodies are associated with acquired aHUS and can be pathogenic by blocking factor H regulatory function. Testing for anti-FH should include both qualitative detection and quantitation, and can be followed by functional assays demonstrating impaired factor H cofactor activity.

Other useful tests: Measurement of C3d, C3 breakdown products (iC3b, C3dg), or soluble C5b-9 (sC5b-9) can indicate ongoing complement activation. sC5b-9 (normal range depends on assay; often <0.5–1.0 µg/mL) reflects terminal pathway activation and can be elevated in active complement-mediated pathology. Measurement of C1q can be helpful in suspected hereditary deficiency or immune complex-mediated disease. C1-INH levels and activity are measured when hereditary angioedema is in the differential.

Key diagnostic patterns:
- Low C3 and low C4: activation of the classical pathway (e.g., immune complex disease, lupus) or severe consumption.
- Low C3 with normal C4: predominant alternative pathway activation (e.g., C3 glomerulopathy, some aHUS).
- Normal C3 and C4 with low CH50: terminal pathway deficiency (C5–C9) or functional inhibitor.
- Low CH50 and low AH50: extensive consumption or multiple pathway defects.

# Pre-analytical and post-treatment effects on complement assays (plasmapheresis, complement inhibitors)

Complement assays are particularly sensitive to pre-analytical handling and to therapeutic interventions that alter complement levels or activity. Awareness of these effects is essential for correct interpretation.

Pre-analytical considerations:
- Sample type: Complement testing requires serum or plasma collected under conditions that avoid ex vivo activation. For functional hemolytic assays and for CH50/AH50, serum collected without anticoagulant is typically used, allowed to clot for a standardized short interval, then centrifuged promptly to separate serum. For measurements vulnerable to in vitro activation, EDTA plasma is preferred for some component assays because EDTA chelates calcium and magnesium and prevents complement activation after phlebotomy; however EDTA plasma cannot be used for functional CH50/AH50 assays since divalent cations are needed for activity.
- Temperature and timing: Complement proteins, particularly convertases and labile components, are heat-labile and degrade with improper handling. Samples should be kept on ice and frozen at -70°C if not assayed promptly. Multiple freeze–thaw cycles degrade activity; aliquoting is recommended.
- Hemolysis and lipemia: Hemolyzed specimens can interfere with colorimetric and hemolytic assays. Lipemia may also affect optical readings in ELISA-based tests.
- Transport delay: Delays that permit in vitro complement activation (e.g., at room temperature) can artifactually lower component levels (consumption) or increase breakdown products like C3d and sC5b-9.

Effects of treatments:
- Plasmapheresis/plasma exchange (PEX) removes circulating complement proteins (both activators and regulators), leading to decreased concentrations of C3, C4, factor H, factor I, etc., for hours to days depending on replacement fluid and frequency. Thus, testing immediately after PEX will reflect acute removal rather than underlying disease activity. If monitoring disease, sample timing relative to PEX must be documented; ideally, baseline samples are drawn before PEX and serial measurements are standardized.
- Plasma infusion replenishes complement components and regulators transiently; replacement with fresh frozen plasma contains factor H, factor I and other regulators and can transiently normalize levels.
- Complement inhibitors: Therapeutic agents targeting complement radically alter assays. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody to C5, prevents generation of C5a and C5b and abolishes hemolytic activity attributable to the terminal pathway. After eculizumab, CH50 and AH50 become undetectable despite preserved upstream components (C3, C4). sC5b-9 levels may also fall. Newer agents (ravulizumab, crovalimab, anti-factor D, anti-C3) have pathway-specific effects: anti-C3 inhibitors reduce C3 and downstream activation and will lower C3 fragment detection; factor D inhibitors impair alternative pathway activity and will reduce AH50 but may leave classical pathway (CH50) intact. Sample timing relative to dosing matters because trough versus peak drug levels may produce different assay results. Some assays may be falsely interpreted as deficiency if the patient is receiving complement blockade.
- Immunosuppressive treatments that reduce antibody formation (rituximab, steroids) can reduce immune-complex–mediated classical pathway activation and normalize C4/C3 over time; interpreting changes requires consideration of therapy timing.

Clinicians and laboratorians must communicate regarding recent plasmapheresis, plasma infusions, and complement-targeted therapies when ordering and interpreting complement tests. Many laboratories request that specimens be drawn before PEX or immediately prior to the next cycle to make results interpretable.

# Interpretation pitfalls and clinical correlations

Interpreting complement tests requires integration of clinical presentation, timing, therapy, and the biology of the assays. Several common pitfalls and pearls are outlined below.

Acute-phase effects and synthesis versus consumption: C3 and C4 are acute-phase reactants and can be elevated during inflammation, infection, or corticosteroid therapy, potentially masking consumption. For example, in active immune complex disease, ongoing synthesis may keep C3 in or near the reference range despite activation. Conversely, genetic deficiencies cause low baseline levels that persist independent of activity. Serial measurements and measurement of activation products (C3d, C3a, sC5b-9) help distinguish consumption from low synthesis.

Whole-pathway assays versus component levels: A normal CH50 does not exclude partial deficiency in a single component or regulator. For instance, an acquired autoantibody blocking a single regulator may allow some residual activity. Conversely, an undetectable CH50 can result from terminal component deficiency (C5–C9), but normal CH50 in the presence of clinical complement-mediated disease may occur when complement activation is localized (e.g., glomerular complement deposition) rather than systemic consumption. AH50 adds information about alternative pathway function.

Interfering substances and heterophile antibodies: Hemolysis, bilirubin, and paraproteins may interfere with hemolytic and ELISA assays; autoantibodies can bind assay components and produce false results. Anti-complement autoantibodies (e.g., C3 nephritic factor) stabilize convertases and may not be detected unless specific assays are used.

Timing relative to disease course: Complement activation is dynamic. In acute exacerbations, complement consumption may be maximal early; after resolution, component levels rebound. In diseases with chronic deposition (C3 glomerulopathy) complement levels may be normal while deposits persist. Thus, single-point testing can be misleading.

Assay-specific reference ranges and methods: Different laboratories use different assays and reference intervals; CH50 and AH50 units are not universally standardized. Always interpret results in the context of the sending lab’s normal ranges and assay method. ELISA-based functional pathway assays are more standardized across labs but still require method-specific interpretation.

Genetic versus acquired causes: Low C3 with normal C4 suggests alternative pathway dysfunction and prompts evaluation for genetic mutations in CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, or autoantibodies like anti-FH and C3 nephritic factor. Low C4 preferentially suggests classical pathway activation or congenital C4 deficiency; persistent low C4 with normal C3 raises suspicion for homozygous C4 deficiency. Presence of anti-FH antibodies can be diagnostic for acquired aHUS and informs therapy (immunosuppression, plasma exchange).

Clinical correlations:
- Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE): Active lupus typically consumes classical pathway components; low C3 and C4 (often both low) correlate with disease flares, especially lupus nephritis. Serial trending is useful.
- Post-infectious glomerulonephritis: Low C3 with normal or mildly low C4 is typical; C3 typically returns to normal within weeks to months.
- Atypical HUS: Often shows low C3 with normal or near-normal C4; testing should include factor H, factor I, MCP, factor B, C3 genetic testing, and anti-factor H antibodies. CH50 may be preserved unless terminal pathway is affected.
- Terminal complement component deficiencies (C5–C9): Present with recurrent Neisseria meningitidis infections. CH50 is profoundly reduced; specific component assays and genetic testing confirm deficiency.
- Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH): Complement-mediated intravascular hemolysis due to loss of GPI-anchored DAF/CD55 and CD59 from erythrocyte surfaces. Flow cytometry for CD55/CD59-negative clones on erythrocytes and granulocytes is diagnostic; complement assays are not diagnostic but therapeutic complement blockade (eculizumab) dramatically reduces hemolysis and CH50.

Testing strategies and decision points:
- For suspected complement-mediated TMA or aHUS, obtain baseline complement testing (C3, C4, CH50, AH50 when available), assays for anti-FH antibodies, and arrange for complement genetic testing before initiating plasma exchange or complement-targeted therapies if clinically feasible.
- For recurrent meningococcal disease, measure CH50 and specific terminal complement components, and consider genetic testing for C5–C9 defects.
- When results are discordant or unexpected, confirm sample handling, re-test, and consult with the clinical laboratory. Consider measuring activation fragments (C3d, C3a, sC5b-9) for evidence of ongoing activation.

Summary

The complement system is a finely balanced cascade of recognition molecules, enzymatic convertases, and terminal effectors, tightly controlled by multiple regulatory proteins. Disruption of this balance—whether by genetic mutation, autoantibody formation, infection, or loss of membrane-bound regulators—leads to clinically significant disease, particularly of the microvasculature and kidney. Laboratory assessment includes measurement of individual components (C3, C4), global functional assays (CH50, AH50), pathway-specific functional tests, detection of autoantibodies (anti-factor H), and measurement of activation products (sC5b-9). Accurate interpretation requires attention to pre-analytical factors, recent therapeutic interventions (plasmapheresis, complement inhibitors), assay characteristics, and the clinical context. Mastery of complement biology and laboratory principles is essential for diagnosing, monitoring, and guiding therapy in complement-mediated diseases.

Introduction

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), often termed atypical HUS (aHUS) in older literature, is a prototypical thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in which dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway causes endothelial injury with microvascular thrombosis, resulting clinically in microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia and acute kidney injury. Understanding CM-HUS requires integrating complement biochemistry, genetic predisposition, acquired immune mechanisms, and clinical precipitants. This chapter explains the mechanisms by which dysregulated alternative pathway activation produces endothelial injury and TMA, summarizes the inherited and acquired contributors, describes common triggers, outlines diagnostic reasoning to distinguish CM-HUS from other TMAs, and presents evidence-based therapeutic approaches—especially complement blockade—with practical guidance on monitoring, treatment duration and long-term outcomes.

# Mechanistic overview: alternative pathway dysregulation leading to endothelial injury and TMA

The complement system is a proteolytic cascade with three initiation pathways—classical, lectin and alternative—that converge on formation of C3 convertase, eventual generation of C5 convertase, and terminal membrane attack complex (MAC; C5b-9). The alternative pathway (AP) is continuously active at a low level by spontaneous hydrolysis of C3 (“tick-over”), generating C3(H2O) that can associate with factor B and be cleaved by factor D to produce a fluid-phase C3 convertase. Because the AP can amplify complement activation triggered by any pathway, tight regulation is essential to prevent host tissue injury.

Regulation occurs via soluble and membrane-bound regulators: factor H (CFH), factor I (CFI), membrane cofactor protein (MCP/CD46), decay-accelerating factor (DAF/CD55), and complement receptor 1 (CR1), among others. CFH is the principal soluble regulator that binds to host surfaces (recognizes self-surface polyanions) and competes with factor B for binding to C3b; it also acts as a cofactor for factor I-mediated cleavage of C3b. When regulation fails—because of defective regulators, gain-of-function in activators, or blocking autoantibodies—there is uncontrolled AP activation with excessive deposition of C3b on endothelial surfaces.

In CM-HUS, endothelial injury in small renal arterioles and glomerular capillaries is the central pathologic event. Excess complement activation on endothelial cells leads to direct cytotoxicity (sublytic MAC formation), induction of a procoagulant and proinflammatory endothelial phenotype, and upregulation of adhesion molecules and tissue factor. These changes promote platelet activation, fibrin deposition and formation of microthrombi, creating the histologic and clinical picture of TMA: fragmentation of red blood cells producing schistocytes, consumptive thrombocytopenia, and ischemic kidney injury producing rises in serum creatinine and proteinuria. Although the kidney is the most vulnerable organ because of high exposure to plasma complement and unique endothelial characteristics, complement-mediated TMAs can also affect the brain, heart and other organs.

Pathophysiologically, CM-HUS is thus best conceptualized as failure of self-surface-specific APC regulation with amplified complement activation at the endothelial-blood interface leading to a self-perpetuating cycle of endothelial injury, thrombosis, inflammation and further complement activation.

# Genetic contributors: pathogenic variants in CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, MCP (CD46) and their penetrance

Genetic predisposition accounts for a substantial fraction of CM-HUS cases. Variants may be loss-of-function in regulatory genes or gain-of-function in components that promote complement activation.

- CFH (complement factor H): The most commonly implicated gene. Loss-of-function variants—especially those affecting the C-terminal surface recognition domains—reduce CFH binding to host surfaces, permitting uncontrolled C3b deposition on endothelium. CFH variants are associated with severe disease and high risk of recurrence after transplantation unless addressed.

- CFI (complement factor I): Factor I cleaves C3b and C4b in the presence of cofactors (CFH, MCP). CFI loss-of-function leads to inadequate inactivation of C3b, increasing convertase activity.

- MCP (CD46): MCP is a membrane-bound cofactor expressed on host cells, including endothelial cells. Heterozygous variants commonly cause reduced surface expression or impaired cofactor activity; clinically these patients may have a somewhat better prognosis and lower recurrence risk in renal transplant because the graft endothelium expresses normal MCP unless the variant is germline.

- CFB and C3: Gain-of-function variants in CFB or C3 increase formation or stability of the alternative pathway convertases, promoting complement activation despite intact regulators. C3 gain-of-function changes may lead to persistent low C3 levels and aggressive disease.

- Other genes: Rarely variants in thrombomodulin (THBD), DGKE (in infants; non-complement mechanism), and other complement-associated genes are implicated.

Penetrance of pathogenic variants is incomplete: having a predisposing variant does not guarantee disease. Penetrance estimates vary by gene and family studies; broadly, lifetime penetrance for identified pathogenic complement gene variants is incomplete and often quoted in the range of ~20–50%, with variability by variant type, family history, and environmental exposures. For example, some CFH and CFI pathogenic variants demonstrate lower penetrance (often <50%) while combined factors (multiple variants or strong environmental triggers) increase the probability of clinical CM-HUS. This incomplete penetrance underlies the importance of a second hit—acquired triggers—to precipitate clinical disease.

From a clinical standpoint, detection of a pathogenic variant affects prognosis, counseling and management (notably decisions about prophylactic complement inhibition around transplantation or pregnancy). Genetic testing should be comprehensive (sequencing + copy-number analysis) and interpreted with expert genetic and complement consultation.

# Acquired mechanisms: anti-factor H autoantibodies

In addition to germline variants, acquired mechanisms can produce functional CFH deficiency. The most important example is autoantibodies directed against CFH. These anti-factor H autoantibodies typically target the C-terminal domains of CFH, interfering with its ability to bind host surfaces and regulate C3b. Functionally, they phenocopy CFH deficiency and predispose to uncontrolled AP activation.

Anti-CFH autoantibody-associated CM-HUS is especially recognized in children and adolescents, sometimes in association with genetic predisposition such as homozygous deletion of CFHR1 and CFHR3 (complement factor H–related proteins 1 and 3), which appears to increase risk of autoantibody development. The presence of autoantibodies may be transient or persistent; titers can correlate imperfectly with disease activity.

Clinically identifying anti-CFH autoantibodies matters because immunosuppressive therapy (e.g., corticosteroids, rituximab, cyclophosphamide) and plasma exchange can reduce autoantibody levels and restore CFH function. In some patients, combined strategies—complement blockade to control ongoing terminal pathway activation plus directed immunosuppression to reduce autoantibody production—are required.

# Common triggering events: infections, pregnancy, medications and transplant

Because CM-HUS requires a precipitating “second hit” in many patients with underlying predisposition, clinicians must search for and address common triggers.

- Infections: Upper respiratory and gastrointestinal infections are frequent antecedents. Viral infections (e.g., influenza, adenovirus, cytomegalovirus) and bacterial infections (including but not limited to Shiga toxin–producing Escherichia coli, which must be distinguished) can trigger complement activation via innate immune pathways. Even common viral illnesses can tip the balance toward unregulated complement activity in susceptible hosts.

- Pregnancy and the postpartum period: Pregnancy-associated CM-HUS typically occurs in the third trimester or postpartum and can be precipitated by the intense complement activation state of pregnancy, placental ischemia and delivery. Distinguishing CM-HUS from pregnancy-specific TMAs (preeclampsia/HELLP) and thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is clinically challenging but critical because management differs.

- Medications: Certain drugs have been associated with TMAs and may unmask CM-HUS in predisposed individuals. Examples include calcineurin inhibitors, some chemotherapeutics, and drugs causing immune-mediated endothelial injury. Drug cessation is an important initial step when there is suspicion.

- Solid organ and bone marrow transplantation: Transplant-associated TMA can be triggered by immunosuppressants (calcineurin inhibitors), infections, graft-versus-host disease, ischemia-reperfusion and alloimmune injury. In patients with complement pathogenic variants, renal transplantation is particularly high-risk for recurrent CM-HUS unless appropriate prophylactic strategies (e.g., perioperative complement inhibition) are used.

Other triggers include systemic inflammatory states, surgery and malignancy. Because triggers are common and sometimes modifiable, identifying and mitigating them is part of acute and preventive care.

# Diagnostic considerations: exclusion of STEC-HUS and TTP, role of complement testing and genetics

The diagnostic approach to TMA begins with confirming the clinical syndrome and rapidly excluding treatable causes that mandate different therapy.

Clinical and laboratory criteria for a TMA event typically include microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (schistocytes on peripheral smear), elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and indirect bilirubin, low haptoglobin, reticulocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury or other organ dysfunction. Useful typical lab ranges and thresholds: hemoglobin often reduced (varies), platelet count usually <150 x 10^9/L (often <100 x 10^9/L); LDH elevated above the upper limit of normal (variable by lab), haptoglobin low (often <0.3 g/L), serum creatinine elevated relative to baseline. However these findings are not specific for CM-HUS.

Key early discriminations:

- Exclude TTP: ADAMTS13 activity should be measured urgently. Severe deficiency (ADAMTS13 activity <10%) strongly supports a diagnosis of TTP, which is treated with urgent plasma exchange and immunosuppression. ADAMTS13 assays may take time; clinical scoring systems can help decide empiric plasma exchange while awaiting results. Normal or mildly reduced ADAMTS13 activity makes TTP unlikely.

- Exclude Shiga toxin–producing E. coli (STEC-HUS): STEC-HUS is typically preceded by bloody diarrhea and is diagnosed by stool culture/PCR for Shiga toxin or detection of toxin. Because management is supportive and complement blockade is generally not indicated, distinguishing STEC-HUS from CM-HUS is crucial.

When TTP and STEC-HUS are excluded or considered unlikely, the working diagnosis of CM-HUS is appropriate in a patient with TMA and predominant renal involvement, especially with a history or family history suggesting complement disease.

Complement testing and genetics:

- Complement blood tests: Serum C3 and C4 levels are commonly ordered. In CM-HUS, C3 is frequently normal or only modestly low—normal complement levels do not exclude complement dysregulation. C4 is typically normal because the classical and lectin pathways are not primarily involved. Functional assays of complement activation provide more direct evidence: total hemolytic complement activity (CH50) and alternative pathway hemolytic activity (AH50) can be informative. Measurement of soluble C5b-9 (sC5b-9) reflects terminal pathway activation and may be elevated in active disease and can be used to monitor response, but availability varies.

- Anti-CFH autoantibodies: Specific testing for anti-factor H antibodies is indicated, especially in children and adolescents.

- Genetic testing: Comprehensive testing for pathogenic variants in CFH, CFI, MCP (CD46), CFB, C3 and other complement genes is recommended. Results often take weeks, but identification of a pathogenic variant influences prognosis, counseling and management decisions (e.g., lifelong complement inhibitor therapy, transplant prophylaxis). Genetic counseling is essential because of incomplete penetrance and autosomal dominant/recessive patterns depending on the gene.

Practical diagnostic decision points:

- If ADAMTS13 activity <10%, manage as TTP. If Shiga toxin positive, manage as STEC-HUS with supportive care.

- If neither is present and the clinical picture is consistent with HUS (predominant renal involvement, microangiopathic hemolysis and thrombocytopenia), search for triggers, order complement studies including sC5b-9, test for anti-CFH antibodies, and begin discussions about initiating complement blockade if clinical severity (progressive renal failure, need for dialysis, worsening hemolysis) warrants early therapy.

# Therapeutic strategies: supportive care, plasma exchange/plasma infusion, indications for complement inhibitors (eculizumab and newer agents)

Management of CM-HUS integrates supportive care, removal or treatment of triggers, plasma therapies and targeted complement blockade.

Supportive care:

Principles of supportive care are foundational and include hemodynamic stabilization, correction of fluid and electrolyte disturbances, renal replacement therapy when indicated, transfusions for symptomatic anemia (packed red blood cells), and avoiding platelet transfusions unless there is life-threatening bleeding or invasive procedures (platelet transfusion may worsen thrombosis). Blood pressure control and management of complications (electrolyte derangements, volume overload) are essential. In pregnancy, collaboration with obstetrics is required.

Plasma exchange (PLEX) and plasma infusion:

Historically, plasma exchange and plasma infusion were mainstays because they can replace deficient regulators (e.g., CFH) and remove autoantibodies (anti-CFH). PLEX is effective in TTP and can be tried empirically in severe TMA while ADAMTS13 results are pending. In CM-HUS, plasma infusions (to supply functional CFH/CFI) may benefit some patients with regulatory protein deficiencies, and PLEX can remove pathogenic autoantibodies. However, with the advent of complement blockade, the role of PLEX is more limited and its efficacy is variable depending on the mechanism (genetic vs. autoantibody). For anti-CFH autoantibody–mediated disease, PLEX combined with immunosuppression can be effective.

Complement inhibitors:

Terminal complement blockade with C5 inhibitors is the major therapeutic advance for CM-HUS. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that binds C5 and prevents its cleavage to C5a and C5b, abrogates formation of MAC and downstream terminal pathway–mediated endothelial injury. Eculizumab has been shown to induce hematologic remission, improve or stabilize renal function, and reduce the need for plasma exchange and dialysis in CM-HUS. Early initiation is associated with better outcomes; therefore clinical suspicion often prompts empiric therapy even before full genetic results.

Clinical indications for starting a C5 inhibitor include: TMA with suspected CM-HUS (after excluding TTP and STEC-HUS), progressive renal failure or dialysis dependence, ongoing hemolysis and thrombocytopenia despite supportive care, and in some high-risk peri-transplant or pregnancy settings. Dosing and administration follow approved protocols; therapy requires meningococcal vaccination prior to initiation when possible and prophylactic antibiotics when immediate vaccination is not feasible because terminal complement blockade increases susceptibility to Neisseria species.

Newer complement-targeted agents:

- Ravulizumab: A long-acting C5 inhibitor engineered from eculizumab with an extended dosing interval. Clinical evidence supports non-inferiority to eculizumab in complement-mediated TMAs, with reduced infusion frequency.

- C3 inhibitors (e.g., pegcetacoplan): By acting upstream at C3, these agents block all downstream complement effector functions including the alternative pathway amplification loop. C3 inhibitors may be effective but also pose broader immunologic consequences and infection risks.

- Factor B and factor D inhibitors (e.g., iptacopan [a factor B inhibitor], small molecule factor D inhibitors): These agents selectively inhibit the alternative pathway proximal steps, preserving classical and lectin pathway function. They are promising for more targeted control of AP dysregulation and are being evaluated in clinical trials or early clinical use.

- Anti-C5 monoclonals with longer half-lives or alternative routes (subcutaneous) and agents targeting lectin pathway components (e.g., MASP-2 inhibitors) are under investigation.

Choice among agents depends on availability, cost, patient-specific factors (e.g., degree of complement activation, prior response), and clinical data. Importantly, when anti-CFH autoantibodies are present, combining complement blockade with immunosuppression directed at antibody production may be necessary to achieve durable remission.

In all cases, before starting complement inhibitors, clinicians must ensure vaccination against Neisseria meningitidis (both polysaccharide and conjugate vaccines per local guidelines) and consider pneumococcal and Haemophilus immunizations. If immediate therapy is required before vaccine immunity develops, prophylactic antibiotics targeting Neisseria should be given per protocol.

# Monitoring, duration of therapy and factors guiding discontinuation

Monitoring during treatment addresses clinical response, laboratory markers of hemolysis and renal function, and surveillance for infectious complications.

Monitoring parameters and frequency:

- Hematology: hemoglobin, platelet count, LDH, haptoglobin, reticulocyte count, peripheral smear for schistocytes. Improvement in platelet count and decrease in LDH are early markers of therapeutic response. Typical response to effective complement blockade is platelet recovery within days and hemolysis control within days to weeks.

- Renal function: serum creatinine, urine output, electrolytes, and proteinuria. Monitoring dialysis dependence and recovery trajectory guides further management.

- Complement biomarkers: serial sC5b-9 (if available) may reflect terminal pathway activation and help assess drug effect. CH50 is useful to confirm pharmacodynamic blockade of terminal complement (CH50 suppressed on adequate C5 inhibition). Note that CH50 becomes undetectable with effective C5 blockade; restoration of CH50 may indicate subtherapeutic drug levels or nonadherence.

- Infectious risk: watch for signs of meningococcal infection, sepsis, and other encapsulated bacterial infections. Vaccination status should be documented and updated.

Duration of therapy and discontinuation considerations:

There is no universal rule for duration. Decisions must balance relapse risk (dependent on underlying genetics, clinical history) against long-term risks, burdens and costs of therapy.

Factors favoring long-term or lifelong therapy:

- Identification of high-risk pathogenic variants associated with recurrent disease (e.g., CFH, CFI pathogenic variants), particularly when prior relapses or dialysis dependence occurred.

- History of relapsing CM-HUS or requirement for chronic dialysis before or during treatment.

- Patients undergoing renal transplantation with a pathogenic variant that puts them at high recurrence risk; perioperative and long-term prophylaxis with complement inhibitors is often recommended.

Factors supporting cautious discontinuation after sustained remission:

- No identified pathogenic complement variant and single isolated episode that resolved with treatment.

- Normal renal function and sustained hematologic remission for a prolonged interval (commonly considered several months to a year), especially when a plausible reversible trigger was identified and removed.

- Adequate patient education and rapid access to care for prompt re-initiation if relapse occurs.

If discontinuation is attempted, rigorous follow-up is essential: frequent clinical assessments, laboratory monitoring (CBC, creatinine, urinalysis, hemolysis markers) initially weekly to biweekly then spacing out, and patient education about early symptoms. Some centers use monitoring of complement activation biomarkers (sC5b-9) or CH50 to detect subclinical recurrence, though predictive value is imperfect.

In transplant settings, protocols often use perioperative eculizumab with intensive monitoring, and some centers continue long-term prophylaxis in high-risk genotypes. For anti-CFH autoantibody disease, immunosuppression to achieve antibody eradication is often the goal, with complement blockade used during active disease and until antibody titers fall.

# Prognosis and long-term renal outcomes

Prognosis in CM-HUS is highly variable and influenced by the underlying mechanism, promptness of diagnosis and initiation of appropriate therapy, severity at presentation and presence of comorbidities. Before targeted complement therapies, outcomes were poor: up to 50% of patients progressed to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) or died within the initial disease episode or during relapses. With complement blockade, renal recovery rates and survival have substantially improved.

Renal outcomes:

- Acute phase: Many patients treated promptly with complement inhibitors experience hematologic remission and partial or complete recovery of kidney function, but some require temporary or permanent dialysis. The degree of renal recovery depends on the extent of acute injury and chronicity at presentation.

- Chronic course: Patients remain at risk for chronic kidney disease (CKD), hypertension and proteinuria. Even with apparent clinical remission, subclinical complement-mediated damage can progress. Long-term follow-up for renal function and cardiovascular risk factors is necessary.

- Relapse: Relapse rates vary with genotype and management; patients with CFH, CFI and C3 gain-of-function variants have higher relapse risk compared with MCP variants. Relapses can be triggered by infections, pregnancy or other stresses.

- Transplantation: Patients with CM-HUS may receive kidney transplants but those with high-risk complement variants have a high rate of recurrence in the graft without prophylactic complement blockade. Use of perioperative and often continuing complement inhibition has dramatically improved graft survival in many centers.

Other outcomes and quality of life:

- Extra-renal manifestations (neurologic, cardiac) can occur during acute episodes and may leave residual deficits that affect long-term function.

- Lifelong monitoring, medication burdens (including chronic intravenous infusions for some), infection risk management and psychosocial impacts are important considerations in long-term care.

Prognostication involves integrating genetic findings, acute disease severity, response to therapy and trigger identification. Even with excellent initial response to complement inhibitors, some patients will develop progressive CKD; accordingly, long-term multidisciplinary follow-up with nephrology, hematology, genetics and infectious disease expertise is recommended.

Conclusion

CM-HUS is a complement-driven TMA characterized by dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway leading to endothelial injury, thrombosis and renal failure. Genetic variants and acquired autoantibodies underlie susceptibility, while common triggers precipitate clinical events. Accurate and rapid differentiation from TTP and STEC-HUS is essential, because management differs. Complement blockade—principally with C5 inhibitors such as eculizumab and ravulizumab—has transformed outcomes, but requires careful infectious risk mitigation, monitoring and individualized decisions about duration based on genotype and clinical course. Understanding the pathophysiology, diagnostic approach and therapeutic options is key to optimizing outcomes and counseling patients about prognosis and long-term care.

# Introduction

Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is an acute, life‑threatening thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) that results from severe deficiency of the von Willebrand factor (vWF)–cleaving metalloprotease ADAMTS13. Understanding the molecular interplay between vWF multimers and ADAMTS13 provides the foundation for recognizing the pathophysiology, clinical presentation, diagnostic approach, and targeted therapies for TTP. This chapter reviews the structure and function of vWF and ADAMTS13, the mechanisms that produce ADAMTS13 deficiency (congenital and acquired), the clinical syndrome of TTP and the patterns of organ involvement, laboratory evaluation including ADAMTS13 testing, urgent management strategies (plasma exchange, immunosuppression, and targeted agents such as caplacizumab), and principles for distinguishing TTP from other TMAs.

# Structure and function of von Willebrand factor (vWF) multimers and ADAMTS13 metalloprotease

von Willebrand factor is a large multimeric glycoprotein central to primary hemostasis. vWF is synthesized in vascular endothelial cells and megakaryocytes as a prepropeptide that is extensively processed and glycosylated. Monomeric vWF subunits (~280 kDa) dimerize in the endoplasmic reticulum via C‑terminal disulfide bonds and multimerize in the Golgi by N‑terminal disulfide bonding, producing ultra‑large multimers (ULvWF) that can exceed several million Daltons. vWF multimers circulate in plasma and are also stored in endothelial Weibel–Palade bodies and platelet alpha granules. The hemostatic potency of vWF is positively correlated with multimer size: larger multimers are more effective in binding platelet glycoprotein Ib (GPIb) and in supporting platelet adhesion and aggregation under high shear stress.

ULvWF multimers are potentially prothrombotic if not appropriately regulated. ADAMTS13 (A Disintegrin And Metalloprotease with Thrombospondin type 1 motifs, member 13) is a plasma zinc‑dependent metalloprotease responsible for cleaving vWF at the Tyr1605‑Met1606 peptide bond within the A2 domain. Cleavage reduces multimer size and decreases vWF platelet‑binding activity, particularly under conditions of high shear where the A2 domain unfolds and exposes the scissile bond. ADAMTS13 is synthesized primarily in hepatic stellate cells and endothelial cells, circulates as a 150‑kDa protein, and contains several functional domains (signal peptide, metalloprotease domain, disintegrin domain, thrombospondin type 1 repeats, cysteine-rich spacer domain, and CUB domains) that mediate substrate recognition and proteolysis.

Physiologically, the unfolding of vWF multimers under shear stress aligns the A1 domain for platelet GPIbα interaction and exposes the A2 domain for ADAMTS13 cleavage; this creates a dynamic balance that ensures appropriate platelet tethering without uncontrolled occlusive thrombosis. If ADAMTS13 activity is severely reduced, ULvWF multimers persist on endothelial surfaces and in the circulation, promoting spontaneous platelet aggregation and formation of platelet‑rich microthrombi in small arterioles and capillaries, producing the clinical syndrome of TTP.

# Mechanisms of ADAMTS13 deficiency: congenital mutation versus acquired autoantibodies

Severe deficiency of ADAMTS13 can arise from two fundamentally different mechanisms: inherited (congenital, also known as Upshaw–Schulman syndrome) and acquired (autoantibody‑mediated). Both result in markedly decreased proteolytic cleavage of vWF, but they differ in genetics, epidemiology, triggers, and long‑term management.

In congenital TTP, biallelic pathogenic variants in the ADAMTS13 gene (chromosome 9q34) lead to absent or functionally deficient protein. Mutations may affect synthesis, secretion, stability, or catalytic function. Clinical onset varies: some patients present in the neonatal period with severe TTP and hyperbilirubinemia, while others present later in childhood or adulthood, often during physiological stressors such as infection, pregnancy, or surgery that increase vWF release or shear stress. Diagnosis hinges on documentation of persistently low ADAMTS13 activity in the absence of an inhibitor and the identification of pathogenic ADAMTS13 variants. Management in the acute episode relies on plasma replacement (see management section), whereas long‑term management may include scheduled plasma infusions to maintain ADAMTS13 activity above a protective threshold and genetic counseling.

Acquired TTP is typically caused by polyclonal immunoglobulin G (IgG) autoantibodies that inhibit ADAMTS13 proteolytic activity and/or accelerate its clearance. These autoantibodies most often arise de novo but may be associated with autoimmune disorders (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus), certain drugs, pregnancy, infections, or malignancy. The autoantibodies can act by directly blocking the catalytic or substrate‑binding domains of ADAMTS13 (functional inhibitors) or by promoting immune complex formation and rapid clearance (non‑neutralizing antibodies). Laboratory inhibitor testing will distinguish neutralizing inhibitors by demonstrating reduced ADAMTS13 activity in mixing studies; however, some patients have low activity due to accelerated clearance without a strong neutralizing effect, and these may require immunoassays for anti‑ADAMTS13 antibodies.

Pathophysiologically, both congenital and acquired deficiencies converge on the presence of uncleaved ULvWF multimers, platelet aggregation in the microcirculation, thrombocytopenia from platelet consumption, and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) due to mechanical damage to red blood cells. Acquired TTP tends to present abruptly in adults and is more common than congenital forms. Recognition of the underlying mechanism is important for therapy and prognosis: acquired TTP requires immunosuppression to control autoantibody production, whereas congenital TTP does not benefit from immunosuppression and is treated by ADAMTS13 replacement.

# Clinical syndrome of TTP: pentad concept and organ involvement

Classic descriptions of TTP refer to a pentad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, neurological manifestations, renal dysfunction, and fever. In contemporary practice, however, only microangiopathic hemolytic anemia and thrombocytopenia are considered essential for suspecting TTP, because the full pentad occurs in a minority of patients and reliance on the pentad causes diagnostic delay. The core clinical features and organ involvement reflect microvascular platelet‑rich thrombosis and end‑organ ischemia.

Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia manifests with schistocytes on the peripheral blood smear, elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), elevated indirect bilirubin, low haptoglobin, and reticulocytosis. Schistocytes (fragmented erythrocytes) result from mechanical shearing of red blood cells as they traverse partially occluded microvessels. The hemolysis is intravascular and can be brisk; reticulocyte count increases as marrow compensates.

Thrombocytopenia is typically moderate to severe (platelet counts often <30,000–50,000/µL) and results from consumption in microthrombi. Mucocutaneous bleeding may occur but is often less prominent than the thrombotic complications. Thrombocytopenia in TTP is not due to decreased production; therefore, megakaryocytes are usually increased in the marrow.

Neurological symptoms are common and may vary from confusion, headache, transient focal deficits, seizures, and fluctuating levels of consciousness to coma. Neurologic involvement is a hallmark that often prompts consideration of TTP in the differential diagnosis. The underlying mechanism is microvascular ischemia or multifocal infarction due to platelet‑rich thrombi in cerebral arterioles and capillaries; imaging may be normal early or show multifocal ischemic lesions.

Renal involvement in TTP ranges from mild proteinuria and elevated serum creatinine to more significant renal impairment. Severe renal failure is less typical for TTP than for typical hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS), but it can occur, particularly in patients with delayed diagnosis or additional renal insults. Fever is nonspecific but historically part of the pentad; it reflects systemic inflammation or concomitant infection.

Clinically important is the rapidity with which TTP can progress. Without timely therapy, mortality approaches 90%; initiation of plasma exchange and immunosuppression reduces mortality substantially. Therefore, clinicians should have a low threshold for empiric therapy when the clinical picture suggests TTP—specifically, new thrombocytopenia and MAHA without an alternative explanation.

# Laboratory assessment: ADAMTS13 activity assays, inhibitor testing, platelet count and hemolysis markers

Laboratory evaluation in suspected TTP serves two roles: to identify the TMA and to confirm severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and its mechanism. Initial urgent tests guide clinical management; confirmatory ADAMTS13 testing refines therapy and prognosis but may not be rapidly available in all centers.

The initial laboratory screen should include a complete blood count with platelet count, peripheral blood smear for schistocytes, LDH, bilirubin with fractionation, haptoglobin, reticulocyte count, serum creatinine, coagulation studies (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen), and urinalysis. Typical findings in TTP include thrombocytopenia (often <50,000/µL), schistocytes on smear (the presence of ≥1% schistocytes is a strong indicator of MAHA, though counts vary), elevated LDH (often markedly elevated), indirect hyperbilirubinemia, markedly reduced haptoglobin (often <10 mg/dL), and normal or near‑normal PT and aPTT. Normal coagulation studies help differentiate TTP from disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), in which PT and aPTT are typically prolonged and fibrinogen is low.

ADAMTS13 activity assays quantify the ability of plasma to cleave a vWF substrate and are central to diagnosing TTP. Multiple assay platforms exist: fluorogenic peptide cleavage assays, FRET (fluorescence resonance energy transfer) vWF73 peptide assays, collagen-binding assays, and ristocetin cofactor‑based multimer assays. Clinically, severe deficiency is defined as ADAMTS13 activity <10% (often <5–10%). Levels above this threshold make classic TTP due to severe ADAMTS13 deficiency less likely, though there are exceptions and laboratory variability. Laboratories report activity as a percentage of normal (normal range roughly 50–150% depending on assay and lab) or in units. It is important to interpret results in context and to recognize potential preanalytic issues: plasma should be collected in citrate, processed promptly, and frozen if transport is needed; hemolysis, lipemia, and sample handling can affect results.

Inhibitor testing helps distinguish congenital deficiency from acquired autoimmune TTP. The Bethesda‑type mixing study involves mixing patient plasma with normal pooled plasma and reassessing ADAMTS13 activity after incubation; a reduction in activity that cannot be corrected indicates a neutralizing inhibitor. Alternatively, immunoassays detect anti‑ADAMTS13 IgG. A high titer of anti‑ADAMTS13 IgG supports acquired TTP. Some patients have low ADAMTS13 activity due to non‑neutralizing antibodies that increase clearance; these may require enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) to detect bound antibodies even if the inhibitor assay is negative.

While ADAMTS13 testing provides diagnostic specificity, it is not always rapidly available. Clinical decision rules (e.g., the PLASMIC score) combine variables—platelet count, hemolysis markers, absence of active cancer, no history of solid organ or stem cell transplant, MCV, INR, creatinine—to estimate the probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and guide empiric therapy. For example, a high PLASMIC score suggests a high likelihood of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and supports initiating plasma exchange while awaiting assay results.

Key laboratory reference ranges (approximate; laboratory‑specific ranges may vary):
- Platelet count: normal 150,000–400,000/µL; TTP commonly <50,000/µL.
- LDH: normal 120–250 U/L (varies by lab); markedly elevated in hemolysis.
- Haptoglobin: normal 30–200 mg/dL; often very low or undetectable in intravascular hemolysis.
- Indirect bilirubin: normal 0.1–1.0 mg/dL; increased in hemolysis.
- ADAMTS13 activity: normal roughly 50–150%; severe deficiency <10%.

Clinical decision points include: initiate urgent therapy (plasma exchange and immunosuppression) when there is MAHA plus thrombocytopenia without another clear cause and a high pretest probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency. Send ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor testing before plasma therapy if possible, but do not delay treatment to obtain samples.

# Urgent management: plasma exchange, corticosteroids, caplacizumab and immunosuppression

The management of TTP is a medical emergency focused on rapid removal of circulating autoantibodies and replenishment of functional ADAMTS13, prevention of further microvascular thrombosis, and suppression of autoantibody production in acquired disease. For congenital TTP, management centers on ADAMTS13 replacement.

Therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) is the cornerstone of acute TTP care and should be initiated emergently when TTP is suspected. TPE serves two primary purposes in acquired TTP: removal of pathogenic anti‑ADAMTS13 antibodies and replenishment of ADAMTS13 via donor plasma. Typical TPE protocols exchange 1–1.5 plasma volumes (approximately 40–60 mL/kg) daily until clinical remission, defined by sustained platelet count normalization (usually platelets >150,000/µL for at least two days), normalization of LDH, and resolution of hemolysis. Replacement fluid is usually fresh frozen plasma (FFP) or solvent/detergent‑treated plasma; cryosupernatant can be used but evidence favors standard FFP in most settings. During TPE, anticoagulation of the circuit is necessary (commonly citrate); careful monitoring for allergic reactions, citrate toxicity, and bleeding is required. In congenital TTP or when TPE is unavailable, plasma infusion with FFP at doses to raise ADAMTS13 activity (e.g., 10–15 mL/kg) can be used emergently.

Corticosteroids are started promptly in acquired TTP to blunt autoantibody production. Typical regimens include high‑dose prednisone 1 mg/kg/day orally or intravenous methylprednisolone pulses (e.g., 1 g/day for 1–3 days) in severe cases. Steroids are continued during TPE and tapered based on clinical response. Steroids alone are insufficient for many patients but form an essential adjunct to TPE.

Rituximab, an anti‑CD20 monoclonal antibody, targets B cells responsible for producing anti‑ADAMTS13 autoantibodies and is used early in the treatment course for acquired TTP, particularly in patients with severe presentation, refractory disease, or those at high risk of relapse. Typical dosing is 375 mg/m^2 intravenously weekly for 4 weeks. Rituximab reduces antibody titers, shortens time to remission, and decreases relapse risk. It is also used preemptively in relapsing disease guided by ADAMTS13 activity monitoring.

Caplacizumab is a humanized bivalent nanobody that binds the A1 domain of vWF, blocking the interaction between ULvWF multimers and platelet GPIbα. By inhibiting platelet adhesion, caplacizumab rapidly reduces microvascular platelet aggregation and accelerates platelet count recovery. In randomized trials, addition of caplacizumab to standard therapy (TPE + immunosuppression) shortened time to platelet normalization and decreased composite endpoints of TTP‑related death, recurrence, or major thromboembolic events. Caplacizumab is administered intravenously as a loading dose (10 mg) prior to the first plasma exchange, followed by daily subcutaneous 10 mg injections after each subsequent plasma exchange, continued for 30 days after stopping TPE and longer if ADAMTS13 activity remains low. Because caplacizumab interferes with vWF function, it increases the risk of mucocutaneous bleeding; platelet counts and hemoglobin must be monitored. Caplacizumab provides immediate protection against ongoing microvascular thrombosis but does not address autoantibody production; hence it is used in combination with immunosuppression.

Other immunosuppressive agents (e.g., cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil, bortezomib) may be considered in refractory or relapsing acquired TTP, or when rituximab is contraindicated or ineffective. Bortezomib (a proteasome inhibitor) has been used successfully in refractory cases to target plasma cells producing anti‑ADAMTS13 antibodies. The choice of adjunctive immunosuppression depends on the clinical course, comorbidities, and institutional experience.

Supportive care is essential: transfusions of packed red blood cells are given for symptomatic anemia but are generally avoided unless necessary because transfused red cells can be consumed in the microvasculature. Platelet transfusions are relatively contraindicated except for life‑threatening bleeding or urgent invasive procedures, because they can fuel further thrombosis. Renal support, seizure control, and intensive care management of organ dysfunction should be provided as needed.

Clinical decision points include: start TPE as soon as TTP is suspected; start corticosteroids immediately; add caplacizumab in adults when available and especially when neurological symptoms or severe disease is present; add rituximab early for acquired TTP to prevent relapses. For congenital TTP, regular plasma infusions may be required prophylactically during pregnancy or stressful events, but immunosuppression has no role.

# Differentiating TTP from other TMAs

Thrombotic microangiopathies encompass several distinct disorders—TTP, hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS), atypical HUS (complement‑mediated), and secondary TMAs due to drugs, malignancy, autoimmune disease, transplantation, or severe hypertension. Differentiating TTP from these entities is crucial because management differs markedly: TTP requires urgent plasma exchange and may need immunosuppression and caplacizumab, while HUS often requires supportive care or complement blockade (eculizumab) for complement‑mediated disease.

Distinguishing features for TTP include the presence of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10% activity), prominent neurological symptoms, and often only mild to moderate renal involvement. Coagulation studies (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen) are usually normal in TTP. The PLASMIC score and similar clinical prediction tools estimate the likelihood of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency to guide early treatment decisions.

Typical (Shiga toxin–associated) HUS generally follows a diarrheal prodrome caused by Shiga toxin–producing Escherichia coli (classically O157:H7). It more commonly affects children and presents with triad of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and prominent acute kidney injury. ADAMTS13 activity is usually normal. Management is mainly supportive; antibiotics can worsen toxin release in some cases, and plasma exchange is not routinely effective.

Atypical HUS is a complement‑mediated TMA due to dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway from inherited or acquired defects (mutations in complement regulators or autoantibodies to factor H). It presents with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and severe renal failure; extrarenal manifestations are possible. Complement studies and genetic testing may identify causative abnormalities. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody against C5, is effective in halting complement activation and is the main targeted therapy.

Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is a consumptive coagulopathy associated with sepsis, trauma, or malignancy. DIC features thrombocytopenia and microangiopathic hemolysis can coexist, but DIC is distinguished by prolonged PT and aPTT, low fibrinogen, and elevated D‑dimer. Management targets the underlying trigger and may require blood component support.

Heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) produces a hypercoagulable state with thrombocytopenia and thrombosis, usually days after heparin exposure. Laboratory testing for anti‑PF4/heparin antibodies and functional platelet activation assays aid diagnosis. TMAs from drugs or malignancy often have contextual clues (exposure history, concurrent active cancer) and may not show severe ADAMTS13 deficiency.

Important clinical and laboratory pointers that favor TTP include dramatic thrombocytopenia with MAHA, normal coagulation tests, neurologic findings, and severe ADAMTS13 deficiency on assay. If ADAMTS13 testing is not immediately available, high PLASMIC score, presence of schistocytes, and absence of alternative causes should prompt empiric TPE.

Finally, mixed or overlapping presentations occur. For example, severe infection can trigger both DIC and TTP‑like syndromes, and patients with systemic lupus erythematosus may develop immune‑mediated ADAMTS13 autoantibodies. Because therapies differ, clinicians must continuously reassess the diagnosis as new data (ADAMTS13 activity, complement studies, cultures) become available and adjust therapy accordingly.

# Conclusion

ADAMTS13 biology and its interaction with vWF explain the pathogenesis of TTP: severe ADAMTS13 deficiency permits persistence of ULvWF multimers, causing platelet‑rich microvascular thrombosis that manifests clinically as MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and end‑organ ischemia. Distinguishing congenital from acquired ADAMTS13 deficiency guides long‑term strategy, while urgent recognition and treatment—primarily plasma exchange, corticosteroids, and targeted therapies such as caplacizumab and rituximab—are lifesaving. Rapid laboratory evaluation including ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor testing refines diagnosis, but clinical judgment and prompt empiric therapy remain essential given the rapid progression and high untreated mortality of TTP. Understanding the distinctions between TTP and other TMAs ensures appropriate, targeted treatment and optimal patient outcomes.

Introduction

Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) is a pattern of red blood cell (RBC) injury and accelerated destruction that results from mechanical disruption of erythrocytes in the circulation. MAHA is not a single disease but a morphologic and laboratory syndrome that occurs in several clinical settings, including thrombotic microangiopathies (TMA) such as thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and hemolytic-uremic syndrome (HUS), disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), malignant hypertension, vasculitis, mechanical damage from prosthetic heart valves or extracorporeal circuits, and severe burns. Correctly recognizing MAHA and distinguishing it from immune and other nonmechanical hemolytic processes is critical because management differs substantially (for example, plasmapheresis for TTP, reversing the underlying cause in DIC, or surgical/cardiac intervention for prosthetic valve–related hemolysis).

This chapter reviews the pathophysiology of mechanical RBC destruction, the laboratory markers used to detect and quantify hemolysis, the formation and interpretation of schistocytes, the role and limitations of direct antiglobulin testing (DAT, a.k.a. Coombs test), principles for distinguishing autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) from MAHA and other hemolytic conditions, and transfusion considerations when hemolysis is present.

# Pathophysiology of mechanical RBC destruction in MAHA

MAHA reflects physical trauma to red cells as they traverse abnormal vascular beds. The underlying causes fall into two broad mechanistic categories: (1) shear stress and fragmentation across fibrin strands or platelet-rich thrombi in the microvasculature, and (2) high-velocity turbulent flow across prosthetic material or stenotic cardiac valves. In both settings, mechanical forces exceed the structural limits of the RBC membrane and cytoskeleton, producing characteristic fragmented RBCs (schistocytes), hemoglobin release, and hemolysis.

In thrombotic microangiopathies (TTP, HUS), widespread platelet-rich microthrombi composed of ultralarge von Willebrand factor multimers (exacerbated by deficiency of ADAMTS13 in TTP) or complement-mediated endothelial injury (in some forms of HUS) create networks of fibrin and platelets in arterioles and capillaries. As erythrocytes are forced through narrowed and irregular lumens, they are sheared, generating microspherocytes and fragmentocytes. The fragments are variably deformable; the smallest may be rapidly cleared by the spleen, whereas larger fragments circulate and are visible on peripheral smear.

In DIC, systemic activation of coagulation generates fibrin strands that entrap platelets and red cells; the physical lattice contributes to RBC fragmentation similar to TMA, but DIC commonly coexists with consumptive coagulopathy and bleeding, which alters the clinical picture.

Prosthetic heart valves or other mechanical devices produce turbulent flow, high shear rates, and localized jets of velocity. Repeated mechanical trauma yields persistent low-grade intravascular hemolysis. The degree of hemolysis correlates with device type, position, and flow dynamics.

At the cellular level, mechanical rupture leads to loss of membrane integrity and hemoglobin release into plasma. Intravascular hemolysis raises plasma free hemoglobin and, when severe, produces hemoglobinuria and hemosiderinuria. Extravascular removal of RBC fragments by splenic macrophages may predominate in some conditions; this contributes to indirect hyperbilirubinemia and increased reticulocyte production. The pattern of laboratory abnormalities—whether intravascular or extravascular—depends on the balance between direct fragmentation within the circulation and phagocytic clearance of damaged cells in the spleen and liver.

# Laboratory markers: hemoglobin, reticulocyte count, LDH, bilirubin (indirect), haptoglobin, peripheral blood smear

Laboratory testing for suspected hemolysis proceeds from general screening tests to more specific assays. Interpretation requires correlation with clinical context because many analytes are influenced by comorbidities (liver disease, renal disease, recent transfusion), and timing relative to the hemolytic event.

Hemoglobin and hematocrit
Hemoglobin (Hgb) and hematocrit (Hct) reflect the net balance between red cell destruction and production. Reference ranges are approximately: Hgb 13.5–17.5 g/dL in adult males and 12.0–16.0 g/dL in adult females (age- and laboratory-specific). Acute hemolysis can precipitate rapid falls in Hgb, potentially producing symptomatic anemia. In chronic hemolysis, Hgb may be only mildly reduced because of marrow compensation. Severity of anemia guides immediate management such as transfusion.

Reticulocyte count
Reticulocyte count (expressed as percentage and often given as an absolute reticulocyte count per microliter) indicates bone marrow response. Normal retic percentage is about 0.5–2.5% in adults; an absolute retic count is often more informative. In hemolytic anemia with intact marrow, an appropriately elevated reticulocyte count (e.g., retic percentage > 3% with corrected retic index elevated) is expected within 3–5 days. A reticulocyte index or corrected reticulocyte percentage corrects for severity of anemia; values >2 typically indicate an adequate marrow response. A low reticulocyte response in the face of hemolysis suggests marrow suppression, nutrient deficiency (iron, folate), or overwhelming hemolysis exceeding marrow capacity.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)
LDH is abundant in erythrocytes and rises with cell lysis. Normal LDH values vary by assay and lab (commonly about 100–250 U/L). In intravascular hemolysis LDH can be markedly elevated. However, LDH is nonspecific and can be elevated in liver disease, muscle injury, and malignancy—interpretation requires correlation with other hemolysis markers.

Indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin
Indirect bilirubin increases as hemoglobin breakdown products are processed after phagocytosis of RBCs. Normal total bilirubin is typically 0.1–1.2 mg/dL and indirect bilirubin comprises most of this in hemolysis. In hemolytic processes, indirect bilirubin rises, often modestly; pronounced hyperbilirubinemia suggests either massive hemolysis or concomitant hepatobiliary dysfunction.

Haptoglobin
Haptoglobin binds free hemoglobin in plasma; the haptoglobin–hemoglobin complex is cleared by the reticuloendothelial system. Normal haptoglobin is typically 50–200 mg/dL (laboratory dependent). In intravascular hemolysis free hemoglobin is released into plasma and haptoglobin is consumed, resulting in low or undetectable haptoglobin. Extremely low haptoglobin supports intravascular hemolysis, but levels may be normal or elevated in extravascular hemolysis or in inflammatory states because haptoglobin is an acute-phase reactant. Moreover, patients with liver disease may have low baseline haptoglobin.

Peripheral blood smear
The peripheral smear is often the most informative test in suspected hemolysis. Key findings include:

- Schistocytes (fragmented RBCs): irregularly shaped RBC fragments, hallmark of MAHA. Quantification matters (see next section).
- Polychromasia and increased reticulocytes: reflect marrow response.
- Spherocytes: small dense RBCs lacking central pallor, suggestive of extravascular immune hemolysis (AIHA) or loss of membrane after partial phagocytosis.
- Agglutination: RBC clumping at room temperature suggests cold agglutinin disease.
- Bite cells, blister cells: indicate oxidative hemolysis (e.g., G6PD deficiency).
- Nucleated RBCs: can appear in severe hemolysis or marrow stress.

The smear integrates with quantitative markers to help determine whether hemolysis is primarily intravascular (e.g., marked schistocytosis, hemoglobinuria, low haptoglobin, high LDH) or extravascular (predominance of spherocytes, splenomegaly, indirect bilirubinemia, less dramatic hemoglobinuria).

Additional urine studies
Urinalysis may reveal hemoglobinuria (portable dipstick positive for blood but no RBCs on microscopy) indicating intravascular hemolysis. Hemosiderin in urine (Prussian blue stain) appears several days after hemolysis and reflects persistent hemoglobinuria and tubular iron deposition.

# Schistocytes: formation, quantification and differential causes

Formation and morphology
Schistocytes are fragmented red cells produced when RBCs encounter shear forces sufficient to tear the membrane. Morphologically, they include triangular "helmet" cells, triangular fragments, comma-shaped fragments, and irregular jagged pieces. Their presence signifies mechanical damage in the circulation and should prompt urgent evaluation for processes capable of producing microvascular obstruction or turbulent flow.

Quantification and diagnostic thresholds
Accurate identification and quantification of schistocytes is essential. Visual inspection by an experienced morphologist is the standard; automated counters are improving but cannot replace microscopy for diagnostic interpretation. The International Council for Standardization in Hematology (ICSH) recommends that a schistocyte count ≥1% of RBCs on a peripheral smear is significant and supports MAHA in the appropriate clinical context. Counts >2–3% are strongly suggestive of active microangiopathic hemolysis such as TTP/HUS. It is important to count a sufficient number of red cells (e.g., examining 500–1000 RBCs across representative fields) and to report schistocyte percentage in the clinical context. Laboratories should adhere to standardized morphologic criteria to reduce interobserver variability.

Differential causes of schistocytes
Schistocytes are not pathognomonic for TMA; other causes include:

- Thrombotic microangiopathies (TTP, HUS): classically produce pronounced schistocytosis with thrombocytopenia and elevated LDH.
- Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC): produces schistocytes along with prolonged coagulation times, elevated D-dimer, and low fibrinogen.
- Prosthetic cardiac valves and turbulent flow through stenotic valves: chronic low-grade schistocytosis due to mechanical trauma.
- Extracorporeal circuits (ECMO, ventricular assist devices): mechanical hemolysis.
- Severe burns: thermal injury disrupts membranes producing fragmentation.
- Vasculitis with severe endothelial injury.
- Metastatic tumors with microangiopathic features.
- Severe hypertension with endothelial damage.

When schistocytes are present, integrating additional tests such as platelet count (thrombocytopenia suggests TMA), coagulation panel (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer), renal function, and clinical features (neurologic symptoms in TTP, diarrhea preceding HUS) helps narrow the cause.

# Direct antiglobulin test (DAT/Coombs): indications, interpretation and causes of false positives

Indications and principle
The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) detects immunoglobulin (usually IgG) and/or complement (C3) coating the patient’s RBCs in vivo. It is indicated whenever immune-mediated hemolysis is suspected—when there is anemia with spherocytes on smear, positive hemolysis markers, or a history of drug exposure, autoimmune disease, or recent transfusion. The DAT helps distinguish autoimmune hemolytic anemia from nonimmune hemolytic processes.

Method and interpretation
In the DAT, patient RBCs are washed to remove unbound serum immunoglobulins and incubated with anti-human globulin; agglutination indicates the presence of RBC-bound IgG and/or complement. Results are typically reported as positive or negative and may specify which component is detected (anti-IgG, anti-C3). Interpretations:

- Positive anti-IgG DAT (with or without C3): supports warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia (warm AIHA), in which IgG autoantibodies bind RBCs at body temperature and lead to splenic destruction and spherocytosis.
- Positive anti-C3 with negative anti-IgG: suggests cold agglutinin disease or complement-mediated immune hemolysis, often due to IgM antibodies that fix complement at lower temperatures.
- Negative DAT: argues against immune-mediated hemolysis, but does not entirely exclude it (see false negatives below). A negative DAT in a patient with schistocytes favors MAHA/mechanical hemolysis, though mixed processes can coexist.

False positives and causes
The DAT can be falsely positive under several nonimmune conditions or due to technical factors. Causes include:

- Recent or ongoing transfusion: donor RBCs or passively transferred antibodies can coat cells.
- Intravenous immune globulin (IVIG): may passively transfer antibodies or coat RBCs.
- Hypergammaglobulinemia (e.g., multiple myeloma, Waldenström macroglobulinemia): nonspecific coating or increased background can produce weak positive DAT.
- Administration of monoclonal antibody therapies (e.g., daratumumab targeting CD38) or therapeutic antibodies that bind RBC antigens: these can produce strong DAT positivity that complicates transfusion testing.
- Certain drugs can produce positive DATs by modifying the RBC membrane or through immune mechanisms (e.g., penicillins, cephalosporins, alpha-methyldopa).
- Complement activation in sepsis or systemic inflammation may deposit C3 on RBCs.
- Technical errors: inadequate washing, contaminated reagents, or use of polyspecific antihuman globulin reagents can cause spurious positivity.

False negatives
DAT can be falsely negative when antibody coating is below detection threshold, antibodies are of subclasses poorly detected by reagents, or when autoantibodies are predominantly IgA (many labs test only for IgG and C3). Elution studies and more sensitive techniques (e.g., monospecific antisera, flow cytometry) can detect low-level or unusual coatings. In warm AIHA, a small proportion of patients may be DAT-negative despite immune hemolysis.

Clinical decision points
A positive DAT should prompt further evaluation: perform elution to identify the antibody specificity (if clinically indicated), review medication and transfusion history, and assess for associated diseases (autoimmune disease, lymphoproliferative disorder). In suspected hemolysis with negative DAT but features of immune hemolysis (spherocytes, high bilirubin, reticulocytosis) consider more sensitive testing or alternative mechanisms.

# Distinguishing autoimmune hemolytic anemia from MAHA and other causes

Differentiating AIHA from MAHA and other hemolytic processes requires integrating clinical features, peripheral smear morphology, laboratory markers, and immunohematologic testing.

Key distinguishing features
- Smear morphology: AIHA typically produces spherocytes and polychromasia due to partial phagocytosis of IgG-coated cells by splenic macrophages. Schistocytes are scarce in pure AIHA. In contrast, MAHA shows prominent schistocytes with variable spherocyte presence depending on secondary splenic processing.
- Platelet count and coagulation studies: TMA and DIC often manifest with thrombocytopenia; severe thrombocytopenia with schistocytes is classic for TTP/HUS. In AIHA, platelets are usually normal unless there is concurrent autoimmune thrombocytopenia (Evans syndrome).
- DAT: A positive DAT (anti-IgG and/or C3) strongly suggests AIHA, but may be positive in other contexts (see above). A negative DAT with schistocytes points away from AIHA and toward MAHA or mechanical hemolysis.
- Hemolysis pattern: Intravascular hemolysis (hemoglobinuria, markedly low haptoglobin, very elevated LDH) favors mechanical causes or complement-mediated AIHA (cold agglutinins that fix complement). Extravascular hemolysis (predominant indirect bilirubinemia, splenomegaly, spherocytes) is more typical of warm AIHA.
- Clinical context: Presence of autoimmune disease, lymphoproliferative disorders, or certain drugs favors AIHA. Recent cardiac surgery, known prosthetic valve, sepsis, malignant hypertension, or chemotherapeutic agents suggest mechanical or DIC-related hemolysis. Neurologic findings, renal dysfunction, or a prodrome of diarrheal illness may point to specific TMAs.

Algorithmic approach
A practical approach is to: (1) confirm hemolysis with at least two positive markers (low haptoglobin, elevated LDH, elevated indirect bilirubin, increased reticulocytes, hemoglobinuria), (2) examine the smear for schistocytes versus spherocytes, (3) check platelet count and coagulation studies to evaluate for TMA or DIC, (4) perform DAT on all patients with unexplained hemolysis or spherocytes, and (5) order targeted tests based on context—ADAMTS13 activity for suspected TTP, complement testing and stool assays for HUS, and antibody studies/autoimmune markers for AIHA.

Special considerations
Mixed processes can coexist (e.g., a patient with AIHA and DIC). Additionally, warm AIHA may produce fragments from rapid intravascular lysis and result in some schistocytes. The presence of a positive DAT does not exclude mechanical hemolysis; clinical correlation is essential.

# Transfusion considerations in hemolysis

Transfusion decisions in patients with hemolysis must balance the need to restore oxygen-carrying capacity against risks of exacerbating hemolysis and transfusion reactions. Key considerations include timing, indication, antibody compatibility, and product selection.

When to transfuse
Transfusion thresholds should be individualized. General guidance suggests transfusing for symptomatic anemia (tachycardia, hypotension, ischemic symptoms) or for Hgb levels where tissue oxygen delivery is compromised. In stable patients, consider transfusion when Hgb is <7 g/dL; adopt higher thresholds (e.g., <8–9 g/dL) for cardiovascular disease, ongoing blood loss, or severe hemolysis with inadequate compensatory response. In MAHA due to TTP, transfusion decisions are nuanced: red cell transfusion may be given for severe symptomatic anemia, but platelet transfusions are generally avoided unless life-threatening bleeding is present because platelets can fuel microthrombi.

Compatibility testing and DAT-positive patients
Positive DAT or broad autoantibodies complicate pretransfusion testing. In warm AIHA the patient’s plasma often agglutinates all donor cells, producing panagglutination on antibody screens. Strategies include:

- Performing an elution to characterize autoantibody; perform extended phenotyping/genotyping if feasible for future matching.
- Selecting the "least incompatible" unit if compatible blood cannot be found and the patient requires urgent transfusion—transfuse under close monitoring.
- Ideally provide antigen-matched units (e.g., Rh and Kell matched) to reduce risk of alloimmunization.
- For patients recently treated with monoclonal antibodies that interfere with compatibility testing (e.g., daratumumab), use specialized techniques (treat reagent RBCs with dithiothreitol to destroy CD38, or use anti-idiotype adsorption) and communicate with transfusion service.

Product selection and modifications
- Leukoreduced RBCs reduce febrile nonhemolytic reactions and certain alloimmunization risks.
- Irradiated products are indicated for immunocompromised patients (not specifically for hemolysis).
- Washed RBCs may be used in patients with severe allergic reactions to plasma proteins or when removal of plasma proteins is desirable, but washing does not prevent hemolytic reactions due to incompatible RBC antigens.
- For intravascular hemolysis with hemoglobinuria, maintain good hydration and consider diuresis to reduce tubular injury; RBC transfusion may be required if anemia is severe.

Special transfusion issues in mechanically induced hemolysis
Mechanical devices causing chronic hemolysis may lead to alloimmunization over time if transfusions are required. Documentation of baseline phenotype/genotype facilitates future matching. Addressing the mechanical source (valve repair/replacement or device adjustment) is essential for long-term control.

Safety and monitoring
Transfuse with slow infusion rates initially and monitor for signs of transfusion reactions or worsening hemolysis. Recheck hemoglobin and hemolysis markers after transfusion. In suspected acute hemolytic transfusion reaction, stop the transfusion, maintain intravenous access with normal saline, notify blood bank, send blood samples for repeat crossmatch, perform DAT on the post-transfusion sample, and monitor renal function and hemolysis markers.

Adjunctive therapies
Treat the underlying cause. In TTP, urgent plasma exchange is the mainstay and often obviates prolonged transfusion requirements. In AIHA, corticosteroids and immunosuppressants reduce autoantibody production and decrease transfusion needs. For complement-mediated hemolysis (e.g., paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, cold agglutinins causing complement fixation), complement inhibitors may be indicated.

Closing summary

Recognition of MAHA requires awareness of the mechanical mechanisms that fragment RBCs and cause intravascular hemolysis, use of appropriate laboratory markers to demonstrate hemolysis and its pattern (intravascular versus extravascular), and careful morphologic assessment for schistocytes versus spherocytes. The DAT is a critical test to evaluate immune-mediated hemolysis, but must be interpreted in clinical context due to possible false positives and negatives. Distinguishing AIHA from MAHA and other causes directs therapy and transfusion practice. Transfusion in hemolytic patients requires coordination with transfusion medicine specialists to provide compatible blood, minimize adverse reactions, and address the underlying etiology.

Introduction

Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is a histopathologic pattern characterized by injury to small vessels, most prominently arterioles, capillaries and glomerular microvasculature, that results in thrombosis, ischemia and organ dysfunction. In the kidney TMA produces a spectrum of acute and chronic lesions that reflect the timing, severity and mechanism of endothelial injury. Understanding the renal histopathology of TMA is essential for interpreting renal biopsies, integrating light microscopy (LM), immunofluorescence (IF) and electron microscopy (EM) findings with the clinical picture, and guiding prognosis and management. This chapter reviews the characteristic acute, subacute and chronic morphologic changes in the kidney in TMA, associated tubular changes, the typical IF and EM patterns and their role in excluding immune complex disease, practical considerations for biopsy sampling, and how histologic patterns correlate with clinical syndromes and outcome.

# Acute lesions: endothelial swelling, capillary wall thickening, fibrin thrombi, mesangiolysis and cortical necrosis

The earliest and most specific site of injury in TMA is the endothelium. Acute endothelial activation and injury produce a spectrum of LM changes that may be focal or diffuse and are commonly bilateral when systemic TMA is present.

Endothelial swelling (edema or "endothelial cell enlargement") is one of the earliest LM features. Injured endothelial cells become plump, their cytoplasm more eosinophilic, and they may protrude into and occlude capillary lumina. On PAS and H&E stains this can produce apparent narrowing or complete obliteration of capillary loops. Endothelial swelling is reversible in early injury but sets the stage for thrombus formation and loss of capillary perfusion.

Capillary wall thickening in acute TMA is typically due to accumulation of subendothelial material composed of fibrin, platelets, plasma proteins and cellular debris. When this material projects into the lumen and forms so-called "hyaline thrombi," the glomerular tuft appears more cellular and occluded. The capillary wall may demonstrate a laminated or "ballooned" appearance and often loses its normal crisp contour. The term "capillary wall thickening" in acute lesions refers to this accumulation rather than to chronic new basement membrane formation.

Fibrin thrombi are a hallmark of active TMA. On LM they appear as eosinophilic, amorphous, sometimes refractile material occluding capillary lumina, afferent arterioles or small arteries. Special stains such as Martius scarlet blue (MSB) or phosphotungstic acid hematoxylin (PTAH) can highlight fibrin within thrombi. Intraluminal fibrin may co-exist with platelet-rich thrombi and entrapped red blood cells. The presence of occlusive fibrin thrombi is indicative of ongoing endothelial injury and carries immediate clinical implications because it is associated with organ ischemia and acute deterioration of renal function.

Mesangiolysis refers to dissolution or loss of mesangial architecture and support. In severe acute TMA, the mesangial matrix becomes fragmented and the mesangial cells show vacuolation or detachment. Mesangiolysis contributes to capillary wall instability and may be visible as widening of the mesangial regions with vacuolated or "flocculent" material on LM. Mesangiolysis may progress to capillary wall rupture and bleeding.

Cortical necrosis represents the most severe manifestation of acute microvascular occlusion. When a large volume of cortical microvasculature is thrombosed, ischemic necrosis of the renal cortex occurs. Grossly the cortex is pale and often sharply demarcated from the medulla; microscopically there is coagulative necrosis of glomeruli and tubules with loss of nuclei and structural detail. Cortical necrosis typically produces irreversible renal damage and is associated clinically with severe TMA syndromes, obstetric complications, catastrophic complement-mediated injury, or prolonged hypotension with microthrombotic disease.

Key clinical correlation points: acute lesions with endothelial swelling, fibrin thrombi and mesangiolysis are commonly observed in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS), severe malignant hypertension and other causes of systemic microangiopathy. In clinical practice, the presence of fibrin thrombi and acute endothelial injury on biopsy is a strong indicator of ongoing active TMA, and if accompanied by thrombocytopenia and hemolysis, suggests the need for urgent systemic therapy.

# Subacute/chronic changes: double-contour GBM, onion-skin lesions, glomerulosclerosis and interstitial fibrosis

When endothelial injury is prolonged or recurrent but less fulminant than in acute TMA, the kidney develops distinctive subacute and chronic structural changes that reflect reparative and maladaptive responses. These include double contours of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM), concentric ("onion-skin") changes in small arteries and arterioles, glomerulosclerosis, tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis.

Double-contour GBM (also called GBM duplication or "tram-tracking") is produced by repeated cycles of endothelial injury and subendothelial accumulation of matrix and cellular elements followed by new basement membrane formation by endothelial or endothelial–mesangial cells. On LM, especially on silver or PAS stains, capillary walls show two or more parallel lines separated by a widened subendothelial zone; on EM this corresponds to subendothelial widening with new basement membrane material on the endothelial side. Double contours are classic for chronic TMA but are not specific; they may also be seen in membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN) and chronic antibody-mediated rejection. Correlation with IF (absence of granular immune complex deposits) and clinical context (history of recurrent TMA, complement dysregulation, malignancy or calcineurin inhibitor toxicity) helps differentiate causes.

Onion-skin lesions refer to concentric intimal hyperplasia of arterioles and small arteries. The intima becomes thickened with laminated smooth muscle cell proliferation, reduplication of elastic lamina and interposed matrix, producing the characteristic layered appearance. This reflects chronic endothelial and medial injury with reparative smooth muscle proliferation and is frequently seen in chronic malignant hypertension and chronic TMA. The concentric narrowing of arteriolar lumina contributes to ongoing ischemia downstream and to progression to global glomerulosclerosis.

Glomerulosclerosis in chronic TMA usually follows ischemic injury. Global glomerulosclerosis may result from complete obliteration of afferent blood flow or chronic hypoperfusion; segmental scars are also common. Histologically these sclerosis lesions are often ischemic (shrunken, collapsed capillary tuft with wrinkled GBM and increased matrix) rather than the hypercellular scarring of immune-mediated disease. Over time, replacement of nephrons by sclerotic glomeruli contributes to progressive decline in glomerular filtration rate.

Interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (IF/TA) are the downstream consequences of chronic microvascular ischemia and recurrent acute injury. Tubules in affected areas are thinned, with loss of brush border and cytoplasmic simplification; interstitial collagen replaces normal tissue. The pattern and extent of IF/TA on biopsy are strongly predictive of long-term renal outcome; extensive chronic changes indicate irreversible injury and poorer recovery potential.

It is important to recognize that the kidney may show a mixture of acute and chronic lesions in the same biopsy — for example active thrombi in some glomeruli and double-contour GBM in others — reflecting ongoing or relapsing disease. Distinguishing chronic adaptive changes from irreversible scarring helps clinicians estimate the potential for renal recovery.

# Tubular changes in ischemia and ATN

Tubular epithelial cells are highly susceptible to ischemia and are frequently affected in TMA due to microvascular occlusion. The tubular changes span a continuum from reversible epithelial injury to overt acute tubular necrosis (ATN) and chronic tubular atrophy.

Early ischemic tubular injury manifests as epithelial cell swelling, loss of brush border in proximal tubules, cytoplasmic vacuolization, and nuclear crowding or pyknosis. These changes can be patchy and focal, particularly in the outer medulla where oxygen demand is high and perfusion is most precarious. Clinically this corresponds to an acute rise in serum creatinine and oliguria.

ATN features include denuded basement membranes, sloughed tubular epithelial cells and granular cellular debris within tubular lumina, cast formation, and variable degrees of interstitial edema and inflammatory cell infiltration (usually modest and neutrophil-sparse). Tubules may show flattening and simplification of epithelium with loss of polarity. On LM, ATN is best appreciated in the proximal tubules and medullary thick ascending limb in the outer medulla. Recovery of ATN requires intact tubular basement membranes and resolution of ischemia; if vascular supply remains compromised by TMA, ATN can progress to permanent tubular atrophy.

In chronic TMA-related ischemia the tubules become atrophic with thickened tubular basement membranes and loss of epithelial cells. Tubular atrophy often co-localizes with interstitial fibrosis and sclerotic glomeruli. The severity of tubular changes often correlates with chronicity and extent of vascular lesions; marked IF/TA suggests low likelihood of recovery with therapy directed at the microangiopathy.

Clinically, the pattern of tubular injury in TMA interacts with systemic features: in severe microangiopathy with cortical necrosis and extensive thrombi, ATN may coexist and contribute to oliguric renal failure; while in more focal microvascular involvement serum creatinine may be only modestly elevated if sufficient nephrons remain functional.

# Immunofluorescence patterns in TMA and exclusion of immune complex diseases

Immunofluorescence (IF) plays a dual role in the evaluation of suspected renal TMA: it helps to document nonspecific plasma protein and complement deposition related to endothelial injury, and — importantly — it helps exclude immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritides that can present with overlapping histologic features.

In TMA, IF is often pauci-immune or shows only non-specific trapping. The most common IF pattern is weak to moderate granular or smudgy staining for IgM and C3 in areas of mesangial expansion, hyaline thrombi or sclerotic segments. IgA and IgG are usually absent or very faint. C3 deposition may be prominent in some cases and reflect alternative pathway complement activation (for example in complement-mediated aHUS); however, in TMA the C3 pattern tends to be granular and not in the classic subepithelial or subendothelial immune-complex distribution seen in MPGN or lupus nephritis.

Fibrinogen/fibrin immunostaining can highlight intravascular fibrin thrombi that may be inconspicuous on routine stains. Staining for platelet markers (e.g., CD61 or CD42b) can confirm platelet-rich thrombi. In clinical practice, demonstration of fibrin within luminal occluding material supports a diagnosis of TMA.

A critical diagnostic purpose of IF is to exclude immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis. Diseases such as MPGN, lupus nephritis, IgA nephropathy and membranous nephropathy show characteristic patterns of immunoglobulin and complement deposition: for example, granular capillary wall and mesangial staining for IgG and C3 in MPGN, "full house" (IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C1q) in lupus nephritis, or dominant IgA in IgA nephropathy. These immune complex patterns are typically accompanied by electron-dense deposits in characteristic locations on EM. When IF is essentially negative or shows only non-specific IgM/C3, and EM lacks classic immune complex deposits, the pathologist favors TMA over immune complex disease.

One caveat is that certain conditions can combine features: for example, severe malignant hypertension can have secondary IgM trapping, and complement dysregulation states may show C3 deposition without immunoglobulins. Therefore IF findings must be interpreted in the overall clinicopathologic context. When C3 is strongly positive and immunoglobulins are absent, complement-mediated processes should be considered and may prompt serologic complement testing (C3, C4, CH50) and genetic or functional complement studies when clinically indicated. Reference ranges: serum C3 normal roughly 90–180 mg/dL, C4 normal roughly 10–40 mg/dL, though lab-specific ranges vary.

# Electron microscopy findings: endothelial injury, subendothelial widening and electron-dense deposits (when present)

Electron microscopy (EM) provides ultrastructural confirmation of endothelial and capillary wall abnormalities that define TMA and can detect subtle immune complex deposits that are invisible on LM or IF. EM is therefore indispensable in many cases.

The classic EM feature of TMA is endothelial cell injury with widening of the subendothelial zone. Injured endothelial cells become swollen, with cytoplasmic vacuolization, loss of fenestrations and detachment from the GBM. The subendothelial zone is characteristically expanded by an electron-lucent or granular material composed of plasma proteins, fibrin, platelets and cellular debris. This subendothelial widening corresponds to the double-contour appearance on LM when new basement membrane is subsequently formed.

Intraluminal fibrin and platelet aggregates can be identified on EM as amorphous electron-dense fibrillar material and aggregates of platelets, sometimes with entrapped erythrocytes. These findings are the ultrastructural correlate of LM fibrin thrombi and confirm the thrombotic nature of the lesion.

When present, electron-dense immune complex–type deposits are typically located in distinct anatomic compartments (subepithelial, subendothelial, mesangial) and have characteristic appearances: granular immune complexes in MPGN and lupus, "hump"-like subepithelial deposits in postinfectious GN, etc. In TMA, genuine electron-dense immune complex deposits are generally absent; their presence should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis or raise the possibility of overlapping immune-complex mediated disease. Of note, occasionally electron-dense granular material may be seen in the subendothelial zone in TMA representing fibrin/platelet aggregates rather than immunoglobulin complexes; correlation with IF is essential.

Other EM findings in chronic TMA include reduplication of the GBM with interposition of mesangial-like cells or matrix between layers, consistent with chronic reparative responses that produce the double contours seen on LM. Endothelial cell nuclei may show pyknosis and loss of organelles in chronic injury.

In summary, EM confirms the presence and location of endothelial injury, subendothelial electron-lucent zones (or electron-dense thrombotic material), and helps exclude or identify immune complex deposits that would favor other diagnoses.

# Biopsy indications, sampling considerations and limitations

Renal biopsy is a key diagnostic tool in suspected TMA but must be performed with clear indications and an understanding of its limitations. Indications for biopsy in TMA include unexplained acute kidney injury with laboratory evidence of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and thrombocytopenia, nephritic-range proteinuria or hematuria when the distinction between immune disease and TMA is unclear, suspected anti-VEGF or calcineurin inhibitor–related TMA, and when renal-limited TMA is suspected without systemic features.

Timing of biopsy is important. In the acute fulminant setting (rapidly progressive renal failure with systemic TMA), biopsy can demonstrate active fibrin thrombi and endothelial injury and may guide urgent therapy. However, when thrombocytopenia is severe (<50,000/µL), the bleeding risk from percutaneous biopsy increases and alternative strategies (e.g., transjugular biopsy) may be considered. Normal platelet count range: 150,000–450,000/µL; decisions must balance diagnostic need with bleeding risk. When TTP is strongly suspected and urgent plasma exchange is indicated, clinicians sometimes delay biopsy until the patient stabilizes because the biopsy result is unlikely to change immediate management.

Sampling considerations: adequate renal tissue is essential. For glomerular diseases a minimum of 10–12 glomeruli is often recommended to confidently assess lesion distribution; for TMA, because lesions may be focal or segmental, more glomeruli improve sensitivity. Biopsies should include cortex (where glomeruli and cortical thrombi are located); reliance on medullary tissue alone risks missing cortical lesions. For IF and EM studies, separate tissue cores (one for IF in Michel's transport medium or saline if immediate freezing unavailable, and one for EM in glutaraldehyde) are recommended. Coordination with the pathology laboratory before biopsy ensures proper handling.

Limitations: TMA can be patchy; a single core biopsy may miss focal lesions leading to false negatives. Chronic changes may mask acute lesions if sampling occurs late in the disease course. Small thrombi may be missed or misinterpreted when processing artifacts or crush injury are present. Another limitation is that IF may be non-diagnostic in complement-mediated TMA where C3 deposition can be subtle. Finally, biopsy cannot by itself determine the systemic etiology of TMA; clinicopathologic correlation and directed laboratory testing (e.g., ADAMTS13 activity, complement studies, serologies, drug exposures) remain essential.

In situations of high bleeding risk, transjugular renal biopsy provides a safer alternative and can still yield diagnostic tissue. In suspected cortical necrosis or severe TMA, the diagnostic yield may be higher when multiple passes are obtained.

# Correlation of histologic patterns with prognosis

Histologic patterns in renal TMA contain prognostic information but must be interpreted alongside clinical parameters such as serum creatinine, degree of thrombocytopenia, presence of extra-renal organ involvement, and underlying etiology.

Acute features such as widespread fibrin thrombi, extensive cortical necrosis, and severe acute tubular necrosis are associated with poor short-term prognosis and a higher likelihood of progression to dialysis dependence. Fibrin-rich occlusive thrombi that involve a large proportion of glomeruli or afferent arterioles typically predict severe, often irreversible loss of renal mass if not rapidly reversed. Cortical necrosis generally signals irreversible cortical loss and poor recovery.

Conversely, biopsies that show primarily endothelial swelling, focal thrombi and limited ATN, with minimal chronic changes, indicate there may be substantial potential for recovery if the underlying cause is promptly corrected. The reversibility of endothelial swelling and resolution of thrombi under effective therapy (plasmapheresis for TTP, complement blockade for aHUS, withdrawal of offending drugs) underpins the possibility of renal function recovery in such cases.

Chronic features — extensive double-contour GBM formation, widespread glomerulosclerosis, concentric arteriolar intimal hyperplasia (onion-skinning), and marked IF/TA — predict irreversible injury and worse long-term renal outcome. Quantitatively, the proportion of globally sclerotic glomeruli and the percentage area of interstitial fibrosis correlate inversely with the likelihood of renal recovery; for instance, biopsies with >50% cortical fibrosis and widespread global sclerosis generally indicate low probability of regaining adequate renal function.

Mixed patterns require nuanced interpretation. A biopsy showing both active thrombi and significant chronic scarring suggests ongoing recurrences or delayed diagnosis; even if acute components are treated, the chronic component limits recovery. Studies have shown that the presence of chronic vascular lesions (onion-skinning, intimal thickening) is an independent predictor of progression to chronic kidney disease and end-stage renal disease in several microangiopathic contexts.

Finally, the histologic correlation with systemic disease matters. For example, in TTP (severe ADAMTS13 deficiency) renal lesions may be modest compared with systemic findings and renal recovery is often possible with plasma exchange. In contrast, complement-mediated aHUS frequently causes more severe and progressive renal vascular and glomerular lesions and a greater tendency to progress to chronic kidney disease unless complement blockade is instituted early.

In summary, the pattern and severity of acute versus chronic histologic changes on renal biopsy provide important prognostic insight: predominance of acute reversible lesions favors potential recovery; predominance of chronic scarring indicates limited reversibility and poorer outcome.

Concluding remarks

Renal pathology in TMA reflects a dynamic interplay between endothelial injury, coagulation, complement activation and reparative responses. Recognizing the spectrum from acute endothelial swelling and fibrin thrombi to chronic double-contour GBM, onion-skin arteriolopathy and interstitial scarring is essential for diagnosis and prognosis. Integration of LM, IF and EM findings allows robust differentiation of primary TMA from immune complex or other glomerular diseases, and guides further laboratory evaluation and therapeutic decision-making. Adequate biopsy sampling and careful clinicopathologic correlation are critical given the patchy nature of TMA and the profound implications of histologic stage for reversibility and outcome.

Introduction

Capnocytophaga canimorsus and related Capnocytophaga species are gram-negative, fastidious bacteria that form an important though uncommon cause of infections transmitted from dogs and cats. Though often part of the normal oral flora of these animals and frequently associated with minor, self-limited wound infections, Capnocytophaga canimorsus has a disproportionate capacity to cause fulminant invasive disease in certain hosts. Understanding the microbiology, virulence mechanisms, epidemiology, clinical presentations, and appropriate management of Capnocytophaga and other bacterial zoonoses of dog and cat exposures is essential for clinicians who care for patients with animal bites, scratches, licks to broken skin, or close unprotected contact. This chapter reviews the classification and laboratory features of Capnocytophaga species, explores key virulence determinants that explain their clinical behavior, outlines epidemiologic risk factors for severe disease, summarizes the spectrum of clinical syndromes, and describes antimicrobial susceptibility patterns and empiric therapy decisions for animal-bite–associated infections. The final section covers wound-care and prevention strategies that mitigate the risk of infection.

# Classification and microbiology of Capnocytophaga species

Capnocytophaga is a genus of gram-negative rods in the family Flavobacteriaceae. Capnocytophaga species that are most relevant clinically include Capnocytophaga canimorsus and Capnocytophaga cynodegmi, both associated with dogs and cats; other Capnocytophaga species (for example, those in humans such as C. ochracea, C. sputigena) can colonize human oropharynx and cause periodontal disease or occasionally opportunistic infections. Capnocytophaga canimorsus is classically described as a fusiform (spindle-shaped) gram-negative rod. They are non–spore-forming, non–lactose-fermenting, and generally oxidase-negative. They are fastidious organisms: growth is improved in enriched media (e.g., blood or chocolate agar) and under increased carbon dioxide tension (they are capnophilic), and they may take several days (often 48–72 hours or longer) to form visible colonies.

Because Capnocytophaga are slow-growing and fastidious, routine culture techniques may miss them unless laboratory staff are alerted to the clinical context of an animal bite. Blood cultures from bacteremic patients will often become positive, but growth may be delayed. Colonies are typically smooth, glistening, and may produce yellowish pigmentation on prolonged incubation. Modern identification in the clinical microbiology laboratory employs matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF), biochemical identification systems with extended databases, or molecular methods such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing when phenotypic identification is unclear.

Capnophilia is an important laboratory feature — incubating plates in an atmosphere of 5–10% CO2 accelerates growth and increases yield. Routine automated susceptibility testing systems can be used for many isolates, although the slow growth and rarity of the organism may require sending isolates to reference laboratories for definitive identification and susceptibility testing. On Gram stain of a clinical specimen, Capnocytophaga appear as thin, fusiform gram-negative rods; in blood culture Gram stains they may be sparse and require careful examination.

# Virulence determinants: capsule, sialidases, immune evasion strategies and serum susceptibility

The clinical capacity of Capnocytophaga canimorsus to cause fulminant sepsis in otherwise apparently minor inoculation events reflects an array of virulence factors that facilitate survival in mammalian hosts and evade innate and adaptive immune responses.

Capsule and surface polysaccharides: Many C. canimorsus strains produce a polysaccharide capsule that shields them from phagocytosis and complement-mediated killing. The capsule contributes to serum resistance by inhibiting effective opsonization and reducing deposition of complement components on the bacterial surface. Capsule expression is a major determinant of virulence in animal models; non-encapsulated strains are typically less pathogenic and more readily cleared.

Sialidases and glycan-modifying enzymes: C. canimorsus secretes glycosidases, including sialidase (neuraminidase) activity, that cleave terminal sialic acids and other sugar residues from host glycoconjugates. These enzymes have dual roles. First, they liberate host-derived sugars that the organism can use as nutrients, supporting growth in nutrient-poor niches such as the bloodstream. Second, removal or alteration of sialic acid residues on host glycoproteins can interfere with immune recognition and antibody function. In some strains, the organism can deglycosylate the Fc portion of human IgG, impairing antibody effector functions and reducing opsonization.

Complement and neutrophil evasion: Beyond capsule-mediated resistance, C. canimorsus has multiple strategies to blunt complement activation and phagocytic killing. Some strains bind host complement regulatory proteins, such as factor H, which downregulate the alternative complement pathway and protect the bacterium from lytic complement activity. Capnocytophaga also produces factors that impair neutrophil function; for example, they can resist intracellular killing and may blunt neutrophil oxidative burst responses. The net effect is reduced early innate immune control of invasion.

Modulation of innate sensing and inflammatory responses: C. canimorsus elicits a comparatively weak proinflammatory cytokine response relative to many other gram-negative pathogens. The organism’s lipooligosaccharide (LOS) structure tends to be less stimulatory for Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) than typical endotoxin, resulting in muted early cytokine signals that could otherwise recruit immune cells and limit spread. At the same time, the organism can induce certain damaging host responses late in infection, contributing to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and multi-organ dysfunction in severe cases.

Serum susceptibility variability: Although many virulent strains of C. canimorsus are serum-resistant, there is heterogeneity among isolates. Some strains are more susceptible to normal human serum in vitro. Nonetheless, clinical isolates implicated in severe invasive disease frequently demonstrate serum resistance mediated by capsule and complement-regulatory protein interactions. This variability has implications for laboratory interpretation and highlights that host factors (e.g., asplenia) are critical in determining the course after exposure.

Together, these virulence determinants allow Capnocytophaga canimorsus to survive in blood and tissues after inoculation, often replicating without producing an early, robust inflammatory signal. This stealthy behavior explains why patients may present late with fulminant bacteremia, purpura fulminans, or other severe systemic manifestations after exposures that initially seemed trivial.

# Epidemiology and risk factors for invasive disease

Capnocytophaga species are part of the normal oral microbiota of domestic dogs and cats. Prevalence studies show that a high proportion of dogs and cats carry Capnocytophaga in their mouths — estimates vary by population and detection method, but colonization is common. Human infection arises most commonly after a bite, but scratches, close facial contact (licking of mucous membranes or broken skin), and even near-miss exposures can transmit organisms. Not all exposures lead to infection; the absolute risk of infection after a dog or cat bite is modest, but the risk of Capnocytophaga specifically is low in absolute terms while still clinically important because of the potential severity of invasive disease.

Population-level epidemiology: Cases of invasive Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection are rare relative to the frequency of dog bites. Most infections are sporadic; outbreaks are uncommon. Reported cases occur worldwide and are associated with both household pets and stray animals. There is seasonal variation in animal bite incidence in many regions, but no well-established seasonal pattern specific to Capnocytophaga.

Host risk factors for severe disease: Several host factors clearly increase the likelihood of invasive disease or severe outcomes after exposure:

- Asplenia or functional hyposplenism. The spleen is crucial for clearing encapsulated organisms and bacteria in the bloodstream; asplenic patients are at markedly increased risk of overwhelming sepsis from Capnocytophaga. These patients may develop fulminant bacteremia with rapid progression to septic shock and DIC.

- Alcohol use disorder and chronic liver disease. Historically, many severe cases have occurred in patients with alcoholism, cirrhosis, or related immunologic impairment.

- Other immunocompromising conditions. Solid organ or hematologic malignancy, immunosuppressive therapy (including cytotoxic agents, corticosteroids, biologics), HIV infection with low CD4 counts, and hematologic disorders can increase risk.

- Older age. Although invasive disease can occur at any age, older adults appear overrepresented among severe cases.

- Pre-existing skin conditions or poor wound care may facilitate local infection and spread.

Risk factors related to the exposure itself: Bites, especially deep puncture wounds and those involving crushing forces, are more likely to introduce bacteria into deeper tissues and thus increase the risk for infection. Hand wounds and injuries that reach bone or periosteum are high risk for complications because of anatomic constraints (limited capacity to drain, tight fascial compartments). Facial bites, though well vascularized and at low risk for severe infection relative to hands, may prompt different management because of cosmetic concerns. Repeated licking of an existing skin break can transmit Capnocytophaga even without a discrete bite.

Population groups requiring prophylaxis: Clinical guidelines commonly advise considering antibiotic prophylaxis after dog or cat bites in the following scenarios: (a) asplenic patients or those with significant immunocompromise, (b) bites to the hand, face, or over cartilage, (c) deep puncture wounds or crush injuries, (d) wounds requiring surgical repair, and (e) patients with prosthetic joints or other implanted prostheses at the exposure site. The heightened risk of Capnocytophaga and other pathogens in asplenic or immunocompromised hosts makes prophylaxis particularly important in those groups.

# Clinical syndromes: local wound infection, sepsis, meningitis, endocarditis and uncommon complications

Capnocytophaga infections produce a range of clinical syndromes, from localized cellulitis at the site of a bite or scratch to fulminant systemic disease. The timing of onset may vary: localized infections may become clinically apparent within 24–72 hours, whereas invasive disease sometimes presents several days after the exposure and can progress rapidly once bacteremia develops.

Local wound infections: The most frequent clinical manifestation after an animal bite is soft-tissue infection of the wound — cellulitis, localized abscess, or lymphangitis. Infections due to Capnocytophaga may present with erythema, warmth, tenderness, and purulent drainage. Because Capnocytophaga grows slowly, wound cultures may be polymicrobial (dog and cat oral flora include Pasteurella multocida, streptococci, staphylococci, anaerobes such as Bacteroides and Fusobacterium, and others), and clinical management must account for this polymicrobial nature. Capnocytophaga itself can be part of polymicrobial wound flora or the dominant pathogen.

Bacteremia and sepsis: In some patients, particularly those with predisposing host factors (asplenia, cirrhosis, heavy alcohol use, immunosuppression), C. canimorsus can invade the bloodstream and cause bacteremia with sepsis. Clinical features of systemic infection include rapidly progressive fever, chills, hypotension, tachycardia, and signs of organ dysfunction. A distinctive and feared complication is purpura fulminans or disseminated purpura with cutaneous necrosis, often associated with DIC and gangrene of the extremities. The rapidity of progression can be alarming; some patients develop shock and multi-organ failure within hours to days of initial symptoms.

Meningitis: Though relatively uncommon, Capnocytophaga can cause meningitis. Cases are described in adults and children, and presentation is similar to other bacterial meningitides — fever, headache, neck stiffness, altered mental status. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) typically shows pleocytosis with neutrophilic predominance, low glucose, and elevated protein. Diagnosis may be aided by positive blood cultures; CSF cultures can be positive but growth may be delayed.

Endocarditis: Infective endocarditis due to Capnocytophaga species is uncommon but reported. Native valve endocarditis and prosthetic valve infection have both been described. Patients may present subacutely with fever, new murmur, embolic phenomena, or signs of congestive heart failure. Given the organism’s capacity to evade immune mechanisms, valve infection can be indolent and progress before diagnosis.

Osteomyelitis and septic arthritis: Direct inoculation or hematogenous spread can produce osteomyelitis or septic arthritis. Hand infections are particularly prone to involve deep spaces and bone. Imaging and surgical exploration may be necessary for diagnosis and source control.

Ocular and other focal infections: Capnocytophaga can cause corneal ulcers and keratitis, especially after cat or dog claw injuries or contact with saliva. Rare presentations include intraabdominal abscesses, pleuropulmonary infection, and peritonitis in patients on peritoneal dialysis. The breadth of possible focal infections emphasizes the organism’s versatility once host defenses are breached.

Laboratory and diagnostic clues: In severe disease, blood cultures frequently grow gram-negative rods that are later identified as Capnocytophaga. However, because growth is slow, empiric management decisions should not await culture confirmation in high-risk patients. The combination of a history of dog or cat exposure (bite, scratch, lick), rapid progression to sepsis, and the presence of purpura or DIC should raise suspicion for C. canimorsus among other potential pathogens.

# Antimicrobial susceptibility patterns and empirical therapy considerations for animal bite–associated infections

Antimicrobial susceptibility of Capnocytophaga species is generally favorable for several antibiotic classes, but there is variability and occasional beta-lactamase production, so empirical choices must consider both Capnocytophaga and the polymicrobial nature of animal-bite infections.

Typical susceptibility patterns: C. canimorsus is usually susceptible to penicillins (e.g., penicillin G, amoxicillin) and to expanded-spectrum beta-lactams such as third-generation cephalosporins (e.g., ceftriaxone), carbapenems, and beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations (e.g., amoxicillin-clavulanate, piperacillin-tazobactam). It is also generally susceptible to doxycycline, clindamycin (variable), and some fluoroquinolones (e.g., levofloxacin, moxifloxacin). However, some isolates produce beta-lactamases, which can render them resistant to ampicillin or amoxicillin alone. Resistance to aminoglycosides is common due to intrinsic factors (poor activity against this organism).

Empiric therapy for animal bites: Clinical guidelines for management of dog and cat bites typically recommend amoxicillin-clavulanate as first-line oral therapy for prophylaxis or treatment of uncomplicated bite-related infections. Amoxicillin-clavulanate covers the typical aerobic and anaerobic flora of dog and cat mouths, including Pasteurella species, Capnocytophaga, streptococci, staphylococci, and anaerobes. The recommended adult prophylactic dosing commonly used is amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg orally twice daily (or 500/125 mg three times daily) for 3–5 days for prophylaxis in uncomplicated wounds in high-risk patients, with longer courses for established infections.

For patients with penicillin allergy, alternatives depend on the type of allergy. For non–type I (non–immediate) hypersensitivity, cephalosporins may be used cautiously. For true IgE-mediated penicillin allergy, recommended alternatives that cover Capnocytophaga and Pasteurella include doxycycline (100 mg orally twice daily) or a fluoroquinolone (e.g., moxifloxacin) combined with metronidazole or clindamycin to ensure anaerobic coverage. However, doxycycline alone often provides reasonable coverage for Capnocytophaga and Pasteurella but may have incomplete activity against some streptococci and staphylococci; local resistance patterns and the nature of the wound should guide choice. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole provides good coverage for some bite pathogens but has limited activity against anaerobes and variable activity against Pasteurella; it is often paired with metronidazole for anaerobic coverage. Clindamycin alone is not adequate because it lacks reliable activity against Pasteurella and Capnocytophaga.

Management of severe systemic infection: In patients with suspected or confirmed severe C. canimorsus sepsis, empirical parenteral therapy should be broad enough to cover gram-negative rods, Pasteurella, and anaerobes, and to provide reliable bactericidal activity. Intravenous options include piperacillin-tazobactam, a third-generation cephalosporin (e.g., ceftriaxone) plus metronidazole, or a carbapenem in critically ill patients. Ceftriaxone alone is often adequate for Capnocytophaga and Pasteurella, but if polymicrobial infection including anaerobes is suspected (e.g., deep puncture wound), add metronidazole or choose piperacillin-tazobactam. For patients with MRSA risk or severe skin and soft tissue infection where staphylococcal infection is a concern, vancomycin or another anti-MRSA agent may be added empirically pending cultures.

Duration of therapy: Duration must be individualized by clinical syndrome. For uncomplicated prophylaxis, short courses (3–5 days) are typically recommended for high-risk exposures. For established soft-tissue infections, 5–14 days is common, depending on severity and clinical response. For bacteremia, meningitis, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, or deep-seated infections, longer courses consistent with standard treatment durations for those syndromes are indicated (e.g., 4–6 weeks for endocarditis or osteomyelitis depending on source control and response). Clinical response and culture results guide de-escalation and duration.

Laboratory susceptibility testing and consultation: Because Capnocytophaga isolates are uncommon and may show variable susceptibility patterns, obtaining culture and susceptibility testing is important when possible. When isolates are available, clinicians should involve infectious diseases consultation for complex cases to interpret susceptibility results and manage prolonged therapy decisions.

# Prevention of bite-related infections and wound management

Prevention of infection after dog or cat exposures involves prompt wound care, risk stratification for prophylactic antibiotics, consideration of tetanus and rabies prophylaxis when indicated, and education to reduce future risk.

Immediate wound management: The cornerstone of preventing infection is thorough cleansing and irrigation of any bite or scratch. High-pressure irrigation with normal saline or sterile water reduces bacterial inoculum and foreign material. Wounds should be explored for damaged structures (tendons, nerves, vessels), and devitalized tissue should be debrided. Puncture wounds and deep penetrating injuries may trap bacteria in deep tissue and are at higher risk for infection.

Wound closure decisions: Primary closure (suturing) of bite wounds can be considered for carefully selected facial wounds for cosmetic reasons because of the rich vascular supply, but many wounds are better left open to heal by secondary intention if the risk of infection is high (e.g., hand wounds, deep punctures, heavily contaminated wounds). Delayed primary closure after 48–72 hours can be used when initial contamination is controlled and there is no early sign of infection. Suturing should be done in a manner that allows drainage when needed.

Indications for antibiotic prophylaxis: Prophylactic antibiotics should be considered for patients at increased risk for infection and those with wounds that are high risk for poor outcome. Indications commonly include asplenia or functional hyposplenism, immunocompromised states, moderate-to-severe wounds (deep punctures, crushing injuries), wounds to the hand or face requiring surgical repair, wounds in close proximity to bone or joint, and those requiring closure. Amoxicillin-clavulanate is the first-line prophylactic agent for most patients. For asplenic or significantly immunocompromised patients, clinicians should have a low threshold to provide prophylaxis and to observe closely.

Tetanus prophylaxis and rabies assessment: Update tetanus immunization status according to standard immunization guidelines: give tetanus booster if indicated (for example, if >5 years since last tetanus toxoid-containing vaccine in a severe or contaminated wound). Assess rabies risk based on animal behavior, regional rabies prevalence, and availability of the animal for observation or testing. Rabies postexposure prophylaxis is indicated for high-risk exposures or when the animal cannot be observed and rabies is suspected.

Pet-related counseling: Educate patients about safe interactions with animals, avoidance of rough play that may provoke bites, the importance of hand hygiene after animal contact, and the need to seek medical attention for bites that are deep, show signs of infection, or occur in high-risk individuals (asplenic, immunocompromised, elderly). For owners of pets with known bite behavior, referral to veterinary behavioral assessment and training may reduce future incidents.

Special considerations for high-risk patients: Asplenic patients should be advised to seek urgent medical care for any dog or cat bite, and clinicians should consider both prophylactic antibiotics and close observation/hospitalization for early signs of systemic illness. Patients with prosthetic implants or valvular heart disease merit consideration of prophylactic therapy and careful follow-up.

Surveillance and laboratory communication: Given the fastidious nature of Capnocytophaga, clinicians should notify the microbiology laboratory when Capnocytophaga infection is suspected so that culture plates can be incubated appropriately (extended incubation, CO2 atmosphere) and isolates can be processed for identification and susceptibility testing. For severe or unusual presentations, sending isolates to a reference laboratory may aid in definitive characterization.

Conclusion

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an important zoonotic pathogen associated with dog and cat bites, scratches, and close contact. Although infection is uncommon relative to the frequency of animal exposures, the organism’s virulence mechanisms and the enhanced susceptibility of certain hosts (notably asplenic, alcoholic, or immunocompromised individuals) make it a clinically significant cause of severe sepsis, meningitis, and other invasive syndromes. Clinicians must combine careful wound management, appropriate use of prophylactic antibiotics in high-risk situations (first-line amoxicillin-clavulanate), timely empiric therapy for suspected invasive disease, and targeted management based on culture and susceptibility results. Preventive measures, patient education, and prompt recognition remain central to reducing morbidity and mortality from Capnocytophaga and other bacterial infections transmitted by dogs and cats.

Introduction

Bloodstream infection (BSI) is a common and potentially life‑threatening clinical problem. Accurate, timely microbiological diagnosis directly guides antimicrobial therapy and infection control measures. The diagnostic process for bacteremia combines classical culture techniques with an expanding array of molecular methods. Understanding the principles of blood culture collection, the biology and growth requirements of pathogens, and the strengths and limitations of identification technologies is essential for interpreting results in clinical context and selecting when to escalate to specialized molecular testing.

This chapter reviews practical aspects of blood culture methodology and incubation dynamics; factors that influence time‑to‑positivity (TTP) and cause false negatives, particularly with fastidious organisms; organism identification methods ranging from biochemical panels to MALDI‑TOF, 16S rRNA sequencing, whole‑genome sequencing, and nanopore approaches; pathogen‑specific molecular assays (including PCR for Shiga toxin); and the performance characteristics and turnaround times (TAT) of different tests. Finally, it discusses indications for extended diagnostics when an unusual pathogen is suspected.

# Blood culture collection, media types (aerobic/anaerobic) and incubation dynamics

Proper blood culture collection is the foundation of microbiologic diagnosis of BSI. The goal is to maximize recovery of true pathogens while minimizing contamination. Key collection principles include obtaining an adequate volume of blood, appropriate number and timing of sets, aseptic skin preparation, and immediate transport to the laboratory.

Volume and number of sets: For adults, the single most important determinant of blood culture sensitivity is the total blood volume cultured. Guidelines recommend collecting 20–30 mL per set, typically divided between an aerobic and an anaerobic bottle (commonly 8–10 mL per bottle). For pediatric patients, smaller volumes are used according to weight. Multiple sets (usually 2–3) increase sensitivity and help distinguish contaminants from true bacteremia; obtaining sets from separate venipuncture sites is preferred to differentiate skin contaminants versus true bloodstream pathogens.

Aseptic technique and timing: Skin antisepsis with chlorhexidine gluconate (0.5–2%) in alcohol is effective in reducing contamination. Bottle inoculation should occur at the bedside whenever possible to avoid delays. Timing relative to fever spikes is less critical than previously believed; steady detection of pathogens occurs whether cultures are drawn during febrile episodes or not, so the emphasis remains on adequate volume and multiple independent venipunctures.

Bottle types and media: Modern blood culture systems use specialized bottles with media that support growth of bacteria and yeasts and neutralize host antimicrobials. Typical collections include paired aerobic (for facultative and obligate aerobes) and anaerobic (for obligate anaerobes and facultative organisms) bottles. Media formulations often contain enriched broths, resin or charcoal to absorb antibiotics, and selective supplements to support fastidious organisms. There are also specialized bottles for fungi, mycobacteria, and pediatric needs. For suspected fungemia, dedicated fungal bottles or extended incubation may improve yield. For suspected endocarditis, prolonged incubation and multiple sets enhance detection.

Automated incubation and continuous monitoring: Contemporary laboratories use automated continuous-monitoring blood culture systems (e.g., BACTEC, BacT/ALERT), which detect growth by physiologic changes such as CO2 production, colorimetric changes, or pressure changes. Bottles are incubated continuously (usually 35–37°C) and monitored; signals trigger removal and subculture for identification and susceptibility testing. Standard incubation times are typically 5 days for routine bottles; however, some organisms require prolonged incubation (7–14 days) for recovery, and extended incubation may be indicated clinically.

Subculture and direct testing: Once a bottle flags positive, the laboratory performs a Gram stain and typically subcultures to solid media to isolate colonies for identification and susceptibility testing. In some settings, direct identification from positive blood culture fluid is possible via rapid methods (e.g., direct MALDI‑TOF or antigen tests), shortening TAT.

# Factors affecting time-to-positivity and false negatives with fastidious organisms

Time‑to‑positivity (TTP) is the interval from incubation start to the automated signal indicating growth. TTP depends on multiple factors: organism growth rate, inoculum (bacterial load), specimen volume, prior antimicrobial therapy, bottle medium and additives, and incubation conditions.

Organism growth characteristics: Rapidly growing organisms such as Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus often produce positive signals within 8–24 hours. Slower growers (e.g., HACEK organisms—Haemophilus, Aggregatibacter, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella, Kingella), some streptococci, and certain anaerobes may require 48–72 hours or longer. Obligate intracellular pathogens and many fastidious organisms may not grow on standard media or within routine incubation windows.

Inoculum effect: TTP is inversely related to the number of viable organisms in the inoculum; higher bacterial loads yield shorter TTP. Quantitatively, doubling times of bacteria and initial colony counts translate into predictable TTP changes on automated systems: e.g., if an organism doubles every 30 minutes, a tenfold increase in starting concentration shortens TTP by approximately 3.3 doubling times (this is a conceptual relationship often used in clinical reasoning).

Prior antimicrobial therapy and antibiotic neutralization: Antimicrobial agents in patient blood can suppress growth and produce false negatives. Many blood culture bottles contain resins or charcoal to adsorb antibiotics and mitigate this effect, improving recovery when samples are taken during therapy. Nonetheless, cultures obtained while on antimicrobials are less sensitive. If clinically possible, obtaining blood cultures before the first dose of effective antibiotics is recommended.

Specimen volume and collection errors: Insufficient blood volume is a common cause of false negatives. Contaminated or poorly mixed bottles, delayed transport, or prolonged exposure to suboptimal temperatures can reduce viability of organisms. For anaerobes, exposure to oxygen during collection or transport can kill organisms and lead to false negatives.

Fastidious organisms and intracellular pathogens: Certain bacteria have specialized nutritional requirements or require mammalian cell culture for growth (e.g., Coxiella burnetii, Bartonella spp., Tropheryma whipplei, Legionella spp., Brucella spp. depending on strain). Standard blood culture media may fail to support such organisms or require prolonged incubation and enriched conditions. Similarly, organisms that localize to intracellular compartments are less likely to be recovered by routine blood culture; specific serologic, molecular, or culture techniques (cell lines, shell vial culture) may be necessary.

Biofilm and intermittent bacteremia: In device‑associated infections, organisms may be embedded in biofilms and shed bacteria intermittently, reducing recovery probability in any single blood draw. For suspected catheter-related bloodstream infection, paired quantitative cultures from catheter and peripheral blood or differential time to positivity (DTTP) can assist diagnosis; a DTTP of ≥2 hours earlier in the catheter sample suggests catheter source, with sensitivity and specificity that vary by organism.

# Identification methods: biochemical testing, MALDI‑TOF, 16S rRNA sequencing and whole‑genome/nanopore approaches

Once an isolate is recovered, accurate identification to the species level is critical for appropriate therapy. Identification methods span phenotypic biochemical testing to mass spectrometry and nucleic acid sequencing, each with distinct principles and workflows.

Conventional biochemical and phenotypic testing: Traditional methods rely on morphology, Gram stain, growth characteristics on selective/differential media, and biochemical reactions (e.g., catalase, coagulase, oxidase; carbohydrate fermentation profiles; enzymatic assays). Commercial systems (e.g., VITEK, API strips) use panels of biochemical tests interpreted by algorithms to provide species identifications and sometimes susceptibility results. These methods remain widely used and are cost‑effective, but performance depends on growth of sufficient pure colonies and can be slow (24–72 hours after subculture). Unusual or phylogenetically close organisms can be misidentified.

MALDI‑TOF mass spectrometry: Matrix‑assisted laser desorption/ionization time‑of‑flight (MALDI‑TOF) identifies organisms by analyzing protein mass spectra (predominantly ribosomal proteins) from colonies and comparing them to spectral databases. MALDI‑TOF delivers rapid identifications (minutes) once colonies are available and is highly accurate for most bacteria and yeasts, with lower costs per identification after initial capital investment. Limitations include difficulty distinguishing very closely related species in the database (e.g., certain Enterobacterales, Streptococcus mitis/oralis group), dependence on a comprehensive database, and occasional false results for organisms not represented in the library. Direct identification from positive blood culture fluid by MALDI‑TOF can shorten TAT, but may have reduced accuracy compared with colony spectra and often requires sample preparation (extraction steps) to remove blood components.

16S rRNA gene sequencing: For isolates that are difficult to identify or are slow growers, sequencing the 16S ribosomal RNA gene provides robust taxonomic resolution across bacteria. The 16S gene contains conserved regions for primer design and variable regions for species discrimination. Clinical workflows typically PCR amplify ~1,400 bp or targeted variable regions, sequence the amplicon, and compare to curated databases (e.g., NCBI, SILVA, RDP). 16S sequencing is particularly useful for identifying fastidious organisms, anaerobes, or atypical isolates and for culture‑negative infections when applied to sterile site specimens. Limitations include inability to always discriminate among very closely related species (some species have nearly identical 16S sequences), longer TAT (24–72 hours or more depending on lab), and requirement for careful interpretation to avoid misassignment due to database inaccuracies or contamination.

Whole‑genome sequencing (WGS): WGS of bacterial isolates provides the highest resolution for identification, strain typing, and antimicrobial resistance gene detection. WGS can resolve species and subspecies, identify plasmids and resistance determinants, and support outbreak investigations. Short‑read platforms (e.g., Illumina) offer high accuracy but require culture, DNA extraction, library preparation, and batch sequencing—leading to longer TAT (days). WGS workflows are increasingly integrated into reference laboratories for complex cases or public health needs. Interpretation is computationally intensive and requires robust bioinformatics pipelines and validated databases for resistance gene calling.

Nanopore sequencing and real‑time sequencing: Oxford Nanopore Technologies provides long‑read sequencing with portable devices capable of generating data in real time. Nanopore sequencing can be applied to isolates or directly to clinical specimens (metagenomic sequencing). Advantages include rapid generation of sequence data (hours), long reads aiding assembly, and field portability. Challenges include higher raw error rates compared with short‑read platforms (though consensus accuracy has improved), the need for careful library prep to maximize yield, and complex bioinformatics to distinguish true pathogen signal from background host or environmental nucleic acid. For clinical BSI workups, nanopore sequencing can rapidly identify pathogens and detect resistance gene signatures, but rigorous validation and interpretation frameworks are necessary prior to diagnostic deployment.

Integration and practical workflow: In practice, laboratories often use a tiered approach: rapid Gram stain and, when feasible, direct identification (e.g., MALDI‑TOF) from positive blood culture fluid to guide empiric therapy; confirmation by colony identification and susceptibility testing; and reserve sequencing (16S, WGS) for difficult or unexpected isolates. Awareness of each method’s turnaround time and limitations is essential when applying results to clinical decisions.

# Molecular tests for specific pathogens (PCR for Shiga toxin, pathogen‑specific assays)

Molecular assays that target pathogen nucleic acids provide sensitive and specific detection and can be applied to blood, culture material, or other specimens. They are particularly valuable when organisms are fastidious, slow‑growing, or where a specific toxin or virulence determinant (rather than organism recovery) is clinically relevant.

Pathogen‑specific PCR assays: These assays target species‑specific genes (e.g., nuc for Staphylococcus aureus, lytA for Streptococcus pneumoniae) or genes associated with pathogenicity/resistance (e.g., mecA for methicillin resistance, vanA/vanB for vancomycin resistance). They can be performed directly on whole blood, positive blood culture fluid, or isolated colonies. Direct blood PCR increases the chance to detect organisms in culture‑negative cases (e.g., prior antibiotic exposure) but often has lower sensitivity due to low circulating microbial DNA and the presence of PCR inhibitors; enrichment methods or cell‑free DNA assays can help.

PCR for Shiga toxin: Detection of Shiga toxin genes (stx1, stx2) by PCR is commonly used for stool specimens to diagnose Shiga toxin–producing Escherichia coli (STEC). In systemic infections or suspected hemolytic uremic syndrome with bloodstream involvement, PCR of blood or sterile site specimens can identify toxin genes when cultures are negative or when E. coli is difficult to culture. Laboratories use validated real‑time PCR assays for stx genes with high sensitivity and specificity. Importantly, detection of stx indicates presence of genes but does not by itself localize the organism or quantify expression; clinical correlation with stool tests and patient presentation is necessary.

Multiplex panels and syndromic testing: Commercial multiplex PCR platforms detect multiple pathogens simultaneously from positive blood culture fluid or whole blood. Examples include panels that identify common bloodstream pathogens plus key resistance markers (e.g., panels that report S. aureus, Streptococcus spp., Enterococcus spp., Enterobacterales, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Candida spp., plus mecA, vanA/vanB, and certain carbapenemase genes). These platforms offer fast TAT (often 1–2 hours) and can directly inform antimicrobial de‑escalation or escalation. Limitations include finite panels (organisms outside the panel will not be reported), variable sensitivity for certain targets, and cost.

Broad‑range PCR and metagenomic sequencing: Broad‑range PCR (e.g., bacterial 16S PCR) can detect a wide range of bacteria from culture‑negative sterile site specimens. Metagenomic next‑generation sequencing (mNGS) of cell‑free DNA or whole nucleic acids from blood enables unbiased detection of bacteria, fungi, viruses, and parasites. mNGS can be powerful for diagnosing atypical or unexpected pathogens; however, it has challenges with host background nucleic acid, potential contamination, analytic complexity, and interpretation of low‑level detections. Turnaround times vary (often 24–72 hours) and tests are currently costly and available primarily in reference settings.

Detection of resistance determinants: Molecular detection of resistance genes (e.g., mecA, blaKPC, blaNDM, vanA/B, ESBL genes) provides early information about likely resistance mechanisms and can guide antibiotic therapy before phenotypic susceptibilities are available. However, presence of a gene does not always predict phenotypic resistance (e.g., gene expression, porin mutations, efflux mechanisms) and detecting resistance mechanisms outside the scope of the assay will be missed.

# Turnaround times, sensitivity/specificity and interpretation in clinical context

Each diagnostic modality has characteristic TAT, analytic performance, and interpretive caveats that affect clinical decision‑making.

Turnaround times:
- Blood cultures (flagging positive): Automated systems may detect growth as early as 6–8 hours for high inocula, typically within 24–48 hours for many pathogens. Routine incubation is usually 5 days; extended incubation (7–14 days) if indicated.
- Gram stain from positive bottle: minutes to a few hours after positivity.
- Subculture and colony identification (biochemical or MALDI‑TOF from colonies): once colonies are grown (usually 18–48 hours after subculture), MALDI‑TOF identification can be performed in minutes; biochemical panels may take 4–24 hours.
- Direct MALDI‑TOF from positive blood culture: same day, often within 1–4 hours, though accuracy may be slightly lower.
- Rapid molecular panels from positive blood culture: 1–3 hours for multiplex assays.
- 16S rRNA sequencing: typically 24–72 hours depending on lab workflow.
- Targeted PCR from blood or positive culture: same day (hours) if available onsite.
- Whole‑genome sequencing: days to a week depending on batching and platform.
- Metagenomic sequencing (clinical mNGS): 24–72 hours in specialized labs.

Sensitivity and specificity: Performance varies by method and specimen type. Blood cultures are highly specific when positive for pathogens, but sensitivities vary: pooled sensitivities for bacteremia detection are often cited in the 60–95% range depending on organism load, number of sets, and prior antibiotics. For molecular assays, sensitivity and specificity are assay‑dependent. Multiplex panels applied to positive blood cultures commonly achieve >95% sensitivity and specificity for included targets; direct blood PCRs for bacteremia are less sensitive (variable, often <70% for some targets) due to low organism burden and inhibitors. 16S sequencing sensitivity can be high for isolates or sterile site tissues but lower when applied to blood because host DNA predominates.

Interpretation in clinical context: Microbiologic results should always be interpreted alongside clinical presentation:

- Distinguish contamination from true bacteremia: Common skin flora (coagulase‑negative staphylococci, Bacillus spp., Corynebacterium spp., Cutibacterium acnes) frequently contaminate cultures. True infection is more likely when the organism is recovered from multiple separate blood culture sets, isolates match the clinical syndrome and source, or there is supportive evidence (e.g., prosthetic device, endocarditis, elevated inflammatory markers). Single positive bottle for a common skin contaminant in an otherwise low‑risk patient is often contamination; clinical judgment and repeat cultures guide management.

- Time‑to‑positivity as diagnostic tool: Short TTP and growth in multiple bottles support true bacteremia. Differential time to positivity between catheter and peripheral samples can point to catheter‑related infection when the catheter becomes positive ≥2 hours earlier.

- Molecular detections: Positive PCR or sequencing results can detect nonviable organisms (especially after antibiotics) and may persist longer than viable bacteria. Conversely, a negative molecular test does not exclude infection if the test lacks sensitivity for the pathogen or if the pathogen is not targeted by the assay.

- Resistance gene detection: Presence of resistance genes can guide early therapy, but phenotypic susceptibility testing remains the standard for definitive antimicrobial selection. Conversely, phenotypic resistance can occur without detection of known resistance genes if mechanisms are novel or not targeted.

# When to pursue extended diagnostics for unusual pathogens

Extended diagnostics—specialized culture conditions, serology, targeted PCR, 16S or fungal rRNA sequencing, and metagenomic approaches—are indicated when routine methods fail to explain a clinical syndrome suggestive of infection or when particular organisms are suspected based on epidemiology, exposures, or clinical features.

Clinical clues prompting extended diagnostics include:

- Culture‑negative endocarditis: When blood cultures are repeatedly negative in a patient with clinical or echocardiographic evidence of infective endocarditis, consider fastidious causes such as Coxiella burnetii, Bartonella spp., Tropheryma whipplei, Brucella spp., or fungi. Serology (e.g., phase I/II antibodies for C. burnetii), targeted PCR on blood or excised tissue (valve), and prolonged culture attempts may be required. 16S rRNA sequencing of valve tissue can identify bacterial etiologies when cultures are negative.

- Prior antimicrobial therapy with persistent suspicion of BSI: If cultures are negative but infection is suspected, consider molecular tests on blood (broad‑range PCR, targeted PCR), cell‑free DNA assays, or mNGS.

- Exposure history or travel: Certain pathogens (e.g., Brucella, Rickettsia, Leptospira) require specific tests (serology, targeted PCR) and biosafety precautions. Query exposures—animal contact, tick bites, consumption of unpasteurized dairy, travel to endemic regions—to direct testing.

- Zoonotic and intracellular pathogens: Because many intracellular organisms do not grow in routine culture, serology and PCR from blood or tissue are the mainstays. For suspected C. burnetii, serology with phase I titers (high phase I IgG indicates chronic Q fever such as endocarditis) is diagnostic. For suspected acute Brucella, both blood culture using specialized media and serology are informative; PCR on blood or bone marrow may be helpful.

- Unusual organisms or discrepant results: When MALDI‑TOF or biochemical testing gives ambiguous or unexpected identifications, 16S rRNA sequencing or WGS can resolve species identity. These methods are also useful in outbreak investigations to determine clonality.

- Fungal and mycobacterial BSI: Suspected candidemia benefits from fungal blood culture bottles, but species identification and susceptibility testing remain important; PCR or beta‑D‑glucan assays can add diagnostic support. For mycobacteremia, mycobacterial blood culture systems and prolonged incubation, plus molecular probes and sequencing, are required.

Resource and biosafety considerations: Extended diagnostics often require reference laboratory support, biosafety level considerations (e.g., for Brucella), and clinician‑laboratory communication to ensure appropriate sample handling and testing selection. Requesting extended tests should be guided by pretest probability; indiscriminate use of broad molecular tests increases costs and complicates interpretation.

Conclusion

Diagnosing bloodstream infections requires integration of specimen collection best practices, an understanding of microbial growth biology, and knowledge of the capabilities and limits of both phenotypic and molecular diagnostics. Timely gram stain and rapid identification techniques can shorten time to targeted therapy, while molecular assays provide powerful tools for detection of fastidious organisms and resistance determinants. Clinical interpretation must weigh test performance, specimen context, the patient’s antimicrobial exposure, and pretest probability. When routine methods fail or when epidemiology suggests atypical pathogens, extended molecular and sequencing diagnostics become necessary to establish etiologic diagnoses and guide patient care.

This chapter reviews pharmacologic and procedural therapies commonly used in severe infections arising from animal bites and in thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) syndromes where complement-mediated injury is suspected. The first section summarizes the pharmacology, spectrum and dosing considerations for beta-lactam antibiotics commonly used for bite-related sepsis: penicillin V, ampicillin (and combinations with beta-lactamase inhibitors), and piperacillin/tazobactam. The second section discusses principles of empiric and targeted antimicrobial therapy for zoonotic infections, including typical pathogens and clinical decision points. The third section covers plasma exchange (therapeutic plasmapheresis): mechanisms, indications in TMA syndromes, choice of replacement fluids, and important effects on measured drug levels and complement testing. The final sections review terminal complement blockade with monoclonal antibodies (eculizumab as the prototype): mechanism of action, pharmacokinetics, standard dosing regimens, monitoring, and the infection and other safety risks that require prophylaxis and vaccination planning. The material emphasizes integration of pharmacology with clinical reasoning: when to choose particular antibiotics, how to adjust dosing in renal failure, and how plasma exchange and complement inhibitors interact with each other and with diagnostic assays.

# Beta-lactam antibiotics: penicillin V, ampicillin, piperacillin/tazobactam — spectra, mechanisms, dose adjustments in renal failure

Mechanism of action and general spectrum

Beta-lactam antibiotics share a common mechanism of action: they bind penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) on bacterial cell membranes and inhibit the final transpeptidation step of peptidoglycan synthesis, leading to defective cell wall formation and bactericidal activity. Different subclasses (penicillins, aminopenicillins, antipseudomonal penicillins, cephalosporins) vary in PBP affinity and in penetration of Gram-negative outer membranes. Beta-lactamase inhibitors (e.g., tazobactam, clavulanate, sulbactam) extend activity by irreversibly inhibiting many classes of beta-lactamases, restoring activity against beta-lactamase–producing organisms.

Penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin)

Penicillin V is an oral narrow-spectrum penicillin primarily active against many streptococci and Treponema pallidum and retains activity vs. Pasteurella multocida. It has limited Gram-negative coverage and is inactivated by many beta-lactamases. Typical adult dosing for mild infections/prophylaxis is 250–500 mg orally every 6–8 hours. Because it is largely renally excreted as unchanged drug, dose reduction is required in severe renal impairment (see renal dosing principles below). Clinical uses for bite-related infections are limited; however, penicillin-sensitive zoonotic pathogens (Pasteurella species) remain susceptible.

Ampicillin and ampicillin-sulbactam (and oral amoxicillin-clavulanate equivalence)

Ampicillin is an aminopenicillin with expanded activity vs. some Gram-negative bacilli (e.g., enterococci, some Haemophilus species) relative to penicillin V, but it is susceptible to beta-lactamases. Combination with a beta-lactamase inhibitor (e.g., sulbactam, clavulanate) broadens activity substantially to include many beta-lactamase–producing strains and anaerobes. For animal-bite infections, amoxicillin-clavulanate (oral) and ampicillin-sulbactam (IV) are first-line choices because they cover Pasteurella multocida, common oral anaerobes, Staphylococcus and Streptococcus species, and many Gram-negatives.

Common dosing examples:
- Ampicillin IV: 1–2 g every 4–6 hours (typical severe infection dosing 2 g q4–6h).
- Ampicillin-sulbactam IV (Unasyn): typically 1.5–3 g (ampicillin component 1–2 g) every 6 hours depending on severity.
- Oral amoxicillin-clavulanate: usual adult dosing 875/125 mg twice daily for moderate infections; for some bite prophylaxis regimens 500/125 mg tid or 875/125 mg bid.

Because ampicillin/amoxicillin reach high tissue levels and cover Pasteurella, they are preferred for uncomplicated bite prophylaxis when beta-lactam allergy is absent.

Piperacillin/tazobactam

Piperacillin is an extended-spectrum antipseudomonal penicillin with broadened Gram-negative activity (including Pseudomonas aeruginosa) and good activity against many anaerobes. Combined with tazobactam, the agent is protected from many beta-lactamases and is a frequent choice for severe polymicrobial intra-abdominal infections, hospital-acquired infections, and severe sepsis where broad empiric Gram-negative and anaerobic coverage is required.

Common dosing:
- Piperacillin/tazobactam (Zosyn) usual severe-infection dosing: 3.375 g IV q6–8h or 4.5 g IV q6–8h; many centers use extended infusion strategies (e.g., 4.5 g q8h infused over 4 hours) to maximize time above MIC (T>MIC) for beta-lactams.
- For critically ill patients with high-volume distribution or organisms with elevated MICs, continuous infusion or higher intermittent doses may be used, guided by therapeutic targets.

Renal clearance and dosing adjustments

All these penicillins are primarily eliminated by renal excretion as unchanged drug. Dose modification in reduced renal function prevents accumulation and toxicity (seizures with high beta-lactam levels have been described, as well as increased risk of neutropenia with prolonged high exposures). The principles are:
- Estimate renal function (e.g., creatinine clearance, CrCl, using Cockcroft-Gault in adults) and adjust either dose or dosing interval accordingly.
- For mild-to-moderate renal impairment (CrCl >30 mL/min), many standard regimens can be used with little change. For CrCl 10–30 mL/min, extend dosing intervals or reduce dose. For CrCl <10 mL/min or dialysis, larger interval extensions or dose reductions are required; supplemental dosing after hemodialysis is often needed because many beta-lactams are dialyzable.

Representative renal-adjustment examples (institutional practice varies; consult local dosing references):
- Ampicillin: For severe renal impairment (CrCl <10 mL/min), dosing intervals are often extended to every 8–12 hours or smaller doses used; in dialysis, give supplemental dose post-dialysis.
- Piperacillin/tazobactam: For CrCl >40 mL/min: usual dosing. For CrCl 20–40 mL/min: commonly 4.5 g initial then 4.5 g q8–12h or 3.375 g q8h depending on formulation and local guidance. For CrCl 10–20 mL/min: often 2.25–3.375 g q12h. For patients on intermittent hemodialysis, doses are given after dialysis. (Exact dose modification should follow institutional renal dosing charts.)

Toxicity and interactions

Common beta-lactam adverse effects include allergic reactions (ranging from rash to anaphylaxis; IgE-mediated cross-reactivity with cephalosporins varies by agent), gastrointestinal upset, and Clostridioides difficile colitis. High-dose penicillins and accumulation in renal failure can cause neurotoxicity, manifesting as encephalopathy, myoclonus, or seizures. Piperacillin/tazobactam has been associated in observational studies with increased acute kidney injury when combined with vancomycin, though causality is debated. Hematologic adverse effects (neutropenia, thrombocytopenia) occur with prolonged courses.

# Principles of empiric and targeted antimicrobial therapy in zoonotic infections

Pathogen considerations in animal-bite exposures

Management of bite-related infections begins with recognition of the likely pathogens. Common organisms in dog and cat bites include Pasteurella multocida (especially common in cat bites), Capnocytophaga canimorsus (particularly virulent in asplenic, alcoholic, or immunocompromised hosts), Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus species, and mixed anaerobes from animal oral flora. Human bites bring oral streptococci, Eikenella corrodens, and anaerobes. Zoonotic pathogens of particular clinical concern include rabies (viral—preexposure or postexposure prophylaxis protocols), Pasteurella spp., Capnocytophaga spp., Bartonella in cat scratches (Bartonella henselae) and less commonly severe organisms such as Francisella tularensis or Brucella in particular exposures.

Empiric therapy: goals and choices

Empiric therapy should be guided by the severity of infection, anatomic site, time since injury, host risk factors (asplenia, diabetes, immunosuppression), and likely organisms. For mild bite wounds without infection, many bites are managed with local care and selective prophylactic antibiotics in high-risk patients (e.g., cat bites of the hand, puncture wounds, immunocompromised or asplenic patients). For established infection or sepsis, broad-spectrum IV therapy is indicated.

Key empiric principles:
- Cover Pasteurella and anaerobes: amoxicillin-clavulanate (oral) or ampicillin-sulbactam (IV) is first line for many outpatient and inpatient situations because it reliably covers Pasteurella, Staphylococcus, Streptococcus and anaerobes.
- If polymicrobial sepsis, deep tissue infection, or hospital-acquired risk factors are present, broader Gram-negative coverage including Pseudomonas (e.g., piperacillin-tazobactam) or carbapenem may be required.
- For penicillin-allergic patients, alternatives may include doxycycline plus metronidazole or moxifloxacin (depending on local susceptibilities), though these choices must preserve Pasteurella coverage and consider resistance patterns. In beta-lactam–allergic severe cases, consult infectious disease.

Duration and de-escalation

Duration depends on severity and tissue involvement: prophylactic courses are often 3–5 days for clean wounds; infected wounds typically require 7–14 days depending on response and presence of osteomyelitis or deep-seated infection. Always narrow therapy based on culture and susceptibility data: targeted therapy reduces toxicity and resistance selection.

Special high-risk patient considerations

Asplenic or functionally hyposplenic patients, those with chronic liver disease, alcoholism, or other immune compromise have increased risk of fulminant infection from organisms like Capnocytophaga canimorsus. In these patients a lower threshold for hospital admission, IV empiric therapy with broad-spectrum beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations, and early surgical consultation for source control are warranted.

Pharmacodynamic considerations

Beta-lactams are time-dependent killers: efficacy correlates with the time plasma concentrations remain above the minimum inhibitory concentration (T>MIC). Strategies to maximize T>MIC include increasing dosing frequency or using prolonged/continuous infusions for agents such as piperacillin/tazobactam, especially in critically ill patients with augmented renal clearance or infections from organisms with elevated MICs.

# Plasma exchange (plasmapheresis): mechanism, indications in TMA syndromes, replacement fluids (FFP, Octaplas), effects on drug levels and complement assays

Mechanism and physiologic effects

Therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE), commonly called plasmapheresis, removes plasma containing pathogenic circulating substances and replaces it with donor plasma or plasma substitutes. Mechanisms by which TPE benefits patients include removal of pathogenic autoantibodies (e.g., anti-ADAMTS13 autoantibodies in immune thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura [iTTP]), removal of circulating immune complexes and inflammatory mediators, reduction in circulating complement components and paraproteins, and replacement of deficient plasma factors when replacement fluid contains the normal complement of plasma proteins. The procedure exchanges a portion of the patient's plasma volume—commonly 1–1.5 plasma volumes per session—leading to a predictable reduction in plasma concentrations of target substances governed by single-compartment kinetics and redistribution from extravascular compartments.

Indications in TMA syndromes

TPE is a lifesaving, front-line therapy for several TMA syndromes:
- Immune-mediated TTP (iTTP): TPE removes anti-ADAMTS13 autoantibodies and replenishes ADAMTS13 when fresh frozen plasma (FFP) or plasma product is used as replacement. Typical practice is daily TPE until clinical remission (platelet recovery and hemolysis resolution).
- Other TMAs: In atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) due to complement dysregulation, the role of TPE has become more limited since complement inhibitors (eculizumab) are available; historically, TPE was used to remove complement factors or autoantibodies. TPE may be used empirically when diagnostic uncertainty exists (e.g., to treat suspected iTTP while awaiting ADAMTS13 activity results).
- Certain drug- or antibody-mediated TMAs may also respond to TPE.

Replacement fluids: FFP vs. Octaplas (solvent-detergent plasma) vs. albumin

Selection of replacement fluid depends on the indication:
- Fresh frozen plasma (FFP): contains the full complement of coagulation factors and ADAMTS13; used when replacement of plasma proteins is desired (e.g., TTP). FFP is standard when the therapeutic goal includes replenishment of deficient factors.
- Solvent-detergent treated plasma (e.g., Octaplas): pooled, pathogen-reduced plasma product with standardized clotting factor content and lower risk of some transfusion-related reactions or transmission; used interchangeably in many centers for replacement when plasma proteins are needed.
- Albumin solution: often used as replacement for non–factor-replacement indications where removing pathogenic plasma components is the primary goal but exogenous coagulation factor replacement is not required. Albumin lacks clotting factors and ADAMTS13, so it is unsuitable when replenishment of deficient plasma components is necessary (e.g., iTTP).

TPE effects on drug levels and laboratory assays

TPE removes circulating drugs and immunoglobulins in proportion to their plasma distribution and protein binding. The extent of removal depends on drug molecular weight, volume of distribution (Vd), and protein binding. Drugs that are largely confined to the vascular compartment and highly protein bound are most likely to be removed (e.g., monoclonal antibodies, many antithrombin or factor concentrates if circulating). Small molecules with large Vd are less affected.

Clinical consequences:
- Therapeutic monoclonal antibodies (e.g., eculizumab) are significantly removed by TPE because they are large proteins present in plasma. If TPE is performed in a patient receiving monoclonal therapy, dosing adjustments or re-dosing after TPE may be necessary to restore therapeutic levels.
- Antibiotics: many beta-lactams are small molecules with moderate protein binding and relatively small Vd; single TPE sessions may lower plasma concentrations to a modest degree. The clinical impact depends on the timing of dosing relative to exchange and the number of exchanges. For drugs with narrow therapeutic windows or critical dependence on plasma concentration, measuring levels (if available) or re-dosing after exchange is prudent.

Effects on diagnostic complement assays and ADAMTS13 measurements

Because TPE removes plasma proteins and circulating complement components, recent TPE sessions can dramatically alter serologic and functional assays:
- Complement assays (C3, C4, CH50, sC5b-9) may be reduced after TPE or normalized if replacement plasma is used. This can mask underlying complement dysregulation and complicate interpretation of tests intended to diagnose complement-mediated disease. Timing of testing should ideally be before TPE initiation, or samples should be obtained after an adequate washout period when clinically feasible.
- ADAMTS13 activity and anti-ADAMTS13 antibody assays are likewise affected: TPE will transiently increase ADAMTS13 activity when plasma replacement is used and decrease circulating autoantibodies. Therefore, baseline samples should ideally be obtained before initiating TPE to guide diagnosis (i.e., measure ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor/autoantibody).
- When urgent treatment precludes pre-exchange sampling, record the timing of TPE relative to sample collection and interpret results with caution; serial measurements after TPE may be required for diagnosis.

Operational and monitoring considerations

TPE requires vascular access sufficient for extracorporeal flows and monitoring for hypotension, allergic/transfusion reactions, and electrolyte disturbances (citrate anticoagulation can cause hypocalcemia). If FFP or solvent-detergent plasma is used, monitor for allergic reactions and transfusion-related circulatory overload. Replacement fluid choice determines the need for monitoring coagulation factors and for additional infusion of plasma products.

# Eculizumab and terminal complement blockade: mechanism (C5 inhibition), pharmacokinetics, dosing regimens, and monitoring

Mechanism of action: terminal complement blockade via C5 inhibition

Eculizumab is a humanized monoclonal IgG that binds complement component C5 and prevents its proteolytic cleavage into C5a and C5b. By blocking generation of C5a (a potent anaphylatoxin and chemoattractant) and C5b (the initiator of membrane attack complex [MAC] formation via C5b-9), eculizumab prevents terminal complement–mediated cell lysis and inflammatory activation. This is pathogenic in conditions where unchecked complement activation causes hemolysis (paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, PNH) or endothelial injury leading to TMA (atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, aHUS).

Pharmacokinetics and disposition

Eculizumab is administered intravenously. Key pharmacokinetic points:
- Distribution: largely confined to plasma and extracellular fluid (monoclonal antibodies generally have low volumes of distribution).
- Clearance: primarily by reticuloendothelial catabolism (proteolytic degradation), not renal excretion; therefore renal impairment does not require routine dose adjustment.
- Half-life: the terminal elimination half-life of eculizumab is approximately 11 days (range varies by patient and indication). This supports dosing intervals of 1–2 weeks after induction to maintain sufficient trough concentrations to suppress terminal complement activity.
- Therapeutic monitoring: functional assays (e.g., CH50) and measurement of free eculizumab or serum C5 activity can be used to assess whether complement blockade is adequate. However, assay availability varies and results can be impacted by recent TPE (see earlier section).

Standard dosing regimens

Dosing varies by indication and patient weight; here are commonly used adult regimens (note: pediatric dosing and weight-based regimens also exist and vary):

For PNH (typical adult regimen):
- Induction: eculizumab 600 mg IV weekly for 4 weeks.
- Maintenance: 900 mg IV one week after the fourth dose (i.e., Week 5) then 900 mg IV every 2 weeks thereafter.

For aHUS (commonly used regimen):
- Induction: 900 mg IV weekly for 4 weeks.
- Maintenance: 1200 mg IV at Week 5 (or 1500 mg in some dosing schemes depending on label/region and weight) and thereafter 1200 mg IV every 2 weeks. Exact dosing and label specifics vary by region and weight-based considerations; consult current prescribing information.

Dosing modifications and special circumstances

Because eculizumab is cleared by proteolytic pathways, renal failure does not mandate dosing changes. However, TPE rapidly removes eculizumab from circulation; if TPE is used, clinicians typically administer eculizumab after TPE or provide supplemental dosing to restore blockade. If TPE is necessary, timing and re-dosing should be coordinated with hematology/transfusion services.

Monitoring response and therapeutic effect

Clinical monitoring includes resolution of hemolysis (for PNH) or improvement in creatinine/platelets/hemolysis markers (for aHUS). Laboratory monitoring can include:
- CH50 (total hemolytic complement activity): suppression of CH50 to near zero indicates effective terminal complement blockade. Target is typically undetectable or very low CH50 depending on institutional targets.
- Free eculizumab levels and C5 functional assays (when available) help determine whether dosing intervals are adequate, particularly in patients with breakthrough hemolysis or atypical pharmacokinetics.
- Hematologic and renal function tests relevant to the underlying disease (e.g., hemoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, platelet count, creatinine).

# Risks and adverse effects: infection risk with complement inhibitors, infusion reactions

Infection risk with complement inhibition

Blocking terminal complement function impairs the host defense against Neisseria species (Neisseria meningitidis, less so Neisseria gonorrhoeae) and possibly other encapsulated organisms that are particularly dependent on MAC-mediated killing. As a result:
- Eculizumab markedly increases the risk of invasive meningococcal disease. Cases can be fulminant and life-threatening.
- The risk persists while complement blockade is present and may persist transiently after discontinuation because of prolonged biologic effect.

Risk mitigation strategies (see next section on vaccinations and prophylaxis) include:
- Vaccination against Neisseria meningitidis covering conjugate quadrivalent serogroups (A, C, W, Y) and consideration of serogroup B vaccine. Vaccination should be administered at least 2 weeks before initiation of eculizumab when feasible to allow protective immunity to develop.
- If urgent therapy is required and vaccination cannot be given 2 weeks before initiation, start eculizumab with concurrent antibiotic prophylaxis until 2 weeks after vaccination.
- Educate patients thoroughly about signs and symptoms of meningococcal infection and to seek immediate care for febrile illness.

Other infectious risks and general immunosuppression

Although the principal documented increased risk is meningococcal disease, terminal complement blockade may modestly increase susceptibility to other encapsulated bacteria. Additionally, monoclonal antibody therapy can produce hypogammaglobulinemia in some contexts if repeated exposures or co-therapies exist; routine monitoring of immunoglobulin levels is reasonable if recurrent infections occur.

Infusion and hypersensitivity reactions

As with any monoclonal antibody, infusion-related reactions can occur. These vary from mild flushing, fever, or chills to severe hypersensitivity or anaphylaxis, though eculizumab is a humanized antibody and severe reactions are relatively uncommon. Monitor during infusion and be prepared to treat hypersensitivity. Development of anti-drug antibodies is uncommon but can reduce efficacy.

Hematologic and other organ effects

Eculizumab may change laboratory parameters related to its therapeutic target: in PNH it reduces intravascular hemolysis, decreases LDH and transfusion dependence, and may increase hemoglobin. Conversely, terminal complement blockade does not reverse extravascular hemolysis due to C3 opsonization in all cases, and monitoring for hemoglobin trends and transfusion needs is required.

# Drug interactions and special considerations (vaccination timing, prophylactic antibiotics)

Vaccination timing and strategies

Because eculizumab increases meningococcal disease risk, the following vaccination considerations are standard:
- Administer quadrivalent meningococcal conjugate vaccine (MenACWY) and, where available/indicated, meningococcal serogroup B vaccine (MenB) at least 2 weeks before starting eculizumab to allow adequate immune response.
- For patients who require urgent complement blockade and cannot wait 2 weeks, begin eculizumab but provide prophylactic antibiotics (see below) until 2 weeks after completing vaccination.
- If a patient was vaccinated in the past, ensure up-to-date booster vaccinations as recommended by local public health guidance; conjugate vaccine immunity may wane and boosters are often required depending on time since last dose.
- Patients should also receive routine immunizations per guideline (e.g., pneumococcal, influenza) as appropriate for baseline risk; however, live vaccines are generally contraindicated in immunosuppressed patients and timing around eculizumab or other immunomodulatory therapies should be coordinated.

Antibiotic prophylaxis around complement inhibitor initiation

When urgent initiation of eculizumab is necessary before the 2-week post-vaccination immune response, antibiotic prophylaxis is recommended to cover Neisseria until adequate vaccine-induced immunity is established. The choice and duration of prophylaxis vary by local practice; common approaches include:
- Oral ciprofloxacin 500 mg once (single dose) or 500 mg daily for 1–2 weeks, or
- Oral penicillin V (e.g., 250 mg twice daily) if not contraindicated, or
- Other options per local resistance patterns and allergy status.

Note that prophylaxis aims to bridge the window until vaccine immunity develops rather than supplant vaccination. Also recognize that antibiotic prophylaxis does not prevent all meningococcal disease and patient vigilance is required.

Pregnancy, renal failure, and special populations

Eculizumab crosses the placenta to some extent; risks and benefits in pregnancy should be carefully weighed, and peripartum management requires coordination with obstetrics. As mentioned, renal failure does not necessitate eculizumab dose adjustments, but in patients requiring TPE or hemofiltration, pharmacokinetics and timing must be considered because TPE removes the drug.

Impact of plasma exchange and other procedures on complement inhibitors and assays

Because TPE removes plasma proteins, therapeutic plasma exchange will remove eculizumab and restore complement activity; therefore:
- If a patient on eculizumab requires TPE, clinicians should anticipate that the therapeutic monoclonal will be partially or largely removed and plan re-dosing after exchange to restore effective blockade.
- Complement functional assays and serologic testing performed after TPE are unreliable for diagnosis of complement-mediated disease; attempt to obtain baseline samples before plasma exchange.

Other drug interactions

Eculizumab has no traditional cytochrome P450–mediated drug interactions, as monoclonal antibodies are catabolized via proteolytic pathways. However, concomitant immunosuppressive drugs may alter infection risk and vaccine responses. Beta-lactam antibiotics and other antimicrobials have their own interactions (e.g., with probenecid, with renal function when combined with nephrotoxic agents) that must be considered in polypharmacy.

Prophylactic and long-term management considerations

Patients on eculizumab require long-term education and care coordination:
- Clear documentation of vaccine status and provision of vaccination cards and emergency plans.
- Consider long-term antimicrobial counseling and easy access to urgent care if febrile illness develops.
- Periodic reassessment of the ongoing need for therapy, weighing benefits against the infection risk and cost; decisions about discontinuation should be individualized and done with multidisciplinary input.

Summary integration: clinical decision points

- For bite wounds with infection or sepsis, ampicillin-sulbactam (IV) or amoxicillin-clavulanate (oral) are first-line when beta-lactam allergy is absent; escalate to piperacillin/tazobactam when broader Gram-negative (including Pseudomonas) coverage is required or for severely ill patients.
- Adjust beta-lactam dosing based on renal function: estimate CrCl and extend intervals or reduce doses for CrCl <30 mL/min; for severe renal failure or dialysis, follow institutional dosing charts and provide supplemental dosing post-dialysis where appropriate.
- In suspected TMA, TPE is urgent therapy for suspected immune TTP: obtain baseline ADAMTS13 and complement testing before TPE if possible because TPE alters these assays. Use plasma-containing replacement (FFP or Octaplas) when factor replacement is desired.
- Eculizumab is indicated for complement-mediated disorders (PNH, aHUS) and blocks C5 to prevent MAC formation. It is cleared by proteolysis, has an approximate half-life of ~11 days, and is removed by TPE; coordinate dosing with plasmapheresis and monitor functional complement assays (CH50) to assess blockade.
- Prior to initiating eculizumab, ensure meningococcal vaccination (MenACWY and consider MenB) ideally at least 2 weeks before therapy; if urgent therapy is necessary, begin prophylactic antibiotics until 2 weeks after vaccination. Counsel patients on infection risk and early presentation for fever.

This chapter provides the pharmacologic and procedural foundations necessary to make informed, safe choices about antimicrobial therapy for bite-related sepsis, to understand the role and logistics of plasma exchange in TMA syndromes, and to manage patients receiving terminal complement inhibitors like eculizumab. Always combine these principles with institution-specific protocols, local susceptibility patterns, and multidisciplinary consultation for complex cases.

Introduction

Severe sepsis and septic shock remain leading causes of morbidity and mortality in the intensive care unit (ICU). Acute kidney injury (AKI) complicates sepsis in a large proportion of critically ill patients and frequently necessitates renal replacement therapy (RRT). The intersection of sepsis physiology and renal dysfunction imposes complex challenges: optimizing tissue perfusion while avoiding fluid overload, supporting failing organs including the heart and kidneys, choosing the appropriate form and timing of RRT, and preventing ICU-related complications that influence recovery. This chapter synthesizes pathophysiology, monitoring, and evidence-based management strategies for initial resuscitation, hemodynamic support including septic cardiomyopathy, indications and modalities for RRT (intermittent versus continuous), practical CRRT delivery (anticoagulation, dosing, fluid management), and supportive care topics such as glycemic control, nutrition, and strategies for weaning from RRT and recognizing renal recovery.

# Initial resuscitation: fluid strategy, vasopressor indications and choice

Early recognition and timely resuscitation of the septic patient are foundational. Resuscitation goals are to restore effective tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery while minimizing harm from excessive fluids.

Fluid strategy
Initial fluid resuscitation typically begins with crystalloid administration. Current consensus supports isotonic crystalloids (e.g., 0.9% sodium chloride or balanced solutions such as lactated Ringer’s or Plasma-Lyte) over colloids for routine sepsis resuscitation. Several trials and meta-analyses suggest balanced crystalloids may reduce the risk of hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis and possibly kidney injury compared with 0.9% saline in large-volume resuscitation. Typical initial bolus orders are 30 mL/kg within the first 3 hours for patients with sepsis-induced hypoperfusion or septic shock; this recommendation is a starting point and requires frequent reassessment.

Fluid responsiveness must be continually reassessed because many patients are fluid nonresponsive after initial boluses. Static measures such as central venous pressure (CVP) are unreliable predictors of fluid responsiveness. Dynamic assessments—pulse pressure variation, stroke volume variation in patients on controlled mechanical ventilation without significant arrhythmias, or passive leg raise (PLR) testing combined with stroke volume or cardiac output measurement—are preferred. A positive PLR test (a transient increase in stroke volume or cardiac output of ≥10–15%) predicts fluid responsiveness. When bedside cardiac output monitoring is unavailable, surrogates such as changes in systolic blood pressure or pulse pressure with PLR are less precise.

Avoidance of fluid overload is crucial: cumulative positive fluid balance is associated with worsening oxygenation, increased duration of mechanical ventilation, and higher mortality. After initial resuscitation and hemodynamic stabilization, adopt a conservative or de-resuscitation strategy—remove excess fluid with diuretics if targets are met and renal function permits or consider RRT for fluid removal in dialysis-dependent AKI.

Vasopressor indications and choice
When hypotension persists despite adequate fluid resuscitation and ongoing signs of tissue hypoperfusion, vasopressors are indicated to restore and maintain mean arterial pressure (MAP) to a level that ensures organ perfusion. Current guidelines recommend a MAP target of 65 mm Hg for most patients; individualized targets may be higher in patients with chronic hypertension to avoid myocardial or cerebral hypoperfusion.

Norepinephrine is the first-line vasopressor in septic shock because of its predominant alpha-adrenergic vasoconstrictive effects coupled with modest beta-adrenergic support of cardiac output and favorable safety profile. Vasopressin (usually started at a fixed dose of 0.03 units/min) is often employed as an adjunct to norepinephrine to reduce norepinephrine requirements and may be particularly useful in vasopressin-deficient states of prolonged septic shock. Epinephrine is an alternative but may increase lactate levels and arrhythmias. Dopamine is generally avoided due to greater risk of tachyarrhythmias and worse outcomes in some trials.

Titration should aim to achieve organ perfusion (urine output, mentation, skin perfusion, lactate clearance) rather than purely numeric MAP. If myocardial dysfunction is present, consider adding an inotrope such as dobutamine to support cardiac output if signs of hypoperfusion persist despite adequate MAP and fluid status. Phenylephrine may be used in patients with tachyarrhythmias where a purely alpha agonist is desired, but it can reduce cardiac output in patients who are preload- or contractility-limited.

Adjunctive therapies
Early antibiotics and source control are essential and directly influence hemodynamics by addressing the underlying cause. Consider corticosteroids (e.g., hydrocortisone 200 mg/day in divided doses) for patients with refractory shock requiring high-dose vasopressors despite fluid optimization, recognizing the modest mortality benefit and potential side effects.

# Management of septic cardiomyopathy and hemodynamic monitoring

Septic cardiomyopathy refers to reversible myocardial depression occurring in the setting of sepsis, characterized by reduced ejection fraction, ventricular dilatation, and blunted response to catecholamines. Pathophysiologically, it involves inflammatory mediator-induced myocardial depression, mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired calcium handling, and nitric oxide–mediated alterations in myocardial contractility. Clinically, it manifests as hypotension, elevated filling pressures or low systemic vascular resistance with low cardiac output in some cases, and difficulty weaning from vasopressors.

Recognition and assessment
Suspect septic cardiomyopathy when hypotension or signs of poor tissue perfusion persist despite adequate intravascular volume and high-dose vasopressors, or when new cardiac dysfunction is seen on echocardiography. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is the primary bedside tool: assess left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), stroke volume, ventricular dimensions, right ventricular function, valvular abnormalities, and estimates of filling pressures (E/A ratio, tissue Doppler, IVC variability—recognize limitations). Transesophageal echo may be needed in mechanically ventilated or obese patients.

Hemodynamic monitoring
Hemodynamic monitoring strategies range from noninvasive (serial physical exam, echocardiography, noninvasive cardiac output monitors) to invasive (arterial line for continuous blood pressure, central venous catheter for central venous pressure and oxygen saturation monitoring, pulmonary artery catheter for direct cardiac output and filling pressures). Choice depends on patient complexity and clinician expertise. Key physiologic variables to monitor include MAP, heart rate, urine output, lactate (trend and clearance), mixed or central venous oxygen saturation (SvO2 or ScvO2), and serial echocardiography for function and fluid responsiveness assessment.

Management principles
Treatment aims to optimize oxygen delivery while avoiding undue myocardial stress. Ensure adequate preload without volume overload using dynamic measures of fluid responsiveness. If cardiac output is low in the presence of myocardial depression, inotropes such as dobutamine can improve contractility and increase cardiac output; begin at low doses and titrate to hemodynamic response while monitoring for tachyarrhythmias and increased myocardial oxygen demand. If arrhythmias or ischemia limit inotrope use, consider alternative strategies such as milrinone (a phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor) which has inodilator effects and may be preferred in some patients with pulmonary hypertension or right ventricular dysfunction, but be cautious with systemic hypotension.

Vasopressor choices should be individualized in the context of myocardial function—norepinephrine coupled with low-dose vasopressin often provides systemic vascular resistance support with less tachycardia than epinephrine. Continuous reassessment with echocardiography and lactate trends guides titration and the need to escalate mechanical circulatory support (intra-aortic balloon pump is rarely used in sepsis; extracorporeal membrane oxygenation—venoarterial ECMO—might be considered in refractory cardiogenic shock with otherwise reversible pathology and when resources allow).

Prognosis and reversibility
Septic cardiomyopathy is often reversible over days to weeks with supportive care and control of infection. Recognize that myocardial depression increases the complexity of fluid and vasopressor management and may prolong vasopressor dependence. Serial assessments help determine recovery, inform duration of inotropic support, and guide decisions about advanced support.

# Indications for renal replacement therapy (RRT) in AKI and modalities: intermittent hemodialysis vs continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT)

Indications for RRT
RRT is indicated in AKI when supportive measures fail, or when life-threatening complications arise. Traditional absolute indications are summarized by the mnemonic "AEIOU": Acidosis (severe metabolic acidosis refractory to medical therapy, e.g., pH < 7.1–7.2 despite optimization), Electrolyte abnormalities (e.g., severe hyperkalemia with ECG changes or potassium refractory to medical therapy, often K+ > 6.5 mmol/L or rapidly rising), Intoxications (dialyzable toxins such as ethylene glycol, lithium, methanol, certain overdoses), Overload (refractory volume overload causing pulmonary edema and hypoxemia unresponsive to diuretics), and Uremia (pericarditis, encephalopathy, severe bleeding diathesis). In clinical practice, timing decisions also consider trends in BUN/creatinine, urine output, hemodynamic stability, and the trajectory of the patient.

Timing of initiation: early vs delayed
Randomized trials of early versus delayed initiation of RRT have produced mixed results; some suggest no mortality benefit to very early initiation in the absence of life-threatening indications, and others suggest reduction in days on RRT or organ support. The pragmatic approach is to initiate RRT for classical indications or when progressive complications, persistent oliguria/anuria with metabolic derangements, or significant fluid overload that impairs oxygenation are present. Consider early initiation in patients with high risk of fluid overload where ultrafiltration is anticipated (e.g., severe ARDS) or when cumulative positive fluid balance is projected to be detrimental.

Modalities: intermittent hemodialysis (IHD) vs continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT)
IHD and CRRT represent two ends of a spectrum. Intermittent hemodialysis (typically 3–4 hours sessions, higher blood and dialysate flow rates) provides rapid solute and fluid removal and is widely available. CRRT (continuous veno-venous hemofiltration, hemodialysis, or hemodiafiltration) provides slower, more continuous removal over 24 hours.

Clinical factors guiding modality choice:
- Hemodynamic stability: CRRT is preferred in hemodynamically unstable patients because slow continuous fluid and solute removal imposes less intravascular volume shifts and lower hemodynamic stress. IHD can precipitate hypotension and myocardial ischemia in unstable patients.
- Cerebral edema or elevated intracranial pressure: Rapid osmolar shifts with IHD may worsen cerebral edema; CRRT or slow low-efficiency dialysis (SLED) is preferred.
- Need for rapid solute correction: IHD is superior for urgent removal of toxins or severe hyperkalemia when very rapid clearance is required and hemodynamics permit.
- ICU logistics and resources: CRRT requires dedicated machines, trained staff, and continuous anticoagulation or anticoagulation alternatives; IHD may be pragmatic when renal staff and scheduling allow.
- Dose and fluid removal goals: CRRT allows precise hourly fluid balance and continuous uremic toxin control, helpful for fluid-overloaded patients who cannot tolerate intermittent ultrafiltration.

Sustained low-efficiency dialysis (SLED) is an intermediate option: prolonged intermittent therapies over 6–12 hours, allowing slower fluid removal and feasible with dialysis machines but without continuous technician presence. Evidence comparing modalities suggests similar mortality when adjusted for severity of illness; individualization based on hemodynamics, neurologic status, and resource availability remains key.

# Practical aspects of CRRT: anticoagulation, dosing and fluid management

CRRT is complex to deliver safely and effectively. Important practical aspects include vascular access, anticoagulation to maintain circuit patency, prescription of dose and modality, and meticulous fluid management.

Vascular access
A large-bore, double-lumen central venous catheter is required, typically 12–14 French in adults. Preferred sites include the right internal jugular vein for the shortest, straightest path; the femoral vein is acceptable but has higher infection and flow-limitation risks. Avoid subclavian access due to stenosis risk. Catheter position should be confirmed radiographically.

Anticoagulation
Anticoagulation prevents circuit clotting and prolongs filter life. Options include systemic unfractionated heparin, regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA), low molecular weight heparin, and no anticoagulation (saline flushes) when bleeding risk is prohibitive.

Unfractionated heparin: easy to use, monitor with activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) or anti-Xa levels, but systemic anticoagulation raises bleeding risk and complicates other invasive procedures.

Regional citrate anticoagulation: citrate chelates ionized calcium in the circuit, preventing clotting locally; calcium is returned to the patient systemically to avoid hypocalcemia. Benefits include lower bleeding risk and longer filter life; trials show improved circuit patency and possibly reduced mortality compared with systemic heparin in some settings. Monitoring requires serial measurements of ionized calcium (both pre- and post-filter), total calcium, acid-base status, and cumulative citrate load. Watch for citrate accumulation in patients with severe hepatic dysfunction or shock with poor citrate metabolism; markers include rising total calcium to ionized calcium ratio (>2.5), metabolic acidosis, and increasing anion gap.

No anticoagulation: used in high bleeding risk patients; expect shorter filter life and more frequent circuit changes.

Dosing of CRRT
CRRT dose is commonly prescribed as effluent flow rate (the sum of dialysate, ultrafiltration, and replacement fluid) normalized to patient body weight. Landmark randomized trials indicate no survival advantage for very high dosing compared with standard dosing. Current recommendations aim for a delivered effluent rate of approximately 20–25 mL/kg/h to achieve adequate solute control, adjusting the prescribed dose upward (e.g., 25–30 mL/kg/h) to account for interruptions and downtime so that delivered dose remains in target. For example, in a 70 kg patient, a delivered dose of 20 mL/kg/h equals 1400 mL/h. Greater effluent rates may be used transiently for severe metabolic derangements but evidence does not support routine high-intensity CRRT.

Ultrafiltration and fluid management
One of CRRT’s main advantages is precise fluid balance control. Prescribe a net ultrafiltration goal in mL/hour based on fluid status, insensible losses, and ongoing fluid inputs. Be cautious: aggressive ultrafiltration can reduce venous return and precipitate hypotension. Use hemodynamic monitoring to guide safe ultrafiltration rates; in unstable patients, slow and steady fluid removal (e.g., ≤1 mL/kg/h) may be better tolerated.

Replacement fluid and dialysate composition should be chosen based on electrolyte needs and acid-base balance. Bicarbonate-buffered solutions are standard. Monitor electrolytes frequently—potassium, magnesium, phosphate—and replace as necessary because CRRT continuously removes these solutes.

Practical complications and management
Common issues include filter clotting, hypothermia, electrolyte disturbances (hypophosphatemia, hypokalemia), hemodynamic instability related to ultrafiltration, and catheter-related bloodstream infections. Strategies to reduce complications include use of citrate anticoagulation where appropriate, precoagulation of circuits with heparin or citrate, warming of dialysate to prevent hypothermia, regular monitoring of electrolytes with protocolized replacement, and diligent catheter care.

# Glycemic control, nutrition, and prevention of ICU-related complications

Glycemic control
Hyperglycemia is common in critically ill patients, associated with worse outcomes. Conversely, strict glycemic targets increase hypoglycemia risk, which is also harmful. Current practice recommends a target blood glucose range generally between 140 and 180 mg/dL (7.8–10.0 mmol/L) for most critically ill patients; tighter control (110–140 mg/dL) may be considered in selected patients without high hypoglycemia risk and with experienced staff and reliable glucose monitoring. In AKI and CRRT patients, glucose homeostasis is influenced by continuous nutrient delivery and glucose removal through dialysis; frequent monitoring (hourly to every 4 hours depending on the regimen) is required. Insulin infusion protocols with clear titration rules reduce variability and hypoglycemia. Be aware that CRRT may remove insulin and glucose, altering insulin requirements.

Nutrition
Critically ill patients with severe sepsis and AKI are in a catabolic state requiring nutritional support. Early enteral nutrition (within 24–48 hours) is preferred when the gut is functional; it supports gut integrity and reduces infectious complications. If enteral feeding is contraindicated or inadequate, supplemental parenteral nutrition can be considered after the early acute phase. Caloric goals typically start at 20–25 kcal/kg/day in the early phase, progressing toward 25–30 kcal/kg/day as tolerated, and should be individualized based on indirect calorimetry when available.

Protein requirements are increased in critical illness and particularly in AKI and RRT because of amino acid losses. Protein targets commonly range from 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/day, with higher ranges for patients on extracorporeal therapies. In CRRT, protein losses can be significant; higher protein provision (up to 2.5 g/kg/day in some protocols) may be necessary. Monitor nitrogen balance indirectly with trends in urea and clinical response. Fluid-restricted enteral formulations or concentrated parenteral solutions may be required for patients with volume limits.

Prevention of ICU-related complications
Sepsis and AKI patients are at high risk for nosocomial infections, pressure injuries, venous thromboembolism (VTE), delirium, and deconditioning. Implement standard ICU bundles: hand hygiene, ventilator-associated pneumonia prevention (head-of-bed elevation, daily sedation interruptions), central line–associated bloodstream infection prevention (maximal sterile barrier precautions, daily review of line necessity), skin care and pressure area repositioning, pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis unless contraindicated, and early mobilization when feasible.

Electrolyte and mineral disturbances are common: hypophosphatemia (especially with refeeding and CRRT) can impair diaphragmatic function and immune competence; monitor and replete phosphate. Hypomagnesemia predisposes to arrhythmias and should be corrected. Acid-base disturbances should be monitored and managed—CRRT with bicarbonate-buffered solutions helps in correcting metabolic acidosis.

Delirium prevention includes minimizing benzodiazepines, using nonpharmacologic sleep hygiene measures, early mobilization, and orientation strategies. The cumulative burden of sedatives and immobilization affects rehabilitation and long-term functional outcomes.

# Weaning from RRT and markers of renal recovery

Weaning from RRT requires assessing for the return of sufficient renal function to maintain volume, electrolyte, and acid-base homeostasis without extracorporeal support. The timing and approach vary among centers, but several physiologic and laboratory markers guide the decision.

Clinical assessment
Key clinical indicators suggesting possible renal recovery include improving urine output (volume and trend), decreasing serum creatinine and BUN trends (when not confounded by ongoing catabolism or dialysis removal), improving hemodynamics without escalating vasopressor support, and correction of the precipitating illness. A sustained urine output > 0.5 mL/kg/h without diuretics is often used as a pragmatic threshold, though many clinicians look for higher urine outputs (e.g., > 1 mL/kg/h) and stable or improving creatinine. Presence of diuretic-induced urine output should be interpreted cautiously; diuretics can convert oliguric to nonoliguric states without meaningful improvement in glomerular filtration.

Biomarkers and functional tests
Traditional markers (serum creatinine) lag behind actual changes in GFR, especially in the setting of muscle wasting and catabolism. Novel biomarkers such as cystatin C, neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), and others may offer earlier signals of renal recovery but are not yet standard in all centers for RRT discontinuation decisions.

Trial off RRT
A common practical approach is to perform a trial off RRT: hold RRT for 24–48 hours while closely monitoring urine output, creatinine trend, potassium, bicarbonate, and volume status. Ensure the patient is hemodynamically stable enough to tolerate potential fluid accumulation and that there are contingency plans to resume RRT promptly if deterioration occurs. In the outpatient dialysis literature, cessation trials often use criteria such as urine output > 400–500 mL/day and decreasing BUN/creatinine; in critically ill patients, the same concepts apply but with more frequent monitoring.

Diuretic-assisted weaning
Loop diuretics may be used to manage volume and to “test” renal tubular response; a brisk diuretic response suggests tubular function sufficient to promote diuresis. However, diuretics can mask true intrinsic improvement or create electrolyte disturbances; do not rely solely on diuretic-induced urine output to justify stopping RRT.

Markers of durable recovery
After stopping RRT, observe for sustained biochemical stability and urine output. Expect continued improvement in renal function over days to weeks in many cases of acute tubular necrosis and sepsis-associated AKI. Provide outpatient planning and nephrology follow-up for those with incomplete recovery, as progression to chronic kidney disease is common.

Predictors of non-recovery
Factors associated with failure to recover renal function include advanced age, preexisting chronic kidney disease, prolonged duration of AKI, severe comorbidities, higher illness severity scores, and multiorgan failure. Persistent oliguria/anuria, sustained need for high-dose vasopressors, and repeated septic insults reduce likelihood of recovery.

Long-term considerations
Survivors of dialysis-requiring AKI have increased risk of chronic kidney disease, end-stage kidney disease, recurrent AKI episodes, cardiovascular disease, and long-term mortality. Early nephrology involvement and planning for follow-up (serum creatinine, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, blood pressure control, medication reconciliation—avoid nephrotoxins) are necessary components of post-ICU care.

Conclusion

Managing severe sepsis complicated by dialysis-dependent AKI demands a multifaceted approach: judicious fluid resuscitation tailored by dynamic assessments; early and appropriate vasopressor support; recognition and targeted management of septic cardiomyopathy with individualized hemodynamic monitoring; evidence-based indications and modality selection for RRT; meticulous CRRT delivery with attention to anticoagulation, dosing, and fluid balance; and robust supportive care addressing glycemic control, nutrition, and prevention of ICU complications. Weaning from RRT requires integrated consideration of urine output, biochemical trends, functional assessments, and clinical trajectory. Interdisciplinary collaboration among intensivists, nephrologists, nurses, pharmacists, and nutritionists is essential to optimize outcomes and to plan for the long-term renal and functional recovery of survivors.

Introduction

Transfusion therapy is a cornerstone of supportive care for patients with bleeding, coagulopathy, and anemia. Judicious use of blood components—red blood cells (RBCs), platelets, plasma products, and fibrinogen-containing preparations—requires understanding indications, expected physiologic effects, dosing, compatibility testing, and risks. Management integrates knowledge of hemostasis, immunohematology, and the pathophysiology of transfusion-related complications. This chapter reviews practical, evidence-based principles for when and how to transfuse in coagulopathic or hemolyzing patients, how transfusion interacts with laboratory serologic testing, and special considerations when patients undergo plasma exchange.

# Red blood cell transfusion thresholds and crossmatching considerations

Red blood cell transfusion is intended to improve oxygen delivery by increasing hemoglobin concentration and oxygen-carrying capacity. The decision to transfuse should balance the potential benefit in oxygen delivery against risks (infection, immunologic reactions, volume overload, hemolysis, alloimmunization). Evidence-based hemoglobin thresholds guide practice but must be individualized to clinical context.

Transfusion thresholds
- Stable, non-bleeding medical or surgical patients: a restrictive strategy is generally recommended, transfusing when hemoglobin (Hb) is ≤7 g/dL. Randomized trials support equivalence or improved outcomes with a restrictive compared with a liberal (Hb ≤9–10 g/dL) strategy in many settings.
- Patients with active ischemia (acute coronary syndrome) or symptomatic anemia: higher thresholds are often used (commonly Hb ≤8 g/dL) and clinical judgment is essential. In unstable cardiac or septic patients, oxygen delivery demands and end-organ perfusion guide transfusion.
- Perioperative and massive hemorrhage scenarios: transfusion often guided by clinical bleeding, hemodynamics, and laboratory values (hemoglobin, coagulation tests) rather than a single threshold.
- Obstetric hemorrhage: rapid correction of blood loss is prioritized; threshold decisions incorporate hemodynamics, ongoing bleeding, and ability to obtain hemostasis.

Expected hemoglobin response and dosing
- In an adult ~70 kg receiving a single unit of packed RBCs (PRBCs), an increment of approximately $ \sim 1\ \mathrm{g/dL} $ in hemoglobin (or $ \sim 3\% $ in hematocrit) is expected. This is a population average; smaller increases occur in ongoing bleeding, hemodilution, or in larger patients.
- Dosing is therefore pragmatic: transfuse units one at a time in stable patients and reassess clinical response and Hb after each unit; in massive bleeding, transfuse according to protocols.

Crossmatching and compatibility testing
- Pretransfusion testing comprises ABO and Rh(D) typing, antibody screen (indirect antiglobulin test, IAT), and crossmatch. The goals are to detect incompatible ABO typing errors, clinically significant alloantibodies, and to ensure compatibility.
- Type and screen: ABO/Rh plus antibody screen is often sufficient when there is no prior antibody history and no anticipated need for emergent transfusion; if no antibodies are detected, an electronic or immediate spin crossmatch may be performed.
- Crossmatch types:
  - Immediate spin crossmatch for ABO verification (detects ABO incompatibility).
  - Antiglobulin (AHG) crossmatch for patients with positive antibody screen or serologic complexity; this detects clinically significant IgG alloantibodies.
  - Electronic (computer) crossmatch can be used if a validated system is in place and antibody screen is negative.
- In patients with recent transfusion, pregnancy, or prior alloimmunization, a thorough antibody workup is required to identify specific antibodies and provide antigen-negative units (e.g., provision of K-negative or phenotype-matched units for patients with anti-K or multiple antibodies).
- Special situations:
  - Emergency transfusion: if immediate transfusion is needed before crossmatch completion, group O, Rh(D)-negative blood is often used for females of child-bearing potential; group O, Rh(D)-positive may be used for males and post-menopausal females if O negative supply is limited—local policies vary.
  - Patients with warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia (WAIHA) or positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT): crossmatching can be challenging because autoantibodies may react broadly. Compatibility testing still proceeds; transfusion may be necessary when clinically indicated, and the benefit often outweighs the risk of hemolysis. In such cases, select the “least incompatible” unit based on crossmatch and monitor closely.
  - Recent plasma exchange or intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) can interfere with testing by introducing antibodies or diluting patient antibodies; communication with the transfusion service is critical.

Quality control and transfusion decisions should always integrate the clinical context and prior immunohematology history. Timely communication with the blood bank expedites suitable product provision.

# Platelet transfusion indications and dosing

Platelets are transfused to prevent or treat bleeding caused by quantitative thrombocytopenia or qualitative platelet dysfunction. Indications rely on platelet count, bleeding status, planned invasive procedures, and the expected rate of platelet consumption or destruction.

Indications and thresholds
- Prophylactic transfusion in stable, non-bleeding patients:
  - Hematologic malignancy/chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia: transfuse prophylactically when platelet count ≤10 × 10^9/L to prevent spontaneous bleeding.
  - Higher thresholds (e.g., ≤20 × 10^9/L) may be used for patients with additional risk factors such as fever, sepsis, or rapid decline.
- Procedural thresholds:
  - Central venous catheter placement or lumbar puncture: typically ≥50 × 10^9/L (some institutions accept ≥20–50 × 10^9/L depending on procedure and operator).
  - Major surgery, neurosurgery, epidural anesthesia: commonly target ≥100 × 10^9/L.
  - Minor procedures: thresholds vary with procedure and bleeding risk.
- Therapeutic transfusion for bleeding: transfuse at higher platelet counts depending on bleeding severity and the suspected etiology. For life-threatening bleeding or in massive transfusion, give platelets empirically as part of balanced resuscitation.

Dosing and expected increment
- Platelet products are provided as pooled random donor platelets (from 4–6 whole blood donations pooled into one therapeutic dose) or single-donor apheresis platelets (equivalent to one therapeutic dose).
- In adults, one typical therapeutic dose (apheresis unit or pooled platelet dose) is expected to raise platelet count by approximately $ \sim 30$--$60 \times 10^9/\mathrm{L} $ in an average 70 kg adult, although response varies.
- When assessing response, calculate corrected count increment (CCI) if poor response suspected:
  - CCI = (post-transfusion platelet increment × body surface area (m^2)) / number of platelets transfused (in $10^{11}$ units).
  - A CCI < 5,000–7,500 at 24 hours suggests platelet refractoriness.
- Refractoriness may be due to immune causes (alloimmunization against HLA or platelet-specific antigens) or nonimmune causes (fever, sepsis, DIC, splenomegaly, medications).

Product selection
- When alloimmunization is suspected or confirmed, select HLA-matched or crossmatched platelets or antigen-negative units. HLA-matched platelets improve increments for HLA alloimmunized patients.
- For patients with suspected immune-mediated transfusion refractoriness, administration of HLA-matched or crossmatched platelets is indicated.
- Special considerations: irradiated platelets for prevention of transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease in immunocompromised hosts; CMV-seronegative or leukoreduced products for selected high-risk patients.

Transfusion should be coupled with evaluation for reversible causes of low increment and targeted strategies to reduce alloimmunization risk (e.g., universal leukoreduction).

# Plasma products: fresh frozen plasma, solvent-detergent pooled plasma (Octaplas) — uses and pros/cons

Plasma products provide coagulation factors, anticoagulant proteins, albumin, and immunoglobulins to correct deficits in coagulation factor levels, to replace plasma volume, or as part of plasma exchange. Understanding the differences between fresh frozen plasma (FFP) and solvent-detergent–treated pooled plasma (e.g., Octaplas) helps choose an appropriate product.

Fresh frozen plasma (FFP)
- Composition: FFP contains all coagulation factors, fibrinogen, albumin, and immunoglobulins. It is collected from single donors and frozen within a defined period (commonly within 8 hours) to preserve labile factors (e.g., factor V and VIII).
- Indications:
  - Replacement of multiple coagulation factor deficiencies when specific factor concentrates are unavailable (e.g., liver failure with coagulopathy).
  - Emergency reversal of warfarin when prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) is not available or in certain settings where vitamin K reversal is inadequate for immediate correction.
  - Massive transfusion protocols to address dilutional coagulopathy.
  - Plasma exchange replacement fluid in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP).
- Dosing: typical adult dose is 10–15 mL/kg (e.g., 4 units in an average adult) to achieve clinically meaningful increases in coagulation factors. Expected correction depends on baseline levels; to achieve factor activity >30%, often this volume is needed.
- Pros: Widely available, contains the full complement of plasma proteins.
- Cons:
  - Risk of transfusion-transmitted infection (although minimized by donor screening and testing).
  - Risk of allergic reactions, transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO).
  - Variability in factor levels between donors; single-donor product may carry donor-specific antibodies.

Solvent-detergent pooled plasma (e.g., Octaplas)
- Manufacturing: plasma from many donors is pooled and treated with solvent-detergent processes that inactivate enveloped viruses (e.g., HIV, HBV, HCV). The product is then standardized and aliquoted.
- Indications: similar to FFP—replacement of multiple coagulation factor deficits, massive transfusion, and plasma exchange in certain indications. Used where a standardized factor content and improved viral safety are desired.
- Pros:
  - Reduced variability in coagulation factor levels because of pooling.
  - Lower risk of enveloped viral transmission due to pathogen inactivation.
  - Reduced incidence of TRALI in some reports because donor antibodies are diluted across pooled plasma.
- Cons:
  - Does not inactivate non-enveloped viruses or prions; bacterial contamination risk remains for platelets rather than plasma due to storage conditions.
  - Potential for reduced levels of certain labile factors compared with fresh FFP; some products are screened or treated in ways that reduce levels of factor V or VIII.
  - Theoretical risk of transmitting pooled donor antibodies (although dilution generally reduces this risk).
  - Availability and cost may limit use in some centers.

Clinical selection between FFP and Octaplas weighs urgency, infection risk, need for standardized factor levels, and institutional availability. For TTP, many centers prefer plasma (often FFP) as replacement fluid for plasma exchange, but Octaplas can be an alternative depending on local protocols.

# Fibrinogen replacement and cryoprecipitate

Fibrinogen is the precursor of fibrin and is essential for clot formation. Fibrinogen depletion is a key driver of bleeding in trauma, obstetric hemorrhage, cardiac surgery, and certain coagulopathies. Rapid correction of low fibrinogen is frequently necessary in bleeding patients.

Normal ranges and targets
- Normal plasma fibrinogen concentration: approximately 200–400 mg/dL (2.0–4.0 g/L).
- In actively bleeding patients, many guidelines recommend maintaining fibrinogen ≥1.5–2.0 g/L (150–200 mg/dL); in obstetric hemorrhage, a higher minimum (≥2.0 g/L) is often targeted.

Products for replacement
- Cryoprecipitate:
  - Composition: cryoprecipitate is a cold-insoluble concentrate derived from FFP and is rich in fibrinogen, factor VIII, factor XIII, von Willebrand factor, and fibronectin.
  - Typical fibrinogen content: variable, commonly ≈150–250 mg per unit (this varies by donor and processing).
  - Dosing: standard adult dosing often uses 1 unit per 5–10 kg or empirical doses such as 4–6 units for bleeding adults, with larger doses for severe depletion. Because of variability in fibrinogen content per unit, many centers use weight-based or goal-directed dosing guided by fibrinogen measurement or viscoelastic testing (e.g., thromboelastography).
  - Expected effect: approximate increases depend on dose and patient size; for example, transfusing 10 units may raise fibrinogen by roughly $ \sim 50$–$100\ \mathrm{mg/dL} $ in an average adult, but this is variable.
  - Pros: widely available, relatively low cost.
  - Cons: variable fibrinogen content, need for thawing, risk of infectious transmission, allergic reactions, and volume load.
- Fibrinogen concentrate:
  - Composition: lyophilized, purified fibrinogen concentrate derived from pooled plasma; standardized fibrinogen content per vial.
  - Dosing: more predictable. Dosing calculation often uses a formula to determine required mg:
    - A commonly used approach for fibrinogen concentrate is to calculate the required dose (mg) using the target minus measured fibrinogen (in g/L) times a plasma volume estimate. A simplified practical dosing approach is:
      Dose (mg) = (target fibrinogen (g/L) − measured fibrinogen (g/L)) × 0.06 × body weight (kg) × 1000,
    where 0.06 approximates plasma volume fraction. Exact formulas vary in the literature; institutional protocols typically provide the preferred calculation.
  - In practice, doses of 25–50 mg/kg are commonly used for fibrinogen concentrate in bleeding patients. For example, a 70 kg patient receiving 50 mg/kg would receive 3500 mg (3.5 g) of fibrinogen concentrate.
  - Pros: standardized dosing, rapid reconstitution and administration, lower volume, reduced variability, reduced infectious risk compared with cryoprecipitate.
  - Cons: cost and variable availability in some regions.

Goal-directed therapy
- Fibrinogen replacement is most effective when guided by quantitative fibrinogen assays (Clauss method) or point-of-care viscoelastic testing (e.g., TEG, ROTEM), which detect functional fibrinogen contribution to clot strength and help direct targeted therapy quickly.
- Integrate fibrinogen replacement into massive transfusion or hemorrhage protocols, recognizing that fibrinogen falls early in hemorrhage and should be corrected promptly to restore clot formation.

# Impact of transfusion on serologic testing (DAT) and antigenicity

Transfusions and plasma therapies can alter serologic testing results and antigenicity, complicating diagnostic interpretation and compatibility testing.

Direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and transfusion
- DAT detects in vivo coating of red cells with immunoglobulin (usually IgG) and/or complement (C3d). Positive DAT can be seen from various causes: autoimmune hemolytic anemia, alloimmune hemolysis from transfused RBCs, drug-induced antibodies, and passive transfer of antibodies.
- Post-transfusion DAT positivity:
  - Alloantibodies from prior sensitization can bind transfused RBCs, producing hemolysis with a positive DAT.
  - Passive transfer of donor-derived antibodies (rare) through plasma-containing products or in pooled plasma might coat recipient red cells transiently.
  - IVIG administration may cause positive DAT due to passive IgG coating.
- Clinical relevance: a positive DAT merits evaluation (elution studies, antibody identification) to determine whether the coating is due to an alloantibody causing hemolysis or a benign passive process. In transfusion-associated hemolysis, the eluate often contains the implicated alloantibody.

Interference with pretransfusion testing
- Recent transfusions can result in mixed-field typing (presence of donor and recipient RBC populations) and complicate crossmatch interpretation. For example, if a patient was transfused recently, the presence of donor RBCs can obscure antigen typing or mask weak patient antibodies.
- Patients who have received IVIG, plasma exchange, or IV plasma-derived therapies may have transient circulating antibodies, complicating antibody screen interpretation.
- After plasma exchange, recipient alloantibody titers may be reduced, potentially yielding a false-negative antibody screen; however, antibodies may rebound after plasma exchange stops. Communicate recent procedures to the immunohematology laboratory for appropriate interpretation and for planning repeat testing.

Antigenicity and alloimmunization
- Transfusion exposes recipients to foreign RBC antigens (e.g., D, C, E, Kell, Duffy, Kidd), platelet antigens, and HLA antigens (through platelet transfusions and leukocytes). Development of alloantibodies makes future transfusion selection more difficult and increases risk of hemolytic transfusion reactions or platelet refractoriness.
- Strategies to reduce alloimmunization:
  - Leukoreduction reduces HLA alloimmunization and febrile nonhemolytic reactions.
  - Phenotypic or genotypic matching for high-risk patients (e.g., chronic transfusion recipients, sickle cell disease) reduces rate of RBC alloimmunization.
  - For females of childbearing potential, providing Rh(D)-negative RBCs as appropriate reduces sensitization to D antigen.
- Laboratory methods: elution studies and antibody identification panels determine specificity of antibodies; molecular genotyping can be used when serologic testing is complicated by positive DAT or recent transfusion.

Overall, interplay between transfusion and serologic testing mandates careful timing of tests, documentation of transfusion history, and communication between treating teams and transfusion services.

# Transfusion-related complications: TRALI, TACO, alloimmunization, infection transmission

Transfusion is life-saving but carries potential complications. Awareness of mechanisms, recognition, and prevention strategies improves patient safety.

Transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI)
- Definition: acute hypoxemic respiratory failure within 6 hours of transfusion, with bilateral pulmonary infiltrates not explained by circulatory overload or other causes. TRALI is divided into immune-mediated and nonimmune (two-hit) forms.
- Pathophysiology:
  - Immune-mediated TRALI: donor anti-HLA or anti-neutrophil antibodies react with recipient leukocytes, causing neutrophil activation in the pulmonary microcirculation, capillary leak, and noncardiogenic pulmonary edema.
  - Two-hit model: recipient has a predisposing inflammatory state (first hit) that primes neutrophils; transfusion provides biologically active substances (second hit) such as cytokines, lipids, or non-antibody-mediated factors that trigger neutrophil activation.
- Clinical features: sudden onset dyspnea, hypoxemia, fever, hypotension, and hypoxemic respiratory failure with bilateral infiltrates on chest imaging. Typically occurs within 6 hours of transfusion.
- Management: supportive care with oxygen, ventilatory support as needed; diuretics are not helpful unless concurrent volume overload; stop transfusion immediately. Report to transfusion service for investigation.
- Prevention: use of plasma from male donors or screened donors to reduce antibody-containing plasma; minimizing storage time of components may reduce accumulation of biologically active lipids (evidence variable). Surveillance and donor investigation are critical.

Transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO)
- Definition: acute hydrostatic pulmonary edema due to volume or transfusion rate excess, presenting within 6 hours of transfusion.
- Pathophysiology: transfusion increases intravascular volume beyond cardiac reserve, leading to left ventricular failure and pulmonary edema.
- Risk factors: underlying cardiac dysfunction, renal insufficiency, extremes of age, small body size, rapid or large-volume transfusions, and positive fluid balance.
- Clinical features: hypertension, tachycardia, hypoxemia, pulmonary edema on chest imaging, jugular venous distension; response to diuretics supports TACO diagnosis.
- Prevention and management: slow transfusion rates, use of diuretics prophylactically or promptly if signs of TACO develop, monitor volume status, consider splitting units and transfusing more slowly in at-risk patients. Stop transfusion and treat with oxygen and diuretics as indicated. Distinguishing TRALI from TACO relies on the clinical picture (hemodynamic status, radiographic findings, BNP levels, response to diuretics).

Alloimmunization
- Mechanisms: immune response to foreign red cell antigens, platelet antigens, or HLA antigens following transfusion or pregnancy.
- Clinical consequences:
  - RBC alloimmunization can cause delayed hemolytic transfusion reactions on subsequent exposures and complicate crossmatching by creating multiple antibodies requiring antigen-negative units.
  - Platelet alloimmunization (anti-HLA or platelet-specific antibodies) can cause refractoriness to platelet transfusion.
  - Alloimmunization may also affect future pregnancies (e.g., hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn from anti-D or other clinically significant antibodies).
- Prevention: leukoreduction, antigen matching in chronically transfused patients (e.g., sickle cell disease), and judicious transfusion practices.

Infectious transmission
- Despite advances in donor screening, testing, and pathogen reduction, blood transfusion carries residual risk of infection transmission.
- Pathogens of concern:
  - Viruses: HIV, HBV, HCV—modern nucleic acid testing has markedly reduced window-period risks; residual risks are low but nonzero.
  - Bacterial contamination: most common with platelets due to room-temperature storage; presents as febrile reactions or septic transfusion reactions. Platelet bacterial screening and short storage times mitigate risk.
  - Emerging pathogens: West Nile virus, Zika, dengue, Babesia, and other geographically restricted or emerging pathogens can be transmitted; travel screening and donor questioning attempt to reduce risk.
  - Prion diseases: theoretical risk for variant Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease; policies minimize donor risk via geographic deferrals.
- Prevention: donor screening, serologic and nucleic acid testing, pathogen reduction technologies for plasma and platelets where available, sterile collection, and storage practices.

Other complications
- Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction: typically due to ABO incompatibility; manifests with fever, hypotension, hemoglobinuria, DIC, renal failure; prevention through rigorous ABO verification.
- Febrile nonhemolytic transfusion reactions: due to recipient cytokine response or antibodies to donor leukocytes; reduced by leukoreduction.
- Allergic reactions and anaphylaxis: mild urticarial reactions to severe anaphylaxis, more common in plasma-containing products and in IgA-deficient patients with anti-IgA antibodies — for IgA-deficient recipients, provide washed or IgA-deficient products.

Reporting and hemovigilance
- Any serious transfusion reaction must be reported immediately to the transfusion service and local hemovigilance systems. Investigation includes sample collection (posttransfusion blood, urine), repeat testing (ABO, DAT, hemoglobin), and evaluation of implicated donor products.

Conclusion

Safe and effective transfusion in coagulopathy and hemolysis requires integrating physiology, laboratory data, and clinical context. Use restrictive RBC strategies in stable patients while tailoring thresholds to ischemia or ongoing hemorrhage. Platelet transfusion decisions depend on count, bleeding, and procedure-specific thresholds and must consider refractoriness and HLA alloimmunization. Choose plasma products (FFP vs solvent-detergent pooled plasma) based on availability, urgency, and safety considerations. For fibrinogen deficiency, rapid correction with cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate guided by quantitative assays or viscoelastic testing improves hemostasis. Finally, anticipate and recognize transfusion complications—TRALI, TACO, alloimmunization, infectious risks—and work closely with transfusion medicine services to optimize matching, testing, and reporting for each patient.

# Introduction

Therapeutic inhibition of the terminal complement cascade (most commonly via C5 inhibitors such as eculizumab and ravulizumab) has transformed outcomes for patients with diseases such as paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, and refractory myasthenia gravis. These agents, by preventing formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC), reduce complement‑mediated hemolysis or tissue injury, but they also remove a critical component of innate defense against certain encapsulated bacteria. As a result, patients receiving terminal complement inhibitors are at substantially increased risk for invasive infections, most classically meningococcal disease, and must be managed proactively with vaccination, appropriate timing of immunizations, and targeted antimicrobial prophylaxis and education.

This chapter explains the pathophysiologic basis for infection risk during terminal complement blockade, outlines recommended vaccinations (including meningococcal conjugate and serogroup B vaccines, pneumococcal and Haemophilus influenzae vaccines), addresses timing and serologic considerations for vaccination around initiation of complement inhibitors, describes antibiotic prophylaxis strategies when vaccination cannot be completed prior to therapy or in high‑risk scenarios, and details patient education and post‑vaccination monitoring requirements.

# Impact of terminal complement blockade on susceptibility to encapsulated bacteria

The terminal complement pathway (C5 → C5a + C5b → assembly of C5b-9 MAC) is a principal effector of innate immunity against certain encapsulated organisms. The MAC directly lyses susceptible Gram‑negative organisms and contributes to opsonophagocytic killing, while upstream complement fragments (C3b, iC3b) coat bacteria and enhance neutrophil and macrophage phagocytosis via complement receptors. Blocking C5 prevents MAC formation and reduces generation of C5a, impairing chemotaxis, neutrophil activation, and amplification of complement opsonization.

Neisseria meningitidis is particularly dependent on intact terminal complement for host defense. Individuals with congenital deficiencies of C5–C9 have a >1,000‑fold increased susceptibility to invasive meningococcal disease and tend to present with recurrent meningococcemia, often with less fulminant but still severe infections. Therapeutic terminal complement blockade reproduces this vulnerability: prospective and registry data show a markedly increased incidence of invasive meningococcal disease (IMD) in patients receiving C5 inhibitors compared with the general population. Importantly, meningococcal disease in this population can present atypically, with an increased frequency of meningococcemia and disseminated infection without meningitis, and may be caused by non‑groupable strains or serogroups not covered by routine vaccines.

Other encapsulated organisms are also of concern. Complement promotes opsonophagocytic killing of Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib). While terminal complement blockade poses less dramatic relative risk for invasive disease by these organisms than for Neisseria, patients with complement inhibition may nevertheless have increased susceptibility, particularly in the presence of additional risk factors (e.g., asplenia, chronic lung disease, concurrent immunosuppression). Clinically, the principal preventive focus is meningococcal disease, but pneumococcal and H. influenzae prophylaxis and vaccination deserve attention as part of a comprehensive infection‑prevention plan.

# Recommended vaccinations prior to complement inhibitor initiation: meningococcal conjugate and serogroup B vaccines, pneumococcal and Haemophilus influenzae considerations

Vaccination strategy for patients who will receive terminal complement inhibitors aims to induce protective immunity against the most likely invasive pathogens — principally N. meningitidis — before complement blockade impairs vaccine response and reduces ability to control infection. Current consensus recommendations (CDC, infectious disease specialty guidelines, product labeling for complement inhibitors) emphasize the following vaccines.

Meningococcal conjugate vaccines (MenACWY). These vaccines induce immunity against serogroups A, C, W, and Y and are available in conjugated formulations (e.g., MenACWY‑CRM, MenACWY‑D). For persons starting terminal complement inhibitors, a complete MenACWY vaccination is recommended. For adults who have not previously received a MenACWY dose or whose prior vaccination history is uncertain, give a MenACWY dose ideally at least 2 weeks before starting complement inhibition. For patients with ongoing risk (e.g., continuous complement blockade), booster doses are recommended every 5 years to maintain circulating antibody levels.

Serogroup B meningococcal vaccines (MenB). MenB vaccines (e.g., MenB‑4C, MenB‑fHbp) target the often‑excluded serogroup B and are recommended in addition to MenACWY for persons at increased risk for meningococcal disease, including those receiving terminal complement inhibitors. MenB vaccine schedules differ by product (two‑ or three‑dose series). Given the vulnerability of complement‑inhibited patients to MenB infection, clinicians should administer MenB vaccination whenever possible prior to complement inhibitor initiation and complete the full series. Because MenB antigens are not covered by MenACWY, receiving both vaccines broadens coverage.

Pneumococcal vaccines. Because complement contributes to pneumococcal clearance and because some patients receiving complement inhibitors may have additional risk factors (e.g., asplenia, chronic diseases), pneumococcal vaccination should be reviewed and updated according to age and underlying risk. For immunocompetent adults with risk factors for invasive pneumococcal disease or immunocompromise, a schedule including both pneumococcal conjugate (PCV13, PCV15, or PCV20 depending on national guidelines and availability) and pneumococcal polysaccharide (PPSV23) is recommended. For previously unvaccinated higher‑risk adults, typical schedules include PCV15 or PCV20 (one dose) with consideration of PPSV23 at least 8 weeks after PCV15 or as directed by local guidance. For children, follow the routine pediatric pneumococcal immunization schedule.

Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib). Hib vaccination is part of routine pediatric immunization and should be confirmed in younger patients. Adults with anatomical or functional asplenia and other high‑risk conditions should receive Hib vaccination if not previously immunized. Patients starting terminal complement inhibitors who are asplenic or otherwise at high risk should receive Hib vaccination per standard indications.

Other vaccines. Influenza vaccination annually is recommended for all patients, and additional vaccines (e.g., hepatitis B) should be administered according to standard risk‑based recommendations. Live vaccines are generally contraindicated or relatively contraindicated in patients receiving ongoing immunosuppression, but terminal complement inhibitors per se are not live‑vaccine contraindications — however, timing and individual immunosuppressive regimens must be considered.

# Timing of vaccination relative to therapy and serologic response considerations

Timing of vaccination in relation to initiation of complement inhibitors influences the ability to mount an effective immune response and determines whether additional protective measures (e.g., antibiotics) are needed while awaiting vaccine‑induced immunity.

Optimal timing. When possible, complete the recommended meningococcal vaccinations at least 2 weeks prior to the first dose of a complement inhibitor. The 2‑week interval is chosen because serologic responses to conjugate vaccines typically develop within 10–14 days, and antibody titers at that point confer some protection while memory responses mature. For MenB vaccines, which may require multiple doses spaced over weeks, begin the series prior to complement blockade and, if feasible, complete the series before starting therapy. If initiation of complement inhibitor cannot be deferred (for example, in severe thrombotic microangiopathy), administer vaccines as soon as possible and institute antibiotic prophylaxis as described below until an appropriate interval after the vaccine is achieved.

When prior vaccination exists. If a patient has previously received MenACWY but it was given years earlier, assess whether a booster is due: ongoing complement inhibition constitutes continued high risk and typically warrants revaccination if the last MenACWY dose is more than 5 years prior. For MenB, if the series was completed in the past, booster recommendations vary; consider re‑vaccination based on time since completion, local guidance, and serologic risk assessment in consultation with infectious disease specialists.

Serologic testing. Routine measurement of vaccine‑induced antibody titers is not generally recommended in clinical practice because standardized, clinically available correlates of protection are limited. For meningococcal vaccines, functional serum bactericidal antibody (SBA) assays with human complement (hSBA) are the most informative correlate of immunity but are not widely available and are labor‑intensive. Thus, clinicians generally rely on adherence to vaccine schedules and timing to infer protection. Serologic testing may be considered in select situations — for example, in research settings, in patients with severe immunosuppression expected to blunt vaccine response, or when evaluating vaccine failure. If testing is performed, interpret results in consultation with clinical microbiology or infectious disease experts and be aware of assay‑specific protective thresholds (e.g., hSBA titers ≥1:4 often used as an indicator of putative protection for certain serogroups).

Effect of complement inhibitors on vaccine response. Complement inhibition impairs generation of full vaccine responses because complement components contribute to B‑cell activation and antigen presentation. Therefore, vaccinating before complement blockade is preferable. If vaccination occurs after therapy has begun, vaccine immunogenicity may be reduced; clinicians should document the timing and counsel patients regarding potential residual risk even after vaccination. In urgent therapy starts, administer vaccines immediately and cover with antibiotics until the patient is likely to have developed protective antibody levels (commonly taken as 2 weeks after a completed vaccine dose, recognizing that multiple doses may be required for full protection).

# Antibiotic prophylaxis regimens when vaccines are not timely or in certain high‑risk scenarios

Antibiotic prophylaxis is a critical adjunct when vaccination cannot be completed before initiating complement inhibition, when a patient is known or suspected to be unvaccinated, or in periods of high exposure risk. The goals of prophylaxis are to provide immediate protection against invasive meningococcal disease during windows of vulnerability and to mitigate risk in patients who demonstrably do not develop adequate responses to vaccination.

When to use prophylaxis. Antibiotic prophylaxis should be used in the following scenarios: (1) complement inhibitor therapy must be started immediately and meningococcal vaccination has not been completed ≥2 weeks prior; (2) a patient refuses or cannot receive vaccination; (3) documented lack of serologic response to vaccination (rare and usually assessed case‑by‑case); (4) during the interval between vaccination and expected development of protective immunity; (5) in patients with ongoing high risk (e.g., asplenia) who either failed to complete vaccine schedules or who have continued exposure risk despite vaccination. Some authorities recommend offering long‑term prophylaxis for the entire duration of complement inhibitor therapy if vaccines are contraindicated or if there is persistent inability to mount an immune response.

Recommended agents and typical regimens. There is some variation between jurisdictions and institutions; clinicians should follow local public health guidance. Commonly used regimens include:

- Single‑dose oral ciprofloxacin 500 mg. A single oral 500 mg dose is highly effective for eradication of nasopharyngeal carriage of N. meningitidis and is often used for immediate prophylaxis or as close contact chemoprophylaxis. Ciprofloxacin is contraindicated in children and pregnant women in many settings.

- Single‑dose oral azithromycin 500 mg. A single dose of azithromycin is an alternative for eradication of carriage in adults and may be used when fluoroquinolones are contraindicated.

- Rifampin. For adult chemoprophylaxis, rifampin 600 mg orally every 12 hours for 2 days (four doses total) is a traditional regimen to eradicate carriage. Rifampin has significant drug interactions (induces cytochrome P450 enzymes) and is contraindicated in some concomitant therapies.

- Long‑term prophylaxis with oral penicillin or amoxicillin. For prolonged suppression of meningococcal infection risk in patients who cannot be adequately vaccinated, agents such as penicillin V 250–500 mg orally twice daily or amoxicillin 500 mg orally twice daily have been used. The evidence base for long‑term suppression is limited and must be balanced against risks of antimicrobial resistance and adverse effects.

Duration of prophylaxis. If prophylaxis is used as a bridge to vaccination, continue antibiotics until 2 weeks after the vaccine dose that is expected to confer protection. For urgent starts when multiple vaccine doses are required (e.g., MenB series), maintain prophylaxis until at least 2 weeks after completion of the series, if feasible. For patients who are unvaccinated or vaccine‑nonresponders and who cannot be vaccinated, many experts recommend continuing prophylaxis for the duration of complement inhibition; however, decisions about indefinite prophylaxis should be individualized, taking into account local meningococcal epidemiology, the patient’s adherence capability, and potential for adverse events and resistance.

Special populations. In children, pregnant women, and patients with contraindications to specific antibiotics, choose agents with appropriate safety profiles. For pediatric dosing and pregnancy considerations, consult pediatric infectious disease or obstetric infectious disease specialists and local guidelines. For example, ciprofloxacin is generally avoided in children and pregnant women; rifampin, with appropriate dosing, may be used for short courses but has interactions to consider.

Public health notification and contact management. If a patient on complement inhibitors develops suspected or confirmed invasive meningococcal disease, public health authorities should be notified promptly for guidance on close contacts and potential chemoprophylaxis or vaccination campaigns.

# Patient education about infection signs and emergency plans

Effective patient education is central to preventing morbidity and mortality from invasive infections during complement inhibition. Education should be documented, repeated at regular intervals, and reinforced with written materials and an emergency action plan.

Key elements of education.

- Risk counseling. Explain that terminal complement inhibitors markedly increase the risk of invasive meningococcal disease and may increase risk for other encapsulated organisms. Clarify that vaccination reduces but does not eliminate risk; some serogroups and non‑groupable strains may cause disease despite vaccination.

- Symptoms to watch for. Teach patients to recognize early signs and symptoms of meningococcal infection and other invasive bacterial disease. Common early symptoms include sudden fever, headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, confusion, nausea or vomiting, severe myalgias, lethargy, and a rapidly developing petechial or purpuric rash. Meningococcemia can progress very quickly to septic shock; emphasize that even mild constitutional symptoms warrant immediate medical attention in this context.

- Immediate actions. Instruct patients to seek urgent medical care (emergency department or designated facility) at the first sign of fever or other concerning symptoms. They should not wait for symptoms to worsen. Patients should understand that empiric parenteral antibiotic therapy may be administered immediately in the emergency setting prior to diagnostic confirmation.

- Carrying documentation. Provide an alert card or bracelet stating that the patient is receiving terminal complement inhibitor therapy and is at increased risk for meningococcal disease and other encapsulated infections. The card should indicate current vaccination status, last vaccine dates, whether an antibiotic prophylaxis regimen is in place, and contact information for their prescribing clinician and local infectious disease resources.

- Household and close contact information. Inform patients that household members are not inherently at elevated risk because of the patient’s therapy; however, instructions for contacts (e.g., seeking care for fever) should be clarified if the patient develops invasive disease. Advise vaccination of household members per routine public health recommendations when indicated.

- Antibiotic availability. If long‑term prophylaxis is prescribed or if bridging antibiotics are recommended, provide clear instructions, including dosing, duration, and contingency plans if medications are missed. Discuss potential side effects and interactions (e.g., rifampin with many drugs).

- Travel and exposure advice. Counsel patients about travel to areas with increased meningococcal disease prevalence or during outbreaks; ensure vaccines are up to date and discuss additional prophylactic measures as needed.

Emergency plan specifics. Create a written emergency plan describing: (1) symptoms that should prompt immediate action; (2) the local emergency department and contact numbers; (3) that clinicians should be informed that the patient is on complement inhibition; (4) that empiric parenteral antibiotics are appropriate for suspected invasive meningococcal disease; and (5) any individualized instructions for ongoing prophylaxis. Periodically review the plan at clinic visits and update vaccination or prophylaxis details.

# Post‑vaccination monitoring and reporting of adverse events

Monitoring after vaccination serves two purposes: ensuring vaccine safety and maintaining documentation of immunization status. Given that patients on complement inhibitors may receive multiple vaccines in short succession, timing and vigilance for adverse events are important.

Immediate and short‑term monitoring. After vaccination, monitor patients for immediate allergic reactions for the time interval recommended for the particular vaccine (typically 15–30 minutes post‑injection in clinic). Provide patients with instructions to report systemic symptoms such as high fever, severe local reactions, neurological symptoms, or signs suggestive of anaphylaxis. Because several vaccines may be given within a short timeframe, document which vaccines were administered, their lot numbers, and injection sites.

Longer‑term safety surveillance. Encourage patients and clinicians to report suspected adverse events temporally related to vaccination through local and national adverse event reporting systems (e.g., VAERS in the United States). Serious adverse events (e.g., Guillain‑Barré syndrome, severe allergic reactions) should prompt immediate clinical evaluation and reporting. For patients on complement inhibition, also report any post‑vaccination invasive infections to public health authorities because cases may represent vaccine failure or infection with strains not covered by the vaccine.

Serologic follow‑up and documentation. As noted above, routine post‑vaccination serologic testing is not required for most patients because standardized protective thresholds are not universally available. In select high‑risk or immunocompromised patients, or for investigational purposes, functional assays (hSBA) may be used to evaluate response to meningococcal vaccines; interpretation should be guided by microbiology experts. Maintain comprehensive vaccination records in the patient’s chart and provide copies to the patient.

Assessing vaccine failures. If an invasive meningococcal infection occurs despite appropriate vaccination, collect isolates for serogrouping and molecular typing and notify public health authorities. Such cases should be investigated to determine whether the infecting strain was vaccine‑preventable and whether additional public health measures are warranted.

Balancing adverse events and benefits. Vaccination and prophylaxis strategies in patients receiving complement inhibitors balance the small risk of vaccine adverse events against the substantially increased risk of invasive, life‑threatening infection. Because meningococcal disease can progress very rapidly and cause significant morbidity and mortality, vaccination and appropriate prophylaxis are generally favored and should be emphasized as part of routine care.

# Conclusion

Terminal complement inhibition introduces a predictable and preventable infectious risk, most notably from Neisseria meningitidis. Preventive strategies rely on timely vaccination (MenACWY plus MenB), consideration of pneumococcal and Hib vaccination when indicated, prudent timing of vaccine administration relative to therapy, short‑term or long‑term antibiotic prophylaxis when vaccines are not feasible or likely ineffective, and comprehensive patient education with clear emergency plans. Post‑vaccination monitoring and adverse event reporting complete the safety net. Close coordination among hematology/immunology specialists prescribing complement inhibitors, primary care providers, infectious disease consultants, and public health authorities is essential to optimize prevention and to rapidly mobilize response should infection occur.

Introduction

Pancytopenia—the reduction of hemoglobin, leukocytes, and platelets simultaneously—poses a diagnostic and management challenge because it reflects disorders ranging from reversible drug effects to life-threatening bone marrow failure or systemic immune dysregulation. When pancytopenia is accompanied by multisystem signs (fever, organomegaly, transaminitis, coagulopathy, rash, neurologic changes), the differential expands to include severe infections, hematologic malignancy, immune-mediated syndromes, and macrophage activation disorders. A systematic, mechanism-oriented approach ties basic pathophysiology to pragmatic diagnostic steps and early management priorities. This chapter reviews the causes of pancytopenia, guides the selection and interpretation of laboratory and imaging studies, distinguishes key competing diagnoses (hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, acute leukemia, aplastic anemia, sepsis-associated cytopenias), and outlines when to involve hematology and perform bone marrow biopsy. Finally, it describes how to synthesize clinical, laboratory, and microbiologic data to prioritize interventions.

# Causes of pancytopenia: bone marrow failure, marrow infiltration, peripheral destruction/consumption, hypersplenism

Pancytopenia arises when production of one or more hematopoietic lineages is impaired, when produced elements are sequestered or destroyed peripherally, or when a combination of mechanisms is present. Thinking in these categories helps target investigations.

Bone marrow failure (hypoproliferative pancytopenia) results from absolute loss or dysfunction of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells or their supportive microenvironment. Classic etiologies include aplastic anemia (idiopathic, immune-mediated destruction of hematopoietic stem cells), marrow toxic injury from drugs (alkylating agents, antimetabolites, some antibiotics), radiation, and nutritional deficiencies (severe vitamin B12 or folate deficiency). In aplastic anemia, the marrow is characteristically hypocellular with fatty infiltration, and patients have low reticulocyte counts despite cytopenias. Pathophysiologically, immune-mediated T-cell responses and cytokines (e.g., interferon-gamma, TNF-alpha) can suppress stem cell proliferation and survival; genetic predispositions (e.g., Fanconi anemia) cause intrinsic stem cell failure via DNA repair defects.

Marrow infiltration (hypercellular marrow with ineffective hematopoiesis) refers to replacement of normal marrow elements by malignant or granulomatous processes. Acute leukemias, myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS), metastatic solid tumors, lymphoma involving the marrow, and granulomatous infections (tuberculosis, fungal infections) can all produce pancytopenia. In these conditions, marrow cellularity may be normal or increased, but hematopoiesis is ineffective: blasts or malignant cells occupy the niche, dysplasia leads to cell death, and marrow fibrosis may further compromise production.

Peripheral destruction and consumption reduce circulating cell counts despite preserved or increased marrow production. Mechanisms include autoimmune cytopenias (autoimmune hemolytic anemia, immune thrombocytopenia), complement-mediated destruction, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) causing platelet and red cell consumption, and hemophagocytic syndromes in which activated macrophages engulf hematopoietic cells. Peripheral destruction is often suggested by a brisk reticulocyte response in anemia, elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), indirect hyperbilirubinemia (in hemolysis), or schistocytes on the peripheral smear in microangiopathic processes.

Hypersplenism leads to sequestration of circulating blood cells in an enlarged spleen and can produce pancytopenia. Chronic portal hypertension and other causes of splenomegaly (infectious, infiltrative, hematologic) promote pooling of platelets and leukocytes; cell counts often improve after splenectomy but management depends on underlying cause. Distinguishing true hypersplenism from marrow failure requires correlation of marrow studies, imaging, and clinical context.

Often multiple mechanisms coexist—for example, sepsis can combine marrow suppression with consumptive coagulopathy, and hematologic malignancy may coexist with splenic sequestration. The diagnostic approach must therefore remain broad initially but directed by specific clues in history, exam, and initial labs.

# Key laboratory and imaging investigations: peripheral smear, reticulocyte count, bone marrow examination, cultures and serologies

The evaluation of pancytopenia aims to determine whether the problem is hypoproliferative, consumptive, or due to peripheral destruction or infiltration, and to identify reversible causes. Initial and targeted investigations should be done promptly according to clinical severity.

Complete blood count with differential and indices is the foundation. Reported numbers should be placed in clinical context: normal hemoglobin ranges roughly 13.5–17.5 g/dL in adult males and 12.0–15.5 g/dL in adult females; leukocyte count normally 4.0–11.0 × 10^9/L; platelets 150–450 × 10^9/L. Absolute neutrophil count (ANC) is clinically important for infection risk: neutropenia is ANC $<1500/\mu L$, and severe neutropenia is ANC $<500/\mu L$.

Peripheral blood smear examination is essential. Smear morphology can rapidly distinguish causes: circulating blasts suggest acute leukemia or marrow blast proliferation; macro-ovalocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils point to megaloblastic anemia (vitamin B12 or folate deficiency); spherocytes or bite cells suggest hemolysis; schistocytes suggest microangiopathic hemolytic anemia or DIC; clumped platelets or platelet satellitism may artifactually lower automated counts; leukoerythroblastic picture (nucleated red cells, immature myeloid forms) suggests marrow infiltration or severe stress/hemorrhage.

Reticulocyte count assesses marrow responsiveness to anemia. A low reticulocyte percentage despite anemia indicates poor marrow production; a brisk reticulocyte response indicates peripheral destruction or loss. Normal reticulocyte percentage is approximately 0.5–2.5% in adults; interpretation requires correction for degree of anemia (reticulocyte production index). An absolute reticulocyte count or corrected retic is more helpful in severe anemia.

Basic chemistry and hemolysis markers include lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), haptoglobin, indirect bilirubin, and direct antiglobulin test (Coombs) when hemolysis is suspected. Coagulation studies (PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer) are indicated when DIC or consumptive coagulopathy is possible. Triglycerides and ferritin can be helpful when HLH is in the differential (hyperferritinemia, hypertriglyceridemia).

Bone marrow examination—aspirate and biopsy—is the definitive test to distinguish marrow failure, infiltration, or hemophagocytosis. Indications include unexplained pancytopenia after initial reversible causes are excluded, circulating blasts, suspicion for MDS/acute leukemia, and when diagnostic clarity will change urgent management. Aspirate morphology, cytogenetics, flow cytometry, and marrow biopsy cellularity/fibrosis all contribute. Cytogenetic studies and molecular testing (e.g., mutation panels for MDS/AML) should be obtained on marrow samples when malignancy is suspected.

Microbiologic testing is guided by clinical context. Blood cultures (at least two sets) and cultures from other suspected sources should be obtained quickly in febrile or septic patients. Viral testing for HIV, hepatitis B and C, EBV, CMV, parvovirus B19, and others (e.g., SARS-CoV-2) can uncover infectious causes of marrow suppression or hemophagocytic syndromes. Serologic and PCR testing for endemic infections (leishmaniasis, brucellosis, malaria) should be considered by exposure history. Bone marrow studies can also identify intracellular organisms (Leishmania), acid-fast bacilli, or fungal elements.

Autoimmune and rheumatologic testing (ANA, anti-dsDNA, complement levels, antiphospholipid antibodies) is indicated if systemic autoimmune disease is suspected. Drug review is vital: many medications can induce aplasia or immune cytopenias. Nutritional assays (vitamin B12, folate) and thyroid function tests complete initial workup.

Imaging assists in identifying organomegaly, lymphadenopathy, or marrow infiltration. Abdominal ultrasound or CT can detect splenomegaly, focal hepatic lesions, or portal hypertension. Chest radiograph or CT chest can reveal mediastinal masses or pulmonary infections. Whole-body PET/CT may help detect occult lymphoma or metastatic disease when initial workup is unrevealing.

# Distinguishing hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), acute leukemia, aplastic anemia and sepsis-associated cytopenias

Differentiating these entities is critical because they have divergent therapies and prognoses yet may present with overlapping features. Emphasis on key discriminating clinical and laboratory features, and on tests that provide decisive evidence, will guide urgent decisions.

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a hyperinflammatory syndrome characterized by excessive activation of macrophages and cytotoxic lymphocytes leading to cytokine storm, organ dysfunction, and cytopenias. Secondary HLH is commonly triggered by infection (notably EBV), malignancy (especially lymphoma), or rheumatologic disease. Diagnostic criteria commonly used are the HLH-2004 criteria: diagnosis requires either a molecular diagnosis consistent with HLH or meeting $5$ of the following $8$ criteria: fever, splenomegaly, cytopenias affecting $\ge 2$ lineages (hemoglobin $<9$ g/dL, platelets $<100 \times 10^9/L$, neutrophils $<1.0 \times 10^9/L$), hypertriglyceridemia (fasting triglycerides $>265$ mg/dL) and/or hypofibrinogenemia (fibrinogen $<150$ mg/dL), hemophagocytosis in bone marrow/spleen/lymph nodes, low or absent NK cell activity, ferritin $>500$ ng/mL (often markedly elevated, frequently $>10{,}000$ ng/mL), and elevated soluble CD25 (soluble IL-2 receptor). Features that point to HLH include very high ferritin, persistent fevers, rapidly progressive cytopenias, transaminitis, coagulopathy, and splenomegaly. Bone marrow may show hemophagocytosis, but its absence does not exclude HLH. Because clinical deterioration can be rapid, prompt recognition and early hematology/rheumatology consultation are essential.

Acute leukemia typically presents with marrow failure due to blast proliferation and replacement of normal hematopoietic elements. Clinical clues include new-onset cytopenias over days to weeks, the presence of circulating blasts on peripheral smear, bone pain, lymphadenopathy, and organomegaly (especially in acute monocytic leukemia). Diagnostic criteria for acute leukemia require $\ge 20\%$ blasts in bone marrow or peripheral blood (with some exceptions for defined cytogenetic abnormalities). Ancillary testing—bone marrow morphology, flow cytometry immunophenotyping, cytogenetics, and molecular tests—establish lineage and risk stratification. Coagulopathy (e.g., DIC) is classically associated with acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) and requires urgent recognition. Distinguishing leukemia from HLH can be challenging when both present with fevers and organomegaly; flow cytometry and blast quantitation are decisive.

Aplastic anemia is defined by pancytopenia in the setting of a hypocellular marrow devoid of malignant infiltration or fibrosis. Severity is graded by criteria such as Camitta’s: severe aplastic anemia is marrow cellularity $<25\%$ (or $<50\%$ with $<30\%$ residual hematopoietic cells) and at least two of the following: neutrophils $<500/\mu L$, platelets $<20 \times 10^9/L$, absolute reticulocyte count $<1\%$ (some definitions use absolute retic $<60{,}000/\mu L$). Patients often report gradual onset of fatigue, infections, and bleeding tendency. The peripheral smear typically lacks blasts and shows a paucity of leukocytes and platelets. Etiologies include idiopathic immune-mediated aplasia, drug- or toxin-induced injury, viral infections (hepatitis viruses, parvovirus B19), and inherited bone marrow failure syndromes. Bone marrow biopsy is required to confirm hypoplasia and to exclude infiltrative disease.

Sepsis-associated cytopenias arise from complex interplay of marrow suppression by inflammatory mediators, peripheral consumption, and coagulopathy (DIC). In severe infection, neutrophil function may be dysregulated and marrow reserves exhaust. Laboratory clues include elevated inflammatory markers, positive cultures or clear infectious source, concurrent organ dysfunction (renal, hepatic), and coagulation abnormalities (prolonged PT/INR, low fibrinogen, elevated D-dimer). Peripheral smear may show toxic granulation or left shift rather than blasts. Unlike aplastic anemia, the marrow in early sepsis may be cellular but functionally impaired; sometimes a leukoerythroblastic picture emerges in prolonged illness. Management priorities include treating the underlying infection and supporting cell lines; marrow-directed therapies are rarely immediately indicated.

Distinguishing features and practical testing priorities:
- Look first for peripheral blasts—if present, expedite marrow aspirate/flow cytometry because leukemia is likely and early treatment may be lifesaving.
- Very high ferritin and organ dysfunction with cytopenias should prompt consideration of HLH and ordering of triglycerides, fibrinogen, soluble CD25, and NK cell activity when available.
- Profound hypocellularity on marrow biopsy establishes aplastic anemia; evaluation for drug/toxin exposure and viral hepatitis is essential.
- Evidence of infection with DIC and consumptive coagulopathy points toward sepsis-associated cytopenias; obtaining cultures and early antimicrobial therapy is critical.

# Role and timing of hematology consultation and bone marrow biopsy

Hematology consultation should be obtained early in the evaluation of any unexplained pancytopenia, but certain clinical features mandate urgent involvement. Immediate hematology consultation is indicated when there are circulating blasts, very severe neutropenia (ANC $<500/\mu L$) especially with fever, evidence or high suspicion of acute leukemia (e.g., bone pain, rapidly progressive cytopenias), suspected HLH, or when a bone marrow biopsy is being considered emergently. In settings where specialized testing (flow cytometry, cytogenetics, targeted molecular panels) will influence urgent therapy, hematology should be involved from the outset.

Bone marrow aspirate and biopsy are often required to establish diagnosis. Timing depends on the clinical scenario. Urgent bone marrow examination is indicated if:
- Peripheral blasts are seen or acute leukemia is suspected clinically.
- HLH is suspected and marrow hemophagocytosis will aid diagnosis.
- Aplastic anemia is suspected and marrow hypocellularity must be confirmed before initiating disease-specific therapy.
- Pancytopenia is unexplained after initial workup (nutritional deficiencies, drug exposure, infection) or when marrow infiltration (lymphoma, metastatic disease) needs to be excluded.

In febrile neutropenia, bone marrow biopsy is usually deferred unless there is persistent unexplained cytopenia or other signs suggesting marrow pathology, because immediate priorities are infection control and supportive care. In stable patients, marrow biopsy can be scheduled promptly within 24–72 hours after initial labs, cultures, and imaging studies.

When a biopsy is performed, request a comprehensive panel: marrow aspirate for morphology and flow cytometry, trephine biopsy for cellularity and fibrosis, cytogenetics (karyotype), fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) for specific rearrangements when indicated, and molecular testing for mutations (e.g., FLT3, NPM1, TP53 in AML; various MDS-associated mutations). Tissue should also be sent for special stains and culture if infection is suspected.

Communication with hematopathology and microbiology ensures optimal use of limited samples. If peripheral blood flow cytometry suggests a clonal process, this should be communicated so marrow studies and subsequent therapy can be streamlined. In cases where urgent therapies (e.g., empiric high-dose steroids for HLH, or ATRA for suspected APL) may be lifesaving, hematology input guides risk–benefit decisions even before full diagnostic confirmation.

# Integration of clinical, laboratory and microbiologic data to prioritize management steps

Management of pancytopenia with multisystem involvement requires parallel processes: stabilize the patient, search for and treat reversible causes, and obtain tissue diagnosis when needed. Integration of data involves weighing immediacy (risk of bleeding, infection, organ failure) against diagnostic yield and potential therapy consequences.

Stepwise integration:
1. Triage and stabilize. Assess hemodynamic stability, active bleeding, and infection risk. If ANC $<500/\mu L$ with fever, treat as neutropenic fever while pursuing diagnosis. Transfusion thresholds commonly used are hemoglobin <7 g/dL for most patients or higher threshold ($<8$ g/dL) in cardiac disease; platelet transfusion prophylaxis is considered for platelet count $<10 \times 10^9/L$ in stable, nonbleeding patients and higher thresholds for active bleeding or procedures. Correct coagulopathy if there is severe bleeding or planned invasive sampling.

2. Rapid laboratory and microbiologic assessment. Obtain CBC with differential and smear, reticulocyte count, chemistries, LFTs, coagulation panel, blood cultures, and other cultures guided by clinical features. Order ferritin, triglycerides, and fibrinogen if HLH is in consideration. Early infectious testing (HIV, CMV, EBV, hepatitis, parvovirus PCR) may reveal treatable causes. A prompt peripheral smear review can change priorities immediately (e.g., circulating blasts prompt urgent hematology and marrow biopsy).

3. Use marrow response to guide mechanism. A low reticulocyte count and hypocellular marrow suggest hypoproliferation (aplastic anemia or marrow suppression), prompting a focus on exposures, viral etiologies, and consideration of immunosuppressive therapy. A high reticulocyte count suggests peripheral destruction, directing attention to hemolysis workup or immune causes. Elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin with low haptoglobin support hemolysis; Coombs positivity indicates immune-mediated hemolysis.

4. Consider urgent empiric interventions when indicated. In suspected sepsis, begin appropriate antimicrobials immediately after cultures. In severe coagulopathy or DIC, supportive measures to control bleeding are priorities while diagnostic evaluation proceeds. For suspected HLH with rapid clinical decline, initial immunomodulatory therapy may be considered in consultation with hematology and rheumatology while confirmatory tests are pending. For suspected APL, prompt initiation of ATRA can be lifesaving and is often started before cytogenetic confirmation when APL is strongly suspected; such decisions require hematology input.

5. Coordinate tissue diagnosis with clinical urgency. If marrow biopsy is required to secure a diagnosis that will change immediate therapy (e.g., leukemia vs aplasia vs infiltration), perform it without undue delay. If infection is likely the primary driver and the patient is unstable, start empiric therapy while arranging marrow studies once stabilized.

6. Reassess and refine diagnosis with evolving data. Integration is iterative: positive cultures, rising ferritin, or identification of blasts will redirect management. For example, an identified EBV-triggered HLH will prompt antiviral and immunomodulatory strategies, while a confirmed drug-induced aplasia mandates drug cessation and supportive care.

Clinical decision points to remember:
- ANC $<500/\mu L$ with fever mandates empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics and expedited infectious workup.
- Visible peripheral blasts or highly abnormal smear morphology require urgent hematology involvement and marrow exam.
- Very high ferritin (e.g., $>10{,}000$ ng/mL) in the appropriate context strongly suggests HLH and should trigger expedited confirmatory testing and hematology consultation.
- Bone marrow biopsy is essential to distinguish aplastic anemia (hypocellular marrow) from marrow infiltration (hypercellular or fibrotic with blasts) and should be obtained early when the diagnosis is unclear and will affect therapy.

Conclusion

Approach to pancytopenia with multisystem involvement demands a mechanistic framework, rapid targeted testing, and early coordination with hematology and infectious disease services. Distinguishing hypoproliferative states (aplastic anemia), marrow infiltration (leukemia, lymphoma, metastatic disease), peripheral destruction or consumption (immune cytopenias, DIC), and hypersplenism guides diagnostic priorities. Peripheral smear and reticulocyte count frequently offer decisive clues; bone marrow biopsy with comprehensive ancillary testing is often required to establish definitive diagnosis. Timely initiation of empiric supportive and infectious therapies while pursuing diagnostic confirmation can be lifesaving. Consistent communication among clinical teams, hematopathology, and microbiology maximizes diagnostic yield and tailors therapy to the underlying disorder.

Introduction

Immunomodulatory therapies — including conventional immunosuppressants, biologic agents targeting cytokines or complement pathways, and extracorporeal approaches such as therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) — are cornerstone treatments for many immune-mediated diseases. In clinical practice, however, these therapies are frequently needed in patients who have active infection or have recently completed treatment for infection. Immunomodulation can alter host defense, change infection presentation, and affect pathogen clearance. Conversely, delaying immunomodulatory therapy when it is clearly indicated may allow immune-mediated disease to progress, causing irreversible organ damage or life-threatening complications. Clinical reasoning in this context requires rigorous risk–benefit assessment, careful timing, coordinated infection management, vigilant monitoring, and clear communication with patients and the care team.

This chapter provides a framework for decision-making when immune-targeted therapies are being considered in the presence of active or recent infection. It focuses on the pathophysiologic interactions between immunomodulation and infection, practical criteria to guide timing and bridging strategies, principles of source control and antimicrobial stewardship that should accompany immunomodulation, monitoring and mitigation of infectious complications, documentation and informed consent essential for shared decision-making, and ethical and guideline-based lenses through which these decisions should be made.

# Principles of risk–benefit assessment in the presence of active infection

Risk–benefit assessment begins with a clear delineation of the clinical problem that prompts immunomodulation: the diagnosis, severity, trajectory, and the anticipated benefit from therapy. For immune-mediated diseases that are acutely life-threatening (e.g., fulminant autoimmune encephalitis, catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome, severe ANCA-associated vasculitis with rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis or pulmonary hemorrhage), the expected benefit of prompt immunomodulation may outweigh the incremental infection risk. In contrast, for conditions where disease progression is slower or where alternatives exist, postponing or de-escalating immune-targeted therapy until infection is better controlled may be preferable.

Risk assessment requires explicit enumeration of infection-related harms posed by the proposed therapy. Immunosuppression increases susceptibility to new infections and can permit reactivation of latent pathogens (e.g., tuberculosis, hepatitis B virus, herpesviruses). Complement inhibition (e.g., eculizumab) confers a particularly high risk for encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria meningitidis due to impaired terminal complement–mediated bactericidal activity. TPE can remove immunoglobulins, complement factors, and antimicrobial agents, transiently reducing host defense and modifying drug levels. Quantifying these risks depends on agent-specific mechanisms, dosing intensity (e.g., pulse glucocorticoids vs. low-dose prednisone), patient factors (age, comorbidities, prior infections, vaccination status), and pathogen-specific characteristics (virulence, resistance profile).

Benefit quantification entails estimating the probability of therapeutic success and the magnitude of clinical improvement, including avoidance of irreversible organ damage and mortality reduction. This requires familiarity with evidence from clinical trials and observational data about expected response rates and timelines. For many biologics and complement inhibitors, response may be rapid and durable; for traditional cytotoxic agents (cyclophosphamide, rituximab), benefit may accrue over weeks to months, but can be critical to prevent disease progression.

Integrating these elements involves individualized probability calculus. Useful decision frameworks include explicit risk stratification (low, moderate, high infection risk) and estimations of short-term versus long-term benefits. In many cases, one must consider a threshold rule: proceed if anticipated absolute benefit (e.g., survival or organ preservation probability) exceeds anticipated absolute harm from infectious complications, taking into account likelihood of preventing harm through mitigation strategies. For high-stakes scenarios, formal decision aids or multidisciplinary consensus may be helpful.

Importantly, the assessment must also consider dynamic factors: the expected trajectory of infection under current therapy (improving, stable, deteriorating), response to antimicrobials, and availability of source control. The decision must be revisited as new information emerges.

# Timing considerations: when to delay, bridge therapies and criteria for safe initiation

Timing is a central clinical lever. The decision to delay immunomodulatory therapy requires balancing risks of uncontrolled immune disease against the infection risk posed by immunosuppression. General principles include:

- When infection is uncontrolled, particularly if systemic (e.g., sepsis, uncontrolled bacteremia, disseminated fungal infection), delay of nonurgent immunosuppression until infection is controlled is usually indicated. Active uncontrolled infection often increases the risk of morbidity and mortality if immunosuppression is administered.

- When infection is localized and source control is feasible and rapidly achievable (e.g., drainage of abscess), short delay to achieve surgical or procedural source control may suffice before initiating immunomodulation.

- For infections that are being effectively treated with appropriate antimicrobials and show clinical and laboratory improvement (e.g., afebrile, downtrending inflammatory markers, sterilizing blood cultures), immunomodulatory therapy may be started sooner, particularly if immune disease is severe.

Specific criteria that often guide "safe" initiation include: hemodynamic stability, absence of ongoing bacteremia or fungemia (preferably negative blood cultures), decreasing inflammatory markers (e.g., C-reactive protein), clinical resolution or significant improvement (e.g., defervescence for 48–72 hours), completion of a minimal effective course of targeted antimicrobial therapy for certain pathogens, and secured source control.

Bridging strategies aim to temporize immune-mediated pathology while reducing infection risk. Approaches include:

- Use of lower-intensity immunosuppression (e.g., lower steroid dose, steroid-sparing regimens) to balance disease control with reduced infection risk.

- Short-term use of therapies with more rapid reversibility or shorter biologic activity. For example, high-dose intravenous corticosteroid pulses achieve rapid immunosuppression but effects wane; plasmapheresis provides rapid removal of pathogenic autoantibodies with transient immunomodulation and may be preferable when longer-term immunosuppression is unsafe.

- Employing nonimmunosuppressive supportive measures or symptomatic treatment to stabilize the patient while infection control is prioritized.

- Sequential therapy: initiating treatments with less impact on infection risk first, reserving more profound immunosuppression for later once infection is contained.

Timing also depends on the specific immune-targeted agent’s pharmacokinetics and mechanism. Agents with prolonged immune depletion (e.g., anti-CD20 therapies like rituximab) warrant more caution; delaying until infection has resolved or until protective antimicrobials/vaccinations are administered may be prudent. Conversely, short-acting agents or interventions (e.g., IVIG, TPE) can be used as temporizing measures in select cases.

Clinicians must also consider time-sensitive windows where delay is dangerous. For example, in severe ANCA-associated vasculitis with alveolar hemorrhage, even short delays can be catastrophic; in such scenarios, proceed with immunosuppression while intensifying infection surveillance and employing aggressive source control.

# Use of source control and antimicrobial stewardship in parallel with immunomodulation

Effective management requires simultaneous attention to source control and rational antimicrobial therapy. Source control is a cornerstone: removal or drainage of infected material (e.g., infected prosthetic devices, abscess drainage, debridement) often reduces pathogen burden and the need for prolonged or broad-spectrum antimicrobials, thereby mitigating infection risk when immunosuppression is initiated.

Clinical reasoning about source control includes assessing the feasibility, urgency, and expected impact on infection trajectory. Early involvement of surgical teams, interventional radiology, and infectious diseases is critical. When a foreign body is the nidus (e.g., central venous catheter), removal is usually indicated before starting intensive immunosuppression; in scenarios where device removal is risky, weighing risks and planning targeted antimicrobial coverage is essential.

Antimicrobial stewardship should be integrated into the decision pathway. Key stewardship principles include choosing targeted therapy based on microbiological data whenever possible, narrowing empiric broad-spectrum therapy once pathogens and susceptibilities are known, and optimizing dosing considering altered pharmacokinetics under critical illness or when extracorporeal therapies are used. TPE, for example, can remove antimicrobials with high plasma protein binding; clinicians should adjust dosing or timing relative to TPE sessions.

Prophylactic antimicrobials may be indicated to mitigate increased infection risk from specific immunomodulatory therapies. For instance, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is generally recommended for patients receiving prolonged high-dose corticosteroids or certain combination regimens. Complement inhibitors such as eculizumab require vaccination against encapsulated organisms and may warrant prophylactic antibiotics (e.g., penicillin) if vaccination cannot be completed prior to initiation. Reactivation prophylaxis for latent infections (e.g., hepatitis B with certain anti-CD20 agents, tuberculosis with TNF inhibitors) should be considered based on screening results.

Timing of vaccinations is an important stewardship and preventive step. Live vaccines are contraindicated in patients on significant immunosuppression; where possible, non-live vaccinations (e.g., pneumococcal, meningococcal, hepatitis B) should be administered prior to initiating therapy with anticipated immune compromise. When therapy cannot be delayed, passive immunity strategies (e.g., immunoglobulin) or post-exposure prophylaxis may be considered.

Finally, antimicrobial duration decisions should reflect the presence of foreign material, completeness of source control, immune status, and the planned immunosuppressive regimen. Inadequate duration or premature cessation in immunosuppressed patients can lead to relapse.

# Monitoring for infectious complications and mitigation strategies

Once immunomodulation is initiated in the setting of current or recent infection, structured monitoring is essential to detect infectious complications early and mitigate harm. Monitoring strategies should be risk-stratified, agent-specific, and incorporate both clinical and laboratory surveillance.

Clinical monitoring includes regular assessment for new or worsening fever, localized signs of infection (e.g., erythema, purulence), respiratory status, mental status changes, and hemodynamic parameters. For patients on agents with known specific risks (e.g., meningococcal disease with complement inhibitors, reactivation with anti-TNF), educate patients and caregivers on early warning signs that warrant immediate evaluation.

Laboratory surveillance typically includes baseline and periodic complete blood counts (CBC) with differential, liver and renal function tests, and inflammatory markers. Specific tests depend on the clinical context: serial blood cultures in patients with indwelling devices or persistent fever, quantitative PCR for viral reactivation (e.g., cytomegalovirus (CMV), BK virus) in transplant or profoundly immunosuppressed patients, and repeat serologies or antigen tests for certain pathogens. Reference ranges commonly used include CBC: hemoglobin 13.5–17.5 g/dL (males), 12.0–15.5 g/dL (females); absolute neutrophil count (ANC) normally 1500–8000 cells/µL; significant risk of bacterial infection increases when ANC < 500 cells/µL. Liver tests (AST, ALT) and creatinine changes may signal drug toxicity or infection-related organ dysfunction.

Imaging may be used proactively: chest radiographs or CT scans for respiratory symptoms, ultrasound or CT for suspected intra-abdominal infection, and MRI for suspected osteomyelitis or central nervous system involvement. When immunosuppression blunts typical inflammatory signs, an increased index of suspicion is necessary.

Mitigation strategies include antimicrobial prophylaxis as noted, vaccination when feasible, and preemptive therapy guided by surveillance (e.g., preemptive ganciclovir for CMV viremia in high-risk transplant recipients). Dose adjustments for antimicrobials or immunomodulators may be necessary in response to infections. For biologics with long half-lives, temporary cessation may not rapidly reduce infection risk; planning for such pharmacokinetic realities is important.

Additional considerations include infection control practices (hand hygiene, isolation where indicated), minimizing invasive devices, and avoiding unnecessary broad-spectrum antibiotics that predispose to opportunistic infections (e.g., Clostridioides difficile, Candida). In inpatient settings, standardized order sets that include monitoring schedules, prophylaxis checklists, and triggers for infectious diseases consultation help ensure consistent care.

# Documentation, informed consent and multidisciplinary communication

Transparent, thorough documentation and communication are indispensable when initiating immunomodulation in the context of infection. Documentation should capture:

- The clinical problem necessitating immunomodulation, the anticipated benefit, and the estimated risk of infection-related complications.

- The infectious disease assessment: organism(s) identified, susceptibility data, current antimicrobial regimen and duration, status of source control, and markers of infection control (e.g., negative blood cultures, defervescence).

- Rationale for proceeding with, delaying, or modifying immunomodulatory therapy, including alternative options considered and evidence or guidelines supporting the decision.

- Specific mitigation measures planned (vaccinations, prophylactic antimicrobials, monitoring schedule), and contingency plans if infection worsens.

Informed consent must be explicit about the increased infection risks associated with the proposed therapy, alternative strategies (including risks of withholding therapy), and the uncertainties involved. Patients (or surrogates) should be informed about typical timelines for benefits and risks, warning signs of infection, and the plan for surveillance and follow-up. Where possible, provide written materials and engage interpreters or cultural liaisons to ensure comprehension.

Multidisciplinary communication is essential. Early involvement of infectious diseases specialists, surgeons/interventionalists for source control, pharmacists for antimicrobial stewardship and pharmacokinetic considerations, and relevant subspecialists (e.g., nephrology for complement inhibitors in atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome) should be documented. Regular team huddles or formal case conferences improve shared situational awareness and support dynamic reassessment.

When high-risk or novel therapy is considered (e.g., initiating complement inhibition in a patient with recent Neisseria infection), involve institutional safety or ethics committees as needed. Ensure that vaccination status is communicated to primary care or outpatient teams responsible for longitudinal care. Finally, create clear written orders for monitoring and prophylaxis to avoid gaps during transitions of care (e.g., discharge planning).

# Ethical considerations and guideline-based decision making

Ethical decision-making in this domain requires balancing beneficence (providing effective therapy) and nonmaleficence (avoiding harm from infection), while respecting patient autonomy and promoting justice (fair resource allocation). Several ethical principles apply:

- Proportionality: the degree of risk imposed by immunomodulation should be proportionate to the expected benefit. In life-threatening immune disease with high probability of benefit, greater infection risk may be ethically justified.

- Precaution in uncertainty: where evidence is limited, a cautious approach favoring reversible or lower-risk interventions and active monitoring aligns with minimizing potential harm.

- Shared decision-making: patients should be engaged in decisions commensurate with the uncertainty and stakes. When patients lack decisional capacity, proxies should be informed and involved, with decisions made in the patient’s best interest.

- Equity: access to necessary vaccinations, prophylactic antimicrobials, and specialist consultations should not be limited by socioeconomic factors. Institutions should have protocols to minimize disparities.

Guideline-based decision-making helps standardize care and reduce bias. Several specialty societies provide recommendations relevant to specific agents or scenarios: for instance, meningococcal vaccination and consideration of prophylactic antibiotics in patients starting terminal complement inhibitors, screening for latent tuberculosis prior to anti-TNF therapy, and hepatitis B screening before anti-CD20 agents. In transplant medicine, detailed guidelines exist for prophylaxis and monitoring of opportunistic infections. Clinicians should be familiar with these and apply them while recognizing that guidelines may not cover every clinical nuance; clinical judgment remains necessary.

When guidelines conflict or are silent, multidisciplinary deliberation, ethics consultation, and transparent documentation are advisable. Institutions can support clinicians by developing local protocols that integrate national guidelines with pragmatic decision pathways for common scenarios.

Conclusion

Decisions to initiate immunomodulatory therapy in the setting of active or recent infection require a structured, patient-centered approach that integrates pathophysiology, agent-specific risks, infection control principles, and ethical considerations. A dynamic risk–benefit assessment, careful timing with bridging strategies when appropriate, rigorous source control and antimicrobial stewardship, proactive monitoring and mitigation, and clear documentation and communication form the backbone of safe practice. Familiarity with agent-specific recommendations and interdisciplinary collaboration improves outcomes and aligns care with both clinical evidence and patient values.
