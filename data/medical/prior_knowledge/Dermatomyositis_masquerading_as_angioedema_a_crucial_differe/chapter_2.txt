Introduction

Swallowing is a complex, highly coordinated sensorimotor function that safely transports food and liquid from the oral cavity to the stomach while protecting the airway. Disruption at any point along the swallowing process can produce dysphagia, with important consequences including aspiration, malnutrition, dehydration, and reduced quality of life. For the clinician evaluating a patient with dysphagia it is essential to understand the normal phases of swallowing, the muscles and neural circuits that generate and coordinate the act, the mechanisms by which aspiration and penetration occur, and how to distinguish oropharyngeal from esophageal disorders on clinical grounds. Instrumental studies—primarily the video fluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS or modified barium swallow, MBS) and fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES)—provide objective information about physiology and guide therapy. Management requires an individualized combination of compensatory strategies, rehabilitative exercises, diet modifications, and, when necessary, enteral feeding. This chapter reviews these topics in depth, emphasizing pathophysiology and clinical reasoning relevant to bedside and instrumental assessment.

# Phases of swallowing (oral preparatory, oral transit, pharyngeal, esophageal)

Swallowing is traditionally divided into four overlapping phases: oral preparatory, oral transit (oral propulsive), pharyngeal, and esophageal. Each phase has distinct goals, anatomy, and timing, and failure in any phase produces characteristic clinical signs.

Oral preparatory phase
The oral preparatory phase readies the bolus for safe transport. For solids this includes mastication, salivary mixing, and bolus formation; for liquids it involves lip closure, labial containment, and controlled bolus delivery. Major muscle groups include the muscles of mastication (masseter, temporalis, medial and lateral pterygoids), buccinator and orbicularis oris for lip and cheek control, and tongue intrinsic/extrinsic muscles for bolus manipulation. Sensory feedback from the oral cavity (trigeminal V and facial VII for taste and texture) informs timing of the next phase. Failure of labial or buccal control produces anterior or lateral leakage; impaired mastication leads to inadequate bolus breakdown; poor tongue control leads to premature spillage into the pharynx.

Oral transit (oral propulsive) phase
Oral transit begins when the tongue tip elevates against the anterior palate and the tongue base squeezes the bolus posteriorly. This phase is a voluntary action and typically lasts about 1 second for a single liquid bolus in healthy adults. Key muscles are the intrinsic and extrinsic tongue muscles (genioglossus, styloglossus, hyoglossus). Deficits produce prolonged oral transit time, leftover food in the oral cavity, and risk of premature spillage into the pharynx, especially with thin liquids.

Pharyngeal phase
The pharyngeal phase is a rapid, semi-automatic sequence that propels the bolus through the pharynx while protecting the lower airway. Triggering of the pharyngeal swallow occurs when the bolus reaches the level of the faucial pillars/valleculae; sensory input from glossopharyngeal (IX) and vagal (X) branches to the nucleus tractus solitarius initiates the pattern of motor output from the nucleus ambiguus and related centers. Key events include velopharyngeal closure to prevent nasal regurgitation, elevation and anterior movement of the hyoid and larynx, epiglottic inversion to protect the airway, adduction of the vocal folds and approximation of the aryepiglottic folds, pharyngeal constriction, and opening of the upper esophageal sphincter (UES). UES opening is accomplished by cricopharyngeal relaxation and traction from hyolaryngeal excursion. The pharyngeal phase is short—typically less than 1 second—but highly vulnerable; impairment produces coughing, choking, or silent aspiration.

Esophageal phase
After the bolus passes the UES, peristalsis in the tubular esophagus transports the bolus to the stomach and the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) relaxes transiently to allow passage. This phase is involuntary, mediated by vagal efferents and the myenteric plexus. Primary peristalsis follows the pharyngeal swallow; secondary peristalsis clears residual material. Disorders here present differently than pharyngeal problems and are typically perceived as a sensation of food “sticking” in the chest or retrosternal region.

# Muscles and neural control of swallowing

Swallowing is orchestrated by a network of cranial nerves, brainstem nuclei, cortical and subcortical centers, and a central pattern generator (CPG) that produces the coordinated motor sequence. Understanding the neural control helps localize lesions and anticipate physiologic consequences.

Peripheral muscles and their innervation
- Oral and facial muscles: lip closure (orbicularis oris, facial nerve VII), cheek tension (buccinator, VII), mastication (masseter, temporalis, pterygoids, mandibular branch of trigeminal V3). Tongue muscles are innervated by hypoglossal nerve (XII) for motor function (genioglossus, styloglossus, hyoglossus, intrinsic muscles); palatoglossus receives vagal innervation via pharyngeal plexus.
- Pharyngeal muscles: superior, middle, and inferior pharyngeal constrictors contract sequentially under glossopharyngeal (IX) sensory input and vagal (X) motor control via the nucleus ambiguus. Stylopharyngeus (IX) elevates the pharynx.
- Laryngeal muscles: intrinsic muscles controlling vocal fold adduction/abduction are innervated by branches of the vagus—recurrent laryngeal nerve for most intrinsic muscles and superior laryngeal nerve for cricothyroid. Cricopharyngeus (upper esophageal sphincter muscle) is innervated by vagal branches and pharyngeal plexus and acts as a tonic sphincter that relaxes during the swallow.
- Esophageal muscles: the proximal esophagus contains striated muscle under vagal and glossopharyngeal/brainstem control; the distal esophagus relies on smooth muscle and the enteric nervous system under autonomic modulation.

Central control
Sensory inputs from the oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, and esophagus travel via cranial nerves V, VII, IX, and X to the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) in the medulla. The NTS integrates afferent information and interacts with the adjacent nucleus ambiguus and reticular formation to form the brainstem CPG for swallowing. This CPG coordinates the timing of laryngeal closure, pharyngeal constriction, and UES opening. Descending cortical and subcortical pathways modulate initiation and timing of voluntary swallowing; cortical lesions (e.g., stroke) often produce oropharyngeal dysphagia by disrupting initiation and modulation, whereas brainstem lesions can disrupt the CPG and lead to more severe, often bilateral deficits. The cerebellum and basal ganglia also influence timing and coordination.

Clinical implications
Examining strength and symmetry of cranial nerve functions (facial expression, tongue protrusion, palatal elevation, gag and cough reflex) gives key clues about lesion location. For example, unilateral hypoglossal palsy causes tongue deviation toward the weak side and impaired bolus propulsion; recurrent laryngeal nerve injury leads to vocal fold paresis with impaired airway protection and increased aspiration risk. Central lesions can produce delayed swallow initiation and reduced hyolaryngeal elevation; peripheral nerve injuries produce more focal deficits.

# Mechanisms of aspiration and penetration

Understanding how material enters or approaches the airway clarifies why certain patients aspirate and guides targeted intervention. Two related terms are used in instrumental assessment: penetration (entry of material into the laryngeal vestibule above the vocal folds) and aspiration (entry of material below the level of the vocal folds into the trachea). Aspiration can be overt (with coughing) or silent (without effective reflex cough or sensory awareness), and its timing relative to the swallow influences cause and risk.

Timing-related mechanisms
- Premature spillage and pre-swallow aspiration: Poor oral control or improper bolus containment allows liquids to spill into the pharynx before the pharyngeal swallow is triggered. If the airway is not protected (larynx adducted) at that time, aspiration can occur before the swallow.
- Aspiration during the swallow: Inadequate laryngeal closure, incomplete vocal fold adduction, reduced epiglottic inversion, or reduced timing of hyolaryngeal elevation can allow material to enter the airway during the pharyngeal phase. Reduced pharyngeal constriction increases residue that may be propelled into the airway during subsequent inspiration.
- Post-swallow aspiration: Residue in the valleculae or pyriform sinuses can be aspirated after the swallow during subsequent breaths or with a delayed cough. In patients with poor cough reflex or reduced sensation, this residue is especially dangerous.

Pathophysiologic factors that increase aspiration risk
- Sensory deficits: Reduced laryngeal/pharyngeal sensation (e.g., neuropathy, stroke, head and neck radiation) diminishes cough reflex and awareness, producing silent aspiration. Laryngeal anesthesia studies show increased penetration/aspiration when mucosal sensation is absent.
- Motor deficits: Weakness or discoordination of tongue, pharyngeal constrictors, or laryngeal elevators reduces bolus propulsion and airway protection. Reduced hyolaryngeal excursion prevents adequate UES opening and airway closure.
- Structural lesions: Zenker diverticulum, pharyngeal strictures, or neck masses can trap material that then spills into the airway. Poor oral closure (lip incompetence) leads to anterior leakage and drooling, but aspiratory risk is mainly related to pharyngeal residue.
- Gastroesophageal reflux and laryngopharyngeal reflux can produce chronic inflammation, edema and reduced sensation, increasing risk of penetration and chronic aspiration.

Clinical significance
Aspiration is not synonymous with pneumonia, but it increases risk of pulmonary infection and chemical pneumonitis. Silent aspiration is of particular concern because patients may lack cough or subjective symptoms, requiring instrumental studies to detect. Interventions (postural changes, diet modification, exercises, airway protective maneuvers) are chosen to address the specific mechanism identified.

# Clinical features distinguishing oropharyngeal vs esophageal dysphagia

Differentiating oropharyngeal (transfer) dysphagia from esophageal dysphagia guides diagnostic testing and treatment. History and focused exam can usually distinguish the two.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia
Oropharyngeal dysphagia refers to difficulty initiating a swallow or transferring the bolus from the mouth into the esophagus. Typical clinical features include:
- Immediate coughing, choking, or throat clearing upon attempting to swallow, often with thin liquids.
- Nasal regurgitation of liquids during swallowing if velopharyngeal closure is impaired.
- Drooling or difficulty managing oral secretions.
- Hoarseness, wet or gurgly voice quality after swallowing, or recurrent aspiration pneumonia.
- Multiple swallows per mouthful, or need to swallow repeatedly to clear bolus.
- Timing: symptoms occur at the start of a swallow and are noted with mouth-to-throat transfer.
- Common etiologies: neurologic disorders (stroke, Parkinson disease, ALS), neuromuscular junction disorders (myasthenia gravis), structural lesions in the oropharynx, head and neck surgery or radiation.

Esophageal dysphagia
Esophageal dysphagia is a sensation of food sticking or stopping after the swallow, often localized to the chest or lower sternal area. Features include:
- Sensation of obstruction or food “stuck” in the throat/chest, often occurring seconds after the swallow.
- Difficulty with solids progressing to liquids suggests mechanical obstruction (e.g., peptic stricture, rings, malignancy), whereas dysphagia to both solids and liquids from onset suggests motor disorder (e.g., achalasia).
- Regurgitation of undigested food, especially if nocturnal or independent of swallowing, points to esophageal retention or motility disorder.
- Odynophagia (painful swallowing) suggests inflammatory or infectious esophagitis (e.g., pill esophagitis, candida).
- Timing: symptoms are delayed relative to swallow initiation and occur during the esophageal transit phase.
- Common etiologies: peptic stricture, eosinophilic esophagitis, Schatzki ring, esophageal carcinoma, achalasia, scleroderma-related motility disorders.

Clinical decision points
Key historical distinctions include whether coughing/choking occurs at or immediately after swallowing (favoring oropharyngeal) versus fullness/retention feeling in the chest later after swallowing (favoring esophageal). Presence of recurrent aspiration pneumonia, drooling, or an abnormal voice strongly suggests oropharyngeal dysfunction and should prompt instrumental assessment of safety of swallowing. If esophageal dysphagia is suspected, upper endoscopy and esophageal manometry are the primary diagnostic modalities.

# Instrumental assessments (video fluoroscopic swallow study / modified barium swallow, fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing)

Instrumental studies provide objective, physiologic evaluation of swallowing safety (aspiration risk) and efficiency (residue, bolus flow). The two most used are VFSS (MBS) and FEES; each has unique strengths and limitations.

Video fluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS / modified barium swallow)
VFSS is a dynamic radiographic study that visualizes the oral, pharyngeal, and esophageal phases using barium-contrasted boluses of varying consistencies and volumes. It is typically recorded in lateral and anterior–posterior views.

What VFSS shows:
- Oral bolus formation and oral transit time.
- Triggering and timing of the pharyngeal swallow.
- Hyolaryngeal excursion, epiglottic inversion, pharyngeal contraction, UES opening.
- Penetration/aspiration and its timing relative to the swallow.
- Pharyngeal residue in valleculae and pyriform sinuses.
- Proximal esophageal findings such as cricopharyngeal dysfunction, esophageal stasis, and gross motility abnormalities.

Advantages:
- Comprehensive view of all swallow phases.
- Allows observation of compensatory strategies (postural changes, maneuvers) and immediate assessment of their effectiveness.
- Quantitative measures can be derived (e.g., Penetration-Aspiration Scale).

Limitations:
- Exposure to ionizing radiation; study duration and number of swallows are limited.
- Requires patient transport to radiology and cooperation (sitting upright, following commands).
- Visualization is of radiopaque boluses only; secretions are not well visualized unless stained or contrasted.

Clinical indications and interpretation:
VFSS is the preferred initial instrumental test for assessing aspiration risk in patients with suspected oropharyngeal dysphagia when a comprehensive physiologic assessment is needed and when postural or dietary management decisions hinge on visualization of the pharyngeal swallow. Findings guide diet recommendations and therapeutic plans.

Fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES)
FEES uses a transnasal flexible endoscope positioned in the hypopharynx to visualize laryngeal and pharyngeal structures before and after the swallow. It is performed without radiation and can be performed at bedside.

What FEES shows:
- Anatomic structure and mucosal integrity of the nasopharynx, pharynx, larynx.
- Presence and amount of secretions pooling in the hypopharynx.
- Penetration and aspiration of dyed or colored boluses (usually food/liquid dyed blue or green).
- Pharyngeal residue, vocal fold mobility, and response to sensory stimulation.

Advantages:
- No radiation; can be repeated frequently and used for biofeedback during therapy.
- Performed at bedside and in nontransportable patients.
- Direct visualization of secretion management and laryngeal sensation testing (e.g., touch to arytenoids).

Limitations:
- Brief “white-out” period during the actual swallow due to epiglottic inversion, so aspiration occurring exactly at swallow peak may be missed; however, pre- and post-swallow observations usually allow inference.
- Does not visualize oral phase or esophageal transit; proximal esophageal findings may only be inferred.
- Requires nasal passage and patient tolerance.

Clinical indications and choice between tests
Choice depends on clinical question and patient factors. Use VFSS when detailed analysis of bolus flow, UES opening, and esophageal phase is needed or when comparing compensatory strategies’ effects on biomechanical events. Use FEES for bedside safety assessment, to evaluate secretion management, to observe laryngeal structures, in patients who cannot be transported or tolerate radiation, and for therapy biofeedback. Often both tests provide complementary information.

Additional instrumental modalities
- High-resolution manometry assesses esophageal motility and UES/LES function and is indicated when esophageal motility disorders (e.g., achalasia, spasm) are suspected.
- Scintigraphic swallow studies quantitatively measure aspiration of radiolabeled material and can evaluate chronic aspiration but are less practical for immediate clinical decision-making.
- Clinician-administered bedside swallow assessments and standardized screening tools (e.g., water swallow test, Gugging Swallowing Screen) are used for screening but cannot replace instrumental testing when aspiration risk or physiologic detail is required.

# Management principles (diet modification, enteral feeding options, swallowing rehabilitation)

Management of dysphagia aims to maintain nutrition and hydration, prevent aspiration and pulmonary complications, and—when possible—restore safer, more efficient swallowing. Approaches are individualized based on the physiologic diagnosis.

Diet modification and texture/consistency changes
Modifying bolus texture and volume is a common compensatory strategy. Thicker liquids reduce flow rate and pharyngeal spillage and may lower aspiration rates in some patients, whereas thin liquids are more difficult to control for those with impaired oral containment or weak pharyngeal squeeze. Texture modifications include pureed solids, mechanically soft diets, and graduated liquid consistencies (nectar-, honey-thick). Tools such as the International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative (IDDSI) provide standardized levels to describe consistencies. Clinicians should consider patient preferences and hydration/nutrition trade-offs, because thickened liquids may reduce fluid intake.

Postural adjustments and behavioral/postural maneuvers
Simple postural changes can modify bolus flow and airway protection. Chin tuck (neck flexion) may reduce aspiration for some patients by narrowing the airway entrance and reducing premature entry to the airway. Head rotation toward the weak side can close the pyriform sinus on that side and direct bolus down the stronger side. The supraglottic swallow and super-supraglottic swallow are voluntary airway-protective maneuvers that enlist conscious breath-holding and cough to protect the airway during swallow. The Mendelsohn maneuver increases and prolongs laryngeal elevation and UES opening by voluntary sustained elevation of the larynx during swallow. These strategies are most useful when demonstrated effective on instrumental testing.

Swallowing rehabilitation
Rehabilitative exercises aim to improve strength, range, timing, and coordination of swallowing musculature. Common approaches include:
- Tongue and lingual resistance exercises (e.g., pushing against a depressor or using devices) to improve oral pressure generation and bolus propulsion.
- Shaker exercise and chin tuck against resistance to strengthen suprahyoid muscles and improve UES opening.
- Effortful swallow to increase pharyngeal constriction pressure and bolus clearance.
- Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) applied to anterior neck musculature: data are mixed; some patients benefit when NMES is applied as adjunctive therapy, but it is not universally accepted as standard of care.
- Task-specific, progressive swallowing practice and neurorehabilitation principles apply—repetition, intensity, and specificity drive functional gains.

Pharmacologic and surgical interventions
Pharmacologic therapy is limited but includes agents to manage excessive secretions (anticholinergic agents or botulinum toxin for sialorrhea), prokinetics for gastroparesis contributing to reflux, and acid suppression when reflux-associated laryngeal inflammation contributes to dysphagia. Surgical options include cricopharyngeal myotomy for UES dysfunction, procedures to address Zenker diverticulum, or laryngeal medialization for unilateral vocal fold paralysis. Botulinum injection into the cricopharyngeus may be diagnostic and therapeutic for some UES hypertonicity.

Enteral feeding options and decision points
When oral intake is unsafe or insufficient, enteral feeding provides nutrition. Options include nasogastric (NG) tubes, nasojejunal tubes, percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG), and surgical gastrostomy/jejunostomy. Clinical decision points:
- Short-term feeding needs (expected duration < 2–4 weeks) often use NG tubes; longer-term feeding generally favors PEG because of patient comfort, reduced tube dislodgement, and lower likelihood of sinusitis or nasopharyngeal irritation.
- If aspiration of gastric contents or severe reflux is a concern, postpyloric feeding (nasojejunal or jejunostomy) may be preferable.
- Timing of enteral access: in critically ill patients early enteral nutrition (within 24–48 hours) is generally favored. For stroke patients, guidelines vary; if prolonged inability to swallow is anticipated (> 7–14 days), percutaneous feeding access is considered. Ethical and goals-of-care discussions about long-term feeding are essential, particularly in progressive or terminal neurologic diseases.
- Feeding route selection must consider patient prognosis, wishes, and quality-of-life implications.

Multidisciplinary care
Management often requires a team: speech-language pathologists for assessment and therapy, nutritionists for caloric and hydration needs, gastroenterologists/ENT surgeons for structural or esophageal causes, neurologists for central etiologies, and primary care/palliative care for goals-of-care and long-term planning.

# Complications of dysphagia (aspiration pneumonitis, malnutrition, airway compromise)

Dysphagia-related complications are common and can be serious. Recognizing and preventing them is a key clinical priority.

Aspiration pneumonitis vs. aspiration pneumonia
Two concepts are important:
- Aspiration pneumonitis refers to chemical injury from inhalation of sterile gastric contents (acid, digestive enzymes). It typically presents acutely after a witnessed aspiration event with inflammatory lung injury and may progress rapidly to acute lung injury or ARDS. Management is primarily supportive; antibiotics are not indicated unless there is superimposed infection.
- Aspiration pneumonia refers to infection resulting from inhalation of bacteria-laden oropharyngeal secretions and food particles. It often presents with fever, leukocytosis, focal infiltrates on chest imaging, and systemic signs of infection. Treatment requires appropriate antimicrobial therapy targeted to the clinical scenario and likely organisms.

Risk factors and prevention
Patients with impaired consciousness, poor oral hygiene, high-volume pharyngeal secretions, and reduced cough reflex are at high risk. Preventive measures include oral hygiene to reduce bacterial load, positional feeding strategies, swallowing therapy to reduce aspiration, and appropriate enteral access when safe oral intake cannot be maintained.

Malnutrition, dehydration, and metabolic consequences
Inadequate intake due to dysphagia leads to weight loss, protein-energy malnutrition, micronutrient deficiencies, and dehydration. Objective measures include unintended weight loss (e.g., >5% in 1 month or >10% over 6 months is clinically significant) and laboratory markers (e.g., low prealbumin or albumin—though these are affected by inflammation and not specific). Assessment should include weight trends, body mass index (BMI), oral intake records, and screening tools such as the Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST) or Subjective Global Assessment (SGA). Malnutrition increases morbidity, impairs wound healing, and complicates rehabilitation.

Airway compromise and respiratory failure
Bulky aspiration or severe edema of the larynx can cause acute airway obstruction. Recurrent large-volume aspirations may lead to chronic bronchiectasis or recurrent infections. Patients with progressive neuromuscular disease may have combined dysphagia and respiratory muscle weakness, increasing risk for respiratory failure and poor cough clearance.

Other complications
- Social and psychological effects include isolation, anxiety around eating, and reduced quality of life.
- Dental disease from poor oral hygiene and retained food fragments.
- For patients with feeding tubes, tube-related complications: aspiration of gastric contents despite feeding tube, tube dislodgement, local infection at PEG site, or bowel perforation in rare cases.

Monitoring and follow-up
Monitoring for complications involves regular reassessment of swallow safety via clinical and instrumental testing as indicated, nutritional surveillance (weight, intake, labs), pulmonary monitoring for signs of infection, and reassessment of goals of care. Interdisciplinary follow-up improves outcomes.

Conclusion

An understanding of the phases of swallowing, the muscles and neural systems that accomplish and coordinate the act, and the mechanisms that permit aspiration is essential for the assessment and management of dysphagia. Differentiating oropharyngeal from esophageal causes guides diagnostic strategy: instrumental studies such as VFSS and FEES provide complementary physiologic information that informs compensatory strategies, rehabilitative plans, and decisions about enteral feeding. Management is individualized, often multidisciplinary, and must balance aspiration risk, nutritional needs, patient preferences, and prognosis. Vigilance for complications—aspiration pneumonia/pneumonitis, malnutrition, and airway compromise—and proactive measures to prevent them are central to care.
