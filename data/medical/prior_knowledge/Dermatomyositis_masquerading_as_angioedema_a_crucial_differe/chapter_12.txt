Introduction

Neck swelling is a common clinical presentation with a broad differential that ranges from minor self-limited conditions to life-threatening infections and airway emergencies. Deep neck space infections (DNSIs) and Ludwig's angina are paradigmatic examples in which rapid recognition of pathogenesis, early airway assessment, appropriate antimicrobial therapy, and timely surgical intervention can be lifesaving. This chapter reviews the etiology and pathophysiology of DNSIs, highlights the clinical features and red flags of Ludwig's angina and retropharyngeal infections, summarizes the microbiology and sepsis considerations, provides guidance on imaging and indications for surgical drainage, explains antimicrobial and airway-management principles, and outlines the clinical approach to distinguishing infectious from non-infectious causes of neck swelling.

# Etiology and pathogenesis of deep neck space infections (odontogenic, tonsillar, salivary)

Deep neck space infections arise when microbial invasion occurs within the potential spaces and fascial planes of the neck. The neck contains three layers of deep cervical fascia—superficial (investing), middle (pretracheal), and deep (prevertebral)—which compartmentalize the neck into clinically relevant spaces. Key spaces include the submandibular (further divided into sublingual and submylohyoid compartments, relevant to Ludwig's angina), parapharyngeal, retropharyngeal, peritonsillar, and the so-called "danger space" between the alar and prevertebral fascia extending to the posterior mediastinum. Infection in one anatomical compartment tends to follow predictable routes along these fascial planes and may extend contiguously to adjacent spaces, the mediastinum, or thorax.

Odontogenic sources are the most common cause of DNSIs. Dental caries and periapical abscesses—especially infections of the mandibular molars—can breach cortical bone and spread into the submandibular or sublingual spaces depending on the relationship between the mylohyoid muscle and the tooth roots. Infection below the mylohyoid muscle tends to spread into the submandibular space; above it, into the sublingual space. Bilateral extension across the midline predisposes to Ludwig's angina, a potentially fulminant cellulitis of the bilateral submandibular spaces.

Tonsillar and peritonsillar infections are the second common source of DNSIs. Peritonsillar abscesses (quinsy) can extend to the parapharyngeal space, then to the retropharyngeal space. Lymphadenitis from upper respiratory infections may also seed adjacent deep spaces. Salivary gland infections—sialadenitis from stasis (e.g., dehydration), stone obstruction, or ascending infection—can cause local cellulitis and abscess formation in the submandibular or parotid regions and may progress to the parapharyngeal space.

Pathophysiologically, DNSIs progress through stages: mucosal breach or local infection → cellulitis and phlegmon where soft tissues are inflamed and indurated → localized abscess when liquefaction and pus formation occur. Factors that increase risk for aggressive or atypical spread include immunosuppression (e.g., diabetes mellitus, malignancy, chronic corticosteroid use), delayed treatment, virulent organisms, and anatomical variants. The presence of gas-forming organisms or extension into the "danger space" can allow rapid descent into the posterior mediastinum, causing descending necrotizing mediastinitis with high mortality if not recognized early.

# Clinical signs and red flags of Ludwig's angina and retropharyngeal infections

Ludwig's angina describes a rapidly progressive, bilateral cellulitis of the submandibular, sublingual, and submental spaces that classically arises from infected mandibular molars. The hallmark clinical presentation is a firm, "wooden" swelling of the floor of the mouth and bilateral submandibular areas that is often painful. Because the sublingual and submandibular spaces are contiguous, tissue edema elevates the tongue, compromising the oropharyngeal airway. Typical findings include submandibular fullness, elevation of the tongue, drooling, difficulty handling secretions, muffled "hot potato" voice, trismus (limited mouth opening), neck stiffness, and sometimes odynophagia or dysphagia. Importantly, skin erythema may be minimal early because the infection is deep and subcutaneous tissue is tightly bound; thus the absence of marked erythema does not exclude severe disease. Key red flags for imminent airway compromise are stridor (high-pitched inspiratory sound), progressive dyspnea, inability to lie supine, progressive hypoxia, difficulty handling secretions, and rapidly progressive swelling over hours. Any of these signs warrants immediate airway evaluation by experienced personnel.

Retropharyngeal infections typically present with sore throat, fever, neck pain, dysphagia, odynophagia, and often neck stiffness. In children, torticollis is common because retropharyngeal lymph nodes are larger and can become inflamed, leading to head tilt. Odynophagia and drooling may point to oropharyngeal or posterior pharyngeal involvement. Red flags for retropharyngeal abscess include muffled voice, stridor, respiratory distress, signs of sepsis (hypotension, tachycardia, altered mental status), and signs of mediastinal involvement such as chest pain or shortness of breath. Because the retropharyngeal space communicates with the posterior mediastinum via the danger space, extension to the mediastinum with pleural effusion or empyema is a feared complication.

Both Ludwig's and retropharyngeal infections may progress rapidly and unpredictably; therefore, a high index of suspicion and frequent reassessment—especially of airway patency—are essential. Examination should include assessment of vital signs, oxygenation, oral cavity and oropharynx inspection (tongue position, floor of mouth), palpation of cervical lymph nodes, evaluation of trismus, and observation for drooling, voice changes, or respiratory compromise.

# Microbiology, sepsis considerations and indications for imaging and surgical drainage

Microbiology
Deep neck infections are typically polymicrobial, reflecting oral flora. Common aerobic organisms include viridans group streptococci (Streptococcus anginosus/milleri group), Streptococcus pyogenes, Staphylococcus aureus (including methicillin-resistant strains in selected populations), and various gram-negative bacilli. Anaerobes are important and frequently dominant contributors: Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Fusobacterium, and Bacteroides species are common. Odontogenic infections are particularly enriched for anaerobes. In severe or hospital-acquired cases, Pseudomonas and other gram-negatives may be encountered. Culture specimens obtained during drainage (aerobic and anaerobic cultures) guide targeted therapy; blood cultures should be obtained in cases with systemic signs or sepsis.

Sepsis considerations
Patients with DNSIs can develop systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) or sepsis. Early recognition of sepsis and prompt resuscitation are critical. Clinical tools such as qSOFA (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment: altered mental status, systolic blood pressure ≤100 mmHg, respiratory rate ≥22/min) can help identify patients at higher risk of poor outcomes; a qSOFA score ≥2 is associated with increased mortality and mandates prompt evaluation. Serum lactate is an important marker of tissue hypoperfusion: normal lactate is typically 0.5–2.2 mmol/L; lactate >2 mmol/L indicates increased risk and >4 mmol/L suggests severe sepsis/septic shock though interpretation must include the clinical context. Basic labs include complete blood count (normal WBC 4–11 ×10^9/L), electrolytes, renal and liver function, CRP and ESR (markers of inflammation), and blood cultures prior to antibiotic administration when feasible.

Indications for imaging
Imaging is instrumental for diagnosis, delineation of the anatomic extent, and surgical planning. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the neck is the modality of choice in most adults because it delineates soft-tissue planes, loculated collections, gas formation, and extension to the mediastinum. CT with intravenous contrast is preferred to distinguish enhancing inflamed tissues from non-enhancing fluid collections (abscesses). In children or when radiation exposure is a concern, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can provide excellent soft-tissue contrast but has limited availability and requires longer acquisition times; MRI is particularly useful when evaluating for vascular complications or suspected osteomyelitis. Ultrasound is useful for superficial collections and for guiding aspiration, especially in the anterior triangle or superficial submandibular collections, but is operator-dependent and limited for deep or parapharyngeal/retropharyngeal spaces. Plain lateral neck radiographs may show prevertebral soft tissue swelling in retropharyngeal infections; as a guide, prevertebral soft tissue thickness should be small in adults (at C2 usually <7 mm, at C6 <20 mm), though radiographs are insensitive and should not replace CT when there is clinical concern.

Indications for surgical drainage
Not all DNSIs require immediate incision and drainage; early infections may be managed with antibiotics and close observation. However, specific indications for surgical intervention include: radiographic evidence of a well-formed abscess (discrete fluid collection with rim enhancement on CT), clinical fluctuance or a palpable collection amenable to drainage, airway compromise or progressive symptoms despite adequate antibiotics, loculated collections or extension to deep spaces such as parapharyngeal or retropharyngeal spaces, large size (often >2–3 cm in one dimension or causing mass effect), and presence of gas-forming organisms or descending mediastinitis. Failure to improve after 24–48 hours of appropriate antimicrobial therapy also suggests need for drainage. Surgical approaches are dictated by the space involved: transoral drainage for some peritonsillar or superior retropharyngeal collections, transcervical external approaches for parapharyngeal or lateral collections, and combined cervical and thoracic surgery when descending mediastinitis is present. Cultures obtained at drainage help tailor antibiotics.

# Antimicrobial therapy choices and airway management strategies

Antimicrobial therapy
Empiric antimicrobial regimens must cover streptococci, staphylococci, and anaerobes originating from the oral cavity. Choice of regimen is influenced by severity, local resistance patterns, patient drug allergies, and risk factors for MRSA or gram-negative pathogens.

- For many community-acquired DNSIs in non-severely ill patients, an appropriate empiric parenteral regimen is ampicillin-sulbactam (e.g., 1.5–3 g IV every 6–8 hours) because it covers common gram-positive cocci and anaerobes. An alternative is ertapenem (1 g IV daily) in selected cases when broader anaerobic coverage is desirable and Pseudomonas coverage is not needed.

- For severe infections or hospital-acquired cases where broader gram-negative coverage is required, piperacillin-tazobactam (e.g., 3.375–4.5 g IV every 6–8 hours) provides extended coverage. If Pseudomonas is a concern, anti-pseudomonal beta-lactams should be selected.

- If MRSA risk is present (recent hospitalization, IV drug use, known colonization), add vancomycin with dosing guided by troughs or AUC monitoring; alternatives for MRSA coverage include linezolid (oral/IV) depending on the clinical scenario.

- For patients with immediate severe beta-lactam allergy, clindamycin (600–900 mg IV every 8 hours) provides gram-positive and anaerobic coverage but increasing clindamycin resistance among gram-negative organisms must be considered; combination with a fluoroquinolone may sometimes be used to cover gram-negative rods, but fluoroquinolones have adverse-effect considerations.

Duration of therapy is individualized. For infections treated with drainage and clinical improvement, 10–14 days of total therapy is common, sometimes transitioning to oral agents once improvement is clear. Complicated infections (mediastinitis, osteomyelitis) often require longer courses (4–6 weeks).

Airway management strategies
Airway compromise is the most feared and immediate threat with Ludwig’s angina and retropharyngeal abscesses. Early airway planning with involvement of experts (anesthesiology, otolaryngology/head and neck surgery, and intensive care) is crucial. The key principle is to secure the airway electively under controlled conditions before complete obstruction occurs; emergency cricothyrotomy or tracheostomy performed in a fully obstructed airway carries higher complication rates.

Preferred strategies include awake fiberoptic intubation with the patient spontaneously ventilating when feasible. Awake intubation allows maintenance of airway tone and spontaneous respiration, decreasing the risk of complete obstruction during induction. Nasal fiberoptic approaches may be contraindicated if there is significant nasal or base-of-skull infection. In selected cases where oral access is limited by trismus or tongue swelling, a surgical airway (awake tracheostomy) performed by an experienced surgeon may be the safest option. In extreme, rapidly deteriorating situations where neither intubation nor tracheostomy is immediately possible, emergent cricothyrotomy may be life-saving.

Decision points include assessing the trajectory of swelling, the presence of stridor or hypoxia, inability to protect airway or handle secretions, and the feasibility of controlled airway techniques—if these favor imminent failure, early definitive airway control is indicated. Postoperative airway care is also important: many patients require admission to an intensive care unit for close monitoring, and extubation should be approached cautiously, often after edema has decreased and airway patency is confirmed.

# Distinguishing infectious swelling from non-infectious causes

Not all neck swellings are infectious, and differentiating infectious from non-infectious etiologies is essential for appropriate management. Key clinical elements that favor infection include acute onset over hours to days, systemic signs (fever, chills), localized tenderness, erythema, warmth, fluctuance, purulent drainage, and association with a plausible source (recent dental infection, upper respiratory infection, salivary gland obstruction). Laboratory markers of inflammation—leukocytosis, elevated CRP and ESR—support an inflammatory/infectious process but are nonspecific.

Non-infectious causes include congenital cysts (branchial cleft cyst, thyroglossal duct cyst), neoplasms (lymphoma, metastatic squamous cell carcinoma, salivary gland tumors), benign tumors (lipoma), autoimmune or inflammatory disorders (sarcoidosis, IgG4-related disease), endocrine swelling (goiter, thyroid nodules), vascular lesions (carotid body tumors, aneurysms), and angioedema. Neoplastic lymphadenopathy often has a more subacute to chronic course, firm and non-tender nodes, lack of overlying skin inflammation, fixation to underlying structures, constitutional symptoms such as unintentional weight loss, and risk factors such as smoking or a primary mucosal lesion. Congenital cysts typically present earlier in life or as chronic intermittent swellings that may become secondarily infected; a midline, mobile swelling that moves with swallowing or tongue protrusion suggests a thyroglossal duct cyst. Angioedema is usually rapid in onset, non-pitting, non-erythematous swelling often involving the lips, face, or oropharynx, and is usually not tender or fluctuant, often with a history of exposure to an allergen or ACE inhibitor use.

Imaging and, when needed, tissue diagnosis are essential in differentiating causes. Ultrasound can characterize cystic versus solid masses and guide fine-needle aspiration (FNA) of lymph nodes or masses. CT or MRI provide structural details, evaluate invasion, and guide biopsy or surgery. For persistent or suspicious lymphadenopathy, FNA cytology or excisional biopsy is indicated—particularly if nodes are >2 cm, persist beyond 2–4 weeks despite therapy, are progressively enlarging, or are firm and fixed.

Clinical reasoning integrates the timeline, risk factors, examination, and imaging. For example, rapidly progressive, painful, tender swelling with systemic signs and a dental source is most consistent with an odontogenic abscess or Ludwig’s angina and requires urgent airway and infection management. A slowly enlarging, firm, non-tender neck mass without systemic features prompts consideration of neoplasm and expedited imaging with tissue diagnosis rather than empiric antibiotics alone.

Conclusion

Understanding the anatomy of deep neck spaces, typical microbial etiologies, and the clinical hallmarks of severe infections such as Ludwig's angina and retropharyngeal abscess is essential for early recognition, prompt airway assessment, and initiation of appropriate medical and surgical therapy. Decisions about imaging, drainage, and airway intervention should be driven by the anatomic extent, clinical severity, and trajectory of disease, with multidisciplinary involvement. Differentiating infectious from non-infectious causes relies on careful history, examination, targeted imaging, and tissue diagnosis when needed. Early, decisive action in DNSIs can prevent catastrophic complications such as airway obstruction, descending mediastinitis, and septic shock.
