Introduction

Immunosuppressive therapies—whether prescribed for autoimmune disease, organ transplantation, hematologic malignancy, or other indications—substantially increase the risk of infection from reactivation of latent pathogens and from new exposures. Safe initiation and maintenance of these therapies therefore requires systematic pre-treatment screening for latent and chronic infections, targeted prophylactic strategies against opportunistic pathogens, and ongoing monitoring for drug toxicities and vaccine responsiveness. This chapter reviews the evidence-based approach to infection screening (with emphasis on tuberculosis and viral serologies), the role of viral serologies such as varicella-zoster virus (VZV) and cytomegalovirus (CMV) in clinical decision-making, indications and options for prophylaxis against opportunistic infections including Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP), laboratory monitoring protocols for immunosuppressive drugs (including thiopurine methyltransferase testing prior to azathioprine), and practical vaccination guidance before and during immunosuppression. The focus is clinical: how to interpret tests, when to act, and how to integrate these processes into patient care.

# Screening for latent tuberculosis (IGRA/TST) and HBV/HCV/HIV serologies

Latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI)
Reactivation of latent Mycobacterium tuberculosis poses substantial risk in patients receiving many forms of immunosuppression (especially tumor necrosis factor inhibitors, high-dose corticosteroids, certain biologics and small molecules). Two principal tests are used to screen for LTBI: the tuberculin skin test (TST, Mantoux) and interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs; e.g., QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus, T-SPOT.TB).

TST places purified protein derivative intradermally and is read at 48–72 hours as the transverse diameter of induration. Interpretation depends on risk: thresholds commonly used are ≥5 mm (high-risk, e.g., recent contact with TB, immunosuppressed), ≥10 mm (moderate risk), and ≥15 mm (no known risk factors). TST has limitations: false-positives with prior BCG vaccination or exposure to non-tuberculous mycobacteria, and false-negatives in immunosuppressed patients due to anergy.

IGRAs measure interferon-gamma release by T cells in response to TB-specific antigens absent from BCG strains (ESAT-6, CFP-10), making them more specific in BCG-vaccinated persons and less affected by non-tuberculous mycobacteria. IGRAs require blood draw and controlled laboratory processing; indeterminate results can occur with lymphopenia or immunosuppression.

Choice of test: For most adults, an IGRA is the preferred initial test when available, particularly in BCG-vaccinated individuals or those with prior TST. In persons from low-resource settings or when IGRA is not available, TST remains acceptable. For patients with high pre-test probability or immunosuppression, consider performing both tests if initial results are discordant or if clinical suspicion remains high.

Action on positive tests: A positive TST or IGRA suggests LTBI, and further evaluation with chest radiography (posterior-anterior and lateral as indicated) is required to exclude active pulmonary TB before starting LTBI therapy. If chest imaging or symptoms suggest active disease, obtain sputum microscopy, nucleic acid amplification testing, and culture; delay or modify immunosuppression until active disease is treated or judged unlikely. If active TB is excluded, initiate LTBI treatment per local guidelines (e.g., isoniazid daily for 6–9 months with pyridoxine, or shorter rifamycin-based regimens), and coordinate timing relative to immunosuppression: where feasible, complete at least a portion of LTBI therapy (or start LTBI therapy) prior to initiating high-risk immunosuppressives such as anti-TNF agents.

HBV, HCV, and HIV serologies
Chronic viral hepatitis and HIV can flare or progress under immunosuppression, and their presence influences choice of therapy and need for antiviral prophylaxis. Baseline testing is mandatory in most practices prior to moderate or high-intensity immunosuppression.

Hepatitis B virus (HBV): Key baseline tests are hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), total anti–hepatitis B core antibody (anti-HBc total), and hepatitis B surface antibody (anti-HBs). Interpretation:
- HBsAg positive indicates active infection (chronic if >6 months). These patients are at risk of HBV reactivation with immunosuppression and generally require antiviral prophylaxis (entecavir, tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, or tenofovir alafenamide) rather than reactive treatment only.
- Anti-HBc positive with HBsAg negative indicates resolved infection (occult infection possible). The risk of reactivation varies by immunosuppressive regimen; many such patients require either prophylactic antivirals or close monitoring (HBV DNA and ALT testing), especially if receiving B-cell–depleting agents (rituximab) or high-risk regimens.
- Anti-HBs positive with anti-HBc negative indicates immunity from vaccination.

Pre-treatment HBV DNA quantification is recommended if HBsAg positive or if anti-HBc positive and immunosuppression is planned. In general, for HBsAg positive patients starting moderate-to-high risk immunosuppression, begin antiviral prophylaxis before or at the start of immunosuppressive therapy and continue for at least 6–12 months after cessation of immunosuppression (longer for B-cell–depleting agents; some recommend 12–18 months).

Hepatitis C virus (HCV): Screen with anti-HCV antibody followed by HCV RNA if antibody positive. Chronic HCV infection can progress during immunosuppression and also poses considerations for hepatotoxicity and drug–drug interactions (direct-acting antivirals and immunosuppressants). Many centers seek to evaluate and treat HCV prior to elective immunosuppression when feasible, given high cure rates with direct-acting antivirals.

HIV: Baseline HIV antigen/antibody testing (combined p24 antigen/anti-HIV antibody assay) is essential because undiagnosed HIV confers altered infection risks, different vaccination recommendations, and drug-interaction considerations. If positive, quantify HIV RNA and CD4 count and initiate HIV care. Some forms of immunosuppression are contraindicated or require tailoring in uncontrolled HIV.

Integration into workflow: Standard pre-immunosuppression panels should include HBsAg, anti-HBc, anti-HBs, HCV antibody (and HCV RNA if positive), and HIV Ag/Ab. Document vaccination history and prior TB testing. Positive or indeterminate results should prompt consultation with infectious disease or hepatology when appropriate and tailored prophylaxis plans.

# Viral serologies (VZV, CMV) and implications for management

Varicella-zoster virus (VZV)
VZV serostatus (VZV IgG) prior to immunosuppression distinguishes those susceptible to primary varicella from those with prior immunity. Primary varicella (chickenpox) in immunocompromised hosts can be severe, with disseminated disease, pneumonia, and high mortality. VZV IgG positive patients are generally considered immune; IgM is typically not used for baseline screening.

If a patient lacks VZV IgG (seronegative), vaccination should be considered before immunosuppression, but timing and vaccine choice require attention. The older live-attenuated varicella vaccine is contraindicated during significant immunosuppression and should be administered well in advance (generally at least 4 weeks) of planned immunosuppression. For adults, varicella vaccination policy varies by region and patient age.

Regarding zoster prevention, two vaccines are relevant: the live-attenuated zoster vaccine (Zostavax) and the recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV; e.g., Shingrix). Live zoster vaccine is contraindicated in immunocompromised individuals. RZV is a non-live, adjuvanted subunit vaccine and is preferred for immunocompromised adults when available; many guidelines now recommend RZV for adults who are or will be immunocompromised, although the timing relative to initiating immunosuppression should be individualized. For example, when possible give RZV at least 2 weeks before starting moderate immunosuppression (some experts prefer 2–4 weeks) and complete the two-dose series as feasible. If immunosuppression is already established, RZV may still be given in many patients, but efficacy can be lower; consult guidance specific to the immunosuppressive agent and patient condition.

Cytomegalovirus (CMV)
CMV serostatus (anti-CMV IgG) is particularly important in transplantation (solid-organ and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation), where donor and recipient serostatus (D+/R−, D+/R+, D−/R+, D−/R−) defines risk of CMV disease and informs preemptive surveillance or universal prophylaxis strategies. In nontransplant immunosuppressed patients (e.g., those on biologics), CMV reactivation is less commonly a primary concern but can occur in profoundly immunocompromised hosts.

If the recipient is CMV IgG negative and the donor is positive (D+/R−), risk of primary CMV infection is high and prophylactic antivirals (valganciclovir) or intensive preemptive monitoring are often employed. In R+ recipients, risk is lower but reactivation can occur with intensive immunosuppression; many centers adopt preemptive strategies using regular quantitative CMV PCR to detect early viremia and initiate therapy before tissue-invasive disease.

Implications for management: For both VZV and CMV, serologies guide (1) whether vaccination is feasible and indicated prior to immunosuppression, (2) need for antiviral prophylaxis (CMV in transplant), and (3) the intensity of surveillance during immunosuppression (e.g., serial CMV PCR monitoring). When considering antiviral prophylaxis, also account for antiviral toxicities, drug–drug interactions, and renal dosing.

# Prophylaxis for opportunistic infections (Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis options and indications)

General principles
Opportunistic infections (OIs) cause high morbidity and mortality in immunosuppressed patients. OI prophylaxis aims to prevent infections for which risk is predictable on the basis of the degree and type of immunosuppression. Decisions are individualized by the underlying disease, intensity and duration of immunosuppressive therapy, comorbidities, and concomitant therapies.

Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis
PJP is a prototypical prophylaxis target. Indications for PJP prophylaxis vary by guideline but converge around similar principles: patients with impaired cell-mediated immunity are at highest risk. Common triggers for prophylaxis include:

- High-dose systemic corticosteroids: many guidelines recommend prophylaxis for patients receiving prednisone (or equivalent) ≥20 mg/day for ≥4 weeks. Risk is modulated by concomitant immunosuppressives.
- Hematologic malignancy, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, or solid organ transplantation: prophylaxis is standard in many regimens.
- Certain biologic agents: for example, patients on prolonged or combination regimens including agents like rituximab, or multiple immunosuppressives, may require prophylaxis. Patients receiving calcineurin inhibitors plus mycophenolate with additional risk factors often require PJP prophylaxis.
- HIV: PJP prophylaxis when CD4 <200 cells/μL; this is a well-established, evidence-based threshold in HIV care (CD4 count threshold for starting prophylaxis is <200 cells/μL).

First-line regimen: Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP–SMX)
TMP–SMX is the most effective and preferred agent for PJP prophylaxis.

Dosing options:
- Single-strength tablet (TMP 80 mg/SMX 400 mg) orally once daily.
- Double-strength tablet (TMP 160 mg/SMX 800 mg) orally three times weekly (e.g., Monday-Wednesday-Friday).
- Alternative dosing regimens (e.g., double-strength once daily) are sometimes used.

TMP–SMX also provides protection against other organisms (Toxoplasma gondii) in some settings. Adverse effects to monitor include hypersensitivity (rashes, Stevens–Johnson syndrome—rare), cytopenias (neutropenia), renal dysfunction (interstitial nephritis), and hyperkalemia. In patients with sulfa allergy or intolerance, desensitization can be considered if clinically important and feasible.

Alternative regimens
When TMP–SMX is contraindicated or not tolerated, alternatives include:

- Dapsone (100 mg orally daily): requires normal glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) activity (risk of hemolysis in deficiency) and may cause methemoglobinemia.
- Atovaquone (1500 mg orally daily with fat-containing meal): well tolerated but expensive and less well studied.
- Aerosolized pentamidine (300 mg via nebulization monthly): effective for preventing pulmonary PJP but provides less extrapulmonary protection and has disadvantages including bronchospasm risk and logistical complexity.
Choice among alternatives depends on patient comorbidities (e.g., G6PD status), ability to tolerate oral agents, and cost/availability.

Duration of prophylaxis
Duration depends on the ongoing level of immunosuppression and recovery of immune function. In many non-HIV settings, prophylaxis continues while immunosuppression is at risk-producing levels and for a period after tapering; for corticosteroids, continue while prednisone ≥20 mg/day and for at least 4 weeks after tapering below threshold, with some clinicians extending longer if other risk factors exist. In transplant and hematologic settings, duration is protocol-driven (e.g., at least 6–12 months post-transplant, longer with ongoing high-risk therapy). For HIV, prophylaxis is discontinued when CD4 count rises above 200 cells/μL for at least 3 months on antiretroviral therapy.

Other opportunistic prophylaxis
- Toxoplasma gondii: TMP–SMX prophylaxis generally covers Toxoplasma in susceptible patients (e.g., HIV with CD4 <100 cells/μL and seropositive).
- Herpesvirus prophylaxis (HSV/VZV): Acyclovir/valacyclovir prophylaxis is used in selected high-risk groups (e.g., some transplant recipients, patients receiving alemtuzumab) to prevent severe herpesvirus disease.
- Fungal prophylaxis: Fluconazole or mold-active agents are employed in specific high-risk hematologic or transplant populations (e.g., neutropenia, GVHD).
- Mycobacterial prophylaxis: In certain regimens (e.g., TNF inhibitors in TB-endemic regions), isoniazid for LTBI is prophylaxis for TB reactivation.

Clinical decision points
Decisions regarding OI prophylaxis require balancing risks and benefits: assess the absolute risk of the infection without prophylaxis, drug toxicities and interactions, and patient preferences. Many centers have protocolized criteria for PJP and CMV prophylaxis; when in doubt, consult infectious disease specialists.

# Monitoring protocols for drug toxicities (CBC, LFTs, renal function, TPMT testing for azathioprine)

Rationale and general approach
Immunosuppressive agents have predictable organ-specific toxicities and hematologic effects. Baseline testing and scheduled monitoring detect adverse effects early, allow dose adjustment, and reduce morbidity. Monitoring frequency depends on the drug, patient comorbidities, and phase of therapy (induction vs maintenance).

Common baseline tests
Before initiating many immunosuppressants, obtain:
- Complete blood count (CBC) with differential (baseline cytopenias place patients at higher risk for severe myelosuppression).
- Liver function tests (LFTs): AST, ALT, alkaline phosphatase, total bilirubin.
- Renal function: serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).
- Blood glucose and lipids if starting agents that affect metabolic parameters (e.g., corticosteroids).
- Specific drug-related baseline tests as indicated (e.g., chest radiograph for TB screening, hepatitis and HIV serologies as above).

Monitoring by drug class/examples

Azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine
Azathioprine (AZA) is a thiopurine prodrug converted to 6-mercaptopurine and active thioguanine nucleotides. Myelosuppression is the major toxicity; hepatotoxicity also occurs.

Thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) testing: Before starting AZA or 6-mercaptopurine, screen for TPMT enzyme activity (phenotype) or TPMT genotype (common variant alleles). TPMT deficiency is associated with high levels of active thioguanine nucleotides and severe, potentially life-threatening myelosuppression. TPMT test results guide dosing:
- Normal TPMT activity: start standard doses (e.g., azathioprine 1–2 mg/kg/day) with routine monitoring.
- Intermediate activity: begin at reduced dose (e.g., 30–70% of standard) with close CBC monitoring.
- Absent activity: avoid standard thiopurines or use dramatically reduced dosing with extreme caution; consideration of alternative agents is common.

NUDT15 genotyping: NUDT15 variants are particularly relevant in people of Asian or Hispanic ancestry and confer risk of thiopurine-induced leukopenia; many centers incorporate NUDT15 testing alongside TPMT.

Ongoing monitoring: CBC and LFTs should be checked frequently after initiation (e.g., every 1–2 weeks for the first month or two, then monthly for several months, then every 3 months if stable). Any unexplained fever, sore throat, or bruising prompts immediate CBC.

Methotrexate
Methotrexate can cause hepatotoxicity, cytopenias, and pulmonary toxicity. Baseline CBC, AST/ALT, serum creatinine are required. Folic acid supplementation reduces some toxicities. Monitoring typically includes CBC, LFTs, and creatinine every 1–2 months early in therapy, extending to every 1–3 months once stable. Evaluate for pulmonary symptoms; obtain chest imaging and consider pulmonary specialist input for suspected methotrexate pneumonitis.

Mycophenolate mofetil
Mycophenolate predominantly causes gastrointestinal side effects and bone marrow suppression. Baseline CBC and LFTs, and periodic CBC monitoring (monthly initially, then every 1–3 months), with dose reduction for cytopenias.

Calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine, tacrolimus)
These agents are nephrotoxic and can cause hypertension, neurotoxicity, and metabolic disturbance. Baseline renal function and blood pressure should be documented. Therapeutic drug monitoring (trough levels) guides dosing and prevents toxicity. Check renal function frequently (e.g., weekly to monthly) and monitor electrolytes and blood pressure.

mTOR inhibitors (sirolimus, everolimus)
mTOR inhibitors cause hyperlipidemia, delayed wound healing, cytopenias, and proteinuria. Monitor CBC, lipid profile, LFTs, and renal function; therapeutic drug monitoring for trough levels is standard in transplant settings.

Biologic agents (e.g., anti-TNF, rituximab)
In addition to infection screening, monitor for cytopenias and infusion reactions. Rituximab (anti-CD20) can cause late-onset neutropenia and hypogammaglobulinemia; monitor CBC and immunoglobulin levels (IgG) periodically, and consider immunoglobulin replacement for recurrent infections with low IgG. Check for HBV reactivation risk in anti-CD20 recipients; prolonged antiviral prophylaxis may be required.

Corticosteroids
Systemic corticosteroids cause hyperglycemia, hypertension, osteoporosis, adrenal suppression, and increased infection risk. Monitor blood pressure, glucose, weight, and counsel on bone protection (calcium, vitamin D, bisphosphonates as indicated). Tapering strategies are important to mitigate adrenal insufficiency.

Frequency and thresholds
Monitoring frequency should be most intensive during the initial weeks to months of therapy, when adverse events are most likely to appear. Trigger values prompting dose reduction or discontinuation vary by drug; examples:
- Neutrophils <1.0 x 10^9/L or an absolute neutrophil count fall >50% from baseline often prompt holding the drug and rechecking urgently.
- Transaminases >3 times the upper limit of normal (ULN) for many drugs warrant dose reduction or discontinuation and evaluation for alternative causes (viral hepatitis, alcohol, etc.).
- Rise in serum creatinine or decline in eGFR beyond established thresholds for nephrotoxic agents triggers dose adjustments or drug substitution.

Documentation and patient education
Provide patients with written guidance about which symptoms warrant urgent evaluation (fever, signs of infection, easy bruising/bleeding, yellowing of skin, new cough or dyspnea). Ensure coordination between prescribers and primary care for monitoring labs and immunizations.

# Vaccination recommendations before and during immunosuppression

Principles of vaccination in immunosuppressed patients
Vaccination reduces the risk of vaccine-preventable infections that can be severe in immunocompromised hosts. However, live-attenuated vaccines are generally contraindicated during moderate-to-severe immunosuppression because of the risk of vaccine-associated disease. Inactivated vaccines are safe but immunogenicity may be reduced; therefore, when possible, complete vaccination series early—ideally before immunosuppression—to maximize immune response.

Timing relative to immunosuppression
- Prefer to administer vaccines at least 2 weeks before starting immunosuppression for inactivated vaccines; some experts recommend 2–4 weeks for sufficient antibody development.
- Live vaccines (e.g., measles-mumps-rubella, varicella, live zoster) should generally be given at least 4 weeks (often recommended 4 weeks, sometimes 3–4 weeks) before initiating immunosuppressive therapy.
- After stopping immunosuppression, timing of when live vaccines can be given varies by agent and immune reconstitution. For many biologics and conventional immunosuppressants, waiting at least 3 months after cessation is common, but for B-cell–depleting agents (e.g., rituximab) immune recovery may take 6–12 months; specialist guidance is recommended.

Specific vaccine recommendations (generalized; adapt to local guidelines)

Influenza
- Annual inactivated influenza vaccine is recommended for all immunosuppressed patients (including those with chronic disease or on immunosuppression). It can be administered at any time during the influenza season and, when possible, before exposure to influenza season or immunosuppression.
- Live-attenuated influenza vaccine (intranasal) is contraindicated in immunocompromised persons and household contacts should receive inactivated vaccine when possible.

Pneumococcal vaccines
- Two types: pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13/PCV15/PCV20 depending on availability) and pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23). For immunocompromised adults, many guidelines recommend PCV followed by PPSV23 at a designated interval (commonly 8 weeks to 1 year depending on sequence and vaccines used). Check up-to-date local recommendations for sequencing and timing.
- Immunocompromised patients have higher risk of invasive pneumococcal disease and derive substantial benefit from vaccination.

Hepatitis B
- Vaccinate susceptible patients (anti-HBs negative) before immunosuppression when possible. Higher or additional vaccine doses may be needed to achieve seroprotection in immunosuppressed hosts; post-vaccination anti-HBs testing can guide need for revaccination or booster.

Human papillomavirus (HPV)
- Recommend HPV vaccination for eligible age groups (often up to 26 years; some guidelines extend to 45 years on shared clinical decision-making), including those who may become or are immunocompromised. Non-live vaccine and safe in immunosuppressed patients.

Varicella and zoster vaccines
- For varicella-susceptible patients (VZV IgG negative), varicella vaccination should be administered before planned immunosuppression. This is a live vaccine and is contraindicated during immunosuppression.
- For herpes zoster prevention, the recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV; non-live) is preferred for immunocompromised adults when permitted by regulatory guidance; it should ideally be given before immunosuppression if possible. Live zoster vaccine is contraindicated once immunosuppression is present.

Meningococcal and Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib)
- Vaccinate persons as indicated by underlying conditions or planned therapy (e.g., complement inhibitor therapy or asplenia). Immunocompromised patients and those on certain complement inhibitors require meningococcal vaccination prior to therapy initiation.

Other considerations
- Household contacts: Encourage up-to-date inactivated vaccinations for household members (e.g., inactivated influenza) to reduce exposure risk. Live vaccines for household contacts generally do not contraindicate contact (e.g., MMR or varicella vaccines), but specific precautions may apply in some scenarios (e.g., vaccine strain shedding for oral polio vaccine; note oral polio vaccine is rarely used in many countries).
- Post-vaccination serologic testing: For vaccines where serologic correlates are available and critical (e.g., hepatitis B), checking post-vaccine titers may be useful to confirm protection in immunocompromised patients.
- Reduced vaccine efficacy: Immunosuppression can blunt vaccine responses. When feasible, vaccinate before starting therapy. Even partial protection may reduce disease severity.

Clinical decision-making and documentation
Vaccination planning should be an explicit part of pre-immunosuppression counseling and workflow. Document vaccine history, serologic results, planned timing, and rationale for deferring live vaccines. Coordinate with primary care, infectious disease, and transplant teams to ensure appropriate immunization.

Conclusion

Preventing and managing infections in patients who require immunosuppression is a multifaceted endeavor. Adequate pre-treatment screening (for TB and viral infections such as HBV, HCV, HIV, VZV, and CMV) informs risk stratification and prophylactic plans. Targeted prophylaxis—most importantly for Pneumocystis jirovecii—should be applied when expected benefit exceeds risks, with TMP–SMX as first-line and rational alternatives available. Robust monitoring protocols (CBC, LFTs, renal function, TPMT/NUDT15 testing before thiopurine therapy) detect toxicities early and guide dose modification. Vaccination is a critical preventive measure: inactivated vaccines should be given whenever possible before immunosuppression, and live vaccines should be administered only when safe and timed appropriately. Integrating these practices into routine pre-treatment workflows and maintaining communication among specialists, primary care, and patients optimizes safety and outcomes for immunosuppressed individuals.
