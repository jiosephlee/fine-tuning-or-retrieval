# Introduction

Muscle biopsy remains a cornerstone diagnostic tool in the evaluation of suspected myopathies. When interpreted in the context of clinical presentation, serum studies, electrodiagnostic testing, and imaging, histopathology can identify distinct patterns that direct diagnosis, prognostication, and therapy. This chapter reviews the indications for and techniques of muscle biopsy, then examines the characteristic histologic and immunohistochemical findings in dermatomyositis and other major inflammatory myopathies (immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy and inclusion body myositis). It closes with common interpretive pitfalls and how to integrate biopsy findings with clinical and imaging data to reach a defensible diagnosis.

# Indications and techniques for muscle biopsy (open vs needle, site selection)

Indications for muscle biopsy include unexplained proximal or distal weakness when noninvasive tests are inconclusive, suspected inflammatory myopathy that may require histology to differentiate subtypes, suspected genetic myopathy when molecular diagnosis is not available or when histology will guide targeted genetic testing, and atypical presentations such as rapidly progressive weakness or suspected vasculitis. Biopsy is also indicated when electrodiagnostic testing suggests a myopathic process but cannot discriminate primary muscle disease from denervation or metabolic/toxic causes. Prior to biopsy, clinicians should ensure appropriate pretest workup: serum muscle enzymes (creatine kinase, aldolase), myositis-specific and myositis-associated antibodies, EMG, and muscle MRI when available; these direct site selection and improve yield.

Two principal sampling techniques are used: open (surgical) biopsy and percutaneous needle biopsy. Open biopsy is typically performed in the operating room under local or general anesthesia. It allows retrieval of a larger specimen including ample muscle tissue, subcutaneous fat, and fascia—useful when histology requires multiple special stains, electron microscopy, or when sampling for frozen and fixed sections is needed. Percutaneous needle biopsy is less invasive, can be done under local anesthesia, and has lower morbidity; modern suction‑assisted needle systems (e.g., Bergström or vacuum‑assisted needles) yield adequate tissue in many settings. The choice depends on local expertise, suspected diagnosis, and whether extensive tissue is required. For example, suspected mitochondrial disorders sometimes require larger samples and simultaneous nerve or skin samples; open biopsy may be preferable. Conversely, when risk of general anesthesia is high or the clinical picture is straightforward, needle biopsy is an acceptable alternative.

Site selection is critical to avoid false negatives. Biopsy should target a muscle that is clinically weak but not end-stage (severely atrophic or fibrotic) and that has abnormal findings on imaging or EMG. Muscles with mild-to-moderate weakness are most likely to show active pathology. MRI with fluid-sensitive sequences (STIR, T2 fat-sat) is extremely useful to localize areas of edema/inflammation and to visualize patchy disease patterns typical of inflammatory myopathies; inflammation on MRI correlates with increased diagnostic yield. EMG helps by identifying myopathic motor unit potentials or fibrillation potentials that localize affected muscles, but EMG sampling is less specific than MRI for focal selection. Avoid muscles that have recently undergone injection or procedures, and avoid muscles with significant fatty replacement or chronic denervation changes because they may yield nonspecific end-stage changes. Commonly biopsied muscles include vastus lateralis, biceps brachii, deltoid, and tibialis anterior—choose based on distribution of weakness and imaging.

Specimen handling is as important as the biopsy technique. Immediate processing is required for most special stains and immunohistochemistry. A portion should be snap‑frozen in isopentane cooled in liquid nitrogen for cryosectioning and enzyme histochemistry; another portion can be fixed in formalin for routine histology and immunostains. Electron microscopy, when indicated, requires glutaraldehyde fixation of a fresh fragment. Transport media, temperature control, and communication with the receiving pathology lab are essential to prevent artifactual changes (e.g., freeze‑thaw damage). Proper labeling of proximal versus distal ends and of orientation facilitates interpretation of fiber grouping or fiber type distribution.

# Histopathologic features of dermatomyositis (perifascicular atrophy, perimysial inflammation, capillary loss)

Dermatomyositis (DM) shows a reproducible and distinctive pattern on histopathology reflecting a primary microvascular and perimysial process that secondarily injures muscle fibers. The classic and most characteristic lesion is perifascicular atrophy: groups of small, angular, and atrophic fibers preferentially located at the periphery of muscle fascicles. These perifascicular fibers often show rounded atrophic contours and loss of type I/type II pattern uniformity on fiber‑type–specific stains. The term “perifascicular atrophy” refers to the topographic distribution rather than a pure atrophic mechanism; it reflects ischemic or inflammatory-mediated injury to fiber populations at the fascicle border.

The inflammatory infiltrate in DM tends to be perivascular and perimysial rather than endomysial. Lymphocytes accumulate in the perimysium and around small blood vessels; histologically these are predominantly CD4+ T cells and B cells, often accompanied by plasmacytoid dendritic cells. Inflammatory cells may be sparse in chronic cases or after immunotherapy, and some biopsies show minimal inflammatory infiltrate despite clinical DM—hence the importance of recognizing pattern changes such as perifascicular atrophy even in paucicellular specimens.

A key vascular feature of DM is capillary loss and endothelial injury, which can be appreciated on morphologic stains and by immunohistochemistry for vascular markers. Capillary drop‑out is most pronounced in perifascicular regions and is accompanied by basement membrane thickening and perivascular deposition of complement components. On a morphologic level, endomysial capillary rarefaction leads to relative ischemia of perifascicular fibers, contributing to their atrophy. On electron microscopy, endothelial cell swelling and tubuloreticular inclusions in endothelial cells and intracytoplasmic interferon signatures may be observed, reflecting the type I interferon–driven pathogenesis of DM.

Other secondary features include myofiber necrosis and regeneration; necrotic fibers are often scattered and may be relatively minor compared with the degree of weakness. Chronic DM can progress to fibrosis and fatty replacement with loss of distinct perifascicular architecture. Small vessel vasculopathy may be seen in juvenile DM and in severe adult disease and can produce ischemic myofiber changes and subcutaneous fat infarcts in cutaneous tissues.

# Complement and immunohistochemistry findings (C5b-9 deposition, MHC-I expression)

Immunohistochemical studies provide both diagnostic clues and mechanistic insight into inflammatory myopathies. In dermatomyositis, deposition of the membrane attack complex (MAC; C5b-9) on the endomysial capillaries and on the endothelial cell surface is a reproducible finding. This complement activation is believed to be antibody‑mediated or triggered by immune complexes and is thought to be causal in capillary injury and the resultant ischemic pattern of perifascicular atrophy. Detection of sarcolemmal versus vascular C5b‑9 deposition is important: in classic DM, C5b‑9 is concentrated on capillary walls and less often on individual muscle fiber membranes. Techniques for detecting MAC include immunofluorescence or immunoperoxidase staining for C5b‑9 components.

Major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC‑I) antigen upregulation on muscle fibers is another hallmark across inflammatory myopathies. Normally MHC‑I expression on skeletal muscle fibers is absent or minimal; in inflammatory myopathies MHC‑I is diffusely upregulated on the sarcolemma and in the sarcoplasm. In dermatomyositis, MHC‑I expression is often seen but may be patchy and accentuated in perifascicular fibers. MHC‑I upregulation is associated with antigen presentation to CD8+ T cells and is thought to participate in immune–mediated myofiber injury; however, the pattern and distribution differ among myopathy subtypes and can help in discrimination.

Other immunohistochemical markers useful in DM include markers of plasmacytoid dendritic cells (such as BDCA‑2) and myxovirus resistance protein A (MxA), which is induced by type I interferons and is considered a sensitive marker of DM-related interferon activity. MxA staining in muscle fibers and perifascicular regions supports a DM diagnosis and correlates with the interferon signature found in blood and tissue. Endothelial markers (CD31, CD34) and basement membrane markers (collagen IV) may be used to quantify capillary loss and to evaluate vascular integrity.

It is essential to interpret immunostains in the correct context, because complement deposition and MHC‑I upregulation can be secondary phenomena in other myopathies or even in noninflammatory conditions after muscle injury. The pattern—vascular versus sarcolemmal, perifascicular concentration, and concordance with light microscopic features—guides diagnostic weighting.

# Findings in immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and inclusion body myositis

Immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) and inclusion body myositis (IBM) are two inflammatory myopathies with distinct histologic signatures that must be distinguished from dermatomyositis and from one another.

Immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy is characterized histologically by myofiber necrosis with myophagocytosis and regeneration, often with relatively sparse inflammatory infiltrates. Necrotic fibers are frequently widespread and can be clustered; regeneration with basophilic fibers and central nuclei is common. The inflammatory cells that are present tend to be macrophage‑predominant and located endophlebitically or scattered within endomysium rather than forming dense lymphocytic foci. IMNM is associated with specific autoantibodies—anti‑signal recognition particle (anti‑SRP) and anti‑3‑hydroxy‑3‑methylglutaryl‑coenzyme A reductase (anti‑HMGCR)—and with exposures such as statin therapy in anti‑HMGCR positive cases. Immunohistochemistry may show sarcolemmal MHC‑I upregulation and deposition of complement (C5b‑9) on the sarcolemma of necrotic fibers. Unlike DM, IMNM does not typically show perifascicular atrophy or prominent perivascular inflammation. The absence of prominent endomysial lymphocytic invasion helps differentiate IMNM from polymyositis or active IBM.

Inclusion body myositis has a mixed myopathic and neurogenic appearance and is most common in older adults. Histologically, IBM shows endomysial inflammation composed of CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes invading nonnecrotic muscle fibers—so called "endomyseal infiltrates with invasion"—but this inflammatory feature alone is not specific. A defining characteristic of IBM is the presence of rimmed vacuoles within muscle fibers: single or multiple clear vacuoles rimmed by basophilic granular material and immune-reactive proteins. These vacuoles represent autophagic vacuoles and are often accompanied by deposits of protein aggregates containing amyloid precursor protein, phosphorylated tau, ubiquitin, p62/sequestosome-1, and TDP‑43. Congo red or Thioflavin S staining may demonstrate congophilic or fluorescent inclusions; immunostaining for p62 and TDP-43 is commonly positive and used diagnostically. On electron microscopy, characteristic 15–18 nm tubulofilamentous inclusions are described within affected fibers.

IBM commonly shows prominent fiber size variability, scattered necrosis and regeneration, and mitochondrial abnormalities in some fibers manifesting as cytochrome oxidase (COX)–negative fibers and ragged‑blue fibers on special stains indicating mitochondrial dysfunction. MHC‑I is diffusely upregulated on muscle fibers in IBM. Clinically and histologically, IBM is slowly progressive, often asymmetric, and has an early involvement of finger flexors and knee extensors, which guides selection of biopsy site (e.g., flexor digitorum profundus or tibialis anterior in suspected IBM) to maximize diagnostic yield.

# Interpretation pitfalls and correlation with clinical/imaging data

Interpreting muscle biopsy requires awareness of numerous potential pitfalls that can lead to misdiagnosis if histology is considered in isolation. Sampling error is a major concern: focal or patchy diseases (e.g., focal myositis, early inflammatory myopathies, or ischemic lesions) may be missed if the biopsy is taken from an uninvolved area. Prior corticosteroid or immunosuppressive therapy can reduce inflammatory infiltrates and alter patterns such as capillary deposition, leading to false‑negative biopsies. Therefore, when possible, biopsy is ideally performed before initiation of high-dose immunosuppression; if not feasible, the treating team should document therapy and interpret minimal infiltrates cautiously.

Artifact from specimen handling can mimic disease. Freeze‑thaw artifact produces vacuolar change and fiber fragmentation that may be misread as necrosis. Delayed freezing or inappropriate transport can degrade enzyme histochemical patterns used for fiber typing or mitochondrial assessment. Overstretching or surgical trauma can generate fiber damage and inflammatory responses. Communication with the pathology laboratory about the clinical question and intended studies (frozen sections, enzyme histochemistry, immunostains, electron microscopy) prevents loss of diagnostic opportunities.

Several disease processes show overlapping features: for example, perifascicular atrophy can be seen in non-DM contexts such as vasculitis, severe ischemia, or in some paraneoplastic syndromes; endomysial inflammation with necrosis can be observed in dystrophinopathies and other muscular dystrophies undergoing inflammation. Similarly, MHC‑I upregulation is not pathognomonic of immune myopathies and can be encountered in toxic, metabolic, or genetic myopathies as a nonspecific injury response. Therefore, combining histologic pattern recognition with clinical data is essential.

Clinical correlation begins with the distribution and timing of weakness, age at onset, associated systemic features (cutaneous findings in DM, interstitial lung disease, dysphagia), and exposure history (statins, toxins, infections). Serum CK elevation is a useful adjunct: normal CK does not rule out inflammatory myopathy (for example, IBM can show normal or mildly elevated CK), whereas very high CK (often several thousand U/L) is typical of IMNM and other necrotizing processes. Reference ranges vary by laboratory, but a common adult CK reference is approximately 20–200 U/L; clinicians must use lab‑specific normals. Myositis‑specific autoantibodies (anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑MDA5, anti‑NXP2, anti‑TIF1γ, anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR) strongly guide interpretation: a positive anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP in the setting of necrotizing myopathy supports IMNM; myositis‑specific antibodies associated with dermatomyositis may support a DM interpretation even when inflammatory infiltrates are minimal.

Electrodiagnostic testing and MRI further refine biopsy interpretation. EMG demonstrating myopathic motor unit potentials and fibrillation potentials indicates active myofiber membrane instability and supports an inflammatory or necrotic process, increasing the chance that biopsy will show diagnostic changes. Muscle MRI identifies areas of edema (T2/STIR hyperintensity) that represent active inflammation and should guide biopsy location; MRI also shows chronic fatty replacement on T1 sequences that suggests end‑stage disease where biopsy is less likely to be diagnostic. In IBM, MRI patterns of selective muscle involvement (e.g., quadriceps, flexor digitorum profundus) correlate with histologic findings and guide targeted biopsy.

Finally, classification criteria and a multidisciplinary approach are important. The 2017 EULAR/ACR classification criteria for idiopathic inflammatory myopathies integrate clinical features, muscle biopsy findings, and serology to yield probabilistic classification; within this framework, histologic features weigh heavily but are not sole determinants. Multidisciplinary case review involving neurologists, rheumatologists, pathologists, and radiologists improves diagnostic accuracy, particularly in atypical or therapy‑exposed cases.

# Summary

Muscle biopsy is a high‑value diagnostic procedure whose yield depends on appropriate indications, meticulous technique, targeted site selection informed by EMG and MRI, and careful specimen handling. Dermatomyositis is characterized by perifascicular atrophy, perimysial/perivascular inflammation, capillary loss, and complement deposition, with immunohistochemical markers such as C5b‑9 and MxA reflecting key pathogenic pathways. Immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy shows prominent necrosis with sparse inflammation and is associated with anti‑SRP and anti‑HMGCR antibodies, while inclusion body myositis features endomysial CD8+ invasion, rimmed vacuoles, and protein aggregates. Awareness of confounding factors—sampling error, prior treatment, artifacts, and overlapping histologic patterns—and integration of clinical, serologic, electrophysiologic, and imaging data are essential to reach a reliable diagnosis and to guide appropriate treatment.
