Introduction

Neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness are common and potentially serious clinical presentations that span multiple organ systems. A practical, evidence-informed approach integrates a focused history, directed physical examination, targeted laboratory testing, and appropriate imaging to separate infectious, allergic/angioedema, inflammatory/myopathic, neoplastic, and structural causes. The clinician must rapidly identify life‑threatening complications—particularly impending airway compromise and systemic sepsis—while also assembling a differential diagnosis that accounts for autoimmune myopathies, medication toxicities, metabolic myopathies, and local neck processes (eg, cellulitis, lymphadenitis, abscess, hematoma). This chapter provides a structured framework for history-taking, focused examination, stepwise laboratory and imaging use, criteria for specialty consultation, and risk stratification for urgent airway intervention.

# History-taking priorities: temporal course, systemic symptoms, medication exposures, preceding infections

A high-yield history begins with defining the temporal pattern and tempo of both the neck swelling and the muscle weakness. Temporal characteristics help narrow the differential: rapid onset (hours to days) favors allergic reactions/angioedema, hemorrhage/hematoma, acute infectious processes (eg, bacterial neck infections, parapharyngeal or retropharyngeal abscess), or iatrogenic etiologies. Subacute to chronic onset (days to weeks to months) suggests inflammatory myopathies, chronic infections (tuberculosis, atypical mycobacteria), lymphoproliferative disease, thyroid disease, or chronic medication effects.

Document the precise chronology: which symptom appeared first (swelling or weakness), whether they evolved together or independently, and whether the weakness is progressive, fluctuating, or relapsing. Proximal weakness that evolves over days to weeks with difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs, or lifting objects points to an inflammatory myopathy (eg, polymyositis, dermatomyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy) or medication/toxin-related myopathy (eg, statin-associated, corticosteroid myopathy). A more acute fluctuating weakness with diurnal variation suggests a neuromuscular junction disorder such as myasthenia gravis; bulbar features may dominate in myasthenia and can present with neck and facial weakness.

Systemic symptoms: fever, night sweats, weight loss, anorexia, fatigue, and rigors support infectious or inflammatory etiologies. Fever accompanying tender, erythematous neck swelling favors bacterial cellulitis or abscess; fever with generalized myalgias and elevated creatine kinase (CK) may represent viral myositis. Constitutional B symptoms (weight loss, drenching night sweats) raise suspicion for malignancy or lymphoma. Raynaud phenomenon, photosensitivity, and skin rash point toward connective tissue disease—eg, dermatomyositis frequently presents with proximal weakness and characteristic cutaneous findings.

Medication and exposure history: list all prescription medications, over‑the‑counter drugs, herbal supplements, and illicit substances. Particular agents are strongly associated with myopathy or angioedema: statins (HMG‑CoA reductase inhibitors) and fibrates (can cause myositis and myopathy), corticosteroids (chronic use leads to steroid myopathy), colchicine, antiretroviral drugs, alcohol, and illicit drugs (eg, cocaine). Recent initiation or uptitration is relevant when considering drug-induced causes. Angiotensin‑converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors are a common cause of medication‑induced angioedema and can present with rapid neck and oropharyngeal swelling; angiotensin receptor blockers, neprilysin inhibitors, or DPP‑4 inhibitors have also been implicated. A detailed allergy history (prior angioedema, urticaria, atopy) is essential.

Preceding infections and immunization history: ask about recent upper respiratory infections, pharyngitis, cutaneous infections, dental procedures, or systemic viral illnesses. Many bacterial neck infections follow oropharyngeal infections or dental abscesses; retropharyngeal abscesses follow pharyngitis or cervical trauma. Viral prodromes (fever, myalgia) often precede viral myositis. A history of recent vaccination or viral exposure can be relevant in the context of postinfectious inflammatory syndromes that affect the muscles.

Exposure and travel history: recent travel, animal exposures, tick bites, and occupational/environmental risks may suggest zoonoses or regional infectious agents (eg, cat scratch disease, endemic mycobacteria, tularemia). Immunization status and exposure to tuberculosis are relevant for chronic infectious causes. Also ascertain risk factors for opportunistic infections (HIV status, immunosuppressive medications).

Family and social history: a family history of hereditary myopathies or connective tissue disease can orient toward genetic causes. Social history should emphasize alcohol use, bodybuilding supplements (eg, anabolic steroids), and recent tattoos or piercings that could seed infection.

Red-flag features in the history that suggest urgency include rapid progression of swelling, dysphagia, odynophagia, hoarseness, inspiratory noises (stridor), breathing difficulty, drooling, inability to manage secretions, high fever, severe pain out of proportion to exam, or focal neurological deficits suggesting spinal or central nervous system involvement.

# Focused physical examination for cutaneous signs of connective tissue disease and neuromuscular weakness

The physical examination should be systematic and targeted to identify signs that distinguish inflammatory myopathies, neuromuscular junction disorders, infectious processes, and allergic reactions.

General inspection and vital signs: record temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and blood pressure. Fever and hemodynamic instability suggest infectious or systemic inflammatory processes. Tachypnea, hypoxia, and altered mental status increase the likelihood of severe systemic illness or impending respiratory compromise.

Neck examination: inspect for asymmetry, erythema, ecchymosis, and superficial venous distention. Palpate for focal tenderness, fluctuance (suggests abscess), warmth, cellulitis, and regional lymphadenopathy. Assess mobility and any induration tracking along fascial planes. Examine the oropharynx using adequate tongue depression and good illumination to evaluate tonsillar asymmetry, uvular deviation, visible pharyngeal swelling, pooling of secretions, and airway patency. Evaluate for trismus, voice changes (muffled "hot potato" voice), and drooling.

Cutaneous assessment for connective tissue disease: carefully examine exposed skin for rashes and characteristic lesions. In dermatomyositis, identify heliotrope rash (violaceous periorbital discoloration), Gottron papules (erythematous, scaly papules over extensor surfaces of the metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints), shawl sign (photosensitive erythema over the shoulders/upper back), and V sign (anterior chest erythema). Mechanic’s hands (hyperkeratotic fissured skin on the lateral and palmar aspects of the fingers) suggest antisynthetase syndrome. Nailfold capillary changes—telangiectasias or dilated capillary loops—may be visible with dermatomyositis or systemic sclerosis and can be examined with handheld magnification. Urticaria, angioedema, or widespread wheals suggest allergic causes.

Neuromuscular examination: quantify the pattern and severity of weakness. Document whether weakness is proximal (deltoids, hip flexors, quadriceps) versus distal, symmetric versus asymmetric, and whether it affects bulbar, respiratory, or neck extensor muscles. Common inflammatory myopathies produce symmetric, predominantly proximal weakness affecting shoulder girdle and hip flexors, with early difficulty rising from chair, climbing stairs, or combing hair. Neck flexor paresis—head drop—is a notable feature of severe myopathic weakness. In contrast, neuromuscular junction disorders such as myasthenia gravis often produce fatigable weakness that worsens with repetitive use (eg, ptosis, diplopia, fluctuating bulbar symptoms). Test for fatigability (repetition of sustained upgaze, repetitive limb movements) and observe whether administration of rest improves strength.

Manual muscle testing should use a 0–5 grading scale; document functional tests (timed rise from chair, Gowers’ sign, timed walk) and respiratory muscle strength when relevant (eg, single-breath count, measurement of vital capacity). Cranial nerve testing is critical: evaluate extraocular movements, facial strength, palatal elevation, gag reflex, and tongue strength. Bulbar weakness increases risk for aspiration.

Sensory examination: most primary inflammatory myopathies spare sensory nerves, whereas radiculopathies, polyneuropathies, or compressive lesions will produce sensory deficits. Presence of sensory loss directs workup away from pure myopathic processes.

Musculoskeletal and joint exam: look for arthritis, synovitis, and tenosynovitis that may suggest overlap connective tissue disease (eg, rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus). Examine for joint contractures (can be a late feature) and for muscle atrophy or fasciculations (which would suggest neuropathic or motor neuron disease).

Systemic exam: examine lungs for crackles (interstitial lung disease is associated with antisynthetase syndrome and some myositis subtypes), heart for murmurs/pericardial rubs, and abdomen for hepatosplenomegaly or signs of malignancy. For suspected angioedema, inspect airway and facial structures for edema distribution; check for urticaria and wheeze. For infectious causes, search for portals of entry such as dental disease, skin breaks, or lymphadenopathy patterns (cervical vs supraclavicular).

Documentation of dynamic features—change in strength over minutes, effect of maneuvers such as ice pack test (myasthenia gravis) or repetitive nerve stimulation—can be informative at the bedside.

# Algorithmic use of laboratory tests (CK, autoantibodies, complement studies) and imaging to distinguish infectious, allergic and inflammatory causes

A targeted laboratory and imaging strategy should be guided by clinical impression but executed rapidly when the etiology is uncertain or the patient is ill.

Initial basic labs: Complete blood count with differential, basic metabolic panel, liver function tests, inflammatory markers (C‑reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)), and blood cultures when infection is suspected. Leukocytosis with a left shift supports bacterial infection; markedly elevated CRP/ESR is nonspecific but supports inflammation or infection. Basic metabolic derangements (eg, hypokalemia, hypophosphatemia) can cause acute weakness and should be corrected emergently.

Creatine kinase (CK) and muscle enzymes: CK is a cornerstone test in suspected myositis or myopathy. Normal CK varies by laboratory and sex but commonly is approximately 26–192 U/L for adults; clinicians should reference local lab ranges. CK elevations in inflammatory myopathies can range from modest (a few hundred U/L) to markedly elevated (>10,000 U/L) although some myositis subtypes (eg, inclusion body myositis, immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy early after statin exposure) may have variable CK. Aldolase, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), AST, and ALT may also be elevated from muscle breakdown. Extreme CK elevations (>5,000–10,000 U/L) raise concern for rhabdomyolysis with risk of acute kidney injury; check serum creatinine and urine myoglobin.

Autoantibody testing: Screen for systemic autoimmune diseases with antinuclear antibody (ANA) and more specific myositis serologies if clinical suspicion exists. ANA is positive in many connective tissue diseases and may be positive in a proportion of inflammatory myopathies. Myositis‑specific antibodies (MSA) include anti‑Jo‑1 (histidyl tRNA synthetase), anti‑PL‑7, anti‑PL‑12, anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑SRP, anti‑MDA5, and others. Anti‑Jo‑1 is associated with antisynthetase syndrome (myositis, interstitial lung disease, mechanic’s hands, arthritis). Anti‑SRP and anti‑HMGCR (3‑hydroxy‑3‑methylglutaryl‑coenzyme A reductase) correlate with immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy. Anti‑MDA5 is associated with rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease and characteristic skin findings. Rheumatoid factor, anti‑CCP, anti‑dsDNA, ENA panel (anti‑SSA, anti‑SSB, anti‑RNP) and complement levels (C3, C4) may be indicated depending on overlap syndromes. Normal complement ranges vary by lab; low complement levels can support complement‑mediated processes (eg, immune complex disease).

Complement testing and C1 esterase inhibitor for angioedema: In angioedema without urticaria, measure C4 and C1 esterase inhibitor levels and function. Hereditary angioedema (HAE) typically shows low C4 and either low C1 esterase inhibitor level or dysfunctional C1 inhibitor. Acquired C1 inhibitor deficiency produces similar findings. ACE inhibitor–induced angioedema often has normal complement studies but a suggestive medication history.

Microbiology: For suspected bacterial neck infection, obtain blood cultures before antibiotics if feasible. Culture of draining fluid or aspirated material from abscesses is essential. Rapid strep testing or throat swabs may be helpful in pharyngitis-associated complications. In suspected mycobacterial disease, order acid‑fast bacilli stains and mycobacterial cultures or PCR.

Thyroid and metabolic tests: Thyroid dysfunction (hypothyroidism) can cause myalgias, elevated CK, and myopathy; check TSH and free T4. Electrolyte disturbances (hypokalemia, hypophosphatemia, hypocalcemia) can cause proximal weakness and should be corrected promptly.

Specialized neuromuscular testing: When neuromuscular junction disorders are suspected, acetylcholine receptor (AChR) antibodies and muscle‑specific tyrosine kinase (MuSK) antibodies should be measured; electrophysiologic testing (repetitive nerve stimulation and single‑fiber electromyography) helps confirm the diagnosis. In suspected inflammatory myopathies, electromyography (EMG) often demonstrates myopathic motor unit potentials and increased spontaneous activity, supporting muscle pathology.

Muscle imaging and biopsy: MRI of muscles with STIR sequences is sensitive for detecting edema and active inflammation; it helps guide biopsy to an affected muscle and monitor disease activity. Muscle biopsy remains the diagnostic standard for many inflammatory myopathies, revealing patterns such as perifascicular atrophy (dermatomyositis), endomysial inflammation with invasion of nonnecrotic fibers (polymyositis), or necrotizing myopathy. Indications for biopsy depend on clinical context and will usually follow noninvasive testing that supports inflammatory myopathy.

Cross‑sectional imaging of the neck: When an infectious process, deep neck space infection, neoplasm, or structural cause is suspected, obtain contrast‑enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the neck in stable patients. CT provides excellent delineation of abscesses, fluid collections, gas, and extent of infection across fascial planes. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers superior soft tissue contrast and is preferred for suspected vascular or neural involvement, vertebral osteomyelitis, or when radiation avoidance is desired. Ultrasound is useful for superficial collections and for guiding aspiration. In the setting of suspected airway compromise, dynamic assessment (flexion/extension) and direct laryngoscopy by ENT may be required.

Algorithmic approach summary: If neck swelling is rapid with urticaria/angioedema features and a history of ACE inhibitor or allergen exposure, prioritize airway assessment and measure C4/C1 inhibitor when appropriate. If there is focal tenderness, erythema, fever, and leukocytosis, prioritize CT neck with contrast, blood cultures, and early empiric antibiotics; arrange drainable source identification (aspiration/operative drainage) in consultation with ENT or surgery. If predominant proximal weakness with elevated CK and skin findings, evaluate for inflammatory myopathy with autoantibodies, MRI of muscles, EMG, and consider biopsy. If neuromuscular junction disease is suspected, obtain AChR/MuSK antibodies and EMG. Tailor testing to clinical suspicion and severity.

# When to involve ENT, rheumatology, neurology, immunology and surgical services

Timely specialty input is critical for complex presentations. The decision to consult is guided by clinical severity, diagnostic uncertainty, and the need for procedural interventions.

ENT (otolaryngology) should be involved immediately for any sign of potential airway compromise (stridor, voice changes, progressive dysphagia, drooling, signs of deep neck space infection), when there is an expanding neck hematoma, suspected parapharyngeal or retropharyngeal abscess, or need for direct laryngoscopy and airway control. ENT is the primary team for examination under anesthesia, endoscopic evaluation, securing the airway (intubation vs tracheostomy), and surgical drainage of deep neck space infections. In cases of peritonsillar abscess, Ludwig angina, or progressive base‑of‑tongue swelling, early ENT consultation reduces morbidity.

Rheumatology is indicated when autoimmune connective tissue disease or inflammatory myopathy is suspected—eg, when there is proximal muscle weakness with characteristic skin findings, positive myositis‑specific autoantibodies, or multisystem autoimmune features (arthritis, ILD, Raynaud phenomenon). Rheumatologists guide immunologic testing, interpretation of serologies, initiation and tapering of immunosuppressive therapy (corticosteroids, steroid‑sparing agents, IVIG, biologics), and long‑term monitoring for complications including interstitial lung disease and malignancy screening in dermatomyositis.

Neurology should be consulted for complex neuromuscular presentations: suspected myasthenia gravis (bulbar weakness, fatigability), motor neuron disease, unclear electrophysiologic findings, or when EMG, nerve conduction studies, and muscle biopsy are being coordinated. Neurology helps interpret electrophysiologic testing and manage diagnostic and therapeutic trials (eg, edrophonium if available historically, pyridostigmine). For severe weakness with respiratory compromise, neurology input helps prioritize respiratory monitoring (serial vital capacity) and EMG logistics.

Immunology/allergy consultation is appropriate when angioedema of unclear cause occurs, especially in the absence of urticaria, or when hereditary angioedema is suspected; immunologists help with complement and C1 inhibitor testing and guide long‑term management and prophylaxis. For complicated cases requiring immune modulation (eg, unusual antibody profiles, complement‑mediated disease), immunology may provide guidance on targeted therapies.

Surgery (general, head and neck, or otolaryngologic surgeons) should be involved for source control in deep neck infections requiring operative drainage, for resection of obstructing masses, or for traumatic/iatrogenic neck hematomas that threaten the airway. In suspected necrotizing fasciitis of the neck, emergent surgical evaluation and debridement are lifesaving. Plastic surgery or maxillofacial surgery may be needed for odontogenic sources.

Coordination and communication among specialties are essential. For example, an immunosuppressed patient with a neck abscess may require simultaneous ENT drainage, infectious diseases input for antimicrobial selection, and rheumatology consideration for adjusting immunosuppression. Early multidisciplinary discussions improve outcomes.

# Risk stratification for airway compromise and indications for urgent intervention

Airway compromise is the most immediate risk when neck swelling involves the oropharynx, supraglottic structures, or produces external compression. Rapid recognition and action are vital.

Clinical features that indicate high risk for airway compromise include stridor (inspiratory or expiratory), hoarseness or muffled (“hot potato”) voice, progressive dysphagia or odynophagia, drooling and inability to handle secretions, signs of respiratory distress (tachypnea, accessory muscle use), hypoxia (oxygen saturation < 92% on room air, or declining trend), cyanosis, and loss of consciousness. An expanding neck hematoma or rapidly enlarging mass should be treated as an impending airway emergency. Bulbar weakness with inability to protect the airway (poor cough, weak swallow) in the setting of neuromuscular disorders or severe myopathy also necessitates urgent airway management.

Objective measures to monitor respiratory muscle involvement in neuromuscular disease: serial forced vital capacity (FVC) is the standard bedside metric. A declining FVC to less than $20$ mL/kg of predicted body weight (or absolute FVC < 1.0 L in many adults) indicates significant respiratory compromise and need for consideration of noninvasive ventilation or elective intubation, although thresholds may vary with comorbidities. Negative inspiratory force (NIF) or maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP) less negative than $-30$ cm H$_2$O suggests respiratory muscle weakness, and values less negative than $-20$ cm H$_2$O are of particular concern. In patients with bulbar dysfunction, even preserved FVC may not ensure airway protection because of aspiration risk.

Airway evaluation and planning: any patient with signs of airway compromise should be triaged to a monitored setting (ICU) and have early involvement of the most experienced airway operator available (anesthesiology and ENT). Early controlled intubation is preferred over emergency cricothyrotomy; in some neck processes, fiberoptic nasoendoscopy or awake fiberoptic intubation with ENT present may be safest. If anatomical distortion is likely (deep neck infection, large mass, prior neck surgery/radiation), plan for awake tracheostomy in the operating room or awake fiberoptic intubation with surgical airway backup. Avoid blind or forceful manipulation in cases of infected swelling that may spread infection or hemorrhage.

Indications for urgent intervention include: progressive airway compromise signs noted above; evidence of expanding hematoma after trauma or surgery; CT evidence of retropharyngeal abscess or parapharyngeal collection extending toward the airway; descending necrotizing mediastinitis; severe sepsis with neck source; or neuromuscular respiratory failure indicated by declining FVC and worsening gas exchange. For angioedema with progressive oropharyngeal edema, early airway control is critical because edema can progress unpredictably; fiberoptic assessment and early intubation are often recommended if any concern exists.

Decision-making in the face of uncertainty favors early, controlled airway management in a controlled environment with multidisciplinary support. The risks of elective intubation or tracheostomy are typically outweighed by the catastrophic consequences of a lost airway.

Conclusion

Approaching patients with neck swelling and proximal weakness requires an integrated, systematic assessment that prioritizes airway safety while efficiently pursuing diagnostic clues. A structured history looking for temporal patterns, preceding infections, medication exposures, and systemic symptoms, coupled with a focused physical exam for cutaneous manifestations and neuromuscular deficits, directs rational laboratory and imaging choices. CK and antibody testing, targeted imaging (CT/MRI/ultrasound), and electrophysiologic studies help distinguish infectious, allergic, and inflammatory etiologies. Early multidisciplinary consultation—ENT for airway and drainage issues, rheumatology for inflammatory myopathies, neurology for neuromuscular disorders, immunology for complex complement/angioedema problems, and surgery when source control is needed—improves care. Finally, proactively stratifying airway risk and intervening early when indicated prevents catastrophic outcomes.
