Introduction

Soft tissue swelling of the face, neck and subcutaneous tissues of the anterior chest and upper shoulders is a frequent and sometimes alarming clinical presentation. While angioedema is a commonly considered cause, a broad differential must be entertained because the pathophysiology, prognosis, and management differ substantially among etiologies. A systematic approach that integrates history, focused physical examination, directed laboratory testing, and imaging allows clinicians to distinguish vascular, inflammatory/autoimmune, infectious, neoplastic, and dermatologic causes. This chapter reviews the major non-angioedema causes of subcutaneous and cervical edema with emphasis on pathophysiology, clinical clues, diagnostic criteria and decision points relevant to initial evaluation and management.

# Vascular causes

Vascular mechanisms produce soft tissue swelling by altering hydrostatic and oncotic forces, obstructing lymphatic or venous drainage, or by causing increased vascular permeability. In the neck and face, the principal vascular causes are superior vena cava (SVC) syndrome, local venous thrombosis (including jugular vein thrombosis), and systemic congestive heart failure (CHF). Each has distinct presentations and diagnostic implications.

Superior vena cava syndrome

SVC syndrome results from obstruction of venous return through the superior vena cava, which increases venous pressure in the head, neck, upper limbs and chest wall. Malignancy (bronchogenic carcinoma, lymphoma, metastatic tumor) has historically been the most common cause, but indwelling central venous catheters and thrombosis are increasingly recognized etiologies. Pathophysiologically, obstruction raises hydrostatic pressure in veins and capillaries drained by the SVC, favoring transudation of fluid into the interstitial space and causing pitting or nonpitting edema. Collateral venous pathways may develop over time, mitigating symptoms in chronic cases.

Clinically, patients report facial fullness, periorbital edema, neck and upper chest swelling, distended superficial neck/chest veins, and sometimes dyspnea, cough, hoarseness, or dysphagia. Symptoms that worsen when leaning forward or when supine are characteristic. On exam, there may be nonpitting edema of the face and neck, plethora, conjunctival injection and visible collateral veins across the chest. SVC syndrome is a clinical diagnosis supported by imaging: contrast-enhanced CT of the chest (CT angiography) is the preferred initial test because it localizes the level of obstruction and suggests cause (mass versus thrombus). If CT is contraindicated, magnetic resonance venography or duplex ultrasound (for more peripheral venous obstruction) may be used. Management prioritizes airway and cerebral perfusion if severe; treatment is directed to the cause (oncologic therapy, stenting, anticoagulation for thrombotic obstruction). Urgent intervention is indicated if there is laryngeal edema, cerebral edema, or hemodynamic compromise.

Venous thrombosis (internal jugular, facial veins)

Thrombosis of the internal jugular, facial, or retromandibular veins impairs venous drainage and produces localized swelling, pain, erythema, and sometimes systemic inflammatory signs. Causes include central venous catheters, head and neck infection (Lemierre syndrome), prothrombotic states, and direct trauma. Pathophysiologically, an obstructive thrombus elevates upstream venous pressures and can cause venous congestion of subcutaneous tissues and skin. The region of involvement tends to be focal and unilateral unless the thrombosis is extensive or bilateral.

Clinical features include a tender, indurated cord along the course of the affected vein, ipsilateral swelling of the neck or face, and sometimes fever. Doppler ultrasound is the first-line diagnostic modality for accessible veins such as the internal jugular; contrast-enhanced CT or MR venography is used for deeper or more proximal segments. Management involves systemic anticoagulation unless contraindicated, broad-spectrum antibiotics if septic thrombosis is suspected, and treatment of underlying triggers (e.g., removal of infected catheter, drainage of adjacent infection). The decision to anticoagulate requires balancing hemorrhagic risk and extent of thrombosis; consultation with hematology or vascular surgery is often appropriate for extensive or complicated cases.

Congestive heart failure

In congestive heart failure, increased systemic venous pressure from right-sided or biventricular dysfunction leads to dependent edema. While classic lower-extremity and sacral swelling are most prominent, elevated central venous pressure can cause facial and periorbital puffiness, especially in the morning or when recumbent. Edema due to CHF is typically pitting and symmetric; it fluctuates with diuretic therapy and posture.

Physical examination findings supporting CHF include elevated jugular venous pressure, peripheral edema, hepatomegaly, and pulmonary crackles if pulmonary congestion is present. Natriuretic peptide measurements (BNP or NT-proBNP) are useful laboratory adjuncts: BNP < 100 pg/mL and NT-proBNP < 300 pg/mL make heart failure unlikely, while markedly elevated values support the diagnosis (interpretation varies by age and renal function). Chest radiography and echocardiography provide structural and functional information. Management focuses on optimizing volume status with diuretics and treating the underlying cardiac dysfunction; improvement in facial/neck edema with diuresis supports a cardiac etiology.

Clinical decision points for vascular causes

When swelling is bilateral, involves the entire face and upper chest and is associated with distended chest wall veins, consider SVC syndrome and obtain emergent imaging. Localized, tender swelling along venous routes suggests thrombosis—use Doppler ultrasound and consider anticoagulation. If findings are consistent with systemic volume overload (pitting edema, elevated JVP, cardiopulmonary signs), evaluate for CHF with BNP and echocardiography and initiate diuresis as appropriate.

# Inflammatory and autoimmune causes

Autoimmune and inflammatory conditions produce cutaneous and subcutaneous swelling via immune complex deposition, complement activation, vasculitis, and inflammatory infiltration. In the neck and face, important entities include dermatomyositis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and various vasculitides, especially urticarial vasculitis. Recognizing the pattern and associated systemic features guides serologic testing, biopsy decisions, and immunomodulatory therapy.

Dermatomyositis

Dermatomyositis is an autoimmune inflammatory myopathy associated with characteristic cutaneous manifestations and proximal muscle weakness. Cutaneous findings relevant to facial and cervical swelling include periorbital edema (“heliotrope” sign), facial erythema, and shawl sign (erythema and edema across the shoulders and posterior neck). The periorbital edema in dermatomyositis may be prominent and nonpitting, and it frequently coexists with violaceous heliotrope discoloration. Pathophysiology involves complement-mediated microangiopathy with capillary destruction in skin and muscle, leading to ischemia, inflammation and tissue edema. Dermatomyositis can be paraneoplastic, particularly in adults, so evaluation for occult malignancy is recommended.

Diagnosis is based on clinical features, elevated muscle enzymes (creatine kinase; normal CK varies by laboratory but roughly up to 200 U/L), electromyography, and muscle biopsy showing perivascular/perimysial inflammation with perifascicular atrophy. Myositis-specific and associated autoantibodies (e.g., anti-Mi-2, anti-TIF1-gamma, anti-MDA5) can aid classification and assess malignancy risk. Facial and cervical edema in dermatomyositis often responds to systemic immunosuppression (corticosteroids, steroid-sparing agents), but management must be individualized.

Systemic lupus erythematosus

SLE is a multisystem autoimmune disease that can cause subcutaneous edema through multiple mechanisms: nephrotic-range proteinuria causing hypoalbuminemia and decreased oncotic pressure, serositis with superior mediastinal or cervical lymphatic obstruction, and inflammation of cutaneous tissues including angioedema-like mechanisms. Lupus-specific rashes (malar rash) can be accompanied by swelling. Diagnostic criteria for SLE are based on classification systems (e.g., the 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria), which integrate clinical features and autoantibodies—anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), anti-dsDNA, anti-Smith, complement levels (C3, C4) are commonly assessed. Low complement and high anti-dsDNA titers often correlate with disease activity. Proteinuria thresholds for nephrotic syndrome are protein excretion > 3.5 g/day or urine protein-to-creatinine ratio > 3.5 g/g, associated with hypoalbuminemia and generalized edema.

When facial and cervical swelling occur in a patient with known or suspected SLE, assessment should include urinalysis, serum albumin, complement levels, and targeted imaging for lymphatic obstruction if indicated. Treatment addresses the underlying disease activity (steroids, immunosuppressants) and the mechanisms producing edema (diuretics, albumin replacement in some cases).

Vasculitis and urticarial vasculitis

Small- and medium-vessel vasculitides can cause localized or diffuse soft tissue swelling via vessel wall inflammation, increased permeability and tissue ischemia. Vasculitis involving cutaneous vessels may produce palpable purpura, urticarial lesions that persist beyond 24 hours, and subcutaneous nodules. Urticarial vasculitis is a clinical-pathologic entity characterized by urticarial lesions that show small-vessel leukocytoclastic vasculitis on biopsy. Unlike ordinary urticaria, lesions are often painful or burning, last > 24 hours, and may leave residual hyperpigmentation.

Facial or periorbital swelling in vasculitis can be accompanied by systemic signs (fever, arthralgias, renal involvement). Laboratory evaluation includes inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP), complement levels (hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis may show low C3/C4), urinalysis for renal involvement, and autoantibody testing (ANCA for certain vasculitides). Skin biopsy is often diagnostic; direct immunofluorescence can demonstrate immune complex deposition. Management depends on severity and systemic involvement, ranging from antihistamines and short-course corticosteroids for limited cutaneous disease to systemic immunosuppression (e.g., corticosteroids, cyclophosphamide, rituximab) for organ-threatening disease.

Clinical reasoning in inflammatory/autoimmune causes

Key clinical clues that point to autoimmune or inflammatory etiologies include chronicity, bilateral symmetric edema with accompanying dermatologic signs (heliotrope rash, shawl sign, malar rash), systemic features such as fever, arthralgia or muscle weakness, laboratory evidence of systemic inflammation or autoimmunity, and lack of response to antibiotics or diuretics. Skin and muscle biopsy may be required for definitive diagnosis; early collaboration with rheumatology and dermatology is beneficial when systemic immunosuppression may be needed.

# Infectious causes

Infections are a leading cause of localized soft tissue swelling of the face and neck. The spectrum ranges from superficial cellulitis to salivary gland infections (sialadenitis) and life-threatening deep neck space infections. Infectious processes produce edema through inflammatory vasodilation and increased vascular permeability, tissue necrosis and lymphatic obstruction. Prompt recognition and delineation of the anatomic extent are essential for appropriate antimicrobial therapy and surgical management when indicated.

Cellulitis and erysipelas

Cellulitis is acute bacterial infection of the skin and subcutaneous tissues, often caused by streptococci and staphylococci. On the face and neck, erysipelas (a superficial form of streptococcal cutaneous infection with sharply demarcated raised borders) classically affects the face. Cellulitis presents with erythema, warmth, tenderness, and edema; systemic symptoms such as fever and leukocytosis may be present. Facial cellulitis may be associated with sinusitis, dental infections, trauma, or insect bites.

Distinguishing cellulitis from other causes requires attention to skin changes (induration and warmth), progression, and systemic features. Imaging is not usually required for simple cellulitis but is indicated when deep tissue involvement is suspected. Empiric antibiotic choice depends on suspected pathogens and MRSA prevalence: for nonpurulent facial cellulitis with likely streptococcal etiology, beta-lactam antibiotics (e.g., amoxicillin-clavulanate) may be chosen; purulent infections require coverage for S. aureus (including MRSA). Clinical reassessment within 24–48 hours is important to ensure response; lack of improvement warrants imaging and consideration of deeper infection or an alternative diagnosis.

Sialadenitis

Sialadenitis is inflammation of the salivary glands—most commonly the parotid or submandibular glands—and can be infectious or obstructive. Acute bacterial sialadenitis often presents with unilateral glandular swelling, pain that worsens with meals, erythema of overlying skin, trismus or difficulty opening the mouth, and purulent discharge from the duct orifices. Risk factors include dehydration, recent surgery, immunosuppression, sialolithiasis, and poor oral hygiene. Organisms include Staphylococcus aureus, oral anaerobes and, in some settings, gram-negative bacteria.

Diagnosis is largely clinical, but ultrasound or CT imaging helps if abscess or deep extension is suspected or to detect sialolithiasis. Management includes hydration, gland massage, sialogogues (lemon drops), anti-staphylococcal antibiotics, and drainage if abscess develops. Chronic sialadenitis and autoimmune sialadenitis (e.g., Sjögren syndrome) produce recurrent painless or painful swelling and require different management.

Deep neck space infections

Deep neck space infections—peritonsillar abscess, parapharyngeal abscess, retropharyngeal abscess—are potentially life-threatening because of airway compromise, mediastinal spread, and vascular involvement (e.g., internal jugular vein thrombophlebitis). They often present with fever, severe unilateral pain, swelling of the neck, trismus, odynophagia, muffled voice, and respiratory distress in severe cases. Physical exam may reveal fluctuance, bulging of the pharyngeal wall, and neck stiffness.

CT scan of the neck with contrast is the diagnostic modality of choice to delineate the location and extent of the infection and to identify abscess formation versus cellulitis. Management necessitates prompt airway assessment, intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics that cover oral flora (including anaerobes), and timely surgical drainage for established abscesses. Recognition of red flags—stridor, drooling, rapid progression of swelling, signs of sepsis—should lower the threshold for urgent imaging and otolaryngology consultation.

Clinical pearls for infectious etiologies

- Infectious causes commonly present with pain, erythema, systemic signs, and unilateral localization. Rapid progression and systemic toxicity suggest deep infection requiring imaging and possibly surgical intervention.
- When gland involvement is suspected, ask about changes with meals and inspect duct orifices for purulence.
- In immunocompromised patients, unusual organisms (fungi, atypical mycobacteria) and less typical presentations should be considered; broader microbiologic testing and early specialist involvement are indicated.

# Neoplastic and obstructive causes

Neoplastic processes can produce subcutaneous and cervical edema directly by mass effect—compressing veins or lymphatic channels—or indirectly via tumor-related lymphadenopathy. Salivary gland tumors, lymphomas, metastatic adenopathy, and parapharyngeal space masses can all manifest as neck swelling, often with progressive, nonfluctuant enlargement and less inflammatory change than infectious causes.

Lymphadenopathy: benign and malignant

Enlarged lymph nodes in the cervical chain are a frequent cause of neck swelling. Reactive lymphadenopathy from infections or inflammatory conditions tends to be tender, mobile, soft and may regress over weeks. Malignant nodes—due to lymphoma or metastatic carcinoma—are often painless, firm, hard, fixed, and progressively enlarging. Supraclavicular nodes, particularly on the left (Virchow node), are classically associated with intra-abdominal malignancy metastases.

When lymphadenopathy persists beyond 2–4 weeks without clear infectious cause, is progressively enlarging, or shows suspicious features (size > 2 cm, hard/fixed, systemic “B” symptoms such as fever, weight loss, night sweats), further evaluation is warranted. Core needle biopsy or excisional biopsy is preferred over fine-needle aspiration when lymphoma is suspected because tissue architecture is necessary for histopathological subtyping. Imaging—ultrasound for superficial nodes; contrast-enhanced CT or PET-CT for staging and search for occult primaries—is used according to the clinical context.

Salivary gland tumors and parapharyngeal masses

Neoplasms of the salivary glands (parotid, submandibular, sublingual) present as painless enlarging masses in the corresponding region; facial nerve weakness suggests malignant parotid disease. Parapharyngeal space masses may present as lateral pharyngeal wall bulging, dysphagia, voice changes, or external neck swelling. Tumors may be benign (pleomorphic adenoma) or malignant (mucoepidermoid carcinoma, adenoid cystic carcinoma). Imaging with contrast-enhanced CT or MRI defines the lesion’s size, extent and relationship to neurovascular structures; fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) or core biopsy aids diagnosis but carries concern for tumor seeding in rare cases.

Obstructive/space-occupying lesions causing edema

Masses within the thoracic inlet, mediastinum or lower neck can obstruct venous or lymphatic outflow, leading to facial and neck edema reminiscent of SVC syndrome. Thyroid enlargement (goiter) or retrosternal masses may produce visible neck swelling and compress adjacent structures causing cough, dyspnea or dysphagia. Lymphatic obstruction secondary to tumor infiltration or prior surgery/radiation may produce lymphedema, which tends to be nonpitting and chronic.

Key diagnostic and management considerations

- Progressive, noninflammatory neck masses merit imaging (ultrasound for superficial lesions; CT/MRI for deep lesions) and tissue diagnosis.
- Consider age and risk factors: in adults >40 with new unilateral neck mass, malignancy risk is higher and prompt evaluation is needed.
- Multidisciplinary involvement (head and neck surgery, ENT, oncology) is often required for definitive management including resection, radiotherapy, or systemic therapy.

# Drug reactions, contact dermatitis and orofacial granulomatosis

Dermatologic and hypersensitivity processes of the face and neck produce soft tissue swelling through mechanisms of allergic inflammation, delayed-type hypersensitivity, or granulomatous inflammation. These entities often target mucocutaneous tissues and may be mistaken for angioedema or infectious cellulitis.

Drug reactions

Drug-induced facial swelling can result from immediate-type hypersensitivity (angioedema), delayed allergic dermatitis, or fixed drug eruptions. Distinguishing features include temporal relation to drug exposure, pruritus, rash distribution and associated systemic signs. Common culprits for facial/neck swelling include antibiotics, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, ACE inhibitors (classically causing bradykinin-mediated angioedema), and chemotherapeutic or biologic agents that induce immune dysregulation.

Immediate allergic reactions present rapidly (minutes to hours) and often involve urticaria and systemic symptoms; management includes antihistamines, corticosteroids and epinephrine for anaphylaxis. Delayed reactions may manifest as eczematous dermatitis with localized edema and require withdrawal of the offending agent and topical/systemic anti-inflammatory therapy. A thorough medication history—including new prescription drugs, over-the-counter agents, supplements and recent vaccinations—is essential.

Contact dermatitis

Contact dermatitis of the face and neck is most commonly allergic or irritant in type. Allergic contact dermatitis develops after sensitization to a contactant (nickel, fragrances, topical antibiotics, herbal products, cosmetics) and presents with pruritic erythematous papules or plaques with edema limited to areas of exposure. Irritant contact dermatitis results from direct epithelial injury and can present similarly but without an allergic mechanism. Patch testing identifies specific allergens in chronic or recurrent cases. Management involves avoidance, topical corticosteroids, and emollients; severe cases may require systemic steroids.

Orofacial granulomatosis and granulomatous reactions

Orofacial granulomatosis (OFG) is a clinical syndrome of chronic, noncaseating granulomatous inflammation affecting the lips, perioral area and sometimes the cheeks and face. Clinically it causes persistent lip swelling, firm nonpitting edema, and sometimes mucosal ulceration. OFG is heterogeneous in etiology and may be idiopathic or associated with systemic diseases such as Crohn disease, sarcoidosis, and foreign-body reactions. Histology shows noncaseating granulomas; workup includes gastrointestinal evaluation when Crohn disease is suspected, chest imaging and ACE levels if sarcoidosis is a consideration.

Management typically begins with topical or intralesional corticosteroids for localized disease; refractory or systemic disease may require immunomodulators (e.g., azathioprine, methotrexate, anti-TNF agents) after specialist input. Avoidance of potential triggers identified by patch testing (metals, food additives) can be helpful in some patients.

Clinical reasoning for dermatologic causes

Dermatologic etiologies tend to be characterized by cutaneous signs—pruritus, rash, scaling or ulceration—localized to areas of exposure or distribution consistent with specific syndromes. A careful history of exposures and medications, inspection of skin lesions, and, when needed, dermatologic tests (patch testing, skin biopsy) help confirm the diagnosis. Response to withdrawal of the suspected agent or to topical therapy can be diagnostic and therapeutic.

# Integration and approach to evaluation

A systematic approach to a patient presenting with subcutaneous or cervical edema beyond suspected angioedema includes:

1. Rapid assessment for airway compromise, respiratory distress, or hemodynamic instability. If present, secure the airway and manage emergently.
2. Focused history: onset (sudden versus progressive), laterality (unilateral versus bilateral), associated symptoms (pain, pruritus, fever, dysphagia, dyspnea, visual changes, muscle weakness), relation to posture or Valsalva, recent infections or dental/ENT procedures, medication exposures, cancer history, and systemic symptoms (weight loss, night sweats).
3. Physical examination: inspect skin (erythema, rash, induration), palpate for lymphadenopathy, tenderness, fluctuance, and venous distension; assess for JVP, chest wall collaterals, and facial plethoric changes; evaluate cranial nerve and facial nerve function if mass effect suspected.
4. Initial laboratory and bedside tests: CBC with differential (leukocytosis suggests infection), basic metabolic panel (renal function impacts edema and imaging choices), liver tests and albumin (hypoalbuminemia contributes to generalized edema), inflammatory markers (ESR/CRP), BNP if heart failure suspected, urinalysis for proteinuria, and targeted autoantibodies (ANA, ANCA, complement) based on clinical suspicion.
5. Imaging: select modality to elucidate anatomic extent—ultrasound for superficial masses and vascular thrombosis; contrast-enhanced CT for deep neck spaces, SVC syndrome evaluation, and salivary gland or parapharyngeal lesions; MRI for soft tissue characterization; chest imaging for suspected thoracic causes or malignancy. Duplex ultrasound is first-line for suspected vein thrombosis.
6. Tissue diagnosis: perform skin biopsy for vasculitis or granulomatous disease, FNAC or core/excisional lymph node biopsy for persistent lymphadenopathy or suspected malignancy, and culture/drainage of purulent collections.
7. Management decisions: tailor therapy to cause—antibiotics and drainage for infection, anticoagulation for venous thrombosis, diuretics for cardiac volume overload, immunosuppression for autoimmune disease, and oncologic or surgical intervention for neoplasms. Consult relevant specialties early (ENT/head and neck surgery, infectious disease, rheumatology, hematology/oncology, vascular surgery).

Conclusion

The differential diagnosis for subcutaneous and cervical edema is broad. Distinguishing among vascular obstruction, inflammatory/autoimmune disease, infection, neoplasm, and dermatologic reactions requires synthesizing clinical clues with directed laboratory testing and imaging. A methodical approach—prioritizing airway assessment, detailed history, targeted physical examination, appropriate imaging and timely tissue diagnosis—enables efficient triage and management. Awareness of key distinguishing features and potential red flags (rapid progression, airway compromise, signs of systemic disease or malignancy) is essential for safe and effective care.
