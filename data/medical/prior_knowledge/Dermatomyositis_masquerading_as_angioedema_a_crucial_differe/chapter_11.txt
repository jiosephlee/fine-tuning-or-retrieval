Introduction

Angioedema is localized, transient swelling of the deeper dermis, subcutaneous tissue, mucosa or submucosa resulting from increased vascular permeability and extravasation of fluid into the interstitial space. Although clinically recognizable, angioedema is heterogeneous in mechanism, natural history and therapeutic responsiveness. A precise mechanistic classification—histamine-mediated (mast cell–dependent), bradykinin-mediated, hereditary (C1 inhibitor [C1-INH] defects) and acquired C1-INH dysfunction—directly informs acute management, prophylaxis and diagnostic testing. This chapter reviews the pathophysiology that underlies these forms, clarifies clinical clues that separate them from each other and from inflammatory or infectious “pseudoangioedema,” outlines relevant laboratory testing (with practical interpretation), and highlights emergency airway priorities and evidence-based acute therapies.

# Mediators of angioedema: histamine vs bradykinin and implications for treatment

Two broad mediator pathways account for most clinically encountered angioedema: histamine-mediated (mast cell–dependent) and bradykinin-mediated angioedema. Their pathophysiology, clinical presentation, diagnostic tests and therapeutic responses differ substantially.

Histamine-mediated angioedema arises from activation and degranulation of mast cells and basophils with release of histamine, tryptase, prostaglandins and other mediators. Mechanisms include IgE-mediated allergic reactions to foods, drugs, insect stings and biologic agents, direct mast cell activators (e.g., radiocontrast, opiates), and nonimmune mechanisms. Histamine increases microvascular permeability by acting on endothelial H1 and H2 receptors and causes vasodilation and pruritus. Clinically, histamine-mediated angioedema tends to have: rapid onset (minutes to hours), often associated urticaria (hives), pruritus, flushing, and other signs of systemic anaphylaxis (hypotension, bronchospasm). Tryptase, a marker of mast cell degranulation, is often elevated acutely. Importantly, these episodes typically respond to epinephrine, H1/H2 antihistamines and systemic glucocorticoids.

Bradykinin-mediated angioedema results from excessive generation or impaired degradation of bradykinin, a potent vasodilator and endothelial permeability mediator that acts predominantly at bradykinin B2 receptors. Bradykinin induces increased vascular permeability without histamine-mediated symptoms such as urticaria and pruritus. Common contexts include hereditary C1-INH deficiency (see below), acquired C1-INH deficiency (consumption or autoantibodies), and drug-induced forms related to inhibition of bradykinin breakdown (notably angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors [ACEi]). Bradykinin-mediated attacks are frequently characterized by slower onset (hours), prominent submucosal edema (including laryngeal and gastrointestinal mucosa causing pain and vomiting), recurrent abdominal pain mimicking surgical abdomen, and poor or absent response to epinephrine, antihistamines and steroids. Tryptase is usually normal.

Therapeutic implications are decisive. For suspected histamine-mediated angioedema or anaphylaxis, immediate intramuscular epinephrine (0.3–0.5 mg of 1:1000 solution in adults), oxygen, airway support and H1/H2 antihistamines plus glucocorticoids are the first-line measures. For bradykinin-mediated angioedema, these measures are generally ineffective; targeted therapies that inhibit bradykinin production or block its receptor are required. Effective agents include plasma-derived or recombinant C1-INH concentrate (replacement therapy), icatibant (a selective bradykinin B2 receptor antagonist; 30 mg subcutaneously single dose), and kallikrein inhibitors such as ecallantide (30 mg subcutaneously). Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) contains C1-INH and kallikrein inhibitors and can be used when specific agents are unavailable, although theoretical and occasional paradoxical worsening may occur. Recognizing the mediator is therefore essential because empiric, time-critical airway interventions differ little, but pharmacologic therapy differs fundamentally.

# Hereditary angioedema (C1 inhibitor deficiency) and acquired C1-inhibitor dysfunction

Hereditary angioedema (HAE) is a genetic disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of bradykinin-mediated angioedema. Classic HAE results from deficiency or dysfunction of C1-INH (serpin that inhibits C1r/C1s of complement classical pathway and plasma kallikrein and factor XII in the contact system). There are three major HAE phenotypes relevant to testing and management:

- Type I HAE: Reduced quantitative C1-INH levels (approximately 85% of cases). Low antigenic C1-INH and low functional activity.
- Type II HAE: Normal or elevated antigenic C1-INH but reduced functional activity due to dysfunctional protein (approximately 15% of cases).
- "HAE with normal C1-INH" (historically Type III): Normal C1-INH antigen and function; several subtypes are described, some associated with mutations in factor XII (FXII), plasminogen, angiopoietin-1, or other genes. This phenotype is less well understood and may be estrogen-sensitive.

Pathophysiology in C1-INH deficiency: unregulated activation of plasma kallikrein leads to increased conversion of high-molecular-weight kininogen (HMWK) to bradykinin. The result is episodic bradykinin surge causing localized endothelial gap formation and edema. Laryngeal involvement is a feared complication with the potential for fatal airway obstruction. Abdominal attacks with severe colicky pain, vomiting and diarrhea are common and reflect bowel wall edema.

Acquired C1-INH deficiency is phenotypically similar but not hereditary. Mechanisms include increased consumption of C1-INH (for example, in lymphoproliferative disorders) or autoantibody-mediated neutralization of C1-INH; these are most often seen in middle-aged or older adults. Clinically, acquired C1-INH deficiency tends to be associated with underlying malignancy or autoimmune disease. Measurement of C1q protein is useful because C1q is often reduced in acquired forms (reflecting classical pathway activation), whereas it is typically normal in hereditary C1-INH deficiency.

Clinical evaluation should include family history, age of onset (HAE often presents in childhood or adolescence; acquired disease presents later), recurrent abdominal pain, lack of response to antihistamines/steroids and absence of urticaria. Management principles overlap with bradykinin-mediated therapy: on-demand treatment with C1-INH concentrate, icatibant or kallikrein inhibitors; prophylactic strategies for frequent attacks (e.g., attenuated androgens, regular C1-INH replacement, or monoclonal kallikrein inhibitors such as lanadelumab). In pregnancy, attenuated androgens are contraindicated and C1-INH concentrates are preferred for prophylaxis and acute therapy.

# Drug-induced angioedema (ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, NSAIDs)

Medications are a common and clinically important cause of angioedema. The three categories of medications most relevant to clinical practice are ACE inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). The mechanisms and clinical fingerprints differ.

ACE inhibitor–associated angioedema is a bradykinin-mediated process. ACE (kininase II) degrades bradykinin and substance P; ACE inhibition decreases bradykinin breakdown and can lead to accumulation. ACE inhibitor angioedema typically involves facial, perioral, tongue and laryngeal swelling and may present after days to years of therapy—onset may be delayed and unpredictable. There is no urticaria or pruritus, and routine antihistamine/steroid/epinephrine therapy is often ineffective. Management requires immediate cessation of the ACE inhibitor. Targeted therapies for bradykinin-mediated angioedema (icatibant, C1-INH, kallikrein inhibitors) are effective in many cases. There is no cross-reactivity between ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) regarding bradykinin, so ARBs are generally safe alternatives, though caution is reasonable in individual cases.

DPP-4 inhibitors (gliptins) used in diabetes can predispose to angioedema, particularly when combined with ACE inhibitors. DPP-4 degrades several vasoactive peptides including bradykinin and substance P; inhibition can therefore prolong their activity and potentially contribute to angioedema. The phenotype overlaps with bradykinin-mediated angioedema: mucosal swelling without urticaria. Clinical vigilance is warranted when prescribing DPP-4 inhibitors to patients on ACE inhibitors.

NSAIDs provoke angioedema through a predominately non–IgE-mediated, pseudoallergic mechanism. In susceptible individuals, cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibition shunts arachidonic acid metabolism toward leukotriene production, promoting bronchospasm and increased vascular permeability. NSAID-induced angioedema frequently occurs in patients with chronic urticaria and aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD). Presentation commonly includes urticaria, pruritus and sometimes respiratory symptoms; response to antihistamines may be partial. Avoidance of implicated NSAIDs and use of COX-2 selective agents with caution (and under specialist guidance) are typical strategies.

Recognizing the offending drug is essential: immediate discontinuation is indicated for ACE inhibitors and DPP-4 inhibitors if angioedema is suspected. For NSAID-induced reactions, discontinuation and substitution with safer analgesics are appropriate. Preventive strategies include counseling patients about recurrence risk and ensuring alternative medications are provided.

# Clinical clues to angioedema vs pseudoangioedema from inflammatory edema

Distinguishing true angioedema from other causes of localized swelling—termed pseudoangioedema—is critical. Pseudoangioedema includes infectious cellulitis, contact dermatitis, venous/lymphatic obstruction, inferior vena cava syndrome, or inflammatory edema from autoimmune disease. Misclassification can lead to inappropriate therapy and missed diagnoses (for example, failure to treat sepsis or a surgical abdomen).

Key clinical differentiators:

- Onset and speed: Histamine-mediated angioedema typically develops over minutes to hours. Bradykinin-mediated attacks often evolve over hours. Infectious cellulitis generally develops over days.
- Associated cutaneous findings: Urticaria (wheals) and pruritus strongly favor histamine-mediated angioedema. Absence of urticaria with painless swelling argues for bradykinin-mediated angioedema. Redness with warmth, tenderness, and induration are more consistent with cellulitis or localized inflammatory processes.
- Laterality and distribution: Angioedema is often asymmetric but more limited to dependent sites of mast cell activation or areas of bradykinin effect; venous/lymphatic obstruction often causes progressive dependent swelling with pitting edema. Facial angioedema frequently affects periorbital, perioral and tongue tissues; generalized nonpitting swelling is typical of angioedema. Pitting suggests fluid overload or venous/lymphatic etiologies rather than angioedema.
- Systemic signs: Fever, elevated inflammatory markers and leukocytosis support infectious or inflammatory etiologies. Rapid bronchospasm, hypotension, and mucocutaneous signs of anaphylaxis point to histamine-mediated pathology.
- Response to therapy: Rapid resolution with epinephrine, antihistamines and corticosteroids supports histamine-mediated etiology. Lack of response to these agents suggests bradykinin-mediated angioedema or non–mast cell causes.
- History: Recurrent episodic swelling with family history suggests hereditary angioedema. Medication history (ACEi, DPP-4 inhibitor, NSAID) is vital. Association with malignancy or autoimmune disease may suggest acquired C1-INH deficiency.

Clinical reasoning must integrate these observations. For example, a patient with painless abdominal pain and recurrent episodes of colicky pain with normal inflammatory markers and no dermatologic signs suggests bradykinin-mediated HAE. Conversely, a febrile, tender, erythematous, warm swollen limb that is pitting is more likely cellulitis or deep venous thrombosis.

# Laboratory tests: C3/C4, C1 esterase inhibitor level and function, tryptase, complement activation markers

Laboratory evaluation should be directed by the suspected mechanism. Timing and sample handling matter for interpretation.

Complement testing for suspected C1-INH deficiency:
- Serum complement C4: In C1-INH deficiency (both hereditary and acquired) the C4 level is typically reduced even between attacks because uncontrolled classical pathway activation consumes C4. A low C4 is a sensitive screening test. Typical adult reference range for C4 is approximately 10–40 mg/dL (0.1–0.4 g/L), though ranges vary by laboratory; many reference labs consider values below the lower limit as abnormal. In suspected HAE, a markedly low C4 is highly supportive.
- C1q: Useful to differentiate hereditary from acquired C1-INH deficiency. C1q is typically normal in hereditary disease but often decreased in acquired forms (due to immune complex–mediated classical pathway activation). No universal numeric cutoff eliminates the need to use local lab reference ranges; interpret relative to laboratory normals.
- C3: Usually normal in HAE because the classical pathway activation primarily consumes C4; C3 levels are often within normal limits (reference ~90–180 mg/dL). Decreased C3 suggests other complement-mediated diseases.
- C1-INH antigenic level and functional assay: Two key measurements are required to define HAE type. Antigenic C1-INH (quantity) is measured by immunoassay (reference ranges vary; typical antigenic C1-INH may be reported as 21–39 mg/dL or in mg/L depending on lab). Functional C1-INH activity is measured by functional assays (chromogenic or ELISA-based); normal activity is generally >50–70% (exact cutoffs depend on assay). In Type I HAE both antigenic level and function are low; in Type II antigen is normal or elevated while function is low. Testing should be performed twice on separate occasions for confirmation because acute-phase events can transiently alter levels and replacement therapy can confound results.
- Anti–C1-INH autoantibodies: In suspected acquired C1-INH deficiency, testing for anti–C1-INH antibodies is appropriate where available.

Practical testing strategy:
- First-line screening: serum C4; if low, measure C1-INH antigenic level and C1-INH functional activity.
- Include C1q when acquired deficiency is suspected (middle-age onset, associated autoimmune or lymphoproliferative disease).
- Obtain samples before giving C1-INH concentrate or FFP for diagnostic clarity; replacement may normalize levels and obscure diagnosis.

Mast cell activation testing:
- Serum tryptase: Tryptase is released from mast cells and is a useful marker of systemic mast cell activation/anaphylaxis. For suspected anaphylaxis, draw a sample as early as possible, ideally within 1–3 hours of symptom onset; obtain a baseline sample at least 24 hours later for comparison. An acute increase of tryptase by greater than $1.2\times$ baseline plus $2\ \mathrm{ng/mL}$ (i.e., acute tryptase > baseline $\times 1.2 + 2$ ng/mL) is often used to support mast cell activation. Typical normal reference upper limit is approximately 11.4 ng/mL but varies across labs. Chronic elevations in baseline tryptase (>11.4 ng/mL) suggest mastocytosis or baseline mast cell burden.
- Histamine levels are less practical because histamine is cleared rapidly.

Complement activation markers:
- In research or specialized centers, markers of complement activation such as C4a, C1r/C1s complexes, and terminal complement complex (sC5b-9) can be measured; these are not routinely necessary for initial clinical management but can inform pathophysiology in complex or research cases.

Other labs:
- When infectious or inflammatory pseudoangioedema is considered, check CBC, inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR), blood cultures and imaging as indicated to evaluate for cellulitis or deep-seated infection.
- In suspected ACEi-induced angioedema, routine complement testing is usually not required acutely unless recurrent or atypical features prompt investigation for underlying C1-INH dysfunction.

# Emergency airway considerations and acute management

Airway compromise is the most life-threatening complication of angioedema. Early recognition and a low threshold for securing the airway are essential. The approach differs little between histamine- and bradykinin-mediated angioedema with respect to airway management steps, but pharmacologic adjuncts differ.

Assessment and monitoring:
- Evaluate for stridor, voice change (dysphonia, hoarseness), difficulty swallowing (dysphagia), drooling, progressive tongue or floor-of-mouth edema, dyspnea, hypoxia, respiratory distress, and work of breathing. Laryngoscopic examination (flexible nasopharyngolaryngoscopy) when available can visualize laryngeal edema and guide timing of intervention.
- Patients with progressive oropharyngeal edema, rapid symptom evolution, or signs of impending obstruction should be managed proactively—prefer early airway control rather than waiting for complete obstruction.

Airway strategies:
- Prepare and mobilize resources: skilled airway operator (anesthesiologist/ENT/critical care), suction, difficult airway cart, video or flexible fiberoptic laryngoscope, surgical airway equipment, and operating room availability if possible.
- Intubation approach: Awake fiberoptic naso- or orotracheal intubation is often preferred when feasible and when the patient can cooperate, because it preserves spontaneous breathing and allows visualization of the airway through edematous tissues. Topical anesthesia and sedation must be used cautiously.
- If fiberoptic intubation is not feasible, prepare for rapid sequence intubation with experienced personnel; use video laryngoscopy as a first-line tool if available. However, there can be situations where visualization remains difficult because of severe edema.
- Surgical airway: Emergency cricothyrotomy (or tracheostomy if expert teams are available) must be considered when intubation fails or anatomical distortion precludes endotracheal establishment of the airway. There should be no hesitation to proceed to surgical airway in a patient with impending complete airway obstruction.

Acute pharmacologic management:
- Histamine-mediated angioedema/anaphylaxis: administer intramuscular epinephrine promptly (adult dose 0.3–0.5 mg IM of 1:1000 into lateral thigh). Supplemental oxygen, intravenous fluids for hypotension, nebulized bronchodilators for bronchospasm, H1 antihistamines (e.g., diphenhydramine 25–50 mg IV), H2 antihistamines (e.g., famotidine 20 mg IV) and systemic glucocorticoids (e.g., methylprednisolone 125 mg IV) are adjuncts. Epinephrine is life-saving for anaphylaxis; do not delay for diagnostics.
- Bradykinin-mediated angioedema: epinephrine, antihistamines and steroids are usually ineffective. When bradykinin-mediated angioedema is suspected (HAE, ACEi-related), institution of targeted therapy is indicated:
  - C1-INH concentrate (plasma-derived or recombinant): dosing varies by product; for Berinert (plasma-derived C1-INH), recommended dose for acute HAE attack is 20 U/kg IV. Ruconest (recombinant C1-INH) dosing and product specifics vary—follow local product guidance.
  - Icatibant: bradykinin B2 receptor antagonist; adult dose 30 mg subcutaneous once (some patients may require repeat dosing per protocol or clinical response).
  - Ecallantide: kallikrein inhibitor; 30 mg subcutaneous (given as 3 subcutaneous injections of 10 mg each); requires monitoring for anaphylaxis, and is available under restricted programs in some jurisdictions.
  - Fresh frozen plasma (FFP): contains C1-INH and can be used when specific agents are unavailable. Typical dosing is 10–15 mL/kg (approx. 1–2 units adult), but institutional protocols vary. Be aware of potential for transient worsening from additional complement substrates in some cases; weigh risks and benefits.
  - For ACEi-induced angioedema, targeted therapies (icatibant and C1-INH) have been used successfully; evidence is mixed in randomized studies but case series and guidelines support their use in severe cases. Immediate cessation of the ACE inhibitor is required.
- Adjunctive measures: sedatives and airway maneuvers as indicated. For severe laryngeal edema, early otolaryngology involvement and consideration of elective surgical airway in controlled setting are advised.

Disposition and observation:
- Patients with oropharyngeal involvement, progressive symptoms, or requirement for advanced airway interventions should be admitted to an intermediate or intensive care unit for close monitoring. Observation periods depend on etiology and severity; for histamine-mediated reactions, observation of 4–24 hours may suffice if symptoms resolve. For bradykinin-mediated attacks, variable durations of observation are necessary depending on response to targeted therapy and the risk of recurrence.

Prophylaxis and longer-term management:
- For patients with recurrent HAE attacks, discuss short-term prophylaxis for procedures that might provoke attacks (dental work, intubation, surgical procedures)—administer C1-INH concentrate prior to the procedure when indicated. Long-term prophylaxis options include regular C1-INH replacement (IV or subcutaneous products), attenuated androgens (danazol, stanozolol) with consideration of adverse effects and contraindications (e.g., pregnancy, liver disease, lipid abnormalities), antifibrinolytics (e.g., tranexamic acid) with modest efficacy, and newer therapies such as lanadelumab (monoclonal antibody against kallikrein) 300 mg subcutaneously every 2–4 weeks for prophylaxis. Treatment selection should be individualized based on attack frequency, severity, comorbidities, pregnancy status and patient preference.

Summary of critical decision points:
- Rapidly determine whether airway is at risk—if yes, proceed toward securing a protected airway early with all appropriate preparations.
- Differentiate likely mediator (histamine vs bradykinin) based on history, physical exam and response to initial therapy; start epinephrine for suspected anaphylaxis without delay.
- For bradykinin-mediated suspicion (e.g., ACEi use, recurrent nonpruritic swelling, abdominal pain, family history), prioritize targeted bradykinin-directed therapy (C1-INH, icatibant, ecallantide) and avoid relying solely on antihistamines/steroids.

Concluding remarks

Angioedema encompasses distinct mechanistic categories that require different diagnostic tests and therapies. Careful clinical assessment—focusing on onset, associated symptoms (urticaria, pruritus, abdominal pain), medication history and family history—guides immediate management and appropriate laboratory evaluation. Complement testing (C4, C1-INH antigenic level and function, and C1q when needed) is pivotal in suspected hereditary or acquired C1-INH dysfunction. In acute settings, protect the airway and provide mediator-appropriate pharmacotherapy: epinephrine and antihistamines for histamine-mediated disease; C1-INH replacement, bradykinin B2 receptor antagonists and kallikrein inhibitors for bradykinin-mediated disease. Familiarity with the distinguishing features and treatment algorithms reduces morbidity and prevents fatal airway complications.
