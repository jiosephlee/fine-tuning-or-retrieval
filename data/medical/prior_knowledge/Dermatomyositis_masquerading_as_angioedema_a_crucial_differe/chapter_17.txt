Introduction

Immunosuppressive therapy is central to the management of immune-mediated myopathies and other autoimmune diseases. While these agents frequently provide major clinical benefit, they carry distinct and potentially serious toxicities. Recognition of predictable adverse effects, routine monitoring to detect early complications, and strategies to prevent or mitigate harm are essential skills for clinicians caring for patients on immunosuppression. This chapter reviews the major complications of commonly used immunosuppressive agents, focusing on hematologic toxicities, hepatic and renal injury, infection risk including opportunistic pathogens, drug–drug interactions and peri-procedural management, and infusion-related reactions with strategies for prevention and response.

# Hematologic toxicities (cytopenias, hemolytic anemia) and monitoring

Hematologic toxicities are among the most common and clinically consequential adverse effects of immunosuppressive drugs. The spectrum includes bone marrow suppression causing neutropenia, anemia and thrombocytopenia; drug-induced immune cytopenias (autoimmune hemolytic anemia, immune thrombocytopenia); and hemolysis mediated by specific drug mechanisms. Recognition requires knowledge of which agents are implicated, typical time courses, pathophysiology, and the appropriate laboratory surveillance and thresholds for intervention.

Pathophysiology and offending agents
- Antimetabolites (azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurine, mycophenolate mofetil, methotrexate) commonly cause bone marrow suppression by interfering with nucleotide synthesis and DNA replication in rapidly dividing marrow progenitors. The risk correlates with dose and host factors (e.g., TPMT deficiency increases risk with azathioprine/6-MP).
- Alkylating agents (cyclophosphamide) produce direct cytotoxicity to marrow progenitors and can cause profound and sometimes prolonged cytopenias.
- Calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine, tacrolimus) are less likely to cause frank marrow aplasia but can produce thrombotic microangiopathy or anemia via hemolysis in rare cases.
- Biologics and targeted therapies (rituximab, tocilizumab, JAK inhibitors) have characteristic hematologic profiles: rituximab can cause delayed-onset neutropenia, and some agents may unmask cytopenias by altering cytokine balance.
- Drug-induced immune hemolytic anemia (DIHA) can occur with penicillins, cephalosporins, alpha-methyldopa, and rarely with immunomodulatory agents through hapten formation or autoimmune mechanisms. Warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) is immune-mediated and may be triggered or exacerbated by immune dysregulation after immunosuppression changes.
- Pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) is a rare complication of some agents (e.g., azathioprine in viral infections or parvovirus B19 reactivation).

Clinical presentation and diagnosis
Cytopenias may be asymptomatic and detected on routine labs or present with infections (neutropenia), fatigue and pallor (anemia), or bleeding/bruising (thrombocytopenia). Specific diagnostic steps:
- Complete blood count (CBC) with differential; examine trends over time.
- Peripheral blood smear to look for schistocytes (microangiopathic hemolysis), reticulocytes, dysplastic changes, or blasts.
- Reticulocyte count, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), haptoglobin, indirect bilirubin, and direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test to evaluate for hemolysis/AIHA.
- Bone marrow biopsy when marrow failure, unexplained pancytopenia, or concern for marrow infiltration/dysplasia persists.

Normal reference ranges important for monitoring
- White blood cell (WBC) count: approximately 4.0–11.0 × 10^9/L
- Absolute neutrophil count (ANC): ≥1.8 × 10^9/L (neutropenia defined as ANC <1.5 × 10^9/L; severe neutropenia <0.5 × 10^9/L)
- Hemoglobin: males 13.5–17.5 g/dL, females 12.0–15.5 g/dL
- Platelets: 150–450 × 10^9/L

Monitoring schedules and action thresholds
Monitoring frequency should be tailored to the drug, dose, and patient factors:
- Azathioprine/6-MP: CBC at baseline; then weekly for the first month, every 2 weeks for months 2–3, and then monthly for months 4–6; thereafter every 1–3 months if stable. TPMT (thiopurine methyltransferase) genotype or phenotype should be checked before starting; TPMT deficiency mandates dose reduction or alternative therapy.
- Methotrexate: baseline CBC, then every 1–2 weeks for the first month or two, then every 4–8 weeks when stable.
- Mycophenolate mofetil: baseline CBC and then monthly for 3–6 months, then every 1–3 months.
- Cyclophosphamide: CBC weekly during induction pulses and for several weeks after; consider more frequent checks in high-dose regimens.

Action thresholds:
- ANC <1.0 × 10^9/L generally prompts drug hold and evaluation for infection; ANC <0.5 × 10^9/L (severe neutropenia) requires urgent hold of myelosuppressive drugs, infectious workup, and often hospital-level management, including granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) in severe or febrile neutropenia.
- Platelets <50 × 10^9/L usually warrants holding the drug and hematology consultation; <20–30 × 10^9/L is a threshold for urgent intervention to prevent bleeding.
- Hemoglobin drop greater than 2 g/dL or symptomatic anemia prompts investigation for hemolysis, marrow suppression, or bleeding.

Management principles
First steps are drug cessation or dose reduction and supportive care. For marrow suppression, consider G-CSF for neutropenia, transfusions for symptomatic anemia or severe thrombocytopenia, and hematology consultation for persistent pancytopenia or atypical features. For suspected DIHA or AIHA, stop the offending agent, confirm with Coombs testing, and treat with corticosteroids and supportive red cell transfusion as needed. Identify and treat coexisting infections (e.g., parvovirus B19 causing PRCA). Rechallenge or switching agents should consider severity of reaction, availability of alternatives, and the presence of predisposing genetic factors (e.g., TPMT testing).

# Hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity with immunosuppressants

Hepatic and renal toxicities are common complications of immunosuppressive therapy that can range from mild, transient enzyme elevations to fulminant liver failure or irreversible kidney injury. Understanding drug-specific mechanisms, monitoring protocols, and thresholds for intervention is critical to prevent permanent organ damage.

Hepatotoxicity
Drugs commonly associated with liver injury include methotrexate, azathioprine, leflunomide, high-dose corticosteroids (fatty liver), certain antibiotics used in combination, and some biologics. Mechanisms include direct hepatocellular toxicity, cholestatic injury, immune-mediated hepatitis, and reactivation of viral hepatitis (hepatitis B, less commonly hepatitis C). Some agents (e.g., methotrexate) can cause cumulative, dose-related hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis; others (e.g., isoniazid, sulfonamides, azathioprine) are associated with idiosyncratic or immune-mediated reactions.

Monitoring and normal ranges
- Baseline ALT and AST, alkaline phosphatase, total bilirubin, and albumin before initiating therapy.
- Normal reference ranges: ALT (approximately 7–56 U/L), AST (approximately 10–40 U/L), alkaline phosphatase 44–147 U/L, total bilirubin 0.1–1.2 mg/dL (laboratory-specific).
- For methotrexate: baseline LFTs, then every 1–2 weeks during dose escalation and every 1–3 months when stable. Consider baseline hepatic imaging or fibrosis assessment when risk factors (alcohol use, obesity, diabetes) exist.
- For azathioprine and leflunomide: baseline and periodic (e.g., monthly for first 3 months, then periodically) LFTs.

Clinical decision points
- Mild transaminase elevations (<2–3 × upper limit of normal, ULN) without symptoms may be observed with closer monitoring.
- Persistent ALT/AST elevations >3 × ULN usually require holding or reducing the drug and investigating other causes (viral hepatitis reactivation, alcohol, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, other medications).
- Symptomatic hepatitis, jaundice, or progressive enzyme rise demands immediate cessation and hepatology consultation.

Special considerations: viral hepatitis
- Screen for hepatitis B (HBsAg, anti-HBc, anti-HBs) and hepatitis C (anti-HCV and confirmatory RNA when positive) before initiating immunosuppression. Immunosuppression (particularly B-cell depleting therapy and steroids) can reactivate resolved or occult HBV; prophylactic antiviral therapy (e.g., entecavir, tenofovir) for HBsAg-positive or anti-HBc–positive patients per hepatology guidance reduces risk.
- Monitor HBV DNA periodically in at-risk patients.

Nephrotoxicity
Nephrotoxicity may result from direct tubular injury, hemodynamic changes, crystal nephropathy, interstitial nephritis, thrombotic microangiopathy, or glomerular disease. Agents with notable renal effects:
- Calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine, tacrolimus): cause dose-dependent vasoconstriction of afferent arterioles, reducing renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate (GFR); chronic use causes arteriolar hyalinosis and chronic kidney disease. Toxicity is closely related to blood concentration and drug interactions.
- Cyclophosphamide: can cause hemorrhagic cystitis and, rarely, acute and chronic kidney injury.
- IV immunoglobulin (IVIG): may precipitate osmotic nephrosis or acute kidney injury in patients with underlying renal impairment, especially formulations with sucrose stabilizer.
- Drug-induced interstitial nephritis: beta-lactams, NSAIDs, proton pump inhibitors, certain biologics may cause immune-mediated tubulointerstitial nephritis.

Monitoring and thresholds
- Baseline serum creatinine and eGFR prior to initiating nephrotoxic agents; urinalysis to detect hematuria or proteinuria.
- Routine monitoring frequency depends on agent: for CNIs, check serum creatinine and drug trough levels frequently (e.g., twice weekly early, then every 1–3 months when stable); for cyclophosphamide, monitor renal function and urinalysis before each cycle.
- Normal serum creatinine: roughly 0.6–1.3 mg/dL (age- and sex-dependent); interpret relative changes from baseline and use eGFR.
- An increase in serum creatinine >30% from baseline or an absolute rise above expected for the patient should prompt dose adjustment and evaluation for other causes (volume depletion, obstruction, drug interaction).

Management
- For CNI nephrotoxicity: reduce dose or consider switching to non-nephrotoxic alternatives; correct contributing factors (volume status, blood pressure, drug interactions) and monitor levels. In suspected thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) with hemolysis and schistocytes, stop the agent and pursue urgent hematology evaluation.
- For methotrexate-related renal dysfunction: discontinue drug if creatinine rises substantially; methotrexate is renally excreted and accumulation increases toxicity.
- For cyclophosphamide: aggressive hydration and mesna (in certain regimens) reduce hemorrhagic cystitis risk; evaluate for urinary RBCs and hematuria between doses.

# Infection risk and opportunistic infections (presentation and management)

Immunosuppressive therapies increase susceptibility to infections both by diminishing host immune defenses and by altering the balance of microbial flora. The degree and pattern of risk vary with the agent, dose, duration, combination therapy, and patient comorbidities. Clinicians must combine an understanding of typical presentations, targeted screening, prophylaxis, and early empiric therapy to reduce morbidity and mortality.

General principles and risk stratification
- Glucocorticoids at moderate-to-high doses, especially prolonged prednisone-equivalent ≥20 mg/day, impair neutrophil function, macrophage activation, and cell-mediated immunity, increasing risk for bacterial infections and opportunistic pathogens.
- Antimetabolites and alkylators suppress lymphoid and myeloid compartments, increasing risk of neutropenic infections (bacterial and fungal).
- Calcineurin inhibitors impair T-cell function and increase risk of viral and certain fungal infections.
- B-cell depleting agents (rituximab) predispose to hypogammaglobulinemia and recurrent sinopulmonary infections, hepatitis B reactivation, and rarely progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) associated with JC virus.
- JAK inhibitors and some biologics targeting cytokines may increase susceptibility to herpes zoster, tuberculosis reactivation, and other intracellular pathogens.

Common and opportunistic infections to consider
- Typical bacterial infections: skin/soft tissue, respiratory tract, urinary tract; presentation may be blunted (e.g., minimal fever).
- Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP/PCP): risk increased with prolonged high-dose steroids and combination immunosuppression. Presentation includes progressive dyspnea, nonproductive cough, hypoxemia with diffuse bilateral interstitial infiltrates on chest imaging. Diagnosis by induced sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage with PCR or immunofluorescence; elevated serum beta-D-glucan supports the diagnosis. First-line therapy: trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP–SMX) high-dose for 21 days (with adjunctive corticosteroids for moderate–severe hypoxemia). Prophylaxis: TMP–SMX (single-strength daily or three times weekly) for patients at moderate-to-high risk.
- Cytomegalovirus (CMV): disease may present with fever, cytopenias, hepatitis, colitis, pneumonitis. Diagnosis by quantitative PCR (CMV DNAemia) and tissue biopsy for organ disease. Treatment: ganciclovir or valganciclovir; consider prophylaxis in high-risk settings.
- Fungal infections: invasive candidiasis and aspergillosis in profoundly immunosuppressed patients; diagnosis via culture, galactomannan, and imaging; treat with echinocandins or mold-active azoles depending on organism.
- Nocardia: subacute pulmonary or disseminated infection with brain abscesses in patients on prolonged steroids or calcineurin inhibitors; diagnosis via culture and molecular methods; treat with TMP–SMX (often prolonged therapy).
- Herpesviridae: varicella zoster virus (VZV) reactivation (shingles) is common; prophylactic antiviral therapy (acyclovir/valacyclovir) is considered in some high-risk regimens.
- Tuberculosis: reactivation risk with TNF inhibitors and some other agents. Screen with interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) or tuberculin skin test and treat latent TB before starting high-risk agents.

Screening and prophylactic measures
- Vaccination: administer appropriate inactivated vaccines before initiating immunosuppression when possible. Live vaccines are generally contraindicated during significant immunosuppression; any live vaccines should be given at least 4 weeks before starting therapy when feasible.
- TB screening (IGRA or TST) prior to biologics/CNI initiation for high-risk agents.
- Hepatitis B screening (HBsAg, anti-HBc) especially before B-cell depleting therapies. Offer antiviral prophylaxis for patients at risk.
- PJP prophylaxis with TMP–SMX for patients receiving prolonged high-dose corticosteroids or combination immunosuppression; alternatives (dapsone, atovaquone, pentamidine) for those intolerant to TMP–SMX.
- Consider antifungal prophylaxis in profoundly neutropenic patients or those with prior invasive fungal infection.

Recognition and early management of infection
- Maintain a high index of suspicion: immunosuppressed patients may present atypically with subtle signs (e.g., absence of fever).
- Rapid diagnostic testing: blood cultures, imaging (chest radiograph or CT), viral PCRs (CMV, HSV), antigen assays (galactomannan, beta-D-glucan), and site-specific cultures/biopsies.
- Early empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics in febrile neutropenia, following institutional guidelines; for severe sepsis, treat based on likely pathogens and local antibiograms.
- For opportunistic infections, initiate pathogen-specific therapy promptly once suspected; consider infectious disease consultation for complex cases or treatment of CMV, fungal infections, or CNS involvement.
- Adjust or temporarily halt immunosuppressive therapy in the setting of severe infection; decisions must balance infection control with the risk of disease flare.

# Drug–drug interactions and peri-procedural considerations

Understanding pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interactions is essential for safe immunosuppressive therapy. Many immunosuppressive agents are metabolized by cytochrome P450 enzymes, transported by P-glycoprotein, or interact with metabolic pathways (e.g., TPMT) thereby producing clinically significant interactions with commonly used drugs.

Key interaction mechanisms and examples
- Cytochrome P450 (CYP3A4) inhibitors (azole antifungals, macrolides like clarithromycin, some calcium channel blockers, protease inhibitors) increase serum levels of calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine, tacrolimus) and mTOR inhibitors (sirolimus), raising risk of nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and other adverse effects. Conversely, CYP3A4 inducers (rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, St. John’s wort) decrease levels and risk graft/disease activity.
- TPMT deficiency increases active thiopurine metabolites and risk of myelosuppression with azathioprine/6-MP. Allopurinol inhibits xanthine oxidase and shifts 6-MP metabolism toward active metabolites, requiring significant azathioprine dose reductions (often to one-third to one-quarter) if coadministration is necessary; avoid unless carefully managed.
- Methotrexate interactions: NSAIDs, penicillins, sulfonamides, and trimethoprim can decrease renal clearance of methotrexate and increase toxicity. Proton pump inhibitors can modestly affect methotrexate elimination. Concurrent use of high-dose NSAIDs and methotrexate increases risk of mucositis, myelosuppression, and renal injury.
- Statins with CNIs increase statin levels and risk of myopathy/rhabdomyolysis; select statins with less interaction (e.g., pravastatin) or reduce the dose and monitor CK.
- Live vaccines with immunosuppression: live-attenuated vaccines (e.g., MMR, varicella, live zoster) are contraindicated in patients with significant immunosuppression. Inactivated vaccines may have reduced efficacy; consider timing before therapy initiation to maximize immunogenicity.

Peri-procedural management
- Vaccination timing: ideally, administer recommended vaccines at least 2–4 weeks before starting immunosuppression (longer for live vaccines, generally ≥4 weeks). For patients already on immunosuppression, give inactivated vaccines when possible; response may be suboptimal.
- Elective surgery: consider holding certain immunosuppressants perioperatively to reduce infectious risk and promote healing. There is no universal rule, but common approaches:
  - Methotrexate: many rheumatology guidelines suggest continuing low-dose methotrexate perioperatively is safe and may reduce risk of disease flare, but decisions should be individualized.
  - Azathioprine/mycophenolate: often continued for moderate procedures but may be held for major surgeries if infection risk is high.
  - Biologics: holding one dosing cycle before planned surgery (often 1–2 times the dosing interval) is common, resuming after wound healing and absence of infection; coordinate with surgical and specialty teams.
  - High-dose steroids: avoid abrupt withdrawal; use stress-dose steroids perioperatively if chronic steroid exposure >3 weeks to prevent adrenal insufficiency.
- Antibiotic prophylaxis: follow surgical prophylaxis guidelines; may need to broaden coverage for specific infections in profoundly immunosuppressed patients.
- Periprocedural antiviral prophylaxis when indicated (e.g., herpes zoster prevention) for high-risk patients undergoing certain immunosuppressive regimens.

Clinical decision points in drug interactions
- Routinely review medication lists for CYP interactions, overlapping toxicities (e.g., myelosuppression, hepatotoxicity), and cumulative immunosuppression when adding new agents.
- Monitor therapeutic drug levels when available (tacrolimus, cyclosporine, sirolimus) and adjust dosing when interacting drugs are started or stopped.
- Use drug interaction resources and pharmacy consultation for complex polypharmacy scenarios.

# Infusion-related reactions (IVIG, rituximab) and premedication strategies

IV-administered immunomodulatory therapies can cause infusion-related adverse events ranging from mild headache and flushing to severe anaphylaxis, aseptic meningitis, acute renal failure, thromboembolism, or cytokine-release syndrome. Preparation, prevention, and early recognition are key.

IVIG infusion reactions
- Mechanisms and common reactions: reactions may be due to rate-dependent cytokine release, complement activation, osmotic effects of stabilizers (sucrose-containing preparations increase risk of renal injury), and preexisting anti-IgA antibodies in IgA-deficient recipients causing anaphylaxis.
- Typical presentations: headaches (sometimes severe), flushing, fever, chills, myalgias, nausea; less commonly aseptic meningitis, acute kidney injury, hemolytic anemia (IVIG can induce hemolysis via passive transfer of anti-A/B isoagglutinins), and thrombotic events.
- Risk factors for serious complications: renal insufficiency, high infusion rate, older age, vascular risk factors, sucrose-stabilized IVIG formulations, and known IgA deficiency with anti-IgA antibodies.
- Prevention and premedication: baseline assessment of renal function (serum creatinine, eGFR) and volume status. Use low-osmolarity, non-sucrose preparations for patients at risk. Premedication commonly includes acetaminophen and an antihistamine (H1 blocker); consider low-dose corticosteroids if prior reactions occurred. Start at low infusion rates and titrate slowly while monitoring vital signs.
- Management of reactions: pause infusion for moderate reactions, provide symptomatic treatment (antipyretics, antihistamines), and consider reinitiating at lower rate if benefits outweigh risks. For severe reactions (anaphylaxis), stop the infusion, administer intramuscular or intravenous epinephrine, secure airway, give fluid resuscitation, and seek urgent critical care.

Rituximab and other monoclonal antibody infusions
- Mechanisms: infusion-related reactions often result from cytokine release after target antigen engagement (e.g., CD20 B-cell lysis with rituximab) and IgE-mediated hypersensitivity in some patients.
- Typical presentation: fever, rigors, hypotension or hypertension, bronchospasm, rash, angioedema, and in severe cases cytokine-release syndrome with multi-organ dysfunction.
- Timing: most reactions occur during the first infusion and are less common and typically milder with subsequent infusions.
- Risk factors: high circulating tumor or targeted-cell burden (in oncology), first infusion, rapid infusion rate, and history of allergic reactions.
- Premedication strategies: standard premedication for rituximab typically includes acetaminophen, an H1-antagonist (e.g., diphenhydramine), and sometimes corticosteroids (e.g., methylprednisolone 100 mg IV) particularly when treating autoimmune disease or with prior reactions. For certain agents, H2 blockers and bronchodilators may be added.
- Infusion rate protocols: start at a slow rate per product monograph and gradually increase if no reactions occur. Observe patients closely during the early period of infusion.
- Management of reactions: mild to moderate reactions warrant temporary cessation, symptomatic therapy (antihistamines, bronchodilators, corticosteroids), and consideration of resuming at a lower rate. For severe anaphylaxis or life-threatening events, stop infusion permanently, treat per advanced cardiac life support protocols, and avoid re-exposure to the same agent unless there is a compelling reason and a desensitization protocol is available.

Additional considerations
- For patients with known IgA deficiency scheduled for IVIG, test for anti-IgA antibodies; use IgA-depleted products or alternative therapies if at risk of anaphylactic reactions.
- Thromboembolic risk with IVIG: minimize risk by ensuring adequate hydration, using the lowest effective dose and infusion rate, and addressing vascular risk factors.
- Aseptic meningitis post-IVIG: presents with severe headache, neck stiffness, photophobia; manage supportively and consider alternative therapies for future dosing.

Conclusion

Managing complications of immunosuppressive therapy requires anticipatory care: baseline risk assessment, targeted screening (e.g., TPMT, hepatitis, TB), individualized monitoring for hematologic, hepatic, and renal toxicities, and vigilant surveillance for infections. Awareness of pharmacologic interactions and peri-procedural strategies prevents many adverse outcomes, and appropriate premedication and infusion protocols reduce infusion-related morbidity. When complications occur, prompt diagnosis, cessation or adjustment of the offending agent, supportive care, and specialist consultation (hematology, infectious disease, nephrology, hepatology) form the foundation of safe management while preserving disease control.
