Introduction

The head and neck comprise a compact arrangement of vital structures—airway, digestive tract, neurovascular bundles, salivary glands, lymph nodes and an elaborate system of fascial planes. Knowledge of the layered anatomy and functional relationships is essential for understanding the patterns of soft tissue swelling, the routes of infection spread, mechanisms of airway compromise and dysphagia, and for planning safe endoscopic, percutaneous and surgical interventions. This chapter reviews the superficial and deep cervical fascia and the key deep compartments of the neck; the musculature that influences hyoid position and pharyngeal dynamics; the detailed anatomy of the pharynx, larynx and upper airway; the salivary glands and their ducts; neurovascular elements that supply sensation and motor control; the lymphatic drainage patterns that determine nodal metastasis and infection routes; and finally the anatomy most relevant to airway management, endoscopy and surgical access.

# Superficial and deep cervical fascia and compartments (pretracheal, retropharyngeal, parapharyngeal spaces)

The fascial layers of the neck create compartments that both constrain and guide the spread of infection, hemorrhage and neoplastic processes. The superficially visible platysma muscle lies within the superficial cervical fascia, which is contiguous with superficial fascia elsewhere and contains variable amounts of fat, cutaneous nerves, and the external jugular vein system. Deep to the superficial layer lies the investing layer of the deep cervical fascia, which encircles the neck and splits to enclose the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius muscles; this layer forms the roof over several potential spaces and is an important surgical landmark.

The deeper fascia traditionally is described as consisting of three primary layers: the investing layer, the pretracheal (visceral) fascia and the prevertebral fascia. The pretracheal fascia invests the visceral compartment—trachea, thyroid gland, larynx and esophagus—and extends into the thorax, where it blends with the fibrous pericardium. The prevertebral fascia invests the vertebral column and the deep muscles of the neck and continues laterally as the axillary sheath. Between the pretracheal and prevertebral layers lies the retropharyngeal space, which is clinically important because it provides a potential route for infections from the pharynx to descend into the mediastinum. The so-called "danger space" lies posterior to the retropharyngeal space, between the alar layer and the prevertebral fascia, and communicates inferiorly with the posterior mediastinum—allowing pathogens and inflammatory material to spread from the pharyngeal region down to the diaphragm.

Anatomic compartments of special clinical relevance include the parapharyngeal space, the retropharyngeal space and the pretracheal (or visceral) compartment. The parapharyngeal space is a potential inverted pyramid lateral to the pharynx; superiorly it reaches the skull base and inferiorly it tapers toward the hyoid bone. The parapharyngeal space is subdivided by the styloid process and associated muscles/tendons into prestyloid and poststyloid compartments; the poststyloid portion contains the carotid sheath and lower cranial nerves, so lesions here may present with neurovascular signs. Deep neck infections originating in the tonsil, teeth, or salivary glands often track into these spaces. The retropharyngeal space lies posterior to the pharynx and anterior to the alar fascia; it contains lymph nodes (the nodes of Rouvière) in children and is a common site of retropharyngeal abscess, which can cause bulging of the posterior pharyngeal wall and airway compromise. The pretracheal space surrounds the thyroid and upper trachea; swelling here presents anteriorly as midline or paramedian neck masses and may extend into the superior mediastinum.

From a pathophysiologic standpoint, the presence of these confined fascial spaces explains why infections often produce focal collections and localized signs (e.g., bulging lateral pharyngeal wall with parapharyngeal abscess), while breaches of a fascial layer or tracking into a communicating space produce rapid, sometimes dramatic spread (e.g., descending mediastinitis from a retropharyngeal infection). Clinically the recognition of which fascial space is involved guides imaging selection, needle vs open drainage, and urgency of airway control.

# Strap muscles, suprahyoid and infrahyoid muscles, and their relationships to neck soft tissue swelling

The strap (infrahyoid) and suprahyoid muscle groups are central to the mechanics of swallowing, airway protection, phonation and the positional relationships of neck soft tissues. The infrahyoid (strap) muscles—sternohyoid, sternothyroid, thyrohyoid and omohyoid—span from the sternum, scapula and thyroid cartilage to the hyoid bone; they act primarily to depress the hyoid and larynx following swallowing. The anterior compartment formed by these muscles, covered by the investing fascia, lies superficial to the pretracheal fascia and the thyroid gland. Edema, hematoma, goiter or infection in the visceral space deep to the strap muscles often first presents as anterior swelling that displaces or obscures the normal muscular contours.

Suprahyoid muscles—mylohyoid, geniohyoid, stylohyoid and digastric (anterior and posterior bellies)—elevate the hyoid and floor of mouth during the initial phase of swallowing. The mylohyoid forms a muscular sling separating the sublingual space above from the submandibular space below, and defects or dehiscence in the mylohyoid can permit spread of sublingual infections into the submandibular space and vice versa. The digastric tendon courses through a fibrous sling attached to the hyoid, and the posterior belly passes adjacent to the parotid and stylomandibular region—important considerations when mapping spread of parotid infections.

Functionally, these muscle groups control airway patency through their influence on hyoid and laryngeal position. Dysfunction—whether from nerve injury (e.g., hypoglossal nerve palsy affecting geniohyoid), mechanical displacement from space-occupying lesions, or edema—can result in dysphagia, impaired airway protection, aspiration risk or visible anterior neck swelling. In the context of rapidly expanding infections (for example, Ludwig's angina), firm cellulitis and edema of the submandibular and sublingual spaces tether and elevate the floor of mouth and tongue by fixing the suprahyoid apparatus, producing posterior displacement of the tongue base and acute airway obstruction. Understanding which muscles form the boundaries of potential spaces is therefore directly relevant to anticipating clinical collapse and selecting airway strategies.

# Pharynx, larynx and upper airway anatomy (vallecula, pyriform fossa, vocal cords, epiglottis)

The upper aerodigestive tract is anatomically and functionally subdivided into the nasopharynx, oropharynx and laryngopharynx (hypopharynx), and the larynx sits at the junction between the pharynx and the trachea. The oropharynx includes the tonsillar pillars, soft palate and the base of tongue; the vallecula is the mucosal recess between the base of tongue and the epiglottis—an important landmark during direct laryngoscopy and endotracheal intubation because the tip of a laryngoscope blade often sits in the vallecula to lift the epiglottis anteriorly. The pyriform fossa (pyriform sinus) are paired mucosal recesses lateral to the laryngeal inlet that funnel material during deglutition into the esophagus; foreign bodies or mucosal lesions in this region can produce referred otalgia due to shared innervation.

The larynx is composed of cartilages (thyroid, cricoid, arytenoids, epiglottis), intrinsic and extrinsic muscles, and mucosal coverings. The vocal cords (true vocal folds) are mucosal folds overlying the thyroarytenoid muscles and vocal ligaments; the space between the cords is the glottis. Above the true cords lie the false cords (vestibular folds) which provide some protection and resonance. The epiglottis, a leaf-shaped elastic cartilage, acts as a dynamic protective valve during swallowing, retroflexing to cover the laryngeal inlet. Clinically, inflammation of supraglottic structures (epiglottitis) causes dysphagia, drooling and inspiratory stridor, and can progress to rapid airway obstruction because edema is confined within the tight mucosal envelope.

Pitch, voice quality, airway protection and cough depend on coordinated motion of the vocal cords and arytenoids; recurrent laryngeal nerve dysfunction results in vocal cord immobility, hoarseness and risk of aspiration, whereas superior laryngeal nerve sensory deficits blunt cough and place the patient at aspiration risk. The precise anatomic relationships are crucial for both diagnosis and intervention: a lesion at the level of the aryepiglottic fold will present differently than one in the pyriform sinus or vallecula. On flexible nasoendoscopy, the normal adult arytenoids appose in phonation and are mobile on respiration—immobility indicates neuropathy, mechanical fixation, or edema.

From an airway management perspective, understanding these structures directs how you visualize the larynx, where to place retracting instruments, and how pathology alters landmarks. For example, large vallecular cysts can obscure the epiglottis; periglottic edema shifts landmarks and may necessitate awake fiberoptic intubation. Recognition of bleeding risks—tonsillar or pyriform region vascularity—also influences procedural planning.

# Salivary glands and ducts (submandibular, sublingual, parotid)

The major salivary glands—the parotid, submandibular and sublingual glands—are both a source of infection and spaces that communicate with the fascial planes of the neck. The parotid gland lies superficially, overlying the posterior mandible and extending superiorly to the zygomatic arch; the facial nerve (cranial nerve VII) divides within the parotid into its branches, making parotid surgery and infection management risky for facial paralysis. The parotid duct (Stensen duct) opens into the oral cavity opposite the second upper molar; obstruction (sialolithiasis) or ductal stenosis produces glandular swelling, pain with meals (mealtime syndrome) and may predispose to ascending infection.

The submandibular gland rests in the submandibular triangle, partly superficial and partly deep to the mylohyoid muscle, with its duct (Wharton's duct) running forward in the floor of mouth to open at the sublingual caruncle adjacent to the lingual frenulum. Because part of the gland is below the mylohyoid and part above, infections or stones can present as swelling either in the submandibular triangle or as sublingual swelling that elevates the tongue. The sublingual glands lie in the floor of mouth above the mylohyoid bone as multiple small glands that empty via the duct of Rivinus or via accessory ducts into Wharton's duct.

Clinically, sialadenitis presents with localized pain, swelling, erythema and occasionally pus expressed from the ductal orifice. Ultrasonography is the first-line imaging for sialolithiasis and acute sialadenitis; computed tomography provides excellent delineation of deep glandular extension and abscess. Because the submandibular space communicates with the parapharyngeal space and because the floor of mouth is a potential route to the airway, submandibular space infections can quickly result in tongue elevation and airway compromise (e.g., Ludwig's angina). Parotid infections and neoplasms present more laterally and posteriorly but can affect the facial nerve; metastatic disease may manifest as firm, fixed parotid masses or as intraparotid lymph nodes.

# Neurovascular structures (cranial nerves IX–XII, cervical plexus, carotid sheath contents)

A clear understanding of the cranial nerves and major cervical neurovascular bundles is necessary to interpret deficits seen in head and neck disease and to avoid iatrogenic injury during interventions. Cranial nerve IX (glossopharyngeal) provides sensory innervation to the oropharynx and posterior tongue, taste to the posterior one-third of the tongue, and motor input to the stylopharyngeus muscle; its dysfunction presents with impaired gag reflex and altered taste, and lesions can produce referred otalgia. Cranial nerve X (vagus) controls motor function of most pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles via its pharyngeal and recurrent laryngeal branches, contributes to sensory input of the larynx and carries parasympathetic fibers; vagal injury results in dysphonia, dysphagia and aspiration risk. Cranial nerve XI (spinal accessory) innervates the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius; injury causes shoulder droop and impaired head rotation. Cranial nerve XII (hypoglossal) supplies tongue muscles; unilateral palsy produces tongue deviation toward the side of the lesion and dysarthria/dysphagia.

The cervical plexus (C1–C4) provides cutaneous sensation to the anterior and lateral neck via superficial branches (e.g., lesser occipital, great auricular, transverse cervical, supraclavicular), and motor innervation to infrahyoid muscles via the ansa cervicalis (C1–C3). The ansa cervicalis runs along the carotid sheath and is sometimes identified and preserved or intentionally used during surgical nerve transfers.

The carotid sheath is a condensation of deep cervical fascia that encloses the common and internal carotid arteries, the internal jugular vein and the vagus nerve within its posterior-lateral aspect. The close relationship between artery, vein and nerve explains clinical syndromes when masses distort the sheath: pulsatile masses suggest vascular lesions; thrombosis or compression can produce cranial nerve dysfunction or cerebral ischemia. The internal jugular vein offers a route for retrograde spread of infection (septic thrombophlebitis), and the carotid bifurcation at approximately the level of C3–C4 is a critical surgical landmark. Knowledge of the course of the hypoglossal nerve as it crosses the carotid arteries is also important—injury during carotid surgery can lead to tongue weakness.

From a clinical perspective, head and neck infections that involve the poststyloid parapharyngeal space may present with cranial neuropathies (IX–XII) and Horner's syndrome due to sympathetic chain involvement. Similarly, malignancies at the skull base may produce lower cranial nerve palsies because of tight confinement of these nerves in their exit foramina. Preoperative mapping of these neurovascular structures by imaging and careful neurologic exam are essential for risk stratification.

# Lymphatic drainage of head and neck

Lymphatic drainage of the head and neck follows predictable patterns that guide the evaluation of infection and malignancy. The regional lymph nodes are classically grouped into levels I–VI for surgical and radiologic communication. Level I comprises submental (Ia) and submandibular (Ib) nodes. Level II nodes lie along the upper internal jugular chain from the skull base to the level of the hyoid; level III nodes occupy the mid-jugular region; level IV nodes are the low jugular nodes extending to the clavicle; level V comprises the posterior triangle nodes; level VI are the central compartment nodes around the thyroid and trachea.

Primary drainage sites are specific: the oral cavity largely drains to levels I–III; the oropharynx and tonsillar region commonly to level II and III; the hypopharynx and larynx drain to level II–IV; and the thyroid drains to central compartment (level VI) and paratracheal nodes. Cutaneous lesions of the scalp and lateral face often drain to level V and the posterior auricular nodes; the parotid contains intraglandular nodes that drain both to preauricular and upper cervical nodes. Knowledge of these pathways aids staging of head and neck cancer, selection of neck dissection levels, and interpretation of radiologic findings: for example, an enlarged level II node with unknown primary suggests oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal origin, while involvement of level IV nodes raises concern for laryngeal or hypopharyngeal disease.

In infection, the same anatomy explains why dental infections produce ipsilateral submandibular and submental lymphadenopathy, whereas tonsillitis causes tender level II nodes. Normal cervical lymph node size varies by location and age, but generally nodes larger than 1 cm (except for jugulodigastric and posterior auricular nodes where slightly larger size may be acceptable) or nodes with concerning features (hard, fixed, matted, progressively enlarging) require further evaluation. When evaluating septic or potentially malignant nodes, adjuncts include ultrasound, fine needle aspiration cytology, and cross-sectional imaging.

# Anatomy relevant to endoscopy, surgical access and airway management

Successful endoscopic and surgical procedures in the head and neck rely on precise knowledge of surface and deep anatomy, predictable landmarks and relationships that affect instrument passage and safety. For endoscopy and airway management, the external landmarks—mentum, hyoid bone (at level C3), thyroid cartilage (C4–C5), cricoid cartilage (C6), and sternal notch—help estimate internal levels. The vallecula and epiglottis are used during laryngoscopy to lift and expose the glottis; alternatively, direct lift of the epiglottis can be achieved in some cases. For flexible fiberoptic intubation, the nasal cavity, nasopharynx and oropharynx should be navigated with awareness of the adenoids, tonsils and tongue base that may obscure the view.

Awake fiberoptic intubation is indicated when high risk of difficult ventilation or intubation exists—for example, substantial anterior neck swelling, limited mouth opening, or distorted anatomy from tumors or infection. Contraindications or challenges include uncontrolled bleeding or excessive secretions that obscure optics. Emergency surgical access to the airway includes cricothyrotomy and tracheotomy. The cricothyroid membrane lies between the thyroid and cricoid cartilages and is preferred for rapid front-of-neck access because it is relatively avascular and superficial. Tracheotomy, performed lower at the tracheal rings, provides more durable access but is more technically demanding and has additional anatomic considerations (thyroid isthmus, large midline vessels, aberrant innominate artery). Recognition of the midline structures, vascular anomalies and previous surgery or radiation scarring is crucial; ultrasound can identify the cricoid, tracheal rings and thyroid isthmus and help avoid vascular injury.

Surgical approaches to deep neck spaces require knowledge of the relationship of the facial planes and neurovascular bundles. For drainage of a parapharyngeal abscess, a transcervical approach may be chosen if the collection is lateral and accessible, whereas a transoral approach is favored when the collection abuts the pharyngeal lumen and is amenable to endoscopic drainage—each approach carries distinct risks to nerves (e.g., glossopharyngeal, hypoglossal, facial) and vessels. When performing cervical lymph node dissections, level-specific anatomical knowledge allows selective removal while sparing non-involved structures such as the spinal accessory nerve to preserve shoulder function.

Intraoperative and bedside decision points frequently revolve around airway security: whether to attempt endotracheal intubation, perform awake fiberoptic intubation, proceed directly to surgical airway, or secure the airway before imaging or definitive drainage. Factors that raise concern include severe stridor, rapid progression of neck swelling, inability to lie supine, trismus, and signs of impending obstruction (tachypnea, use of accessory muscles, oxygen desaturation). Laboratory and imaging adjuncts—complete blood count (normal adult white blood cell count $4.0$–$11.0 \times 10^{9}$/L), markers of systemic inflammation such as C-reactive protein (CRP reference varies by lab but typically <5 mg/L), and computed tomography with contrast—help determine the presence and extent of abscess versus cellulitis and inform the timing and type of airway intervention.

Conclusion

An integrated understanding of the fascial planes, muscle groups, salivary glands, pharyngeal and laryngeal anatomy, neurovascular bundles and lymphatic pathways of the head and neck permits anticipation of disease spread, recognition of signs that predict airway compromise, and safe performance of endoscopic and surgical interventions. Clinicians must synthesize the anatomic knowledge with dynamic clinical findings—voice change, dysphagia, trismus, tracheal deviation, stridor, and progressive hypoxia—to determine the urgency and manner of airway management and to choose the most appropriate approach for drainage or definitive surgery.
