Introduction

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy characterized by a distinctive cutaneous eruption and immune-mediated injury to muscle, in which microvascular pathology and immune signaling pathways converge to produce selective tissue damage. Pathologically, dermatomyositis differs from the cytotoxic T cell–driven processes that predominate in polymyositis and immune-mediated necrotizing myopathies. Instead, dermatomyositis is best conceptualized as a complement- and interferon-driven microangiopathy of skin and muscle. Understanding the humoral and complement-mediated mechanisms, the importance of a type I interferon signature, and how these processes translate to the typical histologic findings — especially perifascicular atrophy and capillary loss — is essential for interpreting clinical presentations, laboratory results, and complications such as interstitial lung disease and cardiac involvement. This chapter integrates the molecular and cellular immunopathology of dermatomyositis with the downstream anatomic and physiologic consequences that explain clinical manifestations.

# Humoral and complement-mediated mechanisms (capillary injury, C5b-9 membrane attack complex deposition)

A central pathogenic feature of dermatomyositis is antibody- and complement-mediated injury to the endomysial and perimysial microvasculature. Early light microscopic and electron microscopic studies identified selective loss of capillaries in affected muscle and deposition of complement components in the endomysium and on endothelial cell surfaces. The membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9) deposited on endothelial cell membranes is a reproducible finding in affected muscle and skin. This suggests that activation of the classical and/or lectin complement pathways — often triggered by immune complexes and antibody binding — leads to assembly of the MAC and consequent endothelial cell injury.

Endothelial injury from MAC has several immediate consequences. Direct sublethal or lytic injury to endothelial cells increases vascular permeability and can trigger apoptosis or detachment of endothelial cells, leading to microvascular rarefaction and thrombosis. Loss of capillaries produces regional tissue hypoperfusion, particularly affecting the distal portions of muscle fascicles that are relatively dependent on local microcirculatory flow. On immunohistochemistry, deposition of C5b-9 is typically found on the endothelial surface and sometimes within perivascular spaces, even in muscle with relatively little inflammatory cell infiltration. Complement activation also generates anaphylatoxins (C3a, C5a) that recruit and activate inflammatory leukocytes, amplifying local inflammation and promoting further endothelial damage.

The humoral drivers of this complement activation are incompletely defined but likely include both myositis-specific and -associated autoantibodies as well as immune complexes that form in skin and muscle microvessels. B cells and plasma cells are often present in the perimysium and perivascular regions and provide a local source of antibody. Fc receptor–bearing innate immune cells — macrophages and neutrophils — are recruited by complement fragments and engage antibody-opsonized structures, contributing to bystander tissue injury through release of reactive oxygen species and proteases. Thus, the humoral response in dermatomyositis functions not only as a source of antigen-specific immunity but also as a trigger for complement cascade activation that targets the endothelium and microvasculature.

# Type I interferon signature and downstream inflammatory cascades

Dermatomyositis exhibits a pronounced type I interferon (IFN-I) gene expression signature in affected tissues and peripheral blood. IFN-I (principally interferon-alpha and interferon-beta) signaling is induced by nucleic acid–sensing pathways in plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), myeloid dendritic cells, and other innate immune cells that recognize viral RNA/DNA or self-nucleic acids in the context of immune complexes. Evidence for IFN-I activity includes upregulation of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) such as MX1 (encoding Myxovirus resistance protein A, MxA), IFIT1, ISG15, and OAS family members; immunohistochemical detection of MxA protein in muscle fibers and dermal keratinocytes is often used as a tissue marker of IFN-I activity.

The IFN-I response links innate sensing to adaptive immunity and to vascular injury in several ways. IFN-I promotes maturation and antigen-presenting function of dendritic cells, enhances B cell differentiation to antibody-producing plasma cells, and upregulates expression of MHC class I on muscle fibers and on endothelial cells. Overexpression of MHC class I in skeletal muscle can render muscle fibers more immunogenic and susceptible to immune-mediated damage. IFN-I signaling also induces a prothrombotic and proinflammatory endothelial phenotype: it upregulates adhesion molecules (e.g., ICAM-1, VCAM-1), chemokines (e.g., CXCL9, CXCL10), and molecules that perturb nitric oxide signaling, all of which promote leukocyte recruitment and microvascular dysfunction.

Importantly, the IFN-I pathway contributes to the cutaneous features of dermatomyositis. Keratinocytes exposed to IFN-I upregulate ISGs and chemokines that perpetuate local immune cell infiltration and epidermal injury, generating the typical heliotrope rash, Gottron papules, and photosensitive eruptions. Some myositis-specific autoantibody subsets are associated with distinctive IFN signatures; for example, anti-MDA5 disease displays strong IFN-I pathway activation and particular clinical associations (e.g., rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease). Clinically, measurement of circulating ISG expression or surrogate markers (e.g., serum CXCL10) has been explored as a biomarker of disease activity and therapeutic response.

# Perifascicular atrophy and selective fiber injury

Perifascicular atrophy — the focal atrophy of muscle fibers located at the periphery of fascicles — is a pathognomonic histologic hallmark of dermatomyositis. This pattern reflects selective ischemic and inflammatory injury to fibers most susceptible to reduced capillary perfusion. The microvascular injury and capillary dropout described above produce uneven tissue perfusion; the distal portions of muscle fascicles are anatomically more vulnerable because they are supplied by terminal capillary networks. As capillaries are lost or their function compromised, perifascicular fibers experience relative hypoxia and nutrient deprivation, resulting in fiber shrinkage, loss of oxidative enzymes on histochemistry, and eventual atrophy.

At the molecular level, affected perifascicular fibers commonly show upregulation of ISGs and MxA expression, as well as increased expression of MHC class I on the sarcolemma. The combination of an ischemic milieu and an IFN-primed, antigen-presenting muscle fiber can lead to further immune targeting. The pattern of fiber injury is typically more pronounced among type I (slow-twitch, oxidative) fibers because of their reliance on oxidative metabolism and microvascular supply, though exact fiber-type susceptibility shows some variability between patients. Electron microscopy may reveal endothelial cell swelling, basement membrane reduplication, and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates in affected regions.

Clinically, perifascicular atrophy explains why serum muscle enzymes may be mildly to moderately elevated despite striking histologic abnormalities: ischemic atrophy does not always produce frank necrosis. It also explains the distribution of weakness, which often affects proximal muscles but may show unusual patterns if microvascular compromise is patchy. Recognition of perifascicular atrophy on muscle biopsy supports a diagnosis of dermatomyositis and helps distinguish it from other inflammatory myopathies in which endomysial cytotoxic T cell–mediated myofiber necrosis, rather than microvascular ischemia, predominates.

# Mechanisms of subcutaneous edema in inflammatory myopathies

Subcutaneous edema is a frequent clinical and radiologic finding in inflammatory myopathies, including dermatomyositis, and arises from several convergent mechanisms that increase fluid accumulation in the interstitial spaces. The principal drivers are increased vascular permeability from endothelial injury, local inflammation with cytokine-mediated vasodilation, lymphatic dysfunction, and hypoalbuminemia in certain systemic states.

Endothelial injury from complement activation (C5b-9), IFN-I–induced endothelial activation, and inflammatory mediators (e.g., histamine, bradykinin, prostaglandins) increases the transvascular passage of plasma proteins and fluid into the interstitial compartment. Complement-mediated pore formation in endothelial membranes and cytokine-driven reorganization of interendothelial junctions both facilitate leakage. An increase in interstitial oncotic pressure from protein extravasation sustains edema. In addition, the inflammatory milieu recruits neutrophils and macrophages whose proteolytic enzymes can damage the extracellular matrix and lymphatic channels, diminishing lymphatic drainage capacity.

Hypoalbuminemia, although not a primary feature of dermatomyositis, can exacerbate edema when present; reduced plasma oncotic pressure favors movement of fluid into tissues. Moreover, impaired muscle pump function due to weakness reduces venous return, promoting dependent edema. Imaging modalities such as MRI may demonstrate T2-weighted hyperintensity in subcutaneous tissues and muscles consistent with edema; ultrasonography can similarly reveal increased subcutaneous thickness and fluid. Treating the underlying inflammation tends to reduce edema, but in some patients lymphatic repair or adjunctive measures (compression, physiotherapy) are required.

# Relationship between autoantibodies and pathogenic pathways

Autoantibodies play multiple roles in dermatomyositis: as diagnostic biomarkers, as predictors of clinical phenotype (cutaneous involvement, interstitial lung disease, malignancy risk), and potentially as contributors to pathogenesis. Myositis-specific autoantibodies (MSAs) such as anti-Mi-2, anti-MDA5 (also called anti-CADM-140), anti-TIF1-gamma (anti-p155/140), anti-NXP-2 (anti-MJ), and anti-SAE are associated with distinct clinical syndromes. Myositis-associated autoantibodies (MAAs) — for example anti-Ro/SSA, anti-La/SSB, and anti-U1RNP — often indicate overlap with systemic autoimmune disorders.

The pathogenicity of these antibodies varies and remains incompletely resolved. Some, like anti-MDA5, are strongly associated with a phenotype of cutaneous ulceration and rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease, suggesting a role in promoting vascular injury and dysregulated innate immune signaling. Anti-TIF1-gamma and anti-NXP-2 are associated with cancer-associated dermatomyositis in adults, and their presence likely reflects an immune response to tumor antigens that cross-reacts with muscle or skin antigens. Whether these antibodies directly mediate tissue injury or primarily reflect B cell activation and a broader autoreactive immune state is debated. For instance, circulating immune complexes containing nucleic acids and autoantibodies can stimulate plasmacytoid dendritic cells and trigger IFN-I production, creating a feed-forward inflammatory loop.

Some autoantibodies target intracellular antigens (e.g., nuclear or cytoplasmic proteins) and are less likely to bind native extracellular targets, suggesting an indirect pathogenic role. Others may form immune complexes that deposit in the microvasculature and activate complement locally. Furthermore, certain autoantibody specificities correlate with specific histologic and molecular features; for example, anti-MDA5–positive patients often show strong IFN-I signatures and vascular injury, while anti-Mi-2 is more associated with classic cutaneous disease and a favorable response to therapy. Clinically, identifying the autoantibody profile helps stratify patients for risk of interstitial lung disease, malignancy screening, and expected treatment responsiveness.

# Overlap syndromes and secondary organ involvement (lung, GI tract, heart)

Dermatomyositis is a systemic disease and frequently overlaps with other autoimmune conditions or affects organs beyond skin and muscle. The most clinically consequential extracutaneous involvements are interstitial lung disease (ILD), cardiac manifestations, and gastrointestinal (GI) complications, each reflecting extension of immune-mediated vascular and inflammatory mechanisms into other tissues.

Interstitial lung disease is the most common and often most serious visceral manifestation. Immune-mediated injury to the pulmonary interstitium and the pulmonary microvasculature — mediated by autoantibodies, complement activation, and IFN-I driven inflammation — can produce a spectrum from chronic fibrosing ILD to rapidly progressive organizing pneumonia or diffuse alveolar damage. Certain autoantibodies (notably anti-MDA5) are strongly associated with rapidly progressive ILD. Clinically, screening involves pulmonary function testing with diffusion capacity (DLCO) and high-resolution chest CT (HRCT); DLCO reduction and reticular or ground-glass opacities on HRCT raise concern. Management and prognosis hinge on early recognition, as respiratory failure can be swift and refractory.

Cardiac involvement results from microvascular injury, myocarditis, conduction system disease, and accelerated atherosclerosis. Myocarditis arises from immune cell infiltration and possibly antibody- or complement-mediated endothelial injury within coronary microvessels and myocardial capillaries. Patients may present with arrhythmias, conduction block, heart failure, or elevated cardiac biomarkers. Troponin I and T can be elevated; however, skeletal muscle disease can confound interpretation of troponin T because of cross-reactivity, so troponin I is generally preferred. Reference upper limits vary by assay, but troponin I values >0.04 ng/mL are often considered suggestive of myocardial injury depending on laboratory standards. Echocardiography, ECG, and cardiac MRI are useful for detection and monitoring.

Gastrointestinal involvement is less common but can be severe when present, particularly in juvenile dermatomyositis or in disease complicated by vasculopathy. Microvascular occlusion of mesenteric vessels can cause mucosal ischemia, ulceration, bleeding, and in extreme cases, perforation. Dysphagia is a frequent clinical problem due to involvement of oropharyngeal and esophageal striated muscle and from cricopharyngeal dysfunction; this results in aspiration risk and malnutrition. Assessment includes swallow studies, endoscopic evaluation when indicated, and nutritional support.

Because dermatomyositis may coexist with systemic autoimmune diseases (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus, systemic sclerosis) or with malignancy (particularly in adults with certain autoantibody profiles), a broad approach to evaluation is warranted. Baseline and periodic screening for ILD with spirometry and HRCT in at-risk patients, cardiac evaluation for those with symptoms or elevated biomarkers, and attention to GI symptoms are important components of care. Recognition that the same immunopathologic drivers — complement-mediated microangiopathy and type I interferon–dominated inflammation — can produce multi-organ effects helps to rationalize surveillance and treatment strategies.

Conclusion

Dermatomyositis is fundamentally a microvascular and interferon-driven disease in which humoral immunity, complement activation, and innate immune signaling converge to cause selective injury of skin and muscle. Complement-mediated endothelial injury, a robust type I interferon signature, and resultant microvascular loss explain the characteristic histology of perifascicular atrophy, the clinical features of edema and skin disease, and the propensity for extracutaneous manifestations such as interstitial lung disease and myocarditis. Autoantibodies serve as both important clinical biomarkers and potential mechanistic contributors, linking adaptive immunity with innate inflammatory cascades. Appreciating these interconnected pathways is critical for interpreting diagnostic studies, prognosticating complications, and selecting immunomodulatory therapies targeted at the appropriate components of the immune response.
