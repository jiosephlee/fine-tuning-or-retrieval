Introduction

Nailfold capillaroscopy is a noninvasive, bedside-to-clinic technique for direct visualization of the microcirculation in the periungual region. By allowing repeated, magnified inspection of dermal capillary loops at the nailfold, capillaroscopy provides pathophysiologic insight into microvascular injury, repair and remodeling that occur in systemic microangiopathies—most notably systemic sclerosis (SSc), idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (including dermatomyositis), and other connective tissue diseases (CTDs) that present with Raynaud phenomenon. For the clinician and trainee, understanding the technique, the spectrum of normal findings, the characteristic patterns of disease, and the implications of specific abnormalities is essential to apply capillaroscopy effectively for diagnosis, prognostication and longitudinal monitoring.

This chapter reviews practical technique and normal capillaroscopic findings, describes the major disease patterns (especially SSc and dermatomyositis), explains the pathophysiology and interpretation of microhemorrhages, avascular areas, dilation and dropout, and summarizes clinical utility and limitations.

# Technique and normal capillaroscopic findings

Technique

Nailfold capillaroscopy examines the periungual dermal capillary loops where capillaries lie parallel to the skin surface—making their entire loop morphology visible. Common equipment ranges from simple dermatoscopes (10x) to stereomicroscopes and dedicated videocapillaroscopes that provide magnification typically in the 50–200x range and permit image capture and measurement. Videocapillaroscopy has become the reference standard for research and serial clinical assessment because it allows image storage, quantitative analysis and standardized magnification.

Patient preparation and environment matter for reproducible results. Allow patients to acclimatize to a stable room temperature (about 20–22°C) for 10–20 minutes because cold or stress can induce transient vasoconstriction and alter capillary morphology. Instruct the patient to avoid smoking, caffeine and vasoactive medications (if clinically permissible) for several hours before the exam. Position the hand comfortably at heart level to minimize hydrostatic effects. Apply a drop of immersion oil (or a water-based gel) to the nailfold to increase transparency and reduce light reflection. Examine digits 2–5 (index to little finger) of both hands; the thumb is usually avoided because of different anatomy. For each finger, inspect the distal row of capillary loops within 1 mm of the proximal nailfold. Capture images from several adjacent fields to sample adequately.

Image acquisition and reporting should document magnification, side and digit, and the fields examined. Quantitative metrics commonly reported include capillary density (number of capillary loops per linear millimeter), presence and size of dilated or "giant" capillaries, hemorrhages, avascular areas, loop morphology (tortuous, meandering, neoangiogenic/bushy or bizarre loops) and presence of microvascular ramifications or tortuosity. Semi-quantitative scoring systems (0–3 scales) are widely used in clinical studies for density, hemorrhages, dilatation and neoangiogenesis.

Normal capillaroscopic findings

A normal nailfold capillary pattern varies with age, gender and ethnicity but shares consistent features. The capillary loops are arranged in a regular, parallel “comb-like” pattern with a uniform semi-oval or hairpin shape. Typical findings in healthy adult subjects include:

- Capillary density: Commonly cited normal values are approximately 9–14 capillaries per linear millimeter in the distal row of the nailfold, though published ranges vary with digit and population. Values below this range may indicate capillary loss, while higher density may reflect technical factors or physiologic variation.
- Loop morphology: Capillaries are typically thin, hairpin-shaped loops oriented parallel to each other with minimal tortuosity. Small degrees of tortuosity or crossing are common and not necessarily pathologic.
- Diameters: Normal loop width is small and fairly uniform; markedly dilated loops are not seen. In practice, capillary dilation is considered significant when diameters exceed typical microvascular dimensions or when conspicuous “giant” capillaries are present (see below).
- Hemodynamics: No microhemorrhages or only occasional tiny hemosiderin dots may be observed. Petechial or hemorrhagic spots are uncommon in healthy nailfolds.
- Neoangiogenesis: Little or no ramified or bushy neoangiogenesis is present. Occasional tortuous loops can occur with aging.

Interpreting what is “normal” requires understanding that variability exists; therefore, a formal report should compare findings to reference ranges appropriate to the technique and population and, when possible, to prior studies in the same patient.

# Patterns seen in systemic sclerosis, dermatomyositis and other connective tissue diseases

Overview of scleroderma (systemic sclerosis) pattern

One of the most clinically useful applications of capillaroscopy is the recognition of the scleroderma pattern, a spectrum of microvascular changes that reflect a characteristic microangiopathy. The Scleroderma (SSc) pattern is classically divided into early, active and late phases:

- Early pattern: Scattered giant capillaries (markedly dilated apical loops) and a few microhemorrhages are present, but capillary density remains relatively preserved and architectural disorganization is minimal.
- Active pattern: Frequent giant capillaries and microhemorrhages with a moderate reduction in capillary density are seen. Capillary architecture begins to show irregular spacing.
- Late pattern: Severe capillary loss with extensive avascular areas, disorganization of the capillary array and evidence of neoangiogenesis with bizarre, ramified capillaries (bushy or branching forms) occur. Hemorrhages and giant capillaries may be less prominent at this stage because the capillary bed is largely destroyed.

The presence of an SSc pattern on capillaroscopy is highly specific for systemic sclerosis and strongly predictive when combined with Raynaud phenomenon and autoantibody findings. The 2013 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for systemic sclerosis do not require capillaroscopy for diagnosis, but a capillaroscopic scleroderma pattern is recognized as a strong supportive feature in clinical assessment and can help differentiate primary from secondary Raynaud phenomenon.

Dermatomyositis and idiopathic inflammatory myopathies

In idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, particularly dermatomyositis, capillaroscopic abnormalities reflect an immune-mediated microangiopathy affecting skin and muscle. Typical findings include:

- Dilated and tortuous capillary loops; giant capillaries may be present but are generally less prominent than in scleroderma.
- Capillary dropout with reduced density, often correlating with active muscle or skin disease.
- Abnormal loops with ramification or branching in prolonged disease.
- Periungual erythema and periungual telangiectasias are commonly noted clinically and correlate with capillary loss and telangiectatic changes on capillaroscopy.

In dermatomyositis, capillary loss correlates with disease activity and may parallel complement-mediated endothelial injury seen in biopsies. Certain myositis-specific autoantibodies (for example, anti-Mi-2, anti-MDA5) have been associated with distinct capillaroscopic features and different clinical phenotypes; however, patterns are heterogeneous and not pathognomonic.

Mixed connective tissue disease, systemic lupus erythematosus and other CTDs

Capillaroscopic patterns in other CTDs are more variable and often less specific than in SSc. Examples:

- Mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD): May show scleroderma-like changes, including dilated loops and variable capillary loss, reflecting overlap features.
- Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE): Nailfold capillary abnormalities are often nonspecific—mild tortuosity, decreased density, and occasional hemorrhages. A true "scleroderma pattern" can occur in some SLE patients with overlap features, but this is uncommon.
- Primary Raynaud phenomenon: Capillary morphology is typically normal or shows only nonspecific changes (mild tortuosity). The distinction between primary and secondary Raynaud's is a central clinical application of capillaroscopy: a scleroderma-pattern or marked capillary loss indicates secondary Raynaud’s likely due to CTD.
- Vasculitides and antiphospholipid syndrome: Findings are variable, sometimes showing microhemorrhages or capillary dropout related to focal ischemia, but no pathognomonic pattern.

Key point: The pattern-based approach (recognizing scleroderma patterns vs. non-specific changes) has high clinical utility in triaging patients with Raynaud phenomenon and suspected CTD and should always be interpreted in the clinical context including history, exam, serology and, when relevant, pulmonary evaluation.

# Microhemorrhages, avascular areas, capillary dilation and dropout

Microhemorrhages

Microhemorrhages appear as dark brown to black punctate or linear deposits adjacent to capillary loops and correspond to extravasated red blood cells or hemosiderin following endothelial or perivascular damage. They are most numerous in the early and active phases of the SSc pattern, where immune-mediated endothelial injury and increased permeability permit red-cell leakage. In dermatomyositis, microhemorrhages may also occur during active skin disease.

Clinical interpretation: The presence of multiple recent hemorrhages suggests ongoing active microvascular injury. Serial observations showing decreasing hemorrhages may indicate treatment response or natural evolution, whereas new or persistent hemorrhages may alert to continuing disease activity and increased risk of ischemic complications.

Avascular areas and capillary dropout

Avascular areas denote focal regions in the distal row in which capillary loops are absent over a measurable linear distance. Capillary dropout is quantified by reduced capillary density (fewer loops per mm) and by the size and number of avascular areas. Definitions vary, but clinically significant avascular areas typically represent a loss of adjacent capillary loops over distances of several tenths of a millimeter, and when numerous, they indicate severe microangiopathy.

Pathophysiology: Progressive endothelial cell injury, obliterative microangiopathy and failure of normal angiogenic repair mechanisms result in permanent loss of capillary loops. In SSc this is an important mechanism underlying digital ischemia, ulceration and the vasculopathic complications of the disease.

Clinical interpretation: Capillary dropout and large avascular areas are strongly associated with more severe disease manifestations, including a higher risk of digital ulcers, and correlate with systemic vascular complications such as pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) and interstitial lung disease (ILD) in SSc. Quantitative measures of density and avascular areas are useful prognostic markers and for monitoring progression.

Capillary dilation and "giant" capillaries

Capillary dilation refers to enlargement of the capillary loop, typically most evident at the apical loop. "Giant" or megacapillaries are defined by markedly enlarged apical loops; a commonly used threshold in research settings is an apical loop diameter exceeding about 50 µm, though exact numeric cutoffs depend on magnification and measurement technique. These giant capillaries are characteristic of early and active scleroderma patterns.

Pathophysiology: Dilation reflects endothelial dysfunction, impaired perivascular support, and increased intraluminal pressure within damaged loops. In early disease, dilation may represent a compensatory change before loss; in later disease, dilated loops may be destroyed, producing avascular areas.

Clinical interpretation: The presence of giant capillaries—particularly when multiple and combined with hemorrhages—supports a diagnosis of scleroderma-spectrum microangiopathy and, in an appropriate clinical context, systemic sclerosis. In dermatomyositis, dilated loops are also seen but giant capillaries are less specific.

Neoangiogenesis and bizarre loops

Neoangiogenesis denotes the formation of new, often abnormal capillary loops that appear as tortuous, ramified, bushy or “bizarre” shapes. These are more prominent in the late scleroderma pattern and reflect attempted but abnormal vascular repair.

Pathophysiology: Chronic ischemia stimulates angiogenic signaling, but the repair process in SSc is disordered, leading to defective, fragile and morphologically aberrant capillaries. The presence of ramified capillaries indicates longstanding injury and remodeling rather than acute activity.

Clinical interpretation: Neoangiogenesis signals chronicity and microvascular remodeling. It is associated with advanced peripheral microangiopathy and, when extensive, correlates with more severe systemic disease.

# Clinical utility in diagnosis, prognostication and monitoring

Diagnostic utility

Nailfold capillaroscopy is most valuable diagnostically in patients who present with Raynaud phenomenon, unexplained digital ischemia, or clinical features suggesting CTD. The central diagnostic uses are:

- Distinguishing primary from secondary Raynaud phenomenon: A normal capillaroscopic pattern supports primary Raynaud's, whereas scleroderma-pattern changes or marked capillary loss strongly suggest secondary Raynaud’s due to CTD.
- Supporting a diagnosis of systemic sclerosis: The presence of an SSc capillaroscopic pattern in a patient with Raynaud phenomenon and appropriate clinical features increases diagnostic confidence and can be an early marker of disease even before fibrosis develops. Capillaroscopic abnormalities may precede serologic markers and other manifestations.
- Identifying microangiopathy in dermatomyositis and other inflammatory myopathies: Capillary loss and dilated loops support the concept of a microvascular pathogenesis and can help corroborate clinical suspicion.
- Differentiating overlap syndromes: Patterns that combine scleroderma-like changes with other features can suggest mixed connective tissue disease or overlap syndromes.

Prognostication

Capillaroscopy provides prognostic information in several contexts:

- Risk of digital ulcers and ischemic complications: Reduced capillary density and larger avascular areas are associated with higher incidence of digital ulcers. Patients with extensive capillary loss require closer monitoring and aggressive prevention of ischemia.
- Prediction of internal organ involvement in systemic sclerosis: Several studies have shown correlations between the extent of capillary loss/abnormality and development of pulmonary arterial hypertension and interstitial lung disease. Patients with severe late-phase capillaroscopic changes have increased likelihood of systemic vasculopathy.
- Disease activity in inflammatory myopathies: In dermatomyositis, capillary density and the presence of hemorrhages may correlate with active disease and muscle involvement; improvements in capillary morphology may accompany clinical remission.

Monitoring and measuring response to therapy

One of the advantages of capillaroscopy is that it is repeatable and noninvasive, allowing serial assessment to monitor natural disease evolution and response to therapy. Practical monitoring uses both qualitative pattern recognition (improvement from “active” to “early” or more normal-appearing loops) and quantitative metrics (changes in capillary density, number of giant capillaries, hemorrhage counts, avascular area extent).

Examples of serial use:

- In SSc, therapy that modifies vascular injury (e.g., vasodilators, immunomodulatory agents in selected situations) may be associated with stabilization or modest improvement in capillary morphology, although reversal of late-stage avascular areas is uncommon. A reduction in hemorrhages and fewer new avascular areas may indicate disease control.
- In dermatomyositis, improvement in capillary density or reduction of hemorrhages can parallel improvement in muscle enzymes, strength and cutaneous disease, and may support tapering of immunosuppression.

Clinical decision points and integration with other data

Capillaroscopy should be integrated with clinical history, physical examination, serologic testing (antinuclear antibodies including SSc-specific autoantibodies such as anti-centromere, anti-topoisomerase I, anti-RNA polymerase III), and relevant imaging (e.g., echocardiography and pulmonary function tests for SSc). Specific decision points include:

- In a patient with Raynaud phenomenon and scleroderma-pattern capillaroscopy, proceed with targeted serologic testing and periodic screening for systemic sclerosis–related organ involvement (baseline and periodic pulmonary function tests, echocardiography, assessment for digital ulcers).
- In suspected dermatomyositis with capillary dropout, correlate with serum muscle enzyme levels (CK, aldolase), electromyography, muscle MRI and autoantibodies, and use capillaroscopy as a supportive metric of microvascular involvement and disease activity.
- Use capillaroscopy findings to stratify risk for digital ischemia and to guide preventive measures (patient education, smoking cessation, digital wound care, use of vasodilator therapy such as calcium channel blockers or phosphodiesterase inhibitors, and consideration of more aggressive interventions when avascular areas are progressive).

Limitations and standardization

Capillaroscopy has limitations that clinicians must recognize. There is interobserver variability unless images are obtained and scored with standardized protocols. Technical factors—magnification, immersion medium, available reference ranges for that system—affect measurements. Some capillary abnormalities are nonspecific and occur in healthy aging, chronic smokers, diabetes and after trauma. Thus interpreting capillaroscopy requires clinical correlation.

Efforts to standardize acquisition and scoring (e.g., EULAR recommendations and standardized definitions for scleroderma patterns) improve reproducibility. Videocapillaroscopy with image storage and software-assisted measurement provides the best platform for serial monitoring and research.

Conclusion

Nailfold capillaroscopy is a low-risk, high-yield tool for evaluating microvascular structure and pathology in patients with Raynaud phenomenon, suspected systemic sclerosis, dermatomyositis and related connective tissue diseases. Mastery of technique, awareness of normal reference ranges and patterns, and understanding of the pathophysiologic significance of microhemorrhages, avascular areas, capillary dilation and neoangiogenesis allow clinicians to use capillaroscopy effectively for diagnosis, prognosis and monitoring. Integration with clinical assessment and appropriate ancillary testing maximizes its clinical value while recognizing its limitations.
