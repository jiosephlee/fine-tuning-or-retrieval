Introduction

Inflammatory myopathies are a heterogeneous group of autoimmune diseases characterized by immune-mediated muscle inflammation, weakness, and extramuscular manifestations. Dermatomyositis (DM), polymyositis (PM), immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), and inclusion body myositis (IBM) differ in pathophysiology, natural history, and treatment responsiveness. This chapter focuses on evidence-based pharmacologic therapies used to treat dermatomyositis and other inflammatory myopathies, emphasizing mechanisms of action, dosing strategies, monitoring, adverse effects, and how to select and sequence therapies according to disease severity, organ involvement, and patient comorbidities. Supportive care and rehabilitation measures are equally important to maximize functional recovery and reduce complications. The chapter is intended to provide the practical pharmacologic foundation necessary for clinical decision-making.

# Glucocorticoids: mechanisms, pulse dosing, tapering strategies, adverse effects

Glucocorticoids remain the first-line therapy for most inflammatory myopathies because of their rapid and broad immunosuppressive effects. Mechanistically, glucocorticoids bind to the cytosolic glucocorticoid receptor, translocate to the nucleus, and alter gene transcription to suppress proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α), reduce expression of adhesion molecules, impair antigen presentation, and induce apoptosis of certain immune cells. They also have non-genomic effects, including modulation of cell membrane function and rapid suppression of inflammatory mediator release. These combined effects reduce muscle inflammation and improve strength and function.

Induction dosing depends on severity and organ threat. For most patients with active DM or PM without severe weakness or organ involvement, oral prednisone is started at 0.5–1.0 mg/kg/day (commonly 40–60 mg/day in adults). In patients with severe weakness, rapidly progressive disease, dysphagia with risk of aspiration, respiratory muscle involvement, or life-threatening extramuscular disease (e.g., myocarditis, severe interstitial lung disease), high-dose “pulse” intravenous methylprednisolone is often employed. Typical pulse regimens are methylprednisolone 500–1000 mg IV daily for 3 consecutive days; in very severe cases repeated pulses and/or adjunct high-dose oral prednisone may be used. Pulse steroids may produce a faster clinical response and allow earlier initiation of steroid-sparing agents.

Tapering strategies must balance disease control with minimizing glucocorticoid toxicity. Once clinical improvement is achieved (strength gains, improved CK or other biomarkers, symptom relief), many clinicians begin to taper prednisone after 2–4 weeks on the initial dose. A common approach is to reduce prednisone by 10%–20% of the initial dose every 1–2 weeks until a moderate dose (e.g., 20 mg/day) is reached, then slower taper by 2.5–5 mg every 2–4 weeks based on disease activity. The ultimate goal is to reach the lowest effective dose or discontinuation, often using steroid-sparing agents to permit taper. Some patients require long-term low-dose prednisone (e.g., ≤5–10 mg/day) to maintain remission.

Adverse effects of glucocorticoids are dose- and duration-dependent and include metabolic, musculoskeletal, infectious, cardiovascular, neuropsychiatric, and endocrine complications. Short-term adverse effects include hyperglycemia, mood changes, insomnia, and fluid retention. Long-term effects include osteoporosis and increased fracture risk, avascular necrosis, weight gain, central adiposity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cataracts, glaucoma, skin thinning and bruising, muscle wasting (steroid myopathy), impaired wound healing, and increased susceptibility to infections. Prophylactic measures are recommended: bone protection (calcium 1000–1200 mg/day, vitamin D 800–2000 IU/day, and bisphosphonates for patients at significant risk), screening and treatment for hyperglycemia and hypertension, and infection risk mitigation (vaccination, PJP prophylaxis in some cases — see below). Because chronic glucocorticoid therapy can suppress the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, tapering should be cautious if long-term high-dose therapy has been used to avoid adrenal insufficiency.

Monitoring during glucocorticoid therapy includes periodic assessment of blood pressure, weight, glucose/HbA1c, lipids, bone density (DEXA), ophthalmologic exams for cataracts and glaucoma, and screening for infections. Serial clinical measures of muscle strength and functional status, and serum muscle enzymes (creatine kinase; normal adult upper limit varies by lab but commonly ~40–200 U/L) help assess response, though CK may not correlate with disease activity in all subtypes (e.g., anti-synthetase patients with ILD).

# Conventional steroid-sparing agents: methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, tacrolimus (mechanisms, dosing, toxicities)

Rationale and general principles
Because many patients require prolonged therapy, steroid-sparing immunosuppressants are routinely used to reduce glucocorticoid exposure and manage refractory disease. Choice among agents depends on disease phenotype, comorbidities, childbearing potential, organ involvement (e.g., lung), safety profile, and clinician experience. Onset of action for these agents is slower than glucocorticoids; clinical improvement may take weeks to months. Baseline screening (infectious disease testing, pregnancy testing, CBC, renal and liver function, and vaccine status) should precede initiation. Drug-specific details follow.

Methotrexate (MTX)
Mechanism: Methotrexate is a folate antagonist that inhibits dihydrofolate reductase and other enzymes in purine and pyrimidine synthesis; at the low doses used for autoimmune disease, it also increases extracellular adenosine with anti-inflammatory effects and modulates immune cell function.

Dosing: Typical dosing in inflammatory myopathies is methotrexate 15–25 mg once weekly, given orally or subcutaneously. Many clinicians start at 15 mg weekly and increase to 20–25 mg weekly depending on tolerability and response. Folic acid 1 mg daily or 5–10 mg weekly (excluding the dosing day) is given to reduce mucosal and hematologic side effects.

Monitoring and toxicities: Baseline labs include CBC with differential, liver function tests (LFTs), serum creatinine, hepatitis B/C serologies, and chest imaging if indicated. Monitor CBC and LFTs every 2–4 weeks during early therapy, then every 1–3 months. Methotrexate can cause hepatotoxicity (transaminase elevations, fibrosis), cytopenias, pulmonary toxicity (rare but potentially severe interstitial pneumonitis), mucositis, and alopecia. Avoid in pregnancy — methotrexate is teratogenic and requires contraception; men should also be counseled regarding paternal exposure. Dose adjustments are needed for renal impairment.

Azathioprine (AZA)
Mechanism: Azathioprine is converted to 6-mercaptopurine and metabolites that interfere with purine synthesis, leading to reduced lymphocyte proliferation. It exerts an immunosuppressive effect on both T and B cells.

Dosing: Azathioprine is typically started at 1–2 mg/kg/day and titrated up to 2–3 mg/kg/day as tolerated; once-daily dosing is common. Therapeutic effects may take 3–6 months.

Monitoring and toxicities: TPMT (thiopurine methyltransferase) enzyme activity or genotype should be checked before initiation because low TPMT activity predisposes to myelosuppression. Baseline CBC, LFTs, and viral serologies are recommended; monitor CBC and LFTs frequently (e.g., every 1–2 weeks during dose escalation then every 1–3 months). Toxicities include bone marrow suppression (leukopenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia), hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, and increased infection risk. Azathioprine is teratogenic risk category; discuss pregnancy planning — some clinicians use azathioprine in pregnancy with caution (it is relatively safer than methotrexate which is contraindicated).

Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)
Mechanism: MMF is converted to mycophenolic acid, which inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH), a key enzyme in de novo guanosine nucleotide synthesis; lymphocytes are particularly dependent on this pathway, leading to selective inhibition of T- and B-cell proliferation.

Dosing: Typical dosing in inflammatory myopathies ranges from 2 g/day (1000 mg twice daily) up to 3 g/day (1500 mg twice daily), depending on tolerance. Start at lower doses and escalate as tolerated. Clinical response may take several months.

Monitoring and toxicities: Baseline CBC, LFTs, and pregnancy testing are essential. MMF commonly causes gastrointestinal side effects (diarrhea, nausea), leukopenia, and increased infection risk; less hepatotoxicity than azathioprine or methotrexate. MMF is teratogenic and contraindicated in pregnancy; effective contraception is required.

Tacrolimus
Mechanism: Tacrolimus is a calcineurin inhibitor that binds FK506-binding protein (FKBP) and inhibits calcineurin phosphatase, preventing dephosphorylation of NFAT and downstream transcription of IL-2 and other cytokines, thus suppressing T-cell activation.

Dosing: Tacrolimus has been used off-label for refractory dermatomyositis and in patients with significant interstitial lung disease associated with myositis. Oral tacrolimus dosing often begins at 0.075 mg/kg/day to 0.15 mg/kg/day in two divided doses, typically adjusted to achieve trough levels in the range of 5–15 ng/mL (target depends on disease and center practice). Some regimens use a fixed dose of 1–3 mg twice daily with trough monitoring. Therapeutic adjustments are common.

Monitoring and toxicities: Monitor tacrolimus trough levels, renal function, blood pressure, electrolytes (especially potassium and magnesium), fasting glucose, and hepatic function. Adverse effects include nephrotoxicity (dose- and duration-dependent), hypertension, neurotoxicity (tremor, headache), hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and increased infection risk. Drug interactions (CYP3A4 inhibitors/inducers) are common and important to manage.

Comparative notes and sequencing
No high-quality randomized trials dictate an absolute hierarchy among agents; selection is individualized. Methotrexate and azathioprine are widely used first-line steroid-sparing agents due to clinician familiarity, evidence from observational cohorts and trials, and cost. MMF is favored in patients with concomitant interstitial lung disease and in those intolerant to MTX or AZA. Tacrolimus and cyclosporine are reserved for refractory disease, especially with significant pulmonary involvement or where T-cell-directed therapy is desired. When choosing agents, consider reproductive plans, comorbid liver or renal disease, infection history, and monitoring capacity.

# Biologic therapies: rituximab (anti-CD20), tofacitinib/JAK inhibitors and emerging agents

Rituximab (anti-CD20)
Mechanism: Rituximab is a chimeric monoclonal antibody targeting CD20 on B lymphocytes, leading to B-cell depletion through complement-dependent cytotoxicity, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, and induction of apoptosis. B cells contribute to myositis pathogenesis via autoantibody production, antigen presentation, and cytokine secretion; therefore, rituximab can be effective in autoantibody-associated myositis and refractory cases.

Evidence and indications: Rituximab has been studied in dermatomyositis and polymyositis, including the RIM trial (rituximab in myositis), which showed mixed primary endpoint results but suggested benefit in subgroups and in open-label and registry data; it is commonly used for refractory disease or in cases with prominent autoantibody-mediated pathology (e.g., anti-synthetase, anti-Mi-2, anti-SRP disease depending on context). Rituximab is also preferred for certain extramuscular manifestations such as severe cutaneous disease refractory to conventional agents and for some patients with concurrent systemic autoimmune diseases.

Dosing: Two common regimens are 375 mg/m^2 weekly for 4 weeks (oncology regimen) or 1000 mg IV given on days 1 and 15 (rheumatologic regimen). Repeat courses are guided by clinical response and B-cell repopulation (CD19+/CD20+ counts), typically every 6–12 months or sooner if relapse. Pre-medication with acetaminophen, antihistamine, and corticosteroids reduces infusion reactions.

Monitoring and adverse effects: Baseline screening for hepatitis B and C, tuberculosis, and evaluation of vaccination status is required. Reactivation of hepatitis B is a serious risk; appropriate prophylaxis for HBV carriers is mandatory. Infusion reactions are common during the first infusion and are manageable. Rituximab increases infection risk, including rare progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) due to JC virus. Measure immunoglobulin levels (IgG) before and periodically during therapy because hypogammaglobulinemia can develop and predispose to infections; IVIG replacement may be required in selected patients.

Tofacitinib and other JAK inhibitors
Mechanism: Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors (e.g., tofacitinib, baricitinib, ruxolitinib) block intracellular signaling downstream of cytokine receptors by inhibiting JAK family kinases (JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, TYK2 depending on agent). This interrupts signaling pathways for many proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., type I interferons, IL-6, IL-2 family) implicated in myositis, especially in dermatomyositis where a type I interferon signature is prevalent.

Evidence and emerging use: Case reports, small case series, and early-phase studies suggest that JAK inhibitors may be effective in refractory dermatomyositis, particularly for cutaneous disease and some systemic manifestations. Tofacitinib (typical dose 5 mg twice daily) has been used off-label, and baricitinib (2–4 mg/day) has shown promise in limited data. Controlled trials are emerging. JAK inhibitors may be particularly attractive for patients with interferon-driven disease or refractory skin disease.

Dosing and monitoring: Tofacitinib is typically dosed at 5 mg twice daily or extended-release 11 mg once daily; dose adjustments are required for renal impairment. Baseline screening similar to other biologics: TB, hepatitis B/C, CBC, LFTs, lipid panel, and vaccination status. Monitor CBC, LFTs, and lipids after initiation. Adverse effects include increased risk of infection (including herpes zoster), laboratory abnormalities (cytopenias, transaminase and lipid elevations), and, in some populations, increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events, venous thromboembolism, and malignancy as suggested by safety signals in rheumatoid arthritis populations with certain JAK inhibitors. Risk–benefit assessment is essential.

Emerging agents
Other biologic therapies under investigation target different pathways: anti-TNF agents (limited efficacy and risk profile), abatacept (CTLA4-Ig) to modulate T-cell co-stimulation, anti–type I interferon therapies, complement inhibitors, and agents targeting the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) to reduce pathogenic autoantibody levels. Clinical trial data are evolving; patients with refractory disease may be candidates for enrollment in trials.

Clinical considerations when using biologics
Use of biologic agents requires careful baseline infectious disease screening, consideration of concomitant immunosuppressive drugs, and vaccination planning (ideally complete inactivated vaccines before initiation; live vaccines generally contraindicated during therapy). Cost and access considerations are substantial. Sequential or combination use (e.g., rituximab + conventional agents) is common, but combination therapy increases infection risk and requires close monitoring.

# Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG): mechanisms, indications, dosing regimens and adverse effects

Mechanisms of action
Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is pooled human IgG from donors and exerts pleiotropic immunomodulatory effects. Proposed mechanisms relevant to inflammatory myopathies include neutralization of autoantibodies, modulation of Fc receptor expression on innate immune cells, inhibition of complement activation, modulation of cytokine networks, interference with antigen presentation, and expansion of regulatory T-cell populations. IVIG can reduce muscle inflammation and improve strength, particularly in dermatomyositis and some cases of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy.

Indications and evidence
IVIG is an approved therapy for refractory dermatomyositis (parenteral immunoglobulin [IVIG] has randomized controlled trial evidence showing benefit) and is widely used for severe or refractory cutaneous manifestations, dysphagia, or rapidly progressive disease. It is particularly useful when rapid immunomodulation is desired, when other immunosuppressants are contraindicated (e.g., pregnancy in some contexts), or when infections / comorbidities preclude stronger immunosuppression. IVIG may be less effective in inclusion body myositis. In IMNM with anti–HMGCR antibodies, IVIG has shown effectiveness in some series, particularly in statin-related cases.

Dosing regimens
The most commonly used induction regimen is IVIG 2 g/kg total dose given over 2–5 days (e.g., 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days, or 1 g/kg/day for 2 days). For maintenance, regimens vary: 1 g/kg every 4 weeks, or 2 g/kg every 4–8 weeks depending on clinical response and tolerability. Individualization is required: some patients maintain remission with infusions every 4–8 weeks; others require more frequent dosing initially. When dosing for obese patients, ideal body weight adjustment or capped doses are sometimes used to avoid excessive dosing; local protocols vary.

Monitoring and adverse effects
Before infusion, obtain baseline renal function, CBC, and consider serum IgA measurement (to assess risk of anaphylactic reactions in IgA-deficient patients with anti-IgA antibodies). Monitor vital signs during infusion for infusion reactions. Common adverse effects are infusion-related: headache (including aseptic meningitis), flushing, fever, chills, myalgias, and nausea. Serious but uncommon adverse effects include thromboembolic events (venous and arterial thrombosis), acute kidney injury (more frequent with sucrose-containing IVIG preparations and in patients with pre-existing renal insufficiency, advanced age, diabetes, volume depletion), hemolytic anemia (due to blood group antibodies in the product), and anaphylaxis (rare, especially in IgA-deficient patients). Risk mitigation includes using slower infusion rates in high-risk patients, adequate hydration, choosing products without sucrose in renal-risk patients, and prophylactic measures for headaches (e.g., pre-infusion acetaminophen, antihistamine, and hydration).

Practical considerations
IVIG is expensive and resource-intensive. It is typically used when rapid effect is needed, when other agents have failed, or in specific clinical scenarios such as severe dysphagia, cutaneous DM not responsive to topical/systemic agents, or IMNM with severe weakness. Documenting objective clinical improvement (strength testing, functional status) guides continuation and intervals. Concomitant therapies (corticosteroids, steroid-sparing agents) are usually continued during IVIG induction and may be adjusted as response allows.

# Treatment selection principles based on disease severity, organ involvement and comorbidities

Principles of individualized therapy
Therapeutic decisions in inflammatory myopathies hinge on disease severity, organ system involvement, myositis subtype, autoantibody profile, comorbidities, pregnancy status, infection risk, and patient preferences. A stepwise and often combination approach is used: induction with glucocorticoids for rapid control, early addition of a steroid-sparing agent to permit taper, and escalation to biologics or IVIG for refractory or severe disease.

Severity categories and corresponding strategies
- Mild to moderate muscle-predominant disease without major extramuscular manifestations: Begin oral prednisone (0.5–1.0 mg/kg/day) and start a steroid-sparing agent (commonly methotrexate 15–25 mg weekly or azathioprine 2 mg/kg/day). Monitor response and taper steroids over weeks–months as improvement permits.

- Severe muscle weakness, rapidly progressive disease, dysphagia, respiratory muscle involvement, or significant extramuscular disease (e.g., myocarditis, severe ILD): Use aggressive induction with IV methylprednisolone pulses (e.g., 0.5–1 g/day for 3 days) followed by high-dose oral prednisone, and simultaneously initiate a steroid-sparing agent. Early consideration of IVIG and/or rituximab is appropriate, particularly for dysphagia, cutaneous DM refractory to other agents, or certain autoantibody-associated phenotypes (e.g., anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR, or anti-MDA5 with rapidly progressive ILD). For severe ILD, combine immunosuppressants (e.g., high-dose steroids + tacrolimus or cyclosporine ± cyclophosphamide) based on pulmonary and rheumatologic expertise.

- Refractory disease: For patients not improving on conventional DMARDs and steroids, consider IVIG, rituximab, or JAK inhibitors depending on phenotype, prior therapy, and safety considerations. For refractory cutaneous disease, JAK inhibitors have increasing evidence. For IMNM and anti-HMGCR–positive disease, IVIG is frequently effective.

Organ-specific considerations
- Interstitial lung disease (ILD): Some myositis subtypes (anti-synthetase, anti-MDA5) have prominent ILD. Agents with anti-T-cell effects (calcineurin inhibitors such as tacrolimus, cyclosporine), MMF, and cyclophosphamide are commonly used; early aggressive therapy is warranted for rapidly progressive ILD. Rituximab is also used in refractory cases. Pulmonary specialists should be involved early.

- Cutaneous disease in dermatomyositis: Skin disease may be refractory to systemic immunosuppression; antimalarials (hydroxychloroquine 200–400 mg/day) are often used for cutaneous DM, although photosensitivity and limited efficacy in some patients limit benefit. IVIG and JAK inhibitors have documented benefit for refractory dermatomyositis skin disease. Sun protection and topical therapies are adjuncts.

- Dysphagia and aspiration risk: Dysphagia is an indication for early IVIG and more aggressive therapy because of aspiration risk and nutritional compromise.

- Cardiac involvement: Myocarditis requires prompt, aggressive immunosuppression (high-dose steroids ± cyclophosphamide or other agents) and cardiology input.

Comorbidities and safety considerations
- Pregnancy: Avoid methotrexate, MMF, and mycophenolate in pregnancy. Azathioprine and IVIG are considered relatively safer options; rituximab has been used but requires nuanced counseling regarding timing and neonatal B-cell effects. Preconception counseling and coordination with obstetrics are essential.

- Infection or chronic viral infection: Screen for TB, HBV, HCV, and consider prophylaxis or alternative agents if infections are present. Rituximab requires HBV screening and prophylaxis for carriers; high immunosuppression may require PJP prophylaxis (TMP-SMX) when patients are on high-dose steroids plus another immunosuppressant.

- Malignancy risk: Dermatomyositis has an association with malignancy; initial cancer screening (age-appropriate plus attention to DM-associated malignancy patterns) is important. Presence of malignancy influences immunosuppressive choices, and some paraneoplastic myopathies respond better to cancer-directed therapy.

Decision points and monitoring of response
Clinical decision points include assessing response at 4–12 weeks for conventional agents; inadequate improvement after an appropriate trial (often 3–6 months) typically prompts escalation. Objective measures include manual muscle testing, quantitative muscle testing, functional scales (e.g., MMT-8, patient-reported outcomes), pulmonary function testing for ILD, repeat CK (not always reflective), and skin activity assessment. Tapering of immunosuppression should be considered after sustained clinical remission (often at least 6–12 months) but individualized due to relapse risk.

# Supportive care and rehabilitation (physiotherapy, nutritional support, speech/swallow therapy)

Role of multidisciplinary supportive care
Supportive measures are fundamental components of management, often as important as immunosuppression for restoring function and preventing complications. A multidisciplinary team — physical and occupational therapists, speech-language pathologists, dietitians, pulmonologists, cardiologists, dermatologists, and mental health professionals — should be engaged early.

Physiotherapy and exercise
Once significant inflammatory activity is controlled and there is medical stability, graded exercise and physiotherapy are safe and beneficial. Historically, concerns about exercise worsening inflammation have been allayed by trials showing that tailored, progressive aerobic and resistance training improves muscle strength, endurance, physical function, and quality of life. Programs should start with low-intensity isometric and concentric exercises, gradually increasing to resistance and aerobic conditioning as tolerated. Avoid excessive eccentric and maximal-load activities during active severe inflammation to reduce risk of muscle damage. Physical therapists experienced with inflammatory myopathies can design individualized home and supervised programs focusing on core strength, posture, and activities of daily living. Occupational therapy can assist with adaptive equipment and energy conservation strategies.

Nutritional support
Malnutrition, weight loss, or obesity may complicate inflammatory myopathies. Dysphagia increases aspiration risk and may necessitate texture modification or enteral nutrition. Dietitian involvement helps optimize caloric and protein intake to support muscle repair and immune competence, manage steroid-induced weight gain and metabolic complications, and address micronutrient needs (e.g., calcium and vitamin D for bone health). Monitor for and manage dysphagia-related dehydration or aspiration pneumonia.

Speech and swallow therapy
Dysphagia affects a subset of patients with dermatomyositis and polymyositis and is associated with morbidity. Early evaluation by a speech-language pathologist, bedside swallow assessments, and instrumental studies (videofluoroscopic swallow study or fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing) guide therapy. Interventions include swallowing maneuvers, exercises to strengthen oropharyngeal muscles, diet modification, and when necessary, temporary enteral feeding (nasogastric or percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy) to maintain nutrition and reduce aspiration risk. Improvement in dysphagia often parallels immunosuppressive therapy but rehabilitation measures are critical adjuncts.

Prevention and management of complications
Osteoporosis prevention should be instituted for patients expected to receive prolonged glucocorticoids: baseline DEXA scan and calcium/vitamin D supplementation, with bisphosphonates for patients at high fracture risk. Vaccinations (inactivated) should be updated before initiating immunosuppression when possible (influenza, pneumococcal, HPV, hepatitis B). Live vaccines are contraindicated during significant immunosuppression. Prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) should be considered for patients receiving high-dose glucocorticoids (e.g., prednisone ≥20 mg/day for several weeks) combined with other immunosuppressants; trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) is typically used (e.g., TMP-SMX DS 3 times weekly or daily depending on protocol). Monitor for steroid-related metabolic complications and treat accordingly.

Psychosocial support, vocational rehabilitation and patient education
Chronic disease management requires attention to psychological impact: anxiety, depression, and reduced quality of life are common. Referral to mental health professionals, support groups, patient education about disease, medication side effects, and strategies for self-monitoring empower patients. Vocational rehabilitation and social work can assist with disability evaluation, workplace modifications, and financial resources.

Follow-up and long-term monitoring
Regular follow-up assesses disease control and medication toxicity. Schedule visits to track muscle strength, function, CK levels, pulmonary function for ILD patients, and organ-specific monitoring as indicated. Periodic cancer surveillance (age-appropriate and DM-directed) is recommended, particularly in the initial years after diagnosis when risk is higher.

Conclusion

Effective management of dermatomyositis and other inflammatory myopathies integrates rapid and appropriate immunosuppression with long-term steroid-sparing strategies, biologic use for refractory disease, or IVIG for specific indications. Treatment selection must be individualized based on severity, organ involvement, comorbidities, reproductive plans, and safety considerations. Concurrent supportive care — physiotherapy, nutritional optimization, swallowing rehabilitation, and preventive measures — is essential for functional recovery and minimizing complications. Close monitoring for therapeutic efficacy and adverse effects, plus coordination across specialties, underpins successful outcomes.
