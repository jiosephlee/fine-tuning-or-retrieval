Introduction

Imaging plays a central role in the evaluation and management of inflammatory myopathies and associated neck and thoracic pathology. Proper selection and interpretation of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) informs diagnosis, guides biopsy, stages disease, and screens for complications such as interstitial lung disease (ILD), deep neck infections or occult malignancy. This chapter reviews the imaging appearances of active and chronic myositis on MRI, the application of CT in assessing neck soft tissues and airway compromise, high-resolution CT (HRCT) patterns of myositis-associated ILD and pulmonary nodules, the utility of FDG-PET in inflammatory myopathies and cancer screening, and the limitations, artifacts, and complementary use of these modalities. Emphasis is placed on pathophysiologic correlates, practical interpretation, and clinical decision points relevant to the medical student or resident.

# MRI sequences and findings of active myositis (STIR/T2 hyperintensity, gadolinium enhancement) vs chronic atrophy/fibrosis

Magnetic resonance imaging is the most sensitive modality for detecting muscle inflammation and for distinguishing active myositis from chronic inactive changes. The pathophysiology of inflammatory myopathies—such as polymyositis, dermatomyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, and inclusion body myositis—involves variable degrees of myofiber necrosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, edema, and later fatty replacement and fibrosis. MRI signal characteristics reflect these tissue changes: increased free water in edema and active inflammation produces increased signal on fluid-sensitive sequences, while fatty infiltration and atrophy produce high signal on fat-sensitive sequences and muscle volume loss respectively. Contrast enhancement highlights areas of vascular permeability and inflammation.

Sequence selection and their interpretive roles: T1-weighted sequences provide anatomical detail and show muscle bulk and fatty infiltration. T2-weighted sequences with fat suppression and short-tau inversion recovery (STIR) sequences are fluid-sensitive and highlight edema and active inflammation as hyperintense signal relative to normal muscle. STIR is robust across varying field strengths and less susceptible to inhomogeneous fat suppression, making it widely used for myositis imaging. Post-gadolinium T1 fat-suppressed sequences demonstrate enhancement in areas of active inflammation due to increased capillary permeability and neovascularity. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) has been explored but is less standardized; it may show restricted diffusion in some inflammatory or necrotic regions. Newer quantitative methods such as T2 mapping and fat fraction (Dixon or multi-echo techniques) provide objective metrics for longitudinal monitoring and for distinguishing fat from edema.

Patterns of active myositis: Active disease typically shows muscle enlargement with hyperintense signal on STIR/T2 images, often diffuse or patchy within affected muscles. Edema tends to be peri-fascicular or more prominent in proximal muscle groups (shoulder girdle, pelvic girdle) in dermatomyositis and polymyositis. Gadolinium enhancement is usually present in areas corresponding to STIR hyperintensity; enhancement can be diffuse, patchy, or perimysial/perifascicular depending on the subtype. Fascial and subcutaneous edema may accompany dermatomyositis due to microvascular involvement and superficial inflammation. Inflammatory myopathies associated with overlap syndromes (e.g., antisynthetase syndrome) may demonstrate more subacute or patchy muscle edema.

Chronic atrophy and fibrosis: When inflammation is longstanding or severe, muscle fibers are lost and replaced by fat and scar. On T1-weighted images, fatty replacement appears as increased signal intensity within muscle, often associated with reduction in muscle bulk. On STIR/T2 images, chronic fatty infiltration shows lower or isointense signal relative to acutely inflamed muscle; chronic fibrosis is typically low signal on both T1 and T2 sequences because collagenous scar has low water content. Fat-suppressed T1 post-contrast imaging may show little enhancement in purely fibrotic tissue, although adjacent mildly inflamed areas can enhance. Clinically, the presence of fatty replacement and volume loss indicates irreversible damage and has prognostic implications.

Temporal and distributional clues: MRI can help stage disease activity. Predominant STIR/T2 hyperintensity without much fatty replacement suggests active or early disease that may respond to immunotherapy. Conversely, prominent fatty infiltration and atrophy indicate chronicity. Distribution—symmetric proximal muscle involvement is typical of classic inflammatory myopathies, yet asymmetric or distal patterns may occur (e.g., inclusion body myositis shows selective involvement of finger flexors and quadriceps). Recognizing perimysial/perifascicular patterns supports dermatomyositis; focal multifocal involvement may raise suspicion for necrotizing myopathy or focal infection/myositis. MRI also guides biopsy: targeting an area of maximal STIR hyperintensity increases diagnostic yield by sampling active inflammatory tissue rather than fibrotic or fatty tissue.

Practical interpretation and pitfalls: Interpreters must distinguish physiological variations and other causes of muscle T2 hyperintensity. Exercise or recent trauma can produce transient muscle edema. Denervation from nerve injury causes edema in the acute phase and fatty atrophy later; pattern typically follows a nerve distribution. Infectious pyomyositis tends to show focal fluid collections with rim enhancement and restricted diffusion, distinguishing it from diffuse inflammatory myositis. Patient positioning artifacts, motion, and inadequate fat suppression can mimic pathology; correlation with clinical context and laboratory markers (creatine kinase, inflammatory markers) is essential. Quantitative measures such as T2 relaxation times and muscle fat fraction thresholds are emerging to standardize assessments; normal T2 values vary by sequence and magnet strength, so institutional reference ranges are needed.

# CT neck for deep neck space infections, soft tissue edema and airway assessment

Computed tomography of the neck is the workhorse for assessing deep neck spaces, airway patency, and osseous or foreign-body-related causes of soft tissue infection or edema. CT excels at rapid evaluation in acute settings, delineating gas, calcification, abscesses, and bony involvement, and providing multiplanar reconstructions helpful for surgical planning. Understanding the fascial spaces of the neck—submandibular, parapharyngeal, retropharyngeal, prevertebral, and visceral spaces—guides interpretation and correlates with routes of infection spread and potential airway compromise.

Indications and technical considerations: Contrast-enhanced CT of the neck is indicated when deep neck infection, Ludwig angina, suspected abscess, cellulitis with potential airway compromise, or penetrating injury are clinical concerns. Intravenous iodinated contrast enhances soft tissue planes and highlights rim enhancement around collections (abscesses). Thin-slice axial imaging with coronal and sagittal reformats improves anatomic localization. Oral contrast is rarely necessary in acute soft tissue evaluation and may obscure local detail. For airway assessment, CT can be performed with the patient supine; however, because airway caliber can change with position, clinical examination and endoscopic assessment remain complementary.

Findings in deep neck infections and edema: CT differentiates phlegmonous inflammation (confluent soft tissue swelling without a discrete fluid collection) from abscess formation (fluid collection with peripheral enhancement and often internal gas). Abscesses commonly show rim enhancement after IV contrast and may have loculations. Early phlegmon may show diffuse fat-stranding and soft tissue swelling without a clear liquid center. Gas within a collection suggests anaerobic infection or gas-producing organisms and often indicates a need for surgical drainage. Cervical lymphadenopathy is often reactive but can accompany infection or malignancy. Assessment of involvement of the parapharyngeal and retropharyngeal spaces is critical because infection can descend into the mediastinum along fascial planes, leading to mediastinitis.

Airway assessment and urgent decision points: CT provides objective measurements of airway narrowing and displacement. Infections like Ludwig angina (bilateral submandibular and sublingual space involvement) can rapidly compromise the upper airway by elevating the floor of mouth and posterior displacement of the tongue. Obstruction risk is assessed by cross-sectional area reduction, subglottic edema, and associated mediastinal extension. However, decisions regarding airway control should be based on a combination of imaging, clinical signs (stridor, drooling, inability to handle secretions, oxygenation), and endoscopic findings; CT is an adjunct for planning intubation or surgical airway. Imaging findings such as a near-occlusive retropharyngeal abscess or rapidly expanding collection prompt early otolaryngology or surgical consultation.

Complications and extensions: CT can detect jugular vein thrombosis, erosion into adjacent bones (osteomyelitis), vascular involvement, and mediastinal extension. Identification of a foreign body (e.g., fish bone) or calcified sialolithiasis that predisposed to infection is often possible. In immunocompromised patients, CT may show atypical features such as more extensive necrosis or less robust rim enhancement. Radiologists must be vigilant for signs of sepsis and guide drainage procedures by identifying a safe route that avoids major vessels, nerves, and the airway.

Limitations and alternative modalities: CT exposes the neck to ionizing radiation and involves iodinated contrast, which is contraindicated in severe contrast allergy or significant renal failure. MRI can better delineate soft tissue and vascular structures without radiation, and it is superior for differentiating cellulitis from neoplasm and for characterizing vascular malformations, but it is slower and less available in emergencies. Ultrasound is valuable for superficial abscesses and guiding drainage but cannot evaluate deep or retropharyngeal spaces reliably.

# High-resolution CT chest for ILD patterns and pulmonary nodules

High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) is the imaging standard for the evaluation of interstitial lung disease (ILD) and provides critical information in patients with inflammatory myopathies, many of whom develop myositis-associated ILD, particularly those with certain autoantibodies (e.g., anti-synthetase). HRCT characterizes patterns of lung involvement, extent of disease, and presence of pulmonary nodules or malignancy, which affect prognosis and management.

Technique and interpretation principles: HRCT is performed with thin-section (typically 1-mm to 1.5-mm) axial acquisitions at full inspiration with high spatial frequency reconstruction algorithms. Images are reviewed in lung windows for parenchymal detail and in mediastinal windows for nodules, lymphadenopathy, and pleural disease. Prone images and expiratory views may help differentiate dependent atelectasis from early fibrosis or air-trapping suggestive of small airways disease. Semi-quantitative scoring of disease extent can be useful for monitoring progression.

Common ILD patterns and their pathophysiology: HRCT patterns are described using standardized terms because they correlate with histopathology and prognosis. Key patterns include:

- Usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP): Patchy subpleural and basilar-predominant reticulation, traction bronchiectasis, and honeycombing (clustered cystic airspaces typically subpleural). UIP shows heterogeneous fibrosis with temporal and spatial variability and is associated with poorer prognosis. In the context of myositis, UIP pattern is less common than nonspecific interstitial pneumonia but may occur.

- Nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP): More uniform ground-glass opacity and reticulation with basal predominance, relative subpleural sparing early on, and less honeycombing. NSIP often reflects a more inflammatory and potentially steroid-responsive process; it is the most common pattern in connective tissue disease–associated ILD, including myositis.

- Organizing pneumonia (OP): Patchy, often peripheral or peribronchial consolidation with a tendency to migrate and resolve with therapy; may coexist with ground-glass changes. OP often improves with corticosteroids.

- Acute interstitial pneumonia/diffuse alveolar damage (AIP/DAD): Diffuse bilateral ground-glass opacities and consolidation, often rapidly progressive, associated with high morbidity and mortality.

- Mixed patterns: Patients with inflammatory myopathies can have mixed NSIP-OP or NSIP-UIP features. Recognizing mixed patterns guides therapy and prognosis.

Ground-glass opacity (GGO) represents partial airspace filling, interstitial inflammation, or fine fibrosis. Reticulation indicates fibrotic interstitial thickening. Traction bronchiectasis indicates architectural distortion from fibrosis and portends irreversible disease. Honeycombing is an indicator of established fibrosis.

Pulmonary nodules and malignancy screening: HRCT also evaluates pulmonary nodules, which may represent infection, inflammation, or malignancy. Myositis, particularly dermatomyositis and certain antibody-positive subtypes, is associated with an increased risk of malignancy. HRCT is sensitive for nodules producing guidance about size, number, attenuation, and growth on serial imaging. Management follows established nodule guidelines (e.g., Fleischner Society): solitary pulmonary nodules larger than certain thresholds (e.g., >6 mm in high-risk patients) generally require risk stratification, interval follow-up imaging, or PET/biopsy. In the myositis population, a lower threshold for investigation may be considered given the heightened malignancy risk.

Clinical decision points: Distinguishing inflammatory (potentially reversible) from fibrotic (irreversible) disease is essential. Predominant GGO and minimal reticulation suggest more inflammation and possible responsiveness to immunosuppression; prominent reticulation, traction bronchiectasis, and honeycombing indicate advanced fibrosis with limited reversibility. In rapidly progressive respiratory decline with diffuse GGO, consider acute exacerbation or DAD; urgent management and intensivist involvement are required. HRCT should be integrated with pulmonary function tests (e.g., forced vital capacity, diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide—DLCO) and serologic markers to guide therapy and monitoring. Reference ranges: DLCO normal approximately 80–120% predicted; values <60% predicted often indicate significant gas exchange impairment warranting closer attention.

Limitations: HRCT involves ionizing radiation and may not distinguish active inflammation from fibrosis in all cases. Serial imaging and adjunctive modalities (FDG-PET, MRI-based pulmonary imaging experimental techniques) can help when the distinction is unclear. Tissue diagnosis through bronchoalveolar lavage or surgical biopsy is sometimes necessary when imaging and clinical data are discordant.

# FDG-PET utility for identifying widespread muscle inflammation and occult malignancy

FDG-PET/CT measures glucose metabolism and is widely used for oncologic staging. Because activated inflammatory cells (neutrophils, macrophages, lymphocytes) exhibit increased glucose uptake, FDG-PET can detect inflammatory foci, including inflamed muscle in inflammatory myopathies. Its dual role—identifying widespread muscle inflammation and screening for occult malignancy—makes it valuable in the workup of myositis, especially when paraneoplastic processes are a concern.

Principles and patterns in myositis: In active myositis, FDG uptake in affected muscles is increased relative to background soft tissues and may be symmetric or asymmetric. The uptake pattern may be focal, patchy, or diffuse, often involving proximal muscle groups. FDG-PET can detect subclinical muscle involvement not evident on MRI or clinical examination and can help localize sites for biopsy when MRI is unavailable or inconclusive. Standardized uptake value (SUV) measurements provide semi-quantitative assessment; however, absolute SUV thresholds distinguishing inflammation from normal muscle are not universally established and vary with scanner, timing, glycemic status, and muscle activity. Careful patient preparation—fasting, avoiding strenuous exercise prior to scanning—is necessary because physiologic muscle uptake increases after exertion.

Occult malignancy screening: Dermatomyositis and some forms of polymyositis have epidemiologic associations with occult malignancy. PET/CT has higher sensitivity than conventional staging in detecting many malignancies and can identify primary tumors or metastatic disease in the abdomen, pelvis, and chest. PET can be particularly informative when initial cancer screening (mammography, colonoscopy, CT) is negative but clinical suspicion remains high. PET/CT may find hypermetabolic lymphadenopathy or small metastatic deposits that are occult on other modalities. However, PET has limited sensitivity for certain low-metabolic tumors (e.g., some prostate cancers, indolent lymphomas) and for small lesions below spatial resolution (~5–7 mm), so a negative PET does not entirely exclude malignancy.

Interpretation caveats: FDG uptake is not specific for malignancy; infections, inflammatory processes, recent surgical sites, and physiological uptake produce increased signal. In myositis patients, diffuse muscle uptake may complicate detection of adjacent malignancy due to increased background. False positives can result from recent vaccination, infections, or inflammatory arthropathies. Conversely, hyperglycemia reduces tracer uptake by tumors and inflammatory cells; blood glucose should be controlled (preferably <160 mg/dL) for optimal imaging. The timing of PET relative to onset of therapy matters: immunosuppression reduces inflammatory uptake and may decrease sensitivity for detecting active disease. Therefore, when the goal is to detect active inflammation or occult malignancy, obtaining PET prior to initiation of high-dose immunosuppression is ideal if clinically feasible.

Clinical integration and follow-up: PET/CT findings should be correlated with clinical examination, muscle enzymes (creatine kinase: typical normal range ~25–200 U/L, though lab-specific ranges vary), autoantibody profiles, and MRI findings. Focal hypermetabolic lesions identified on PET that could represent malignancy often require directed anatomic imaging, biopsy, or further endoscopic testing. PET can also be used to monitor treatment response in myositis: decreasing FDG uptake correlates with clinical improvement in many cases, but standardized criteria for response remain under development.

# Limitations, artifacts and combining modalities for diagnosis

Each imaging modality has strengths and limitations; optimal patient care requires integrating modalities, clinical data, laboratory findings, and sometimes histopathology. Understanding common artifacts and limitations avoids misinterpretation and guides appropriate use.

MRI limitations and artifacts: MRI is sensitive but not entirely specific. Motion artifact from patient movement or respiration can mimic or obscure pathology; susceptibility artifacts near metallic implants can distort images. Inhomogeneous fat suppression and poor field homogeneity can produce false areas of high signal. Gadolinium-based contrast agents are contraindicated in advanced renal failure due to the risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis with certain agents, and there is theoretical concern regarding gadolinium deposition with repeated dosing—particularly linear agents—so risk-benefit assessment is necessary. Time, cost, and availability limit MRI use in acute settings. Additionally, MRI may be poorly tolerated by claustrophobic patients or those with incompatible implants (some pacemakers), though many modern devices are MRI-conditional.

CT limitations and artifacts: CT is highly available and fast but involves ionizing radiation and iodinated contrast exposure. Beam-hardening artifacts from dense structures (e.g., dental fillings) can obscure adjacent soft tissues on neck CT. Motion artifacts and swallowing can blur images. CT has limited soft-tissue contrast compared with MRI, making differentiation of cellulitis versus early neoplasm more challenging. In the chest, CT may reveal non-specific findings such as ground-glass opacity that require clinical correlation.

PET limitations and artifacts: FDG is a glucose analog and accumulates in tissues with high glucose metabolism. Physiologic muscle uptake is increased after exercise, cold exposure, or anxiety. Brown adipose tissue can take up FDG in the neck and supraclavicular regions, mimicking pathological uptake. Spatial resolution limits detection of small lesions, and some malignancies and low-grade infections/inflammations may show low FDG avidity. PET/CT uses ionizing radiation and needs careful preparation to minimize false positives (fasting, avoid exercise, measure glucose). Cost and limited availability may restrict routine use.

Combining modalities for optimal diagnosis: Multimodality imaging leverages complementary strengths. MRI is preferred to evaluate muscle inflammation and to target biopsy, while CT is superior for rapid evaluation of deep neck spaces, airway compromise, and detection of gas or calcified structures. HRCT is indispensable for detailed lung parenchymal assessment and nodule characterization. FDG-PET/CT is useful when whole-body assessment is needed—either to detect widespread muscle involvement not covered by localized MRI, to screen for occult malignancy, or to identify metastatic disease.

A pragmatic approach is to tailor imaging to the clinical question. For a patient with suspected myositis and focal muscle symptoms, MRI of the symptomatic region with STIR/T2 and post-contrast T1 fat-suppressed sequences is first-line to document active inflammation and guide biopsy. If there are symptoms or signs suggesting deep neck infection (fever, severe odynophagia, trismus, airway compromise), contrast-enhanced CT neck is urgent to assess abscess and plan drainage. For respiratory symptoms, HRCT chest is essential to characterize ILD pattern and disease extent. If paraneoplastic myositis is a concern or initial cancer screening is unrevealing, FDG-PET/CT can be performed to evaluate the whole body for occult malignancy and to survey for widespread muscle inflammation. In complex cases, combining PET and targeted MRI provides both metabolic and anatomic detail: PET identifies hypermetabolic regions and MRI characterizes tissue architecture and guides biopsy.

Clinical decision thresholds and follow-up imaging: Use imaging findings to guide therapy and interventions. Presence of STIR hyperintensity and enhancement on MRI in multiple muscles supports starting or escalating immunosuppression and selecting biopsy site. HRCT patterns influence prognosis and choice of therapy for ILD: NSIP and OP patterns are more likely to respond to immunosuppression, whereas UIP may require antifibrotic strategies and has a poorer prognosis. On PET, a newly identified hypermetabolic lesion consistent with malignancy requires tissue diagnosis; a negative PET does not definitively exclude cancer, so age-appropriate and risk-adapted screening should continue.

In summary, imaging in myositis and neck pathology relies on understanding the pathophysiologic basis of signal changes across modalities. MRI best detects muscle inflammation and chronic replacement changes; CT is essential for neck emergency assessment and detailed chest parenchymal evaluation via HRCT; FDG-PET adds whole-body metabolic assessment for inflammation and malignancy screening. Awareness of limitations and artifacts—combined with clinical judgment and targeted use of complementary modalities—optimizes diagnostic accuracy and patient care.
