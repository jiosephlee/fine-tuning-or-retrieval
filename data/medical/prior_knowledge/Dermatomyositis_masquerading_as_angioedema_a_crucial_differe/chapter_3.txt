Introduction

Skeletal muscle is the largest organ system by mass in most adults and serves essential roles in posture, locomotion, respiration, and metabolic homeostasis. Understanding the microanatomy, fiber-type specialization, and the tissue-level organization of muscle is critical for interpreting clinical syndromes of weakness, exercise intolerance, and myoglobinuric kidney injury. Equally important is familiarity with the mechanisms by which muscle is injured and regenerates, and with the laboratory and electrodiagnostic tools used to detect and localize muscle pathology. This chapter reviews skeletal muscle structure and organization, the cellular and molecular bases of muscle injury and regeneration, the common biochemical markers used to detect myocyte damage (their isoforms and interpretation), the significance of myoglobinuria and its contribution to acute kidney injury, the electrophysiologic distinctions between myopathic and neuropathic processes, and the typical MRI and PET imaging features of active inflammatory myositis versus chronic fibrotic or fatty replacement.

# Muscle fiber types, fascicular organization, connective tissue (endomysium, perimysium, epimysium)

Skeletal muscle is organized hierarchically from the molecular contractile apparatus to whole muscles. At the cellular level, the muscle fiber (myocyte) is a multinucleated syncytium formed by fusion of myoblasts during development. Each fiber contains parallel arrays of myofibrils composed of repeating sarcomeres—structures of interdigitating actin and myosin filaments whose regulated sliding generates force.

Muscle fiber types are commonly divided into slow-twitch (type I) and fast-twitch (type II) classes, with further subdivisions of type II into IIa, IIx (IIb in some species), and IIb in experimental contexts. These types reflect differences in contractile speed, metabolic substrate utilization, fatigue resistance, and innervation patterns.

- Type I fibers (slow oxidative): These fibers express myosin heavy chain isoforms adapted for slow, sustained contraction. They have abundant mitochondria, high capillary density, and rely primarily on oxidative phosphorylation. They are fatigue-resistant and predominate in postural and endurance muscles. Histochemically they are dark on oxidative enzyme stains (e.g., succinate dehydrogenase) and show low glycogen content relative to fast-glycolytic fibers.

- Type IIa fibers (fast oxidative-glycolytic): Intermediate in contractile speed and fatigue resistance, type IIa fibers use both oxidative and glycolytic metabolism. They are recruited for activities requiring more power than pure endurance but more endurance than pure sprinting.

- Type IIx/IIb fibers (fast glycolytic): These fibers have high contraction speed and generate large forces but fatigue rapidly. They have fewer mitochondria, lower capillary density, and rely heavily on anaerobic glycolysis and stored glycogen. They are prevalent in muscles used for brief, powerful bursts.

Fiber-type composition varies between muscles and between individuals and can adapt with training, denervation, or disease. Motor unit recruitment follows the size principle: small motor neurons innervating type I fibers are recruited first, then progressively larger motor units with type II fibers.

At the tissue level, muscle fibers are bundled within fascicles surrounded by connective tissue layers with distinct roles:

- Endomysium: A fine network of reticular connective tissue and capillary-rich extracellular matrix that envelops individual muscle fibers. The endomysium contains satellite cells (muscle stem cells), fibroblasts, microvasculature, and nerve terminals. It transmits forces between adjacent fibers and provides the extracellular niche for regeneration.

- Perimysium: Denser connective tissue that groups fibers into fascicles. It contains larger blood vessels, nerves, and lymphatics. The perimysium contributes to passive elastic properties and transmits force from fascicles to the tendinous insertion.

- Epimysium: A sheath of dense irregular connective tissue that encases the entire muscle. It integrates with the deep fascia and tendon, providing continuity of force transmission from muscle to bone and structural protection.

These connective tissue layers contain extracellular matrix components—collagens (types I and III predominating in perimysium and epimysium), proteoglycans, elastin, and glycoproteins—that influence mechanical properties and repair responses. Fibrosis following chronic injury or unresolved inflammation results from dysregulated matrix deposition, altering compliance and function.

Neuromuscular junctions are specialized sites where motor neuron terminals contact the sarcolemma at the motor endplate; their integrity is essential for voluntary contraction. Vascular supply is arranged so that capillary density correlates with oxidative capacity; reduced perfusion or microvascular injury can precipitate muscle ischemia and necrosis.

# Mechanisms of muscle injury and regeneration

Muscle injury may originate from mechanical trauma, ischemia-reperfusion, metabolic disturbances, toxins, immune-mediated inflammation, genetic defects, or denervation. Despite diverse etiologies, common final pathways of myofiber damage include sarcolemmal disruption, excitation-contraction uncoupling, mitochondrial dysfunction, intracellular calcium overload, oxidative stress, and activation of proteases.

- Sarcolemmal disruption: Mechanical strains (e.g., crush injury), exercise-induced eccentric contraction, or membrane-targeting toxins can create breaches in the sarcolemma. Loss of membrane integrity allows uncontrolled influx of extracellular calcium and sodium, efflux of cytosolic enzymes (e.g., creatine kinase), and activation of degradative pathways.

- Calcium overload: Elevated intracellular calcium activates calpains and other proteases and phospholipases, degrading structural proteins and membranes. Calcium handling defects (e.g., ryanodine receptor mutations) can produce malignant hyperthermia or exertional rhabdomyolysis.

- Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress: Impaired oxidative phosphorylation reduces ATP needed for ion pumps, exacerbating ion gradients collapse. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) from dysfunctional mitochondria further damage lipids, proteins, and DNA.

- Inflammatory responses: Immune-mediated myopathies (polymyositis, dermatomyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy) or secondary inflammation following necrosis recruit macrophages, T cells, and complement. These cells can clear necrotic debris but may also extend tissue injury through cytokine-mediated cytotoxicity.

- Ischemia-reperfusion: Vascular occlusion produces energy failure; reperfusion introduces ROS and a neutrophil-mediated inflammatory surge that magnifies injury.

- Genetic defects: Mutations in structural proteins (e.g., dystrophin in Duchenne muscular dystrophy) or in metabolic enzymes predispose fibers to mechanical stress or metabolic failure, respectively, leading to chronic degeneration-regeneration cycles.

Regeneration in skeletal muscle is robust relative to many tissues and relies on satellite cells—resident muscle stem cells located beneath the basal lamina in the endomysium. Following injury, a coordinated sequence occurs:

1. Degeneration and inflammation: Necrotic fibers are cleared by neutrophils and macrophages. Macrophage phenotype shifts from pro-inflammatory (M1) during debris clearance to anti-inflammatory/pro-repair (M2) during resolution.

2. Satellite cell activation: Quiescent satellite cells express Pax7; upon activation they upregulate MyoD and Myf5, proliferate, and generate myoblasts.

3. Differentiation and fusion: Myoblasts differentiate into myocytes expressing myogenin and fuse with one another or with surviving fibers to form multinucleated myotubes that mature into new fibers. The basal lamina provides a scaffold for correct alignment. Successful regeneration restores sarcomeric architecture.

4. Remodeling: Revascularization, reinnervation, and reconstitution of connective tissue occur. If the basal lamina is preserved and innervation reestablished, fiber size and function can be near-normal.

Poor regenerative outcomes arise when satellite cell pools are depleted, basal lamina is destroyed (e.g., extensive crush injury), chronic inflammation persists, or fibrosis/fatty infiltration dominates. Repeated cycles of necrosis and repair—as in many muscular dystrophies—lead to progressive loss of functional muscle and replacement by collagen and adipose tissue.

Clinically, timing matters: serum enzymes rise early after acute injury and fall as regeneration proceeds; biopsy features distinguish active necrosis with macrophages and regenerating fibers (rounded fibers with basophilic cytoplasm and central nuclei) from chronic changes of fiber size variation, endomysial fibrosis, and fatty replacement.

# Creatine kinase (isoforms), lactate dehydrogenase, aldolase and transaminases as muscle injury markers

Biochemical markers detect myocyte membrane disruption or increased membrane permeability, releasing cytosolic enzymes into the circulation. The most widely used marker is creatine kinase (CK). Other enzymes—lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), aldolase, and transaminases (AST and ALT)—are also informative but less muscle-specific.

Creatine kinase

CK catalyzes the reversible transfer: creatine + ATP ↔ phosphocreatine + ADP. Skeletal muscle contains high concentrations of CK to buffer ATP during contraction. CK is a dimeric enzyme with tissue-specific isoenzymes formed from combinations of M (muscle) and B (brain) subunits:

- CK-MM: Predominant in skeletal and cardiac muscle; accounts for the majority of serum CK in skeletal muscle injury.
- CK-MB: Enriched in myocardium but also present in skeletal muscle at low levels.
- CK-BB: Found in brain and smooth muscle.

Measurement:

- Reference ranges vary by laboratory; approximate normal total CK (adult male): up to ~200 U/L. Women and older adults may have lower normal values. Exercise, intramuscular injections, and recent trauma can raise CK. Many labs use units U/L with assay-specific cutoffs.

Interpretation:

- CK rises within 2–12 hours after acute muscle injury, peaks at about 24–72 hours, and declines with a half-life of roughly 36–48 hours if no ongoing injury.
- Very high CK values (>5000–10,000 U/L) raise concern for rhabdomyolysis and risk of myoglobinuria and acute kidney injury.
- CK isoenzyme analysis (CK-MB fraction) is important in distinguishing myocardial infarction from skeletal muscle injury; however, skeletal muscle injury can raise CK-MB modestly. Cardiac troponins are more specific for myocardial necrosis.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)

LDH catalyzes the interconversion of lactate and pyruvate and is present in virtually all tissues. Serum LDH comprises five isoenzymes (LDH-1 to LDH-5). LDH-5 is predominant in liver and skeletal muscle. LDH rises with muscle injury but lacks specificity and is often used in conjunction with CK. LDH has a longer serum half-life than CK and may remain elevated after CK normalizes.

Aldolase

Aldolase participates in glycolysis (aldol cleavage of fructose-1,6-bisphosphate). It is abundant in skeletal muscle, especially glycolytic fibers, and serum aldolase is sometimes measured when CK is normal but clinical suspicion for myopathy remains (e.g., some inflammatory myopathies). Normal aldolase: approximately 1–7 U/L depending on lab. Like LDH, aldolase lacks tissue specificity.

Transaminases (AST and ALT)

AST (aspartate aminotransferase) is present in liver, heart, and skeletal muscle; ALT (alanine aminotransferase) is more liver-predominant but also present in muscle. Isolated elevation of AST or ALT may stem from muscle injury rather than hepatic pathology, especially when accompanied by elevated CK. In practice, a markedly elevated AST with proportionate CK elevation suggests muscle source; the AST/ALT ratio may be >1 in muscle injury. Normal ranges: AST ~10–40 U/L, ALT ~7–56 U/L (lab-dependent). Always interpret in clinical context and consider hepatic testing if needed.

Practical clinical decision points

- When CK is mildly elevated (e.g., <2–3× upper limit of normal) in an asymptomatic patient, consider exercise, intramuscular injections, or statin therapy before exhaustive workup. Repeat testing after rest can clarify trend.
- CK elevations >5× upper limit (and particularly >10×) warrant evaluation for rhabdomyolysis risk and possible renal injury. Aggressive fluid management and monitoring of electrolytes may be appropriate.
- When distinguishing myocardial vs skeletal sources of CK-MB elevation, use cardiac troponins and clinical context; CK-MB alone is insufficiently specific.
- In suspected inflammatory myositis, CK is frequently elevated but may be normal in some subtypes (e.g., inclusion body myositis or amyopathic dermatomyositis). Aldolase and LDH can help in such scenarios.

# Urine myoglobin and risk of myoglobinuria/acute kidney injury

Myoglobin is a small (17 kDa) oxygen-binding heme protein abundant in skeletal and cardiac muscle. Significant myofiber necrosis releases myoglobin into plasma; when serum myoglobin exceeds renal handling capacity, it is filtered and appears in urine—myoglobinuria—which can be directly nephrotoxic.

Pathophysiology of myoglobin-mediated kidney injury

- Tubular obstruction: Myoglobin can precipitate in the acidic distal tubule, forming casts that obstruct tubular flow. Cast formation is promoted by hypovolemia and acidic urine (pH <6).

- Direct tubular toxicity: The heme moiety of myoglobin catalyzes formation of reactive oxygen species, causing lipid peroxidation and direct cytotoxicity to tubular epithelial cells.

- Vasoconstriction and ischemia: Free heme and products of muscle breakdown promote renal vasoconstriction, worsening perfusion and exacerbating tubular injury.

Risk factors

- Massive muscle injury with large CK increases (often CK >5000–10,000 U/L) and elevated plasma myoglobin.
- Volume depletion, hypotension, sepsis, preexisting chronic kidney disease, concurrent nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, contrast agents), and acidic urine increase risk.
- Prolonged immobilization and crush injuries classically precipitate rhabdomyolysis and myoglobinuric renal failure.

Clinical recognition

- Urine may be dark brown or tea-colored; a positive urine dipstick for blood with microscopic absence or paucity of red blood cells suggests myoglobinuria (dipstick reacts to heme). Confirmatory testing for myoglobin in urine is not universally available; clinical inference is common.
- Laboratory workup: Serum CK, serum creatinine, electrolytes (especially hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia early then hypercalcemia in recovery), plasma myoglobin (if available), and urinalysis. Monitor urine output.

Management principles

- Early aggressive isotonic fluid resuscitation to maintain urine output (target often ≥0.5–1 mL/kg/hr, tailored to clinical context) to dilute nephrotoxins and prevent cast formation.
- Administration of bicarbonate to alkalinize urine (controversial; some guidelines suggest considering urinary alkalinization in severe cases to reduce myoglobin precipitation) — typical approach is to target urine pH >6.5, recognizing evidence is mixed.
- Diuretics such as mannitol or loop diuretics are sometimes used to promote urine flow after volume resuscitation.
- Avoid nephrotoxins and monitor/treat electrolyte abnormalities (hyperkalemia is life-threatening).
- Renal replacement therapy for severe acute kidney injury, refractory hyperkalemia, volume overload, or uremic complications.

Quantitative thresholds for risk are imprecise, but higher CK levels correlate with greater risk. Not all patients with high CK develop acute kidney injury; clinical context and supportive measures determine outcome.

# Electromyography basics for myopathy vs neuropathy

Electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies (NCS) are electrodiagnostic tools used to localize neuromuscular disorders and to distinguish myopathic from neuropathic processes. EMG assesses electrical activity of muscles at rest and during voluntary contraction via needle electrodes; NCS examine conduction velocity and amplitude of peripheral nerves.

Key principles and findings

- Motor unit potential (MUP): When a motor neuron and its innervated muscle fibers fire synchronously, the summated waveform is a MUP. EMG examines MUP amplitude, duration, and morphology.

- Recruitment: Refers to how additional motor units are activated to increase force. Recruitment pattern is informative: early or reduced recruitment occurs when fewer motor units are available or functioning; increased recruitment (i.e., small MUPs but high recruitment) indicates loss of force per motor unit.

Myopathic patterns

- At rest: Usually no spontaneous activity in mild chronic myopathies. Active myositis or necrosis may show spontaneous fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves due to irritable muscle fibers.

- During voluntary contraction: Myopathic processes characteristically produce small-amplitude, short-duration MUPs with early recruitment. Why? Because individual muscle fibers are lost or weakened within motor units, so the surviving fibers generate smaller potentials, and the nervous system recruits more motor units to achieve force.

- Complex repetitive discharges and myotonic discharges may be present in specific myopathies (e.g., myotonic dystrophy, myotonia congenita).

Neuropathic patterns

- Denervation: Focal or generalized axonal loss produces spontaneous activity at rest—fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves—reflecting denervated single muscle fibers. Fasciculation potentials (visible twitching) are often present.

- MUPs: Chronic reinnervation yields large-amplitude, long-duration polyphasic MUPs because surviving motor neurons sprout collaterals to reinnervate denervated fibers, increasing motor unit size. Recruitment is reduced; there are fewer motor units to generate force.

- NCS: In neuropathies, conduction velocity slowing or decreased amplitudes depend on demyelinating versus axonal pathophysiology. Demyelination shows slowed conduction velocity and prolonged distal latencies. Axonal loss shows reduced compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitudes.

Distinguishing features and clinical reasoning

- Small, short-duration MUPs with early recruitment point to a myopathy, particularly when correlated with weak muscles and preserved reflexes (or relatively less reflex loss than would be expected for the degree of weakness). EMG may help localize active inflammation by sampling multiple muscles.

- Large, long-duration MUPs with reduced recruitment and prominent spontaneous activity suggest chronic neuropathic reinnervation.

- Acute denervation from neuropathy can produce fibrillations before reinnervation changes, which helps timing.

- EMG is a functional test and should be interpreted with clinical exam and laboratory data (CK, imaging). Muscle fiber loss without denervation can produce myopathic EMG; however, severe atrophy can also reduce MUP amplitudes. In inflammatory myopathies, EMG is sensitive but not specific; biopsy and serology are necessary for definitive diagnosis.

Technical considerations

- Sampling multiple muscles from different limbs and different spinal levels improves diagnostic yield and helps decide a biopsy site (choose muscles that are weak but not end-stage fibrotic).

- EMG is operator-dependent and can be uncomfortable. Its sensitivity varies by disease stage and subtype.

# MRI and PET imaging appearances of active myositis vs chronic changes

Imaging of muscle provides anatomical localization, assessment of disease activity, and evaluation of chronic structural changes. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the modality of choice for muscle imaging, offering high soft-tissue contrast, assessment of edema/inflammation, and detection of fatty replacement and atrophy. Positron emission tomography (PET), typically combined with CT (PET/CT) or MRI (PET/MR) using 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG), can detect metabolically active inflammation.

MRI features

- Acute/active myositis: On MRI, active inflammatory myositis characteristically demonstrates increased signal intensity on fluid-sensitive sequences (T2-weighted with fat suppression or short tau inversion recovery—STIR). This hyperintensity reflects increased free water from edema and inflammatory cell infiltration. Post-contrast T1-weighted sequences with gadolinium often show enhancement of involved muscles, reflecting increased vascular permeability and inflammation. The pattern of involvement may be symmetric or asymmetric depending on etiology; proximal muscle groups (pelvic and shoulder girdles) are commonly involved in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies.

Acute myositis MRI findings often include fascial enhancement (in dermatomyositis), perifascicular edema, and muscle belly edema with relative sparing of fat. In some disorders (e.g., inclusion body myositis), active inflammation may be patchy and less prominent.

- Chronic changes: Chronic muscle disease demonstrates atrophy of muscle bellies, loss of bulk, and a replacement of muscle tissue by fat and fibrous tissue. On MRI, fatty infiltration is best seen on T1-weighted sequences as increased high signal intensity within muscle; fat-suppressed sequences confirm fat content. Chronic changes result in reduced or absent STIR/T2 hyperintensity (no active edema), and the distribution of fatty replacement helps differentiate myopathies (e.g., specific patterns in muscular dystrophies). Fibrosis contributes to volume loss and decreased signal heterogeneity.

- Patterns that aid diagnosis: The distribution (proximal vs distal), symmetry, selective muscle involvement within compartments, and localization to fascial planes can suggest specific diagnoses. For example, symmetric proximal thigh involvement suggests polymyositis or dermatomyositis, while selective quadriceps and deep finger flexor involvement suggests inclusion body myositis.

PET imaging (FDG-PET)

- FDG uptake increases in tissues with enhanced glucose metabolism—activated inflammatory cells (e.g., macrophages, lymphocytes) take up FDG. In active myositis, affected muscles can demonstrate increased FDG uptake, correlating with inflammatory activity. PET can therefore detect active inflammation even when MRI is equivocal and may be useful for whole-body assessment of occult inflammatory myopathies or paraneoplastic associations.

- Limitations: FDG uptake is not specific for inflammatory cells and may occur with infection, recent exercise, or neoplastic processes. Standardization of uptake quantification (SUV values) for muscle inflammation is evolving. PET is less spatially detailed than MRI for architectural changes but offers a whole-body perspective and can identify metabolically active extraskeletal sites (e.g., associated malignancies).

Clinical use and decision points

- MRI guides biopsy: Identify muscles showing edema and enhancement but not end-stage fatty replacement to increase diagnostic yield of biopsy.

- MRI monitors therapy: Reduction of T2/STIR hyperintensity and normalization of enhancement correspond to decreased inflammatory activity. Persistent fatty replacement indicates irreversible damage.

- Combined modalities: In difficult cases, combining MRI and FDG-PET may help distinguish active inflammation (FDG-avid, T2 hyperintense) from chronic changes (T1 hyperintense due to fat, low FDG uptake). PET may detect subclinical metabolic activity when MRI is normal or when muscles are diffusely involved.

- Electrophysiologic and imaging correlation: EMG may show irritability in areas of MRI-defined edema. Use imaging to localize areas for EMG or biopsy rather than random sampling.

Summary

A sound understanding of skeletal muscle architecture and fiber specialization sets the stage for interpreting pathophysiologic mechanisms in muscle disease. Injury pathways converge on sarcolemmal disruption, calcium overload, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammation; regeneration depends on preserved basal lamina and satellite cell function. Biochemical markers—CK and its isoforms, LDH, aldolase, and transaminases—help detect and estimate the magnitude and timing of muscle injury; very high CK values raise concern for rhabdomyolysis and myoglobin-mediated renal injury. EMG and NCS distinguish myopathic from neuropathic processes based on MUP morphology and recruitment patterns and help localize disease for biopsy. MRI (and adjunctive PET) provides powerful anatomical and functional assessment, distinguishing active inflammatory edema from chronic fatty and fibrotic replacement, guiding biopsy site selection, and tracking treatment response. Integration of clinical history, laboratory data, electrodiagnostics, and imaging yields the most accurate assessment of neuromuscular disease.
