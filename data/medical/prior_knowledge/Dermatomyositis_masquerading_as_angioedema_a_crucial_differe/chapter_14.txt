Introduction

Dermatomyositis (DM) is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy characterized by proximal muscle weakness and distinctive cutaneous manifestations. Beyond the morbidity attributable to muscle inflammation and skin disease, DM carries a well-established association with an increased risk of malignancy. Recognizing this association and adopting a rational, evidence-informed approach to cancer screening are essential components of the initial evaluation and ongoing management of patients with DM. This chapter reviews the epidemiologic data that link DM with specific cancers, summarizes clinical and serologic risk factors that stratify patients for malignancy, and provides practical guidance on baseline and interval screening strategies, including the role and limitations of PET-CT. Finally, it summarizes major guideline and consensus recommendations to help formulate a pragmatic, patient-centered cancer screening approach in DM.

# Epidemiologic evidence linking dermatomyositis and specific cancers

Epidemiologic studies consistently demonstrate an elevated risk of cancer in patients with DM compared with the general population. The magnitude and pattern of risk vary by study design, geographic region, and the time window considered relative to DM onset. Standardized incidence ratios (SIRs) for cancer in DM have commonly been reported in the range of approximately 2–7, with the highest risks occurring within the first 1–3 years after DM diagnosis. In contrast, polymyositis generally carries a lower cancer risk than DM, although some studies still show modestly increased rates.

The spectrum of associated malignancies includes both solid tumors and hematologic cancers. Recurrently reported solid tumors include lung, breast, colorectal, ovarian, pancreatic, stomach (gastric), and nasopharyngeal carcinomas. The relative importance of different tumor types varies by region: for example, nasopharyngeal carcinoma is overrepresented in East and Southeast Asia, whereas ovarian and lung cancers are more commonly reported in European and North American cohorts. Hematologic malignancies, particularly non-Hodgkin lymphoma, are also reported with increased frequency in some series.

Time relationship is important. A substantial proportion of cancers are detected within a narrow window around DM onset, often defined as within 3 years before to 3 years after DM diagnosis, supporting the concept of DM as a paraneoplastic phenomenon in some patients. However, an elevated long-term cancer risk persists beyond the initial years in some cohorts, and therefore long-term vigilance is reasonable, particularly in patients with ongoing risk factors or serologic markers suggestive of paraneoplastic DM.

Epidemiologic heterogeneity arises from differences in cohort selection (hospital-based vs. population-based), case ascertainment, follow-up duration, and screening intensity. These factors influence reported cancer incidence and the apparent utility of screening strategies; nevertheless, the aggregate literature supports targeted cancer screening at the time of DM diagnosis and close surveillance during the early years after diagnosis.

# Risk factors that increase likelihood of associated malignancy (age, certain autoantibodies, dysphagia, cutaneous features)

Identifying clinical and laboratory features that stratify cancer risk in DM helps tailor screening intensity. Important risk factors supported by multiple studies include older age at DM onset, certain myositis-specific autoantibodies, severe or atypical cutaneous findings, and features such as dysphagia.

Age. Advancing age at DM onset is among the most consistent predictors of associated malignancy. Most studies demonstrate a markedly higher risk of cancer in patients older than approximately 40–50 years at diagnosis, with risk climbing further with increasing age. In practical terms, age is often used to determine whether to pursue routine age-appropriate cancer screening alone or to adopt a more aggressive, targeted search for occult malignancy.

Myositis-specific autoantibodies. Autoantibody profiling has proved particularly valuable for cancer risk stratification. Anti-transcriptional intermediary factor 1-gamma (anti–TIF1-γ, also called anti–TIF1 or anti-p155/p140) antibodies are strongly associated with an elevated risk of malignancy in adults with DM. Multiple studies report large effect sizes: presence of anti–TIF1-γ may increase the odds of an associated cancer several-fold relative to antibody-negative patients. Another antibody, anti–nuclear matrix protein 2 (anti–NXP-2, formerly anti-MJ), has also been linked to increased cancer risk in some series. Anti–small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme (anti–SAE) has been associated with malignancy in smaller studies. Conversely, antibodies directed against aminoacyl tRNA synthetases (e.g., anti–Jo-1) and antibodies typically associated with classic, non-paraneoplastic DM (e.g., anti–Mi-2) are generally associated with lower cancer risk. Thus, autoantibody profile is a key determinant of whether a patient should undergo intensified cancer evaluation.

Clinical features: dysphagia and cutaneous findings. Dysphagia is both common in DM and independently associated with an increased risk of cancer in some cohorts. Severe or rapidly progressive dysphagia—suggesting more extensive disease—warrants particular attention because it may coexist with occult malignancy.

Certain cutaneous phenotypes have also been tied to higher cancer risk. Classic DM skin findings include Gottron papules, heliotrope rash, V-sign and shawl sign photodistribution, and mechanic’s hands. Some studies have suggested that cutaneous necrosis, vasculopathic lesions, and rapidly progressive skin disease may correlate with a greater likelihood of an associated malignancy, possibly reflecting paraneoplastic immune mechanisms. However, the association is less robust than that for age and specific autoantibodies, and cutaneous features should be integrated with other clinical and serologic data.

Other factors. Male sex has sometimes been associated with higher cancer risk in DM, though sex effects vary by study and tumor type. Systemic inflammatory markers have no consistent predictive value for cancer. A history of smoking increases risk for lung cancer and may modify the overall probability of malignancy in individual patients.

Integrating risk factors. In clinical practice, a patient with DM who is older (commonly >40–50 years), anti–TIF1-γ or possibly anti–NXP-2 positive, and/or has dysphagia or atypical skin features would be considered high risk and merits an intensive malignancy search. Younger patients, those with anti–Jo-1 or anti–Mi-2 antibodies, and those without other risk features may be managed with standard age-appropriate cancer screening plus targeted testing guided by symptoms and physical examination.

# Baseline and interval screening strategies (imaging, tumor markers, endoscopic evaluations)

Screening aims to detect occult malignancy early enough to affect outcomes while minimizing unnecessary tests, false positives, and harms. Screening should be individualized based on risk stratification described above, resources, and patient preferences. A practical approach combines a careful history and physical examination with baseline targeted investigations, and repeated assessments during the period of greatest risk.

Baseline evaluation. All patients with newly diagnosed DM should receive an organized baseline assessment focused on identifying features suggestive of malignancy and ensuring they are up to date with routine, age-/sex-appropriate cancer screening. Key components include a thorough review of systems (weight loss, fevers, anorexia, focal pain, change in bowel or bladder habits, vaginal bleeding), comprehensive skin and lymph node exam, chest auscultation, abdominal palpation, pelvic and breast exams in appropriate patients, and ENT/oral cavity inspection when indicated (especially in regions with endemic nasopharyngeal carcinoma).

Age-appropriate screening. All patients should receive standard population screening per local guidelines: mammography for women (commonly biennial or annual depending on regional recommendations; start ages typically 40–50), Pap smear and HPV testing as indicated, colonoscopy or fecal immunochemical testing for colorectal cancer per recommended schedules (colonoscopy typically every 10 years if normal starting at age 45–50 in many regions), and PSA testing in men according to shared decision-making. These baseline preventive measures are essential because certain cancers associated with DM (e.g., breast, colorectal) are amenable to detection by routine screening.

Targeted imaging. For high-risk patients, baseline cross-sectional imaging of chest, abdomen, and pelvis is commonly recommended. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of chest/abdomen/pelvis (CT CAP) is frequently used because it surveys the common sites for occult solid tumors. In women, pelvic imaging with transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS) or pelvic MRI may be added, particularly to evaluate for ovarian or uterine malignancy. For suspected head and neck cancers (e.g., nasopharyngeal carcinoma), contrast-enhanced MRI of the nasopharynx or dedicated ENT evaluation with flexible nasopharyngoscopy is appropriate.

Tumor markers. Serum tumor markers are imperfect as primary screening tests due to limited sensitivity and specificity, but they can play a role as adjuncts in a targeted evaluation. Commonly used markers include CA-125 for ovarian cancer (reference range commonly ≤35 U/mL), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) for colorectal cancer (reference usually <3–5 ng/mL depending on lab), CA 19-9 for pancreaticobiliary malignancy (reference often <37 U/mL), and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) and beta-hCG in specific clinical contexts. PSA (prostate-specific antigen) in men is used for prostate cancer screening (commonly considered abnormal >4.0 ng/mL, though age-adjusted thresholds and shared decision-making apply). Elevations should prompt directed imaging and specialist referral but must be interpreted with caution given false positives from benign conditions.

Endoscopic evaluations. Endoscopic studies are indicated when symptoms or other screening tests suggest gastrointestinal pathology. Because gastric and colorectal cancers have been reported in DM cohorts, many clinicians recommend colonoscopy as part of baseline evaluation for patients who are not up to date with screening, particularly in older adults. Upper endoscopy (esophagogastroduodenoscopy) is considered when symptoms such as dysphagia, odynophagia, persistent upper GI pain, or unexplained iron deficiency anemia are present, or in geographic regions with high gastric cancer prevalence. For patients with dysphagia, a swallow study and ENT/esophageal evaluation are important both to assess the muscular cause and to exclude a structural or malignant obstruction.

Interval screening. The cancer risk in DM is highest in the early years after diagnosis, particularly within the first 1–3 years. Most experts advocate repeat cancer assessment at regular intervals during this window. A practical schedule used by many centers is evaluation at diagnosis with targeted imaging, then repeat assessments at 6–12 months and annually for up to 3 years in high-risk patients. For low-risk patients, maintaining routine age-appropriate screening and symptom-based reassessment may suffice. Any new systemic symptoms, unexplained weight loss, refractory DM features, or lab abnormalities (e.g., new cytopenias) should prompt expedited cancer re-evaluation irrespective of timing.

Decision points. The clinician must weigh pretest probability against test performance: in high pretest probability (older age, high-risk antibodies), the threshold for comprehensive imaging (CT CAP, pelvic imaging, or PET-CT) is lower. In low-risk patients, the harms and costs of broad imaging may outweigh benefits. Shared decision-making and documentation of the rationale for the chosen screening intensity are recommended.

# Role of PET-CT in occult cancer detection and limitations

Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography combined with computed tomography (FDG PET-CT) is increasingly used as a “one-stop” modality to search for occult malignancy in DM. PET-CT offers whole-body metabolic imaging and can detect metabolically active lesions that are occult on anatomical imaging. Several studies report high sensitivity of PET-CT for detecting malignancy in DM, and some series suggest similar or better detection rates compared with conventional CT CAP combined with focused investigations.

Advantages. PET-CT can detect nodal and extranodal disease, provide metabolic information that helps distinguish benign from malignant lesions, and identify occult primaries that would otherwise require multiple imaging modalities to find. PET-CT is particularly useful where the differential includes lymphoma or cancers that are FDG-avid, and it can simultaneously evaluate the extent of disease for staging purposes.

Limitations and pitfalls. Despite its strengths, PET-CT has important limitations:

- False positives: Infectious or inflammatory processes—including active myositis, cutaneous inflammation, and reactive lymphadenopathy—can show increased FDG uptake, leading to false-positive findings requiring further invasive evaluation such as biopsy. In DM patients, active inflammatory muscle and skin disease can confound PET-CT interpretation.

- False negatives: Some tumor types are poorly FDG-avid (e.g., certain low-grade tumors, some prostate cancers, mucinous adenocarcinomas), or lesions may be below the spatial resolution of PET-CT (typically lesions <5–7 mm), leading to false-negative results. Small mucosal lesions in the nasopharynx or gastrointestinal tract may be missed.

- Cost, accessibility, and radiation: PET-CT is expensive and may not be readily available in some settings. It imparts a radiation dose comparable to or greater than CT, which is a consideration for younger patients or repeated studies.

- Lack of standardized protocols: There is no universally accepted algorithm for the use of PET-CT in DM; practices vary between centers. Differences in timing (baseline vs. after initial negative CT) and patient selection affect yield and cost-effectiveness.

Clinical positioning. PET-CT is most useful in patients at elevated risk for malignancy—those older at onset, anti–TIF1-γ or anti–NXP-2 positive, with systemic features suggestive of paraneoplastic disease, or in whom conventional directed testing is unrevealing despite high clinical suspicion. In centers with resources, a baseline PET-CT may be reasonable for high-risk patients as part of an initial comprehensive evaluation. If PET-CT is performed, clinicians should recognize potential false positives from inflammatory DM activity and interpret findings in the clinical context, often requiring histologic confirmation of suspected lesions. Where PET-CT is unavailable or contraindicated, contrast-enhanced CT CAP plus targeted imaging and endoscopy remains an appropriate strategy.

# Guidelines and consensus statements for myositis-associated cancer screening

Multiple national and international organizations and expert groups have issued guidance on cancer screening in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, including DM. While recommendations vary in specifics, common themes include: perform a careful baseline cancer assessment; prioritize age-appropriate routine screening; more intensive targeted screening for older patients and those with high-risk features; and heightened surveillance during the first 1–3 years after DM onset when cancer risk is highest. Below are the key principles distilled from major guidelines and consensus statements.

General recommendations. Organizations such as the European League Against Rheumatism/American College of Rheumatology (EULAR/ACR) myositis working groups, the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), and various national rheumatology and dermatology societies advocate a structured approach to cancer screening in DM:

- Baseline: At diagnosis, complete a thorough history and physical exam; ensure adherence to routine age-/sex-appropriate cancer screening; and consider targeted investigations directed by risk factors and symptoms.

- High-risk patients: For patients deemed high risk (age >40–50 years, anti–TIF1-γ or anti–NXP-2 positivity, dysphagia, rapid-onset or atypical skin findings), many experts recommend more intensive baseline evaluation, including cross-sectional imaging of chest/abdomen/pelvis (CT CAP or PET-CT), pelvic imaging in women (TVUS or MRI), and appropriate tumor markers. ENT evaluation and nasopharyngoscopy are recommended in regions with high nasopharyngeal carcinoma prevalence.

- Timing and frequency: Repeat focused assessments at 6–12 months and then annually for up to 3 years for high-risk patients is a commonly endorsed strategy. Continued vigilance beyond 3 years is individualized based on ongoing risk factors and clinical course.

Role of PET-CT. Consensus statements acknowledge the utility of PET-CT for whole-body assessment in detecting occult malignancy but stop short of a blanket recommendation for universal use, citing resource constraints, potential false positives, and variability in availability. PET-CT is often recommended as an option or as the preferred modality in centers where available for high-risk patients.

Tumor markers and endoscopy. Guidelines typically advise against indiscriminate use of serum tumor markers in low-risk patients but regard them as adjunctive tests in targeted evaluations. Endoscopic procedures are reserved for symptom-guided investigation or when regional cancer prevalence or risk factors indicate higher yield.

Shared decision-making and local adaptation. Given variation in resources, cancer epidemiology, and healthcare systems, guidelines emphasize individualized care and shared decision-making. Strategies should be adapted to local cancer prevalence (e.g., routine nasopharyngeal surveillance in endemic regions), availability of PET-CT, and patient comorbidities and preferences.

Evidence gaps and need for further research. Guidelines note the limited high-quality trial data on optimal screening algorithms and intervals, and the heterogeneity of existing studies. Consequently, many recommendations are based on expert consensus and observational data, and clinicians should apply them pragmatically while awaiting stronger evidence.

Practical algorithm (synthesis). A commonly used practical approach derived from guideline principles is:

- All patients: Detailed history and physical exam; ensure up-to-date routine, age-appropriate cancer screening (mammography, colonoscopy, Pap/HPV, PSA as applicable).

- High-risk features present: Add contrast-enhanced CT chest/abdomen/pelvis and pelvic imaging in women ± PET-CT where available; consider tumor markers (e.g., CA-125) and ENT evaluation for regional risks; repeat at 6–12 months and annually for 2–3 years.

- Low-risk patients: If no high-risk features and up to date with routine screening, maintain standard screening intervals and reassess for new symptoms; reserve broad imaging for clinical suspicion.

Conclusion

Dermatomyositis carries a clearly increased risk of malignancy, most pronounced in the early years after diagnosis. Clinical risk stratification—most prominently age and myositis-specific autoantibodies such as anti–TIF1-γ—permits targeted screening strategies that balance detection yield against costs and harms. Baseline assessment should combine careful history and examination, age-appropriate routine screening, and targeted imaging and endoscopic evaluation for high-risk features. PET-CT is a powerful tool for whole-body assessment but has limitations including false positives from inflammatory disease and variable availability. Major guidelines converge on intensified screening for older patients and those with high-risk serologic or clinical features, with close surveillance during the first 1–3 years after diagnosis. Clinicians should individualize screening plans, involve patients in decision-making, and maintain a low threshold to re-evaluate if new symptoms or refractory disease suggest an occult malignancy.
