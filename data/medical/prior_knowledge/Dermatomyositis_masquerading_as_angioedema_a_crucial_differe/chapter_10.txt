Introduction

Inflammatory myopathies, classically including polymyositis, dermatomyositis, and the antisynthetase syndrome, are systemic autoimmune diseases in which skeletal muscle inflammation is the dominant feature. The lungs are a common and clinically important site of involvement. Pulmonary manifestations range from interstitial lung disease (ILD) with a spectrum of radiologic and histologic patterns to complications of bulbar and respiratory muscle weakness such as aspiration and respiratory failure. For a clinician evaluating a patient with myositis, understanding the patterns of myositis-associated ILD, the distinction between aspiration pneumonitis and aspiration pneumonia, interpretation of pulmonary function testing (PFTs) including features of extrathoracic (neuromuscular) restriction, and practical approaches to monitoring respiratory muscle weakness is essential. This chapter synthesizes pathophysiology and clinical reasoning relevant to these topics and summarizes management principles for ILD in the context of connective tissue disease.

# Types and radiologic patterns of myositis-associated ILD (NSIP, OP, UIP patterns)

Myositis-associated ILD encompasses several radiologic and histopathologic patterns, the most common being nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) and organizing pneumonia (OP), with usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) and mixed patterns occurring less commonly. Different patterns carry implications for prognosis and response to therapy, and chest imaging—principally high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT)—provides the foundation for classification in clinical practice.

Nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) is the pattern most frequently observed in connective tissue disease-related ILD including myositis. On HRCT NSIP is characterized by ground-glass opacities and fine reticular abnormalities that are typically symmetric and predominantly basilar and peripheral but may demonstrate relative subpleural sparing. Traction bronchiectasis may be present in more fibrotic NSIP, but honeycombing is minimal or absent compared with UIP. NSIP represents a spectrum from cellular-predominant disease—where inflammation predominates and is more steroid-responsive—to fibrotic NSIP in which architectural distortion and fibrosis are present and the disease may be less reversible. In myositis, a cellular NSIP pattern often correlates with a better prognosis and higher likelihood of response to immunosuppression.

Organizing pneumonia (OP) (sometimes called bronchiolitis obliterans organizing pneumonia, BOOP) occurs in myositis patients either alone or superimposed on NSIP. HRCT features include patchy, often peribronchovascular and peripheral consolidations, frequently with a perilobular distribution and sometimes migratory behavior. Ground-glass opacity and small nodular consolidations are common. OP generally shows more rapid clinical symptoms and often a prompt clinical and radiologic response to corticosteroids, although relapses are common when steroids are tapered.

Usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) is less characteristic of connective tissue disease than NSIP but can occur in myositis and is associated with a worse prognosis. HRCT shows reticular opacities with traction bronchiectasis and honeycombing, with a subpleural and basal predominance. Compared with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), CTD-associated UIP may have a greater inflammatory component, but once established fibrosis with honeycombing is present, reversibility is limited.

Beyond these canonical patterns, myositis-associated ILD may show mixed patterns or atypical features including diffuse alveolar damage in fulminant presentations, or rapidly progressive interstitial pneumonia (RP-ILD), particularly associated with some autoantibodies (e.g., anti-MDA5). Clinical and serologic context influences pretest probability for specific patterns: antisynthetase antibodies (anti-Jo-1, PL-7, PL-12, EJ, OJ) are strongly associated with ILD (often NSIP or OP or mixed), whereas certain autoantibody profiles—anti-MDA5—are linked to rapidly progressive ILD with a high risk of respiratory failure.

When to suspect each pattern clinically follows from tempo and symptomatology: subacute dyspnea and cough over weeks to months with systemic myositis signs suggest NSIP or OP; rapidly progressive respiratory failure in days to weeks, with severe hypoxemia and diffuse bilateral consolidation, raises concern for diffuse alveolar damage or fulminant OP, and may require urgent aggressive immunosuppression. HRCT combined with PFTs and, when necessary, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) or surgical lung biopsy are used to confirm the pattern and guide therapy. Multidisciplinary discussion with radiology and pathology improves diagnostic accuracy.

# Pathophysiology of aspiration pneumonitis vs aspiration pneumonia

Aspiration into the lower respiratory tract can result in two distinct but sometimes overlapping clinical entities: aspiration pneumonitis and aspiration pneumonia. Discriminating them is important because management differs—aspiration pneumonitis is often primarily an inflammatory chemical injury for which supportive care is the mainstay, whereas aspiration pneumonia is a bacterial infection requiring antimicrobial therapy.

Aspiration pneumonitis (Mendelson’s syndrome) refers to the acute chemical injury produced by inhalation of sterile gastric contents, especially acidic material. The pathophysiology is primarily direct caustic injury to the airway and alveolar epithelium. Acid causes denaturation of surfactant, disruption of alveolar epithelial and capillary endothelial integrity, and triggers an intense local inflammatory response characterized by neutrophil influx and cytokine release. Clinically, aspiration pneumonitis typically presents shortly after a witnessed or suspected aspiration event with sudden cough, dyspnea, hypoxemia, tachypnea, and potential development of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) in severe cases. Fever and marked leukocytosis may be absent or minimal in the immediate post-aspiration period. Chest imaging initially may show focal airspace consolidation in dependent lobes (e.g., right lower lobe when aspiration occurs in upright patients), or diffuse bilateral alveolar infiltrates in more severe injury. The mainstay of management for aspiration pneumonitis is supportive: supplemental oxygen, airway protection, bronchodilators if bronchospasm is present, and close observation. Routine empiric antibiotics are not indicated unless there is clinical or microbiologic evidence suggesting bacterial infection or if the aspiration event was accompanied by or followed by fever, purulent sputum, or progressive infiltrates beyond the first 48 hours.

Aspiration pneumonia, by contrast, refers to a lung infection resulting from inhalation of oropharyngeal secretions that are colonized with pathogenic bacteria. Risk factors include impaired consciousness, impaired cough or swallow (bulbar dysfunction), poor oral hygiene, and hospitalization with exposure to multidrug-resistant organisms. Pathogenesis involves aspiration of bacteria-laden secretions into dependent airways where host defenses are overwhelmed, allowing microbial proliferation and host inflammatory response. The clinical course is often more indolent than chemical pneumonitis: fever, leukocytosis, productive cough, and consolidation on imaging develop over 24–72 hours. Empiric antibiotic therapy is indicated and should be chosen according to aspiration risk; community-acquired anaerobes and streptococci are typical in community cases, whereas gram-negative bacilli and Staphylococcus aureus are more likely in hospitalized patients or those with recent antibiotic exposure.

There is substantial clinical overlap: an initial chemical pneumonitis can be secondarily infected and become aspiration pneumonia, and presentation may be atypical in immunocompromised patients. In myositis, bulbar weakness predisposes to both acute aspiration events and chronic microaspiration, increasing risk for both entities. Clinical decision-making should integrate timing relative to aspiration, systemic inflammatory signs, purulence of secretions, radiographic progression after 48–72 hours, and microbiology from sputum or BAL when obtainable. In short: chemical pneumonitis equals sudden injury from sterile gastric contents requiring supportive care; aspiration pneumonia equals delayed bacterial infection requiring targeted antibiotics.

# Pulmonary function testing in restrictive vs obstructive disease and extrathoracic restriction

Pulmonary function testing (PFTs) provides objective quantification of ventilatory impairment and gas exchange abnormalities and is indispensable for diagnosing and monitoring both parenchymal lung disease and respiratory muscle dysfunction. Interpretation requires integration of spirometry, lung volumes (preferably measured by body plethysmography), and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO). Reference values are reported as percent predicted for age, sex, height, and ethnicity.

In intrinsic restrictive lung disease (parenchymal ILD), the hallmark PFT abnormalities are a reduced total lung capacity (TLC) and reduced forced vital capacity (FVC). TLC <80% predicted indicates restriction, and severity is often graded by FVC: mild restriction FVC 65–79% predicted, moderate 50–64%, severe <50%. The forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) is reduced proportionally to FVC, so the FEV1/FVC ratio is normal or increased (≥0.70 and often >0.80). Residual volume (RV) is reduced or normal. The single-breath DLCO is frequently reduced—in ILD due to thickening of the alveolar–capillary membrane and loss of alveolar surface area—and is a sensitive early marker. Normal DLCO ranges approximately 80–120% predicted; a DLCO <80% suggests impaired gas transfer, with DLCO <40% reflecting severe impairment. DLCO corrected for hemoglobin is more precise. Arterial blood gas may show hypoxemia at rest or with exertion, and significant desaturation with exertion is prognostically important.

In obstructive disease (e.g., COPD, asthma), TLC may be normal or increased due to air trapping, FEV1 is reduced more than FVC, and the FEV1/FVC ratio is decreased (<0.70 post-bronchodilator per many guidelines). The RV and RV/TLC ratio are typically increased. DLCO may be reduced in emphysema due to loss of alveolar-capillary surface area.

Extrathoracic restrictive physiology, as seen in neuromuscular disorders and respiratory muscle weakness in myositis, shares some spirometric features with parenchymal restriction but with important distinctions. Respiratory muscle weakness typically produces a reduction in maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures, a decline in vital capacity (VC) that is often out of proportion to any parenchymal abnormalities, and a characteristic fall in VC when measured supine. In neuromuscular weakness, TLC and FRC may be normal or reduced depending on the severity and elasticity of the chest wall; the hallmark is reduced VC and preserved flow-volume curve shapes relative to intrinsic lung disease. The FEV1/FVC ratio is normal or increased because expiratory airflow is preserved relative to reduced lung volumes. Because the lung parenchyma is otherwise normal, DLCO may be preserved or only mildly reduced; a normal DLCO in the setting of low VC suggests extrathoracic restriction rather than intrinsic ILD.

Specific PFT maneuvers and thresholds useful in neuromuscular disease include maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP, also called PImax) and maximal expiratory pressure (MEP, PEmax). Normal MIP in healthy adults is roughly -80 to -120 cmH2O for men and -60 to -80 cmH2O for women; values depend on age and effort. Normal MEP is roughly 100–140 cmH2O for men and 80–100 cmH2O for women. Values less negative than these norms signify weakness; clinically significant inspiratory muscle weakness is often considered when MIP is less negative than -30 cmH2O to -60 cmH2O, depending on context and symptomatology. Sniff nasal inspiratory pressure (SNIP) is an alternative measure of inspiratory strength—values <40 cmH2O in adults suggest clinically important weakness, though normal ranges vary. Importantly, VC that falls by more than 10–20% when the patient moves from upright to supine is a sensitive indicator of diaphragmatic weakness; a supine VC decrease >20% is strongly suggestive of diaphragmatic dysfunction. Moreover, in suspected neuromuscular respiratory failure, arterial blood gas demonstrating hypercapnia (PaCO2 >45 mmHg) or nocturnal oximetry showing desaturation are signs of ventilatory failure warranting prompt intervention.

Decision thresholds used in monitoring ILD and neuromuscular weakness: a decline in FVC ≥10% predicted over time is generally considered clinically significant and associated with worse outcomes; a decline in FVC of 5–10% plus worsening symptoms or imaging can also indicate progression. For DLCO, a decline of ≥15% is often used as significant. In neuromuscular disease, progressive decline in MIP/MEP or development of hypercapnia or nocturnal hypoventilation indicates need for escalating respiratory support.

# Clinical assessment and monitoring of respiratory muscle weakness

In myositis, respiratory compromise arises both from involvement of respiratory muscles (diaphragm, intercostals) and from bulbar dysfunction causing impaired airway protection and aspiration. Clinical assessment requires careful history, physical examination, bedside tests, and formal pulmonary and sleep studies when indicated.

History should elicit dyspnea at rest and on exertion, orthopnea, morning headache, daytime somnolence, cough, dysphagia, choking episodes, weight loss, and decreased exercise tolerance. Orthopnea and dyspnea that worsen when supine suggest diaphragmatic weakness. Nocturnal symptoms—frequent awakenings, nonrestorative sleep, morning headaches, daytime somnolence—may reflect nocturnal hypoventilation. Bulbar symptoms—dysphagia, nasal regurgitation, or choking—imply increased risk of aspiration.

Physical examination may reveal shallow rapid breathing, use of accessory muscles, paradoxical abdominal movements during inspiration (diaphragmatic weakness), reduced chest wall expansion, and hypoventilation signs such as hypercapnic facies. Auscultation may be unremarkable unless aspiration or parenchymal disease is present.

Bedside objective tests include measurement of vital capacity (sitting and supine), maximal voluntary ventilation, cough peak flow, MIP and MEP, and SNIP. A cough peak flow <160 L/min is associated with ineffective airway clearance and higher risk for secretion retention and respiratory complications; values <270 L/min indicate impaired cough for some interventions. As noted above, a supine VC drop >10–20% strongly suggests diaphragmatic weakness. Spirometry alone cannot reliably distinguish neuromuscular restriction from parenchymal disease; the pattern of preserved flows with low volumes and normal DLCO, along with muscle testing, supports extrathoracic restriction.

Nocturnal monitoring with pulse oximetry or polysomnography is useful: nocturnal hypoventilation often precedes daytime hypercapnia, manifesting as sustained or frequent oxygen desaturations during sleep and morning hypercapnia. Arterial blood gas analysis provides definitive evidence of hypercapnic respiratory failure; rising PaCO2 indicates alveolar hypoventilation and progressive ventilatory failure. When clinical suspicion is high, early collaboration with respiratory therapy and consideration of noninvasive ventilation (NIV) trials are appropriate.

Frequency of monitoring depends on severity. Patients with established weakness or progressive decline should have serial spirometry and MIP/MEP measurements at regular intervals (for example, every 3 months in active disease or sooner if symptoms change). For those with mild disease and stable function, every 6–12 months may suffice. Any new orthopnea, morning headache, daytime somnolence, or decline in supine VC should prompt urgent reassessment.

# Management principles for ILD in connective tissue disease

Treating ILD associated with inflammatory myopathies requires integrating severity assessment, pattern recognition, and balancing immunosuppression to control inflammation against risks of therapy. Management principles emphasize early recognition, risk stratification, and individualized immunomodulatory therapy, with supportive care and adjunctive measures.

Initial evaluation and risk stratification should include HRCT to define pattern and extent of disease, baseline spirometry with TLC and DLCO, oxygenation assessment at rest and with exertion (pulse oximetry or 6-minute walk test), and measurement of serologies including myositis-specific antibodies (e.g., antisynthetase antibodies, anti-MDA5) that inform prognosis and likelihood of progression. Severity thresholds that guide treatment intensity include FVC <50% predicted, DLCO <40% predicted, rapid decline in FVC (≥10% over 6–12 months), or rapid clinical deterioration such as worsening hypoxemia. Rapidly progressive ILD (RP-ILD), often associated with anti-MDA5, is a medical emergency and warrants aggressive immunosuppression and consideration of intensive care support.

Immunosuppressive therapy is the mainstay for inflammatory (cellular) ILD. High-dose systemic corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone 0.5–1 mg/kg/day, sometimes with pulsed intravenous methylprednisolone in fulminant cases) are commonly used as initial therapy, particularly when imaging suggests cellular NSIP or OP. Because prolonged steroid monotherapy carries toxicity and relapse risk, steroid-sparing agents are introduced early: mycophenolate mofetil is frequently used because of tolerability and evidence supporting benefit in CTD-ILD; azathioprine is an alternative. For more severe or refractory disease, cyclophosphamide (often IV monthly pulses) has been used; calcineurin inhibitors such as tacrolimus or cyclosporine may be particularly effective in antisynthetase syndrome–associated ILD. Rituximab, a B-cell–depleting agent, has efficacy in refractory myositis-associated ILD and is often considered for steroid-refractory disease or when an antibody-mediated mechanism is suspected. Choice among agents depends on disease severity, comorbidities, reproductive considerations, infection risk, and local practice.

Antifibrotic therapy has an emerging role in connective tissue disease–associated progressive fibrosing ILD. Nintedanib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, demonstrated benefit in slowing FVC decline in progressive fibrosing ILD of various etiologies, including CTD-ILD, and may be considered for patients with progressive fibrotic phenotypes despite immunosuppression. Pirfenidone has less robust data in CTD-ILD. Antifibrotics do not replace immunosuppression when active inflammation is present but are adjunctive when fibrosis advances despite control of inflammation.

Supportive measures are equally important. Supplemental oxygen for resting or exertional hypoxemia improves symptoms and may reduce pulmonary hypertension risk. Pulmonary rehabilitation improves exercise tolerance and quality of life. Vaccination against influenza and pneumococcus is recommended. For patients with significant respiratory muscle weakness and nocturnal hypoventilation, noninvasive ventilation can stabilize gas exchange and improve daytime symptoms. Early referral for lung transplantation evaluation is appropriate for patients with progressive disease despite optimal medical therapy and who meet transplant criteria.

Monitoring response to therapy relies on serial clinical assessment, PFTs (notably FVC and DLCO), 6-minute walk distance and oxygen saturation, and follow-up HRCT when clinically indicated. Clinically significant progression is commonly defined as FVC decline ≥10% predicted over 6–12 months, or an FVC decline of 5–10% combined with worsening symptoms or imaging; DLCO decline ≥15% over the same interval is also significant. Decisions to escalate therapy, add steroid-sparing agents, transition to biologic therapy, or introduce antifibrotic therapy are guided by these objective measures and the clinical course.

Infectious risk is a major consideration given immunosuppressive regimens. Baseline screening for latent infections (e.g., tuberculosis, hepatitis B/C) and prophylaxis (e.g., Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis when using high-dose steroids and additional immunosuppression) should be individualized. Drug toxicities necessitate surveillance (e.g., cytopenias, hepatic dysfunction, renal toxicity), and vaccination status should be optimized before intensive immunosuppression.

Finally, management of aspiration risk and respiratory muscle weakness in myositis may overlap with ILD care. Bulbar dysfunction requires speech and swallow evaluation, dietary modification, and possibly enteral feeding in severe dysphagia. Preventing aspiration reduces pulmonary complications and supports pulmonary rehabilitation and recovery.

Conclusion

Pulmonary complications of inflammatory myopathies encompass a broad spectrum from inflammatory, potentially reversible ILD patterns (NSIP, OP) to fibrotic UIP-like disease, along with the mechanical and infectious consequences of bulbar and respiratory muscle dysfunction. Accurate radiologic characterization, careful PFT interpretation distinguishing intrinsic parenchymal restriction from extrathoracic neuromuscular restriction, and vigilant monitoring for aspiration and respiratory failure are central to clinical care. Management integrates immunosuppression targeting inflammation, antifibrotic therapy for progressive fibrosis, supportive measures including oxygen and pulmonary rehabilitation, and proactive strategies to prevent and treat aspiration. Early multidisciplinary collaboration among rheumatology, pulmonology, radiology, speech/swallow specialists, and, when needed, critical care or transplant teams optimizes outcomes for patients with myositis-associated pulmonary disease.
