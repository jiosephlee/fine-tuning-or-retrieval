# Introduction

Immune‑mediated myopathies (IIMs), a heterogenous group that includes polymyositis, dermatomyositis, immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy, antisynthetase syndrome, and inclusion body myositis (distinct in pathogenesis and responsiveness), commonly present with proximal muscle weakness, elevated muscle enzymes, and systemic features. Management requires balancing rapid control of immune‑mediated muscle injury to preserve strength and prevent life‑threatening complications (bulbar dysfunction, respiratory failure) against the short‑ and long‑term toxicities of immunosuppression. This chapter summarizes evidence‑based approaches to immunosuppressive therapy in IIMs, strategies for acute interventions in severe disease, monitoring response to therapy, and measures to mitigate infection risk during immunosuppression. The emphasis is clinical: dosing, typical sequences of therapy, decision points for escalation, and practical monitoring and preventive measures.

# First-line therapies: high-dose corticosteroids—dosing, tapering and adverse effects

Corticosteroids are the cornerstone of initial therapy for most inflammatory myopathies because of their rapid anti‑inflammatory effects. They are usually started at high doses to achieve prompt disease control, then tapered and transitioned to steroid‑sparing agents to limit steroid toxicity.

For moderate to severe active disease (significant proximal weakness, rapidly rising creatine kinase, or extramuscular involvement such as interstitial lung disease), oral prednisone is commonly initiated at a dose of 0.5–1.0 mg/kg/day, with many clinicians using 1 mg/kg/day (maximum commonly 60–80 mg/day) for severe presentations. In life‑threatening situations (e.g., imminent respiratory compromise, severe dysphagia with aspiration risk, rapidly progressive weakness), pulse intravenous methylprednisolone 500–1000 mg daily for 3 days can be used to obtain a faster effect before switching to high‑dose oral prednisone.

Tapering is individualized. Once clinical improvement is evident (usually within 2–6 weeks) and muscle enzymes decline, the prednisone dose should be reduced gradually to a maintenance or physiologic range. A practical approach is to lower by 10 mg every 1–2 weeks until 30 mg/day, then reduce by 2.5–5 mg every 2–4 weeks toward 10–15 mg/day. The goal is to reach a dose less than 10 mg/day if disease control permits; many patients will require a steroid‑sparing agent to achieve this. Very slow tapers and prolonged low‑dose maintenance (e.g., 5–10 mg/day) may be necessary in refractory disease.

Adverse effects of systemic corticosteroids are frequent and cumulative. Short‑term effects of high doses include mood changes, insomnia, hyperglycemia, fluid retention, and hypertension. Long‑term risks include osteoporosis and fractures, weight gain, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, cataracts and glaucoma, opportunistic infections, adrenal suppression, and skin thinning with easy bruising. Prophylactic measures should be instituted when prolonged courses are anticipated: bone health optimization with calcium (typically 1000–1200 mg/day) and vitamin D (800–2000 IU/day), assessment of fracture risk (DEXA scan) and institution of bisphosphonates for patients at high risk, monitoring and management of blood glucose and blood pressure, and counseling regarding infection risk. Consider prophylaxis for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia when combining high‑dose corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone ≥20 mg/day for >4 weeks) with additional immunosuppressive agents; a common regimen is trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole one double strength tablet three times weekly or single strength daily depending on tolerance.

Clinical decision points include when to escalate if steroids alone are ineffective (usually by 4–8 weeks if there is minimal improvement) and when to add additional immunosuppression to enable steroid tapering. Steroid refractoriness or dependence should prompt early consideration of adjunctive agents tailored to myositis subtype and comorbidities.

# Adjunctive and steroid-sparing agents: methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, cyclosporine

Steroid‑sparing agents are used to maintain disease control, reduce cumulative steroid exposure, and treat chronic or refractory disease. Choice among agents is influenced by disease phenotype, comorbidities, pregnancy potential, toxicity profiles, and clinician experience. Evidence for efficacy comes from randomized trials (limited size), cohort studies, and clinical experience.

Methotrexate (MTX) is one of the most commonly used agents. Typical dosing for myositis is 15–25 mg weekly, either orally or subcutaneously; some regimens begin at 10–15 mg weekly and escalate as tolerated. Folic acid 1 mg daily or 5–10 mg weekly is given to reduce side effects. Monitor complete blood count (CBC), liver function tests (LFTs), and renal function prior to initiation and every 1–3 months thereafter. Contraindications include significant hepatic disease, excessive alcohol use, pregnancy (teratogenic), and severe renal impairment (dose adjustment required). Adverse effects include transaminitis, cytopenias, stomatitis, and pulmonary toxicity (rare). Clinically, expect a response over 2–3 months; MTX is often combined with corticosteroids.

Azathioprine (AZA) is useful as an alternative, especially when methotrexate is contraindicated or not tolerated. Typical dosing is 2–3 mg/kg/day (commonly 50–150 mg/day) given once daily. Measure thiopurine S‑methyltransferase (TPMT) activity or genotype prior to initiation because low TPMT activity increases risk of severe myelosuppression. Monitor CBC and LFTs every 1–2 weeks for the first 1–2 months, then every 1–3 months. Adverse effects include leukopenia, hepatotoxicity, gastrointestinal intolerance, and increased risk of infections. AZA is safe in many women planning pregnancy after appropriate counseling, unlike methotrexate.

Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) is increasingly used, particularly when interstitial lung disease coexists, because of good tolerability and evidence for efficacy in ILD associated with antisynthetase syndrome or dermatomyositis. Dosing typically starts at 500 mg twice daily and increases to 1000–1500 mg twice daily as tolerated (total 1000–3000 mg/day). Monitor CBC and LFTs periodically. Adverse effects include gastrointestinal upset, leukopenia, and increased infection risk. MMF is teratogenic and contraindicated in pregnancy.

Calcineurin inhibitors—cyclosporine and tacrolimus—are considered in refractory cases, particularly when there is associated interstitial lung disease or when other agents have failed. Cyclosporine dosing for myositis is often 3–5 mg/kg/day in divided doses (adjusted to clinical response and side effects). Monitor blood pressure, renal function, and trough levels as appropriate. Adverse effects include nephrotoxicity, hypertension, hypertrichosis, tremor, and metabolic derangements. Tacrolimus may be used at 0.075–0.2 mg/kg/day in divided doses targeted to trough concentrations, and is associated with similar toxicities including nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity. Calcineurin inhibitors are generally reserved for refractory disease or specific phenotypes (e.g., anti‑synthetase with ILD) because of toxicity and the need for drug monitoring.

Clinical practice commonly employs an early combination of corticosteroids plus a steroid‑sparing agent (MTX, AZA, or MMF) to allow steroid taper and reduce long‑term toxicity. Choice is individualized: methotrexate is often preferred for isolated muscle‑predominant disease in nonpregnant patients, AZA for those contemplating pregnancy or with hepatic disease, MMF for those with ILD, and calcineurin inhibitors for refractory cases with lung involvement.

# Biologic and targeted therapies: rituximab, tofacitinib and other emerging agents

Biologic and targeted therapies are increasingly important for refractory IIM or specific autoantibody‑defined phenotypes. Randomized controlled data are limited but evolving; clinical decisions often draw on registry data, small trials, and extrapolation from other autoimmune diseases.

Rituximab, an anti‑CD20 monoclonal antibody causing B‑cell depletion, has the strongest evidence among biologics for myositis. The randomized RIM trial did not meet its primary endpoint but subsequent analyses and real‑world data support rituximab efficacy in refractory dermatomyositis and polymyositis and particularly in certain autoantibody subsets (e.g., anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR, and some antisynthetase patients). Typical regimens include either 375 mg/m^2 weekly for 4 weeks or two doses of 1000 mg given two weeks apart; some clinicians repeat dosing at 6 months depending on clinical response and B‑cell recovery. Prior to initiation, screen for hepatitis B (HBsAg, anti‑HBc total) and tuberculosis; vaccinate ideally before B‑cell depletion. Monitor CBC and immunoglobulin levels (IgG) as hypogammaglobulinemia can develop, predisposing to infections. Live vaccines are contraindicated during and for months after rituximab therapy; inactivated vaccines have reduced immunogenicity.

Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors such as tofacitinib target intracellular signaling pathways downstream of multiple cytokine receptors and have shown promise, particularly in refractory dermatomyositis with cutaneous manifestations and some myositis subtypes. Tofacitinib dosing commonly used in rheumatology is 5 mg twice daily (some data support 5 mg once daily for certain indications); dose adjustments for renal or hepatic impairment are required. Adverse effects include increased risk of infections (including herpes zoster), cytopenias, elevated lipids, and potential thrombotic risk at higher doses in certain populations. Evidence for JAK inhibitors in IIM remains limited to case series and small trials, but they are considered for refractory disease, especially with prominent skin disease.

Other targeted agents under investigation include abatacept (CTLA‑4‑Ig) which modulates T‑cell costimulation and has shown benefit in some refractory myositis cohorts; anti‑interferon therapies (e.g., sifalimumab, anifrolumab) given interferon signatures in dermatomyositis; complement inhibitors (e.g., eculizumab) in complement‑mediated processes; and anti‑IL‑6 agents (tocilizumab) with mixed results. Clinical trial enrollment should be considered for refractory cases. Use of these agents requires awareness of mechanisms, potential infectious complications, vaccination planning, and organ‑specific monitoring.

Choice to escalate to biologic or targeted therapy is driven by lack of adequate response to optimized standard immunosuppression, intolerable toxicity from conventional agents, or presence of particular phenotypes where biologic data are more compelling. Rituximab remains a common next step after failure of corticosteroids plus an initial steroid‑sparing agent.

# Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG): mechanisms of action, indications, dosing and monitoring

IVIG is an important therapeutic tool in IIM, especially dermatomyositis and immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy, and in acute severe presentations. IVIG has multi‑faceted immunomodulatory effects: it provides anti‑idiotypic antibodies that neutralize pathogenic autoantibodies, modulates Fc receptor expression, interferes with complement activation, alters cytokine production, and modulates B cell and T cell function. Because of these diverse mechanisms, IVIG can be effective in antibody‑mediated myopathies and in severe inflammatory states refractory to other immunosuppressants.

Indications for IVIG include dermatomyositis with significant skin or muscle disease refractory to or intolerant of standard immunosuppression, severe or rapidly progressive muscle weakness, treatment of dysphagia or respiratory muscle involvement when rapid effect is required, and certain antibody‑positive necrotizing myopathies (e.g., anti‑SRP and anti‑HMGCR) in which IVIG has shown benefit. IVIG is also used as bridging therapy in severe disease while other agents take effect or when conventional agents are contraindicated (e.g., pregnancy, severe infection risk).

Common dosing regimens are 2 g/kg total per treatment cycle, typically administered as 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 consecutive days or 1 g/kg on two consecutive days. For maintenance therapy, some clinicians give 1 g/kg every 2–4 weeks or 2 g/kg every 4–8 weeks depending on durability of response and tolerability. IVIG infusion rates should be adjusted based on patient tolerance and coexisting cardiac or renal disease.

Monitoring includes baseline renal function (creatinine), CBC, and consideration of serum IgA levels because patients with IgA deficiency are at risk for severe anaphylactic reactions if they have anti‑IgA antibodies. Monitor for infusion reactions (headache, fever, chills), thromboembolic events, aseptic meningitis, hemolysis (IVIG can cause hemolysis due to blood group antibodies—check hemoglobin and direct antiglobulin test if suspected), and renal impairment (prefer subcutaneous or lower infusion rates in patients at risk). Pre‑medication with acetaminophen and antihistamine can reduce infusion reactions; corticosteroid pre‑medication is sometimes used in patients prone to severe reactions. IVIG is expensive and supply may be limited; decisions to continue should be based on objective clinical improvement and functional gains.

# Acute management of severe features: treatment of dysphagia, respiratory failure, immunotherapy escalation

Severe features of immune‑mediated myopathies include bulbar dysfunction (dysphagia, aspiration risk), respiratory muscle weakness leading to ventilatory failure, rapidly progressive weakness, and severe systemic involvement (e.g., myocarditis). Management requires a coordinated multidisciplinary approach with early recognition and aggressive supportive and immunomodulatory therapies.

Dysphagia: Bulbar weakness can cause aspiration, weight loss, and recurrent pneumonias. Initial assessment includes clinical bedside swallow evaluation by speech‑language pathology and instrumental studies such as videofluoroscopic swallow study (modified barium swallow) or fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) to define anatomy and physiology. For patients with severe dysphagia or aspiration, temporary enteral feeding via nasogastric tube may be necessary; for persistent dysfunction or prolonged need for feeding (>4–6 weeks anticipated), percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) placement should be considered. Therapeutically, high‑dose corticosteroids and IVIG can produce relatively rapid improvement; in severe or refractory cases, escalation to rituximab or plasmapheresis may be required depending on phenotype and suspected antibody‑mediated mechanisms. Swallow exercises and rehabilitation are essential adjuncts. Airway protection is paramount; recurrent aspiration or severe inability to manage secretions may necessitate more definitive airway decisions and aggressive immunotherapy.

Respiratory failure: Respiratory muscle weakness manifests as orthopnea, dyspnea, reduced cough, nocturnal hypoventilation, and in severe cases, hypercapnic respiratory failure. Pulmonary function testing with supine and upright forced vital capacity (FVC) is an important objective measurement; a drop in FVC greater than 10–15% when moving from upright to supine suggests diaphragmatic weakness. An absolute FVC below 50% predicted or a decline of >10–20% over a short interval warrants close monitoring and consideration of respiratory support. Measurement of maximal inspiratory pressure and sniff nasal inspiratory pressure can further quantify inspiratory muscle strength. Arterial blood gases or capnography can detect hypercapnia; overnight oximetry or polysomnography may identify nocturnal hypoventilation.

Management of acute respiratory failure mirrors other neuromuscular respiratory failures: initiate noninvasive ventilation (NIV) such as bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) for hypercapnic failure when the patient is cooperative and has preserved airway protective reflexes. Indications for intubation include inability to protect the airway, severe hypoxemia, high secretions, progressive hypercapnia despite NIV, or decreased level of consciousness. In parallel with respiratory support, aggressive immunotherapy escalation is indicated to reverse the inflammatory myopathy: high‑dose IV methylprednisolone pulses, IVIG, and consideration of plasmapheresis or rituximab in antibody‑mediated disease. Early involvement of pulmonary, neurology/rheumatology, and critical care teams is essential. When ventilation is prolonged, tracheostomy may be needed.

Immunotherapy escalation: Acute severe manifestations typically require escalation beyond oral prednisone. Common approaches include combining pulse IV methylprednisolone with IVIG, adding or switching to a second‑line agent such as rituximab or cyclophosphamide (particularly if concomitant severe interstitial lung disease or vasculitic features), and considering plasmapheresis for fulminant, antibody‑mediated disease or when rapid antibody removal is desired. Cyclophosphamide (IV pulses 500–1000 mg/m^2 every 2–4 weeks) may be used for life‑threatening disease (e.g., severe ILD or myocarditis), with careful monitoring of cytopenias and bladder toxicity. Plasmapheresis can be used as adjunctive therapy in select, severe, antibody‑mediated cases to remove pathogenic autoantibodies; benefits are variable and often transient, so it is typically used as a bridge while other therapies take effect.

All acute decisions should weigh the risk of immunosuppression against the immediate threat to life and function. Early aggressive therapy is justified in life‑threatening situations; conversely, for less severe presentations, stepwise escalation allows assessment of response and minimizes toxicity.

# Monitoring response: CK, strength testing, imaging, and relapse management

Monitoring therapeutic response in IIM requires serial clinical, biochemical, and sometimes imaging assessments. No single metric is sufficient; integration of patient symptoms, objective strength testing, functional measures, muscle enzyme trends, and imaging guides management.

Creatine kinase (CK) is the most commonly used laboratory marker. Typical normal ranges vary by laboratory; a representative reference range is approximately 26–192 U/L, though clinicians should use local laboratory normals. CK correlates with muscle injury but is imperfect: some patients (especially those with chronic disease or inclusion body myositis) have only modest CK elevations or normal values despite active weakness. Serial CK measurements are useful to track trends; falling CK supports biochemical response, but clinical improvement in strength and function is more important.

Strength testing should be performed systematically. The Medical Research Council (MRC) scale grades muscle strength from 0 (no contraction) to 5 (normal) for specific muscle groups; more sensitive tools include manual muscle testing (MMT‑8) used in research and bedside dynamometry for quantitative measurement. Functional assessments such as timed up and go, 6‑minute walk distance, and patient‑reported outcomes (fatigue scales, activities of daily living) complement strength testing. Improvement in proximal limb strength typically lags behind biochemical improvement by several weeks.

Imaging, particularly muscle MRI, provides anatomic and disease activity information. T2/STIR sequences and short tau inversion recovery images detect muscle edema and inflammation; T1 sequences assess fatty replacement and chronic atrophy. MRI helps identify biopsy targets and can document ongoing inflammation despite normal CK. Ultrasound is emerging as a bedside tool to assess muscle texture and echogenicity but is less validated for serial monitoring.

Electromyography (EMG) may show myopathic features and spontaneous activity; it is useful diagnostically but less practical for frequent monitoring. Muscle biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard when subtype clarification is required; repeat biopsy is rarely needed for monitoring.

Relapse management: Relapse is common and may present with recurrent weakness, rising CK, or new extramuscular features. Management depends on severity. For mild flares, increase corticosteroid dose transiently and optimize steroid‑sparing agents; for moderate to severe flares, consider pulse IV steroids, add or switch immunosuppressive therapy (e.g., escalate to rituximab, add IVIG), and re‑evaluate adherence, intercurrent infections, and other contributors (thyroid disease, statin exposure). For certain antibody‑positive necrotizing myopathies, recurrent activity may necessitate long‑term IVIG. Documenting objective changes in strength and function guides escalation. In recurrent refractory disease, referral to specialized centers and clinical trial consideration are appropriate.

# Infection risk mitigation and vaccination considerations in immunosuppressed patients

Immunosuppressive therapies increase infection risk both by impairing cellular and humoral immunity and by altering mucosal defenses. Mitigating that risk requires pre‑therapy screening, vaccination planning, infection prophylaxis when indicated, and ongoing vigilance.

Pre‑therapy screening: Before initiating significant immunosuppression (particularly biologics or alkylating agents), screen for latent infections. Obtain hepatitis B serologies (HBsAg, anti‑HBc total, anti‑HBs) and hepatitis C antibody and reflex RNA testing if positive. Screen for latent tuberculosis with interferon‑gamma release assay (IGRA) or tuberculin skin test; chest radiography if indicated. Test for HIV where risk factors exist. For rituximab or prolonged B‑cell–depleting therapies, assess baseline immunoglobulin levels (IgG) and vaccination status. If hepatitis B core antibody is positive, consult hepatology for antiviral prophylaxis (e.g., entecavir or tenofovir) during and after B‑cell‑depleting therapy to prevent reactivation.

Vaccination: Optimize vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppression when feasible, ideally completing immunizations at least 2–4 weeks before starting therapy (longer if possible for live vaccines). Inactivated vaccines (influenza, pneumococcal, Tdap, hepatitis B) are safe during immunosuppression though responses may be attenuated. Annual inactivated influenza vaccine is recommended for all immunosuppressed patients. Pneumococcal vaccination strategy commonly follows a sequential approach with PCV13 followed by PPSV23 according to guidelines. For rituximab recipients, vaccinate before B‑cell depletion; vaccines given after rituximab may have reduced efficacy for 6–12 months or until B‑cell recovery. Live attenuated vaccines (e.g., zoster live vaccine, MMR, varicella) are contraindicated during significant immunosuppression and for variable periods thereafter (generally until immunosuppression is stopped and immune recovery is confirmed). Newer recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV, non‑live) is preferred and can be administered to immunosuppressed adults; timing relative to immunosuppression should be individualized.

Prophylaxis: Specific prophylactic strategies include Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis with trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole for patients on prolonged high‑dose corticosteroids (prednisone ≥20 mg/day for >4 weeks) in combination with other immunosuppressants, or those receiving cyclophosphamide or rituximab in regimens associated with significant risk. Herpes zoster prophylaxis with antivirals may be considered for patients receiving JAK inhibitors or other agents associated with elevated zoster risk. For HBV exposed patients, antiviral prophylaxis is indicated during and after B‑cell‑depleting therapy.

Patient counseling and preventive measures: Educate patients on infection signs, hand hygiene, avoiding sick contacts, and early reporting of fever or respiratory symptoms. Consider prophylactic measures in high‑risk settings (e.g., influenza prophylaxis antivirals during outbreaks for severely immunosuppressed individuals). Monitor for cytopenias and hypogammaglobulinemia, which may necessitate dose adjustment, IVIG replacement, or infectious disease consultation.

Monitoring during therapy: Regular clinical assessments for infection, periodic CBC, and measurement of immunoglobulin levels (particularly IgG) during B‑cell–depleting therapies guide management. If recurrent severe infections or sustained hypogammaglobulinemia occur, consider IVIG replacement or modifying immunotherapy.

Clinical decision points include balancing infection risk against the consequences of undertreated myositis. In severe disease, aggressive immunosuppression may be lifesaving; in stable disease, de‑escalation and minimizing cumulative immunosuppression reduce infection risk. Coordination with primary care and infectious disease specialists optimizes preventive strategies.

# Conclusion

Management of immune‑mediated myopathies demands individualized, multidisciplinary care that balances rapid disease control with mitigation of treatment toxicity. High‑dose corticosteroids remain first‑line for induction, but early introduction of steroid‑sparing agents (methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate, calcineurin inhibitors) is common to minimize steroid exposure. Biologics such as rituximab and targeted agents like JAK inhibitors are valuable for refractory or phenotype‑specific disease. IVIG has a unique role for antibody‑mediated and severe presentations. Acute complications—dysphagia and respiratory failure—require urgent supportive care and aggressive immunotherapy. Careful monitoring with CK, objective strength and functional measures, and imaging guides treatment response and relapse management. Finally, infection risk mitigation through pre‑treatment screening, vaccination, prophylaxis, and patient education is essential to safely deliver immunosuppressive care. Clinical judgment, patient preference, and evolving evidence inform therapy sequencing and escalation.
