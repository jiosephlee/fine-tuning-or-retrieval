Introduction

Drug‑induced autoimmune conditions constitute an important and challenging subset of adverse drug reactions (ADRs). They arise when exposure to a xenobiotic — a medication, its metabolite, or an excipient — triggers a self‑directed immune response that mimics, unmasks, or exacerbates idiopathic autoimmune disease. Among these, drug‑associated inflammatory myopathies (including polymyositis, dermatomyositis, immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy, and antisynthetase syndromes) are particularly consequential because they may produce profound weakness, systemic complications, and require prolonged immunosuppression. Clinicians must therefore integrate epidemiologic knowledge, an understanding of host susceptibility, temporal relationships between exposure and disease onset, and structured causality assessment to make rational decisions about diagnosis, reporting, and management. This chapter reviews the epidemiology of inflammatory myopathies with attention to drug and comorbidity associations, host risk factors for drug‑induced autoimmunity, typical latency patterns, commonly used causality assessment tools and their application to suspected autoimmune ADRs, pharmacovigilance principles for detection and reporting, and the ethical and practical considerations governing drug rechallenge and documentation.

# Epidemiology of inflammatory myopathies and associations with drugs and comorbidities

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs) form a heterogeneous group of immune‑mediated disorders characterized by muscle inflammation and weakness with varied systemic involvement. Epidemiologic estimates vary by region, case definitions, and whether inclusion criteria capture children and adults. Broadly, incidence estimates for adult IIM range from roughly 1 to 10 per 100,000 person‑years, with prevalence estimates commonly reported in the range of about 5–20 per 100,000 persons in many populations; some registries and referral cohorts report higher prevalence reflecting case ascertainment bias. Specific subtypes show differing epidemiology: dermatomyositis tends to have a bimodal age distribution (younger adults and middle‑aged adults), immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy and antisynthetase syndromes often present in middle age, and inclusion body myositis predominates in older adults. Women are more commonly affected than men for most subtypes, and older age is associated with worse outcomes and with paraneoplastic associations.

A distinct but overlapping phenomenon is drug‑associated inflammatory myopathy or drug‑induced autoimmune myositis. Several classes of medications have been linked to myopathic syndromes through case reports, series, and pharmacovigilance signals. Well‑recognized examples include statins — classically associated with self‑limited myalgias and creatine kinase (CK) elevation but also with immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy associated with anti‑HMGCR antibodies; immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) used in oncology, which have been increasingly reported to produce myositis often accompanied by myocarditis; interferons and certain biologics that can precipitate autoimmune phenomena; and some antibiotics, antithyroid drugs, and recreational agents reported less frequently. The spectrum of drug‑associated muscle disease ranges from toxic myopathy (direct myocyte injury often dose‑related and reversible on withdrawal), to immune‑mediated myopathy characterized by autoantibodies, inflammatory infiltrates on biopsy, and the need for immunosuppression.

Comorbidities influence both the risk of developing IIM and the presentation. Cancer is a well‑established associated condition particularly with dermatomyositis and late‑onset cases; paraneoplastic mechanisms may contribute to immune activation. Infectious triggers (viral and bacterial) are repeatedly implicated as both primary precipitants and cofactors that modulate immune responses. Metabolic conditions such as diabetes and thyroid disease may complicate presentation and management, and prior or coexisting autoimmune diseases (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis) can increase baseline autoimmune susceptibility and confound attribution when new symptoms arise. Importantly, medications used to treat comorbid conditions (e.g., statins for dyslipidemia or immune checkpoint inhibitors for cancer) are themselves potential causal agents; disentangling direct drug effects from disease‑related or paraneoplastic immune activation requires careful clinical and epidemiologic reasoning.

Clinicians should be aware that background rates of IIM are low, so sporadic temporal associations with drug exposure may occur by chance. Robust attribution requires integration of epidemiologic context (how common is the disease in the relevant population), strength of association from observational data or mechanistic plausibility, and the individual clinical pattern.

# Host risk factors for drug‑induced autoimmunity (genetic predisposition, prior autoimmunity, exposures)

Drug‑induced autoimmunity arises at the interface of exogenous exposure and host susceptibility. Several classes of host factors modulate risk and phenotype: genetic predisposition, prior or concurrent autoimmune disease, intrinsic demographic characteristics, and environmental or comorbid exposures.

Genetic predisposition is a major determinant. Human leukocyte antigen (HLA) alleles shape antigen presentation and have been linked to susceptibility for both idiopathic and drug‑induced autoimmune conditions. For example, specific HLA class II alleles have been associated with higher risk of anti‑HMGCR myopathy in patients exposed to statins, and HLA alleles such as HLA‑DRB1 variants are associated with idiopathic forms and some drug‑related syndromes. Single nucleotide polymorphisms in immune regulatory genes (e.g., PTPN22, CTLA4) can increase risk for autoimmune reactions more broadly. Pharmacogenetic differences in drug metabolism (e.g., variants of cytochrome P450 enzymes, N‑acetyltransferase) influence drug levels and the formation of reactive metabolites that can form adducts with self‑proteins, promoting neoantigen formation and autoimmune responses.

Prior autoimmunity or family history of autoimmune disease increases baseline susceptibility. A patient with an existing autoimmune disease may have a lower threshold for developing additional autoimmune phenomena following an immunogenic exposure. For example, patients with systemic autoimmune diseases treated with biologics have been reported to develop paradoxical autoimmune manifestations attributable to immune modulation. Likewise, a personal or family history of drug reactions (e.g., drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms) can indicate an immune‑responsive phenotype.

Non‑genetic host factors include age and sex: women are generally at higher risk for autoimmune disease; certain drug‑induced autoimmune phenomena track with age (e.g., inclusion body myositis in older adults). Smoking is a well‑established environmental risk factor for some autoimmune diseases (notably rheumatoid arthritis) and may potentiate drug‑induced autoimmunity by altering immune regulation and antigen presentation. Infectious exposures, either antecedent or concurrent, can prime immune responses through molecular mimicry or by providing danger signals that break tolerance. Organ dysfunction such as renal or hepatic impairment affects drug clearance and increases exposure to parent compounds and reactive metabolites, elevating risk. Nutritional factors, microbiome composition, and prior vaccinations have been proposed modulators of immune responsiveness, though direct causal links in drug‑induced autoimmunity are less well established.

Finally, the pharmacologic context matters: polypharmacy increases the chance of interactions and cumulative immune stimulation, and prior sensitization to structurally related compounds can produce cross‑reactivity. Clinically, detailed history taking should elicit family history, prior autoimmune disease, smoking and infection history, organ dysfunction, and prior drug exposures to estimate individual risk and to guide diagnostic and management decisions.

# Temporal relationships and latency periods for drug‑induced immune events

Time is a critical dimension in causality assessment. A plausible temporal relationship strengthens inference that a drug contributed to an adverse immune event, but the expected latency varies widely by mechanism and by drug.

In general, several temporal patterns are recognized:

- Immediate hypersensitivity reactions (IgE mediated) occur within minutes to hours of exposure and are typically not autoimmune in nature.

- Type II and III immune‑mediated reactions (antibody dependent or immune complex mediated) often emerge within days to weeks after exposure, although prior sensitization can shorten latency.

- Drug‑induced autoimmunity that resembles classic autoimmune disease (i.e., the induction of autoantibodies and organ‑specific inflammation) tends to appear over weeks to months, because the generation of high‑titer, pathogenic autoantibodies and the organization of effector cellular responses usually require time. For instance, classic drug‑induced lupus from hydralazine or procainamide often develops after months to years of exposure, with latency commonly 6–24 months but sometimes longer.

- Some drug‑associated myopathies have characteristic latencies: statin‑associated self‑limited myalgias may occur within weeks of initiation or dose escalation, whereas statin‑associated immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (anti‑HMGCR associated) can develop after variable exposure durations — months to years — and may even arise after statin discontinuation, reflecting an immune process sustained independent of ongoing exposure. Immune checkpoint inhibitor‑related myositis often emerges early in the course of therapy, commonly within the first 1–3 months, but can occur later.

Latency also depends on dose and cumulative exposure. Direct toxic myopathies are more likely to be dose dependent and to occur with higher cumulative doses, while immune‑mediated processes can be idiosyncratic and not strictly dose related. Reexposure after a prior sensitization often results in shorter latency and more rapid or severe recurrence.

When assessing a suspected drug‑related autoimmune event, clinicians should construct a detailed timeline: dates of initiation, dose changes, temporary interruptions, and discontinuation; onset and evolution of symptoms; and the timing of key laboratory and imaging abnormalities. The temporal relationship must be interpreted in the context of known latency windows for the suspect agent (when available), biologic plausibility, and alternative explanations such as coincident infection, paraneoplastic processes, or progression of an underlying disease. Absence of an expected temporal relationship weakens causality but does not definitively exclude a drug-induced mechanism, particularly when the pathogenesis involves immune priming and delayed loss of tolerance.

# Structured causality assessment tools (WHO‑UMC system, Naranjo algorithm) and their application

Causality assessment tools provide standardized frameworks to estimate the likelihood that a drug caused an adverse event. Two widely used instruments are the WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO‑UMC) system and the Naranjo algorithm. Both are designed for general ADR assessment, not specifically for autoimmune diseases, and each has strengths and limitations when applied to complex immune phenomena.

The WHO‑UMC system is a qualitative, expert‑opinion based framework that classifies causality into categories: Certain, Probable/Likely, Possible, Unlikely, Conditional/Unclassified, and Unassessable/Unclassifiable. Assessment is based on factors including temporal relationship, dechallenge and re‑challenge information, alternative causes, known pharmacology, and previous conclusive reports. Key features:

- "Certain" requires a plausible time relationship, clinically reasonable response on withdrawal, and definitive evidence excluding alternative causes; re‑challenge results positive where ethical and feasible.

- "Probable/Likely" applies when a reasonable time relationship exists, withdrawal is clinically reasonable and leads to improvement, and alternative causes are less likely.

- "Possible" is used when time relationship is compatible but alternative causes are plausible.

When applying WHO‑UMC to drug‑induced autoimmunity, clinicians must recognize that dechallenge effect may be delayed or absent (autoimmune processes may persist after drug withdrawal). Thus, lack of immediate improvement does not exclude causality, and absence of re‑challenge is common because re‑exposure may be unethical or dangerous.

The Naranjo algorithm is a structured, point‑based questionnaire of 10 items summing to categorize an ADR as definite, probable, possible, or doubtful. Items include timing, previous conclusive reports, dechallenge/rechallenge responses, alternative causes, drug levels, dose–response relationships, and objective evidence. Scores ≥9 indicate definite, 5–8 probable, 1–4 possible, and ≤0 doubtful.

Naranjo strengths include reproducibility and objectivity; weaknesses include limited applicability to immune‑mediated ADRs and to delayed‑onset phenomena. For example, questions about dose‑response and drug levels are often irrelevant for idiosyncratic autoimmune reactions; re‑challenge is often withheld for safety; and improvement after drug withdrawal may be absent or delayed, reducing the score despite strong biologic plausibility.

Other approaches include the Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM) tailored to drug‑induced liver injury and specialized scales developed for specific organ systems. For autoimmune conditions, modifications or expert consensus panels may better capture the complexities: evaluation of autoantibody presence and specificity, biopsy features consistent with immune‑mediated injury, and mechanistic data (e.g., detection of drug‑protein adducts) can strengthen causal inference beyond what general ADR tools capture.

Best practice for clinicians is to use these structured tools as aids, not as substitutes for clinical reasoning. Document the application of the tool and the rationale for responses to each item, interpret scores in the context of disease‑specific features (e.g., presence of disease‑specific autoantibodies, characteristic histopathology), and transparently record uncertainties. For publication or regulatory reporting, stating the specific tool used and its scored outcome enhances reproducibility and aids pharmacovigilance.

# Pharmacovigilance: reporting adverse drug reactions, post‑marketing surveillance

Pharmacovigilance is the science and activities related to the detection, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse effects or drug‑related problems. For drug‑induced autoimmune diseases, robust pharmacovigilance is essential because randomized clinical trials may be underpowered to detect rare idiosyncratic immune events, and signals often emerge in the post‑marketing phase.

At the clinician level, spontaneous reporting to national or regional pharmacovigilance centers (e.g., FDA MedWatch in the United States, MHRA Yellow Card in the UK, EMA EudraVigilance in the EU) is the principal mechanism to accumulate case data. A high‑quality report includes patient demographics, suspected drug(s) with brand and generic names, dosing, dates of exposure and symptom onset, concomitant medications, detailed clinical description, laboratory and biopsy data, management and outcome, and the reporter’s assessment of causality. Inclusion of dechallenge and rechallenge information when available is valuable. Photographs (e.g., skin manifestations) and biopsy excerpts can strengthen the signal.

Passive surveillance is complemented by active methods: registries for specific drug classes or diseases (e.g., statin myopathy registries, immune checkpoint inhibitor registries), sentinel surveillance networks, electronic health record (EHR) mining, and pharmacoepidemiologic studies using claims databases. These approaches allow estimation of incidence rates, risk ratios, and adjustment for confounding. Signal detection methods use disproportionality metrics (e.g., reporting odds ratio, proportional reporting ratio) in large spontaneous reporting databases to flag potential associations that are more frequently reported than expected.

Once a signal is detected, causality assessment proceeds from case series characterization to analytic studies: cohort or case‑control studies, nested case‑control studies, and self‑controlled case series can evaluate temporality and estimate relative risks while controlling for time‑invariant confounders. Biological plausibility, dose–response gradients, and mechanistic studies (e.g., demonstration of drug‑dependent antibodies, HLA associations) strengthen causality.

Regulatory actions depend on signal strength and clinical seriousness: label updates/warnings, restricted use, additional monitoring, or withdrawal. Clinicians should be aware of and use safety communications and updated prescribing information.

Barriers to effective pharmacovigilance include underreporting (particularly for events with insidious onset or when attribution is uncertain), variable report quality, diagnostic heterogeneity, and reporting bias (e.g., notoriety bias after publicized cases). To improve detection, clinicians should report suspected autoimmune ADRs even when causality is uncertain. Well‑documented single cases can seed further investigation, and combined case series frequently form the basis for regulatory review.

# Principles and ethics of drug rechallenge and documentation of suspected associations

Rechallenge — reintroducing a suspected offending drug after an adverse reaction — can provide strong evidence for causality when a clear, reproducible response occurs. However, in drug‑induced autoimmune disease rechallenge raises significant ethical and safety concerns. Decisions about rechallenge should consider the severity of the prior event, availability of alternatives, the seriousness of the underlying condition for which the drug is indicated, the degree of uncertainty regarding causality, and the patient’s preferences and values.

Ethical principles applicable include nonmaleficence (do no harm), beneficence (balance potential therapeutic benefit), autonomy (informed consent), and justice. For life‑saving therapies with no alternatives (e.g., certain oncologic agents), a carefully monitored, informed rechallenge with patient consent may be justifiable despite risk. For nonessential agents or when safer alternatives exist, rechallenge is generally contraindicated if the index reaction was severe (e.g., necrotizing myopathy, myocarditis, Stevens–Johnson syndrome). Rechallenge after a mild, transient reaction may be reasonable if the drug provides substantial benefit; however, the clinician must ensure patient understanding of risks and establish monitoring protocols.

When rechallenge is contemplated, strategies to mitigate risk include using the lowest effective dose, slow dose escalation, concurrent prophylactic measures when evidence supports them, close clinical and laboratory monitoring (e.g., regular CK, troponin, autoantibody panels), and ready access to immunosuppressive therapy if immune activation recurs. A formal informed consent process should document the rationale, potential benefits, possible risks including severe recurrence, and alternative treatments.

From a documentation standpoint, clear, thorough records are essential. These should include a timeline of exposure and symptom onset, objective findings (CK, aldolase, autoantibodies, MRI, electromyography, biopsy), treatments and outcomes, rationale for attributing causality, documentation of informed consent for any rechallenge, and the results of dechallenge/rechallenge. Sharing anonymized, high‑quality reports with pharmacovigilance authorities is an ethical responsibility and enhances the collective understanding of drug safety.

Rechallenge is not the only avenue to strengthen causality. Alternative approaches include careful dechallenge observation, immune profiling (autoantibody specificity such as anti‑HMGCR, anti‑Jo‑1), biopsy confirmation of immune pathology, and pharmacogenomic analyses. In many cases, these approaches provide sufficient evidence to guide clinical management without exposing patients to additional risk.

In situations where rechallenge is contraindicated yet evidence for causality remains uncertain, multidisciplinary case review (including rheumatology, neurology, pathology, pharmacology, and, when relevant, oncology) and consultation with pharmacovigilance authorities can be valuable.

Conclusion

Assessing suspected drug‑induced autoimmune disease requires synthesis of epidemiology, host susceptibility, temporal patterns, clinical and laboratory evidence, and structured causality tools, all within the constraints of patient safety and ethical practice. Awareness of the epidemiologic context, recognition of host risk factors, careful timeline construction, judicious use of causality algorithms, responsible pharmacovigilance reporting, and principled decision‑making about rechallenge are essential components of high‑quality clinical care and contribute to safer pharmacotherapy for all patients. Clinicians should document suspected associations carefully and report them to appropriate surveillance systems to support the broader detection and analysis of drug‑induced autoimmune phenomena.

Appendix — Selected laboratory reference ranges and diagnostic decision points (typical adult values)

- Creatine kinase (CK): approximately 30–200 U/L (laboratory‑specific reference ranges vary; interpret relative to lab standard and muscle mass). In myositis, CK can be mildly to markedly elevated (from modest increases to >10 × upper limit).

- Aldolase: typical reference 1–7 U/L (variable by method).

- Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR): commonly <20 mm/hr in adults; may be elevated in systemic inflammation.

- C‑reactive protein (CRP): commonly <5 mg/L; elevated in active inflammatory states.

- Autoantibodies: antinuclear antibody (ANA) positivity is nonspecific; myositis‑specific antibodies (MSA) include anti‑Jo‑1 (histidyl tRNA synthetase), anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑MDA5, anti‑TIF1‑gamma, anti‑SRP, and anti‑HMGCR — the presence of specific MSAs lends etiologic and prognostic information.

- Diagnostic criteria: modern classification of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (e.g., 2017 EULAR/ACR classification) weights features including symmetric proximal muscle weakness, elevated muscle enzymes, electromyography and MRI changes consistent with myositis, and muscle biopsy findings. Classification yields probabilistic thresholds for “definite” or “probable” IIM, and specificity is increased when combined with serologic and histopathologic features.

Clinicians should always consult current laboratory reference ranges at their institution and apply disease‑specific classification criteria and local guidelines in diagnostic and management decisions.
