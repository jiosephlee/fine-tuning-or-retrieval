Introduction

Autoimmune myopathies are a group of disorders in which dysregulated immune responses target skeletal muscle, causing weakness, elevated muscle enzymes, inflammatory infiltrates, and a range of histopathologic patterns. Understanding the immunology underlying these diseases requires integration of innate and adaptive immune mechanisms, cellular and humoral effectors, complement pathways, antigen presentation, and the processes that normally maintain self-tolerance. This chapter reviews core immunologic concepts relevant to antibody-mediated and cell-mediated mechanisms that drive autoimmune muscle injury, emphasizing pathophysiology and clinical reasoning that connect laboratory tests, histology, and therapeutic strategies.

# Innate and adaptive immune responses overview

The immune system comprises two interdependent arms: innate immunity, which provides rapid, non-specific defense, and adaptive immunity, which generates antigen-specific responses and immunologic memory. Innate immunity is mediated by physical barriers, soluble pattern recognition molecules (such as complement and collectins), and cellular effectors including neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), natural killer (NK) cells, and innate lymphoid cells. Innate cells recognize conserved pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs), NOD-like receptors, and RIG-I–like receptors. Signaling through PRRs triggers secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), type I interferons, chemokines, and upregulation of antigen presentation machinery.

Adaptive immunity comprises B lymphocytes and T lymphocytes. B cells produce antibodies (immunoglobulins) after differentiation into plasma cells, and present antigen to T cells. T cells include CD4+ helper T cells (Th) that support B-cell responses and orchestrate inflammation via cytokines, and CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) which directly kill target cells presenting peptide in the context of class I major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules. Adaptive responses are initiated when antigen-presenting cells (APCs), particularly dendritic cells, process antigen and provide three signals to naïve T cells: (1) peptide-MHC interaction with the T-cell receptor (TCR), (2) costimulatory signals (e.g., CD80/CD86 on APC binding CD28 on T cells), and (3) cytokines that polarize differentiation (e.g., IL-12 for Th1, IL-4 for Th2, IL-6/TGF-β for Th17).

Innate and adaptive immunity are tightly integrated. Innate cytokines shape adaptive differentiation; conversely, adaptive effectors modulate innate cell function (e.g., Th1 IFN-γ activation of macrophages). In autoimmune myopathies, both arms contribute: innate-driven complement activation and macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis on one hand, and adaptive-driven autoantibody production and T-cell–mediated myocyte injury on the other. Understanding which mechanisms predominate in a given myopathy (e.g., antibody/complement in dermatomyositis versus CD8+ T-cell–mediated myofiber necrosis in polymyositis or inclusion body myositis) helps interpret biopsy findings and guide therapy.

# B-cell and T-cell roles in autoimmunity

B cells and T cells play complementary and sometimes independent roles in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases. B cells contribute via antibody production, antigen presentation to T cells, and secretion of pro-inflammatory or regulatory cytokines. T cells, depending on subset, mediate direct cytotoxicity (CD8+ CTLs), help B-cell maturation (follicular helper T cells, Tfh), or orchestrate inflammation (Th1, Th17). A breakdown in central or peripheral tolerance results in self-reactive lymphocytes that escape deletion or regulation.

B cells: Naïve B cells recognizing antigen via the B-cell receptor (BCR) may internalize the antigen, present peptides on MHC class II to CD4+ T cells, and receive T-cell help (via CD40L–CD40 interactions and cytokines) to undergo class-switch recombination and somatic hypermutation within germinal centers. This process yields high-affinity, class-switched antibodies (IgG, IgA, IgE). Pathogenic autoantibodies are often IgG subclasses capable of fixing complement and engaging Fc receptors (FcγRs) on innate cells, leading to opsonization, complement activation, and antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC). In autoimmune myopathies, myositis-specific autoantibodies (MSAs) and myositis-associated autoantibodies (MAAs) are important diagnostic markers and sometimes pathogenic mediators (e.g., anti–signal recognition particle, anti–Mi-2, anti–MDA5), though the pathogenicity of specific autoantibodies varies by disease.

T cells: CD4+ T helper cells differentiate into functionally distinct subsets. Th1 cells produce IFN-γ and support macrophage activation and CD8+ responses; Th2 cells assist humoral immunity; Th17 cells produce IL-17, IL-21, and IL-22 and are implicated in many autoimmune inflammatory processes. Tfh cells (CXCR5+, PD-1 high) are specialized to help B cells in germinal centers and are central to autoantibody formation. CD8+ T cells recognize peptides presented on MHC class I molecules expressed on nearly all nucleated cells, enabling direct cytotoxicity through perforin/granzyme release and Fas–FasL interactions. In some autoimmune myopathies, notably polymyositis and inclusion body myositis (IBM), cytotoxic CD8+ T cells invade non-necrotic myofibers that aberrantly express MHC class I, suggesting direct antigen-driven attack.

Interactions and cross-talk: B and T cell interactions form a self-reinforcing loop. Autoantigen release from injured muscle feeds APCs, which activate T cells that promote B-cell differentiation and class switching, producing antibodies that further injure tissue. B cells also serve as APCs and cytokine producers; depletion of B cells (e.g., with rituximab) can modulate both humoral and cellular responses. The relative contribution of B-cell versus T-cell mechanisms informs treatment choices and biomarkers; for example, complement-targeted therapies may be more relevant when immune complexes and complement deposition predominate, while therapies targeting T-cell activation (calcineurin inhibitors, IVIG modulation, or glucocorticoids) may be prioritized when cytotoxic T-cell infiltrates are prominent.

# Autoantibody formation and pathogenic mechanisms

Autoantibody formation begins with failure of tolerance mechanisms, leading to B-cell recognition of self-antigen and subsequent T-cell help. Key steps include antigen availability, presentation by APCs, activation of autoreactive CD4+ T cells, and germinal center reactions that generate high-affinity, class-switched autoantibodies. The antigens targeted in autoimmune myopathies are often intracellular or nucleic acid–associated proteins (e.g., anti–signal recognition particle [SRP], anti–HMGCR, anti–Mi-2, anti–MDA5) or muscle membrane–associated components (e.g., in immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy). Autoantibodies can be diagnostic biomarkers, correlate with clinical phenotypes, and sometimes be directly pathogenic.

Mechanisms by which autoantibodies mediate tissue injury include:

1. Complement activation: IgG subclasses (particularly IgG1 and IgG3 in humans) and IgM can activate the classical pathway by binding C1q, leading to downstream complement cascade activation, generation of C3a/C5a anaphylatoxins, opsonization, and formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC or C5b-9) that can injure cell membranes.

2. Fc receptor–mediated effector functions: Fc portions of IgG engage Fcγ receptors on macrophages, neutrophils, NK cells, and dendritic cells, leading to phagocytosis of opsonized targets, release of inflammatory mediators, and ADCC. This is particularly relevant when autoantibodies target surface antigens accessible on intact cells.

3. Direct functional blockade or receptor modulation: Autoantibodies can directly interfere with protein function (blocking or agonist activity), leading to dysfunction without overt complement activation. In muscle disease, a classic example outside myopathy is myasthenia gravis where anti-acetylcholine receptor antibodies block neuromuscular transmission; in myopathies, similar mechanisms may operate if surface-expressed proteins are targeted.

4. Immune complex deposition: Soluble antigen–antibody complexes can deposit in tissues, activate complement, and recruit inflammatory cells. In certain autoimmune myopathies with vascular involvement (e.g., dermatomyositis), immune complex deposition in small vessels contributes to microvascular injury.

Diagnostics and laboratory correlations: Autoantibody testing is central to the evaluation of suspected autoimmune myopathies. Common tests include myositis-specific autoantibodies (MSAs): anti–Jo-1 (histidyl tRNA synthetase), anti–SRP, anti–Mi-2, anti–MDA5, anti–TIF1-γ, anti–NXP2, anti–HMGCR, and others. Myositis-associated antibodies (MAAs) such as anti–PM/Scl or anti–Ku are seen in overlap syndromes. Interpretation requires assay knowledge (immunoprecipitation, line blot, ELISA) and clinical correlation, as false positives/negatives occur. Complement levels (C3, C4) may be consumed in active immune complex disease, and circulating complement activation products (C5a, sC5b-9) can sometimes be measured for research or clinical purposes.

Reference ranges: Normal serum complement concentrations vary by assay, but typical adult reference ranges are approximately C3: 90–180 mg/dL; C4: 10–40 mg/dL. Normal creatine kinase (CK), reflecting muscle injury, varies by lab and sex; common ranges are CK (male): 30–200 U/L, CK (female): 30–170 U/L, with substantial elevations in active myositis.

# Complement cascade and membrane attack complex (C5b-9) mediated tissue injury

The complement system consists of three activation pathways—classical, lectin, and alternative—that converge on the central component C3 and proceed to form the terminal membrane attack complex (MAC), C5b-9. The classical pathway is initiated by antigen–antibody complexes (IgM or certain IgG subclasses) binding C1q. The lectin pathway begins with mannose-binding lectin (MBL) or ficolins binding carbohydrate patterns on pathogens. The alternative pathway provides amplification through spontaneous hydrolysis of C3 ("tickover") and factor B/D–mediated amplification loops. All pathways generate C3 convertases that cleave C3 into C3a (an anaphylatoxin) and C3b (an opsonin), followed by formation of C5 convertases that cleave C5 into C5a (potent chemoattractant/anaphylatoxin) and C5b, initiating assembly of the MAC (C5b-6-7-8-9).

Pathogenic roles in autoimmune myopathies: Complement activation can injure muscle through multiple mechanisms. Deposition of C5b-9 on the sarcolemma causes sublytic or lytic injury to myofibers and endothelial cells. In dermatomyositis, capillary endothelial deposition of MAC is a characteristic finding in many cases and is associated with perifascicular muscle fiber ischemia and atrophy. The presence of C5b-9 within endomysial capillaries and along sarcolemma supports a complement-mediated microangiopathy model. Complement activation also yields C3a and C5a, which recruit neutrophils, monocytes, and other inflammatory cells; these cells release proteases, reactive oxygen species, and cytokines that amplify tissue damage.

Regulation: Complement activity is tightly regulated by soluble and membrane-bound inhibitors such as C1 inhibitor (C1-INH), factor H, factor I, membrane cofactor protein (MCP/CD46), decay accelerating factor (DAF/CD55), and CD59, which prevents MAC insertion. Dysfunction or overwhelming activation leads to tissue injury. In autoimmune myopathies, targeted complement inhibitors (e.g., anti-C5 monoclonal antibodies) have theoretical and, in some cases, emerging clinical relevance when complement-mediated pathology predominates.

Diagnostics: Tissue immunohistochemistry for complement components (C3d, C4d, C5b-9) on muscle biopsy can demonstrate complement deposition patterns that support a diagnosis of complement-mediated injury (e.g., perifascicular C5b-9 deposition in dermatomyositis). Circulating complement levels may be low with complement consumption, though normal levels do not exclude local complement activation. Measurement of C5b-9 (soluble MAC) in serum can reflect systemic activation.

Clinical implications: Recognition of complement-mediated mechanisms influences therapeutic choices. Therapies that reduce autoantibody production (B-cell depletion), inhibit complement directly (anti-C5), or block downstream inflammatory mediators (corticosteroids, IVIG which can modulate complement) may be appropriate depending on the dominant pathophysiology.

# Macrophage activation, antigen presentation and myophagocytosis

Macrophages are versatile innate immune cells that participate in host defense, tissue remodeling, antigen presentation, and resolution of inflammation. In skeletal muscle, macrophages can adopt a spectrum of activation states rather than discrete M1/M2 polarities: pro-inflammatory phenotypes secrete TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and nitric oxide; anti-inflammatory/regulatory phenotypes produce IL-10, TGF-β, and growth factors that promote repair. In autoimmune myopathies, macrophages are prominent effectors of tissue injury and clearance of necrotic fibers.

Activation and recruitment: Tissue injury, immune complexes, complement activation, and chemokines (e.g., CCL2/MCP-1) recruit monocytes that differentiate into macrophages. Fcγ receptor engagement by IgG-opsonized targets and signaling via PRRs amplifies macrophage activation. IFN-γ from Th1 and CD8+ T cells polarizes macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory, microbicidal phenotype, increasing expression of MHC class II and co-stimulatory molecules (CD80/CD86), thereby enhancing antigen presentation.

Antigen presentation: Macrophages are professional antigen-presenting cells capable of processing and presenting peptides on MHC class II molecules to CD4+ T cells. They can also cross-present exogenous antigen on MHC class I to prime CD8+ T cells under certain contexts. In autoimmune myopathies, macrophages can present muscle-derived antigens to T cells, sustaining T-cell activation and perpetuating local immune responses. Upregulation of MHC molecules on muscle fibers themselves may permit direct presentation to CD8+ T cells, as seen in polymyositis/IBM, but macrophage presentation helps maintain local immune circuits.

Myophagocytosis and muscle fiber injury: Macrophages phagocytose necrotic muscle fibers and cell debris (myophagocytosis), which is an appropriate response in injury and repair. However, in autoimmune contexts, macrophages may phagocytose viable or sublytic-injured myofibers opsonized by antibody or tagged by complement. Cytotoxic mediators including reactive oxygen species, proteases (matrix metalloproteinases, cathepsins), and pro-inflammatory cytokines can extend tissue damage. Histologically, myophagocytosis is a hallmark of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy and macrophage-rich polymyositis variants; CD68 immunostaining highlights macrophage infiltrates.

Repair versus pathology: Macrophages also orchestrate muscle regeneration by clearing debris and secreting growth factors that activate satellite cells and fibroblasts. Chronic or dysregulated macrophage activation, persistent antigenic stimuli, and profibrotic cytokines (e.g., TGF-β) may impair regeneration and promote fibrosis. Therapeutic strategies aim to attenuate pathogenic macrophage activation while preserving regenerative functions, for example by modulating cytokine milieus or targeting upstream drivers like autoantibodies and complement.

# Immunologic tolerance, triggers for autoimmunity and environmental/drug associations

Immunologic tolerance prevents detrimental responses to self-antigens and operates centrally (during lymphocyte development) and peripherally (in mature lymphocytes). Central tolerance deletes strongly self-reactive lymphocytes via negative selection in the thymus (for T cells) and bone marrow (for B cells). Peripheral tolerance involves anergy (functional unresponsiveness), suppression by regulatory T cells (Tregs, CD4+CD25+FOXP3+), deletion, and immune privilege in certain tissues. Failure at any checkpoint can permit autoimmunity.

Mechanisms of tolerance breakdown: Several mechanisms can precipitate loss of tolerance in autoimmune myopathies:

1. Molecular mimicry: Structural homology between foreign antigens (infectious agents) and self-proteins leads to cross-reactive T or B cell responses that target both pathogen and host tissues. For example, viral infections have been implicated as triggers in some myositis subsets.

2. Epitope spreading: Initial immune responses to a dominant epitope extend to subdominant or cryptic epitopes of the same or different proteins, broadening autoreactivity. Ongoing muscle injury releases intracellular antigens and enhances epitope spreading.

3. Bystander activation and tissue damage: Infection or mechanical injury induces local inflammation, upregulating MHC class I/II expression and costimulatory molecules on resident cells and APCs, enabling activation of autoreactive lymphocytes.

4. Defective Treg number/function: Reduced Treg suppression allows expansion of autoreactive effector T cells. Alterations in cytokine milieus (e.g., increased IL-6) can both promote effector T-cell differentiation and impair Treg stability.

5. Genetic predisposition: HLA alleles (e.g., certain HLA-DR and HLA-B types) confer susceptibility by influencing antigen presentation repertoires. Non-HLA genes involved in immune regulation (PTPN22, STAT4, CTLA4) also modulate risk.

Environmental and drug associations: Several environmental exposures and medications have been associated with triggering or unmasking autoimmune myopathies:

- Infections: Viral (e.g., Coxsackievirus, enteroviruses, hepatitis viruses), bacterial, and other infections may precede myositis onset by initiating immune activation and antigen release.

- Statins: Exposure to HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors has a recognized association with immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) characterized by anti–HMGCR antibodies. In such cases, statin exposure likely provides an initial antigenic stimulus or alters muscle antigen expression leading to autoimmunity.

- Checkpoint inhibitors: Immune checkpoint blockade (anti–CTLA-4, anti–PD-1/PD-L1) used in oncology may induce immune-related adverse events, including inflammatory myopathies, due to removal of inhibitory signals that enforce peripheral tolerance.

- Vaccines and adjuvants: Rarely, vaccines have temporal associations with myositis onset, potentially via innate immune activation; causality is difficult to establish and generally rare relative to benefits.

- Environmental toxins: Certain exposures (e.g., recreational drugs, toxins) have been reported anecdotally in association with myositis, but evidence is limited.

Clinical and diagnostic implications: Eliciting exposures, medications, and infection history is essential in the diagnostic evaluation. Identifying a trigger (e.g., statin) can prompt specific testing (anti–HMGCR) and influence management, though disease may persist after withdrawal of the trigger, indicating established autoimmunity. Genetic testing is not routinely performed for most myopathies but HLA associations provide mechanistic insight and may explain clustering of autoantibody specificities with particular clinical phenotypes.

Maintenance of tolerance and therapeutic strategies: Therapies that restore or mimic tolerance are attractive. Approaches include antigen-specific tolerance induction (experimental), expansion of Tregs (low-dose IL-2), and therapies that blunt co-stimulatory signals (abatacept, CTLA4-Ig) to prevent T-cell activation. Non-specific immunosuppression reduces effector function but does not restore antigen-specific tolerance unless combined with strategies to delete or re-educate autoreactive clones.

Conclusion

A nuanced appreciation of innate and adaptive interactions, the roles of B cells and T cells, the formation and effects of autoantibodies, complement-mediated injury, macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis, and the mechanisms by which tolerance is lost provides a framework for understanding autoimmune myopathies. These immunologic principles have direct clinical relevance: they guide interpretation of serologic tests and muscle biopsy findings, inform pathogenetic models for specific myositis subtypes, and influence therapeutic decisions from immunosuppression to targeted biologics and complement inhibitors. Mastery of this integrated immunology equips clinicians to synthesize diagnostic data and tailor evidence-based management for patients with inflammatory muscle disease.
