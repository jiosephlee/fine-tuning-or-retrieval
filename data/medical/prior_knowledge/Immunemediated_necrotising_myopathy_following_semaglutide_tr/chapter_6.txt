Chapter 6: Pharmacology of Glucagon‑Like Peptide‑1 (GLP‑1) Receptor Agonists

Introduction

Glucagon‑like peptide‑1 (GLP‑1) receptor agonists are a class of incretin‑based therapies that have transformed the management of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and obesity and have emerging roles in cardiovascular risk reduction. Their actions span multiple organ systems because the GLP‑1 receptor is expressed in pancreatic islets, the central nervous system, the gastrointestinal tract, the heart, vasculature, kidney, and other tissues. Clinically, GLP‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs) lower plasma glucose by enhancing glucose‑dependent insulin secretion and suppressing glucagon, slow gastric emptying, and reduce appetite and body weight. This chapter reviews the physiological basis of GLP‑1 action and the pharmacology of GLP‑1 RAs, compares representative agents and their pharmacokinetic profiles, summarizes approved and off‑label therapeutic indications including cardiovascular outcome data, and examines the adverse effect profile with attention to multisystem consequences—including gastrointestinal effects, pancreatitis risk, effects on appetite and body composition, rare muscle injury reports, and renal complications driven by volume depletion and interactions with other drugs. The goal is to provide a clinically useful reference that links mechanism to patient management.

# Physiologic role of GLP‑1 and mechanism of GLP‑1 receptor agonists

GLP‑1 is an incretin hormone secreted primarily by L‑cells in the distal small intestine and colon in response to nutrient ingestion. Endogenous GLP‑1 circulates in multiple active forms but is rapidly inactivated by the enzyme dipeptidyl peptidase‑4 (DPP‑4), giving it a plasma half‑life on the order of 1–2 minutes. Physiologic effects of GLP‑1 are mediated by the GLP‑1 receptor, a G protein‑coupled receptor that signals predominantly through Gs to increase intracellular cyclic AMP and has downstream effects on ion channels and gene expression.

In pancreatic β cells, GLP‑1 enhances glucose‑dependent insulin secretion by increasing cAMP and potentiating ATP‑sensitive potassium channel closure and voltage‑dependent calcium influx. This amplifies insulin release when ambient glucose is elevated but has little effect in hypoglycemia, which underlies the low intrinsic hypoglycemia risk of GLP‑1 RAs when used alone. In α cells, GLP‑1 suppresses glucagon secretion, reducing hepatic glucose output. GLP‑1 also slows gastric emptying through vagal and enteric neuronal pathways and direct effects on the stomach, which blunts postprandial glucose excursions. Central actions in the hypothalamus and brainstem reduce appetite and food intake, contributing to weight loss.

GLP‑1 receptors are present in multiple non‑pancreatic tissues. In the cardiovascular system, GLP‑1 RAs can have modest positive inotropic effects, improve endothelial function, and alter inflammatory pathways. Renal effects include natriuresis and changes in renal hemodynamics mediated by both direct and indirect mechanisms. The widespread receptor distribution explains why GLP‑1 RAs produce multisystem effects—therapeutic and adverse.

Pharmacologically, GLP‑1 receptor agonists are peptide drugs that resist DPP‑4 degradation (either by modification of native GLP‑1 structure or by being structurally distinct yet receptor‑active). They bind and activate the GLP‑1 receptor, producing the physiologic effects described above but with extended duration to enable therapeutic dosing intervals ranging from twice daily to once weekly or daily oral dosing (for an oral formulation). Because their insulinotropic action is glucose‑dependent, the risk of hypoglycemia is low unless combined with agents that independently increase hypoglycemia risk (sulfonylureas, insulin).

# Representative agents, dosing forms (short‑acting vs long‑acting) and pharmacokinetics

A number of GLP‑1 RAs are available; they differ in molecular structure, pharmacokinetic properties, route and frequency of administration, and clinical effects. Representative agents include exenatide (twice daily immediate‑release and once‑weekly extended‑release formulations), liraglutide (daily), lixisenatide (daily), dulaglutide (weekly), semaglutide (weekly subcutaneous and daily oral formulations), and albiglutide (formerly used but now withdrawn in some regions). Some agents are peptide analogues of native GLP‑1 with amino acid substitutions to prevent DPP‑4 cleavage and/or fusion to larger proteins or albumin‑binding moieties to prolong half‑life.

Clinically it is helpful to distinguish short‑acting from long‑acting GLP‑1 RAs. Short‑acting agents (for example, immediate‑release exenatide and lixisenatide) have prominent effects on gastric emptying and thus particularly reduce postprandial glucose excursions. Their half‑lives are on the order of hours, and they are typically dosed twice daily or once daily around meals. Long‑acting agents (for example, liraglutide, dulaglutide, semaglutide, weekly exenatide extended‑release) have more sustained receptor activation, greater effects on fasting glucose and overall hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and somewhat attenuated effects on gastric emptying because tachyphylaxis to gastric emptying occurs with continuous receptor stimulation. Long‑acting agents are dosed once daily or once weekly.

Pharmacokinetic parameters vary by agent. For example, liraglutide (daily subcutaneous) has a half‑life of about 13 hours allowing once‑daily dosing; dulaglutide and weekly semaglutide formulations have effective half‑lives that permit once‑weekly dosing. Oral semaglutide is formulated with an absorption enhancer to permit transmucosal uptake and requires fasting administration with limited water and post‑dose fasting to ensure bioavailability. Bioavailability of peptide agents administered subcutaneously is high compared with oral protein absorption and is not subject to first‑pass hepatic metabolism. Clearance mechanisms include proteolytic degradation and, for agents with albumin‑binding, protected distribution that extends half‑life.

From a clinical perspective, dosing initiation typically uses stepwise titration to mitigate gastrointestinal adverse effects. For example, many agents start at a lower dose for 1–2 weeks before escalating to the therapeutic dose. The exact doses and titration schedules are agent‑specific and should be followed according to product labeling.

# Therapeutic indications: type 2 diabetes, weight management, cardiovascular outcomes

Type 2 diabetes mellitus: GLP‑1 RAs are approved for glycemic control in T2DM, either as monotherapy or in combination with oral agents and insulin. Their glucose‑lowering effect reduces HbA1c by approximately 0.5–1.5 percentage points depending on baseline A1c, agent, and dosing. They are particularly useful when weight gain is undesirable, as most agents produce clinically meaningful weight loss. GLP‑1 RAs are recommended in modern diabetes guidelines both for their glycemic efficacy and for their cardiovascular and weight benefits; selection among agents depends on patient comorbidities, tolerability, route preference, and cost.

Weight management: Several GLP‑1 RAs have been approved specifically for chronic weight management at higher (or differently labeled) doses than those used for diabetes. These agents promote sustained reductions in body weight through reduced appetite, increased satiety, and decreased energy intake. Typical weight loss in clinical trials varies by agent and dose; higher potency agents (e.g., higher‑dose semaglutide) have produced mean weight reductions of 10%–15% or more in controlled trials, with individual variability. In addition to absolute weight reduction, GLP‑1 RAs alter body composition, reducing fat mass more than lean mass in most studies (see section on muscle mass below).

Cardiovascular outcomes: Because many glucose therapies have neutral or adverse cardiovascular effects, GLP‑1 RAs have been evaluated in large cardiovascular outcomes trials. Several agents have demonstrated a reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE: composite of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and nonfatal stroke) in patients with established cardiovascular disease or at high cardiovascular risk. Benefit appears to be more consistent with certain longer‑acting agents and may relate to direct vascular and anti‑inflammatory effects, as well as indirect benefits from weight loss, blood pressure reduction, and improved glycemic control. Not all GLP‑1 RAs have demonstrated identical cardiovascular benefit, and when prescribing for cardiovascular risk reduction clinicians should use agents with proven benefit in trials for that indication.

Other investigational and off‑label uses: GLP‑1 RAs have been explored for nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), and neuroprotection in selected settings; however, routine use outside approved indications should be based on evidence and regulatory guidance.

# Common adverse effects: gastrointestinal symptoms, nausea/vomiting, pancreatitis risk, effects on appetite and weight

Gastrointestinal effects: The most frequent adverse effects of GLP‑1 RAs are gastrointestinal. Nausea is the most common complaint, often occurring during initiation and dose escalation, and typically diminishing over weeks to months. Vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation are also reported. Short‑acting agents that slow gastric emptying more intensely can produce more pronounced nausea and delayed gastric emptying symptoms initially. Because these effects are central and peripheral (gastric), they often respond to slower titration, antiemetic measures, and supportive care. Severe, persistent vomiting that results in volume depletion or electrolyte disturbances warrants prompt evaluation and possible dose interruption.

Pancreatitis risk: Cases of acute pancreatitis have been reported in patients receiving GLP‑1 RAs, leading to concern about a causal relationship. Large randomized trials and observational studies have produced mixed results; while some analyses suggest a small increased risk, other studies do not confirm a significant association. Regulatory agencies recommend caution: avoid GLP‑1 RAs in patients with a history of pancreatitis (or use individualized risk‑benefit analysis), and discontinue therapy if pancreatitis is suspected. Typical clinical guidance includes immediate cessation of the GLP‑1 RA if acute pancreatitis is diagnosed and not to restart until the etiology is clarified. Serum amylase and lipase reference ranges vary by lab; lipase elevations of several fold above the upper limit of normal (ULN) plus compatible clinical features suggest pancreatitis. Routine monitoring of pancreatic enzymes in asymptomatic patients is not generally recommended.

Appetite and weight effects: By central appetite suppression and delayed gastric emptying, GLP‑1 RAs induce decreased caloric intake. Weight loss is a common and often desirable therapeutic outcome in T2DM and obesity management. Clinicians should counsel patients regarding expected weight changes, monitor nutritional status, and assess for excessive weight loss. Weight loss is typically greatest in the first months of therapy and may plateau with continued treatment. Because weight loss includes both fat and lean mass, monitoring and interventions to preserve muscle mass (dietary protein, resistance exercise) are relevant for long‑term health, particularly in older or frail patients.

Other adverse events: Cholelithiasis and biliary disease have been reported more frequently with substantial weight loss, and some agents carry animal data showing thyroid C‑cell hyperplasia and tumors in rodents; although human risk is uncertain, GLP‑1 RAs are contraindicated in patients with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2) in several jurisdictions. Injection‑site reactions, hypersensitivity, and rare pancreatobiliary complications are described.

# Reported effects on muscle mass, rare reports of muscle injury and rhabdomyolysis

Body composition changes: Weight loss produced by GLP‑1 RAs generally preferentially reduces fat mass, but a proportion of the lost weight is lean body mass, including skeletal muscle. Clinical studies using dual‑energy X‑ray absorptiometry (DEXA) or bioelectrical impedance have consistently shown that lean mass loss comprises a variable but non‑negligible fraction of total weight loss—often around 20%–30% of the weight lost (percentage varies with study population, baseline characteristics, and duration). This is clinically relevant because loss of muscle mass can impair strength, metabolic health, and physical function, particularly in older adults or those with preexisting sarcopenia. To mitigate lean mass loss, strategies include ensuring adequate protein intake, engaging patients in resistance‑training exercise, and monitoring functional status during therapy.

Reports of myopathy and rhabdomyolysis: True myonecrosis and clinically significant rhabdomyolysis associated directly with GLP‑1 RAs appear to be rare. Case reports have described elevations in creatine kinase (CK) and instances labeled as myopathy or rhabdomyolysis temporally associated with GLP‑1 RA initiation. A direct myotoxic mechanism has not been definitively established. Several plausible mechanisms may underlie reported associations: (1) rapid weight loss leading to catabolism and increased susceptibility of muscle to injury; (2) decreased oral intake and vomiting resulting in hypovolemia, electrolyte disturbances (e.g., hypokalemia), and secondary muscle injury; (3) drug‑drug interactions—most importantly with statins and other agents known to cause myopathy—where GLP‑1 RA–induced changes in drug absorption, distribution, or renal function could alter exposure to myotoxic drugs; and (4) idiosyncratic immune‑mediated reactions. Laboratory monitoring in suspected cases should include CK, renal function tests (serum creatinine), electrolytes (potassium, phosphate, calcium), and urinalysis for myoglobinuria. Normal CK reference ranges depend on sex and laboratory method but are commonly roughly 20–200 U/L; levels >5 times the ULN warrant attention, and CK elevations accompanied by muscle pain, weakness, dark urine, or acute kidney injury suggest rhabdomyolysis requiring urgent management.

Clinical approach: When a patient on a GLP‑1 RA develops muscle pain, weakness, or notable CK elevation, consider evaluating for alternative or contributing causes (recent exercise, trauma, seizures, alcohol use, hypothyroidism, statin use). Temporarily holding the GLP‑1 RA (and any concomitant myotoxic medications) until the diagnosis is clarified is prudent. If rhabdomyolysis is confirmed, standard management with aggressive intravenous fluids, electrolyte correction, and monitoring for renal complications is indicated; decision to reintroduce GLP‑1 RA should be made cautiously and on a case‑by‑case basis.

# Renal adverse events secondary to volume depletion and GI losses; interaction with other drugs

Renal risks related to volume depletion: GLP‑1 RAs do not typically have intrinsic nephrotoxic effects, but renal adverse events can arise indirectly. Severe vomiting, diarrhea, or reduced oral intake can cause dehydration and pre‑renal azotemia; in susceptible patients (elderly, baseline chronic kidney disease [CKD], on diuretics or renin‑angiotensin system blockers), this can progress to acute kidney injury (AKI). There are reported cases of AKI temporally linked to GLP‑1 RA use where the precipitant was persistent gastrointestinal losses leading to volume depletion. Clinicians should counsel patients about maintaining hydration, recognizing signs of volume depletion, and seeking care for severe gastrointestinal symptoms.

Monitoring and risk stratification: Baseline renal function assessment (serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]) is reasonable before starting therapy in patients with risk factors for renal dysfunction. Common thresholds: eGFR values ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m^2 indicate normal or mildly decreased kidney function; stage 3 CKD corresponds to eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m^2, stage 4 to 15–29 mL/min/1.73 m^2. Most GLP‑1 RAs can be used in patients with mild to moderate CKD, but some agents have specific labeling and dosing considerations at lower eGFRs. If a patient develops AKI or severe dehydration, the GLP‑1 RA should be temporarily stopped until renal function stabilizes. Medication reconciliation is important because volume depletion can potentiate adverse effects of other drugs (e.g., NSAIDs, diuretics, ACE inhibitors) and may necessitate temporary dose adjustments.

Drug interactions mediated by gastrointestinal effects: GLP‑1 RAs slow gastric emptying, which can alter the pharmacokinetics of orally administered medications. This is particularly relevant for drugs where timing of peak concentration affects efficacy or safety (e.g., oral contraceptives, certain antibiotics, anticoagulants). Oral medications with narrow therapeutic windows may have delayed or reduced absorption; for antiplatelet agents or oral anticoagulants such as warfarin, altered absorption could theoretically change INR, although clinically significant interactions are uncommon and individualized monitoring is prudent. For oral semaglutide specifically, administration instructions (fasting, limited water) are designed to maximize absorption; concomitant ingestion of other oral medications shortly before or after oral semaglutide can reduce their absorption or that of semaglutide itself.

Interactions with renally cleared drugs and statins: Because GLP‑1 RA–related volume depletion can precipitate AKI, this may impair clearance of renally eliminated drugs, necessitating dose adjustments. Agents that are cleared renally and have narrow therapeutic windows (e.g., certain antibiotics, digoxin in older people) should be reviewed. Statins are among the most widely co‑prescribed drugs in patients with T2DM and cardiovascular risk. Although GLP‑1 RAs do not directly interact with statins pharmacokinetically, occurrences of muscle complaints in patients on combined therapy should prompt evaluation for statin‑related myopathy with consideration of the potential for additive risk from dehydration, electrolyte disturbances, or unmasking of a predisposition to myopathy during weight loss. Temporarily stopping statins or GLP‑1 RAs may be necessary if severe muscle injury is suspected.

Practical management points: When initiating GLP‑1 RA therapy in a patient on diuretics, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, NSAIDs, or other agents that influence renal perfusion, consider baseline renal function and counsel regarding volume status. For patients with baseline CKD or on multiple nephrotoxic medications, closer follow‑up of renal function after initiation and during dose escalation is reasonable. When severe gastrointestinal adverse effects occur, clinicians should evaluate for dehydration and AKI and hold GLP‑1 RA until the patient is euvolemic and renal function has recovered.

Conclusion

GLP‑1 receptor agonists offer potent glucose‑lowering, weight‑reducing, and cardioprotective benefits in appropriately selected patients. Their multisystem actions underlie both therapeutic efficacy and a characteristic adverse‑effect profile centered on the gastrointestinal tract, appetite regulation, and indirect effects on the pancreas, kidney, and muscle. Understanding the physiologic mechanisms—glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, glucagon suppression, slowed gastric emptying, and central appetite suppression—helps clinicians anticipate benefits and risks, choose among agents (short‑acting versus long‑acting; subcutaneous versus oral), and manage complications. Key clinical considerations include careful titration to minimize nausea, vigilance for severe gastrointestinal losses that can precipitate dehydration and AKI, awareness of rare but important reports of pancreatitis and muscle injury, and assessment of drug interactions arising from altered gastric emptying or changes in renal function. Preservation of lean mass during weight loss and individualized monitoring in patients with comorbid conditions are important components of safe, effective use.
