Introduction

Elevated transaminases—serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT)—are among the most common abnormalities encountered in medical practice. Interpreting these laboratory signals requires integration of epidemiology, physiology, and clinical context. Two frequent and intertwined clinical problems are non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and its progressive form non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), and the challenge of distinguishing hepatic sources of transaminase elevation from muscle-derived elevations in patients with myopathy or muscle injury. This chapter reviews the pathogenesis and population impact of NAFLD/NASH, delineates enzyme patterns and concomitant laboratory clues that distinguish hepatic from muscle etiologies, discusses imaging and biopsy indications, provides a practical approach to elevated transaminases in the setting of myopathy, addresses overlap syndromes where both liver and muscle disease coexist, and outlines recommended monitoring of liver tests during systemic therapies and immunosuppression.

Epidemiology and pathogenesis of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)

Epidemiology
NAFLD is now the most common chronic liver disease in Western countries and is increasing worldwide in parallel with obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and the metabolic syndrome. Prevalence estimates vary with the diagnostic modality: population imaging and biopsy studies suggest NAFLD affects approximately 25%–30% of adults globally, with higher rates (up to 60%–80%) among individuals with obesity or T2DM. NASH—the subset with hepatocellular injury and inflammation, and which may progress to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma—occurs in a smaller but clinically important proportion of patients with NAFLD. The annual risk of progression from simple steatosis to NASH or to advanced fibrosis is variable, but the presence of NASH and fibrosis stage are the principal determinants of liver-related morbidity and mortality.

Risk factors for NAFLD include central (visceral) obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia (elevated triglycerides, low HDL), T2DM, older age, male sex in some cohorts, and certain genetic variants (for example, PNPLA3 I148M, TM6SF2). Alcohol consumption must be assessed carefully: by definition, NAFLD occurs in individuals with alcohol intake below specified thresholds (commonly <30 g/day for men and <20 g/day for women), but clinically modest alcohol use can coexist and influence disease course.

Pathogenesis
NAFLD is a complex, multifactorial disease involving metabolic perturbations, adipose tissue dysfunction, lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and maladaptive immune responses. The earlier “two-hit” hypothesis (insulin resistance leading to steatosis followed by oxidative injury) has evolved into a more nuanced “multiple parallel hits” model. Key pathophysiological steps include:

1. Hepatic lipid accumulation: Excess caloric intake, adipose tissue insulin resistance, and increased lipolysis deliver elevated free fatty acids to the liver. De novo lipogenesis is upregulated by hyperinsulinemia and hyperglycemia. When fatty acid uptake and synthesis exceed the liver’s capacity for oxidation and export (as very-low-density lipoprotein), triglycerides accumulate within hepatocytes as lipid droplets.

2. Lipotoxicity and cellular stress: Accumulation of certain lipid species (diacylglycerols, ceramides, free fatty acids) induces mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and impaired autophagy. These processes lead to hepatocyte injury and cell death by apoptosis or necrosis.

3. Oxidative stress and inflammation: Reactive oxygen species generated by dysfunctional mitochondria and cytochrome P450 enzymes cause lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial DNA damage. Injured hepatocytes release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that activate resident Kupffer cells and recruit inflammatory cells. Cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) and chemokines amplify inflammation.

4. Fibrogenesis: Hepatic stellate cells are activated by inflammatory mediators, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and oxidative stress, leading to extracellular matrix deposition and progressive fibrosis. Parenchymal remodeling underlies progression to cirrhosis.

Clinical and histologic spectrum
NAFLD represents a continuum from simple steatosis (hepatic steatosis without significant inflammation or fibrosis) to NASH, characterized histologically by steatosis, lobular inflammation, ballooning degeneration of hepatocytes, and varying degrees of portal and perisinusoidal fibrosis. The NASH Clinical Research Network scoring systems and fibrosis staging (F0–F4) are commonly used in research and clinical contexts. Clinically, many patients with NAFLD are asymptomatic; fatigue or nonspecific right upper quadrant discomfort may occur. Elevated transaminases are common but not universal; normal transaminases do not exclude significant histologic disease including advanced fibrosis.

Implications for practice
From a clinical standpoint, identifying patients at risk for progressive disease is critical. Risk stratification uses clinical variables (age, diabetes, obesity), laboratory markers, and noninvasive fibrosis tests (Fibrosis-4 index, NAFLD fibrosis score, transient elastography). Lifestyle intervention (weight loss through diet and exercise) is the cornerstone of therapy; pharmacologic options are emerging for high-risk NASH with fibrosis.

Patterns of liver enzyme abnormalities and clues to origin (AST/ALT ratio, concurrent CK elevations)

Interpreting transaminase elevations requires recognizing both the magnitude and pattern of abnormality, and integrating other laboratory results.

Magnitude and pattern
Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) is more specific to liver because it is concentrated in hepatocytes; aspartate aminotransferase (AST) is distributed in liver mitochondria and cytosol, but is also present in cardiac and skeletal muscle, erythrocytes, kidney, and other tissues. Normal reference ranges vary by laboratory and population; approximate typical ranges are ALT: 7–56 U/L, AST: 10–40 U/L, and creatine kinase (CK): 20–200 U/L (depending on sex and assay). Many labs and professional societies have recommended lower upper limits of normal for ALT (for example, ~30 U/L for men and ~20 U/L for women) to improve sensitivity for liver disease. Mild isolated elevations (<2–3× ULN) are common and often benign; values greater than 5–10× ULN suggest significant hepatocellular injury or extrahepatic muscle breakdown.

AST/ALT ratio
The AST/ALT ratio is an often-cited heuristic. In most forms of acute hepatocellular injury not due to alcohol or advanced fibrosis, ALT tends to be greater than AST (AST/ALT < 1). In alcoholic liver disease, AST is frequently greater than ALT with AST/ALT > 2, partly because alcohol induces mitochondrial injury and pyridoxine deficiency affects ALT activity. With advancing fibrosis/cirrhosis from any cause, AST may predominate, producing AST/ALT ≥ 1. However, AST/ALT ratio must be interpreted cautiously: moderate muscle injury elevates AST more than ALT because AST is abundant in muscle; hemolysis can artifactually raise AST; and the ratio varies by timing of sampling.

Concurrent CK elevations
Measurement of serum CK (creatine kinase), especially the CK-MM isoform from skeletal muscle, is a critical adjunct when distinguishing muscle versus hepatic sources of transaminase elevations. Muscle injury or myopathy typically produces marked CK elevation—often several-fold to tens of-fold above the upper limit of normal—while hepatic injury usually yields normal or only mildly elevated CK. In muscle injury, AST and sometimes ALT rise but the magnitude is usually less than CK, and the AST/ALT ratio may be >1 reflecting muscle AST content. For example, an asymptomatic patient with AST 250 U/L, ALT 80 U/L, and CK 10,000 U/L is most consistent with muscle injury; conversely, AST 200 U/L, ALT 400 U/L, and CK 100 U/L points to hepatic injury.

Other clues
- Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT): Elevation of ALP with GGT suggests cholestatic or biliary injury. GGT is also inducible by alcohol and some drugs.
- Bilirubin: Elevated conjugated bilirubin suggests impaired excretion from hepatocytes or biliary obstruction; significant hyperbilirubinemia points to more severe liver dysfunction.
- Synthetic function: International normalized ratio (INR), albumin, and platelet count reflect hepatic synthetic function and portal hypertension. Abnormalities indicate advanced liver disease rather than isolated transaminase leak.
- Aminotransferase kinetics: The temporal pattern matters. Rapid rise and fall of transaminases in the thousands suggests acute ischemic or toxin-mediated liver injury (e.g., acetaminophen). Chronic mild elevations point to steatosis, metabolic disease, or chronic viral hepatitis.

In sum, interpretation integrates AST/ALT magnitudes and ratios with CK, cholestatic markers, bilirubin, and synthetic function to localize the source and severity of injury.

Imaging and biopsy indications for liver disease evaluation

Imaging modalities
Noninvasive imaging is the first-line tool to assess hepatic steatosis and structural liver disease.

1. Ultrasound: Abdominal ultrasound is widely available and sensitive for moderate-to-severe steatosis (generally >20%–30% hepatic fat content) but less sensitive for mild steatosis. It detects increased echogenicity of the liver relative to the kidney and can suggest cirrhosis features. Ultrasound cannot reliably stage fibrosis.

2. Controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) and transient elastography (FibroScan): FibroScan measures liver stiffness as a surrogate for fibrosis and can quantify steatosis via CAP. It offers point-of-care, reproducible assessment, with thresholds for fibrosis staging and steatosis grades; however, obesity and narrow intercostal spaces can limit accuracy. Normal liver stiffness is typically <6 kPa; thresholds for significant fibrosis vary, often >8 kPa suggests ≥F2; >12–14 kPa suggests cirrhosis.

3. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI): MRI-based techniques—proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) for quantifying hepatic fat and magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) for fibrosis—are highly sensitive and specific and can quantify fat down to low levels. MRI-PDFF is the most accurate noninvasive measure of liver fat but is costly and not always available. MRE is the most accurate imaging test for fibrosis.

4. CT scan: Non-contrast CT can detect moderate-to-severe steatosis by hepatic attenuation; contrast reduces sensitivity. CT is not preferred for quantitative assessment due to radiation exposure and lower sensitivity compared with MRI.

Indications for biopsy
Liver biopsy remains the reference standard for diagnosing NASH and staging fibrosis, but it is invasive and subject to sampling variability and complications. Indications to consider liver biopsy include:

- Diagnostic uncertainty: When noninvasive testing is inconclusive and knowing the exact histologic diagnosis will change management.
- Suspected NASH with potential advanced fibrosis: Especially when noninvasive fibrosis tests are indeterminate or discordant and determining fibrosis stage would guide treatment decisions or trial eligibility.
- Excluding other causes: When autoimmune hepatitis, drug-induced liver injury, genetic/metabolic liver disease, or overlapping pathologies are suspected.
- Clinical trials: Histologic confirmation is often required for enrollment.

In practice, the current paradigm emphasizes noninvasive risk stratification: use clinical scores (Fibrosis-4, NAFLD fibrosis score), elastography, and other biomarkers to identify patients at low risk (who typically do not require biopsy) versus those at high or indeterminate risk (for whom biopsy may be warranted). Key decision thresholds vary by algorithm; for example, Fibrosis-4 index uses age, AST, ALT, and platelet count with cutoffs tailored to optimize sensitivity versus specificity.

Pre-biopsy evaluation should include bleeding risk assessment, imaging to exclude focal vascular lesions or ascites, and consideration of percutaneous versus transjugular biopsy in certain scenarios. Histologic assessment should document steatosis, lobular inflammation, hepatocyte ballooning, and fibrosis stage.

Approach to elevated transaminases in the setting of myopathy

Clinical assessment
When transaminases are elevated in a patient with suspected or known myopathy, the clinician must determine whether the elevations reflect hepatic injury, muscle breakdown, or both. The evaluation begins with a careful history and physical exam focused on features that suggest muscle disease (myalgias, weakness, exercise intolerance, muscle swelling, dark urine), hepatobiliary symptoms (jaundice, pruritus, abdominal pain), medication and supplement use (statins, fibrates, herbal products), alcohol history, and temporal relationship of symptoms and laboratory trends.

Laboratory strategy
Key laboratories to distinguish sources include:

- Serum CK (total): A markedly elevated CK supports muscle origin. Typical upper limit of normal for CK varies by laboratory and sex but often ~200 U/L; levels in myopathies may range from several-fold to >10,000 U/L in rhabdomyolysis.
- CK isoenzymes and aldolase: Aldolase can also be elevated in muscle disease and may be used alongside CK if CK is normal in chronic myopathy.
- AST and ALT: Both may be elevated in muscle injury; AST more so. ALT is less specific but may be elevated with prolonged muscle inflammation.
- Liver-specific tests: GGT and ALP are liver-enriched; their elevation supports hepatic origin. Albumin and INR assess liver synthetic function; abnormal values indicate hepatic dysfunction.
- Urinalysis and myoglobin: Myoglobinuria supports rhabdomyolysis.
- Viral hepatitis serologies and autoimmune markers if hepatic origin suspected.

Interpretation frameworks
- Predominant muscle pattern: Markedly elevated CK with modest transaminase elevations, normal GGT/ALP, normal bilirubin and synthetic function, and clinical features of myopathy favors muscle source. Serial testing can document parallel decline of CK and transaminases with muscle recovery.
- Predominant hepatic pattern: Elevated ALT >> AST, increased GGT/ALP, elevated bilirubin or INR, or imaging confirming steatosis or biliary disease supports hepatic cause.
- Mixed pattern: Coexisting elevations across both domains require further assessment for concurrent processes.

Imaging and specialist input
If muscle origin is strongly suspected but diagnosis is uncertain, electromyography (EMG), nerve conduction studies, or muscle MRI and consultation with neurology or rheumatology may be helpful. If hepatic injury cannot be excluded based on labs and imaging, hepatology referral and further evaluation (viral serologies, autoantibodies, metabolic tests) are appropriate.

Medication and toxin review
Many drugs and supplements can cause muscle injury (statins, fibrates, immune checkpoint inhibitors, antipsychotics) and hepatic injury (acetaminophen, methotrexate, isoniazid). Statins, in particular, pose a clinical challenge because they can cause asymptomatic CK elevations or rare myopathy; they can also cause mild transaminase elevations. If statins are used, simultaneous CK and transaminase monitoring when symptoms arise is essential, and guidelines recommend stopping statins in suspected statin-induced myopathy or rhabdomyolysis.

Practical algorithm
A pragmatic approach: in any patient with elevated transaminases, obtain CK and GGT. If CK is high and GGT normal, consider muscle etiology and investigate for myopathy. If GGT is elevated or bilirubin/INR abnormal, pursue hepatic evaluation with imaging and serologies. If results are mixed or if there is concern for both, refer to both hepatology and neuromuscular specialists and consider liver imaging and muscle studies concurrently.

Overlap syndromes and coexisting hepatic and muscle pathology

Conceptual overview
Overlap syndromes occur when patients have both primary liver disease and primary muscle disease, or when systemic diseases affect both tissues. Recognizing overlap is crucial because assuming a single etiology may delay appropriate diagnosis and management.

Examples and mechanisms
1. Metabolic and mitochondrial disorders: Certain inherited metabolic diseases (for example, glycogen storage diseases, mitochondrial cytopathies) can affect both liver and skeletal muscle, producing elevations of transaminases and CK, hypoglycemia, exercise intolerance, and multisystem features. These disorders may present in childhood but can also manifest in adults.

2. Autoimmune diseases: Autoimmune hepatitis can co-occur with inflammatory myopathies (dermatomyositis, polymyositis) as part of systemic autoimmune syndromes. Dermatomyositis is associated with characteristic skin findings and can have paraneoplastic associations that also affect the liver indirectly.

3. Drug-induced toxicity: Some systemic therapies cause concurrent hepatotoxicity and myotoxicity. For instance, certain antiretroviral therapies, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and chemotherapeutic agents can injure both tissues via immune-mediated mechanisms or toxic metabolic effects.

4. Metabolic syndrome: NAFLD and sarcopenia or myosteatosis can coexist as manifestations of the underlying metabolic milieu. In frail or elderly patients, the reduction in muscle mass and quality may intersect with fatty infiltration of the liver.

Clinical implications
When overlap is suspected, clinicians should expand evaluation beyond standard hepatocellular injury workup. Specific testing might include genetic/metabolic panels, autoantibody screening (ANA, anti–smooth muscle, myositis-specific antibodies), muscle biopsy, and liver biopsy if the pattern suggests independent hepatic pathology. Management may require addressing each disorder specifically (immunosuppression for autoimmune disease, enzyme replacement or metabolic therapy for inherited disorders), and treatment decisions must weigh potential cross-toxicity: for example, immunosuppressive regimens for myositis may influence hepatic disease activity or susceptibility to infection.

Case-seeking vigilance
Overlap states are often under-recognized because clinicians may attribute all laboratory abnormalities to the better-known condition (for example, ascribing ALT elevation to NAFLD in a patient with myositis). Persistently abnormal or discordant laboratory trends, atypical clinical features, or poor response to expected therapy should prompt re-evaluation for coexisting pathology.

Monitoring liver tests during systemic therapy and immunosuppression

Rationale
Many systemic therapies—antimicrobials, antineoplasics, immunomodulators, and biologic agents—have potential hepatotoxicity. Monitoring transaminases, cholestatic enzymes, and synthetic markers is essential to detect early drug-induced liver injury (DILI), minimize progression to liver failure, and guide therapy adjustments. Similarly, immunosuppression can predispose to reactivation of viral hepatitis and opportunistic infections affecting the liver.

Baseline evaluation
Before initiating potentially hepatotoxic therapy or immunosuppressive regimens, obtain baseline liver tests: ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, total bilirubin, albumin, INR, and viral hepatitis serologies (hepatitis B surface antigen, hepatitis B core antibody, hepatitis C antibody with reflex RNA if positive). Assess alcohol use, prior liver disease history, and medications/supplements. For therapies with known hepatotoxic risk, consider quantitative hepatitis B DNA for those with serologic evidence of prior exposure, as occult infection can reactivate with immunosuppression.

Monitoring frequency and thresholds
Monitoring schedules depend on the agent’s risk profile and patient comorbidities.

- Low-risk agents: Routine monitoring at baseline and periodically (for example, every 3–6 months) unless symptoms develop.
- Moderate-risk agents (eg, statins in certain contexts, some antibiotics): Monitor at baseline, 4–12 weeks after initiation, then periodically.
- High-risk agents (eg, methotrexate, isoniazid, certain chemotherapies, immune checkpoint inhibitors): More frequent monitoring is recommended—baseline, every 1–4 weeks during dose escalation or early therapy, then every 4–12 weeks depending on stability.

Action thresholds typically used:
- If ALT or AST rises to greater than 3× upper limit of normal (ULN) with symptoms or >5× ULN without symptoms, consider holding or stopping the drug and evaluating for DILI.
- For patients with baseline elevations, thresholds for action should be individualized; for example, an increase greater than 3× baseline or new worsening of bilirubin or INR warrants reassessment.
- Any elevation of bilirubin, especially when concurrent with transaminase elevation, is more ominous and may prompt immediate cessation.

Specific considerations with immunosuppression
- Hepatitis B reactivation: Patients who are hepatitis B surface antigen positive or hepatitis B core antibody positive are at risk of reactivation with immunosuppression. Prophylactic antiviral therapy (entecavir, tenofovir) is indicated in many settings (eg, rituximab-containing regimens), and close monitoring of HBV DNA is essential.
- Hepatitis C: Direct-acting antivirals are highly effective; timing of therapy relative to immunosuppression should be individualized.
- Immune-mediated hepatitis: Immune checkpoint inhibitors can trigger autoimmune-like hepatitis. Management often involves corticosteroids and, if severe, other immunosuppressants; early hepatology consultation is appropriate.
- Opportunistic infections: Immunosuppressed patients may develop fungal, viral, or mycobacterial infections affecting the liver; atypical presentations require high clinical suspicion.

Drug choice and dose adjustments
When transaminase elevations occur, clinicians balance the benefits of ongoing therapy against hepatic risk. For some drugs, dose reduction or temporary interruption with close monitoring may be acceptable; for others with high hepatotoxic potential or when evidence of progressive liver dysfunction exists, permanent discontinuation is required. Consultation with hepatology is advisable in moderate-to-severe cases.

Follow-up and long-term monitoring
After stopping a suspected hepatotoxic agent, follow liver tests until normalization or stabilization. If DILI resolved and therapy must be reintroduced for compelling reasons, re-challenge is usually avoided unless benefits outweigh risks and with close monitoring; alternative medications should be sought when possible. For patients on long-term immunosuppression, periodic reassessment of viral hepatitis status, vaccination update (hepatitis A and B immunization when appropriate), and counseling on hepatotoxic exposures (alcohol, recreational drugs, herbal supplements) are integral to care.

Conclusion

Differentiating hepatic from muscle causes of transaminase elevation and recognizing the clinical significance of hepatic steatosis are essential competencies for clinicians. Accurate interpretation rests on integrating enzyme patterns, CK and other ancillary tests, imaging, and clinical context. Noninvasive tools have transformed NAFLD assessment, reserving liver biopsy for targeted indications. In patients with myopathy, a structured approach—history, CK measurement, liver-specific markers, and targeted imaging or specialist referral—enables appropriate diagnosis. Awareness of overlap syndromes and careful monitoring during systemic therapy and immunosuppression prevents missed diagnoses and medication-related liver injury. With the rising prevalence of metabolic disease, clinicians must remain vigilant in evaluating and managing transaminase abnormalities to reduce long-term hepatic and extrahepatic morbidity.
