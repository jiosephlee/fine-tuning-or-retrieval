Introduction

Muscle biopsy remains a cornerstone diagnostic procedure in the evaluation of patients with suspected inflammatory myopathies and many other myopathic disorders. When interpreted in the context of clinical presentation, electromyography, laboratory tests including creatine kinase (CK) and disease-specific autoantibodies, and imaging, the histopathologic pattern observed on a skeletal muscle sample can distinguish among immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), polymyositis (PM), dermatomyositis (DM), inclusion body myositis (IBM) and metabolic or dystrophic processes. This chapter provides a practical, pathology-oriented discussion for the clinician and trainee: when and how to perform and handle a muscle biopsy, the key light microscopic features to assess, the role of special histochemical stains and immunohistochemistry, patterns that characterize major inflammatory myopathies, the meaning of complement deposition and macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis, and the limitations of muscle biopsy with guidance on integrating histology with clinical and serologic data.

# Indications, technique and sample handling for muscle biopsy

Indications
Muscle biopsy is indicated when there is diagnostic uncertainty after history, exam, CK and other labs, and when histology will alter management. Typical indications include: unexplained proximal or distal weakness, elevated CK (commonly >3 times the upper limit of normal; normal CK varies by lab but is generally ~20–200 U/L for adults), suspected inflammatory myopathy, suspected muscular dystrophy or mitochondrial disease, atypical neuropathies where a myopathic process is possible, and when EMG or MRI suggest focal or patchy disease. Biopsy may also be required to evaluate myopathies in which serologies are negative or non-diagnostic.

Technique
Open (surgical) biopsy and percutaneous needle biopsy are both used. Open biopsy yields larger tissue, facilitating multiple studies (fresh frozen, fixed for electron microscopy, formalin-fixed paraffin), and is preferred when an extensive panel of histochemistry and immunostains is anticipated. Needle biopsy (Bergstrom) is less invasive and acceptable when adequate tissue can be obtained and when experienced pathology services are available. The choice depends on local expertise, patient factors and the suspected diagnosis.

Selecting the biopsy site
Select an affected but not end-stage muscle. Muscles with mild-to-moderate weakness and recent changes on imaging (MRI STIR hyperintensity) or EMG abnormalities are ideal. Avoid very weak, profoundly fibrotic or fatty-replaced muscles (long-standing disease) and avoid muscles recently injected with steroids into the site. Commonly biopsied muscles include vastus lateralis, deltoid, biceps brachii and tibialis anterior; pattern of clinical weakness guides selection.

Sample handling
Proper handling is critical because many diagnostic stains require fresh frozen tissue. A typical protocol:
- Divide the sample immediately at bedside. One piece (optimally 50–100 mg, depending on lab needs) should be rapidly frozen for histochemistry and immunohistochemistry. Standard freezing uses isopentane cooled in liquid nitrogen to avoid freeze artifacts; snap-freezing directly into liquid nitrogen is discouraged due to cracking.
- Place the frozen tissue on cork or embedding medium and store at −80°C or transport on dry ice. Do not fix the portion intended for enzyme histochemistry or immunofluorescence.
- Place a second portion in 10% neutral buffered formalin for paraffin embedding and routine H&E, and a small piece (1–2 mm^3) in glutaraldehyde for electron microscopy if ultrastructural evaluation is needed.
- If immunofluorescence for complement or immunoglobulin deposition is planned, consider placing a small sample in Michel’s media or other transport medium specified by the laboratory.
- Communicate with the pathology laboratory before the procedure to confirm required sample sizes and fixation methods.

Documentation and clinical information
Provide a concise clinical summary: age, pattern of weakness, CK level and trend, current therapies (especially immunosuppressive agents or statins), recent infections or vaccinations, MRI/EMG findings, and relevant serologies (anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP, myositis-specific antibodies). This clinical context guides the pathologist’s panel of stains and interpretation.

Complications and follow-up
Muscle biopsy is low risk but can produce pain, hematoma, infection, or neuropathic injury if neurovascular structures are nearby. Ensure hemostasis and counsel patients about wound care. Recognize that pre-biopsy corticosteroids or immune therapy may modify histologic findings and decrease sensitivity; when feasible, obtain biopsy before initiating immunosuppression.

# Light microscopy features: necrosis, regeneration, fiber size variation, inflammation, perifascicular changes

The pathologist’s first step is assessment on routine H&E and various histochemical stains to characterize the pattern of muscle fiber injury. Several morphologic features have particular interpretive value.

Necrosis and myofiber degeneration
Necrotic fibers are pale, fragmented and often infiltrated by inflammatory cells and macrophages. Early necrosis may show loss of cross-striations and sarcoplasmic pallor; later stages show phagocytosis and removal. The presence, distribution and extent of necrosis help separate IMNM (prominent necrosis with relatively sparse lymphocytic inflammation) from other entities. Note that necrosis can be focal and patchy—sampling error is a concern.

Regeneration
Regenerating fibers are basophilic with centralized or internal nuclei and often increased expression of developmental myosin heavy chain (detectable with immunostains). Small angular fibers suggest chronic denervation, while centralized nuclei in groups suggest a dystrophic or regenerative process. The coexistence of extensive regeneration with necrosis favors active destructive myopathy.

Fiber size variation
Variation in myofiber diameter—atrophy and hypertrophy—is a hallmark of chronic muscle pathology. Grouped atrophy suggests denervation, while random atrophy is more typical of inflammatory myopathies. Marked fiber size variability with end-stage fibrofatty replacement suggests chronic dystrophy.

Inflammation: location and cell types
Characterize inflammatory infiltrates by their location: endomysial (between individual fibers), perimysial (in connective tissue septa), perivascular, or perimysial and perifascicular. In PM and IBM, endomysial inflammatory infiltrates composed predominantly of CD8+ T cells invade intact, non-necrotic fibers. In DM, inflammation is often perivascular and perimysial, with a predilection for perifascicular regions. IMNM shows necrosis with macrophage-rich myophagocytosis and comparatively sparse lymphocytic infiltrates. Quantify inflammatory burden and note whether inflammatory cells are in direct contact with non-necrotic fibers—this is a key differentiator.

Perifascicular changes
Perifascicular atrophy (atrophy confined to fibers at the fascicle periphery) is classic for dermatomyositis and correlates with microvascular injury and ischemia. Perifascicular necrosis can be seen in severe DM and some immune necrotizing myopathies. Distinguish perifascicular atrophy from focal neurogenic atrophy and from artifact.

Additional features
- Endomysial fibrosis and fatty infiltration indicate chronicity.
- Vacuoles, basophilic inclusions, and rimmed vacuoles suggest inclusion body myositis or certain dystrophic processes.
- Mitochondrial changes—ragged-red fibers (Gomori trichrome) and cytochrome c oxidase (COX)-negative fibers—indicate mitochondrial dysfunction and are particularly relevant to IBM and mitochondrial myopathies.
- Myofibrillar disorganization and cytoplasmic protein aggregates require special stains and immunohistochemistry (p62, TDP-43, ubiquitin).

# Special stains and immunohistochemistry: CD68 (macrophages), CD3 (T cells), MHC class I, acid phosphatase, membrane attack complex (C5b-9)

Special histochemical stains and immunohistochemistry extend the diagnostic yield of routine microscopy by identifying cell types, enzyme activities, mitochondrial function and complement or membrane antigen expression.

Histochemical stains
- Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) is the initial screen.
- Modified Gomori trichrome highlights ragged-red fibers and inclusion bodies.
- NADH-TR, succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), and cytochrome c oxidase (COX) assess oxidative enzyme activity and mitochondrial architecture; COX-negative fibers and subsarcolemmal aggregates are important in IBM and mitochondrial disorders.
- ATPase stains (pH preincubation) define fiber-type grouping and type I/II distribution, useful for detecting neurogenic change.
- Acid phosphatase (lysosomal phosphatase) is increased in areas of macrophage activity and in autophagic vacuoles; strong staining may accompany myophagocytosis and inflammatory lysosomal activity.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence
- CD68 (KP1) labels macrophages. In inflammatory myopathies, abundant CD68+ macrophages are seen in IMNM and in necrotic regions of other myopathies. The pattern—interstitial, endomysial, targeting necrotic fibers—helps interpretation.
- CD3 marks pan-T cells; CD8 and CD4 subsets identify cytotoxic versus helper T cells. In PM and IBM, CD8+ T cells are the dominant effector population and are often seen in close apposition to, and invading, non-necrotic muscle fibers that express MHC class I.
- MHC class I (HLA-ABC) expression on myofibers is normally low or absent; upregulation is an early and sensitive marker of immune-mediated myopathy. Diffuse sarcolemmal MHC class I expression is common in PM, IMNM and DM but may be highest in PM/IBM where direct cytotoxic T-cell interactions are present. Note that MHC class I can be upregulated non-specifically in many myopathies and after exercise or injury.
- Membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9) detection by immunofluorescence or IHC serves two key roles: capillary MAC deposition is characteristic of dermatomyositis (microangiopathy), whereas sarcolemmal MAC deposition on individual fibers may be seen in IMNM and other necrotizing processes and likely reflects complement-mediated myofiber injury.
- Acid phosphatase (histochemical) highlights lysosomal and phagolysosomal activity and augments identification of macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis.
- Additional immunostains: MxA (myxovirus resistance protein A) is a type I interferon–induced protein sensitive for DM; p62, TDP-43, and ubiquitin mark protein aggregates in IBM. Performing a panel tailored to the differential diagnosis increases sensitivity.

Interpretation
Combine patterns: a sample showing abundant CD68+ macrophages with large zones of necrosis but minimal CD3+/CD8+ invasion suggests IMNM. Endomysial CD8+ T-cell invasion of non-necrotic fibers with sarcolemmal MHC I upregulation favors PM or IBM; presence of rimmed vacuoles and protein aggregates favors IBM. Perifascicular MxA expression, capillary MAC deposition and perifascicular atrophy support DM.

# Patterns characteristic of IMNM vs polymyositis vs dermatomyositis vs inclusion body myositis

Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)
IMNM is defined by pronounced myofiber necrosis and regeneration with relatively scant lymphocytic inflammation. The prominent histology is extensive myofiber necrosis with abundant CD68+ macrophages engaged in myophagocytosis; necrotic fibers may be widely distributed. Lymphocytes, if present, are sparse and not typically invading intact fibers. MHC class I is often upregulated on sarcolemma and sarcoplasm. Complement deposition may be seen on the sarcolemma of necrotic fibers (C5b-9), though capillary MAC is not a defining feature. Clinical correlation with specific autoantibodies (anti–signal recognition particle [SRP], anti–3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl–coenzyme A reductase [HMGCR]) supports the diagnosis. Elevated CK levels (often markedly high) and the absence of perifascicular atrophy or perivascular inflammation help differentiate IMNM from DM.

Polymyositis (PM)
True PM is less common than historically thought and is characterized by endomysial inflammatory infiltrates composed predominantly of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells that invade non-necrotic muscle fibers. These invaded fibers typically express MHC class I, suggesting antigen presentation by myofibers. There is usually minimal perivascular or perifascicular distribution. Necrosis and regeneration can occur but are not the dominant feature. The diagnosis of PM requires careful exclusion of IBM, DM and IMNM; PM should be reserved for cases with classic endomysial CD8+ invasion without rimmed vacuoles or perifascicular changes.

Dermatomyositis (DM)
DM is a microangiopathy of muscle with characteristic perifascicular changes. Histology demonstrates perivascular and perimysial inflammation, complement-mediated injury to endomysial capillaries with MAC (C5b-9) deposition on capillary endothelium, and perifascicular atrophy (atrophy of fibers at the fascicular edges). MxA expression (marker of type I interferon activity) is typically upregulated in perifascicular regions. Endomysial inflammation is usually less prominent than in PM or IBM. Myofiber necrosis can be present but is frequently perifascicular. Skin findings and DM-specific autoantibodies can provide strong corroborative evidence.

Inclusion body myositis (IBM)
IBM typically affects older adults and has a distinctive mixed myopathic and neurogenic pattern. Histology shows endomysial inflammation with CD8+ T cells, but key distinguishing features are rimmed vacuoles (autophagic vacuoles with granular basophilic material on H&E, highlighted by Congo red or Gomori trichrome), intracytoplasmic protein aggregates that stain for p62, TDP-43, ubiquitin and amyloid-β, and mitochondrial abnormalities including COX-negative fibers. CD8+ cells often invade non-necrotic fibers, similar to PM, but the presence of rimmed vacuoles and protein inclusions is diagnostic for IBM. CK values are often only mildly to moderately elevated.

Summary comparison
- IMNM: prominent necrosis/regeneration, abundant macrophages (CD68+), sparse lymphocytes, MHC I upregulation, sarcolemmal complement possible, strong correlation with anti-SRP/anti-HMGCR antibodies.
- PM: endomysial CD8+ T-cell–mediated invasion of intact fibers, MHC I upregulation, minimal perifascicular or vascular changes.
- DM: perivascular/perimysial inflammation, perifascicular atrophy, capillary MAC deposition, MxA positivity.
- IBM: endomysial CD8+ T cells with invasion, rimmed vacuoles, protein aggregates (p62/TDP-43), mitochondrial COX-negative fibers, often refractory to immunotherapy.

# Interpretation of complement deposition and macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis

Complement deposition: significance and patterns
The membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9) is detected by immunofluorescence or IHC and informs pathophysiology. In dermatomyositis, MAC deposition on endomysial capillaries is a key pathogenic and diagnostic feature: complement activation leads to capillary injury, endothelium loss, ischemia, and subsequent perifascicular atrophy. Capillary MAC deposition correlates with microvascular compromise and the perifascicular pattern of fiber injury.

In contrast, sarcolemmal MAC deposition on the surface of individual myofibers suggests complement-mediated myofiber injury rather than primary microvascular disease. This pattern is seen in IMNM—where antibody-mediated complement activation can target muscle fibers leading to necrosis with subsequent macrophage recruitment—and in some necrotizing myopathies of other etiologies. Distinguishing capillary versus sarcolemmal MAC, and appreciating the distribution (diffuse, perifascicular, focal), assists in differentiating DM from IMNM and other necrotizing processes.

Macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis
Macrophages (CD68+) play a central role in clearance of necrotic fibers and in perpetuating inflammation. In IMNM, macrophages are often abundant, intermingled with necrotic fibers, and show marked acid phosphatase activity reflecting phagolysosomal activity. Macrophages may present as large sheets or focal aggregates and are typically located within necrotic zones rather than diffusely invading intact fibers. Their presence is a hallmark of ongoing necrosis and correlates clinically with elevated CK. However, macrophage infiltration can also be secondary to other forms of muscle injury (toxic, metabolic, dystrophic), so macrophage abundance must be interpreted in the clinical context.

Pathophysiologic implications
Complement deposition on capillaries implies an antibody-mediated microangiopathy (classically DM), whereas sarcolemmal MAC suggests complement activation directed at muscle fibers (as in some IMNM cases). Macrophage predominance without substantial T-cell infiltration favors humoral or complement-mediated mechanisms of injury and supports testing for associated autoantibodies (anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP). Conversely, prominent CD8+ T-cell–mediated fiber invasion points toward cell-mediated immunity (PM/IBM).

# Limitations of biopsy and correlation with clinical and serologic data

Sampling error and patchiness
Muscle disease can be patchy. A small biopsy may miss diagnostic lesions, especially perifascicular changes or focal necrosis. The probability of a representative biopsy increases with careful selection of a muscle showing recent abnormality on MRI or EMG.

Effect of therapy and disease stage
Immunosuppression started before biopsy can markedly diminish inflammatory infiltrates and obscure diagnostic features. Similarly, chronic or end-stage disease shows fibrosis and fatty replacement with reduced active inflammation, limiting diagnostic specificity. Early immune-mediated changes may primarily be molecular (MHC I upregulation) rather than morphologic.

Nonspecific findings
Some features—myofiber necrosis, macrophage infiltration, MHC class I upregulation—are not pathognomonic and may appear in toxic, metabolic, or dystrophic myopathies, as well as in systemic illness and after exercise. For example, statin-associated necrotizing myopathy (often with anti-HMGCR antibodies) can mimic IMNM histologically. Thus, histology must be integrated with medication history and serologies.

Interpretive overlap and evolving classifications
Entities such as PM, DM, IMNM and IBM overlap clinically and pathologically. The prevalence of PM as a diagnostic label has decreased as more precise clinicopathologic and serologic classifications have emerged (e.g., antisynthetase syndrome, IMNM). Recognize that no single histologic feature is diagnostic in isolation.

Integration with clinical and serologic data
A practical diagnostic approach synthesizes biopsy results with: pattern and distribution of weakness, CK levels and trend, EMG findings, MRI (edema vs fatty replacement), and serologies for myositis-specific and myositis-associated antibodies (e.g., anti-Jo-1, anti-Mi-2, anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR, antisynthetase antibodies). For example, a biopsy showing necrosis with macrophages and minimal lymphocytes strongly suggests IMNM; identification of anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP antibodies corroborates the diagnosis and guides therapy. Likewise, capillary MAC deposition and perifascicular atrophy plus compatible cutaneous findings and DM-specific antibodies support dermatomyositis.

Communication between clinician and pathologist
Because interpretation is context-dependent, detailed clinical information significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy. Discussing differential diagnoses and requesting specific stains (MHC class I, MAC, MxA, CD68/CD3/CD8, p62/ubiquitin) tailored to the clinical question improves yield and avoids unnecessary testing.

Conclusions

Muscle biopsy remains an indispensable tool in diagnosing inflammatory and necrotizing myopathies when properly performed and interpreted. Recognizing patterns—extensive necrosis with macrophages for IMNM, endomysial CD8+ invasion for PM, perifascicular atrophy and capillary MAC for DM, rimmed vacuoles and protein aggregates for IBM—allows clinicians to generate a focused differential diagnosis. Specialized histochemical and immunohistochemical stains (CD68, CD3/CD8, MHC class I, acid phosphatase, C5b-9, MxA, p62) elucidate underlying immune mechanisms. However, biopsy findings are neither fully sensitive nor specific in isolation; they must be integrated with clinical history, imaging, EMG and serologic tests to reach an accurate diagnosis and to guide management. Appropriate site selection, meticulous sample handling, and pre-procedure coordination with pathology are essential to maximize the diagnostic value of the biopsy.
