Chapter 5: Drug- and Toxin-Induced Myopathies and Rhabdomyolysis

Introduction

Drug- and toxin-induced muscle disorders form a clinically and pathophysiologically heterogeneous group of conditions that range from mild, transient myalgias to life‑threatening rhabdomyolysis with acute kidney injury. For clinicians and trainees, recognizing the spectrum of presentations, understanding the mechanisms by which pharmaceuticals and toxins damage muscle, and applying evidence‑based approaches to diagnosis and management are essential. This chapter reviews definitions and the clinical spectrum, the major pathogenic mechanisms, classic offending agents, features that help distinguish toxic (direct) muscle injury from immune‑mediated myopathy, acute management strategies for rhabdomyolysis and severe myopathy, and principles for rechallenge, causality assessment, and adverse‑event reporting.

# Definitions and spectrum: myalgias, myositis, necrotizing myopathy, rhabdomyolysis

Drug- and toxin‑related muscle disease can present along a continuum. Myalgias describe subjective muscle pain without necessary objective weakness or marked biochemical abnormalities. Patients with myalgias may have normal or mildly elevated muscle enzymes and typically retain normal strength on examination. Myalgia is a nonspecific symptom and may reflect mild inflammatory changes, metabolic intolerance of muscle, or peripheral neuropathic pain referred to muscles.

Myositis denotes active inflammation of muscle manifested clinically by proximal symmetric weakness, myopathic features on electrodiagnostic testing, inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy, and typically elevated serum creatine kinase (CK). In drug‑associated myositis the inflammatory infiltrate can be lymphocytic and sometimes indistinguishable from idiopathic inflammatory myopathies; it may be either directly drug triggered or represent an immune‑mediated process unmasked by medication exposure.

Necrotizing myopathy is a histopathologic pattern characterized by myofiber necrosis with minimal or absent inflammatory cell infiltrate. Clinically it presents with progressive weakness and markedly elevated CK. In the context of drugs, immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), such as statin‑associated anti‑HMGCR myopathy, is an important subset: despite drug discontinuation, muscle necrosis continues due to an autoantibody‑mediated process and often requires immunotherapy.

Rhabdomyolysis is the extreme end of the spectrum and refers to the breakdown of skeletal muscle with release of intracellular constituents—myoglobin, CK, potassium, phosphate, uric acid—into the circulation. Clinical features range from muscle pain, swelling and weakness to systemic complications: acute kidney injury (AKI) from myoglobin nephrotoxicity, electrolyte disturbances (notably hyperkalemia), disseminated intravascular coagulation, and compartment syndrome. Laboratory criteria for rhabdomyolysis are not universally standardized, but many clinicians use a CK elevation several times above the upper limit of normal (commonly CK > 5 times ULN, often CK > 1,000 U/L) in the appropriate clinical context as supportive of rhabdomyolysis. Typical normal CK reference ranges depend on lab and sex; approximate normal ranges are 30–200 U/L for men and 30–135 U/L for women. Myoglobinuria causing a positive urine dipstick for blood with few red cells on microscopy is a classic diagnostic clue.

# Mechanisms of drug-induced muscle injury: direct toxicity, immune-mediated mechanisms, metabolic/mitochondrial dysfunction

Understanding mechanisms helps rationalize differences in presentation, expected course after drug cessation, and therapeutic approaches.

Direct toxic injury occurs when drugs or metabolites exert structural or functional damage to myocytes. Some agents disrupt sarcolemma integrity causing increased permeability and leakage of intracellular contents; others impair excitation–contraction coupling or calcium homeostasis, producing sustained cytosolic calcium elevation that activates proteases and phospholipases leading to necrosis. Direct toxins include alcohol, heroin, cocaine, and certain statins at high concentrations in predisposed individuals. Amphotericin and certain chemotherapeutics can also cause direct muscle injury. Direct toxic injury often has a relatively acute onset related temporally to exposure and frequently improves after removal of the offending agent, provided irreversible damage has not occurred.

Immune‑mediated mechanisms involve drug‑induced dysregulation of immune tolerance or drug‑related modification of self‑antigens leading to an adaptive immune response against muscle. Drugs can act as haptens or modify muscle proteins, creating neoantigens that provoke T‑cell or B‑cell responses. Statins exemplify this: in rare cases, statin exposure induces an antibody response to 3‑hydroxy‑3‑methylglutaryl‑CoA reductase (HMGCR), leading to an antibody‑mediated necrotizing myopathy that persists despite statin withdrawal. Other medications (e.g., immune checkpoint inhibitors) can trigger polymyositis‑like syndromes or autoimmune myositis. Immune‑mediated myopathies typically have subacute to chronic progressive weakness, persist or worsen after drug discontinuation, demonstrate markedly elevated CK, and respond to immunosuppressive therapy rather than simple withdrawal.

Metabolic and mitochondrial dysfunction is another important category. Drugs that impair energy production, fatty acid oxidation, or mitochondrial respiratory chain function can render muscle unable to meet energy demands. Examples include fibrates with statins producing impaired mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, zidovudine causing mitochondrial DNA depletion, and valproic acid interfering with beta‑oxidation. In these cases, muscle may tolerate basal activity but becomes susceptible to injury with exertion, fever, or fasting. Histology often shows ragged‑red fibers or subsarcolemmal mitochondrial aggregates, and clinical features can include exercise intolerance, myalgias, and elevated CK. The onset may be gradual, and the severity correlates with cumulative mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic stressors.

Other contributory mechanisms include drug–drug interactions that raise systemic concentrations of myotoxic agents (pharmacokinetic), genetic predisposition (e.g., polymorphisms in SLCO1B1 increasing statin myopathy risk), and comorbid conditions such as hypothyroidism, renal impairment, or sepsis that increase susceptibility to muscle injury.

# Classic drug culprits: statins (and statin-associated immune-mediated necrotising myopathy), corticosteroids, antipsychotics, illicit drugs, statin plus interacting medications

Several agents and classes are notably associated with muscle toxicity.

Statins are the most commonly implicated prescription drugs for muscle complaints. Most statin‑related adverse effects are benign myalgias or self‑limited myopathy with modest CK elevations. Risk increases with higher statin dose, lipophilicity (simvastatin, atorvastatin more than pravastatin or rosuvastatin in some reports), older age, female sex, hypothyroidism, renal or hepatic dysfunction, and use of interacting drugs (notably strong CYP3A4 inhibitors and certain transport inhibitors). Statin‑associated immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (anti‑HMGCR myopathy) is rare but distinctive: patients develop progressive proximal weakness and very high CK (often > 3,000–10,000 U/L), autoantibodies to HMGCR are detectable in many cases, and weakness persists or worsens after statin withdrawal. Muscle biopsy characteristically shows widespread necrosis with minimal inflammation. Management requires immunosuppression.

Corticosteroids classically cause a steroid myopathy characterized by painless proximal muscle weakness with relative preservation of CK; the pathogenesis relates to steroid‑induced muscle protein catabolism, reduced synthesis of myosin, and type II fiber atrophy. Onset may be after weeks to months of systemic steroid therapy and is dose‑dependent; high‑dose or chronic use is more likely. In contrast to inflammatory myopathies, steroid myopathy shows low or normal CK, and muscle biopsy demonstrates selective type II fiber atrophy with minimal inflammation. Improvement occurs with tapering of steroids and rehabilitation.

Antipsychotics and other dopamine‑blockers can induce neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS), a syndrome of severe rigidity, hyperthermia, autonomic instability, and elevated CK that can culminate in rhabdomyolysis. NMS pathophysiology is complex and involves central dopamine blockade leading to sustained muscle rigidity and hypermetabolism; management is supportive with sedation, cooling, and in some cases dantrolene or bromocriptine.

Illicit drugs and alcohol are frequent causes of toxic rhabdomyolysis. Alcohol can cause direct myotoxicity and predispose to prolonged immobilization and seizures. Cocaine, amphetamines, and other sympathomimetics cause vasoconstriction and muscle ischemia as well as direct myocyte toxicity, raising risk of rhabdomyolysis. Opioid or sedative intoxication increases risk via prolonged immobilization and pressure‑induced ischemic injury.

Statin plus interacting medications merit special attention. Drugs that inhibit statin metabolism or hepatic uptake elevate statin plasma concentration and risk myotoxicity. Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g., certain macrolides such as clarithromycin, azole antifungals like itraconazole, protease inhibitors) raise levels of simvastatin, lovastatin, and atorvastatin. Inhibitors of the hepatic uptake transporter OATP1B1 (encoded by SLCO1B1), such as cyclosporine or gemfibrozil (which also impairs statin glucuronidation), markedly increase risk, with gemfibrozil plus cerivastatin famously causing fatal rhabdomyolysis historically. Clinically significant interactions are the combination of statins with fibrates, especially gemfibrozil, which can produce severe myotoxicity.

# Clinical features differentiating toxic rhabdomyolysis vs immune-mediated myopathy (time course, CK trends, response to drug withdrawal)

Distinguishing toxic (direct) muscle injury and rhabdomyolysis from immune‑mediated drug‑related myopathies is critical because management differs—withdrawal and supportive care is typically sufficient for toxic injury, whereas immune‑mediated disease often requires immunosuppression.

Time course: Toxic rhabdomyolysis often has an acute onset within hours to days of exposure, overdose, immobilization, strenuous exertion, or secondary systemic stressors (seizure, hyperthermia). The precipitant is usually evident, and symptoms evolve rapidly. In contrast, immune‑mediated myopathies related to drugs often have a subacute or insidious onset over weeks to months. For example, statin‑associated IMNM may develop weeks to years after exposure, and symptoms continue or progress after the statin is stopped.

CK trends: In toxic rhabdomyolysis CK levels typically rise rapidly, often reaching very high values (several thousand to > 100,000 U/L in extreme cases), then decline after cessation of the inciting factor and with supportive measures. The CK half‑life is approximately 1.5 days; thus, with cessation of injury, levels fall predictably. Conversely, immune‑mediated myopathies often show persistent or relapsing elevations in CK despite drug discontinuation. In IMNM, CK may be persistently elevated until immunotherapy is instituted.

Clinical response to drug withdrawal: Toxic myopathies and rhabdomyolysis commonly improve after removal of the offending agent combined with appropriate supportive care; muscle strength and CK typically recover. Immune‑mediated myopathies generally fail to remit with drug withdrawal alone; progressive weakness and ongoing necrosis are common, and immunosuppressive therapy is usually necessary to halt the immune process. Histologically, toxic rhabdomyolysis shows necrosis and breakdown, sometimes with regeneration, whereas immune‑mediated disease shows characteristic patterns—necrotizing myopathy with minimal inflammation for IMNM or lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates in classic polymyositis.

Other distinguishing features include systemic signs: NMS‑related rhabdomyolysis will be accompanied by fever, marked rigidity, and autonomic dysfunction, while immune myopathies are not typically hyperthermic unless there is superimposed infection. Electromyography (EMG) in toxic and inflammatory myopathies often shows myopathic motor unit potentials and fibrillation potentials, but the pattern is nonspecific. Muscle biopsy and serology (myositis‑specific antibodies such as anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) are most helpful in diagnosing immune causes.

# Management of rhabdomyolysis and acute drug-induced myopathy: hydration, electrolyte management, renal support

Initial management focuses on stabilization, preventing and treating complications (especially hyperkalemia and AKI), and addressing the underlying cause.

Early assessment: Measure serum CK, electrolytes (potassium, calcium, phosphate), renal function (BUN, creatinine), urinalysis for blood and myoglobinuria, and baseline ECG. Reassess serially. Look for precipitating factors (seizure, immobilization, heat stroke, trauma, known offending drugs) and discontinue any suspected myotoxin immediately.

Volume resuscitation: Aggressive intravenous fluid administration is the cornerstone of rhabdomyolysis management to maintain renal perfusion, dilute nephrotoxic pigments, and promote urinary excretion of myoglobin. Isotonic crystalloid (normal saline) is commonly used. While randomized controlled trial data are limited, practical recommendations often target an initial bolus of 1–2 L followed by infusion rates adjusted to achieve a urine output of approximately 200–300 mL/hour in adults (a frequently cited target is urine output > 0.5–1 mL/kg/hour for general AKI prevention, and some protocols use higher targets for rhabdomyolysis of 200–300 mL/hour). These are clinical targets and must be individualized for patients at risk of volume overload (e.g., heart failure). Close monitoring for pulmonary edema and volume status is necessary.

Urine alkalinization and diuretics: Urine alkalinization with intravenous sodium bicarbonate has been used to reduce myoglobin precipitation in renal tubules and mitigate free iron‑mediated oxidative injury. Recommendations are heterogeneous: it is often considered for patients with severe rhabdomyolysis (very high CK, dark urine, oliguria) though high‑quality evidence is lacking. Typical regimens use bicarbonate to achieve urine pH > 6.5. Mannitol has been proposed as an osmotic diuretic and free radical scavenger, but routine use is not universally endorsed. Diuretics should not be used as a substitute for adequate volume resuscitation.

Electrolyte management: Monitor and treat hyperkalemia promptly; hyperkalemia is the most immediately life‑threatening metabolic disturbance. Follow established protocols: intravenous calcium gluconate for membrane stabilization if ECG changes or severe hyperkalemia, insulin with glucose and inhaled or nebulized beta‑agonists to shift potassium intracellularly, and sodium bicarbonate if acidotic. Consider emergent renal replacement therapy (RRT) for refractory hyperkalemia, severe acidosis, or volume overload. Hypocalcemia is common early and typically corrects as muscle injury resolves; asymptomatic hypocalcemia is usually not treated acutely because correcting calcium can predispose to calcium deposition in injured muscle and worsen outcomes. Hyperphosphatemia may need to be controlled with phosphate binders in prolonged illness. Monitor uric acid; hyperuricemia can occur and contribute to AKI.

Renal support: Indications for dialysis include refractory hyperkalemia, severe metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.1) despite medical therapy, volume overload not responsive to diuretics, or established oliguric AKI with worsening uremia. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is frequently chosen for hemodynamically unstable patients; conventional dialysis clears small solutes but removes myoglobin variably. Intermittent hemodialysis does not reliably remove myoglobin because myoglobin is a medium molecular weight protein that tends to be protein bound, but modern high‑cutoff filters and hemoadsorption techniques may augment removal in selected centers.

Supportive care and monitoring: Provide analgesia, manage complications (compartment syndrome may require fasciotomy), and treat underlying causes (control hyperthermia, seizures, arrhythmias). Serial CK and renal function monitoring guide ongoing care. Physical therapy should be initiated early once the acute phase resolves to prevent deconditioning and facilitate recovery in nonnecrotic myopathies.

Management of immune‑mediated drug myopathy: If immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy is suspected (e.g., persistent weakness and high CK after statin cessation), initiate workup for myositis‑specific autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP), and consider muscle MRI and biopsy. Treatment is with immunosuppression—high‑dose corticosteroids often combined with steroid‑sparing agents such as methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, IVIG, or rituximab depending on severity and response. These therapies are guided by disease severity, comorbidities, and specialist consultation. Early recognition and institution of immunotherapy are important to prevent irreversible muscle loss.

# Principles of rechallenge, causality assessment and reporting adverse drug reactions

Decisions about rechallenging a drug that may have caused myopathy require careful risk–benefit analysis, consideration of alternative therapies, and patient preferences.

Rechallenge: In cases of mild, transient myalgias without significant CK elevation and minimal functional impairment, rechallenge at a lower dose or switching to a different agent within the same therapeutic class may be reasonable, particularly when the drug confers a clear clinical benefit (e.g., statins for secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease). For statin‑related myalgias, strategies include lowering dose, switching to a hydrophilic statin (pravastatin, rosuvastatin), alternate day dosing, or use of non‑statin lipid‑lowering agents (ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors) if intolerance persists. Rechallenge should be deferred or approached cautiously if there is a history of severe myopathy, CK > 10 times ULN, rhabdomyolysis, or documented immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy; in IMNM, rechallenge with the same statin is contraindicated because the immune process targets HMGCR. When rechallenge is attempted, it should occur after biochemical and clinical recovery with close monitoring of symptoms and CK.

Causality assessment: Several structured tools assist in assessing the likelihood that a drug caused an adverse reaction. The Naranjo algorithm is a commonly used questionnaire that assigns a probability score (definite, probable, possible, or doubtful) based on temporal relation, dechallenge and rechallenge, alternative causes, and objective evidence. The World Health Organization–Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO‑UMC) system is another standardized method. Causality assessment must integrate clinical judgment: temporality (onset after exposure), biologic plausibility, dose‑response relationship, dechallenge (improvement after withdrawal), rechallenge (recurrence on re‑exposure), exclusion of other causes (metabolic, infectious, traumatic), and objective evidence (serologic antibodies, biopsy findings).

Reporting adverse drug reactions: Suspected drug‑induced myopathies and rhabdomyolysis should be reported to local pharmacovigilance authorities and national programs (e.g., FDA MedWatch in the United States, Yellow Card Scheme in the U.K.). Reporting serves public health surveillance, helps detect rare but serious adverse effects, and can lead to evidence‑based labeling changes. Reports should include detailed clinical information: timing of drug exposure, dose, concomitant medications, laboratory data (CK, electrolytes, renal function), diagnostic studies (EMG, biopsy), management provided, and outcome. When immune‑mediated myopathy is suspected, specialized tests (myositis antibodies) and biopsy results strengthen reports.

Informed consent and patient counseling are key. Patients should be informed about the signs and symptoms that warrant urgent reassessment (severe muscle pain, profound weakness, dark urine, palpitations or syncope suggesting hyperkalemia) and about safer alternative therapies when available.

Conclusion

Drug‑ and toxin‑induced muscle disorders span a broad clinical and pathophysiologic spectrum. Distinguishing direct toxic injury and rhabdomyolysis from immune‑mediated myopathies guides appropriate management: immediate withdrawal and supportive care for toxic injury versus immunosuppression for immune‑mediated processes. Recognition of classic culprits, appreciation of pharmacokinetic interactions (especially with statins), and early, targeted interventions to prevent or treat complications such as hyperkalemia and AKI are essential. Systematic causality assessment, cautious approaches to rechallenge, and timely reporting of adverse events help optimize patient safety and inform public health.
