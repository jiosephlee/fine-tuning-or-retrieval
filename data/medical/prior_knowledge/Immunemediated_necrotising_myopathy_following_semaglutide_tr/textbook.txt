Introduction

Skeletal muscle is a specialized tissue optimized for force generation, movement, posture and metabolic homeostasis. Understanding the organization of muscle from the whole-organ level down to molecular motors is essential for interpreting clinical presentations of weakness, myalgias, exercise intolerance and laboratory abnormalities such as elevated muscle enzymes. This chapter reviews the gross anatomy and functional distribution of skeletal muscle, the cellular basis of contractile function including sarcomere structure and excitation–contraction coupling, the types of muscle fibers and their physiologic trade-offs, membrane integrity and repair processes, the energetics and mitochondrial role in muscle metabolism, the cellular program of regeneration after injury, and common physiologic reasons for release of intracellular muscle enzymes into blood. Throughout, emphasis is placed on pathophysiologic mechanisms that link structural and biochemical defects to clinical disease.

# Gross anatomy of skeletal muscle (muscle groups, fiber distribution, proximal vs distal muscles)

Skeletal muscles are organized into discrete muscles composed of bundles of muscle fibers (fascicles) enclosed by connective tissue layers that permit force transmission and vascular/nerve supply. At the macroscopic level, muscles are grouped by location and function: axial muscles (posture and trunk movement), proximal limb muscles (shoulder girdle, hip girdle; important for gross movements and load-bearing), and distal limb muscles (forearm, hand, lower leg and foot; responsible for fine motor control and rapid, repeated contractions). Clinically, patterns of weakness that preferentially affect proximal versus distal muscles help localize disease: many myopathies (inflammatory, genetic metabolic) cause predominant proximal weakness (eg, shoulder and pelvic girdle), whereas neuropathic processes classically produce distal, length-dependent weakness and atrophy.

Individual muscles are composed of motor units — a single alpha motor neuron and all the muscle fibers it innervates. Motor unit size varies according to function: muscles requiring fine control (extraocular muscles, intrinsic hand muscles) have small motor units with few fibers per neuron; antigravity or force muscles have large motor units innervating many fibers. Fiber distribution within a muscle may be heterogeneous: pennate muscles have fibers oriented obliquely to the force-generating axis, increasing fiber packing and force per unit volume, while parallel-fibered muscles favor excursion and velocity. Tendinous insertions and aponeuroses transmit the summed contractile force to bone.

Muscle vascular supply and innervation reflect metabolic demands. High-capillary-density muscles (e.g., postural muscles) are enriched in oxidative fibers and mitochondria. Lymphatic drainage and interstitial connective tissue architecture influence edema and inflammatory cell trafficking in disease. Clinically relevant gross patterns include scapular winging from trapezius or serratus anterior dysfunction, foot drop from weakness of tibialis anterior, and calf hypertrophy or pseudohypertrophy in certain muscular dystrophies.

# Muscle fiber types (type I vs type II) and functional implications

Skeletal muscle fibers are commonly categorized into type I (slow-twitch, oxidative) and type II (fast-twitch) subtypes, with further sub-classification of type II into IIa (fast oxidative-glycolytic) and IIb/IIx (fast glycolytic) depending on species. Type I fibers express myosin heavy chain isoforms that hydrolyze ATP more slowly and possess abundant mitochondria, dense capillary networks and high myoglobin content; they are adapted for sustained, low-level contractions and resistance to fatigue. Type II fibers generate greater peak power and faster contraction velocities due to isoforms with higher ATPase activity, but type IIx fibers fatigue rapidly because they rely more on anaerobic glycolysis and have fewer mitochondria.

The functional implications of fiber-type composition are manifold. Endurance-trained muscles exhibit a relative increase in type I and IIa fibers, mitochondrial biogenesis, and enhanced capillarity; conversely, strength training and disuse produce hypertrophy of type II fibers. Certain myopathies and neuropathies produce selective fiber-type involvement or fiber-type grouping on histology: denervation followed by reinnervation tends to produce grouping of fiber types as one motor unit's fibers adopt the phenotype of the reinnervating neuron. Metabolic myopathies may disproportionately affect oxidative fibers, while channelopathies and congenital myopathies can have variable fiber-type distributions.

Biochemically, fiber type determines substrate utilization: oxidative fibers preferentially oxidize fatty acids and utilize the tricarboxylic acid cycle and electron transport chain for ATP production, whereas glycolytic fibers rely heavily on glycogen and anaerobic glycolysis for rapid ATP generation. These differences underlie clinical variability in exercise intolerance — for example, patients with defects in fatty acid oxidation manifest symptoms during prolonged, low-intensity exercise that relies on lipid metabolism, whereas glycogen storage disorders produce premature fatigue during high-intensity short-duration exercise.

# Sarcomere structure, excitation–contraction coupling and neuromuscular junction

The sarcomere is the elementary contractile unit of striated muscle, delimited by Z-disks with interdigitating thick filaments (myosin) in the A-band and thin filaments (actin, troponin, tropomyosin) in the I-band. Sarcomere shortening results from relative sliding of thin filaments past thick filaments via cyclic interactions of myosin heads with actin, powered by ATP hydrolysis in the cross-bridge cycle. Key structural proteins include titin (a giant spring-like molecule that provides passive elasticity and sarcomeric alignment), nebulin (regulates thin filament length), and dystrophin (links the cytoskeleton to the extracellular matrix via the dystrophin–glycoprotein complex). Mutations in these structural proteins underlie many inherited myopathies and dystrophies.

Excitation–contraction (E–C) coupling converts an action potential arriving at the muscle fiber membrane into Ca2+ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), thereby triggering contraction. The process begins at the neuromuscular junction (NMJ), where an alpha motor neuron releases acetylcholine (ACh) into the synaptic cleft. ACh binds postsynaptic nicotinic receptors on the motor endplate, generating an endplate potential that, if above threshold, triggers action potentials propagated along the sarcolemma and into the transverse (T)-tubule system. Voltage-sensing dihydropyridine receptors (DHPRs; L-type Ca2+ channels) in the T-tubule membrane mechanically couple to ryanodine receptor type 1 (RyR1) channels in the adjacent SR membrane, causing massive release of Ca2+ into the cytosol. Ca2+ binds troponin C on thin filaments, inducing conformational changes that displace tropomyosin and allow myosin heads to bind actin, commencing the cross-bridge cycle.

Clinical disorders can affect any step: autoimmune antibodies against postsynaptic ACh receptors cause myasthenia gravis with fatigable weakness; presynaptic defects such as botulinum toxin inhibit ACh release; congenital myasthenic syndromes affect synaptic proteins; mutations in RyR1 produce malignant hyperthermia susceptibility and certain congenital myopathies; defects in structural proteins like dystrophin compromise the mechanical stability of sarcolemma and the sarcomere. Electrophysiologic testing (e.g., repetitive nerve stimulation, single-fiber EMG) assesses NMJ function, while muscle biopsy and genetic testing can identify structural and molecular abnormalities.

# Muscle cell membrane integrity and repair mechanisms

The sarcolemma and its associated extracellular matrix interactions maintain the physical barrier that preserves ionic gradients and prevents leakage of intracellular proteins. The dystrophin–glycoprotein complex (DGC) links the actin cytoskeleton to the extracellular matrix; dystrophin provides mechanical stabilization during contraction. Loss or dysfunction of dystrophin destabilizes the sarcolemma leading to increased susceptibility to contraction-induced microtears, elevated membrane permeability, and progressive myofiber degeneration, as seen in Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophies.

Membrane repair is an active, rapid process necessary to reseal disruptions caused by mechanical stress. Critical molecular players include dysferlin, mitsugumin 53 (MG53; TRIM72), annexins, and a localized Ca2+-dependent vesicle-trafficking response that recruits intracellular vesicles to the injury site to patch the membrane. Dysferlin deficiency (as in limb-girdle muscular dystrophy type 2B/ dysferlinopathy) impairs this resealing mechanism and leads to chronic cycles of necrosis and inadequate repair. Similarly, defects in other repair proteins compromise the ability of fibers to restore membrane integrity, leading to chronic inflammation and fibrosis.

Beyond mechanical injury, the sarcolemma contains ion channels and transporters (e.g., Na+/K+ ATPase, voltage-gated sodium and calcium channels) whose dysfunction can alter membrane excitability and secondarily damage fibers. For example, persistent membrane depolarization due to channelopathies can increase intracellular Na+ and Ca2+, activating proteases and mitochondrial dysfunction. Clinically, loss of membrane integrity manifests as leakage of intracellular enzymes such as creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and myoglobin into the bloodstream and urine; this has implications for diagnosis (serum enzyme measurement) and acute complications (myoglobinuria causing acute kidney injury).

# Skeletal muscle metabolism: energy substrates, mitochondrial function, anaerobic vs aerobic metabolism

Muscle contraction demands rapid and flexible ATP supply. Three major systems provide ATP: the phosphagen system (creatine phosphate and creatine kinase), anaerobic glycolysis, and aerobic oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria. At the onset of sudden, intense exercise (e.g., sprinting), preformed creatine phosphate buffers ATP via the reaction creatine-P + ADP → creatine + ATP (catalyzed by creatine kinase), allowing high-power output for a few seconds. Glycolysis then predominates for high-intensity short-duration efforts, generating ATP rapidly but producing lactate when pyruvate is reduced to lactate to regenerate NAD+. For prolonged, lower-intensity exercise, oxidative metabolism of glucose and fatty acids in mitochondria supplies the majority of ATP through the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and electron transport chain.

Mitochondria are central not only for ATP production but also for intermediate metabolism and regulation of apoptosis and reactive oxygen species (ROS). Their density correlates with oxidative capacity; type I fibers have abundant mitochondria and enzymes of beta-oxidation and the TCA cycle. Disorders of mitochondrial DNA or nuclear-encoded mitochondrial proteins impair oxidative phosphorylation, presenting as exercise intolerance, lactic acidosis, and multisystem disease depending on tissue distribution and heteroplasmy. Laboratory markers of mitochondrial dysfunction include elevated lactate and pyruvate at rest or after exercise, and abnormal lactate/pyruvate ratios in some contexts (normal lactate ~0.5–2.2 mmol/L; normal pyruvate ~30–125 µmol/L, though ranges depend on assay and lab).

Substrate selection shifts with exercise intensity and duration: initially muscle glycogen and intramuscular triglycerides supply energy; as exercise continues, hepatic glucose production, adipose lipolysis and muscle fatty acid uptake sustain ATP production. Disorders of glycogen metabolism (glycogen storage diseases) produce impaired high-intensity exercise tolerance with cramps and myoglobinuria, whereas defects in fatty acid oxidation (e.g., carnitine palmitoyltransferase II deficiency) lead to symptoms during prolonged exertion or fasting.

Clinically relevant metabolic measurements include serum lactate (normal approximately 0.5–2.2 mmol/L) and creatine kinase (CK). CK isoforms are tissue-specific; CK-MM predominates in skeletal muscle. Fatigue patterns, onset of symptoms with specific exercise types, and laboratory values guide diagnostic consideration of metabolic myopathies versus structural or inflammatory myopathies.

# Regeneration and repair: satellite cells, myofibre necrosis and regeneration

Skeletal muscle has a robust but finite regenerative capacity mediated primarily by satellite cells, quiescent myogenic progenitors located between the sarcolemma and basal lamina. Satellite cells express the transcription factor Pax7 in the quiescent state. Following muscle injury or necrosis, satellite cells are activated, proliferate, and differentiate under the control of myogenic regulatory factors (MRFs) such as MyoD, Myf5, myogenin and MRF4, fusing to form new myotubes and restoring myofiber architecture. The basal lamina provides a scaffold guiding regeneration and facilitating satellite cell progeny to reconstitute the fiber; preservation of the basal lamina correlates with more complete regeneration.

The regenerative process is tightly coordinated with an inflammatory response. Acute injury triggers a biphasic macrophage response: early pro-inflammatory (M1-like) macrophages clear necrotic debris and stimulate satellite cell proliferation, while later anti-inflammatory (M2-like) macrophages promote differentiation and tissue remodeling. Dysregulated inflammation, persistent necrosis, or loss of basal lamina disrupts effective regeneration and predisposes to fibro-adipose replacement and contracture. Chronic cycles of degeneration and regeneration, as seen in dystrophinopathies, eventually exhaust the satellite cell pool and lead to progressive weakness and fibrosis.

Histologically, successful regeneration is marked by centrally located nuclei in newly regenerated fibers and restoration of fiber size. Pathologic patterns—such as fibers with variable sizes, internal nuclei, endomysial fibrosis, and fatty infiltration—reflect chronicity or repeated injury. Therapeutic strategies in muscle disease (gene therapy, cell-based therapies, anti-inflammatory modulation) aim to restore the satellite cell niche, enhance regenerative capacity, or prevent ongoing injury that exceeds regenerative potential.

# Physiologic causes of muscle enzyme release

Skeletal muscle contains several intracellular enzymes that leak into serum when the sarcolemma is disrupted, including creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), aldolase, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and myoglobin. Understanding physiologic causes of enzyme elevation helps to distinguish benign from pathologic states.

Exercise is the most common physiologic cause of transient CK elevation. Intense or unaccustomed exercise, particularly eccentric exercise (lengthening contractions), causes microinjury and increases CK; levels typically rise within 24 hours, peak at 24–72 hours, and normalize over days to weeks. The magnitude of rise depends on exercise intensity, muscle mass involved, and individual susceptibility; values may reach several-fold above normal. Normal serum CK reference ranges vary by sex, race and laboratory, but common adult upper limits are approximately 150–200 U/L for women and 200–300 U/L for men (laboratory-specific ranges should be used). Markedly elevated CK (e.g., >5,000 U/L) raises concern for rhabdomyolysis, particularly if accompanied by myoglobinuria, electrolyte abnormalities, or acute kidney injury. In practical terms, CK elevations less than 3–5 times the upper limit of normal after strenuous exercise are often physiologic, whereas sustained or very high levels warrant evaluation.

Other benign causes include intramuscular injections, vigorous manual labor, and recent intramuscular vaccinations. Seizures, particularly generalized tonic–clonic seizures, can also cause significant CK elevation. Minor elevations of aminotransferases (AST, ALT) can accompany muscle injury because AST is abundant in muscle and can be mistaken for hepatic injury; comparing CK levels and other liver-specific tests helps localize the source.

Medications and toxins may increase muscle enzyme release physiologically by causing subclinical muscle injury. Statins commonly cause myalgias and small CK elevations; true statin-associated myopathy (with substantial CK elevations) is less common but more concerning. Alcohol intoxication and withdrawal can cause muscle injury with elevated CK. Heat stroke and exertional heat illness cause muscle breakdown and enzyme release.

Clinically, the decision points revolve around the degree and time course of enzyme elevation, presence of symptoms (weakness, myalgia), and systemic consequences. A single moderate CK elevation in an asymptomatic person after exercise can be observed without extensive workup; persistent elevations, progressive weakness, or levels associated with dark urine, hyperkalemia, or renal impairment require urgent evaluation and management for rhabdomyolysis and investigation for underlying myopathy.

Summary

A mechanistic understanding of skeletal muscle anatomy, fiber types, sarcomeric and membrane structures, excitation–contraction coupling, metabolic pathways and regenerative biology is essential for interpreting the clinical manifestations of muscle disease. The interplay between mechanical stress, membrane integrity, mitochondrial energy production and the capacity for regeneration determines whether muscle injury is transient and reparable or progressive and disabling. Clinicians integrate history (pattern of weakness, relation to activity), examination (distribution of weakness), laboratory studies (CK, lactate), electrophysiology, imaging and pathology to localize disease processes and identify underlying molecular defects that guide diagnosis and therapy.

Introduction

Accurate interpretation of laboratory markers is essential for distinguishing muscle injury from hepatic disease, for gauging severity of muscle disease, and for guiding management of conditions such as inflammatory myopathies and rhabdomyolysis. Many enzymes and proteins used as clinical biomarkers—creatine kinase (CK), aminotransferases (AST and ALT), aldolase, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), myoglobin, and cardiac biomarkers—are not uniquely produced by one tissue. Their kinetics (time to rise and fall, half-life, and patterns of isoenzymes) and the clinical context determine diagnostic specificity. This chapter reviews the biochemical characteristics, pathophysiology, laboratory behavior, and clinical interpretation of these markers, emphasizing common pitfalls and monitoring strategies during treatment.

# Creatine kinase (CK): isoenzymes, kinetics, correlation with muscle injury severity

Creatine kinase (CK) is the most widely used laboratory marker for skeletal muscle injury. CK catalyzes the reversible transfer of a phosphate group between creatine and ATP, an energy-buffering reaction important in tissues with high and fluctuating energy requirements (skeletal muscle, heart, brain). Total CK (often reported as CK activity in U/L) is the standard clinical assay, but clinically important information comes from CK isoenzymes and temporal dynamics.

Isoenzymes and tissue distribution
CK exists as three major isoenzyme dimers composed of B (brain-type) and M (muscle-type) subunits: CK-BB (CK-1), CK-MB (CK-2), and CK-MM (CK-3). CK-MM predominates in skeletal muscle (and constitutes the majority of total CK in healthy individuals), CK-MB is abundant in cardiac muscle (though CK-MB also exists in skeletal muscle and is released with large skeletal injuries), and CK-BB is primarily in brain, lung, and smooth muscle. Modern clinical practice rarely uses CK isoenzyme electrophoresis, but CK-MB fraction historically aided myocardial infarction diagnosis; troponins have largely replaced CK-MB for cardiac specificity. When interpreting CK in muscle disease, recognizing CK-MM predominance helps attribute elevations to skeletal muscle rather than hepatic or cardiac sources.

Kinetics of rise and fall
After acute muscle injury, CK typically begins to rise within 2–12 hours, peaks between 24–72 hours, and then declines over days, with an estimated plasma half-life of roughly 36 hours (range varies). The peak magnitude depends on extent and timing of injury relative to sampling. Persistent or relapsing muscle necrosis (ongoing trauma, compartment syndrome, inflammatory myopathy) results in sustained or rising CK rather than a single transient peak. CK can be chronically mildly elevated in many neuromuscular disorders without acute necrosis—e.g., Becker muscular dystrophy, limb-girdle muscular dystrophies, and some metabolic myopathies.

Correlation with severity
CK magnitude correlates imperfectly with severity. In general, higher CK levels reflect greater muscle membrane disruption and necrosis, but absolute CK values depend on muscle mass, recent exertion, race (higher baseline CK in individuals of African descent), intramuscular injections, seizures, and medications (e.g., statins). Rhabdomyolysis is often defined clinically and biochemically, commonly using thresholds such as CK >5 × upper limit of normal (ULN) or more conservatively CK >1,000–5,000 U/L as evidence of significant muscle necrosis. Severe rhabdomyolysis associated with risk of acute kidney injury (AKI) is frequently seen with CK in the thousands to >10,000 U/L. Nonetheless, AKI can occur with more modest CK elevations when accompanied by dehydration, acidosis, or nephrotoxins, and some patients with very high CK never develop AKI. Thus CK should be interpreted alongside clinical status, renal function, urine findings, and electrolytes.

Pitfalls
Because CK is not tissue-specific, clinicians must consider non-muscle contributions to elevations. Intramuscular injections, vigorous exercise, and small membrane injuries raise CK. Also, laboratory reference ranges vary; many labs report CK ULN for men in the hundreds of U/L and for women lower. Always compare to the laboratory’s reference. When differentiating hepatic from muscular sources of AST/ALT elevations, a markedly elevated CK supports a muscle source.

# AST and ALT: hepatic vs muscle sources; patterns of elevation

Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) are aminotransferase enzymes widely used to detect hepatocellular injury, but their tissue distribution and isoenzyme forms create diagnostic ambiguity when muscle disease is present.

Biochemistry and tissue sources
ALT is more liver-specific than AST because it is present predominantly in hepatocytes and in lesser amounts in skeletal muscle. Normal ALT reference ranges differ by lab, but commonly are approximately 7–56 U/L. AST is found both in the cytosol and mitochondria of hepatocytes and is also abundant in skeletal and cardiac muscle, red blood cells, kidney, brain, and other tissues. Typical AST reference range is roughly 10–40 U/L. Because AST is not liver-specific, elevation of AST out of proportion to ALT may suggest a non-hepatic source or specific hepatic pathologies involving mitochondrial injury.

Patterns of elevation
Patterns of aminotransferase elevation provide clues:

- Predominant ALT elevation (ALT > AST) typically indicates hepatocellular injury, with ALT generally higher than AST in viral hepatitis, autoimmune hepatitis, and toxic liver injury due to various agents. The magnitude of elevation—mild (up to 5× ULN), moderate (5–15× ULN), or severe (>15× ULN)—helps narrow differential diagnoses.
- AST > ALT may occur in alcoholic liver disease (due to mitochondrial injury and pyridoxine deficiency) where AST:ALT ratio often exceeds 2:1. However, AST elevation out of proportion to ALT also occurs with muscle injury because of high AST content in muscle. In severe rhabdomyolysis, AST can be markedly elevated (sometimes into the thousands) with smaller or absent ALT elevation.
- Mild aminotransferase elevations (e.g., <3× ULN) are nonspecific and common. When aminotransferases are elevated in the setting of elevated CK, a muscular source should be strongly considered.

Discriminating hepatic from muscular sources
Some practical approaches:

- Correlate with CK. A high CK concurrent with elevated AST (and perhaps ALT) points to muscle as the likely source. For example, AST markedly elevated with CK in the thousands and only mild ALT elevation suggests skeletal muscle release.
- Look at the broader hepatic panel. If other liver markers—bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), coagulation tests (INR)—are normal, an isolated AST/ALT rise favors extrahepatic origin. GGT and ALP are more specific for cholestatic or biliary pathology.
- Consider AST isoforms and mitochondrial AST: mitochondrial AST increases in severe hepatic mitochondrial injury but is not routinely measured.
- Clinical context: muscle pain, weakness, trauma, statin use, seizures, or exertion favor muscle injury; jaundice, hepatomegaly, risk factors for viral hepatitis, hepatotoxic medications, and cholestatic signs favor hepatic involvement.

Pitfalls
Relying solely on AST:ALT ratio can be misleading. Also, muscle disease may produce mild ALT elevations because skeletal muscle contains some ALT, and in certain chronic myopathies ALT may be persistently mildly elevated. Serial measurements and other markers (CK, bilirubin, GGT, INR) are essential for proper attribution.

# Other muscle-associated labs: aldolase, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), myoglobin

Beyond CK and aminotransferases, other enzymes and proteins provide complementary information about muscle injury.

Aldolase
Aldolase (fructose-bisphosphate aldolase) is a glycolytic enzyme present in skeletal muscle. Historically used in myositis diagnosis, serum aldolase increases with muscle damage and is particularly useful in some inflammatory myopathies (e.g., polymyositis, dermatomyositis) where it may be elevated even when CK is normal or minimally raised. Typical reference range is laboratory-dependent; many report aldolase <7.6 U/L as normal. Aldolase lacks specificity—hepatic disease and hemolysis can raise levels—and is less commonly used now that CK and more specific immune and imaging tests are available. However, it remains a useful adjunct when CK behavior is atypical.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)
LDH catalyzes the interconversion of pyruvate and lactate and exists as five isoenzymes (LDH-1 through LDH-5) with differing tissue distributions: LDH-1 (heart, red cells), LDH-5 (liver, skeletal muscle). Total LDH rises in both hepatic and muscular injury, and isoenzyme fractionation can help distinguish sources: a relative increase in LDH-5 supports skeletal muscle or hepatic origin, while LDH-1 predominance suggests cardiac or erythrocyte sources. Total LDH reference ranges commonly fall around 140–280 U/L. LDH rises later than CK in muscle injury and tends to remain elevated longer; thus LDH is useful when sampling is delayed or for detecting subacute or chronic muscle disease.

Myoglobin
Myoglobin is a small heme-containing protein that binds oxygen in muscle. It is rapidly released into plasma after muscle injury and is freely filtered by the glomerulus. Serum myoglobin rises within 1–3 hours of muscle injury and peaks much earlier than CK, often within 6–12 hours, but it also clears quickly (plasma half-life roughly 2–3 hours). Urine myoglobin (myoglobinuria) results in dark, tea- or cola-colored urine and gives a positive "blood" on urine dipstick without red blood cells on microscopy because the dipstick detects heme. Myoglobin is nephrotoxic under conditions of hypovolemia and acidic urine and contributes to AKI in rhabdomyolysis. Measurement of serum myoglobin can be useful for early detection of muscle injury but is less commonly ordered because of its brief elevation window and limited correlation with outcomes; moreover, tests may not be available in all hospitals.

Integration
These enzymes and proteins are best interpreted together and with the clinical picture. For example, in suspected inflammatory myopathy, CK and aldolase complement each other; if CK normal but aldolase elevated, consider certain myositis subtypes. In rhabdomyolysis, rapid elevations of myoglobin and CK with LDH and AST rises support acute muscle necrosis. Use isoenzyme analysis selectively when tissue attribution is ambiguous.

# Cardiac biomarkers in myopathy: troponin (cardiac vs skeletal isoforms) and natriuretic peptides (BNP, proBNP)

Muscle disease may involve the heart (cardiomyopathy, conduction disease) or produce laboratory confounders for cardiac biomarker interpretation. Understanding troponin isoforms and natriuretic peptides is critical when evaluating cardiac involvement in systemic myopathies.

Troponin biology and isoforms
Cardiac troponins (cTnI and cTnT) are regulatory proteins specific to the cardiac myocyte contractile apparatus and are the preferred biomarkers for myocardial injury. High-sensitivity cardiac troponin assays detect very low concentrations and are widely used for diagnosis of acute coronary syndromes. Reference values vary by assay and lab; many high-sensitivity troponin I assays use 99th percentile cutoffs around 0.01–0.04 ng/mL, and troponin T assays similarly have assay-specific cutoffs. Important considerations:

- Specificity: cTnI is encoded by genes expressed exclusively in cardiac tissue; cTnI assays are generally highly specific for cardiac muscle. cTnT, however, while considered cardiac-specific, can be falsely elevated in some neuromuscular disorders because regenerating skeletal muscle may express isoforms that cross-react with certain troponin T assays. This leads to confusing elevations of cTnT in conditions such as inflammatory myopathies and muscular dystrophies, despite absence of cardiac ischemia.
- Kinetics: After acute myocardial injury, troponin levels rise within 3–6 hours, peak at ~12–24 hours, and may remain elevated for 7–10 days (or longer with high-sensitivity assays), reflecting ongoing intracellular troponin release and slow clearance.
- Clinical approach: In a patient with elevated troponin and known myopathy, use cTnI when available to increase cardiac specificity; interpret troponin rises in the context of ECG changes, cardiac imaging (echocardiography, MRI), and natriuretic peptide levels. Any troponin elevation warrants consideration of myocardial injury until cardiac causes are excluded.

Natriuretic peptides (BNP, NT-proBNP)
B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) and its inactive N-terminal fragment NT-proBNP are released by ventricular myocytes in response to wall stress (volume overload, increased filling pressures). They are useful biomarkers for diagnosing and monitoring heart failure. Typical cutoffs: BNP <100 pg/mL argues against acute heart failure, while BNP >400 pg/mL supports diagnosis; NT-proBNP cutoffs vary with age—NT-proBNP <300 pg/mL often used to exclude acute heart failure, and age-stratified higher cutoffs used to rule in disease.

In myopathies with cardiac involvement (inflammatory, dystrophic), BNP/NT-proBNP may be elevated due to cardiomyopathy and provide a noninvasive marker of ventricular dysfunction. However, natriuretic peptides are affected by renal function (higher in renal failure), age, and body mass (lower in obesity). Combining natriuretic peptide levels with troponin, ECG, and imaging helps determine whether cardiac involvement is present in a patient with systemic myopathy.

Clinical implications
When assessing for cardiac involvement in myopathy:

- Use cTnI if available to minimize false positives from skeletal muscle.
- Investigate any new troponin or BNP elevation with ECG and echocardiography; consider cardiac MRI for suspected myocarditis or infiltrative disease.
- Serial biomarker measurements can show trends consistent with evolving cardiac injury versus chronic elevation.
- Be aware that some neuromuscular diseases produce chronically elevated cTnT assays without cardiac injury—this is assay- and disease-dependent.

# Urinalysis and myoglobinuria in rhabdomyolysis

Urinalysis is a rapid bedside tool that helps identify myoglobinuria and the risk of renal complications in rhabdomyolysis.

Urine dipstick findings
Myoglobin contains heme and yields a positive "blood" test on urine dipsticks that detect peroxidase-like activity. A characteristic finding in rhabdomyolysis is a strongly positive dipstick for blood with few or no red blood cells on microscopic urine sediment. This disparity should prompt suspicion for myoglobinuria (or hemoglobinuria if intravascular hemolysis exists). The dipstick cannot distinguish hemoglobin from myoglobin.

Macroscopic appearance and sediment
Urine may be dark, tea-colored, or cola-colored. Microscopy typically shows minimal RBCs; granular casts and tubular epithelial cells may appear as tubular injury evolves. Myoglobin is rapidly filtered and may precipitate within renal tubules in the presence of acidic urine and volume depletion, contributing to obstruction and oxidative injury.

Quantitative tests and limitations
Direct assays for urine myoglobin exist but are not always available and may lack standardization. Clinicians primarily rely on the combination of positive blood dipstick with absent or minimal RBCs, elevated serum CK and myoglobin, and clinical context to diagnose myoglobinuria.

Pathophysiology of renal injury
Myoglobin causes AKI through multiple mechanisms: renal vasoconstriction (mediated by hypovolemia and vasoactive substances), cast formation with tubular obstruction (heme pigment complexes), and direct proximal tubular toxicity via oxidative injury, particularly in acidic urine. Risk factors for myoglobin-induced AKI include sustained hypotension, dehydration, aciduria, concomitant nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, contrast), and prolonged muscle ischemia.

Management implications from urinalysis
Urine findings guide early management. A positive blood dipstick with few RBCs in a patient with elevated CK should prompt aggressive volume repletion to maintain urine output, correction of acidosis when present, avoidance of nephrotoxins, and consideration of urinary alkalinization in selected cases (though evidence for routine alkalinization is limited). Monitor urine output (target often >0.5–1 mL/kg/hr or institutional targets) and serial creatinine. Early nephrology consultation is appropriate for severe or refractory cases.

# Laboratory monitoring timelines and interpretation during treatment

Understanding the expected time course of biomarker changes and which tests to monitor during treatment is crucial for clinical decision-making in muscle and liver-related disorders.

Initial assessment and early monitoring
On presentation with suspected muscle injury or rhabdomyolysis, baseline laboratory tests should include: serum CK, AST, ALT, LDH, aldolase (if available and if inflammatory myopathy suspected), serum myoglobin (if available), serum electrolytes with particular attention to potassium, phosphate, calcium, bicarbonate, creatinine, BUN, urinalysis with dipstick and microscopy, and baseline ECG when significant hyperkalemia is a possibility. For suspected cardiac involvement, obtain troponin (preferably cTnI), BNP/NT-proBNP, and ECG.

Time course and serial sampling
- CK: rises within hours, peaks 24–72 hours, declines over days once muscle injury ceases. In acute rhabdomyolysis, check CK at presentation and then every 6–24 hours depending on severity to follow the trend. A falling CK generally indicates cessation of ongoing muscle necrosis; a plateau or rising trend suggests continued injury.
- Myoglobin: rises early (1–6 hours) and clears rapidly (hours). Because of rapid kinetics, a single normal myoglobin does not exclude recent muscle injury; conversely, very elevated myoglobin early on suggests extensive damage. Serum myoglobin is most useful for very early detection; its levels do not reliably predict the need for dialysis.
- AST/ALT: AST often rises in parallel to CK in muscle injury but may lag slightly; ALT may be mildly increased. Recheck aminotransferases every 1–3 days as clinically indicated, especially if liver disease is suspected.
- LDH and aldolase: LDH rises later and falls more slowly than CK; these markers are helpful for delayed presentations or in chronic muscle disease monitoring. Aldolase can be useful for inflammatory myopathy monitoring when CK is discordant.
- Troponin: if concern for myocardial injury, measure troponin at presentation and repeat at 3–6 hours (or per institutional protocol for the specific assay) to document rise and/or fall, and then serially as clinically indicated.
- Electrolytes and renal function: check serum potassium, phosphate, calcium, BUN, creatinine, and urinalysis frequently—initially every 6–12 hours in severe rhabdomyolysis, then less often as the patient stabilizes. Monitor ECG for hyperkalemia effects.

Interpreting trends during treatment
- Hydration and urine output: the primary initial therapy for rhabdomyolysis is aggressive intravenous isotonic fluids to preserve renal perfusion and dilute nephrotoxic pigments. Clinical response is judged by urine output, hemodynamics, and laboratory markers. A rising or plateaued CK despite fluids suggests ongoing muscle damage and should prompt search for missed sources (compartment syndrome, ongoing seizures, continued exertion, ongoing ischemia).
- Declining CK: a sustained decrease in CK over consecutive measurements, together with improving myalgias and renal indices, supports recovery. Because CK has a fairly long plasma half-life, improvement in clinical status often precedes normalization of CK.
- Persistent or worsening renal dysfunction: monitor creatinine and urine output. Indications for dialysis in rhabdomyolysis include refractory hyperkalemia, severe metabolic acidosis, or volume overload unresponsive to medical management—decisions based on standard nephrology criteria rather than absolute CK cutoffs.
- Aminotransferases: if AST/ALT are elevated due to muscle injury, they should fall as CK and clinical muscle injury resolve. If aminotransferases remain elevated or liver-specific markers change (rising bilirubin, increasing INR, elevated GGT, alkaline phosphatase), investigate hepatic causes concurrently.
- Cardiac markers: rising troponin with ischemic ECG changes or imaging abnormalities requires standard cardiac evaluation. In patients with neuromuscular disease and isolated elevated cTnT without other cardiac evidence, correlate with cTnI and consider cardiology consultation.

Decision points and thresholds
- Rhabdomyolysis concern: many clinicians use CK >5 × ULN or absolute CK thresholds (e.g., >1,000–5,000 U/L) and clinical context to diagnose rhabdomyolysis. The risk of AKI increases notably with CK >5,000–10,000 U/L, although AKI can occur at lower CK levels.
- Monitoring frequency: in severe cases with CK >5,000 U/L or electrolyte abnormalities, check CK and electrolytes every 6–12 hours initially. In more stable patients, daily monitoring may suffice.
- When to stop aggressive measures: there is no universally agreed CK cutoff to stop fluids; commonly clinicians consider de-escalation once CK is steadily falling and urine output and renal function are stable. Continue monitoring until CK declines substantially toward baseline and renal function returns to baseline.

Summary and key practical points

- CK is the primary marker of skeletal muscle necrosis; interpret its magnitude with knowledge of baseline variability and clinical context. Rhabdomyolysis often involves CK in the thousands; AKI risk increases with very high CK but is not determined by CK alone.
- AST and ALT are not liver-specific. Correlate aminotransferase elevations with CK and with other liver-specific tests (GGT, bilirubin, ALP, INR). An isolated AST rise with high CK strongly suggests muscle origin.
- Aldolase and LDH provide adjunctive information; aldolase can be helpful in inflammatory myopathies, and LDH isoenzyme patterns can aid source attribution when needed.
- Myoglobin rises early and is nephrotoxic; urine dipstick positive for blood with few RBCs is a rapid bedside clue to myoglobinuria.
- Cardiac markers: use cTnI for greater cardiac specificity in patients with skeletal muscle disease; be aware that cTnT assay cross-reactivity can cause false positives in some myopathies. BNP/NT-proBNP aid detection of cardiac dysfunction in myopathy-related cardiomyopathy.
- Monitor electrolytes, renal function, urine output, and serial CK/troponin as clinically indicated. Tailor monitoring frequency to the severity of muscle injury and the presence of renal or cardiac complications.

Knowing the kinetics, tissue distribution, and limitations of muscle and liver laboratory markers allows clinicians to integrate laboratory data with clinical findings, prioritize diagnostic testing, and implement timely management to reduce complications such as acute kidney injury and unrecognized cardiac involvement.

Introduction

Inflammatory myopathies are a heterogeneous group of immune-mediated disorders characterized by skeletal muscle inflammation and weakness. Although historically conceptualized as a spectrum with overlapping clinical and histopathologic features, modern advances in immunology, serology, and muscle pathology have refined classification and allowed recognition of distinct entities. Precise understanding of the underlying pathophysiology is crucial for diagnosis, prognostication, and targeted therapy. This chapter reviews the major categories of inflammatory myopathies, emphasizing the pathobiology of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), a syndrome increasingly recognized as distinct from dermatomyositis, polymyositis, and inclusion body myositis. We will integrate histologic hallmarks, immunologic mechanisms (including autoantibodies such as anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP), the processes that lead to myofibre necrosis, the typical clinical phenotype, and the expected natural history including treatment responsiveness and risk of chronicity.

# Overview of inflammatory myopathy classification (dermatomyositis, polymyositis, IMNM, inclusion body myositis)

Classification of inflammatory myopathies has evolved from broad clinicopathologic groupings to a more nuanced schema incorporating clinical presentation, muscle biopsy features, electromyography, magnetic resonance imaging, and specific autoantibodies. The major contemporary categories are dermatomyositis (DM), polymyositis (PM), immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), and inclusion body myositis (IBM). Each entity has characteristic pathologic mechanisms and patterns of muscle involvement that inform diagnosis and management.

Dermatomyositis is defined by a combination of proximal muscle weakness and distinctive cutaneous manifestations (heliotrope rash, Gottron papules) and by a microvascular and complement-mediated pathogenesis. On biopsy, DM shows perimysial and perivascular inflammation, perifascicular atrophy, and deposition of complement membrane attack complex (MAC; C5b-9) in endomysial capillaries. The pathophysiology is thought to involve complement activation directed at the microvasculature, leading to chronic ischemic injury of muscle fibers, particularly at the periphery of fascicles. DM is also associated with specific myositis-associated and myositis-specific autoantibodies (e.g., anti-Mi-2, anti-MDA5), which correlate with distinct clinical phenotypes and extra-muscular involvement, such as interstitial lung disease or increased malignancy risk in certain subtypes.

Polymyositis is a diagnosis now used sparingly and largely in patients showing a T cell–mediated endomysial inflammatory process with invasion of non-necrotic muscle fibers by CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes that express major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I). True PM is relatively rare; many historical PM diagnoses are now reclassified after targeted immunohistochemical and serologic evaluation. PM is characterized by symmetric proximal weakness and elevated muscle enzymes, and it may occur in association with other autoimmune disorders. The central pathogenetic mechanism is thought to be antigen-driven cytotoxic T cell attack against myofibre antigens presented on upregulated MHC-I molecules.

Inclusion body myositis is characterized clinically by a distinctive combination of proximal and distal weakness, often asymmetric, with early involvement of finger flexors and knee extensors, and poor responsiveness to immunotherapy. Pathologically, IBM shows a characteristic constellation: endomysial inflammation with CD8+ T cell invasion, vacuolated fibers, and intracellular inclusions that contain aggregated proteins such as amyloid precursor protein, phosphorylated tau, and TDP-43. Mitochondrial abnormalities and rimmed vacuoles are prominent. IBM appears to combine autoimmune inflammation with degenerative protein-handling defects and defective autophagy, which explains the refractoriness to immunosuppression and progressive course.

Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) sits apart from these categories because its defining histopathologic lesion is prominent myofibre necrosis with associated regeneration and relatively sparse lymphocytic inflammation. IMNM is most commonly associated with specific autoantibodies—anti-3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (anti-HMGCR) and anti–signal recognition particle (anti-SRP)—and can be triggered or unmasked by statin exposure in the anti-HMGCR subtype. Clinically, IMNM presents with subacute severe symmetric proximal weakness and markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) in many but not all patients. The pathogenesis involves antibody- and complement-mediated mechanisms, macrophage-driven myophagocytosis, and muscle fiber necrosis, rather than purely T cell–mediated cytotoxicity.

These entities overlap clinically and sometimes histologically, which can complicate diagnosis. A modern approach integrates serologic testing for myositis-specific antibodies, MRI patterns of muscle edema and fatty replacement, and detailed histopathologic analysis including immunohistochemistry for MHC-I, complement components, and cellular markers, to arrive at the most accurate classification.

# Histologic features of IMNM: myofibre necrosis, regeneration, paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates

The histologic hallmark of IMNM is widespread myofibre necrosis accompanied by active regeneration and a relative paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates. On routine hematoxylin and eosin staining, necrotic fibers appear pale, with loss of cross-striations, fragmentation, and myofibrillar dissolution. Regenerating fibers are basophilic with enlarged nuclei and prominent nucleoli; they often express developmental myosins on immunohistochemistry. The pattern of necrosis is typically multifocal and diffuse rather than clustered perivascular or perifascicular, distinguishing IMNM from DM.

Inflammatory cell infiltrates in IMNM are typically sparse compared with DM or PM. When present, inflammatory cells are often dominated by macrophages (CD68+), frequently localized within the endomysium and actively engaged in phagocytosis of necrotic debris. Scattered T lymphocytes may be present, but they do not show the classic feature of invading intact, non-necrotic fibers that is characteristic of PM or IBM. B cells and plasma cells may be identified in some cases, particularly in association with specific autoantibodies; however, dense lymphoid aggregates and perimysial inflammation are uncommon.

Complement deposition, particularly the membrane attack complex (C5b-9), may be observed on the sarcolemma of necrotic and occasionally non-necrotic fibers, supporting a role for complement-mediated membrane damage in pathogenesis. Upregulation of major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) on muscle fibers is frequently seen, reflecting an inflammatory milieu and potentially facilitating recognition by immune effectors. Electron microscopy is not required for diagnosis but can reveal sarcolemmal disruption and myofibrillar disorganization. Muscle biopsy in IMNM also demonstrates early regeneration and variable degrees of endomysial fibrosis if the disease is chronic. Fatty replacement may be present in longstanding disease.

Immunostaining for developmental myosin heavy chain (dMHC) helps document active regeneration; staining for complement components and for markers of macrophage activation (e.g., CD163) is useful to demonstrate active immune effector mechanisms. Taken together, the pathological picture emphasizes muscle fiber necrosis and repair with macrophage-mediated clearance and relatively limited lymphocytic cytotoxic invasion.

# Mechanisms of muscle fibre necrosis: antibody-mediated injury, complement activation, macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis

Muscle fiber necrosis in IMNM arises from a convergence of humoral immune mechanisms and innate immune effector pathways rather than predominant T cell–mediated cytotoxicity. Three interrelated processes—autoantibody binding, complement activation (leading to membrane attack complex formation), and macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis—explain much of the observed pathology.

Autoantibody-mediated injury begins with the presence of circulating autoantibodies directed at muscle-related antigens accessible at or near the sarcolemma or internal structures exposed on damaged fibers. In IMNM, the two most frequently implicated autoantibodies are anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. These antibodies may bind to target antigens expressed or transiently presented on the muscle fiber surface, or to antigenic complexes that become exposed after minor injury. Antibody binding to the sarcolemmal surface can lead to classical pathway complement activation. Deposition of complement components, culminating in assembly of C5b-9, forms pores in the sarcolemma, causing ionic dysregulation, calcium influx, and necrotic cell death. Immunohistochemical demonstration of C5b-9 on muscle fibers (particularly necrotic or at-risk fibers) supports this mechanism.

Complement-mediated lysis is complemented by Fc receptor–dependent processes that recruit and activate innate immune cells. Macrophages, drawn to sites of necrosis and antibody deposition, engage in phagocytosis of damaged fibers. Muscle biopsy commonly reveals CD68+ macrophages containing myofibrillar debris—myophagocytosis—consistent with clearance of necrotic tissue. Activated macrophages secrete proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-alpha, IL-1) and reactive oxygen species that can amplify local tissue injury. The phenotype of macrophages (pro-inflammatory M1-like versus reparative M2-like) influences whether the local milieu promotes further necrosis or supports regeneration and repair.

Concurrently, upregulation of MHC-I on muscle fibers—likely induced by interferons and other inflammatory cytokines—renders fibers more susceptible to immune recognition and potentially engages cytotoxic lymphocytes when present. However, in IMNM, direct CD8+ T cell–mediated invasion of intact fibers is usually minimal, distinguishing it from PM and IBM.

Other mechanisms that may contribute include complement-independent antibody functions such as antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) mediated by natural killer cells and macrophages, disruption of essential sarcolemmal proteins by autoantibodies interfering with their function, and immune complex deposition with local activation of inflammatory cascades. In some cases (for example, statin-associated anti-HMGCR IMNM), there may be a sustained autoimmune response even after removal of the triggering drug, suggesting that epitope spreading and a break in peripheral tolerance lead to chronic autoreactivity.

Understanding these mechanisms informs therapeutic choices: therapies that reduce autoantibody production (e.g., B cell–targeted therapies such as rituximab), block complement activation, or modulate macrophage activation and cytokine networks can be rationally applied.

# Role of autoantibodies (e.g., anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP) in pathogenesis

Autoantibodies are central to the modern understanding of IMNM. The two autoantibodies most specifically associated with IMNM are anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP; their presence not only assists classification but also provides insights into pathogenesis and prognosis.

Anti-HMGCR antibodies target 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol biosynthesis and the target of statin therapy. Many patients with anti-HMGCR IMNM have a history of exposure to statins, although the antibody can occur in statin-naïve individuals, including children. Proposed pathogenic sequences include statin-induced upregulation of HMGCR expression in regenerating muscle fibers, leading to increased antigen presentation and eventual autoantibody formation in genetically susceptible hosts. Once generated, anti-HMGCR antibodies may bind to HMGCR expressed on or nearby the sarcolemma in regenerating fibers or to intracellular antigens exposed upon injury, initiating complement activation and macrophage recruitment. Anti-HMGCR positive patients often present with severe weakness and very high CK levels, though phenotype variability exists.

Anti-SRP antibodies target components of the signal recognition particle, a ubiquitous ribonucleoprotein complex involved in co-translational targeting of nascent polypeptides to the endoplasmic reticulum. Anti-SRP-associated IMNM is often linked to a more aggressive and refractory clinical course with a higher risk of residual weakness and atrophy. The mechanisms by which anti-SRP antibodies induce muscle injury are less clearly defined, since SRP is primarily intracellular; however, antibody binding may occur to SRP components that are abnormally expressed or exposed on the muscle surface or released into the extracellular space during injury. Alternatively, anti-SRP autoimmunity may reflect a broader dysregulation of protein handling and antigen presentation that promotes immune-mediated necrosis.

Serologic detection of these antibodies relies on validated assays—immunoprecipitation, ELISA, and line blot assays—with differing sensitivity and specificity. Positive anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP antibodies in the appropriate clinical and pathologic context strongly support an IMNM diagnosis and can guide risk stratification. For instance, anti-SRP positivity is associated with more severe disease and less favorable response to conventional immunosuppression, while anti-HMGCR-associated disease may be more responsive to therapy but can also persist after statin cessation.

The pathogenic role of these antibodies is supported by observations of complement deposition on muscle fibers and by experimental models wherein passive transfer of patient antibodies or immunization with target antigens can provoke myopathic changes. Nevertheless, autoantibodies may also serve as biomarkers of a broader autoimmune process rather than sole effectors of tissue damage. Their presence often correlates with distinct clinical features (e.g., statin exposure history, age distribution, extramuscular involvement) and can inform therapeutic decisions, such as the early use of B-cell depleting agents in refractory or anti-SRP–positive disease.

# Clinical phenotype of IMNM: proximal weakness, dysphagia, variable creatine kinase elevations

Clinically, IMNM typically presents with subacute to acute symmetric proximal muscle weakness, often evolving over weeks to months. Patients commonly report difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs, or lifting objects above shoulder level. The weakness tends to involve both the shoulder girdle and pelvic girdle musculature prominently. Unlike IBM, distal and asymmetric weakness are uncommon early in IMNM.

Dysphagia is an important clinical feature seen in a subset of patients with IMNM and can range from mild oropharyngeal dysfunction to severe swallowing impairment that requires enteral support. Facial weakness and respiratory muscle involvement are less common but can occur, particularly in severe or rapidly progressive cases. Extramuscular manifestations such as interstitial lung disease are less typical than in some forms of DM, but subclinical pulmonary involvement can occur, especially in certain serologic subgroups.

Creatine kinase (CK) elevation is a characteristic laboratory finding but is variable. Many patients have markedly elevated CK, often in the thousands of units per liter. Typical CK values in IMNM frequently exceed 1,000 U/L; normal CK ranges vary by laboratory but are generally approximately 20–200 U/L in adults, with higher upper limits in men and in individuals of African descent. Some patients, however, present with only modest CK elevations or even normal CK despite clinically significant weakness, particularly when disease has progressed to a chronic stage with fatty replacement of muscle. Therefore, CK is a useful monitoring tool but should not be used to exclude IMNM when clinical suspicion is high.

Electrodiagnostic testing (EMG) usually demonstrates an irritable myopathy with short-duration, low-amplitude motor unit potentials and frequent fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves, consistent with ongoing fiber necrosis and regeneration. MRI of involved muscles often shows edema on T2-weighted and STIR sequences reflecting active inflammation and necrosis; extent and pattern of muscle involvement can help guide biopsy site selection. Muscle biopsy, as described above, shows necrosis with regeneration and macrophage predominance.

Systemic laboratory evaluation should include myositis-specific and myositis-associated autoantibody panels (including anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP), pulmonary function testing and high-resolution chest CT if respiratory symptoms or serologic risk factors for ILD are present, and cancer screening guided by age and subtype when appropriate. It is important to recognize that the severity of weakness does not always correlate linearly with CK levels; clinical assessment of strength and function remains primary in management decisions.

# Natural history: treatment responsiveness, potential for chronicity and relapse

The natural history of IMNM is variable and influenced by autoantibody status, treatment timing and intensity, and individual factors. Generally, IMNM is considered a potentially severe but often treatment-responsive disease, especially when recognized early and managed with aggressive immunotherapy. However, a significant proportion of patients may experience chronic weakness, relapses, or incomplete recovery.

Anti-HMGCR–associated IMNM often responds to immunosuppression, particularly when therapy is initiated promptly and includes agents that reduce autoantibody-producing B cells (e.g., rituximab), high-dose corticosteroids, and steroid-sparing immunosuppressants (e.g., methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil). In many patients, CK levels fall and strength improves over weeks to months. Statin exposure should be discontinued in patients with anti-HMGCR positivity, although stopping the statin alone is insufficient in most cases; immunotherapy is commonly required. Some patients, particularly older adults or those with delayed diagnosis, have residual weakness and may develop muscle atrophy and fatty replacement on imaging.

Anti-SRP–positive IMNM historically carries a more aggressive course and can be refractory to standard immunosuppressive regimens. These patients more frequently require combination therapy and may have a higher risk of chronic disability. Early combination therapy, including intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), calcineurin inhibitors, or B-cell depletion, is often considered in severe presentations. Longitudinal follow-up frequently shows that achieving sustained remission can be challenging, and relapse is common when therapy is tapered.

Relapses occur in a substantial minority of patients across serologic subtypes. Monitoring strategies include serial clinical strength assessments, patient-reported function, and CK measurements, recognizing the limitations of CK as noted above. MRI can be valuable to detect subclinical disease activity and to document chronic structural change. Long-term maintenance immunosuppression may be required in many patients to prevent relapses; the regimen must balance efficacy against risks of infection and medication toxicity.

Chronicity manifests as muscle atrophy, fibrosis, and fatty infiltration leading to persistent functional impairment even after inflammatory activity is controlled. Rehabilitation with physical and occupational therapy is essential throughout disease course to maximize strength, function, and quality of life. Early mobilization and carefully tailored exercise programs are safe and beneficial in most patients, contrasting with older advice to rest.

Finally, treatment decision points include the severity of weakness, rate of progression, CK trends, presence of dysphagia or respiratory involvement, and serologic profile. Severe, rapidly progressive disease warrants early aggressive combination immunotherapy (high-dose corticosteroids plus one or more steroid-sparing agents or IVIG), while milder cases may be managed with oral corticosteroids and a single steroid-sparing agent with close monitoring. Refractory disease merits escalation to IVIG, rituximab, or other targeted agents. Multidisciplinary care—including neurology or rheumatology, pulmonology, rehabilitation medicine, and sometimes gastroenterology or otolaryngology for swallowing dysfunction—optimizes outcomes.

Conclusion

IMNM is a distinct and important subtype of inflammatory myopathy characterized by prominent myofibre necrosis, regeneration, and relatively sparse lymphocytic infiltrates, frequently associated with myositis-specific autoantibodies such as anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. Understanding the interplay of antibody-mediated complement activation, macrophage-driven myophagocytosis, and altered antigen presentation is essential to interpret muscle biopsy findings and to select rational therapies. Clinically, IMNM presents with subacute symmetric proximal weakness and variable CK elevations and can be severe but often responsive to immunotherapy; nevertheless, chronicity and relapse are common. A thoughtful, multidisciplinary approach guided by serology, pathology, and clinical course yields the best chance for recovery and minimizes long-term disability.

Introduction

Autoantibodies play a central role in the diagnosis, classification, prognostication and sometimes pathophysiologic understanding of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM, commonly referred to as “myositis”). Detection of myositis-specific antibodies (MSAs) and myositis-associated antibodies (MAAs) can help distinguish subtypes (dermatomyositis, polymyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy [IMNM], antisynthetase syndrome, inclusion body myositis), guide malignancy screening, and identify risks for extramuscular complications such as interstitial lung disease (ILD) and cardiac involvement. This chapter reviews the major MSAs and MAAs, their clinical correlations, the performance characteristics and limitations of serologic testing, and the laboratory methods used to detect these autoantibodies.

# Common myositis-specific antibodies: anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP, anti-Jo-1 and other tRNA synthetase antibodies

Myositis-specific antibodies (MSAs) are detected primarily in patients with IIM and are rarely seen in other rheumatic or infectious diseases. They tend to be mutually exclusive, and each is associated with characteristic clinical phenotypes.

Anti-HMGCR
Anti-3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (anti-HMGCR) antibodies were first identified in patients with a necrotizing myopathy often associated with statin exposure, although they also occur in statin-naive patients, including children. Clinically, anti-HMGCR–positive myopathy is characterized by subacute to chronic symmetric proximal muscle weakness, markedly elevated serum creatine kinase (CK) levels (often >1000 U/L and frequently >3000–10,000 U/L), prominent myofiber necrosis on muscle biopsy with minimal inflammatory infiltrate, and variable extra-muscular involvement.

Pathophysiology: HMGCR is the rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol synthesis and is ubiquitously expressed, including in regenerating muscle fibers where expression is upregulated. The prevailing model is that statin exposure (or other triggers) increases HMGCR expression in regenerating myofibers, promoting an autoimmune response in genetically susceptible individuals (HLA-DRB1*11:01 has been associated). Anti-HMGCR antibodies are predominantly IgG and are thought to be pathogenic or at least a marker of the humoral autoimmune process driving necrosis.

Therapeutic and prognostic implications: Anti-HMGCR myopathy can be severe, relapsing, and often requires aggressive immunotherapy including corticosteroids, steroid-sparing agents, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and sometimes rituximab. Importantly, stopping statins alone is usually insufficient.

Anti-SRP
Antibodies to the signal recognition particle (SRP) are associated with a severe necrotizing myopathy with rapid onset of profound weakness, very high CK levels, and poor spontaneous recovery. SRP is a ribonucleoprotein involved in targeting nascent peptide chains to the endoplasmic reticulum.

Clinical phenotype: Anti-SRP–positive patients frequently present with rapidly progressive symmetric proximal weakness that can involve respiratory muscles and sometimes cardiac muscle. Muscle biopsy typically shows necrosis and regeneration with scant lymphocytic inflammation. Compared to anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP myopathy often has earlier and more aggressive course with worse long-term functional outcomes.

Pathophysiology: The presence of anti-SRP antibodies suggests a humoral immune mechanism; complement-mediated myofiber injury and muscle fiber apoptosis have been proposed. Associations with other autoimmune features are less prominent than for some other MSAs.

Anti-Jo-1 and other aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase antibodies (antisynthetase antibodies)
The best-known antisynthetase antibody is anti-Jo-1 (histidyl-tRNA synthetase). Other antisynthetase antibodies include anti-PL-7 (threonyl), anti-PL-12 (alanyl), anti-EJ (glycyl), anti-OJ (isoleucyl), anti-KS (asparaginyl), anti-Zo and others. These antibodies define the antisynthetase syndrome, a clinically coherent entity.

Clinical phenotype: The antisynthetase syndrome typically features myositis (often subacute), inflammatory arthritis, “mechanic’s hands” (hyperkeratotic cracked skin at the fingertips), Raynaud phenomenon, and interstitial lung disease (ILD). ILD may be severe and sometimes precedes muscle disease. Anti-Jo-1 is the most common antisynthetase antibody and tends to be associated with more prominent myositis and arthritis; some non-Jo-1 synthetase antibodies are associated with more severe or isolated ILD and can be missed if only anti-Jo-1 is tested.

Pathophysiology and immune features: The aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases are ubiquitous cytoplasmic enzymes; the reason for autoimmunity directed at these proteins is uncertain. The antisynthetase antibodies are associated with a cytoplasmic staining pattern on immunofluorescence and are thought to reflect a systemic autoimmune response where both cell-mediated and humoral mechanisms contribute to tissue injury.

Clinical implications: Identifying a synthetase antibody informs screening for ILD (high-resolution chest CT and pulmonary function testing) and alerts clinicians to increased risk of arthritis and mechanics hands. Treatment often requires immunosuppression tailored to ILD severity, with corticosteroids, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide or rituximab used depending on organ involvement.

# Myositis-associated antibodies: ANA, SSA/SSB, TIF1-γ, MDA5, NXP-2, cN-1A

The category of “myositis-associated antibodies” sometimes overlaps with MSAs; some antibodies traditionally considered MAAs are associated with specific myositis subtypes and have diagnostic and prognostic significance. We will review several important antibodies: TIF1-γ, MDA5, NXP-2, cN-1A, as well as commonly encountered nonspecific autoantibodies such as ANA and SSA/SSB.

ANA (antinuclear antibody)
ANA testing by indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) remains a screening test. A positive ANA (titer ≥1:160 often considered clinically meaningful, though laboratories vary) is common in dermatomyositis and some other inflammatory myopathies, but it lacks specificity. A negative ANA does not exclude myositis; some MSAs (e.g., anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR) may be present with negative ANA by IIF.

SSA (anti-SSA/Ro) and SSB (anti-La)
Anti-SSA and anti-SSB antibodies are more classically associated with Sjögren syndrome and systemic lupus erythematosus, but they can be present in connective tissue overlap syndromes that include myositis. Presence of SSA, especially cytoplasmic Ro52 (sometimes reported as anti-Ro52), can co-occur with antisynthetase antibodies and is associated with more severe ILD in some studies. Ro52 is sometimes tested separately and may be more informative in the myositis context than conventional SSA/Ro60.

TIF1-γ (anti-transcription intermediary factor 1-gamma, also called TRIM33)
Anti-TIF1-γ antibodies are strongly associated with classic dermatomyositis and are of particular importance because they are linked to a markedly increased risk of malignancy in adult-onset dermatomyositis. The magnitude of cancer risk varies with study cohort and age: the association is strongest for adults over age 50, where anti-TIF1-γ positivity confers a substantially increased odds of an underlying occult neoplasm discovered within 1–3 years of myositis onset. Common associated cancers include adenocarcinomas of the lung, breast, ovary, and gastrointestinal tract among others.

Clinical application: Identification of anti-TIF1-γ should trigger an age-appropriate yet intensified malignancy screen and close surveillance for at least 2–3 years after myositis onset. The exact screening strategy is individualized but often includes thorough history, physical exam, targeted imaging (e.g., CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, mammography, pelvic imaging), fecal occult blood testing/colonoscopy as indicated, and directed evaluation for symptoms.

MDA5 (anti–melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5, also called anti-CADM-140)
Anti-MDA5 is classically associated with clinically amyopathic dermatomyositis (CADM) and a phenotype of dermatomyositis with minimal muscle involvement but severe and rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease—especially in East Asian cohorts, but also seen worldwide. Patients may present with characteristic cutaneous findings (e.g., skin ulceration, palmar papules), arthritis, and disproportionately severe ILD that can be refractory to therapy.

Prognosis and management: MDA5-associated ILD can be rapidly progressive with high mortality. Early recognition and aggressive combination immunosuppression (high-dose corticosteroids plus calcineurin inhibitors, cyclophosphamide or rituximab, and sometimes IVIG) are often used. Serum ferritin may be elevated in severe cases and has been proposed as a biomarker of disease activity.

NXP-2 (anti-nuclear matrix protein 2, previously MJ)
Anti-NXP-2 is associated with dermatomyositis and is particularly notable in pediatric DM, where it is associated with severe muscle involvement, calcinosis, and joint contractures. In adults, anti-NXP-2 may also carry an increased cancer risk in some series, though associations are less robust and variable between studies. NXP-2 positivity is linked to prominent muscle weakness, sometimes with myalgia, and may be associated with dysphagia.

cN-1A (anti–cytosolic 5′-nucleotidase 1A)
cN-1A antibodies have been identified in a subset of patients with inclusion body myositis (IBM) and can be helpful in distinguishing IBM from other myopathies. Sensitivity for IBM is modest (reported roughly 30–60% in various studies) and specificity variable, with occasional positivity in other autoimmune conditions. When present in the appropriate clinical context—older patient, asymmetric finger flexor and quadriceps weakness, characteristic muscle biopsy findings—cN-1A supports a diagnosis of IBM. However, cN-1A alone is insufficient for diagnosis.

Other MAAs
Other antibodies encountered in overlap syndromes include anti-U1-RNP (mixed connective tissue disease features), anti-PM/Scl (associated with overlapping scleroderma features), and anti-Ku. These antibodies often indicate connective tissue overlap and may prompt evaluation for skin, pulmonary, or esophageal involvement consistent with systemic sclerosis, SLE or mixed connective tissue disease.

# Clinical correlations: antibody-specific phenotypes, malignancy risk, cardiac and pulmonary associations

Understanding the clinical associations tied to specific autoantibodies aids diagnostic classification, prognostication and management.

Antibody-specific phenotypes
Each MSA largely defines a phenotype:

- Anti-Jo-1 and other antisynthetase antibodies: antisynthetase syndrome with myositis, ILD (which can precede muscle disease), non-erosive inflammatory arthritis, mechanic’s hands, and Raynaud phenomenon. ILD is a major determinant of morbidity and mortality.
- Anti-MDA5: amyopathic or hypomyopathic dermatomyositis with severe, often rapidly progressive ILD, distinctive cutaneous ulcerations, and arthritis.
- Anti-TIF1-γ: classic dermatomyositis with strong association with underlying malignancy in adults, particularly older adults.
- Anti-NXP-2: severe muscle disease, calcinosis in children, sometimes dysphagia and increased malignancy risk in adults.
- Anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP: necrotizing myopathy pattern with prominent CK elevation and muscle fiber necrosis on biopsy; anti-SRP often more aggressive with more frequent respiratory and cardiac involvement.
- cN-1A: supports diagnosis of inclusion body myositis, often in older adults, with characteristic pattern of weakness.

Malignancy risk
Certain autoantibodies confer an increased risk of malignancy in adult myositis, most notably anti-TIF1-γ and, in some series, anti-NXP-2. The risk is greatest around the time of myositis onset and within the first 1–3 years thereafter. Clinical implications:

- For anti-TIF1-γ–positive adult patients, particularly those older than 50, clinicians should perform an intensified cancer screening: complete history and physical exam, age- and sex-appropriate screening (mammography, Pap smear, colonoscopy), targeted imaging (contrast-enhanced CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or PET-CT), and directed testing based on symptoms and risk factors.
- Anti-NXP-2 positivity in adults may prompt similar vigilance, but the strength of association varies by cohort.
- Anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP, and antisynthetase antibodies do not have consistent or strong malignancy associations, though malignancy can still occur.

Cardiac associations
Cardiac involvement in IIM ranges from subclinical myocardial inflammation to symptomatic myocarditis, conduction disease, arrhythmia and heart failure. Certain antibodies are more frequently associated with cardiac disease:

- Anti-SRP–positive patients appear to have higher risk of cardiac involvement, including myocarditis and cardiomyopathy.
- IMNM, especially anti-HMGCR–positive disease, has been associated with cardiac involvement in some cohorts.
- Antisynthetase syndrome patients may have cardiac conduction abnormalities or pulmonary hypertension secondary to ILD. However, the risk is variable.
Clinical approach: Because cardiac involvement can be clinically silent but prognostically important, baseline cardiovascular assessment (history, ECG, troponin if indicated, and consideration of transthoracic echocardiography or cardiac MRI in symptomatic patients or those with concerning antibodies) is reasonable.

Pulmonary associations
ILD is one of the most clinically significant extramuscular manifestations in myositis, particularly in antisynthetase syndrome and MDA5-associated disease.

- Antisynthetase antibodies (anti-Jo-1, PL-7, PL-12, EJ, OJ): ILD is common; the pattern can be chronic or progressive and determines long-term outcomes. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) is the preferred imaging modality to detect ILD, and pulmonary function testing (PFTs) with diffusing capacity (DLCO) and FVC should be performed at baseline and serially.
- Anti-MDA5: associated with rapidly progressive ILD with high mortality; clinical vigilance and early treatment are crucial.
- Anti-TIF1-γ and anti-NXP-2: ILD is less prominent, though possible.
Clinical decision points: Any patient with an MSA associated with ILD (particularly antisynthetase or MDA5 antibodies) should undergo prompt pulmonary evaluation—HRCT chest and PFTs—even if respiratory symptoms are absent. The severity of ILD guides immunosuppressive intensity.

# Sensitivity, specificity and false-negative/seronegative IMNM

Understanding test performance characteristics is critical to interpreting serologic results and managing patients with suspected myositis.

Sensitivity and specificity
No single antibody test is perfectly sensitive for myositis overall. The prevalence of specific antibodies varies by myositis subtype and population. Thus:

- MSAs generally have high specificity for IIM (often >90% for many MSAs when tested against healthy controls and other autoimmune diseases), so a positive MSA is strongly supportive of the diagnosis and can be phenotype-defining.
- Sensitivity is variable and often low for individual antibodies: for example, anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP are seen in a minority of all IIM cases, but in a higher proportion of patients with IMNM. Anti-Jo-1 is the most common MSA but still present in only ~15–30% of adult dermatomyositis/polymyositis cohorts depending on the population and assay.
- ANA has higher sensitivity but lower specificity.

False-negative results and seronegative IMNM
A substantial subset of patients with clinical and histologic features of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) or other myositides are seronegative—that is, standard panels do not detect an MSA. Reasons for seronegativity include:

- The target antibody not included in the testing panel. Commercial panels vary in which MSAs they include (e.g., some may not include less common synthetase antibodies, or may omit certain antigens like NXP-2 or HMGCR in some laboratories).
- Antibody titers below assay detection thresholds, particularly early in disease or after immunosuppression.
- Technical limitations of the assay used (different methods have variable sensitivity for specific antibodies).
- Novel or undefined myositis autoantibodies not yet identified or not commercially available.

Clinical approach to seronegative patients: Diagnosis should be based on integrated clinical, laboratory, imaging, and histopathologic data rather than serology alone. In suspected IMNM with negative commercial panels, consider testing at specialized reference laboratories using immunoprecipitation or expanded panels, review medication history (statin exposure for anti-HMGCR), and proceed with muscle biopsy, EMG and MRI to guide diagnosis and therapy. Seronegative IMNM still responds to immunotherapy at least in many patients, so absence of a detectable MSA should not preclude treatment when clinical evidence is strong.

Predictive values
Positive and negative predictive values depend on disease prevalence in the tested population. In specialized clinics with high pretest probability, a positive MSA is more predictive of true disease than in low-prevalence screening contexts.

# Laboratory techniques: immunoassays, line blots, ELISA, immunoprecipitation and interpretation caveats

Multiple laboratory methods are used to detect myositis-related autoantibodies; each has advantages, limitations and specific interpretation caveats. Familiarity with assay characteristics is necessary when ordering and interpreting tests.

Indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) on HEp-2 cells
IIF is a screening method commonly reported as ANA with a titer and staining pattern (e.g., homogeneous, speckled, cytoplasmic). Cytoplasmic staining patterns may suggest antisynthetase antibodies or other cytoplasmic autoantibodies. Strengths include broad screening capability and pattern recognition; limitations include operator dependence, lack of antigen specificity, and potential false-negatives when antibodies are low-titer or directed at epitopes not well displayed on HEp-2 substrate.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
ELISAs are antigen-specific, quantitative or semi-quantitative assays useful for detection of antibodies like anti-HMGCR, anti-MDA5, anti-Jo-1 and others in many laboratories. Advantages are relatively high throughput and reproducibility for single-antigen tests. Limitations: antigens must be properly folded and presented; linear vs conformational epitope differences can reduce sensitivity. Cutoffs for positivity vary by assay and manufacturer; clinicians should interpret results in context of clinical pretest probability.

Line blot / multiplex immunoassays
Commercial line blot panels and multiplex immunoassays allow simultaneous detection of multiple MSAs and MAAs. These are convenient and cost-effective, but performance characteristics vary by antigen and platform. False positives can occur due to cross-reactivity or low-specificity antigens included in panels. Some antibodies (e.g., anti-cN-1A) may show different specificity across platforms. Line blot results should be correlated with clinical features; weak bands in a panel may be of uncertain significance. Positive and negative predictive values depend on the lab’s validation and the tested population.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and immunoprecipitation-Western blot
IP using radiolabeled cell lysates or other substrates has historically been the gold standard for many MSAs because it detects antibodies based on their ability to precipitate native antigen complexes. IP can detect antibodies against conformational epitopes and novel antigens. However, IP is labor-intensive, requires specialized laboratories, lacks standardization and is not widely available clinically. For ambiguous results or research settings, IP remains valuable.

Immunoblot and dot blot
Similar to line blot, these techniques present purified or recombinant antigens on a membrane. Sensitivity and specificity depend on antigen quality and folding. Cross-platform variability is common.

Cell-based assays (CBAs)
For some autoantibodies, CBAs that express the antigen in live or fixed cells better preserve conformational epitopes and improve detection (e.g., certain neuronal and myositis-related antigens). Availability for myositis antibodies is variable.

Interpretation caveats and pre-analytic/post-analytic considerations
- Heterogeneity of assays: Different laboratories and commercial kits use variable antigen sources, preparations and cutoffs. A positive result in one lab may be negative in another. Always review methodologic details and, when possible, use a consistent laboratory for longitudinal testing.
- False positives: Low-titer antibodies or weak bands—particularly on multiplex panels—may be clinically irrelevant. Correlate with phenotype. False positives are more likely when testing low-prevalence populations.
- False negatives: Early disease, low titer, antigen conformation issues, or assays lacking particular antigens can yield false negatives. Consider repeat testing or alternative methods when clinical suspicion is high.
- Cross-reactivity and coexisting autoantibodies: Some antibodies co-occur (e.g., Ro52 with antisynthetase antibodies) and can influence phenotype. Also, nonspecific ANA positivity can obscure interpretation. Recognize that MAAs (e.g., PM/Scl, U1-RNP) indicate overlap syndromes rather than classic dermatomyositis/polymyositis alone.
- Quantitation and serial measurements: For most myositis autoantibodies, serial titers have limited proven utility in monitoring disease activity; trending titers may be informative in select cases but should not replace clinical assessment, muscle enzyme levels, imaging and function tests.
- Reporting conventions: Reports may provide qualitative (positive/negative) and semi-quantitative results (titers or band intensity). Interpretations must integrate clinical context.
- Pretest probability: Testing panels in low-probability settings increases false discovery rates. Test when clinical features suggest IIM or an overlap syndrome.

# Practical approach to serology in suspected myositis

A pragmatic approach maximizes diagnostic yield and minimizes misinterpretation:

1. Choose appropriate tests based on phenotype. In suspected antisynthetase syndrome, request a comprehensive synthetase panel (not just anti-Jo-1). In necrotizing myopathy, include anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. In dermatomyositis with features suggesting malignancy, test for anti-TIF1-γ and consider age-appropriate cancer screening.

2. Use high-quality, validated laboratories. If initial commercial panels are negative and suspicion remains high, consider send-out testing to reference labs that perform immunoprecipitation or expanded antigen panels.

3. Correlate serology with clinical and other objective data. Muscle enzymes (CK; normal CK varies by lab but typical normal upper limit ~170 U/L for men and ~150 U/L for women, with wide assay-dependent variation), EMG, MRI, pulmonary evaluation and muscle biopsy provide essential context. Neither the presence nor absence of a particular antibody alone should dictate management.

4. Reassess over time. Antibody results may appear or change after disease onset or immunotherapy; a single negative test early in disease does not rule out an MSA. Conversely, new symptoms in a seropositive patient warrant re-evaluation for complications associated with that antibody.

5. Communicate with the laboratory. If a result seems discordant with clinical findings, discuss assay specificity, potential cross-reactivity, and availability of confirmatory testing with laboratory specialists or refer to specialized myositis centers.

Conclusion

Autoantibody testing is indispensable in modern evaluation of inflammatory myopathies, providing diagnostic classification, prognostic information and guidance for organ-specific screening (especially for ILD and malignancy). However, clinicians must understand the heterogeneity of assays, the variable sensitivity and specificity of individual antibodies, and the possibility of seronegative disease. Integrating serology with clinical assessment, imaging, electrophysiology and histopathology yields the most accurate diagnoses and informs appropriate, timely management.

Chapter 5: Drug- and Toxin-Induced Myopathies and Rhabdomyolysis

Introduction

Drug- and toxin-induced muscle disorders form a clinically and pathophysiologically heterogeneous group of conditions that range from mild, transient myalgias to life‑threatening rhabdomyolysis with acute kidney injury. For clinicians and trainees, recognizing the spectrum of presentations, understanding the mechanisms by which pharmaceuticals and toxins damage muscle, and applying evidence‑based approaches to diagnosis and management are essential. This chapter reviews definitions and the clinical spectrum, the major pathogenic mechanisms, classic offending agents, features that help distinguish toxic (direct) muscle injury from immune‑mediated myopathy, acute management strategies for rhabdomyolysis and severe myopathy, and principles for rechallenge, causality assessment, and adverse‑event reporting.

# Definitions and spectrum: myalgias, myositis, necrotizing myopathy, rhabdomyolysis

Drug- and toxin‑related muscle disease can present along a continuum. Myalgias describe subjective muscle pain without necessary objective weakness or marked biochemical abnormalities. Patients with myalgias may have normal or mildly elevated muscle enzymes and typically retain normal strength on examination. Myalgia is a nonspecific symptom and may reflect mild inflammatory changes, metabolic intolerance of muscle, or peripheral neuropathic pain referred to muscles.

Myositis denotes active inflammation of muscle manifested clinically by proximal symmetric weakness, myopathic features on electrodiagnostic testing, inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy, and typically elevated serum creatine kinase (CK). In drug‑associated myositis the inflammatory infiltrate can be lymphocytic and sometimes indistinguishable from idiopathic inflammatory myopathies; it may be either directly drug triggered or represent an immune‑mediated process unmasked by medication exposure.

Necrotizing myopathy is a histopathologic pattern characterized by myofiber necrosis with minimal or absent inflammatory cell infiltrate. Clinically it presents with progressive weakness and markedly elevated CK. In the context of drugs, immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), such as statin‑associated anti‑HMGCR myopathy, is an important subset: despite drug discontinuation, muscle necrosis continues due to an autoantibody‑mediated process and often requires immunotherapy.

Rhabdomyolysis is the extreme end of the spectrum and refers to the breakdown of skeletal muscle with release of intracellular constituents—myoglobin, CK, potassium, phosphate, uric acid—into the circulation. Clinical features range from muscle pain, swelling and weakness to systemic complications: acute kidney injury (AKI) from myoglobin nephrotoxicity, electrolyte disturbances (notably hyperkalemia), disseminated intravascular coagulation, and compartment syndrome. Laboratory criteria for rhabdomyolysis are not universally standardized, but many clinicians use a CK elevation several times above the upper limit of normal (commonly CK > 5 times ULN, often CK > 1,000 U/L) in the appropriate clinical context as supportive of rhabdomyolysis. Typical normal CK reference ranges depend on lab and sex; approximate normal ranges are 30–200 U/L for men and 30–135 U/L for women. Myoglobinuria causing a positive urine dipstick for blood with few red cells on microscopy is a classic diagnostic clue.

# Mechanisms of drug-induced muscle injury: direct toxicity, immune-mediated mechanisms, metabolic/mitochondrial dysfunction

Understanding mechanisms helps rationalize differences in presentation, expected course after drug cessation, and therapeutic approaches.

Direct toxic injury occurs when drugs or metabolites exert structural or functional damage to myocytes. Some agents disrupt sarcolemma integrity causing increased permeability and leakage of intracellular contents; others impair excitation–contraction coupling or calcium homeostasis, producing sustained cytosolic calcium elevation that activates proteases and phospholipases leading to necrosis. Direct toxins include alcohol, heroin, cocaine, and certain statins at high concentrations in predisposed individuals. Amphotericin and certain chemotherapeutics can also cause direct muscle injury. Direct toxic injury often has a relatively acute onset related temporally to exposure and frequently improves after removal of the offending agent, provided irreversible damage has not occurred.

Immune‑mediated mechanisms involve drug‑induced dysregulation of immune tolerance or drug‑related modification of self‑antigens leading to an adaptive immune response against muscle. Drugs can act as haptens or modify muscle proteins, creating neoantigens that provoke T‑cell or B‑cell responses. Statins exemplify this: in rare cases, statin exposure induces an antibody response to 3‑hydroxy‑3‑methylglutaryl‑CoA reductase (HMGCR), leading to an antibody‑mediated necrotizing myopathy that persists despite statin withdrawal. Other medications (e.g., immune checkpoint inhibitors) can trigger polymyositis‑like syndromes or autoimmune myositis. Immune‑mediated myopathies typically have subacute to chronic progressive weakness, persist or worsen after drug discontinuation, demonstrate markedly elevated CK, and respond to immunosuppressive therapy rather than simple withdrawal.

Metabolic and mitochondrial dysfunction is another important category. Drugs that impair energy production, fatty acid oxidation, or mitochondrial respiratory chain function can render muscle unable to meet energy demands. Examples include fibrates with statins producing impaired mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, zidovudine causing mitochondrial DNA depletion, and valproic acid interfering with beta‑oxidation. In these cases, muscle may tolerate basal activity but becomes susceptible to injury with exertion, fever, or fasting. Histology often shows ragged‑red fibers or subsarcolemmal mitochondrial aggregates, and clinical features can include exercise intolerance, myalgias, and elevated CK. The onset may be gradual, and the severity correlates with cumulative mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic stressors.

Other contributory mechanisms include drug–drug interactions that raise systemic concentrations of myotoxic agents (pharmacokinetic), genetic predisposition (e.g., polymorphisms in SLCO1B1 increasing statin myopathy risk), and comorbid conditions such as hypothyroidism, renal impairment, or sepsis that increase susceptibility to muscle injury.

# Classic drug culprits: statins (and statin-associated immune-mediated necrotising myopathy), corticosteroids, antipsychotics, illicit drugs, statin plus interacting medications

Several agents and classes are notably associated with muscle toxicity.

Statins are the most commonly implicated prescription drugs for muscle complaints. Most statin‑related adverse effects are benign myalgias or self‑limited myopathy with modest CK elevations. Risk increases with higher statin dose, lipophilicity (simvastatin, atorvastatin more than pravastatin or rosuvastatin in some reports), older age, female sex, hypothyroidism, renal or hepatic dysfunction, and use of interacting drugs (notably strong CYP3A4 inhibitors and certain transport inhibitors). Statin‑associated immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (anti‑HMGCR myopathy) is rare but distinctive: patients develop progressive proximal weakness and very high CK (often > 3,000–10,000 U/L), autoantibodies to HMGCR are detectable in many cases, and weakness persists or worsens after statin withdrawal. Muscle biopsy characteristically shows widespread necrosis with minimal inflammation. Management requires immunosuppression.

Corticosteroids classically cause a steroid myopathy characterized by painless proximal muscle weakness with relative preservation of CK; the pathogenesis relates to steroid‑induced muscle protein catabolism, reduced synthesis of myosin, and type II fiber atrophy. Onset may be after weeks to months of systemic steroid therapy and is dose‑dependent; high‑dose or chronic use is more likely. In contrast to inflammatory myopathies, steroid myopathy shows low or normal CK, and muscle biopsy demonstrates selective type II fiber atrophy with minimal inflammation. Improvement occurs with tapering of steroids and rehabilitation.

Antipsychotics and other dopamine‑blockers can induce neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS), a syndrome of severe rigidity, hyperthermia, autonomic instability, and elevated CK that can culminate in rhabdomyolysis. NMS pathophysiology is complex and involves central dopamine blockade leading to sustained muscle rigidity and hypermetabolism; management is supportive with sedation, cooling, and in some cases dantrolene or bromocriptine.

Illicit drugs and alcohol are frequent causes of toxic rhabdomyolysis. Alcohol can cause direct myotoxicity and predispose to prolonged immobilization and seizures. Cocaine, amphetamines, and other sympathomimetics cause vasoconstriction and muscle ischemia as well as direct myocyte toxicity, raising risk of rhabdomyolysis. Opioid or sedative intoxication increases risk via prolonged immobilization and pressure‑induced ischemic injury.

Statin plus interacting medications merit special attention. Drugs that inhibit statin metabolism or hepatic uptake elevate statin plasma concentration and risk myotoxicity. Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g., certain macrolides such as clarithromycin, azole antifungals like itraconazole, protease inhibitors) raise levels of simvastatin, lovastatin, and atorvastatin. Inhibitors of the hepatic uptake transporter OATP1B1 (encoded by SLCO1B1), such as cyclosporine or gemfibrozil (which also impairs statin glucuronidation), markedly increase risk, with gemfibrozil plus cerivastatin famously causing fatal rhabdomyolysis historically. Clinically significant interactions are the combination of statins with fibrates, especially gemfibrozil, which can produce severe myotoxicity.

# Clinical features differentiating toxic rhabdomyolysis vs immune-mediated myopathy (time course, CK trends, response to drug withdrawal)

Distinguishing toxic (direct) muscle injury and rhabdomyolysis from immune‑mediated drug‑related myopathies is critical because management differs—withdrawal and supportive care is typically sufficient for toxic injury, whereas immune‑mediated disease often requires immunosuppression.

Time course: Toxic rhabdomyolysis often has an acute onset within hours to days of exposure, overdose, immobilization, strenuous exertion, or secondary systemic stressors (seizure, hyperthermia). The precipitant is usually evident, and symptoms evolve rapidly. In contrast, immune‑mediated myopathies related to drugs often have a subacute or insidious onset over weeks to months. For example, statin‑associated IMNM may develop weeks to years after exposure, and symptoms continue or progress after the statin is stopped.

CK trends: In toxic rhabdomyolysis CK levels typically rise rapidly, often reaching very high values (several thousand to > 100,000 U/L in extreme cases), then decline after cessation of the inciting factor and with supportive measures. The CK half‑life is approximately 1.5 days; thus, with cessation of injury, levels fall predictably. Conversely, immune‑mediated myopathies often show persistent or relapsing elevations in CK despite drug discontinuation. In IMNM, CK may be persistently elevated until immunotherapy is instituted.

Clinical response to drug withdrawal: Toxic myopathies and rhabdomyolysis commonly improve after removal of the offending agent combined with appropriate supportive care; muscle strength and CK typically recover. Immune‑mediated myopathies generally fail to remit with drug withdrawal alone; progressive weakness and ongoing necrosis are common, and immunosuppressive therapy is usually necessary to halt the immune process. Histologically, toxic rhabdomyolysis shows necrosis and breakdown, sometimes with regeneration, whereas immune‑mediated disease shows characteristic patterns—necrotizing myopathy with minimal inflammation for IMNM or lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates in classic polymyositis.

Other distinguishing features include systemic signs: NMS‑related rhabdomyolysis will be accompanied by fever, marked rigidity, and autonomic dysfunction, while immune myopathies are not typically hyperthermic unless there is superimposed infection. Electromyography (EMG) in toxic and inflammatory myopathies often shows myopathic motor unit potentials and fibrillation potentials, but the pattern is nonspecific. Muscle biopsy and serology (myositis‑specific antibodies such as anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) are most helpful in diagnosing immune causes.

# Management of rhabdomyolysis and acute drug-induced myopathy: hydration, electrolyte management, renal support

Initial management focuses on stabilization, preventing and treating complications (especially hyperkalemia and AKI), and addressing the underlying cause.

Early assessment: Measure serum CK, electrolytes (potassium, calcium, phosphate), renal function (BUN, creatinine), urinalysis for blood and myoglobinuria, and baseline ECG. Reassess serially. Look for precipitating factors (seizure, immobilization, heat stroke, trauma, known offending drugs) and discontinue any suspected myotoxin immediately.

Volume resuscitation: Aggressive intravenous fluid administration is the cornerstone of rhabdomyolysis management to maintain renal perfusion, dilute nephrotoxic pigments, and promote urinary excretion of myoglobin. Isotonic crystalloid (normal saline) is commonly used. While randomized controlled trial data are limited, practical recommendations often target an initial bolus of 1–2 L followed by infusion rates adjusted to achieve a urine output of approximately 200–300 mL/hour in adults (a frequently cited target is urine output > 0.5–1 mL/kg/hour for general AKI prevention, and some protocols use higher targets for rhabdomyolysis of 200–300 mL/hour). These are clinical targets and must be individualized for patients at risk of volume overload (e.g., heart failure). Close monitoring for pulmonary edema and volume status is necessary.

Urine alkalinization and diuretics: Urine alkalinization with intravenous sodium bicarbonate has been used to reduce myoglobin precipitation in renal tubules and mitigate free iron‑mediated oxidative injury. Recommendations are heterogeneous: it is often considered for patients with severe rhabdomyolysis (very high CK, dark urine, oliguria) though high‑quality evidence is lacking. Typical regimens use bicarbonate to achieve urine pH > 6.5. Mannitol has been proposed as an osmotic diuretic and free radical scavenger, but routine use is not universally endorsed. Diuretics should not be used as a substitute for adequate volume resuscitation.

Electrolyte management: Monitor and treat hyperkalemia promptly; hyperkalemia is the most immediately life‑threatening metabolic disturbance. Follow established protocols: intravenous calcium gluconate for membrane stabilization if ECG changes or severe hyperkalemia, insulin with glucose and inhaled or nebulized beta‑agonists to shift potassium intracellularly, and sodium bicarbonate if acidotic. Consider emergent renal replacement therapy (RRT) for refractory hyperkalemia, severe acidosis, or volume overload. Hypocalcemia is common early and typically corrects as muscle injury resolves; asymptomatic hypocalcemia is usually not treated acutely because correcting calcium can predispose to calcium deposition in injured muscle and worsen outcomes. Hyperphosphatemia may need to be controlled with phosphate binders in prolonged illness. Monitor uric acid; hyperuricemia can occur and contribute to AKI.

Renal support: Indications for dialysis include refractory hyperkalemia, severe metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.1) despite medical therapy, volume overload not responsive to diuretics, or established oliguric AKI with worsening uremia. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is frequently chosen for hemodynamically unstable patients; conventional dialysis clears small solutes but removes myoglobin variably. Intermittent hemodialysis does not reliably remove myoglobin because myoglobin is a medium molecular weight protein that tends to be protein bound, but modern high‑cutoff filters and hemoadsorption techniques may augment removal in selected centers.

Supportive care and monitoring: Provide analgesia, manage complications (compartment syndrome may require fasciotomy), and treat underlying causes (control hyperthermia, seizures, arrhythmias). Serial CK and renal function monitoring guide ongoing care. Physical therapy should be initiated early once the acute phase resolves to prevent deconditioning and facilitate recovery in nonnecrotic myopathies.

Management of immune‑mediated drug myopathy: If immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy is suspected (e.g., persistent weakness and high CK after statin cessation), initiate workup for myositis‑specific autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP), and consider muscle MRI and biopsy. Treatment is with immunosuppression—high‑dose corticosteroids often combined with steroid‑sparing agents such as methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, IVIG, or rituximab depending on severity and response. These therapies are guided by disease severity, comorbidities, and specialist consultation. Early recognition and institution of immunotherapy are important to prevent irreversible muscle loss.

# Principles of rechallenge, causality assessment and reporting adverse drug reactions

Decisions about rechallenging a drug that may have caused myopathy require careful risk–benefit analysis, consideration of alternative therapies, and patient preferences.

Rechallenge: In cases of mild, transient myalgias without significant CK elevation and minimal functional impairment, rechallenge at a lower dose or switching to a different agent within the same therapeutic class may be reasonable, particularly when the drug confers a clear clinical benefit (e.g., statins for secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease). For statin‑related myalgias, strategies include lowering dose, switching to a hydrophilic statin (pravastatin, rosuvastatin), alternate day dosing, or use of non‑statin lipid‑lowering agents (ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors) if intolerance persists. Rechallenge should be deferred or approached cautiously if there is a history of severe myopathy, CK > 10 times ULN, rhabdomyolysis, or documented immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy; in IMNM, rechallenge with the same statin is contraindicated because the immune process targets HMGCR. When rechallenge is attempted, it should occur after biochemical and clinical recovery with close monitoring of symptoms and CK.

Causality assessment: Several structured tools assist in assessing the likelihood that a drug caused an adverse reaction. The Naranjo algorithm is a commonly used questionnaire that assigns a probability score (definite, probable, possible, or doubtful) based on temporal relation, dechallenge and rechallenge, alternative causes, and objective evidence. The World Health Organization–Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO‑UMC) system is another standardized method. Causality assessment must integrate clinical judgment: temporality (onset after exposure), biologic plausibility, dose‑response relationship, dechallenge (improvement after withdrawal), rechallenge (recurrence on re‑exposure), exclusion of other causes (metabolic, infectious, traumatic), and objective evidence (serologic antibodies, biopsy findings).

Reporting adverse drug reactions: Suspected drug‑induced myopathies and rhabdomyolysis should be reported to local pharmacovigilance authorities and national programs (e.g., FDA MedWatch in the United States, Yellow Card Scheme in the U.K.). Reporting serves public health surveillance, helps detect rare but serious adverse effects, and can lead to evidence‑based labeling changes. Reports should include detailed clinical information: timing of drug exposure, dose, concomitant medications, laboratory data (CK, electrolytes, renal function), diagnostic studies (EMG, biopsy), management provided, and outcome. When immune‑mediated myopathy is suspected, specialized tests (myositis antibodies) and biopsy results strengthen reports.

Informed consent and patient counseling are key. Patients should be informed about the signs and symptoms that warrant urgent reassessment (severe muscle pain, profound weakness, dark urine, palpitations or syncope suggesting hyperkalemia) and about safer alternative therapies when available.

Conclusion

Drug‑ and toxin‑induced muscle disorders span a broad clinical and pathophysiologic spectrum. Distinguishing direct toxic injury and rhabdomyolysis from immune‑mediated myopathies guides appropriate management: immediate withdrawal and supportive care for toxic injury versus immunosuppression for immune‑mediated processes. Recognition of classic culprits, appreciation of pharmacokinetic interactions (especially with statins), and early, targeted interventions to prevent or treat complications such as hyperkalemia and AKI are essential. Systematic causality assessment, cautious approaches to rechallenge, and timely reporting of adverse events help optimize patient safety and inform public health.

Chapter 6: Pharmacology of Glucagon‑Like Peptide‑1 (GLP‑1) Receptor Agonists

Introduction

Glucagon‑like peptide‑1 (GLP‑1) receptor agonists are a class of incretin‑based therapies that have transformed the management of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and obesity and have emerging roles in cardiovascular risk reduction. Their actions span multiple organ systems because the GLP‑1 receptor is expressed in pancreatic islets, the central nervous system, the gastrointestinal tract, the heart, vasculature, kidney, and other tissues. Clinically, GLP‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs) lower plasma glucose by enhancing glucose‑dependent insulin secretion and suppressing glucagon, slow gastric emptying, and reduce appetite and body weight. This chapter reviews the physiological basis of GLP‑1 action and the pharmacology of GLP‑1 RAs, compares representative agents and their pharmacokinetic profiles, summarizes approved and off‑label therapeutic indications including cardiovascular outcome data, and examines the adverse effect profile with attention to multisystem consequences—including gastrointestinal effects, pancreatitis risk, effects on appetite and body composition, rare muscle injury reports, and renal complications driven by volume depletion and interactions with other drugs. The goal is to provide a clinically useful reference that links mechanism to patient management.

# Physiologic role of GLP‑1 and mechanism of GLP‑1 receptor agonists

GLP‑1 is an incretin hormone secreted primarily by L‑cells in the distal small intestine and colon in response to nutrient ingestion. Endogenous GLP‑1 circulates in multiple active forms but is rapidly inactivated by the enzyme dipeptidyl peptidase‑4 (DPP‑4), giving it a plasma half‑life on the order of 1–2 minutes. Physiologic effects of GLP‑1 are mediated by the GLP‑1 receptor, a G protein‑coupled receptor that signals predominantly through Gs to increase intracellular cyclic AMP and has downstream effects on ion channels and gene expression.

In pancreatic β cells, GLP‑1 enhances glucose‑dependent insulin secretion by increasing cAMP and potentiating ATP‑sensitive potassium channel closure and voltage‑dependent calcium influx. This amplifies insulin release when ambient glucose is elevated but has little effect in hypoglycemia, which underlies the low intrinsic hypoglycemia risk of GLP‑1 RAs when used alone. In α cells, GLP‑1 suppresses glucagon secretion, reducing hepatic glucose output. GLP‑1 also slows gastric emptying through vagal and enteric neuronal pathways and direct effects on the stomach, which blunts postprandial glucose excursions. Central actions in the hypothalamus and brainstem reduce appetite and food intake, contributing to weight loss.

GLP‑1 receptors are present in multiple non‑pancreatic tissues. In the cardiovascular system, GLP‑1 RAs can have modest positive inotropic effects, improve endothelial function, and alter inflammatory pathways. Renal effects include natriuresis and changes in renal hemodynamics mediated by both direct and indirect mechanisms. The widespread receptor distribution explains why GLP‑1 RAs produce multisystem effects—therapeutic and adverse.

Pharmacologically, GLP‑1 receptor agonists are peptide drugs that resist DPP‑4 degradation (either by modification of native GLP‑1 structure or by being structurally distinct yet receptor‑active). They bind and activate the GLP‑1 receptor, producing the physiologic effects described above but with extended duration to enable therapeutic dosing intervals ranging from twice daily to once weekly or daily oral dosing (for an oral formulation). Because their insulinotropic action is glucose‑dependent, the risk of hypoglycemia is low unless combined with agents that independently increase hypoglycemia risk (sulfonylureas, insulin).

# Representative agents, dosing forms (short‑acting vs long‑acting) and pharmacokinetics

A number of GLP‑1 RAs are available; they differ in molecular structure, pharmacokinetic properties, route and frequency of administration, and clinical effects. Representative agents include exenatide (twice daily immediate‑release and once‑weekly extended‑release formulations), liraglutide (daily), lixisenatide (daily), dulaglutide (weekly), semaglutide (weekly subcutaneous and daily oral formulations), and albiglutide (formerly used but now withdrawn in some regions). Some agents are peptide analogues of native GLP‑1 with amino acid substitutions to prevent DPP‑4 cleavage and/or fusion to larger proteins or albumin‑binding moieties to prolong half‑life.

Clinically it is helpful to distinguish short‑acting from long‑acting GLP‑1 RAs. Short‑acting agents (for example, immediate‑release exenatide and lixisenatide) have prominent effects on gastric emptying and thus particularly reduce postprandial glucose excursions. Their half‑lives are on the order of hours, and they are typically dosed twice daily or once daily around meals. Long‑acting agents (for example, liraglutide, dulaglutide, semaglutide, weekly exenatide extended‑release) have more sustained receptor activation, greater effects on fasting glucose and overall hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and somewhat attenuated effects on gastric emptying because tachyphylaxis to gastric emptying occurs with continuous receptor stimulation. Long‑acting agents are dosed once daily or once weekly.

Pharmacokinetic parameters vary by agent. For example, liraglutide (daily subcutaneous) has a half‑life of about 13 hours allowing once‑daily dosing; dulaglutide and weekly semaglutide formulations have effective half‑lives that permit once‑weekly dosing. Oral semaglutide is formulated with an absorption enhancer to permit transmucosal uptake and requires fasting administration with limited water and post‑dose fasting to ensure bioavailability. Bioavailability of peptide agents administered subcutaneously is high compared with oral protein absorption and is not subject to first‑pass hepatic metabolism. Clearance mechanisms include proteolytic degradation and, for agents with albumin‑binding, protected distribution that extends half‑life.

From a clinical perspective, dosing initiation typically uses stepwise titration to mitigate gastrointestinal adverse effects. For example, many agents start at a lower dose for 1–2 weeks before escalating to the therapeutic dose. The exact doses and titration schedules are agent‑specific and should be followed according to product labeling.

# Therapeutic indications: type 2 diabetes, weight management, cardiovascular outcomes

Type 2 diabetes mellitus: GLP‑1 RAs are approved for glycemic control in T2DM, either as monotherapy or in combination with oral agents and insulin. Their glucose‑lowering effect reduces HbA1c by approximately 0.5–1.5 percentage points depending on baseline A1c, agent, and dosing. They are particularly useful when weight gain is undesirable, as most agents produce clinically meaningful weight loss. GLP‑1 RAs are recommended in modern diabetes guidelines both for their glycemic efficacy and for their cardiovascular and weight benefits; selection among agents depends on patient comorbidities, tolerability, route preference, and cost.

Weight management: Several GLP‑1 RAs have been approved specifically for chronic weight management at higher (or differently labeled) doses than those used for diabetes. These agents promote sustained reductions in body weight through reduced appetite, increased satiety, and decreased energy intake. Typical weight loss in clinical trials varies by agent and dose; higher potency agents (e.g., higher‑dose semaglutide) have produced mean weight reductions of 10%–15% or more in controlled trials, with individual variability. In addition to absolute weight reduction, GLP‑1 RAs alter body composition, reducing fat mass more than lean mass in most studies (see section on muscle mass below).

Cardiovascular outcomes: Because many glucose therapies have neutral or adverse cardiovascular effects, GLP‑1 RAs have been evaluated in large cardiovascular outcomes trials. Several agents have demonstrated a reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE: composite of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and nonfatal stroke) in patients with established cardiovascular disease or at high cardiovascular risk. Benefit appears to be more consistent with certain longer‑acting agents and may relate to direct vascular and anti‑inflammatory effects, as well as indirect benefits from weight loss, blood pressure reduction, and improved glycemic control. Not all GLP‑1 RAs have demonstrated identical cardiovascular benefit, and when prescribing for cardiovascular risk reduction clinicians should use agents with proven benefit in trials for that indication.

Other investigational and off‑label uses: GLP‑1 RAs have been explored for nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), and neuroprotection in selected settings; however, routine use outside approved indications should be based on evidence and regulatory guidance.

# Common adverse effects: gastrointestinal symptoms, nausea/vomiting, pancreatitis risk, effects on appetite and weight

Gastrointestinal effects: The most frequent adverse effects of GLP‑1 RAs are gastrointestinal. Nausea is the most common complaint, often occurring during initiation and dose escalation, and typically diminishing over weeks to months. Vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation are also reported. Short‑acting agents that slow gastric emptying more intensely can produce more pronounced nausea and delayed gastric emptying symptoms initially. Because these effects are central and peripheral (gastric), they often respond to slower titration, antiemetic measures, and supportive care. Severe, persistent vomiting that results in volume depletion or electrolyte disturbances warrants prompt evaluation and possible dose interruption.

Pancreatitis risk: Cases of acute pancreatitis have been reported in patients receiving GLP‑1 RAs, leading to concern about a causal relationship. Large randomized trials and observational studies have produced mixed results; while some analyses suggest a small increased risk, other studies do not confirm a significant association. Regulatory agencies recommend caution: avoid GLP‑1 RAs in patients with a history of pancreatitis (or use individualized risk‑benefit analysis), and discontinue therapy if pancreatitis is suspected. Typical clinical guidance includes immediate cessation of the GLP‑1 RA if acute pancreatitis is diagnosed and not to restart until the etiology is clarified. Serum amylase and lipase reference ranges vary by lab; lipase elevations of several fold above the upper limit of normal (ULN) plus compatible clinical features suggest pancreatitis. Routine monitoring of pancreatic enzymes in asymptomatic patients is not generally recommended.

Appetite and weight effects: By central appetite suppression and delayed gastric emptying, GLP‑1 RAs induce decreased caloric intake. Weight loss is a common and often desirable therapeutic outcome in T2DM and obesity management. Clinicians should counsel patients regarding expected weight changes, monitor nutritional status, and assess for excessive weight loss. Weight loss is typically greatest in the first months of therapy and may plateau with continued treatment. Because weight loss includes both fat and lean mass, monitoring and interventions to preserve muscle mass (dietary protein, resistance exercise) are relevant for long‑term health, particularly in older or frail patients.

Other adverse events: Cholelithiasis and biliary disease have been reported more frequently with substantial weight loss, and some agents carry animal data showing thyroid C‑cell hyperplasia and tumors in rodents; although human risk is uncertain, GLP‑1 RAs are contraindicated in patients with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2) in several jurisdictions. Injection‑site reactions, hypersensitivity, and rare pancreatobiliary complications are described.

# Reported effects on muscle mass, rare reports of muscle injury and rhabdomyolysis

Body composition changes: Weight loss produced by GLP‑1 RAs generally preferentially reduces fat mass, but a proportion of the lost weight is lean body mass, including skeletal muscle. Clinical studies using dual‑energy X‑ray absorptiometry (DEXA) or bioelectrical impedance have consistently shown that lean mass loss comprises a variable but non‑negligible fraction of total weight loss—often around 20%–30% of the weight lost (percentage varies with study population, baseline characteristics, and duration). This is clinically relevant because loss of muscle mass can impair strength, metabolic health, and physical function, particularly in older adults or those with preexisting sarcopenia. To mitigate lean mass loss, strategies include ensuring adequate protein intake, engaging patients in resistance‑training exercise, and monitoring functional status during therapy.

Reports of myopathy and rhabdomyolysis: True myonecrosis and clinically significant rhabdomyolysis associated directly with GLP‑1 RAs appear to be rare. Case reports have described elevations in creatine kinase (CK) and instances labeled as myopathy or rhabdomyolysis temporally associated with GLP‑1 RA initiation. A direct myotoxic mechanism has not been definitively established. Several plausible mechanisms may underlie reported associations: (1) rapid weight loss leading to catabolism and increased susceptibility of muscle to injury; (2) decreased oral intake and vomiting resulting in hypovolemia, electrolyte disturbances (e.g., hypokalemia), and secondary muscle injury; (3) drug‑drug interactions—most importantly with statins and other agents known to cause myopathy—where GLP‑1 RA–induced changes in drug absorption, distribution, or renal function could alter exposure to myotoxic drugs; and (4) idiosyncratic immune‑mediated reactions. Laboratory monitoring in suspected cases should include CK, renal function tests (serum creatinine), electrolytes (potassium, phosphate, calcium), and urinalysis for myoglobinuria. Normal CK reference ranges depend on sex and laboratory method but are commonly roughly 20–200 U/L; levels >5 times the ULN warrant attention, and CK elevations accompanied by muscle pain, weakness, dark urine, or acute kidney injury suggest rhabdomyolysis requiring urgent management.

Clinical approach: When a patient on a GLP‑1 RA develops muscle pain, weakness, or notable CK elevation, consider evaluating for alternative or contributing causes (recent exercise, trauma, seizures, alcohol use, hypothyroidism, statin use). Temporarily holding the GLP‑1 RA (and any concomitant myotoxic medications) until the diagnosis is clarified is prudent. If rhabdomyolysis is confirmed, standard management with aggressive intravenous fluids, electrolyte correction, and monitoring for renal complications is indicated; decision to reintroduce GLP‑1 RA should be made cautiously and on a case‑by‑case basis.

# Renal adverse events secondary to volume depletion and GI losses; interaction with other drugs

Renal risks related to volume depletion: GLP‑1 RAs do not typically have intrinsic nephrotoxic effects, but renal adverse events can arise indirectly. Severe vomiting, diarrhea, or reduced oral intake can cause dehydration and pre‑renal azotemia; in susceptible patients (elderly, baseline chronic kidney disease [CKD], on diuretics or renin‑angiotensin system blockers), this can progress to acute kidney injury (AKI). There are reported cases of AKI temporally linked to GLP‑1 RA use where the precipitant was persistent gastrointestinal losses leading to volume depletion. Clinicians should counsel patients about maintaining hydration, recognizing signs of volume depletion, and seeking care for severe gastrointestinal symptoms.

Monitoring and risk stratification: Baseline renal function assessment (serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]) is reasonable before starting therapy in patients with risk factors for renal dysfunction. Common thresholds: eGFR values ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m^2 indicate normal or mildly decreased kidney function; stage 3 CKD corresponds to eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m^2, stage 4 to 15–29 mL/min/1.73 m^2. Most GLP‑1 RAs can be used in patients with mild to moderate CKD, but some agents have specific labeling and dosing considerations at lower eGFRs. If a patient develops AKI or severe dehydration, the GLP‑1 RA should be temporarily stopped until renal function stabilizes. Medication reconciliation is important because volume depletion can potentiate adverse effects of other drugs (e.g., NSAIDs, diuretics, ACE inhibitors) and may necessitate temporary dose adjustments.

Drug interactions mediated by gastrointestinal effects: GLP‑1 RAs slow gastric emptying, which can alter the pharmacokinetics of orally administered medications. This is particularly relevant for drugs where timing of peak concentration affects efficacy or safety (e.g., oral contraceptives, certain antibiotics, anticoagulants). Oral medications with narrow therapeutic windows may have delayed or reduced absorption; for antiplatelet agents or oral anticoagulants such as warfarin, altered absorption could theoretically change INR, although clinically significant interactions are uncommon and individualized monitoring is prudent. For oral semaglutide specifically, administration instructions (fasting, limited water) are designed to maximize absorption; concomitant ingestion of other oral medications shortly before or after oral semaglutide can reduce their absorption or that of semaglutide itself.

Interactions with renally cleared drugs and statins: Because GLP‑1 RA–related volume depletion can precipitate AKI, this may impair clearance of renally eliminated drugs, necessitating dose adjustments. Agents that are cleared renally and have narrow therapeutic windows (e.g., certain antibiotics, digoxin in older people) should be reviewed. Statins are among the most widely co‑prescribed drugs in patients with T2DM and cardiovascular risk. Although GLP‑1 RAs do not directly interact with statins pharmacokinetically, occurrences of muscle complaints in patients on combined therapy should prompt evaluation for statin‑related myopathy with consideration of the potential for additive risk from dehydration, electrolyte disturbances, or unmasking of a predisposition to myopathy during weight loss. Temporarily stopping statins or GLP‑1 RAs may be necessary if severe muscle injury is suspected.

Practical management points: When initiating GLP‑1 RA therapy in a patient on diuretics, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, NSAIDs, or other agents that influence renal perfusion, consider baseline renal function and counsel regarding volume status. For patients with baseline CKD or on multiple nephrotoxic medications, closer follow‑up of renal function after initiation and during dose escalation is reasonable. When severe gastrointestinal adverse effects occur, clinicians should evaluate for dehydration and AKI and hold GLP‑1 RA until the patient is euvolemic and renal function has recovered.

Conclusion

GLP‑1 receptor agonists offer potent glucose‑lowering, weight‑reducing, and cardioprotective benefits in appropriately selected patients. Their multisystem actions underlie both therapeutic efficacy and a characteristic adverse‑effect profile centered on the gastrointestinal tract, appetite regulation, and indirect effects on the pancreas, kidney, and muscle. Understanding the physiologic mechanisms—glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, glucagon suppression, slowed gastric emptying, and central appetite suppression—helps clinicians anticipate benefits and risks, choose among agents (short‑acting versus long‑acting; subcutaneous versus oral), and manage complications. Key clinical considerations include careful titration to minimize nausea, vigilance for severe gastrointestinal losses that can precipitate dehydration and AKI, awareness of rare but important reports of pancreatitis and muscle injury, and assessment of drug interactions arising from altered gastric emptying or changes in renal function. Preservation of lean mass during weight loss and individualized monitoring in patients with comorbid conditions are important components of safe, effective use.

Introduction

Muscle biopsy remains a cornerstone diagnostic procedure in the evaluation of patients with suspected inflammatory myopathies and many other myopathic disorders. When interpreted in the context of clinical presentation, electromyography, laboratory tests including creatine kinase (CK) and disease-specific autoantibodies, and imaging, the histopathologic pattern observed on a skeletal muscle sample can distinguish among immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), polymyositis (PM), dermatomyositis (DM), inclusion body myositis (IBM) and metabolic or dystrophic processes. This chapter provides a practical, pathology-oriented discussion for the clinician and trainee: when and how to perform and handle a muscle biopsy, the key light microscopic features to assess, the role of special histochemical stains and immunohistochemistry, patterns that characterize major inflammatory myopathies, the meaning of complement deposition and macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis, and the limitations of muscle biopsy with guidance on integrating histology with clinical and serologic data.

# Indications, technique and sample handling for muscle biopsy

Indications
Muscle biopsy is indicated when there is diagnostic uncertainty after history, exam, CK and other labs, and when histology will alter management. Typical indications include: unexplained proximal or distal weakness, elevated CK (commonly >3 times the upper limit of normal; normal CK varies by lab but is generally ~20–200 U/L for adults), suspected inflammatory myopathy, suspected muscular dystrophy or mitochondrial disease, atypical neuropathies where a myopathic process is possible, and when EMG or MRI suggest focal or patchy disease. Biopsy may also be required to evaluate myopathies in which serologies are negative or non-diagnostic.

Technique
Open (surgical) biopsy and percutaneous needle biopsy are both used. Open biopsy yields larger tissue, facilitating multiple studies (fresh frozen, fixed for electron microscopy, formalin-fixed paraffin), and is preferred when an extensive panel of histochemistry and immunostains is anticipated. Needle biopsy (Bergstrom) is less invasive and acceptable when adequate tissue can be obtained and when experienced pathology services are available. The choice depends on local expertise, patient factors and the suspected diagnosis.

Selecting the biopsy site
Select an affected but not end-stage muscle. Muscles with mild-to-moderate weakness and recent changes on imaging (MRI STIR hyperintensity) or EMG abnormalities are ideal. Avoid very weak, profoundly fibrotic or fatty-replaced muscles (long-standing disease) and avoid muscles recently injected with steroids into the site. Commonly biopsied muscles include vastus lateralis, deltoid, biceps brachii and tibialis anterior; pattern of clinical weakness guides selection.

Sample handling
Proper handling is critical because many diagnostic stains require fresh frozen tissue. A typical protocol:
- Divide the sample immediately at bedside. One piece (optimally 50–100 mg, depending on lab needs) should be rapidly frozen for histochemistry and immunohistochemistry. Standard freezing uses isopentane cooled in liquid nitrogen to avoid freeze artifacts; snap-freezing directly into liquid nitrogen is discouraged due to cracking.
- Place the frozen tissue on cork or embedding medium and store at −80°C or transport on dry ice. Do not fix the portion intended for enzyme histochemistry or immunofluorescence.
- Place a second portion in 10% neutral buffered formalin for paraffin embedding and routine H&E, and a small piece (1–2 mm^3) in glutaraldehyde for electron microscopy if ultrastructural evaluation is needed.
- If immunofluorescence for complement or immunoglobulin deposition is planned, consider placing a small sample in Michel’s media or other transport medium specified by the laboratory.
- Communicate with the pathology laboratory before the procedure to confirm required sample sizes and fixation methods.

Documentation and clinical information
Provide a concise clinical summary: age, pattern of weakness, CK level and trend, current therapies (especially immunosuppressive agents or statins), recent infections or vaccinations, MRI/EMG findings, and relevant serologies (anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP, myositis-specific antibodies). This clinical context guides the pathologist’s panel of stains and interpretation.

Complications and follow-up
Muscle biopsy is low risk but can produce pain, hematoma, infection, or neuropathic injury if neurovascular structures are nearby. Ensure hemostasis and counsel patients about wound care. Recognize that pre-biopsy corticosteroids or immune therapy may modify histologic findings and decrease sensitivity; when feasible, obtain biopsy before initiating immunosuppression.

# Light microscopy features: necrosis, regeneration, fiber size variation, inflammation, perifascicular changes

The pathologist’s first step is assessment on routine H&E and various histochemical stains to characterize the pattern of muscle fiber injury. Several morphologic features have particular interpretive value.

Necrosis and myofiber degeneration
Necrotic fibers are pale, fragmented and often infiltrated by inflammatory cells and macrophages. Early necrosis may show loss of cross-striations and sarcoplasmic pallor; later stages show phagocytosis and removal. The presence, distribution and extent of necrosis help separate IMNM (prominent necrosis with relatively sparse lymphocytic inflammation) from other entities. Note that necrosis can be focal and patchy—sampling error is a concern.

Regeneration
Regenerating fibers are basophilic with centralized or internal nuclei and often increased expression of developmental myosin heavy chain (detectable with immunostains). Small angular fibers suggest chronic denervation, while centralized nuclei in groups suggest a dystrophic or regenerative process. The coexistence of extensive regeneration with necrosis favors active destructive myopathy.

Fiber size variation
Variation in myofiber diameter—atrophy and hypertrophy—is a hallmark of chronic muscle pathology. Grouped atrophy suggests denervation, while random atrophy is more typical of inflammatory myopathies. Marked fiber size variability with end-stage fibrofatty replacement suggests chronic dystrophy.

Inflammation: location and cell types
Characterize inflammatory infiltrates by their location: endomysial (between individual fibers), perimysial (in connective tissue septa), perivascular, or perimysial and perifascicular. In PM and IBM, endomysial inflammatory infiltrates composed predominantly of CD8+ T cells invade intact, non-necrotic fibers. In DM, inflammation is often perivascular and perimysial, with a predilection for perifascicular regions. IMNM shows necrosis with macrophage-rich myophagocytosis and comparatively sparse lymphocytic infiltrates. Quantify inflammatory burden and note whether inflammatory cells are in direct contact with non-necrotic fibers—this is a key differentiator.

Perifascicular changes
Perifascicular atrophy (atrophy confined to fibers at the fascicle periphery) is classic for dermatomyositis and correlates with microvascular injury and ischemia. Perifascicular necrosis can be seen in severe DM and some immune necrotizing myopathies. Distinguish perifascicular atrophy from focal neurogenic atrophy and from artifact.

Additional features
- Endomysial fibrosis and fatty infiltration indicate chronicity.
- Vacuoles, basophilic inclusions, and rimmed vacuoles suggest inclusion body myositis or certain dystrophic processes.
- Mitochondrial changes—ragged-red fibers (Gomori trichrome) and cytochrome c oxidase (COX)-negative fibers—indicate mitochondrial dysfunction and are particularly relevant to IBM and mitochondrial myopathies.
- Myofibrillar disorganization and cytoplasmic protein aggregates require special stains and immunohistochemistry (p62, TDP-43, ubiquitin).

# Special stains and immunohistochemistry: CD68 (macrophages), CD3 (T cells), MHC class I, acid phosphatase, membrane attack complex (C5b-9)

Special histochemical stains and immunohistochemistry extend the diagnostic yield of routine microscopy by identifying cell types, enzyme activities, mitochondrial function and complement or membrane antigen expression.

Histochemical stains
- Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) is the initial screen.
- Modified Gomori trichrome highlights ragged-red fibers and inclusion bodies.
- NADH-TR, succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), and cytochrome c oxidase (COX) assess oxidative enzyme activity and mitochondrial architecture; COX-negative fibers and subsarcolemmal aggregates are important in IBM and mitochondrial disorders.
- ATPase stains (pH preincubation) define fiber-type grouping and type I/II distribution, useful for detecting neurogenic change.
- Acid phosphatase (lysosomal phosphatase) is increased in areas of macrophage activity and in autophagic vacuoles; strong staining may accompany myophagocytosis and inflammatory lysosomal activity.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence
- CD68 (KP1) labels macrophages. In inflammatory myopathies, abundant CD68+ macrophages are seen in IMNM and in necrotic regions of other myopathies. The pattern—interstitial, endomysial, targeting necrotic fibers—helps interpretation.
- CD3 marks pan-T cells; CD8 and CD4 subsets identify cytotoxic versus helper T cells. In PM and IBM, CD8+ T cells are the dominant effector population and are often seen in close apposition to, and invading, non-necrotic muscle fibers that express MHC class I.
- MHC class I (HLA-ABC) expression on myofibers is normally low or absent; upregulation is an early and sensitive marker of immune-mediated myopathy. Diffuse sarcolemmal MHC class I expression is common in PM, IMNM and DM but may be highest in PM/IBM where direct cytotoxic T-cell interactions are present. Note that MHC class I can be upregulated non-specifically in many myopathies and after exercise or injury.
- Membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9) detection by immunofluorescence or IHC serves two key roles: capillary MAC deposition is characteristic of dermatomyositis (microangiopathy), whereas sarcolemmal MAC deposition on individual fibers may be seen in IMNM and other necrotizing processes and likely reflects complement-mediated myofiber injury.
- Acid phosphatase (histochemical) highlights lysosomal and phagolysosomal activity and augments identification of macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis.
- Additional immunostains: MxA (myxovirus resistance protein A) is a type I interferon–induced protein sensitive for DM; p62, TDP-43, and ubiquitin mark protein aggregates in IBM. Performing a panel tailored to the differential diagnosis increases sensitivity.

Interpretation
Combine patterns: a sample showing abundant CD68+ macrophages with large zones of necrosis but minimal CD3+/CD8+ invasion suggests IMNM. Endomysial CD8+ T-cell invasion of non-necrotic fibers with sarcolemmal MHC I upregulation favors PM or IBM; presence of rimmed vacuoles and protein aggregates favors IBM. Perifascicular MxA expression, capillary MAC deposition and perifascicular atrophy support DM.

# Patterns characteristic of IMNM vs polymyositis vs dermatomyositis vs inclusion body myositis

Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)
IMNM is defined by pronounced myofiber necrosis and regeneration with relatively scant lymphocytic inflammation. The prominent histology is extensive myofiber necrosis with abundant CD68+ macrophages engaged in myophagocytosis; necrotic fibers may be widely distributed. Lymphocytes, if present, are sparse and not typically invading intact fibers. MHC class I is often upregulated on sarcolemma and sarcoplasm. Complement deposition may be seen on the sarcolemma of necrotic fibers (C5b-9), though capillary MAC is not a defining feature. Clinical correlation with specific autoantibodies (anti–signal recognition particle [SRP], anti–3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl–coenzyme A reductase [HMGCR]) supports the diagnosis. Elevated CK levels (often markedly high) and the absence of perifascicular atrophy or perivascular inflammation help differentiate IMNM from DM.

Polymyositis (PM)
True PM is less common than historically thought and is characterized by endomysial inflammatory infiltrates composed predominantly of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells that invade non-necrotic muscle fibers. These invaded fibers typically express MHC class I, suggesting antigen presentation by myofibers. There is usually minimal perivascular or perifascicular distribution. Necrosis and regeneration can occur but are not the dominant feature. The diagnosis of PM requires careful exclusion of IBM, DM and IMNM; PM should be reserved for cases with classic endomysial CD8+ invasion without rimmed vacuoles or perifascicular changes.

Dermatomyositis (DM)
DM is a microangiopathy of muscle with characteristic perifascicular changes. Histology demonstrates perivascular and perimysial inflammation, complement-mediated injury to endomysial capillaries with MAC (C5b-9) deposition on capillary endothelium, and perifascicular atrophy (atrophy of fibers at the fascicular edges). MxA expression (marker of type I interferon activity) is typically upregulated in perifascicular regions. Endomysial inflammation is usually less prominent than in PM or IBM. Myofiber necrosis can be present but is frequently perifascicular. Skin findings and DM-specific autoantibodies can provide strong corroborative evidence.

Inclusion body myositis (IBM)
IBM typically affects older adults and has a distinctive mixed myopathic and neurogenic pattern. Histology shows endomysial inflammation with CD8+ T cells, but key distinguishing features are rimmed vacuoles (autophagic vacuoles with granular basophilic material on H&E, highlighted by Congo red or Gomori trichrome), intracytoplasmic protein aggregates that stain for p62, TDP-43, ubiquitin and amyloid-β, and mitochondrial abnormalities including COX-negative fibers. CD8+ cells often invade non-necrotic fibers, similar to PM, but the presence of rimmed vacuoles and protein inclusions is diagnostic for IBM. CK values are often only mildly to moderately elevated.

Summary comparison
- IMNM: prominent necrosis/regeneration, abundant macrophages (CD68+), sparse lymphocytes, MHC I upregulation, sarcolemmal complement possible, strong correlation with anti-SRP/anti-HMGCR antibodies.
- PM: endomysial CD8+ T-cell–mediated invasion of intact fibers, MHC I upregulation, minimal perifascicular or vascular changes.
- DM: perivascular/perimysial inflammation, perifascicular atrophy, capillary MAC deposition, MxA positivity.
- IBM: endomysial CD8+ T cells with invasion, rimmed vacuoles, protein aggregates (p62/TDP-43), mitochondrial COX-negative fibers, often refractory to immunotherapy.

# Interpretation of complement deposition and macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis

Complement deposition: significance and patterns
The membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9) is detected by immunofluorescence or IHC and informs pathophysiology. In dermatomyositis, MAC deposition on endomysial capillaries is a key pathogenic and diagnostic feature: complement activation leads to capillary injury, endothelium loss, ischemia, and subsequent perifascicular atrophy. Capillary MAC deposition correlates with microvascular compromise and the perifascicular pattern of fiber injury.

In contrast, sarcolemmal MAC deposition on the surface of individual myofibers suggests complement-mediated myofiber injury rather than primary microvascular disease. This pattern is seen in IMNM—where antibody-mediated complement activation can target muscle fibers leading to necrosis with subsequent macrophage recruitment—and in some necrotizing myopathies of other etiologies. Distinguishing capillary versus sarcolemmal MAC, and appreciating the distribution (diffuse, perifascicular, focal), assists in differentiating DM from IMNM and other necrotizing processes.

Macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis
Macrophages (CD68+) play a central role in clearance of necrotic fibers and in perpetuating inflammation. In IMNM, macrophages are often abundant, intermingled with necrotic fibers, and show marked acid phosphatase activity reflecting phagolysosomal activity. Macrophages may present as large sheets or focal aggregates and are typically located within necrotic zones rather than diffusely invading intact fibers. Their presence is a hallmark of ongoing necrosis and correlates clinically with elevated CK. However, macrophage infiltration can also be secondary to other forms of muscle injury (toxic, metabolic, dystrophic), so macrophage abundance must be interpreted in the clinical context.

Pathophysiologic implications
Complement deposition on capillaries implies an antibody-mediated microangiopathy (classically DM), whereas sarcolemmal MAC suggests complement activation directed at muscle fibers (as in some IMNM cases). Macrophage predominance without substantial T-cell infiltration favors humoral or complement-mediated mechanisms of injury and supports testing for associated autoantibodies (anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP). Conversely, prominent CD8+ T-cell–mediated fiber invasion points toward cell-mediated immunity (PM/IBM).

# Limitations of biopsy and correlation with clinical and serologic data

Sampling error and patchiness
Muscle disease can be patchy. A small biopsy may miss diagnostic lesions, especially perifascicular changes or focal necrosis. The probability of a representative biopsy increases with careful selection of a muscle showing recent abnormality on MRI or EMG.

Effect of therapy and disease stage
Immunosuppression started before biopsy can markedly diminish inflammatory infiltrates and obscure diagnostic features. Similarly, chronic or end-stage disease shows fibrosis and fatty replacement with reduced active inflammation, limiting diagnostic specificity. Early immune-mediated changes may primarily be molecular (MHC I upregulation) rather than morphologic.

Nonspecific findings
Some features—myofiber necrosis, macrophage infiltration, MHC class I upregulation—are not pathognomonic and may appear in toxic, metabolic, or dystrophic myopathies, as well as in systemic illness and after exercise. For example, statin-associated necrotizing myopathy (often with anti-HMGCR antibodies) can mimic IMNM histologically. Thus, histology must be integrated with medication history and serologies.

Interpretive overlap and evolving classifications
Entities such as PM, DM, IMNM and IBM overlap clinically and pathologically. The prevalence of PM as a diagnostic label has decreased as more precise clinicopathologic and serologic classifications have emerged (e.g., antisynthetase syndrome, IMNM). Recognize that no single histologic feature is diagnostic in isolation.

Integration with clinical and serologic data
A practical diagnostic approach synthesizes biopsy results with: pattern and distribution of weakness, CK levels and trend, EMG findings, MRI (edema vs fatty replacement), and serologies for myositis-specific and myositis-associated antibodies (e.g., anti-Jo-1, anti-Mi-2, anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR, antisynthetase antibodies). For example, a biopsy showing necrosis with macrophages and minimal lymphocytes strongly suggests IMNM; identification of anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP antibodies corroborates the diagnosis and guides therapy. Likewise, capillary MAC deposition and perifascicular atrophy plus compatible cutaneous findings and DM-specific antibodies support dermatomyositis.

Communication between clinician and pathologist
Because interpretation is context-dependent, detailed clinical information significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy. Discussing differential diagnoses and requesting specific stains (MHC class I, MAC, MxA, CD68/CD3/CD8, p62/ubiquitin) tailored to the clinical question improves yield and avoids unnecessary testing.

Conclusions

Muscle biopsy remains an indispensable tool in diagnosing inflammatory and necrotizing myopathies when properly performed and interpreted. Recognizing patterns—extensive necrosis with macrophages for IMNM, endomysial CD8+ invasion for PM, perifascicular atrophy and capillary MAC for DM, rimmed vacuoles and protein aggregates for IBM—allows clinicians to generate a focused differential diagnosis. Specialized histochemical and immunohistochemical stains (CD68, CD3/CD8, MHC class I, acid phosphatase, C5b-9, MxA, p62) elucidate underlying immune mechanisms. However, biopsy findings are neither fully sensitive nor specific in isolation; they must be integrated with clinical history, imaging, EMG and serologic tests to reach an accurate diagnosis and to guide management. Appropriate site selection, meticulous sample handling, and pre-procedure coordination with pathology are essential to maximize the diagnostic value of the biopsy.

Introduction

Imaging and electrophysiologic studies are essential tools in the workup of patients with suspected myopathies. They do not replace careful history, examination, and laboratory testing, but they refine diagnostic hypotheses, guide site selection for muscle biopsy, direct additional organ evaluation (for example cardiac or pulmonary involvement), and help distinguish myopathic from neurogenic processes. This chapter reviews the major modalities used in modern practice—magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and ultrasound of skeletal muscle, electrodiagnostic testing (EMG and nerve conduction studies), cardiac imaging for suspected myocarditis, and chest imaging for extramuscular pulmonary disease—and explains how to integrate these results with clinical and laboratory data to reach diagnostic and management decisions.

# MRI of muscle: sequences, findings in inflammatory myopathies, and use in biopsy targeting

Magnetic resonance imaging has become the most informative imaging modality for detailed assessment of skeletal muscle. MRI provides high soft-tissue contrast and allows visualization of both active (reversible) and chronic (irreversible) pathologic changes. Appropriate sequence selection and interpretation are critical.

Sequences and technical considerations
A standard muscle MRI protocol typically includes T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and fat-suppressed or fluid-sensitive sequences such as short tau inversion recovery (STIR) or T2 with fat suppression. T1-weighted sequences are best for assessing chronic structural change and fat infiltration; fatty replacement appears hyperintense on T1. T2-weighted fat-suppressed or STIR sequences highlight increased free water content and are sensitive for edema and active inflammation. Gadolinium-enhanced T1 sequences with fat suppression can detect areas of hyperemia and breakdown of the blood–muscle barrier and can improve detection of active inflammation or abscess. Diffusion-weighted imaging is investigated in research settings but is not yet routine for most myopathy evaluations. Imaging should include symmetry assessment and coverage of proximal and distal muscle groups relevant to the clinical pattern; bilateral imaging is often performed, since asymmetric involvement can be diagnostically informative.

Findings in inflammatory myopathies
Inflammatory myopathies (polymyositis, dermatomyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, and inclusion body myositis) show characteristic MRI patterns, though there is overlap. Active inflammatory changes manifest as hyperintense signal on T2/STIR images consistent with muscle edema; this tends to involve fascial planes and perifascial tissues in dermatomyositis, whereas polymyositis and immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy may produce more diffuse intramuscular edema. Edema is often patchy; its distribution—proximal dominant, symmetric versus asymmetric, distal or selective muscle involvement—helps narrow differential diagnosis. Chronic changes are signaled by increased T1 signal consistent with fatty replacement and muscle atrophy. Inflammatory myopathies may also show gadolinium enhancement of involved muscle reflecting hyperemia and increased vascular permeability.

Pattern recognition has diagnostic value. Dermatomyositis often displays subcutaneous and fascial edema and selective involvement of flexor or extensor compartments depending on the muscle group; muscle edema can be perivascular and patchy. Inclusion body myositis (IBM) classically shows selective involvement and atrophy of the finger flexors and quadriceps, with relative sparing of other proximal muscles and a combination of fatty replacement and mild edema. Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy commonly produces marked edema acutely with rapid progression to fatty replacement if chronic. Vasculitic or infectious myositis may display more focal abscess-like collections or diffuse edema with associated fascial enhancement.

Quantitative assessment and longitudinal change
MRI can be used semi-quantitatively (scoring systems for edema and fatty infiltration) and increasingly by direct quantification using Dixon sequences that separate fat and water and by T2 mapping to measure muscle water content. These approaches allow objective monitoring of disease progression or response to therapy. For example, increased T2 relaxation times correlate with edema and active inflammation; increased fat fraction on Dixon sequences corresponds to irreversible muscle loss.

Use of MRI to guide biopsy
One of the most practical and evidence-based uses of MRI in myopathy evaluation is biopsy targeting. Because inflammatory or necrotic foci may be patchy, blind biopsy of a weakened muscle can yield false-negative results. MRI identifies areas of active inflammation (T2/STIR hyperintensity, gadolinium enhancement) that are more likely to provide diagnostic tissue. The ideal biopsy target is a muscle showing T2/STIR hyperintensity with relatively preserved bulk (avoiding end-stage fatty replacement visible on T1) and that is safely accessible. Bilateral planning may be needed if one side has been pretreated or is not available. Radiology reports should describe both the lesion location and depth relative to skin and neurovascular structures to facilitate surgical planning.

Clinical decision points
Imaging should be obtained early when there is diagnostic uncertainty, prior to steroid or immunosuppressive therapy if possible (as treatment can reduce edema and mask active inflammation). A practical approach is to perform MRI when CK is elevated with proximal weakness, when EMG is equivocal, or when biopsy is being planned. Repeat MRI may be useful for monitoring therapy or when clinical course changes.

# Ultrasound of muscle: utility and characteristic findings

Muscle ultrasound is a rapid, bedside, noninvasive tool that complements MRI. Its portability and lower cost make it appealing for screening, follow-up, and guiding injections; however, it is operator-dependent and has lower tissue contrast than MRI.

Technique and sequences
Muscle ultrasound uses high-frequency linear transducers (7–18 MHz) to evaluate muscle architecture, echogenicity, and dynamic behavior. Examination typically compares symptomatic and contralateral muscles, assesses fascial planes, tendons, and surrounding soft tissues, and may include Doppler imaging for vascularity. Elastography techniques (shear-wave or strain) can be used experimentally to assess stiffness.

Characteristic findings
Normal muscle has a characteristic appearance with hypoechoic muscle bundles separated by hyperechoic perimysial and epimysial connective tissue, producing a “starry” or “feathery” pattern. Pathologic changes include increased intramuscular echogenicity, loss of normal architecture, muscle atrophy, and focal fluid collections. Inflammatory myopathies commonly demonstrate increased muscle echogenicity (reflecting edema and later fibrosis/fatty infiltration) and increased thickness of fascia; acute inflammation may show hypoechogenicity due to edema, whereas chronic fatty infiltration typically increases echogenicity. Doppler signal can be increased in active inflammatory myositis, reflecting hyperemia. In IBM, ultrasound may show selective muscle atrophy and increased echogenicity of quadriceps or flexor digitorum profundus. Ultrasound is particularly sensitive for detecting focal lesions such as intramuscular fluid collections, abscesses, hematomas, and guiding needle placement for biopsy or injections.

Advantages and limitations
Ultrasound allows dynamic examination and real-time guidance for procedures, and is valuable in pediatric or critically ill patients where MRI is difficult. It lacks the comprehensive field of view and deep-tissue contrast of MRI, and inter-operator variability limits standardization. For biopsy targeting, ultrasound can help locate focal lesions and avoid vessels but is less reliable than MRI for delineating patchy edema within deep muscles.

# Electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies: myopathic vs neuropathic patterns, irritability and fibrillation potentials

Electrodiagnostic testing is a cornerstone in differentiating primary muscle disease from neurogenic disorders and motor neuron disease. EMG assesses the electrical activity of muscles at rest and during contraction, while nerve conduction studies (NCS) evaluate peripheral nerve function.

Nerve conduction studies
NCS measure the speed and amplitude of responses in motor and sensory nerves. In pure myopathies, motor and sensory conduction velocities and distal latencies are typically normal because the peripheral nerve is intact. Motor response amplitudes can be normal or mildly reduced if there is substantial muscle fiber loss; importantly, the compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitude reduction in myopathy is usually out of proportion to slowing or conduction block. In neuropathic processes, reduced amplitudes with slowed conduction velocities or conduction block (for demyelinating neuropathies) and abnormal sensory studies help localize the lesion to the nerve.

Needle EMG: motor unit potentials and recruitment
Needle EMG examines spontaneous activity at rest and motor unit potential (MUP) morphology and recruitment during voluntary contraction. Myopathic patterns are characterized by MUPs of short duration and low amplitude, often with increased polyphasia, and early recruitment—meaning that many small MUPs are activated at relatively low force because fewer muscle fibers contribute to each motor unit. In contrast, neurogenic disorders show long-duration, high-amplitude MUPs reflecting collateral reinnervation, reduced recruitment (fewer motor units available), and often complex repetitive discharges in chronic denervation-reinnervation.

Spontaneous activity and irritability
The presence of spontaneous activity at rest—fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves—indicates muscle membrane irritability and is commonly seen in active inflammatory myopathies, toxic myopathies, and denervation. Fibrillations and positive sharp waves are evidence of active muscle fiber necrosis, inflammation, or recent denervation. Myotonic discharges (waxing and waning high-frequency discharges) indicate membrane hyperexcitability and are classic in myotonic dystrophy and myotonia congenita. Complex repetitive discharges and myokymic discharges may also be seen in specific disorders. It is important to recognize that spontaneous activity can occur in both primary myopathies and acute denervation; correlation with clinical and imaging data is therefore essential.

Utility and caveats
EMG is highly sensitive for detecting muscle disease but not fully specific. A myopathic EMG pattern supports a primary muscle disorder and helps localize which muscles are most affected, thereby guiding biopsy selection. However, EMG may be normal in early or very focal myopathies, in pure metabolic myopathies (when performed between symptomatic episodes), or if muscles selected for testing are already end-stage fibrotic and thus electrically inactive. Conversely, chronic myopathy with fatty replacement can produce neurogenic-like EMG features due to secondary denervation. Thus, EMG findings must be interpreted in the context of CK levels, imaging, and clinical pattern.

Key diagnostic points and criteria
There are no absolute numeric cutoffs analogous to lab reference ranges for EMG, but typical descriptions guide interpretation: MUP duration less than approximately 6 ms and amplitude less than 4 mV suggest myopathic change, while durations greater than 14 ms and amplitudes over 10 mV suggest neurogenic change; these are approximate and dependent on muscle and technique. The detection of fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves indicates active muscle membrane instability. When EMG suggests a myopathic process, the muscles chosen for biopsy should be those with abnormal EMG findings but not so severely replaced by fat that diagnostic histology is lost; combining EMG with MRI yields higher diagnostic yield.

# Cardiac imaging in suspected myocarditis: echocardiography and cardiac MRI

Cardiac involvement is an important extramuscular manifestation of several myopathies and systemic inflammatory myopathies and can be life-threatening. Imaging plays a central role in detecting myocarditis, assessing function, and guiding management.

Echocardiography
Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is usually the first-line cardiac imaging study. It evaluates left and right ventricular size and systolic function (ejection fraction), regional wall motion abnormalities, chamber dimensions, valvular function, pericardial effusion, and diastolic parameters. In myocarditis, echocardiography findings are variable and may be normal early. Echocardiography can show global or regional left ventricular systolic dysfunction, increased wall thickness due to edema, pericardial effusion, or regional wall motion abnormalities that do not conform to coronary distribution. Strain imaging (speckle-tracking global longitudinal strain) increases sensitivity for subtle systolic dysfunction and may detect subclinical involvement before dropout of ejection fraction occurs. Serial echocardiography is useful for monitoring ventricular function in patients receiving cardiotoxic therapies or immunosuppression.

Cardiac MRI (cMRI)
Cardiac MRI is the most informative noninvasive test for myocardial inflammation and necrosis. The updated Lake Louise Criteria integrate T2-based markers of myocardial edema and T1-based markers of myocardial injury/fibrosis. According to the revised criteria, acute myocarditis is supported when there is at least one T2-based criterion (regional or global increased myocardial T2 relaxation time or T2 signal intensity) indicating edema and at least one T1-based criterion (increased native T1, increased extracellular volume fraction, or nonischemic late gadolinium enhancement [LGE]) indicating myocardial injury. LGE in myocarditis typically shows a nonischemic pattern: mid-wall or epicardial enhancement sparing the subendocardium, often in the lateral wall. T1 and T2 mapping provide quantitative measures: native T1 and T2 relaxation times are elevated in acute inflammation, and extracellular volume fraction (ECV) is increased with expansion of the interstitial space. Exact numerical cutoffs vary by scanner, pulse sequence, and institution; typical native T1 values might range from 950–1050 ms at 1.5T, with higher values suggesting pathology, and T2 values above institution-specific references suggest edema. When suspected myocarditis occurs in the setting of a myopathy, cMRI also assesses ventricular volumes, ejection fraction, and can detect concomitant pericarditis.

Clinical application and decision points
Echocardiography is widely available and appropriate for initial evaluation, particularly to assess hemodynamics and screen for gross dysfunction. Cardiac MRI is indicated when myocarditis is suspected based on symptoms, biomarkers (troponin elevation), ECG changes, or in systemic inflammatory disease when cardiac involvement is a possibility. cMRI findings influence treatment decisions (e.g., immunosuppression) and prognosis; the extent of LGE correlates with adverse outcomes. Endomyocardial biopsy remains the reference standard for histologic confirmation but is invasive and reserved for cases where biopsy-driven specific therapy is needed or when alternate diagnoses (e.g., giant cell myocarditis) are considered.

# Chest imaging for extramuscular involvement (interstitial lung disease) and CT criteria

Pulmonary involvement, particularly interstitial lung disease (ILD), is an important extramuscular manifestation of several inflammatory myopathies (for example antisynthetase syndrome and dermatomyositis) and affects prognosis and therapy. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest is the imaging modality of choice.

HRCT technique and indications
HRCT uses thin-section (typically 1–1.5 mm) slices reconstructed at high spatial frequency, often with inspiratory and expiratory phases, and sometimes with prone imaging to distinguish dependent atelectasis from true disease. HRCT should be performed when patients have respiratory symptoms (dyspnea, cough), abnormal pulmonary function tests (restrictive pattern or decreased diffusion capacity for carbon monoxide), or positive myositis-specific autoantibodies associated with ILD (for example anti-Jo-1, anti-PL-7, anti-PL-12). Baseline HRCT is often obtained in patients with antisynthetase or other high-risk serologies, even if asymptomatic, as radiographic ILD can be subclinical.

CT patterns and differential diagnosis
HRCT patterns help characterize the type and stage of ILD and guide prognosis. Common patterns include nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP), and organizing pneumonia (OP). NSIP, the most frequent pattern in connective tissue disease–associated ILD, shows ground-glass opacities with a basal and peripheral predominance, fine reticular markings, and volume loss; honeycombing is less frequent than in UIP. UIP, characterized by subpleural basal-predominant reticulation, traction bronchiectasis, and honeycombing, portends worse prognosis and resembles idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Organizing pneumonia appears as patchy bilateral consolidations, often peribronchovascular and subpleural, and typically responds to steroids. Other features to report include extent (percent involvement), presence of traction bronchiectasis, emphysema, pleural effusion, and lymphadenopathy. Serial HRCTs assess progression or response to therapy, but radiation exposure must be weighed.

CT criteria and quantification
While no single numeric cutoff defines ILD on HRCT, radiologists commonly estimate the extent of involvement (for example, <10%, 10–30%, >30% lung involvement), which correlates with pulmonary function. Pulmonary function tests are complementary; a restrictive pattern (reduced total lung capacity) and a reduced diffusion capacity of carbon monoxide (DLCO <80% predicted; severe impairment <50%) often accompany ILD. Radiologic features suggesting active inflammation (ground-glass opacities) may predict steroid responsiveness, while extensive fibrosis and honeycombing suggest irreversible disease.

Clinical decision points
In a patient with suspected inflammatory myopathy, presence of ILD or high-risk serologies should prompt early pulmonary evaluation with PFTs and HRCT. Findings of active inflammatory change (ground-glass) favor immunosuppressive therapy, whereas fibrosis-oriented changes may require antifibrotic consideration and have implications for prognosis and referral for lung transplant evaluation in severe cases.

# Integration of imaging and electrophysiology with clinical and laboratory data

Effective diagnosis and management of myopathies require synthesis of clinical history, physical exam, laboratory studies (including CK and myositis-specific autoantibodies), imaging, and electrodiagnostics. Each modality addresses different aspects of disease and has strengths and limitations; diagnostic accuracy improves when data are integrated.

Clinical context and laboratory correlation
History and physical exam determine the pattern of weakness (proximal, distal, scapular, ocular), onset (acute, subacute, chronic), presence of systemic features (rash, fever, arthralgia, respiratory or cardiac symptoms), and family history suggesting hereditary myopathies. Serum CK provides a biochemical measure of muscle injury; normal ranges commonly lie below 200 U/L (laboratory-dependent), with mild elevation defined as 2–3 times upper limit of normal and severe elevations often in the thousands. Highly elevated CK suggests necrotic or inflammatory myopathy but can be normal in disorders such as IBM or some metabolic myopathies. Myositis-specific and myositis-associated autoantibodies (for example anti-Jo-1, anti-SRP, anti-Mi-2, anti-TIF1-γ, anti-MDA5) refine diagnosis, predict extramuscular involvement (e.g., antisynthetase antibodies with ILD), and inform prognosis.

Sequential and complementary testing
Electrodiagnostic testing clarifies whether weakness is myopathic or neurogenic and helps localize affected muscles for biopsy. MRI refines the anatomical extent of involvement, highlights active inflammatory regions amenable to biopsy, and screens for patterns suggestive of specific diagnoses (for example quadriceps and finger flexor involvement in IBM). Ultrasound offers bedside assessment or dynamic evaluation and can guide percutaneous procedures. If cardiac symptoms or elevated troponin are present, echocardiography provides an initial assessment and cMRI confirms myocarditis and quantifies myocardial injury. Suspicion for ILD or positive high-risk autoantibodies warrants PFTs and HRCT. The timing and sequence of tests should be driven by the clinical urgency (for example, rapidly progressive weakness or respiratory compromise mandates expedited imaging and possibly empiric therapy).

Interpretive examples
A patient with subacute symmetric proximal weakness, markedly elevated CK, and MRI showing diffuse muscle edema on STIR sequences is likely to have an active inflammatory myopathy; EMG showing fibrillation potentials and short-duration, low-amplitude MUPs supports a myopathic process, and targeted biopsy of an MRI-active, not yet fat-replaced muscle will likely return diagnostic histopathology. Conversely, a slowly progressive asymmetric pattern with selective quadriceps atrophy on MRI, mild CK elevation, EMG showing chronic myopathic changes and low-grade spontaneous activity, and absence of systemic inflammation may suggest IBM, in which immunosuppression is often ineffective.

Decision points for biopsy and therapy
Imaging and EMG guide biopsy selection to maximize diagnostic yield. Biopsy should sample an affected muscle with active inflammation on imaging and electrical activity on EMG, but not an end-stage fat-replaced muscle on T1. If cardiac or pulmonary involvement is suspected based on symptoms or serology, prioritize appropriate cardiac or chest imaging early, as these manifestations often dictate urgency and therapy (for example severe ILD or myocarditis may require prompt immunosuppression or ICU care). Where diagnostic uncertainty remains, multidisciplinary discussion among neurologists, rheumatologists, radiologists, and pathologists improves test selection and interpretation.

Monitoring and prognosis
Serial imaging (MRI, HRCT, cMRI) and functional testing (PFTs, echocardiography) track disease progression and response to therapy. MRI reductions in T2 signal and decreases in fat-free fraction may indicate therapeutic response, whereas increasing fat fraction or progressive fibrosis on HRCT indicates irreversible damage. EMG may show decreased spontaneous activity as inflammation responds to treatment, but recovery of normal MUP morphology lags behind clinical improvement.

Conclusion

Imaging and electrophysiologic modalities provide complementary, high-yield information in the evaluation of myopathies. MRI is the most informative modality for muscular structural and inflammatory assessment and optimizes biopsy targeting; ultrasound is a practical bedside tool for screening and procedural guidance. Electrodiagnostic studies differentiate myopathic from neurogenic processes and identify electrically active disease. Cardiac MRI and echocardiography detect myocardial involvement, while HRCT evaluates pulmonary complications. Integration of these modalities with clinical assessment and laboratory testing—including CK, troponin, and autoantibodies—allows tailored diagnostic pathways, more accurate prognostication, and informed therapeutic decision-making.

# Introduction

Inflammatory myopathies are a heterogeneous group of autoimmune disorders characterized predominantly by skeletal muscle inflammation and weakness. Beyond skeletal muscle, these diseases—including polymyositis, dermatomyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, and inclusion body myositis, and more recently recognized entities such as anti-synthetase syndrome and myositis associated with myositis-specific autoantibodies—have systemic manifestations. Cardiac involvement is an important extra-skeletal manifestation that can substantially affect morbidity and mortality. It ranges from subclinical biochemical abnormalities to fulminant myocarditis with cardiogenic shock. Recognition of cardiac disease in patients with inflammatory myopathies is essential because early diagnosis and appropriate management can alter outcomes.

This chapter reviews the types of cardiac involvement seen in inflammatory myopathies, the underlying pathophysiology linking skeletal and cardiac muscle inflammation, typical clinical presentations and red flags that should prompt cardiac evaluation, diagnostic tools and their interpretation, treatment strategies integrating immunosuppression with standard cardiovascular care, and prognosis with follow-up recommendations. The intended reader is a medical student or resident seeking a clinically oriented, mechanistic understanding to apply in the assessment and management of patients with inflammatory myopathies.

# Types of cardiac involvement: myocarditis, arrhythmias, conduction disease, heart failure

Cardiac manifestations in inflammatory myopathies are protean. They may occur in isolation or coexist. The major categories are myocarditis, arrhythmias (including tachyarrhythmias), conduction system disease, and heart failure from either systolic or diastolic dysfunction.

Myocarditis: True myocardial inflammation—myocarditis—is a key mechanism of cardiac morbidity. Histologically, myocarditis may show interstitial and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates composed of lymphocytes, macrophages, and occasionally plasma cells and eosinophils, with associated myocyte necrosis. Clinically, myocarditis may manifest as chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, or unexplained troponin elevation. Myocardial involvement may be focal or diffuse; focal disease can lead to regional wall motion abnormalities mimicking ischemic cardiomyopathy.

Arrhythmias: Both supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias are reported. Atrial fibrillation or atrial tachycardias can occur in the context of atrial inflammation or secondary to chamber dilation and fibrosis. Ventricular arrhythmias, including non-sustained or sustained ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation, are of particular concern because they carry the risk of sudden cardiac death. Arrhythmogenic substrate may be active inflammation, scar formation, or both.

Conduction disease: Inflammatory involvement of the conduction system can produce a spectrum of conduction abnormalities, from first-degree atrioventricular (AV) block to complete heart block. Bundle branch blocks and intraventricular conduction delays are also described. Conduction disease may be intermittent and may progress; hence, ambulatory monitoring is often necessary to detect clinically significant events.

Heart failure: Heart failure in inflammatory myopathies may result from direct myocardial inflammation (acute myocarditis), chronic myocardial damage with remodeling and fibrosis leading to dilated cardiomyopathy, or secondary causes such as long-standing arrhythmia, ischemic heart disease, or medication-related cardiotoxicity. Both heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) can occur. Right ventricular involvement is reported and may be prominent in some patients.

These cardiac phenotypes may overlap in an individual patient. For example, myocarditis may present with chest pain, elevated cardiac biomarkers, and new left ventricular dysfunction that precipitates arrhythmias and heart failure. Conversely, conduction disease may be the first or only manifestation in some patients with systemic myositis.

# Pathophysiology: immune-mediated myocardial injury, overlap with systemic autoimmunity

The pathophysiology of cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathies centers on immune-mediated injury to cardiac myocytes and the supporting interstitium. Several mechanisms are implicated, often overlapping and influenced by the specific myositis subtype and autoantibody profile.

Cell-mediated immunity: T lymphocytes, particularly CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, have been identified in myocardial inflammatory infiltrates. These cells recognize antigen presented on major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules expressed by myocytes and mediate cytotoxicity. Upregulation of MHC class I and II molecules has been observed in both skeletal and cardiac muscle in inflammatory myopathies, facilitating autoimmune targeting.

Humoral immunity: Autoantibodies characteristic of systemic myositis may also target cardiac antigens directly or form immune complexes that deposit in the myocardium. Myositis-specific autoantibodies (for example, anti-signal recognition particle [anti-SRP], anti-Mi-2, anti-synthetase antibodies such as anti-Jo-1) and myositis-associated antibodies have varying associations with cardiac disease. For instance, anti-SRP has been associated with severe necrotizing myopathy and a higher risk of cardiomyopathy. Autoantibody-mediated mechanisms may induce complement activation and membrane injury.

Cytokines and innate immunity: Proinflammatory cytokines—tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 (IL-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interferons—contribute to myocardial inflammation, apoptosis, and remodeling. Type I interferon signatures are prominent in certain myositis subtypes like dermatomyositis and may mediate endothelial and myocyte dysfunction. These cytokine pathways can perpetuate inflammation and promote fibrosis.

Microvascular injury and ischemia: Dermatomyositis, in particular, features microangiopathy in skeletal muscle; similar microvascular changes may affect myocardial capillaries and small arterioles, leading to ischemia, myocyte injury, and fibrosis. Microvascular compromise may explain chest pain with non-obstructive coronary arteries and regional myocardial dysfunction.

Fibrosis and remodeling: Chronic inflammatory insults lead to replacement fibrosis and extracellular matrix remodeling. Fibrosis creates arrhythmogenic substrate and contributes to systolic and diastolic dysfunction. The distribution and extent of fibrosis help determine clinical manifestations—diffuse fibrosis may lead to heart failure, while patchy fibrosis elevates arrhythmic risk.

Medication and overlap syndromes: Some immunotherapies and other medications (e.g., certain chemotherapeutic agents) can be cardiotoxic and must be considered when evaluating cardiac disease. Additionally, overlap with systemic autoimmune disorders such as systemic lupus erythematosus or systemic sclerosis can compound myocardial involvement through their own disease mechanisms.

Genetic and environmental modifiers: Genetic susceptibility loci influence immune response; infections and other environmental triggers may provoke or unmask autoimmune myocarditis. The interplay between systemic autoimmunity and organ-specific factors (e.g., myocardial expression of autoantigens) determines the clinical phenotype.

Understanding these mechanisms is clinically relevant: the presence of active inflammation suggests potential responsiveness to immunosuppression, whereas extensive fibrosis predicts less reversibility and higher arrhythmic risk. Thus, identifying inflammation versus scar is central to diagnostic evaluation and therapeutic decisions.

# Clinical presentation and red flags warranting cardiac workup

Clinical manifestations of cardiac involvement are variable and may be subtle or acute. Some patients are asymptomatic at rest but have detectable abnormalities on imaging or cardiac biomarkers. Clinicians should maintain a low threshold for cardiac evaluation in patients with inflammatory myopathy who present with concerning features.

Common presentations include exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, unexplained fatigue, chest pain (often non-ischemic in nature), palpitations, presyncope or syncope, and reduced exercise tolerance. On examination, findings may include tachycardia, new murmurs (e.g., functional mitral regurgitation from dilated cardiomyopathy), signs of heart failure (pulmonary crackles, elevated jugular venous pressure, peripheral edema), or signs of low cardiac output.

Red flags that should prompt immediate cardiac workup:

- New-onset chest pain in a patient with inflammatory myopathy, particularly if associated with ECG changes, troponin elevation, or hemodynamic instability. Although ischemic causes must be considered, myocarditis can present similarly.
- Unexplained elevation of serum cardiac biomarkers (troponin I or T) not attributable to skeletal muscle injury alone.
- New ECG abnormalities such as pathologic Q waves, ST-T changes, new bundle branch block, new AV block, or unexplained prolonged QT interval.
- New arrhythmias or palpitations, particularly sustained ventricular arrhythmias or recurrent syncope/presyncope.
- New or worsening heart failure symptoms or signs, including reduced exercise tolerance, dyspnea on exertion, orthopnea, or peripheral edema.
- High-risk myositis subtypes or autoantibodies known to associate with cardiac disease (for example, anti-SRP, anti-mitochondrial antibodies in some reports), or overlap syndromes.
- Perioperative or therapy-related concerns (e.g., before initiating cardiotoxic immunosuppressive agents or in the setting of hemodynamic compromise).
- Unexplained troponin elevation with normal coronary arteries on angiography.

Because cardiac involvement can be occult, some centers perform baseline cardiac screening in all patients with newly diagnosed inflammatory myopathy, including history, physical exam, ECG, and consideration of troponin and echocardiography, especially when risk factors or symptoms are present. Clinical judgment should guide the extent of evaluation.

# Diagnostic tools: troponin interpretation, ECG, echocardiography, cardiac MRI (Lake Louise criteria), endomyocardial biopsy indications

A multimodality approach optimizes detection and characterization of cardiac involvement. Each tool has strengths and limitations; integration of findings is required for accurate diagnosis and management.

Troponin interpretation: Cardiac troponins (troponin I and troponin T) are sensitive markers of myocardial injury. Lab-specific reference ranges vary, but a common 99th percentile upper reference limit for high-sensitivity troponin I is approximately < 34 ng/L in men and < 16 ng/L in women (values vary by assay); high-sensitivity troponin T 99th percentile is often ~14 ng/L. It is crucial to recognize that some patients with inflammatory myopathy may have low-level troponin elevations from skeletal muscle expression of troponin T or renal dysfunction; troponin I is generally more specific for cardiac injury than troponin T in this context. Serial measurements and correlation with clinical features, ECG, and imaging help distinguish true myocardial injury from assay interference or non-cardiac sources. A rising and/or falling pattern supports acute myocardial injury.

Electrocardiogram (ECG): The ECG is an essential initial tool. Findings may be non-specific but can include sinus tachycardia, ST-segment and T-wave abnormalities, pathologic Q waves, low-voltage QRS, PR prolongation, new bundle branch blocks, and arrhythmias. Conduction disturbances such as AV block should prompt further evaluation with telemetry or ambulatory monitoring. Because ECG sensitivity for myocarditis is limited, a normal ECG does not exclude cardiac involvement, but abnormal findings increase pretest probability and urgency for more advanced testing.

Echocardiography: Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) provides rapid assessment of cardiac structure and function. It can detect global or regional left ventricular systolic dysfunction, diastolic dysfunction, wall motion abnormalities, chamber enlargement, pericardial effusion, and valvular abnormalities. Strain imaging (global longitudinal strain) may reveal subclinical myocardial dysfunction before ejection fraction declines; normal left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) with abnormal strain suggests early involvement. Echocardiography is also useful for hemodynamic assessment and monitoring response to therapy. However, its ability to characterize tissue (inflammation vs fibrosis) is limited compared with cardiac magnetic resonance imaging.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR): CMR is the noninvasive gold standard for myocardial tissue characterization. It detects myocardial edema, hyperemia, necrosis, and fibrosis. The Lake Louise criteria are the accepted CMR diagnostic framework for myocarditis and were updated in 2018 to combine T2-based markers of myocardial edema and T1-based markers of non-ischemic myocardial injury. The 2018 Lake Louise criteria require at least one T2-based criterion (e.g., regional or global increase in myocardial T2 relaxation time or visible myocardial edema on T2-weighted imaging) and at least one T1-based criterion (e.g., increased native T1, increased extracellular volume fraction, or non-ischemic late gadolinium enhancement [LGE]) for a diagnosis of active myocardial inflammation. LGE patterns that spare the subendocardium and are patchy or mid-wall are consistent with non-ischemic injury such as myocarditis. CMR can also quantify ventricular volumes and function, detect small pericardial effusions, and guide prognosis—the extent of LGE correlates with risk of adverse events including arrhythmias.

Endomyocardial biopsy (EMB): EMB is the definitive diagnostic procedure for myocarditis because it permits histologic and immunohistochemical assessment and, when indicated, molecular testing for infectious agents. However, EMB is invasive and carries small but significant risks (e.g., perforation, arrhythmia). Indications for EMB in the context of inflammatory myopathy are similar to those for myocarditis generally and include: (1) unexplained, new-onset heart failure of less than 2 weeks’ duration associated with hemodynamic compromise; (2) new-onset heart failure of 2 weeks to 3 months duration with a dilated left ventricle and new ventricular arrhythmias or high-grade AV block; (3) suspicion of specific myocarditis subtypes where histology would change management (e.g., giant cell myocarditis, eosinophilic myocarditis, or sarcoidosis vs autoimmune myocarditis); and (4) failure to respond to empiric therapy where tissue diagnosis could alter treatment. In inflammatory myopathies, EMB is often considered when CMR is equivocal and the result would alter immunosuppressive strategy, or when there are severe conduction abnormalities or refractory ventricular arrhythmias. Sampling error is a limitation: focal lesions can be missed, and targeted biopsy guided by imaging (e.g., electroanatomic mapping or CMR-guided) improves diagnostic yield.

Other diagnostic tools: Ambulatory ECG monitoring (24–72 hour Holter or longer-term event monitors) detects intermittent arrhythmias and conduction pauses. Implantable loop recorders can be useful in those with unexplained syncope. Coronary angiography or coronary CT may be required to exclude obstructive coronary disease when chest pain or regional wall motion abnormalities are present. Laboratory evaluation should include natriuretic peptides (BNP or NT-proBNP) for heart failure assessment; typical NT-proBNP normal ranges vary with age—values < 125 pg/mL are often used in younger adults as a rule-out for heart failure. Autoantibody panels and inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) provide systemic disease context.

Integration of findings: Diagnosis of myocarditis or other cardiac involvement requires synthesis of clinical features, biomarkers, ECG, imaging (preferably CMR), and selective use of EMB. Distinguishing active inflammation amenable to immunosuppression from chronic scar is the key clinical task.

# Management strategies: immunosuppression, heart failure therapy, arrhythmia management and monitoring

Management of cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathies requires a dual approach: control of the underlying immune-mediated process and application of contemporary cardiovascular therapies to manage symptoms, prevent complications, and improve outcomes.

Immunosuppression: When myocarditis or active myocardial inflammation is suspected or confirmed, immunosuppressive therapy is often indicated. Treatment strategies are extrapolated from experience with skeletal muscle disease and autoimmune myocarditis, and must be individualized.

- Corticosteroids: High-dose corticosteroids are commonly used as first-line therapy for suspected inflammatory cardiac involvement. Intravenous methylprednisolone pulses (e.g., 500–1000 mg daily for 1–3 days) may be used in severe or fulminant presentations, followed by high-dose oral prednisone (e.g., 1 mg/kg/day) with gradual taper. Response should be monitored closely with clinical assessment, biomarkers, ECG, and imaging.

- Steroid-sparing agents: Because long-term corticosteroid side effects are substantial, steroid-sparing immunosuppressants are added early. Agents include methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, and calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine, tacrolimus). Choice depends on comorbidities, fertility considerations, and organ involvement. In refractory or severe cases, more aggressive agents such as cyclophosphamide may be used.

- Biological therapies and targeted agents: Rituximab (anti-CD20) has been used in refractory myositis and cases with prominent autoantibody-mediated disease. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) can be beneficial, particularly in dermatomyositis and necrotizing myopathy and has been employed for cardiac disease. Emerging targeted agents (e.g., Janus kinase inhibitors, complement inhibitors) are under investigation. Use of biologics for myocarditis should be undertaken in conjunction with rheumatology/immunology and cardiology specialists.

- Duration and monitoring: The intensity and duration of immunosuppression depend on response; relapse risk necessitates close follow-up. Tapering should be guided by clinical stability, biomarkers, and imaging evidence of resolution of inflammation.

Heart failure therapy: Standard guideline-directed medical therapy applies for systolic dysfunction, irrespective of etiology. This includes:

- Renin–angiotensin system blockade (ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers, or angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor when appropriate).
- Beta-blockers (e.g., bisoprolol, carvedilol, or metoprolol succinate) once hemodynamically tolerated.
- Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (spironolactone or eplerenone) in appropriate patients with reduced ejection fraction.
- Diuretics for symptomatic congestion.
- Consideration of device therapies per heart failure guidelines: implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) for primary prevention when LVEF remains ≤ 35% on optimized medical therapy after appropriate waiting period (usually 3 months) or earlier in certain high-risk scenarios, and cardiac resynchronization therapy for select patients with wide QRS and reduced LVEF.

- Advanced therapies: In fulminant myocarditis with cardiogenic shock, temporary mechanical circulatory support (intra-aortic balloon pump, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, ventricular assist devices) may be lifesaving while awaiting recovery or for bridge to transplant in irreversible cases.

Arrhythmia management and monitoring: Arrhythmias in inflammatory myopathies require both acute management and long-term risk reduction.

- Acute arrhythmias: Hemodynamically unstable ventricular arrhythmias mandate ACLS protocols and urgent cardiology input. Antiarrhythmic drugs (e.g., amiodarone) may be used acutely.

- Long-term: For sustained ventricular arrhythmias or syncope attributable to ventricular tachycardia, ICD implantation is indicated. In patients with active inflammation, suppression of inflammation can reduce arrhythmia burden; however, ICD therapy may still be necessary because of scar-related risk.

- Conduction disease: High-grade AV block often requires permanent pacemaker implantation. The potential reversibility of conduction abnormalities with immunosuppression should be considered, but pacing should not be delayed in the presence of symptomatic high-grade block.

- Monitoring: Ambulatory ECG monitoring is recommended for detection of intermittent arrhythmias. Inpatient telemetry is appropriate during active evaluation and treatment of myocarditis. Periodic reassessment after immunosuppression is needed to document resolution or progression.

Antithrombotic therapy: In myocarditis with severe LV dysfunction, the risk of mural thrombus and embolic events is increased; anticoagulation should be considered based on echocardiographic findings and thromboembolic risk. Atrial arrhythmias should be managed according to standard stroke risk assessment (CHA2DS2-VASc) with anticoagulation as indicated.

Multidisciplinary care: Management benefits from coordination between rheumatology/immunology, cardiology (including heart failure, electrophysiology), and, when needed, critical care and cardiac surgery. Treatment decisions—especially regarding potent immunosuppressants—must weigh infection risk and other toxicities. Vaccinations and prophylaxis (e.g., for Pneumocystis jirovecii) may be appropriate when using certain agents.

# Prognosis and follow-up recommendations

Prognosis depends on the severity and extent of cardiac involvement, timeliness of diagnosis, response to immunosuppression, and presence of fibrosis and arrhythmogenic substrate. Patients with early detection and predominantly inflammatory disease often respond to immunosuppression with improvement in function. Those with extensive fibrosis, dilated cardiomyopathy, or recurrent ventricular arrhythmias have higher morbidity and mortality.

Risk stratification considerations:

- Extent of LGE on CMR correlates with adverse outcomes: larger and transmural patterns are associated with higher risk of heart failure and sudden cardiac death.
- Persistent LV dysfunction (reduced LVEF) after treatment predicts worse prognosis and guides device therapy decisions.
- Presence of high-grade AV block or sustained ventricular arrhythmias carries significant risk and often necessitates device therapy.
- Certain autoantibodies (for example, anti-SRP) and disease subtypes may portend more severe cardiac involvement.

Follow-up recommendations:

- Baseline assessment: At diagnosis of inflammatory myopathy, obtain a careful cardiac history and physical examination and consider baseline ECG and measurement of cardiac biomarkers (troponin I preferred) and natriuretic peptides. Echocardiography should be performed if symptoms, abnormal ECG, or elevated biomarkers are present; some centers perform baseline echocardiography more broadly.

- Monitoring during active disease or treatment: Frequency depends on severity. For suspected or confirmed cardiac involvement, inpatients require continuous telemetry initially. Serial troponin and BNP/NT-proBNP should be trended to assess response. Repeat ECGs and ambulatory monitoring as indicated. CMR is valuable to document resolution of edema and to quantify fibrosis; a repeat CMR is often performed after 3–6 months of immunosuppressive therapy to assess treatment response and to guide further immunotherapy decisions.

- Long-term follow-up: Patients with any documented cardiac involvement require ongoing cardiology follow-up. For those with ventricular dysfunction, follow heart failure clinic protocols including optimization of guideline-directed medical therapy. Device follow-up (ICD/pacemaker) as indicated. Monitoring for late arrhythmic events is important; in patients with initial normal studies but high-risk disease features, periodic reassessment (ECG and possibly imaging) at intervals such as 6–12 months may be appropriate.

- Rehabilitation and lifestyle: Cardiac rehabilitation and graded physical activity are beneficial once acute inflammation resolves; training should be individualized. Vaccination and infection prevention are important when immunosuppression is used.

- Counseling: Patients should be counseled on symptoms that warrant urgent evaluation (worsening dyspnea, chest pain, syncope, palpitations) and on medication adherence. Discuss reproductive planning if potent immunosuppressants are used.

In summary, cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathies is clinically important and requires early recognition and a coordinated, individualized management strategy combining immunosuppression and standard cardiovascular therapies. Prognosis varies widely; careful monitoring and use of advanced diagnostic tools such as CMR and, when necessary, EMB, allow targeted therapy and improved outcomes.

Introduction

Proximal muscle weakness is a common and diagnostically challenging clinical presentation encountered in internal medicine, neurology, and rheumatology. The differential is broad, encompassing primary muscle disorders (myopathies), neuromuscular junction and motor neuron diseases, systemic illnesses with secondary muscle involvement, endocrine and metabolic derangements, drugs and toxins, and critical illness-related muscle dysfunction. Accurate and timely differentiation among these causes is essential because treatment and prognosis vary widely: some etiologies are immunotherapy-responsive inflammatory myopathies, others are genetic metabolic disorders with supportive care and genetic counseling, while drug-induced myopathies require cessation of the offending agent. This chapter presents a structured, pathophysiology-focused approach to the differential diagnosis of progressive proximal weakness. It emphasizes clinical reasoning that integrates history and examination with laboratory testing, electrophysiology, imaging, and muscle biopsy to arrive at a diagnosis.

# Inflammatory myopathies (dermatomyositis, polymyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, inclusion body myositis)

Inflammatory myopathies are immune-mediated muscle disorders characterized by varying degrees of muscle fiber necrosis, inflammatory infiltrates, and—depending on the subtype—distinct clinical, serologic, and histopathologic signatures. Recognizing features that differentiate dermatomyositis (DM), polymyositis (PM), immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), and inclusion body myositis (IBM) is critical because each follows a different course and responds differently to immunotherapy.

Dermatomyositis is an inflammatory myopathy with characteristic skin manifestations and a predilection for microvascular injury. Clinically, DM presents with symmetric, subacute to chronic proximal muscle weakness often accompanied by heliotrope rash, Gottron papules or sign, Shawl sign, and photodistributed poikiloderma. Pathophysiologically, complement-mediated microangiopathy leads to perifascicular atrophy and capillary dropout. Histology commonly shows perivascular and perimysial inflammatory infiltrates rich in B cells and CD4+ T cells, perifascicular fiber atrophy, and myxovirus resistance protein A (MxA) expression. Adults with DM carry an increased risk of internal malignancy, particularly in the first three years after onset; therefore age-appropriate malignancy screening is a component of the workup.

Polymyositis is classically described as a proximal, symmetric, subacute myopathy without the cutaneous features of DM. It is less common than previously thought when strict diagnostic criteria and modern serology and pathology are applied; many cases previously labeled PM are now reclassified as IMNM or overlap myositis. PM is characterized by endomysial inflammatory infiltrates composed predominantly of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells invading non-necrotic muscle fibers that express class I major histocompatibility complex (MHC I) molecules. PM should be a diagnosis of exclusion in adults after other causes (DM, IMNM, IBM, hereditary myopathies) are considered.

Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) presents with marked, often rapidly progressive proximal weakness and prominently elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels. IMNM is histologically dominated by muscle fiber necrosis with relatively sparse inflammatory infiltrate. Two key serologic markers are associated with IMNM: anti–signal recognition particle (anti-SRP) and anti–3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (anti-HMGCR) antibodies. Anti-HMGCR-associated IMNM has been linked to prior statin exposure in some patients, though cases occur without statin exposure. IMNM tends to be more refractory to standard corticosteroid monotherapy and often requires combined immunosuppressive strategies including intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), rituximab, or other steroid-sparing agents.

Inclusion body myositis (IBM) is the most common acquired myopathy in patients over the age of 50 years and has a characteristic clinical phenotype: slowly progressive, often asymmetric weakness with preferential involvement of finger flexors, knee extensors (quadriceps), and proximal limb muscles. Dysphagia is common. IBM is refractory to conventional immunosuppression. Pathology demonstrates a combination of inflammatory and degenerative features: endomysial T-cell infiltrates invading non-necrotic fibers, rimmed vacuoles, protein aggregates with beta-amyloid and phosphorylated tau, and mitochondrial abnormalities such as ragged-red fibers. Clinically, modest elevations of CK (often <10 times the upper limit of normal) are typical; marked CK elevations suggest alternative diagnoses. The combination of clinical pattern (finger flexor/ quadriceps-predominant weakness in an older patient), characteristic biopsy, and poor response to immunotherapy supports the diagnosis.

For inflammatory myopathies, CK is a useful screening and monitoring tool: normal CK range varies by laboratory and sex but is often quoted approximately as 20–200 U/L; IMNM and DM may show very high levels (into the thousands), whereas IBM often shows only modest elevations. Myositis-specific antibodies (MSAs) and myositis-associated antibodies (MAAs) now guide diagnosis and subtyping; examples include anti-Jo-1 and other aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase antibodies (antisynthetase syndrome), anti-Mi-2, anti-TIF1-γ (cancer-associated DM), anti-MDA5 (amyopathic DM with interstitial lung disease), anti-SRP, and anti-HMGCR. Imaging with MRI can show muscle edema indicative of active inflammation; patterns of muscle involvement on MRI can support certain diagnoses and guide biopsy location. Muscle biopsy remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis and for distinguishing between PM, DM, IMNM, and IBM.

# Endocrine causes: hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, Cushing syndrome

Endocrine disorders can produce proximal muscle weakness by distinct mechanisms: metabolic derangement of muscle energy use, structural protein changes, and steroid-mediated catabolism. Recognizing endocrine myopathies is important because many are reversible with appropriate hormonal treatment.

Hypothyroidism leads to a chronic myopathy characterized by weakness, cramps, stiffness, and delayed relaxation of deep tendon reflexes. The pathophysiology involves slowed muscle fiber metabolism, accumulation of mucopolysaccharides within muscle, and reduced mitochondrial oxidative activity. Laboratory findings often include elevated CK levels (which may be markedly increased in some hypothyroid patients), hypercholesterolemia, and typical thyroid function tests of elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) with low free thyroxine (free T4). Reference ranges vary; a typical TSH reference range is approximately 0.4–4.5 mIU/L and free T4 0.8–1.8 ng/dL, but ranges depend on assay. Muscle symptoms often improve over weeks to months after thyroid hormone replacement, although very prolonged hypothyroidism may lead to persistent weakness.

Hyperthyroidism produces a catabolic myopathy with preferential atrophy of type II (fast-twitch) fibers leading to proximal weakness, myalgias, and occasionally myopathy with markedly elevated CK in severe cases. The mechanism is increased protein catabolism and increased metabolic rate leading to muscle wasting. Thyroid function tests show suppressed TSH and elevated free T4 and/or free T3. Management revolves around treating the thyrotoxicosis, which usually reverses the myopathic symptoms.

Endogenous or exogenous excess glucocorticoids—Cushing syndrome or chronic corticosteroid therapy—cause steroid myopathy characterized by predominant proximal muscle weakness with relative preservation of muscle enzymes. The pathophysiology involves glucocorticoid-induced upregulation of ubiquitin–proteasome proteolysis, suppression of protein synthesis, and selective type II fiber atrophy. Serum CK is often normal or only mildly elevated; aldolase may also be normal. Clinical clues include other stigmata of glucocorticoid excess (central obesity, skin thinning, easy bruising, hypertension, glucose intolerance). Management requires reducing glucocorticoid exposure when possible and supportive rehabilitation; recovery may be incomplete if exposure is prolonged.

# Metabolic and inherited myopathies (glycogen storage diseases, mitochondrial myopathies)

Inherited metabolic myopathies present across the lifespan with variable onset patterns—some with exercise intolerance, episodic rhabdomyolysis, or progressive weakness. A strong family history, early age of onset, exercise-provoked symptoms, and specific biochemical clues point toward genetic etiologies.

Glycogen storage diseases (GSDs) affecting muscle include Pompe disease (acid maltase deficiency, GSD II), McArdle disease (myophosphorylase deficiency, GSD V), and others. Pompe disease—caused by deficiency of lysosomal acid alpha-glucosidase—can present as an infantile cardiomyopathic form or late-onset limb-girdle–type myopathy with progressive proximal weakness and respiratory insufficiency from diaphragmatic involvement. Diagnosis is supported by elevated CK (variable), muscle biopsy showing vacuolar myopathy with glycogen accumulation, and confirmatory enzymatic assay or genetic testing for GAA mutations; enzyme replacement therapy is available and effective in many patients, making early recognition critical.

McArdle disease classically presents with exercise intolerance, muscle cramps, and myoglobinuria during strenuous activity. Patients may report the "second wind" phenomenon—improvement in exercise tolerance after a brief rest as muscle perfusion and alternate fuel use improve. CK can be markedly elevated after exercise; forearm exercise testing demonstrates absent rise in lactate with normal ammonia elevation. Diagnosis is by myophosphorylase activity assay or PYGM gene testing.

Mitochondrial myopathies are genetically heterogeneous disorders that impair oxidative phosphorylation. They commonly present with exercise intolerance, fatigue, ptosis, ophthalmoparesis, and myalgias; multisystem involvement—sensorineural hearing loss, cardiomyopathy, diabetes, and neurological deficits—is typical. Muscle biopsy may reveal ragged-red fibers on modified Gomori trichrome stain and cytochrome c oxidase–deficient fibers. Lactate and pyruvate may be elevated at rest or with exercise. Genetic testing may identify mitochondrial DNA or nuclear DNA mutations; because heteroplasmy influences clinical expression, testing strategies depend on clinical suspicion and tissue selection.

Other metabolic myopathies include lipid metabolism disorders (e.g., carnitine palmitoyltransferase II deficiency) that cause recurrent myoglobinuria and weakness precipitated by fasting or prolonged exercise, and defects of purine metabolism that present with episodic weakness. Screening tests include baseline CK, lactate, acylcarnitine profile, and targeted genetic testing. In suspected hereditary diseases, referral to genetics and consideration of disease-specific therapies (such as dietary modification, avoidance of triggers, enzyme replacement) are important clinical actions.

# Drug/toxin-induced myopathies and critical illness myopathy

Numerous medications and toxins cause myopathy by mechanisms that include direct myofiber toxicity, immune-mediated necrosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and steroid effects. Iatrogenic myopathies are often reversible when the causative agent is stopped, but in some cases—especially with prolonged exposure—irreversible damage can occur.

Statin-associated myopathy is commonest among drug-induced myopathies. Clinical manifestations range from mild myalgia with normal CK to myositis with marked CK elevation and rare rhabdomyolysis. Risk factors include higher statin doses, concomitant drugs that inhibit statin metabolism (e.g., CYP3A4 inhibitors), hypothyroidism, and certain genetic predispositions (e.g., SLCO1B1 variants). Anti-HMGCR positive IMNM is a distinct immune-mediated process that can be triggered by statin exposure and typically requires immunotherapy.

Glucocorticoid-induced myopathy was discussed above as an endocrine cause but is also relevant in the context of chronic medication exposure. Prolonged use leads to type II fiber atrophy and proximal weakness, often without CK elevation.

Other agents associated with myopathy include alcohol (toxic myopathy and alcoholic rhabdomyolysis), alcohol withdrawal, colchicine, antiretrovirals (notably zidovudine causing mitochondrial toxicity), amphotericin, chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine (vacuolar myopathy with autophagic vacuoles), and various chemotherapeutic agents. Immune checkpoint inhibitors have been implicated in immune-mediated myositis often with myocarditis, necessitating prompt recognition.

Critical illness myopathy (CIM) and polyneuropathy (CIP) are frequent causes of profound weakness in ICU patients, particularly those who have sepsis, multiorgan failure, prolonged immobilization, or receive high-dose corticosteroids and neuromuscular blocking agents. CIM is characterized by diffuse symmetric limb and respiratory muscle weakness, reduced or absent deep tendon reflexes, and difficulty weaning from ventilatory support. Laboratory features can include low-amplitude compound muscle action potentials on nerve conduction studies, and myopathic changes on electromyography. Muscle biopsy shows muscle fiber atrophy, myosin loss, and sometimes necrosis. Management is supportive: mobilization, glycemic control, minimization of corticosteroids and neuromuscular blockers when feasible, and rehabilitation. Recovery is variable and can be protracted.

When evaluating suspected drug-related myopathy, a careful medication and toxin history is essential, including over-the-counter drugs, supplements, and illicit substances. Temporal association, pattern of CK change, and response to drug withdrawal support causality. In cases with immune-mediated features (e.g., anti-HMGCR), immunosuppressive therapy may be necessary.

# Neuromuscular junction disorders and motor neuron disease considerations

Although classic proximal myopathies primarily affect muscle fibers, disorders of the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) and motor neuron disease can present with proximal weakness and must be differentiated because management differs fundamentally.

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disorder of the NMJ characterized by fluctuating weakness that worsens with exertion and improves with rest. Although ocular involvement (ptosis, diplopia) is common, generalized MG can cause prominent proximal limb weakness that may be mistaken for a myopathy. Pathophysiologically, autoantibodies target the acetylcholine receptor (AChR) or muscle-specific kinase (MuSK), leading to reduced neuromuscular transmission. Diagnostic testing includes serum antibodies (AChR or MuSK), repetitive nerve stimulation showing decremental response, single-fiber EMG demonstrating increased jitter, and edrophonium testing historically (now rarely used). Treatment with acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, immunosuppression, IVIG, plasmapheresis, or thymectomy (in selected AChR-positive patients) contrasts with therapies for inflammatory myopathies.

Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS) is a presynaptic disorder with proximal weakness, autonomic dysfunction, and hyporeflexia; it is classically paraneoplastic, associated with small cell lung cancer. Electrophysiologically, LEMS shows small compound muscle action potentials that increment with high-frequency stimulation or after exercise. Antibodies against presynaptic P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channels support the diagnosis.

Motor neuron disease, particularly amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), can occasionally present with prominent proximal weakness. However, ALS usually combines lower motor neuron signs (atrophy, fasciculations) with upper motor neuron signs (hyperreflexia, spasticity), and shows a pattern spreading across regions rather than isolated symmetric proximal weakness. Electrophysiological studies in ALS demonstrate widespread denervation and reinnervation (fibrillation potentials, positive sharp waves, large amplitude motor unit potentials) in multiple limbs and regions. Preserving sensory function helps distinguish ALS from many myopathies and peripheral neuropathies.

Distinguishing NMJ and motor neuron disorders from primary myopathies relies on recognizing fluctuating vs static weakness, fatigability, distribution (ocular and bulbar involvement common in NMJ disease), presence of fasciculations, reflex changes, sensory symptoms, and electrophysiologic patterns. Serologic tests and targeted imaging for paraneoplastic causes are indicated as clinically relevant.

# Systemic diseases with muscle involvement: connective tissue diseases, paraneoplastic syndromes

Muscle involvement occurs in many systemic diseases, where muscle weakness may be a primary autoimmune myopathy, part of a multisystem inflammatory process, or a paraneoplastic phenomenon. Evaluating for systemic features—skin rash, arthritis, interstitial lung disease, Raynaud phenomenon, constitutional symptoms, and signs of malignancy—guides the diagnostic workup.

Connective tissue diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), systemic sclerosis, mixed connective tissue disease, and overlap syndromes can be associated with inflammatory myositis or myositis as part of the disease spectrum. Myositis in overlap syndromes often carries myositis-associated antibodies (e.g., anti-U1 RNP, anti-PM/Scl) and may present with extramuscular manifestations like interstitial lung disease and esophageal dysmotility. Clinical suspicion should prompt testing for antinuclear antibody (ANA) and directed extractable nuclear antigen panels, and careful assessment for pulmonary involvement.

Paraneoplastic myositis, most notably dermatomyositis, is associated with an increased risk of underlying malignancy, especially in adults over age 50. The type of associated cancer varies with age, sex, and specific myositis antibody profile (for example, anti-TIF1-γ and anti-NXP2 in DM are associated with higher cancer risk). Screening for occult malignancy in appropriate patients often includes age-appropriate cancer screening, imaging (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis), mammography or pelvic imaging in women, colonoscopy, and targeted investigations guided by clinical clues.

Infectious causes can also produce myositis, either as direct invasion (e.g., pyomyositis, trichinosis) or via immune mechanisms (e.g., HIV-associated myopathy). HIV-associated inflammatory myopathy can resemble polymyositis or immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy and requires consideration of antiretroviral status, HIV viral load, and opportunistic infections.

When systemic disease is suspected, a multidisciplinary approach integrating rheumatology, oncology, infectious disease, and neurology is often necessary. Treating the underlying systemic disorder can result in improvement of muscle symptoms, and conversely, absence of systemic features may prompt focused myositis evaluation.

# Diagnostic algorithm including history, exam, labs, EMG, imaging and biopsy

A systematic diagnostic algorithm enhances the accuracy and efficiency of evaluating proximal muscle weakness. The clinician should synthesize the history and physical to direct targeted testing rather than ordering a broad panel indiscriminately.

History should focus on the onset (acute, subacute, chronic), progression (rapidly progressive vs slowly progressive), symmetry (symmetric vs asymmetric), distribution (proximal vs distal, involvement of finger flexors or quadriceps), associated symptoms (dysphagia, respiratory difficulty, ocular symptoms, myalgias, systemic features like rash, fever, weight loss), exercise-related triggers (exercise intolerance, second-wind phenomenon, rhabdomyolysis), medication history (statins, steroids, antimalarials, antiretrovirals, immune checkpoint inhibitors, illicit drugs), family history of muscle disease, and occupational exposures. Temporal relationships (e.g., onset after starting a medication) are especially informative.

Physical examination should document strength using Medical Research Council (MRC) grading, distribution of weakness, presence of bulbar or respiratory muscle involvement, presence of skin findings (heliotrope rash, Gottron papules), reflexes, sensory examination (to help exclude neuropathic causes), presence of fasciculations (suggesting lower motor neuron disease), and signs of systemic illness (cachexia, organomegaly, skin changes). Careful observation for asymmetric weakness, selective muscle groups (e.g., finger flexors in IBM), and the fatigability characteristic of NMJ disorders is essential.

Initial laboratory testing usually includes serum CK, aldolase (less commonly used), thyroid-stimulating hormone and free T4, electrolytes, renal and hepatic function, inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP), and autoimmune serology including ANA and myositis-specific/associated antibody panels (e.g., anti-Jo-1 and other antisynthetase antibodies, anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR, anti-Mi-2, anti-TIF1-γ, anti-MDA5). Typical CK normal ranges vary by lab and sex but are often approximately 30–200 U/L; levels in inflammatory myopathies can range from mildly to markedly elevated (hundreds to thousands U/L). In suspected metabolic myopathy, lactate (resting and post-exercise), ammonia, and acylcarnitine profiles are useful. In suspected paraneoplastic or infectious etiologies, directed serologies and imaging are performed.

Electrophysiology (EMG and nerve conduction studies) is a crucial next step when the diagnosis is unclear. EMG can distinguish myopathic from neuropathic processes and identify neuromuscular junction dysfunction. Classic myopathic EMG features include short-duration, low-amplitude motor unit potentials with early recruitment and spontaneous fibrillations/positive sharp waves in active myositis. In NMJ disorders, repetitive nerve stimulation may show decrement (MG) or incremental response (LEMS), and single-fiber EMG shows increased jitter. Denervation and reinnervation patterns suggest motor neuron disease. Electrophysiology also helps to localize affected muscles to guide biopsy.

MRI of muscle using T1, T2, and STIR sequences can detect muscle edema (active inflammation) and chronic fatty replacement and atrophy. MRI helps select the optimal biopsy site—an area with edema but not end-stage fatty replacement—and documents the pattern of muscle involvement, which can be disease-specific (for example, scapular stabilizers in certain myopathies). Ultrasound is an alternative imaging modality that can identify edema and guide needle biopsy.

If noninvasive testing fails to establish a diagnosis, or when biopsy is required to confirm inflammatory versus noninflammatory myopathy, muscle biopsy is performed. Proper biopsy technique is critical: select an affected muscle that is not end-stage fibrofatty replaced and avoid sampling very weak or atrophic muscles that may yield nonspecific changes. Pathologic evaluation should include routine histology (H&E), modified Gomori trichrome, enzyme histochemistry (ATPase, COX, SDH), immunohistochemistry (MHC I, membrane attack complex), and electron microscopy when indicated. Biopsy findings are interpreted in clinical context: perifascicular atrophy and perivascular inflammation support dermatomyositis; endomysial invasion by CD8+ T cells is seen in polymyositis; prominent necrosis with sparse inflammation favors IMNM; rimmed vacuoles and inclusion bodies indicate IBM; vacuolar changes with autophagosomal markers suggest drug-induced chloroquine myopathy.

Key clinical decision points emerge from combining the above data. For example, a patient with subacute symmetric proximal weakness, markedly elevated CK, positive anti-SRP antibodies, and biopsy showing necrosis with scant inflammation should be treated as IMNM with aggressive immunosuppression. An older patient with asymmetric finger flexor and quadriceps weakness, minimal CK elevation, and biopsy showing rimmed vacuoles is likely to have IBM, where immunosuppression is generally not effective. If NMJ testing demonstrates decremental responses and positive AChR antibodies, start appropriate therapy for MG. If thyroid dysfunction is identified, treating the endocrine disorder is the priority before pursuing invasive testing unless features strongly suggest primary myopathy.

Algorithmic approach in brief: start with targeted history and physical, obtain baseline labs including CK and thyroid tests, review medications, perform serologic testing guided by clinical features, use EMG and imaging to distinguish myopathy from neuropathy/NMJ disease and to select a biopsy site, and reserve muscle biopsy and genetic testing for cases where needed to reach a diagnosis. Throughout, monitor for complications such as respiratory involvement and cardiac involvement (arrhythmia, myocarditis), particularly in inflammatory myopathies and mitochondrial disease; consider cardiology evaluation (ECG, echocardiography, troponin) when indicated.

Conclusion

Progressive proximal muscle weakness requires a comprehensive, systematic approach that integrates clinical pattern recognition with targeted laboratory, electrophysiologic, imaging, and pathologic investigations. Differentiating inflammatory myopathies, endocrine and metabolic causes, drug-induced and critical illness myopathies, neuromuscular junction and motor neuron disorders, and systemic diseases guides appropriate therapy and prognosis. Early identification of treatable conditions—including immune-mediated myopathies, endocrine disorders, and drug-induced causes—can markedly improve outcomes. A collaborative, multidisciplinary approach is often necessary for complex cases, and clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for overlapping and paraneoplastic processes.

# Introduction

Immune‑mediated myopathies (IIMs), a heterogenous group that includes polymyositis, dermatomyositis, immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy, antisynthetase syndrome, and inclusion body myositis (distinct in pathogenesis and responsiveness), commonly present with proximal muscle weakness, elevated muscle enzymes, and systemic features. Management requires balancing rapid control of immune‑mediated muscle injury to preserve strength and prevent life‑threatening complications (bulbar dysfunction, respiratory failure) against the short‑ and long‑term toxicities of immunosuppression. This chapter summarizes evidence‑based approaches to immunosuppressive therapy in IIMs, strategies for acute interventions in severe disease, monitoring response to therapy, and measures to mitigate infection risk during immunosuppression. The emphasis is clinical: dosing, typical sequences of therapy, decision points for escalation, and practical monitoring and preventive measures.

# First-line therapies: high-dose corticosteroids—dosing, tapering and adverse effects

Corticosteroids are the cornerstone of initial therapy for most inflammatory myopathies because of their rapid anti‑inflammatory effects. They are usually started at high doses to achieve prompt disease control, then tapered and transitioned to steroid‑sparing agents to limit steroid toxicity.

For moderate to severe active disease (significant proximal weakness, rapidly rising creatine kinase, or extramuscular involvement such as interstitial lung disease), oral prednisone is commonly initiated at a dose of 0.5–1.0 mg/kg/day, with many clinicians using 1 mg/kg/day (maximum commonly 60–80 mg/day) for severe presentations. In life‑threatening situations (e.g., imminent respiratory compromise, severe dysphagia with aspiration risk, rapidly progressive weakness), pulse intravenous methylprednisolone 500–1000 mg daily for 3 days can be used to obtain a faster effect before switching to high‑dose oral prednisone.

Tapering is individualized. Once clinical improvement is evident (usually within 2–6 weeks) and muscle enzymes decline, the prednisone dose should be reduced gradually to a maintenance or physiologic range. A practical approach is to lower by 10 mg every 1–2 weeks until 30 mg/day, then reduce by 2.5–5 mg every 2–4 weeks toward 10–15 mg/day. The goal is to reach a dose less than 10 mg/day if disease control permits; many patients will require a steroid‑sparing agent to achieve this. Very slow tapers and prolonged low‑dose maintenance (e.g., 5–10 mg/day) may be necessary in refractory disease.

Adverse effects of systemic corticosteroids are frequent and cumulative. Short‑term effects of high doses include mood changes, insomnia, hyperglycemia, fluid retention, and hypertension. Long‑term risks include osteoporosis and fractures, weight gain, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, cataracts and glaucoma, opportunistic infections, adrenal suppression, and skin thinning with easy bruising. Prophylactic measures should be instituted when prolonged courses are anticipated: bone health optimization with calcium (typically 1000–1200 mg/day) and vitamin D (800–2000 IU/day), assessment of fracture risk (DEXA scan) and institution of bisphosphonates for patients at high risk, monitoring and management of blood glucose and blood pressure, and counseling regarding infection risk. Consider prophylaxis for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia when combining high‑dose corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone ≥20 mg/day for >4 weeks) with additional immunosuppressive agents; a common regimen is trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole one double strength tablet three times weekly or single strength daily depending on tolerance.

Clinical decision points include when to escalate if steroids alone are ineffective (usually by 4–8 weeks if there is minimal improvement) and when to add additional immunosuppression to enable steroid tapering. Steroid refractoriness or dependence should prompt early consideration of adjunctive agents tailored to myositis subtype and comorbidities.

# Adjunctive and steroid-sparing agents: methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, cyclosporine

Steroid‑sparing agents are used to maintain disease control, reduce cumulative steroid exposure, and treat chronic or refractory disease. Choice among agents is influenced by disease phenotype, comorbidities, pregnancy potential, toxicity profiles, and clinician experience. Evidence for efficacy comes from randomized trials (limited size), cohort studies, and clinical experience.

Methotrexate (MTX) is one of the most commonly used agents. Typical dosing for myositis is 15–25 mg weekly, either orally or subcutaneously; some regimens begin at 10–15 mg weekly and escalate as tolerated. Folic acid 1 mg daily or 5–10 mg weekly is given to reduce side effects. Monitor complete blood count (CBC), liver function tests (LFTs), and renal function prior to initiation and every 1–3 months thereafter. Contraindications include significant hepatic disease, excessive alcohol use, pregnancy (teratogenic), and severe renal impairment (dose adjustment required). Adverse effects include transaminitis, cytopenias, stomatitis, and pulmonary toxicity (rare). Clinically, expect a response over 2–3 months; MTX is often combined with corticosteroids.

Azathioprine (AZA) is useful as an alternative, especially when methotrexate is contraindicated or not tolerated. Typical dosing is 2–3 mg/kg/day (commonly 50–150 mg/day) given once daily. Measure thiopurine S‑methyltransferase (TPMT) activity or genotype prior to initiation because low TPMT activity increases risk of severe myelosuppression. Monitor CBC and LFTs every 1–2 weeks for the first 1–2 months, then every 1–3 months. Adverse effects include leukopenia, hepatotoxicity, gastrointestinal intolerance, and increased risk of infections. AZA is safe in many women planning pregnancy after appropriate counseling, unlike methotrexate.

Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) is increasingly used, particularly when interstitial lung disease coexists, because of good tolerability and evidence for efficacy in ILD associated with antisynthetase syndrome or dermatomyositis. Dosing typically starts at 500 mg twice daily and increases to 1000–1500 mg twice daily as tolerated (total 1000–3000 mg/day). Monitor CBC and LFTs periodically. Adverse effects include gastrointestinal upset, leukopenia, and increased infection risk. MMF is teratogenic and contraindicated in pregnancy.

Calcineurin inhibitors—cyclosporine and tacrolimus—are considered in refractory cases, particularly when there is associated interstitial lung disease or when other agents have failed. Cyclosporine dosing for myositis is often 3–5 mg/kg/day in divided doses (adjusted to clinical response and side effects). Monitor blood pressure, renal function, and trough levels as appropriate. Adverse effects include nephrotoxicity, hypertension, hypertrichosis, tremor, and metabolic derangements. Tacrolimus may be used at 0.075–0.2 mg/kg/day in divided doses targeted to trough concentrations, and is associated with similar toxicities including nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity. Calcineurin inhibitors are generally reserved for refractory disease or specific phenotypes (e.g., anti‑synthetase with ILD) because of toxicity and the need for drug monitoring.

Clinical practice commonly employs an early combination of corticosteroids plus a steroid‑sparing agent (MTX, AZA, or MMF) to allow steroid taper and reduce long‑term toxicity. Choice is individualized: methotrexate is often preferred for isolated muscle‑predominant disease in nonpregnant patients, AZA for those contemplating pregnancy or with hepatic disease, MMF for those with ILD, and calcineurin inhibitors for refractory cases with lung involvement.

# Biologic and targeted therapies: rituximab, tofacitinib and other emerging agents

Biologic and targeted therapies are increasingly important for refractory IIM or specific autoantibody‑defined phenotypes. Randomized controlled data are limited but evolving; clinical decisions often draw on registry data, small trials, and extrapolation from other autoimmune diseases.

Rituximab, an anti‑CD20 monoclonal antibody causing B‑cell depletion, has the strongest evidence among biologics for myositis. The randomized RIM trial did not meet its primary endpoint but subsequent analyses and real‑world data support rituximab efficacy in refractory dermatomyositis and polymyositis and particularly in certain autoantibody subsets (e.g., anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR, and some antisynthetase patients). Typical regimens include either 375 mg/m^2 weekly for 4 weeks or two doses of 1000 mg given two weeks apart; some clinicians repeat dosing at 6 months depending on clinical response and B‑cell recovery. Prior to initiation, screen for hepatitis B (HBsAg, anti‑HBc total) and tuberculosis; vaccinate ideally before B‑cell depletion. Monitor CBC and immunoglobulin levels (IgG) as hypogammaglobulinemia can develop, predisposing to infections. Live vaccines are contraindicated during and for months after rituximab therapy; inactivated vaccines have reduced immunogenicity.

Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors such as tofacitinib target intracellular signaling pathways downstream of multiple cytokine receptors and have shown promise, particularly in refractory dermatomyositis with cutaneous manifestations and some myositis subtypes. Tofacitinib dosing commonly used in rheumatology is 5 mg twice daily (some data support 5 mg once daily for certain indications); dose adjustments for renal or hepatic impairment are required. Adverse effects include increased risk of infections (including herpes zoster), cytopenias, elevated lipids, and potential thrombotic risk at higher doses in certain populations. Evidence for JAK inhibitors in IIM remains limited to case series and small trials, but they are considered for refractory disease, especially with prominent skin disease.

Other targeted agents under investigation include abatacept (CTLA‑4‑Ig) which modulates T‑cell costimulation and has shown benefit in some refractory myositis cohorts; anti‑interferon therapies (e.g., sifalimumab, anifrolumab) given interferon signatures in dermatomyositis; complement inhibitors (e.g., eculizumab) in complement‑mediated processes; and anti‑IL‑6 agents (tocilizumab) with mixed results. Clinical trial enrollment should be considered for refractory cases. Use of these agents requires awareness of mechanisms, potential infectious complications, vaccination planning, and organ‑specific monitoring.

Choice to escalate to biologic or targeted therapy is driven by lack of adequate response to optimized standard immunosuppression, intolerable toxicity from conventional agents, or presence of particular phenotypes where biologic data are more compelling. Rituximab remains a common next step after failure of corticosteroids plus an initial steroid‑sparing agent.

# Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG): mechanisms of action, indications, dosing and monitoring

IVIG is an important therapeutic tool in IIM, especially dermatomyositis and immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy, and in acute severe presentations. IVIG has multi‑faceted immunomodulatory effects: it provides anti‑idiotypic antibodies that neutralize pathogenic autoantibodies, modulates Fc receptor expression, interferes with complement activation, alters cytokine production, and modulates B cell and T cell function. Because of these diverse mechanisms, IVIG can be effective in antibody‑mediated myopathies and in severe inflammatory states refractory to other immunosuppressants.

Indications for IVIG include dermatomyositis with significant skin or muscle disease refractory to or intolerant of standard immunosuppression, severe or rapidly progressive muscle weakness, treatment of dysphagia or respiratory muscle involvement when rapid effect is required, and certain antibody‑positive necrotizing myopathies (e.g., anti‑SRP and anti‑HMGCR) in which IVIG has shown benefit. IVIG is also used as bridging therapy in severe disease while other agents take effect or when conventional agents are contraindicated (e.g., pregnancy, severe infection risk).

Common dosing regimens are 2 g/kg total per treatment cycle, typically administered as 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 consecutive days or 1 g/kg on two consecutive days. For maintenance therapy, some clinicians give 1 g/kg every 2–4 weeks or 2 g/kg every 4–8 weeks depending on durability of response and tolerability. IVIG infusion rates should be adjusted based on patient tolerance and coexisting cardiac or renal disease.

Monitoring includes baseline renal function (creatinine), CBC, and consideration of serum IgA levels because patients with IgA deficiency are at risk for severe anaphylactic reactions if they have anti‑IgA antibodies. Monitor for infusion reactions (headache, fever, chills), thromboembolic events, aseptic meningitis, hemolysis (IVIG can cause hemolysis due to blood group antibodies—check hemoglobin and direct antiglobulin test if suspected), and renal impairment (prefer subcutaneous or lower infusion rates in patients at risk). Pre‑medication with acetaminophen and antihistamine can reduce infusion reactions; corticosteroid pre‑medication is sometimes used in patients prone to severe reactions. IVIG is expensive and supply may be limited; decisions to continue should be based on objective clinical improvement and functional gains.

# Acute management of severe features: treatment of dysphagia, respiratory failure, immunotherapy escalation

Severe features of immune‑mediated myopathies include bulbar dysfunction (dysphagia, aspiration risk), respiratory muscle weakness leading to ventilatory failure, rapidly progressive weakness, and severe systemic involvement (e.g., myocarditis). Management requires a coordinated multidisciplinary approach with early recognition and aggressive supportive and immunomodulatory therapies.

Dysphagia: Bulbar weakness can cause aspiration, weight loss, and recurrent pneumonias. Initial assessment includes clinical bedside swallow evaluation by speech‑language pathology and instrumental studies such as videofluoroscopic swallow study (modified barium swallow) or fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) to define anatomy and physiology. For patients with severe dysphagia or aspiration, temporary enteral feeding via nasogastric tube may be necessary; for persistent dysfunction or prolonged need for feeding (>4–6 weeks anticipated), percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) placement should be considered. Therapeutically, high‑dose corticosteroids and IVIG can produce relatively rapid improvement; in severe or refractory cases, escalation to rituximab or plasmapheresis may be required depending on phenotype and suspected antibody‑mediated mechanisms. Swallow exercises and rehabilitation are essential adjuncts. Airway protection is paramount; recurrent aspiration or severe inability to manage secretions may necessitate more definitive airway decisions and aggressive immunotherapy.

Respiratory failure: Respiratory muscle weakness manifests as orthopnea, dyspnea, reduced cough, nocturnal hypoventilation, and in severe cases, hypercapnic respiratory failure. Pulmonary function testing with supine and upright forced vital capacity (FVC) is an important objective measurement; a drop in FVC greater than 10–15% when moving from upright to supine suggests diaphragmatic weakness. An absolute FVC below 50% predicted or a decline of >10–20% over a short interval warrants close monitoring and consideration of respiratory support. Measurement of maximal inspiratory pressure and sniff nasal inspiratory pressure can further quantify inspiratory muscle strength. Arterial blood gases or capnography can detect hypercapnia; overnight oximetry or polysomnography may identify nocturnal hypoventilation.

Management of acute respiratory failure mirrors other neuromuscular respiratory failures: initiate noninvasive ventilation (NIV) such as bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) for hypercapnic failure when the patient is cooperative and has preserved airway protective reflexes. Indications for intubation include inability to protect the airway, severe hypoxemia, high secretions, progressive hypercapnia despite NIV, or decreased level of consciousness. In parallel with respiratory support, aggressive immunotherapy escalation is indicated to reverse the inflammatory myopathy: high‑dose IV methylprednisolone pulses, IVIG, and consideration of plasmapheresis or rituximab in antibody‑mediated disease. Early involvement of pulmonary, neurology/rheumatology, and critical care teams is essential. When ventilation is prolonged, tracheostomy may be needed.

Immunotherapy escalation: Acute severe manifestations typically require escalation beyond oral prednisone. Common approaches include combining pulse IV methylprednisolone with IVIG, adding or switching to a second‑line agent such as rituximab or cyclophosphamide (particularly if concomitant severe interstitial lung disease or vasculitic features), and considering plasmapheresis for fulminant, antibody‑mediated disease or when rapid antibody removal is desired. Cyclophosphamide (IV pulses 500–1000 mg/m^2 every 2–4 weeks) may be used for life‑threatening disease (e.g., severe ILD or myocarditis), with careful monitoring of cytopenias and bladder toxicity. Plasmapheresis can be used as adjunctive therapy in select, severe, antibody‑mediated cases to remove pathogenic autoantibodies; benefits are variable and often transient, so it is typically used as a bridge while other therapies take effect.

All acute decisions should weigh the risk of immunosuppression against the immediate threat to life and function. Early aggressive therapy is justified in life‑threatening situations; conversely, for less severe presentations, stepwise escalation allows assessment of response and minimizes toxicity.

# Monitoring response: CK, strength testing, imaging, and relapse management

Monitoring therapeutic response in IIM requires serial clinical, biochemical, and sometimes imaging assessments. No single metric is sufficient; integration of patient symptoms, objective strength testing, functional measures, muscle enzyme trends, and imaging guides management.

Creatine kinase (CK) is the most commonly used laboratory marker. Typical normal ranges vary by laboratory; a representative reference range is approximately 26–192 U/L, though clinicians should use local laboratory normals. CK correlates with muscle injury but is imperfect: some patients (especially those with chronic disease or inclusion body myositis) have only modest CK elevations or normal values despite active weakness. Serial CK measurements are useful to track trends; falling CK supports biochemical response, but clinical improvement in strength and function is more important.

Strength testing should be performed systematically. The Medical Research Council (MRC) scale grades muscle strength from 0 (no contraction) to 5 (normal) for specific muscle groups; more sensitive tools include manual muscle testing (MMT‑8) used in research and bedside dynamometry for quantitative measurement. Functional assessments such as timed up and go, 6‑minute walk distance, and patient‑reported outcomes (fatigue scales, activities of daily living) complement strength testing. Improvement in proximal limb strength typically lags behind biochemical improvement by several weeks.

Imaging, particularly muscle MRI, provides anatomic and disease activity information. T2/STIR sequences and short tau inversion recovery images detect muscle edema and inflammation; T1 sequences assess fatty replacement and chronic atrophy. MRI helps identify biopsy targets and can document ongoing inflammation despite normal CK. Ultrasound is emerging as a bedside tool to assess muscle texture and echogenicity but is less validated for serial monitoring.

Electromyography (EMG) may show myopathic features and spontaneous activity; it is useful diagnostically but less practical for frequent monitoring. Muscle biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard when subtype clarification is required; repeat biopsy is rarely needed for monitoring.

Relapse management: Relapse is common and may present with recurrent weakness, rising CK, or new extramuscular features. Management depends on severity. For mild flares, increase corticosteroid dose transiently and optimize steroid‑sparing agents; for moderate to severe flares, consider pulse IV steroids, add or switch immunosuppressive therapy (e.g., escalate to rituximab, add IVIG), and re‑evaluate adherence, intercurrent infections, and other contributors (thyroid disease, statin exposure). For certain antibody‑positive necrotizing myopathies, recurrent activity may necessitate long‑term IVIG. Documenting objective changes in strength and function guides escalation. In recurrent refractory disease, referral to specialized centers and clinical trial consideration are appropriate.

# Infection risk mitigation and vaccination considerations in immunosuppressed patients

Immunosuppressive therapies increase infection risk both by impairing cellular and humoral immunity and by altering mucosal defenses. Mitigating that risk requires pre‑therapy screening, vaccination planning, infection prophylaxis when indicated, and ongoing vigilance.

Pre‑therapy screening: Before initiating significant immunosuppression (particularly biologics or alkylating agents), screen for latent infections. Obtain hepatitis B serologies (HBsAg, anti‑HBc total, anti‑HBs) and hepatitis C antibody and reflex RNA testing if positive. Screen for latent tuberculosis with interferon‑gamma release assay (IGRA) or tuberculin skin test; chest radiography if indicated. Test for HIV where risk factors exist. For rituximab or prolonged B‑cell–depleting therapies, assess baseline immunoglobulin levels (IgG) and vaccination status. If hepatitis B core antibody is positive, consult hepatology for antiviral prophylaxis (e.g., entecavir or tenofovir) during and after B‑cell‑depleting therapy to prevent reactivation.

Vaccination: Optimize vaccines prior to initiating immunosuppression when feasible, ideally completing immunizations at least 2–4 weeks before starting therapy (longer if possible for live vaccines). Inactivated vaccines (influenza, pneumococcal, Tdap, hepatitis B) are safe during immunosuppression though responses may be attenuated. Annual inactivated influenza vaccine is recommended for all immunosuppressed patients. Pneumococcal vaccination strategy commonly follows a sequential approach with PCV13 followed by PPSV23 according to guidelines. For rituximab recipients, vaccinate before B‑cell depletion; vaccines given after rituximab may have reduced efficacy for 6–12 months or until B‑cell recovery. Live attenuated vaccines (e.g., zoster live vaccine, MMR, varicella) are contraindicated during significant immunosuppression and for variable periods thereafter (generally until immunosuppression is stopped and immune recovery is confirmed). Newer recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV, non‑live) is preferred and can be administered to immunosuppressed adults; timing relative to immunosuppression should be individualized.

Prophylaxis: Specific prophylactic strategies include Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis with trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole for patients on prolonged high‑dose corticosteroids (prednisone ≥20 mg/day for >4 weeks) in combination with other immunosuppressants, or those receiving cyclophosphamide or rituximab in regimens associated with significant risk. Herpes zoster prophylaxis with antivirals may be considered for patients receiving JAK inhibitors or other agents associated with elevated zoster risk. For HBV exposed patients, antiviral prophylaxis is indicated during and after B‑cell‑depleting therapy.

Patient counseling and preventive measures: Educate patients on infection signs, hand hygiene, avoiding sick contacts, and early reporting of fever or respiratory symptoms. Consider prophylactic measures in high‑risk settings (e.g., influenza prophylaxis antivirals during outbreaks for severely immunosuppressed individuals). Monitor for cytopenias and hypogammaglobulinemia, which may necessitate dose adjustment, IVIG replacement, or infectious disease consultation.

Monitoring during therapy: Regular clinical assessments for infection, periodic CBC, and measurement of immunoglobulin levels (particularly IgG) during B‑cell–depleting therapies guide management. If recurrent severe infections or sustained hypogammaglobulinemia occur, consider IVIG replacement or modifying immunotherapy.

Clinical decision points include balancing infection risk against the consequences of undertreated myositis. In severe disease, aggressive immunosuppression may be lifesaving; in stable disease, de‑escalation and minimizing cumulative immunosuppression reduce infection risk. Coordination with primary care and infectious disease specialists optimizes preventive strategies.

# Conclusion

Management of immune‑mediated myopathies demands individualized, multidisciplinary care that balances rapid disease control with mitigation of treatment toxicity. High‑dose corticosteroids remain first‑line for induction, but early introduction of steroid‑sparing agents (methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate, calcineurin inhibitors) is common to minimize steroid exposure. Biologics such as rituximab and targeted agents like JAK inhibitors are valuable for refractory or phenotype‑specific disease. IVIG has a unique role for antibody‑mediated and severe presentations. Acute complications—dysphagia and respiratory failure—require urgent supportive care and aggressive immunotherapy. Careful monitoring with CK, objective strength and functional measures, and imaging guides treatment response and relapse management. Finally, infection risk mitigation through pre‑treatment screening, vaccination, prophylaxis, and patient education is essential to safely deliver immunosuppressive care. Clinical judgment, patient preference, and evolving evidence inform therapy sequencing and escalation.

Introduction

Severe myopathies—whether hereditary, inflammatory, metabolic, infectious, or toxic—can produce profound systemic complications that are often the major determinants of morbidity and mortality. Beyond treating the underlying muscle disease, clinicians must anticipate, detect, and manage problems that arise from loss of muscle function: impaired swallowing with aspiration risk, respiratory failure from respiratory muscle weakness, venous thromboembolism and pressure injury related to immobility, kidney injury from myocyte breakdown, deconditioning, and the psychosocial stresses of chronic disability. Effective supportive care requires timely assessment, objective monitoring, and coordinated interventions delivered by a multidisciplinary team. This chapter synthesizes practical, evidence-based approaches to the common complications of severe myopathy and describes clinical decision points useful at the bedside.

# Assessment and management of dysphagia and aspiration risk (speech/swallow evaluation, enteral feeding options)

Dysphagia is common in severe myopathies that involve bulbar musculature and contributes to malnutrition, dehydration, and aspiration pneumonia. Clinical assessment begins with a structured history probing symptoms of oropharyngeal dysfunction (dysphagia for liquids versus solids, drooling, nasal regurgitation), voice changes, coughing during or after eating, recurrent chest infections, weight loss, and functional impact on meal preparation. Physical examination should evaluate cranial nerve function, tongue strength and mobility, palatal elevation, gag reflex (noting that gag presence does not exclude significant dysphagia), cough strength, and the ability to manage secretions. Observe a meal where possible to assess coordination, mastication, and postural compensation.

Instrumental assessment should be obtained when clinical concern is moderate to high or when the examination is unreliable. Videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS), also called a modified barium swallow, provides dynamic visualization of oral and pharyngeal phases, identifies penetration or aspiration, and can evaluate the effectiveness of compensatory maneuvers and diet modifications. Fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) allows bedside assessment of secretion management, pharyngeal residue, delayed swallow, and aspiration with different consistencies; FEES is particularly useful when transport to radiology is unsafe or when repeated assessments are needed. Objective measures of cough and respiratory reserve (peak cough flow, forced vital capacity) inform aspiration risk; a peak cough flow <160 L/min suggests ineffective airway clearance and increased aspiration risk. Pulse oximetry and continuous monitoring during trials can detect aspiration-related desaturation.

Management goals are to prevent aspiration and malnutrition while maximizing safe oral intake. Acute bedside strategies include texture modification (thickened liquids, pureed solids), posture adjustments (chin tuck, head rotation), pacing and small bites, and swallowing maneuvers taught by speech–language pathologists. These interventions must be individualized and re-evaluated frequently because bulbar weakness can fluctuate and fatigue can worsen swallowing late in a meal.

Enteral feeding is indicated when safe oral intake cannot meet nutritional needs or when aspiration risk is unacceptable despite compensations. Short-term options include nasogastric (NG) or orogastric tubes. NG tubes are easy to insert and suitable for weeks to a few months; they carry risks of sinusitis, tube displacement, and may worsen lower esophageal sphincter competence. For anticipated long-term enteral feeding (commonly >4–6 weeks), percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) or radiologically inserted gastrostomy should be considered. Decision-making should weigh respiratory status (patients with severe respiratory compromise may have higher PEG-related sedation risk), anticipated disease trajectory, patient goals, and caregiver capacity.

Feeding route selection should also consider the type of dysphagia: patients with severe pharyngeal-phase dysfunction who aspirate despite gastric feeding may require additional strategies to manage secretions (saliva aspiration) and may be candidates for jejunal feeding in rare cases when reflux is a major contributor to aspiration risk. Collaborate with dietitians to calculate caloric and protein goals (commonly 25–30 kcal/kg/day and 1.2–2.0 g protein/kg/day depending on catabolic state), correct micronutrient deficiencies, and tailor enteral formulas for tolerance and glycemic control.

Ongoing reassessment is critical. Speech–language pathologists should reassess swallowing function at regular intervals and after major changes in respiratory or neuromuscular status. Involving patients and families early in shared decision-making about the balance of aspiration risk, quality of life, and feeding preferences improves adherence and aligns interventions with patient goals.

# Respiratory muscle weakness: monitoring, indications for ventilatory support, pulmonary rehabilitation

Respiratory complications are a leading cause of morbidity in severe myopathy. Weakness of the diaphragm and accessory muscles causes hypoventilation, impaired cough, secretion retention, and vulnerability to infections. Early recognition and objective monitoring guide timely interventions to prevent acute respiratory failure.

Monitoring includes serial clinical assessment and objective measurements. Clinically, note orthopnea, sleep-disordered breathing (morning headaches, daytime somnolence, nonrestorative sleep), exertional dyspnea disproportionate to limb weakness, and recurrent lower respiratory tract infections. Bedside measures include forced vital capacity (FVC), maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP or negative inspiratory force, NIF), maximal expiratory pressure (MEP), and peak cough flow (PCF). Supine FVC is particularly informative because a fall in FVC when supine compared with upright position suggests diaphragmatic weakness. Key thresholds used in decision-making include: FVC <50% predicted signals significant restriction; more conservative action thresholds are often FVC <30–40% predicted or an absolute FVC <1.0 L (varies with body habitus). A NIF less negative than -20 cmH2O (for example, -20 cmH2O or less negative) indicates significant inspiratory muscle weakness; many centers use NIF < -30 cmH2O as safer, while NIF > -20 cmH2O raises concern. Peak cough flow <160 L/min suggests ineffective cough and inability to clear secretions.

Arterial blood gas analysis and nocturnal oximetry or polysomnography help detect hypoventilation and sleep-disordered breathing. Early hypoventilation can present with nocturnal hypoxemia and hypercapnia, with daytime hypercapnia a late sign. A rising pCO2 or daytime hypercapnia warrants prompt ventilatory support.

Indications for initiating ventilatory support include acute respiratory failure with hypoxemia and/or hypercapnia, progressive daytime hypercapnia or symptomatic nocturnal hypoventilation, severe impairment in respiratory mechanics (e.g., FVC declining rapidly or below threshold values), inability to protect the airway, and recurrent respiratory infections due to ineffective cough. Noninvasive ventilation (NIV) with bilevel positive airway pressure is the preferred initial approach when patients are cooperative and hemodynamically stable. NIV supports ventilation, reduces work of breathing, improves gas exchange, and may prevent intubation. Indications for NIV initiation include symptoms of nocturnal hypoventilation with documented nocturnal hypercapnia or hypoxemia, resting hypercapnia (PaCO2 >45–50 mmHg depending on local practice), or inability to maintain oxygenation with conservative measures. Settings are individualized to correct hypercapnia and relieve dyspnea; titration commonly targets normocapnia or symptom resolution while ensuring patient comfort.

Invasive mechanical ventilation is indicated when NIV fails, when there is apnea or inability to protect the airway, or when secretions cannot be managed noninvasively. Predictors of NIV failure include severe bulbar dysfunction with inability to clear secretions, excessive aspiration, and progressive mental status deterioration. For patients with chronic progressive myopathy who require long-term mechanical ventilation, options include tracheostomy with invasive ventilation or long-term NIV if feasible. Decisions regarding long-term invasive ventilation should be discussed in the context of patient goals and expected quality of life.

Pulmonary rehabilitation is a cornerstone of care for patients stable from an acute exacerbation. Rehabilitation focuses on breathing retraining, inspiratory muscle training (IMT) where evidence supports benefit in selected patients, airway clearance techniques, optimizing posture and mobility to improve ventilation, and physical conditioning to maximize residual function. Airway clearance methods include manual assisted cough, mechanical insufflation-exsufflation devices, and assisted cough techniques performed by caregivers. Use of mechanically assisted cough may be indicated when PCF is persistently low and secretion burden is high. Rehabilitation plans should be staged and progressive, avoiding overuse fatigue which can worsen muscle weakness; sessions may be brief and frequent initially, increasing duration with tolerance.

# Prevention and management of thromboembolic events and immobility complications

Patients with severe myopathy are at elevated risk for venous thromboembolism (VTE), pressure ulcers, contractures, and deconditioning as a consequence of reduced mobility, venous stasis, and systemic inflammation. Prevention strategies should be instituted early and tailored to bleeding risk.

Pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH; e.g., enoxaparin 40 mg subcutaneously daily in many adults) or unfractionated heparin (e.g., 5000 units subcutaneously every 8–12 hours) is indicated in hospitalized patients with limited mobility and no contraindications. Dose adjustment is required for renal impairment (for example, enoxaparin dosing in severe renal dysfunction) and extremes of body weight. Mechanical prophylaxis—intermittent pneumatic compression devices or graduated compression stockings—should be used when pharmacologic prophylaxis is contraindicated (active bleeding, high bleeding risk). For patients with very high thrombotic risk (previous VTE, known thrombophilia), individualized higher-intensity prophylaxis or early diagnostic surveillance (ultrasound) may be appropriate.

Clinical vigilance for deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism is important: unilateral limb swelling, pain, unexplained tachycardia, hypoxemia, or sudden deterioration should prompt diagnostic evaluation with compression ultrasonography and, when appropriate, computed tomography pulmonary angiography. If VTE is diagnosed, therapeutic anticoagulation is indicated unless contraindicated; agents and regimens follow standard VTE management and must be adapted to renal function and bleeding risk.

Pressure injury prevention requires frequent repositioning (turning every 2 hours for high-risk immobile patients), use of pressure-redistribution mattresses, meticulous skin care, and nutritional optimization for wound healing (adequate protein intake, 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day as indicated). Early involvement of wound care specialists can guide staging and local care. Range-of-motion exercises, splinting, and positioning reduce the risk of joint contractures; contractures should be managed proactively with serial casting, orthoses, and, when necessary, surgical consultation in chronic settings.

Other immobility complications include osteoporosis and loss of bone mass, urinary stasis with urinary tract infections, and deconditioning of cardiovascular and autonomic responses. Prevention strategies include early mobilization as medically safe, calcium and vitamin D supplementation as indicated, fall prevention measures, and bowel/bladder regimens to reduce urinary retention and constipation.

# Renal protection in muscle injury and rhabdomyolysis: fluid management, electrolyte correction, indications for renal replacement therapy

Rhabdomyolysis from muscle necrosis releases intracellular contents—myoglobin, potassium, phosphate, uric acid, and creatine kinase (CK)—that can precipitate acute kidney injury (AKI). Prevention and early management focus on aggressive fluid resuscitation, monitoring and correction of electrolytes, and timely renal replacement therapy (RRT) when indicated.

Diagnosis is suggested by history of muscle injury or disease exacerbation and by laboratory abnormalities. Serum CK elevation is characteristic; values vary widely, but CK >5 times the upper limit of normal is commonly used as a screening threshold. Typical reference ranges: CK male 20–200 U/L, female 20–170 U/L (laboratory-dependent). CK values in severe rhabdomyolysis often exceed 5,000–10,000 U/L and may reach >100,000 U/L. Myoglobinuria may cause dark (tea-colored) urine and is detected clinically by a positive dipstick for blood with few red blood cells on microscopy. Monitor serum creatinine and urine output; AKI is defined and staged by KDIGO criteria: an increase in serum creatinine by ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, or a 1.5× increase from baseline within 7 days, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hr for 6 hours.

Fluid resuscitation is the mainstay of prevention for pigment nephropathy. Begin isotonic crystalloid (0.9% saline) early to restore intravascular volume and enhance renal perfusion and myoglobin washout. Typical initial rates are aggressive—e.g., 1–2 L bolus followed by maintenance rates titrated to achieve urine output targets. Urine output goals commonly suggested for rhabdomyolysis are at least 0.5 mL/kg/hr in general medical patients, and many centers aim for higher output (1.0–2.0 mL/kg/hr) in patients with heavy myoglobin loads while monitoring for fluid overload risk. Monitor hemodynamics, central venous pressure when indicated, and diuresis to guide therapy.

Urinary alkalinization with sodium bicarbonate has been proposed to reduce myoglobin nephrotoxicity by increasing myoglobin solubility and reducing cast formation. Evidence is mixed and routine use is not universally recommended. Consider bicarbonate therapy when large myoglobin burdens exist or when urine pH remains low despite adequate fluids, especially if myoglobinuria is heavy and patient is at high risk for AKI; a common regimen is to add bicarbonate to IV fluids to maintain urine pH >6.5. Avoid aggressive alkalinization in patients with hypocalcemia or volume overload risk. Mannitol and loop diuretics have been used historically to promote diuresis, but routine use lacks strong evidence and should be individualized; diuretics are considered only if euvolemia is established and urine flow remains inadequate.

Electrolyte abnormalities are common and can be life-threatening. Hyperkalemia from release of intracellular potassium can cause arrhythmias; monitor serum potassium frequently (e.g., every 2–6 hours initially). Critical hyperkalemia (commonly K+ ≥6.0–6.5 mEq/L or with ECG changes such as peaked T waves, widened QRS) warrants immediate treatment: intravenous calcium to stabilize the myocardium, insulin with glucose to shift potassium intracellularly, nebulized or IV beta-agonists as adjuncts, and measures to remove potassium (diuretics if urine output sufficient, potassium-binding resin, and RRT). Hypocalcemia may be present but asymptomatic; treat symptomatic hypocalcemia (tetany, arrhythmia) with IV calcium, but avoid routine correction of asymptomatic hypocalcemia because calcium may later deposit in necrotic muscle. Hyperphosphatemia and secondary hypocalcemia should be monitored and managed; phosphate binders and dialysis may be needed for severe derangement.

Indications for renal replacement therapy include refractory hyperkalemia, severe metabolic acidosis unresponsive to medical therapy (for example, pH <7.1–7.2 depending on clinical context), volume overload compromising oxygenation or hemodynamics, uremic complications, and oliguria/anuria with progressive azotemia despite conservative measures. In patients with severe rhabdomyolysis, early initiation of RRT may be considered when biochemical derangements are pronounced and not rapidly correctable or in the setting of established AKI with fluid overload. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is often preferred in hemodynamically unstable patients; conventional intermittent hemodialysis is effective for removing potassium and correcting acidosis in stable patients. Note that standard dialysis removes myoglobin inefficiently due to molecular size and protein binding, so dialysis is mainly used to manage complications rather than to eliminate myoglobin.

Ongoing management includes serial laboratory monitoring (CK, creatinine, electrolytes, bicarbonate), urine output monitoring, and careful fluid balance. Consult nephrology early when CK is very high, creatinine is rising, or electrolyte abnormalities emerge. Preventive measures—early aggressive fluids and close surveillance—remain the most effective strategies to reduce risk of AKI.

# Physical therapy and staged rehabilitation strategies after acute myopathy

Rehabilitation in severe myopathy must balance restoring function and avoiding overexertion that can exacerbate muscle damage. Staged, individualized rehabilitation plans delivered by experienced physical and occupational therapists optimize recovery, prevent complications, and facilitate reintegration into activities of daily living.

The rehabilitation process begins with an early assessment focusing on baseline functional status, cardiopulmonary reserve, joint range of motion, presence of contractures, spasticity or flaccidity, pain, swallowing and communication needs, and psychosocial factors. In the acute phase, the priority is prevention: passive range-of-motion exercises to prevent contractures, positioning to protect skin and reduce pressure, bed mobility training when possible, and respiratory muscle facilitation where indicated. Therapy sessions in this phase are short, frequent, and closely monitored for fatigue, respiratory compromise, and pain. Energy conservation techniques are introduced early.

As medical stability is achieved, transition to active-assisted and then active strengthening exercises is appropriate. Strength training should be low-intensity and low-load initially, guided by perceived exertion and objective markers; typical protocols use higher repetitions with lower resistance, progressing only when the patient tolerates without prolonged post-exertional weakness. Avoid eccentric-heavy exercises that may increase muscle damage in susceptible myopathies. Aerobic conditioning adapted to tolerance (e.g., recumbent cycle ergometry, bedside pedalers, arm ergometry) improves cardiovascular fitness without excessive load on weakened muscles. Monitor for delayed-onset muscle soreness and overtraining; if significant fatigue or CK elevations follow exercise, intensity should be reduced.

Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) can be a useful adjunct for patients with severe weakness who cannot actively participate in strengthening; NMES may maintain muscle mass and facilitate re-education. Functional training focuses on transfers, gait retraining, balance, and activities of daily living (ADL) retraining with adaptive devices as needed—walkers, orthoses (ankle-foot orthoses), and environmental modifications. Occupational therapists address fine motor skills, upper limb strengthening, adaptive techniques for self-care, and work re-integration planning.

Pulmonary rehabilitation components overlap with general rehabilitation and include inspiratory muscle training, cough augmentation techniques, secretion clearance strategies, and graded cardiovascular conditioning. Exercise prescriptions should take into account respiratory limitations; monitor oxygen saturation and provide supplemental oxygen if desaturation occurs.

Rehabilitation goals must be realistic and aligned with prognosis and patient priorities. For progressive myopathies, emphasis may be on maximizing current function, independence in ADLs, and caregiver training. For potentially reversible causes, the emphasis shifts to recovery of strength and function with progressive intensity. Throughout rehabilitation, clinicians should reassess for comorbidities (cardiac involvement is a concern in many myopathies), incorporate fall prevention strategies, and liaise with prosthetics and orthotics services when needed.

# Psychosocial support and coordination of multidisciplinary care

Severe myopathy imposes substantial psychosocial burdens on patients and caregivers: loss of independence, role changes, anxiety about respiratory failure and dependency on ventilatory support, grief over functional losses, financial stress, and social isolation. Comprehensive care addresses these needs proactively through integrated multidisciplinary teams.

A core multidisciplinary team typically includes neurology or neuromuscular specialists, pulmonology/critical care, rehabilitation medicine, physical and occupational therapy, speech–language pathology, nutrition, nursing with expertise in neuromuscular disease, social work, psychology or psychiatry, pain management, wound care, and palliative care when appropriate. Early involvement of care coordinators or case managers facilitates transitions from acute care to rehabilitation or home-based services, helps arrange equipment (ventilators, mobility aids), and connects families with community resources and home health services.

Psychosocial support begins with open, empathetic communication about diagnosis, prognosis, and expected disease trajectory. Routine screening for depression and anxiety is recommended because these conditions are common and treatable; validated tools (e.g., Patient Health Questionnaire-9) can guide detection. Provide timely referral to mental health professionals and consider pharmacologic treatment where indicated, bearing in mind potential interactions and the patient’s respiratory status. Encourage peer support groups and patient advocacy organizations which often provide disease-specific education, caregiver training, and emotional support.

Advance care planning is an essential component of comprehensive care. Discuss goals of care, preferences regarding life-sustaining therapies (noninvasive ventilation, invasive mechanical ventilation, tracheostomy), and code status early, revisiting these discussions as the disease evolves. Document advance directives and involve family members or surrogate decision-makers in planning. Palliative care teams can assist with symptom management, complex decision-making, and psychosocial support even early in the disease course.

Address practical concerns: coordinate home modifications (ramps, bathroom adaptations), arrange for durable medical equipment (hospital beds, lifts), and ensure training of caregivers in ventilator management, suctioning, enteral feeding, and emergency plans. Work with social services to navigate insurance, disability benefits, and employment issues. Educate patients and families about infection prevention and emergency contacts.

Finally, maintain continuity of care with clear documentation, scheduled follow-ups, and timely communication among team members. Multidisciplinary clinics for neuromuscular disease consolidate expertise, streamline care, and have been associated with improved outcomes and patient satisfaction.

Conclusion

Managing severe myopathy extends far beyond treating muscle pathology; it requires anticipatory, system-based care that protects the airway, preserves renal function, prevents thrombotic and immobility-related complications, restores function through staged rehabilitation, and supports the patient and family psychosocially. Objective monitoring, early interventions, and coordinated multidisciplinary care are the cornerstones of improving outcomes. Clinicians must individualize decision-making to the patient’s clinical status, comorbidities, and personal goals, and be prepared to adapt plans as the disease and patient circumstances change.

Introduction

Elevated transaminases—serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT)—are among the most common abnormalities encountered in medical practice. Interpreting these laboratory signals requires integration of epidemiology, physiology, and clinical context. Two frequent and intertwined clinical problems are non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and its progressive form non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), and the challenge of distinguishing hepatic sources of transaminase elevation from muscle-derived elevations in patients with myopathy or muscle injury. This chapter reviews the pathogenesis and population impact of NAFLD/NASH, delineates enzyme patterns and concomitant laboratory clues that distinguish hepatic from muscle etiologies, discusses imaging and biopsy indications, provides a practical approach to elevated transaminases in the setting of myopathy, addresses overlap syndromes where both liver and muscle disease coexist, and outlines recommended monitoring of liver tests during systemic therapies and immunosuppression.

#
Epidemiology and pathogenesis of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)

Epidemiology
NAFLD is now the most common chronic liver disease in Western countries and is increasing worldwide in parallel with obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and the metabolic syndrome. Prevalence estimates vary with the diagnostic modality: population imaging and biopsy studies suggest NAFLD affects approximately 25%–30% of adults globally, with higher rates (up to 60%–80%) among individuals with obesity or T2DM. NASH—the subset with hepatocellular injury and inflammation, and which may progress to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma—occurs in a smaller but clinically important proportion of patients with NAFLD. The annual risk of progression from simple steatosis to NASH or to advanced fibrosis is variable, but the presence of NASH and fibrosis stage are the principal determinants of liver-related morbidity and mortality.

Risk factors for NAFLD include central (visceral) obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia (elevated triglycerides, low HDL), T2DM, older age, male sex in some cohorts, and certain genetic variants (for example, PNPLA3 I148M, TM6SF2). Alcohol consumption must be assessed carefully: by definition, NAFLD occurs in individuals with alcohol intake below specified thresholds (commonly <30 g/day for men and <20 g/day for women), but clinically modest alcohol use can coexist and influence disease course.

Pathogenesis
NAFLD is a complex, multifactorial disease involving metabolic perturbations, adipose tissue dysfunction, lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and maladaptive immune responses. The earlier “two-hit” hypothesis (insulin resistance leading to steatosis followed by oxidative injury) has evolved into a more nuanced “multiple parallel hits” model. Key pathophysiological steps include:

1. Hepatic lipid accumulation: Excess caloric intake, adipose tissue insulin resistance, and increased lipolysis deliver elevated free fatty acids to the liver. De novo lipogenesis is upregulated by hyperinsulinemia and hyperglycemia. When fatty acid uptake and synthesis exceed the liver’s capacity for oxidation and export (as very-low-density lipoprotein), triglycerides accumulate within hepatocytes as lipid droplets.

2. Lipotoxicity and cellular stress: Accumulation of certain lipid species (diacylglycerols, ceramides, free fatty acids) induces mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and impaired autophagy. These processes lead to hepatocyte injury and cell death by apoptosis or necrosis.

3. Oxidative stress and inflammation: Reactive oxygen species generated by dysfunctional mitochondria and cytochrome P450 enzymes cause lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial DNA damage. Injured hepatocytes release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that activate resident Kupffer cells and recruit inflammatory cells. Cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) and chemokines amplify inflammation.

4. Fibrogenesis: Hepatic stellate cells are activated by inflammatory mediators, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and oxidative stress, leading to extracellular matrix deposition and progressive fibrosis. Parenchymal remodeling underlies progression to cirrhosis.

Clinical and histologic spectrum
NAFLD represents a continuum from simple steatosis (hepatic steatosis without significant inflammation or fibrosis) to NASH, characterized histologically by steatosis, lobular inflammation, ballooning degeneration of hepatocytes, and varying degrees of portal and perisinusoidal fibrosis. The NASH Clinical Research Network scoring systems and fibrosis staging (F0–F4) are commonly used in research and clinical contexts. Clinically, many patients with NAFLD are asymptomatic; fatigue or nonspecific right upper quadrant discomfort may occur. Elevated transaminases are common but not universal; normal transaminases do not exclude significant histologic disease including advanced fibrosis.

Implications for practice
From a clinical standpoint, identifying patients at risk for progressive disease is critical. Risk stratification uses clinical variables (age, diabetes, obesity), laboratory markers, and noninvasive fibrosis tests (Fibrosis-4 index, NAFLD fibrosis score, transient elastography). Lifestyle intervention (weight loss through diet and exercise) is the cornerstone of therapy; pharmacologic options are emerging for high-risk NASH with fibrosis.

#
Patterns of liver enzyme abnormalities and clues to origin (AST/ALT ratio, concurrent CK elevations)

Interpreting transaminase elevations requires recognizing both the magnitude and pattern of abnormality, and integrating other laboratory results.

Magnitude and pattern
Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) is more specific to liver because it is concentrated in hepatocytes; aspartate aminotransferase (AST) is distributed in liver mitochondria and cytosol, but is also present in cardiac and skeletal muscle, erythrocytes, kidney, and other tissues. Normal reference ranges vary by laboratory and population; approximate typical ranges are ALT: 7–56 U/L, AST: 10–40 U/L, and creatine kinase (CK): 20–200 U/L (depending on sex and assay). Many labs and professional societies have recommended lower upper limits of normal for ALT (for example, ~30 U/L for men and ~20 U/L for women) to improve sensitivity for liver disease. Mild isolated elevations (<2–3× ULN) are common and often benign; values greater than 5–10× ULN suggest significant hepatocellular injury or extrahepatic muscle breakdown.

AST/ALT ratio
The AST/ALT ratio is an often-cited heuristic. In most forms of acute hepatocellular injury not due to alcohol or advanced fibrosis, ALT tends to be greater than AST (AST/ALT < 1). In alcoholic liver disease, AST is frequently greater than ALT with AST/ALT > 2, partly because alcohol induces mitochondrial injury and pyridoxine deficiency affects ALT activity. With advancing fibrosis/cirrhosis from any cause, AST may predominate, producing AST/ALT ≥ 1. However, AST/ALT ratio must be interpreted cautiously: moderate muscle injury elevates AST more than ALT because AST is abundant in muscle; hemolysis can artifactually raise AST; and the ratio varies by timing of sampling.

Concurrent CK elevations
Measurement of serum CK (creatine kinase), especially the CK-MM isoform from skeletal muscle, is a critical adjunct when distinguishing muscle versus hepatic sources of transaminase elevations. Muscle injury or myopathy typically produces marked CK elevation—often several-fold to tens of-fold above the upper limit of normal—while hepatic injury usually yields normal or only mildly elevated CK. In muscle injury, AST and sometimes ALT rise but the magnitude is usually less than CK, and the AST/ALT ratio may be >1 reflecting muscle AST content. For example, an asymptomatic patient with AST 250 U/L, ALT 80 U/L, and CK 10,000 U/L is most consistent with muscle injury; conversely, AST 200 U/L, ALT 400 U/L, and CK 100 U/L points to hepatic injury.

Other clues
- Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT): Elevation of ALP with GGT suggests cholestatic or biliary injury. GGT is also inducible by alcohol and some drugs.
- Bilirubin: Elevated conjugated bilirubin suggests impaired excretion from hepatocytes or biliary obstruction; significant hyperbilirubinemia points to more severe liver dysfunction.
- Synthetic function: International normalized ratio (INR), albumin, and platelet count reflect hepatic synthetic function and portal hypertension. Abnormalities indicate advanced liver disease rather than isolated transaminase leak.
- Aminotransferase kinetics: The temporal pattern matters. Rapid rise and fall of transaminases in the thousands suggests acute ischemic or toxin-mediated liver injury (e.g., acetaminophen). Chronic mild elevations point to steatosis, metabolic disease, or chronic viral hepatitis.

In sum, interpretation integrates AST/ALT magnitudes and ratios with CK, cholestatic markers, bilirubin, and synthetic function to localize the source and severity of injury.

#
Imaging and biopsy indications for liver disease evaluation

Imaging modalities
Noninvasive imaging is the first-line tool to assess hepatic steatosis and structural liver disease.

1. Ultrasound: Abdominal ultrasound is widely available and sensitive for moderate-to-severe steatosis (generally >20%–30% hepatic fat content) but less sensitive for mild steatosis. It detects increased echogenicity of the liver relative to the kidney and can suggest cirrhosis features. Ultrasound cannot reliably stage fibrosis.

2. Controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) and transient elastography (FibroScan): FibroScan measures liver stiffness as a surrogate for fibrosis and can quantify steatosis via CAP. It offers point-of-care, reproducible assessment, with thresholds for fibrosis staging and steatosis grades; however, obesity and narrow intercostal spaces can limit accuracy. Normal liver stiffness is typically <6 kPa; thresholds for significant fibrosis vary, often >8 kPa suggests ≥F2; >12–14 kPa suggests cirrhosis.

3. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI): MRI-based techniques—proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) for quantifying hepatic fat and magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) for fibrosis—are highly sensitive and specific and can quantify fat down to low levels. MRI-PDFF is the most accurate noninvasive measure of liver fat but is costly and not always available. MRE is the most accurate imaging test for fibrosis.

4. CT scan: Non-contrast CT can detect moderate-to-severe steatosis by hepatic attenuation; contrast reduces sensitivity. CT is not preferred for quantitative assessment due to radiation exposure and lower sensitivity compared with MRI.

Indications for biopsy
Liver biopsy remains the reference standard for diagnosing NASH and staging fibrosis, but it is invasive and subject to sampling variability and complications. Indications to consider liver biopsy include:

- Diagnostic uncertainty: When noninvasive testing is inconclusive and knowing the exact histologic diagnosis will change management.
- Suspected NASH with potential advanced fibrosis: Especially when noninvasive fibrosis tests are indeterminate or discordant and determining fibrosis stage would guide treatment decisions or trial eligibility.
- Excluding other causes: When autoimmune hepatitis, drug-induced liver injury, genetic/metabolic liver disease, or overlapping pathologies are suspected.
- Clinical trials: Histologic confirmation is often required for enrollment.

In practice, the current paradigm emphasizes noninvasive risk stratification: use clinical scores (Fibrosis-4, NAFLD fibrosis score), elastography, and other biomarkers to identify patients at low risk (who typically do not require biopsy) versus those at high or indeterminate risk (for whom biopsy may be warranted). Key decision thresholds vary by algorithm; for example, Fibrosis-4 index uses age, AST, ALT, and platelet count with cutoffs tailored to optimize sensitivity versus specificity.

Pre-biopsy evaluation should include bleeding risk assessment, imaging to exclude focal vascular lesions or ascites, and consideration of percutaneous versus transjugular biopsy in certain scenarios. Histologic assessment should document steatosis, lobular inflammation, hepatocyte ballooning, and fibrosis stage.

#
Approach to elevated transaminases in the setting of myopathy

Clinical assessment
When transaminases are elevated in a patient with suspected or known myopathy, the clinician must determine whether the elevations reflect hepatic injury, muscle breakdown, or both. The evaluation begins with a careful history and physical exam focused on features that suggest muscle disease (myalgias, weakness, exercise intolerance, muscle swelling, dark urine), hepatobiliary symptoms (jaundice, pruritus, abdominal pain), medication and supplement use (statins, fibrates, herbal products), alcohol history, and temporal relationship of symptoms and laboratory trends.

Laboratory strategy
Key laboratories to distinguish sources include:

- Serum CK (total): A markedly elevated CK supports muscle origin. Typical upper limit of normal for CK varies by laboratory and sex but often ~200 U/L; levels in myopathies may range from several-fold to >10,000 U/L in rhabdomyolysis.
- CK isoenzymes and aldolase: Aldolase can also be elevated in muscle disease and may be used alongside CK if CK is normal in chronic myopathy.
- AST and ALT: Both may be elevated in muscle injury; AST more so. ALT is less specific but may be elevated with prolonged muscle inflammation.
- Liver-specific tests: GGT and ALP are liver-enriched; their elevation supports hepatic origin. Albumin and INR assess liver synthetic function; abnormal values indicate hepatic dysfunction.
- Urinalysis and myoglobin: Myoglobinuria supports rhabdomyolysis.
- Viral hepatitis serologies and autoimmune markers if hepatic origin suspected.

Interpretation frameworks
- Predominant muscle pattern: Markedly elevated CK with modest transaminase elevations, normal GGT/ALP, normal bilirubin and synthetic function, and clinical features of myopathy favors muscle source. Serial testing can document parallel decline of CK and transaminases with muscle recovery.
- Predominant hepatic pattern: Elevated ALT >> AST, increased GGT/ALP, elevated bilirubin or INR, or imaging confirming steatosis or biliary disease supports hepatic cause.
- Mixed pattern: Coexisting elevations across both domains require further assessment for concurrent processes.

Imaging and specialist input
If muscle origin is strongly suspected but diagnosis is uncertain, electromyography (EMG), nerve conduction studies, or muscle MRI and consultation with neurology or rheumatology may be helpful. If hepatic injury cannot be excluded based on labs and imaging, hepatology referral and further evaluation (viral serologies, autoantibodies, metabolic tests) are appropriate.

Medication and toxin review
Many drugs and supplements can cause muscle injury (statins, fibrates, immune checkpoint inhibitors, antipsychotics) and hepatic injury (acetaminophen, methotrexate, isoniazid). Statins, in particular, pose a clinical challenge because they can cause asymptomatic CK elevations or rare myopathy; they can also cause mild transaminase elevations. If statins are used, simultaneous CK and transaminase monitoring when symptoms arise is essential, and guidelines recommend stopping statins in suspected statin-induced myopathy or rhabdomyolysis.

Practical algorithm
A pragmatic approach: in any patient with elevated transaminases, obtain CK and GGT. If CK is high and GGT normal, consider muscle etiology and investigate for myopathy. If GGT is elevated or bilirubin/INR abnormal, pursue hepatic evaluation with imaging and serologies. If results are mixed or if there is concern for both, refer to both hepatology and neuromuscular specialists and consider liver imaging and muscle studies concurrently.

#
Overlap syndromes and coexisting hepatic and muscle pathology

Conceptual overview
Overlap syndromes occur when patients have both primary liver disease and primary muscle disease, or when systemic diseases affect both tissues. Recognizing overlap is crucial because assuming a single etiology may delay appropriate diagnosis and management.

Examples and mechanisms
1. Metabolic and mitochondrial disorders: Certain inherited metabolic diseases (for example, glycogen storage diseases, mitochondrial cytopathies) can affect both liver and skeletal muscle, producing elevations of transaminases and CK, hypoglycemia, exercise intolerance, and multisystem features. These disorders may present in childhood but can also manifest in adults.

2. Autoimmune diseases: Autoimmune hepatitis can co-occur with inflammatory myopathies (dermatomyositis, polymyositis) as part of systemic autoimmune syndromes. Dermatomyositis is associated with characteristic skin findings and can have paraneoplastic associations that also affect the liver indirectly.

3. Drug-induced toxicity: Some systemic therapies cause concurrent hepatotoxicity and myotoxicity. For instance, certain antiretroviral therapies, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and chemotherapeutic agents can injure both tissues via immune-mediated mechanisms or toxic metabolic effects.

4. Metabolic syndrome: NAFLD and sarcopenia or myosteatosis can coexist as manifestations of the underlying metabolic milieu. In frail or elderly patients, the reduction in muscle mass and quality may intersect with fatty infiltration of the liver.

Clinical implications
When overlap is suspected, clinicians should expand evaluation beyond standard hepatocellular injury workup. Specific testing might include genetic/metabolic panels, autoantibody screening (ANA, anti–smooth muscle, myositis-specific antibodies), muscle biopsy, and liver biopsy if the pattern suggests independent hepatic pathology. Management may require addressing each disorder specifically (immunosuppression for autoimmune disease, enzyme replacement or metabolic therapy for inherited disorders), and treatment decisions must weigh potential cross-toxicity: for example, immunosuppressive regimens for myositis may influence hepatic disease activity or susceptibility to infection.

Case-seeking vigilance
Overlap states are often under-recognized because clinicians may attribute all laboratory abnormalities to the better-known condition (for example, ascribing ALT elevation to NAFLD in a patient with myositis). Persistently abnormal or discordant laboratory trends, atypical clinical features, or poor response to expected therapy should prompt re-evaluation for coexisting pathology.

#
Monitoring liver tests during systemic therapy and immunosuppression

Rationale
Many systemic therapies—antimicrobials, antineoplasics, immunomodulators, and biologic agents—have potential hepatotoxicity. Monitoring transaminases, cholestatic enzymes, and synthetic markers is essential to detect early drug-induced liver injury (DILI), minimize progression to liver failure, and guide therapy adjustments. Similarly, immunosuppression can predispose to reactivation of viral hepatitis and opportunistic infections affecting the liver.

Baseline evaluation
Before initiating potentially hepatotoxic therapy or immunosuppressive regimens, obtain baseline liver tests: ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, total bilirubin, albumin, INR, and viral hepatitis serologies (hepatitis B surface antigen, hepatitis B core antibody, hepatitis C antibody with reflex RNA if positive). Assess alcohol use, prior liver disease history, and medications/supplements. For therapies with known hepatotoxic risk, consider quantitative hepatitis B DNA for those with serologic evidence of prior exposure, as occult infection can reactivate with immunosuppression.

Monitoring frequency and thresholds
Monitoring schedules depend on the agent’s risk profile and patient comorbidities.

- Low-risk agents: Routine monitoring at baseline and periodically (for example, every 3–6 months) unless symptoms develop.
- Moderate-risk agents (eg, statins in certain contexts, some antibiotics): Monitor at baseline, 4–12 weeks after initiation, then periodically.
- High-risk agents (eg, methotrexate, isoniazid, certain chemotherapies, immune checkpoint inhibitors): More frequent monitoring is recommended—baseline, every 1–4 weeks during dose escalation or early therapy, then every 4–12 weeks depending on stability.

Action thresholds typically used:
- If ALT or AST rises to greater than 3× upper limit of normal (ULN) with symptoms or >5× ULN without symptoms, consider holding or stopping the drug and evaluating for DILI.
- For patients with baseline elevations, thresholds for action should be individualized; for example, an increase greater than 3× baseline or new worsening of bilirubin or INR warrants reassessment.
- Any elevation of bilirubin, especially when concurrent with transaminase elevation, is more ominous and may prompt immediate cessation.

Specific considerations with immunosuppression
- Hepatitis B reactivation: Patients who are hepatitis B surface antigen positive or hepatitis B core antibody positive are at risk of reactivation with immunosuppression. Prophylactic antiviral therapy (entecavir, tenofovir) is indicated in many settings (eg, rituximab-containing regimens), and close monitoring of HBV DNA is essential.
- Hepatitis C: Direct-acting antivirals are highly effective; timing of therapy relative to immunosuppression should be individualized.
- Immune-mediated hepatitis: Immune checkpoint inhibitors can trigger autoimmune-like hepatitis. Management often involves corticosteroids and, if severe, other immunosuppressants; early hepatology consultation is appropriate.
- Opportunistic infections: Immunosuppressed patients may develop fungal, viral, or mycobacterial infections affecting the liver; atypical presentations require high clinical suspicion.

Drug choice and dose adjustments
When transaminase elevations occur, clinicians balance the benefits of ongoing therapy against hepatic risk. For some drugs, dose reduction or temporary interruption with close monitoring may be acceptable; for others with high hepatotoxic potential or when evidence of progressive liver dysfunction exists, permanent discontinuation is required. Consultation with hepatology is advisable in moderate-to-severe cases.

Follow-up and long-term monitoring
After stopping a suspected hepatotoxic agent, follow liver tests until normalization or stabilization. If DILI resolved and therapy must be reintroduced for compelling reasons, re-challenge is usually avoided unless benefits outweigh risks and with close monitoring; alternative medications should be sought when possible. For patients on long-term immunosuppression, periodic reassessment of viral hepatitis status, vaccination update (hepatitis A and B immunization when appropriate), and counseling on hepatotoxic exposures (alcohol, recreational drugs, herbal supplements) are integral to care.

Conclusion

Differentiating hepatic from muscle causes of transaminase elevation and recognizing the clinical significance of hepatic steatosis are essential competencies for clinicians. Accurate interpretation rests on integrating enzyme patterns, CK and other ancillary tests, imaging, and clinical context. Noninvasive tools have transformed NAFLD assessment, reserving liver biopsy for targeted indications. In patients with myopathy, a structured approach—history, CK measurement, liver-specific markers, and targeted imaging or specialist referral—enables appropriate diagnosis. Awareness of overlap syndromes and careful monitoring during systemic therapy and immunosuppression prevents missed diagnoses and medication-related liver injury. With the rising prevalence of metabolic disease, clinicians must remain vigilant in evaluating and managing transaminase abnormalities to reduce long-term hepatic and extrahepatic morbidity.

Introduction

Autoimmune myopathies are a group of disorders in which dysregulated immune responses target skeletal muscle, causing weakness, elevated muscle enzymes, inflammatory infiltrates, and a range of histopathologic patterns. Understanding the immunology underlying these diseases requires integration of innate and adaptive immune mechanisms, cellular and humoral effectors, complement pathways, antigen presentation, and the processes that normally maintain self-tolerance. This chapter reviews core immunologic concepts relevant to antibody-mediated and cell-mediated mechanisms that drive autoimmune muscle injury, emphasizing pathophysiology and clinical reasoning that connect laboratory tests, histology, and therapeutic strategies.

# Innate and adaptive immune responses overview

The immune system comprises two interdependent arms: innate immunity, which provides rapid, non-specific defense, and adaptive immunity, which generates antigen-specific responses and immunologic memory. Innate immunity is mediated by physical barriers, soluble pattern recognition molecules (such as complement and collectins), and cellular effectors including neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), natural killer (NK) cells, and innate lymphoid cells. Innate cells recognize conserved pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs), NOD-like receptors, and RIG-I–like receptors. Signaling through PRRs triggers secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), type I interferons, chemokines, and upregulation of antigen presentation machinery.

Adaptive immunity comprises B lymphocytes and T lymphocytes. B cells produce antibodies (immunoglobulins) after differentiation into plasma cells, and present antigen to T cells. T cells include CD4+ helper T cells (Th) that support B-cell responses and orchestrate inflammation via cytokines, and CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) which directly kill target cells presenting peptide in the context of class I major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules. Adaptive responses are initiated when antigen-presenting cells (APCs), particularly dendritic cells, process antigen and provide three signals to naïve T cells: (1) peptide-MHC interaction with the T-cell receptor (TCR), (2) costimulatory signals (e.g., CD80/CD86 on APC binding CD28 on T cells), and (3) cytokines that polarize differentiation (e.g., IL-12 for Th1, IL-4 for Th2, IL-6/TGF-β for Th17).

Innate and adaptive immunity are tightly integrated. Innate cytokines shape adaptive differentiation; conversely, adaptive effectors modulate innate cell function (e.g., Th1 IFN-γ activation of macrophages). In autoimmune myopathies, both arms contribute: innate-driven complement activation and macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis on one hand, and adaptive-driven autoantibody production and T-cell–mediated myocyte injury on the other. Understanding which mechanisms predominate in a given myopathy (e.g., antibody/complement in dermatomyositis versus CD8+ T-cell–mediated myofiber necrosis in polymyositis or inclusion body myositis) helps interpret biopsy findings and guide therapy.

# B-cell and T-cell roles in autoimmunity

B cells and T cells play complementary and sometimes independent roles in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases. B cells contribute via antibody production, antigen presentation to T cells, and secretion of pro-inflammatory or regulatory cytokines. T cells, depending on subset, mediate direct cytotoxicity (CD8+ CTLs), help B-cell maturation (follicular helper T cells, Tfh), or orchestrate inflammation (Th1, Th17). A breakdown in central or peripheral tolerance results in self-reactive lymphocytes that escape deletion or regulation.

B cells: Naïve B cells recognizing antigen via the B-cell receptor (BCR) may internalize the antigen, present peptides on MHC class II to CD4+ T cells, and receive T-cell help (via CD40L–CD40 interactions and cytokines) to undergo class-switch recombination and somatic hypermutation within germinal centers. This process yields high-affinity, class-switched antibodies (IgG, IgA, IgE). Pathogenic autoantibodies are often IgG subclasses capable of fixing complement and engaging Fc receptors (FcγRs) on innate cells, leading to opsonization, complement activation, and antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC). In autoimmune myopathies, myositis-specific autoantibodies (MSAs) and myositis-associated autoantibodies (MAAs) are important diagnostic markers and sometimes pathogenic mediators (e.g., anti–signal recognition particle, anti–Mi-2, anti–MDA5), though the pathogenicity of specific autoantibodies varies by disease.

T cells: CD4+ T helper cells differentiate into functionally distinct subsets. Th1 cells produce IFN-γ and support macrophage activation and CD8+ responses; Th2 cells assist humoral immunity; Th17 cells produce IL-17, IL-21, and IL-22 and are implicated in many autoimmune inflammatory processes. Tfh cells (CXCR5+, PD-1 high) are specialized to help B cells in germinal centers and are central to autoantibody formation. CD8+ T cells recognize peptides presented on MHC class I molecules expressed on nearly all nucleated cells, enabling direct cytotoxicity through perforin/granzyme release and Fas–FasL interactions. In some autoimmune myopathies, notably polymyositis and inclusion body myositis (IBM), cytotoxic CD8+ T cells invade non-necrotic myofibers that aberrantly express MHC class I, suggesting direct antigen-driven attack.

Interactions and cross-talk: B and T cell interactions form a self-reinforcing loop. Autoantigen release from injured muscle feeds APCs, which activate T cells that promote B-cell differentiation and class switching, producing antibodies that further injure tissue. B cells also serve as APCs and cytokine producers; depletion of B cells (e.g., with rituximab) can modulate both humoral and cellular responses. The relative contribution of B-cell versus T-cell mechanisms informs treatment choices and biomarkers; for example, complement-targeted therapies may be more relevant when immune complexes and complement deposition predominate, while therapies targeting T-cell activation (calcineurin inhibitors, IVIG modulation, or glucocorticoids) may be prioritized when cytotoxic T-cell infiltrates are prominent.

# Autoantibody formation and pathogenic mechanisms

Autoantibody formation begins with failure of tolerance mechanisms, leading to B-cell recognition of self-antigen and subsequent T-cell help. Key steps include antigen availability, presentation by APCs, activation of autoreactive CD4+ T cells, and germinal center reactions that generate high-affinity, class-switched autoantibodies. The antigens targeted in autoimmune myopathies are often intracellular or nucleic acid–associated proteins (e.g., anti–signal recognition particle [SRP], anti–HMGCR, anti–Mi-2, anti–MDA5) or muscle membrane–associated components (e.g., in immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy). Autoantibodies can be diagnostic biomarkers, correlate with clinical phenotypes, and sometimes be directly pathogenic.

Mechanisms by which autoantibodies mediate tissue injury include:

1. Complement activation: IgG subclasses (particularly IgG1 and IgG3 in humans) and IgM can activate the classical pathway by binding C1q, leading to downstream complement cascade activation, generation of C3a/C5a anaphylatoxins, opsonization, and formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC or C5b-9) that can injure cell membranes.

2. Fc receptor–mediated effector functions: Fc portions of IgG engage Fcγ receptors on macrophages, neutrophils, NK cells, and dendritic cells, leading to phagocytosis of opsonized targets, release of inflammatory mediators, and ADCC. This is particularly relevant when autoantibodies target surface antigens accessible on intact cells.

3. Direct functional blockade or receptor modulation: Autoantibodies can directly interfere with protein function (blocking or agonist activity), leading to dysfunction without overt complement activation. In muscle disease, a classic example outside myopathy is myasthenia gravis where anti-acetylcholine receptor antibodies block neuromuscular transmission; in myopathies, similar mechanisms may operate if surface-expressed proteins are targeted.

4. Immune complex deposition: Soluble antigen–antibody complexes can deposit in tissues, activate complement, and recruit inflammatory cells. In certain autoimmune myopathies with vascular involvement (e.g., dermatomyositis), immune complex deposition in small vessels contributes to microvascular injury.

Diagnostics and laboratory correlations: Autoantibody testing is central to the evaluation of suspected autoimmune myopathies. Common tests include myositis-specific autoantibodies (MSAs): anti–Jo-1 (histidyl tRNA synthetase), anti–SRP, anti–Mi-2, anti–MDA5, anti–TIF1-γ, anti–NXP2, anti–HMGCR, and others. Myositis-associated antibodies (MAAs) such as anti–PM/Scl or anti–Ku are seen in overlap syndromes. Interpretation requires assay knowledge (immunoprecipitation, line blot, ELISA) and clinical correlation, as false positives/negatives occur. Complement levels (C3, C4) may be consumed in active immune complex disease, and circulating complement activation products (C5a, sC5b-9) can sometimes be measured for research or clinical purposes.

Reference ranges: Normal serum complement concentrations vary by assay, but typical adult reference ranges are approximately C3: 90–180 mg/dL; C4: 10–40 mg/dL. Normal creatine kinase (CK), reflecting muscle injury, varies by lab and sex; common ranges are CK (male): 30–200 U/L, CK (female): 30–170 U/L, with substantial elevations in active myositis.

# Complement cascade and membrane attack complex (C5b-9) mediated tissue injury

The complement system consists of three activation pathways—classical, lectin, and alternative—that converge on the central component C3 and proceed to form the terminal membrane attack complex (MAC), C5b-9. The classical pathway is initiated by antigen–antibody complexes (IgM or certain IgG subclasses) binding C1q. The lectin pathway begins with mannose-binding lectin (MBL) or ficolins binding carbohydrate patterns on pathogens. The alternative pathway provides amplification through spontaneous hydrolysis of C3 ("tickover") and factor B/D–mediated amplification loops. All pathways generate C3 convertases that cleave C3 into C3a (an anaphylatoxin) and C3b (an opsonin), followed by formation of C5 convertases that cleave C5 into C5a (potent chemoattractant/anaphylatoxin) and C5b, initiating assembly of the MAC (C5b-6-7-8-9).

Pathogenic roles in autoimmune myopathies: Complement activation can injure muscle through multiple mechanisms. Deposition of C5b-9 on the sarcolemma causes sublytic or lytic injury to myofibers and endothelial cells. In dermatomyositis, capillary endothelial deposition of MAC is a characteristic finding in many cases and is associated with perifascicular muscle fiber ischemia and atrophy. The presence of C5b-9 within endomysial capillaries and along sarcolemma supports a complement-mediated microangiopathy model. Complement activation also yields C3a and C5a, which recruit neutrophils, monocytes, and other inflammatory cells; these cells release proteases, reactive oxygen species, and cytokines that amplify tissue damage.

Regulation: Complement activity is tightly regulated by soluble and membrane-bound inhibitors such as C1 inhibitor (C1-INH), factor H, factor I, membrane cofactor protein (MCP/CD46), decay accelerating factor (DAF/CD55), and CD59, which prevents MAC insertion. Dysfunction or overwhelming activation leads to tissue injury. In autoimmune myopathies, targeted complement inhibitors (e.g., anti-C5 monoclonal antibodies) have theoretical and, in some cases, emerging clinical relevance when complement-mediated pathology predominates.

Diagnostics: Tissue immunohistochemistry for complement components (C3d, C4d, C5b-9) on muscle biopsy can demonstrate complement deposition patterns that support a diagnosis of complement-mediated injury (e.g., perifascicular C5b-9 deposition in dermatomyositis). Circulating complement levels may be low with complement consumption, though normal levels do not exclude local complement activation. Measurement of C5b-9 (soluble MAC) in serum can reflect systemic activation.

Clinical implications: Recognition of complement-mediated mechanisms influences therapeutic choices. Therapies that reduce autoantibody production (B-cell depletion), inhibit complement directly (anti-C5), or block downstream inflammatory mediators (corticosteroids, IVIG which can modulate complement) may be appropriate depending on the dominant pathophysiology.

# Macrophage activation, antigen presentation and myophagocytosis

Macrophages are versatile innate immune cells that participate in host defense, tissue remodeling, antigen presentation, and resolution of inflammation. In skeletal muscle, macrophages can adopt a spectrum of activation states rather than discrete M1/M2 polarities: pro-inflammatory phenotypes secrete TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and nitric oxide; anti-inflammatory/regulatory phenotypes produce IL-10, TGF-β, and growth factors that promote repair. In autoimmune myopathies, macrophages are prominent effectors of tissue injury and clearance of necrotic fibers.

Activation and recruitment: Tissue injury, immune complexes, complement activation, and chemokines (e.g., CCL2/MCP-1) recruit monocytes that differentiate into macrophages. Fcγ receptor engagement by IgG-opsonized targets and signaling via PRRs amplifies macrophage activation. IFN-γ from Th1 and CD8+ T cells polarizes macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory, microbicidal phenotype, increasing expression of MHC class II and co-stimulatory molecules (CD80/CD86), thereby enhancing antigen presentation.

Antigen presentation: Macrophages are professional antigen-presenting cells capable of processing and presenting peptides on MHC class II molecules to CD4+ T cells. They can also cross-present exogenous antigen on MHC class I to prime CD8+ T cells under certain contexts. In autoimmune myopathies, macrophages can present muscle-derived antigens to T cells, sustaining T-cell activation and perpetuating local immune responses. Upregulation of MHC molecules on muscle fibers themselves may permit direct presentation to CD8+ T cells, as seen in polymyositis/IBM, but macrophage presentation helps maintain local immune circuits.

Myophagocytosis and muscle fiber injury: Macrophages phagocytose necrotic muscle fibers and cell debris (myophagocytosis), which is an appropriate response in injury and repair. However, in autoimmune contexts, macrophages may phagocytose viable or sublytic-injured myofibers opsonized by antibody or tagged by complement. Cytotoxic mediators including reactive oxygen species, proteases (matrix metalloproteinases, cathepsins), and pro-inflammatory cytokines can extend tissue damage. Histologically, myophagocytosis is a hallmark of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy and macrophage-rich polymyositis variants; CD68 immunostaining highlights macrophage infiltrates.

Repair versus pathology: Macrophages also orchestrate muscle regeneration by clearing debris and secreting growth factors that activate satellite cells and fibroblasts. Chronic or dysregulated macrophage activation, persistent antigenic stimuli, and profibrotic cytokines (e.g., TGF-β) may impair regeneration and promote fibrosis. Therapeutic strategies aim to attenuate pathogenic macrophage activation while preserving regenerative functions, for example by modulating cytokine milieus or targeting upstream drivers like autoantibodies and complement.

# Immunologic tolerance, triggers for autoimmunity and environmental/drug associations

Immunologic tolerance prevents detrimental responses to self-antigens and operates centrally (during lymphocyte development) and peripherally (in mature lymphocytes). Central tolerance deletes strongly self-reactive lymphocytes via negative selection in the thymus (for T cells) and bone marrow (for B cells). Peripheral tolerance involves anergy (functional unresponsiveness), suppression by regulatory T cells (Tregs, CD4+CD25+FOXP3+), deletion, and immune privilege in certain tissues. Failure at any checkpoint can permit autoimmunity.

Mechanisms of tolerance breakdown: Several mechanisms can precipitate loss of tolerance in autoimmune myopathies:

1. Molecular mimicry: Structural homology between foreign antigens (infectious agents) and self-proteins leads to cross-reactive T or B cell responses that target both pathogen and host tissues. For example, viral infections have been implicated as triggers in some myositis subsets.

2. Epitope spreading: Initial immune responses to a dominant epitope extend to subdominant or cryptic epitopes of the same or different proteins, broadening autoreactivity. Ongoing muscle injury releases intracellular antigens and enhances epitope spreading.

3. Bystander activation and tissue damage: Infection or mechanical injury induces local inflammation, upregulating MHC class I/II expression and costimulatory molecules on resident cells and APCs, enabling activation of autoreactive lymphocytes.

4. Defective Treg number/function: Reduced Treg suppression allows expansion of autoreactive effector T cells. Alterations in cytokine milieus (e.g., increased IL-6) can both promote effector T-cell differentiation and impair Treg stability.

5. Genetic predisposition: HLA alleles (e.g., certain HLA-DR and HLA-B types) confer susceptibility by influencing antigen presentation repertoires. Non-HLA genes involved in immune regulation (PTPN22, STAT4, CTLA4) also modulate risk.

Environmental and drug associations: Several environmental exposures and medications have been associated with triggering or unmasking autoimmune myopathies:

- Infections: Viral (e.g., Coxsackievirus, enteroviruses, hepatitis viruses), bacterial, and other infections may precede myositis onset by initiating immune activation and antigen release.

- Statins: Exposure to HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors has a recognized association with immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) characterized by anti–HMGCR antibodies. In such cases, statin exposure likely provides an initial antigenic stimulus or alters muscle antigen expression leading to autoimmunity.

- Checkpoint inhibitors: Immune checkpoint blockade (anti–CTLA-4, anti–PD-1/PD-L1) used in oncology may induce immune-related adverse events, including inflammatory myopathies, due to removal of inhibitory signals that enforce peripheral tolerance.

- Vaccines and adjuvants: Rarely, vaccines have temporal associations with myositis onset, potentially via innate immune activation; causality is difficult to establish and generally rare relative to benefits.

- Environmental toxins: Certain exposures (e.g., recreational drugs, toxins) have been reported anecdotally in association with myositis, but evidence is limited.

Clinical and diagnostic implications: Eliciting exposures, medications, and infection history is essential in the diagnostic evaluation. Identifying a trigger (e.g., statin) can prompt specific testing (anti–HMGCR) and influence management, though disease may persist after withdrawal of the trigger, indicating established autoimmunity. Genetic testing is not routinely performed for most myopathies but HLA associations provide mechanistic insight and may explain clustering of autoantibody specificities with particular clinical phenotypes.

Maintenance of tolerance and therapeutic strategies: Therapies that restore or mimic tolerance are attractive. Approaches include antigen-specific tolerance induction (experimental), expansion of Tregs (low-dose IL-2), and therapies that blunt co-stimulatory signals (abatacept, CTLA4-Ig) to prevent T-cell activation. Non-specific immunosuppression reduces effector function but does not restore antigen-specific tolerance unless combined with strategies to delete or re-educate autoreactive clones.

Conclusion

A nuanced appreciation of innate and adaptive interactions, the roles of B cells and T cells, the formation and effects of autoantibodies, complement-mediated injury, macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis, and the mechanisms by which tolerance is lost provides a framework for understanding autoimmune myopathies. These immunologic principles have direct clinical relevance: they guide interpretation of serologic tests and muscle biopsy findings, inform pathogenetic models for specific myositis subtypes, and influence therapeutic decisions from immunosuppression to targeted biologics and complement inhibitors. Mastery of this integrated immunology equips clinicians to synthesize diagnostic data and tailor evidence-based management for patients with inflammatory muscle disease.

Introduction

Serologic testing for myositis-specific and myositis-associated autoantibodies has become a central component of the evaluation of patients with suspected idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM). Commercial "myositis panels" bundle many antibodies together and are widely available to clinicians. These panels can be powerful when integrated with clinical phenotype, muscle enzyme levels, electromyography (EMG), imaging, and muscle histopathology, but they are neither perfectly sensitive nor perfectly specific. Understanding the analytic methods driving different commercial assays, their inherent limitations, and how pretest probability shapes interpretation is essential to avoid misdiagnosis, unnecessary treatments, and delays in definitive diagnosis. This chapter reviews the common assay technologies and their performance characteristics, summarizes the sensitivity and specificity ranges of key antibodies (anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP, antisynthetase antibodies including anti-Jo-1), discusses the implications of seronegative results, explores causes of cross-reactivity and false positives (for example isolated SSA/SSB on a myositis panel), and provides practical guidance on when to repeat testing, pursue biopsy or imaging, or send samples to specialized laboratories for confirmation.

# Types of assays used (line immunoassay, ELISA, immunoprecipitation) and performance characteristics

Assays used to detect myositis-related autoantibodies fall into several methodological categories. Each method has particular advantages and limitations that affect clinical interpretation.

Line immunoassay (LIA) and dot-blot arrays. LIAs immobilize discrete recombinant or purified autoantigens as strips (lines) and detect binding of patient IgG with labeled secondary antibodies. Commercial myositis panels frequently use LIA because they allow multiplexing of many antigens on a single strip, are relatively inexpensive, and are automated in many laboratories. Performance characteristics depend heavily on antigen source and preparation (full-length protein vs fragment, native vs denatured conformations). LIAs generally provide semi-quantitative signal intensities that vendors call "weak," "moderate," or "strong." Strengths are throughput and convenience; weaknesses include variable antigen conformation that can miss conformational epitopes, and susceptibility to nonspecific binding producing false positives—especially for low-intensity bands.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). ELISA formats can be used for single antigens or small panels. They are quantitative and typically more standardized than LIAs, making them suitable for follow-up measurements and screening for antibodies with well-characterized antigens (for example anti-Jo-1, anti-HMGCR in some settings). ELISAs use purified antigens coated onto plates; antigen preparation and epitope preservation remain critical. ELISAs typically have good analytic sensitivity and specificity for well-behaved antigens but lack the multiplex convenience of LIAs.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and radioimmunoprecipitation (RIP). IP is considered the historical "gold standard" for many myositis antibodies. Patient serum is incubated with radiolabeled or otherwise tagged cell extracts; immune complexes are precipitated and then analyzed by gel electrophoresis to identify antigen molecular weights and patterns. IP detects antibodies to native, often conformational epitopes in multi-protein complexes (for example some Mi-2 and SRP components) and has excellent specificity. However, IP is labor-intensive, requires specialized expertise and reagents, is not widely available in commercial reference labs, and may be less quantitative. For certain less common or newly described antibodies, IP remains the confirmatory method.

Immunoblot and western blot. These methods separate proteins by electrophoresis and transfer them to membranes for probing with patient serum. They can confirm antibodies to denatured epitopes and are used by some reference labs. Like LIA, antigen conformation matters and detection is often qualitative.

Multiplex bead-based assays (Luminex). These newer platforms couple individual antigens to beads with unique signatures and measure binding in a flow-based fluorescent readout. Multiplex bead assays can be highly sensitive, quantitative, and allow many analytes in a single reaction. However, cross-reactivity between beads, interference from heterophile antibodies, and calibration differences between vendors can affect results.

Analytic performance considerations. For each assay type, important performance characteristics for clinicians to know are analytic sensitivity (ability to detect antibody if present), analytic specificity (ability to avoid cross-reacting signals), reproducibility, and the extent to which assays preserve native antigenic epitopes. Assay cutoffs are vendor-defined and sometimes vary between lots. Many commercial panels do not publish full validation data relevant to diverse clinical populations. Thus positive and negative results should be considered in clinical context rather than as definitive on their own.

# Sensitivity and specificity of common antibodies (anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP, antisynthetase antibodies)

Antibody testing for myositis is heterogeneous: different antibodies are associated with distinct clinical phenotypes and histopathologic patterns, and their diagnostic performance varies by disease subset and the assay used.

Anti-HMGCR antibodies. Anti-3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (anti-HMGCR) antibodies are strongly associated with immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM). The prevalence of anti-HMGCR among all patients with IIM is modest, but among IMNM patients it is substantially higher. Reported sensitivity for anti-HMGCR in IMNM ranges widely depending on cohort selection and assay: roughly 40–80% in IMNM cohorts, but when considering all IIM, sensitivity may be much lower (single-digit to low double-digit percentages). Specificity is high in most studies, often reported >90% when validated by gold-standard methods, because anti-HMGCR is uncommon outside the IMNM phenotype. However, false positives can occur with some commercial assays, particularly those that use fragments or improperly folded antigens. In practice, a clearly positive anti-HMGCR in the right clinical context (marked necrotizing myopathy, high creatine kinase, minimal inflammatory infiltrate) has high diagnostic utility.

Anti-SRP antibodies. Antibodies against the signal recognition particle (anti-SRP) define another subset of IMNM characterized clinically by severe weakness, high CK, and relatively minimal inflammatory infiltrates on biopsy. The sensitivity of anti-SRP for IMNM is variable; in IMNM cohorts reported sensitivity ranges roughly 10–40%. Specificity is generally high (>90%) when measured by IP, but some commercial EIAs/LIAs report lower specificity. Anti-SRP-positive myopathy tends to have more frequent cardiac involvement and refractory weakness, making correct identification clinically meaningful.

Antisynthetase antibodies (including anti-Jo-1, PL-7, PL-12, EJ, OJ). Antibodies directed at aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases define the antisynthetase syndrome, which includes myositis, interstitial lung disease (ILD), mechanic's hands, arthritis, and Raynaud phenomenon. Anti-Jo-1 (histidyl-tRNA synthetase) is the most common antisynthetase antibody. Sensitivity of anti-Jo-1 among patients with myositis is typically in the 15–30% range, but among patients with antisynthetase clinical features it is higher. Specificity of anti-Jo-1 is high, often >95%, particularly when detected by IP or validated ELISA. Non-Jo-1 antisynthetase antibodies (PL-7, PL-12, OJ, EJ) are less common but specific for the antisynthetase phenotype; assays for these antibodies may be less well standardized and have variable sensitivity.

General considerations. Across antibodies, specificity tends to be higher when assays use native antigens and when confirmatory testing by IP is performed. Sensitivity depends on disease prevalence in the tested cohort and on technical factors: antigen source, conformational integrity, and assay cutoffs. For many antibodies, commercial assays will detect the majority of truly positive samples but will miss a proportion that would be positive by IP; conversely, some low-level positives on LIAs or multiplex assays may not be reproducible on confirmatory testing.

# Pretest probability and implications of seronegative results

Interpreting myositis panel results requires application of Bayesian reasoning: the positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) of any test depend on pretest probability (disease prevalence in the tested individual). In a population with low pretest probability (for example, an isolated mild CK elevation without weakness or systemic features), even a test with high specificity may produce more false positives than true positives. Conversely, in a patient with a classic myositis phenotype—proximal symmetric weakness, markedly elevated CK, myopathic changes on EMG, and compatible muscle MRI or biopsy—a negative panel does not exclude disease.

Seronegative myositis is a recognized entity. Up to 30–40% of patients meeting diagnostic criteria for IIM may lack detectable myositis-specific autoantibodies on commercial panels, although some of these patients may have antibodies detectable only by IP or newly described specificities not included on a given panel. Causes of seronegativity include true absence of antibody, technical false negative due to assay limitations (antigen conformation or epitope masking), low antibody titers below assay detection, or sampling at an early stage before seroconversion. Importantly, a seronegative result does not obviate further evaluation when clinical suspicion remains high.

Clinical implications. In practice, if pretest probability is high and panel results are negative or discordant with the phenotype, clinicians should not dismiss the diagnosis solely on the basis of serology. Additional diagnostics—muscle MRI to localize involvement, EMG, targeted repeat serology, and muscle biopsy with appropriate histopathologic and immunohistochemical stains—are warranted. Alternatively, in low pretest probability settings, an unexpected positive antibody should prompt reassessment of the entire clinical picture, consideration of assay-related false positivity, and possibly confirmatory testing before embarking on immunosuppressive therapy.

# Cross-reactivity and false positives (eg, isolated SSA/SSB results)

Cross-reactivity and false positives are important pitfalls, especially when panels include many antigens and use immobilized recombinant or denatured proteins. Sources of false positives include nonspecific binding, cross-reactive epitopes shared among autoantigens, heterophile antibodies, and technical artifacts.

SSA/SSB (anti-Ro/La) on myositis panels. Some commercial myositis panels include extractable nuclear antigens such as SSA (Ro) and SSB (La). An isolated SSA/SSB positivity in a patient being evaluated primarily for myositis can be misleading. Anti-SSA/SSB is classically associated with Sjögren syndrome and systemic lupus erythematosus, not with classic antisynthetase or IMNM phenotypes; however, anti-Ro can occasionally associate with myositis overlap syndromes (for instance overlap with systemic sclerosis or Sjögren syndrome) and with certain myositis subsets like juvenile dermatomyositis. When an isolated SSA/SSB appears on a myositis panel without other clinical features of Sjögren/Lupus and without other myositis-specific antibodies, two possibilities should be considered: (1) a true coexisting autoimmune process (eg, subclinical Sjögren), or (2) a false positive from assay cross-reactivity or low analytic specificity. The pretest probability and confirmatory assays (independent anti-Ro/La ELISA, immunodiffusion, or testing at a reference lab) help discriminate these.

Other cross-reactivities. Autoantibodies against ribonucleoprotein complexes, histidyl tRNA synthetase (Jo-1), or components of the SRP may cross-react in assays that do not preserve native structure. Additionally, rheumatoid factor or other high-titer antibodies can produce background signal and false positives in assays that do not adequately block IgM or heterophile interactions. Post-translational modifications, antigen fragments, or contaminating bacterial products in recombinant antigen preps can also create spurious reactivity.

Interpreting low-intensity bands. Many LIA reports list weak or borderline bands. These should be interpreted cautiously: low-intensity bands often have lower reproducibility on repeat testing and lower concordance with IP. A pragmatic approach is to regard strong, reproducible bands in the correct clinical context as more meaningful; borderline or isolated weak bands warrant confirmation.

Confirmatory strategies. If a result would change management—for example, initiation of high-dose immunosuppression—it is prudent to confirm unexpected positives using an alternative method (ELISA if initial is LIA, or ideally immunoprecipitation at a reference lab). Communication with the laboratory about the assay used and its known cross-reactivities can guide interpretation.

# When to repeat testing or pursue alternative diagnostics (biopsy, imaging)

Repeat testing. Repeat serologic testing is reasonable in several circumstances: when the initial result is borderline or low-titer and clinical suspicion persists; when testing was performed early in disease and seroconversion is plausible; when immunosuppressive therapy may have been present at the time of sampling (some therapies can lower antibody titers, though most myositis-specific antibodies are relatively stable); or when the initial assay platform is known to have limited sensitivity for a suspected antibody and a different assay is available. Timing for repeat testing is not rigid but often occurs within 4–12 weeks if the pretest probability remains high.

Indications to pursue muscle biopsy. Muscle biopsy remains the most definitive diagnostic tool for many forms of IIM. Indications include: a high clinical suspicion of inflammatory myopathy with seronegative or nondiagnostic serology; atypical presentations where alternative diagnoses are possible (eg, muscular dystrophy, metabolic myopathy, toxin-related myopathy); when histopathology will directly influence management (for example differentiating IMNM from polymyositis or inflammatory inclusion body myositis); and when malignancy-associated myositis is a concern and biopsy can identify paraneoplastic patterns. Biopsy provides information about fiber necrosis, regeneration, inflammatory cell types and distribution (endomysial versus perimysial), complement deposition, MHC class I upregulation, and features such as rimmed vacuoles that suggest inclusion body myositis. Sampling error can be mitigated by selecting an actively involved muscle based on clinical weakness and confirmatory imaging (MRI or ultrasound).

Role of imaging. Muscle MRI is a noninvasive way to localize active inflammation or edema with STIR/T2 sequences and to identify patterns of muscle involvement characteristic of certain myopathies. MRI guides biopsy site selection and can help monitor response to therapy. Ultrasound can detect muscle edema and atrophy and is portable and rapid, though less standardized than MRI. Chest CT or high-resolution CT is essential when antibodies associated with interstitial lung disease are present or suspected (eg, antisynthetase antibodies), and pulmonary evaluation may proceed even if muscle serology is negative when respiratory symptoms or abnormal PFTs exist.

Electromyography (EMG). EMG can support a myopathic process through short-duration, low-amplitude motor unit potentials and early recruitment. Myopathic fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves suggest active inflammation or necrosis. EMG can help distinguish neuropathic processes and is complementary to serology and biopsy.

Genetic testing and other alternatives. When clinical or histologic features suggest a genetic myopathy (for example distal weakness, very long disease course, family history, or specific biopsy features), targeted genetic testing or next-generation sequencing panels should be pursued. Metabolic or toxic causes of myopathy warrant relevant biochemical and medication review. Muscle enzyme trends (eg, creatine kinase) and response to brief empiric therapy may also inform diagnosis, but definitive decisions often rely on histology, imaging, and confirmatory serology.

# Role of referral to specialized laboratories for confirmatory testing

When to use specialized/reference labs. Referral to specialized laboratories for confirmatory testing is appropriate when assay results would materially change diagnosis or management and when the initial panel uses methods prone to false results for the antibody in question. Scenarios include unexpected isolated positives that conflict with the clinical phenotype, weak or borderline reactivity in LIA that needs confirmation, suspected antibodies that are poorly captured by the available commercial assay, or when research-level analyses (eg, immunoprecipitation, immunoblot with full-length native antigens, or epitope mapping) could clarify uncertain findings.

Advantages of reference labs. Specialized labs offer methods such as immunoprecipitation with radiolabeled cell extracts, high-resolution immunoblotting, cell-based assays preserving native conformations, and orthogonal testing across different platforms. These methods have higher specificity for many antibodies and can detect antibodies missed by common commercial assays. Reference labs often have expert interpretation and can relate serologic patterns to phenotypic data, which is valuable for rare antibodies that lack standardized commercial tests.

Practical considerations. Sending samples to a specialized lab requires planning: some methods (especially radiolabeling IP) require fresh or properly frozen sera, and shipping constraints apply. Turnaround times can be longer and cost higher. Clinicians should document the clinical phenotype and relevant labs when submitting specimens; such contextual information assists laboratory experts in choosing appropriate confirmatory tests and in interpreting equivocal bands. If confirmatory testing is pursued, maintaining communication with the laboratory regarding assay limitations and expected performance will streamline interpretation.

Integration into clinical workflow. Consider using an algorithmic approach: initial testing with a validated commercial panel appropriate to local prevalence and lab expertise, followed by reflex or directed confirmatory testing at a reference center for unexpected positives or high-stakes clinical decisions. This approach balances resource use with diagnostic accuracy.

# Practical interpretation framework and clinical decision points

A practical strategy for interpreting myositis panels:

1. Assess pretest probability before ordering tests. Evaluate clinical phenotype (pattern and severity of weakness), CK and other muscle enzymes (normal CK ranges: typical laboratory references are roughly 30–200 U/L for adult males and 25–170 U/L for adult females, but local lab ranges vary), EMG/MRI findings, and features suggesting specific subtypes (ILD, skin rash, mechanic’s hands, dysphagia, cardiac involvement).

2. Choose an assay appropriate to the clinical question, and be aware of the platform used by your laboratory (LIA vs ELISA vs multiplex). If a specific antibody is strongly suspected clinically (for example anti-HMGCR in statin-exposed patients with necrotizing myopathy), consider ordering a validated ELISA or direct testing to a reference lab rather than a broad panel that may give ambiguous low-intensity results.

3. Interpret positive results in context. Strong, reproducible positivity for a myositis-specific antibody matching the phenotype increases diagnostic confidence. Borderline or isolated antibodies not matching the clinical picture should prompt confirmatory testing before major treatment decisions.

4. Manage negative or indeterminate results. If serology is negative but clinical suspicion is high, proceed with imaging and targeted muscle biopsy. Consider repeating serology with an alternate platform or sending for IP if available.

5. Use confirmatory testing for critical decisions. For unexpected or practice-changing results, seek confirmation by a method with higher specificity, ideally at a specialized laboratory.

6. Re-evaluate if new clinical data emerge. Serology and histopathology should inform but not supplant ongoing clinical assessment; disease evolution may clarify the diagnosis and warrant repeat testing.

Conclusion

Myositis antibody panels are valuable tools that, when properly used, increase diagnostic precision and guide prognosis and management. However, interpretation requires understanding assay types and their limitations, applying Bayesian reasoning to account for pretest probability, recognizing the possibility of seronegative disease, and appreciating sources of cross-reactivity and false positives. Muscle biopsy, imaging, EMG, and referral to specialized laboratories for confirmatory testing remain essential components of a comprehensive diagnostic approach. Clinicians should integrate serologic results with the entire clinical picture and seek confirmatory testing when results are unexpected or when high-stakes management decisions depend on antibody status.

Introduction

Drug‑induced autoimmune conditions constitute an important and challenging subset of adverse drug reactions (ADRs). They arise when exposure to a xenobiotic — a medication, its metabolite, or an excipient — triggers a self‑directed immune response that mimics, unmasks, or exacerbates idiopathic autoimmune disease. Among these, drug‑associated inflammatory myopathies (including polymyositis, dermatomyositis, immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy, and antisynthetase syndromes) are particularly consequential because they may produce profound weakness, systemic complications, and require prolonged immunosuppression. Clinicians must therefore integrate epidemiologic knowledge, an understanding of host susceptibility, temporal relationships between exposure and disease onset, and structured causality assessment to make rational decisions about diagnosis, reporting, and management. This chapter reviews the epidemiology of inflammatory myopathies with attention to drug and comorbidity associations, host risk factors for drug‑induced autoimmunity, typical latency patterns, commonly used causality assessment tools and their application to suspected autoimmune ADRs, pharmacovigilance principles for detection and reporting, and the ethical and practical considerations governing drug rechallenge and documentation.

# Epidemiology of inflammatory myopathies and associations with drugs and comorbidities

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs) form a heterogeneous group of immune‑mediated disorders characterized by muscle inflammation and weakness with varied systemic involvement. Epidemiologic estimates vary by region, case definitions, and whether inclusion criteria capture children and adults. Broadly, incidence estimates for adult IIM range from roughly 1 to 10 per 100,000 person‑years, with prevalence estimates commonly reported in the range of about 5–20 per 100,000 persons in many populations; some registries and referral cohorts report higher prevalence reflecting case ascertainment bias. Specific subtypes show differing epidemiology: dermatomyositis tends to have a bimodal age distribution (younger adults and middle‑aged adults), immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy and antisynthetase syndromes often present in middle age, and inclusion body myositis predominates in older adults. Women are more commonly affected than men for most subtypes, and older age is associated with worse outcomes and with paraneoplastic associations.

A distinct but overlapping phenomenon is drug‑associated inflammatory myopathy or drug‑induced autoimmune myositis. Several classes of medications have been linked to myopathic syndromes through case reports, series, and pharmacovigilance signals. Well‑recognized examples include statins — classically associated with self‑limited myalgias and creatine kinase (CK) elevation but also with immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy associated with anti‑HMGCR antibodies; immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) used in oncology, which have been increasingly reported to produce myositis often accompanied by myocarditis; interferons and certain biologics that can precipitate autoimmune phenomena; and some antibiotics, antithyroid drugs, and recreational agents reported less frequently. The spectrum of drug‑associated muscle disease ranges from toxic myopathy (direct myocyte injury often dose‑related and reversible on withdrawal), to immune‑mediated myopathy characterized by autoantibodies, inflammatory infiltrates on biopsy, and the need for immunosuppression.

Comorbidities influence both the risk of developing IIM and the presentation. Cancer is a well‑established associated condition particularly with dermatomyositis and late‑onset cases; paraneoplastic mechanisms may contribute to immune activation. Infectious triggers (viral and bacterial) are repeatedly implicated as both primary precipitants and cofactors that modulate immune responses. Metabolic conditions such as diabetes and thyroid disease may complicate presentation and management, and prior or coexisting autoimmune diseases (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis) can increase baseline autoimmune susceptibility and confound attribution when new symptoms arise. Importantly, medications used to treat comorbid conditions (e.g., statins for dyslipidemia or immune checkpoint inhibitors for cancer) are themselves potential causal agents; disentangling direct drug effects from disease‑related or paraneoplastic immune activation requires careful clinical and epidemiologic reasoning.

Clinicians should be aware that background rates of IIM are low, so sporadic temporal associations with drug exposure may occur by chance. Robust attribution requires integration of epidemiologic context (how common is the disease in the relevant population), strength of association from observational data or mechanistic plausibility, and the individual clinical pattern.

# Host risk factors for drug‑induced autoimmunity (genetic predisposition, prior autoimmunity, exposures)

Drug‑induced autoimmunity arises at the interface of exogenous exposure and host susceptibility. Several classes of host factors modulate risk and phenotype: genetic predisposition, prior or concurrent autoimmune disease, intrinsic demographic characteristics, and environmental or comorbid exposures.

Genetic predisposition is a major determinant. Human leukocyte antigen (HLA) alleles shape antigen presentation and have been linked to susceptibility for both idiopathic and drug‑induced autoimmune conditions. For example, specific HLA class II alleles have been associated with higher risk of anti‑HMGCR myopathy in patients exposed to statins, and HLA alleles such as HLA‑DRB1 variants are associated with idiopathic forms and some drug‑related syndromes. Single nucleotide polymorphisms in immune regulatory genes (e.g., PTPN22, CTLA4) can increase risk for autoimmune reactions more broadly. Pharmacogenetic differences in drug metabolism (e.g., variants of cytochrome P450 enzymes, N‑acetyltransferase) influence drug levels and the formation of reactive metabolites that can form adducts with self‑proteins, promoting neoantigen formation and autoimmune responses.

Prior autoimmunity or family history of autoimmune disease increases baseline susceptibility. A patient with an existing autoimmune disease may have a lower threshold for developing additional autoimmune phenomena following an immunogenic exposure. For example, patients with systemic autoimmune diseases treated with biologics have been reported to develop paradoxical autoimmune manifestations attributable to immune modulation. Likewise, a personal or family history of drug reactions (e.g., drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms) can indicate an immune‑responsive phenotype.

Non‑genetic host factors include age and sex: women are generally at higher risk for autoimmune disease; certain drug‑induced autoimmune phenomena track with age (e.g., inclusion body myositis in older adults). Smoking is a well‑established environmental risk factor for some autoimmune diseases (notably rheumatoid arthritis) and may potentiate drug‑induced autoimmunity by altering immune regulation and antigen presentation. Infectious exposures, either antecedent or concurrent, can prime immune responses through molecular mimicry or by providing danger signals that break tolerance. Organ dysfunction such as renal or hepatic impairment affects drug clearance and increases exposure to parent compounds and reactive metabolites, elevating risk. Nutritional factors, microbiome composition, and prior vaccinations have been proposed modulators of immune responsiveness, though direct causal links in drug‑induced autoimmunity are less well established.

Finally, the pharmacologic context matters: polypharmacy increases the chance of interactions and cumulative immune stimulation, and prior sensitization to structurally related compounds can produce cross‑reactivity. Clinically, detailed history taking should elicit family history, prior autoimmune disease, smoking and infection history, organ dysfunction, and prior drug exposures to estimate individual risk and to guide diagnostic and management decisions.

# Temporal relationships and latency periods for drug‑induced immune events

Time is a critical dimension in causality assessment. A plausible temporal relationship strengthens inference that a drug contributed to an adverse immune event, but the expected latency varies widely by mechanism and by drug.

In general, several temporal patterns are recognized:

- Immediate hypersensitivity reactions (IgE mediated) occur within minutes to hours of exposure and are typically not autoimmune in nature.

- Type II and III immune‑mediated reactions (antibody dependent or immune complex mediated) often emerge within days to weeks after exposure, although prior sensitization can shorten latency.

- Drug‑induced autoimmunity that resembles classic autoimmune disease (i.e., the induction of autoantibodies and organ‑specific inflammation) tends to appear over weeks to months, because the generation of high‑titer, pathogenic autoantibodies and the organization of effector cellular responses usually require time. For instance, classic drug‑induced lupus from hydralazine or procainamide often develops after months to years of exposure, with latency commonly 6–24 months but sometimes longer.

- Some drug‑associated myopathies have characteristic latencies: statin‑associated self‑limited myalgias may occur within weeks of initiation or dose escalation, whereas statin‑associated immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (anti‑HMGCR associated) can develop after variable exposure durations — months to years — and may even arise after statin discontinuation, reflecting an immune process sustained independent of ongoing exposure. Immune checkpoint inhibitor‑related myositis often emerges early in the course of therapy, commonly within the first 1–3 months, but can occur later.

Latency also depends on dose and cumulative exposure. Direct toxic myopathies are more likely to be dose dependent and to occur with higher cumulative doses, while immune‑mediated processes can be idiosyncratic and not strictly dose related. Reexposure after a prior sensitization often results in shorter latency and more rapid or severe recurrence.

When assessing a suspected drug‑related autoimmune event, clinicians should construct a detailed timeline: dates of initiation, dose changes, temporary interruptions, and discontinuation; onset and evolution of symptoms; and the timing of key laboratory and imaging abnormalities. The temporal relationship must be interpreted in the context of known latency windows for the suspect agent (when available), biologic plausibility, and alternative explanations such as coincident infection, paraneoplastic processes, or progression of an underlying disease. Absence of an expected temporal relationship weakens causality but does not definitively exclude a drug-induced mechanism, particularly when the pathogenesis involves immune priming and delayed loss of tolerance.

# Structured causality assessment tools (WHO‑UMC system, Naranjo algorithm) and their application

Causality assessment tools provide standardized frameworks to estimate the likelihood that a drug caused an adverse event. Two widely used instruments are the WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO‑UMC) system and the Naranjo algorithm. Both are designed for general ADR assessment, not specifically for autoimmune diseases, and each has strengths and limitations when applied to complex immune phenomena.

The WHO‑UMC system is a qualitative, expert‑opinion based framework that classifies causality into categories: Certain, Probable/Likely, Possible, Unlikely, Conditional/Unclassified, and Unassessable/Unclassifiable. Assessment is based on factors including temporal relationship, dechallenge and re‑challenge information, alternative causes, known pharmacology, and previous conclusive reports. Key features:

- "Certain" requires a plausible time relationship, clinically reasonable response on withdrawal, and definitive evidence excluding alternative causes; re‑challenge results positive where ethical and feasible.

- "Probable/Likely" applies when a reasonable time relationship exists, withdrawal is clinically reasonable and leads to improvement, and alternative causes are less likely.

- "Possible" is used when time relationship is compatible but alternative causes are plausible.

When applying WHO‑UMC to drug‑induced autoimmunity, clinicians must recognize that dechallenge effect may be delayed or absent (autoimmune processes may persist after drug withdrawal). Thus, lack of immediate improvement does not exclude causality, and absence of re‑challenge is common because re‑exposure may be unethical or dangerous.

The Naranjo algorithm is a structured, point‑based questionnaire of 10 items summing to categorize an ADR as definite, probable, possible, or doubtful. Items include timing, previous conclusive reports, dechallenge/rechallenge responses, alternative causes, drug levels, dose–response relationships, and objective evidence. Scores ≥9 indicate definite, 5–8 probable, 1–4 possible, and ≤0 doubtful.

Naranjo strengths include reproducibility and objectivity; weaknesses include limited applicability to immune‑mediated ADRs and to delayed‑onset phenomena. For example, questions about dose‑response and drug levels are often irrelevant for idiosyncratic autoimmune reactions; re‑challenge is often withheld for safety; and improvement after drug withdrawal may be absent or delayed, reducing the score despite strong biologic plausibility.

Other approaches include the Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM) tailored to drug‑induced liver injury and specialized scales developed for specific organ systems. For autoimmune conditions, modifications or expert consensus panels may better capture the complexities: evaluation of autoantibody presence and specificity, biopsy features consistent with immune‑mediated injury, and mechanistic data (e.g., detection of drug‑protein adducts) can strengthen causal inference beyond what general ADR tools capture.

Best practice for clinicians is to use these structured tools as aids, not as substitutes for clinical reasoning. Document the application of the tool and the rationale for responses to each item, interpret scores in the context of disease‑specific features (e.g., presence of disease‑specific autoantibodies, characteristic histopathology), and transparently record uncertainties. For publication or regulatory reporting, stating the specific tool used and its scored outcome enhances reproducibility and aids pharmacovigilance.

# Pharmacovigilance: reporting adverse drug reactions, post‑marketing surveillance

Pharmacovigilance is the science and activities related to the detection, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse effects or drug‑related problems. For drug‑induced autoimmune diseases, robust pharmacovigilance is essential because randomized clinical trials may be underpowered to detect rare idiosyncratic immune events, and signals often emerge in the post‑marketing phase.

At the clinician level, spontaneous reporting to national or regional pharmacovigilance centers (e.g., FDA MedWatch in the United States, MHRA Yellow Card in the UK, EMA EudraVigilance in the EU) is the principal mechanism to accumulate case data. A high‑quality report includes patient demographics, suspected drug(s) with brand and generic names, dosing, dates of exposure and symptom onset, concomitant medications, detailed clinical description, laboratory and biopsy data, management and outcome, and the reporter’s assessment of causality. Inclusion of dechallenge and rechallenge information when available is valuable. Photographs (e.g., skin manifestations) and biopsy excerpts can strengthen the signal.

Passive surveillance is complemented by active methods: registries for specific drug classes or diseases (e.g., statin myopathy registries, immune checkpoint inhibitor registries), sentinel surveillance networks, electronic health record (EHR) mining, and pharmacoepidemiologic studies using claims databases. These approaches allow estimation of incidence rates, risk ratios, and adjustment for confounding. Signal detection methods use disproportionality metrics (e.g., reporting odds ratio, proportional reporting ratio) in large spontaneous reporting databases to flag potential associations that are more frequently reported than expected.

Once a signal is detected, causality assessment proceeds from case series characterization to analytic studies: cohort or case‑control studies, nested case‑control studies, and self‑controlled case series can evaluate temporality and estimate relative risks while controlling for time‑invariant confounders. Biological plausibility, dose–response gradients, and mechanistic studies (e.g., demonstration of drug‑dependent antibodies, HLA associations) strengthen causality.

Regulatory actions depend on signal strength and clinical seriousness: label updates/warnings, restricted use, additional monitoring, or withdrawal. Clinicians should be aware of and use safety communications and updated prescribing information.

Barriers to effective pharmacovigilance include underreporting (particularly for events with insidious onset or when attribution is uncertain), variable report quality, diagnostic heterogeneity, and reporting bias (e.g., notoriety bias after publicized cases). To improve detection, clinicians should report suspected autoimmune ADRs even when causality is uncertain. Well‑documented single cases can seed further investigation, and combined case series frequently form the basis for regulatory review.

# Principles and ethics of drug rechallenge and documentation of suspected associations

Rechallenge — reintroducing a suspected offending drug after an adverse reaction — can provide strong evidence for causality when a clear, reproducible response occurs. However, in drug‑induced autoimmune disease rechallenge raises significant ethical and safety concerns. Decisions about rechallenge should consider the severity of the prior event, availability of alternatives, the seriousness of the underlying condition for which the drug is indicated, the degree of uncertainty regarding causality, and the patient’s preferences and values.

Ethical principles applicable include nonmaleficence (do no harm), beneficence (balance potential therapeutic benefit), autonomy (informed consent), and justice. For life‑saving therapies with no alternatives (e.g., certain oncologic agents), a carefully monitored, informed rechallenge with patient consent may be justifiable despite risk. For nonessential agents or when safer alternatives exist, rechallenge is generally contraindicated if the index reaction was severe (e.g., necrotizing myopathy, myocarditis, Stevens–Johnson syndrome). Rechallenge after a mild, transient reaction may be reasonable if the drug provides substantial benefit; however, the clinician must ensure patient understanding of risks and establish monitoring protocols.

When rechallenge is contemplated, strategies to mitigate risk include using the lowest effective dose, slow dose escalation, concurrent prophylactic measures when evidence supports them, close clinical and laboratory monitoring (e.g., regular CK, troponin, autoantibody panels), and ready access to immunosuppressive therapy if immune activation recurs. A formal informed consent process should document the rationale, potential benefits, possible risks including severe recurrence, and alternative treatments.

From a documentation standpoint, clear, thorough records are essential. These should include a timeline of exposure and symptom onset, objective findings (CK, aldolase, autoantibodies, MRI, electromyography, biopsy), treatments and outcomes, rationale for attributing causality, documentation of informed consent for any rechallenge, and the results of dechallenge/rechallenge. Sharing anonymized, high‑quality reports with pharmacovigilance authorities is an ethical responsibility and enhances the collective understanding of drug safety.

Rechallenge is not the only avenue to strengthen causality. Alternative approaches include careful dechallenge observation, immune profiling (autoantibody specificity such as anti‑HMGCR, anti‑Jo‑1), biopsy confirmation of immune pathology, and pharmacogenomic analyses. In many cases, these approaches provide sufficient evidence to guide clinical management without exposing patients to additional risk.

In situations where rechallenge is contraindicated yet evidence for causality remains uncertain, multidisciplinary case review (including rheumatology, neurology, pathology, pharmacology, and, when relevant, oncology) and consultation with pharmacovigilance authorities can be valuable.

Conclusion

Assessing suspected drug‑induced autoimmune disease requires synthesis of epidemiology, host susceptibility, temporal patterns, clinical and laboratory evidence, and structured causality tools, all within the constraints of patient safety and ethical practice. Awareness of the epidemiologic context, recognition of host risk factors, careful timeline construction, judicious use of causality algorithms, responsible pharmacovigilance reporting, and principled decision‑making about rechallenge are essential components of high‑quality clinical care and contribute to safer pharmacotherapy for all patients. Clinicians should document suspected associations carefully and report them to appropriate surveillance systems to support the broader detection and analysis of drug‑induced autoimmune phenomena.

Appendix — Selected laboratory reference ranges and diagnostic decision points (typical adult values)

- Creatine kinase (CK): approximately 30–200 U/L (laboratory‑specific reference ranges vary; interpret relative to lab standard and muscle mass). In myositis, CK can be mildly to markedly elevated (from modest increases to >10 × upper limit).

- Aldolase: typical reference 1–7 U/L (variable by method).

- Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR): commonly <20 mm/hr in adults; may be elevated in systemic inflammation.

- C‑reactive protein (CRP): commonly <5 mg/L; elevated in active inflammatory states.

- Autoantibodies: antinuclear antibody (ANA) positivity is nonspecific; myositis‑specific antibodies (MSA) include anti‑Jo‑1 (histidyl tRNA synthetase), anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑MDA5, anti‑TIF1‑gamma, anti‑SRP, and anti‑HMGCR — the presence of specific MSAs lends etiologic and prognostic information.

- Diagnostic criteria: modern classification of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (e.g., 2017 EULAR/ACR classification) weights features including symmetric proximal muscle weakness, elevated muscle enzymes, electromyography and MRI changes consistent with myositis, and muscle biopsy findings. Classification yields probabilistic thresholds for “definite” or “probable” IIM, and specificity is increased when combined with serologic and histopathologic features.

Clinicians should always consult current laboratory reference ranges at their institution and apply disease‑specific classification criteria and local guidelines in diagnostic and management decisions.
