Introduction

Severe myopathies—whether hereditary, inflammatory, metabolic, infectious, or toxic—can produce profound systemic complications that are often the major determinants of morbidity and mortality. Beyond treating the underlying muscle disease, clinicians must anticipate, detect, and manage problems that arise from loss of muscle function: impaired swallowing with aspiration risk, respiratory failure from respiratory muscle weakness, venous thromboembolism and pressure injury related to immobility, kidney injury from myocyte breakdown, deconditioning, and the psychosocial stresses of chronic disability. Effective supportive care requires timely assessment, objective monitoring, and coordinated interventions delivered by a multidisciplinary team. This chapter synthesizes practical, evidence-based approaches to the common complications of severe myopathy and describes clinical decision points useful at the bedside.

# Assessment and management of dysphagia and aspiration risk (speech/swallow evaluation, enteral feeding options)

Dysphagia is common in severe myopathies that involve bulbar musculature and contributes to malnutrition, dehydration, and aspiration pneumonia. Clinical assessment begins with a structured history probing symptoms of oropharyngeal dysfunction (dysphagia for liquids versus solids, drooling, nasal regurgitation), voice changes, coughing during or after eating, recurrent chest infections, weight loss, and functional impact on meal preparation. Physical examination should evaluate cranial nerve function, tongue strength and mobility, palatal elevation, gag reflex (noting that gag presence does not exclude significant dysphagia), cough strength, and the ability to manage secretions. Observe a meal where possible to assess coordination, mastication, and postural compensation.

Instrumental assessment should be obtained when clinical concern is moderate to high or when the examination is unreliable. Videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS), also called a modified barium swallow, provides dynamic visualization of oral and pharyngeal phases, identifies penetration or aspiration, and can evaluate the effectiveness of compensatory maneuvers and diet modifications. Fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) allows bedside assessment of secretion management, pharyngeal residue, delayed swallow, and aspiration with different consistencies; FEES is particularly useful when transport to radiology is unsafe or when repeated assessments are needed. Objective measures of cough and respiratory reserve (peak cough flow, forced vital capacity) inform aspiration risk; a peak cough flow <160 L/min suggests ineffective airway clearance and increased aspiration risk. Pulse oximetry and continuous monitoring during trials can detect aspiration-related desaturation.

Management goals are to prevent aspiration and malnutrition while maximizing safe oral intake. Acute bedside strategies include texture modification (thickened liquids, pureed solids), posture adjustments (chin tuck, head rotation), pacing and small bites, and swallowing maneuvers taught by speech–language pathologists. These interventions must be individualized and re-evaluated frequently because bulbar weakness can fluctuate and fatigue can worsen swallowing late in a meal.

Enteral feeding is indicated when safe oral intake cannot meet nutritional needs or when aspiration risk is unacceptable despite compensations. Short-term options include nasogastric (NG) or orogastric tubes. NG tubes are easy to insert and suitable for weeks to a few months; they carry risks of sinusitis, tube displacement, and may worsen lower esophageal sphincter competence. For anticipated long-term enteral feeding (commonly >4–6 weeks), percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) or radiologically inserted gastrostomy should be considered. Decision-making should weigh respiratory status (patients with severe respiratory compromise may have higher PEG-related sedation risk), anticipated disease trajectory, patient goals, and caregiver capacity.

Feeding route selection should also consider the type of dysphagia: patients with severe pharyngeal-phase dysfunction who aspirate despite gastric feeding may require additional strategies to manage secretions (saliva aspiration) and may be candidates for jejunal feeding in rare cases when reflux is a major contributor to aspiration risk. Collaborate with dietitians to calculate caloric and protein goals (commonly 25–30 kcal/kg/day and 1.2–2.0 g protein/kg/day depending on catabolic state), correct micronutrient deficiencies, and tailor enteral formulas for tolerance and glycemic control.

Ongoing reassessment is critical. Speech–language pathologists should reassess swallowing function at regular intervals and after major changes in respiratory or neuromuscular status. Involving patients and families early in shared decision-making about the balance of aspiration risk, quality of life, and feeding preferences improves adherence and aligns interventions with patient goals.

# Respiratory muscle weakness: monitoring, indications for ventilatory support, pulmonary rehabilitation

Respiratory complications are a leading cause of morbidity in severe myopathy. Weakness of the diaphragm and accessory muscles causes hypoventilation, impaired cough, secretion retention, and vulnerability to infections. Early recognition and objective monitoring guide timely interventions to prevent acute respiratory failure.

Monitoring includes serial clinical assessment and objective measurements. Clinically, note orthopnea, sleep-disordered breathing (morning headaches, daytime somnolence, nonrestorative sleep), exertional dyspnea disproportionate to limb weakness, and recurrent lower respiratory tract infections. Bedside measures include forced vital capacity (FVC), maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP or negative inspiratory force, NIF), maximal expiratory pressure (MEP), and peak cough flow (PCF). Supine FVC is particularly informative because a fall in FVC when supine compared with upright position suggests diaphragmatic weakness. Key thresholds used in decision-making include: FVC <50% predicted signals significant restriction; more conservative action thresholds are often FVC <30–40% predicted or an absolute FVC <1.0 L (varies with body habitus). A NIF less negative than -20 cmH2O (for example, -20 cmH2O or less negative) indicates significant inspiratory muscle weakness; many centers use NIF < -30 cmH2O as safer, while NIF > -20 cmH2O raises concern. Peak cough flow <160 L/min suggests ineffective cough and inability to clear secretions.

Arterial blood gas analysis and nocturnal oximetry or polysomnography help detect hypoventilation and sleep-disordered breathing. Early hypoventilation can present with nocturnal hypoxemia and hypercapnia, with daytime hypercapnia a late sign. A rising pCO2 or daytime hypercapnia warrants prompt ventilatory support.

Indications for initiating ventilatory support include acute respiratory failure with hypoxemia and/or hypercapnia, progressive daytime hypercapnia or symptomatic nocturnal hypoventilation, severe impairment in respiratory mechanics (e.g., FVC declining rapidly or below threshold values), inability to protect the airway, and recurrent respiratory infections due to ineffective cough. Noninvasive ventilation (NIV) with bilevel positive airway pressure is the preferred initial approach when patients are cooperative and hemodynamically stable. NIV supports ventilation, reduces work of breathing, improves gas exchange, and may prevent intubation. Indications for NIV initiation include symptoms of nocturnal hypoventilation with documented nocturnal hypercapnia or hypoxemia, resting hypercapnia (PaCO2 >45–50 mmHg depending on local practice), or inability to maintain oxygenation with conservative measures. Settings are individualized to correct hypercapnia and relieve dyspnea; titration commonly targets normocapnia or symptom resolution while ensuring patient comfort.

Invasive mechanical ventilation is indicated when NIV fails, when there is apnea or inability to protect the airway, or when secretions cannot be managed noninvasively. Predictors of NIV failure include severe bulbar dysfunction with inability to clear secretions, excessive aspiration, and progressive mental status deterioration. For patients with chronic progressive myopathy who require long-term mechanical ventilation, options include tracheostomy with invasive ventilation or long-term NIV if feasible. Decisions regarding long-term invasive ventilation should be discussed in the context of patient goals and expected quality of life.

Pulmonary rehabilitation is a cornerstone of care for patients stable from an acute exacerbation. Rehabilitation focuses on breathing retraining, inspiratory muscle training (IMT) where evidence supports benefit in selected patients, airway clearance techniques, optimizing posture and mobility to improve ventilation, and physical conditioning to maximize residual function. Airway clearance methods include manual assisted cough, mechanical insufflation-exsufflation devices, and assisted cough techniques performed by caregivers. Use of mechanically assisted cough may be indicated when PCF is persistently low and secretion burden is high. Rehabilitation plans should be staged and progressive, avoiding overuse fatigue which can worsen muscle weakness; sessions may be brief and frequent initially, increasing duration with tolerance.

# Prevention and management of thromboembolic events and immobility complications

Patients with severe myopathy are at elevated risk for venous thromboembolism (VTE), pressure ulcers, contractures, and deconditioning as a consequence of reduced mobility, venous stasis, and systemic inflammation. Prevention strategies should be instituted early and tailored to bleeding risk.

Pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH; e.g., enoxaparin 40 mg subcutaneously daily in many adults) or unfractionated heparin (e.g., 5000 units subcutaneously every 8–12 hours) is indicated in hospitalized patients with limited mobility and no contraindications. Dose adjustment is required for renal impairment (for example, enoxaparin dosing in severe renal dysfunction) and extremes of body weight. Mechanical prophylaxis—intermittent pneumatic compression devices or graduated compression stockings—should be used when pharmacologic prophylaxis is contraindicated (active bleeding, high bleeding risk). For patients with very high thrombotic risk (previous VTE, known thrombophilia), individualized higher-intensity prophylaxis or early diagnostic surveillance (ultrasound) may be appropriate.

Clinical vigilance for deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism is important: unilateral limb swelling, pain, unexplained tachycardia, hypoxemia, or sudden deterioration should prompt diagnostic evaluation with compression ultrasonography and, when appropriate, computed tomography pulmonary angiography. If VTE is diagnosed, therapeutic anticoagulation is indicated unless contraindicated; agents and regimens follow standard VTE management and must be adapted to renal function and bleeding risk.

Pressure injury prevention requires frequent repositioning (turning every 2 hours for high-risk immobile patients), use of pressure-redistribution mattresses, meticulous skin care, and nutritional optimization for wound healing (adequate protein intake, 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day as indicated). Early involvement of wound care specialists can guide staging and local care. Range-of-motion exercises, splinting, and positioning reduce the risk of joint contractures; contractures should be managed proactively with serial casting, orthoses, and, when necessary, surgical consultation in chronic settings.

Other immobility complications include osteoporosis and loss of bone mass, urinary stasis with urinary tract infections, and deconditioning of cardiovascular and autonomic responses. Prevention strategies include early mobilization as medically safe, calcium and vitamin D supplementation as indicated, fall prevention measures, and bowel/bladder regimens to reduce urinary retention and constipation.

# Renal protection in muscle injury and rhabdomyolysis: fluid management, electrolyte correction, indications for renal replacement therapy

Rhabdomyolysis from muscle necrosis releases intracellular contents—myoglobin, potassium, phosphate, uric acid, and creatine kinase (CK)—that can precipitate acute kidney injury (AKI). Prevention and early management focus on aggressive fluid resuscitation, monitoring and correction of electrolytes, and timely renal replacement therapy (RRT) when indicated.

Diagnosis is suggested by history of muscle injury or disease exacerbation and by laboratory abnormalities. Serum CK elevation is characteristic; values vary widely, but CK >5 times the upper limit of normal is commonly used as a screening threshold. Typical reference ranges: CK male 20–200 U/L, female 20–170 U/L (laboratory-dependent). CK values in severe rhabdomyolysis often exceed 5,000–10,000 U/L and may reach >100,000 U/L. Myoglobinuria may cause dark (tea-colored) urine and is detected clinically by a positive dipstick for blood with few red blood cells on microscopy. Monitor serum creatinine and urine output; AKI is defined and staged by KDIGO criteria: an increase in serum creatinine by ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, or a 1.5× increase from baseline within 7 days, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hr for 6 hours.

Fluid resuscitation is the mainstay of prevention for pigment nephropathy. Begin isotonic crystalloid (0.9% saline) early to restore intravascular volume and enhance renal perfusion and myoglobin washout. Typical initial rates are aggressive—e.g., 1–2 L bolus followed by maintenance rates titrated to achieve urine output targets. Urine output goals commonly suggested for rhabdomyolysis are at least 0.5 mL/kg/hr in general medical patients, and many centers aim for higher output (1.0–2.0 mL/kg/hr) in patients with heavy myoglobin loads while monitoring for fluid overload risk. Monitor hemodynamics, central venous pressure when indicated, and diuresis to guide therapy.

Urinary alkalinization with sodium bicarbonate has been proposed to reduce myoglobin nephrotoxicity by increasing myoglobin solubility and reducing cast formation. Evidence is mixed and routine use is not universally recommended. Consider bicarbonate therapy when large myoglobin burdens exist or when urine pH remains low despite adequate fluids, especially if myoglobinuria is heavy and patient is at high risk for AKI; a common regimen is to add bicarbonate to IV fluids to maintain urine pH >6.5. Avoid aggressive alkalinization in patients with hypocalcemia or volume overload risk. Mannitol and loop diuretics have been used historically to promote diuresis, but routine use lacks strong evidence and should be individualized; diuretics are considered only if euvolemia is established and urine flow remains inadequate.

Electrolyte abnormalities are common and can be life-threatening. Hyperkalemia from release of intracellular potassium can cause arrhythmias; monitor serum potassium frequently (e.g., every 2–6 hours initially). Critical hyperkalemia (commonly K+ ≥6.0–6.5 mEq/L or with ECG changes such as peaked T waves, widened QRS) warrants immediate treatment: intravenous calcium to stabilize the myocardium, insulin with glucose to shift potassium intracellularly, nebulized or IV beta-agonists as adjuncts, and measures to remove potassium (diuretics if urine output sufficient, potassium-binding resin, and RRT). Hypocalcemia may be present but asymptomatic; treat symptomatic hypocalcemia (tetany, arrhythmia) with IV calcium, but avoid routine correction of asymptomatic hypocalcemia because calcium may later deposit in necrotic muscle. Hyperphosphatemia and secondary hypocalcemia should be monitored and managed; phosphate binders and dialysis may be needed for severe derangement.

Indications for renal replacement therapy include refractory hyperkalemia, severe metabolic acidosis unresponsive to medical therapy (for example, pH <7.1–7.2 depending on clinical context), volume overload compromising oxygenation or hemodynamics, uremic complications, and oliguria/anuria with progressive azotemia despite conservative measures. In patients with severe rhabdomyolysis, early initiation of RRT may be considered when biochemical derangements are pronounced and not rapidly correctable or in the setting of established AKI with fluid overload. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is often preferred in hemodynamically unstable patients; conventional intermittent hemodialysis is effective for removing potassium and correcting acidosis in stable patients. Note that standard dialysis removes myoglobin inefficiently due to molecular size and protein binding, so dialysis is mainly used to manage complications rather than to eliminate myoglobin.

Ongoing management includes serial laboratory monitoring (CK, creatinine, electrolytes, bicarbonate), urine output monitoring, and careful fluid balance. Consult nephrology early when CK is very high, creatinine is rising, or electrolyte abnormalities emerge. Preventive measures—early aggressive fluids and close surveillance—remain the most effective strategies to reduce risk of AKI.

# Physical therapy and staged rehabilitation strategies after acute myopathy

Rehabilitation in severe myopathy must balance restoring function and avoiding overexertion that can exacerbate muscle damage. Staged, individualized rehabilitation plans delivered by experienced physical and occupational therapists optimize recovery, prevent complications, and facilitate reintegration into activities of daily living.

The rehabilitation process begins with an early assessment focusing on baseline functional status, cardiopulmonary reserve, joint range of motion, presence of contractures, spasticity or flaccidity, pain, swallowing and communication needs, and psychosocial factors. In the acute phase, the priority is prevention: passive range-of-motion exercises to prevent contractures, positioning to protect skin and reduce pressure, bed mobility training when possible, and respiratory muscle facilitation where indicated. Therapy sessions in this phase are short, frequent, and closely monitored for fatigue, respiratory compromise, and pain. Energy conservation techniques are introduced early.

As medical stability is achieved, transition to active-assisted and then active strengthening exercises is appropriate. Strength training should be low-intensity and low-load initially, guided by perceived exertion and objective markers; typical protocols use higher repetitions with lower resistance, progressing only when the patient tolerates without prolonged post-exertional weakness. Avoid eccentric-heavy exercises that may increase muscle damage in susceptible myopathies. Aerobic conditioning adapted to tolerance (e.g., recumbent cycle ergometry, bedside pedalers, arm ergometry) improves cardiovascular fitness without excessive load on weakened muscles. Monitor for delayed-onset muscle soreness and overtraining; if significant fatigue or CK elevations follow exercise, intensity should be reduced.

Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) can be a useful adjunct for patients with severe weakness who cannot actively participate in strengthening; NMES may maintain muscle mass and facilitate re-education. Functional training focuses on transfers, gait retraining, balance, and activities of daily living (ADL) retraining with adaptive devices as needed—walkers, orthoses (ankle-foot orthoses), and environmental modifications. Occupational therapists address fine motor skills, upper limb strengthening, adaptive techniques for self-care, and work re-integration planning.

Pulmonary rehabilitation components overlap with general rehabilitation and include inspiratory muscle training, cough augmentation techniques, secretion clearance strategies, and graded cardiovascular conditioning. Exercise prescriptions should take into account respiratory limitations; monitor oxygen saturation and provide supplemental oxygen if desaturation occurs.

Rehabilitation goals must be realistic and aligned with prognosis and patient priorities. For progressive myopathies, emphasis may be on maximizing current function, independence in ADLs, and caregiver training. For potentially reversible causes, the emphasis shifts to recovery of strength and function with progressive intensity. Throughout rehabilitation, clinicians should reassess for comorbidities (cardiac involvement is a concern in many myopathies), incorporate fall prevention strategies, and liaise with prosthetics and orthotics services when needed.

# Psychosocial support and coordination of multidisciplinary care

Severe myopathy imposes substantial psychosocial burdens on patients and caregivers: loss of independence, role changes, anxiety about respiratory failure and dependency on ventilatory support, grief over functional losses, financial stress, and social isolation. Comprehensive care addresses these needs proactively through integrated multidisciplinary teams.

A core multidisciplinary team typically includes neurology or neuromuscular specialists, pulmonology/critical care, rehabilitation medicine, physical and occupational therapy, speech–language pathology, nutrition, nursing with expertise in neuromuscular disease, social work, psychology or psychiatry, pain management, wound care, and palliative care when appropriate. Early involvement of care coordinators or case managers facilitates transitions from acute care to rehabilitation or home-based services, helps arrange equipment (ventilators, mobility aids), and connects families with community resources and home health services.

Psychosocial support begins with open, empathetic communication about diagnosis, prognosis, and expected disease trajectory. Routine screening for depression and anxiety is recommended because these conditions are common and treatable; validated tools (e.g., Patient Health Questionnaire-9) can guide detection. Provide timely referral to mental health professionals and consider pharmacologic treatment where indicated, bearing in mind potential interactions and the patient’s respiratory status. Encourage peer support groups and patient advocacy organizations which often provide disease-specific education, caregiver training, and emotional support.

Advance care planning is an essential component of comprehensive care. Discuss goals of care, preferences regarding life-sustaining therapies (noninvasive ventilation, invasive mechanical ventilation, tracheostomy), and code status early, revisiting these discussions as the disease evolves. Document advance directives and involve family members or surrogate decision-makers in planning. Palliative care teams can assist with symptom management, complex decision-making, and psychosocial support even early in the disease course.

Address practical concerns: coordinate home modifications (ramps, bathroom adaptations), arrange for durable medical equipment (hospital beds, lifts), and ensure training of caregivers in ventilator management, suctioning, enteral feeding, and emergency plans. Work with social services to navigate insurance, disability benefits, and employment issues. Educate patients and families about infection prevention and emergency contacts.

Finally, maintain continuity of care with clear documentation, scheduled follow-ups, and timely communication among team members. Multidisciplinary clinics for neuromuscular disease consolidate expertise, streamline care, and have been associated with improved outcomes and patient satisfaction.

Conclusion

Managing severe myopathy extends far beyond treating muscle pathology; it requires anticipatory, system-based care that protects the airway, preserves renal function, prevents thrombotic and immobility-related complications, restores function through staged rehabilitation, and supports the patient and family psychosocially. Objective monitoring, early interventions, and coordinated multidisciplinary care are the cornerstones of improving outcomes. Clinicians must individualize decision-making to the patient’s clinical status, comorbidities, and personal goals, and be prepared to adapt plans as the disease and patient circumstances change.
