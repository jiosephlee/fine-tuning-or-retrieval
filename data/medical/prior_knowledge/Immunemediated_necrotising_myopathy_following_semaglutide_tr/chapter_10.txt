Introduction

Proximal muscle weakness is a common and diagnostically challenging clinical presentation encountered in internal medicine, neurology, and rheumatology. The differential is broad, encompassing primary muscle disorders (myopathies), neuromuscular junction and motor neuron diseases, systemic illnesses with secondary muscle involvement, endocrine and metabolic derangements, drugs and toxins, and critical illness-related muscle dysfunction. Accurate and timely differentiation among these causes is essential because treatment and prognosis vary widely: some etiologies are immunotherapy-responsive inflammatory myopathies, others are genetic metabolic disorders with supportive care and genetic counseling, while drug-induced myopathies require cessation of the offending agent. This chapter presents a structured, pathophysiology-focused approach to the differential diagnosis of progressive proximal weakness. It emphasizes clinical reasoning that integrates history and examination with laboratory testing, electrophysiology, imaging, and muscle biopsy to arrive at a diagnosis.

# Inflammatory myopathies (dermatomyositis, polymyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, inclusion body myositis)

Inflammatory myopathies are immune-mediated muscle disorders characterized by varying degrees of muscle fiber necrosis, inflammatory infiltrates, and—depending on the subtype—distinct clinical, serologic, and histopathologic signatures. Recognizing features that differentiate dermatomyositis (DM), polymyositis (PM), immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), and inclusion body myositis (IBM) is critical because each follows a different course and responds differently to immunotherapy.

Dermatomyositis is an inflammatory myopathy with characteristic skin manifestations and a predilection for microvascular injury. Clinically, DM presents with symmetric, subacute to chronic proximal muscle weakness often accompanied by heliotrope rash, Gottron papules or sign, Shawl sign, and photodistributed poikiloderma. Pathophysiologically, complement-mediated microangiopathy leads to perifascicular atrophy and capillary dropout. Histology commonly shows perivascular and perimysial inflammatory infiltrates rich in B cells and CD4+ T cells, perifascicular fiber atrophy, and myxovirus resistance protein A (MxA) expression. Adults with DM carry an increased risk of internal malignancy, particularly in the first three years after onset; therefore age-appropriate malignancy screening is a component of the workup.

Polymyositis is classically described as a proximal, symmetric, subacute myopathy without the cutaneous features of DM. It is less common than previously thought when strict diagnostic criteria and modern serology and pathology are applied; many cases previously labeled PM are now reclassified as IMNM or overlap myositis. PM is characterized by endomysial inflammatory infiltrates composed predominantly of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells invading non-necrotic muscle fibers that express class I major histocompatibility complex (MHC I) molecules. PM should be a diagnosis of exclusion in adults after other causes (DM, IMNM, IBM, hereditary myopathies) are considered.

Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) presents with marked, often rapidly progressive proximal weakness and prominently elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels. IMNM is histologically dominated by muscle fiber necrosis with relatively sparse inflammatory infiltrate. Two key serologic markers are associated with IMNM: anti–signal recognition particle (anti-SRP) and anti–3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (anti-HMGCR) antibodies. Anti-HMGCR-associated IMNM has been linked to prior statin exposure in some patients, though cases occur without statin exposure. IMNM tends to be more refractory to standard corticosteroid monotherapy and often requires combined immunosuppressive strategies including intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), rituximab, or other steroid-sparing agents.

Inclusion body myositis (IBM) is the most common acquired myopathy in patients over the age of 50 years and has a characteristic clinical phenotype: slowly progressive, often asymmetric weakness with preferential involvement of finger flexors, knee extensors (quadriceps), and proximal limb muscles. Dysphagia is common. IBM is refractory to conventional immunosuppression. Pathology demonstrates a combination of inflammatory and degenerative features: endomysial T-cell infiltrates invading non-necrotic fibers, rimmed vacuoles, protein aggregates with beta-amyloid and phosphorylated tau, and mitochondrial abnormalities such as ragged-red fibers. Clinically, modest elevations of CK (often <10 times the upper limit of normal) are typical; marked CK elevations suggest alternative diagnoses. The combination of clinical pattern (finger flexor/ quadriceps-predominant weakness in an older patient), characteristic biopsy, and poor response to immunotherapy supports the diagnosis.

For inflammatory myopathies, CK is a useful screening and monitoring tool: normal CK range varies by laboratory and sex but is often quoted approximately as 20–200 U/L; IMNM and DM may show very high levels (into the thousands), whereas IBM often shows only modest elevations. Myositis-specific antibodies (MSAs) and myositis-associated antibodies (MAAs) now guide diagnosis and subtyping; examples include anti-Jo-1 and other aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase antibodies (antisynthetase syndrome), anti-Mi-2, anti-TIF1-γ (cancer-associated DM), anti-MDA5 (amyopathic DM with interstitial lung disease), anti-SRP, and anti-HMGCR. Imaging with MRI can show muscle edema indicative of active inflammation; patterns of muscle involvement on MRI can support certain diagnoses and guide biopsy location. Muscle biopsy remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis and for distinguishing between PM, DM, IMNM, and IBM.

# Endocrine causes: hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, Cushing syndrome

Endocrine disorders can produce proximal muscle weakness by distinct mechanisms: metabolic derangement of muscle energy use, structural protein changes, and steroid-mediated catabolism. Recognizing endocrine myopathies is important because many are reversible with appropriate hormonal treatment.

Hypothyroidism leads to a chronic myopathy characterized by weakness, cramps, stiffness, and delayed relaxation of deep tendon reflexes. The pathophysiology involves slowed muscle fiber metabolism, accumulation of mucopolysaccharides within muscle, and reduced mitochondrial oxidative activity. Laboratory findings often include elevated CK levels (which may be markedly increased in some hypothyroid patients), hypercholesterolemia, and typical thyroid function tests of elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) with low free thyroxine (free T4). Reference ranges vary; a typical TSH reference range is approximately 0.4–4.5 mIU/L and free T4 0.8–1.8 ng/dL, but ranges depend on assay. Muscle symptoms often improve over weeks to months after thyroid hormone replacement, although very prolonged hypothyroidism may lead to persistent weakness.

Hyperthyroidism produces a catabolic myopathy with preferential atrophy of type II (fast-twitch) fibers leading to proximal weakness, myalgias, and occasionally myopathy with markedly elevated CK in severe cases. The mechanism is increased protein catabolism and increased metabolic rate leading to muscle wasting. Thyroid function tests show suppressed TSH and elevated free T4 and/or free T3. Management revolves around treating the thyrotoxicosis, which usually reverses the myopathic symptoms.

Endogenous or exogenous excess glucocorticoids—Cushing syndrome or chronic corticosteroid therapy—cause steroid myopathy characterized by predominant proximal muscle weakness with relative preservation of muscle enzymes. The pathophysiology involves glucocorticoid-induced upregulation of ubiquitin–proteasome proteolysis, suppression of protein synthesis, and selective type II fiber atrophy. Serum CK is often normal or only mildly elevated; aldolase may also be normal. Clinical clues include other stigmata of glucocorticoid excess (central obesity, skin thinning, easy bruising, hypertension, glucose intolerance). Management requires reducing glucocorticoid exposure when possible and supportive rehabilitation; recovery may be incomplete if exposure is prolonged.

# Metabolic and inherited myopathies (glycogen storage diseases, mitochondrial myopathies)

Inherited metabolic myopathies present across the lifespan with variable onset patterns—some with exercise intolerance, episodic rhabdomyolysis, or progressive weakness. A strong family history, early age of onset, exercise-provoked symptoms, and specific biochemical clues point toward genetic etiologies.

Glycogen storage diseases (GSDs) affecting muscle include Pompe disease (acid maltase deficiency, GSD II), McArdle disease (myophosphorylase deficiency, GSD V), and others. Pompe disease—caused by deficiency of lysosomal acid alpha-glucosidase—can present as an infantile cardiomyopathic form or late-onset limb-girdle–type myopathy with progressive proximal weakness and respiratory insufficiency from diaphragmatic involvement. Diagnosis is supported by elevated CK (variable), muscle biopsy showing vacuolar myopathy with glycogen accumulation, and confirmatory enzymatic assay or genetic testing for GAA mutations; enzyme replacement therapy is available and effective in many patients, making early recognition critical.

McArdle disease classically presents with exercise intolerance, muscle cramps, and myoglobinuria during strenuous activity. Patients may report the "second wind" phenomenon—improvement in exercise tolerance after a brief rest as muscle perfusion and alternate fuel use improve. CK can be markedly elevated after exercise; forearm exercise testing demonstrates absent rise in lactate with normal ammonia elevation. Diagnosis is by myophosphorylase activity assay or PYGM gene testing.

Mitochondrial myopathies are genetically heterogeneous disorders that impair oxidative phosphorylation. They commonly present with exercise intolerance, fatigue, ptosis, ophthalmoparesis, and myalgias; multisystem involvement—sensorineural hearing loss, cardiomyopathy, diabetes, and neurological deficits—is typical. Muscle biopsy may reveal ragged-red fibers on modified Gomori trichrome stain and cytochrome c oxidase–deficient fibers. Lactate and pyruvate may be elevated at rest or with exercise. Genetic testing may identify mitochondrial DNA or nuclear DNA mutations; because heteroplasmy influences clinical expression, testing strategies depend on clinical suspicion and tissue selection.

Other metabolic myopathies include lipid metabolism disorders (e.g., carnitine palmitoyltransferase II deficiency) that cause recurrent myoglobinuria and weakness precipitated by fasting or prolonged exercise, and defects of purine metabolism that present with episodic weakness. Screening tests include baseline CK, lactate, acylcarnitine profile, and targeted genetic testing. In suspected hereditary diseases, referral to genetics and consideration of disease-specific therapies (such as dietary modification, avoidance of triggers, enzyme replacement) are important clinical actions.

# Drug/toxin-induced myopathies and critical illness myopathy

Numerous medications and toxins cause myopathy by mechanisms that include direct myofiber toxicity, immune-mediated necrosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and steroid effects. Iatrogenic myopathies are often reversible when the causative agent is stopped, but in some cases—especially with prolonged exposure—irreversible damage can occur.

Statin-associated myopathy is commonest among drug-induced myopathies. Clinical manifestations range from mild myalgia with normal CK to myositis with marked CK elevation and rare rhabdomyolysis. Risk factors include higher statin doses, concomitant drugs that inhibit statin metabolism (e.g., CYP3A4 inhibitors), hypothyroidism, and certain genetic predispositions (e.g., SLCO1B1 variants). Anti-HMGCR positive IMNM is a distinct immune-mediated process that can be triggered by statin exposure and typically requires immunotherapy.

Glucocorticoid-induced myopathy was discussed above as an endocrine cause but is also relevant in the context of chronic medication exposure. Prolonged use leads to type II fiber atrophy and proximal weakness, often without CK elevation.

Other agents associated with myopathy include alcohol (toxic myopathy and alcoholic rhabdomyolysis), alcohol withdrawal, colchicine, antiretrovirals (notably zidovudine causing mitochondrial toxicity), amphotericin, chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine (vacuolar myopathy with autophagic vacuoles), and various chemotherapeutic agents. Immune checkpoint inhibitors have been implicated in immune-mediated myositis often with myocarditis, necessitating prompt recognition.

Critical illness myopathy (CIM) and polyneuropathy (CIP) are frequent causes of profound weakness in ICU patients, particularly those who have sepsis, multiorgan failure, prolonged immobilization, or receive high-dose corticosteroids and neuromuscular blocking agents. CIM is characterized by diffuse symmetric limb and respiratory muscle weakness, reduced or absent deep tendon reflexes, and difficulty weaning from ventilatory support. Laboratory features can include low-amplitude compound muscle action potentials on nerve conduction studies, and myopathic changes on electromyography. Muscle biopsy shows muscle fiber atrophy, myosin loss, and sometimes necrosis. Management is supportive: mobilization, glycemic control, minimization of corticosteroids and neuromuscular blockers when feasible, and rehabilitation. Recovery is variable and can be protracted.

When evaluating suspected drug-related myopathy, a careful medication and toxin history is essential, including over-the-counter drugs, supplements, and illicit substances. Temporal association, pattern of CK change, and response to drug withdrawal support causality. In cases with immune-mediated features (e.g., anti-HMGCR), immunosuppressive therapy may be necessary.

# Neuromuscular junction disorders and motor neuron disease considerations

Although classic proximal myopathies primarily affect muscle fibers, disorders of the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) and motor neuron disease can present with proximal weakness and must be differentiated because management differs fundamentally.

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disorder of the NMJ characterized by fluctuating weakness that worsens with exertion and improves with rest. Although ocular involvement (ptosis, diplopia) is common, generalized MG can cause prominent proximal limb weakness that may be mistaken for a myopathy. Pathophysiologically, autoantibodies target the acetylcholine receptor (AChR) or muscle-specific kinase (MuSK), leading to reduced neuromuscular transmission. Diagnostic testing includes serum antibodies (AChR or MuSK), repetitive nerve stimulation showing decremental response, single-fiber EMG demonstrating increased jitter, and edrophonium testing historically (now rarely used). Treatment with acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, immunosuppression, IVIG, plasmapheresis, or thymectomy (in selected AChR-positive patients) contrasts with therapies for inflammatory myopathies.

Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS) is a presynaptic disorder with proximal weakness, autonomic dysfunction, and hyporeflexia; it is classically paraneoplastic, associated with small cell lung cancer. Electrophysiologically, LEMS shows small compound muscle action potentials that increment with high-frequency stimulation or after exercise. Antibodies against presynaptic P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channels support the diagnosis.

Motor neuron disease, particularly amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), can occasionally present with prominent proximal weakness. However, ALS usually combines lower motor neuron signs (atrophy, fasciculations) with upper motor neuron signs (hyperreflexia, spasticity), and shows a pattern spreading across regions rather than isolated symmetric proximal weakness. Electrophysiological studies in ALS demonstrate widespread denervation and reinnervation (fibrillation potentials, positive sharp waves, large amplitude motor unit potentials) in multiple limbs and regions. Preserving sensory function helps distinguish ALS from many myopathies and peripheral neuropathies.

Distinguishing NMJ and motor neuron disorders from primary myopathies relies on recognizing fluctuating vs static weakness, fatigability, distribution (ocular and bulbar involvement common in NMJ disease), presence of fasciculations, reflex changes, sensory symptoms, and electrophysiologic patterns. Serologic tests and targeted imaging for paraneoplastic causes are indicated as clinically relevant.

# Systemic diseases with muscle involvement: connective tissue diseases, paraneoplastic syndromes

Muscle involvement occurs in many systemic diseases, where muscle weakness may be a primary autoimmune myopathy, part of a multisystem inflammatory process, or a paraneoplastic phenomenon. Evaluating for systemic features—skin rash, arthritis, interstitial lung disease, Raynaud phenomenon, constitutional symptoms, and signs of malignancy—guides the diagnostic workup.

Connective tissue diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), systemic sclerosis, mixed connective tissue disease, and overlap syndromes can be associated with inflammatory myositis or myositis as part of the disease spectrum. Myositis in overlap syndromes often carries myositis-associated antibodies (e.g., anti-U1 RNP, anti-PM/Scl) and may present with extramuscular manifestations like interstitial lung disease and esophageal dysmotility. Clinical suspicion should prompt testing for antinuclear antibody (ANA) and directed extractable nuclear antigen panels, and careful assessment for pulmonary involvement.

Paraneoplastic myositis, most notably dermatomyositis, is associated with an increased risk of underlying malignancy, especially in adults over age 50. The type of associated cancer varies with age, sex, and specific myositis antibody profile (for example, anti-TIF1-γ and anti-NXP2 in DM are associated with higher cancer risk). Screening for occult malignancy in appropriate patients often includes age-appropriate cancer screening, imaging (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis), mammography or pelvic imaging in women, colonoscopy, and targeted investigations guided by clinical clues.

Infectious causes can also produce myositis, either as direct invasion (e.g., pyomyositis, trichinosis) or via immune mechanisms (e.g., HIV-associated myopathy). HIV-associated inflammatory myopathy can resemble polymyositis or immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy and requires consideration of antiretroviral status, HIV viral load, and opportunistic infections.

When systemic disease is suspected, a multidisciplinary approach integrating rheumatology, oncology, infectious disease, and neurology is often necessary. Treating the underlying systemic disorder can result in improvement of muscle symptoms, and conversely, absence of systemic features may prompt focused myositis evaluation.

# Diagnostic algorithm including history, exam, labs, EMG, imaging and biopsy

A systematic diagnostic algorithm enhances the accuracy and efficiency of evaluating proximal muscle weakness. The clinician should synthesize the history and physical to direct targeted testing rather than ordering a broad panel indiscriminately.

History should focus on the onset (acute, subacute, chronic), progression (rapidly progressive vs slowly progressive), symmetry (symmetric vs asymmetric), distribution (proximal vs distal, involvement of finger flexors or quadriceps), associated symptoms (dysphagia, respiratory difficulty, ocular symptoms, myalgias, systemic features like rash, fever, weight loss), exercise-related triggers (exercise intolerance, second-wind phenomenon, rhabdomyolysis), medication history (statins, steroids, antimalarials, antiretrovirals, immune checkpoint inhibitors, illicit drugs), family history of muscle disease, and occupational exposures. Temporal relationships (e.g., onset after starting a medication) are especially informative.

Physical examination should document strength using Medical Research Council (MRC) grading, distribution of weakness, presence of bulbar or respiratory muscle involvement, presence of skin findings (heliotrope rash, Gottron papules), reflexes, sensory examination (to help exclude neuropathic causes), presence of fasciculations (suggesting lower motor neuron disease), and signs of systemic illness (cachexia, organomegaly, skin changes). Careful observation for asymmetric weakness, selective muscle groups (e.g., finger flexors in IBM), and the fatigability characteristic of NMJ disorders is essential.

Initial laboratory testing usually includes serum CK, aldolase (less commonly used), thyroid-stimulating hormone and free T4, electrolytes, renal and hepatic function, inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP), and autoimmune serology including ANA and myositis-specific/associated antibody panels (e.g., anti-Jo-1 and other antisynthetase antibodies, anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR, anti-Mi-2, anti-TIF1-γ, anti-MDA5). Typical CK normal ranges vary by lab and sex but are often approximately 30–200 U/L; levels in inflammatory myopathies can range from mildly to markedly elevated (hundreds to thousands U/L). In suspected metabolic myopathy, lactate (resting and post-exercise), ammonia, and acylcarnitine profiles are useful. In suspected paraneoplastic or infectious etiologies, directed serologies and imaging are performed.

Electrophysiology (EMG and nerve conduction studies) is a crucial next step when the diagnosis is unclear. EMG can distinguish myopathic from neuropathic processes and identify neuromuscular junction dysfunction. Classic myopathic EMG features include short-duration, low-amplitude motor unit potentials with early recruitment and spontaneous fibrillations/positive sharp waves in active myositis. In NMJ disorders, repetitive nerve stimulation may show decrement (MG) or incremental response (LEMS), and single-fiber EMG shows increased jitter. Denervation and reinnervation patterns suggest motor neuron disease. Electrophysiology also helps to localize affected muscles to guide biopsy.

MRI of muscle using T1, T2, and STIR sequences can detect muscle edema (active inflammation) and chronic fatty replacement and atrophy. MRI helps select the optimal biopsy site—an area with edema but not end-stage fatty replacement—and documents the pattern of muscle involvement, which can be disease-specific (for example, scapular stabilizers in certain myopathies). Ultrasound is an alternative imaging modality that can identify edema and guide needle biopsy.

If noninvasive testing fails to establish a diagnosis, or when biopsy is required to confirm inflammatory versus noninflammatory myopathy, muscle biopsy is performed. Proper biopsy technique is critical: select an affected muscle that is not end-stage fibrofatty replaced and avoid sampling very weak or atrophic muscles that may yield nonspecific changes. Pathologic evaluation should include routine histology (H&E), modified Gomori trichrome, enzyme histochemistry (ATPase, COX, SDH), immunohistochemistry (MHC I, membrane attack complex), and electron microscopy when indicated. Biopsy findings are interpreted in clinical context: perifascicular atrophy and perivascular inflammation support dermatomyositis; endomysial invasion by CD8+ T cells is seen in polymyositis; prominent necrosis with sparse inflammation favors IMNM; rimmed vacuoles and inclusion bodies indicate IBM; vacuolar changes with autophagosomal markers suggest drug-induced chloroquine myopathy.

Key clinical decision points emerge from combining the above data. For example, a patient with subacute symmetric proximal weakness, markedly elevated CK, positive anti-SRP antibodies, and biopsy showing necrosis with scant inflammation should be treated as IMNM with aggressive immunosuppression. An older patient with asymmetric finger flexor and quadriceps weakness, minimal CK elevation, and biopsy showing rimmed vacuoles is likely to have IBM, where immunosuppression is generally not effective. If NMJ testing demonstrates decremental responses and positive AChR antibodies, start appropriate therapy for MG. If thyroid dysfunction is identified, treating the endocrine disorder is the priority before pursuing invasive testing unless features strongly suggest primary myopathy.

Algorithmic approach in brief: start with targeted history and physical, obtain baseline labs including CK and thyroid tests, review medications, perform serologic testing guided by clinical features, use EMG and imaging to distinguish myopathy from neuropathy/NMJ disease and to select a biopsy site, and reserve muscle biopsy and genetic testing for cases where needed to reach a diagnosis. Throughout, monitor for complications such as respiratory involvement and cardiac involvement (arrhythmia, myocarditis), particularly in inflammatory myopathies and mitochondrial disease; consider cardiology evaluation (ECG, echocardiography, troponin) when indicated.

Conclusion

Progressive proximal muscle weakness requires a comprehensive, systematic approach that integrates clinical pattern recognition with targeted laboratory, electrophysiologic, imaging, and pathologic investigations. Differentiating inflammatory myopathies, endocrine and metabolic causes, drug-induced and critical illness myopathies, neuromuscular junction and motor neuron disorders, and systemic diseases guides appropriate therapy and prognosis. Early identification of treatable conditions—including immune-mediated myopathies, endocrine disorders, and drug-induced causes—can markedly improve outcomes. A collaborative, multidisciplinary approach is often necessary for complex cases, and clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for overlapping and paraneoplastic processes.
