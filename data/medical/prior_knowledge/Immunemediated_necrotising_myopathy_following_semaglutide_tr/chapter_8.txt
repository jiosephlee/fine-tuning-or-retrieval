Introduction

Imaging and electrophysiologic studies are essential tools in the workup of patients with suspected myopathies. They do not replace careful history, examination, and laboratory testing, but they refine diagnostic hypotheses, guide site selection for muscle biopsy, direct additional organ evaluation (for example cardiac or pulmonary involvement), and help distinguish myopathic from neurogenic processes. This chapter reviews the major modalities used in modern practice—magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and ultrasound of skeletal muscle, electrodiagnostic testing (EMG and nerve conduction studies), cardiac imaging for suspected myocarditis, and chest imaging for extramuscular pulmonary disease—and explains how to integrate these results with clinical and laboratory data to reach diagnostic and management decisions.

# MRI of muscle: sequences, findings in inflammatory myopathies, and use in biopsy targeting

Magnetic resonance imaging has become the most informative imaging modality for detailed assessment of skeletal muscle. MRI provides high soft-tissue contrast and allows visualization of both active (reversible) and chronic (irreversible) pathologic changes. Appropriate sequence selection and interpretation are critical.

Sequences and technical considerations
A standard muscle MRI protocol typically includes T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and fat-suppressed or fluid-sensitive sequences such as short tau inversion recovery (STIR) or T2 with fat suppression. T1-weighted sequences are best for assessing chronic structural change and fat infiltration; fatty replacement appears hyperintense on T1. T2-weighted fat-suppressed or STIR sequences highlight increased free water content and are sensitive for edema and active inflammation. Gadolinium-enhanced T1 sequences with fat suppression can detect areas of hyperemia and breakdown of the blood–muscle barrier and can improve detection of active inflammation or abscess. Diffusion-weighted imaging is investigated in research settings but is not yet routine for most myopathy evaluations. Imaging should include symmetry assessment and coverage of proximal and distal muscle groups relevant to the clinical pattern; bilateral imaging is often performed, since asymmetric involvement can be diagnostically informative.

Findings in inflammatory myopathies
Inflammatory myopathies (polymyositis, dermatomyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, and inclusion body myositis) show characteristic MRI patterns, though there is overlap. Active inflammatory changes manifest as hyperintense signal on T2/STIR images consistent with muscle edema; this tends to involve fascial planes and perifascial tissues in dermatomyositis, whereas polymyositis and immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy may produce more diffuse intramuscular edema. Edema is often patchy; its distribution—proximal dominant, symmetric versus asymmetric, distal or selective muscle involvement—helps narrow differential diagnosis. Chronic changes are signaled by increased T1 signal consistent with fatty replacement and muscle atrophy. Inflammatory myopathies may also show gadolinium enhancement of involved muscle reflecting hyperemia and increased vascular permeability.

Pattern recognition has diagnostic value. Dermatomyositis often displays subcutaneous and fascial edema and selective involvement of flexor or extensor compartments depending on the muscle group; muscle edema can be perivascular and patchy. Inclusion body myositis (IBM) classically shows selective involvement and atrophy of the finger flexors and quadriceps, with relative sparing of other proximal muscles and a combination of fatty replacement and mild edema. Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy commonly produces marked edema acutely with rapid progression to fatty replacement if chronic. Vasculitic or infectious myositis may display more focal abscess-like collections or diffuse edema with associated fascial enhancement.

Quantitative assessment and longitudinal change
MRI can be used semi-quantitatively (scoring systems for edema and fatty infiltration) and increasingly by direct quantification using Dixon sequences that separate fat and water and by T2 mapping to measure muscle water content. These approaches allow objective monitoring of disease progression or response to therapy. For example, increased T2 relaxation times correlate with edema and active inflammation; increased fat fraction on Dixon sequences corresponds to irreversible muscle loss.

Use of MRI to guide biopsy
One of the most practical and evidence-based uses of MRI in myopathy evaluation is biopsy targeting. Because inflammatory or necrotic foci may be patchy, blind biopsy of a weakened muscle can yield false-negative results. MRI identifies areas of active inflammation (T2/STIR hyperintensity, gadolinium enhancement) that are more likely to provide diagnostic tissue. The ideal biopsy target is a muscle showing T2/STIR hyperintensity with relatively preserved bulk (avoiding end-stage fatty replacement visible on T1) and that is safely accessible. Bilateral planning may be needed if one side has been pretreated or is not available. Radiology reports should describe both the lesion location and depth relative to skin and neurovascular structures to facilitate surgical planning.

Clinical decision points
Imaging should be obtained early when there is diagnostic uncertainty, prior to steroid or immunosuppressive therapy if possible (as treatment can reduce edema and mask active inflammation). A practical approach is to perform MRI when CK is elevated with proximal weakness, when EMG is equivocal, or when biopsy is being planned. Repeat MRI may be useful for monitoring therapy or when clinical course changes.

# Ultrasound of muscle: utility and characteristic findings

Muscle ultrasound is a rapid, bedside, noninvasive tool that complements MRI. Its portability and lower cost make it appealing for screening, follow-up, and guiding injections; however, it is operator-dependent and has lower tissue contrast than MRI.

Technique and sequences
Muscle ultrasound uses high-frequency linear transducers (7–18 MHz) to evaluate muscle architecture, echogenicity, and dynamic behavior. Examination typically compares symptomatic and contralateral muscles, assesses fascial planes, tendons, and surrounding soft tissues, and may include Doppler imaging for vascularity. Elastography techniques (shear-wave or strain) can be used experimentally to assess stiffness.

Characteristic findings
Normal muscle has a characteristic appearance with hypoechoic muscle bundles separated by hyperechoic perimysial and epimysial connective tissue, producing a “starry” or “feathery” pattern. Pathologic changes include increased intramuscular echogenicity, loss of normal architecture, muscle atrophy, and focal fluid collections. Inflammatory myopathies commonly demonstrate increased muscle echogenicity (reflecting edema and later fibrosis/fatty infiltration) and increased thickness of fascia; acute inflammation may show hypoechogenicity due to edema, whereas chronic fatty infiltration typically increases echogenicity. Doppler signal can be increased in active inflammatory myositis, reflecting hyperemia. In IBM, ultrasound may show selective muscle atrophy and increased echogenicity of quadriceps or flexor digitorum profundus. Ultrasound is particularly sensitive for detecting focal lesions such as intramuscular fluid collections, abscesses, hematomas, and guiding needle placement for biopsy or injections.

Advantages and limitations
Ultrasound allows dynamic examination and real-time guidance for procedures, and is valuable in pediatric or critically ill patients where MRI is difficult. It lacks the comprehensive field of view and deep-tissue contrast of MRI, and inter-operator variability limits standardization. For biopsy targeting, ultrasound can help locate focal lesions and avoid vessels but is less reliable than MRI for delineating patchy edema within deep muscles.

# Electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies: myopathic vs neuropathic patterns, irritability and fibrillation potentials

Electrodiagnostic testing is a cornerstone in differentiating primary muscle disease from neurogenic disorders and motor neuron disease. EMG assesses the electrical activity of muscles at rest and during contraction, while nerve conduction studies (NCS) evaluate peripheral nerve function.

Nerve conduction studies
NCS measure the speed and amplitude of responses in motor and sensory nerves. In pure myopathies, motor and sensory conduction velocities and distal latencies are typically normal because the peripheral nerve is intact. Motor response amplitudes can be normal or mildly reduced if there is substantial muscle fiber loss; importantly, the compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitude reduction in myopathy is usually out of proportion to slowing or conduction block. In neuropathic processes, reduced amplitudes with slowed conduction velocities or conduction block (for demyelinating neuropathies) and abnormal sensory studies help localize the lesion to the nerve.

Needle EMG: motor unit potentials and recruitment
Needle EMG examines spontaneous activity at rest and motor unit potential (MUP) morphology and recruitment during voluntary contraction. Myopathic patterns are characterized by MUPs of short duration and low amplitude, often with increased polyphasia, and early recruitment—meaning that many small MUPs are activated at relatively low force because fewer muscle fibers contribute to each motor unit. In contrast, neurogenic disorders show long-duration, high-amplitude MUPs reflecting collateral reinnervation, reduced recruitment (fewer motor units available), and often complex repetitive discharges in chronic denervation-reinnervation.

Spontaneous activity and irritability
The presence of spontaneous activity at rest—fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves—indicates muscle membrane irritability and is commonly seen in active inflammatory myopathies, toxic myopathies, and denervation. Fibrillations and positive sharp waves are evidence of active muscle fiber necrosis, inflammation, or recent denervation. Myotonic discharges (waxing and waning high-frequency discharges) indicate membrane hyperexcitability and are classic in myotonic dystrophy and myotonia congenita. Complex repetitive discharges and myokymic discharges may also be seen in specific disorders. It is important to recognize that spontaneous activity can occur in both primary myopathies and acute denervation; correlation with clinical and imaging data is therefore essential.

Utility and caveats
EMG is highly sensitive for detecting muscle disease but not fully specific. A myopathic EMG pattern supports a primary muscle disorder and helps localize which muscles are most affected, thereby guiding biopsy selection. However, EMG may be normal in early or very focal myopathies, in pure metabolic myopathies (when performed between symptomatic episodes), or if muscles selected for testing are already end-stage fibrotic and thus electrically inactive. Conversely, chronic myopathy with fatty replacement can produce neurogenic-like EMG features due to secondary denervation. Thus, EMG findings must be interpreted in the context of CK levels, imaging, and clinical pattern.

Key diagnostic points and criteria
There are no absolute numeric cutoffs analogous to lab reference ranges for EMG, but typical descriptions guide interpretation: MUP duration less than approximately 6 ms and amplitude less than 4 mV suggest myopathic change, while durations greater than 14 ms and amplitudes over 10 mV suggest neurogenic change; these are approximate and dependent on muscle and technique. The detection of fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves indicates active muscle membrane instability. When EMG suggests a myopathic process, the muscles chosen for biopsy should be those with abnormal EMG findings but not so severely replaced by fat that diagnostic histology is lost; combining EMG with MRI yields higher diagnostic yield.

# Cardiac imaging in suspected myocarditis: echocardiography and cardiac MRI

Cardiac involvement is an important extramuscular manifestation of several myopathies and systemic inflammatory myopathies and can be life-threatening. Imaging plays a central role in detecting myocarditis, assessing function, and guiding management.

Echocardiography
Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is usually the first-line cardiac imaging study. It evaluates left and right ventricular size and systolic function (ejection fraction), regional wall motion abnormalities, chamber dimensions, valvular function, pericardial effusion, and diastolic parameters. In myocarditis, echocardiography findings are variable and may be normal early. Echocardiography can show global or regional left ventricular systolic dysfunction, increased wall thickness due to edema, pericardial effusion, or regional wall motion abnormalities that do not conform to coronary distribution. Strain imaging (speckle-tracking global longitudinal strain) increases sensitivity for subtle systolic dysfunction and may detect subclinical involvement before dropout of ejection fraction occurs. Serial echocardiography is useful for monitoring ventricular function in patients receiving cardiotoxic therapies or immunosuppression.

Cardiac MRI (cMRI)
Cardiac MRI is the most informative noninvasive test for myocardial inflammation and necrosis. The updated Lake Louise Criteria integrate T2-based markers of myocardial edema and T1-based markers of myocardial injury/fibrosis. According to the revised criteria, acute myocarditis is supported when there is at least one T2-based criterion (regional or global increased myocardial T2 relaxation time or T2 signal intensity) indicating edema and at least one T1-based criterion (increased native T1, increased extracellular volume fraction, or nonischemic late gadolinium enhancement [LGE]) indicating myocardial injury. LGE in myocarditis typically shows a nonischemic pattern: mid-wall or epicardial enhancement sparing the subendocardium, often in the lateral wall. T1 and T2 mapping provide quantitative measures: native T1 and T2 relaxation times are elevated in acute inflammation, and extracellular volume fraction (ECV) is increased with expansion of the interstitial space. Exact numerical cutoffs vary by scanner, pulse sequence, and institution; typical native T1 values might range from 950–1050 ms at 1.5T, with higher values suggesting pathology, and T2 values above institution-specific references suggest edema. When suspected myocarditis occurs in the setting of a myopathy, cMRI also assesses ventricular volumes, ejection fraction, and can detect concomitant pericarditis.

Clinical application and decision points
Echocardiography is widely available and appropriate for initial evaluation, particularly to assess hemodynamics and screen for gross dysfunction. Cardiac MRI is indicated when myocarditis is suspected based on symptoms, biomarkers (troponin elevation), ECG changes, or in systemic inflammatory disease when cardiac involvement is a possibility. cMRI findings influence treatment decisions (e.g., immunosuppression) and prognosis; the extent of LGE correlates with adverse outcomes. Endomyocardial biopsy remains the reference standard for histologic confirmation but is invasive and reserved for cases where biopsy-driven specific therapy is needed or when alternate diagnoses (e.g., giant cell myocarditis) are considered.

# Chest imaging for extramuscular involvement (interstitial lung disease) and CT criteria

Pulmonary involvement, particularly interstitial lung disease (ILD), is an important extramuscular manifestation of several inflammatory myopathies (for example antisynthetase syndrome and dermatomyositis) and affects prognosis and therapy. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest is the imaging modality of choice.

HRCT technique and indications
HRCT uses thin-section (typically 1–1.5 mm) slices reconstructed at high spatial frequency, often with inspiratory and expiratory phases, and sometimes with prone imaging to distinguish dependent atelectasis from true disease. HRCT should be performed when patients have respiratory symptoms (dyspnea, cough), abnormal pulmonary function tests (restrictive pattern or decreased diffusion capacity for carbon monoxide), or positive myositis-specific autoantibodies associated with ILD (for example anti-Jo-1, anti-PL-7, anti-PL-12). Baseline HRCT is often obtained in patients with antisynthetase or other high-risk serologies, even if asymptomatic, as radiographic ILD can be subclinical.

CT patterns and differential diagnosis
HRCT patterns help characterize the type and stage of ILD and guide prognosis. Common patterns include nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP), and organizing pneumonia (OP). NSIP, the most frequent pattern in connective tissue disease–associated ILD, shows ground-glass opacities with a basal and peripheral predominance, fine reticular markings, and volume loss; honeycombing is less frequent than in UIP. UIP, characterized by subpleural basal-predominant reticulation, traction bronchiectasis, and honeycombing, portends worse prognosis and resembles idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Organizing pneumonia appears as patchy bilateral consolidations, often peribronchovascular and subpleural, and typically responds to steroids. Other features to report include extent (percent involvement), presence of traction bronchiectasis, emphysema, pleural effusion, and lymphadenopathy. Serial HRCTs assess progression or response to therapy, but radiation exposure must be weighed.

CT criteria and quantification
While no single numeric cutoff defines ILD on HRCT, radiologists commonly estimate the extent of involvement (for example, <10%, 10–30%, >30% lung involvement), which correlates with pulmonary function. Pulmonary function tests are complementary; a restrictive pattern (reduced total lung capacity) and a reduced diffusion capacity of carbon monoxide (DLCO <80% predicted; severe impairment <50%) often accompany ILD. Radiologic features suggesting active inflammation (ground-glass opacities) may predict steroid responsiveness, while extensive fibrosis and honeycombing suggest irreversible disease.

Clinical decision points
In a patient with suspected inflammatory myopathy, presence of ILD or high-risk serologies should prompt early pulmonary evaluation with PFTs and HRCT. Findings of active inflammatory change (ground-glass) favor immunosuppressive therapy, whereas fibrosis-oriented changes may require antifibrotic consideration and have implications for prognosis and referral for lung transplant evaluation in severe cases.

# Integration of imaging and electrophysiology with clinical and laboratory data

Effective diagnosis and management of myopathies require synthesis of clinical history, physical exam, laboratory studies (including CK and myositis-specific autoantibodies), imaging, and electrodiagnostics. Each modality addresses different aspects of disease and has strengths and limitations; diagnostic accuracy improves when data are integrated.

Clinical context and laboratory correlation
History and physical exam determine the pattern of weakness (proximal, distal, scapular, ocular), onset (acute, subacute, chronic), presence of systemic features (rash, fever, arthralgia, respiratory or cardiac symptoms), and family history suggesting hereditary myopathies. Serum CK provides a biochemical measure of muscle injury; normal ranges commonly lie below 200 U/L (laboratory-dependent), with mild elevation defined as 2–3 times upper limit of normal and severe elevations often in the thousands. Highly elevated CK suggests necrotic or inflammatory myopathy but can be normal in disorders such as IBM or some metabolic myopathies. Myositis-specific and myositis-associated autoantibodies (for example anti-Jo-1, anti-SRP, anti-Mi-2, anti-TIF1-γ, anti-MDA5) refine diagnosis, predict extramuscular involvement (e.g., antisynthetase antibodies with ILD), and inform prognosis.

Sequential and complementary testing
Electrodiagnostic testing clarifies whether weakness is myopathic or neurogenic and helps localize affected muscles for biopsy. MRI refines the anatomical extent of involvement, highlights active inflammatory regions amenable to biopsy, and screens for patterns suggestive of specific diagnoses (for example quadriceps and finger flexor involvement in IBM). Ultrasound offers bedside assessment or dynamic evaluation and can guide percutaneous procedures. If cardiac symptoms or elevated troponin are present, echocardiography provides an initial assessment and cMRI confirms myocarditis and quantifies myocardial injury. Suspicion for ILD or positive high-risk autoantibodies warrants PFTs and HRCT. The timing and sequence of tests should be driven by the clinical urgency (for example, rapidly progressive weakness or respiratory compromise mandates expedited imaging and possibly empiric therapy).

Interpretive examples
A patient with subacute symmetric proximal weakness, markedly elevated CK, and MRI showing diffuse muscle edema on STIR sequences is likely to have an active inflammatory myopathy; EMG showing fibrillation potentials and short-duration, low-amplitude MUPs supports a myopathic process, and targeted biopsy of an MRI-active, not yet fat-replaced muscle will likely return diagnostic histopathology. Conversely, a slowly progressive asymmetric pattern with selective quadriceps atrophy on MRI, mild CK elevation, EMG showing chronic myopathic changes and low-grade spontaneous activity, and absence of systemic inflammation may suggest IBM, in which immunosuppression is often ineffective.

Decision points for biopsy and therapy
Imaging and EMG guide biopsy selection to maximize diagnostic yield. Biopsy should sample an affected muscle with active inflammation on imaging and electrical activity on EMG, but not an end-stage fat-replaced muscle on T1. If cardiac or pulmonary involvement is suspected based on symptoms or serology, prioritize appropriate cardiac or chest imaging early, as these manifestations often dictate urgency and therapy (for example severe ILD or myocarditis may require prompt immunosuppression or ICU care). Where diagnostic uncertainty remains, multidisciplinary discussion among neurologists, rheumatologists, radiologists, and pathologists improves test selection and interpretation.

Monitoring and prognosis
Serial imaging (MRI, HRCT, cMRI) and functional testing (PFTs, echocardiography) track disease progression and response to therapy. MRI reductions in T2 signal and decreases in fat-free fraction may indicate therapeutic response, whereas increasing fat fraction or progressive fibrosis on HRCT indicates irreversible damage. EMG may show decreased spontaneous activity as inflammation responds to treatment, but recovery of normal MUP morphology lags behind clinical improvement.

Conclusion

Imaging and electrophysiologic modalities provide complementary, high-yield information in the evaluation of myopathies. MRI is the most informative modality for muscular structural and inflammatory assessment and optimizes biopsy targeting; ultrasound is a practical bedside tool for screening and procedural guidance. Electrodiagnostic studies differentiate myopathic from neurogenic processes and identify electrically active disease. Cardiac MRI and echocardiography detect myocardial involvement, while HRCT evaluates pulmonary complications. Integration of these modalities with clinical assessment and laboratory testing—including CK, troponin, and autoantibodies—allows tailored diagnostic pathways, more accurate prognostication, and informed therapeutic decision-making.
