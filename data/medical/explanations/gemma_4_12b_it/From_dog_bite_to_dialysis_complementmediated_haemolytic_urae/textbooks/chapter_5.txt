Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 5: ## Therapeutic Management and Clinical Dilemmas

The management of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) requires a rapid transition from general supportive care to specific, targeted immunomodulation. In the early stages of this patient’s presentation, the clinical priority was the management of septic shock and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). This involved aggressive fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin/tazobactam), and blood product support. However, as the clinical picture evolved to include persistent microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and anuria despite resolving sepsis, the diagnostic focus shifted toward thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Once the diagnosis of CM-HUS was suspected, the therapeutic goal moved toward inhibiting the dysregulated alternative complement pathway.

In cases of suspected TMA, plasma exchange (PEX) is often initiated as a "bridging therapy" while awaiting definitive results for ADAMTS13 activity or specialized complement assays. While PEX is the cornerstone of treatment for thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), its role in CM-HUS is secondary; it works by removing circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators, providing transient stabilization rather than correcting the underlying complement dysfunction. In this case, PEX provided a period of clinical stabilization, evidenced by the return of spontaneous diuresis, but it was the subsequent initiation of eculizumab that facilitated sustained haematological remission and renal recovery.

Eculizumab is the standard of care for CM-HUS, functioning as a monoclonal antibody that binds to and inhibits terminal complement component 5 (C5). By blocking C5, eculizumab prevents the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC), thereby halting the complement-mediated destruction of the vascular endothelium. For patients with CM-HUS, the timing of eculizumab initiation is the primary determinant of prognosis. Early intervention is critical because irreversible renal damage, such as severe acute tubular necrosis and glomerular scarring, can occur rapidly. The patient in this report achieved partial renal recovery because the blockade was initiated while there was still viable tissue to salvage, highlighting the importance of a low threshold for starting complement inhibition in the setting of TMA.

A significant clinical dilemma arises when managing CM-HUS in the context of active infection, as seen with this *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia. Because terminal complement components are essential for the host's defense against encapsulated bacteria (such as *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*), administering eculizumab can theoretically increase the risk of life-threatening infections. To navigate this, clinicians must balance the risk of end-organ failure from TMA against the risk of overwhelming sepsis. In this case, the team minimized this risk by delaying eculizumab induction until the blood cultures were negative, ensuring the primary infection was controlled before neutralizing the complement system.

To further mitigate the risk of opportunistic infection during complement blockade, rigorous prophylactic measures are mandatory. This patient received meningococcal vaccinations (Menveo and Bexsero) and a course of prophylactic antibiotics (Apocillin). These measures are standard protocols to protect the patient from encapsulated pathogens while their primary immune defense is suppressed. This case underscores that while CM-HUS treatment is highly effective, it requires a sophisticated, multi-layered approach that integrates rapid diagnostic reasoning, precise timing of targeted therapy, and proactive measures to manage the inherent risks of immunosuppression.
