Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 1: ## The Zoonotic Trigger: Capnocytophaga canimorsus

The clinical journey of this patient began with a common domestic incident—a dog bite—which served as the portal of entry for *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. To understand the patient’s rapid progression from a localized injury to systemic collapse, it is necessary to understand the unique biology of this pathogen. *C. canimorsus* is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod that typically resides as part of the normal oral flora in dogs and cats. While it is generally commensal in these animals, it can become an opportunistic and highly aggressive pathogen in humans. Human infection is relatively rare but is well-documented following bites, scratches, or even close physical contact with infected animals.

The clinical spectrum of *C. canimorsus* infection in humans is remarkably broad, which can complicate early diagnosis. While some patients may present with relatively isolated symptoms such as meningitis, others experience fulminant sepsis. The patient in this case experienced the latter extreme: a rapid progression toward septic shock, characterized by high fever, malaise, and systemic inflammation. In severe cases, the pathogen can trigger multi-organ failure and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). The patient’s initial presentation—including mottled extremities, lactic acidosis, and severe coagulopathy—is a classic manifestation of the profound systemic inflammatory response and microvascular compromise induced by *C. canimorsus* bacteraemia.

The pathophysiology of *C. canimorsus*-induced sepsis involves a combination of direct tissue invasion and a systemic host response. The bacteria can trigger a cascade of inflammatory mediators that lead to the activation of the coagulation system. This explains the patient's early development of DIC, where widespread activation of coagulation leads to the formation of microthrombi, contributing to her initial organ dysfunction and mottled skin changes. It is also important to note that while *C. canimorsus* can cause severe systemic illness, its mortality rate can approach 30%, underscoring the need for aggressive early intervention with broad-spectrum antibiotics.

A significant challenge in managing this infection is the laboratory diagnosis. *C. canimorsus* is a fastidious organism, meaning it has complex nutritional requirements and grows very slowly in standard culture media. This can lead to significant delays in identification, often allowing the patient's condition to deteriorate before the specific pathogen is confirmed. In this case, the patient was initially treated with broad-spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam to cover suspected dog-associated pathogens while awaiting cultures. The eventual confirmation of *C. canimorsus* seven days after admission highlights the necessity of maintaining a high index of suspicion for zoonotic pathogens in patients with rapid sepsis following animal contact, and emphasizes the growing role of molecular methods, such as real-time nanopore sequencing, in overcoming the limitations of traditional culture.
