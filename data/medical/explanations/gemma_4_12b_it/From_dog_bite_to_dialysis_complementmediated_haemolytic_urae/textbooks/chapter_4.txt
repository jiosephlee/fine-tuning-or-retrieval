Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 4: ## Pathophysiology of Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS)

Complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), historically referred to as atypical HUS (aHUS), is a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) characterized by the systemic overactivation of the alternative complement pathway (ACP). Unlike Shiga toxin-associated HUS (STEC-HUS), which is driven by bacterial toxins, or thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which is caused by a protease deficiency (ADAMTS13), CM-HUS is fundamentally a disorder of complement regulation. In the clinical context of this case, the patient presented with the hallmarks of TMA—microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI)—following a *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection, which served as the catalyst for this systemic dysregulation.

The alternative complement pathway is designed to provide a rapid, "always-on" surveillance system to identify and neutralize pathogens. Under normal physiological conditions, the ACP maintains a low level of continuous activity, known as "tick-over," where C3 is spontaneously hydrolyzed. This process is strictly governed by a suite of regulatory proteins that prevent the pathway from attacking host cells. These regulators include Factor H (the primary fluid-phase regulator), Factor I, Factor B, and membrane cofactor protein (CD46). When these regulatory "brakes" are defective—either due to genetic mutations or acquired factors—the C3 convertase enzyme remains active, leading to the continuous cleavage of C3 and the subsequent formation of the C5 convertase. This results in the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9) on host endothelial cells, particularly in the renal microvasculature, leading to inflammation, thrombosis, and tissue damage.

The development of CM-HUS often follows the "second hit" theory of disease. Many individuals may carry subclinical genetic variants or predispositions in complement regulatory genes (such as Factor H) that do not manifest as disease in daily life. However, an external physiological stressor—the "second hit"—can push the system beyond its compensatory capacity. In this case, the *C. canimorsus* bacteraemia acted as the trigger. The immune system's attempt to clear the encapsulated bacteria activated the complement system; in a host with insufficient regulatory capacity, this activation became uncontrolled, transitioning from a protective defense to a destructive systemic process that caused the patient's rapid progression to anuria and TMA.

This creates a significant clinical paradox: the complement system is essential for host defense against pathogens like *C. canimorsus*, yet its dysregulation is the direct cause of the patient’s multi-organ failure. This explains why the patient’s clinical course was so complex; while antibiotics successfully addressed the primary infection, the "fire" of complement activation continued to damage the endothelium. Furthermore, CM-HUS is a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion. Because the alternative pathway is independent of the classical pathway, standard laboratory tests for C3 and C4 levels are often normal during an acute episode, as seen in this patient. This necessitates a high index of suspicion for CM-HUS when a patient presents with TMA and fails to respond to standard treatments for sepsis or other TMA-inducing conditions.

Distinguishing between hereditary complement deficiencies and infection-triggered CM-HUS is vital for long-term management. Hereditary forms typically involve high-penetrance mutations and often manifest earlier in life. However, as demonstrated in this case, CM-HUS can occur in immunocompetent adults without detectable pathogenic genetic variants, suggesting that triggers can occasionally overwhelm even a "normal" complement system. The management principle highlighted here is that early identification of this pathway dysregulation—evidenced by the rapid response to eculizumab, a C5 inhibitor—is the most critical factor in preventing irreversible renal damage and achieving haematological remission.
