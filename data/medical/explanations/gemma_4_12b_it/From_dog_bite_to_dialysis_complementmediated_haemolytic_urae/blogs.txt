Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# When Sepsis Masks a TMA: Recognizing the Clinical Pivot

In the "fog of war" that is a patient in septic shock, the clinical picture is often so loud that it drowns out the subtler signals of underlying pathology. When you are managing a patient with profound metabolic acidosis, refractory hypoglycemia, and a rapidly failing multi-organ system, your primary focus is almost always the "big" answers: Sepsis, DIC, and maybe necrotizing fasciitis. 

But what happens when the sepsis begins to stabilize, the antibiotics are working, and the inflammatory markers start to trend down—yet the patient's hematological status continues to plummet? This is the "clinical pivot," and missing it can be the difference between a recovery and a dialysis-dependent outcome.

## The Noise of Sepsis and the DIC Trap

In the case we are looking at today, a healthy woman in her 40s presented with a classic, albeit severe, presentation of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis following a dog bite. She was a "textbook" mess: high fever, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), and a coagulopathy consistent with Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC).

In this early phase, diagnosing a Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) is notoriously difficult. DIC and TMA share many overlapping features—thrombocytopenia, AKI, and microvascular thrombosis. In a septic patient, the "noise" of DIC (systemic consumption of clotting factors) makes it incredibly hard to see the "signal" of TMA (a specific microvascular "traffic jam"). 

The team correctly identified the infection, started appropriate broad-spectrum antibiotics, and initiated supportive care. For the first week, the diagnosis of sepsis/DIC was the most logical and necessary focus.

## The Pivot: When the Signal Breaks Through

The clinical pivot occurred roughly 8 days into her stay. The patient was becoming clinically stable, and her inflammatory profile was improving. However, she suddenly developed a dramatic drop in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L) without any evidence of active bleeding. 

This is the moment where the "sepsis" story no longer explains the "hemolysis" story. 

When you see isolated, progressive hemolysis (low hemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH) and persistent thrombocytopenia in a patient whose infection is being managed, you must pivot your thinking. You are no longer looking at a complication of sepsis; you are looking at a secondary, potentially life-threatening pathology. 

## Decoding the TMA

Once the team identified Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anaemia (MAHA) via a peripheral smear showing 15–20% schistocytes, the differential diagnosis for TMA narrowed significantly. In any adult with MAHA, the first priority is to rule out Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP). 

By checking the ADAMTS13 activity (which was 43.5%), the team could effectively rule out TTP (which typically requires activity <10%). They also ruled out Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC-HUS) via PCR. 

This left a primary suspicion of Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS). It is a fascinating clinical paradox: the *C. canimorsus* infection served as the "second hit" that triggered the alternative complement pathway. While the complement system is essential for clearing the bacteria, in this patient, it triggered an uncontrolled, self-sustaining loop of endothelial damage.

## The Treatment Dilemma: Complement Blockade in Sepsis

One of the most challenging aspects of this case was the timing of eculizumab. Eculizumab is the gold standard for CM-HUS, but it carries a theoretical risk of exacerbating infections by inhibiting the terminal complement components necessary to fight encapsulated bacteria.

The team navigated this by waiting until the blood cultures were negative before initiating eculizumab, while using Plasma Exchange (PEX) as a "bridge." It is important to note that while PEX is the mainstay of TTP treatment, it only provides a transient benefit in CM-HUS by temporarily removing circulating complement fragments. The real "cure" in CM-HUS is the blockade of the pathway itself.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Listen for the Pivot:** If a patient’s primary infection stabilizes but their hematological parameters (Hb, platelets, LDH) continue to deteriorate, stop looking at the sepsis and start looking for a secondary TMA.
*   **DIC vs. TMA:** In the early stages of sepsis, they look nearly identical. Don't let the DIC "noise" prevent you from recognizing a TMA "signal" that may be complicating the clinical course.
*   **Rule Out the "Big Two":** For any new-onset MAHA, your immediate workup should include ADAMTS13 activity (to rule out TTP) and Shiga toxin testing (to rule out STEC-HUS).
*   **Complement Levels Can Be Deceptive:** In CM-HUS, standard C3 and C4 levels can be completely normal. Do not let a normal C3/C4 level rule out a complement-mediated process.
*   **Timing is Everything:** For CM-HUS, the earlier the initiation of eculizumab, the better the renal recovery. PEX is a bridge, not a destination.



# Beyond the Bite: Capnocytophaga canimorsus as a Fulminant Systemic Trigger

Most of us view a dog bite as a routine clinical encounter—perhaps a few stitches, a tetanus shot, and a course of prophylactic antibiotics. However, for a small subset of patients, a simple nip from a pet can trigger a catastrophic systemic collapse. *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a fastidious gram-negative rod commonly found in the oral flora of dogs and cats, is a "wolf in sheep’s clothing" that can transition from a minor zoonotic risk to fulminant sepsis, DIC, and even life-threatening thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

## The Case: A Hidden Pathology

Consider a previously healthy woman in her 40s who presented with rapid progression following a dog bite. She didn't just have a wound; she arrived in the ED with high fever, mottled extremities, metabolic acidosis, and profound pancytopenia. The initial clinical picture was one of textbook sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). She was started on broad-spectrum coverage (Piperacillin/Tazobactam) and moved to the ICU for management of what appeared to be multi-organ failure.

Here is where the case takes a fascinating turn. As the initial septic shock began to stabilize with antibiotics and supportive care, a new pathology emerged. While her inflammatory markers were improving, she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia. This wasn't just "lingering sepsis"—it was the hallmark of a secondary complication: microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA).

## The Diagnostic Pivot: Identifying the TMA

When a patient shows signs of MAHA (schistocytes on smear, low platelets, and AKI), the clinical priority is to differentiate between the "Big Three" of TMA: Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP), Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC-HUS), and Complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS).

In this case, the team efficiently narrowed the differential. ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5% (ruling out TTP, which typically requires <10%), and a negative stool PCR for Shiga toxin moved the needle toward CM-HUS. However, CM-HUS is a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion. Because C3 and C4 levels can remain within normal limits during the disease course, they cannot be used to rule out complement-mediated disease.

The confirmation came via kidney biopsy, which showed ischemic glomeruli and fibrin thrombi, and the dramatic clinical response to eculizumab—a monoclonal antibody targeting the terminal complement component C5.

## The Complement Paradox

Why did a dog bite lead to complement-mediated HUS? This highlights a cruel clinical paradox. *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated bacterium. To clear it, the host's immune system relies heavily on the complement system. However, in certain individuals—even those without a clear genetic "smoking gun"—the activation of the alternative complement pathway can become dysregulated. 

Instead of just clearing the bacteria, the complement system begins to cause "friendly fire," leading to uncontrolled endothelial damage and the formation of microthrombi. This case is particularly striking because the patient lacked a known hereditary complement defect, suggesting that the infection served as the primary "second hit" required to trigger the pathology in an otherwise immunocompetent host.

## Clinical Management and the Eculizumab Dilemma

Managing CM-HUS in the setting of active bacteraemia presents a significant therapeutic tightrope. Eculizumab is the gold standard for CM-HUS, but because it inhibits the terminal complement components necessary to fight encapsulated organisms (like *Neisseria* or *Capnocytophaga*), there is a theoretical risk of worsening the underlying infection.

In this patient, the team navigated this by initiating plasma exchange (PEX) as a bridging therapy to stabilize the patient before starting eculizumab. Once the blood cultures were negative, they proceeded with complement blockade. The result was a rapid haematological remission and a return of spontaneous diuresis.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Look beyond the initial insult:** In cases of severe sepsis or DIC, always remain vigilant for the development of superimposed TMA. A patient who "improves" from sepsis but develops new-onset hemolysis or persistent thrombocytopenia needs a close look at the peripheral smear.
*   **Complement levels are not definitive:** Normal C3 and C4 levels do **not** rule out Complement-mediated HUS. The diagnosis is primarily clinical and supported by the response to treatment and biopsy findings.
*   **Rule out TTP early:** Obtain ADAMTS13 activity as soon as possible; a result >10% significantly narrows the differential toward STEC-HUS or CM-HUS.
*   **The "Fastidious" Pathogen:** *C. canimorsus* is slow-growing and can be difficult to culture. If you suspect it based on a pet bite and clinical severity, early molecular methods (like nanopore sequencing) can be invaluable for rapid identification.
*   **Eculizumab Timing:** While eculizumab is highly effective, try to initiate it once the primary infectious source is controlled to minimize the risk of overwhelming infection, unless the patient is rapidly deteriorating and requires immediate blockade.



# Navigating the TMA Maze: TTP, STEC-HUS, and the Diagnosis of Exclusion

A dog bite is usually a trip to the ER for a few stitches and a tetanus shot. It rarely ends with a patient on dialysis for a rare, complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Yet, this is exactly what happened to a previously healthy woman in her 40s. After a *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection triggered a massive septic response and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), she experienced a sudden, isolated "second hit": profound hemolysis and worsening renal failure. 

This case perfectly illustrates the diagnostic "maze" we face when a patient presents with the triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI). When you see this triad, your brain should immediately fire off three primary differential diagnoses: Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP), Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC-HUS), and Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS). 

The challenge is that these conditions often overlap clinically, and the "rule-out" process is what ultimately leads to the correct treatment.

## The Diagnostic Triage: How to Navigate the Maze

When a patient presents with MAHA and thrombocytopenia, the first priority is always to rule out TTP because it is a medical emergency requiring immediate plasma exchange (PEX). In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was low, and the definitive answer came from the ADAMTS13 activity level. With an activity of 43.5%, TTP was effectively ruled out (TTP typically requires activity <10%). 

Next, we look for the classic "trigger" of STEC-HUS. While our patient had a clear infection (the dog bite), she lacked the typical prodromal enteric illness, and a negative stool PCR for Shiga toxin helped steer us away from this diagnosis. 

This leaves us in the territory of CM-HUS. This is where the "maze" gets tricky. CM-HUS is often a diagnosis of exclusion, and a common pitfall is assuming that normal complement levels (C3 and C4) rule it out. In this case, the patient’s C3 and C4 levels were completely normal, but she was still progressing toward end-stage renal failure. It is a critical clinical pearl: **Normal C3 and C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS.** The complement system can be dysregulated in ways that don't deplete these specific markers, or the levels may simply remain within the reference range during the acute phase of the disease.

## The "Second Hit" and the Treatment Dilemma

One of the most interesting aspects of this case is the timing. The patient initially presented with sepsis and DIC, which are "noisy" clinical states that can mask an underlying TMA. It was only after the sepsis began to stabilize and the inflammatory profile improved that the isolated hemolysis emerged. This suggests that the *C. canimorsus* infection acted as the "second hit"—the trigger that pushed a potentially predisposed complement system into a state of uncontrolled activation.

This leads us to a significant therapeutic dilemma: how do we treat a patient with a complement-mediated TMA who is still fighting an infection? Eculizumab is the gold standard for CM-HUS, but it carries a known risk of severe infection (particularly *Neisseria* species) because it blocks terminal complement components. In this case, the team navigated this by waiting until the blood cultures were negative before initiating eculizumab, while using PEX as a bridging therapy. The fact that the patient’s diuresis returned after PEX but only stabilized with eculizumab underscores the difference between "cleaning the pipes" (PEX) and "fixing the engine" (complement blockade).

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points

*   **Think TMA early:** If you see the triad of MAHA (schistocytes on smear), thrombocytopenia, and AKI, start your workup immediately.
*   **ADAMTS13 is your first line:** Rule out TTP early. If activity is >10%, your focus shifts elsewhere.
*   **Don't let "Normal" C3/C4 fool you:** Complement-mediated HUS can occur with normal C3 and C4 levels. If TTP and STEC-HUS are ruled out and the patient is failing to improve, suspect CM-HUS.
*   **The Sepsis Mask:** Be aware that the initial chaos of sepsis and DIC can obscure the development of a TMA. Watch the patient closely as the initial "fire" of sepsis begins to cool.
*   **Eculizumab Timing:** While eculizumab is the definitive treatment for CM-HUS, the risk of infection remains. Aim to initiate blockade once the primary infectious trigger is controlled, using PEX as a bridge if necessary.



# The Double-Edged Sword: Complement Activation as Defense and Destroyer

We’ve all seen the classic presentation of sepsis: a patient with a clear source of infection, skyrocketing inflammatory markers, and a rapid slide into multi-organ failure. But what happens when the very system designed to save the patient—the complement cascade—becomes the primary driver of their destruction? 

This case presents a fascinating, almost cinematic progression. A healthy woman in her 40s is bitten by her dog and develops fulminant *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis. She survives the initial septic shock and the DIC-like coagulopathy. However, as the infection begins to stabilize, she hits a secondary, devastating wall: sudden-onset isolated hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, and worsening renal failure. This wasn't just "lingering sepsis." It was the transition from infection to Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS).

## The Clinical Paradox: Defense vs. Destruction

The core of this case lies in a biological paradox. *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is an encapsulated gram-negative rod. To clear such a pathogen, our bodies rely heavily on the complement system to opsonize the bacteria and facilitate clearance. In a healthy individual, this is a success story. But in this patient, the complement system essentially "over-performed."

While the complement system successfully mobilized to fight the *C. canimorsus* bacteraemia, it simultaneously triggered uncontrolled activation of the alternative pathway. This leads to the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) on host endothelial cells. The result is a catastrophic "friendly fire" scenario: the same pathway required to kill the dog-borne bacteria begins to shred the lining of the patient's blood vessels, leading to microthrombi, schistocytes, and the classic triad of Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA).

## "Second Hits" and the Spectrum of TMA

When we think of CM-HUS, we often think of a "second hit" model. This is the idea that a patient has a pre-existing, often subclinical, genetic defect in complement regulation (like Factor H, I, or B) and needs a trigger—like pregnancy, surgery, or infection—to manifest the disease. 

However, this case adds an important nuance to the discussion. While the patient showed a reduction in alternative pathway activity during treatment, no major pathogenic variants were identified in her genetic screening. This suggests that in some cases, a sufficiently virulent or "complement-heavy" infection can trigger TMA even in the absence of a clear hereditary defect. It highlights that the "trigger" isn't just a helper; sometimes, the trigger is the primary driver.

## Navigating the Diagnostic Maze

The clinical challenge here was the "masking" effect of early sepsis. Because the patient presented with profound pancytopenia, DIC, and acute kidney injury (AKI), the initial focus was rightfully on managing the septic shock. HUS was on the differential, but the presence of leukopaenia and a high inflammatory profile often leads clinicians to prioritize the infection over a microangiopathic process.

A key clinical pearl from this case is the "clinical pivot." When the patient’s inflammatory profile began to improve but her hemoglobin plummeted while her platelets remained stubbornly low, the diagnosis shifted. The appearance of 15-20% schistocytes on a peripheral smear and a positive (though eventually ruled out as a false positive) DAT should have been the "red alert" for TMA. 

## The Therapeutic Dilemma: When to Block?

The management of CM-HUS in the setting of active bacteraemia is a high-stakes balancing act. Eculizumab, the gold standard for CM-HUS, works by blocking the terminal complement component C5. This is lifesaving for the kidneys and blood cells, but it also creates a "blind spot" in the immune system, making the patient highly susceptible to encapsulated bacteria like *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*.

In this case, the team navigated this by waiting until the blood cultures were negative before initiating eculizumab, while using plasma exchange (PEX) as a bridging therapy. While PEX is the cornerstone of TTP management, it only provides transient benefit in CM-HUS by clearing inflammatory mediators. The definitive "fix" is complement blockade.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **Watch for the "Second Wave":** In cases of severe sepsis/DIC, if the patient shows clinical improvement but develops sudden, isolated hemolysis or worsening AKI, urgently consider a superimposed TMA.
*   **TMA Differential:** Always rule out TTP by checking ADAMTS13 activity. A level >10% significantly shifts the probability away from TTP and toward HUS/CM-HUS.
*   **Complement isn't always "Normal":** Do not rely solely on C3 and C4 levels to rule out CM-HUS; these can remain within normal limits during the acute phase of the disease.
*   **Early Intervention is Key:** The primary determinants of renal recovery in CM-HUS are the speed of eculizumab initiation and the severity of the initial TMA. 
*   **The "Paradox" to Remember:** The immune system's most potent weapons against encapsulated bacteria can, in certain contexts, become the primary drivers of endothelial destruction.



# The Therapeutic Tug-of-War: Timing Eculizumab in the Setting of Infection

It starts with a dog bite. For most, it’s a trip to the ER for a tetanus shot and some antibiotics. But for this patient in her 40s, it was the trigger for a clinical rollercoaster that moved from septic shock to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), then to end-stage renal failure, and finally to a rare, complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

What makes this case so fascinating—and clinically challenging—is the "diagnostic fog." In the early days of her admission, the patient was crashing from *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia. The overwhelming clinical picture of sepsis, pancytopenia, and DIC effectively masked the underlying complement dysregulation. It wasn't until the infection began to stabilize and she developed isolated, profound hemolysis and schistocytes that the team realized they weren't just looking at "post-septic" complications—they were looking at Complement-mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS).

## The Diagnostic Maze: When HUS is Hiding in Plain Sight

When you see a patient with acute kidney injury (AKI), thrombocytopenia, and hemolysis, your mind should immediately jump to the "big three" of TMA: TTP, STEC-HUS, and CM-HUS. 

In this case, the initial presentation actually made HUS seem *less* likely. The patient had severe leukopenia, which isn't your classic HUS presentation. Furthermore, her C3 and C4 levels were normal. However, there is a critical pearl here: **Normal C3 and C4 levels do not rule out CM-HUS.** Because CM-HUS is often a "second hit" phenomenon—where a genetic predisposition meets an external trigger like infection—the complement system might not be constantly "depleted." It’s the *dysregulation* of the alternative pathway that causes the endothelial damage, not necessarily a low circulating level of C3.

Once ADAMTS13 activity came back normal (43.5%), TTP was off the table. With a negative Shiga toxin PCR, STEC-HUS was ruled out. The remaining diagnosis was CM-HUS, likely triggered by the *C. canimorsus* infection.

## The Tug-of-War: Complement Blockade vs. Infection Risk

This brings us to the primary clinical dilemma of the case: How do you treat a complement-mediated TMA in a patient who has just survived (or is currently fighting) a systemic bacterial infection?

Eculizumab is the gold standard for CM-HUS. It targets the terminal complement component C5, halting the cascade that leads to microthrombi and renal destruction. But there is a massive caveat. The terminal complement pathway is our primary defense against encapsulated organisms, most notably *Neisseria meningitidis*. Initiating eculizumab while a patient is still actively bacteremic or has a high risk of encapsulated infection is a high-stakes gamble.

In this case, the team had to navigate a delicate transition. They utilized Plasma Exchange (PEX) as a "bridge." While PEX is the primary treatment for TTP, in CM-HUS it serves to remove circulating inflammatory mediators and complement fragments, providing a window of stabilization. This "bought time" for the infection to clear.

## The Strategy for Safe Transition

The move from PEX to eculizumab requires a structured safety net. In this patient, the team waited until blood cultures were negative before initiating the first dose of eculizumab. This is the standard of care: you want to clear the "fire" of the infection before you turn off the "sprinkler system" of the complement cascade.

To mitigate the risk of subsequent infection, the team employed a triple-threat approach:
1.  **Vaccination:** Administering meningococcal vaccines (Menveo and Bexsero) before starting the blockade.
2.  **Antimicrobial Prophylaxis:** Using prophylactic antibiotics (Apocillin) to cover the period of heightened vulnerability.
3.  **Close Monitoring:** Vigilant observation for any signs of new-onset sepsis.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **Don't let sepsis mask the TMA:** If a patient’s hemolysis and thrombocytopenia persist or worsen as the primary infection improves, suspect an underlying TMA (like CM-HUS) rather than just "sepsis-related" coagulopathy.
*   **Normal C3/C4 is not a "No":** Complement levels can be normal in CM-HUS. If you suspect TMA and TTP/STEC-HUS are ruled out, think complement dysregulation regardless of baseline levels.
*   **PEX as a bridge:** In the setting of active infection, PEX can be a valuable bridge to stabilize the patient while waiting for the "safe window" to start eculizumab.
*   **The Eculizumab Rule:** Ideally, wait for negative cultures before starting terminal complement blockade. Always pair it with meningitis vaccination and appropriate antimicrobial prophylaxis to manage the risk of encapsulated organisms.
*   **The Trigger matters:** This case reminds us that even rare organisms like *C. canimorsus* can act as the "second hit" for complement-mediated disease. Always consider the infectious trigger in your TMA workup.
