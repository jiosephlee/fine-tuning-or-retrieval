Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# When Sepsis Masks a TMA: Recognizing the Clinical Pivot

In the "fog of war" that is a patient in septic shock, the clinical picture is often so loud that it drowns out the subtler signals of underlying pathology. When you are managing a patient with profound metabolic acidosis, refractory hypoglycemia, and a rapidly failing multi-organ system, your primary focus is almost always the "big" answers: Sepsis, DIC, and maybe necrotizing fasciitis. 

But what happens when the sepsis begins to stabilize, the antibiotics are working, and the inflammatory markers start to trend down—yet the patient's hematological status continues to plummet? This is the "clinical pivot," and missing it can be the difference between a recovery and a dialysis-dependent outcome.

## The Noise of Sepsis and the DIC Trap

In the case we are looking at today, a healthy woman in her 40s presented with a classic, albeit severe, presentation of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis following a dog bite. She was a "textbook" mess: high fever, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), and a coagulopathy consistent with Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC).

In this early phase, diagnosing a Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) is notoriously difficult. DIC and TMA share many overlapping features—thrombocytopenia, AKI, and microvascular thrombosis. In a septic patient, the "noise" of DIC (systemic consumption of clotting factors) makes it incredibly hard to see the "signal" of TMA (a specific microvascular "traffic jam"). 

The team correctly identified the infection, started appropriate broad-spectrum antibiotics, and initiated supportive care. For the first week, the diagnosis of sepsis/DIC was the most logical and necessary focus.

## The Pivot: When the Signal Breaks Through

The clinical pivot occurred roughly 8 days into her stay. The patient was becoming clinically stable, and her inflammatory profile was improving. However, she suddenly developed a dramatic drop in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L) without any evidence of active bleeding. 

This is the moment where the "sepsis" story no longer explains the "hemolysis" story. 

When you see isolated, progressive hemolysis (low hemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH) and persistent thrombocytopenia in a patient whose infection is being managed, you must pivot your thinking. You are no longer looking at a complication of sepsis; you are looking at a secondary, potentially life-threatening pathology. 

## Decoding the TMA

Once the team identified Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anaemia (MAHA) via a peripheral smear showing 15–20% schistocytes, the differential diagnosis for TMA narrowed significantly. In any adult with MAHA, the first priority is to rule out Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP). 

By checking the ADAMTS13 activity (which was 43.5%), the team could effectively rule out TTP (which typically requires activity <10%). They also ruled out Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC-HUS) via PCR. 

This left a primary suspicion of Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS). It is a fascinating clinical paradox: the *C. canimorsus* infection served as the "second hit" that triggered the alternative complement pathway. While the complement system is essential for clearing the bacteria, in this patient, it triggered an uncontrolled, self-sustaining loop of endothelial damage.

## The Treatment Dilemma: Complement Blockade in Sepsis

One of the most challenging aspects of this case was the timing of eculizumab. Eculizumab is the gold standard for CM-HUS, but it carries a theoretical risk of exacerbating infections by inhibiting the terminal complement components necessary to fight encapsulated bacteria.

The team navigated this by waiting until the blood cultures were negative before initiating eculizumab, while using Plasma Exchange (PEX) as a "bridge." It is important to note that while PEX is the mainstay of TTP treatment, it only provides a transient benefit in CM-HUS by temporarily removing circulating complement fragments. The real "cure" in CM-HUS is the blockade of the pathway itself.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Listen for the Pivot:** If a patient’s primary infection stabilizes but their hematological parameters (Hb, platelets, LDH) continue to deteriorate, stop looking at the sepsis and start looking for a secondary TMA.
*   **DIC vs. TMA:** In the early stages of sepsis, they look nearly identical. Don't let the DIC "noise" prevent you from recognizing a TMA "signal" that may be complicating the clinical course.
*   **Rule Out the "Big Two":** For any new-onset MAHA, your immediate workup should include ADAMTS13 activity (to rule out TTP) and Shiga toxin testing (to rule out STEC-HUS).
*   **Complement Levels Can Be Deceptive:** In CM-HUS, standard C3 and C4 levels can be completely normal. Do not let a normal C3/C4 level rule out a complement-mediated process.
*   **Timing is Everything:** For CM-HUS, the earlier the initiation of eculizumab, the better the renal recovery. PEX is a bridge, not a destination.
