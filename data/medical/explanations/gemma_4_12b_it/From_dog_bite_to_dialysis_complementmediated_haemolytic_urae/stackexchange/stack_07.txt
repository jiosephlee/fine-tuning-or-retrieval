Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] Which slow-growing, encapsulated gram-negative rod is a known zoonotic pathogen capable of causing fulminant sepsis and, rarely, triggering complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy?

The organism described is **_Capnocytophaga canimorsus_**.

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a fastidious, slow-growing, encapsulated gram-negative rod that is a common member of the normal oral flora in dogs and cats. While human infections are relatively rare, they are typically zoonotic, occurring following dog or cat bites, scratches, or even through close contact with infected animals. In humans, the clinical spectrum of *C. canimorsus* infection is notably severe, often progressing rapidly to fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and multi-organ failure. The mortality rate can be as high as 30%.

The pathophysiology of the rare but severe complication—complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), specifically complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS)—involves the dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway. While CM-HUS is frequently associated with underlying genetic mutations in complement regulatory proteins (such as factors H, I, or B), infections like *C. canimorsus* can act as a critical "second hit" or trigger. The pathogen can activate the complement system; in susceptible individuals, this activation may become uncontrolled, leading to systemic endothelial damage, microthrombi formation, and the characteristic triad of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury.

Diagnosis can be challenging due to the organism's fastidious nature and slow growth in standard culture media, which may delay the identification of the causative agent. Clinical recognition of *C. canimorsus* as a potential trigger for TMA is essential, as it may necessitate early intervention with complement inhibitors, such as eculizumab, once the acute infectious phase is stabilized.
