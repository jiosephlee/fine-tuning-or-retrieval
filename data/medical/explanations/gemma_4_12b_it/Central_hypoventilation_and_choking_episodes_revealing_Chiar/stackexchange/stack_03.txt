Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Clinical Pearls] Why is it clinically important to consider Chiari malformation type I (CMI) in a patient who presents with persistent oropharyngeal dysphagia despite normal results on flexible laryngoscopy and upper gastrointestinal endoscopy?

In clinical practice, when a patient presents with persistent oropharyngeal dysphagia—specifically involving both solids and liquids—but undergoes a "negative" workup with flexible laryngoscopy and upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, the clinician must shift their focus from mechanical obstructions to neurogenic causes. Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is a critical consideration in this context because it can cause significant oropharyngeal dysfunction through structural brainstem compression.

The pathophysiology of CMI involves the caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum. This anatomical crowding can compress the brainstem and the nuclei of the lower cranial nerves. In many patients, this results in "neurogenic" dysphagia, which may manifest as palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia. Because these issues are functional and neurological rather than structural obstructions (such as a tumor, stricture, or diverticulum), they will not be visible on a standard endoscopy.

This case highlights the diagnostic pitfall of relying solely on traditional gastroenterological or otolaryngological examinations for swallowing disorders. The patient’s seven-year history of choking episodes, which persisted despite multiple normal endoscopies and laryngoscopies, was eventually explained by an 11 mm tonsillar descent. Furthermore, the case demonstrates that CMI can also present with life-threatening respiratory complications, such as acquired central hypoventilation (resembling Ondine’s curse), where brainstem compression disrupts automatic respiratory control.

Therefore, in any patient with refractory oropharyngeal dysphagia or unexplained respiratory dysfunction (such as nocturnal choking or asymptomatic hypoxia) and normal endoscopic findings, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for underlying structural brain abnormalities. A review of historical imaging or a dedicated MRI of the craniocervical junction is an appropriate next step to rule out CMI.
