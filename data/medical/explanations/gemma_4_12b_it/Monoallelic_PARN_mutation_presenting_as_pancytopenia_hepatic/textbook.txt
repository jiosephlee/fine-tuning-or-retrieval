Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Pathophysiology of Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs)

To understand the underlying pathology of this case, one must first understand the role of telomeres in maintaining genomic stability. Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences located at the terminal ends of linear chromosomes. They function as protective "caps" that prevent the cell's repair machinery from recognizing the ends of chromosomes as double-stranded DNA breaks. Without these caps, chromosomes would be susceptible to degradation, fusion with other chromosomes, or deleterious rearrangements. However, because DNA polymerase is unable to fully replicate the extreme 3' end of a linear DNA strand—a phenomenon known as the "end replication problem"—telomeres naturally shorten with every round of cell division.

In most somatic cells, this shortening acts as a biological clock. When telomeres reach a critically short length, they can no longer protect the chromosome ends, triggering a DNA damage response. This leads to either cellular senescence (a state of permanent cell cycle arrest) or apoptosis (programmed cell death). To counteract this attrition in specific tissues, the enzyme telomerase is utilized. Telomerase is a ribonucleoprotein complex consisting of a catalytic protein subunit, telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), and a non-coding RNA component, telomerase RNA component (TERC). Together, they extend the telomeres, allowing cells with high proliferative demands—such as hematopoietic stem cells, skin cells, and rapidly dividing mucosal cells—to maintain their replicative capacity.

Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs) arise from germline mutations in genes essential for this maintenance process. When these genes are defective, telomeres shorten at an accelerated rate, leading to premature cellular senescence and the clinical manifestation of "premature aging." Dyskeratosis congenita (DC) is the prototypical TBD, traditionally characterized by a mucocutaneous triad: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. However, TBDs are a heterogeneous group of syndromes (including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson and Revesz syndromes) that can present with various combinations of bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, and hepatic involvement, even in the absence of classical cutaneous features.

The specific gene identified in this patient, *PARN*, plays a critical but distinct role in telomere maintenance. *PARN* encodes an exoribonuclease involved in the deadenylation of mRNA poly(A) tails, which regulates gene expression. Crucially, *PARN* is required for the 3-prime-end maturation of *TERC* (the RNA component of telomerase). If *PARN* function is impaired, the processing of *TERC* is disrupted, leading to reduced telomerase activity and subsequent telomere attrition. While biallelic mutations in *PARN* are associated with autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), monoallelic mutations are increasingly recognized as the underlying cause of a spectrum of disorders, including pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure.

The clinical phenotype of a TBD is heavily influenced by the inheritance pattern and the specific gene involved. Biallelic mutations typically result in severe, early-onset multisystem disease. In contrast, monoallelic mutations often exhibit variable penetrance and may present with more localized or organ-specific manifestations, such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or isolated bone marrow failure. Because these disorders can mimic acquired conditions—such as nutritional anemias, autoimmune hepatitis, or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis—recognizing the underlying pathophysiology of telomere attrition is essential for diagnosing these rare genetic syndromes in patients with unexplained, multi-organ systemic decline.

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Workup

The patient, a woman in her late 20s, presented with a five-year history of progressive fatigue and generalized weakness. The clinical trajectory was notable for a significant acceleration in symptoms over the final three months, which resulted in a loss of functional independence. In addition to these systemic symptoms, she reported exertional dyspnea, weight loss, and anorexia. This presentation—generalized weakness and declining functional capacity in a young adult—should always prompt a broad differential, ranging from chronic infectious diseases and autoimmune disorders to primary hematologic malignancies and rare genetic syndromes.

Physical examination provided critical clues toward a systemic, perhaps genetic, pathology. The presence of pallor and icterus suggested an underlying hematologic or hepatic issue. However, several "premature aging" features were prominent: premature greying of the hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a "bald tongue" characterized by the atrophy of lingual papillae. These mucocutaneous findings, while often associated with specific syndromes like dyskeratosis congenita, can also be seen in severe nutritional deficiencies. Furthermore, signs of musculoskeletal involvement—including thoracic scoliosis and thenar atrophy—and hepatomegaly further complicated the clinical picture, suggesting a multi-organ process rather than an isolated hematologic disorder.

The initial hematologic workup revealed pancytopenia, a condition where all three major blood cell lines (red cells, white cells, and platelets) are deficient. A key diagnostic discriminator in this case was the low reticulocyte count (0.39%) in the presence of a peripheral smear showing dimorphic anemia, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils. These findings are classic hallmarks of ineffective erythropoiesis, a process where red blood cell precursors die within the bone marrow before they can mature and enter circulation. This contrasts with peripheral hemolysis, where the marrow compensates for the loss of circulating cells by increasing production.

Differentiating ineffective erythropoiesis from peripheral hemolysis is a critical clinical step. In this patient, despite markers of hemolysis (elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinemia) and a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG, the low reticulocyte count pointed toward a production problem within the marrow rather than a destruction problem in the periphery. The presence of dimorphic erythrocytes—the coexistence of two distinct populations of red blood cells—further supports a state of high marrow stress or a maturation defect.

The laboratory investigations also identified severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiencies. In many clinical scenarios, these deficiencies alone could explain the patient’s pancytopenia and lingual atrophy. The patient’s duodenal biopsy showed mild chronic gastritis with reduced parietal cells, providing a potential mechanism for impaired intrinsic factor production and subsequent B12 malabsorption. However, the severity of her multisystem involvement—specifically the presence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis—remained disproportionate to what would be expected from nutritional deficiencies alone.

Initial evaluation of the liver and lungs focused on identifying signs of fibrosis or inflammation. Abdominal ultrasound and transient elastography confirmed hepatosplenomegaly and significant hepatic steatosis and cirrhosis. While inflammatory markers were elevated, the absence of autoimmune antibodies and infectious markers suggested a non-inflammatory, potentially fibrotic, underlying etiology. These early findings, combined with the evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis and premature aging, shifted the diagnostic focus toward a systemic genetic disorder capable of affecting multiple organ systems.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: Navigating the Differential Diagnosis

The diagnostic journey for complex multisystemic diseases often begins with the most common and easily reversible causes of the primary symptoms. In this case, the patient’s initial presentation of progressive fatigue and pancytopenia strongly suggested a nutritional deficiency. The identification of severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiencies provided a logical starting point, as these are well-known causes of megaloblastic anemia and ineffective erythropoiesis, the latter of which was supported by the peripheral smear findings of hypersegmented neutrophils and basophilic stippling. However, a critical step in clinical reasoning is the evaluation of the patient's response to treatment. While the patient showed some improvement after receiving intravenous cyanocobalamin, her systemic dysfunction—specifically the persistent hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis and the severity of her physical decline—remained disproportionate to a nutritional deficit alone. This discrepancy serves as a clinical "red flag," indicating that the nutritional deficiency was likely a co-morbid finding or an exacerbating factor rather than the primary underlying pathology.

When a patient presents with organ-specific complications like hepatic fibrosis without a clear cause, the clinician must systematically rule out the most prevalent etiologies. This involves a comprehensive investigation into infectious, autoimmune, and metabolic triggers. In this case, the medical team performed a thorough workup including viral hepatitis screening, HIV, and Cytomegalovirus testing, alongside autoimmune serologies such as antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis markers. The fact that these tests were largely unremarkable, combined with the absence of classical markers of autoimmune disease, shifted the focus away from acquired inflammatory conditions. By excluding these common drivers, the clinical team was forced to consider "idiopathic" processes, which often point toward either a rare systemic disease or a primary genetic disorder.

The shift toward a genetic diagnosis was driven by the recognition of a multisystemic pattern involving high-turnover tissues. The patient exhibited a constellation of symptoms—pancytopenia (bone marrow), hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis (liver and lungs), and premature aging (greying hair, osteoporosis). When several disparate organ systems fail simultaneously in a young adult, the differential diagnosis should expand to include systemic syndromes. In the context of progressive fibrosis and cytopenia, telomere biology disorders (TBDs) become a high-priority consideration. TBDs are characterized by the premature shortening of telomeres, which leads to cellular senescence and apoptosis in rapidly dividing cell populations, explaining the involvement of the bone marrow, skin, and internal organs.

A significant challenge in this case was the "cryptic" nature of the presentation. Most medical students are taught that dyskeratosis congenita (DC), the prototype of TBDs, presents with a classic mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. However, this patient lacked these hallmark features. It is essential to recognize that TBDs can manifest as "cryptic" syndromes, where the primary defect is masked by more overt complications like idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). In such cases, the absence of the classical triad should not rule out a genetic telomere disorder. Instead, the clinician must look for the underlying consequences of telomere attrition—organ-specific fibrosis and hematologic failure—which may present more subtly or occur in isolation.

Ultimately, this case illustrates the importance of systematic exclusion in diagnosing rare inherited syndromes. By methodically ruling out nutritional, infectious, and autoimmune causes, the clinical team was able to identify a "residual" multisystemic pathology that could not be explained by common diseases. This process of elimination, combined with a high index of suspicion for premature aging syndromes in a young patient with unexplained fibrosis, provided the necessary clinical justification for performing genetic testing. This systematic approach ensures that while common conditions are treated promptly, the underlying drivers of rare and life-altering genetic conditions are not overlooked.

## Advanced Imaging, Neuromuscular Evaluation, and Genetic Correlation

When a patient presents with a multisystemic, progressive decline that does not conform to a single clear etiology, advanced imaging and specialized physiological testing are required to map the extent of organ involvement. In this case, the diagnostic journey moved from identifying general symptoms to characterizing specific fibrotic and neurological pathologies. To assess the hepatic involvement, transient elastography was employed to provide a non-invasive measurement of liver stiffness. A stiffness score of 18.1 kPa, combined with grade II steatosis, provided evidence of cirrhosis in a patient who had tested negative for common autoimmune and infectious causes of liver disease. Concurrently, PET-CT imaging offered a systemic overview of the disease. The "ground-glass haziness" and subpleural atelectatic changes in the lungs provided a structural correlate to the patient’s exertional dyspnea, while the inhomogeneous uptake in the axial and appendicular bone marrow suggested active, systemic involvement of the hematopoietic system without the presence of overt malignancy.

Beyond the primary respiratory and hepatic findings, a comprehensive evaluation included a neuromuscular assessment and an analysis of systemic metabolic health. The presence of thenar atrophy and sensory changes prompted electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies (NCS). These tests revealed an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic changes, which serves to differentiate primary muscle wasting from peripheral nerve involvement. Furthermore, the patient’s bone pain and fractures necessitated an evaluation of bone mineral density. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) confirmed osteoporosis, which, when combined with laboratory evidence of secondary hyperparathyroidism, hypocalcaemia, and vitamin D deficiency, highlighted the systemic nature of the patient's decline. While these metabolic and neuromuscular findings could be secondary to nutritional deficiencies or the general systemic stress of a chronic illness, they provide essential context for the severity of the patient's presentation.

The definitive diagnostic pivot in this case was the use of clinical exome sequencing (WES) to investigate a suspected genetic etiology. WES identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (p.Cys205Arg). Because the specific functional impact of this variant had not been previously established, it was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). To navigate this uncertainty, clinicians apply the ACMG/AMP standards, which provide a framework for weighing evidence: the variant is located in a critical, well-established functional domain (PM1), is absent from large population databases (PM2), and is predicted as damaging by multiple computational tools (PP3). Furthermore, the *PARN* gene is known to have a low rate of benign missense variation (PP2). Collectively, these criteria provide high clinical suspicion that the variant is pathogenic, even in the absence of a definitive "pathogenic" classification.

Understanding the correlation between *PARN* mutations and clinical phenotypes is vital for long-term management. The *PARN* gene is essential for the 3-prime-end maturation of *TERC* RNA, a core component of the telomerase complex. While biallelic *PARN* mutations cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), monoallelic mutations are increasingly linked to a spectrum of telomere biology disorders (TBDs), most notably idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and bone marrow failure (BMFS). This patient’s presentation—marked by pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and cytopenia—exemplifies the expanding clinical spectrum of *PARN*-associated disorders. While her cytopenia was likely exacerbated by severe vitamin B12 deficiency, the presence of a *PARN* mutation underscores a genetic predisposition to marrow failure. This requires a shift in management from purely nutritional correction to long-term surveillance of the bone marrow and pulmonary function to monitor for the progression of TBD.

## Management, Prognosis, and Clinical Significance

The management of telomere biology disorders (TBDs) is primarily supportive, focusing on the correction of secondary complications, symptomatic relief, and long-term surveillance of progressive organ involvement. In this case, the immediate clinical priority was the correction of severe nutritional deficiencies. The patient received high-dose intravenous vitamin B12 and oral folic acid to address the ineffective erythropoiesis and neurological symptoms. Additionally, her osteoporosis—a common manifestation of accelerated aging in TBDs—was managed with vitamin D supplementation and calcium. While these interventions provided symptomatic relief and stabilized her blood counts, they do not alter the underlying genetic defect in the *PARN* gene. Consequently, management must remain longitudinal, involving regular monitoring of pulmonary function, hepatic enzymes, and complete blood counts to detect early signs of worsening fibrosis or overt bone marrow failure.

Prognostically, TBDs are progressive conditions with variable penetrance. While this patient showed initial improvement following the correction of B12 deficiency, the presence of a *PARN* mutation carries a significant risk of future hematologic and pulmonary decline. In patients who develop progressive bone marrow failure, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) may be considered as a potentially curative intervention, although its feasibility depends on the severity of the marrow failure and the availability of a suitable donor. For the pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis components, management is more limited, often involving standard care for interstitial lung disease and monitoring for signs of portal hypertension or liver failure.

Genetic counseling is a cornerstone of management for any suspected TBD. Even when a variant is classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), the identification of a mutation in a known TBD-associated gene like *PARN* provides critical information for the family. Counseling should address the inheritance patterns (autosomal recessive or dominant depending on the specific gene and clinical presentation), the risks to offspring, and the necessity of screening for other family members who may be asymptomatic carriers or have milder phenotypes. This is particularly important given that TBDs can manifest with high variability, where some relatives may only present with isolated pulmonary fibrosis while others may develop severe bone marrow failure.

This case provides several clinical pearls for the management of young adults with multisystemic disease. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for TBDs when a patient presents with a "constellation" of seemingly unrelated symptoms—such as cytopenia, premature greying, and organ fibrosis—particularly when standard workups for autoimmune, infectious, or nutritional causes are negative. The absence of the "classic" mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and skin pigmentation) should not rule out a telomere biology disorder. Early recognition is essential not only for accurate diagnosis but also for early intervention in marrow failure and for providing timely genetic guidance to the patient’s family.

Looking forward, the management of TBDs remains a burgeoning field of research. While currently limited to supportive care, there is ongoing investigation into pharmacological interventions aimed at telomere elongation. This includes the study of androgens like danazol and nandrolone, which have shown some promise in preclinical models for extending telomere length. However, these remain experimental, and their long-term safety and efficacy in humans are not yet established. For the current patient, the focus remains on diligent surveillance and proactive management of the clinical manifestations of her *PARN* mutation.
