Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# When the Triad is Missing: Recognizing Telomere Biology Disorders in Adults

If you’re looking for a textbook definition of a Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD), your mind probably jumps straight to Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC). You think of the classic "triad": oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. But what happens when those hallmark features are completely absent? 

In the clinical world, "missing" symptoms often lead to a diagnostic merry-go-round. We see a young patient with "idiopathic" pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), an "unexplained" case of marrow failure, or a perplexing constellation of organ-specific fibrosis, and we treat the symptoms as isolated pathologies. However, as this recent case demonstrates, these seemingly disconnected "idiopathic" conditions can actually be different faces of the same underlying genetic coin: premature telomere attrition.

## The Case: A Multisystem Puzzle

Consider a woman in her late 20s presenting with a five-year history of progressive fatigue. By the time she reached the tertiary care center, her functional capacity was severely impaired. On examination, the "clinical noise" was significant: she had pallor, icterus, and—crucially—signs of premature aging, including greying hair and knuckle hyperpigmentation. 

The laboratory workup initially pointed toward a common culprit: severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency. She had pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count and a peripheral smear showing dimorphic erythrocytes and basophilic stippling—classic signs of ineffective erythropoiesis. However, even after correcting her nutritional deficiencies, her clinical state remained disproportionately severe.

When the investigation broadened, the picture became even more complex. She had grade II hepatic steatosis and cirrhosis, secondary hyperparathyroidism with osteoporosis, and an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. Imaging revealed diffuse ground-glass haziness and fibrotic changes in both lungs. 

The "aha!" moment came when the team looked past the individual organ failures and toward a unifying diagnosis. Despite the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad of DC, the constellation of cytopenia, premature aging, and multi-organ fibrosis raised a high suspicion for a Telomere Biology Disorder. Genetic testing eventually identified a monoallelic *PARN* missense variant.

## Why PARN Matters

Telomeres are the protective caps on our chromosomes. In TBDs, mutations in genes like *TERT*, *TERC*, and *PARN* impair the maintenance of these caps. When telomeres shorten too quickly, actively dividing cells—like those in the bone marrow, skin, and liver—enter senescence or apoptosis prematurely.

The *PARN* gene is particularly interesting here. While biallelic mutations in *PARN* cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), monoallelic variants are increasingly linked to a spectrum of disorders including pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure. In this patient, the *PARN* mutation provided the genetic "glue" that connected her pulmonary fibrosis to her hepatic cirrhosis and her hematologic issues. It highlights that TBDs are not a monolith; they are a spectrum where the phenotype can shift from isolated organ-specific disease to systemic failure depending on the specific gene and the degree of penetrance.

## Clinical Pearls: Staying One Step Ahead

The biggest takeaway here is that we must not let the absence of "textbook" signs lead to a premature closure of the differential diagnosis. If a young adult presents with a multi-systemic "mess" that doesn't quite fit a single autoimmune or infectious category, look closer at the evidence of premature aging.

*   **Look for the "Hidden" Triad:** Even if you don't see leukoplakia, keep an eye out for premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, or a "bald tongue" (atrophy of lingual papillae). These are significant clues in a young patient.
*   **Ineffective Erythropoiesis vs. Destruction:** If you see pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count and dimorphic RBCs, think ineffective erythropoiesis. While nutritional deficiencies (like B12) are common, if the clinical severity outstrips the lab findings, consider a primary marrow failure syndrome or a TBD.
*   **The Fibrosis Connection:** Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) and hepatic fibrosis in a young adult should always trigger a search for a genetic etiology. Emerging evidence suggests that *PARN* mutations are becoming an increasingly recognized cause of these fibrotic conditions.
*   **Early Recognition is Key:** Identifying a TBD early is vital for genetic counseling, long-term surveillance of organ function, and identifying candidates for potential bone marrow transplantation in progressive cases.

**Take-Home Messages:**
*   TBDs can present without the classic mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita.
*   Maintain a high index of suspicion for TBD in young adults with "idiopathic" pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic cirrhosis, or persistent bone marrow failure.
*   Premature aging features (greying hair, hyperpigmentation) are critical clinical clues that can unify a multisystem presentation.
*   Genetic testing (exome sequencing) is the gold standard for confirming TBD and guiding long-term management.
