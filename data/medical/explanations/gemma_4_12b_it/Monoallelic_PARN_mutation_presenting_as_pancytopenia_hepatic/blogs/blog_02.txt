Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# The Anchor Bias of Nutritional Deficiencies: When 'Corrected' Labs Aren't the End of the Story

You’ve seen this scenario play out dozens of times: a patient presents with profound fatigue and anemia. You run the standard battery, and—boom—there it is. A glaring, "textbook" vitamin B12 deficiency. You start the parenteral replacement, the labs start to normalize, and you mentally check the box. But then, the patient still isn't getting better. They are still exhausted, they still have multi-organ symptoms, and their clinical picture remains stubbornly complex.

This is the classic "diagnostic anchor." We see a clear, easy-to-treat explanation (the B12 deficiency) and we subconsciously stop looking for the harder, more complex underlying pathology. In the case we're looking at today, the B12 deficiency was very real, but it was merely a secondary manifestation of a much deeper, systemic genetic disaster.

## The Case: When "Fixed" Labs Don't Equal a "Fixed" Patient

A woman in her late 20s presented with a five-year history of progressive weakness and fatigue that eventually became so severe she required assistance with daily self-care. On exam, things started to look "off" beyond simple anemia. She had premature greying of her hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue with atrophic papillae. She also had hepatosplenomegaly, thoracic scoliosis, and a profound sensorimotor polyneuropathy.

Initial labs confirmed the "easy" answer: pancytopenia with severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiencies. The peripheral smear showed the classic hallmarks of ineffective erythropoiesis—dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, and hypersegmented neutrophils. The team correctly identified that while B12 deficiency could cause these findings, the clinical severity was disproportionate. Even after aggressive B12 and folate correction, the patient’s systemic dysfunction persisted.

When the team stopped looking at the B12 and started looking at the "whole" patient, the picture changed. They found hepatic fibrosis (with cirrhosis on elastography), osteoporosis, and evidence of pulmonary fibrosis on PET-CT. This constellation—pancytopenia, premature aging, multi-organ fibrosis, and neuropathy—shifted the suspicion from "nutritional" to "syndromic."

## Beyond the Anchor: Recognizing Telomere Biology Disorders

The final piece of the puzzle was a monoallelic variant in the *PARN* gene. This placed the patient in the category of Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs). 

To understand why this matters, we have to look at telomeres—the protective caps on our chromosomes. In TBDs, mutations in genes like *TERT*, *TERC*, or *PARN* impair the maintenance of these caps. Every time a cell divides, the telomeres shorten. When they hit a critical threshold, the cell enters senescence or dies. This explains why TBDs hit "high-turnover" tissues first: bone marrow (pancytopenia), skin and hair (premature aging), and the liver and lungs (fibrosis).

While the patient's pancytopenia was likely exacerbated by her B12 deficiency (which creates a "double hit" on the marrow), the *PARN* mutation provided the underlying vulnerability. The mutation made her hematopoietic stem cells more susceptible to stress, while simultaneously driving the progressive fibrosis in her lungs and liver.

## Clinical Pearls: Avoiding the Trap

This case serves as a powerful reminder that a lab result is an observation, not necessarily a diagnosis. If the clinical "story" doesn't match the "correction," you haven't found the primary driver.

*   **Trust the Clinical Story:** If a patient’s functional decline remains severe after "correcting" a nutritional deficiency, the deficiency is likely a symptom, not the cause.
*   **Look for "Premature Aging" Markers:** In a young adult, signs like premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, or significant osteoporosis should trigger a high suspicion for genetic syndromes, specifically TBDs.
*   **Think "High Turnover" Tissues:** When you see multi-organ involvement involving the marrow, skin, and fibrotic changes in the liver or lungs, start thinking about telomere biology.
*   **The "Double Hit" Phenomenon:** Genetic predispositions (like TBDs) can stay "silent" until an environmental or nutritional stressor (like B12 deficiency) pushes the system over the edge.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **Beware of Diagnostic Anchoring:** A glaring lab abnormality (like B12 deficiency) can mask a more complex underlying pathology.
*   **Persistent Symptoms = Incomplete Diagnosis:** If clinical improvement doesn't track with laboratory "correction," you must broaden your differential.
*   **TBDs are Multisystemic:** Telomere Biology Disorders can present without the "classic" triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita (nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, skin pigmentation). Look for isolated pulmonary fibrosis, bone marrow failure, or hepatic fibrosis in young adults.
*   **PARN and Fibrosis:** While *PARN* mutations are frequently associated with Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF), they can also present with hepatic fibrosis and hematologic issues.
