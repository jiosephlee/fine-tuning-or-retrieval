Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] Which clinical features or complications in a pregnant patient provide a sufficient rationale for surgical intervention on a uterine leiomyoma, despite the risks of laparotomy and preterm delivery?

Surgical intervention for uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy is generally avoided due to the inherent risks of hemorrhage, uterine rupture, and preterm labor. However, as illustrated in this case, a multidisciplinary team may justify surgical management when specific clinical thresholds are met. The primary indications for surgical intervention include:

1.  **Acute Mechanical Complications:** The presence of acute complications such as torsion, infarction, or rupture of a pedunculated leiomyoma necessitates urgent intervention. In this report, the upper 18 cm mass was found to be "initially twisted and leaking," which likely contributed to the patient's fever and acute symptoms.
2.  **Significant Maternal Morbidity:** Severe clinical symptoms that cannot be managed conservatively are a primary indication for surgery. This patient presented with severe abdominal pain (VAS 5) and, crucially, "restrictive lung disease dyspnoea" caused by the "steric bulk" of the massive lesions compressing the diaphragm. When a mass compromises maternal respiratory function or causes intractable pain, surgical decompression becomes necessary.
3.  **Diagnostic Uncertainty and Oncological Suspicion:** When the imaging characteristics (e.g., inhomogeneous fluid content, thick walls) and laboratory trends (e.g., the rising CA-125 levels seen here) suggest a potential malignancy or require definitive differentiation from adnexal masses like endometriomas or ovarian cancer, surgery may be indicated to establish a conclusive diagnosis.
4.  **Risk of Impaired Fetal Growth:** Extremely large masses that occupy significant abdominal space may limit the physiological enlargement of the uterus. Surgery may be considered if the mass poses a risk of hypoxia or prevents adequate space for fetal growth.

In such cases, the management principle is to be as conservative as possible—performing a "selective" myomectomy. In this instance, the surgical team opted to excise only the symptomatic, twisted cranial mass while leaving the caudal mass *in situ* to minimize the risk of uterine injury and preserve the pregnancy. These complex cases require a balance of obstetric risks against the immediate clinical threats to the mother.
