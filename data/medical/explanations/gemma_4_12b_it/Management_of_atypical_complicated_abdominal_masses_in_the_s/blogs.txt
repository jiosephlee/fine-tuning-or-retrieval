Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# The Great Mimickers: Distinguishing Cystic Leiomyomas from Endometriomas in Pregnancy

Imagine a patient in her second trimester of pregnancy, presenting with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. On physical exam, the abdomen is significantly more enlarged than expected for 21 weeks, with a taut, tender 18 cm mass occupying the upper left quadrant. Your mind immediately goes to the "usual" suspects of pregnancy-related pelvic pathology: an ovarian cyst or perhaps a massive endometrioma. 

This is the classic diagnostic trap of the "great mimickers." In this case, the patient—a woman in her 40s—presented with what appeared to be two huge decidualized endometriomas. However, the surgical reality provided a different story: these were actually massive, cystic, and pedunculated uterine leiomyomas. This case serves as a high-yield reminder of how easily uterine pathology can masquerade as adnexal disease, and how our management must shift when "steric bulk" meets clinical complication.

## The Diagnostic Maze: Imaging and Biomarkers

When faced with large abdominal masses in pregnancy, the diagnostic toolkit is intentionally restricted to prioritize fetal safety. We rely on clinical examination, ultrasound (US) with color Doppler, and non-contrast MRI. In this case, the ultrasound initially suggested two unilocular cystic masses. While the caudal mass seemed adherent to a posterior myoma, the "cranial" mass (18 cm) was so large that a clear connective stalk or feeding vessel couldn't be identified due to the sheer volume of the gravid uterus.

Then there is the CA-125 dilemma. We know that CA-125 levels fluctuate physiologically during pregnancy, typically peaking in the first and third trimesters. In this patient, the CA-125 was slightly elevated (41.8 U/mL) with an increasing trend. While this might lean toward an endometrioma or even an oncological process, it is notoriously unreliable in the context of pregnancy and should never be the primary driver of surgical decision-making.

To get a clearer picture, the team utilized a non-contrast MRI (staying under 1.5T to ensure safety). The MRI revealed thick walls (1.4 cm) and inhomogeneous fluid content with focal solid components. While these features are common to both cystic leiomyomas and complex endometriomas, the "steric bulk" of the masses was the most pressing clinical concern, as they were causing mechanical compression and restrictive-type dyspnea.

## Surgical Reality: When "Watchful Waiting" Isn't Enough

The standard of care for most uterine myomas during pregnancy is expectant management. However, the management calculus changes when the patient becomes symptomatic or when there is a high risk of acute complications like torsion or necrosis. 

In this case, the combination of severe pain, fever (suggesting possible infection or ischemia), and significant respiratory distress from mechanical compression necessitated a laparotomy. The surgical "reveal" was striking: the cranial mass was a huge, pedunculated, atypical cystic leiomyoma. It was twisted and leaking, likely the source of the patient’s fever. 

The surgical team performed a delicate myomectomy on the twisted mass while leaving the caudal mass in situ. The goal was clear: alleviate the immediate mechanical and ischemic complications while minimizing the risk of uterine rupture or preterm labor. The patient’s subsequent recovery—including management of iron deficiency anemia and a successful elective caesarean delivery—highlights the importance of a multidisciplinary approach that balances maternal morbidity against obstetric risk.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Don't let the "Cystic" label fool you:** Large uterine leiomyomas can undergo cystic degeneration, making them nearly indistinguishable from adnexal cysts on initial ultrasound.
*   **CA-125 is messy in pregnancy:** Because CA-125 rises physiologically in the first and third trimesters, an isolated elevation in the second trimester is non-specific. Use it for trends, not for definitive diagnosis.
*   **MRI is your best friend (without the contrast):** In cases of large, complex masses where ultrasound is inconclusive, non-contrast MRI is the gold standard for determining the distance between a mass and the uterine cavity or identifying solid components.
*   **Identify the "Red Flags" for surgery:** While conservative management is the default, the presence of torsion, necrosis (suggested by fever and acute pain), or significant mechanical compromise (like severe dyspnea) justifies surgical intervention.
*   **Consider the "Steric Bulk":** In obese patients or those with multiple large masses, the anatomical distortion can make identifying a pedunculated stalk difficult on imaging, increasing the suspicion for a uterine origin.

**Key Take-Home Messages:**
*   Uterine leiomyomas can mimic adnexal masses (endometriomas/ovarian cysts) due to cystic degeneration.
*   Clinical symptoms (pain, fever, dyspnea) should drive the decision for surgical intervention over expectant management.
*   Non-contrast MRI is a safe and essential tool for characterizing large pelvic masses in pregnancy.
*   Surgical management should be as conservative as possible, aiming only to resolve the immediate complication while preserving the pregnancy.



# Navigating the CA-125 Conundrum: Tumor Markers in the Gravid Patient

If there is one thing we know about clinical medicine, it’s that pregnancy throws every physiological "rule" out the window. When a pregnant patient presents with a concerning clinical finding—like a massive abdominal mass—the standard diagnostic algorithms we use for the general population can quickly become a minefield of false positives and misleading trends. This is particularly true when we start looking at tumor markers like CA-125.

In a recent case involving a woman in her 40s at 21 weeks gestation, the clinical picture was striking: she presented with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. Physical exam revealed an 18 cm taut, elastic mass in the left subphrenic space and a 9 cm fixed mass in the pelvis. Ultrasound and non-contrast MRI identified two large cystic masses. The diagnostic dilemma was immediate: were these decidualized endometriomas, cystic degenerating leiomyomas, or something more sinister?

The lab work provided a piece of the puzzle that was both helpful and frustrating. The patient’s CA-125 was 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL), showing a slight upward trend from 36 U/mL just two weeks prior. In a non-pregnant patient, this "slight" elevation over the cutoff might trigger an intensive workup for ovarian malignancy. However, in the context of a second-trimester pregnancy, CA-125 is notoriously fickle.

We have to remember that CA-125 levels physiologically fluctuate during gestation. Research shows that CA-125 typically reaches two peaks—one in the first trimester and another in the third—with lower median values observed during the second trimester. Because these levels can rise due to benign conditions like endometriosis, adenomyosis, and even the physiological changes of pregnancy itself, an isolated CA-125 measurement is rarely diagnostic. In this case, the slight elevation was high enough to be noted, but it wasn't the primary driver of the management plan.

Ultimately, the decision to proceed with a laparotomy was driven by the "steric bulk" and clinical symptoms. The patient was experiencing significant pain and restrictive dyspnea caused by mechanical compression from the masses. The risk of torsion, rupture, or infection from the large, vascularized masses outweighed the risks of surgical intervention. Surgery revealed a huge, pedunculated, twisted, and leaking leiomyoma—a find that perfectly explains the patient's fever and acute pain, but one that highlights how easily these masses can mimic adnexal pathology on imaging.

When managing these complex scenarios, the goal is to balance obstetric safety with the necessity of treating acute complications. The multidisciplinary team’s approach—utilizing non-contrast MRI (to avoid gadolinium), serial ultrasound to monitor fetal heart rate, and proactive progesterone prophylaxis—serves as a blueprint for managing large masses in pregnancy.

**Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points:**

*   **Context is King:** Never interpret tumor markers in pregnancy in a vacuum. CA-125 fluctuates physiologically; isolated values are rarely diagnostic, and serial trends are more informative.
*   **Imaging Priorities:** In pregnancy, ultrasound with color Doppler is your first line. If further characterization is needed, non-contrast MRI is the gold standard for determining the distance between a mass and the uterine cavity while avoiding gadolinium.
*   **Symptom-Driven Management:** While conservative management is usually preferred for uterine myomas in pregnancy, surgical intervention is indicated for complicated masses (torsion, suspected necrosis, or severe mechanical symptoms).
*   **The "Mimic" Factor:** Large leiomyomas with cystic degeneration can closely mimic ovarian masses or endometriomas on imaging. Always maintain a broad differential that includes benign uterine pathology.
*   **Multidisciplinary Approach:** These cases require a tight loop between surgery, gynecology, and obstetrics to minimize maternal and fetal risk during operative management.



# Surgical Triage: When to Intervene on Symptomatic Abdominal Masses

Imagine a patient who presents to the emergency department in her second trimester of pregnancy. She is in significant pain—a VAS of 5 in both the upper left quadrant and the pelvis—and she’s struggling to catch her breath. You perform a primary survey: her oxygen saturation is 97%, her heart rate is stable, and her ECG is normal. You realize her dyspnea isn't a primary pulmonary or cardiac issue; it is "steric bulk." The massive growth of her abdomen is mechanically compressing her diaphragm. 

This is the clinical dilemma of the symptomatic abdominal mass in pregnancy. When does the risk of a mass—be it torsion, ischemia, or mechanical compromise—outweigh the inherent risks of surgical intervention during a viable pregnancy?

## The Diagnostic Maze

In this case, a nulliparous woman in her 40s presented at 21 weeks gestation with two massive abdominal lesions. One was a 18cm mass occupying the subphrenic space, and the other was a 9.5cm mass in the pouch of Douglas. The clinical picture was complicated by a low-grade fever and severe pain. 

The diagnostic workup highlights a common pitfall: the interpretation of tumor markers. While her CA-125 was elevated (41.8 U/mL), we have to remember that CA-125 levels physiologically rise during the first and third trimesters. An isolated measurement is rarely diagnostic. Furthermore, ultrasound and MRI (non-contrast) are our primary tools here. Because gadolinium is generally avoided in pregnancy unless absolutely necessary, non-contrast MRI became the gold standard for determining the relationship between these masses and the uterine cavity.

The differential diagnosis was a toss-up between huge decidualised endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus makes it incredibly difficult to visualize the origin of these masses, but the presence of a solid component on the caudal mass pointed toward a myomatous origin.

## The Triage: When to Cut?

The decision to move from expectant management to the operating room is rarely easy in obstetrics. We generally prefer a "watchful waiting" approach because of the risks of hemorrhage and preterm labor. However, this patient presented with three "red flags" that shifted the needle toward surgery:

1.  **Mechanical Compromise:** The sheer size of the masses was causing restrictive-type dyspnea.
2.  **Suspected Torsion/Ischemia:** The combination of acute pain and low-grade fever suggested that the cranial mass—a pedunculated leiomyoma—had likely twisted on its stalk and was beginning to necrose.
3.  **Predictable Growth:** Leiomyomas tend to grow during pregnancy (roughly 6.9% per week in the first and second trimesters). Waiting might only make the mechanical and vascular complications worse.

The surgical team opted for a laparotomy rather than a laparoscopic approach. While laparoscopy is often preferred for smaller, isolated myomas, the "steric bulk," the numerous masses, and their intramural/posterior locations made a longitudinal xiphos-pubic cut the safer route to ensure fetal monitoring and manage the massive size of the lesions.

## The Outcome and Take-Home Points

The surgery revealed the cranial mass was indeed a huge, vascularized, pedunculated leiomyoma that was twisted and leaking. By excising only the symptomatic, twisted mass and leaving the caudal mass in situ, the team balanced the need for maternal decompression with the need to minimize obstetric risk. The patient had a successful outcome, and an elective caesarean was planned later to navigate the remaining pelvic mass.

**Clinical Pearls:**

*   **Recognize "Steric Bulk":** If a pregnant patient has dyspnea but normal vitals and clear lungs, look for mechanical compression from large abdominal masses.
*   **MRI Safety:** Non-contrast MRI is a safe and highly effective modality for characterizing pelvic masses in pregnancy when ultrasound is inconclusive.
*   **CA-125 Context:** Always interpret CA-125 in the context of the gestational age; isolated elevations are common in normal pregnancy and other benign conditions like endometriosis.
*   **Surgical Indications:** Consider surgical intervention for abdominal masses in pregnancy when there is evidence of torsion, ischemia, infection, or significant mechanical respiratory compromise.
*   **Tailor the Approach:** While laparoscopy is often the "go-to," large, multiple, or intramural masses may necessitate a laparotomy to ensure maternal safety and fetal viability.



# Imaging Safety: Maximizing Yield with Non-Contrast MRI in Pregnancy

Imagine a woman in her second trimester of pregnancy, presenting with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. On physical exam, she has a "taut-elastic" mass in the upper quadrant so large it’s actually compromising her breathing. Your first thought is likely an abdominal aortic aneurysm or a massive malignancy, but in a pregnant patient, the differential quickly narrows toward complicated uterine leiomyomas or large adnexal masses. 

The real challenge here isn't just the diagnosis—it's the "how." How do you get the high-resolution clarity needed to plan a safe surgical intervention when the standard toolkit of gadolinium-enhanced MRI is off the table? This case highlights the art and science of maximizing diagnostic yield using non-contrast MRI and ultrasound to navigate the complexities of "steric bulk" in pregnancy.

## The Diagnostic Workflow: Ultrasound First

In the pregnant patient, ultrasound remains our frontline workhorse. It is safe, accessible, and excellent at identifying symptomatic uterine leiomyomas, particularly those located on the anterior wall or those that are pedunculated. In this case, the ultrasound initially suggested two huge cystic masses—potentially decidualized endometriomas. 

However, ultrasound has its limits when masses are massive or overlapping. When we see "atypical" presentations—such as a mass that seems to occupy the entire subphrenic space or one that lacks a clear connective stalk—we need to move to the next level of imaging.

## The Power of Non-Contrast MRI

When the clinical picture remains murky, MRI is the gold standard for determining the exact spatial relationship between a mass and the uterine cavity. While we generally avoid gadolinium due to potential risks of stillbirth or neonatal death, non-contrast MRI is a remarkably potent tool.

To maximize yield without contrast, the focus shifts to specific weighting:
*   **T1 and T2 fat-sat weighted images:** These are essential for identifying inhomogeneous fluid content and solid components.
*   **Spatial Mapping:** MRI allows us to see exactly how far a myoma is from the uterine cavity, which is critical for deciding if a mass is intramural, subserosal, or an independent adnexal lesion.

In this case, the non-contrast MRI was the turning point. It clarified that the caudal mass was actually in continuity with a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration. It moved the needle from "uncertain adnexal cyst" toward "complex uterine mass," which is a vital distinction for surgical planning.

## Navigating the "Steric Bulk" and Tumor Markers

One of the most interesting aspects of this case was the patient’s dyspnea. It wasn't a primary pulmonary issue; it was "steric bulk"—mechanical compression of the diaphragm by the sheer volume of the abdominal masses. When a mass is large enough to compromise respiratory mechanics or cause acute pain, the balance shifts toward surgical intervention.

We also have to be careful with serum tumor markers. While the patient’s CA-125 was slightly elevated (41.8 U/mL), we must remember that CA-125 levels fluctuate physiologically during pregnancy, typically peaking in the first and third trimesters. An isolated measurement in the second trimester is rarely diagnostic. Instead, we look for *trends*. The slight upward trend in this patient, combined with the "bulky" MRI findings, helped tip the scales toward a more aggressive surgical evaluation.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points

*   **Ultrasound is the primary screen:** Use it to identify myoma location, size, and proximity to the placenta.
*   **Non-contrast MRI is a safe powerhouse:** When contrast is contraindicated, leverage T1 and T2 fat-sat weighting to differentiate between myomatous origin and adnexal masses.
*   **Contextualize CA-125:** Remember that CA-125 is not a standalone diagnostic tool in pregnancy; look for serial trends rather than isolated values.
*   **Identify "Steric Bulk":** Recognize that massive abdominal masses can cause restrictive-type dyspnea through mechanical compression, independent of pulmonary pathology.
*   **Surgical Threshold:** Consider surgical intervention for large symptomatic masses when there is a high risk of complication (torsion, rupture, infection) or when the mass compromises maternal physiology (e.g., respiratory distress).



# From Myomectomy to Delivery: Obstetric Planning for Large Uterine Masses

When we encounter a patient in the second trimester with massive abdominal masses and acute pain, the immediate "fire" is surgical stabilization. But for the obstetrician and the surgical team, the "fire" doesn't go out once the patient is stabilized in the recovery room. The management of a large uterine mass during pregnancy is a marathon, not a sprint. Once the immediate surgical emergency is resolved, the focus must immediately pivot to a long-term obstetric roadmap.

In this case, a 40-year-old woman at 21 weeks gestation presented with severe pain and dyspnoea—the latter likely caused by the "steric bulk" of two massive abdominal lesions. While the upper, pedunculated mass was a surgical priority due to signs of torsion and leakage, the caudal mass remained in situ. This decision was a calculated risk: prioritizing the immediate safety of the pregnancy over a more extensive, potentially morbid surgery. However, leaving a 7.5 cm mass in the pelvis creates a significant logistical challenge for the final weeks of gestation.

## The Pelvic "Speed Bump": Mechanics of Labor
The primary concern with a large residual pelvic myoma is its role as a mechanical obstacle. A mass occupying the pouch of Douglas or the lesser pelvis can significantly impede the descent of the fetal head during the expulsive phase of labor. Even if the myoma is relatively soft or cystic, its sheer size can lead to obstructed labor, increased risk of maternal exhaustion, and potential fetal distress. 

Furthermore, the surgical history of the patient adds another layer of complexity. Having undergone a longitudinal xiphos-pubic laparotomy to address the upper mass, the risk of uterine scar rupture during a trial of labor is elevated. In many cases of large, centrally or posteriorly located myomas, the combination of mechanical obstruction and the previous surgical incision makes a vaginal delivery—even with adequate analgesia—high-risk.

## Planning the Exit Strategy
In this scenario, the gold standard is an elective caesarean delivery. The timing of this delivery is critical. While the goal is to reach a full-term pregnancy, the size and location of the mass dictate the timeline. In this case, the team opted for a 37-week delivery. 

Why 37 weeks? It balances the risks of prematurity against the risks of the mass becoming more symptomatic or causing further complications as the uterus continues to expand. It is also important to note that myomas do not follow a linear growth trajectory; they can increase in size significantly in the early trimesters and may even undergo some regression or stabilization in the third trimester. However, relying on "shrinkage" is not a safe strategy for delivery planning.

## Monitoring and Maintenance
Between the myomectomy and the delivery, the "maintenance" phase of management is vital. This includes:
*   **Serial Ultrasound:** Monitoring the size and location of the residual mass in relation to the placenta.
*   **Fetal Growth Tracking:** Ensuring that the massive abdominal lesions aren't compromising uterine blood flow or fetal nutrition. In this case, the fetus maintained a healthy 86th percentile growth.
*   **Hematologic Support:** Post-operative management of anemia (as seen with the patient’s Hb dropping to 7.5 g/L) is essential to ensure the mother has the physiological reserve to carry the pregnancy to term.

## Clinical Pearls
*   **Surgery is the Midpoint:** A successful myomectomy in the second trimester is only the first half of the management plan. The obstetric plan must be finalized immediately post-operatively.
*   **Identify the Obstacle:** Any pelvic mass >5 cm should be considered a potential mechanical obstruction to the birth canal.
*   **Elective C-Section is Often Mandatory:** Given the risk of obstructed labor from large masses and the risk of scar rupture from previous laparotomy, elective C-section is generally the safest route for the mother and neonate.
*   **Multidisciplinary Communication:** The transition from "surgical emergency" to "obstetric follow-up" requires seamless communication between the gynecologic surgeon and the obstetrician to ensure the delivery plan accounts for both the residual mass and the surgical scar.
