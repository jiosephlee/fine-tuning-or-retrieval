Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment of Abdominal Masses in Pregnancy

The clinical presentation of an abdominal mass during pregnancy requires a high index of suspicion and a systematic approach to differentiate between normal gestational growth, benign gynecological pathologies, and acute surgical emergencies. In this case, the patient presented in her second trimester (21 weeks gestation) with acute, severe abdominal pain and mild dyspnoea. In a pregnant patient, sudden-onset pain must immediately prompt a search for complications such as uterine rupture, placental abruption, or, as seen here, complications related to large adnexal or uterine masses, such as torsion, rupture, or necrosis.

A critical component of the initial assessment is distinguishing the etiology of respiratory distress. The patient’s dyspnoea was characterized as "mechanical" rather than primary pulmonary or cardiac dysfunction. In the presence of massive abdominal lesions—in this instance, masses measuring 18 cm and 9.5 cm—the sheer "steric bulk" of the pathology can physically restrict diaphragmatic excursion. Clinicians must perform a thorough thoracic examination, including heart rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation, to rule out primary respiratory failure before attributing dyspnoea to mechanical compression from external masses.

Physical examination serves as a cornerstone for localizing the pathology. While the abdomen of a woman in her second trimester is expected to be enlarged, the presence of a "taut-elastic" mass that is disproportionate to the gestational age suggests an underlying abnormality. The objective description of the mass—tender, firm, and occupying a specific quadrant—helps narrow the differential. Furthermore, a bimanual vaginal examination is essential to assess the mobility and relationship of pelvic masses to the uterus and adnexa. In this case, the identification of a "fixed" hard mass in the lesser pelvis, bulging at the posterior fornix, provided vital clues regarding the mass's proximity to the uterine wall and its potential origin.

Initial laboratory investigations in a pregnant patient must be interpreted with an understanding of physiological changes. While the patient exhibited a neutrophilic count and a slight increase in C-reactive protein (CRP), these findings can often be attributed to the inflammatory state of pregnancy. However, when paired with systemic symptoms like a low-grade fever and acute pain, these markers become more significant. The clinical team must also account for common pregnancy-related complications, such as iron deficiency anaemia (noted here with a hemoglobin of 9.9 g/L and low ferritin). In this case, the laboratory workup also served to rule out concurrent infections, such as the detected *Klebsiella pneumoniae* urinary tract infection, which could otherwise complicate the surgical management of the abdominal masses.

Ultimately, the presence of systemic symptoms like fever and escalating pain in the context of a large, vascularized abdominal mass is a major clinical "red flag." These symptoms suggest acute complications such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma or infection of a cystic structure. For the resident, this highlights the necessity of rapid multidisciplinary evaluation: the goal is to stabilize the patient, manage acute pain, and determine if the mass represents a stable condition requiring expectant management or an acute complication necessitating urgent surgical intervention.

## Diagnostic Imaging and Differential Diagnosis

The diagnostic evaluation of large abdominal masses during pregnancy is complicated by the physiological expansion of the uterus and the relative limitations on imaging modalities. In this case, the clinical presentation of acute pain and dyspnoea necessitated a rapid but cautious diagnostic pathway. Ultrasound (US) served as the primary imaging tool, revealing two large unilocular cystic masses. A critical diagnostic challenge in such cases is differentiating between adnexal masses, such as ovarian cysts or endometriomas, and uterine leiomyomas that have undergone cystic degeneration. While the upper mass (18 cm) appeared somewhat independent, the lower mass (9.5 cm) was notably adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. The presence of cystic degeneration within a myoma can mimic the appearance of an adnexal cyst on ultrasound, as both can present as fluid-filled structures with regular margins and peripheral vascularization.

When ultrasound provides insufficient clarity regarding the exact origin or relationship of a mass to the uterus, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is an essential next step. In pregnancy, MRI is a safe and highly effective modality provided it is performed without gadolinium contrast, which is generally avoided due to potential risks to the fetus. In this patient, non-contrast MRI was utilized to better localize the lesions. The MRI revealed thick walls (1.4 cm) and focal hyperintense solid components, which are important markers for characterizing the complexity of the masses. Crucially, MRI helped determine the continuity of the caudal mass with the posterior leiomyoma. However, the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus and the sheer size of the masses hindered the identification of a connecting stalk for the upper mass, leaving its exact origin—whether ovarian or uterine—ambiguous.

The interpretation of serum tumor markers, specifically CA-125, requires a nuanced understanding of pregnancy physiology. CA-125 levels typically exhibit a "bimodal" trend, rising during the first and third trimesters and remaining lower during the second trimester. In this case, the patient’s CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL was higher than the expected median for the second trimester. While isolated measurements are often non-diagnostic due to these physiological fluctuations, a rising trend in the second trimester can provide a clinical hint toward pathological processes, such as endometrioma or, less commonly, malignancy. In this patient, the slight elevation favored an endometrioma for the upper cyst but did not provide a definitive diagnosis, necessitating a correlation with imaging and clinical findings.

The final differential diagnosis was narrowed down to decidualised endometriomas versus cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The upper mass’s appearance and the slight CA-125 elevation pointed toward a decidualised endometrioma. Conversely, the lower mass's anatomical continuity with a known posterior myoma strongly suggested a myomatous origin. The diagnostic reasoning in this case relied on integrating these conflicting cues: the physical symptoms (dyspnoea caused by mechanical compression), the imaging characteristics (thick walls and solid components), and the anatomical limitations imposed by the enlarging uterus. This highlights a common clinical dilemma in obstetric surgery where the goal is to differentiate between an adnexal pathology and a complex uterine mass to determine the safest surgical approach.

## Pathophysiology and Management Decision-Making

The management of uterine leiomyomas (fibroids) during pregnancy requires a nuanced understanding of how these tumors interact with the physiological changes of gestation. While leiomyomas are benign, their growth patterns and potential for acute complications can significantly impact maternal and fetal outcomes. Understanding the underlying pathophysiology of these masses is essential for determining when conservative management is sufficient and when surgical intervention becomes a clinical necessity.

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear growth trend throughout pregnancy, primarily driven by increased levels of estrogen and progesterone. Research indicates that fibroids grow at an average rate of approximately 6.90% per week. Notably, this growth is most aggressive during the first and second trimesters, with a significant increase observed between the first and second trimesters (approximately 6.12%) compared to a slower rate in the third trimester (approximately 3.10%). For the clinician, this means that a mass that appears manageable in early pregnancy may expand rapidly, leading to escalating symptoms or mechanical complications as the gestational age progresses.

When a leiomyoma is pedunculated—attached to the uterus by a stalk—it is susceptible to torsion, which is a primary driver of acute clinical deterioration. In this case, the cranial mass was found to be twisted and leaking, a condition that triggers a cascade of ischemia, infarction, and necrotic degeneration. Pathophysiologically, torsion compromises the blood supply, leading to tissue death and potential infection, which may manifest clinically as acute abdominal pain and fever. These complications necessitate prompt intervention to prevent peritonitis or systemic sepsis, especially when the mass is large enough to cause significant mechanical stress, such as the restrictive lung disease (dyspnea) seen in this patient due to diaphragmatic compression.

The decision-making process in these scenarios involves a careful balance between obstetric risks and maternal complications. The primary goal is to preserve the pregnancy while mitigating life-threatening symptoms. Conservative measures, such as the administration of analgesics and the use of semi-orthostatic positioning, are often the first line of defense. Positioning can temporarily alleviate symptoms by reducing the mechanical pressure of the masses on the diaphragm. However, when conservative management fails to control pain, or when there is a high risk of rupture, torsion, or infection, the multidisciplinary team must consider surgical intervention.

Surgical criteria are generally met when a mass is symptomatic, shows signs of acute complication (like the torsion seen here), or presents a significant risk to fetal development. A critical factor in this assessment is the "steric bulk" of the masses. As the uterus expands to accommodate the growing fetus, large intramural or subserosal myomas can limit the available space for uterine enlargement, potentially leading to fetal hypoxia or abnormal growth. In this case, the presence of two massive, vascularized lesions—one of which was symptomatic and potentially undergoing necrosis—provided the clinical rationale for a laparotomy. By excising only the pedunculated, twisted mass while leaving the fixed caudal mass in situ, the surgical team aimed to resolve the acute maternal crisis while minimizing the risk of uterine rupture or preterm labor.

## Surgical Management and Intraoperative Safety

In the management of large abdominal masses during pregnancy, the standard of care typically favors expectant management. However, surgical intervention becomes necessary when clinical complications arise that threaten maternal health or fetal viability. In this case, the decision to proceed with a laparotomy at 21 weeks of gestation was driven by the patient’s worsening clinical state: severe abdominal pain and restrictive dyspnoea caused by the "steric bulk" of the masses. Surgery was also indicated to provide a definitive diagnosis, as the slightly elevated CA-125 and the indeterminate imaging features necessitated a distinction between benign leiomyomas and potential malignancy. Furthermore, the risk of acute complications, such as torsion, rupture, or infection of the large masses, necessitated a proactive surgical approach to stabilize the patient.

The surgical technique employed in this case highlights the principle of "minimal intervention" required to preserve the pregnancy. The surgical team performed a longitudinal xiphos-pubic laparotomy to gain adequate exposure to the large masses. Upon exploration, the cranial mass was identified as a pedunculated, atypical cystic leiomyoma that was twisted and leaking. Because this mass was the primary source of the patient’s acute symptoms (pain and fever), it was surgically excised. In contrast, the caudal mass, which appeared fixed and adherent to the posterior uterine wall, was left in situ. This selective management was a deliberate choice to minimize surgical trauma to the uterus, thereby reducing the risk of excessive hemorrhage or uterine wall compromise, which is critical during the second trimester.

Intraoperative safety during pregnancy requires rigorous fetal monitoring. Standard protocols dictate that fetal cardiac activity must be documented before, during, and after the procedure to ensure the fetus is not compromised by anesthesia or surgical manipulation. In this case, the team verified fetal heart activity immediately upon entering the abdominal cavity and maintained monitoring throughout the two-hour and 25-minute procedure. This ensures that any acute fetal distress can be identified and addressed immediately.

Pharmacological support is an essential component of perioperative care in this scenario. To minimize the risk of preterm labor or pregnancy loss following uterine manipulation, the patient received progesterone prophylaxis. This was initiated intraoperatively and continued through the postpartum period. Additionally, because the patient had a pre-existing *Klebsiella pneumoniae* urinary tract infection and the upper myoma was found to be leaking, a targeted antibiotic regimen (cefazolin and clarithromycin) was administered. This dual approach addressed both the systemic infection and the potential for peritonitis resulting from the necrotic, twisted myoma.

Postoperative management focused on stabilizing the patient’s hemodynamic status and addressing the complications of the surgery. The patient developed significant acute anemia (Hb 7.5 g/L) following the myomectomy, likely due to the high vascularity of the excised mass. This was managed promptly with intravenous iron (ferric carboxymaltose) and blood transfusions. By monitoring the patient's hemoglobin levels and ensuring adequate oxygenation and hydration, the clinical team was able to manage the immediate surgical fallout, allowing for a successful transition to obstetric follow-up and a subsequent elective caesarean delivery.

## Obstetric Outcomes and Long-Term Management

The management of a patient with large uterine masses during pregnancy requires a transition from acute surgical stabilization to long-term obstetric planning. In this case, the decision-making process for delivery was dictated by the anatomical location and "steric bulk" of the residual caudal mass. Although the upper pedunculated leiomyoma was excised to alleviate acute symptoms (pain and dyspnoea), the lower mass was left in situ to minimize surgical risks during the second trimester. However, because this mass was a large, fixed, posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma, it presented a significant mechanical obstacle. Such masses can impede the descent of the fetal head during the expulsive phase of labor, increasing the risk of obstructed labor or uterine rupture. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team determined that a vaginal delivery was not feasible, necessitating a planned obstetric intervention.

To mitigate the risks associated with the residual mass, the patient was scheduled for an elective caesarean section at 37 weeks of gestation. Selecting the timing of the delivery is critical; waiting until term ensures fetal maturity while providing a window to manage the patient's clinical stability. From a surgical perspective, the team chose to perform the caesarean section through the existing longitudinal xiphos-pubic scar from the initial myomectomy. While reusing a previous incision site requires careful assessment of scar integrity, it is a recognized approach in complex cases where the primary goal is to navigate the anatomical challenges posed by large, adherent uterine lesions.

Pathological correlation provided definitive evidence for the clinical diagnosis, confirming that the masses were benign uterine leiomyomas. The "atypical" cystic appearance and the patient’s transient low-grade fever were explained by the surgical findings: the upper mass had a twisted pedicle, leading to ischaemic necrosis. Understanding these pathological changes is essential for residents, as it highlights how mechanical complications—such as torsion—can alter the sonographic and clinical presentation of even benign tumours. Furthermore, the case illustrates the physiological regression of leiomyomas following delivery. The patient’s residual mass decreased in size slightly postpartum, a common occurrence attributed to the rapid decline in circulating progesterone and estrogen levels after the placenta is delivered.

Long-term management for patients with residual pelvic masses focuses on symptom surveillance and patient-centered goals. In this case, the patient remained asymptomatic and was successfully breastfeeding, which influenced the decision to defer further surgical intervention. Clinical follow-up involves monitoring the size and symptoms of the residual mass, particularly if the patient desires future pregnancies or if the mass begins to cause compressive symptoms. Counseling should emphasize that while surgical removal of the remaining myoma is an option, a "watchful waiting" approach is often appropriate for asymptomatic, regressed masses in patients who have achieved their primary obstetric goals.
