Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 5: ## Obstetric Outcomes and Long-Term Management

The management of a patient with large uterine masses during pregnancy requires a transition from acute surgical stabilization to long-term obstetric planning. In this case, the decision-making process for delivery was dictated by the anatomical location and "steric bulk" of the residual caudal mass. Although the upper pedunculated leiomyoma was excised to alleviate acute symptoms (pain and dyspnoea), the lower mass was left in situ to minimize surgical risks during the second trimester. However, because this mass was a large, fixed, posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma, it presented a significant mechanical obstacle. Such masses can impede the descent of the fetal head during the expulsive phase of labor, increasing the risk of obstructed labor or uterine rupture. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team determined that a vaginal delivery was not feasible, necessitating a planned obstetric intervention.

To mitigate the risks associated with the residual mass, the patient was scheduled for an elective caesarean section at 37 weeks of gestation. Selecting the timing of the delivery is critical; waiting until term ensures fetal maturity while providing a window to manage the patient's clinical stability. From a surgical perspective, the team chose to perform the caesarean section through the existing longitudinal xiphos-pubic scar from the initial myomectomy. While reusing a previous incision site requires careful assessment of scar integrity, it is a recognized approach in complex cases where the primary goal is to navigate the anatomical challenges posed by large, adherent uterine lesions.

Pathological correlation provided definitive evidence for the clinical diagnosis, confirming that the masses were benign uterine leiomyomas. The "atypical" cystic appearance and the patient’s transient low-grade fever were explained by the surgical findings: the upper mass had a twisted pedicle, leading to ischaemic necrosis. Understanding these pathological changes is essential for residents, as it highlights how mechanical complications—such as torsion—can alter the sonographic and clinical presentation of even benign tumours. Furthermore, the case illustrates the physiological regression of leiomyomas following delivery. The patient’s residual mass decreased in size slightly postpartum, a common occurrence attributed to the rapid decline in circulating progesterone and estrogen levels after the placenta is delivered.

Long-term management for patients with residual pelvic masses focuses on symptom surveillance and patient-centered goals. In this case, the patient remained asymptomatic and was successfully breastfeeding, which influenced the decision to defer further surgical intervention. Clinical follow-up involves monitoring the size and symptoms of the residual mass, particularly if the patient desires future pregnancies or if the mass begins to cause compressive symptoms. Counseling should emphasize that while surgical removal of the remaining myoma is an option, a "watchful waiting" approach is often appropriate for asymptomatic, regressed masses in patients who have achieved their primary obstetric goals.
