Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# When Angioedema Isn't Angioedema: Recognizing the Masquerade of Dermatomyositis

Imagine a 40-year-old man presenting to the emergency department with a three-week history of progressive, severe neck swelling. As a clinician, your "red flag" radar immediately starts pinging. Is it an escalating deep neck space infection like Ludwig’s angina? Or is it a life-threatening episode of idiopathic angioedema? 

In this case, the initial workup followed the classic trajectory: a CT scan ruled out an obvious abscess, and an ENT evaluation showed no structural obstructions. When the "obvious" answers were off the table, the suspicion shifted toward angioedema. However, there was a nagging clinical "itch"—the patient’s presentation didn't quite fit the mold of a typical histamine or bradykinin-mediated event. He had subtle skin changes, proximal muscle weakness, and a neck swelling that felt slightly different from the classic "fluid shift" of angioedema.

## The Diagnostic Pivot: When "Normal" Tests Suggest a Different Path

The shift from an ENT-led workup to a primary immunological one is a common crossroads in these cases. When a patient presents with significant edema but has normal C3 and C4 complement levels and normal C1 esterase function, the likelihood of hereditary or acquired angioedema drops significantly. At this point, the clinician must broaden the differential.

The "aha!" moment in this case came from looking beyond the neck. While the swelling was the most dramatic feature, it was the subtle findings—periungual erythema and a slight reduction in hip flexor strength—that provided the breadcrumbs. A single, high-yield laboratory test—a creatine kinase (CK) level—proved to be the game-changer. The patient’s CK was massive (over 16,000 U/L). 

This immediately shifted the narrative from a fluid-distribution problem to a primary inflammatory myositis. The "pseudoangioedema" wasn't caused by leaky vessels in the classic sense; it was the result of intense muscle inflammation involving the strap muscles and extending into the retropharyngeal space.

## The Masquerade of Dermatomyositis

Dermatomyositis is often visualized as a disease of proximal muscle weakness and classic "heliotrope" rashes. However, this case reminds us that it is a systemic inflammatory myopathy that can present in highly atypical ways. While only about 6% of dermatomyositis patients exhibit edema, when they do, it can completely overshadow the underlying myositis.

In this patient, the involvement was severe enough to cause significant pharyngeal dysphagia, requiring a nasogastric tube for several months. The underlying driver was an anti-SAE1 antibody. This specific antibody is often associated with a high risk of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease. Furthermore, because this patient met several high-risk criteria (dermatomyositis, dysphagia, and male sex), a rigorous screening for underlying malignancy became an essential part of the management plan.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points

This case serves as a powerful reminder that "pseudoangioedema" is a real clinical entity that can delay the diagnosis of serious systemic diseases. When the classic triggers for angioedema are absent, look closer at the rest of the body.

*   **Think broader for "idiopathic" edema:** If a patient presents with unexplained neck or facial swelling and the complement/C1 esterase tests are normal, consider inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis.
*   **Don't ignore the "subtle" signs:** Periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages, and mild proximal weakness are often the keys to unlocking a diagnosis that is being masked by more dramatic symptoms.
*   **The power of the CK:** In any patient with unexplained swelling and even a hint of muscle involvement, a creatine kinase level should be a priority. A massive elevation is a massive red flag for myositis.
*   **Acknowledge the risk of malignancy:** Patients with dermatomyositis—especially those with dysphagia or specific antibodies like anti-SAE1—are at a significantly increased risk for occult malignancy and require diligent screening.
*   **Treatment requires intensity:** Because of the risk of airway compromise and severe dysphagia, aggressive early treatment (high-dose steroids, IVIG, and steroid-sparing agents like mycophenolate) is often necessary to stabilize the patient.



# Look Closer at the Nails: Identifying Subtle Clues of Inflammatory Myopathy

Imagine a patient presenting to the Emergency Department with a three-week history of progressive, severe neck swelling. To the initial examiner, this looks like a classic "red flag" scenario: a potential deep neck space infection or, perhaps more likely given the lack of fever, an episode of idiopathic angioedema. This is the "Angioedema Trap"—a clinical presentation so common that it can easily lead us to overlook a far more systemic and potentially devastating underlying pathology.

In a recent case of a man in his 40s, this was exactly what happened. After ENT surgeons ruled out a deep neck space infection (like Ludwig’s angina), the patient was referred to immunology for suspected angioedema. However, something felt "off." The swelling was persistent, and the clinical picture didn't perfectly align with a standard histamine or bradykinin-mediated reaction. The pivot point in this case was a simple but critical "rule-out" test: a creatine kinase (CK) level.

When the CK returned at a staggering 16,184 U/L, the conversation shifted from "allergic reaction" to "inflammatory myopathy." The patient was subsequently diagnosed with anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis (DM). While DM is often associated with the classic heliotrope rash or Gottron’s papules, this patient lacked those hallmark features. Instead, his presentation was dominated by "pseudoangioedema"—subcutaneous edema caused by underlying muscle inflammation rather than an upper airway allergic response.

## The Art of the Subtle Physical Exam

This case serves as a powerful reminder that we cannot rely solely on "textbook" cutaneous findings to rule out dermatomyositis. When a patient presents with atypical swelling or proximal muscle weakness, we must train our eyes to look for the subtle markers that often go unnoticed in a fast-paced clinical setting.

One of the most underrated tools in the inflammatory myopathy toolkit is the **nailfold capillaroscopy**. In this case, even though the patient didn't have the classic rashes, capillaroscopy revealed proximal nailfold microhemorrhages and avascular areas. These are high-yield indicators of microvascular changes associated with systemic myositis. Similarly, a close inspection of the nail folds can reveal **periungual telangiectasias**—dilated capillaries that may be faint but are highly suggestive of DM.

We should also be looking for the **Holster sign**—erythema over the lateral thighs—and other less common cutaneous manifestations. In patients where the "classic" signs are absent, these subtle clues can be the difference between a misdiagnosis of angioedema and the timely initiation of aggressive immunosuppression.

## Why the Distinction Matters

The stakes in this case were high. Because the patient had significant muscle involvement and dysphagia (confirmed via barium swallow), he required immediate, high-dose intervention, including IV methylprednisolone and IVIG. Furthermore, identifying dermatomyositis early is crucial for cancer screening. This patient was classified as "high risk" for malignancy due to his age, sex, and the presence of dysphagia, necessitating a comprehensive screening workup including PET scans and multiple endoscopies.

When "idiopathic" angioedema doesn't quite fit the mold—especially when accompanied by even mild proximal weakness or a vague sense of malaise—stop and look closer. Check the nails, check the thighs, and always, always order a CK level.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Think Dermatomyositis in "Angioedema":** If neck or facial swelling is persistent, atypical in its progression, or associated with dysphagia, consider dermatomyositis as a primary differential, especially in the absence of a clear trigger.
*   **The CK "Pivot":** In any case of unexplained soft tissue edema or proximal weakness, a creatine kinase level is a low-cost, high-yield test that can quickly steer the diagnosis toward myositis.
*   **Look Beyond the Rash:** Do not rule out dermatomyositis simply because the heliotrope rash or Gottron’s papules are absent. Perform a detailed exam for periungual telangiectasias, nailfold microhemorrhages, and the Holster sign.
*   **The Malignancy Connection:** Dermatomyositis is frequently paraneoplastic. Patients with DM, particularly those with dysphagia or specific antibodies (like anti-SAE1), require a rigorous and ongoing malignancy screening protocol.
*   **Pseudoangioedema:** Recognize that subcutaneous edema in myositis is a result of muscle inflammation and won't respond to standard angioedema treatments (like ACE inhibitors or antihistamines); it requires systemic immunosuppression.



# The Diagnostic Pivot: Why 'Mystery' Swelling Demands a Creatine Kinase Level

Imagine a 40-year-old man presenting to your Emergency Department with a three-week history of worsening neck swelling and proximal weakness. Your first thought? Ludwig’s angina or a deep neck space infection. You order a CT, see the strap muscle edema, and initiate the "infection" workup. But he’s afebrile, his white cell count is normal, and the ENT surgeons find no anatomical abnormalities.

The diagnostic track shifts. You think: "Maybe it’s idiopathic angioedema." You check for ACE inhibitor use—none. You check for C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency—normal. You’re stuck in a clinical loop of "unexplained" swelling. This is the classic diagnostic trap: when a presentation doesn't fit the common buckets of infection or allergy, it’s easy to label it "idiopathic" and move on. But in this case, a single, high-yield lab test—the Creatine Kinase (CK)—was the "circuit breaker" that redirected the entire clinical pathway.

## The Trap of the "Standard Path"

In clinical practice, we are trained to prioritize the most common life-threats. For neck swelling, that’s airway compromise due to infection or anaphylaxis. When the imaging shows "edema" but the patient isn't "sick" enough for a severe infection, our brains naturally gravitate toward angioedema. 

However, the "pseudoangioedema" seen in dermatomyositis (DM) is a formidable masquerader. Because it involves inflammatory swelling of the soft tissues—often secondary to underlying muscle inflammation—it can look remarkably like a primary otolaryngology disorder. If we stop at the "vascular" explanation, we miss the systemic inflammatory myopathy that is actually destroying the patient's muscle tissue and risking his airway through dysphagia.

## The Pivot Point: Why CK Matters

The turning point in this case was the realization that the neck swelling was *atypical*. The patient had subtle nailfold changes and mild hip flexor weakness. This should have triggered a "look deeper" reflex. 

By ordering a CK level, the team moved the diagnostic needle from a fluid/vascular problem to a cellular/muscle problem. The result was a staggering 16,184 U/L. 

When you see a CK level this high in a patient with unexplained swelling and weakness, the diagnosis is no longer "idiopathic." You are now looking at an inflammatory myopathy. This single lab value effectively ruled out simple angioedema and forced the team to investigate autoimmune inflammatory myopathies (AIM). It led directly to the identification of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, a diagnosis that required intense immunosuppression rather than simple antihistamines or antibiotics.

## Looking Closer at the Skin and Nails

While the CK was the pivot, the physical exam provided the breadcrumbs. Dermatomyositis is often associated with specific cutaneous markers that can be easily overlooked in an acute setting. In this patient, periungual erythema and nailfold microhaemorrhages were subtle but critical. 

When "mystery" swelling presents, we must look for the "hidden" signs of systemic disease:
*   Is there a subtle rash on the face or hands?
*   Are there changes to the nail folds?
*   Is there a proximal muscle weakness that doesn't quite fit a neurological distribution?

Recognizing these signs allows you to move faster toward the CK level before the patient ends up in a "diagnostic limbo" of suspected angioedema.

## Take-Home Pearls

*   **Don't settle for "Idiopathic":** If a patient presents with significant soft tissue swelling that doesn't respond to standard infection or allergy workups, demand a deeper look at the underlying physiology.
*   **The CK "Circuit Breaker":** In any patient with unexplained swelling, periungual changes, or proximal weakness, a Creatine Kinase level is a high-yield, low-cost test that can pivot the diagnosis from "vascular" to "myopathic."
*   **Dermatomyositis is a Systemic Threat:** It is not just a skin condition. It can cause severe dysphagia, respiratory compromise, and internal malignancy. Early recognition is vital for initiating the necessary heavy-hitting immunosuppression (e.g., steroids, IVIG, and mycophenolate).
*   **Screen for Malignancy:** Patients with dermatomyositis, especially those with dysphagia or specific antibodies like SAE1, are at a high risk for underlying malignancy. Always include a comprehensive cancer screening in your workup.



# Dysphagia and the Danger of Aspiration: Managing the Respiratory Risks of Myositis

When we think of dermatomyositis, the clinical "hallmarks" usually involve skin changes like the heliotrope rash or Gottron’s papules, and perhaps some proximal muscle weakness in the hips and shoulders. It is often framed as a rheumatological or dermatological issue. However, as this case demonstrates, dermatomyositis can present with an acute, life-threatening complication that looks more like an ENT emergency or a primary airway problem: severe pharyngeal dysphagia and subsequent aspiration pneumonitis.

In this case, a man in his 40s presented with significant neck swelling. The initial suspicion was Ludwig’s angina or idiopathic angioedema. While the neck oedema was a striking physical finding, the underlying driver was actually severe muscle inflammation. The real "hidden" danger, however, was the patient's inability to swallow safely. A video fluoroscopic swallow study revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration into the vocal cords. This wasn't just a matter of "difficulty eating"—it was a direct pathway to pulmonary compromise.

The resulting aspiration pneumonitis, evidenced by patchy peribronchial consolidation on CT, highlights a critical teaching point: in patients with inflammatory myopathies, we must look beyond the "main" muscles. When the inflammation hits the muscles of the upper aerodigestive tract, the risk of aspiration becomes immediate. In this patient, the dysphagia was so profound that it necessitated the placement of a nasogastric (NG) tube to provide nutritional support during the acute inflammatory phase. This is a vital bridge; while we are "putting out the fire" with high-dose steroids and IVIG, the patient cannot be expected to maintain nutrition or safe swallows until the acute inflammation subsides.

Managing these patients requires a multi-pronged approach. First, aggressive immunosuppression is the priority to halt the underlying inflammatory process. In this case, the patient received a massive dose of IV methylprednisolone followed by IVIG and rituximab. Second, we must protect the airway and lungs. Recognizing the signs of aspiration early allows for prompt intervention—whether that is escalating steroids, managing the secretions, or ensuring the patient remains NPO (nothing by mouth) while an NG tube manages their caloric needs. Finally, we have to distinguish between aspiration and interstitial lung disease (ILD), which is another common pulmonary complication of dermatomyositis. In this case, the resolution of the opacities on follow-up imaging confirmed that the primary acute issue was aspiration-related.

**Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points:**

*   **Think Myositis in "Airway" Cases:** If a patient presents with unexplained neck swelling, dysphagia, or rapid-onset respiratory distress without a clear infectious source, include inflammatory myopathy in your differential, especially if there is any evidence of proximal muscle weakness.
*   **Assess Swallowing Early:** In patients with suspected myositis and facial/neck involvement, a swallow study (like video fluoroscopy) is essential to quantify the risk of aspiration.
*   **The NG Tube is a Tool, Not a Failure:** During the peak of an inflammatory flare, providing nutrition via an NG tube is a standard and necessary safety measure to prevent aspiration while waiting for the systemic inflammation to respond to treatment.
*   **Differentiate Aspiration from ILD:** While both can cause pulmonary opacities in myositis, aspiration pneumonitis often presents more acutely and responds to the resolution of the primary inflammatory insult and pulmonary hygiene.
*   **The CK "Safety Net":** When the clinical picture is "atypical" (like neck swelling masquerading as angioedema), always order a creatine kinase (CK) level. A significantly elevated CK should immediately shift your focus toward a myopathic process.



# The Oncology Shadow: Risk Stratification and Screening in Dermatomyositis

When a patient presents to the emergency department with a rapidly progressing, swollen neck, your mind usually goes straight to the "great imitators" of the airway: Ludwig’s angina, deep neck space infections, or perhaps the scary stuff like superior vena cava syndrome. In the case we are looking at today, a man in his 40s presented with exactly that—severe neck oedema and proximal muscle weakness. 

The "twist" here is that this wasn't an infection or a primary ENT issue. It wasn't even classic angioedema. It was dermatomyositis (DM). While DM is well-known for its classic "heliotrope" rash and "Gottron’s papules," it can occasionally masquerade as subcutaneous oedema, a rare but critical presentation that can delay diagnosis and treatment. But as we peel back the layers of this case—specifically the SAE1-positive status and the patient's dysphagia—we encounter the most sobering aspect of inflammatory myopathies: the "Oncology Shadow."

## The Paraneoplastic Connection
For many of us in the clinical space, dermatomyositis isn't just a primary autoimmune disease; it is frequently a paraneoplastic phenomenon. That is, the myositis may be the body’s inflammatory response to an underlying, occult malignancy. Because of this, our primary goal in managing DM isn't just treating the muscle inflammation—it’s finding the cancer before it becomes a terminal diagnosis.

This is where the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines become our roadmap. These guidelines provide a standardized way to stratify risk and determine how aggressively we need to hunt for a primary tumor.

## Calculating the Risk: The IMACS Framework
In this case, the patient was a male in his 40s with SAE1-positive dermatomyositis and significant pharyngeal dysphagia. To determine his risk, we look at several specific markers:

**High-Risk Factors:**
*   **Dermatomyositis:** The diagnosis itself carries inherent risk compared to polymyositis.
*   **Dysphagia:** This is a major red flag. In the IMACS framework, the presence of dysphagia significantly elevates the risk score.

**Intermediate-Risk Factors:**
*   **SAE1 Antibody:** While not as "high-risk" as certain other antibodies (like anti-TIF1-$\gamma$), the presence of any specific myositis antibody moves the needle.
*   **Male Sex:** Demographics play a role in the statistical probability of underlying malignancy.

By combining these factors, this patient was classified as "High Risk." This isn't just a theoretical exercise; it dictates the intensity of our workup. For a high-risk patient, we cannot rely on a simple physical exam and a few blood tests. We need a multi-modal screening approach.

## The Screening Strategy
Because this patient was high-risk, a comprehensive "search and destroy" mission for malignancy was initiated. This included:
*   **Imaging:** CT scans of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis to look for primary tumors or metastatic spread.
*   **Functional Imaging:** A PET scan was utilized to identify metabolically active sites of disease that might be missed on standard CT.
*   **Laboratory Markers:** Tumor markers (CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, PSA) were ordered to provide a biochemical snapshot.
*   **Endoscopy:** Given the severe dysphagia, gastroscopy and colonoscopy were planned to rule out occult GI malignancies, which are common culprits in these presentations.

While this patient’s results remained reassuring to date, the "shadow" remains. Patients with these profiles require long-term, serial surveillance. We aren't just looking for cancer today; we are looking for it six months, twelve months, and two years from now.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points
*   **Look beyond the "standard" presentation:** If a patient presents with neck swelling or "angioedema" that doesn't respond to standard treatments, consider inflammatory myopathies. A high Creatine Kinase (CK) level is a game-changer in the differential.
*   **Dysphagia is a Red Flag:** In the context of myositis, dysphagia is not just a functional deficit; it is a high-risk clinical marker for underlying malignancy.
*   **Know your IMACS score:** Always calculate the malignancy risk early in the diagnostic workup. The presence of DM, dysphagia, and specific antibodies (like SAE1) should immediately trigger a high-intensity screening protocol.
*   **Persistence is key:** Cancer screening in myositis is not a "one and done" event. Ensure the patient is entered into a long-term surveillance loop of serial imaging and physical exams.



# Aggressive Immunosuppression: Navigating Treatment in Severe Inflammatory Myopathies

Imagine a patient presents to your ED with a neck so swollen it looks like a surgical emergency. The clinical team is sweating, fearing Ludwig’s angina or a rapidly progressing deep neck space infection. Then comes the pivot: the patient is afebrile, the nasendoscopy is clear of structural abnormalities, and the "angioedema" doesn't seem to respond to standard treatments. 

This is the "masquerade" of dermatomyositis (DM). While we usually think of DM as a skin and muscle disease, it can manifest as pseudoangioedema—non-histamine, non-bradykinin mediated swelling. In this specific case, a man in his 40s presented with severe neck oedema and dysphagia. The diagnosis was eventually confirmed as SAE1-antibody-positive dermatomyositis, with a staggering creatine kinase (CK) level of over 16,000 U/L. 

When dermatomyositis presents this aggressively—involving the airway muscles and causing significant systemic inflammation—"standard" management isn't enough. We have to move into the realm of aggressive, multi-modal immunosuppression.

## The "Firefighting" Phase: Pulse Steroids and IVIG

When a patient presents with acute, life-threatening involvement (like severe dysphagia or impending airway compromise from muscle inflammation), the goal is to "put out the fire" as quickly as possible. 

In this case, the patient received 3 days of high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily). Pulse steroids are the gold standard for inducing rapid remission in severe inflammatory myopathies. However, when steroids alone aren't sufficient or the disease is particularly refractory, we bring in the heavy artillery: Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG). 

IVIG remains a cornerstone for severe DM, especially when there is significant soft tissue oedema or rapid progression. This patient received a massive loading dose (2 g/kg) followed by multiple cycles. The mechanism isn't fully understood, but it likely involves neutralizing pathogenic autoantibodies and modulating the innate immune response. In the setting of "pseudoangioedema" caused by myositis, IVIG can be a game-changer for rapid de-escalation of swelling.

## The Long Game: Steroid-Sparing Agents and the Pulmonary Nuance

Once the acute crisis is stabilized, the focus shifts to maintaining remission while minimizing the long-term toxicity of systemic steroids. This is where the choice of "steroid-sparing" agents becomes a nuanced clinical decision.

The standard workhorses are often Methotrexate or Mycophenolate Mofetil (MMF). However, this case highlights a critical clinical pearl regarding pulmonary involvement. This patient had an extrathoracic restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests and patchy consolidation on CT. 

Because of the risk of exacerbating interstitial lung disease (ILD) or inducing pulmonary fibrosis, **Methotrexate was intentionally avoided.** In patients with concurrent pulmonary concerns, Mycophenolate Mofetil is often the preferred steroid-sparing agent. It offers a robust profile for managing inflammatory myopathy without the same level of pulmonary risk associated with methotrexate.

## Adding Precision: Rituximab

Finally, we have to consider biological modifiers. Rituximab, a chimeric monoclonal antibody against CD20, has become increasingly common in the management of refractory or severe dermatomyositis. By depleting B-cells, it hits the underlying autoimmune engine of the disease. In this case, two doses of 1000 mg were added to the regimen of steroids and MMF, providing a multi-pronged attack on the inflammatory process.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Look beyond the surface:** If a patient presents with "idiopathic" angioedema or neck swelling that doesn't fit the classic clinical picture (no ACE inhibitor use, no C1-esterase deficiency), consider dermatomyositis.
*   **The CK is your friend:** A significantly elevated creatine kinase (CK) in a patient with unexplained oedema should immediately trigger a search for inflammatory myopathy.
*   **Don't ignore the "subtle" signs:** Look for periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages, and subtle rashes on the thighs or face (Heliotrope eruption).
*   **Tailor the immunosuppression:** When pulmonary issues are present, be extremely cautious with Methotrexate; Mycophenolate Mofetil may be a safer long-term steroid-sparing option.
*   **Malignancy is a must:** In any adult with dermatomyositis (especially those with dysphagia or SAE1 antibodies), a rigorous malignancy screening is mandatory.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema.** If the diagnosis is unclear, check the CK and look for skin/nail changes.
*   **Severe presentations require "heavy" initial therapy.** High-dose pulse steroids and IVIG are vital for rapid stabilization of airway-threatening muscle inflammation.
*   **Pulmonary status dictates drug choice.** Avoid methotrexate in patients with existing pulmonary restrictive patterns; consider Mycophenolate Mofetil instead.
*   **Multi-modal therapy is often necessary.** Combining steroids, MMF, and Rituximab can be effective for achieving remission in severe, antibody-positive cases.
