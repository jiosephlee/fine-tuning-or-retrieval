Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Pathophysiology and Clinical Spectrum of Dermatomyositis

Dermatomyositis (DM) is a rare, idiopathic inflammatory myopathy characterized by systemic involvement of the skin, skeletal muscles, and occasionally the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and cardiovascular systems. It is a primary component of the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM), which are characterized by immune-mediated destruction of muscle fibers. Epidemiologically, DM follows a bimodal distribution, with peaks in children (aged 5–14 years) and adults (aged 45–64 years). In the context of this case, the patient’s presentation in his 40s falls within the adult peak, a demographic where the risk of underlying malignancy is a critical clinical consideration.

The pathogenesis of dermatomyositis involves an autoimmune-mediated inflammatory process. Unlike polymyositis, which primarily targets the muscle fibers directly, dermatomyositis is characterized by a prominent microvasculopathy. This involves an inflammatory attack on the capillaries, leading to ischemia and subsequent muscle fiber necrosis. Histologically, this often manifests as perifascicular capillary-associated inflammation and the deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) in the endomysial capillaries—a hallmark finding in the muscle biopsy of this patient. This inflammatory process results in the significant elevation of creatine kinase (CK) and other muscle enzymes (e.g., AST, ALT, and LDH) observed in the clinical presentation.

While the muscle involvement is profound, the cutaneous manifestations are often the most recognizable features of the disease. Classic markers include the heliotrope eruption (a violaceous rash on the upper eyelids), Gottron’s papules (erythematous scales over the knuckles), and the "shawl sign" (erythema over the upper back and shoulders). Periungual telangiectasias and microhaemorrhages are also common. However, as demonstrated in this case, these classic markers may be subtle or entirely absent. The patient presented with "pseudoangioedema"—subcutaneous tissue oedema arising from inflammatory myositis rather than a histamine- or bradykinin-mediated response. Recognizing that DM can manifest as unexplained neck or facial swelling is essential for avoiding a missed diagnosis of inflammatory myopathy.

Beyond the skin and muscle, systemic involvement can be life-threatening. Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a frequent and serious complication, often presenting with a restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests and findings of ground-glass opacities or consolidation on CT imaging. Furthermore, pharyngeal dysphagia occurs in a significant subset of patients due to the involvement of the striated muscles of the upper esophagus and pharynx. This patient’s severe dysphagia, requiring a nasogastric tube, highlights the need for early identification of muscle involvement in the head and neck region.

Specific myositis-specific antibodies (MSAs) provide significant prognostic and clinical information. This patient tested positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies. These antibodies target a protein involved in the "sumoylation" process, a post-translational modification of proteins. Patients with anti-SAE1 antibodies have a high clinical association with both dysphagia and pulmonary involvement. Furthermore, the presence of anti-SAE1 antibodies, combined with the patient's sex and the presence of dysphagia, places him in a high-risk category for underlying malignancy, necessitating comprehensive and ongoing oncological screening.

## Clinical Presentation and the Diagnostic Challenge of Pseudoangioedema

The diagnostic journey of this patient highlights a significant clinical pitfall: the tendency to interpret localized swelling through the lens of the most common presenting symptoms rather than considering underlying systemic pathology. This patient presented with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling, proximal muscle weakness, and myalgia. In an emergency department setting, these symptoms—particularly the rapid onset of neck edema—frequently trigger a suspicion of infectious processes. The initial clinical impression was Ludwig’s angina or a deep neck space infection, a high-priority differential because of the potential for rapid airway compromise. Imaging via CT scan confirmed diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space, yet the absence of fever and the lack of a clear bacterial source created a diagnostic stalemate.

When an infectious cause is ruled out, clinicians often pivot to angioedema. However, this case illustrates the concept of "pseudoangioedema," where tissue swelling occurs due to an inflammatory myopathy rather than the classic histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated pathways associated with idiopathic or hereditary angioedema. In dermatomyositis, pseudoangioedema is an uncommon but recognized manifestation where inflammation of the muscles (myositis) leads to secondary subcutaneous and deep tissue edema. Distinguishing between these two is critical; while true angioedema is often a transient, episodic allergic or genetic reaction, pseudoangioedema in dermatomyositis is a manifestation of a chronic, progressive autoimmune disease that requires intensive immunosuppression.

The transition from an ENT-focused workup to an immunological investigation was driven by the absence of traditional angioedema triggers. The patient had no history of ACE inhibitor use, no family history of angioedema, and subsequent laboratory testing showed normal C3, C4, and C1 esterase levels. In a patient with unexplained, persistent neck swelling and no clear triggers, the absence of these markers should heighten suspicion for an alternative etiology. Furthermore, the concurrent presence of proximal hip flexor weakness and myalgia served as a "red flag" that the primary pathology was likely muscular rather than purely cutaneous or allergic.

A meticulous physical examination provided the subtle clues necessary to bridge these findings. While classic dermatomyositis hallmarks like Gottron’s papules and heliotrope eruptions were absent, the identification of periungual erythema and a subtle rash on the lateral thigh were pivotal. Additionally, nailfold capillaroscopy revealed microhaemorrhages and avascular areas, which are highly suggestive of an underlying connective tissue disease. These subtle signs, combined with the clinical suspicion of an inflammatory myopathy, justified the request for a creatine kinase (CK) level. The discovery of a significantly elevated CK (16,184 U/L) served as the definitive turning point, shifting the diagnostic focus from a localized ENT/allergic issue to a systemic inflammatory myopathy.

## Diagnostic Workup and Evidence for Myositis

The diagnostic journey for this patient began with a focus on the primary presenting symptom: significant neck swelling. Because the swelling was subacute and lacked a clear infectious or structural cause, the clinical team initially investigated idiopathic angioedema. In such cases, a standard workup includes assessing the complement system. The patient’s C3, C4, and C1 esterase function levels were all within the normal range, effectively ruling out hereditary angioedema and many common forms of acquired angioedema. This "negative" workup was a critical turning point; it suggested that the neck edema was not a primary histamine or bradykinin-mediated process, but rather a secondary manifestation of a different underlying pathology.

The diagnostic pivot occurred when the clinical team looked beyond the neck swelling to the patient’s systemic symptoms, such as proximal muscle weakness and subtle cutaneous changes. The request for a creatine kinase (CK) level was instrumental. The patient’s CK was profoundly elevated at 16,184 U/L, a hallmark of significant muscle fiber necrosis or inflammation. Furthermore, the elevation of transaminases (AST and ALT) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) provided additional evidence of muscle involvement, as these enzymes are released into the bloodstream during myositis. These laboratory findings shifted the differential diagnosis from a localized inflammatory condition of the neck to a systemic inflammatory myopathy.

Serological testing further narrowed the diagnosis by identifying specific autoimmune markers. A high-titer anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) of 1:1280 confirmed an underlying autoimmune process. The subsequent myositis antibody panel was highly specific, showing a strong positive result for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies. While other common myositis antibodies, such as anti-Jo-1 or anti-Mi-2α, were negative, the presence of SAE1 antibodies provided a distinct serological profile often associated with dermatomyositis and specific systemic features like dysphagia and interstitial lung disease.

To localize the extent of the disease, advanced imaging was employed to correlate clinical findings with physiological activity. MRI of the thighs identified active myositis in several muscle groups, including the obturator and gracilis muscles, confirming that the muscle weakness was due to active inflammation. This was complemented by an FDG-PET scan, which demonstrated diffuse, high metabolic activity within multiple muscle groups. Crucially, the PET scan showed prominent FDG accumulation in the head and neck, providing objective evidence that the patient’s "angioedema" was actually intense inflammation of the neck muscles.

The final definitive diagnosis was established through a muscle biopsy of the thigh, which remains the gold standard for inflammatory myopathies. The histopathology revealed necrotic, vacuolated, and regenerative myofibers. Most importantly, the inflammation followed a "perifascicular" distribution—meaning the damage was concentrated at the edges of the muscle fascicles. The presence of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposition in the capillaries confirmed the underlying pathophysiology of dermatomyositis: a complement-mediated microangiopathy. Together, these laboratory, imaging, and histological findings confirmed a diagnosis of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema.

## Management Strategies and Long-term Surveillance

The management of severe inflammatory myopathies, such as dermatomyositis, requires a rapid and aggressive multi-modal approach, particularly when systemic complications like severe soft tissue swelling or dysphagia are present. In this case, the severity of the patient's cervical edema and the threat to his airway necessitated high-dose "pulse" therapy. The patient was initiated on intravenous methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for three days) to rapidly dampen the systemic inflammatory response. Furthermore, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered. While the exact indications for IVIG in myositis can vary, it is frequently employed in severe presentations, including those with significant edema or refractory symptoms, to provide rapid immunomodulation.

Once the acute inflammatory phase was stabilized, the focus shifted to a long-term maintenance strategy designed to minimize the morbidity associated with chronic corticosteroid use. Steroid-sparing agents are essential in managing dermatomyositis to allow for a gradual taper of prednisone. A critical clinical decision in this case was the selection of mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) over methotrexate. While methotrexate is a common first-line steroid-sparing agent, it is often avoided or used with extreme caution in patients with existing pulmonary involvement or a restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests, as it can exacerbate interstitial lung disease (ILD) and fibrosis. Given the patient’s restrictive lung pattern and aspiration-related consolidation, MMF provided a safer profile for achieving remission while protecting pulmonary function.

Managing the acute complications of the disease is as vital as the primary pharmacological treatment. The patient suffered from severe pharyngeal dysphagia, which presented a significant risk for aspiration. This was clinically evidenced by the presence of peribronchial consolidation on CT, likely representing aspiration pneumonitis. To ensure adequate nutrition and prevent further pulmonary complications, a nasogastric tube was required for two months. The successful resolution of these complications—evidenced by the eventual removal of the tube and the resolution of pulmonary opacities—serves as a primary indicator of treatment efficacy in severe myositis.

A cornerstone of the long-term management of adult dermatomyositis is the systematic screening for underlying malignancy. Because dermatomyositis is frequently paraneoplastic, clinicians must use standardized protocols like the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines to stratify risk. This patient was classified as high risk due to several factors: the diagnosis of dermatomyositis, the presence of dysphagia, his male sex, and the presence of the SAE1 antibody. Consequently, comprehensive screening including CT imaging, tumor markers, and a PET scan were performed. Ongoing surveillance, including planned gastroscopy and colonoscopy, is mandatory to ensure early detection of any occult malignancy.

The clinical outcome for this patient highlights the importance of early diagnosis and aggressive intervention. The normalization of creatine kinase (CK) and the full recovery of proximal muscle strength within three weeks of treatment demonstrate the effectiveness of the initial pulse therapy and IVIG. Furthermore, the complete resolution of the "pseudoangioedema" and the successful tapering of prednisone over seven months underscore the importance of a structured, step-down treatment plan. For the clinician, this case reinforces that while dermatomyositis can present with atypical features like neck swelling, standard management principles—rapid immunosuppression, careful selection of steroid-sparing agents, and rigorous malignancy screening—remain the pillars of care.
