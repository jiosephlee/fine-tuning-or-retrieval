Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Differential Diagnosis] When a patient presents with subacute, progressive neck swelling and no evidence of infection or common triggers for angioedema (e.g., ACE inhibitors), what systemic inflammatory conditions should be included in the differential diagnosis?

When a patient presents with subacute, progressive neck swelling that lacks evidence of infection or common triggers for angioedema (such as ACE inhibitors), clinicians must broaden their differential diagnosis to include systemic inflammatory conditions, specifically **dermatomyositis** and other inflammatory myopathies.

While dermatomyositis is classically characterized by proximal muscle weakness and distinct cutaneous findings—such as Gottron’s papules, the heliotrope eruption, and shawl sign—it can present atypically as "pseudoangioedema." In these instances, the swelling is not mediated by the histamine or bradykinin pathways characteristic of idiopathic or hereditary angioedema. Instead, the edema arises from profound underlying inflammation and infiltration of the neck muscles (myositis).

As demonstrated in this case, this presentation can be highly deceptive. The patient initially presented with neck swelling and dysphagia that mimicked a deep neck space infection or idiopathic angioedema. However, the presence of subtle clinical clues—such as periungual erythema, proximal muscle weakness (hip flexor power of 4/5), and a high clinical suspicion for myopathy—should prompt an early investigation into systemic inflammatory disease. 

The diagnostic standard in this scenario is the measurement of **creatine kinase (CK)**. A significantly elevated CK level, as seen in this patient (16,184 U/L), serves as a critical pivot point, shifting the differential from a primary allergic or complement-mediated process to an inflammatory myopathy. Further evaluation with an autoantibody panel (e.g., identifying anti-SAE1 antibodies) and a muscle biopsy can confirm the diagnosis. Recognizing dermatomyositis as a cause of "unexplained" neck swelling is vital for initiating prompt, high-dose immunosuppression to prevent further multiorgan damage and manage airway risks associated with severe dysphagia and muscle involvement.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the specific pathophysiological mechanism of muscle fiber destruction in dermatomyositis regarding the membrane attack complex (C5b-9), and what is its characteristic distribution on muscle biopsy?

The destruction of muscle fibers in dermatomyositis is primarily driven by a complement-mediated microangiopathy. In this pathophysiological process, the complement cascade is activated, leading to the deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on the walls of small endomysial capillaries. This deposition causes capillary wall damage and subsequent capillary loss, which results in localized ischemia. The muscle fibers surrounding these compromised capillaries are deprived of adequate blood flow and nutrients, leading to fiber necrosis and degeneration.

On muscle biopsy, this mechanism manifests as a characteristic **perifascicular distribution** of muscle fiber damage. Specifically, necrotic, vacuolated, and myophagocytosed fibers are primarily observed at the periphery of the muscle fascicles, where the capillary density is highest and the microangiopathy is most severe. As demonstrated in this case, immunohistochemistry for C5b-9 confirms deposition in the perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a classic histological finding that helps distinguish dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies, such as polymyositis, where the inflammatory infiltrate and fiber damage are typically more randomly distributed throughout the endomysium.

### [Clinical Pearls] In this clinical scenario, which specific laboratory findings were most critical in shifting the diagnostic focus from idiopathic angioedema to dermatomyositis?

In this clinical scenario, the pivotal laboratory finding that shifted the diagnostic focus from idiopathic angioedema to dermatomyositis was the markedly elevated **creatine kinase (CK) level of 16,184 U/L**. 

While the patient initially presented with neck swelling that mimicked angioedema, the presence of proximal muscle weakness and subtle cutaneous changes (such as periungual erythema) prompted the clinician to consider an inflammatory myopathy. In the context of suspected angioedema—which typically involves the swelling of subcutaneous and submucosal tissues without muscle fiber necrosis—such a profound elevation in CK is highly atypical and points directly toward significant myolysis. 

This suspicion was further reinforced by the concurrent elevation of transaminases (AST 396 U/L and ALT 143 U/L) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), which are common markers of systemic muscle involvement in inflammatory myopathies. The diagnosis was ultimately confirmed through serological testing, which revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) titer of 1:1280 and a strongly positive **anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) antibody**. 

This case serves as a critical reminder that "pseudoangioedema" can occur in dermatomyositis due to profound muscle inflammation. When a patient presents with unexplained soft tissue edema, especially when accompanied by even subtle signs of proximal weakness or skin changes, a creatine kinase level should be ordered early to differentiate between a primary fluid-mediated process like angioedema and a systemic inflammatory myopathy.

### [Management] The patient was identified as having a 'high risk' for malignancy based on IMACS guidelines. Which clinical and laboratory factors contributed to this classification?

According to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines, the patient was classified as "high risk" for malignancy based on a combination of clinical, laboratory, and demographic factors. Specifically, the patient met two **high-risk** criteria: the diagnosis of **dermatomyositis** itself and the clinical presence of **dysphagia**. Additionally, he possessed two **intermediate-risk** factors: his **male sex** and the laboratory finding of being **anti-SAE1 antibody positive**.

In clinical practice, dermatomyositis—particularly in adult-onset cases—is recognized as a potentially paraneoplastic syndrome, where the inflammatory myopathy may be the first manifestation of an underlying occult malignancy. The IMACS guidelines use a weighted scoring system to standardize screening; in this case, the presence of two high-risk factors combined with two intermediate-risk factors necessitates a rigorous and comprehensive malignancy workup. Dysphagia is a particularly significant clinical "red flag," as it may indicate primary involvement of the upper aerodigestive tract or severe myogenic dysfunction. Consequently, the patient required extensive screening, including CT imaging of the chest and abdomen, tumor markers (CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, and PSA), and plans for upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy to ensure no underlying primary tumor was overlooked.

### [Management] Why did the clinical team opt for mycophenolate mofetil and rituximab over methotrexate for this patient's management?

The clinical team opted for mycophenolate mofetil and rituximab over methotrexate primarily due to the patient's pulmonary involvement. In the management of dermatomyositis, methotrexate is a standard steroid-sparing agent; however, it carries a significant risk of inducing or exacerbating pulmonary toxicity and interstitial lung disease (ILD). This patient presented with an abnormal pulmonary function test showing an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, and a CT scan revealed pulmonary nodules and peribronchial consolidation. Given these findings, methotrexate was considered potentially hazardous.

Mycophenolate mofetil was selected as a preferred alternative because it is well-tolerated and allows for a daily oral regimen, which was favorable for the patient's management. Furthermore, the decision to employ a more intensive immunosuppressive strategy—including intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and rituximab—was driven by the severity of the patient's presentation. The patient exhibited a very high creatine kinase level (16,184 U/L), severe dysphagia, and significant cervical soft tissue swelling, necessitating prompt and potent treatment to mitigate multiorgan damage and stabilize his airway and muscle function.

### [Pathophysiology] The term 'pseudoangioedema' was used to describe the patient's neck swelling. How does the mechanism of this swelling differ from histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema?

The distinction between pseudoangioedema in the setting of dermatomyositis and classic angioedema lies in the underlying trigger of increased vascular permeability. 

In standard **histaminergic angioedema**, the mechanism is typically driven by mast cell degranulation, which releases histamine—a potent vasodilator that increases capillary permeability. In **bradykinin-mediated angioedema** (such as that seen in hereditary angioedema or as a side effect of ACE inhibitors), the swelling is mediated by the kinin pathway, where an overabundance of bradykinin leads to profound vascular leakage.

In contrast, the **pseudoangioedema** observed in this patient is a secondary manifestation of **myositis**. Rather than a primary vascular event driven by histamine or bradykinin, the subcutaneous swelling in dermatomyositis arises from intense, primary inflammation of the underlying musculature. In this case, the inflammatory process within the muscles (confirmed by the significantly elevated creatine kinase and the presence of anti-SAE1 antibodies) leads to localized edema and soft tissue expansion. 

This distinction is clinically significant: because the "angioedema" in dermatomyositis is an inflammatory byproduct of myositis, it does not respond to standard angioedema treatments like antihistamines or H1 antagonists. Instead, it requires the systemic immunosuppression and corticosteroids necessary to treat the underlying autoimmune myopathy.

### [Clinical Pearls] A patient with dermatomyositis presents with pharyngeal dysphagia and a restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests. What are the most important associated pulmonary complications to monitor for?

In patients with dermatomyositis presenting with pharyngeal dysphagia and a restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests, the most important pulmonary complications to monitor for are interstitial lung disease (ILD) and aspiration pneumonitis.

ILD is a common systemic manifestation of dermatomyositis and frequently presents with a restrictive defect on pulmonary function testing. In this case, while the patient's CT scan did not show radiological evidence of pulmonary fibrosis, the restrictive pattern necessitates ongoing monitoring for progressive parenchymal involvement. 

However, the presence of severe pharyngeal dysphagia—confirmed in this patient by video fluoroscopy showing pyriform fossae residue and penetration of fluids to the vocal cords—significantly increases the risk of aspiration. The case report highlights this complication through the observation of peribronchial consolidations on CT, which were attributed to aspiration pneumonitis. 

Furthermore, because patients with dermatomyositis often require intensive immunosuppression (as seen here with high-dose corticosteroids, IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab), clinicians must also remain vigilant for opportunistic pulmonary infections, such as *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia, and ensure appropriate prophylaxis is maintained.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the significance of the anti-SAE1 antibody in the context of autoimmune inflammatory myopathies, and what common clinical features are often associated with it?

The anti-SAE1 antibody is a myositis-specific antibody (MSA) found in patients with dermatomyositis. It targets the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein involved in "sumoylation"—a post-translational modification process structurally similar to ubiquitination that regulates protein stability and localization.

In the clinical setting of autoimmune inflammatory myopathies, the presence of anti-SAE1 antibodies is significant because it often correlates with specific systemic involvements. While patients typically exhibit the classic cutaneous hallmarks of dermatomyositis—such as Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash, and the shawl sign—anti-SAE1 positivity is particularly associated with a high prevalence of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease (ILD). In the reported case, the patient demonstrated these features, specifically severe pharyngeal dysphagia and a restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests.

Furthermore, this case highlights an important diagnostic pearl: the possibility of "pseudoangioedema." The patient presented with severe neck swelling that mimicked idiopathic angioedema but was actually a manifestation of underlying muscle inflammation (myositis) involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space. Because the subcutaneous edema in anti-SAE1-positive dermatomyositis is driven by primary inflammatory myopathy rather than histamine or bradykinin pathways, clinical suspicion should be high when "angioedema" presents with proximal muscle weakness, elevated creatine kinase (CK), or subtle periungual changes. 

Finally, from a prognostic standpoint, the presence of anti-SAE1 antibodies is utilized in risk stratification; per the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines, it is considered an intermediate risk factor for associated malignancy, necessitating diligent long-term surveillance.
