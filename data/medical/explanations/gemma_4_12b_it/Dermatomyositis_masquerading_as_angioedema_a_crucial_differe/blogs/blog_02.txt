Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Look Closer at the Nails: Identifying Subtle Clues of Inflammatory Myopathy

Imagine a patient presenting to the Emergency Department with a three-week history of progressive, severe neck swelling. To the initial examiner, this looks like a classic "red flag" scenario: a potential deep neck space infection or, perhaps more likely given the lack of fever, an episode of idiopathic angioedema. This is the "Angioedema Trap"—a clinical presentation so common that it can easily lead us to overlook a far more systemic and potentially devastating underlying pathology.

In a recent case of a man in his 40s, this was exactly what happened. After ENT surgeons ruled out a deep neck space infection (like Ludwig’s angina), the patient was referred to immunology for suspected angioedema. However, something felt "off." The swelling was persistent, and the clinical picture didn't perfectly align with a standard histamine or bradykinin-mediated reaction. The pivot point in this case was a simple but critical "rule-out" test: a creatine kinase (CK) level.

When the CK returned at a staggering 16,184 U/L, the conversation shifted from "allergic reaction" to "inflammatory myopathy." The patient was subsequently diagnosed with anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis (DM). While DM is often associated with the classic heliotrope rash or Gottron’s papules, this patient lacked those hallmark features. Instead, his presentation was dominated by "pseudoangioedema"—subcutaneous edema caused by underlying muscle inflammation rather than an upper airway allergic response.

## The Art of the Subtle Physical Exam

This case serves as a powerful reminder that we cannot rely solely on "textbook" cutaneous findings to rule out dermatomyositis. When a patient presents with atypical swelling or proximal muscle weakness, we must train our eyes to look for the subtle markers that often go unnoticed in a fast-paced clinical setting.

One of the most underrated tools in the inflammatory myopathy toolkit is the **nailfold capillaroscopy**. In this case, even though the patient didn't have the classic rashes, capillaroscopy revealed proximal nailfold microhemorrhages and avascular areas. These are high-yield indicators of microvascular changes associated with systemic myositis. Similarly, a close inspection of the nail folds can reveal **periungual telangiectasias**—dilated capillaries that may be faint but are highly suggestive of DM.

We should also be looking for the **Holster sign**—erythema over the lateral thighs—and other less common cutaneous manifestations. In patients where the "classic" signs are absent, these subtle clues can be the difference between a misdiagnosis of angioedema and the timely initiation of aggressive immunosuppression.

## Why the Distinction Matters

The stakes in this case were high. Because the patient had significant muscle involvement and dysphagia (confirmed via barium swallow), he required immediate, high-dose intervention, including IV methylprednisolone and IVIG. Furthermore, identifying dermatomyositis early is crucial for cancer screening. This patient was classified as "high risk" for malignancy due to his age, sex, and the presence of dysphagia, necessitating a comprehensive screening workup including PET scans and multiple endoscopies.

When "idiopathic" angioedema doesn't quite fit the mold—especially when accompanied by even mild proximal weakness or a vague sense of malaise—stop and look closer. Check the nails, check the thighs, and always, always order a CK level.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Think Dermatomyositis in "Angioedema":** If neck or facial swelling is persistent, atypical in its progression, or associated with dysphagia, consider dermatomyositis as a primary differential, especially in the absence of a clear trigger.
*   **The CK "Pivot":** In any case of unexplained soft tissue edema or proximal weakness, a creatine kinase level is a low-cost, high-yield test that can quickly steer the diagnosis toward myositis.
*   **Look Beyond the Rash:** Do not rule out dermatomyositis simply because the heliotrope rash or Gottron’s papules are absent. Perform a detailed exam for periungual telangiectasias, nailfold microhemorrhages, and the Holster sign.
*   **The Malignancy Connection:** Dermatomyositis is frequently paraneoplastic. Patients with DM, particularly those with dysphagia or specific antibodies (like anti-SAE1), require a rigorous and ongoing malignancy screening protocol.
*   **Pseudoangioedema:** Recognize that subcutaneous edema in myositis is a result of muscle inflammation and won't respond to standard angioedema treatments (like ACE inhibitors or antihistamines); it requires systemic immunosuppression.
