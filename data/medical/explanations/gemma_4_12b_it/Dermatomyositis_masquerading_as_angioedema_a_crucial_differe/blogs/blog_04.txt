Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Dysphagia and the Danger of Aspiration: Managing the Respiratory Risks of Myositis

When we think of dermatomyositis, the clinical "hallmarks" usually involve skin changes like the heliotrope rash or Gottron’s papules, and perhaps some proximal muscle weakness in the hips and shoulders. It is often framed as a rheumatological or dermatological issue. However, as this case demonstrates, dermatomyositis can present with an acute, life-threatening complication that looks more like an ENT emergency or a primary airway problem: severe pharyngeal dysphagia and subsequent aspiration pneumonitis.

In this case, a man in his 40s presented with significant neck swelling. The initial suspicion was Ludwig’s angina or idiopathic angioedema. While the neck oedema was a striking physical finding, the underlying driver was actually severe muscle inflammation. The real "hidden" danger, however, was the patient's inability to swallow safely. A video fluoroscopic swallow study revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration into the vocal cords. This wasn't just a matter of "difficulty eating"—it was a direct pathway to pulmonary compromise.

The resulting aspiration pneumonitis, evidenced by patchy peribronchial consolidation on CT, highlights a critical teaching point: in patients with inflammatory myopathies, we must look beyond the "main" muscles. When the inflammation hits the muscles of the upper aerodigestive tract, the risk of aspiration becomes immediate. In this patient, the dysphagia was so profound that it necessitated the placement of a nasogastric (NG) tube to provide nutritional support during the acute inflammatory phase. This is a vital bridge; while we are "putting out the fire" with high-dose steroids and IVIG, the patient cannot be expected to maintain nutrition or safe swallows until the acute inflammation subsides.

Managing these patients requires a multi-pronged approach. First, aggressive immunosuppression is the priority to halt the underlying inflammatory process. In this case, the patient received a massive dose of IV methylprednisolone followed by IVIG and rituximab. Second, we must protect the airway and lungs. Recognizing the signs of aspiration early allows for prompt intervention—whether that is escalating steroids, managing the secretions, or ensuring the patient remains NPO (nothing by mouth) while an NG tube manages their caloric needs. Finally, we have to distinguish between aspiration and interstitial lung disease (ILD), which is another common pulmonary complication of dermatomyositis. In this case, the resolution of the opacities on follow-up imaging confirmed that the primary acute issue was aspiration-related.

**Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points:**

*   **Think Myositis in "Airway" Cases:** If a patient presents with unexplained neck swelling, dysphagia, or rapid-onset respiratory distress without a clear infectious source, include inflammatory myopathy in your differential, especially if there is any evidence of proximal muscle weakness.
*   **Assess Swallowing Early:** In patients with suspected myositis and facial/neck involvement, a swallow study (like video fluoroscopy) is essential to quantify the risk of aspiration.
*   **The NG Tube is a Tool, Not a Failure:** During the peak of an inflammatory flare, providing nutrition via an NG tube is a standard and necessary safety measure to prevent aspiration while waiting for the systemic inflammation to respond to treatment.
*   **Differentiate Aspiration from ILD:** While both can cause pulmonary opacities in myositis, aspiration pneumonitis often presents more acutely and responds to the resolution of the primary inflammatory insult and pulmonary hygiene.
*   **The CK "Safety Net":** When the clinical picture is "atypical" (like neck swelling masquerading as angioedema), always order a creatine kinase (CK) level. A significantly elevated CK should immediately shift your focus toward a myopathic process.
