Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 1: ## Pathophysiology and Clinical Spectrum of Dermatomyositis

Dermatomyositis (DM) is a rare, idiopathic inflammatory myopathy characterized by systemic involvement of the skin, skeletal muscles, and occasionally the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and cardiovascular systems. It is a primary component of the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM), which are characterized by immune-mediated destruction of muscle fibers. Epidemiologically, DM follows a bimodal distribution, with peaks in children (aged 5–14 years) and adults (aged 45–64 years). In the context of this case, the patient’s presentation in his 40s falls within the adult peak, a demographic where the risk of underlying malignancy is a critical clinical consideration.

The pathogenesis of dermatomyositis involves an autoimmune-mediated inflammatory process. Unlike polymyositis, which primarily targets the muscle fibers directly, dermatomyositis is characterized by a prominent microvasculopathy. This involves an inflammatory attack on the capillaries, leading to ischemia and subsequent muscle fiber necrosis. Histologically, this often manifests as perifascicular capillary-associated inflammation and the deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) in the endomysial capillaries—a hallmark finding in the muscle biopsy of this patient. This inflammatory process results in the significant elevation of creatine kinase (CK) and other muscle enzymes (e.g., AST, ALT, and LDH) observed in the clinical presentation.

While the muscle involvement is profound, the cutaneous manifestations are often the most recognizable features of the disease. Classic markers include the heliotrope eruption (a violaceous rash on the upper eyelids), Gottron’s papules (erythematous scales over the knuckles), and the "shawl sign" (erythema over the upper back and shoulders). Periungual telangiectasias and microhaemorrhages are also common. However, as demonstrated in this case, these classic markers may be subtle or entirely absent. The patient presented with "pseudoangioedema"—subcutaneous tissue oedema arising from inflammatory myositis rather than a histamine- or bradykinin-mediated response. Recognizing that DM can manifest as unexplained neck or facial swelling is essential for avoiding a missed diagnosis of inflammatory myopathy.

Beyond the skin and muscle, systemic involvement can be life-threatening. Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a frequent and serious complication, often presenting with a restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests and findings of ground-glass opacities or consolidation on CT imaging. Furthermore, pharyngeal dysphagia occurs in a significant subset of patients due to the involvement of the striated muscles of the upper esophagus and pharynx. This patient’s severe dysphagia, requiring a nasogastric tube, highlights the need for early identification of muscle involvement in the head and neck region.

Specific myositis-specific antibodies (MSAs) provide significant prognostic and clinical information. This patient tested positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies. These antibodies target a protein involved in the "sumoylation" process, a post-translational modification of proteins. Patients with anti-SAE1 antibodies have a high clinical association with both dysphagia and pulmonary involvement. Furthermore, the presence of anti-SAE1 antibodies, combined with the patient's sex and the presence of dysphagia, places him in a high-risk category for underlying malignancy, necessitating comprehensive and ongoing oncological screening.
