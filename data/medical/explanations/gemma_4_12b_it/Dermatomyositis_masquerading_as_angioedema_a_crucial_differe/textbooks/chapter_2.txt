Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 2: ## Clinical Presentation and the Diagnostic Challenge of Pseudoangioedema

The diagnostic journey of this patient highlights a significant clinical pitfall: the tendency to interpret localized swelling through the lens of the most common presenting symptoms rather than considering underlying systemic pathology. This patient presented with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling, proximal muscle weakness, and myalgia. In an emergency department setting, these symptoms—particularly the rapid onset of neck edema—frequently trigger a suspicion of infectious processes. The initial clinical impression was Ludwig’s angina or a deep neck space infection, a high-priority differential because of the potential for rapid airway compromise. Imaging via CT scan confirmed diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space, yet the absence of fever and the lack of a clear bacterial source created a diagnostic stalemate.

When an infectious cause is ruled out, clinicians often pivot to angioedema. However, this case illustrates the concept of "pseudoangioedema," where tissue swelling occurs due to an inflammatory myopathy rather than the classic histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated pathways associated with idiopathic or hereditary angioedema. In dermatomyositis, pseudoangioedema is an uncommon but recognized manifestation where inflammation of the muscles (myositis) leads to secondary subcutaneous and deep tissue edema. Distinguishing between these two is critical; while true angioedema is often a transient, episodic allergic or genetic reaction, pseudoangioedema in dermatomyositis is a manifestation of a chronic, progressive autoimmune disease that requires intensive immunosuppression.

The transition from an ENT-focused workup to an immunological investigation was driven by the absence of traditional angioedema triggers. The patient had no history of ACE inhibitor use, no family history of angioedema, and subsequent laboratory testing showed normal C3, C4, and C1 esterase levels. In a patient with unexplained, persistent neck swelling and no clear triggers, the absence of these markers should heighten suspicion for an alternative etiology. Furthermore, the concurrent presence of proximal hip flexor weakness and myalgia served as a "red flag" that the primary pathology was likely muscular rather than purely cutaneous or allergic.

A meticulous physical examination provided the subtle clues necessary to bridge these findings. While classic dermatomyositis hallmarks like Gottron’s papules and heliotrope eruptions were absent, the identification of periungual erythema and a subtle rash on the lateral thigh were pivotal. Additionally, nailfold capillaroscopy revealed microhaemorrhages and avascular areas, which are highly suggestive of an underlying connective tissue disease. These subtle signs, combined with the clinical suspicion of an inflammatory myopathy, justified the request for a creatine kinase (CK) level. The discovery of a significantly elevated CK (16,184 U/L) served as the definitive turning point, shifting the diagnostic focus from a localized ENT/allergic issue to a systemic inflammatory myopathy.
