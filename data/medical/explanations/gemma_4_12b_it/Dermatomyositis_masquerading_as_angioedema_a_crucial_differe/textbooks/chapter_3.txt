Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Evidence for Myositis

The diagnostic journey for this patient began with a focus on the primary presenting symptom: significant neck swelling. Because the swelling was subacute and lacked a clear infectious or structural cause, the clinical team initially investigated idiopathic angioedema. In such cases, a standard workup includes assessing the complement system. The patient’s C3, C4, and C1 esterase function levels were all within the normal range, effectively ruling out hereditary angioedema and many common forms of acquired angioedema. This "negative" workup was a critical turning point; it suggested that the neck edema was not a primary histamine or bradykinin-mediated process, but rather a secondary manifestation of a different underlying pathology.

The diagnostic pivot occurred when the clinical team looked beyond the neck swelling to the patient’s systemic symptoms, such as proximal muscle weakness and subtle cutaneous changes. The request for a creatine kinase (CK) level was instrumental. The patient’s CK was profoundly elevated at 16,184 U/L, a hallmark of significant muscle fiber necrosis or inflammation. Furthermore, the elevation of transaminases (AST and ALT) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) provided additional evidence of muscle involvement, as these enzymes are released into the bloodstream during myositis. These laboratory findings shifted the differential diagnosis from a localized inflammatory condition of the neck to a systemic inflammatory myopathy.

Serological testing further narrowed the diagnosis by identifying specific autoimmune markers. A high-titer anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) of 1:1280 confirmed an underlying autoimmune process. The subsequent myositis antibody panel was highly specific, showing a strong positive result for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies. While other common myositis antibodies, such as anti-Jo-1 or anti-Mi-2α, were negative, the presence of SAE1 antibodies provided a distinct serological profile often associated with dermatomyositis and specific systemic features like dysphagia and interstitial lung disease.

To localize the extent of the disease, advanced imaging was employed to correlate clinical findings with physiological activity. MRI of the thighs identified active myositis in several muscle groups, including the obturator and gracilis muscles, confirming that the muscle weakness was due to active inflammation. This was complemented by an FDG-PET scan, which demonstrated diffuse, high metabolic activity within multiple muscle groups. Crucially, the PET scan showed prominent FDG accumulation in the head and neck, providing objective evidence that the patient’s "angioedema" was actually intense inflammation of the neck muscles.

The final definitive diagnosis was established through a muscle biopsy of the thigh, which remains the gold standard for inflammatory myopathies. The histopathology revealed necrotic, vacuolated, and regenerative myofibers. Most importantly, the inflammation followed a "perifascicular" distribution—meaning the damage was concentrated at the edges of the muscle fascicles. The presence of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposition in the capillaries confirmed the underlying pathophysiology of dermatomyositis: a complement-mediated microangiopathy. Together, these laboratory, imaging, and histological findings confirmed a diagnosis of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema.
