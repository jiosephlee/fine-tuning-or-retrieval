# Rhabdomyolysis vs. Myopathy: When a 'Simple' Muscle Injury Needs Immunosuppression

We’ve all seen the "classic" presentation of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis: a patient starts a new medication, presents with profound muscle pain, a skyrocketing Creatine Kinase (CK), and potentially some acute kidney injury. The standard of care is straightforward—stop the offending agent, aggressive hydration, and "watchful waiting." In most cases, the CK drops, the patient recovers, and we move on.

But what happens when the "watchful waiting" period doesn't result in improvement? What if the muscle breakdown isn't just a direct toxic hit to the fibers, but rather the spark that ignited a full-blown autoimmune fire?

## The Case: A Slow Burn, Not a Flash Fire

Consider a woman in her 60s with obesity and NAFLD. She was started on semaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist. After just one dose, she had a rough time with GI side effects and a brief stint of AKI. However, the real story began months later. Over a four-month period, she didn't just "feel tired"—she developed progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. 

By the time she reached the hospital, her CK was nearly 4,000 U/L. This is where the clinical nuance becomes vital. If this were simple, drug-induced rhabdomyolysis from semaglutide, we would expect her CK to have peaked and resolved shortly after she stopped the medication. Instead, her symptoms were worsening months after the drug was out of her system. 

## Rhabdomyolysis vs. IMNM: Knowing the Difference

This case highlights the critical distinction between drug-induced rhabdomyolysis and Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM). 

Rhabdomyolysis is typically a primary toxic effect. The drug causes direct cellular damage. Once the drug is gone, the insult stops, and the body begins to repair. 

IMNM, however, is an autoimmune process. While a drug (like a statin or, potentially, a GLP-1 agonist) can act as the initial trigger, the body’s immune system takes over, launching an aggressive attack on the muscle fibers. In this patient, the muscle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis: necrotic and regenerating fibers with significant macrophage infiltration (myophagocytosis) and a membrane attack complex (MAC) positive stain. 

The "smoking gun" in this case was the timeline. A 12-fold rise in CK over four months *after* drug cessation is a massive red flag that the pathology is not just toxicity, but an underlying immune-mediated process.

## The Danger of "Watchful Waiting"

The most alarming feature of this patient’s presentation was the development of dysphagia and dysphonia. In the context of proximal muscle weakness, these are "red alert" symptoms. If we treat this as a simple case of "deconditioning" or "mild rhabdo" and wait for the CK to normalize on its own, we risk permanent functional loss or even aspiration.

This patient required high-dose pulse methylprednisolone and IVIG. The fact that she made a remarkable recovery on this regimen—and was able to wean off steroids relatively quickly—further reinforces the diagnosis of an immune-mediated process rather than a persistent toxic myopathy.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **Watch the Timeline:** If a patient’s muscle weakness is progressing or their CK is rising months after stopping a suspected offending agent, stop thinking "toxicity" and start thinking "autoimmunity."
*   **Identify the Red Flags:** Proximal muscle weakness coupled with dysphagia or dysphonia should prompt an immediate, high-acuity workup. These are not just "symptoms"; they are potential emergencies.
*   **The "Statins" Lesson:** We learned the importance of HMGCR antibodies because many patients were initially misdiagnosed with "toxic myopathy" until we realized their bodies were producing autoantibodies. This case suggests that GLP-1 agonists might be a new, emerging trigger for similar immune-mediated pathways.
*   **Biopsy is King:** When the clinical picture is muddy, a muscle biopsy remains the gold standard to differentiate between simple necrosis and the inflammatory signatures of IMNM.
*   **Aggressive Intervention:** If you suspect IMNM, "watchful waiting" is dangerous. Early initiation of corticosteroids and IVIG can be the difference between a return to baseline and permanent disability.



# The GLP-1 Revolution and the New Frontier of Adverse Effects

We are currently living in the era of the "GLP-1 revolution." Medications like semaglutide and tirzepatide have moved from niche diabetes treatments to blockbuster metabolic superstars, fundamentally changing how we approach obesity and type 2 diabetes. Most of us are well-versed in the "expected" side effects: the inevitable GI upset, the rapid weight loss, and the concerns regarding lean muscle mass wasting. However, as these drugs move into the mainstream, we must remain vigilant for the "weird" stuff—the rare, high-acuity toxicities that only appear once a drug hits massive scale.

Enter a fascinating and somewhat alarming case of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following a single dose of semaglutide. This isn't your standard "I feel a bit weak" muscle wasting. This was a patient in her 60s who, after just one dose of semaglutide, began a progressive four-month slide into severe proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. By the time she reached the tertiary center, her creatine kinase (CK) had skyrocketed to nearly 4,000 U/L, and she was struggling to sit upright or swallow safely.

The clinical nuance here lies in the distinction between drug-induced rhabdomyolysis and immune-mediated myopathy. In cases of pure toxic rhabdomyolysis (which has been reported with other GLP-1 agonists), we typically see a massive, acute spike in CK that resolves rapidly once the offending agent is discontinued. In this case, however, the CK rose steadily over months, and the patient's symptoms persisted despite stopping the medication. This suggested that the drug didn't just "poison" the muscle directly; it may have triggered an underlying autoimmune cascade.

The diagnosis was solidified by a muscle biopsy showing dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibers with myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex (MAC) staining. Interestingly, this patient was seronegative for the classic IMNM biomarkers like anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. This highlights a critical diagnostic hurdle: roughly a third of IMNM patients are seronegative. When the antibodies are missing, we have to rely on the "triad" of progressive proximal weakness, significant CK elevation, and histological evidence of necrosis.

The patient’s rapid recovery with a short course of high-dose corticosteroids and IVIG—combined with the cessation of semaglutide—strongly points toward a drug-induced immune-mediated process. While we don't yet know the exact mechanism—whether it's a molecular mimicry or a specific inflammatory trigger—this case serves as a potent reminder that the more common a drug becomes, the more important it is to keep a wide net out for rare systemic reactions.

As we continue to prescribe these medications to millions, we need to know when to move past "let's wait and see" and call in the rheumatology consult.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **Distinguish Rhabdo vs. IMNM:** If a patient on a GLP-1 agonist shows a rapid CK spike that corrects quickly after stopping the drug, think toxic rhabdomyolysis. If the weakness is progressive over weeks or months and persists after drug withdrawal, suspect an immune-mediated process like IMNM.
*   **The "Red Flags":** Be highly suspicious of any patient on GLP-1 agonists who presents with progressive proximal muscle weakness, particularly if accompanied by dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) or dysphonia (voice changes).
*   **Don't rely solely on serology:** Remember that up to 30% of IMNM cases are seronegative. If the clinical picture and biopsy are consistent with necrotising myopathy, don't let a negative myositis panel rule out the diagnosis.
*   **Pharmacovigilance is key:** This is a first-of-its-kind report. Keep an eye out for these "outlier" cases in your practice; early recognition of these rare toxicities is essential for patient safety in the era of blockbuster metabolic meds.



# Navigating the 'Seronegative' Maze in Immune-Mediated Myopathy

We’ve all been there: a patient presents with devastating proximal muscle weakness, a soaring creatine kinase (CK) level, and a clinical picture that screams inflammatory myopathy. You order the "myositis panel," expecting to see a clear-cut positive for anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP antibodies to guide your management. But then, the results come back: negative. 

It’s a frustrating clinical crossroads. Does a negative myositis panel rule out Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM)? Absolutely not. In fact, an estimated third of IMNM patients are seronegative. This case—a 60-year-old woman who developed rapid-onset muscle breakdown following a single dose of semaglutide—serves as a perfect reminder that when the lab work is silent, the biopsy and the clinical trajectory must speak louder.

## The Clinical Trap: When "Standard" Tests Fall Short

In this case, the patient’s decline was insidious. Following a single dose of semaglutide, she developed progressive proximal weakness and, more alarmingly, dysphagia and dysphonia. While her initial CK was only modestly elevated, it skyrocketed to nearly 4,000 U/L over four months. 

The most striking part of this report isn't just the potential association between GLP-1 receptor agonists and myopathy—it’s the diagnostic hurdles. Her myositis panel was entirely negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, and the rest of the usual suspects. Without those "smoking gun" antibodies, clinicians can easily fall into the trap of over-diagnosing "toxic myopathy" or simply waiting for a lab result that may never come. However, the rapid progression of her weakness and the presence of dysphagia (a high-risk indicator for aspiration) demanded an aggressive diagnostic pivot.

## The Diagnostic Pivot: MRI and the Power of Pathology

When the serology is unhelpful, we have to lean on imaging and tissue. In this patient, an MRI of the femurs confirmed symmetric muscular edema in the proximal vastus lateralis and quadriceps. While MRI is excellent for identifying active inflammation and guiding biopsy sites, it still doesn't provide the definitive diagnosis of necrotising myopathy.

That’s where the muscle biopsy becomes the gold standard. The biopsy of the right vastus lateralis was definitive: it showed dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers. More importantly, the immunohistochemistry provided the hallmark features of IMNM: 
*   **CD68 staining:** A macrophage marker showing significant myophagocytosis.
*   **Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) positivity:** Staining on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres, indicating a robust inflammatory response at the cellular level.

These histological findings, combined with the patient’s rapid response to pulse methylprednisolone and IVIG, confirmed the diagnosis of IMNM despite the lack of specific autoantibodies.

## Emerging Triggers: The GLP-1 Connection

While we are focusing on the "seronegative" aspect, we cannot ignore the elephant in the room: the trigger. With the meteoric rise of GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide for obesity and type 2 diabetes, we are beginning to see a cluster of muscle-related adverse effects. 

While some cases have presented as acute rhabdomyolysis (which resolves quickly with hydration), this case suggests a more complex, immune-mediated pathway. The fact that her symptoms persisted and worsened for months after a single dose—and only resolved with significant immunosuppression—points toward a drug-induced IMNM rather than a simple toxic effect.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **Don't let a negative myositis panel stop you:** Up to 33% of IMNM patients are seronegative. If the clinical suspicion is high (proximal weakness + significantly elevated CK), move to imaging and biopsy.
*   **Identify "Red Flag" symptoms early:** Dysphagia and dysphonia in a patient with myopathy are critical findings. They indicate involvement of the striated muscles of the upper airway and esophagus, increasing the risk of aspiration and requiring urgent intervention.
*   **Value the Biopsy:** In seronegative cases, look for the "classic" IMNM triad on biopsy: myofibre necrosis, myophagocytosis (CD68+ macrophages), and Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) positivity.
*   **Be mindful of new pharmacology:** As GLP-1 agonists become first-line therapies, stay vigilant for non-GI side effects. A sudden onset of muscle weakness in these patients should prompt a work-up for myositis, not just "deconditioning."
*   **Treatment Response is a Diagnostic Clue:** A rapid response to corticosteroids and IVIG in a patient with necrotising features is highly suggestive of an immune-mediated process, even in the absence of specific antibodies.



# Diagnostic Confounders: Sorting NAFLD, Stress, and Muscle Breakdown

In the world of metabolic medicine, "messy" labs are practically the standard. When you’re treating a patient with obesity, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes, you expect to see elevated transaminases, slightly deranged electrolytes, and perhaps some borderline cardiac markers. The challenge arises when these "expected" findings begin to overlap with an acute, life-altering pathology. 

This case is a masterclass in navigating those overlapping signals. We see a patient who presents with a classic metabolic profile (NAFLD, obesity) but develops progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and a massive spike in creatine kinase (CK) following a single dose of semaglutide. The diagnostic hurdle here isn't just identifying the myopathy—it’s untangling the signals from the liver, the heart, and the skeletal muscle.

## The Transaminase Trap: Liver vs. Muscle

One of the most common diagnostic pitfalls in myopathy is interpreting transaminases (AST and ALT). In this case, the patient had a persistent elevation in AST/ALT. When a patient presents with muscle weakness and high transaminases, the reflex is often to calculate the AST/ALT ratio or look for signs of rhabdomyolysis. However, if the patient has known NAFLD, the liver is often "leaking" enzymes regardless of what’s happening in the periphery.

How do we differentiate? The key here was the temporal history. This patient had a biopsy-proven diagnosis of NAFLD three years *prior* to her semaglutide use. Her transaminase levels remained relatively stable even as her CK levels skyrocketed. When you see a massive rise in CK (nearly 4,000 U/L) alongside only modest elevations in AST/ALT in a patient with known hepatosteatosis, the liver is likely a bystander. The muscle is the primary source of the new pathology. Always look for the "baseline" of the patient's metabolic disease before attributing every enzyme elevation to acute muscle breakdown.

## Cardiac Markers and the "Stress" Mimic

Then there’s the heart. The patient presented with a proBNP of 4358 pg/mL and a cardiac MRI showing mild hypokinesis and myocardial edema. In a patient with proximal muscle weakness and systemic inflammation, is this active myocarditis, or is it something else?

In systemic inflammatory myopathies like Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM), we have to consider several possibilities. It could be true inflammatory myocarditis, but it could also be a result of "stress-induced" cardiomyopathy or simply the systemic inflammatory milieu of the myopathy itself. Because the cardiac MRI did not show late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) and the patient responded well to corticosteroids without developing acute heart failure, the "stress-induced" or "inflammatory-related" etiology becomes highly plausible. When the cardiac findings are inconclusive, look at the systemic picture: is there a clear inflammatory driver (like IMNM) that could explain the multi-organ involvement?

## Rhabdomyolysis vs. IMNM: The Timeline Matters

Perhaps the most striking part of this case is the relationship with semaglutide. We are seeing an increasing number of reports of rhabdomyolysis associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists. However, there is a crucial clinical distinction between *drug-induced rhabdomyolysis* and *drug-induced IMNM*.

In a typical case of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis (like the tirzepatide case mentioned in the report), the CK rises precipitously and normalizes rapidly once the drug is stopped. In this patient, the CK rose over four months, and the symptoms were progressive. This suggests that the semaglutide may have acted as a trigger for an autoimmune process—an Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy—rather than just causing direct chemical toxicity to the muscle fibers. 

The histological evidence—necrotic fibers, macrophage infiltration (CD68+), and membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition—confirms this inflammatory pathway. While she was seronegative for the common HMGCR and SRP antibodies, this reinforces the fact that up to a third of IMNM cases are seronegative.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Contextualize the Enzymes:** In patients with known NAFLD, don't let mild AST/ALT elevations distract you from a massive CK rise. Check the patient's "metabolic baseline."
*   **Watch the Timeline:** Rapid CK resolution after drug cessation suggests toxic rhabdomyolysis. Progressive weakness and a slow, steady rise in CK (as seen here) should trigger suspicion for an autoimmune myopathy like IMNM.
*   **The "Seronegative" Caveat:** Just because a patient is negative for HMGCR and SRP antibodies doesn't rule out IMNM. Histology remains the gold standard when the myositis panel comes back clean.
*   **Cardiac Confounders:** High proBNP and myocardial edema in the setting of systemic myopathy can be "noisy" data. Evaluate for stress-induced changes vs. primary myocarditis by looking for LGE on MRI and clinical response to steroids.
*   **GLP-1 Awareness:** As GLP-1 agonists become more common, keep an eye out for non-GI side effects. While rhabdomyolysis is the "headline" side effect, we may be seeing more complex immune-mediated reactions that require steroids and IVIG.



# Aggressive Intervention: When to Escalate to IVIG and Pulse Steroids

We are all very familiar with the "GLP-1 revolution." Semaglutide and its cousins are standard of care for obesity and type 2 diabetes. Most of us are trained to look for the expected side effects: nausea, vomiting, and perhaps some weight-related muscle loss. However, this case highlights a much more sinister, rare, and aggressive complication that should be on your radar: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) potentially triggered by a GLP-1 receptor agonist.

In this case, a woman in her 60s presented with a classic "red flag" triad—progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. While she initially had a modest CK elevation after her first dose of semaglutide, her condition deteriorated over four months. By the time she reached the tertiary center, her CK was nearly 4,000 U/L, and she was functionally impaired, unable to sit or stand without assistance. The muscle biopsy was definitive: necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage infiltration (CD68+), but a paucity of lymphocytes—the histological hallmark of IMNM.

## The Triage of Myopathy: When to "Go Big"

One of the hardest things in clinical practice is knowing when to escalate. When a patient presents with myopathy, the initial instinct might be to start low-dose prednisone and wait for the CK to trend down. But this case illustrates the "danger zone" where watchful waiting is no longer an option.

The primary trigger for aggressive intervention here was the involvement of the bulbar muscles. Dysphagia and dysphonia are not just symptoms; they are functional emergencies. The risk of aspiration pneumonia or complete inability to maintain nutrition necessitates rapid stabilization. When a patient exhibits severe proximal weakness that limits basic mobility (sitting/standing) alongside these upper airway/swallowing issues, we need to move toward high-dose corticosteroids immediately.

In this report, the team utilized pulse methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for 5 days) followed by IVIG (2 g/kg). Why the escalation to IVIG? While corticosteroids are the backbone of IMNM treatment, IVIG is often the "bridge" for patients who are rapidly declining or who have severe features that require a faster anti-inflammatory hit. The patient showed a marked improvement in CK and functional status following this aggressive window, which is a key indicator that the intervention was appropriately timed.

## Drug-Induced vs. Idiopathic IMNM

A nuance we should discuss is the distinction between drug-induced and idiopathic IMNM. Typically, a "toxic" myopathy (like a direct drug insult causing rhabdomyolysis) should see a rapid normalization of CK once the offending agent is stopped. We saw a similar pattern with tirzepatide, where CK levels crashed back to normal within days of discontinuation.

In contrast, this patient’s CK rose 12-fold over four months *after* the semaglutide dose. This suggests that the drug didn't just "poison" the muscle; it likely triggered an underlying autoimmune cascade. The fact that she responded well to a short course of pulse steroids and IVIG, and was then able to wean off steroids successfully, supports the "drug-induced" theory. Idiopathic IMNM (often associated with HMGCR or SRP antibodies) usually requires much more intensive, long-term immunosuppression.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Watch the Bulbar Muscles:** Dysphagia and dysphonia in a patient with proximal weakness should be treated as a clinical emergency. Do not wait for a "perfect" CK trend before initiating aggressive therapy.
*   **GLP-1 Awareness:** While rare, be mindful of IMNM as a potential adverse effect of GLP-1 receptor agonists. If a patient on these meds develops progressive weakness rather than just "sore muscles," think beyond simple rhabdomyolysis.
*   **The "Pulse" Strategy:** Reserve high-dose pulse steroids and IVIG for patients with severe functional impairment, risk of aspiration, or rapid clinical decline.
*   **Histology Matters:** If you suspect IMNM but the myositis panel (Jo-1, SRP, HMGCR) is negative, remember that approximately one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative. A muscle biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosis in these "muddled" presentations.

**Take-home messages:**
*   **Dysphagia + Proximal Weakness = High Urgency.** Escalate to pulse steroids and consider IVIG.
*   **Drug-induced IMNM** may show a more robust response to short-term high-dose therapy compared to idiopathic variants.
*   **Stay Vigilant:** As GLP-1 agonists become more prevalent, our differential for myopathy must expand to include these newer metabolic agents.
