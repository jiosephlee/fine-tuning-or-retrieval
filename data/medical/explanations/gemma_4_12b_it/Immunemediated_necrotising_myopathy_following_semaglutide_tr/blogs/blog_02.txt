Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# The GLP-1 Revolution and the New Frontier of Adverse Effects

We are currently living in the era of the "GLP-1 revolution." Medications like semaglutide and tirzepatide have moved from niche diabetes treatments to blockbuster metabolic superstars, fundamentally changing how we approach obesity and type 2 diabetes. Most of us are well-versed in the "expected" side effects: the inevitable GI upset, the rapid weight loss, and the concerns regarding lean muscle mass wasting. However, as these drugs move into the mainstream, we must remain vigilant for the "weird" stuff—the rare, high-acuity toxicities that only appear once a drug hits massive scale.

Enter a fascinating and somewhat alarming case of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following a single dose of semaglutide. This isn't your standard "I feel a bit weak" muscle wasting. This was a patient in her 60s who, after just one dose of semaglutide, began a progressive four-month slide into severe proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. By the time she reached the tertiary center, her creatine kinase (CK) had skyrocketed to nearly 4,000 U/L, and she was struggling to sit upright or swallow safely.

The clinical nuance here lies in the distinction between drug-induced rhabdomyolysis and immune-mediated myopathy. In cases of pure toxic rhabdomyolysis (which has been reported with other GLP-1 agonists), we typically see a massive, acute spike in CK that resolves rapidly once the offending agent is discontinued. In this case, however, the CK rose steadily over months, and the patient's symptoms persisted despite stopping the medication. This suggested that the drug didn't just "poison" the muscle directly; it may have triggered an underlying autoimmune cascade.

The diagnosis was solidified by a muscle biopsy showing dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibers with myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex (MAC) staining. Interestingly, this patient was seronegative for the classic IMNM biomarkers like anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. This highlights a critical diagnostic hurdle: roughly a third of IMNM patients are seronegative. When the antibodies are missing, we have to rely on the "triad" of progressive proximal weakness, significant CK elevation, and histological evidence of necrosis.

The patient’s rapid recovery with a short course of high-dose corticosteroids and IVIG—combined with the cessation of semaglutide—strongly points toward a drug-induced immune-mediated process. While we don't yet know the exact mechanism—whether it's a molecular mimicry or a specific inflammatory trigger—this case serves as a potent reminder that the more common a drug becomes, the more important it is to keep a wide net out for rare systemic reactions.

As we continue to prescribe these medications to millions, we need to know when to move past "let's wait and see" and call in the rheumatology consult.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **Distinguish Rhabdo vs. IMNM:** If a patient on a GLP-1 agonist shows a rapid CK spike that corrects quickly after stopping the drug, think toxic rhabdomyolysis. If the weakness is progressive over weeks or months and persists after drug withdrawal, suspect an immune-mediated process like IMNM.
*   **The "Red Flags":** Be highly suspicious of any patient on GLP-1 agonists who presents with progressive proximal muscle weakness, particularly if accompanied by dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) or dysphonia (voice changes).
*   **Don't rely solely on serology:** Remember that up to 30% of IMNM cases are seronegative. If the clinical picture and biopsy are consistent with necrotising myopathy, don't let a negative myositis panel rule out the diagnosis.
*   **Pharmacovigilance is key:** This is a first-of-its-kind report. Keep an eye out for these "outlier" cases in your practice; early recognition of these rare toxicities is essential for patient safety in the era of blockbuster metabolic meds.
