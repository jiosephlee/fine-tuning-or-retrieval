Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Diagnostic Confounders: Sorting NAFLD, Stress, and Muscle Breakdown

In the world of metabolic medicine, "messy" labs are practically the standard. When you’re treating a patient with obesity, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes, you expect to see elevated transaminases, slightly deranged electrolytes, and perhaps some borderline cardiac markers. The challenge arises when these "expected" findings begin to overlap with an acute, life-altering pathology. 

This case is a masterclass in navigating those overlapping signals. We see a patient who presents with a classic metabolic profile (NAFLD, obesity) but develops progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and a massive spike in creatine kinase (CK) following a single dose of semaglutide. The diagnostic hurdle here isn't just identifying the myopathy—it’s untangling the signals from the liver, the heart, and the skeletal muscle.

## The Transaminase Trap: Liver vs. Muscle

One of the most common diagnostic pitfalls in myopathy is interpreting transaminases (AST and ALT). In this case, the patient had a persistent elevation in AST/ALT. When a patient presents with muscle weakness and high transaminases, the reflex is often to calculate the AST/ALT ratio or look for signs of rhabdomyolysis. However, if the patient has known NAFLD, the liver is often "leaking" enzymes regardless of what’s happening in the periphery.

How do we differentiate? The key here was the temporal history. This patient had a biopsy-proven diagnosis of NAFLD three years *prior* to her semaglutide use. Her transaminase levels remained relatively stable even as her CK levels skyrocketed. When you see a massive rise in CK (nearly 4,000 U/L) alongside only modest elevations in AST/ALT in a patient with known hepatosteatosis, the liver is likely a bystander. The muscle is the primary source of the new pathology. Always look for the "baseline" of the patient's metabolic disease before attributing every enzyme elevation to acute muscle breakdown.

## Cardiac Markers and the "Stress" Mimic

Then there’s the heart. The patient presented with a proBNP of 4358 pg/mL and a cardiac MRI showing mild hypokinesis and myocardial edema. In a patient with proximal muscle weakness and systemic inflammation, is this active myocarditis, or is it something else?

In systemic inflammatory myopathies like Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM), we have to consider several possibilities. It could be true inflammatory myocarditis, but it could also be a result of "stress-induced" cardiomyopathy or simply the systemic inflammatory milieu of the myopathy itself. Because the cardiac MRI did not show late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) and the patient responded well to corticosteroids without developing acute heart failure, the "stress-induced" or "inflammatory-related" etiology becomes highly plausible. When the cardiac findings are inconclusive, look at the systemic picture: is there a clear inflammatory driver (like IMNM) that could explain the multi-organ involvement?

## Rhabdomyolysis vs. IMNM: The Timeline Matters

Perhaps the most striking part of this case is the relationship with semaglutide. We are seeing an increasing number of reports of rhabdomyolysis associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists. However, there is a crucial clinical distinction between *drug-induced rhabdomyolysis* and *drug-induced IMNM*.

In a typical case of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis (like the tirzepatide case mentioned in the report), the CK rises precipitously and normalizes rapidly once the drug is stopped. In this patient, the CK rose over four months, and the symptoms were progressive. This suggests that the semaglutide may have acted as a trigger for an autoimmune process—an Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy—rather than just causing direct chemical toxicity to the muscle fibers. 

The histological evidence—necrotic fibers, macrophage infiltration (CD68+), and membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition—confirms this inflammatory pathway. While she was seronegative for the common HMGCR and SRP antibodies, this reinforces the fact that up to a third of IMNM cases are seronegative.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Contextualize the Enzymes:** In patients with known NAFLD, don't let mild AST/ALT elevations distract you from a massive CK rise. Check the patient's "metabolic baseline."
*   **Watch the Timeline:** Rapid CK resolution after drug cessation suggests toxic rhabdomyolysis. Progressive weakness and a slow, steady rise in CK (as seen here) should trigger suspicion for an autoimmune myopathy like IMNM.
*   **The "Seronegative" Caveat:** Just because a patient is negative for HMGCR and SRP antibodies doesn't rule out IMNM. Histology remains the gold standard when the myositis panel comes back clean.
*   **Cardiac Confounders:** High proBNP and myocardial edema in the setting of systemic myopathy can be "noisy" data. Evaluate for stress-induced changes vs. primary myocarditis by looking for LGE on MRI and clinical response to steroids.
*   **GLP-1 Awareness:** As GLP-1 agonists become more common, keep an eye out for non-GI side effects. While rhabdomyolysis is the "headline" side effect, we may be seeing more complex immune-mediated reactions that require steroids and IVIG.
