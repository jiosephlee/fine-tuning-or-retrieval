Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 2: ## Clinical Presentation: Acute Reaction and Progressive Myopathy

The patient’s clinical profile is characterized by significant metabolic comorbidities, including obesity, hypertension, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). These conditions provided the clinical rationale for initiating semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist commonly used to manage weight and metabolic dysfunction. Understanding this baseline is crucial, as metabolic disorders are frequently associated with other medications—such as statins—that are known triggers for immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

The patient’s clinical course began with an immediate, acute adverse reaction following a single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose of semaglutide. Within hours, she experienced severe gastrointestinal distress, including nausea and vomiting. This led to a secondary hospital admission two days later for severe dehydration, poor oral intake, dyspnea, and acute renal failure accompanied by severe metabolic acidosis. While these symptoms were managed successfully with aggressive intravenous hydration and bicarbonate, the initial laboratory work-up showed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 337 U/L. This mild elevation was notably insufficient to suggest acute rhabdomyolysis, which typically presents with much higher CK concentrations and more severe muscle breakdown.

Following the resolution of her acute gastrointestinal issues, the patient underwent a significant period of clinical stability before a delayed progression of symptoms began. Over a four-month period, she developed a steady decline in physical function, characterized by progressive proximal muscle weakness. Critically, she also began to experience dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) and dysphonia (difficulty with voice production). These symptoms are clinical "red flags" in the context of myopathy, as they indicate involvement of the striated muscles of the upper esophagus and the larynx, respectively, which can lead to significant morbidity or aspiration risks.

The temporal relationship between the medication exposure and the onset of myopathy is a key diagnostic differentiator in this case. In drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, symptoms and CK elevations typically peak rapidly and begin to resolve shortly after the drug is discontinued. In contrast, this patient’s deterioration occurred over four months following a single dose. This delayed progression suggests that the semaglutide may have acted as a trigger for an underlying immune-mediated process—specifically IMNM—rather than causing a direct, acute toxic effect on the muscle fibers.

Upon physical examination during the peak of her symptoms, the patient exhibited severe weakness primarily localized to the proximal muscle groups. The examination was notable for the absence of systemic inflammatory signs, such as rashes, synovitis, or pulmonary crackles. The focus on proximal weakness (such as the quadriceps and hip flexors) is a hallmark of myopathic processes, distinguishing it from primary neurological disorders which might present with more distal weakness or diminished deep tendon reflexes. This presentation, combined with the clinical history, necessitated a thorough investigation into inflammatory and necrotising myopathies.
