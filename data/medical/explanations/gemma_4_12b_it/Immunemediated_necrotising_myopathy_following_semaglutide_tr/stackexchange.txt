Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Pathophysiology] What are the hallmark histological features of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) that distinguish it from classic inflammatory myopathies such as polymyositis or dermatomyositis?

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is histologically distinguished from classic inflammatory myopathies, such as polymyositis and dermatomyositis, primarily by the predominance of myofiber necrosis and a relative paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates. 

While polymyositis and dermatomyositis are characterized by prominent inflammatory cell clusters—typically involving dense T-lymphocytic infiltrates invading non-necrotic fibers or surrounding perifascicular areas—IMNM is defined by widespread, aggressive myofiber necrosis and subsequent regeneration. In the provided case, the muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with significant myophagocytosis, highlighted by cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68) staining for macrophages.

Key histological hallmarks of IMNM observed in this case that contrast with classic myositis include:

1.  **Prominent Necrosis and Regeneration:** The primary finding is the destruction of muscle fibers rather than primary inflammatory evasion of the fibers.
2.  **Myophagocytosis:** Extensive involvement of macrophages (CD68+ cells) in the clearance of necrotic debris is a defining feature.
3.  **Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) Deposition:** The presence of MAC on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres reflects the complement-mediated component of the pathology.
4.  **Paucity of Lymphocytes:** Unlike the robust lymphocytic presence in polymyositis, this patient exhibited only rare CD3-positive T-cells, a hallmark of the "necrotising-heavy, inflammatory-light" histological profile of IMNM.

Identifying these features is clinically significant because IMNM often presents with more severe, rapid proximal weakness and higher creatine kinase levels than classic inflammatory myopathies, often requiring aggressive immunosuppression and specific consideration of triggers like statins or, as seen in this case, GLP-1 receptor agonists.

### [Clinical Pearls] In patients with IMNM, what are the two most common autoantibodies associated with the disease, and what is the clinical significance of the approximately one-third of patients who test negative for these markers?

In patients with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), the two most common autoantibodies associated with the disease are **anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR)** and **anti-signal recognition particle (SRP)**. 

The clinical significance of the approximately one-third of patients who test negative for these specific markers is twofold. First, it presents a significant diagnostic challenge, as the absence of these hallmark biomarkers necessitates a higher reliance on clinical presentation and histological findings—such as the necrotising myofibres and paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates seen in this case—to establish the diagnosis. Second, the existence of a seronegative subset suggests that there are likely other associated autoantibodies and potential triggers (environmental, pharmacological, or otherwise) that have not yet been discovered. This underscores the importance of considering IMNM in the differential diagnosis of aggressive proximal muscle weakness even when standard myositis panels are negative.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Based on the clinical course described, how does the progression of this patient's symptoms and creatine kinase (CK) levels differentiate an immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from acute drug-induced rhabdomyolysis?

The differentiation between immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) and acute drug-induced rhabdomyolysis in this case hinges on the temporal progression of the creatine kinase (CK) levels and the clinical kinetics of the patient's weakness.

In a classic case of acute drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, such as the tirzepatide-associated case described in the background of this report, one expects a rapid, massive spike in CK levels (often into the tens of thousands) that begins to resolve within days of discontinuing the offending agent and initiating hydration. In this patient, however, the CK was only modestly elevated (337 U/L) immediately following the first dose of semaglutide. The subsequent 12-fold increase in CK to 3992 U/L over a 4-month period—coupled with the progressive development of severe proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia—is much more characteristic of a subacute to chronic necrotising myopathy.

Furthermore, the clinical response to treatment provides a key diagnostic clue. While rhabdomyolysis typically resolves with supportive care and drug withdrawal, this patient required and responded significantly to aggressive immunosuppression, specifically pulse methylprednisolone and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). This response suggests an underlying auto-inflammatory or immune-mediated mechanism rather than a direct toxic effect on the muscle fibers. These clinical observations, supported by the histopathology showing necrotising myofibres and macrophage infiltration (CD68 staining), point toward a drug-induced IMNM rather than a simple acute rhabdomyolytic event.

### [Management] Which imaging modality is most useful for identifying active muscle inflammation/oedema in a patient with suspected myopathy, and what specific findings would support a diagnosis of necrotising myopathy?

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the preferred imaging modality for identifying active muscle inflammation and edema in patients with suspected myopathy. MRI is highly sensitive for detecting increased water content within muscle tissue—an early indicator of inflammatory processes and myofiber damage—often preceding clinical symptoms or visible changes on plain radiography. 

In this case, MRI of the bilateral femurs demonstrated symmetric muscular edema, most prominent in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles. While MRI is excellent for characterizing the distribution and activity of inflammation, the specific diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is ultimately confirmed by muscle biopsy. In IMNM, the hallmark histological findings include dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with prominent macrophage infiltration (myophagocytosis) and membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers. 

Clinically, MRI serves a dual purpose: it helps differentiate inflammatory myopathies from non-inflammatory causes (such as primary metabolic myopathies or certain toxicities) by highlighting active edema, and it allows the clinician to identify the most affected muscle groups to guide a targeted, diagnostic biopsy.

### [Management] What is the recommended first-line management for a patient presenting with severe proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and elevated CK levels consistent with IMNM?

The recommended first-line management for a patient presenting with severe immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) includes high-dose corticosteroids, often initiated as pulse therapy, frequently in combination with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG).

IMNM is an aggressive inflammatory myopathy characterized by rapid-onset proximal muscle weakness and significant elevations in creatine kinase (CK). Histopathologically, it is distinguished by necrotising myofibres with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates. Because the disease can progress quickly and lead to severe functional impairment or life-threatening complications—such as the dysphagia seen in this case—prompt initiation of potent immunosuppressive therapy is essential to limit muscle damage and facilitate recovery.

In the reported case, the patient presented with severe proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and a CK level of 3992 U/L. She was managed with 5 days of intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy (1000 mg daily) followed by oral prednisone (1 mg/kg daily). Due to the severity of her symptoms and persistent weakness, IVIG (2 g/kg) was also administered. This intervention led to a marked improvement in her CK levels within two weeks and allowed the patient to regain her baseline functional status. Additionally, the discontinuation of the suspected offending agent (semaglutide) is a critical component of management in cases where drug-induced IMNM is suspected.

### [Clinical Pearls] While GLP-1 receptor agonists are commonly associated with gastrointestinal side effects, what specific muscular adverse effects have been reported, and how does the mechanism of IMNM differ from simple toxic myopathy?

While glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists are predominantly associated with gastrointestinal side effects, they have also been reported to cause a loss of skeletal muscle mass and, in rare instances, rhabdomyolysis. Distinguishing between a simple toxic myopathy and immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) is essential, as their clinical courses and management strategies differ significantly.

Simple toxic myopathy, such as drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, is typically characterized by a marked, acute elevation of creatine kinase (CK) that improves rapidly—often within days—following the discontinuation of the offending agent and initiation of intravenous hydration. In such cases, clinical resolution occurs without the need for systemic immunosuppression.

In contrast, IMNM is a distinct autoimmune entity characterized by aggressive proximal muscle weakness and specific histological features, including necrotizing myofibers with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates. Histopathology in IMNM often reveals myophagocytosis (macrophage infiltration) and membrane attack complex deposition on necrotic fibers. Clinically, as illustrated in the reported case of semaglutide-associated IMNM, the progression is often more protracted; this patient demonstrated a 12-fold increase in CK over four months rather than a rapid decline. Furthermore, a hallmark of IMNM—whether idiopathic or drug-induced—is the requirement for significant immunosuppressive therapy, such as pulse corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), to achieve clinical improvement and normalization of CK levels.

### [Pathophysiology] How should a clinician differentiate between transaminasemia of hepatic origin (e.g., NAFLD) and muscle-derived transaminasemia in a patient presenting with myopathic symptoms?

To differentiate between transaminasemia of hepatic origin and muscle-derived transaminasemia in a patient presenting with myopathic symptoms, a clinician must integrate the magnitude of enzyme elevation, the temporal relationship between symptoms and laboratory findings, and the presence of concurrent biomarkers for muscle integrity.

While alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) are commonly associated with liver injury, they are also present in skeletal muscle. In cases of significant muscle breakdown—such as rhabdomyolysis or immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)—these enzymes will leak into the systemic circulation. The primary diagnostic standard for identifying muscle-derived transaminasemia is the measurement of creatine kinase (CK). A profound elevation in CK (often in the thousands, as seen in this case where the patient’s CK peaked at 3992 U/L) provides definitive evidence that the transaminases are reflecting myocyte necrosis rather than primary hepatocyte injury.

Clinical reasoning should also focus on the temporal and historical context of the laboratory values. In this case, the patient had a known history of hepatosteatosis and was diagnosed with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) three years prior to her myopathic symptoms. Her transaminasemia was documented as a "mild elevation" (approximately twice the upper limit of normal) long before the onset of proximal muscle weakness. The fact that her AST and ALT remained only modestly elevated even as her CK and muscle symptoms fluctuated suggests a baseline hepatic source. 

Furthermore, if transaminasemia is muscle-derived, it should correlate with the clinical presentation of weakness or pain and should normalize following the resolution of the myopathic process. In this patient, the persistence of the transaminase elevation after her CK levels normalized and she achieved functional recovery confirmed that her underlying NAFLD was the primary driver of her baseline transaminasemia, whereas the acute myopathy was a distinct, superimposed pathology. Therefore, when faced with mixed elevations, the clinician should prioritize CK levels and the patient’s established history of liver disease to differentiate the sources of transaminase release.
