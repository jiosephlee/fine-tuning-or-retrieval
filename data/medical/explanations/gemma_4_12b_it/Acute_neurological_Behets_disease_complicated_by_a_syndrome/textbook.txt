Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Behçet’s Disease and Neurological Involvement

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a chronic, multisystem inflammatory disorder characterized by recurrent and self-limiting inflammatory lesions. It is most prevalent in regions along the "Silk Road," including the Middle East and East Asia. The clinical hallmark of systemic BD is the presence of mucocutaneous lesions, specifically recurrent oral aphthae (ulcers), genital ulcers, and various skin manifestations such as erythema nodosum or folliculitis-like rashes. Ocular involvement, particularly uveitis, is also a significant feature that can lead to vision loss if left untreated. Because BD is a systemic inflammatory process, it can affect nearly any organ system, but its primary presentation remains centered on these cutaneous and mucosal landmarks.

To standardize diagnosis, clinicians utilize the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD), which employs a point-based system. Patients are evaluated on the presence of oral ulcers, genital ulcers, skin lesions, and ocular lesions. A score of four or more points is required to classify a patient as having "definite" BD. In this case, the patient’s score of five—derived from oral aphthae (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points), and a folliculitis-like rash (1 point)—provided the definitive evidence needed to establish a diagnosis of systemic BD despite the early complexity of his presentation.

Neurological involvement, known as neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), occurs in approximately 10% to 20% of patients with systemic BD. The underlying pathophysiology of neuro-BD is driven by a robust inflammatory response, specifically involving neutrophil hyperreactivity and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-alpha. In the central nervous system (CNS), this inflammation can lead to various degrees of tissue damage and functional impairment. While neuro-BD typically develops several years after the initial diagnosis of systemic BD, the concurrent presentation of both systemic and neurological symptoms—as seen in this case—is relatively rare and necessitates a high index of clinical suspicion.

The clinical presentation of neuro-BD generally falls into two categories: acute and chronic. Acute neuro-BD most frequently presents as a meningoencephalitis-like syndrome, characterized by fever, headache, and sometimes altered mental status. In contrast, chronic neuro-BD is more likely to involve parenchymal brain lesions, with a predilection for the brainstem. A critical diagnostic pearl for the resident is that acute neuro-BD can present with aseptic meningitis and a normal brain MRI, especially in the early stages of the disease. The absence of radiological abnormalities does not rule out neuro-BD, as the inflammatory process may be diffuse rather than localized to a specific parenchymal structure.

Finally, while genetic markers play a role in the predisposition to BD, they are not diagnostic tools. HLA-B51 is the most commonly associated genetic marker with Behçet’s disease in many populations. However, its sensitivity and specificity are variable, and it is not a requirement for diagnosis under the ICBD. Consequently, while a positive HLA-B51 test may support the clinical suspicion of BD in an uncertain case, the diagnosis must remain grounded in the clinical manifestation of mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms.

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Challenges

The initial presentation of this patient highlights a significant diagnostic challenge: distinguishing between a primary infection, a malignancy, and a systemic inflammatory disorder when symptoms are non-specific. The patient arrived with a constellation of fever, headache, and malaise—symptoms that could suggest a wide range of etiologies, from viral meningoencephalitis to a systemic inflammatory condition. However, the presence of specific mucocutaneous findings provided the first major clue toward a diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD). The triad of oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk, and a painful scrotal ulcer (genital aphthosis) are hallmark clinical features. In this case, these findings were pivotal; specifically, genital aphthosis is highly specific for BD and served as a cornerstone for the "definite" diagnosis according to international criteria.

While the mucocutaneous symptoms pointed toward BD, the neurological and systemic symptoms presented a more complex diagnostic picture. The patient’s headache and fever, in the absence of clear focal deficits or a positive response to initial antibiotics, necessitated a broad differential diagnosis that included infectious (bacterial, viral, and tuberculous meningitis), neoplastic (primary CNS malignancy), and autoimmune (neurosarcoidosis) causes. The diagnostic process in such cases requires a systematic "exclusion-based approach." By ruling out common pathogens through CSF analysis and imaging, and by screening for markers of malignancy and sarcoidosis, the clinical team was able to narrow the focus toward an inflammatory etiology, eventually correlating the systemic features with the neurological symptoms.

A critical component of this case was the interpretation of severe hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L) in the context of acute CNS symptoms and fever. In a febrile patient, profound hyponatraemia should immediately trigger a search for Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH), which is frequently triggered by CNS inflammation. This case illustrates how electrolyte disturbances can serve as a diagnostic bridge; the hyponatraemia was not an isolated finding but a potential manifestation of the underlying neuro-inflammatory process. Recognizing this link is essential for identifying systemic diseases that may not initially present with "textbook" neurological signs.

Furthermore, the case underscores the importance of identifying "red flag" complications of severe hyponatraemia that are often overlooked in routine examinations. The patient’s persistent hiccups and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels are classic, yet frequently underappreciated, consequences of profound hyponatraemia. Hiccups can be a specific clinical clue to significant electrolyte imbalance secondary to CNS inflammation, such as meningitis or, in this instance, acute neuro-BD. Additionally, the elevated CK indicated rhabdomyolysis—a complication of severe hyponatraemia that can lead to acute kidney injury if not recognized. For the clinician, these "secondary" symptoms should prompt a thorough evaluation of the patient's serum sodium and osmolality.

Finally, the concurrent diagnosis of systemic and neurological BD represents a distinct clinical challenge. Typically, neuro-BD is a late-stage complication developing years after the initial diagnosis of systemic disease. When both manifestations appear simultaneously, the neurological symptoms can mask the underlying systemic disease, or the systemic disease may be so acute that the neurological involvement is initially dismissed as a non-specific symptom of fever. This case emphasizes that clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for neuro-BD even in patients presenting with new-onset systemic symptoms, as the typical temporal progression of the disease is not always followed.

## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Workup

The clinical presentation of fever, headache, and profound hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L) creates a diagnostic challenge that requires a systematic exclusion of life-threatening conditions. In a patient with these symptoms, the primary concern is a central nervous system (CNS) infection or a primary CNS malignancy, both of which can trigger the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The clinician's logic in this case follows a standard "rule-out" pathway: first addressing acute infectious processes, then evaluating for neoplastic involvement, and finally considering systemic inflammatory disorders once more common causes are excluded.

The first priority in a patient with fever and neurological symptoms is to rule out infectious meningitis. Bacterial meningitis was considered but excluded based on the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, which showed a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis rather than the neutrophilic predominance typical of bacterial infection, alongside negative cultures. Similarly, viral encephalitis—specifically Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) and Varicella Zoster Virus (VZV)—is a frequent cause of acute CNS inflammation and SIADH. However, these were ruled out via negative CSF PCR and antigen testing. Tuberculous meningitis was also excluded through negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing. By methodically clearing these infectious etiologies, the diagnostic focus can shift toward non-infectious inflammatory or systemic pathologies.

Once infection is excluded, the clinician must consider primary CNS malignancies or haematological disorders, as these are classic causes of both SIADH and neurological deficits. In this case, the brain MRI was unremarkable, and CSF cytology showed no malignant cells, providing strong evidence against a primary brain tumor or CNS lymphoma. While the soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) was mildly elevated, it is important to recognize that this marker is non-specific; it can be elevated in various inflammatory states and is insufficient on its own to confirm malignancy in the absence of other supporting evidence.

The differential diagnosis further extends to other systemic inflammatory diseases, such as sarcoidosis, which can manifest with neuro-inflammation. Because neuro-sarcoidosis can sometimes present with subtle imaging findings, the clinician utilized laboratory markers to assist in exclusion. The patient’s normal angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) and lysozyme levels served as supporting evidence against sarcoidosis. Similarly, the absence of characteristic autoimmune markers helped rule out other connective tissue diseases. This highlights a key principle in diagnosing rare conditions like Behçet’s disease: the diagnosis is often achieved through a process of elimination, where systemic inflammatory markers and specific clinical findings (such as the patient's genital ulcers and oral aphthae) eventually point toward a single unified diagnosis.

A critical diagnostic nuance in this case is the interpretation of the neuroimaging and CSF results. The CSF demonstrated a profile of aseptic meningitis (elevated protein and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis without identified pathogens). Crucially, the brain MRI was normal. Students should note that a normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), particularly in the early stages or when the pathology is primarily meningeal rather than parenchymal. The diagnosis of neuro-BD in this patient was ultimately established by integrating these "negative" findings (no infection, no malignancy, no structural brain lesions) with the "positive" clinical markers of systemic BD, such as the hallmark mucocutaneous lesions and positive HLA-B51 status.

## Pathophysiology of SIADH in Neuro-BD

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is characterized by the persistent release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH), also known as vasopressin, despite the absence of physiological triggers such as hypovolemia or hyperosmolality. In a healthy physiological state, ADH is secreted by the posterior pituitary in response to high serum osmolality or significant decreases in blood volume. These hormones act on the collecting ducts of the kidneys to increase water reabsorption. In the context of SIADH, however, the secretion of ADH is "inappropriate" because it occurs in the setting of low serum osmolality and euvolemia. In this case, the underlying driver of SIADH was likely the acute CNS inflammation associated with neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD). Neuro-BD frequently presents as a form of aseptic meningoencephalitis; the inflammatory response within the central nervous system can disrupt the homeostatic mechanisms that regulate ADH, leading to excessive water retention and subsequent hyponatraemia.

The molecular link between neuro-BD and SIADH involves a cascade of proinflammatory cytokines, specifically interleukin-6 (IL-6). Behçet’s disease is characterized by aberrant monocyte activation, which leads to the spontaneous production of pro-inflammatory mediators. In patients with neuro-BD, these activated monocytes can lead to elevated IL-6 levels within the cerebrospinal fluid. Research suggests that IL-6 serves as a potent mediator of ADH release; high concentrations of this cytokine can stimulate the hypothalamus to secrete ADH regardless of the body's osmotic status. While IL-6 levels were not measured in this specific patient, his markedly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) serves as a systemic surrogate for this intense inflammatory activity. Thus, the patient’s SIADH can be understood as an indirect consequence of the cytokine storm generated by the neuro-inflammatory process of BD.

To establish a clinical diagnosis of SIADH, specific laboratory parameters must be met to differentiate it from other causes of hyponatraemia, such as hypovolemia or renal failure. In this case, the diagnosis was confirmed by demonstrating hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia (serum sodium of 124 mmol/L and serum osmolality of 267 mOsm/kg) paired with inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality of 900 mOsm/kg). Furthermore, the presence of high urinary sodium (88 mmol/L) in a euvolemic patient indicates that the kidneys are not attempting to conserve sodium (as they would in dehydration), but are instead actively excreting it while retaining water. The measurement of an elevated ADH level (22.4 pg/mL) in the presence of low serum osmolality provides the definitive biochemical evidence of "inappropriate" secretion.

Management of SIADH focuses on correcting the water excess while avoiding the risks associated with rapid sodium correction. Because the primary issue is water retention rather than sodium loss, the cornerstone of treatment is fluid restriction—limiting the patient's intake to a level that prevents further water accumulation. In this patient, fluid was restricted to less than 800 mL per day. It is critical for the clinician to monitor serum sodium levels closely during this period; rapid correction of chronic hyponatraemia carries a significant risk of osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS), a permanent neurological injury. In this case, the gradual normalization of sodium over two weeks, coupled with the resolution of the underlying neuro-inflammatory process via colchicine, allowed for a safe and effective recovery without the need for intensive interventions like hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists.

## Management Strategies and Clinical Significance

In clinical practice, the management of neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) typically centers on rapid and aggressive anti-inflammatory intervention. Because neuro-BD can involve life-threatening meningoencephalitis or brainstem lesions, corticosteroids are generally considered the first-line therapy to achieve quick control of CNS inflammation. In cases of severe or refractory disease, clinicians may escalate to more potent immunosuppressive agents, such as cyclophosphamide or azathioprine. However, this case presents a notable deviation from the standard protocol by successfully managing acute neuro-BD, systemic manifestations, and associated syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) using colchicine monotherapy.

To understand why colchicine was effective in this specific instance, it is necessary to consider its pharmacological mechanism. Colchicine exerts its anti-inflammatory effects primarily by inhibiting neutrophil migration and activation. In Behçet’s disease, where aberrant neutrophil behavior and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and IL-6 play central roles in pathogenesis, colchicine acts to dampen the underlying inflammatory cascade. By suppressing these cytokines, colchicine may not only alleviate mucocutaneous symptoms but also mitigate the CNS inflammation that triggers the dysregulated secretion of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) in SIADH.

While systemic reviews often suggest that colchicine alone is insufficient for acute neuro-BD—usually recommending it only as an adjunct to corticosteroids—this case demonstrates its potential as a viable primary treatment in certain clinical contexts. The patient achieved complete resolution of fever, neurological symptoms (vertigo and hiccups), and hyponatremia without the need for systemic steroids. This highlights a critical clinical consideration: while steroids remain the gold standard, colchicine may offer a practical and effective alternative for patients with acute neuro-BD who respond well to it, particularly when minimizing systemic steroid exposure is desired.

The long-term outcome for this patient reinforces the success of this management strategy. The patient remained stable for four years following the initiation of colchicine, with no recurrence of neurological symptoms or mucocutaneous flares. This stabilization underscores the importance of recognizing that while neuro-BD is a serious complication, it can often be managed effectively with targeted anti-inflammatory therapy once the underlying inflammatory trigger is addressed.

The clinical significance of this case lies in three key takeaways for the clinician:
1.  **Broaden the Differential:** In patients presenting with fever, headache, and hyponatraemia, clinicians must consider neuro-BD as a potential cause of SIADH, especially if there are concurrent mucocutaneous signs like oral aphthae or genital ulcers.
2.  **Atypical Presentations:** Neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis with a normal brain MRI; a lack of radiological findings does not rule out a significant neurological inflammatory process.
3.  **Clinical Clues:** Be alert for non-specific symptoms such as persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, which may be secondary manifestations of profound hyponatraemia caused by CNS inflammation.
