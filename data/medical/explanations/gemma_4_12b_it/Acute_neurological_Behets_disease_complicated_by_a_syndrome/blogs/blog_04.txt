Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Colchicine vs. Steroids: Rethinking the Management of Acute Neuro-Behçet’s Disease

We are taught that neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) is a high-stakes inflammatory emergency. When a patient presents with CNS involvement, the reflex is almost always to reach for high-dose corticosteroids or even more aggressive immunosuppression. But what happens when the "gold standard" isn't the only path? 

Consider a case of a man in his 60s presenting with a febrile headache and a startlingly low sodium level of 124 mmol/L. While the initial workup points toward SIADH, the clinical picture—oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and a painful scrotal ulcer—unveils a concurrent diagnosis of systemic and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. Despite the severity of the neurological and endocrine involvement, this patient achieved a full recovery using nothing but colchicine monotherapy.

## The Clinical Puzzle: SIADH and the "Hidden" Neuro-BD

In many cases of neuro-BD, we expect to see significant parenchymal lesions on MRI, particularly in the brainstem. However, as this case highlights, acute neuro-BD can present as an aseptic meningitis with a completely normal brain MRI. This "radiologically quiet" presentation can make the diagnosis incredibly difficult, leading clinicians down a rabbit hole of testing for malignancies, sarcoidosis, and various viral encephalitides.

The presence of SIADH in this patient provides a fascinating link between systemic inflammation and endocrine dysfunction. The pathophysiology likely involves the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines—specifically IL-6—which are known to trigger the release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH). For the clinician on the floor, this means that when you see profound hyponatraemia in a patient with non-specific CNS symptoms, you must look closer at the underlying inflammatory drivers.

## Don't Miss the "Extra" Signs

One of the most striking parts of this case is the presentation of the hyponatraemia itself. The patient suffered from persistent hiccups and elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L), indicating rhabdomyolysis. These are often overlooked "red flags" of severe, acute hyponatraemia. If a patient has a persistent hiccup and a low sodium, don't just treat the cough; look for the electrolyte disturbance.

## The Colchicine Controversy

Here is where we hit the clinical tension: the literature generally suggests that colchicine is insufficient for managing acute neuro-BD, which usually requires steroids. Yet, in this case, colchicine monotherapy successfully resolved the mucocutaneous lesions, the neurological symptoms, and the SIADH-induced hyponatraemia.

Why did it work? Colchicine’s mechanism involves the inhibition of neutrophil migration and the suppression of IL-1β and IL-6 production. Since IL-6 is a key driver of both the neuro-inflammatory cascade in Behçet’s and the subsequent ADH release in SIADH, colchicine may be hitting the "master switch" of this specific inflammatory cycle. While we shouldn't abandon steroids as the first-line treatment for severe neuro-BD, this case opens the door to considering colchicine as a potent steroid-sparing strategy, especially in patients with early-stage presentations or those who are poor candidates for long-term corticosteroid use.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Think Neuro-BD Early:** In patients with Behçet’s disease (or even those with new mucocutaneous lesions), a fever and headache should trigger an immediate evaluation for neuro-BD, even if the brain MRI is initially unremarkable.
*   **SIADH as a Proxy for Inflammation:** Recognize that CNS inflammation (like neuro-BD) can manifest as SIADH. If you see hyponatraemia without a clear renal or pulmonary cause, look for an inflammatory trigger.
*   **Watch for "Hiccup" Clues:** Persistent hiccups can be a sentinel sign of severe hyponatraemia secondary to CNS inflammation or meningitis.
*   **The Colchicine Factor:** While steroids remain the standard, colchicine’s ability to suppress IL-6 makes it a viable consideration for managing the inflammatory drivers of both neuro-BD and associated SIADH.
*   **Avoid Diagnostic Over-Testing:** This case emphasizes the danger of "shotgunning" tests for sarcoidosis and malignancy without clear clinical indicators. Focus on the clinical triad: mucocutaneous findings, CNS symptoms, and electrolyte disturbances.
