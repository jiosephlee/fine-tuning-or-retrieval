Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# When the 'Hidden' Behçet’s Strikes: Navigating Concurrent Systemic and Neurological Involvement

In the world of Behçet’s disease (BD), we are often taught to think in terms of timelines. Typically, the "classic" progression involves a patient presenting with recurrent oral ulcers or genital aphthae, followed by a multi-year odyssey of systemic involvement before neurological manifestations—neuro-BD—eventually emerge. It’s a predictable, if grueling, trajectory.

However, this case serves as a powerful reminder that clinical reality doesn't always follow the textbook. Here, we see a patient where the systemic and neurological "clocks" started at the exact same time. When neuro-BD presents concurrently with the first signs of systemic disease, the diagnostic challenge shifts from "monitoring a known progression" to "unmasking a hidden systemic inflammatory disorder."

## The Diagnostic Trap: "Normal" MRIs and Non-Specific Symptoms

The patient—a man in his 60s—presented with a constellation of symptoms that could easily point toward a common infection: fever, headache, and significant hyponatraemia (sodium 124 mmol/L). In an emergency setting, the immediate suspicion is often bacterial or viral meningitis. 

While the CSF analysis did confirm aseptic meningitis, the initial lack of focal neurological deficits and a remarkably unremarkable brain MRI might lead a clinician to look elsewhere. It is a common pitfall to assume that a "clean" MRI rules out neuro-BD. In the acute phase of neuro-BD, the presentation can be a self-limiting meningoencephalitis without significant parenchymal lesions or hypothalamic involvement. 

The "anchor" in this case was the mucocutaneous findings: oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and eventually, a painful scrotal ulcer. When you see these specific markers alongside CNS inflammation, neuro-BD must move to the top of your differential, even if the imaging doesn't show a "smoking gun."

## The Electrolyte Connection: SIADH and the "Extra" Clues

One of the most interesting aspects of this case is the presence of SIADH. While we often associate SIADH with specific medications (like cyclophosphamide) or known CNS malignancies, this case highlights it as a potential direct manifestation of the neuro-inflammatory process in acute neuro-BD. 

The inflammatory cascade—specifically the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6—can disrupt the delicate balance of ADH secretion. But there's a secondary lesson here regarding the *consequences* of that SIADH. The patient presented with persistent hiccups and elevated creatine kinase (rhabdomyolysis). While these might seem like "noise" in a complex case, they are classic, often underappreciated clinical clues to severe, profound hyponatraemia. If the sodium is low enough, the brain and muscles start to complain in very specific ways; don't let the primary diagnosis distract you from these secondary metabolic red flags.

## Treatment: When Colchicine Steps Up

Standard of care for acute neuro-BD usually involves heavy-hitting corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. However, this case offers a fascinating observation: the patient responded remarkably well to colchicine monotherapy. 

While colchicine is the mainstay for the "skin and mouth" aspects of BD, its role in neuro-BD is less clear and often considered secondary. Yet, by inhibiting neutrophil migration and suppressing the production of IL-1β and IL-6, colchicine may provide a potent enough anti-inflammatory effect to manage certain presentations of acute neuro-BD, particularly when systemic symptoms are prominent. It suggests that in some patients, we might be able to achieve a "clinical win" without jumping straight to high-dose steroids.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Think Broadly on Concurrent Presentation:** Don't assume neuro-BD will wait years to appear. If a patient presents with "new" systemic BD symptoms (ulcers, rashes) and CNS symptoms (headache, fever) simultaneously, keep neuro-BD high on your list.
*   **The MRI "False Negative":** A normal brain MRI does *not* exclude acute neuro-BD, especially when the primary pathology is aseptic meningitis rather than a parenchymal lesion.
*   **Recognize the SIADH Link:** CNS inflammation from neuro-BD can trigger SIADH. If your patient with suspected neuro-BD has unexplained hyponatraemia, look for signs of impaired water excretion.
*   **Connect the Dots on Electrolytes:** Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis in a patient with CNS inflammation and low sodium should immediately trigger a high suspicion for severe, acute hyponatraemia.
*   **Colchicine as a Tool:** While not the standard for severe neuro-BD, colchicine monotherapy may be a viable and effective strategy for patients presenting with concurrent systemic and neurological manifestations.



# Inflammation-Induced SIADH: Is Neuro-Behçet’s a New Player?

When a patient presents with fever, headache, and profound hyponatremia, your mind likely jumps to the usual suspects: bacterial meningitis, viral encephalitis, or perhaps a systemic pulmonary or renal issue. We are trained to look for the "big" pathology. However, sometimes the culprit is a rare systemic inflammatory disorder lurking in the shadows. This case highlights an intriguing intersection between Behçet’s disease (BD) and the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), providing a masterclass in how systemic inflammation can manifest as a localized endocrine derangement.

## The Case: A Diagnostic Puzzle
A man in his 60s presented with a three-day history of fever, headache, and general malaise. To the uninitiated, the initial workup—showing a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L and an elevated CRP—might suggest a standard infectious process. However, the clinical picture was nuanced. The patient had oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and eventually, a painful scrotal ulcer. 

While the differential diagnosis was broad—including HSV encephalitis, TB, and even CNS lymphoma—the brain MRI was surprisingly unremarkable. The gold standard for the diagnosis of neuro-Behçet’s (neuro-BD) was found in the CSF: aseptic meningitis with mild lymphocytic pleocytosis. The diagnosis of "definite" Behçet’s was confirmed by the combination of mucocutaneous findings and positive HLA-B51. 

## The SIADH Connection and the "Hidden" Clues
One of the most compelling aspects of this case is the presence of SIADH in the setting of acute neuro-BD. While SIADH is a well-known consequence of any CNS inflammation, its specific association with neuro-BD is rarely discussed in clinical literature. 

The patient presented with two classic, yet sometimes overlooked, physiological "red flags" of severe hyponatremia: persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK). Hiccups are a known, albeit underappreciated, clinical clue to severe hyponatremia caused by CNS inflammation. Similarly, rhabdomyolysis can occur as a metabolic consequence of profound sodium depletion. These details serve as a reminder that electrolyte disturbances are not just numbers on a lab report; they have systemic physiological consequences that can mimic other pathologies.

## Pathophysiology: The IL-6 Bridge
How does a systemic inflammatory disease like Behçet’s trigger the secretion of ADH? The answer likely lies in the cytokine storm. Neuro-BD is characterized by aberrant monocyte activation, which leads to the spontaneous production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly Interleukin-6 (IL-6). 

Research suggests that IL-6 is a potent driver of ADH release. In the context of neuro-BD, the inflammatory environment of the CNS likely stimulates the release of ADH despite low serum osmolality, leading to the water retention seen in SIADH. This provides a clear mechanistic link: Systemic Inflammation $\rightarrow$ Monocyte Activation $\rightarrow$ IL-6 Production $\rightarrow$ ADH Release $\rightarrow$ Hyponatremia.

## Treatment: The Power of Colchicine
Perhaps the most surprising "pearl" from this case is the treatment. While high-dose corticosteroids are the traditional mainstay for acute neuro-BD, this patient saw a rapid resolution of his mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological manifestations, and even his hyponatremia with **colchicine monotherapy**. 

While colchicine is often considered insufficient for acute neuro-BD (which usually requires aggressive immunosuppression), its role in inhibiting neutrophil migration and suppressing the production of IL-1$\beta$ and IL-6 may have been sufficient to dampen the specific inflammatory pathway driving this patient's SIADH and neuro-inflammation. It serves as a reminder that while "gold standard" treatments exist, the specific inflammatory drivers of a patient’s presentation can sometimes be managed with targeted, conventional therapies.

## Clinical Pearls
*   **Think Neuro-BD in Systemic Inflammation:** In patients with mucocutaneous findings (ulcers, aphthae) who present with fever and headache, neuro-BD should be high on your differential, even if the initial MRI is unremarkable.
*   **Hiccups are a Clue:** Persistent hiccups in a patient with CNS symptoms and low sodium should immediately trigger a high suspicion for SIADH secondary to meningitis or encephalitis.
*   **The SIADH-IL-6 Link:** Consider the role of IL-6 in mediating ADH secretion. If a patient has a known inflammatory condition and develops SIADH, look for the cytokine "bridge."
*   **Don't Over-Test:** The authors noted that while they screened for sarcoidosis and malignancy, these tests didn't provide the diagnosis. Use a targeted approach—investigate the symptoms, but avoid "shotgun" testing for every possible autoimmune disease unless the clinical picture demands it.
*   **Colchicine's Role:** While not the standard for acute neuro-BD, colchicine's ability to suppress IL-6 makes it a viable consideration in managing the inflammatory components of Behçet’s disease.



# Beyond the Sodium Level: Hiccups and Rhabdomyolysis as Red Flags for Hyponatremia

When we see a sodium level of 124 mmol/L on a lab report, our clinical reflexes usually kick into high gear regarding fluid restriction and monitoring for cerebral edema. We start checking the volume status, calculating the corrected sodium, and looking for the "why"—is it a medication, a primary endocrine failure, or a systemic inflammatory process? But as this fascinating case of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) reminds us, the "why" is often hiding in plain sight within the patient's more subtle, "soft" clinical symptoms.

In this case, a man in his 60s presented with a triad of fever, headache, and a persistent, irritating symptom: hiccups. While often dismissed as a minor nuisance or a gastrointestinal quirk, persistent hiccups in the setting of CNS symptoms can be a significant clinical "red flag" for profound hyponatremia, particularly when secondary to meningitis or other forms of CNS inflammation. Here, the hiccups served as a sentinel sign for the underlying Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH) triggered by acute neuro-Behçet’s.

The complexity of this case was compounded by another "hidden" laboratory finding: a significantly elevated creatine kinase (CK) level indicating rhabdomyolysis. While we often associate rhabdomyolysis with extreme physical exertion or crush injuries, it is a recognized—though frequently overlooked—complication of severe hyponatremia. The profound electrolyte imbalance can lead to muscle cell membrane instability and metabolic muscle damage. In the whirlwind of managing a patient with a sodium of 124 and a worsening headache, it is easy to overlook the CK and the hiccups, yet together they paint a picture of a patient experiencing a severe, systemic metabolic crisis driven by neuro-inflammation.

The diagnosis of acute neuro-BD was a diagnostic journey in itself. The patient presented with classic mucocutaneous features of Behçet’s disease (BD)—oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes—but the neurological involvement was the primary driver of his acute presentation. Because the brain MRI was initially unremarkable (a common finding in the early phases of neuro-BD), the clinical suspicion had to remain high for aseptic meningitis. The link here is the inflammatory cytokine cascade; specifically, elevated IL-6 levels—common in neuro-Behçet’s—can drive the inappropriate release of ADH, leading directly to SIADH.

Perhaps the most surprising takeaway from this case is the management. While corticosteroids are the standard of care for neuro-BD, this patient achieved a resolution of his mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological manifestations, and even his hyponatremia with colchicine monotherapy. While colchicine is traditionally reserved for the "skin and mouth" manifestations of BD, its ability to inhibit neutrophil activation and suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and IL-6 may provide a pathway for managing the underlying inflammatory driver of SIADH in some patients.

**Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points:**

*   **Listen to the "Soft" Symptoms:** Persistent hiccups should be viewed as a potential neurological "red flag" for severe hyponatremia or CNS irritation (e.g., meningitis, SIADH).
*   **Look for Hidden Complications:** In patients with profound hyponatremia, check the CK. Rhabdomyolysis is a recognized metabolic complication of severe electrolyte shifts that can be easily missed in the acute setting.
*   **Neuro-BD Can be "Radiologically Quiet":** A normal brain MRI does not rule out acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, particularly in the early stages of presentation.
*   **Inflammatory Link:** Consider the role of cytokines (like IL-6) in bridging the gap between systemic inflammatory diseases (like BD) and endocrine complications like SIADH.
*   **Broaden Your Treatment Horizon:** While steroids are standard for neuro-BD, colchicine may have a role in modulating the underlying inflammatory response in specific clinical contexts.



# Colchicine vs. Steroids: Rethinking the Management of Acute Neuro-Behçet’s Disease

We are taught that neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) is a high-stakes inflammatory emergency. When a patient presents with CNS involvement, the reflex is almost always to reach for high-dose corticosteroids or even more aggressive immunosuppression. But what happens when the "gold standard" isn't the only path? 

Consider a case of a man in his 60s presenting with a febrile headache and a startlingly low sodium level of 124 mmol/L. While the initial workup points toward SIADH, the clinical picture—oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and a painful scrotal ulcer—unveils a concurrent diagnosis of systemic and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease. Despite the severity of the neurological and endocrine involvement, this patient achieved a full recovery using nothing but colchicine monotherapy.

## The Clinical Puzzle: SIADH and the "Hidden" Neuro-BD

In many cases of neuro-BD, we expect to see significant parenchymal lesions on MRI, particularly in the brainstem. However, as this case highlights, acute neuro-BD can present as an aseptic meningitis with a completely normal brain MRI. This "radiologically quiet" presentation can make the diagnosis incredibly difficult, leading clinicians down a rabbit hole of testing for malignancies, sarcoidosis, and various viral encephalitides.

The presence of SIADH in this patient provides a fascinating link between systemic inflammation and endocrine dysfunction. The pathophysiology likely involves the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines—specifically IL-6—which are known to trigger the release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH). For the clinician on the floor, this means that when you see profound hyponatraemia in a patient with non-specific CNS symptoms, you must look closer at the underlying inflammatory drivers.

## Don't Miss the "Extra" Signs

One of the most striking parts of this case is the presentation of the hyponatraemia itself. The patient suffered from persistent hiccups and elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L), indicating rhabdomyolysis. These are often overlooked "red flags" of severe, acute hyponatraemia. If a patient has a persistent hiccup and a low sodium, don't just treat the cough; look for the electrolyte disturbance.

## The Colchicine Controversy

Here is where we hit the clinical tension: the literature generally suggests that colchicine is insufficient for managing acute neuro-BD, which usually requires steroids. Yet, in this case, colchicine monotherapy successfully resolved the mucocutaneous lesions, the neurological symptoms, and the SIADH-induced hyponatraemia.

Why did it work? Colchicine’s mechanism involves the inhibition of neutrophil migration and the suppression of IL-1β and IL-6 production. Since IL-6 is a key driver of both the neuro-inflammatory cascade in Behçet’s and the subsequent ADH release in SIADH, colchicine may be hitting the "master switch" of this specific inflammatory cycle. While we shouldn't abandon steroids as the first-line treatment for severe neuro-BD, this case opens the door to considering colchicine as a potent steroid-sparing strategy, especially in patients with early-stage presentations or those who are poor candidates for long-term corticosteroid use.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Think Neuro-BD Early:** In patients with Behçet’s disease (or even those with new mucocutaneous lesions), a fever and headache should trigger an immediate evaluation for neuro-BD, even if the brain MRI is initially unremarkable.
*   **SIADH as a Proxy for Inflammation:** Recognize that CNS inflammation (like neuro-BD) can manifest as SIADH. If you see hyponatraemia without a clear renal or pulmonary cause, look for an inflammatory trigger.
*   **Watch for "Hiccup" Clues:** Persistent hiccups can be a sentinel sign of severe hyponatraemia secondary to CNS inflammation or meningitis.
*   **The Colchicine Factor:** While steroids remain the standard, colchicine’s ability to suppress IL-6 makes it a viable consideration for managing the inflammatory drivers of both neuro-BD and associated SIADH.
*   **Avoid Diagnostic Over-Testing:** This case emphasizes the danger of "shotgunning" tests for sarcoidosis and malignancy without clear clinical indicators. Focus on the clinical triad: mucocutaneous findings, CNS symptoms, and electrolyte disturbances.



# The Art of Exclusion: Navigating the Differential for Fever, Headache, and Hyponatremia

We’ve all seen the "triad" that makes a clinician pause: a patient presenting with fever, headache, and profound hyponatremia. It is a diagnostic labyrinth where the wrong turn can lead to missing a life-threatening infection, a hidden malignancy, or a rare systemic inflammatory flare. In many cases, the diagnosis is found not by finding the "smoking gun" immediately, but by systematically stripping away the possibilities until only the truth remains.

In a recent case, a man in his 60s presented with this exact triad, complicated by persistent hiccups and a "folliculitis-like" rash. While his labs showed a striking sodium level of 124 mmol/L and an elevated CRP, the initial challenge was determining whether we were looking at a standard "medical" hyponatremia or something much more sinister involving the central nervous system (CNS).

## The Systematic Strip-Down

When you see CNS symptoms combined with systemic inflammation, the "rule-out" list must be aggressive. The first priority is always infectious. Given the headache and fever, we look for the usual suspects: HSV, VZV, and bacterial meningitis. In this case, the CSF analysis was the primary tool of exclusion—showing a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis but remaining negative for viral antigens, bacterial cultures, and mycobacterial markers. 

Once the "germs" are off the table, the focus shifts to the "hidden" killers: malignancy and autoimmune disease. This is where the "art" of the differential comes in. The patient had a mildly elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R). While often a red flag for hematologic malignancy, it can be a non-specific marker of general inflammation. When paired with a clean brain MRI and normal CSF cytology, it allows us to pivot away from lymphoma and toward systemic inflammatory disorders. Similarly, while sarcoidosis is a high-priority differential for CNS inflammation, normal ACE and lysozyme levels helped narrow the field, even if they aren't perfectly sensitive.

## The Rare Reveal: Neuro-Behçet’s and the SIADH Connection

After ruling out the common and the catastrophic, we were left with a rare clinical scenario: acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) presenting concurrently with systemic BD. Typically, neuro-BD develops years after the initial diagnosis of mucocutaneous lesions, but here, the oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and skin rashes appeared simultaneously with the neurological symptoms.

One of the most interesting nuances of this case is the presence of SIADH. While we often associate SIADH with medications (like cyclophosphamide) or specific tumors, this case highlights a potential direct link between neuro-inflammatory states—specifically neuro-BD—and the dysregulation of antidiuretic hormone. The mechanism may be driven by proinflammatory cytokines like IL-6, which are known to trigger ADH release. It serves as a reminder that hyponatremia in a patient with CNS inflammation shouldn't just be managed as an electrolyte imbalance; it must be managed as a symptom of the underlying inflammatory process.

## Clinical Pearls and Management

This case offers several high-yield takeaways for the ED and the clinic:

*   **The "Hiccup" Clue:** Persistent hiccups can be a subtle but significant clinical indicator of severe hyponatremia caused by SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation (like meningitis). 
*   **The Rhabdomyolysis Connection:** Severe hyponatremia can cause muscle damage. In this patient, a CK of 1349 U/L was identified—a complication of profound hyponatremia that is frequently overlooked in the initial workup of a febrile patient.
*   **Imaging Limits:** A normal brain MRI does *not* rule out acute neuro-BD. In the early stages, the disease can present as aseptic meningitis without significant parenchymal lesions.
*   **Treatment Nuance:** While corticosteroids are the standard for neuro-BD, this patient responded remarkably well to colchicine monotherapy. While colchicine is usually considered insufficient for acute neuro-BD, this case suggests it may be a viable option for managing the mucocutaneous and SIADH components when steroids are undesirable.

**Take-Home Messages:**

*   In a patient with fever, headache, and hyponatremia, systematically exclude infection and malignancy before settling on rare inflammatory diseases.
*   Consider neuro-Behçet’s disease in patients with mucocutaneous lesions who present with non-specific CNS symptoms, even if neuro-involvement is concurrent with the initial diagnosis.
*   Be aware that SIADH can be a direct manifestation of neuro-inflammation via cytokine-mediated pathways.
*   Look for "extra" symptoms like hiccups or elevated CK as potential red flags for the severity of an underlying electrolyte disturbance.
