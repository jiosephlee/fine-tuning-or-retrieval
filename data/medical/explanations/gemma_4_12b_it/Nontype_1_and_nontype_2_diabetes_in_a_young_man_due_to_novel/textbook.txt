## Foundations of Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY)

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is defined as a group of monogenic, non-autoimmune diabetes disorders characterized by a primary defect in the development and function of pancreatic $\beta$-cells. Unlike Type 1 diabetes, which involves the autoimmune destruction of $\beta$-cells, or Type 2 diabetes, which is primarily driven by insulin resistance and polygenic factors, MODY is caused by a mutation in a single gene. These conditions typically manifest before the age of 25 and follow an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. This genetic structure means that symptomatic diabetes is frequently observed across multiple generations of a family, often presenting in young adulthood.

The epidemiology of MODY suggests that it accounts for approximately 1% to 6% of young-onset diabetes cases. However, prevalence rates vary significantly across different ethnic and racial groups. Historically, clinical research has been heavily concentrated on White European populations, which may lead to an underestimation of MODY's prevalence in other regions. Recent investigations into Asian and Australian populations are beginning to provide a more global perspective. A significant challenge in determining accurate prevalence is that genetic screening is often limited to individuals who exhibit specific clinical characteristics, potentially overlooking cases that do not conform to the "classic" presentation.

Clinically, MODY encompasses several distinct genetic subtypes, with over 15 different genes linked to the monogenic phenotype. The specific gene involved is critical because it dictates the underlying pathophysiology and, consequently, the appropriate therapeutic strategy. The most frequently identified genes include *HNF1A*, *GCK*, *HNF4A*, and *HNF1B*. Among these, *HNF1A* is the most prevalent, accounting for approximately 30% to 65% of all MODY cases. Identifying the specific mutation is essential for precision medicine, as it allows clinicians to move beyond general glucose management toward targeted therapies that preserve $\beta$-cell function.

*HNF1A*-MODY presents a specific clinical profile that can often be mistaken for other forms of diabetes. In early adolescence, the disease may be relatively "silent," with elevated blood glucose levels discovered unexpectedly during routine screening rather than through acute symptoms. A hallmark of *HNF1A*-MODY is significant postprandial hyperglycemia (typically $\ge$11.1 mmol/L) in the absence of clinical signs of insulin resistance. Additionally, patients frequently present with glycosuria. This occurs because the *HNF1A* mutation leads to a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, resulting from compromised glucose transport in the renal tubules. While these hallmarks are diagnostic clues, the disease may eventually progress to present with polydipsia, polyuria, and weight loss, which can complicate the differentiation between MODY and common diabetes types.

## Clinical Presentation and the Diagnostic Dilemma

The initial presentation of this patient underscores the significant diagnostic challenges inherent in identifying Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY). The patient, a young adult, presented with classic symptoms of hyperglycemia—polydipsia, polyphagia, and weight loss—alongside significant glycosuria and an elevated HbA1c of 10.2%. While these symptoms are universal across the major forms of diabetes, the absence of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and the patient's relatively healthy BMI of 24.6 kg/m² provided early clues that the underlying pathology might not be standard Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM). Furthermore, the absence of acanthosis nigricans—a physical marker of significant insulin resistance—further distanced the clinical picture from a typical T2DM presentation, though it did not rule out other forms of glucose intolerance.

A critical component of the diagnostic workup in this case was the thorough assessment of family history. The report identifies a clear pedigree of early-onset diabetes across three generations: the maternal grandmother (diagnosed in her 30s), the mother (late 20s), and the patient’s younger brother (age 20). This pattern strongly suggests an autosomal dominant inheritance, which is a hallmark of MODY. In clinical practice, when a patient presents with new-onset diabetes before the age of 25 and possesses a multi-generational family history of early-onset diabetes, the suspicion for a monogenic form of the disease must be high. This distinguishes it from Type 1 Diabetes (T1D), which is typically sporadic or polygenic and autoimmune, and T2DM, which is usually associated with a more complex, multifactorial inheritance.

Differentiating MODY from T1D and T2DM remains a common clinical dilemma because their phenotypes frequently overlap. T1D is characterized by autoimmune destruction of beta cells, whereas MODY involves a primary genetic defect in beta-cell function or development. In this case, the absence of islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A) was a pivotal finding, as it helped rule out T1D and pointed toward a non-autoimmune etiology. Conversely, while T2DM is often associated with obesity and metabolic syndrome, the patient’s lean profile and the specific family history made MODY a more likely candidate. However, the clinical similarity between these conditions can lead to misdiagnosis, often resulting in inappropriate treatment—such as the initial use of insulin and metformin before the genetic confirmation of an *HNF1A* mutation.

It is also important to recognize the evolving limitations of using anthropometric markers like BMI and metabolic syndrome indicators to rule out T2DM. Historically, a lean BMI was a strong indicator for MODY over T2DM. However, as the prevalence of obesity rises globally, the "lean" phenotype is becoming less specific. Patients with T2DM can now present with a normal BMI, and conversely, those with MODY may develop metabolic features that mimic T2DM. This shift in the modern epidemiological landscape emphasizes that while BMI and metabolic markers are useful, they are insufficient on their own. Instead, clinicians must rely on a combination of age of onset, family pedigree, and the presence or absence of autoantibodies to trigger the necessary genetic screening for MODY.

## Diagnostic Workup: From Biomarkers to Genetics

The diagnostic journey for Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) begins with identifying clinical features that deviate from the typical presentation of Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes. In this case, the patient presented during a routine health examination with hyperglycemia (HbA1c 10.2%) and glycosuria. A hallmark of HNF1A-MODY is a reduced renal threshold for glucose, which can lead to glycosuria even in the absence of significant insulin resistance. Furthermore, a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) revealed a significantly elevated postprandial glucose level of 19.8 mmol/L. While these biochemical markers confirm a state of diabetes, they are not specific to MODY; therefore, the clinician must look for specific clues, such as the patient's strong family history of early-onset diabetes and the absence of severe obesity or metabolic syndrome.

Once a suspicion of MODY is raised, the clinician must differentiate the condition from Type 1 diabetes (T1D) and Type 2 diabetes (T2D). This is primarily achieved by assessing the patient's autoimmune status and residual beta-cell function. The patient’s islet autoantibody tests (GADA and IA-2A) were negative, which helps rule out the autoimmune destruction of beta-cells characteristic of T1D. To evaluate insulin secretion, a glucagon stimulation test was performed. By measuring C-peptide levels—a byproduct of insulin production—after a glucagon injection, clinicians can gauge the pancreas's ability to secrete insulin. In this case, the stimulated C-peptide reached 0.83 nmol/L. Because a level greater than 0.60 nmol/L generally indicates preserved endogenous insulin secretion, this finding supports a diagnosis of a non-autoimmune, monogenic form of diabetes like MODY, rather than the profound beta-cell failure seen in advanced T1D.

While biochemical and functional assays provide strong evidence, molecular genetic testing is the gold standard for confirming a MODY diagnosis and determining the specific gene involved. In this case, Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) was utilized to screen for mutations in known MODY genes. This revealed a novel heterozygous variant in the *HNF1A* gene (p.N270S). Because this specific mutation had not been previously reported in databases like ClinVar or VarSome, the clinical team had to apply the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines to interpret its pathogenicity. By evaluating the variant’s location (the DNA-binding domain), its high AlphaMissense score, and its segregation with the disease within the family, the variant was classified as pathogenic. This precision medicine approach allows for a definitive diagnosis that directly informs the treatment strategy.

To assist in clinical reasoning before genetic results are available, clinicians often use tools like the MODY probability calculator developed by the Exeter group. In this patient, the calculator provided a 62.4% probability of MODY. However, it is important for the clinician to recognize the limitations of such tools; many were derived from White European populations and may yield less accurate results in other ethnic groups, such as Asian populations. Therefore, while these calculators are excellent for identifying candidates for genetic screening, they should be used in conjunction with a thorough family history, biochemical assessment, and eventually, confirmatory genetic sequencing to ensure an accurate diagnosis.

## Molecular Pathophysiology of HNF1A Mutations

To understand the clinical progression of Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) in this patient, one must first understand the role of the *HNF1A* gene. *HNF1A* (Hepatocyte Nuclear Factor 1-alpha) is a critical transcription factor located on the q24 region of chromosome 12. As a transcription factor, the HNF1A protein acts as a molecular "switch," binding to specific DNA sequences to regulate the expression of various downstream genes. While it is named for its prominent role in the liver, it is expressed across several tissues, including the kidneys and the pancreas. This broad expression explains why mutations in *HNF1A* can lead to multi-organ manifestations, specifically affecting both glucose metabolism and renal glucose handling.

In the pancreas, the primary driver of the patient's hyperglycemia is the progressive decline in $\beta$-cell function resulting from *HNF1A* mutations. The HNF1A protein is essential for the transcription of several key genes involved in insulin production and glucose sensing. Specifically, mutations in *HNF1A* lead to the impaired expression of the insulin gene itself, as well as the *SLC2A2* gene, which encodes GLUT2—a glucose transporter crucial for the $\beta$-cell’s ability to "sense" blood glucose levels. Furthermore, these mutations result in reduced activity of pyruvate kinase, a rate-limiting enzyme in glycolysis. The cumulative effect of these defects is a diminished capacity for the $\beta$-cells to secrete sufficient insulin in response to glucose, which correlates with the insufficient C-peptide levels observed during the patient's glucagon stimulation test.

Beyond the pancreas, *HNF1A* plays a significant role in the renal physiology of glucose reabsorption. A hallmark of *HNF1A*-MODY, as seen in this case, is glycosuria (glucose in the urine) occurring even when blood glucose levels are not excessively high. This is due to the role of HNF1A in regulating the expression of SGLT2 (Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter 2) in the renal tubules. SGLT2 is responsible for reabsorbing the majority of filtered glucose from the primary urine back into the bloodstream. When *HNF1A* function is compromised, SGLT2 expression is reduced, lowering the renal threshold for glucose. Consequently, glucose is excreted in the urine more readily, a distinguishing feature from many other forms of diabetes and a key diagnostic clue for HNF1A-MODY.

The severity and phenotype of the disease are often dictated by the specific genomic architecture of the mutation. The HNF1A protein consists of several functional domains, most notably the DNA-binding domain and the transactivation domain. Research indicates that the majority of pathogenic mutations identified in MODY cases are located within the DNA-binding domain, which directly interferes with the protein's ability to latch onto target genes. The novel p.N270S mutation identified in this patient is situated within this DNA-binding domain. Understanding this architecture is vital for precision medicine; while some mutations cause severe, early-onset MODY, others may result in a milder phenotype or even only increase the risk for Type 2 Diabetes, depending on how significantly they disrupt the protein's structural integrity.

## Precision Medicine: Tailored Management and Outcomes

The transition from a presumptive diagnosis of Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes to a confirmed diagnosis of Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) represents a shift toward precision medicine. In this case, the initial management strategy was dictated by the patient’s acute clinical presentation: significant hyperglycemia (HbA1c 10.2%) and symptoms of polyuria and weight loss. Because the underlying etiology was unknown at the time of admission, the patient was started on a standard Type 2 diabetes (T2D) regimen involving basal insulin (degludec), metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor (saxagliptin). This approach aimed to achieve rapid glycemic control by addressing perceived insulin resistance and insufficient secretion. However, once genetic testing identified a novel *HNF1A* mutation, the treatment plan was refined to target the specific pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY—a primary defect in $\beta$-cell function rather than insulin resistance.

Pharmacological management of HNF1A-MODY is uniquely tailored to the patient's remaining $\beta$-cell capacity and the risk of hypoglycemia. While low-dose sulfonylureas are traditionally considered first-line therapy because they enhance insulin secretion via potassium-sensitive ATP channels, GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) offer a sophisticated alternative. In this case, the patient was transitioned to dulaglutide after achieving initial stability. GLP-1 RAs are particularly advantageous in HNF1A-MODY as they provide effective glycemic control with a lower risk of mild hypoglycemia compared to sulfonylureas like glimepiride. Furthermore, GLP-1 RAs are valued for their ability to support and potentially preserve residual $\beta$-cell function, which is critical in a progressive monogenic disorder.

A cornerstone of precision medicine in HNF1A-MODY is the identification of specific contraindications based on the molecular pathway. Because the *HNF1A* transcription factor regulates the expression of the sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2), patients with this mutation often have reduced renal glucose reabsorption. Consequently, the use of SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY patients is potentially dangerous. These medications can lead to severe dehydration, vascular collapse, and a higher risk of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) due to the inhibition of already compromised SGLT-2 activity. Recognizing this genetic-pharmacological interaction is essential to prevent iatrogenic complications that would not typically be expected in standard T2D management.

The outcome of this case underscores the clinical utility of genetic-informed therapy. By pivoting from a broad insulin-based regimen to a targeted GLP-1 RA and metformin combination, the patient’s HbA1c was successfully reduced from 10.2% to a stable 6.0% within six months. This transition not only optimized glycemic control but also simplified the maintenance regimen, avoiding the risks associated with unnecessary insulin. Long-term management now focuses on regular surveillance for microalbuminuria and retinopathy, demonstrating that while genetic diagnosis dictates the "how" of treatment, the "what" remains the standard of care: the prevention of chronic diabetic complications.

## Clinical Significance and Future Directions

The primary clinical takeaway from this case is that an accurate diagnosis of Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) is not merely a taxonomic exercise; it is a fundamental requirement for appropriate clinical management. In this patient, the initial management reflected a common clinical pitfall: treating hyperglycaemia with a broad-spectrum regimen—including basal insulin—based on a presumptive diagnosis of type 1 or type 2 diabetes. However, the identification of the *HNF1A* mutation allowed for a significant shift in the treatment strategy. Because HNF1A-MODY involves a specific defect in $\beta$-cell function and glucose sensing rather than the autoimmune destruction seen in type 1 diabetes or the profound insulin resistance often seen in type 2 diabetes, patients can frequently be managed with oral agents or GLP-1 receptor agonists. By switching the patient from insulin to dulaglutide, the clinical team was able to achieve excellent glycaemic control while sparing the patient from unnecessary insulin therapy, thereby illustrating the practical benefits of precision medicine.

Precision medicine in the context of monogenic diabetes involves tailoring pharmacological interventions to the specific genetic driver of the disease. For *HNF1A*-MODY, this means selecting agents that support residual $\beta$-cell function or address the specific pathways of impaired insulin secretion. However, as this case demonstrates, the path to precision medicine is often obstructed by significant barriers to diagnosis. MODY remains frequently underdiagnosed because its clinical presentation—including symptoms like polyuria and weight loss—overlaps significantly with more common forms of diabetes. Furthermore, the utility of family history can be diminished by under-reporting or the high prevalence of type 2 diabetes in the general population. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for MODY in any young patient with early-onset diabetes who lacks clear evidence of insulin resistance or significant autoimmune markers, regardless of whether they fit a "classic" phenotype.

Looking forward, the integration of Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) into routine endocrinology represents a major shift in how early-onset diabetes will be managed. This case highlights the power of NGS to identify novel, previously unreported pathogenic variants, such as the *HNF1A* p.N270S mutation, which would be missed by standard diagnostic assays. As NGS technology becomes more cost-effective and accessible, it will transition from a "last-resort" genetic test to a frontline screening tool. This evolution will improve diagnostic rates, reduce the incidence of inappropriate insulin therapy, and allow for earlier, more targeted interventions. For the clinician, this underscores a shift toward a proactive diagnostic model where genetic screening is a standard component of the workup for young-onset diabetes, ultimately leading to better long-term outcomes and more personalized patient care.
