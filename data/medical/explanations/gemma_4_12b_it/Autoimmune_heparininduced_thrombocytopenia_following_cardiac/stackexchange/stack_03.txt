Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient who undergoes cardiac surgery and develops profound thrombocytopenia and an acute cerebral infarct, what are the primary differential diagnoses to exclude before confirming a diagnosis of HIT?

In a patient who undergoes cardiac surgery and presents with the triad of profound thrombocytopenia and acute thrombosis (in this case, a cerebral infarct), clinical suspicion for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is high. However, several critical differential diagnoses must be systematically excluded to confirm HIT, particularly given the potential for overlapping clinical features.

The primary differential diagnoses to exclude include:

*   **Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP):** This is a critical differential in any patient with concurrent thrombocytopenia and thrombosis. TTP is characterized by microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA). In this case, TTP was excluded by the absence of schistocytes on the initial peripheral blood film and a normal haptoglobin level (3.19 g/L), which argued against significant intravascular hemolysis.
*   **Drug-Induced Immune Thrombocytopenia (DITP):** Because many medications are administered perioperatively, DITP must be considered. In this patient, DITP was ruled out based on the timing of the platelet decline. The fall began too early to be attributed to postoperative medications (such as vancomycin, cefazolin, or metoprolol) and was not consistent with the typical temporal relationship between drug administration and immune-mediated destruction.
*   **Post-Transfusion Purpura:** This must be excluded by reviewing the patient's transfusion history. In this specific case, it was ruled out as no blood transfusions were administered during the perioperative period.
*   **Sepsis and Critical Illness:** Common causes of post-surgical thrombocytopenia, such as systemic infection or cardiogenic shock, must be evaluated. In this patient, these were considered unlikely due to stable hemodynamics, a normal white blood cell count with a normal differential, and negative blood and urine cultures.

By systematically ruling out these conditions—specifically by assessing for hemolysis, confirming the timing of drug exposure, and checking transfusion history—clinicians can more confidently attribute the presentation to HIT. In this case, the early onset (before postoperative day 5) was ultimately explained by preoperative heparin exposure during cardiac catheterization, leading to the diagnosis of autoimmune HIT (aHIT).
