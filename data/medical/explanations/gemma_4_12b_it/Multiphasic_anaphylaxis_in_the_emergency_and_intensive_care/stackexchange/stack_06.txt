Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Differential Diagnosis] How does a clinician differentiate between 'biphasic anaphylaxis' and 'sustained anaphylaxis' caused by ongoing allergen absorption (e.g., from a slow-release medication or residual food)?

To differentiate between biphasic anaphylaxis and sustained anaphylaxis caused by ongoing allergen absorption, the clinician must evaluate two primary factors: the presence of an asymptomatic interval and the status of the allergen source.

**Biphasic anaphylaxis** is defined by a period of "apparent symptom resolution"—a window where the patient is clinically stable and asymptomatic—followed by a recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms in the absence of further allergen exposure. Pathophysiologically, this is thought to represent a delayed release of inflammatory mediators or a secondary wave of mast cell and basophil degranulation. In the provided case, while the patient experienced a multiphasic course, the initial trigger was a single oral dose of clarithromycin, which complicates the distinction but highlights the challenge of managing recurrence when the primary exposure has already occurred.

In contrast, **sustained anaphylaxis** (or recurrent anaphylaxis due to ongoing absorption) occurs when symptoms persist or recur without a clear period of clinical resolution. This is typically driven by the continuous entry of the allergen into the systemic circulation. Examples include the ingestion of slow-release medications, residual food remaining in the gastrointestinal tract, or a continuous intravenous infusion of a triggering agent. 

**Clinical Reasoning for Differentiation:**
1.  **Clinical Course:** If the patient remains symptomatic or shows progressive worsening despite epinephrine, the clinician should first rule out and mitigate ongoing exposure (e.g., stopping a continuous infusion or ensuring no further ingestion). If the patient achieves a full clinical response, remains stable for several hours, and then suddenly relapses, biphasic anaphylaxis is the more likely diagnosis.
2.  **Source Identification:** The clinician must actively investigate the "source" of the allergen. In the case report, the patient took only one dose of clarithromycin, which points away from ongoing absorption of the primary drug and toward a multiphasic/biphasic mechanism. 
3.  **Monitoring Implications:** For sustained reactions, management focuses on the removal of the trigger. For biphasic reactions, because the trigger is no longer present, management focuses on prolonged observation (traditionally 4–6 hours, though this varies) and risk-stratification, as there are currently no reliable predictors to determine which patients will experience a relapse.
