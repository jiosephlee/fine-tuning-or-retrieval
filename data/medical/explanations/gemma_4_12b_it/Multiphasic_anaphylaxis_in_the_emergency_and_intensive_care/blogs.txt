Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Beyond the First Wave: Navigating Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

We’ve all seen the classic anaphylaxis script: a patient presents with urticaria, hypotension, and stridor; we give epinephrine, they stabilize, and we move toward discharge. But what happens when the "resolution" is just a pause?

Anaphylaxis isn't always a single event. For a subset of patients, the clinical course is biphasic (a recurrence of symptoms after apparent resolution) or even multiphasic—where the patient relapses into anaphylaxis multiple times despite adequate initial treatment. These cases are notoriously difficult to manage because they sit in a gray area of current guidelines: how long do we observe? What is the threshold for "refractory" vs. "biphasic"? And how do we manage a patient who keeps crashing after we’ve already "fixed" them?

## The Case: A Rebellious Reaction
Consider a woman in her 60s who presented to the ED in anaphylactic shock after a single dose of oral clarithromycin. She was hypotensive (74/50 mmHg), hypoxemic (88% on room air), and showed significant facial swelling and urticaria. This was her first true anaphylactic event. 

The initial management worked: she received an IM epinephrine auto-injector, fluids, and oxygen, and her blood pressure and symptoms stabilized. However, the clinical picture quickly turned complex. Over the next 12 hours, she experienced three additional episodes of anaphylactic shock. Each time, she required further doses of epinephrine to resolve the symptoms. By the final episode, the intensity had actually lessened—moving from deep hypoxemia and cyanosis to primarily dyspnea and rash—but the "waves" of reaction continued to disrupt her recovery.

## Decoding the "Waves"
One of the biggest challenges in these cases is distinguishing between true biphasic anaphylaxis and other clinical scenarios. Is the patient experiencing a delayed mast cell degranulation (biphasic)? Or are they experiencing *refractory* anaphylaxis because the initial dose of epinephrine was insufficient? Or, perhaps, is there a "slow-release" mechanism occurring, where the allergen is still being absorbed from the GI tract?

In this case, the distinction was blurred. Because the patient showed a "good" clinical response initially, it was framed as a multiphasic course. However, the report highlights a critical nuance in epinephrine dosing. The patient received 0.3 mg doses initially. For a patient weighing 104 kg, 0.3 mg is a significant underdose (the recommendation is 0.01 mg/kg, which would be closer to 1.0 mg for her weight, capped at 0.5 mg). Emerging data suggests that patients receiving the 0.3 mg auto-injector dose are significantly more likely to require escalation of care and additional injections compared to those receiving the 0.5 mg dose.

## The Search for an Underlying Driver
When we see "rebellious" anaphylaxis—where the reaction seems disproportionate to the trigger or persists in waves—we have to look for underlying predispositions. In this patient, further workup suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis. 

Systemic mastocytosis involves an abnormal increase in mast cells, which can predispose individuals to severe, prolonged, and complex anaphylactic reactions. While she didn't meet every formal diagnostic criterion (due to limited marrow biopsy material), the combination of multiphasic reactions, elevated tryptase levels, and hepatomegaly points toward a systemic mast cell disorder as a potential "engine" driving her repeated relapses.

## Clinical Management and the Steroid Myth
Management of multiphasic anaphylaxis remains largely expert-based due to a lack of robust evidence. There is a lingering habit of using corticosteroids to "prevent" biphasic reactions. However, current evidence is increasingly leaning away from this; several systematic reviews show no benefit of steroids in preventing biphasic events, and some even suggest they might increase the risk of hospital admission due to side effects.

In this case, the team debated between continuous IV epinephrine (pre-emptive treatment) and an expectant approach. Ultimately, as the intensity of the waves decreased, the patient stabilized without the need for continuous infusions. It serves as a reminder that while we must be aggressive in the first wave, the "waves" that follow may require a balance of vigilant observation and titrated intervention.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Dose Matters:** Be mindful of the 0.3 mg vs. 0.5 mg epinephrine dose. In patients over 50 kg, 0.3 mg may be insufficient, potentially leading to a "pseudo-refractory" presentation where the patient appears to improve but subsequently relapses.
*   **Define the Course:** Differentiate between biphasic (recurrence after resolution), refractory (failure to respond to initial epi), and ongoing absorption (persistent reaction due to remaining allergen).
*   **The Steroid Reality:** Don't rely on corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions; current evidence suggests they do not reduce the risk of recurrence.
*   **Look for the "Engine":** If a patient presents with atypical, multiphasic, or severe anaphylaxis, consider underlying mast cell disorders like systemic mastocytosis, especially if tryptase levels remain elevated or if there are extra-cutaneous symptoms.
*   **Observation is Key:** Because there are no reliable predictors for biphasic reactions, risk-stratified observation remains the standard. However, a multiphasic course necessitates much longer observation and a lower threshold for repeated epinephrine administration.



# Is 0.3mg Enough? Rethinking Epinephrine Dosing in the Obese Patient

We’ve all been there: a patient is crashing in anaphylaxis, the atmosphere is frantic, and the easiest, fastest route to treatment is grabbing the epinephrine auto-injector off the shelf. It’s a life-saving tool designed to bridge the gap between "recognition" and "intervention." But while the auto-injector is a triumph of public health engineering, it may be creating a clinical "underdosing trap" for our larger patients.

Consider the case of a woman in her 60s who presented to the emergency department with severe anaphylaxis following a single dose of clarithromycin. She was a large patient (104 kg) who presented with hypotension, hypoxemia, and widespread urticaria. Because the situation was time-sensitive and the clinical team was in the early stages of stabilization, they reached for the auto-injector. She received 0.3 mg of intramuscular (IM) epinephrine. 

While she initially stabilized, the "resolution" was deceptive. Over the next 12 hours, she suffered through a multiphasic course of anaphylaxis, requiring a total of four separate episodes of shock. Interestingly, the first two doses were the 0.3 mg auto-injectors, while the subsequent two doses were 0.5 mg doses prepared manually. Each time she relapsed, the team had to scramble to provide more intervention. 

This case highlights a growing concern in emergency medicine: the discrepancy between "available" dosing and "appropriate" dosing. For a patient weighing over 100 kg, a 0.3 mg dose—while better than nothing—falls significantly short of the recommended 0.01 mg/kg (up to a maximum of 0.5 mg). Recent data suggests that patients who start with 0.3 mg are significantly more likely to require escalated care and additional injections compared to those who receive the full 0.5 mg dose upfront. 

Why is this a problem? When we underdose the initial "hit," we aren't just slightly delaying the resolution; we may be failing to achieve the necessary physiological threshold to arrest the systemic mast cell degranulation. In this patient’s case, her history suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis—a condition where mast cells are primed for explosive mediator release. In such a "hyper-reactive" physiological state, starting with a sub-therapeutic dose can lead to the exhausting, multiphasic "yo-yo" effect we saw here, where the patient cycles between clinical improvement and profound shock.

Furthermore, this case reminds us to look beyond the immediate "fix." While the patient's symptoms lessened with each relapse, the recurring nature of her reaction underscores the need for prolonged observation. We often hear about "biphasic" reactions, but "multiphasic" reactions—multiple relapses despite adequate treatment—are a clinical minefield. Because there is currently no gold-standard for monitoring these complex courses, our best tool is a high index of suspicion and a commitment to robust, weight-appropriate dosing from the very first minute.

We also need to address the "steroid myth." In this case, the patient received several doses of prednisone and dexamethasone. While tradition dictates steroids prevent biphasic reactions, the evidence is increasingly thin, with some studies even suggesting they might increase hospital admission rates. Our priority remains the rapid administration of epinephrine—at the correct dose—followed by aggressive fluid resuscitation.

**Clinical Pearls:**

*   **Dose Matters:** For adults over 50 kg, 0.5 mg is the standard IM dose. If you are relying on an auto-injector, be aware that many only deliver 0.3 mg, which may be insufficient for larger patients or those with suspected mast cell disorders.
*   **The Underdosing Trap:** Starting with a 0.3 mg dose in an obese patient increases the likelihood of needing multiple "rescue" injections, which can lead to cumulative side effects and prolonged distress for the patient.
*   **Recognize Multiphasic Patterns:** If a patient shows a "good" response to epinephrine but remains in a high-risk environment or has underlying risk factors (like suspected mastocytosis), maintain a low threshold for continued monitoring and repeated dosing.
*   **Move Past Steroids:** Don't rely on corticosteroids to "prevent" a relapse. They do not work fast enough to stop an ongoing reaction and have shown little to no efficacy in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis.
*   **Think About Mast Cells:** If a patient presents with unusually severe, multiphasic, or refractory anaphylaxis, consider underlying mast cell disorders (like systemic mastocytosis) as a potential complicating factor.



# The Steroid Debate: Do Corticosteroids Actually Prevent Biphasic Reactions?

Anaphylaxis is already one of the most high-stakes scenarios in the emergency department. When a patient presents with hypotension, stridor, and generalized urticaria, your adrenaline (pun intended) is rightfully spiked. But what happens when the "all clear" feels premature? We’ve all seen it: the patient stabilizes, the blood pressure returns to baseline, the skin clears, and then—suddenly—they crash again. This is the biphasic (or multiphasic) reaction, and it’s the primary reason why many of us still reflexively reach for the steroid vial.

In a recent case report, a woman in her 60s presented with a classic, severe anaphylactic reaction to clarithromycin. She was initially stabilized with epinephrine and fluids, but the "resolution" was short-lived. She suffered four distinct episodes of anaphylaxis within 12 hours. In her case, the medical team followed the "traditional" playbook: because she relapsed, they administered both IV prednisone and dexamethasone. But as we look closer at the evolving evidence, we have to ask: were those steroids actually doing anything to prevent the next wave?

The historical rationale for using corticosteroids in anaphylaxis was to "dampen" the late-phase inflammatory response and prevent that dreaded biphasic recurrence. It was the "better safe than sorry" approach. However, the data is increasingly telling a different story. A 2017 systematic review looked at 22 studies and found that only one supported the use of corticosteroids in preventing biphasic reactions. Even more concerning, some data suggests that prehospital steroid use might actually increase the likelihood of hospital admission. 

So, if the evidence says they don't work for biphasic prevention, why are they still in almost every hospital's anaphylaxis protocol? It’s a classic example of clinical practice lagging behind the data. We are conditioned to fear the recurrence, and steroids are a low-risk, easy-to-administer "safety net." In the case of this patient, despite the administration of multiple steroids, she continued to experience relapses until the intensity of the reactions naturally diminished.

While we wait for better predictors of biphasic reactions—which currently remain elusive—we should be scrutinizing our "just in case" medications. The focus should remain squarely on the definitive treatment: epinephrine. Interestingly, this case also highlights a logistical hurdle in epinephrine delivery. The patient received multiple doses from 0.3 mg auto-injectors. While these are staples in the community, the literature suggests that for adults over 50 kg, a 0.3 mg dose may lead to a higher need for escalated care compared to the recommended 0.5 mg dose. 

When managing complex, multiphasic cases, our goal should be precision. We need to move away from "legacy" treatments that lack evidence and toward robust, dose-appropriate epinephrine management and vigilant observation.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Steroids are not "preventatives":** Current evidence (including a 2017 systematic review) suggests that corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. They should be reserved for symptom management rather than as a prophylaxis for recurrence.
*   **Epinephrine Dosing Matters:** For adults weighing over 50 kg, 0.5 mg IM is the recommended dose. Relying solely on 0.3 mg auto-injectors in a clinical setting may lead to frequent needs for additional doses and escalated care.
*   **The "Biphasic" Trap:** Differentiating a true biphasic reaction from "sustained anaphylaxis" (where the allergen is still being absorbed or the initial dose of epinephrine was insufficient) is difficult. If the patient is still symptomatic, give more epinephrine, don't just start a steroid drip.
*   **Identify the "High Risk" Patients:** While no perfect predictor exists, history of severe initial presentation, delayed epinephrine administration, and multiple required doses of epinephrine are all red flags for potential biphasic recurrence.
*   **Look for Underlying Drivers:** In cases of unusually severe or multiphasic reactions, consider underlying conditions like systemic mastocytosis, which can predispose patients to exaggerated inflammatory responses.



# The Hidden Driver: Systemic Mastocytosis and Anaphylaxis Risk

We’ve all seen the "classic" anaphylaxis case: a patient reacts to an allergen, you give them epinephrine, they stabilize, and they go home after a few hours of observation. It’s a high-stakes scenario, but the playbook is well-established. 

However, what do you do when the playbook fails? What happens when you successfully treat the initial shock, only to watch the patient "crash" again and again? This is the world of multiphasic anaphylaxis—a clinical curveball where symptoms resolve, only to return in waves without any new exposure to the trigger.

## The Case of the Recurring Waves
Consider a woman in her 60s who presented to the ED with a severe reaction after taking a single dose of clarithromycin. She was hypotensive (74/50 mmHg), hypoxemic (88% on room air), and showing generalized urticaria and facial swelling. 

The initial management was textbook: epinephrine, oxygen, and fluids. She responded well and was moved to an internal ward. But then, the nightmare began. Within 12 hours, she started relapsing into anaphylactic shock. This wasn't a one-time event; she suffered a total of four distinct anaphylactic episodes. Each time, she required more epinephrine to stabilize. 

What makes this case fascinating—and frankly, a bit unsettling—is the "multiphasic" nature of the reaction. It wasn't just one delayed response (biphasic); it was a series of relapses. While the intensity of the symptoms actually decreased with each wave, the sheer frequency of the relapses posed a massive challenge for the ICU team. How do you manage a patient who keeps "re-anaphylaxising" despite appropriate treatment?

## Looking Beneath the Surface: Systemic Mastocytosis
When a patient presents with this level of complexity, we have to ask: Is there an underlying pathology making their mast cells "hyper-reactive"? 

In this case, the clinical picture pointed toward systemic mastocytosis—a condition characterized by an abnormal increase in mast cells. While this patient didn't meet every single formal diagnostic criterion, the suspicion was high. Patients with mast cell disorders are predisposed to severe, systemic, and sometimes refractory reactions because their bodies are essentially primed to release a flood of inflammatory mediators at the slightest provocation.

When you see "weird" anaphylaxis—multiphasic courses, refractory symptoms, or extreme severity—start looking for systemic clues. In this patient, these included hepatomegaly on sonography and neuropsychiatric symptoms like depression and memory issues. These aren't "standard" anaphylaxis symptoms, but they are key pieces of the puzzle in identifying a systemic mast cell disorder.

## Clinical Pearls for the Front Line
This case highlights several critical nuances in managing complex allergic reactions that often get lost in the heat of the moment.

**1. The Epinephrine Dose Gap**
One of the most striking points in the report is the reliance on 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injectors. While these are fantastic for the preclinical setting and for quick stabilization, they are often a significant underdose for an adult. This patient weighed 104 kg. Studies show that patients receiving 0.3 mg initially require care escalation much more frequently than those receiving the recommended 0.5 mg. In the hospital setting, we should strive for weight-based dosing whenever possible to avoid the "rebound" effect of under-treatment.

**2. The Steroid Debate**
There is still a lot of "legacy" thinking around using corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions. However, current evidence is moving away from this. Multiple studies show no significant benefit in preventing the recurrence of symptoms, and some even suggest they might increase the risk of hospital admission due to side effects. If the goal is to prevent a second wave, epinephrine is your primary tool; steroids are not a magic shield.

**3. The Tryptase Timeline**
If you suspect a systemic mast cell issue, timing is everything. To accurately assess tryptase levels, you need a baseline and a peak. The goal is to get a sample as soon as possible, a second sample 1–2 hours after symptom onset (when levels typically peak), and a third sample at least 24 hours after resolution. In this case, the timing was suboptimal, but it underscores the need for a proactive lab order at the first sign of a severe reaction.

## Take-Home Messages
*   **Recognize Multiphasic Patterns:** If a patient "re-crashes" after an initial successful response to epinephrine, consider multiphasic anaphylaxis rather than just "sustained" anaphylaxis.
*   **Audit Your Epinephrine:** Be aware that 0.3 mg auto-injectors may be insufficient for larger patients. Aim for 0.5 mg (or 0.01 mg/kg) in the clinical setting to reduce the need for repeat dosing.
*   **Think Beyond the Trigger:** For unusually complex or refractory reactions, screen for systemic mast cell disorders. Look for clues like hepatomegaly and neuropsychiatric symptoms.
*   **Tryptase Timing Matters:** If you are ordering tryptase to investigate a mast cell disorder, ensure you have a plan to draw blood at the 1–2 hour peak, not just at the end of the shift.



# Proactive vs. Reactive: Management Tensions in Recurrent Anaphylaxis

We are all comfortable with the "standard" anaphylaxis algorithm: recognize the symptoms, give epinephrine, and monitor for a potential biphasic reaction. But what happens when the "biphasic" reaction isn't a one-time sequel? What happens when a patient enters a multiphasic loop—repeatedly crashing into anaphylactic shock despite repeated doses of epinephrine and "adequate" initial management?

This is where the clinical guidelines start to feel a bit thin. We move from a well-defined emergency into a clinical "grey zone" where the decision-making shifts from following a flowchart to balancing high-stakes risks.

## The Case: A Recurring Cycle
Consider a 60-year-old woman weighing 104 kg who presented to the ED with anaphylaxis after a single dose of clarithromycin. She was hypotensive, hypoxemic, and showing classic signs of systemic involvement. She received 0.3 mg of IM epinephrine (via auto-injector) and responded well initially.

However, the "resolution" was short-lived. Within 12 hours, she experienced three more distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock. Each time, she required more epinephrine. Interestingly, while the severity of the symptoms decreased with each recurrence, the clinical picture was one of persistent, recurring instability.

This patient eventually moved to the ICU, where the team faced a difficult management fork in the road: Do we stay reactive, or do we go proactive?

## The Management Dilemma: Proactive vs. Reactive
In this case, the clinical team weighed two primary strategies for managing multiphasic anaphylaxis, both of which carry significant risks due to a lack of clear evidence-based guidance.

**The Proactive Approach: Continuous IV Epinephrine**
The logic here is preemptive. If the patient is cycling through anaphylactic episodes, why wait for the next crash? Continuous infusion aims to maintain a steady state of vasoconstriction and bronchodilation, essentially "blunting" the peaks of the mast cell degranulation. 
*   *The Risk:* Continuous pressors carry inherent risks of tachycardia, arrhythmias, and potential tissue ischemia. Is it necessary if the episodes are becoming milder?

**The Expectant Approach: Reactive Management**
This is the "wait and see" method—treating each episode as it arises with IM epinephrine and supportive care. In this case, the team watched as the symptoms gradually tapered off over several hours.
*   *The Risk:* This approach relies on the team’s ability to recognize and treat the next "crash" instantly. If the next episode is severe enough to cause profound hypotension or airway compromise before the next dose can be administered, the outcome could be catastrophic.

Ultimately, the patient’s symptoms stabilized without either extreme measure, but the case highlights a glaring gap in our protocols: we lack a consensus on how to transition from "episodic" to "continuous" management in multiphasic cases.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **Watch the Weight:** This patient weighed 104 kg. Many standard auto-injectors provide only 0.3 mg of epinephrine. In a patient of this size, 0.3 mg may be a significant underdose. While auto-injectors are excellent for speed, we must be mindful of weight-based requirements (0.01 mg/kg, up to 0.5 mg) to avoid the need for frequent dose escalations.
*   **The Steroid Myth:** While this patient received corticosteroids (prednisone and dexamethasone) based on traditional protocols, modern evidence is increasingly shifting away from their use in preventing biphasic reactions. Recent reviews suggest they may provide no benefit and could even increase the risk of hospital admission.
*   **Identify the "Why":** Multiphasic reactions should trigger a search for underlying pathology. In this case, the patient’s high tryptase levels and clinical history suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis. Patients with mast cell disorders are predisposed to severe, complex anaphylactic courses.
*   **Tryptase Timing:** If you suspect a mast cell disorder, get those tryptase levels. Ideally, you need three samples: one as soon as possible (without delaying treatment), one at the peak (1–4 hours after onset), and one at least 24 hours after resolution to establish a baseline.

**Key Takeaways:**
*   **Multiphasic anaphylaxis** is distinct from biphasic reactions and requires a higher level of suspicion and prolonged monitoring.
*   **Dosing matters:** Be wary of 0.3 mg auto-injectors in large adults; ensure you have a plan for 0.5 mg doses or escalating care.
*   **Corticosteroids** are not a "magic bullet" for preventing biphasic relapses; focus on prompt epinephrine and hemodynamic support.
*   **Consider the underlying pathology:** Recurrent, severe reactions may point toward systemic mastocytosis, requiring specialized long-term management.
