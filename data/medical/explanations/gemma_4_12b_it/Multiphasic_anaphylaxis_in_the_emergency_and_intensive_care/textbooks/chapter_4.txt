Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 4: ## Pharmacological Management and Evidence-Based Protocols

The management of multiphasic anaphylaxis presents a significant clinical challenge because the patient’s condition oscillates between periods of apparent stability and life-threatening relapse. For the clinician, this requires a nuanced understanding of epinephrine dosing, the limitations of current adjunctive therapies, and the high-stakes decision-making involved when standard protocols fail to prevent recurrence.

The cornerstone of anaphylaxis treatment remains intramuscular (IM) epinephrine. Standard guidelines recommend a weight-based dose of 0.01 mg/kg, with a maximum dose of 0.5 mg for adults. In this case, the patient’s weight of 104 kg highlights a critical pharmacological consideration: while the weight-based calculation would technically suggest a higher dose, the 0.5 mg cap is the standard clinical ceiling. However, the use of 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injectors—the first two doses administered to this patient—represents a potential underdose for an adult of her size. Clinical evidence suggests that patients receiving the 0.3 mg dose may require more frequent escalation of care and additional injections compared to those receiving the 0.5 mg dose. In a multiphasic setting, where the goal is to aggressively stabilize the patient to prevent progression, selecting the higher 0.5 mg dose (as was done in the patient’s final two episodes) is a vital step in ensuring adequate physiological response.

The role of corticosteroids in anaphylaxis is currently a subject of significant clinical debate. Historically, corticosteroids were administered with the intent of preventing biphasic reactions—the recurrence of symptoms after the initial phase has resolved. In this case, the patient received both prednisone and dexamethasone following her first relapse, adhering to traditional practices. However, contemporary evidence, including systematic reviews, has increasingly demonstrated that corticosteroids do not reduce the risk of biphasic reactions. Furthermore, some studies suggest they may increase the risk of hospital admission due to side effects. While they remain a common practice in many hospital protocols, the clinical focus should remain on the prompt administration of epinephrine, as steroids do not provide acute reversal of anaphylactic symptoms.

Adjunctive therapies, such as antihistamines, serve a supportive rather than a primary role in anaphylaxis management. In this report, clemastine was utilized to address the patient's cutaneous symptoms, such as pruritus and urticaria. It is essential for the clinician to remember that while antihistamines are effective for managing the "skin" manifestations of an allergic reaction, they have no role in treating airway obstruction, hypotension, or other life-threatening systemic components of anaphylaxis. They are purely symptomatic and should never delay the administration of epinephrine.

Perhaps the most complex aspect of this case is the management of the multiphasic relapses, where the clinician must choose between aggressive preemptive treatment and expectant management. Because there is no established consensus for multiphasic anaphylaxis, the team considered two distinct strategies: continuous intravenous epinephrine to prevent the next predicted episode, or an expectant approach of monitoring the patient as symptoms slowly decreased in intensity. Continuous epinephrine carries risks of cardiovascular complications, while the expectant approach risks a sudden, fatal relapse. This patient’s course illustrates the difficulty of these decisions; fortunately, her symptoms diminished with each episode, eventually allowing for a transition to standard observation. This case underscores the need for expert-based consensus and perhaps a registry of multiphasic cases to develop more reliable, evidence-based management protocols for these rare but severe presentations.
