Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Reasoning in Multiphasic and Refractory Courses

When a patient presents with anaphylaxis that appears to resolve only to recur, the clinician must distinguish between different types of recurrent reactions to determine the appropriate level of monitoring and intervention. The first diagnostic challenge is differentiating **biphasic anaphylaxis** from **refractory anaphylaxis**. Biphasic anaphylaxis is characterized by a period of clinical improvement—where symptoms subside significantly or resolve entirely—followed by a recurrence of symptoms in the absence of further allergen exposure. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis refers to a persistent, severe reaction that fails to respond adequately to standard doses of epinephrine. In the presented case, the patient exhibited a multiphasic course, which is a more complex subset of biphasic reactions involving multiple relapses of anaphylactic shock despite repeated successful administrations of epinephrine.

A critical step in this diagnostic reasoning is determining whether the recurrence is a true biphasic reaction or merely a **prolonged reaction due to ongoing allergen absorption**. For example, if a patient has ingested a large volume of a slow-release medication or a large meal containing a common allergen, the allergen may continue to enter the systemic circulation for several hours. This can cause symptoms to fluctuate or persist, mimicking a biphasic course. Clinicians must take a detailed history of the timing of allergen ingestion and the nature of the substance to rule out continued absorption before labeling a recurrence as biphasic. In this case, the patient had taken a single 500 mg dose of clarithromycin, and the recurrence occurred several hours after the initial presentation, making ongoing absorption less likely than a true biphasic/multiphasic process.

Because there are currently no definitive biomarkers or reliable predictors to identify which patients will experience a biphasic reaction, clinicians must rely on **risk stratification** based on the initial presentation. While the incidence of biphasic reactions is relatively low (approximately 4–6%), the risk increases with the severity of the initial event. Factors that suggest a need for prolonged observation include a severe initial presentation (such as the profound hypotension and hypoxemia seen in this patient), a delay in the administration of epinephrine, and the requirement for multiple doses of epinephrine to achieve initial stabilization. In this case, the patient’s requirement for four separate doses of epinephrine and her initial state of shock necessitated her admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) for continuous monitoring.

To confirm that a systemic anaphylactic reaction has occurred—rather than a localized allergic reaction—**serum tryptase** is the gold standard laboratory marker. Tryptase is a protease released primarily by mast cells during degranulation. However, the utility of tryptase depends entirely on the **kinetics of the sampling**. To accurately quantify the "tryptase rise" that confirms anaphylaxis, guidelines recommend a three-sample protocol:
1.  **Baseline/Resolution:** A sample taken at least 24 hours after the resolution of symptoms to establish the patient's normal level.
2.  **Peak:** A sample taken 1 to 4 hours after the onset of symptoms, when tryptase levels typically reach their zenith.
3.  **Immediate:** A sample taken as soon as possible without delaying life-saving treatment.

In the presented case, the diagnostic workup was complicated by suboptimal timing. The first tryptase sample was taken only one hour after the *third* episode of anaphylaxis, and the second was taken 27 hours after the final recurrence. Because the peak of the first and second episodes was missed, the clinicians could not perfectly calculate the tryptase delta. However, the fact that both the peak-related and baseline levels remained elevated provided significant clues toward an underlying pathology. Specifically, a persistently high baseline tryptase level, combined with the patient's clinical history, pointed toward a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, a condition that predisposes individuals to severe, multiphasic anaphylactic reactions.
