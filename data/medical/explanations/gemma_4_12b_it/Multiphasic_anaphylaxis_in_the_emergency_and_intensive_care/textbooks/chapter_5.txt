Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 5: ## Underlying Predispositions and Long-term Clinical Management

While the immediate priority in anaphylaxis is the stabilization of the airway, breathing, and circulation, the atypical multiphasic course of this patient necessitated a deeper investigation into underlying predispositions. In cases of severe, recurrent, or refractory anaphylaxis, clinicians should consider mast cell disorders, specifically systemic mastocytosis (SM). SM is characterized by an abnormal increase in mast cells and the excessive release of inflammatory mediators. In this case, several clinical indicators pointed toward SM, including the patient's history of neuropsychiatric symptoms—such as depression and memory problems—and the presence of hepatomegaly on abdominal sonography. These findings are recognized clinical features of systemic mastocytosis, where mast cell infiltration and mediator release can affect various organ systems beyond the skin and respiratory tract.

The definitive diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis typically relies on specific WHO criteria, which include bone marrow biopsy findings, serum tryptase levels, and the presence of specific mutations. However, as seen in this case, clinical practice often faces limitations. Although the patient showed a high probability of SM based on her clinical presentation and elevated tryptase levels, she did not meet the formal diagnostic criteria because the bone marrow biopsy provided insufficient material for a complete assessment. For the clinician, this highlights an important diagnostic principle: a "high clinical suspicion" of an underlying mast cell disorder is often sufficient to guide management and risk stratification, even when a definitive formal diagnosis remains elusive due to technical or logistical constraints.

Once the acute phase of multiphasic anaphylaxis has resolved, the focus shifts to long-term patient safety and education. A cornerstone of this management is ensuring the patient is equipped to recognize early warning signs and can independently administer life-saving medication. In this case, the patient was taught the proper use of an epinephrine auto-injector. It is crucial to emphasize that while 0.3 mg auto-injectors are common in emergency departments, clinicians should educate patients on the importance of rapid administration, as even a slightly lower dose can be life-saving in a pre-hospital setting. Furthermore, providing a clear emergency action plan helps the patient and their support network navigate the uncertainty of potential recurrence.

Long-term pharmacotherapy for patients with a history of severe anaphylaxis often involves prophylactic medications to manage cutaneous symptoms and reduce the burden of daily allergic triggers. This patient was started on levocetirizine, a second-generation antihistamine, to manage chronic pruritus and urticaria. It is important to teach patients that while antihistamines are effective for mild symptoms, they are not a substitute for epinephrine in the event of a systemic reaction. Finally, because multiphasic anaphylaxis remains a rare and complex clinical entity, the authors suggest that the development of registries is essential. By systematically collecting data on patient profiles, triggers, and management outcomes in these rare cases, the medical community can move toward evidence-based, standardized protocols for complex anaphylactic courses.
