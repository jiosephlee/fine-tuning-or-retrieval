Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 1: ## Pathophysiology of Anaphylaxis and Biphasic Phenomena

Anaphylaxis is a severe, systemic type I hypersensitivity reaction characterized by the rapid release of inflammatory mediators from mast cells and basophils. The primary mechanism involves IgE-mediated degranulation, where allergen-specific IgE antibodies, previously bound to high-affinity receptors (FcεRI) on these cells, are cross-linked by a new exposure to the allergen. This cross-linking triggers an immediate influx of calcium, leading to the release of preformed and newly synthesized mediators. While most classic cases are IgE-mediated, non-IgE-mediated reactions can also occur through direct mast cell activation or complement-mediated pathways. In the case presented, the patient’s reaction to clarithromycin—a common antibiotic trigger—illustrates this rapid systemic activation.

The clinical manifestations of anaphylaxis are driven by a specific cocktail of chemical mediators. Histamine is the most immediate mediator, causing rapid vasodilation, increased capillary permeability (leading to edema and hypotension), and smooth muscle contraction in the airways. Simultaneously, the synthesis of leukotrienes and prostaglandins contributes to sustained bronchoconstriction and mucus production. Later, the release of cytokines and chemokines recruits additional inflammatory cells, potentially perpetuating the reaction. These physiological changes manifest as the classic clinical hallmarks of anaphylaxis: airway compromise (stridor, laryngeal edema), breathing difficulties (wheezing, dyspnea), circulatory collapse (hypotension, tachycardia), and cutaneous signs such as urticaria, flushing, or angioedema.

A critical distinction in the management of anaphylaxis is the difference between refractory, biphasic, and multiphasic reactions. Refractory anaphylaxis is defined as a persistent reaction that does not respond adequately to standard doses of epinephrine. In contrast, biphasic anaphylaxis occurs when symptoms resolve following initial treatment, only to recur later in the absence of further allergen exposure. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, as seen in this patient, involves several such relapses. Biphasic reactions typically occur within 1 to 12 hours of the initial event, though they can occur up to 72 hours later. Understanding these definitions is vital because it differentiates a failed initial treatment (refractory) from a delayed physiological recurrence (biphasic/multiphasic), necessitating prolonged observation even after the patient appears clinically stable.

The severity and recurrence of these reactions can be influenced by underlying systemic conditions. Systemic mastocytosis is a rare disorder characterized by an abnormal proliferation of mast cells in various tissues. Patients with mastocytosis have a significantly higher risk of severe, recurrent, and complex anaphylactic reactions because their baseline "load" of mast cells is higher, and these cells may be hyper-responsive to even minor stimuli. In this case, the patient’s multiphasic course and elevated tryptase levels suggested a predisposition toward such severe mast cell activation, providing a potential underlying reason for the repeated relapses despite initial successful treatment.
