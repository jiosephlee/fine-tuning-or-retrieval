## Pathophysiology of Anaphylaxis and Biphasic Phenomena

Anaphylaxis is a severe, systemic type I hypersensitivity reaction characterized by the rapid release of inflammatory mediators from mast cells and basophils. The primary mechanism involves IgE-mediated degranulation, where allergen-specific IgE antibodies, previously bound to high-affinity receptors (FcεRI) on these cells, are cross-linked by a new exposure to the allergen. This cross-linking triggers an immediate influx of calcium, leading to the release of preformed and newly synthesized mediators. While most classic cases are IgE-mediated, non-IgE-mediated reactions can also occur through direct mast cell activation or complement-mediated pathways. In the case presented, the patient’s reaction to clarithromycin—a common antibiotic trigger—illustrates this rapid systemic activation.

The clinical manifestations of anaphylaxis are driven by a specific cocktail of chemical mediators. Histamine is the most immediate mediator, causing rapid vasodilation, increased capillary permeability (leading to edema and hypotension), and smooth muscle contraction in the airways. Simultaneously, the synthesis of leukotrienes and prostaglandins contributes to sustained bronchoconstriction and mucus production. Later, the release of cytokines and chemokines recruits additional inflammatory cells, potentially perpetuating the reaction. These physiological changes manifest as the classic clinical hallmarks of anaphylaxis: airway compromise (stridor, laryngeal edema), breathing difficulties (wheezing, dyspnea), circulatory collapse (hypotension, tachycardia), and cutaneous signs such as urticaria, flushing, or angioedema.

A critical distinction in the management of anaphylaxis is the difference between refractory, biphasic, and multiphasic reactions. Refractory anaphylaxis is defined as a persistent reaction that does not respond adequately to standard doses of epinephrine. In contrast, biphasic anaphylaxis occurs when symptoms resolve following initial treatment, only to recur later in the absence of further allergen exposure. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, as seen in this patient, involves several such relapses. Biphasic reactions typically occur within 1 to 12 hours of the initial event, though they can occur up to 72 hours later. Understanding these definitions is vital because it differentiates a failed initial treatment (refractory) from a delayed physiological recurrence (biphasic/multiphasic), necessitating prolonged observation even after the patient appears clinically stable.

The severity and recurrence of these reactions can be influenced by underlying systemic conditions. Systemic mastocytosis is a rare disorder characterized by an abnormal proliferation of mast cells in various tissues. Patients with mastocytosis have a significantly higher risk of severe, recurrent, and complex anaphylactic reactions because their baseline "load" of mast cells is higher, and these cells may be hyper-responsive to even minor stimuli. In this case, the patient’s multiphasic course and elevated tryptase levels suggested a predisposition toward such severe mast cell activation, providing a potential underlying reason for the repeated relapses despite initial successful treatment.

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Emergency Stabilization

The patient, a woman in her 60s with a history of mild allergic reactions, presented to the emergency department (ED) with acute symptoms following the ingestion of her first dose of oral clarithromycin. Her presentation highlights a critical clinical challenge: the transition from a known history of minor allergies—such as facial swelling from amoxicillin or local reactions to wasp stings—to a life-threatening systemic reaction. The sudden onset of pruritus, flushing, increased transpiration, and a sensation of throat swelling within one hour of drug ingestion is highly suggestive of an IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reaction. In such cases, the immediate priority is to recognize the rapid progression of symptoms and initiate life-saving interventions before the patient's condition deteriorates further.

Upon arrival at the ED, the patient exhibited the classic hallmarks of anaphylactic shock, a severe form of anaphylaxis characterized by systemic circulatory collapse. Her vital signs revealed significant hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxaemia (88% on room air), indicating that the systemic release of mediators—such as histamine, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins—had caused profound vasodilation, increased capillary permeability, and potential airway compromise. The clinical examination further corroborated these findings with generalized erythema, urticaria, and facial swelling. Recognizing these signs as a medical emergency is paramount; the objective in the initial phase is not to determine the specific allergen but to stabilize the patient's airway, breathing, and circulation (the ABCs).

The immediate management of anaphylaxis centers on the rapid administration of intramuscular (IM) epinephrine. In this case, the patient received 0.3 mg of epinephrine via an auto-injector. Epinephrine is the first-line treatment because it acts as an alpha-1 agonist (causing vasoconstriction to increase blood pressure), a beta-1 agonist (increasing heart rate and contractility), and a beta-2 agonist (promoting bronchodilation and stabilizing mast cells). Supporting these measures, the patient was provided with high-flow supplemental oxygen (15 L via non-rebreather mask) and an initial bolus of 500 mL of normal saline to address hypoxaemia and volume depletion caused by capillary leak. While antihistamines like clemastine were also administered to manage cutaneous symptoms, they are secondary to epinephrine and do not treat the life-threatening components of anaphylactic shock.

A rapid assessment of the clinical response to these interventions is necessary to determine the need for escalating care. In this report, the patient showed a "good response," evidenced by the normalization of her blood pressure and the visible reduction of facial swelling and erythema. This stabilization confirms the effectiveness of the initial epinephrine dose and allows for a transition to a period of observation. Crucially, the patient’s clarithromycin was discontinued immediately. Removing the offending allergen is a fundamental principle of management; while the acute stabilization addresses the current physiological crisis, preventing further exposure is essential to stop the ongoing inflammatory cascade and prevent further episodes of anaphylaxis.

## Diagnostic Reasoning in Multiphasic and Refractory Courses

When a patient presents with anaphylaxis that appears to resolve only to recur, the clinician must distinguish between different types of recurrent reactions to determine the appropriate level of monitoring and intervention. The first diagnostic challenge is differentiating **biphasic anaphylaxis** from **refractory anaphylaxis**. Biphasic anaphylaxis is characterized by a period of clinical improvement—where symptoms subside significantly or resolve entirely—followed by a recurrence of symptoms in the absence of further allergen exposure. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis refers to a persistent, severe reaction that fails to respond adequately to standard doses of epinephrine. In the presented case, the patient exhibited a multiphasic course, which is a more complex subset of biphasic reactions involving multiple relapses of anaphylactic shock despite repeated successful administrations of epinephrine.

A critical step in this diagnostic reasoning is determining whether the recurrence is a true biphasic reaction or merely a **prolonged reaction due to ongoing allergen absorption**. For example, if a patient has ingested a large volume of a slow-release medication or a large meal containing a common allergen, the allergen may continue to enter the systemic circulation for several hours. This can cause symptoms to fluctuate or persist, mimicking a biphasic course. Clinicians must take a detailed history of the timing of allergen ingestion and the nature of the substance to rule out continued absorption before labeling a recurrence as biphasic. In this case, the patient had taken a single 500 mg dose of clarithromycin, and the recurrence occurred several hours after the initial presentation, making ongoing absorption less likely than a true biphasic/multiphasic process.

Because there are currently no definitive biomarkers or reliable predictors to identify which patients will experience a biphasic reaction, clinicians must rely on **risk stratification** based on the initial presentation. While the incidence of biphasic reactions is relatively low (approximately 4–6%), the risk increases with the severity of the initial event. Factors that suggest a need for prolonged observation include a severe initial presentation (such as the profound hypotension and hypoxemia seen in this patient), a delay in the administration of epinephrine, and the requirement for multiple doses of epinephrine to achieve initial stabilization. In this case, the patient’s requirement for four separate doses of epinephrine and her initial state of shock necessitated her admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) for continuous monitoring.

To confirm that a systemic anaphylactic reaction has occurred—rather than a localized allergic reaction—**serum tryptase** is the gold standard laboratory marker. Tryptase is a protease released primarily by mast cells during degranulation. However, the utility of tryptase depends entirely on the **kinetics of the sampling**. To accurately quantify the "tryptase rise" that confirms anaphylaxis, guidelines recommend a three-sample protocol:
1.  **Baseline/Resolution:** A sample taken at least 24 hours after the resolution of symptoms to establish the patient's normal level.
2.  **Peak:** A sample taken 1 to 4 hours after the onset of symptoms, when tryptase levels typically reach their zenith.
3.  **Immediate:** A sample taken as soon as possible without delaying life-saving treatment.

In the presented case, the diagnostic workup was complicated by suboptimal timing. The first tryptase sample was taken only one hour after the *third* episode of anaphylaxis, and the second was taken 27 hours after the final recurrence. Because the peak of the first and second episodes was missed, the clinicians could not perfectly calculate the tryptase delta. However, the fact that both the peak-related and baseline levels remained elevated provided significant clues toward an underlying pathology. Specifically, a persistently high baseline tryptase level, combined with the patient's clinical history, pointed toward a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis, a condition that predisposes individuals to severe, multiphasic anaphylactic reactions.

## Pharmacological Management and Evidence-Based Protocols

The management of multiphasic anaphylaxis presents a significant clinical challenge because the patient’s condition oscillates between periods of apparent stability and life-threatening relapse. For the clinician, this requires a nuanced understanding of epinephrine dosing, the limitations of current adjunctive therapies, and the high-stakes decision-making involved when standard protocols fail to prevent recurrence.

The cornerstone of anaphylaxis treatment remains intramuscular (IM) epinephrine. Standard guidelines recommend a weight-based dose of 0.01 mg/kg, with a maximum dose of 0.5 mg for adults. In this case, the patient’s weight of 104 kg highlights a critical pharmacological consideration: while the weight-based calculation would technically suggest a higher dose, the 0.5 mg cap is the standard clinical ceiling. However, the use of 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injectors—the first two doses administered to this patient—represents a potential underdose for an adult of her size. Clinical evidence suggests that patients receiving the 0.3 mg dose may require more frequent escalation of care and additional injections compared to those receiving the 0.5 mg dose. In a multiphasic setting, where the goal is to aggressively stabilize the patient to prevent progression, selecting the higher 0.5 mg dose (as was done in the patient’s final two episodes) is a vital step in ensuring adequate physiological response.

The role of corticosteroids in anaphylaxis is currently a subject of significant clinical debate. Historically, corticosteroids were administered with the intent of preventing biphasic reactions—the recurrence of symptoms after the initial phase has resolved. In this case, the patient received both prednisone and dexamethasone following her first relapse, adhering to traditional practices. However, contemporary evidence, including systematic reviews, has increasingly demonstrated that corticosteroids do not reduce the risk of biphasic reactions. Furthermore, some studies suggest they may increase the risk of hospital admission due to side effects. While they remain a common practice in many hospital protocols, the clinical focus should remain on the prompt administration of epinephrine, as steroids do not provide acute reversal of anaphylactic symptoms.

Adjunctive therapies, such as antihistamines, serve a supportive rather than a primary role in anaphylaxis management. In this report, clemastine was utilized to address the patient's cutaneous symptoms, such as pruritus and urticaria. It is essential for the clinician to remember that while antihistamines are effective for managing the "skin" manifestations of an allergic reaction, they have no role in treating airway obstruction, hypotension, or other life-threatening systemic components of anaphylaxis. They are purely symptomatic and should never delay the administration of epinephrine.

Perhaps the most complex aspect of this case is the management of the multiphasic relapses, where the clinician must choose between aggressive preemptive treatment and expectant management. Because there is no established consensus for multiphasic anaphylaxis, the team considered two distinct strategies: continuous intravenous epinephrine to prevent the next predicted episode, or an expectant approach of monitoring the patient as symptoms slowly decreased in intensity. Continuous epinephrine carries risks of cardiovascular complications, while the expectant approach risks a sudden, fatal relapse. This patient’s course illustrates the difficulty of these decisions; fortunately, her symptoms diminished with each episode, eventually allowing for a transition to standard observation. This case underscores the need for expert-based consensus and perhaps a registry of multiphasic cases to develop more reliable, evidence-based management protocols for these rare but severe presentations.

## Underlying Predispositions and Long-term Clinical Management

While the immediate priority in anaphylaxis is the stabilization of the airway, breathing, and circulation, the atypical multiphasic course of this patient necessitated a deeper investigation into underlying predispositions. In cases of severe, recurrent, or refractory anaphylaxis, clinicians should consider mast cell disorders, specifically systemic mastocytosis (SM). SM is characterized by an abnormal increase in mast cells and the excessive release of inflammatory mediators. In this case, several clinical indicators pointed toward SM, including the patient's history of neuropsychiatric symptoms—such as depression and memory problems—and the presence of hepatomegaly on abdominal sonography. These findings are recognized clinical features of systemic mastocytosis, where mast cell infiltration and mediator release can affect various organ systems beyond the skin and respiratory tract.

The definitive diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis typically relies on specific WHO criteria, which include bone marrow biopsy findings, serum tryptase levels, and the presence of specific mutations. However, as seen in this case, clinical practice often faces limitations. Although the patient showed a high probability of SM based on her clinical presentation and elevated tryptase levels, she did not meet the formal diagnostic criteria because the bone marrow biopsy provided insufficient material for a complete assessment. For the clinician, this highlights an important diagnostic principle: a "high clinical suspicion" of an underlying mast cell disorder is often sufficient to guide management and risk stratification, even when a definitive formal diagnosis remains elusive due to technical or logistical constraints.

Once the acute phase of multiphasic anaphylaxis has resolved, the focus shifts to long-term patient safety and education. A cornerstone of this management is ensuring the patient is equipped to recognize early warning signs and can independently administer life-saving medication. In this case, the patient was taught the proper use of an epinephrine auto-injector. It is crucial to emphasize that while 0.3 mg auto-injectors are common in emergency departments, clinicians should educate patients on the importance of rapid administration, as even a slightly lower dose can be life-saving in a pre-hospital setting. Furthermore, providing a clear emergency action plan helps the patient and their support network navigate the uncertainty of potential recurrence.

Long-term pharmacotherapy for patients with a history of severe anaphylaxis often involves prophylactic medications to manage cutaneous symptoms and reduce the burden of daily allergic triggers. This patient was started on levocetirizine, a second-generation antihistamine, to manage chronic pruritus and urticaria. It is important to teach patients that while antihistamines are effective for mild symptoms, they are not a substitute for epinephrine in the event of a systemic reaction. Finally, because multiphasic anaphylaxis remains a rare and complex clinical entity, the authors suggest that the development of registries is essential. By systematically collecting data on patient profiles, triggers, and management outcomes in these rare cases, the medical community can move toward evidence-based, standardized protocols for complex anaphylactic courses.
