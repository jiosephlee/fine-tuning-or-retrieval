Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Proactive vs. Reactive: Management Tensions in Recurrent Anaphylaxis

We are all comfortable with the "standard" anaphylaxis algorithm: recognize the symptoms, give epinephrine, and monitor for a potential biphasic reaction. But what happens when the "biphasic" reaction isn't a one-time sequel? What happens when a patient enters a multiphasic loop—repeatedly crashing into anaphylactic shock despite repeated doses of epinephrine and "adequate" initial management?

This is where the clinical guidelines start to feel a bit thin. We move from a well-defined emergency into a clinical "grey zone" where the decision-making shifts from following a flowchart to balancing high-stakes risks.

## The Case: A Recurring Cycle
Consider a 60-year-old woman weighing 104 kg who presented to the ED with anaphylaxis after a single dose of clarithromycin. She was hypotensive, hypoxemic, and showing classic signs of systemic involvement. She received 0.3 mg of IM epinephrine (via auto-injector) and responded well initially.

However, the "resolution" was short-lived. Within 12 hours, she experienced three more distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock. Each time, she required more epinephrine. Interestingly, while the severity of the symptoms decreased with each recurrence, the clinical picture was one of persistent, recurring instability.

This patient eventually moved to the ICU, where the team faced a difficult management fork in the road: Do we stay reactive, or do we go proactive?

## The Management Dilemma: Proactive vs. Reactive
In this case, the clinical team weighed two primary strategies for managing multiphasic anaphylaxis, both of which carry significant risks due to a lack of clear evidence-based guidance.

**The Proactive Approach: Continuous IV Epinephrine**
The logic here is preemptive. If the patient is cycling through anaphylactic episodes, why wait for the next crash? Continuous infusion aims to maintain a steady state of vasoconstriction and bronchodilation, essentially "blunting" the peaks of the mast cell degranulation. 
*   *The Risk:* Continuous pressors carry inherent risks of tachycardia, arrhythmias, and potential tissue ischemia. Is it necessary if the episodes are becoming milder?

**The Expectant Approach: Reactive Management**
This is the "wait and see" method—treating each episode as it arises with IM epinephrine and supportive care. In this case, the team watched as the symptoms gradually tapered off over several hours.
*   *The Risk:* This approach relies on the team’s ability to recognize and treat the next "crash" instantly. If the next episode is severe enough to cause profound hypotension or airway compromise before the next dose can be administered, the outcome could be catastrophic.

Ultimately, the patient’s symptoms stabilized without either extreme measure, but the case highlights a glaring gap in our protocols: we lack a consensus on how to transition from "episodic" to "continuous" management in multiphasic cases.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **Watch the Weight:** This patient weighed 104 kg. Many standard auto-injectors provide only 0.3 mg of epinephrine. In a patient of this size, 0.3 mg may be a significant underdose. While auto-injectors are excellent for speed, we must be mindful of weight-based requirements (0.01 mg/kg, up to 0.5 mg) to avoid the need for frequent dose escalations.
*   **The Steroid Myth:** While this patient received corticosteroids (prednisone and dexamethasone) based on traditional protocols, modern evidence is increasingly shifting away from their use in preventing biphasic reactions. Recent reviews suggest they may provide no benefit and could even increase the risk of hospital admission.
*   **Identify the "Why":** Multiphasic reactions should trigger a search for underlying pathology. In this case, the patient’s high tryptase levels and clinical history suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis. Patients with mast cell disorders are predisposed to severe, complex anaphylactic courses.
*   **Tryptase Timing:** If you suspect a mast cell disorder, get those tryptase levels. Ideally, you need three samples: one as soon as possible (without delaying treatment), one at the peak (1–4 hours after onset), and one at least 24 hours after resolution to establish a baseline.

**Key Takeaways:**
*   **Multiphasic anaphylaxis** is distinct from biphasic reactions and requires a higher level of suspicion and prolonged monitoring.
*   **Dosing matters:** Be wary of 0.3 mg auto-injectors in large adults; ensure you have a plan for 0.5 mg doses or escalating care.
*   **Corticosteroids** are not a "magic bullet" for preventing biphasic relapses; focus on prompt epinephrine and hemodynamic support.
*   **Consider the underlying pathology:** Recurrent, severe reactions may point toward systemic mastocytosis, requiring specialized long-term management.
