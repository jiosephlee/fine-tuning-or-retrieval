Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Is 0.3mg Enough? Rethinking Epinephrine Dosing in the Obese Patient

We’ve all been there: a patient is crashing in anaphylaxis, the atmosphere is frantic, and the easiest, fastest route to treatment is grabbing the epinephrine auto-injector off the shelf. It’s a life-saving tool designed to bridge the gap between "recognition" and "intervention." But while the auto-injector is a triumph of public health engineering, it may be creating a clinical "underdosing trap" for our larger patients.

Consider the case of a woman in her 60s who presented to the emergency department with severe anaphylaxis following a single dose of clarithromycin. She was a large patient (104 kg) who presented with hypotension, hypoxemia, and widespread urticaria. Because the situation was time-sensitive and the clinical team was in the early stages of stabilization, they reached for the auto-injector. She received 0.3 mg of intramuscular (IM) epinephrine. 

While she initially stabilized, the "resolution" was deceptive. Over the next 12 hours, she suffered through a multiphasic course of anaphylaxis, requiring a total of four separate episodes of shock. Interestingly, the first two doses were the 0.3 mg auto-injectors, while the subsequent two doses were 0.5 mg doses prepared manually. Each time she relapsed, the team had to scramble to provide more intervention. 

This case highlights a growing concern in emergency medicine: the discrepancy between "available" dosing and "appropriate" dosing. For a patient weighing over 100 kg, a 0.3 mg dose—while better than nothing—falls significantly short of the recommended 0.01 mg/kg (up to a maximum of 0.5 mg). Recent data suggests that patients who start with 0.3 mg are significantly more likely to require escalated care and additional injections compared to those who receive the full 0.5 mg dose upfront. 

Why is this a problem? When we underdose the initial "hit," we aren't just slightly delaying the resolution; we may be failing to achieve the necessary physiological threshold to arrest the systemic mast cell degranulation. In this patient’s case, her history suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis—a condition where mast cells are primed for explosive mediator release. In such a "hyper-reactive" physiological state, starting with a sub-therapeutic dose can lead to the exhausting, multiphasic "yo-yo" effect we saw here, where the patient cycles between clinical improvement and profound shock.

Furthermore, this case reminds us to look beyond the immediate "fix." While the patient's symptoms lessened with each relapse, the recurring nature of her reaction underscores the need for prolonged observation. We often hear about "biphasic" reactions, but "multiphasic" reactions—multiple relapses despite adequate treatment—are a clinical minefield. Because there is currently no gold-standard for monitoring these complex courses, our best tool is a high index of suspicion and a commitment to robust, weight-appropriate dosing from the very first minute.

We also need to address the "steroid myth." In this case, the patient received several doses of prednisone and dexamethasone. While tradition dictates steroids prevent biphasic reactions, the evidence is increasingly thin, with some studies even suggesting they might increase hospital admission rates. Our priority remains the rapid administration of epinephrine—at the correct dose—followed by aggressive fluid resuscitation.

**Clinical Pearls:**

*   **Dose Matters:** For adults over 50 kg, 0.5 mg is the standard IM dose. If you are relying on an auto-injector, be aware that many only deliver 0.3 mg, which may be insufficient for larger patients or those with suspected mast cell disorders.
*   **The Underdosing Trap:** Starting with a 0.3 mg dose in an obese patient increases the likelihood of needing multiple "rescue" injections, which can lead to cumulative side effects and prolonged distress for the patient.
*   **Recognize Multiphasic Patterns:** If a patient shows a "good" response to epinephrine but remains in a high-risk environment or has underlying risk factors (like suspected mastocytosis), maintain a low threshold for continued monitoring and repeated dosing.
*   **Move Past Steroids:** Don't rely on corticosteroids to "prevent" a relapse. They do not work fast enough to stop an ongoing reaction and have shown little to no efficacy in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis.
*   **Think About Mast Cells:** If a patient presents with unusually severe, multiphasic, or refractory anaphylaxis, consider underlying mast cell disorders (like systemic mastocytosis) as a potential complicating factor.
