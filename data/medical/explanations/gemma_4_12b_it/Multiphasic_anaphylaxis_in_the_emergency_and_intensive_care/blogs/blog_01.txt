Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Beyond the First Wave: Navigating Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

We’ve all seen the classic anaphylaxis script: a patient presents with urticaria, hypotension, and stridor; we give epinephrine, they stabilize, and we move toward discharge. But what happens when the "resolution" is just a pause?

Anaphylaxis isn't always a single event. For a subset of patients, the clinical course is biphasic (a recurrence of symptoms after apparent resolution) or even multiphasic—where the patient relapses into anaphylaxis multiple times despite adequate initial treatment. These cases are notoriously difficult to manage because they sit in a gray area of current guidelines: how long do we observe? What is the threshold for "refractory" vs. "biphasic"? And how do we manage a patient who keeps crashing after we’ve already "fixed" them?

## The Case: A Rebellious Reaction
Consider a woman in her 60s who presented to the ED in anaphylactic shock after a single dose of oral clarithromycin. She was hypotensive (74/50 mmHg), hypoxemic (88% on room air), and showed significant facial swelling and urticaria. This was her first true anaphylactic event. 

The initial management worked: she received an IM epinephrine auto-injector, fluids, and oxygen, and her blood pressure and symptoms stabilized. However, the clinical picture quickly turned complex. Over the next 12 hours, she experienced three additional episodes of anaphylactic shock. Each time, she required further doses of epinephrine to resolve the symptoms. By the final episode, the intensity had actually lessened—moving from deep hypoxemia and cyanosis to primarily dyspnea and rash—but the "waves" of reaction continued to disrupt her recovery.

## Decoding the "Waves"
One of the biggest challenges in these cases is distinguishing between true biphasic anaphylaxis and other clinical scenarios. Is the patient experiencing a delayed mast cell degranulation (biphasic)? Or are they experiencing *refractory* anaphylaxis because the initial dose of epinephrine was insufficient? Or, perhaps, is there a "slow-release" mechanism occurring, where the allergen is still being absorbed from the GI tract?

In this case, the distinction was blurred. Because the patient showed a "good" clinical response initially, it was framed as a multiphasic course. However, the report highlights a critical nuance in epinephrine dosing. The patient received 0.3 mg doses initially. For a patient weighing 104 kg, 0.3 mg is a significant underdose (the recommendation is 0.01 mg/kg, which would be closer to 1.0 mg for her weight, capped at 0.5 mg). Emerging data suggests that patients receiving the 0.3 mg auto-injector dose are significantly more likely to require escalation of care and additional injections compared to those receiving the 0.5 mg dose.

## The Search for an Underlying Driver
When we see "rebellious" anaphylaxis—where the reaction seems disproportionate to the trigger or persists in waves—we have to look for underlying predispositions. In this patient, further workup suggested a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis. 

Systemic mastocytosis involves an abnormal increase in mast cells, which can predispose individuals to severe, prolonged, and complex anaphylactic reactions. While she didn't meet every formal diagnostic criterion (due to limited marrow biopsy material), the combination of multiphasic reactions, elevated tryptase levels, and hepatomegaly points toward a systemic mast cell disorder as a potential "engine" driving her repeated relapses.

## Clinical Management and the Steroid Myth
Management of multiphasic anaphylaxis remains largely expert-based due to a lack of robust evidence. There is a lingering habit of using corticosteroids to "prevent" biphasic reactions. However, current evidence is increasingly leaning away from this; several systematic reviews show no benefit of steroids in preventing biphasic events, and some even suggest they might increase the risk of hospital admission due to side effects.

In this case, the team debated between continuous IV epinephrine (pre-emptive treatment) and an expectant approach. Ultimately, as the intensity of the waves decreased, the patient stabilized without the need for continuous infusions. It serves as a reminder that while we must be aggressive in the first wave, the "waves" that follow may require a balance of vigilant observation and titrated intervention.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Dose Matters:** Be mindful of the 0.3 mg vs. 0.5 mg epinephrine dose. In patients over 50 kg, 0.3 mg may be insufficient, potentially leading to a "pseudo-refractory" presentation where the patient appears to improve but subsequently relapses.
*   **Define the Course:** Differentiate between biphasic (recurrence after resolution), refractory (failure to respond to initial epi), and ongoing absorption (persistent reaction due to remaining allergen).
*   **The Steroid Reality:** Don't rely on corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions; current evidence suggests they do not reduce the risk of recurrence.
*   **Look for the "Engine":** If a patient presents with atypical, multiphasic, or severe anaphylaxis, consider underlying mast cell disorders like systemic mastocytosis, especially if tryptase levels remain elevated or if there are extra-cutaneous symptoms.
*   **Observation is Key:** Because there are no reliable predictors for biphasic reactions, risk-stratified observation remains the standard. However, a multiphasic course necessitates much longer observation and a lower threshold for repeated epinephrine administration.
