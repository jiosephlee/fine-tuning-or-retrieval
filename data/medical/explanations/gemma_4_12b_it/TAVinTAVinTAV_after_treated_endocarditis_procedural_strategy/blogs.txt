Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Breaking the Rules: When TAVI is the Only Option for Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis

If you’ve spent any time looking at the ESC guidelines for valvular heart disease, you know the "gold standard" for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) is surgical intervention. The logic is straightforward: surgery allows for definitive source control, debridement of infected tissue, and replacement of the compromised valve. However, in the real world of clinical practice, we often encounter the "no-win" scenario. What do you do when the patient is a surgical "no-go" but the prosthetic valve is failing due to infection-related degeneration?

This case report presents a fascinating look into "forbidden territory"—the use of a transcatheter approach to manage a failed prosthesis in a patient with a history of PVE. It highlights the shift from treating an acute infection to managing chronic structural deterioration in a patient whose comorbidities make the operating room a prohibitively dangerous environment.

## The Clinical Pivot: From Infection to Degradation

The patient in this case—a man in his late 70s—had already undergone two prior TAVI procedures (TAV-in-TAV) by 2022. He then developed PVE caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*. He received a full six-week course of IV antibiotics, followed by oral therapy. By early 2024, the infection was clinically and radiologically resolved; there were no vegetations, no active infection, and no need for ongoing suppressive antibiotics.

However, the "scars" of the infection remained. The patient presented with severe aortic stenosis (mean gradient of 49 mm Hg, AVA of 0.5 cm²) and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. At this point, the clinical team faced a dilemma. While the guidelines favor surgery for PVE, the patient’s EuroSCORE II was a staggering 27.36%, compounded by COPD, diabetes, and a history of gastrointestinal bleeding. A surgical replacement would have required a total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement due to the deep fixation of the existing transcatheter frames. 

The decision to proceed with a third transcatheter intervention (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) was not a rejection of the guidelines, but a pragmatic application of the "individualized management" caveat. Once the infection was gone, the primary problem shifted from infectious biology to mechanical engineering: how do we restore valve function in a triple-stented anatomy?

## Engineering a Solution: The TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV Strategy

Performing a third valve implantation is an exercise in high-stakes geometry. With three overlapping frames, the risks of coronary artery obstruction and severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM) are magnified. The team’s success relied on three specific pillars:

First, **meticulous CT-guided planning**. They didn't just look at the anatomy; they used multislice CT (MSCT) to model the relationship between the anticipated neo-skirt and the coronary ostia. This allowed them to plan a "high implantation" strategy. By placing the third valve slightly higher, they could fix the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame, preventing inward leaflet folding and preserving coronary flow without the need for specialized coronary protection devices.

Second, **deliberate valve selection**. The Acurate neo2 was chosen specifically for its supra-annular leaflet positioning and relatively low frame height. In a "nesting doll" of three valves, every millimeter of frame height counts toward maintaining the effective orifice area.

Third, **commissural alignment**. By aligning the new valve with the previous ones, the team aimed to minimize the cumulative "mesh burden," which is the biggest hurdle for future coronary access.

## The Result and The Reality

The outcome was a triumph of planning. At one year, the patient remained asymptomatic, with a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg and an AVA of 1.1 cm². It proves that with sufficient imaging and a multidisciplinary "Heart Team" approach, transcatheter technology can bridge the gap when surgery is off the table.

However, we must remain grounded in the limitations. This was a "one-way" success. Because of the triple-stented anatomy, the patient’s options for any future failure are now extremely limited. We are effectively "maxing out" the anatomical real estate of the aortic root.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **The Infection vs. Degradation Distinction:** When managing PVE, the clinical strategy can shift from "infection control" to "structural repair" once the infection is fully resolved and the patient is stable.
*   **Individualize the Guidelines:** A high EuroSCORE II and complex anatomical fixation (like deep transcatheter stents) can make surgical root replacement higher risk than a complex transcatheter reintervention.
*   **CT is Non-Negotiable:** For TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, 3D modeling and CT-guided planning are essential to predict coronary clearance and determine the optimal implantation height.
*   **The "High Implantation" Strategy:** In multi-valve scenarios, intentionally high placement can help prevent neo-skirt interference with coronary ostia and improve the effective orifice area by fixing existing leaflets.
*   **Lifetime Management:** Every transcatheter reintervention reduces the "room" available for future procedures. Early discussion of the procedural trajectory is vital, especially in younger patients or those with significant CAD.



# The Nesting Doll Challenge: Navigating TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV Anatomy

We are rapidly entering an era of "lifetime management" for structural heart disease. As transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) becomes the standard of care across broader risk profiles, we are no longer just treating acute stenosis; we are managing the long-term evolution of prosthetic valves. This shift brings us to a fascinating, albeit technically daunting, frontier: the "nesting doll" scenario. 

Imagine performing aortic root archaeology. You aren't just looking at a native annulus; you’re navigating a complex architecture of overlapping metal frames, previous leaflet fibrosis, and—in some cases—the lingering shadows of past infections. This case of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV highlights the shift from "standard" intervention to a highly tailored, strategy-driven engineering problem.

## The Case: A Triple-Stent Saga
The patient was a man in his late 70s with a high surgical risk (EuroSCORE II of 27.36%) and significant comorbidities, including COPD and diabetes. His journey began in 2014 with a Lotus valve. By 2022, that valve had degenerated, leading to a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut prosthesis. 

The plot thickened three months later when he developed prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*. After a successful six-week course of targeted antibiotics and a confirmed resolution of the infection, he presented again in 2024 with severe stenosis. With an AVA of just 0.5 cm² and two existing frames already integrated into the aortic wall, the question wasn't just "can we do it?" but "how do we do it without occluding his coronary arteries?"

## Mapping the Archaeology: The Role of MSCT
In a triple-stented anatomy, the margin for error is razor-thin. The team utilized multislice CT (MSCT) and 3D modeling to perform a pre-procedural "reconnaissance." They needed to identify the minimum lumen diameter (found to be 13.7 mm) and, more importantly, assess the interaction between the existing frames and the coronary ostia.

The primary technical hurdle in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is the "mesh burden." Every time you add a frame, you risk obstructing coronary flow or reducing the effective orifice area (EOA) to a point of clinical insignificance (patient-prosthesis mismatch). By using MSCT to measure coronary ostial height and the distance to the previous frames, the team was able to plan a specific implantation depth.

## The Strategy: High Implantation and "Cracking" the Frame
The procedural strategy here was elegant in its simplicity but required precise execution. The team opted for a "high implantation" of the third valve (an Acurate neo2). By positioning the new device at the level of the Evolut leaflets, they achieved two goals: they fixed the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame to prevent inward folding (which would further shrink the orifice) and kept the neo-skirt away from the coronary ostia.

A subtle but critical clinical pearl in this case was the use of high-pressure balloon post-dilation. While they didn't intentionally "fracture" the previous Evolut frame, the post-dilation likely provided enough radial expansion to "crack" the previous frame slightly, improving leaflet motion and central flow dynamics. This resulted in a massive hemodynamic win: the mean gradient dropped from 49 mm Hg to 26 mm Hg at the one-year follow-up.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points

*   **Aortic Root Archaeology:** In multi-procedure cases, MSCT is not just a "nice to have"—it is the roadmap. You must map the "nesting" frames, the neo-skirt's proximity to coronary ostia, and the landing zone for the third device.
*   **The PVE Nuance:** While guidelines often favor surgery for PVE, the high surgical risk in this patient (EuroSCORE II 27.36%) made TAVI the only viable path. Once the infection is clinically and immunologically resolved, the strategy shifts back to anatomical feasibility and hemodynamic optimization.
*   **High Implantation Strategy:** In complex TAV-in-TAV scenarios, "high" implantation can be a deliberate move to preserve coronary access and prevent leaflet "sandwiching" that reduces the effective orifice area.
*   **The "Cracking" Effect:** Post-dilation in a triple-stented anatomy serves a dual purpose: it optimizes the new valve expansion and can provide a mechanical "nudge" to the previous frame to improve overall leaflet mobility.
*   **Hemodynamic Trade-offs:** Even with a successful TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, be prepared for a significant patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). In this case, the AVA remained modest (1.1 cm²), but the patient remained asymptomatic and clinically stable.



# Maximizing the Orifice: Strategies to Overcome Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch in Triple-Stented Valves

How many "Russian nesting dolls" can you fit inside an aortic valve before the flow just... stops? 

In the era of expanding TAVI indications and improving longevity, we are increasingly moving away from "one and done" interventions toward a lifetime of structural valve management. While TAV-in-TAV procedures are becoming a staple of the interventional toolkit, we are now entering the frontier of TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV. This case presents a fascinating—and technically daunting—challenge: how do you achieve adequate hemodynamics when you have three overlapping frames competing for the same tiny piece of real estate?

### The Clinical Scenario: A Triple-Stented Anatomy
The patient in this case is a man in his late 70s with a high surgical risk (EuroSCORE II of 27.36%). His journey began in 2014 with a Lotus valve. By 2022, that valve had degenerated, leading to a second intervention with an Evolut prosthesis. The complication? He developed prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). After successful antimicrobial treatment, he returned in 2024 with a devastating hemodynamic profile: a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg and a calculated aortic valve area (AVA) of just 0.5 cm².

At 0.5 cm², the valve isn't just "stenotic"—it’s functionally obstructed. To save the patient, the team had to plan a third transcatheter implantation (Acurate neo2). The primary hurdle wasn't just getting the third valve in; it was ensuring that the third valve didn't turn the aortic root into a solid block of metal and tissue.

### The "Orifice Crisis" and the Strategy for Success
The biggest enemy in serial TAVI is Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM). Every time you add a frame, you lose effective orifice area. In this case, the team utilized three specific strategies to fight back against the shrinking orifice:

**1. Precision Planning via MSCT**
You cannot "wing" a triple-stented anatomy. The team used multislice CT (MSCT) to map the interaction between the three frames. They specifically looked at coronary ostial height and the relationship between the anticipated "neo-skirt" of the new valve and the coronary arteries. This allowed them to determine the exact implantation depth needed to preserve coronary flow without the need for extra-procedural protection.

**2. High Implantation Depth to Prevent Leaflet Folding**
One of the cleverest maneuvers in this case was the deliberate high implantation of the third valve. By seating the Acurate neo2 at the level of the previous Evolut valve leaflets, the team was able to "fix" the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame. This is a crucial distinction: by pinning those leaflets back, they prevented them from folding inward into the lumen of the new valve, which would have further constricted the already tiny AVA.

**3. Commissural Alignment and "Mechanical Expansion"**
The team prioritized commissural alignment to ensure that the flow dynamics remained as central as possible and to preserve future coronary access. Furthermore, they utilized high-pressure balloon post-dilation. While the goal wasn't necessarily to fracture the previous frames, the high-pressure expansion likely provided enough radial force to "crack" the surrounding structures, allowing the new valve to expand more fully than it would have in a static environment.

### The Result
The results speak for themselves. Post-procedure, the AVA jumped from a dismal 0.5 cm² to 1.1 cm² at one month, eventually stabilizing at 1.1 cm² at one year. The patient remained asymptomatic, showing that with meticulous planning and a strategy-driven approach, we can successfully navigate even the most complex "nesting doll" anatomies.

### Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Messages
*   **CT is Non-Negotiable:** For TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, pre-procedural MSCT is the gold standard for mapping frame-to-frame interactions and assessing coronary clearance.
*   **Think About Leaflet Geometry:** High implantation depth isn't just about staying away from the coronaries; it's about anchoring the previous valve's leaflets to prevent inward folding and further EOA loss.
*   **The Power of Post-Dilation:** In triple-stented cases, high-pressure balloon post-dilation is a critical tool to overcome the cumulative radial resistance of multiple overlapping frames.
*   **Alignment Matters:** Maintaining commissural alignment across multiple generations of valves is essential for optimizing flow and preserving the "last remaining" coronary access.
*   **Lifetime Management:** We must start thinking about the "redo" at the time of the first TAVI, especially in patients with high surgical risk and significant coronary disease.



# Running Out of Space: The Limits of Serial TAV-in-TAV Interventions

We are entering the era of the "Russian Doll" heart. As transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) becomes the standard of care for a broader range of patients—and as those patients live longer—we are increasingly seeing the "redo" and "redo-redo" scenarios. 

The case at hand is a perfect example: a man in his late 70s who already had two transcatheter valves in place. After a bout of infective endocarditis (PVE) and subsequent severe restenosis, he required a third intervention—a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV. While the procedural success was excellent at the one-year mark, it forces us to confront a looming clinical reality: we are running out of anatomical real estate.

## The Technical Tightrope

In a triple-stented anatomy, the margin for error practically vanishes. You aren't just navigating a single prosthesis; you are navigating the interaction between three different frames, each with its own height, radial strength, and commissural alignment. 

In this case, the team had to contend with a massive reduction in the effective orifice area (AVA of 0.5 cm²) and the ghost of previous infection. The strategy here was masterclass in preprocedural planning. By using multi-slice CT (MSCT) to model the landing zone and coronary ostial heights, the team opted for a "high implantation" strategy. The goal was to fix the third valve (an Acurate neo2) at the level of the second valve's leaflets. This served two purposes: it prevented the leaflets of the previous valve from folding inward and further choking the orifice, and it kept the "neo-skirt" of the new valve away from the coronary ostia.

## The "End-Game": What happens at Valve #4?

This brings us to the elephant in the room. If this patient develops severe stenosis again in five years, what is the move? We are reaching the limits of how many frames can be stacked before the cumulative mesh burden renders the aortic root a solid block of metal.

When we exhaust the "real estate," we move into the realm of high-risk, "Hail Mary" maneuvers. We are starting to see discussions around:

*   **Bioprosthetic Valve Fracture:** Intentionally fracturing the frame of a previous valve to create space for a new one. This is technically daunting and carries a high risk of embolization or malpositioning.
*   **Intentional Leaflet Laceration (e.g., BASILICA):** While currently used to prevent iatrogenic coronary obstruction by cutting native leaflets, could a variation of this be used to "clear the way" in a multi-stented environment? 
*   **Surgical Explantation:** In some cases, the "last resort" might actually be the most complex surgery of all—an open aortic root replacement to remove the "nest" of transcatheter valves.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

The success of this TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV highlights that while these procedures are feasible, they are not "plug and play." They require a transition from standard TAVI thinking to a high-level engineering mindset.

*   **CT-Guided Planning is Non-Negotiable:** In multi-prosthesis cases, you cannot fly blind. You need 3D modeling to understand frame interaction, coronary clearance, and the specific landing zone available.
*   **Commissural Alignment is Key:** Aligning the leaflets of the new valve with the previous ones is essential to maximize the residual effective orifice area (AVA) and preserve future coronary access.
*   **The "Lifetime Management" Mindset:** We need to start discussing the "next" valve during the "first" valve. If a patient is high-risk for surgery now, they are high-risk for a complex redo later. We need to map out the procedural trajectory early.
*   **PVE and TAVI:** While surgical replacement is the gold standard for PVE, extreme surgical risk (as seen in this patient with a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%) can make transcatheter redo strategies the only viable path. Once the infection is cleared, the focus shifts entirely to anatomy and hemodynamics.
