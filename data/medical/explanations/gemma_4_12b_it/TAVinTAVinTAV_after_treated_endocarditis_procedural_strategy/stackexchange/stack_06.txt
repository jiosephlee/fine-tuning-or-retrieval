Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Morning Report] How does the cumulative 'mesh burden' of multiple overlapping transcatheter frames impact the long-term management of a patient with known coronary artery disease?

In the context of serial transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), the cumulative "mesh burden" refers to the progressive accumulation of metallic frames within the aortic root. For a patient with known coronary artery disease, such as the one in this case who has a history of three-vessel disease and multiple percutaneous coronary interventions (PCIs), this burden creates significant challenges for long-term management, primarily concerning coronary access and the risk of iatrogenic coronary obstruction.

Each successive transcatheter valve frame occupies physical space and may alter the local anatomy of the aortic root. With three overlapping frames (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), the risk is twofold: 
First, the "neo-skirt" of the most recent prosthesis can encroach upon the coronary ostia, potentially causing acute occlusion. 
Second, the cumulative density of the metal frames can make it technically difficult, or even impossible, to navigate guidewires and catheters into the coronary arteries for future revascularization.

In this case, the management of these risks was predicated on meticulous preprocedural planning. The use of multislice CT (MSCT) was essential to evaluate the "landing zone," coronary ostial height, and the specific interaction between the frames of the previously implanted valves. To mitigate the mesh burden, the clinical team employed a "high implantation" strategy. By positioning the third valve (Acurate neo2) slightly higher, the team aimed to:
1.  **Preserve coronary flow:** Ensure the neo-skirt did not sit directly over the coronary ostia.
2.  **Maintain future access:** Provide enough clearance for future coronary interventions, which are highly likely given the patient's underlying coronary anatomy.
3.  **Optimize hemodynamics:** Align the new valve with the existing leaflets to maximize the effective orifice area and minimize patient-prosthesis mismatch.

Ultimately, as the number of overlapping frames increases, the "anatomical real estate" of the aortic root becomes severely limited. For patients with known CAD, the clinical priority shifts toward preserving a viable pathway for coronary access, as the options for subsequent interventions become increasingly restricted once a triple-stented configuration is established.
