Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Breaking the Rules: When TAVI is the Only Option for Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis

If you’ve spent any time looking at the ESC guidelines for valvular heart disease, you know the "gold standard" for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) is surgical intervention. The logic is straightforward: surgery allows for definitive source control, debridement of infected tissue, and replacement of the compromised valve. However, in the real world of clinical practice, we often encounter the "no-win" scenario. What do you do when the patient is a surgical "no-go" but the prosthetic valve is failing due to infection-related degeneration?

This case report presents a fascinating look into "forbidden territory"—the use of a transcatheter approach to manage a failed prosthesis in a patient with a history of PVE. It highlights the shift from treating an acute infection to managing chronic structural deterioration in a patient whose comorbidities make the operating room a prohibitively dangerous environment.

## The Clinical Pivot: From Infection to Degradation

The patient in this case—a man in his late 70s—had already undergone two prior TAVI procedures (TAV-in-TAV) by 2022. He then developed PVE caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*. He received a full six-week course of IV antibiotics, followed by oral therapy. By early 2024, the infection was clinically and radiologically resolved; there were no vegetations, no active infection, and no need for ongoing suppressive antibiotics.

However, the "scars" of the infection remained. The patient presented with severe aortic stenosis (mean gradient of 49 mm Hg, AVA of 0.5 cm²) and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. At this point, the clinical team faced a dilemma. While the guidelines favor surgery for PVE, the patient’s EuroSCORE II was a staggering 27.36%, compounded by COPD, diabetes, and a history of gastrointestinal bleeding. A surgical replacement would have required a total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement due to the deep fixation of the existing transcatheter frames. 

The decision to proceed with a third transcatheter intervention (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) was not a rejection of the guidelines, but a pragmatic application of the "individualized management" caveat. Once the infection was gone, the primary problem shifted from infectious biology to mechanical engineering: how do we restore valve function in a triple-stented anatomy?

## Engineering a Solution: The TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV Strategy

Performing a third valve implantation is an exercise in high-stakes geometry. With three overlapping frames, the risks of coronary artery obstruction and severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM) are magnified. The team’s success relied on three specific pillars:

First, **meticulous CT-guided planning**. They didn't just look at the anatomy; they used multislice CT (MSCT) to model the relationship between the anticipated neo-skirt and the coronary ostia. This allowed them to plan a "high implantation" strategy. By placing the third valve slightly higher, they could fix the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame, preventing inward leaflet folding and preserving coronary flow without the need for specialized coronary protection devices.

Second, **deliberate valve selection**. The Acurate neo2 was chosen specifically for its supra-annular leaflet positioning and relatively low frame height. In a "nesting doll" of three valves, every millimeter of frame height counts toward maintaining the effective orifice area.

Third, **commissural alignment**. By aligning the new valve with the previous ones, the team aimed to minimize the cumulative "mesh burden," which is the biggest hurdle for future coronary access.

## The Result and The Reality

The outcome was a triumph of planning. At one year, the patient remained asymptomatic, with a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg and an AVA of 1.1 cm². It proves that with sufficient imaging and a multidisciplinary "Heart Team" approach, transcatheter technology can bridge the gap when surgery is off the table.

However, we must remain grounded in the limitations. This was a "one-way" success. Because of the triple-stented anatomy, the patient’s options for any future failure are now extremely limited. We are effectively "maxing out" the anatomical real estate of the aortic root.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **The Infection vs. Degradation Distinction:** When managing PVE, the clinical strategy can shift from "infection control" to "structural repair" once the infection is fully resolved and the patient is stable.
*   **Individualize the Guidelines:** A high EuroSCORE II and complex anatomical fixation (like deep transcatheter stents) can make surgical root replacement higher risk than a complex transcatheter reintervention.
*   **CT is Non-Negotiable:** For TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, 3D modeling and CT-guided planning are essential to predict coronary clearance and determine the optimal implantation height.
*   **The "High Implantation" Strategy:** In multi-valve scenarios, intentionally high placement can help prevent neo-skirt interference with coronary ostia and improve the effective orifice area by fixing existing leaflets.
*   **Lifetime Management:** Every transcatheter reintervention reduces the "room" available for future procedures. Early discussion of the procedural trajectory is vital, especially in younger patients or those with significant CAD.
