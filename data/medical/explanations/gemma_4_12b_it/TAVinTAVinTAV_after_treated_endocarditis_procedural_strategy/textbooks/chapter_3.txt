Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 3: ## Preprocedural Planning and Diagnostic Reasoning for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV

The decision to proceed with a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) requires a rigorous shift from standard treatment protocols toward a highly individualized, strategy-driven approach. In this case, the primary diagnostic reasoning was dictated by the patient’s prohibitive surgical risk and the complex anatomical "nesting" of two previously implanted transcatheter valves. With a EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and significant comorbidities—including COPD, diabetes, and a history of gastrointestinal bleeding—the patient was deemed a high-risk candidate for open surgery. Furthermore, the deep integration of the existing valve frames into the aortic wall meant that any surgical intervention would likely have necessitated a high-risk total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement. Consequently, the clinical team had to determine if a third transcatheter intervention was anatomically feasible and how to mitigate the inherent risks of triple-stented anatomy.

Multislice Computed Tomography (MSCT) serves as the cornerstone of preprocedural planning in complex multi-stented cases. Unlike standard TAVI planning, which focuses on the native anatomy, TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV requires a detailed assessment of the "lumen within a lumen." In this patient, MSCT was utilized to measure the minimum lumen diameter of the second prosthesis (the Evolut valve), which was found to be 13.7 mm. This measurement is critical because it defines the maximum allowable size of the third valve and helps predict the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). MSCT also allows the clinician to visualize the spatial relationship between the existing frames, the dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, and the specific height of the coronary ostia.

A significant challenge in sequential TAVIs is the cumulative "mesh burden" of overlapping frames, which can lead to two primary complications: a reduced effective orifice area (EOA) and iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction. To address these, the clinical team employed 3D modeling to assess the interaction between the frames. The goal was to ensure that the third valve would not only fit within the second but would also be positioned to maximize the EOA. This involves planning for commissural alignment—matching the leaflets of the new valve with the existing structures to optimize blood flow dynamics and minimize the risk of the new valve's fabric (the neo-skirt) obstructing the coronary openings.

The final procedural strategy was centered on determining the optimal implantation depth. Because the patient already had two overlapping frames, there was a high risk that a standard implantation would place the third valve’s neo-skirt too low, potentially occluding the coronary arteries. The team opted for a "high implantation" strategy, aiming to seat the third valve at the level of the second valve's leaflets. This approach served a dual purpose: it prevented the leaflets of the second valve from folding inward—which would have further constricted the opening—and ensured that the neo-skirt of the third valve remained above the coronary ostia. By combining these anatomical measurements and 3D modeling, the team was able to select a specific prosthesis (the Acurate neo2) and a deployment depth that balanced the need for hemodynamic improvement with the necessity of preserving future coronary access.
