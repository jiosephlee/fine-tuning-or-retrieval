Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 5: ## Postoperative Outcomes, Surveillance, and Lifetime Management

The success of a complex transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) procedure is first evaluated through immediate hemodynamic assessment. In this case, the procedure was judged successful according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) criteria, which are the standard for evaluating TAVI outcomes. While the immediate post-procedural echocardiogram confirmed the absence of aortic regurgitation (Grade 0) and showed mobile leaflets, the initial hemodynamics were challenging, with a mean transvalvular gradient of 67 mm Hg. This high gradient indicated that while the valve was physically deployed, the three layers of overlapping frames significantly restricted the effective orifice area (EOA).

A critical consideration in multi-valve interventions is Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM). PPM occurs when the effective orifice area of the implanted prosthesis is too small relative to the patient’s body surface area, potentially leading to persistent symptoms of aortic stenosis. In this patient, the initial AVA of 0.9 cm² represented severe PPM. However, the clinical course demonstrated a favorable "settling" period; by one month, the mean gradient improved to 27 mm Hg and the AVA increased to 1.1 cm². This improvement, alongside observed left ventricular (LV) remodeling—characterized by a reduction in chamber volumes and an improved ejection fraction—suggests that while the triple-stented anatomy limits the maximum possible orifice area, the procedural strategy (high implantation and commissural alignment) successfully optimized the remaining space to achieve functional stability.

Long-term surveillance is paramount, particularly in a patient with a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). At the one-year follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with a stable mean gradient of 26 mm Hg and an AVA of 1.1 cm². For the clinician, the primary goals of long-term monitoring in this context are the identification of structural valve deterioration (SVD) and the detection of recurrent infection. Because the patient had completed a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy for *Enterococcus faecalis* and showed no clinical signs of active endocarditis on imaging, the focus shifted toward monitoring the mechanical integrity and hemodynamics of the triple-stented valve.

This case illustrates the emerging paradigm of "lifetime management" for structural valve disease. As TAVI becomes a standard of care for a wider range of patients, clinicians must move beyond thinking of the procedure as a single isolated event. Instead, each intervention must be viewed as a step in a potential series of reinterventions. This requires "procedural foresight," where the first valve choice considers the possibility of a second, and the second considers the possibility of a third. Factors such as frame height, coronary access preservation, and the expected "mesh burden" of overlapping stents must be weighed early to ensure that future interventions remain technically feasible.

Looking toward the future, the clinical team must consider the limitations of sequential TAVI. If the third valve were to fail, the risk of open surgical explantation would be prohibitively high due to the deep fixation of the prior frames into the aortic wall. This may necessitate the adoption of emerging catheter-based solutions for terminal valve failure. One such concept is the BASILICA (bioprosthetic aortic scallop intentional laceration) technique, which involves intentional leaflet laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction. While still under investigation and not yet standard for triple-stented configurations, such maneuvers represent the next frontier in managing the "end-of-the-line" complications of multi-prosthetic valve failure.
