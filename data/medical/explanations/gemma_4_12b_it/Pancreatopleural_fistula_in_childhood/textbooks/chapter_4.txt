Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 4: ## Differential Diagnosis of Amylase-Rich Pleural Effusions

When a patient presents with a pleural effusion containing high levels of amylase, the clinical focus must shift toward identifying the specific source of the leakage. While a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a definitive cause of amylase-rich fluid, it is a rare entity that can be easily confused with more common thoracic or systemic pathologies. A systematic approach to the differential diagnosis is essential to avoid unnecessary long-term treatments for the wrong underlying condition.

Infectious and malignant processes are often the primary considerations in cases of hemorrhagic or high-amylase pleural effusion. In this case, the history of contact with a patient with pulmonary tuberculosis made Koch’s a significant consideration. While the pleural fluid showed a slightly elevated Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) level, the negative GeneXpert for *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* helped rule out a primary pulmonary infection. Similarly, while lung cancer, leukemia, or lymphoma can produce amylase-rich fluid, these are typically accompanied by "B-symptoms" such as significant weight loss and fever. The absence of these systemic signs, a normal peripheral blood smear, and a pleural fluid cytology that revealed no malignant cells allowed the clinical team to move away from a neoplastic diagnosis.

Other specific structural and systemic pathologies must also be ruled out. Boerhaave’s syndrome, an esophageal perforation, can result in high amylase levels in the pleural space, but it is usually associated with severe vomiting and acute epigastric distress, which were absent here. Furthermore, other causes of pleural effusion were systematically excluded through imaging and laboratory correlation: hepatic hydrothorax was ruled out by normal liver function tests and imaging; renal pathologies were excluded by normal renal scans; and an acquired diaphragmatic hernia was dismissed because imaging showed no abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity.

The diagnostic reasoning in this case relied heavily on the "pulmonary mask"—a phenomenon where the respiratory symptoms (tachypnea, chest pain, and effusion) distract from the underlying abdominal pathology. By systematically ruling out the absence of fever and neutrophilic exudates (which would suggest empyema) and recognizing the lack of typical signs for malignancy or esophageal perforation, the clinician was able to focus on the history of blunt abdominal trauma. This pivot, supported by the high amylase concentration (6118 U/L) and the subsequent MRCP findings, confirmed that the respiratory distress was a secondary manifestation of a pancreatic ductal communication rather than a primary lung disease.
