Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 2: ## Clinical Presentation and the 'Pulmonary Mask'

A significant diagnostic challenge in pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is the "pulmonary mask," a phenomenon where the primary pathology resides in the abdomen, but the clinical presentation is dominated by thoracic symptoms. Because the pancreas is located in the retroperitoneum, a rupture or ductal leak can allow pancreatic secretions to track upward through the aortic or esophageal hiatus into the pleural space. Consequently, the patient often presents with acute respiratory distress, making it easy for clinicians to focus exclusively on pulmonary pathologies. In this case, the patient's respiratory distress and recurrent pleural effusions initially led the medical team to search for a primary thoracic cause, illustrating how the "mask" can delay the identification of the underlying pancreatic source.

The clinical hallmarks of PPF often center on the characteristics of the pleural fluid and the pattern of the effusion. In this patient, the recurring nature of the right-sided pleural effusion was a key indicator. The fluid was described as "dark red," a finding that can be clinically deceptive; while it might suggest a hemothorax or a hemorrhagic malignancy, in the context of PPF, it reflects the leakage of pancreatic contents and associated blood from a ruptured duct or pseudocyst. Patients typically present with tachypnea, chest pain, and decreased breath sounds—symptoms that are non-specific and common to various conditions such as pneumonia, tuberculosis, or malignancy.

To overcome the pulmonary mask, a meticulous history is essential to identify subtle cues that point toward an abdominal origin. In this case, the diagnosis was eventually reached only after the patient revealed a history of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior. Such history-taking should always include a thorough inquiry into any abdominal injury, even if the patient's primary complaint is respiratory. Because pancreatic fluid can track through the path of least resistance into the chest, any history of pancreatitis or significant abdominal trauma should prompt an investigation of the pancreas, even in the presence of isolated thoracic symptoms.

The danger of the pulmonary mask is most evident in the potential for initial diagnostic pitfalls and unnecessary treatments. Because hemorrhagic-appearing pleural effusions are frequently associated with pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) or malignancy, clinicians may be tempted to start empirical therapy based on these common possibilities. In this report, the child was started on antitubercular drugs due to a family history of TB and mildly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels in the pleural fluid. This highlights the risk of "diagnostic anchoring," where the first recognizable symptoms (pleural effusion) dictate a narrow range of possibilities, potentially leading to the administration of unnecessary medications while the underlying fistula remains untreated.

Physical examination findings in PPF often reflect the severity of the pleural accumulation rather than the underlying pancreatic disease. In this case, the child exhibited classic signs of a large pleural effusion: stony dull percussion, significantly weakened vocal fremitus, and diminished breath sounds on the right side. Notably, however, the abdominal examination was unremarkable, with a soft abdomen and no hepatosplenomegaly. This dissociation—where severe thoracic findings coexist with a benign abdominal exam—should serve as a clinical red flag. When a patient presents with recurrent, unexplained pleural effusions and a normal abdominal exam, clinicians should consider extra-thoracic sources, including the pancreas, as the source of the leakage.
