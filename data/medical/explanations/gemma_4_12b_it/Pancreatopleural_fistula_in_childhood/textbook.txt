Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Pathophysiology and Etiology of Pancreatopleural Fistula (PPF)

A pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a rare and clinically challenging complication characterized by an abnormal communication between the pancreas and the pleural cavity. Its primary clinical significance lies in its tendency to present as a "pulmonary mask," where the patient’s predominant symptoms—such as dyspnea, chest pain, and respiratory distress—mimic common thoracic pathologies like tuberculosis, malignancy, or empyema. This often leads to a diagnostic delay, as clinicians may focus exclusively on the chest while the underlying pathology resides in the abdomen. In this case, the recurrent, dark red pleural effusions initially prompted a suspicion of pulmonary tuberculosis, demonstrating how the thoracic presentation can obscure the pancreatic origin of the disease.

The pathophysiology of PPF involves the leakage of pancreatic secretions into the pleural space, typically resulting from a rupture of the pancreatic duct or the formation and subsequent breach of a pancreatic pseudocyst. Because the pancreas is a retroperitoneal organ, the clinical manifestation depends heavily on the site of the rupture. An anterior rupture typically results in the leakage of fluid into the peritoneal cavity, leading to ascites. Conversely, a posterior rupture allows pancreatic fluid to track into the retroperitoneal space. From this location, the fluid follows the path of least resistance, migrating superiorly into the mediastinum and eventually into the pleural cavity. This migration usually occurs through natural anatomical openings, such as the aortic or esophageal hiatuses, creating a "leaky pipe" effect that leads to the massive, recurrent effusions seen in this patient.

The etiology of PPF varies significantly between adult and pediatric populations. In adults, the vast majority of cases are associated with chronic alcoholic pancreatitis. In children, however, the causes are more diverse, often involving genetic predispositions or acute physical injury. Hereditary pancreatitis in children is frequently linked to mutations in genes such as *PRSS1* (cationic trypsinogen) and *SPINK1* (serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1). While these genetic factors are significant, blunt abdominal trauma is a well-recognized non-hereditary trigger in the pediatric population. In this specific case, the patient's history of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle provided the mechanical catalyst for the pancreatic injury, leading to the ductal disruption and the subsequent formation of the pancreatopleural fistula.

## Clinical Presentation and the 'Pulmonary Mask'

A significant diagnostic challenge in pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is the "pulmonary mask," a phenomenon where the primary pathology resides in the abdomen, but the clinical presentation is dominated by thoracic symptoms. Because the pancreas is located in the retroperitoneum, a rupture or ductal leak can allow pancreatic secretions to track upward through the aortic or esophageal hiatus into the pleural space. Consequently, the patient often presents with acute respiratory distress, making it easy for clinicians to focus exclusively on pulmonary pathologies. In this case, the patient's respiratory distress and recurrent pleural effusions initially led the medical team to search for a primary thoracic cause, illustrating how the "mask" can delay the identification of the underlying pancreatic source.

The clinical hallmarks of PPF often center on the characteristics of the pleural fluid and the pattern of the effusion. In this patient, the recurring nature of the right-sided pleural effusion was a key indicator. The fluid was described as "dark red," a finding that can be clinically deceptive; while it might suggest a hemothorax or a hemorrhagic malignancy, in the context of PPF, it reflects the leakage of pancreatic contents and associated blood from a ruptured duct or pseudocyst. Patients typically present with tachypnea, chest pain, and decreased breath sounds—symptoms that are non-specific and common to various conditions such as pneumonia, tuberculosis, or malignancy.

To overcome the pulmonary mask, a meticulous history is essential to identify subtle cues that point toward an abdominal origin. In this case, the diagnosis was eventually reached only after the patient revealed a history of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior. Such history-taking should always include a thorough inquiry into any abdominal injury, even if the patient's primary complaint is respiratory. Because pancreatic fluid can track through the path of least resistance into the chest, any history of pancreatitis or significant abdominal trauma should prompt an investigation of the pancreas, even in the presence of isolated thoracic symptoms.

The danger of the pulmonary mask is most evident in the potential for initial diagnostic pitfalls and unnecessary treatments. Because hemorrhagic-appearing pleural effusions are frequently associated with pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) or malignancy, clinicians may be tempted to start empirical therapy based on these common possibilities. In this report, the child was started on antitubercular drugs due to a family history of TB and mildly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels in the pleural fluid. This highlights the risk of "diagnostic anchoring," where the first recognizable symptoms (pleural effusion) dictate a narrow range of possibilities, potentially leading to the administration of unnecessary medications while the underlying fistula remains untreated.

Physical examination findings in PPF often reflect the severity of the pleural accumulation rather than the underlying pancreatic disease. In this case, the child exhibited classic signs of a large pleural effusion: stony dull percussion, significantly weakened vocal fremitus, and diminished breath sounds on the right side. Notably, however, the abdominal examination was unremarkable, with a soft abdomen and no hepatosplenomegaly. This dissociation—where severe thoracic findings coexist with a benign abdominal exam—should serve as a clinical red flag. When a patient presents with recurrent, unexplained pleural effusions and a normal abdominal exam, clinicians should consider extra-thoracic sources, including the pancreas, as the source of the leakage.

## Diagnostic Workup and Imaging Modalities

The diagnostic journey in cases of suspected pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is often complicated by a "pulmonary mask," where the primary pathology in the abdomen produces significant thoracic symptoms. Because the patient presents with respiratory distress and pleural effusion, the initial clinical instinct is to search for a primary pulmonary cause, such as tuberculosis, malignancy, or empyema. However, a systematic approach involving biochemical analysis of the pleural fluid and multi-modal imaging is essential to shift the focus from the chest to the pancreas.

The first critical step in the diagnostic workup is the analysis of the pleural fluid. In this case, the aspiration of "dark red" fluid provided a significant clinical clue, but the biochemical markers were definitive. A pleural fluid amylase level $\geq$1000 IU/L is considered pathognomonic for PPF. In this patient, the amylase was markedly elevated at 6118 U/L, with a corresponding elevation in lipase (7211 U/L). When faced with high amylase levels, clinicians must differentiate between pancreatic and salivary amylase. While conditions like Boerhaave’s syndrome (esophageal perforation) can also result in amylase-rich pleural fluid, they typically present with salivary-type amylase. The presence of pancreatic-type amylase strongly points toward a communication with the pancreatic ductal system.

Initial imaging usually focuses on the thorax to characterize the effusion. In this report, a chest X-ray and bedside lung ultrasound were used to identify a right-sided hydropneumothorax and a large volume of pleural fluid (approximately one liter). While these modalities confirm the presence and severity of the respiratory compromise, they do not identify the etiology. To bridge the gap between a thoracic symptom and an abdominal cause, contrast-enhanced CT of the chest is the next logical step. In this case, the CT was pivotal; it revealed a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the tail of the pancreas. This "tracking" finding is a classic radiological sign of a fistula, suggesting that fluid is migrating from a retroperitoneal source into the pleural cavity.

To confirm the specific anatomy of the fistula and the status of the pancreatic duct, Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is the preferred non-invasive imaging modality. MRCP provides high-resolution visualization of the biliary and pancreatic ducts. In this case, MRCP confirmed the diagnosis by showing a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas that communicated directly with the pancreatic duct. It also identified the specific path of the fistula through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, as well as a secondary communication with the left pleural cavity.

While MRCP is excellent for diagnosis, Endoscopic Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the gold standard for both confirming the fistula and planning therapeutic intervention. Although more invasive, ERCP allows for the visualization of the ductal anatomy and the potential placement of trans-papillary stents. In pediatric cases, ERCP has shown high success rates for closing fistulas by reducing intraductal pressure. By moving systematically from pleural fluid biochemistry to advanced cross-sectional imaging, clinicians can peel back the "pulmonary mask" to identify the underlying pancreatic pathology.

## Differential Diagnosis of Amylase-Rich Pleural Effusions

When a patient presents with a pleural effusion containing high levels of amylase, the clinical focus must shift toward identifying the specific source of the leakage. While a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a definitive cause of amylase-rich fluid, it is a rare entity that can be easily confused with more common thoracic or systemic pathologies. A systematic approach to the differential diagnosis is essential to avoid unnecessary long-term treatments for the wrong underlying condition.

Infectious and malignant processes are often the primary considerations in cases of hemorrhagic or high-amylase pleural effusion. In this case, the history of contact with a patient with pulmonary tuberculosis made Koch’s a significant consideration. While the pleural fluid showed a slightly elevated Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) level, the negative GeneXpert for *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* helped rule out a primary pulmonary infection. Similarly, while lung cancer, leukemia, or lymphoma can produce amylase-rich fluid, these are typically accompanied by "B-symptoms" such as significant weight loss and fever. The absence of these systemic signs, a normal peripheral blood smear, and a pleural fluid cytology that revealed no malignant cells allowed the clinical team to move away from a neoplastic diagnosis.

Other specific structural and systemic pathologies must also be ruled out. Boerhaave’s syndrome, an esophageal perforation, can result in high amylase levels in the pleural space, but it is usually associated with severe vomiting and acute epigastric distress, which were absent here. Furthermore, other causes of pleural effusion were systematically excluded through imaging and laboratory correlation: hepatic hydrothorax was ruled out by normal liver function tests and imaging; renal pathologies were excluded by normal renal scans; and an acquired diaphragmatic hernia was dismissed because imaging showed no abdominal organs within the thoracic cavity.

The diagnostic reasoning in this case relied heavily on the "pulmonary mask"—a phenomenon where the respiratory symptoms (tachypnea, chest pain, and effusion) distract from the underlying abdominal pathology. By systematically ruling out the absence of fever and neutrophilic exudates (which would suggest empyema) and recognizing the lack of typical signs for malignancy or esophageal perforation, the clinician was able to focus on the history of blunt abdominal trauma. This pivot, supported by the high amylase concentration (6118 U/L) and the subsequent MRCP findings, confirmed that the respiratory distress was a secondary manifestation of a pancreatic ductal communication rather than a primary lung disease.

## Multimodal Management and Clinical Outcomes

The management of a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) follows a hierarchical approach, beginning with conservative measures and escalating to invasive interventions if the fistula fails to close. The primary goal of initial management is to "rest" the pancreas and reduce the volume of pancreatic secretions leaking into the pleural space. In this case, the child was placed on nil per oral (NPO) status and received total parenteral nutrition (TPN). By withholding oral intake, clinicians aim to minimize the stimulation of pancreatic exocrine secretions. This is complemented by the administration of somatostatin analogues, such as Octreotide. Octreotide functions by inhibiting the secretion of pancreatic enzymes and hormones, thereby reducing the pressure within the pancreatic ductal system and slowing the flow of fluid through the fistulous tract. To maintain the patient’s nutritional status while on NPO, Pancreatic Enzyme Replacement Therapy (PERT) is often initiated to provide necessary enzymes for digestion as the patient eventually transitions back to a diet.

Simultaneously, the patient requires active management of the pleural space. Thoracic drainage, typically via an intercostal drain (ICD) or a chest tube, is essential to evacuate the recurrent, blood-stained pleural fluid. This provides immediate symptomatic relief by improving respiratory mechanics and oxygenation. The clinical success of conservative management is monitored by observing the drainage output; a gradual decrease in the volume of fluid over several days suggests that the fistulous tract is beginning to heal or that the pancreatic secretions have sufficiently diminished. In this case, the child responded well to this multimodal conservative approach, with drainage output decreasing and eventually ceasing within 10 days, allowing for the safe removal of the ICD.

If conservative management fails to resolve the fistula within 2–3 weeks, the management hierarchy shifts toward endoscopic and surgical interventions. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is often considered the gold standard for therapeutic planning and can be used for trans-papillary stenting. The goal of stenting is to reduce intraductal pressure and divert the flow of secretions away from the fistula. While endoscopic success rates in children vary, it is often preferred over surgery due to its lower morbidity. Surgical intervention remains the final resort, reserved for cases with severe ductal obstruction, distal fistulae, or non-responsive leaks. Procedures may include a distal pancreatectomy, the Puestow procedure (pancreatojejunostomy), or cystojejunostomy. These surgeries aim to physically close the fistula or redirect the pancreatic ductal system, though they carry higher risks of complications compared to medical or endoscopic routes.

The ultimate goal of PPF management is a complete resolution of the fistula and a return to normal physiological function. Long-term follow-up is critical to monitor for the recurrence of pleural effusions and to ensure the patient is recovering well. In the reported case, the child achieved a successful outcome, remaining symptom-free and returning to full physical activity, including running and playing. Following such a significant clinical event, patients are often advised on lifestyle modifications, including a balanced diet and regular exercise, to support overall health and prevent future complications related to pancreatic health or weight management.
