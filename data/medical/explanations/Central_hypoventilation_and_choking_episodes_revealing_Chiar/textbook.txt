Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

## Clinical Presentation and Phenotype

The patient’s illness evolved over more than a decade, beginning with a minor motor-vehicle accident 14 years before presentation that was followed immediately by persistent occipital and vertex headaches. The bulbar and respiratory features developed later: she described a 7‑year history of recurrent choking episodes that emerged gradually and progressed in frequency and impact. This temporal pattern — acute onset of head pain after trauma followed by delayed emergence of bulbar and respiratory symptoms — is important because Chiari-related brainstem dysfunction may be present for years before producing prominent or recognised respiratory compromise.

Her choking episodes had several characteristic features. They occurred unpredictably during both meals and sleep, affected solids and liquids equally, and were dramatic (violent coughing and gasping) but never resulted in complete airway obstruction. Equal difficulty with solids and liquids argues against a purely structural or obstructive oesophageal lesion and instead points to a neurogenic swallowing disorder affecting the pharyngeal and brainstem swallowing centres. The events were sometimes nocturnal, awakening her from sleep with coughing or gasping, which raises concern for sleep-related impairment of airway protection and automatic respiratory control rather than only daytime behavioural or voluntary dysfunction.

A striking and diagnostically pivotal finding was recurrent, severe hypoxaemia captured by home pulse oximetry (SpO2 around 70%–80%) on multiple occasions, including a documented episode at an airport where supplemental oxygen promptly relieved symptoms. Importantly, many of these desaturation episodes were asymptomatic from the patient’s perspective: she did not experience the expected dyspnoea or air‑hunger despite profound hypoxaemia. This dissociation between profound hypoxaemia and lack of subjective breathlessness suggests disruption of afferent or central pathways that generate the conscious sensation of dyspnoea — a phenomenon that can occur with brainstem dysfunction and is a red flag for central respiratory control impairment.

Recognition of preserved voluntary breathing with impaired automatic breathing was a key clinical clue. Voluntary respiration is cortically mediated and can be intact even when brainstem automatic respiratory drives (that maintain breathing during sleep and distraction) are compromised. In this patient, wakeful voluntary breathing and daytime respiratory testing were largely normal, but breathing during sleep and during moments of distraction failed, producing central hypoventilation–type events. This clinical phenotype — intact voluntary respiratory capacity with loss of automatic drive during sleep or distraction — mirrors the presentation of acquired central hypoventilation (sometimes referred to historically as “Ondine’s curse”) and should prompt evaluation for structural brainstem or craniocervical junction pathology.

Despite the severity of her subjective and objective events, repeated evaluations by otolaryngology and gastroenterology (three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper endoscopies) were normal, and pulmonary investigations (imaging and pulmonary function tests) showed no intrinsic lung disease. These repeatedly normal ENT/GI and pulmonary studies contributed to delayed diagnosis: when standard investigations fail to identify an explanation for dysphagia and unexplained hypoxaemia, clinicians must broaden the differential to include neurological causes — particularly when bulbar symptoms coexist with sleep-related respiratory disturbance.

The headache phenotype added another distinctive dimension to her presentation. She reported constant occipital and a coin-sized vertex pain with marked cutaneous allodynia of the scalp; even light touch or the weight of hair provoked pain, and she obtained partial relief by cutting her hair short. Such focal occipital or vertex pain with scalp hypersensitivity can reflect occipital neuralgia or other cranial neuralgias and may coexist with posterior fossa pathology. While not diagnostic of Chiari malformation on its own, this pattern of focal pain and allodynia supports the idea of chronic craniocervical irritation or compression and provides an additional clinical clue that the source of symptoms may be intracranial.

The prolonged course and the social and psychological toll were prominent: fear of choking led to avoidance of restaurants and public meals, progressive social withdrawal and considerable emotional distress. This emphasises that atypical presentations with episodic, frightening respiratory events often produce significant functional impairment even when overt neurologic signs are subtle, and that timely diagnosis has important quality‑of‑life implications.

Taken together, the phenotype in this case — long‑standing occipital/vertex pain after head trauma, progressive neurogenic dysphagia affecting all consistencies, nocturnal arousals with coughing/gasping, recurrent asymptomatic profound desaturation, preserved daytime voluntary respiration, and repeatedly negative ENT/pulmonary workups — should raise suspicion for brainstem compression at the craniocervical junction. In this patient, that constellation ultimately led to re‑examination of prior imaging and to the diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I as the unifying explanation for her symptoms.

## Essential Background: Chiari I Malformation and Respiratory Neurophysiology

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is radiologically defined by downward displacement of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on midline MRI. Contemporary imaging series detect this finding in roughly 1% of adults, but only a small fraction (≈0.1%) are symptomatic. Many patients therefore fall along a spectrum from incidental anatomical variant to clinically significant “Chiari syndrome.” In symptomatic cases the clinical decision-making is guided by the concordance of symptoms with the anatomic and physiologic disturbances rather than by tonsillar descent alone.

The pathophysiological core of clinically manifest CMI is posterior fossa crowding: a relative mismatch between posterior fossa volume and neural tissue that forces tonsillar herniation through the foramen magnum. This mechanical crowding can physically compress medullary structures, distort craniovertebral cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways and produce intermittent obstruction of CSF flow. Radiologically this may appear as reduced CSF spaces at the cranio‑cervical junction or direct tonsillar impaction, as was retrospectively noted in the patient’s CT and confirmed on MRI (11 mm descent and crowded foramen magnum). Because the spectrum of posterior fossa sizes and tonsillar position varies among individuals, symptoms depend on the degree and dynamics of mechanical compromise rather than a single measurement.

Mechanically induced brainstem dysfunction explains how CMI produces respiratory and bulbar symptoms. Respiratory automaticity is generated and coordinated within ponto‑medullary networks: rhythmogenesis depends on medullary circuits that include the pre‑Bötzinger complex and adjacent parafacial/ventral respiratory groups, while afferent integration of chemosensory and mechanosensory input occurs in the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) and related dorsal medullary nuclei. Compression, stretch, ischemia or impaired CSF dynamics at the cervicomedullary junction can disrupt these centers or their interconnections, impairing the generation and modulation of automatic respiratory rhythm and the chemoreceptor‑driven ventilatory response to hypoxia and hypercapnia.

A key clinical consequence of focal brainstem involvement is dissociation between voluntary and automatic breathing. Voluntary respiration is mediated by cortical motor pathways (corticospinal and corticobulbar projections) that can drive respiratory muscles even when automatic brainstem mechanisms are dysfunctional. Thus patients with medullary compromise may retain the ability to breathe consciously, cough or speak, yet lose reliable automatic control—most critically during sleep when cortical drive is reduced. This explains why CMI can present with central sleep apnoea or periods of hypoventilation that are disproportionate to daytime respiratory findings, and why sleep studies are recommended when CMI patients have unexplained nocturnal desaturations.

The pattern observed in CMI‑related respiratory dysfunction is therefore typically one of reduced respiratory drive and disrupted rhythm generation rather than primary airway obstruction. On polysomnography this may manifest as central apnoeas, prolonged hypopneas and oxygen desaturations, often without the arousals or respiratory effort patterns that accompany obstructive events. Practical limitations of monitoring (for example, type III studies without EEG) can underestimate severity because total sleep time is not measured, but the predominance of central events and severe desaturations should prompt consideration of structural brainstem pathology, as in the present case.

Lower cranial nerve nuclei (IX, X and XII) lie in close relation to the craniocervical junction and are vulnerable to the same compressive forces. Dysfunction of these nuclei or their efferent fibers produces neurogenic dysphagia characterized by impaired coordination of pharyngeal swallow, reduced laryngeal elevation and poor clearing—manifesting clinically as choking and aspiration symptoms that affect solids and liquids equally. In contrast to obstructive or structural oesophagogastric disease, neurogenic dysphagia typically produces difficulty across consistencies and may be accompanied by silent aspiration because cough and sensory feedback can be impaired.

An additional—and clinically important—feature is attenuated perception of breathlessness. Afferent respiratory signals from carotid body chemoreceptors and vagal pulmonary receptors ascend via the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves to the NTS and then to higher centres (parabrachial nuclei, thalamus, insula and anterior cingulate). Disruption of these ascending pathways in the brainstem can blunt interoceptive signalling to cortical regions (notably the anterior insula) responsible for conscious sensation of dyspnoea, producing “silent” hypoxaemia where patients sustain profound desaturation without the subjective sensation of air hunger. This mechanism helps explain the patient’s striking episodes of very low oxygen saturation without dyspnoea.

It is useful to distinguish acquired central hypoventilation in adults from the congenital form linked to PHOX2B mutations. Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome is a genetic disorder of autonomic respiratory control presenting in infancy with lifelong dependence on ventilatory support during sleep and often associated with other autonomic dysfunction. Acquired central hypoventilation (sometimes termed “Ondine’s curse” in older literature) occurs when structural insults—such as trauma, tumours, ischaemia or extrinsic compression from CMI—selectively damage brainstem respiratory networks, producing a phenotype of impaired automatic ventilation while sparing voluntary control. The case presented fits this acquired pattern: onset after head injury, structural evidence of craniocervical crowding and predominantly central events on sleep study.

From a therapeutic and physiological standpoint, posterior fossa decompression aims to restore normal spatial relationships and CSF dynamics at the foramen magnum, relieve direct compression of medullary centres and allow reversal—or at least partial recovery—of disrupted respiratory and bulbar function. Clinical series and case reports document improvement or resolution of central sleep‑related breathing disorders and dysphagia after decompression in appropriately selected patients. Because tonsillar descent alone does not mandate surgery, the decision to decompress is individualized: it hinges on symptom severity, objective evidence of brainstem compromise (imaging and physiological testing such as polysomnography and swallow studies), and the balance of surgical risks versus expected benefit.

In summary, posterior fossa crowding in CMI can produce a constellation of findings—neurogenic dysphagia, central apnoea/hypoventilation and reduced dyspnoea perception—by compressing medullary respiratory centers, disrupting afferent interoceptive pathways and impairing automatic respiratory drive while sparing voluntary control. Recognition of this pathophysiological linkage is essential when unexplained choking, nocturnal desaturation or “silent” hypoxaemia occur, because timely imaging, sleep testing and neurosurgical consultation can alter outcomes.

## Diagnostic Workup and Reasoning Pathway

The diagnostic pathway in this case hinged on systematic re-evaluation of prior data, targeted new investigations, and stepwise exclusion of more common explanations for choking, intermittent hypoxaemia and headaches. Re‑review of the historical non‑contrast CT performed after the motor vehicle accident was the first pivotal step. Although originally read as normal, careful retrospective review identified crowding of the foramen magnum with loss of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space at the craniocervical junction. This finding raised suspicion for a craniocervical abnormality that could compress the lower brainstem and prompted definitive MRI imaging.

The contemporary MRI confirmed the clinical concern: cerebellar tonsillar descent of 11 mm below the foramen magnum, with radiological crowding at the foramen magnum and features consistent with Chiari malformation type I. By convention, tonsillar descent ≥5 mm is considered diagnostic in adults, so an 11 mm descent represents substantial downward herniation. More importantly for the clinical presentation, the MRI demonstrated reduced CSF space and anatomical crowding at the craniocervical junction—an imaging pattern that plausibly compresses medullary structures responsible for autonomic respiratory control and lower cranial nerve function. In other words, the MRI provided an anatomical substrate that could account for neurogenic dysphagia and impairment of automatic breathing.

At the same time, a broad non‑neurological evaluation was performed to exclude primary pulmonary, gastrointestinal and neuromuscular mimics. Pulmonary assessment (chest imaging and full pulmonary function tests) was normal: FEV1 and FVC were near predicted values, and respiratory muscle strength (maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures) was only mildly reduced (around 75–80% predicted), not meeting criteria for clinically significant neuromuscular respiratory failure. An arterial blood gas obtained while awake was within normal limits (PaCO2 42 mm Hg, PaO2 88 mm Hg), which is expected when measurements are taken during wakefulness. These findings argue against intrinsic lung disease or chronic global hypoventilation as the primary cause of the intermittent profound desaturations; they do not, however, exclude intermittent or sleep‑predominant central hypoventilation.

Otolaryngology and gastroenterology workup sought structural causes for choking and dysphagia. The patient had three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper endoscopies, all reported as normal. These negative studies reduce the likelihood of a focal obstructive lesion, significant laryngopharyngeal reflux with visible mucosal injury, or structural cricopharyngeal stenosis as the explanation for her choking. In the context of normal endoscopic anatomy, and with the symptom pattern of equal difficulty with solids and liquids and episodic coughing, a neurogenic swallowing disorder becomes more likely.

Laboratory screening addressed metabolic, autoimmune and neuromuscular causes that can present with bulbar dysfunction. Thyroid function, vitamin B12, inflammatory markers and routine metabolic tests were normal. Serological testing for myasthenia gravis (acetylcholine receptor and anti‑MuSK antibodies) was negative, and the clinical history lacked fatigability or diurnal fluctuation typical of bulbar myasthenia. Together these findings make myasthenia gravis and common metabolic mimics unlikely.

Sleep‑related testing provided physiologic correlation between the anatomy and the patient’s intermittent hypoxaemia. A type III sleep study recorded an overall apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) of approximately 6 events/hour, but importantly 72% of events were classified as central. The device also documented repeated oxygen desaturations, with some values falling to 70%–80% — concordant with the home oximetry and the airport episode. Interpretation of these results must acknowledge major methodological limitations: type III studies do not include EEG, so total recording time rather than true sleep time is used to calculate the AHI, which commonly underestimates event rates; they also typically lack reliable CO2 monitoring. Thus, while the recorded low AHI might suggest mild sleep-disordered breathing, the high proportion of central events and the severe desaturations point toward physiologically significant central respiratory instability—likely underestimated by the study. For this reason, a full type I polysomnogram has been planned; that study will include EEG to determine true sleep time, end‑tidal or transcutaneous CO2 monitoring to detect hypoventilation and hypercapnia during sleep, and synchronized video to correlate respiratory events with behaviour or arousals. These elements are essential to confirm central hypoventilation and to inform management decisions.

Putting the pieces together, the differential diagnoses were systematically excluded: reflux or structural upper airway pathology (normal endoscopies and lack of response to acid suppression), myasthenia gravis and other neuromuscular disorders (negative serologies and lack of characteristic clinical features), and primary pulmonary disease (normal imaging and PFTs). The combination of (1) consistent radiological evidence of significant tonsillar descent with foramen magnum crowding, (2) episodic profound oxygen desaturations without subjective dyspnoea, and (3) predominant central events on sleep testing despite methodological underestimation, supports a unifying diagnosis of brainstem dysfunction from Chiari I malformation. This pathophysiology explains both the neurogenic dysphagia (lower cranial nerve or nucleus involvement) and selective loss of automatic respiratory drive with preserved voluntary breathing (cortical pathways intact).

Finally, the temporal association with prior head trauma is noted but not definitive. The historical CT already demonstrated craniocervical crowding soon after the accident, so it is impossible to prove whether the Chiari was congenital and unmasked by trauma, or whether trauma contributed to acquired tonsillar herniation or progression. Clinically, the important point is that the anatomical abnormality is present and correlates with the patient’s unexplained respiratory and bulbar symptoms. Remaining uncertainties—chiefly confirmation of sleep‑predominant hypoventilation and documentation of CO2 retention—are being addressed with the planned type I polysomnogram. The case underscores two practical lessons for clinicians: re‑review old imaging when the presentation is unexplained, and pursue full sleep physiologic testing (including EEG and CO2 monitoring) when central hypoventilation from structural brainstem disease is suspected.

## Management Strategy: From Supportive Care to Decompression

Management of Chiari I malformation (CMI) with brainstem compression and bulbar or respiratory manifestations requires a blended strategy: urgent recognition and mitigation of immediate risks, thoughtful bridging therapies to reduce harm and preserve function, and timely surgical consideration when the anatomy and symptoms indicate reversible brainstem compromise. In this case the patient faced recurrent nocturnal choking, profound intermittent desaturations without dyspnoea, and MRI-proven 11 mm tonsillar descent with evidence of cranio‑cervical crowding. Her care therefore exemplifies the full spectrum of management choices—from conservative observation to posterior fossa decompression—and the need to individualise timing based on symptom severity, physiologic impairment, and patient preferences.

Indications for posterior fossa decompression
Surgery is indicated when CMI produces progressive or function‑threatening neurological signs attributable to brainstem or lower cranial nerve compression, or when sleep‑disordered breathing (SDB) and central apnoeas are thought to result from structural brainstem compromise. Clinical red flags that favour decompression include recurrent aspiration or clinically significant dysphagia, persistent or worsening central hypoventilation with marked desaturation events, new or progressive lower cranial nerve palsies, syncope related to respiratory pauses, or the development/progression of syringomyelia. In the reported patient, the combination of recurrent severe desaturation to the 70%–80% range, bulbar symptoms with choking and aspiration risk, and clear radiological brainstem compression are all findings that typically trigger discussion of surgical decompression rather than continued conservative care alone.

Expected benefits of surgery
Posterior fossa decompression (suboccipital craniectomy with C1 laminectomy, with or without duraplasty) aims to increase posterior fossa volume, restore cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow at the foramen magnum and relieve direct tonsillar impingement on the medulla and lower cranial nerve nuclei. Clinically, expected benefits include improvement or resolution of Valsalva‑related occipital headaches, partial or complete reversal of bulbar dysfunction (improved swallowing, reduced choking/aspiration), and reduction in central sleep apnoea and hypoventilation in many patients. Recovery of respiratory automaticity has been reported, particularly when intervention is performed before irreversible brainstem injury or long‑standing atrophy of affected nuclei. However, outcomes are variable: improvement is more likely when symptoms are attributable to reversible compressive physiology rather than chronic neuronal loss, and some patients have persistent SDB despite anatomically successful decompression.

Surgical risks and counselling
Patients must be counselled about both the potential benefits and the real risks of posterior fossa surgery. Common complications include CSF leak, wound infection, postoperative headache, and transient or permanent cranial nerve deficits. Less frequent but serious risks are hemorrhage, cerebellar or brainstem injury, aseptic or bacterial meningitis, and the need for reoperation if decompression is inadequate or scar tissue forms. The choice to perform duraplasty (opening the dura and enlarging with a graft) increases the likelihood of improving CSF flow but also raises the risk of CSF leak compared with bone‑only decompression. Shared decision‑making should address the patient’s symptom burden, the likelihood of reversibility (based on symptom duration and objective testing), and personal values—balanced against perioperative morbidity and recovery time. In the reported case the team presented these options and risks; the patient initially elected conservative management while weighing surgery.

Conservative measures and symptomatic management
Observation with active conservative management is reasonable when symptoms are stable, physiologic impairment is limited, or patients need time to decide about surgery. Conservative care in this setting focuses on risk reduction and symptom control. Headache strategies include avoidance of Valsalva triggers where possible, sleep and posture hygiene, targeted analgesia (acetaminophen, judicious short courses of NSAIDs), and neuropathic agents when occipital neuralgia is suspected. Local therapies such as greater occipital nerve blocks may be considered for refractory focal scalp pain, recognising variable benefit. In patients who prefer to defer surgery—as in this case—a structured monitoring plan should be established (see Monitoring below).

Dysphagia management
Neurogenic dysphagia from lower cranial nerve dysfunction requires early involvement of speech‑language pathology (SLP). Management priorities are aspiration prevention and maintenance of nutrition. A bedside and instrumented swallowing assessment (videofluoroscopic or fibreoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing) guides individualized recommendations for texture modification, compensatory postures and targeted swallowing exercises. Thickened liquids or altered food textures may reduce aspiration risk at the expense of quality of life and should be prescribed only after SLP evaluation. For patients at risk of aspiration pneumonia, vaccination against influenza and pneumococcus is recommended. In more severe or progressive dysphagia with recurrent aspiration or weight loss, enteral feeding options (e.g., percutaneous gastrostomy) and reconsideration of decompression surgery become important.

Bridging strategies for sleep‑disordered breathing and central hypoventilation
When central apnoeas or hypoventilation are suspected, prompt referral to sleep medicine is essential. A type I polysomnogram with EEG, end‑tidal or transcutaneous CO₂ monitoring and video is the gold standard to quantify sleep time, identify central versus obstructive events, and detect nocturnal hypoventilation. While awaiting definitive testing or surgical treatment, non‑invasive ventilatory support can be lifesaving. For central events and hypoventilation, bilevel positive airway pressure in spontaneous/timed (S/T) mode or modes that provide a mandatory backup rate are frequently used because CPAP addresses obstruction but often does not correct central hypoventilation. Adaptive servo‑ventilation (ASV) can stabilise ventilation in certain patterns of central sleep apnoea but must be used with caution and individualised per cardiopulmonary comorbidity; consultation with sleep specialists is required. Trialing home nocturnal non‑invasive ventilation with appropriate settings and monitoring (including capnography where possible) is a reasonable interim measure in symptomatic patients with recurrent desaturations, as long as there is close clinical follow‑up.

Oxygen supplementation: risks and role
Supplemental oxygen can correct hypoxaemia acutely and was diagnostically useful in this patient’s airport episode, but it does not correct hypoventilation and can obscure detection of ventilatory failure by improving saturation while allowing CO₂ retention to progress. In patients with hypoventilation, oxygen should be used cautiously and only in conjunction with measures to support ventilation (e.g., NIV) and with monitoring of CO₂ when possible. Long‑term domiciliary oxygen without ventilatory support is generally not recommended for primary central hypoventilation.

Monitoring plan and triggers for escalation
A proactive, measurable monitoring plan is essential for patients who opt for conservative management. In this case a practical plan included monthly clinical assessments focused on bulbar function and alertness, quarterly overnight oximetry to screen for worsening nocturnal desaturation, and annual MRI to surveil tonsillar position and syrinx development. Full polysomnography with CO₂ monitoring was arranged when feasible. Clear escalation triggers should be defined and communicated: new or progressive lower cranial nerve deficits, increasing frequency or depth of desaturations (particularly SpO₂ <85% or repeated drops into the 70s), recurrent aspiration or pneumonia, syncope related to apnoea, daytime somnolence or cognitive decline, or the development/enlargement of a syrinx on imaging. Any of these should prompt urgent reconsideration of surgical decompression.

Lifestyle, safety and patient education
Counselling on lifestyle measures can reduce risk. Patients should be advised to avoid sedatives, opioids and excessive alcohol, all of which depress respiratory drive and increase aspiration risk. Education in the use and interpretation of a home pulse oximeter can empower early detection of hypoxaemia, but users must be cautioned about false reassurance from isolated normal readings and the potential for anxiety from false alarms. Travel planning is important: patients with documented desaturation should receive pretravel medical assessment, carry documentation of their condition, and consider arrangements for supplemental oxygen on flights or avoidance of air travel until their condition stabilises. Meal safety strategies—eating with supervision if severe choking is a risk, pacing, and consistent swallowing precautions—are practical steps to reduce immediate harm.

Criteria to reconsider and proceed with surgery
Surgical reconsideration is warranted when there is documented progression of central respiratory events (objectively increasing central apnoea index or nocturnal hypoventilation), functional decline attributable to brainstem compression (worsening dysphagia, aspiration, encephalopathy), or development/progression of syringomyelia. Earlier surgery may be favoured when objective testing suggests reversible physiologic compromise (for example, central apnoea that improves with temporary ventilatory support) and when the patient’s goals prioritise definitive treatment over ongoing conservative care.

Putting it together for this patient
For the woman in this report, the treating team appropriately presented posterior fossa decompression as the definitive option because of objective brainstem compression and severe intermittent desaturation combined with bulbar symptoms. Because she elected an initial period of conservative management, the team instituted a clear monitoring schedule (monthly clinical reviews, quarterly oximetry and annual MRI), arranged definitive polysomnography with CO₂ monitoring, and advised lifestyle and dysphagia precautions while planning sleep‑medicine input to consider a trial of nocturnal non‑invasive ventilation if central events were confirmed. This staged approach balances respect for patient autonomy with active risk mitigation and a low threshold for escalation should her physiology or function deteriorate—an approach that is consistent with contemporary, evidence‑informed practice for symptomatic CMI with respiratory and bulbar involvement.

## Outcome, Follow‑up, and Clinical Lessons

The patient ultimately elected conservative management while she considered neurosurgical decompression. Her short‑term plan, agreed with the multidisciplinary team, combined active surveillance and targeted investigations: monthly clinic reviews to document symptom frequency and new neurological signs, quarterly overnight pulse oximetry to capture intermittent desaturations, and annual MRI of the brain and cervical spine to monitor tonsillar position, CSF flow and to screen for syringomyelia. A definitive type I polysomnography (PSG) incorporating EEG, video and objective CO₂ monitoring (end‑tidal or transcutaneous) was arranged at an external sleep centre but remained pending at the time of this report. These steps reflect a pragmatic compromise when a patient defers surgery while still addressing the highest short‑risk concerns (unrecognised, severe nocturnal hypoxaemia and progressive bulbar symptoms).

Over eight months of structured follow‑up the team sought objective triggers that would prompt expedited intervention: increasing frequency or severity of choking episodes, recurrent daytime or nocturnal desaturations to dangerously low saturations, development of new brainstem or long‑tract neurological deficits, or radiographic progression such as syrinx formation. The rationale for close monitoring is twofold. First, CMI with brainstem compression can produce intermittent, potentially life‑threatening central hypoventilation and “silent” hypoxaemia (marked desaturations without dyspnoea), so objective surveillance is essential. Second, posterior fossa decompression can improve or reverse respiratory and bulbar dysfunction when brainstem compression is the mechanism; therefore identifying deterioration early preserves the option of timely surgical intervention.

Several high‑yield clinical lessons emerge from this case that are directly applicable to practice. The most important diagnostic pearl is that persistent choking or dysphagia affecting both solids and liquids, particularly when repeated endoscopic evaluations are normal, should prompt neurological assessment and dedicated cranio‑cervical imaging (MRI of the brain and cervical spine with attention to the foramen magnum and CSF spaces). Neurogenic dysphagia classically causes mixed‑consistency impairment and may be missed by repeated gastrointestinal-focused testing. In this patient the definitive clue was imaging evidence of cerebellar tonsillar descent with crowding at the foramen magnum, which explained the dysphagia through lower cranial nerve and brainstem dysfunction.

Sleep testing nuances are critical when central hypoventilation is suspected. Type III cardiorespiratory studies (home or portable monitors that record airflow, effort and oximetry but lack EEG) can underestimate the severity of sleep‑disordered breathing because they measure events over total recording time rather than true sleep time and lack CO₂ monitoring. When clinical concern exists for central apnoeas or hypoventilation—especially with unexplained nocturnal desaturation, preserved voluntary breathing, or lack of dyspnoea—full in‑lab type I PSG with EEG, video and end‑tidal or transcutaneous CO₂ monitoring should be prioritized. CO₂ measurements are important for detecting hypoventilation that may not produce frequent apnoeas but leads to sustained hypercapnia and oxygen desaturation.

This case also illustrates the value of imaging vigilance. Re‑review of a historically reported “normal” CT revealed cranio‑cervical crowding that had been overlooked initially; this single finding redirected the diagnostic trajectory. Whenever symptoms are unexplained or discordant with prior test results, clinicians should retrieve and re‑examine prior imaging (ideally with neuroradiology input) rather than assuming absence of pathology. In the craniocervical junction, non‑contrast CTs can show crowding but MRI with sagittal T1/T2 and CSF flow sequences provides definitive assessment for Chiari malformation and associated syrinx.

Clinically, avoid diagnostic overshadowing that attributes objective hypoxaemia or recurrent choking solely to anxiety without completing appropriate investigations. Anxiety may co‑exist with organic disease and can amplify symptoms, but profound hypoxaemia (saturations in the 70s–80s) documented objectively merits thorough cardiopulmonary and neurological evaluation. In this patient, repeated emergency visits had initially dismissed desaturation episodes as anxiety‑related despite objective low saturations; later neurosurgical and sleep assessments reframed these events as manifestations of structural brainstem compromise.

Effective care requires interprofessional coordination. Family medicine or primary care often anchors the diagnostic process and coordinates referrals to neurology and neurosurgery for structural evaluation, sleep medicine for definitive PSG and CO₂ monitoring, otolaryngology and gastroenterology to exclude local aerodigestive pathology, and speech‑language pathology for formal swallowing evaluation and rehabilitative strategies. Pulmonology should be involved when significant nocturnal hypoventilation is identified to consider nocturnal ventilatory support if indicated pending or instead of surgery. Early involvement of neuroradiology improves the chance that subtle cranio‑cervical abnormalities are recognised.

Prognostically, symptomatic CMI with demonstrable brainstem compression behaves heterogeneously. Some patients experience marked symptomatic improvement after posterior fossa decompression, including resolution of sleep‑disordered breathing and dysphagia, reflecting reversible compression of respiratory and lower cranial nerve centres. Others have incomplete recovery, especially if damage has progressed or a syrinx is present. Conversely, conservative management requires vigilant surveillance because symptoms can progress insidiously. Therefore, decisions about surgery should balance current symptom burden, objective physiological impairment, imaging severity, surgical risk and patient preferences.

For practical clinical use, a stepwise approach reduces diagnostic delay in patients with unexplained desaturation or neurogenic dysphagia. Start with a focused history (characterise choking episodes, sleep‑related symptoms, preserved voluntary breathing versus automatic failure, and precipitating events), targeted physical and neurological examination, pulse oximetry or overnight oximetry to document events, basic pulmonary evaluation (chest imaging, spirometry), and directed ENT/GI assessment including a modified barium swallow when dysphagia is present. If initial tests are unrevealing but symptoms persist, obtain cranio‑cervical MRI with CSF flow sequences and arrange full in‑lab PSG with CO₂ monitoring if nocturnal hypoxaemia or central events are suspected. If MRI shows CMI with brainstem crowding or syrinx, involve neurosurgery to discuss decompression versus continued surveillance, using objective desaturation trends and PSG/CO₂ data to guide urgency.

In summary, this case highlights that Chiari I malformation can present primarily with choking, dysphagia and acquired central hypoventilation that may be subtle or “silent.” Careful re‑examination of prior imaging, use of appropriate sleep studies including CO₂ monitoring, avoidance of premature psychogenic labeling, and timely multidisciplinary coordination are the key practices that reduce diagnostic delay and optimise outcomes.
