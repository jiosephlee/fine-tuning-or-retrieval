Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Approach

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is classically framed by radiographic findings, but it is the symptom cluster—the “Chiari syndrome”—that drives clinical recognition. The case above illustrates a common and important theme: patients may present for years with apparently isolated ENT or gastrointestinal complaints before the unifying diagnosis is recognised. In this patient, recurrent choking, nocturnal gasping and profound oxygen desaturations were evaluated repeatedly with laryngoscopy and endoscopy and attributed to reflux or anxiety. It was only when the history was integrated with chronic occipital/vertex headache and a remote head injury that a neurological cause was suspected. This clinical pathway underlines two lessons that should guide initial evaluation: (1) equal difficulty with solids and liquids, nocturnal events, and disproportionate hypoxaemia without dyspnoea are red flags for a neurogenic process; and (2) a directed neurological review and reappraisal of prior imaging are often pivotal.

History-taking should therefore probe the pattern and timing of choking, swallowing and breathing complaints. Neurogenic dysphagia tends to affect all consistencies without a clear bolus-related obstruction; patients describe coughing, choking or nasal regurgitation with both solids and liquids, intermittent episodes, and sometimes silent aspiration. Ask specifically about nocturnal symptoms, witnessed apnoeas, daytime somnolence, or episodes of marked oxygen desaturation documented by home oximetry. Enquire about head or neck trauma, even if minor or remote, because CMI may be congenital, unmasked, or exacerbated after an injury. Document headache phenotype carefully: occipital or suboccipital pain, particularly if paroxysmal or related to Valsalva manoeuvres, should raise suspicion for craniocervical pathology; scalp allodynia or a localized “coin-sized” vertex pain—as in this patient—can coexist and point toward occipital nerve irritation or brainstem involvement. Finally, assess psychosocial consequences (avoidance of meals, anxiety, functional impairment) and safety risks such as driving, flying or exposure to sedatives, which may be dangerous in patients with impaired automatic respiration.

The targeted neurological review of systems and bedside examination are critical next steps. Screen for lower cranial nerve dysfunction: ask about dysphonia, nasal regurgitation, change in voice quality, choking on liquids, impaired taste in the posterior tongue, and coughing during swallowing. On exam, assess palate elevation and symmetry with phonation, observe the uvula for deviation, evaluate gag reflex (noting that its absence is nonspecific), inspect the tongue for atrophy or fasciculations and test tongue protrusion and lateralisation, and listen for breathy or nasal speech. Evaluate cough strength and voluntary sniff or breath-hold as crude tests of bulbar and respiratory muscle function. Look for cerebellar signs (limb dysmetria, truncal ataxia, gait impairment) and long-tract findings that might indicate syringomyelia or upper cervical cord involvement (dissociated sensory loss, hand weakness or atrophy). The combination of bulbar signs with headache or ataxia should prompt urgent neuroimaging.

Bedside physiological testing complements the history and exam. Pulse oximetry or continuous overnight oximetry can document intermittent desaturation episodes; portable capnography or end-tidal CO₂ monitoring is useful where available, because hypoventilation is more reliably captured by hypercapnia than by oxygen saturation alone. Basic pulmonary function testing—including spirometry and measurement of maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures (MIP/MEP)—helps exclude primary pulmonary or ventilatory muscle disease and provides baseline data should respiratory support become necessary. An arterial blood gas can detect daytime hypoventilation. Importantly, normal chest imaging and normal spirometry do not exclude central respiratory control disorders.

Interpretation of swallowing and airway examinations needs a neurological lens. Flexible laryngoscopy and upper gastrointestinal endoscopy often return normal results in patients with neurogenic dysphagia because the problem resides in coordination and central control rather than fixed anatomic obstruction. Dynamic studies—videofluoroscopic swallowing study (modified barium swallow) and fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES)—are more sensitive to neurogenic aspiration and dysfunction of the pharyngeal phase. When dysphagia is reported for both solids and liquids, or when aspiration is suspected without structural lesions, prioritise these dynamic studies and involve speech and language pathology early.

Nocturnal respiratory events merit early sleep medicine involvement. Polysomnography (type I attended study) with EEG, respiratory airflow and effort channels, pulse oximetry and capnography is the gold standard because it defines sleep time, differentiates central from obstructive events and quantifies hypoventilation. Be cautious interpreting type III studies (portable monitoring without EEG): absence of EEG typically underestimates event rates by using total recording time rather than true sleep time, and can obscure the severity of central hypoventilation. A predominance of central apnoeas, especially when accompanied by large oxygen desaturations without subjective dyspnoea, strongly suggests brainstem dysfunction rather than primary obstructive sleep apnoea.

Reexamination of prior neuroimaging and obtaining dedicated MRI of the posterior fossa and cervicomedullary junction are essential. CT scans performed for trauma are often read as “normal” unless the craniocervical junction is specifically assessed; subtle crowding at the foramen magnum can be overlooked. Sagittal MRI sequences allow measurement of tonsillar descent (commonly ≥5 mm below the foramen magnum for CMI) and assessment of CSF spaces, brainstem compression, and syringomyelia. Cine CSF flow studies can document CSF flow obstruction and help surgical planning. Because symptoms may be delayed after trauma, reviewing historical scans in light of evolving symptoms can be diagnostic, as in this patient.

A succinct differential diagnosis keeps the clinician from premature closure. Structural laryngeal or oesophageal lesions, gastroesophageal reflux with laryngopharyngeal reflux, and primary otolaryngologic disorders must be considered and excluded. Neuromuscular junction disease (eg, myasthenia gravis) or motor neuron disease can produce bulbar dysfunction but often demonstrate fatigability, diurnal variation, or EMG/antibody abnormalities. Primary pulmonary disease and restrictive ventilatory disorders must be excluded with imaging and pulmonary function testing. When choking, nocturnal apnoeas, occipital headaches and signs of lower cranial nerve or cerebellar dysfunction coexist, a central cause such as CMI or other posterior fossa mass becomes more likely.

Finally, the initial approach should incorporate safety planning and multidisciplinary referral. Patients with documented profound desaturations or suspected central hypoventilation require precautions: avoid sedatives, plan for supervised sleep studies, counsel about aviation and occupational risks, and consider early neurology/neurosurgery and sleep medicine consultation. Speech therapy and diet modification can mitigate aspiration risk while diagnostic evaluation proceeds. Early recognition of the pattern described here—equal-consistency dysphagia, nocturnal central events with severe desaturation without air hunger, concurrent occipital headache and a history of trauma—shortens the diagnostic delay and enables timely imaging and specialist referral that may be disease-modifying.

## Pathophysiology and Epidemiology of Chiari I and Respiratory Control

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is best thought of as an anatomic–clinical spectrum rather than a single, uniformly pathological lesion. By convention CMI is defined radiologically as descent of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on midline sagittal MRI, but modern series make clear that this criterion alone does not distinguish symptomatic disease from an incidental anatomic variant. Cross‑sectional imaging studies identify caudal tonsillar ectopia in roughly 1% of adults, while the proportion of the population with clinicopathologic Chiari syndrome — headaches, brainstem or cerebellar signs, syringomyelia or sleep‑disordered breathing attributable to posterior fossa crowding — is much smaller (estimates ~0.1%). There is a modest female predominance (≈1.3:1) and most symptomatic adults present in the third or fourth decade, although presentation may be earlier or later depending on coexistent factors such as syrinx formation or acquired decompensation.

At the root of symptomatic CMI is a volumetric mismatch between posterior fossa contents and the bony container. Developmental hypoplasia of the occipital bone and posterior cranial fossa produces reduced posterior fossa volume so that the cerebellum and brainstem are relatively crowded and the tonsils are displaced downward through the foramen magnum. This “crowding” has two important mechanical consequences. First, direct compression of the dorsal medulla and lower cranial nerve nuclei can produce focal neurologic dysfunction. Second, narrowing of the craniocervical CSF pathways produces disturbed CSF flow dynamics across the foramen magnum; chronic flow obstruction is a major factor in the formation of syringomyelia and may itself exert intermittent mass effect on adjacent medullary structures. Importantly, posterior fossa crowding may be congenital (primary CMI) or develop or become symptomatic after injury or intracranial pressure changes (secondary or acquired CMI); traumatic events can unmask an anatomic predisposition by altering tissue position or shifting CSF dynamics.

Understanding how CMI produces respiratory and bulbar symptoms requires a brief review of respiratory control anatomy and physiology. Breathing has two separable control systems: automatic respiration, which maintains ventilation unconsciously and continuously, and voluntary respiration, which is driven by cortical motor pathways under conscious control. Automatic breathing arises from interconnected brainstem networks centred in the medulla and pons. Key elements include the pre‑Bötzinger complex (a pacemaker region within the ventrolateral medulla generating rhythmic inspiratory drive), the dorsal respiratory group (primarily integrating afferent inputs in the nucleus tractus solitarius, NTS) and the ventral respiratory group (containing expiratory and premotor neurons). Chemoreceptive drive arises centrally from medullary chemoreceptors responsive to pCO2/pH and peripherally from the carotid and aortic bodies; these signals ascend and are integrated in the brainstem respiratory network to adjust ventilation moment to moment. Voluntary control of breathing is mediated by descending corticospinal and corticobulbar pathways that can override automatic rhythms (for speech, voluntary breath holds, or conscious changes in ventilation). Sensation of breathlessness (dyspnea) depends on intact afferent signalling from peripheral receptors and brainstem relays to cortical interoceptive regions such as the insula and anterior cingulate.

In CMI the structures most vulnerable are those immediately adjacent to the foramen magnum: dorsal medullary respiratory centres, the NTS and associated ascending and descending pathways, and the lower cranial nerve nuclei (IX–XII). Direct compression or distortion of these regions can impair automatic respiratory drive while sparing cortical pathways that permit voluntary breathing — an explanation for the classic clinical pattern in which patients retain normal voluntary respiration but develop severe hypoventilation during sleep or when distracted. This selective failure of automatic control produces central apnoeas and hypoventilation that may be most apparent at night (when voluntary cortical input normally diminishes) or during periods when attention is diverted, as illustrated by the airport episode in the present case where profound desaturation occurred without subjective dyspnea.

Lower cranial nerve involvement explains the frequent bulbar manifestations of CMI. Compression or traction injury of nucleus ambiguus (motor neurons for IX and X), the hypoglossal nucleus (XII), and their axons can produce palatal and pharyngeal weakness, impaired cough and swallowing reflexes, tongue weakness or atrophy, and loss of protective laryngeal closure — all of which manifest clinically as choking, aspiration and neurogenic dysphagia. Because neurogenic dysphagia often affects all consistencies equally, endoscopic upper‑aerodigestive tract examinations can be normal between events, and diagnosis may be delayed unless a neurogenic cause is considered. Coexistent syringomyelia in the cervicothoracic cord can amplify motor and sensory deficits, further worsening cough, airway protection and respiratory mechanics.

The phenomenon of “silent hypoxemia” or blunted dyspnea in CMI deserves specific emphasis. Dyspnea is not a simple function of arterial oxygen tension alone; it requires intact afferent signalling from peripheral chemoreceptors, mechanoreceptors and vagal/glossopharyngeal afferents, and effective transmission of that information through brainstem relays to cortical interoceptive centres. Compression or dysfunction of these ascending pathways at the cranio‑cervical junction can disrupt the interoceptive signal, so that patients do not mount the appropriate sensation of air hunger despite marked hypoxaemia or hypercapnia. This explains why some patients present with dramatic desaturation or even life‑threatening hypoxaemia without the expected subjective distress or dyspneic behaviour, and why careful physiologic monitoring (pulse oximetry, capnography, formal polysomnography with CO2 monitoring) is essential rather than reliance on symptoms alone.

Epidemiologically and clinically, these pathophysiologic principles produce important practical lessons. First, radiographic tonsillar descent does not mandate intervention; the decision to operate must rest on clinicoradiologic correlation — the presence of objective signs (syrinx, progressive neurologic deficit, documented central apnoea/hypoventilation, or intractable Valsalva‑related occipital headaches) increases the likelihood that posterior fossa decompression will be beneficial. Conversely, many individuals with mild tonsillar ectopia are asymptomatic and can be observed. Second, a history of trauma or intracranial pressure perturbation should raise suspicion that a previously silent anatomic predisposition has become symptomatic; retrospective review of prior imaging can be revealing, as in this patient’s CT showing a crowded foramen magnum years before formal diagnosis. Third, because automatic breathing is most vulnerable, polysomnography with full EEG, respiratory effort channels and CO2 monitoring is the preferred test to unmask central hypoventilation; type III devices that lack EEG tend to underestimate event rates by including wake time in the denominator.

Finally, from a therapeutic perspective, the pathophysiology is encouraging: surgical posterior fossa decompression addresses the two principal mechanisms — mechanical compression of medullary centres and CSF flow obstruction — and can result in marked symptomatic improvement or resolution of central apnoea and bulbar dysfunction in appropriately selected patients. The heterogeneity of anatomic findings and clinical courses, however, mandates individualized decision‑making and multidisciplinary care, integrating neurosurgery, sleep medicine, otolaryngology and rehabilitation to balance the risks and expected benefits for each patient.

## Differential Diagnosis of Choking and Central Hypoventilation

When a patient presents with episodic choking and unexplained hypoxaemia or central-predominant apnoeas, developing a structured differential diagnosis is essential because the causes span otolaryngologic and gastro-oesophageal disorders, primary pulmonary and cardiac disease, neuromuscular disorders, primary sleep disorders and structural neurologic lesions. The clinical approach should use targeted history and examination to direct first-line testing, while keeping a low threshold for neuroimaging when bulbar features or central sleep-disordered breathing predominate. The case above—recurrent choking, nocturnal profound desaturations with little dyspnoea and long-standing occipital headache—illustrates how neurologic causes can masquerade as common ENT or pulmonary conditions and why a systematic search for brainstem or craniocervical pathology is necessary.

ENT and upper-GI causes
Problems local to the larynx, pharynx and upper oesophagus commonly produce choking, cough and dysphagia. Laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR) can cause intermittent throat clearing, globus and aspiration-like coughing; diagnosis is aided by response to acid suppression, flexible laryngoscopy findings of laryngeal inflammation, and, when uncertain, 24‑hour pH or impedance testing. Structural lesions—vocal fold paralysis, subglottic or pharyngeal masses, Zenker diverticulum—are identified on laryngoscopy, direct visualization or endoscopy. Vocal cord dysfunction (paradoxical vocal fold motion) classically produces inspiratory stridor, throat tightness and episodic dyspnoea; flexible laryngoscopy during an event shows adduction of the cords on inspiration, and spirometry/flow–volume loops may show extrathoracic obstruction. Cricopharyngeal dysfunction or achalasia of the upper oesophageal sphincter gives food regurgitation and dysphagia to both solids and liquids; videofluoroscopic swallow and high‑resolution manometry are diagnostic and can guide dilatation or myotomy. In practice, normal laryngoscopy/endoscopy and failure to respond to appropriate empiric therapy (for example, maximal anti‑reflux therapy) argue against an ENT/GI primary cause and should prompt consideration of non‑structural or neurologic explanations.

Neuromuscular junction and motor neuron disorders
Bulbar complaints with choking and dysphagia must trigger evaluation for disorders that impair neuromuscular transmission or cranial nerve motor nuclei. Myasthenia gravis (MG) often produces fluctuating ocular and bulbar symptoms, fatigability, diurnal variation and may show improvement with an “ice pack” test for ptosis. Laboratory testing includes acetylcholine receptor and anti‑MuSK antibodies, and electrodiagnostic confirmation uses repetitive nerve stimulation and single‑fiber EMG. Treatment response to pyridostigmine and immunotherapy further supports the diagnosis. Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) may present with progressive bulbar dysfunction; the presence of mixed upper and lower motor neuron signs (spasticity, brisk reflexes plus fasciculations and wasting), progressive course and characteristic electromyographic findings support ALS. The absence of fatigability, normal antibody testing and a normal EMG make MG unlikely; lack of UMN/LMN features and normal electrodiagnostics make ALS unlikely. These tests are high yield when bedside exam is equivocal.

Primary pulmonary and cardiac causes
Many intrinsic respiratory and cardiovascular diseases can produce exertional or nocturnal hypoxaemia and coughing that could be mistaken for choking. Asthma, COPD and interstitial lung disease are suggested by history (wheezing, chronic cough, exertional dyspnoea), abnormalities on chest radiograph/CT and spirometry (obstructive or restrictive patterns, reduced DLCO). Pulmonary embolism or intermittent cardiac failure (with Cheyne–Stokes respiration) can produce exertional desaturation and nocturnal hypoventilation; diagnostic steps include CT pulmonary angiography, ventilation–perfusion scanning, echocardiography and BNP. Importantly, these disorders generally produce dyspnoea or objective abnormal pulmonary tests; normal chest imaging and normal spirometry as in the case here argue against a primary pulmonary or cardiac etiology for profound night‑time desaturations.

Sleep‑disordered breathing: obstructive versus central processes
Polysomnography is the cornerstone to distinguish obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) from central sleep apnoea (CSA) and other patterns such as Cheyne–Stokes respiration or opioid‑related hypoventilation. Obstructive events are characterized by continued respiratory effort with airflow cessation; central events show concurrent loss of airflow and respiratory effort. Cheyne–Stokes respiration (a periodic, crescendo–decrescendo pattern of tidal volume with central apnoeas) is classically associated with congestive heart failure and prolonged circulation time. Opioids and sedatives can depress the central respiratory drive and produce central apnoeas and hypoventilation; a careful medication history is therefore essential. Full (type I) polysomnography with EEG, respiratory inductance plethysmography and capnography provides the most accurate assessment—especially critical when central events predominate. Type III devices (no EEG) may substantially underestimate event indices because total recording time is used instead of true sleep time, a limitation demonstrated in the presented case.

Structural neurologic disease causing central hypoventilation or bulbar dysfunction
When choking coexists with central apnoeas, brainstem or craniocervical structural lesions must be high on the differential. Pathologies include Chiari malformation (tonsillar herniation compressing the medulla), intrinsic brainstem tumours, dorsal medullary infarction, demyelinating lesions (multiple sclerosis or neuromyelitis optica), syringobulbia and inflammatory or infectious processes. Neuroimaging—MRI of the brain with attention to the posterior fossa and cervical spinal cord—is essential in patients with unexplained bulbar dysfunction, absent ENT findings, or central‑predominant sleep disordered breathing. Physical signs that point to a structural cause include lower cranial nerve palsies (dysphonia, dysarthria, palate weakness), ataxia, sensory dissociation in a cape distribution (syrinx), or a history of trauma/unexplained headache. In the case discussed, crowded foramen magnum on prior CT and 11 mm tonsillar descent on MRI provided the unifying explanation for bulbar and respiratory symptoms.

Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome versus acquired central hypoventilation
Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS; “PHOX2B‑related CCHS”) is typically a disease of infancy and early childhood, characterized by failure of autonomic respiratory control, particularly during sleep, and often associated with Hirschsprung disease and cardiac rhythm abnormalities. Genetic testing for PHOX2B polyalanine repeat expansion confirms the diagnosis. By contrast, acquired central hypoventilation occurs at any age secondary to structural brainstem lesions, infection, toxins or trauma. Age of onset, family history, genetic testing, and the presence of a discrete structural lesion on imaging distinguish congenital from acquired forms. Adult onset of selective automatic breathing failure, as in this case, should prompt a search for an acquired structural cause rather than routine PHOX2B testing.

Functional and psychiatric causes
Functional (psychogenic) dysphagia, psychogenic non‑epileptic events, and anxiety can produce sensations of choking or hyperventilation and may coexist with organic disease. However, functional diagnoses should be made only after objective evaluation has excluded structural, neuromuscular and respiratory causes—particularly when there is documented severe hypoxaemia or central‑predominant apnoeas. Psychogenic explanations are tempting when routine ENT and GI studies are normal, but they must not be used to dismiss objective physiologic abnormalities such as persistent oxygen saturations in the 70%–80% range or central apnoeas on monitored sleep testing.

Putting the pieces together: targeted evaluation and red flags
A practical evaluation begins with a directed history: timing (sleep versus wake), triggers (mealtimes, Valsalva, position), progression, antecedent trauma, medication and substance use (opioids), associated neurological symptoms (headache, dysarthria, diplopia, ataxia) and cardiopulmonary symptoms. Examination should include focused ENT assessment, cranial nerves, assessment for UMN/LMN signs, cardiopulmonary exam and overnight pulse oximetry when available. First‑line tests are flexible laryngoscopy and modified barium swallow for choking; chest imaging and spirometry for pulmonary disease; basic labs and ECG to exclude metabolic and cardiac contributors; and full polysomnography (with capnography) if sleep‑related breathing disorder is suspected. Crucially, MRI of the brain and craniocervical junction is indicated when ENT/GI testing is unrevealing, when central events predominate on sleep testing, when there are neurological signs, or when hypoxaemia is profound despite normal pulmonary evaluation.

Urgent referral and treatment implications
Certain features demand expedited neurologic or respiratory assessment: recurrent severe desaturation (<85%), syncope or loss of consciousness during events, progressive bulbar palsy, new neurologic deficits, or evidence of daytime hypercapnia. Identification of a structural lesion such as Chiari I has immediate therapeutic implications: posterior fossa decompression may relieve brainstem compression, restore CSF flow, ameliorate dysphagia and reverse central hypoventilation in some patients. Conversely, obstructive sleep apnoea responds to CPAP and positional or surgical interventions, while neuromuscular disorders require specific medical or immunologic therapies and respiratory support (non‑invasive ventilation) when indicated.

In summary, choking and central hypoventilation sit at the crossroads of several specialties. A careful, stepwise approach—matching the history and physical to targeted investigations and retaining a low threshold for MRI when central mechanisms are suspected—prevents misdiagnosis and delays in treatment. The presented case underscores that persistent choking with normal ENT/GI studies and unexplained nocturnal desaturations should prompt neuroimaging to exclude brainstem and craniocervical structural causes such as Chiari malformation.

## Diagnostic Workup and Interpretation

When confronted with a patient whose dominant complaints are intermittent choking, unexplained nocturnal oxygen desaturation and chronic occipital headaches — as in the case above — a structured, stepwise diagnostic approach is essential. The goal is twofold: (1) establish whether a structural craniocervical abnormality exists and is plausibly responsible for brainstem dysfunction, and (2) characterise the nature and severity of respiratory and bulbar impairment so that appropriate, timely management (including neurosurgical consultation) can be arranged. The following framework integrates neuroimaging, sleep testing, pulmonary physiology, gas-exchange monitoring and swallowing evaluation, and highlights common interpretive pitfalls that can delay diagnosis.

Neuroimaging: what to order and how to read it
The diagnostic cornerstone for suspected Chiari I malformation (CMI) is high‑quality MRI of the brain and entire craniocervical junction (sagittal and axial T1/T2 sequences) with inclusion of the upper cervical spinal cord. MRI allows measurement of tonsillar descent (classically ≥5 mm below the foramen magnum) and assessment for posterior fossa crowding, effacement of CSF spaces (pre‑ and post‑tonsillar cisterns), dorsal medullary compression, and the presence of syringomyelia. Cervical spine imaging is important because a syrinx — when present — both supports the diagnosis and has direct therapeutic implications. Cine phase‑contrast (CSF flow) MRI adds dynamic information and can demonstrate flow obstruction at the foramen magnum that may not be obvious on static images; this can be particularly useful when the degree of descent is borderline but clinical symptoms strongly suggest craniocervical compromise.

A frequent and avoidable pitfall is relying on prior imaging reports without re‑reviewing the actual images. Small degrees of tonsillar descent, effacement of CSF spaces at the foramen magnum or crowding of the posterior fossa may be missed on CT or on limited MRI sequences; in practice, retrospective review of prior head CT or MRI images often reveals signs that were overlooked, as happened in this case. Also remember that tonsillar position alone does not define symptomatic disease: many adults with radiographic descent are asymptomatic. Therefore, clinicoradiologic correlation — tying imaging abnormalities to compatible symptoms and neurologic findings — is crucial before attributing respiratory or bulbar symptoms to CMI.

Polysomnography and measurement of central hypoventilation
Because automatic respiratory control is mediated in the brainstem and central sleep‑related breathing disorders are common in CMI, sleep testing is mandatory when central events, nocturnal desaturation or daytime somnolence are present. A full, attended type I polysomnogram (PSG) is the preferred test: it records EEG (for sleep staging and accurate total sleep time), electrooculogram, chin EMG, respiratory effort (thoracoabdominal belts), airflow, pulse oximetry, and ideally end‑tidal or transcutaneous CO2 and video. EEG is essential — without it, indexes that depend on total sleep time (like the apnea–hypopnea index, AHI) are calculated against total recording time and will therefore underestimate event frequency and severity if substantial wakefulness occurs during the recording.

Type III (cardiorespiratory) devices, which were used in this patient for pragmatic reasons, capture airflow, effort and oximetry but lack EEG; they can be useful as a screening tool but have well‑recognised limitations. In particular, a low AHI on type III testing does not exclude clinically significant sleep‑disordered breathing — especially central apnoea or hypoventilation — because events that occur during wakefulness will be counted as part of the denominator, reducing the computed event rate. Therefore, when central hypoventilation or brainstem involvement is suspected, insist on a full PSG with CO2 monitoring.

CO2 monitoring (either transcutaneous PtcCO2 or end‑tidal) is critical because central hypoventilation may present with profound hypercapnia without severe oxygen desaturation early on, and oximetry alone can be falsely reassuring. Common thresholds used in practice are a sustained PtcCO2 >50 mm Hg or a rise in PtcCO2 of ≥10 mm Hg above awake baseline during sleep as indicators of hypoventilation. When PSG is not immediately available, overnight transcutaneous CO2 monitoring alongside oximetry at home or in clinic provides valuable interim data.

Pulmonary mechanics and arterial blood gas testing
Basic pulmonary function testing (spirometry) and measurements of maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures (MIP/MEP) help exclude primary pulmonary disease or respiratory muscle weakness as causes of hypoventilation. However, normal spirometry and only modestly reduced MIP/MEP do not exclude central hypoventilation because voluntary respiratory musculature and daytime drive are often preserved in brainstem disorders. Likewise, arterial blood gas (ABG) performed while the patient is awake is commonly normal or only mildly abnormal; hence a normal daytime PaCO2 should not be interpreted as excluding nocturnal hypoventilation.

Oximetry and capnography in sleep provide the real diagnostic window for central hypoventilation. Documented episodes of severe, asymptomatic oxygen desaturation (70%–80%), especially when accompanied by a predominance of central apnoeas on PSG, point to impaired automatic respiratory control and brainstem involvement.

Swallowing assessment and bulbar evaluation
Given the high prevalence of oropharyngeal dysfunction in CMI, formal evaluation by speech‑language pathology is essential. Videofluoroscopic swallowing study (modified barium swallow) is the preferred initial test to characterise the biomechanics of swallowing, detect silent aspiration and guide dietary recommendations and therapy. Flexible endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) is a complementary bedside option that provides direct visualisation of pharyngeal anatomy and secretion management. In neurogenic dysphagia, the classic pattern is variable difficulty with both solids and liquids, delayed swallow initiation, reduced hyolaryngeal excursion and penetration/aspiration without overt obstruction — features that should prompt reconsideration of a central cause when endoscopic and GI evaluations are otherwise normal.

Ancillary testing and exclusion of mimics
A comprehensive neurologic examination focused on lower cranial nerves (IX, X, XI, XII) is mandatory: palatal elevation, gag reflex, tongue atrophy or fasciculations, vocal cord mobility and cough effectiveness should be documented. Autonomic testing may be informative when dysautonomia is suspected. Because other central causes of hypoventilation exist (brainstem stroke, tumour, metabolic encephalopathy), targeted imaging and laboratory testing to exclude mimics are appropriate. In younger patients with congenital presentations, PHOX2B testing for congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS) is indicated; in adults, investigate for opioid use, severe heart failure or primary central sleep apnea causes.

Interpreting the data: clinicoradiologic correlation and decision‑making
Interpretation must integrate anatomy, physiology and symptomatology. Radiologic findings that support attributing symptoms to CMI include significant tonsillar descent with posterior fossa crowding, CSF flow obstruction on cine MRI, medullary compression or syrinx formation. Physiologic corroboration includes a predominance of central sleep apnoeas on full PSG, documented nocturnal hypercapnia, recurrent profound desaturations (especially if asymptomatic), and neurogenic dysphagia on videofluoroscopy. Conversely, isolated mild tonsillar descent without compatible symptoms or physiologic abnormalities is more likely to represent an incidental variant.

Be mindful of common pitfalls: (1) accepting a normal type III study as excluding significant sleep‑disordered breathing when clinical suspicion is high, (2) overreliance on daytime ABG or spirometry to exclude nocturnal hypoventilation, (3) failure to re‑review prior neuroimaging for subtle craniocervical junction crowding, and (4) equating any degree of tonsillar descent with causation without supportive clinical or physiologic evidence. When clinicoradiologic correlation is strong, early multidisciplinary discussion with neurosurgery is appropriate because posterior fossa decompression can reverse CSF flow obstruction and, in many patients, improve respiratory and bulbar symptoms.

Practical pathway for this case
For the patient described, the immediate priorities are (1) obtain a type I attended PSG with continuous CO2 monitoring and video to quantify central respiratory events and nocturnal hypercapnia, (2) complete MRI cine flow sequences of the craniocervical junction (if not already available) and an MRI of the cervical cord to look for syrinx, (3) perform a videofluoroscopic swallowing study and formal SLP assessment to document aspiration risk and guide feeding precautions, and (4) repeat focused pulmonary function testing including MIP/MEP and consider ambulatory overnight transcutaneous CO2/oximetry while awaiting PSG. If these investigations confirm significant brainstem compression with physiologic evidence of central hypoventilation or progressive bulbar dysfunction, referral for neurosurgical evaluation for posterior fossa decompression is warranted. Throughout, multidisciplinary coordination between neurology, sleep medicine, pulmonology, ENT/SLP and neurosurgery will ensure the most accurate diagnosis and a safe, patient‑centred treatment plan.

## Management: Conservative Measures and Indications for Decompression

The management of Chiari I malformation (CMI) is fundamentally patient‑centred: treatment decisions must weigh the severity and trajectory of symptoms, objective evidence of brainstem or spinal cord compromise, radiographic markers (tonsillar descent, CSF flow obstruction, syringomyelia) and the risks and potential benefits of posterior fossa decompression. Our patient’s story—longstanding occipital/vertex pain, progressive bulbar dysfunction with choking and aspiration risk, and recurrent, profound oxygen desaturations consistent with acquired central hypoventilation—illustrates the common dilemma. She has objective anatomic brainstem compression (11 mm tonsillar descent with loss of CSF space), functional respiratory compromise (recurrent desaturation to 70%–80% and predominantly central events on a type III study) and meaningful impairment of quality of life. These features place her squarely in the category of patients for whom definitive surgical therapy must be seriously considered; at the same time, perioperative risks and the variable degree of postoperative recovery justify a structured period of conservative optimisation in many cases and shared decision‑making.

Conservative and supportive measures
When immediate surgery is not undertaken or is deferred while the patient considers options, a comprehensive supportive plan is essential—both to reduce near‑term risk (particularly from hypoxaemia and aspiration) and to maximise the patient’s functional status. Respiratory support is a cornerstone. For patients with predominantly obstructive events, CPAP is first‑line. For central events or documented hypoventilation during sleep, noninvasive ventilation (NIV) with a backup rate—bilevel positive airway pressure (BPAP) with a timed backup or volume‑targeted modes—or adaptive servoventilation (ASV) may be required to stabilise ventilation. Selection of the device should be individualised in consultation with sleep medicine and respiratory therapy; ASV can be effective for central apnoea but is contraindicated in some cardiopulmonary contexts (e.g., symptomatic heart failure with reduced ejection fraction), so cardiology input may be necessary. Nocturnal capnography or transcutaneous CO₂ monitoring is strongly advised when there is concern for hypoventilation, because oxygen saturation alone can miss hypercapnia and supplemental oxygen without ventilation can worsen hypercapnia in hypoventilatory states.

Supplemental oxygen may be used for profound desaturations but should be accompanied by close monitoring and, ideally, capnography—because oxygen can mask hypoventilation and delay recognition of rising PaCO₂. Patients should be counselled to avoid or minimise centrally acting respiratory depressants (opioids, benzodiazepines, sedative hypnotics and certain anticholinergics) unless absolutely necessary; coordination with primary care and pain teams is often required to provide alternative pain management given the frequency of headache in CMI. Positional strategies (e.g., avoiding supine sleep if the patient’s desaturations are worse while supine) and sleep hygiene can be helpful adjuncts.

For acute or severe failure of automatic respiration (recurrent severe nocturnal desaturation, daytime hypercapnia or inability to protect the airway), escalation to continuous ventilatory support—short‑term invasive ventilation (intubation) or tracheostomy with mechanical ventilation—may be lifesaving and should not be delayed while arranging definitive surgery. Such measures are considered when NIV fails, when aspiration risk is high, or when the site lacks immediate neurosurgical capacity.

Swallowing and nutrition
Neurogenic dysphagia in CMI commonly presents with equal difficulty for liquids and solids and a high aspiration risk. Management should be multidisciplinary, led by a speech‑language pathologist (SLP). Early objective evaluation (videofluoroscopic swallowing study or fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing) guides therapy. Compensatory strategies—texture modification, smaller bolus sizes, pacing, chin‑tuck or head‑turn manoeuvres—often reduce aspiration and can restore safe oral intake. Strengthening and neuromuscular retraining exercises are useful when appropriate. For persistent aspiration despite best conservative measures, enteral feeding (nasogastric tube short term, percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy for long term) should be considered to prevent recurrent aspiration pneumonia and to maintain nutrition while surgical options are evaluated. Coordination with nutritionists and consideration of the patient’s goals of care are essential.

Headache and scalp allodynia
Headache management is individually tailored. Occipital neuralgia or scalp allodynia may respond to neuropathic agents (gabapentin, pregabalin, low‑dose tricyclics) and to procedural interventions (greater occipital nerve blocks or botulinum toxin in selected cases). Avoidance of medications that depress respiration is particularly important in patients with suspected central hypoventilation. Treating comorbid mood and anxiety disorders and offering psychological support can markedly improve coping and perceived disability, especially when social withdrawal (as in our patient) complicates the clinical picture.

When to recommend posterior fossa decompression
Indications for posterior fossa decompression are symptom and evidence driven. Surgery is strongly indicated when there is: progressive or disabling Chiari‑related symptomatology (especially brainstem or lower cranial nerve dysfunction such as dysphagia, dysphonia, aspiration or central apnoeas); radiographic evidence of CSF flow obstruction at the foramen magnum or a syrinx in the spinal cord that is symptomatic or enlarging; and objective respiratory compromise attributable to brainstem compression (documented central hypoventilation, recurrent severe nocturnal desaturation, daytime hypercapnia). In contrast, asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic patients—particularly those without CSF flow blockage, syringomyelia, or progressive deficits—are often managed conservatively with observation and serial imaging.

Surgical goals and techniques
The surgical objective is anatomic decompression: enlarging the posterior fossa and restoring CSF dynamics at the craniocervical junction to relieve direct tonsillar pressure on the medulla and lower cranial nerve nuclei and to normalise CSF flow that can drive syrinx formation. Common operative approaches include suboccipital craniectomy combined with C1 laminectomy. The dural layer may be opened (with duraplasty) or left intact (bone‑only decompression); a number of centres prefer duraplasty to maximise decompression and improve CSF flow, but this choice balances a higher risk of CSF leak and pseudomeningocele. Tonsillar reduction or coagulation is sometimes performed when small posterior fossa volume or significantly bulked tonsils remain a concern; intradural procedures are tailored to intraoperative findings and preoperative goals. Intraoperative neurophysiologic monitoring (brainstem auditory evoked potentials, cranial nerve monitoring) can be helpful in minimising iatrogenic injury.

Expected outcomes and variability
Many patients experience early improvement in Valsalva‑related headaches and, when present, reductions in brainstem‑mediated symptoms after decompression. Published series and case reports describe meaningful improvement or resolution of sleep‑disordered breathing, central apnoeas, and neurogenic dysphagia after posterior fossa decompression, but results vary. Respiratory improvement may be rapid in some patients and gradual in others; dysphagia may improve over weeks to months as brainstem function recovers. Syringomyelia, when present, often diminishes in size over months after restoration of CSF flow. Importantly, objective reassessment with postoperative polysomnography (including capnography if preoperative hypercapnia was present) and interval MRI is necessary to document response and to guide further management. Patients and clinicians should maintain realistic expectations: residual symptoms, persistent need for nocturnal ventilatory support, or incomplete recovery of swallowing are possible.

Perioperative and anesthetic considerations
Patients with CMI and documented central respiratory impairment present specific perioperative risks. Preoperative evaluation should include a formal sleep study (type I polysomnography with capnography when possible), arterial blood gas if daytime hypoventilation is suspected, and optimisation of NIV if used. Anaesthetic teams must anticipate reduced automatic respiratory drive—extubation should be planned only when the patient is fully awake, airway protective reflexes are intact, and spontaneous ventilation adequate. Many centres elect postoperative monitoring in an intensive care or high‑dependency unit with continuous pulse oximetry and capnography for at least the early postoperative period. Analgesic plans should prioritise non‑opioid multimodal approaches and reserve opioids for controlled settings with monitoring. Early postoperative assessment by SLP before oral intake is imperative in patients with preoperative dysphagia. Finally, detailed communication between neurosurgery, anesthesia, sleep medicine and pulmonology is essential to plan postoperative ventilatory support and escalation pathways.

Risks and complications
As with any cranial surgery, risks include surgical site infection, CSF leak or pseudomeningocele, hemorrhage, wound healing problems, new or worsened neurological deficits (including cranial nerve palsies), and rarely cerebellar or brainstem injury. There are also procedure‑specific considerations: opening the dura increases the risk of CSF leak but may improve dural decompression efficacy; tonsillar manipulation can produce transient breath‑holding or autonomic instability intraoperatively. These risks must be weighed against the patient’s preoperative deficits and the likelihood of progression if untreated.

Monitoring and long‑term follow‑up
Regardless of whether immediate surgery is selected, structured monitoring is key. For conservatively managed patients, set timed reassessments: regular clinical visits with symptom inventories (headaches, bulbar symptoms, sleepiness, choking), periodic overnight oximetry or transcutaneous CO₂ monitoring, and interval MRI when symptoms change or annually if radiographic risk features exist. For patients treated surgically, reassessment includes early postoperative clinical review, repeat polysomnography (commonly at 3–6 months) to quantify improvement in sleep‑disordered breathing, and MRI at 3–6 months to document resolution of tonsillar crowding and syrinx regression if applicable. Thresholds for reoperation or renewed intervention include persistent or worsening brainstem dysfunction, enlarging syrinx, progressive radiographic obstruction of CSF flow, or failure to achieve respiratory stability despite optimal conservative measures.

Multidisciplinary care and psychosocial support
Optimal outcomes for patients with complex Chiari presentations are achieved in multidisciplinary programmes. Core team members include neurosurgery, neurology, sleep medicine, pulmonology, otolaryngology, speech‑language pathology, nutrition, rehabilitation medicine, anesthesia, and primary care. Given the frequent emotional and social impacts—anxiety about choking, social withdrawal, and cultural implications of symptom‑related coping strategies as in our patient—access to psychology or counselling and social work services is important. Shared decision‑making, clear education about the expected course, and documentation of the patient’s values and goals are central to management.

Applying these principles to the case
In this patient, several elements favour recommending posterior fossa decompression: objective radiographic compression of the foramen magnum with significant tonsillar descent, disabling neurogenic dysphagia with social withdrawal and aspiration risk, and recurrent, profound nocturnal desaturations consistent with acquired central hypoventilation. Nevertheless, her decision to delay surgery is reasonable provided a rigorous conservative plan is enacted: initiation or optimisation of nocturnal ventilatory support with capnography monitoring, strict avoidance of respiratory depressants, early SLP–guided swallowing strategies with objective follow‑up studies, psychosocial support, and close surveillance with scheduled reassessments and readiness to proceed to surgery should her respiratory or bulbar function deteriorate. If decompression is performed, preoperative optimisation with sleep medicine and respiratory teams, meticulous perioperative anesthesia planning, early postoperative ICU monitoring, repeat polysomnography and interval MRI will be required to document clinical and physiologic recovery and to guide ongoing support.

## Clinical Pearls and Pitfalls

This case illustrates several practical, high-yield lessons about when to suspect Chiari malformation type I (CMI) and how to avoid common diagnostic and management errors. Keep in mind that CMI is a clinicoradiologic diagnosis: the radiographic finding of tonsillar descent must be interpreted in the context of the patient’s symptoms, examination and objective testing. Conversely, normal-looking airway and pulmonary studies do not exclude a structural cause at the craniocervical junction.

Dysphagia and choking that affect solids and liquids equally should raise suspicion for a neurogenic bulbar process. Mechanical obstruction (stricture, tumours) and reflux-related laryngopharyngeal disease typically produce a different pattern; when flexible laryngoscopy and upper endoscopy are repeatedly normal, neurologic causes must be considered. In particular, lower cranial nerve dysfunction from craniocervical pathology may produce palatal weakness, impaired pharyngeal contraction and disordered coordination of swallowing that manifest as non‑consistency‑specific choking and cough. Early involvement of speech-language pathology with videofluoroscopic swallowing studies helps characterise the physiologic pattern and guide feeding and safety interventions while the underlying cause is pursued.

Unexplained nocturnal desaturation or a predominance of central apnoeic events on sleep testing should prompt MRI of the brain and craniocervical junction. Brainstem compression or CSF flow obstruction at the foramen magnum can selectively impair automatic (brainstem-mediated) respiratory drive while sparing voluntary (cortical) breathing — producing episodes of profound desaturation without dyspnoea. When a sleep study shows a high proportion of central events, or when home oximetry documents recurrent, unexplained drops in saturation (particularly into the 70s–80s), structural lesions of the posterior fossa must be considered and excluded by MRI. The presence of associated symptoms — occipital headaches, Valsalva‑precipitated pain, cranial nerve findings, or progressive bulbar complaints — strengthens the indication for neuroimaging.

Be careful not to treat millimetres instead of the patient: radiographic criteria (typically ≥5 mm tonsillar descent) are a useful threshold, but many adults have incidental tonsillar ectopia without symptoms. Conversely, relatively small radiographic abnormalities can produce severe brainstem dysfunction in the right anatomical context. Always integrate imaging with objective physiologic data (sleep study with CO₂ monitoring, videofluoroscopy, neurologic exam) and the temporal course of symptoms when deciding management. Cine (phase-contrast) MRI to assess CSF flow across the foramen magnum and careful evaluation for syringomyelia are valuable adjuncts when surgical decisions are being made.

Do not over-rely on type III (cardiorespiratory) sleep studies when central hypoventilation or brainstem dysfunction is suspected. Type III devices lack EEG, so they estimate AHI over total recording time rather than true sleep time and therefore commonly underestimate event indices — especially when patients have fragmented sleep or long awake periods during the recording. When central events are suspected or when home oximetry demonstrates severe desaturations, obtain a full (type I) polysomnogram with EEG and continuous CO₂ monitoring (end-tidal or transcutaneous). Capnography is particularly important to detect hypoventilation that may not produce frequent apneas but causes rising PaCO₂ and sustained nocturnal hypoventilation.

Re-review old imaging. CT scans obtained for trauma or other reasons may contain overlooked clues — a crowded foramen magnum, loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction, or tonsillar tissue near the occipital bone — that were not appreciated at the time. If symptoms suggest CMI, retrieve and re-examine prior head and cervical imaging (and request MRI if only CT exists). A prior normal radiology read does not exclude subacute progressive change or subtle crowding that becomes clinically relevant over time.

Counsel patients that brainstem compression can blunt interoceptive signalling; people may experience severe hypoxaemia or hypercapnia without the expected sensation of breathlessness. This has safety implications: advise patients about increased risks with air travel, driving, situations that reduce oxygen reserve, and use of sedating medications or alcohol. Practical precautions include supplying patients with pulse oximetry for home monitoring, establishing thresholds for when to seek urgent medical attention, avoiding opioids and benzodiazepines that worsen central hypoventilation, and considering temporary activity restrictions (e.g., driving or flying) until the degree of respiratory impairment is defined and managed.

Recognize trauma as a potential unmasking event. CMI may be congenital, but symptoms sometimes begin or worsen after head injury; without preinjury imaging it is usually impossible to prove causality. Nonetheless, a history of head trauma preceding symptom onset should increase vigilance for craniocervical pathology, and documentation of timing helps guide diagnosis and counselling.

When severe central hypoventilation or progressive bulbar dysfunction is present, early multidisciplinary referral is essential. Neurosurgery, sleep medicine, ENT/speech pathology, pulmonology and rehabilitation medicine should be involved. Posterior fossa decompression can restore CSF flow, relieve brainstem compression and (in many cases) reverse respiratory and bulbar symptoms — therefore timely recognition and referral can be life‑changing. Indications for surgery commonly include progressive neurological deficit, syringomyelia, severe sleep‑disordered breathing attributable to the craniocervical junction, or intractable pain attributable to CMI. For patients who defer surgery or while awaiting workup, institute interim safety measures: nocturnal oximetry, consideration of nocturnal noninvasive ventilation for hypoventilation rather than oxygen alone (supplemental oxygen can mask hypoventilation and delay detection), and close follow-up with low threshold for escalation.

Common pitfalls to avoid
- Assuming a normal laryngoscopy/upper endoscopy rules out the cause of choking. If swallowing physiology is abnormal, pursue neurological evaluation.  
- Interpreting tonsillar descent in isolation. Many adults have incidental descent; correlate with symptoms, flow studies and objective dysfunction.  
- Relying on type III sleep studies when central hypoventilation is suspected. Obtain full polysomnography with CO₂ monitoring.  
- Missing the craniocervical junction on CT reads; re-examine prior imaging and obtain an MRI when clinical suspicion exists.  
- Underestimating the safety risks posed by lack of dyspnoea during severe desaturation; educate patients and implement monitoring and activity restrictions as needed.

Bottom line: maintain a low threshold to consider craniocervical junction pathology when bulbar dysfunction, unexplained central sleep-disordered breathing, or recurrent nocturnal hypoxemia occur with normal airway and pulmonary evaluations. Correlate imaging with physiologic testing, use full polysomnography with CO₂ monitoring when central hypoventilation is suspected, re-examine prior studies, and refer early for multidisciplinary assessment — timely recognition and appropriate treatment can prevent life‑threatening events and materially improve patients’ quality of life.
