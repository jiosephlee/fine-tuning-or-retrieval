# Central Hypoventilation and Choking Episodes Unmasking Chiari I Malformation: Using a Case to Understand Brainstem Control of Breathing and Swallowing

## Case Vignette and Clinical Framing

A woman in her early 40s presented with a 7-year history of unpredictable choking with both solids and liquids and frequent nocturnal episodes that startled her awake with coughing and gasping. For 14 years she had lived with persistent occipital and vertex headaches accompanied by marked vertex allodynia. Multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies were normal. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home oximetry documented unrecognized desaturations into the 70%–80% range without associated dyspnea. A prior head CT, re-reviewed 14 years after a motor vehicle crash, revealed a crowded foramen magnum. MRI confirmed Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with 11 mm tonsillar descent. A limited sleep study (type III) showed mild sleep apnea (apnea–hypopnea index [AHI] 6) with a predominance of central events (72%). Pulmonary function, daytime arterial blood gases, and neuromuscular screening labs were unremarkable.

This case illustrates a key clinical lesson: in patients with dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally and unexplained hypoxemia—especially when accompanied by occipital headaches—brainstem pathology must be considered. CMI can disrupt automatic control of breathing and bulbar function, mimicking acquired central hypoventilation (Ondine’s curse), and may be reversible with timely posterior fossa decompression.

## Clinical Approach: From Symptoms to a Targeted Differential

The clinical picture blends three vexing complaints: nonobstructive choking, silent hypoxemia, and chronic occipital–vertex headaches. A reasoned approach separates these into problem lists and recombines them around shared neuroanatomy.

- Dysphagia and choking with both solids and liquids suggests a neurogenic or neuromuscular etiology rather than a structural luminal lesion. Mechanical lesions typically affect solids first; equal impairment of solids and liquids points to oropharyngeal phase dysfunction (eg, brainstem disease, cranial neuropathies IX–XII, myasthenia gravis, motor neuron disease, Parkinson disease, or demyelinating processes). Normal endoscopy and laryngoscopy further favor a neurogenic origin.

- Unrecognized, often nocturnal hypoxemia with preserved daytime gas exchange and normal pulmonary mechanics implies intermittent ventilatory control failure rather than intrinsic lung disease. Differential diagnoses include obstructive sleep apnea, central sleep apnea (idiopathic, opioid-related, Cheyne–Stokes respiration with heart failure), obesity hypoventilation, high-altitude periodic breathing, and structural brainstem disorders impairing automatic respiratory drive.

- Occipital headaches and vertex allodynia suggest cervicomedullary or occipital nerve involvement. The association of occipital headache with Valsalva is classic in CMI, although absence of Valsalva sensitivity does not exclude the diagnosis. Vertex allodynia may reflect trigeminocervical convergence or occipital nerve hypersensitivity.

The synthesis of these problems points to the medulla and craniocervical junction—home to the central pattern generators for breathing and swallowing, lower cranial nerve nuclei, ascending interoceptive pathways signaling dyspnea, and the CSF pathways that may be impeded in CMI.

Key findings that narrowed the differential in this case:
- Normal ENT, gastrointestinal endoscopy, and pulmonary evaluations
- Dysphagia pattern consistent with neurogenic oropharyngeal dysfunction
- Silent desaturation events with minimal dyspnea, favoring central drive failure over airway obstruction
- Imaging evidence of foramen magnum crowding and 11 mm tonsillar descent

## Pathophysiology: How Chiari I Perturbs Breathing, Swallowing, and Pain

Chiari I malformation is characterized by caudal descent of the cerebellar tonsils ≥5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. Contemporary understanding reframes CMI as a spectrum: many individuals harbor tonsillar descent as an anatomic variant, while a subset develop “Chiari syndrome”—the symptomatic state—through a combination of posterior fossa crowding, altered CSF hydrodynamics, and direct neural compression. Symptom severity correlates poorly with the absolute millimeters of descent; the degree of crowding, ventral brainstem impingement, and CSF flow obstruction are stronger determinants of clinical expression.

Neurobiology of automatic versus voluntary breathing
- Automatic breathing is a brainstem-driven behavior generated by networks in the ventrolateral medulla (including the pre-Bötzinger and parafacial/retrotrapezoid complexes) and modulated by pontine centers (Kölliker–Fuse and parabrachial nuclei). Chemoreceptive inputs (medullary CO2/pH sensors, carotid body O2/CO2/pH afferents via IX) and mechanoreceptors (lung stretch via vagus) converge in the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) and shape respiratory rhythm and amplitude.
- Voluntary breathing originates in cortical premotor and motor areas and descends via corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts, allowing speech, singing, and breath-holding. In structural brainstem disease, voluntary control may be preserved while automatic drive fails, particularly during sleep, quiet wakefulness, or distraction—explaining “forgetting to breathe” in this case.

Coughing and swallowing: shared circuitry, shared vulnerability
- Swallowing and cough are orchestrated by central pattern generators in the medulla (NTS for sensory integration; nucleus ambiguus for motor output) with contributions from the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus and adjacent reticular formation. Chiari-related compression can disrupt timing and coordination among palatal, pharyngeal, and laryngeal muscles, leading to choking, penetration, and aspiration that affect all consistencies.
- Lower cranial nerve involvement (IX, X, XII) in CMI manifests clinically as palatal weakness, dysphonia, impaired gag, tongue atrophy/fasciculations, and cricopharyngeal dysfunction; subclinical deficits are common and demonstrable on videofluoroscopic swallow studies.

Why dyspnea is blunted
- The conscious sensation of air hunger arises from interoceptive afferents (chemoreceptor and mechanoreceptor pathways) relayed through the NTS and parabrachial complex to limbic–cortical regions (anterior insula, anterior cingulate). Cervicomedullary compression can interrupt these ascending signals, diminishing awareness of hypoventilation and hypoxemia. Patients may present with profound desaturation yet little distress—the dangerous “silent hypoxemia” seen here.

CSF flow and syringomyelia
- A small posterior fossa and tonsillar descent can obstruct CSF egress at the foramen magnum, generating pressure gradients across the craniospinal axis. Pulsatile CSF jets and altered compliance may contribute to cervicomedullary strain and the development of syringomyelia. Syringes can further impair bulbar and respiratory function via disruption of corticospinal and anterior horn pathways.

Trauma as trigger or unmasker
- Head or whiplash trauma may precipitate symptom onset in predisposed individuals, either by accentuating tonsillar descent, provoking CSF pressure disequilibrium, or converting a compensated tight posterior fossa into a symptomatic state. Without preinjury imaging, one cannot distinguish a truly acquired Chiari mimic (eg, intracranial hypotension–related tonsillar descent) from unmasking of a congenital Chiari I.

Sleep-disordered breathing in CMI
- Polysomnographic studies reveal high rates of sleep-related breathing disorders in symptomatic CMI. Central apnea indices may be elevated, reflecting instability or impairment of automatic drive. Respiratory abnormalities range from isolated central apneas to mixed or obstructive events from pharyngeal hypotonia or discoordination. Decompression often improves central events by relieving brainstem compression and normalizing CSF dynamics.

## Diagnostic Workup: Tests That Clarify Mechanism and Guide Therapy

Imaging the craniocervical junction
- MRI brain and cervical spine is the cornerstone. Beyond measuring tonsillar descent, assess for posterior fossa crowding, medullary and upper cervical cord compression, effacement of perimedullary CSF, and associated anomalies (syringomyelia, basilar invagination, platybasia, atlantoaxial instability). Cine phase-contrast MRI can demonstrate CSF flow obstruction at the foramen magnum and is helpful when symptoms are disproportionate to static anatomic findings.
- Re-review prior “normal” studies with attention to the foramen magnum can be revealing, as in this case.

Swallow evaluation
- Videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS) or fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) identifies oropharyngeal timing abnormalities, delayed swallow initiation, penetration/aspiration, vallecular/piriform residue, and cricopharyngeal dysfunction typical of neurogenic dysphagia. Equal difficulty with liquids and solids and lack of structural narrowing support a neurogenic etiology.
- Consider esophageal manometry if symptoms suggest prominent esophageal dysmotility.

Sleep and ventilatory control testing
- Full, attended polysomnography (type I) is preferred when central events are suspected. Incorporate EEG to quantify total sleep time, end-tidal or transcutaneous CO2 to detect hypoventilation, and video to evaluate arousals and behaviors. Type III home studies, which lack EEG and CO2 monitoring, often underestimate AHI and fail to capture hypoventilation; the predominance of central events despite a low AHI in this case should prompt formal laboratory PSG.
- Daytime arterial blood gases may be normal in intermittent or sleep-limited central hypoventilation; do not be reassured by a single normal PaCO2. When available, assess ventilatory chemosensitivity (hypercapnic ventilatory response) and wakefulness drive to breathe.

Exclude mimics and contributors
- Pulmonary function testing (including maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures) screens for neuromuscular weakness; normal results, as here, support central mechanisms.
- Labs to exclude myasthenia gravis (AChR and MuSK antibodies), hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, and inflammatory markers are reasonable when bulbar symptoms are prominent.
- Cardiac evaluation if Cheyne–Stokes pattern or heart failure is suspected.

When to attribute symptoms to CMI
- Diagnosing “Chiari syndrome” requires congruence between symptoms and cervicomedullary pathology: headaches typical of CMI; bulbar, cerebellar, or long-tract signs; sleep-disordered breathing with central events; and/or syringomyelia. Tonsillar descent alone does not obligate surgery. The combination of 11 mm descent, CSF crowding, bulbar symptoms, and central apnea events builds a persuasive case in this patient.

## Management Principles: Restoring Space, Protecting the Airway, and Stabilizing Ventilation

Surgical decompression
- Rationale: Suboccipital craniectomy with C1 laminectomy and duraplasty enlarges the posterior fossa, restores CSF flow at the foramen magnum, and relieves brainstem and upper cervical cord compression. In appropriately selected patients, decompression improves Valsalva-triggered headaches, bulbar dysfunction, syringomyelia, and sleep-disordered breathing with central events. Tonsillar reduction (shrinkage/pexy) may be considered in selected centers.
- Indications: Symptomatic Chiari with classic headaches refractory to conservative measures; progressive neurologic deficits (bulbar, cerebellar, long-tract); syringomyelia; objective sleep-disordered breathing with central hypoventilation/apneas attributable to cervicomedullary compression; or life-threatening respiratory compromise.
- Expectations and counseling: Central apneas often improve postoperatively, sometimes rapidly; dysphagia may recover more gradually with rehabilitation. Discuss risks—CSF leak, pseudomeningocele, meningitis, wound complications, and, rarely, cranial nerve deficits—and the possibility of incomplete symptom resolution.

Nonoperative and supportive strategies
- Ventilatory support: If surgery is deferred or while awaiting surgery, consider nocturnal noninvasive ventilation (eg, bilevel positive airway pressure with a backup rate [BiPAP-ST] or volume-assured pressure support) for documented central hypoventilation. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) may help if obstructive events coexist but is less effective for central events without a backup rate. Adaptive servo-ventilation can stabilize central events in selected patients but should be avoided in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. Supplemental oxygen can reduce loop gain but must be used cautiously and ideally with monitoring, as it can blunt hypoxic drive without correcting hypoventilation.
- Swallowing and aspiration prevention: Early referral to speech–language pathology for targeted exercises (eg, effortful swallow, Mendelsohn maneuver), compensatory strategies (chin-tuck, head-turn), texture modification, and pacing. Institute aspiration precautions, oral hygiene, and vaccination against influenza and pneumococcus. Consider short-term enteral feeding if aspiration risk is high or weight loss occurs.
- Headache and neuropathic pain: Neuromodulators (eg, amitriptyline, gabapentin, duloxetine) may alleviate occipital neuralgia–like pain; greater occipital nerve blocks are an option. Address cervical myofascial contributors. Recognize that decompression can mitigate headache when crowding is causal.
- Risk mitigation and counseling: Avoid respiratory depressants (opioids, benzodiazepines) and alcohol excess. Discuss altitude exposure and long-distance travel; carry medical documentation. Educate patients and families on recognizing respiratory pauses and choking, and consider overnight oximetry while awaiting definitive testing or surgery.

Monitoring and follow-up
- Clinical: Track frequency of choking, witnessed apneas, daytime hypersomnolence, headaches, and neurologic signs. Involve caregivers when unawareness of events is suspected.
- Objective: Repeat PSG after initiating ventilatory support or after decompression to document improvement. Consider periodic VFSS if dysphagia persists. MRI surveillance if symptoms evolve or syringomyelia is present.
- Triggers for re-escalation: Worsening nocturnal hypoxemia, new dysarthria or dysphonia, aspiration pneumonia, progressive ataxia or long-tract findings, or radiographic progression.

## Special Topics Illuminated by the Case

Why the AHI looked “mild” despite severe symptoms
- Type III home testing calculates indices over total recording time and lacks EEG, leading to underestimation when wake periods dilute the denominator. Absence of CO2 monitoring misses sustained hypoventilation. The high proportion of central events and real-world severe desaturations in this patient mandate formal laboratory PSG.

Normal daytime PaCO2 does not exclude central hypoventilation
- Central drive failure in CMI may be intermittent or sleep-predominant. Daytime ABGs can be normal between events or compensated. CO2 monitoring during sleep is essential to detect nocturnal hypoventilation.

Trauma and Chiari I: causation, exacerbation, or coincidence?
- Trauma may precipitate symptom onset by perturbing CSF dynamics or tissue compliance in individuals with a tight posterior fossa. Alternatively, it may reveal a preexisting malformation that had been asymptomatic. Intracranial hypotension from CSF leak is a key Chiari mimic and should be considered if orthostatic headaches dominate.

Vertex allodynia and Chiari
- Scalp allodynia can arise from sensitization within the trigeminocervical complex and from occipital nerve irritation. Although less classically associated with CMI than occipital Valsalva headaches, mechanosensitivity of the scalp (eg, discomfort from hair weight) is biologically plausible via convergence of C2–C3 afferents and trigeminal nociceptive pathways.

## Prognosis

Symptomatic CMI has a variable course. Patients with classic headaches, CSF flow obstruction, and clear cervicomedullary compression often improve substantially after decompression. Bulbar and respiratory manifestations can reverse, particularly when addressed before fixed neural injury occurs. Untreated, central sleep-disordered breathing carries risks of neurocognitive decline, cardiovascular morbidity, accidents, and, rarely, sudden death. When surgery is deferred, careful monitoring and respiratory support mitigate risk.

## Clinical Pearls and Pitfalls

- Equal dysphagia for solids and liquids with normal endoscopy should prompt a neurologic evaluation; the medulla is the central hub for coordinating swallowing and airway protection.
- Central sleep apnea or “silent” hypoxemia in a patient with occipital headaches warrants imaging of the craniocervical junction to evaluate for CMI or other brainstem pathology.
- Tonsillar descent in millimeters is a crude biomarker; symptomatology, brainstem crowding, and CSF flow obstruction better predict clinical relevance.
- Type III sleep studies underestimate AHI and miss hypoventilation; order attended PSG with CO2 monitoring when central events are suspected.
- Preservation of voluntary but not automatic breathing is a hallmark of acquired central hypoventilation from brainstem lesions; patients may desaturate profoundly without dyspnea.
- Posterior fossa decompression can reverse central apneas and neurogenic dysphagia when caused by cervicomedullary compression.
- Avoid anchoring on gastroesophageal or laryngeal causes of choking when studies are normal; think brainstem early to reduce diagnostic delay.
- Re-review prior “normal” scans with a targeted question (foramen magnum crowding) can be diagnostic years later.
- Counsel patients to avoid respiratory depressants and to seek care urgently for escalating nocturnal events or aspiration.

## Take-Home Points

- CMI can present with an acquired central hypoventilation phenotype and neurogenic dysphagia due to compression of medullary pattern generators and lower cranial nerve nuclei.
- The triad of unexplained choking affecting all consistencies, silent hypoxemia with central events on screening sleep study, and occipital headaches should trigger MRI of the brain and craniocervical junction.
- Full polysomnography with CO2 monitoring is essential to characterize central apnea and hypoventilation; limited studies may underestimate severity.
- Posterior fossa decompression is the definitive therapy when symptoms, physiology, and imaging align; noninvasive ventilatory support and swallow therapy are important adjuncts or bridges to surgery.
- Recognizing the dissociation between voluntary and automatic breathing explains the paradox of severe desaturation without distress and guides both diagnosis and management.
