# Choking on Air: When Dysphagia Is Neurologic, Not ENT

What makes this case striking is how a woman endured 7 years of unexplained choking and 14 years of chronic vertex/occipital pain — multiple normal laryngoscopies and endoscopies, normal pulmonary workup — before a rereview of old imaging and a modern MRI revealed an 11 mm tonsillar descent consistent with Chiari I malformation. Her choking affected solids and liquids equally, nocturnal episodes were frequent, and during the COVID era she repeatedly documented asymptomatic desaturations into the 70s. This is a classic teachable moment: when dysphagia and choking don't behave like mechanical or reflux disease, think central causes — and think Chiari.

## Why equal solids-and-liquids matters

Most ENT or GI problems produce dysphagia that’s consistency-dependent — solids first for mechanical obstruction, solids > liquids for structural lesions, and reflux/laryngopharyngeal reflux often produces intermittent throat-clearing, globus or cough but usually with other reflux signs. When both solids and liquids are equally affected, especially with normal scopes, that pattern points toward a neurogenic or bulbar cause.

In this case the pattern was reinforced by:
- multiple normal flexible laryngoscopies and upper GI endoscopies
- normal pulmonary imaging and PFTs
- documented severe oxygen desaturations without dyspnoea (suggesting impaired automatic respiratory drive)
- chronic occipital/vertex headaches and scalp allodynia — red flags for posterior fossa pathology

## Chiari I: the "missing" diagnosis that explains dysphagia and hypoventilation

Chiari I malformation is displacement of cerebellar tonsils ≥5 mm below the foramen magnum. Symptomatic Chiari (Chiari syndrome) commonly gives Valsalva-triggered occipital headache, but lower cranial nerve dysfunction and sleep-disordered breathing are well-described and under-recognized presentations.

Pathophysiology relevant to swallowing and breathing:
- Tonsillar descent compresses/irritates medullary centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei (IX–XII) → neurogenic dysphagia (often affects solids and liquids similarly), impaired gag, palatal weakness, aspiration risk.
- Brainstem compression and CSF flow obstruction disrupt automatic respiratory control → central apnoeas, loss of ventilatory response to hypercapnia/hypoxia, and sometimes profound desaturation without dyspnoea (acquired central hypoventilation/Ondine-like picture).
- Voluntary breathing (cortical drive) may remain intact, so patients can breathe when awake and instructed but 'forget' to breathe during sleep or distraction.

## Bedside clues that should make you stop chasing GERD/ENT and order neuroimaging

Look for combinations of these features in a patient with chronic or intermittent choking/dysphagia:
- Dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally
- Repeated normal flexible laryngoscopy and upper GI endoscopy
- No fatigability or fatigue pattern to suggest myasthenia gravis (and negative MG testing)
- Unexplained nocturnal choking, gasping, or recurrent unexplained desaturations — especially if the patient reports little or no dyspnoea
- Occipital or vertex headaches, especially post-trauma or Valsalva-related, or scalp allodynia/occipital neuralgia features
- Other lower cranial nerve signs: palatal weakness, nasal regurgitation, hoarseness, tongue atrophy/fasciculations, diminished gag
- Evidence of central sleep-disordered breathing on sleep study (high proportion of central events) or home oximetry showing deep desaturations

A single normal scope or two do not exclude a central cause.

## Practical approach — what to do next

1. Reframe the history: document consistency independence (solids = liquids), nocturnal timing, and presence/absence of dyspnoea during events.
2. Re-examine cranial nerve function carefully: gag, palate elevation, tongue movement/atrophy, gag reflex (noting its limitations), observe speech and cough strength.
3. If ENT/GI workup is unrevealing and the pattern fits neurogenic dysphagia or central hypoventilation, order brain and cervical MRI with attention to the craniocervical junction (sagittal T1/T2).
4. Arrange objective breathing assessment: ideally a full in-lab polysomnogram with EEG + capnography. If not available, type III studies and overnight oximetry may be suggestive but can underestimate severity.
5. Multidisciplinary coordination: involve neurosurgery, sleep medicine, and speech/swallow therapy early. Posterior fossa decompression can reverse symptoms in many symptomatic Chiari I patients.
6. Monitor safety while workup proceeds: aspiration precautions, consider speech-language pathology for swallow strategies, and if recurrent severe desaturation occurs, consider temporary supplemental oxygen or earlier neurosurgical evaluation.

## Clinical caveats and nuances

- Imaging interpretation matters: prior CTs can miss a crowded foramen magnum. Always review prior imaging yourself if the clinical picture is discordant with prior reports.
- Not every tonsillar descent is symptomatic. Correlate imaging with the clinical syndrome — Chiari is a radiologic finding; "Chiari syndrome" is the symptomatic diagnosis.
- Sleep studies without EEG can markedly undercount events; type III devices calculate AHI using recording time (including wake) and may underestimate disease severity.
- The absence of dyspnoea despite severe hypoxaemia is a red flag for brainstem involvement impairing afferent interoceptive signaling.

## Take-home messages

- Equal difficulty with solids and liquids + normal laryngoscopy/endoscopy should pivot you toward neurogenic dysphagia and brainstem pathology, not GERD/structural ENT causes.
- Look for associated red flags: nocturnal choking/desaturation without dyspnoea, occipital/vertex headaches, lower cranial nerve signs.
- Order MRI brain/cervical spine when clinical suspicion for Chiari or brainstem compression exists; prior normal CT or scopes don't rule it out.
- Full polysomnography with capnography is needed to accurately detect central hypoventilation; type III studies can underestimate severity.
- Early multidisciplinary involvement (neurosurgery, sleep medicine, speech/swallow) can change management — posterior fossa decompression is potentially curative for symptomatic Chiari.

If you’re seeing a patient who "keeps choking" but ENT and GI have nothing to offer, take a step back: the problem may be higher up — in the posterior fossa — and missing it can cost years of symptoms and serious respiratory events.



## The Silent Desaturator: Central Hypoventilation Without Dyspnea in Chiari I

What makes this case striking is how profoundly dangerous physiology hid behind normal ENT and pulmonary work-ups for years. A woman in her early 40s developed nocturnal and meal-time choking, chronic occipital/vertex pain, and — most alarmingly — repeated episodes of oxygen saturation falling into the 70s without subjective breathlessness. Multiple scopes and lung tests were unrevealing. The unifying clue? Review of old imaging and a modern MRI revealed an 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent consistent with Chiari I malformation compressing the brainstem. A type III sleep study showed mostly central events. In short: acquired central hypoventilation from Chiari I — a structural “Ondine’s curse” that literally made her forget to breathe.

Why this matters right now
- Profound hypoxaemia without dyspnoea is not always “anxiety” or artefact. When automatic respiratory drive is impaired by brainstem dysfunction, patients can desaturate deeply without air hunger or apparent distress.
- Such patients are easily missed in EDs and clinics because pulse oximetry alone, normal awake blood gases, normal pulmonary testing, and non-diagnostic endoscopies or laryngoscopies give false reassurance.
- Chiari I is common on imaging but symptomatic Chiari (Chiari syndrome) is rare — the diagnosis requires integrating symptoms (occipital headaches, Valsalva-provoked pain, lower cranial nerve signs, dysphagia, sleep-disordered breathing) with imaging.

Pathophysiology in one paragraph
Automatic breathing is controlled in the medulla and pons; cortical pathways mediate voluntary breathing. Compression of the brainstem at the cervicomedullary junction can disrupt chemoreceptive pathways and respiratory rhythm generators (and afferent interoceptive signals that generate dyspnoea). The result: voluntary breathing intact when the patient is awake and focused, but automatic drive fails during sleep or distraction — central apnoeas, hypoventilation, and dangerous desaturation without the normal sensation of air hunger.

Where the typical work-up fails
- ENT/GI investigations for choking concentrate on local mechanical causes; neurogenic dysphagia (solids and liquids equally, normal scopes) should prompt a neurologic evaluation.
- Routine pulmonary testing (PFTs, awake ABG) can be normal because automatic failure predominates during sleep/distraction.
- Home pulse oximetry may reveal alarming events but can be dismissed as artefact; repeated documented desaturations to 70s or 80s merit investigation, not reassurance.
- Type III sleep studies (no EEG) can markedly underestimate AHI and miss sleep architecture/context — they calculate AHI over recording time rather than true sleep time, diluting metrics.

Practical monitoring and diagnostic priorities
- If you suspect central hypoventilation or brainstem dysfunction:
  - Get full MRI brain/cervicomedullary junction imaging — review for tonsillar descent, CSF spaces at the foramen magnum, syrinx, or other mass effect. Re-review old CTs — subtle crowding can be missed.
  - Do a type I polysomnogram with full EEG, respiratory channels, oximetry, capnography (end-tidal CO2 or transcutaneous CO2) and video. Capnography is crucial: oxygen can mask hypoventilation; EtCO2/TcCO2 tracks ventilation directly.
  - Consider overnight oximetry only as a screening tool — it cannot discriminate central vs obstructive events nor quantify hypoventilation.
  - Use daytime and nocturnal EtCO2/TcCO2 when possible; daytime ABG may be normal.
  - Multidisciplinary input: neurosurgery, sleep medicine, ENT/neurology, respiratory therapy.

ED/clinic pitfalls and how to avoid them
- Pitfall: Seeing profound desaturation and giving oxygen and sending the patient home after “symptoms resolved.” Oxygen corrects hypoxaemia but not hypoventilation; it can also remove the obvious clinical clue and delay definitive diagnosis.
  - What to do instead: If unexplained severe desaturation (especially without dyspnoea), initiate monitoring with capnography if available, arrange urgent sleep/neurology/neurosurgery follow-up, and consider observation rather than immediate discharge.
- Pitfall: Attributing asymptomatic desaturation to anxiety or poor oximeter technique without corroboration.
  - What to do instead: Re-check with medical-grade oximetry, obtain simultaneous clinical observations, and pursue objective screening (overnight oximetry and capnography), and review prior imaging.
- Pitfall: Overreliance on type III sleep testing for central events.
  - What to do instead: If central events suspected or oxygen drops are profound, arrange a full polysomnogram with capnography.

Management considerations anchored to this case
- Definitive therapy for symptomatic Chiari I with brainstem compression is posterior fossa decompression; respiratory symptoms can improve or reverse post-op.
- Some patients may elect conservative management initially; if so, institute structured monitoring (regular oximetry/capnography at night, symptom diary, neuro follow-up) and counsel about red flags (recurrent profound desaturation, syncope, progressive bulbar signs).
- Acute management of severe hypoventilation episodes may require assisted ventilation strategies (non-invasive ventilation with careful monitoring of ventilation) pending definitive evaluation.
- Avoid giving supplemental oxygen as the only intervention for unexplained central hypoventilation — it can mask hypercapnia and delay recognition of ventilatory failure.

Clinical pearls
- Think “neurologic” when dysphagia affects both solids and liquids and ENT/GI testing is normal.
- Absence of dyspnoea does not rule out life-threatening hypoxaemia — damaged ascending interoceptive pathways can abolish the sensation of breathlessness.
- EtCO2 or transcutaneous CO2 is the monitoring parameter you want when ventilation — not oxygenation — is the problem.
- Review old imaging if the timeline suggests prior head trauma or earlier unexplained headaches; crowding at the foramen magnum on an old CT can be the missed clue.
- Type III sleep studies can undercount central events; a low AHI does not exclude clinically significant central hypoventilation.

Take-home messages
- Profound, unperceived hypoxaemia can reflect brainstem-mediated loss of automatic breathing — consider Chiari I in the right clinical context.
- Prioritize full polysomnography with EEG and capnography (EtCO2 or TcCO2) over pulse oximetry alone when central hypoventilation is suspected.
- Don’t dismiss recurrent, reproducible desaturations as anxiety — re-evaluate with objective monitoring and neuroimaging, and involve neurosurgery/sleep medicine early.
- Oropharyngeal dysphagia with normal scopes should trigger neurological evaluation (and MRI review for Chiari or brainstem lesions).
- If you encounter unexplained nocturnal or distraction-related desaturation, treat oxygen as a temporizing measure and investigate ventilation (capnography) and structural causes promptly.
