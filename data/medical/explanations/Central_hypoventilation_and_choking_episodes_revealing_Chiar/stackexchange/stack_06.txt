Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

### [Differential Diagnosis] List three non-neurologic conditions that should be excluded in an adult with episodic hypoxemia and choking before pursuing central causes of hypoventilation.

Before invoking central hypoventilation, exclude common non‑neurologic explanations that can produce intermittent desaturation and choking. Three important ones are:

1) Upper‑airway mechanical obstruction or oropharyngeal dysphagia. Structural lesions (vocal‑cord paralysis, supraglottic collapse, pharyngeal pouch, neoplasm, cricopharyngeal achalasia) or severe obstructive events can cause choking and abrupt desaturations that mimic central events. Distinguish these by direct visualization (flexible nasolaryngoscopy, repeat videofluoroscopic swallowing study or FEES), targeted ENT evaluation, and, if indicated, neck imaging.

2) Sleep‑related breathing disorder with obstructive sleep apnoea (or mixed apnoea). OSA commonly causes nocturnal gasping, choking arousals and profound desaturations; it may coexist with or be mistaken for central events on limited testing. A full type I polysomnogram with EEG, airflow, respiratory effort and capnography separates obstructive from central events, identifies sleep architecture and determines severity.

3) Pulmonary or aspiration pathology (including intermittent aspiration from reflux or dysphagia, COPD/bronchospasm, pulmonary embolism or transient airway compromise). Recurrent microaspiration or intermittent aspiration events produce coughing, desaturations and normal awake spirometry between episodes. Exclude by chest imaging (CXR, CT when appropriate), targeted pulmonary function testing, bronchoscopy or sputum/aspiration studies, and by evaluating for reflux/aspiration with pH/impedance testing or trial of aspiration precautions.

In practice, use targeted history (positional relation, loud snoring, witnessed apnoeas, daytime somnolence, reflux symptoms, cough with meals), focused bedside exams, and directed tests (laryngoscopy/FEES, videofluoroscopic swallow, chest imaging, full polysomnography) to exclude these causes before concluding a primary brainstem disorder.
