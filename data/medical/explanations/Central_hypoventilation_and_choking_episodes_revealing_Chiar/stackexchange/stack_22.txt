Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the approximate population prevalence of radiographic Chiari malformation type I in adults versus the prevalence of symptomatic cases, and how does this inform decision-making?

Radiographic Chiari I is surprisingly common compared with clinically important disease. Contemporary imaging series report cerebellar tonsillar descent meeting the conventional 5 mm threshold in about 1% of adults, whereas symptomatic Chiari (Chiari “syndrome” causing headaches, lower‑cranial nerve findings, myelopathy or sleep/respiratory dysfunction attributable to the malformation) affects roughly 0.1% of the general population. In other words, roughly ten times more people have the anatomical finding on MRI than will have clinically relevant disease.

How this informs decision‑making: the presence of tonsillar descent on an MRI is a radiographic observation, not an automatic indication for surgery. Management must be driven by the clinical phenotype rather than the sagittal measurement alone. Key clinical principles are (1) correlate symptoms with plausible pathophysiology — classic Valsalva‑provoked occipital headaches, progressive myelopathy, new lower‑cranial‑nerve dysfunction, syringomyelia, or objective respiratory control abnormalities (central sleep apnoea, unattended hypoxaemia or hypoventilation) are reasons to consider definitive neurosurgical referral; (2) recognise that degree of descent correlates imperfectly with symptoms — small posterior fossa, CSF flow obstruction and objective evidence of brainstem or cord compromise (syrinx, CSF flow block on cine MRI) are more important than an absolute millimetre value; and (3) for incidental, asymptomatic radiographic CMI, watchful waiting with clinical follow‑up and repeat imaging only if new symptoms develop is appropriate.

Put simply: find the lesion that explains the patient’s complaints. Treat symptomatic or progressive disease (or objective physiologic compromise such as significant central hypoventilation or growing syrinx) — often with posterior fossa decompression — but avoid operating on isolated radiographic descent without concordant clinical or objective evidence of compromise.
