Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

### [Pathophysiology] What is the proposed mechanism for severe oxygen desaturation occurring without the subjective sensation of air hunger in brainstem pathology such as Chiari malformation type I?

Severe desaturation without the subjective feeling of air hunger in brainstem lesions such as Chiari I is best understood as a disconnection syndrome: the primary sensors and automatic generators of breathing are impaired while the cortical machinery that creates the conscious sensation of dyspnoea is functionally isolated from their signals.

Normally, hypoxaemia and hypercapnia are detected by peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid bodies via glossopharyngeal and vagal afferents) and central chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors in the medulla and lower brainstem. These afferent signals are processed in the nucleus tractus solitarius and adjacent medullary centres, which both drive automatic respiratory rhythm generators and send ascending interoceptive information through brainstem relays to thalamus and cortical regions (notably the anterior insula and anterior cingulate). The subjective feeling of “air hunger” arises when those cortical interoceptive regions receive and interpret the afferent input about rising CO2/low O2 and increased respiratory drive.

In Chiari I with tonsillar descent and crowding at the foramen magnum, three key pathophysiologic events explain profound, unperceived desaturation. First, compression or dysfunction of medullary respiratory centres produces loss of automatic drive (central hypoventilation), so ventilation fails during sleep or distraction. Second, compression can interrupt the ascending afferent pathways that carry chemoreceptor and mechanoreceptor signals to the cortex, so the brain never constructs the conscious percept of dyspnoea even as gas exchange worsens. Third, hypoxaemia per se is a relatively weaker conscious stimulus than hypercapnia; if hypercapnic drive is blunted or PaCO2 remains near normal, patients may be severely hypoxemic without strong ventilatory or perceptual responses. The net result is preserved voluntary breathing (cortical pathways intact) but absent automatic control and absent interoceptive awareness — clinically, “forgetting to breathe” and profound desaturation without air hunger.

Clinical principle: a marked dissociation between physiologic abnormality and symptom severity should prompt consideration of lesions that disrupt brainstem respiratory control or afferent interoceptive pathways, and it mandates objective monitoring (oximetry/ETCO2, sleep study with EEG) rather than relying on patient-reported dyspnoea.
