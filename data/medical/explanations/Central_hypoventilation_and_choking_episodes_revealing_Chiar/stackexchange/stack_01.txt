Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

### [Clinical Pearls] How is Chiari malformation type I defined radiographically in adults, and what is the approximate prevalence of radiographic CMI versus symptomatic Chiari syndrome in the general population?

Clinical answer (Clinical Pearl)

Radiographic definition in adults
- Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is classically defined on sagittal MRI by caudal descent of the cerebellar tonsils of ≥5 mm below the foramen magnum (the basion–opisthion line). In practice this is assessed on midline T1/T2 sagittal images and supplemented when needed by posterior‑fossa morphometry and CSF flow (cine) sequences to document crowding/obstruction. 
- Caveat: the 5‑mm threshold is a pragmatic cutoff, not an absolute marker of disease. There is a spectrum of tonsillar position and posterior‑fossa volume; mild descent may be an incidental anatomic variant and requires clinical correlation.

Prevalence — radiographic versus symptomatic
- Contemporary imaging studies find radiographic CMI in roughly 1% of adults. 
- Symptomatic Chiari syndrome (the clinical constellation attributable to CMI) is much less common — on the order of 0.1% of the general population. 
- Put another way, only a minority (~one‑tenth) of adults with radiographic tonsillar descent have a symptomatic Chiari syndrome that warrants intervention.

Clinical implication (why this matters)
- Radiographic tonsillar descent per se does not mandate surgery. Management must be driven by the clinical syndrome (headache pattern, brainstem or lower cranial‑nerve signs, syringomyelia, documented CSF outflow obstruction, or complications such as sleep‑related breathing disorders/central hypoventilation as in this case). 
- The presented patient had an unequivocal anatomical lesion (11 mm descent) plus compatible brainstem compression and respiratory dysfunction — a constellation that supports considering decompression despite the general rule that many radiographic CMI are asymptomatic.
