Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Chapter 5: ## Outcome, Follow‑up, and Clinical Lessons

The patient ultimately elected conservative management while she considered neurosurgical decompression. Her short‑term plan, agreed with the multidisciplinary team, combined active surveillance and targeted investigations: monthly clinic reviews to document symptom frequency and new neurological signs, quarterly overnight pulse oximetry to capture intermittent desaturations, and annual MRI of the brain and cervical spine to monitor tonsillar position, CSF flow and to screen for syringomyelia. A definitive type I polysomnography (PSG) incorporating EEG, video and objective CO₂ monitoring (end‑tidal or transcutaneous) was arranged at an external sleep centre but remained pending at the time of this report. These steps reflect a pragmatic compromise when a patient defers surgery while still addressing the highest short‑risk concerns (unrecognised, severe nocturnal hypoxaemia and progressive bulbar symptoms).

Over eight months of structured follow‑up the team sought objective triggers that would prompt expedited intervention: increasing frequency or severity of choking episodes, recurrent daytime or nocturnal desaturations to dangerously low saturations, development of new brainstem or long‑tract neurological deficits, or radiographic progression such as syrinx formation. The rationale for close monitoring is twofold. First, CMI with brainstem compression can produce intermittent, potentially life‑threatening central hypoventilation and “silent” hypoxaemia (marked desaturations without dyspnoea), so objective surveillance is essential. Second, posterior fossa decompression can improve or reverse respiratory and bulbar dysfunction when brainstem compression is the mechanism; therefore identifying deterioration early preserves the option of timely surgical intervention.

Several high‑yield clinical lessons emerge from this case that are directly applicable to practice. The most important diagnostic pearl is that persistent choking or dysphagia affecting both solids and liquids, particularly when repeated endoscopic evaluations are normal, should prompt neurological assessment and dedicated cranio‑cervical imaging (MRI of the brain and cervical spine with attention to the foramen magnum and CSF spaces). Neurogenic dysphagia classically causes mixed‑consistency impairment and may be missed by repeated gastrointestinal-focused testing. In this patient the definitive clue was imaging evidence of cerebellar tonsillar descent with crowding at the foramen magnum, which explained the dysphagia through lower cranial nerve and brainstem dysfunction.

Sleep testing nuances are critical when central hypoventilation is suspected. Type III cardiorespiratory studies (home or portable monitors that record airflow, effort and oximetry but lack EEG) can underestimate the severity of sleep‑disordered breathing because they measure events over total recording time rather than true sleep time and lack CO₂ monitoring. When clinical concern exists for central apnoeas or hypoventilation—especially with unexplained nocturnal desaturation, preserved voluntary breathing, or lack of dyspnoea—full in‑lab type I PSG with EEG, video and end‑tidal or transcutaneous CO₂ monitoring should be prioritized. CO₂ measurements are important for detecting hypoventilation that may not produce frequent apnoeas but leads to sustained hypercapnia and oxygen desaturation.

This case also illustrates the value of imaging vigilance. Re‑review of a historically reported “normal” CT revealed cranio‑cervical crowding that had been overlooked initially; this single finding redirected the diagnostic trajectory. Whenever symptoms are unexplained or discordant with prior test results, clinicians should retrieve and re‑examine prior imaging (ideally with neuroradiology input) rather than assuming absence of pathology. In the craniocervical junction, non‑contrast CTs can show crowding but MRI with sagittal T1/T2 and CSF flow sequences provides definitive assessment for Chiari malformation and associated syrinx.

Clinically, avoid diagnostic overshadowing that attributes objective hypoxaemia or recurrent choking solely to anxiety without completing appropriate investigations. Anxiety may co‑exist with organic disease and can amplify symptoms, but profound hypoxaemia (saturations in the 70s–80s) documented objectively merits thorough cardiopulmonary and neurological evaluation. In this patient, repeated emergency visits had initially dismissed desaturation episodes as anxiety‑related despite objective low saturations; later neurosurgical and sleep assessments reframed these events as manifestations of structural brainstem compromise.

Effective care requires interprofessional coordination. Family medicine or primary care often anchors the diagnostic process and coordinates referrals to neurology and neurosurgery for structural evaluation, sleep medicine for definitive PSG and CO₂ monitoring, otolaryngology and gastroenterology to exclude local aerodigestive pathology, and speech‑language pathology for formal swallowing evaluation and rehabilitative strategies. Pulmonology should be involved when significant nocturnal hypoventilation is identified to consider nocturnal ventilatory support if indicated pending or instead of surgery. Early involvement of neuroradiology improves the chance that subtle cranio‑cervical abnormalities are recognised.

Prognostically, symptomatic CMI with demonstrable brainstem compression behaves heterogeneously. Some patients experience marked symptomatic improvement after posterior fossa decompression, including resolution of sleep‑disordered breathing and dysphagia, reflecting reversible compression of respiratory and lower cranial nerve centres. Others have incomplete recovery, especially if damage has progressed or a syrinx is present. Conversely, conservative management requires vigilant surveillance because symptoms can progress insidiously. Therefore, decisions about surgery should balance current symptom burden, objective physiological impairment, imaging severity, surgical risk and patient preferences.

For practical clinical use, a stepwise approach reduces diagnostic delay in patients with unexplained desaturation or neurogenic dysphagia. Start with a focused history (characterise choking episodes, sleep‑related symptoms, preserved voluntary breathing versus automatic failure, and precipitating events), targeted physical and neurological examination, pulse oximetry or overnight oximetry to document events, basic pulmonary evaluation (chest imaging, spirometry), and directed ENT/GI assessment including a modified barium swallow when dysphagia is present. If initial tests are unrevealing but symptoms persist, obtain cranio‑cervical MRI with CSF flow sequences and arrange full in‑lab PSG with CO₂ monitoring if nocturnal hypoxaemia or central events are suspected. If MRI shows CMI with brainstem crowding or syrinx, involve neurosurgery to discuss decompression versus continued surveillance, using objective desaturation trends and PSG/CO₂ data to guide urgency.

In summary, this case highlights that Chiari I malformation can present primarily with choking, dysphagia and acquired central hypoventilation that may be subtle or “silent.” Careful re‑examination of prior imaging, use of appropriate sleep studies including CO₂ monitoring, avoidance of premature psychogenic labeling, and timely multidisciplinary coordination are the key practices that reduce diagnostic delay and optimise outcomes.
