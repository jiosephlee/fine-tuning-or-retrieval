Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Approach

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is classically framed by radiographic findings, but it is the symptom cluster—the “Chiari syndrome”—that drives clinical recognition. The case above illustrates a common and important theme: patients may present for years with apparently isolated ENT or gastrointestinal complaints before the unifying diagnosis is recognised. In this patient, recurrent choking, nocturnal gasping and profound oxygen desaturations were evaluated repeatedly with laryngoscopy and endoscopy and attributed to reflux or anxiety. It was only when the history was integrated with chronic occipital/vertex headache and a remote head injury that a neurological cause was suspected. This clinical pathway underlines two lessons that should guide initial evaluation: (1) equal difficulty with solids and liquids, nocturnal events, and disproportionate hypoxaemia without dyspnoea are red flags for a neurogenic process; and (2) a directed neurological review and reappraisal of prior imaging are often pivotal.

History-taking should therefore probe the pattern and timing of choking, swallowing and breathing complaints. Neurogenic dysphagia tends to affect all consistencies without a clear bolus-related obstruction; patients describe coughing, choking or nasal regurgitation with both solids and liquids, intermittent episodes, and sometimes silent aspiration. Ask specifically about nocturnal symptoms, witnessed apnoeas, daytime somnolence, or episodes of marked oxygen desaturation documented by home oximetry. Enquire about head or neck trauma, even if minor or remote, because CMI may be congenital, unmasked, or exacerbated after an injury. Document headache phenotype carefully: occipital or suboccipital pain, particularly if paroxysmal or related to Valsalva manoeuvres, should raise suspicion for craniocervical pathology; scalp allodynia or a localized “coin-sized” vertex pain—as in this patient—can coexist and point toward occipital nerve irritation or brainstem involvement. Finally, assess psychosocial consequences (avoidance of meals, anxiety, functional impairment) and safety risks such as driving, flying or exposure to sedatives, which may be dangerous in patients with impaired automatic respiration.

The targeted neurological review of systems and bedside examination are critical next steps. Screen for lower cranial nerve dysfunction: ask about dysphonia, nasal regurgitation, change in voice quality, choking on liquids, impaired taste in the posterior tongue, and coughing during swallowing. On exam, assess palate elevation and symmetry with phonation, observe the uvula for deviation, evaluate gag reflex (noting that its absence is nonspecific), inspect the tongue for atrophy or fasciculations and test tongue protrusion and lateralisation, and listen for breathy or nasal speech. Evaluate cough strength and voluntary sniff or breath-hold as crude tests of bulbar and respiratory muscle function. Look for cerebellar signs (limb dysmetria, truncal ataxia, gait impairment) and long-tract findings that might indicate syringomyelia or upper cervical cord involvement (dissociated sensory loss, hand weakness or atrophy). The combination of bulbar signs with headache or ataxia should prompt urgent neuroimaging.

Bedside physiological testing complements the history and exam. Pulse oximetry or continuous overnight oximetry can document intermittent desaturation episodes; portable capnography or end-tidal CO₂ monitoring is useful where available, because hypoventilation is more reliably captured by hypercapnia than by oxygen saturation alone. Basic pulmonary function testing—including spirometry and measurement of maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures (MIP/MEP)—helps exclude primary pulmonary or ventilatory muscle disease and provides baseline data should respiratory support become necessary. An arterial blood gas can detect daytime hypoventilation. Importantly, normal chest imaging and normal spirometry do not exclude central respiratory control disorders.

Interpretation of swallowing and airway examinations needs a neurological lens. Flexible laryngoscopy and upper gastrointestinal endoscopy often return normal results in patients with neurogenic dysphagia because the problem resides in coordination and central control rather than fixed anatomic obstruction. Dynamic studies—videofluoroscopic swallowing study (modified barium swallow) and fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES)—are more sensitive to neurogenic aspiration and dysfunction of the pharyngeal phase. When dysphagia is reported for both solids and liquids, or when aspiration is suspected without structural lesions, prioritise these dynamic studies and involve speech and language pathology early.

Nocturnal respiratory events merit early sleep medicine involvement. Polysomnography (type I attended study) with EEG, respiratory airflow and effort channels, pulse oximetry and capnography is the gold standard because it defines sleep time, differentiates central from obstructive events and quantifies hypoventilation. Be cautious interpreting type III studies (portable monitoring without EEG): absence of EEG typically underestimates event rates by using total recording time rather than true sleep time, and can obscure the severity of central hypoventilation. A predominance of central apnoeas, especially when accompanied by large oxygen desaturations without subjective dyspnoea, strongly suggests brainstem dysfunction rather than primary obstructive sleep apnoea.

Reexamination of prior neuroimaging and obtaining dedicated MRI of the posterior fossa and cervicomedullary junction are essential. CT scans performed for trauma are often read as “normal” unless the craniocervical junction is specifically assessed; subtle crowding at the foramen magnum can be overlooked. Sagittal MRI sequences allow measurement of tonsillar descent (commonly ≥5 mm below the foramen magnum for CMI) and assessment of CSF spaces, brainstem compression, and syringomyelia. Cine CSF flow studies can document CSF flow obstruction and help surgical planning. Because symptoms may be delayed after trauma, reviewing historical scans in light of evolving symptoms can be diagnostic, as in this patient.

A succinct differential diagnosis keeps the clinician from premature closure. Structural laryngeal or oesophageal lesions, gastroesophageal reflux with laryngopharyngeal reflux, and primary otolaryngologic disorders must be considered and excluded. Neuromuscular junction disease (eg, myasthenia gravis) or motor neuron disease can produce bulbar dysfunction but often demonstrate fatigability, diurnal variation, or EMG/antibody abnormalities. Primary pulmonary disease and restrictive ventilatory disorders must be excluded with imaging and pulmonary function testing. When choking, nocturnal apnoeas, occipital headaches and signs of lower cranial nerve or cerebellar dysfunction coexist, a central cause such as CMI or other posterior fossa mass becomes more likely.

Finally, the initial approach should incorporate safety planning and multidisciplinary referral. Patients with documented profound desaturations or suspected central hypoventilation require precautions: avoid sedatives, plan for supervised sleep studies, counsel about aviation and occupational risks, and consider early neurology/neurosurgery and sleep medicine consultation. Speech therapy and diet modification can mitigate aspiration risk while diagnostic evaluation proceeds. Early recognition of the pattern described here—equal-consistency dysphagia, nocturnal central events with severe desaturation without air hunger, concurrent occipital headache and a history of trauma—shortens the diagnostic delay and enables timely imaging and specialist referral that may be disease-modifying.
