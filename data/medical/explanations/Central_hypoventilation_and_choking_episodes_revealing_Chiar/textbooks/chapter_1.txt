Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Phenotype

The patient’s illness evolved over more than a decade, beginning with a minor motor-vehicle accident 14 years before presentation that was followed immediately by persistent occipital and vertex headaches. The bulbar and respiratory features developed later: she described a 7‑year history of recurrent choking episodes that emerged gradually and progressed in frequency and impact. This temporal pattern — acute onset of head pain after trauma followed by delayed emergence of bulbar and respiratory symptoms — is important because Chiari-related brainstem dysfunction may be present for years before producing prominent or recognised respiratory compromise.

Her choking episodes had several characteristic features. They occurred unpredictably during both meals and sleep, affected solids and liquids equally, and were dramatic (violent coughing and gasping) but never resulted in complete airway obstruction. Equal difficulty with solids and liquids argues against a purely structural or obstructive oesophageal lesion and instead points to a neurogenic swallowing disorder affecting the pharyngeal and brainstem swallowing centres. The events were sometimes nocturnal, awakening her from sleep with coughing or gasping, which raises concern for sleep-related impairment of airway protection and automatic respiratory control rather than only daytime behavioural or voluntary dysfunction.

A striking and diagnostically pivotal finding was recurrent, severe hypoxaemia captured by home pulse oximetry (SpO2 around 70%–80%) on multiple occasions, including a documented episode at an airport where supplemental oxygen promptly relieved symptoms. Importantly, many of these desaturation episodes were asymptomatic from the patient’s perspective: she did not experience the expected dyspnoea or air‑hunger despite profound hypoxaemia. This dissociation between profound hypoxaemia and lack of subjective breathlessness suggests disruption of afferent or central pathways that generate the conscious sensation of dyspnoea — a phenomenon that can occur with brainstem dysfunction and is a red flag for central respiratory control impairment.

Recognition of preserved voluntary breathing with impaired automatic breathing was a key clinical clue. Voluntary respiration is cortically mediated and can be intact even when brainstem automatic respiratory drives (that maintain breathing during sleep and distraction) are compromised. In this patient, wakeful voluntary breathing and daytime respiratory testing were largely normal, but breathing during sleep and during moments of distraction failed, producing central hypoventilation–type events. This clinical phenotype — intact voluntary respiratory capacity with loss of automatic drive during sleep or distraction — mirrors the presentation of acquired central hypoventilation (sometimes referred to historically as “Ondine’s curse”) and should prompt evaluation for structural brainstem or craniocervical junction pathology.

Despite the severity of her subjective and objective events, repeated evaluations by otolaryngology and gastroenterology (three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper endoscopies) were normal, and pulmonary investigations (imaging and pulmonary function tests) showed no intrinsic lung disease. These repeatedly normal ENT/GI and pulmonary studies contributed to delayed diagnosis: when standard investigations fail to identify an explanation for dysphagia and unexplained hypoxaemia, clinicians must broaden the differential to include neurological causes — particularly when bulbar symptoms coexist with sleep-related respiratory disturbance.

The headache phenotype added another distinctive dimension to her presentation. She reported constant occipital and a coin-sized vertex pain with marked cutaneous allodynia of the scalp; even light touch or the weight of hair provoked pain, and she obtained partial relief by cutting her hair short. Such focal occipital or vertex pain with scalp hypersensitivity can reflect occipital neuralgia or other cranial neuralgias and may coexist with posterior fossa pathology. While not diagnostic of Chiari malformation on its own, this pattern of focal pain and allodynia supports the idea of chronic craniocervical irritation or compression and provides an additional clinical clue that the source of symptoms may be intracranial.

The prolonged course and the social and psychological toll were prominent: fear of choking led to avoidance of restaurants and public meals, progressive social withdrawal and considerable emotional distress. This emphasises that atypical presentations with episodic, frightening respiratory events often produce significant functional impairment even when overt neurologic signs are subtle, and that timely diagnosis has important quality‑of‑life implications.

Taken together, the phenotype in this case — long‑standing occipital/vertex pain after head trauma, progressive neurogenic dysphagia affecting all consistencies, nocturnal arousals with coughing/gasping, recurrent asymptomatic profound desaturation, preserved daytime voluntary respiration, and repeatedly negative ENT/pulmonary workups — should raise suspicion for brainstem compression at the craniocervical junction. In this patient, that constellation ultimately led to re‑examination of prior imaging and to the diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I as the unifying explanation for her symptoms.
