Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 3: ## Differential Diagnosis of Large Cystic Abdominal Masses in the Second Trimester

The coexistence of two massive cystic lesions in a pregnant patient raises a diagnostic challenge that requires integration of clinical features, serial laboratory trends, and the subtleties of imaging performed in the gravid abdomen. This patient’s presentation of severe upper‑quadrant pain, dyspnea, and a palpable hard mass in the pelvis, coupled with serial stone‑like elevation of CA‑125, guides the differential toward entities that can manifest as unilocular cystic enlargements with variable vascularity while preserving a benign course throughout the pregnancy.

**Decidualised endometrioma** is the most common benign explanation for a cystic adnexal mass acquired during gestation. On ultrasound, these lesions appear as well‑delimited unilocular cysts with homogeneous fluid; they may harbour internal echoes (“shading”) when hemorrhagic. On MRI, the cyst fluid is typically T1‑hyperintense if blood‑product has accumulated, and T2‑signals are homogeneous and low‑to‑intermediate due to iron content, with a thin capsule and no solid enhancing nodules. The patient’s upper‑standing mass displayed regular margins and peripheral vascularity on duplex study, consistent with a solid periphery that could represent a decidualised stromal capsule. Persistently elevated CA‑125 values (41.8 U/mL at 21 weeks GA versus the normal second‑trimester median < 14 U/mL) also suit this entity, as CA‑125 rises physiologically in early pregnancy and can be further increased by ongoing endometriotic activity. In this setting, the pain and mild dyspnea can be explained by mass effect on the diaphragm and, potentially, by localized inflammation or torsion of the cystic mass, while the low‑grade fever could reflect adjacent inflammation secondary to a necrotic cyst wall.

**Cystic or degenerating leiomyoma** provides a compelling alternative because uterine fibroids may undergo cystic degeneration, especially in pregnancy when hormonal milieu and rapid growth predispose to ischemia and infarction. A pedunculated intramural‑subserosal fibroid presents on ultrasonography as a heterogeneous, well‑defined mass with a clear interface to the myometrium; Doppler may reveal a feeding vessel. MRI demonstrates a thick fibrous capsule and variable fluid content; T2‑hypointense fibrous septa frequently intersect the cystic cavity and are best seen on fat‑suppressed sequences. The patient’s cranial mass was described as a pedunculated, taut‑elastic leiomyoma, complete with a twisted pedicle and a vascular base. Ischemic necrosis at the pedicle explains the transient fever; the histology confirmed fibroid origin with necrotic debris. In this context, the severe dyspnea is a mechanical consequence of an 18 cm mass that displaced the uterus upward, compressing the diaphragm between the enlarged placenta and the myoma.

**Ovarian cystic lesions** such as functional cysts, hemorrhagic cysts or benign cystadenomas remain a possibility. Functional cysts are typically simple, thin‑walled, and collapse if drained; hemorrhagic cysts contain internal echoes and may show a mixed T1/T2 signal reflecting age‑related hemoglobin breakdown. Cystadenomas are usually larger, contain septations and occasionally focal mural nodules. In this patient, the lower mass’s apparent continuity with a posterior subserosal myoma on both ultrasound and MRI argues against an independent ovarian origin, although the initial sonographic suggestion of an ovarian mass underscores the difficulty of distinguishing adnexal from uterine lesions when the uterus is massively enlarged. The lack of ascites and the absence of a solid enhancing nodule in magnetic resonance imaging further reduce the likelihood of an ovarian tumor.

Likewise, **rare cystic entities**—pseudomyxoma peritonei, Meigs syndrome, epidermoid cysts—can be considered for completeness. Pseudomyxoma peritonei presents with multicystic ascites and mucinous debris often accompanied by peritoneal implants; Meigs syndrome is characterized by the triad of ovarian fibroma, ascites, and pleural effusion; epidermoid cysts show a well‑circumscribed, irregularly shaped cyst with internal fat or keratin debris and frequently present in the ovary or para‑ovarian region. None of the clinical signs in the index case (absent ascites, pleural effusion, or characteristic imaging features) support these diagnoses, yet a high index of suspicion must be maintained because their presentation may be deceptively benign in early detection.

Distinguishing **benign from malignant** attenuation hinges on the appearance of solid components, border irregularity, rapid mass growth, and tumour marker profile. Malignant adnexal masses typically show irregular or nodular solid areas, thick septations, papillary projections, and ascites. They also produce markedly elevated markers: CA‑125 > 100 U/mL in ovarian carcinoma, albeit any value > 35 U/mL in the setting of a cystic mass warrants closer scrutiny. The masses in this patient possessed a peripheral vascular border and, in the cranial mass, solid endpoints within the fibroid scar, yet there were neither irregular margins nor nodularity on MRI nor a sharp rise in CA‑125 beyond early pregnancy norms, favouring a benign process.

Finally, **symptom correlation** can be informative. Severe localized pain often suggests torsion, necrosis, or infection of the cyst; dyspnea in the second trimester is more likely mechanical, linked to the mass compressing the diaphragm and reducing lung compliance; a fever or leucocytosis may point to ischemic necrosis or superimposed infection. Here, the patient’s worsening pain, dyspnea, and a small but significant fever resolved with analgesia and antibiotics, underscoring a mechanical rather than metastatic origin. The growth rate observed on serial imaging, from 18 cm to a similar size over several weeks, points to a benign fibroid or decidualised cyst that has slowly expanded with uterine enlargement.

In summary, the differential for large cystic abdominal masses in mid‑pregnancy hinges on imaging rhythm of the lesions (unilocular, septated, or complex), their relationship to the myometrium, the presence of solid components or irregular margins, and laboratory cues (serial CA‑125, other tumour markers). A combination of ultrasound, non‑contrast MRI, and careful clinical monitoring remained central to confirming the eventual pathologic diagnosis of a pedunculated, necrotic leiomyoma in the cranial, and a cystic‑degenerating subserosal fibroid in the caudal lesion. This thoughtful evaluation guided the decision to surgically remove only the high‑risk cranial mass while preserving the pregnancy and avoiding overtreatment.
