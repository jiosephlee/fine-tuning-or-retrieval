Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 1: ## Initial Clinical Presentation and Immediate Assessment in Pregnancy

A 41‑year‑old nulliparous woman with a body mass index of 33 presented at 21 weeks of gestation for a sudden episode of upper‑abdominal and pelvic pain accompanied by mild dyspnea. On the Visual Analogue Scale she rated the abdominal and pelvic discomfort at four to five out of ten. She was otherwise in a first‑trimester routine screening and her non‑invasive prenatal testing had confirmed a low risk for aneuploidy.  

On arrival to the emergency department her vital signs were reassuring: her heart rate was 74 bpm, blood pressure 100/60 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation 97 % on room air. The initial laboratory workup revealed a hemoglobin of 9.9 g/dL and a ferritin level of 25 µg/L, indicating iron‑deficiency anemia; a marginally elevated C‑reactive protein (CRP) of 1.39 mg/dL and a leukocytosis of 11.3 × 10^3 /µL (neutrophils 9.27 × 10^3 /µL) were seen, consistent with pregnancy‑related physiological changes but also raising the possibility of infection.  Urine culture was negative for pyuria at that time.  

The abdominal examination was notable for a markedly enlarged abdomen that exceeded the gestational size, with a hardened, taut mass occupying the left upper quadrant and extending into the uterine fundus. The mass was tender to palpation. A bimanual vaginal examination identified a fixed, rounded, hard mass in the lower pelvis that partially bulged into the posterior fornix. No free fluid was palpable, and there were no signs of ascites on clinical exam.  

Respiratory assessment revealed no use of accessory muscles, normal breath sounds, and a mild dyspnea that likely reflected diaphragmatic displacement by the enlarged uterus and the large abdominal masses rather than intrinsic pulmonary pathology.  

In the context of a partially uterine‑compliant mass with associated mechanical symptoms, the immediate differential diagnosis was broadened to include (1) space‑occupying abdominal or pelvic masses (ovarian, uterine, or other pelvic pathology), (2) infectious or inflammatory processes creating fever and leukocytosis, and (3) obstructive gastrointestinal or urinary symptoms secondary to mass effect. The patient’s presentation, obstetric context, and vital stability directed the team toward a rapid, non‑invasive diagnostic work‑up—initially a focused obstetric ultrasonography, followed, if necessary, by a non‑contrast MRI—to delineate the nature, size, and vascularity of the masses while avoiding ionizing radiation.
