Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Workup in Pregnancy: Imaging and Tumor Markers

In pregnant patients presenting with unexplained abdominal pain and the impression of an intra‑abdominal mass, the diagnostic approach must prioritize fetal safety while providing sufficient diagnostic confidence to guide management.  The cornerstone of evaluation is the combination of sonography and, when necessary, non‑contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), accompanied by a targeted panel of serum tumor markers.  Computed tomography (CT) is avoided because of ionising radiation exposure, which carries a dose‑dependent risk for miscarriage and congenital malformations.

### Safe imaging options

**Ultrasound with colour Doppler** is the first‑line modality during all trimesters.  It is free of radiation, inexpensive, and can be rapidly performed in the emergency setting.  Colour Doppler adds functional information about vascularity, helping distinguish vascular pedicles (as seen in pedunculated leiomyomas) from avascular cystic lesions.

**Non‑contrast MRI** is the preferred advanced modality when sonographic findings are equivocal or when a precise delineation of the mass‑uterus interface is required.  MRI at <1.5‑Tesla field strength and without gadolinium offers excellent soft‑tissue contrast, multiplanar capability, and the ability to characterize fluid signal without exposing the fetus to ionising radiation.  Gadolinium contrast is contraindicated in pregnancy except in life‑threatening situations, due to the low‑level risk of fetal gadolinium deposition.

#### Contraindicated modality

**CT** scans deliver a cumulative fetal dose that exceeds the threshold for acceptable risk (<50 mSv). Consequently, CT is reserved only for scenarios where other modalities fail or for evaluation of life‑threatening complications such as intra‑abdominal bleeding or suspected malignancy that requires precise anatomical definition not achievable by US or conventional MRI.

### Ultrasound findings in the case

The obstetric ultrasound disclosed **two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins**.  The **upper lesion** measured 18 × 12 × 7 cm and extended from the left sub‑phrenic space to the uterine fundus.  The **lower lesion** measured 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm and appeared adherent to a posterior subserosal intramural myoma that demonstrated cystic degeneration (dimensions 58 × 71 mm).  Colour Doppler revealed **peripheral vascularisation** around the cyst walls, a feature associated with vascularised pedicles or myomatous tissue rather than simple serous fluid collections.  Importantly, the US could not clearly delineate a stalk or feeding vessel for the larger left‑sided mass because of the bulk of the gravid uterus and the expansile displacement of adjacent structures.

### MRI characteristics

The non‑contrast MRI confirmed the US impression, providing multiplanar resolution of the cyst walls and internal architecture.  Imaging sequences included T1‑weighted, T2‑weighted, and fat‑saturation (fat‑sat) techniques:

- **Wall thickness**: 1.4 cm, thicker than would be expected for a simple serous cyst, suggesting a fibrous capsule or myomatous origin.
- **Slicewise fluid content**: Inhomogeneous signal on both T1‑ and T2‑fat sat, indicating a mixture of fluid and degenerated tissue.
- **Focal solid components**: Hyperintense on T1‑ and T2‑weighted images within the cyst, raising suspicion for solid nuclei of fibroid tissue or blood‑stasis rather than malignant infiltration.
- **No gadolinium**: The absence of contrast precludes assessment of enhancement patterns; however, the lack of rapid contrast uptake is reasonably reassuring against highly vascular malignancies, although it is nonspecific.

The MRI also revealed **no definite cleavage plane** between the caudal cyst and the adherent posterior myoma, underscoring the challenge of differentiating a highly degenerated myoma from an adnexal cystic lesion.

### Interpretation of CA‑125 in pregnancy

CA‑125, typically elevated in epithelial ovarian cancers, is physiologically increased during pregnancy due to decidualisation of the endometrium and the presence of a large uterus.  Two prominent peaks have been documented:
- **First trimester** (median ≈ 23 U/mL)
- **Third trimester** (median ≈ 21 U/mL)

In the second trimester, the expected median value is lower (≈ 14 U/mL).  In this patient, CA‑125 incrementally rose from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier to 41.8 U/mL at presentation, exceeding the usual upper limit of 35 U/mL.  While an isolated elevation in the second trimester can be benign, a **rising trend** in the context of a large, vascularised cystic mass warrants consideration of a malignant or pseudo‑neoplastic process.  Nonetheless, CA‑125 alone cannot establish malignancy; its value lies in serial monitoring and integration with imaging.

### Other tumor markers

The following serologic tests were performed and remained within reference ranges:
- **CA 19‑9** (pancreatic and GI malignancy marker)
- **CA 15‑3** (breast cancer marker)
- **CEA** (colorectal and other GI cancers)
- **β‑2‑microglobulin** (lymphoproliferative disorders)
- **LDH** (cell turnover)

Normal results of these markers reduce the likelihood of systemic malignancy involving the abdomen, yet none are specific enough to exclude a localized ovarian carcinomatosis.

### Role of urine culture and bacterial infection

During the hospital stay a low‑grade fever ensued.  A nasopharyngeal PCR for SARS‑CoV‑2 was negative, but **urine cultures isolated Klebsiella pneumoniae**, a common uropathogen.  The presence of a urinary infection may explain postoperative rises in inflammatory markers (CRP, neutrophils) and could have contributed to the necrotic, perforating component of the large pedunculated leiomyoma, as suggested by the patient’s transient fever.  While infection does not affect tumor markers, it constitutes a clinical variable that necessitates antibiotic therapy and may influence the decision to proceed with surgery.

### Integrating imaging, markers, and clinical data

The imaging modality sequence—US followed by non‑contrast MRI—enabled a detailed assessment of cystic structure, wall thickness, vascularity, and myoma association.  The serial rise in CA‑125, coupled with the existence of large, vascularised lesions and the patient’s deteriorating clinical picture (pain, dyspnea), heightened concern for potential necrosis, torsion, or infection-driven complications.  In light of these findings, the multidisciplinary team chose to excise the cranial mass, which was intraoperatively found to be a pedunculated, cystic leiomyoma with a twisted pedicle.

The case demonstrates that a carefully orchestrated diagnostic workup—grounded in safe imaging and serial tumor marker assessment—can clarify the nature of abdominal masses in pregnancy and support timely, evidence‑based surgical intervention while preserving fetal safety.
