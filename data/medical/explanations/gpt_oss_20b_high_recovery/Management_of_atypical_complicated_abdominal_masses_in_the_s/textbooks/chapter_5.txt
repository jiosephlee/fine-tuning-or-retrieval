Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 5: ## Outcomes, Follow‑Up and Clinical Take‑Aways

**Maternal outcomes**  
The operative intervention resolved the acute complications that prompted surgery. Post‑myomectomy anemia improved markedly: pre‑operative hemoglobin fell to 7.5 g/dL, necessitating both intravenous iron (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) and two packed red blood cell transfusions, raising hemoglobin to 10.0 g/dL, a stable level for the remainder of the pregnancy. The intra‑operative and postoperative antibiotic strategy—cefazolin for prophylaxis followed by cefazolin plus clarithromycin guided by the urine culture—cleared the Klebsiella infection and prevented secondary peritoneal sepsis, as evidenced by negative cytology and culture of peritoneal fluid at follow‑up. The patient tolerated the surgery without obstetric morbidity and reported no subsequent abdominal discomfort. Breastfeeding commenced smoothly; the abrupt cessation of lactation has not been required to postpone further intervention on the residual myoma. Thus, the multidisciplinary approach to infection control, blood management, and hormonal support (progesterone and hydroxyprogesterone caproate) facilitated a safe postpartum recovery.

**Fetal outcomes**  
The infant was delivered at 37 weeks’ gestation, just two weeks early compared with the expected term, after a planned cesarean section through the previous laparotomy scar. Prematurity was elective rather than emergent, allowing for optimal antenatal monitoring. Neonatal growth measures were reassuring—the baby’s weight corresponded to the 86th percentile on WHO growth charts, matching the mother’s mid‑pregnancy fetal measurements. Apgar scores were normal, and the newborn required no intensive care. The fetus benefited from a prolonged intrauterine life, permitting catch‑up in weight after the cesarean delivery without compromising maternal safety.

**Residual myoma management**  
The lower, intramural‑subserosal cystic myoma, left untouched to avoid uterine injury during pregnancy, exhibited a small but clinically significant size reduction: from 9.5 cm pre‑operative to 7.5 cm at 10 weeks postpartum. This regression is consistent with the known transient shrinkage of subserosal leiomyomas during the lactation period, driven by hormonal withdrawal. Given the absence of symptoms and the presumed benign nature of the lesion, the obstetric‑gynecologic team elected to defer definitive surgery until the postpartum period, allowing for natural involution and providing the patient the option of watchful waiting. The residual myoma's regression also mitigated the obstruction risk during labor, thereby supporting the decision for an elective cesarean rather than forceps delivery.

**Clinical lessons**  
Several productively integrated lessons emerge from this case. First, meticulous multidisciplinary coordination—obstetricians, gynecologists, radiologists, anesthesiologists, and neonatologists—is vital when confronting atypical, large pelvic masses during pregnancy; shared decision‑making optimizes timing and modality of intervention. Second, CA‑125 interpretation must be contextualized: a mildly elevated level during the second trimester, although above the classical 35 U/mL cutoff, may reflect benign physiologic or degenerative changes rather than malignancy; serial trends and correlation with imaging are essential. Third, imaging guidance predominated in differential diagnosis: real‑time ultrasonography provided anatomic contour and vascularity, while non‑contrast MRI clarified tissue characteristics and clarified myoma‑uterus continuity without exposing the fetus to gadolinium. Fourth, the timing of surgery—second trimester when the uterine cavity is still distensible yet the placenta is not fully vascularized—was optimal for balancing maternal morbidity against fetal risk; elective pregnancy‑term delivery afterward ensured minimal interruption of uterine incision healing.

**Evidence gaps and future research directions**  
This single‑case analysis underscores a substantial gap in evidence governing the management of large, symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy. Randomized trials comparing conservative versus surgical approaches remain lacking, as does a consensus guideline on the utility of CA‑125 serial measurements for distinguishing benign from malignant masses during gestation. Prospective registries capturing imaging findings, surgical timing, perioperative complications, and long‑term maternal‑fetal outcomes would aid in generating robust risk stratification models. Moreover, studies addressing the natural history of residual myomas post‑myomectomy and their impact on subsequent fertility may help define optimal timing for deferred surgery. Finally, clinical trials evaluating the safety and efficacy of minimally invasive versus open surgical techniques in pregnancies complicated by large extrauterine masses are warranted to refine operative recommendations.
