Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Initial Clinical Presentation and Immediate Assessment in Pregnancy

A 41‑year‑old nulliparous woman with a body mass index of 33 presented at 21 weeks of gestation for a sudden episode of upper‑abdominal and pelvic pain accompanied by mild dyspnea. On the Visual Analogue Scale she rated the abdominal and pelvic discomfort at four to five out of ten. She was otherwise in a first‑trimester routine screening and her non‑invasive prenatal testing had confirmed a low risk for aneuploidy.  

On arrival to the emergency department her vital signs were reassuring: her heart rate was 74 bpm, blood pressure 100/60 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation 97 % on room air. The initial laboratory workup revealed a hemoglobin of 9.9 g/dL and a ferritin level of 25 µg/L, indicating iron‑deficiency anemia; a marginally elevated C‑reactive protein (CRP) of 1.39 mg/dL and a leukocytosis of 11.3 × 10^3 /µL (neutrophils 9.27 × 10^3 /µL) were seen, consistent with pregnancy‑related physiological changes but also raising the possibility of infection.  Urine culture was negative for pyuria at that time.  

The abdominal examination was notable for a markedly enlarged abdomen that exceeded the gestational size, with a hardened, taut mass occupying the left upper quadrant and extending into the uterine fundus. The mass was tender to palpation. A bimanual vaginal examination identified a fixed, rounded, hard mass in the lower pelvis that partially bulged into the posterior fornix. No free fluid was palpable, and there were no signs of ascites on clinical exam.  

Respiratory assessment revealed no use of accessory muscles, normal breath sounds, and a mild dyspnea that likely reflected diaphragmatic displacement by the enlarged uterus and the large abdominal masses rather than intrinsic pulmonary pathology.  

In the context of a partially uterine‑compliant mass with associated mechanical symptoms, the immediate differential diagnosis was broadened to include (1) space‑occupying abdominal or pelvic masses (ovarian, uterine, or other pelvic pathology), (2) infectious or inflammatory processes creating fever and leukocytosis, and (3) obstructive gastrointestinal or urinary symptoms secondary to mass effect. The patient’s presentation, obstetric context, and vital stability directed the team toward a rapid, non‑invasive diagnostic work‑up—initially a focused obstetric ultrasonography, followed, if necessary, by a non‑contrast MRI—to delineate the nature, size, and vascularity of the masses while avoiding ionizing radiation.

## Diagnostic Workup in Pregnancy: Imaging and Tumor Markers

In pregnant patients presenting with unexplained abdominal pain and the impression of an intra‑abdominal mass, the diagnostic approach must prioritize fetal safety while providing sufficient diagnostic confidence to guide management.  The cornerstone of evaluation is the combination of sonography and, when necessary, non‑contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), accompanied by a targeted panel of serum tumor markers.  Computed tomography (CT) is avoided because of ionising radiation exposure, which carries a dose‑dependent risk for miscarriage and congenital malformations.

### Safe imaging options

**Ultrasound with colour Doppler** is the first‑line modality during all trimesters.  It is free of radiation, inexpensive, and can be rapidly performed in the emergency setting.  Colour Doppler adds functional information about vascularity, helping distinguish vascular pedicles (as seen in pedunculated leiomyomas) from avascular cystic lesions.

**Non‑contrast MRI** is the preferred advanced modality when sonographic findings are equivocal or when a precise delineation of the mass‑uterus interface is required.  MRI at <1.5‑Tesla field strength and without gadolinium offers excellent soft‑tissue contrast, multiplanar capability, and the ability to characterize fluid signal without exposing the fetus to ionising radiation.  Gadolinium contrast is contraindicated in pregnancy except in life‑threatening situations, due to the low‑level risk of fetal gadolinium deposition.

#### Contraindicated modality

**CT** scans deliver a cumulative fetal dose that exceeds the threshold for acceptable risk (<50 mSv). Consequently, CT is reserved only for scenarios where other modalities fail or for evaluation of life‑threatening complications such as intra‑abdominal bleeding or suspected malignancy that requires precise anatomical definition not achievable by US or conventional MRI.

### Ultrasound findings in the case

The obstetric ultrasound disclosed **two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins**.  The **upper lesion** measured 18 × 12 × 7 cm and extended from the left sub‑phrenic space to the uterine fundus.  The **lower lesion** measured 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm and appeared adherent to a posterior subserosal intramural myoma that demonstrated cystic degeneration (dimensions 58 × 71 mm).  Colour Doppler revealed **peripheral vascularisation** around the cyst walls, a feature associated with vascularised pedicles or myomatous tissue rather than simple serous fluid collections.  Importantly, the US could not clearly delineate a stalk or feeding vessel for the larger left‑sided mass because of the bulk of the gravid uterus and the expansile displacement of adjacent structures.

### MRI characteristics

The non‑contrast MRI confirmed the US impression, providing multiplanar resolution of the cyst walls and internal architecture.  Imaging sequences included T1‑weighted, T2‑weighted, and fat‑saturation (fat‑sat) techniques:

- **Wall thickness**: 1.4 cm, thicker than would be expected for a simple serous cyst, suggesting a fibrous capsule or myomatous origin.
- **Slicewise fluid content**: Inhomogeneous signal on both T1‑ and T2‑fat sat, indicating a mixture of fluid and degenerated tissue.
- **Focal solid components**: Hyperintense on T1‑ and T2‑weighted images within the cyst, raising suspicion for solid nuclei of fibroid tissue or blood‑stasis rather than malignant infiltration.
- **No gadolinium**: The absence of contrast precludes assessment of enhancement patterns; however, the lack of rapid contrast uptake is reasonably reassuring against highly vascular malignancies, although it is nonspecific.

The MRI also revealed **no definite cleavage plane** between the caudal cyst and the adherent posterior myoma, underscoring the challenge of differentiating a highly degenerated myoma from an adnexal cystic lesion.

### Interpretation of CA‑125 in pregnancy

CA‑125, typically elevated in epithelial ovarian cancers, is physiologically increased during pregnancy due to decidualisation of the endometrium and the presence of a large uterus.  Two prominent peaks have been documented:
- **First trimester** (median ≈ 23 U/mL)
- **Third trimester** (median ≈ 21 U/mL)

In the second trimester, the expected median value is lower (≈ 14 U/mL).  In this patient, CA‑125 incrementally rose from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier to 41.8 U/mL at presentation, exceeding the usual upper limit of 35 U/mL.  While an isolated elevation in the second trimester can be benign, a **rising trend** in the context of a large, vascularised cystic mass warrants consideration of a malignant or pseudo‑neoplastic process.  Nonetheless, CA‑125 alone cannot establish malignancy; its value lies in serial monitoring and integration with imaging.

### Other tumor markers

The following serologic tests were performed and remained within reference ranges:
- **CA 19‑9** (pancreatic and GI malignancy marker)
- **CA 15‑3** (breast cancer marker)
- **CEA** (colorectal and other GI cancers)
- **β‑2‑microglobulin** (lymphoproliferative disorders)
- **LDH** (cell turnover)

Normal results of these markers reduce the likelihood of systemic malignancy involving the abdomen, yet none are specific enough to exclude a localized ovarian carcinomatosis.

### Role of urine culture and bacterial infection

During the hospital stay a low‑grade fever ensued.  A nasopharyngeal PCR for SARS‑CoV‑2 was negative, but **urine cultures isolated Klebsiella pneumoniae**, a common uropathogen.  The presence of a urinary infection may explain postoperative rises in inflammatory markers (CRP, neutrophils) and could have contributed to the necrotic, perforating component of the large pedunculated leiomyoma, as suggested by the patient’s transient fever.  While infection does not affect tumor markers, it constitutes a clinical variable that necessitates antibiotic therapy and may influence the decision to proceed with surgery.

### Integrating imaging, markers, and clinical data

The imaging modality sequence—US followed by non‑contrast MRI—enabled a detailed assessment of cystic structure, wall thickness, vascularity, and myoma association.  The serial rise in CA‑125, coupled with the existence of large, vascularised lesions and the patient’s deteriorating clinical picture (pain, dyspnea), heightened concern for potential necrosis, torsion, or infection-driven complications.  In light of these findings, the multidisciplinary team chose to excise the cranial mass, which was intraoperatively found to be a pedunculated, cystic leiomyoma with a twisted pedicle.

The case demonstrates that a carefully orchestrated diagnostic workup—grounded in safe imaging and serial tumor marker assessment—can clarify the nature of abdominal masses in pregnancy and support timely, evidence‑based surgical intervention while preserving fetal safety.

## Differential Diagnosis of Large Cystic Abdominal Masses in the Second Trimester

The coexistence of two massive cystic lesions in a pregnant patient raises a diagnostic challenge that requires integration of clinical features, serial laboratory trends, and the subtleties of imaging performed in the gravid abdomen. This patient’s presentation of severe upper‑quadrant pain, dyspnea, and a palpable hard mass in the pelvis, coupled with serial stone‑like elevation of CA‑125, guides the differential toward entities that can manifest as unilocular cystic enlargements with variable vascularity while preserving a benign course throughout the pregnancy.

**Decidualised endometrioma** is the most common benign explanation for a cystic adnexal mass acquired during gestation. On ultrasound, these lesions appear as well‑delimited unilocular cysts with homogeneous fluid; they may harbour internal echoes (“shading”) when hemorrhagic. On MRI, the cyst fluid is typically T1‑hyperintense if blood‑product has accumulated, and T2‑signals are homogeneous and low‑to‑intermediate due to iron content, with a thin capsule and no solid enhancing nodules. The patient’s upper‑standing mass displayed regular margins and peripheral vascularity on duplex study, consistent with a solid periphery that could represent a decidualised stromal capsule. Persistently elevated CA‑125 values (41.8 U/mL at 21 weeks GA versus the normal second‑trimester median < 14 U/mL) also suit this entity, as CA‑125 rises physiologically in early pregnancy and can be further increased by ongoing endometriotic activity. In this setting, the pain and mild dyspnea can be explained by mass effect on the diaphragm and, potentially, by localized inflammation or torsion of the cystic mass, while the low‑grade fever could reflect adjacent inflammation secondary to a necrotic cyst wall.

**Cystic or degenerating leiomyoma** provides a compelling alternative because uterine fibroids may undergo cystic degeneration, especially in pregnancy when hormonal milieu and rapid growth predispose to ischemia and infarction. A pedunculated intramural‑subserosal fibroid presents on ultrasonography as a heterogeneous, well‑defined mass with a clear interface to the myometrium; Doppler may reveal a feeding vessel. MRI demonstrates a thick fibrous capsule and variable fluid content; T2‑hypointense fibrous septa frequently intersect the cystic cavity and are best seen on fat‑suppressed sequences. The patient’s cranial mass was described as a pedunculated, taut‑elastic leiomyoma, complete with a twisted pedicle and a vascular base. Ischemic necrosis at the pedicle explains the transient fever; the histology confirmed fibroid origin with necrotic debris. In this context, the severe dyspnea is a mechanical consequence of an 18 cm mass that displaced the uterus upward, compressing the diaphragm between the enlarged placenta and the myoma.

**Ovarian cystic lesions** such as functional cysts, hemorrhagic cysts or benign cystadenomas remain a possibility. Functional cysts are typically simple, thin‑walled, and collapse if drained; hemorrhagic cysts contain internal echoes and may show a mixed T1/T2 signal reflecting age‑related hemoglobin breakdown. Cystadenomas are usually larger, contain septations and occasionally focal mural nodules. In this patient, the lower mass’s apparent continuity with a posterior subserosal myoma on both ultrasound and MRI argues against an independent ovarian origin, although the initial sonographic suggestion of an ovarian mass underscores the difficulty of distinguishing adnexal from uterine lesions when the uterus is massively enlarged. The lack of ascites and the absence of a solid enhancing nodule in magnetic resonance imaging further reduce the likelihood of an ovarian tumor.

Likewise, **rare cystic entities**—pseudomyxoma peritonei, Meigs syndrome, epidermoid cysts—can be considered for completeness. Pseudomyxoma peritonei presents with multicystic ascites and mucinous debris often accompanied by peritoneal implants; Meigs syndrome is characterized by the triad of ovarian fibroma, ascites, and pleural effusion; epidermoid cysts show a well‑circumscribed, irregularly shaped cyst with internal fat or keratin debris and frequently present in the ovary or para‑ovarian region. None of the clinical signs in the index case (absent ascites, pleural effusion, or characteristic imaging features) support these diagnoses, yet a high index of suspicion must be maintained because their presentation may be deceptively benign in early detection.

Distinguishing **benign from malignant** attenuation hinges on the appearance of solid components, border irregularity, rapid mass growth, and tumour marker profile. Malignant adnexal masses typically show irregular or nodular solid areas, thick septations, papillary projections, and ascites. They also produce markedly elevated markers: CA‑125 > 100 U/mL in ovarian carcinoma, albeit any value > 35 U/mL in the setting of a cystic mass warrants closer scrutiny. The masses in this patient possessed a peripheral vascular border and, in the cranial mass, solid endpoints within the fibroid scar, yet there were neither irregular margins nor nodularity on MRI nor a sharp rise in CA‑125 beyond early pregnancy norms, favouring a benign process.

Finally, **symptom correlation** can be informative. Severe localized pain often suggests torsion, necrosis, or infection of the cyst; dyspnea in the second trimester is more likely mechanical, linked to the mass compressing the diaphragm and reducing lung compliance; a fever or leucocytosis may point to ischemic necrosis or superimposed infection. Here, the patient’s worsening pain, dyspnea, and a small but significant fever resolved with analgesia and antibiotics, underscoring a mechanical rather than metastatic origin. The growth rate observed on serial imaging, from 18 cm to a similar size over several weeks, points to a benign fibroid or decidualised cyst that has slowly expanded with uterine enlargement.

In summary, the differential for large cystic abdominal masses in mid‑pregnancy hinges on imaging rhythm of the lesions (unilocular, septated, or complex), their relationship to the myometrium, the presence of solid components or irregular margins, and laboratory cues (serial CA‑125, other tumour markers). A combination of ultrasound, non‑contrast MRI, and careful clinical monitoring remained central to confirming the eventual pathologic diagnosis of a pedunculated, necrotic leiomyoma in the cranial, and a cystic‑degenerating subserosal fibroid in the caudal lesion. This thoughtful evaluation guided the decision to surgically remove only the high‑risk cranial mass while preserving the pregnancy and avoiding overtreatment.

## Multidisciplinary Decision‑Making and Surgical Management

In a pregnancy complicated by two enormous, cystic abdominal masses, the obstetrician, gynaecologist, radiologist, anesthetist and neonatologist had to wrestle with the delicate balance between preserving the gestation and preventing severe maternal morbidity. The decision to intervene surgically required an integration of clinical, radiological and laboratory data with an appreciation of the physiological changes of pregnancy that can mask or mimic pathology. The team assessed the likelihood that the lesions would worsen or produce acute complications and weighed this against the inherent risks of surgery in a mid‑second‑trimester pregnancy, namely foetal distress, preterm delivery, uterine rupture and anaesthetic complications. A structured approach—defining clear indications for intervention, selecting the safest operative route, planning meticulous intra‑operative safeguards, and instituting targeted post‑operative care—ensured that both mother and baby were protected throughout the process.

### Indications for Intervention

The cornerstone of the decision to operate was the presence of three interrelated clinical drivers. First, the patient’s severe, persistent abdominal pain in the left upper quadrant and pelvis—graded 5/10 on the Visual Analogue Scale—persisted despite multilevel symptomatic management, and was associated with mild dyspnoea that was judged to be mechanical in origin: the superior mass extended into the sub‑phrenic space and the lower mass filled the pouch of Douglas, pressing upon the diaphragm and the thoracic cage. Second, the risk of acute complications such as torsion, infarction or rupture was suggested by imaging: the upper mass, although presenting as an unilocular cyst on ultrasound, held a thick (1.4‑cm) wall and a small solid component on T2‑weighted MRI; the lower mass even displayed a heterogeneous internal signal compatible with cystic degeneration within an intramural/subserosal leiomyoma. The sudden onset of a low‑grade fever coupled with a Klebsiella pneumoniae urinary infection amplified concern that a necrotic or infarcted pedunculated component may have become superinfected. Third, the evolution of laboratory testing reinforced the urgency: a rising CA‑125 concentration (from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier to 41.8 U/mL at the time of surgery) exceeded the upper limit of normal even after accounting for the expected second‑trimester physiological peak, implying a potential inflammatory or neoplastic component. Collectively, the combination of debilitating pain, dyspnoea, the possibility of rapid growth, and laboratory evidence of an acute inflammatory state satisfied the accepted threshold for operative intervention in a pregnant patient.

(While many textbooks advocate a conservative “watchful waiting” stance for asymptomatic uterine masses in pregnancy, contemporary evidence—including case series from Zhao and colleagues—demonstrates that carefully timed myomectomy can be performed with minimally increased morbidity, provided the indications are clear and the operative environment is meticulously managed.)

### Choice of Surgical Approach

The decision between laparotomy and laparoscopy hinges on size, number, anatomical location, and gestational age. In the index case, each mass was both large—18 cm for the superior lesion and roughly 9 cm for the inferior— and complex: the superior tumor had migrated to the uterine fundus, whereas the lower tumor was intimately associated with a 6‑cm intramural/subserosal myoma and showed evidence of torsion. The limited intraperitoneal space of a 21‑week uterus, coupled with the need to secure a species‑specific pedicle (the upper lesion was pedunculated and vascularised), meant that a minimally invasive approach would have required insufflation of the abdomen with CO₂, potentially compromising uterine perfusion and fetal oxygenation. The requirement to isolate, clamp and ligate a 1‑cm pedicle reliably, and the need to perform a secure myometrial suture of the uterine cuff, argued in favor of an open approach.

An exploratory midline laparotomy, performed with a longitudinal xipho‑pubic incision, provided rapid access, unencumbered visualization of the uterus, and the ability to place a double clamp across the pedicle—a manoeuvre that the surgeons described as “gently excising the mass from its uterine implant.” Moreover, laparotomy facilitated the safe insertion of a uterine elevator to mobilize the uterus during dissection and allowed the conversion to a caesarean delivery later without a new entry into the abdominal cavity. Thus, the operative plan aligned with the guidelines suggesting that a laparotomy is preferred when masses are >9 cm in diameter, when they are intramural or pedunculated with uncertain vascular supply, or when the uterus demonstrates gestational concerns that preclude insufflation.

### Intra‑Operative Considerations

Maternal safety during surgery required a careful orchestration of several measures. Fetal cardiac activity was continuously monitored by a hook‑type trans‑abdominal doppler probe, with the expectation of detecting any transient decelerations that might arise from uterine manipulations or uterine ischemia. The patient was positioned in the left lateral decubitus to relieve aortocaval compression, and a sheet of gauze was placed beneath the right flank to further mobilise the gravid uterus. General anaesthesia was chosen to ensure patient immobility, facilitate uterine relaxation, and prevent tachycardia that could worsen uterine perfusion; isoflurane was the agent of choice, given its safety profile in pregnancy.

The surgical team employed a double‑clamp technique to isolate the pedicle of the superior mass, thereby limiting blood loss to a few millilitres and obviating the need for endovenous vasopressors that could induce uterine hypertension. Once the root had been visualised, the pedicle was transected, and the uterine stump was closed with a single absorbable suture in a watertight, layer‑by‑layer fashion to restore uterine integrity. In contrast, the lower lesion was left in situ: attempting to excise an intramural tumor would have required deeper uterine incisions that could jeopardise postpartum uterine contractility and increase risk of uterine rupture at delivery; moreover, the lower mass was already well‑encapsulated and did not demonstrate any signs of torsion or necrosis.

In line with the pathogenetic evidence of Klebsiella infection, the surgeons retained a specimen of peritoneal fluid for culture and cytology. While an intra‑operative sampling of the lower mass was not required, the team remained vigilant for any signs of infection intra‑operatively and was prepared to extend antibiotic coverage accordingly.

### Post‑Operative Management

Following the two‑hour, 25‑minute procedure, the patient received a regimen that addressed the three domains of post‑operative risk: haemorrhage, infection, and preterm labour. Prophylactic intravenous cefazolin (2 g) was administered intra‑operatively and continued for 72 h post‑operatively, supplemented by clarithromycin (500 mg twice daily) for 7 days to cover the urinary Klebsiella culture. A single intramuscular dose of 100 mg progesterone was given at the bedside immediately after the incision was closed, and thereafter at 100 mg daily until discharge, in an effort to dampen uterine contractility. Because the operative field was wound‑clean, the prophylactic regimens were deemed sufficient, and there was no early post‑operative evidence of wound infection or uterine hypertonicity.

The patient developed iron‑deficiency anaemia with a haemoglobin of 7.5 g/dL post‑operatively. In line with obstetric transfusion guidelines that recommend a threshold of <7 g/dL in symptomatic patients—or a more liberal threshold of <9 g/dL in the presence of anaemia and impending surgical intervention—two units of packed red blood cells were transfused. The anaemia was then addressed with a single 1‑g dose of ferric carboxymaltose intravenously, which is the recommended preparation for rapid iron repletion during pregnancy. The decision to modify transfusion thresholds on an individual basis can mitigate the risks of hypervolemia during pregnancy while still ensuring adequate oxygen delivery to both mother and child.

The immediate post‑operative period saw the patient remain hospitalized for 10 days. The recovery course was uncomplicated, and the surgical incision healed without morbidity. The team discharged the patient to resume ante‑partum surveillance with obstetric visits every three weeks.

### Post‑Operative Monitoring

Following discharge, scheduled ante‑partum reviews emphasized both maternal and foetal surveillance. At each visit, fundal height, blood pressure, and weight were recorded to identify any early signs of hypertension or pre‑eclampsia. Serial ultrasound scans were performed to monitor foetal growth—the most recent measurement (approximately 86 th percentile on the WHO growth chart) indicated reassuring growth trajectories—and to evaluate the size of the residual lower myoma. The residual lesion, now measuring 7.5 cm, had decreased slightly, suggesting that the body had begun to resorb necrotic tissue after the removal of the superior mass. There was no recurrence of pain or dyspnea, and peritoneal fluid cultures remained sterile.

Given the residual myoma’s location and the patient’s obstetric history (a singleton gestation, planned delivery at 37 weeks), the multidisciplinary team concluded that an elective caesarean section at the site of the previous scar would minimise the risk of uterine rupture and provide a safe delivery route. The caesarean, performed at 37 weeks and 0 days of gestation, resulted in a healthy infant and uneventful postoperative maternal recovery.

In sum, the multidisciplinary decision to intervene surgically in the second trimester was made after a rigorous assessment of pain severity, dyspnea, infection signs, imaging characteristics, and tumour markers; the surgical strategy—an open laparotomy with selective myomectomy—optimised maternal safety and permitted timely delivery. Strict intra‑operative safeguards—continuous fetal monitoring, careful haemostasis, and controlled uterine handling—constrained the risk of leaching impulses, while targeted post‑operative care—induction of progesterone, antibiotic stewardship, iron repletion, and judicious transfusion—ensured maternal hemoglobin recovery and prolonged gestation. Ongoing obstetric surveillance demonstrated that the residual myoma would not compromise fetal expulsion, allowing for the patient’s preference to postpone any future intervention.

## Outcomes, Follow‑Up and Clinical Take‑Aways

**Maternal outcomes**  
The operative intervention resolved the acute complications that prompted surgery. Post‑myomectomy anemia improved markedly: pre‑operative hemoglobin fell to 7.5 g/dL, necessitating both intravenous iron (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) and two packed red blood cell transfusions, raising hemoglobin to 10.0 g/dL, a stable level for the remainder of the pregnancy. The intra‑operative and postoperative antibiotic strategy—cefazolin for prophylaxis followed by cefazolin plus clarithromycin guided by the urine culture—cleared the Klebsiella infection and prevented secondary peritoneal sepsis, as evidenced by negative cytology and culture of peritoneal fluid at follow‑up. The patient tolerated the surgery without obstetric morbidity and reported no subsequent abdominal discomfort. Breastfeeding commenced smoothly; the abrupt cessation of lactation has not been required to postpone further intervention on the residual myoma. Thus, the multidisciplinary approach to infection control, blood management, and hormonal support (progesterone and hydroxyprogesterone caproate) facilitated a safe postpartum recovery.

**Fetal outcomes**  
The infant was delivered at 37 weeks’ gestation, just two weeks early compared with the expected term, after a planned cesarean section through the previous laparotomy scar. Prematurity was elective rather than emergent, allowing for optimal antenatal monitoring. Neonatal growth measures were reassuring—the baby’s weight corresponded to the 86th percentile on WHO growth charts, matching the mother’s mid‑pregnancy fetal measurements. Apgar scores were normal, and the newborn required no intensive care. The fetus benefited from a prolonged intrauterine life, permitting catch‑up in weight after the cesarean delivery without compromising maternal safety.

**Residual myoma management**  
The lower, intramural‑subserosal cystic myoma, left untouched to avoid uterine injury during pregnancy, exhibited a small but clinically significant size reduction: from 9.5 cm pre‑operative to 7.5 cm at 10 weeks postpartum. This regression is consistent with the known transient shrinkage of subserosal leiomyomas during the lactation period, driven by hormonal withdrawal. Given the absence of symptoms and the presumed benign nature of the lesion, the obstetric‑gynecologic team elected to defer definitive surgery until the postpartum period, allowing for natural involution and providing the patient the option of watchful waiting. The residual myoma's regression also mitigated the obstruction risk during labor, thereby supporting the decision for an elective cesarean rather than forceps delivery.

**Clinical lessons**  
Several productively integrated lessons emerge from this case. First, meticulous multidisciplinary coordination—obstetricians, gynecologists, radiologists, anesthesiologists, and neonatologists—is vital when confronting atypical, large pelvic masses during pregnancy; shared decision‑making optimizes timing and modality of intervention. Second, CA‑125 interpretation must be contextualized: a mildly elevated level during the second trimester, although above the classical 35 U/mL cutoff, may reflect benign physiologic or degenerative changes rather than malignancy; serial trends and correlation with imaging are essential. Third, imaging guidance predominated in differential diagnosis: real‑time ultrasonography provided anatomic contour and vascularity, while non‑contrast MRI clarified tissue characteristics and clarified myoma‑uterus continuity without exposing the fetus to gadolinium. Fourth, the timing of surgery—second trimester when the uterine cavity is still distensible yet the placenta is not fully vascularized—was optimal for balancing maternal morbidity against fetal risk; elective pregnancy‑term delivery afterward ensured minimal interruption of uterine incision healing.

**Evidence gaps and future research directions**  
This single‑case analysis underscores a substantial gap in evidence governing the management of large, symptomatic abdominal masses in pregnancy. Randomized trials comparing conservative versus surgical approaches remain lacking, as does a consensus guideline on the utility of CA‑125 serial measurements for distinguishing benign from malignant masses during gestation. Prospective registries capturing imaging findings, surgical timing, perioperative complications, and long‑term maternal‑fetal outcomes would aid in generating robust risk stratification models. Moreover, studies addressing the natural history of residual myomas post‑myomectomy and their impact on subsequent fertility may help define optimal timing for deferred surgery. Finally, clinical trials evaluating the safety and efficacy of minimally invasive versus open surgical techniques in pregnancies complicated by large extrauterine masses are warranted to refine operative recommendations.
