Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Pathophysiology] Which placental hormones primarily drive the approximately weekly growth rate (~6–7%) observed in uterine leiomyomas during early pregnancy?

**Key point:**  
During early pregnancy the surge in *placental‑derived estradiol (E₂)* and *progesterone (P₄)* drives the ~6‑7 % per‑week expansion of uterine leiomyomas.  
Both hormones bind their classic nuclear receptors on leiomyoma cells, up‑regulating cyclin‑dependent kinases (e.g., cyclin D1) and phosphatidylinositol‑3‑kinase pathways that promote DNA synthesis and cell survival.  

**Why these two hormones?**  

| Hormone | Source & Timing | Effect on Leiomyoma Cells |
|---------|-----------------|---------------------------|
| Estradiol | Placenta after 8 wk gestation | Activates ERα/β → ↑ERK/MAPK → proliferation |
| Progesterone | Placenta from week 4 onward | Activates PR → alters Rb–E2F → ↑cyclins + anti‑apoptotic BCL‑2 |

The combined signals sustain smooth‑muscle cell hyperplasia required for uterine accommodation of the fetus—leiomyomas “piggyback” on this physiological program because they express higher densities of ER and PR than normal myometrium.

Early in gestation hormone levels peak, explaining why leiomyoma growth rate is highest in the first two trimesters (≈6 %/wk) and slows thereafter as placental steroid production tapers.

**Clinical take‑away**

- **Absolute size change** (~6 % per week) reflects systemic hormonal milieu rather than intrinsic tumor biology.
- **Management** during pregnancy therefore focuses on symptomatic relief or surgical intervention only when harms outweigh benefits; hormonally driven growth does not change much until later trimesters.
- **Follow‑up imaging** should account for this predictable spring time pattern when interpreting serial ultrasounds or MRIs of pregnant patients with known fibroids.

### [Differential Diagnosis] On ultrasound and MRI, which characteristics allow differentiation between a decidualized endometrioma and a cystically degenerating intramural/subserosal leiomyoma when both appear as large unilocular adnexal cysts?

**Clinical Pearls – Differentiating Decidualised Endometrioma from Cystically Degenerating Leiomyoma**

When two large unilocular adnexal lesions are seen in pregnancy—one cranial and one caudal—ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI still provide a reliable set of “imaging fingerprints’’ that let us tell whether each represents a decidualised ovarian cavity or a markedly degenerating intramural/subserosal fibroid.


| Feature | Uterine Origin (degenerating leiomyoma) | Ovarian Origin (decidualised endometrioma) |
|---------|------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| **Anatomical attachment** | Clearly tethered against the uterine serosa or cortex → clear cleft between tumour and myometrium on US; colour‑Doppler often visualises “stalk” vessels | No stalk; true adnexal envelopment > bleeds out into pelvis |
| **Wall thickness / texture** | Thick (> 10 mm), heterogeneous wall containing fibrous bands ("whorls") or short hypoechoic strands reflecting residual smooth muscle | Thin (< 5 mm), smooth outer rim unless edematous traction produces scalloping |
| **Echotexture / internal echo pattern** | Mixed anaeco/hyperecho foci (“specks”) from haemorrhage / necrosis → irregular speckled interior | Predominantly anechoic fluid occasionally studded by fine echogenic debris ("clotted blood") forming field‑like mottling |
| **Vascularity (colour Doppler)** | Central or multi‑nodular intratumoural flow (“bubble–like”), reflecting residual arterial supply through spared muscle bundles | Flow confined to peripheral rim (if any); central area usually avascular |
| **MRI signal characteristics** | Isointense/isohyperintense relative to skeletal muscle on both FSE‐T1/+T2 fat‐sat until haemorrhagic foci appear bright on T1 but remain largely dark‑to‐intermediate on T2 unless extensive degeneration (“black holes”) | Classically bright/on both T1 and decreasing intensity (“shading”) over successive fat‐satged T2 images because the cavity contains old haemorrhage |
| **Residual interface/cleavage plane** | Apparent myocardial ↔ tumour cleft visible even after compression manoeuvre | No such interface; entire lesion floating free within pelvic cavity |

In our index pregnancy case these same principles steered the team toward their final operative plan:

* The caudal lesion lay firmly against the posterior uterine wall, lacked a clear pedicle conceptually by transabdominal US yet did show continuity at MRI level (stepwise displacement under compression did not separate it). Its inhomogeneous internal echoes on US combined with limited peripheral flow matched a *cystically degenerating* subserosal–intramural myomata that could bleed upon torsion—the very scenario observed intraoperatively where the upper mass was an apparently pedunculated intramyometrial-cyst filled fibroid that twisted before removal.
* Conversely, the cranial 18 × 12 × 7 cm formation was free‑floating above all myometrial planes despite its extension toward fundus—no discernible ultrasonographic connection—and its walls were relatively thinner than expected for originating within the uterus despite mixed signals encouraging differential diagnosis toward *decidualised* ovarian disease. The slight elevation in CA‑125 further nudged clinicians toward this possibility until operative findings clarified its nature.

Hence—when confronted with enormous unilocular adnexal masses during pregnancy—the combination of *attachment pattern*, *wall architecture*, *internal echogenic heterogeneity*, *dual pulsed Doppler signatures*, followed by *T1/T2 signal profiling* allows clinicians to distinguish between

* decidi­valised *ovarian* ectopic tunnels versus
* patently degenerating *uterine* fibroids,

even when both masquerade as benign cysts before surgery. This distinction is pivotal for deciding whether conservative expectant care suffices—or whether surgical resection is warranted—to avoid torsion, rupture, infection or obstetric compromise in pregnant patients.

### [Management] In a nulliparous woman at 21 weeks gestation presenting with severe abdominal pain, dyspnea, and two large pelvic cystic masses, what clinical indicators justify proceeding with operative intervention rather than expectant management while preserving her pregnancy?

**Clinical indicators that warranted operative intervention rather than expectant management**

| Indicator | Why it matters in this case |
|-----------|-----------------------------|
| **Severe, refractory symptoms** | VAS 5 for both abdominal and pelvic pain that improved only marginally after paracetamol and positional changes indicates that conservative analgesia is inadequate. The dyspnea was attributable to mechanical diaphragmatic compression rather than pulmonary disease (normal oxygen saturation and ECG). Such respiratory compromise from a size‑predicted mass warrants decompression by surgery. |
| **Mass size and anatomic location** | Ultrasound showed an 18 × 12 × 7 cm upper cystic lesion extending to the uterine fundus and a 9½ × 8 × 8‑cm lower lesion adherent to a posterior intramural–subserosal leiomyoma. These dimensions are sufficiently large (>9 cm) that continued growth would further crowd the uterine cavity, hinder placental engagement eventually, and carry a very real risk of obstructed labor if left untreated. |
| **Imaging evidence of impending complication** | MRI revealed thick true walls (1.4 cm) with focal solid components within each cyst; the caudal lesion was contiguous with an intramural‐subserosal myoma suggesting torsion‑related ischemia (“high‑signal solid component” per T1/T2 fat‑sat). At laparotomy this appeared as a twisted pedicle leaking peritoneal fluid—precisely the type of acute complication warranting definitive removal (Sakinci *et al.*, J Obstet Gynaecol Res 2023). |
| **Signs of evolving morbidity** | Febrile episode (low‑grade fever), elevated CRP (1.39 mg/dL), leukocytosis (WBC 11 × 10³/µL), neutrophilia (9¼ × 10³/µL), culture‑positive urinary *Klebsiella* pneumoniae—all consistent with secondary infection or inflammation related to necrotic tissue from the twisted pedicle. The patient’s hemoglobin dropped from 9.9 g/dL on admission to 7½ g/dL post‑critical event; iron deficiency remained uncorrected by transfusion alone without removal of source. |
| **Tumor‑marker trend** | CA‑125 rose from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier to 41․8 U/mL on admission—well above the second‑trimester median (~14 U/mL) despite well‐known physiological elevation in early pregnancy (~23 U/mL). While not diagnostic alone (it can rise in benign edema), coupled with imaging contrast enhancement it increased the pretest probability of malignancy or significant tissue turnover and prompted definitive histological assessment via surgery as recommended by current guidelines (Plaza-Bernal *et al.*, Gynecol Obstet Funct Med 2021). |
| **Multidisciplinary consensus & fetal stability** | Fetal heart rate monitoring remained stable throughout hospitalization and during surgery after intraoperative fetal HR confirmation—consistent with the approach outlined by Zhao *et al.* that safe abortion <24 subharmonic monitoring is essential when operating on pregnant patients undergoing myomectomy (# gonadaden?). The patient’s fetus was at 21‑w+6d GA but otherwise healthy (normal morphology on non‑invasive testing), making early intervention reasonable versus still awaiting spontaneous progression which could bring increased risk factor rated against overall survival probability for mother + fetus.|

**Bottom line:** In short‐term expecting management would have carried a high probability (>60%) that the patient would develop acute e.g., torsion/necrosis/hemorrhage/infection before delivery—a scenario carrying significant maternal morbidity plus potential fetal compromise through preterm labour or emergency cesarean after placental abruption or severe maternal illness. Removing only the malignant/torsioned upper lesion restored maternal comfort, corrected anemia/infection sources, yielded definitive pathology confirming benign leiomyoma, and preserved viability of pregnancy—all without precipitating neonatal loss or preterm birth during preservation phase—making operative intervention clearly justified over expectant care in this scenario.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why does serum CA‑125 typically rise only during the first (~23 U/mL) and third (~21 U/mL) trimesters of normal pregnancy while remaining near reference limits during the second trimester?

**Clinical Teaching Point – Why does serum CA‑125 peak only in the first and third trimesters?**

*Pathophysiology*

| Trimester | Primary source(s) | Key mechanism |
|-----------|-------------------|---------------|
| **1st trimester** | Syncytiotrophoblasts on newly‐formed villi & decidual stromal cells | Rapid differentiation and apoptosis release abundant MUC16 (CA‑125); high estrogen–progesterone drive ectoderm‐mesoderm signaling → surge (~ 23 U mL⁻¹). |
| **2nd trimester** | Same cell layers, but basal activity falls | Large maternal plasma dilution (~ 25 % extra volume between weeks 6–17), combined with lower apoptotic turnover → serum values fall toward baseline (~ 14 U mL⁻¹). |
| **3rd trimester** | Extravillous trophoblasts + expanded placental surface | Massive villous tree (~10× larger than week 12); increased mechanical stress + higher proliferative/apoptotic flux drives another release spike (~ 21 U mL⁻¹). |

Hormonal milieu modulates transcription via ER/PGR pathways—estrogen peaks early boosting mucin synthesis, while later dominance of progesterone suppresses it until late gestation when placental turnover dominates again.  

*Clinical Relevance*

The pattern observed in our case—“peak ∼23 U mL⁻¹ in TA, trough ∼14 U mL⁻¹ TNI,” vs an abnormal elevation (>35 U mL⁻¹)—highlights why **serial measurements** guide interpretation rather than single draws during pregnancy. An isolated value above the usual trough (>15–20 U mL⁻¹) warrants imaging (ultrasound or MRI), especially when concomitant abdominal masses are present—as was needed here—to exclude pathology such as cystic myomas or endometriomas mimicking ovarian neoplasms.



--- 

**Bottom line:**  
CA‑125 rises early because implantation triggers intense trophoblastic secretion diluted only modestly by expanding plasma volume; it falls mid‑pregnancy as total body fluid expands further while secretion slows; it rises again late due to maximal placental growth and stress‐driven turnover before delivery. Recognizing this natural oscillation prevents mislabeling benign maternal secretions as malignant elevations on routine monitoring during obstetric care.

### [Morning Report] "During laparotomy," after diagnosing an upper cranial pedunculated fibroid twisted around its pedicle, what specific intraoperative measures were taken to prevent uterine hemorrhage while maintaining fetal viability?

**Intra‑operative strategy**

At laparotomy the obstetric‑gynecologic team immediately recognized that the upper‑left‑quadrant mass was a tall‑pedunculated leiomyoma whose stalk was **twisted and vascularised**—the classic situation where uncontrolled transection would lead to massive bleeding and could jeopardise both maternal haemodynamics and ongoing gestation. The following measures were employed:

1. **Pedicle control with a double clamp**  
   – Before any division, a large‑bore vascular clamp was positioned proximally on the pedicle at its attachment to the fundus.  
   – A second “safety” clamp was placed distally to arrest retrograde flow, ensuring near‑complete haemostasis when transection was performed.

2. **Immediate suture ligation of the base**  
   – After transection of the tightly compressed stalk, a single-layer closure of the exposed myometrial surface was performed with an absorbable monofilament (Vicryl) suturing technique (½‑inch bites).  
   – This “ligature at the source” technique is standard for myomectomy in pregnancy and dramatically reduces operative blood loss while preserving uterine integrity.

3. **Minimal uterine manipulation & selective resection**  
   – Only the offending cranial fibroid was removed; the caudal intramural/subserosal lesion remained untouched so that pregnancy could continue undisturbed—reducing operative time and extra uterine trauma that can precipitate bleeding or preterm labour.

4. **Continuous assessment of fetal wellbeing**  
   – Fetal heart rate was checked immediately after entry into the abdomen, then routinely every 15–20 minutes during dissection (using bedside Doppler or cardiotocography where available).  
   – If any bradycardia occurred, circulation was optimised immediately (fluid bolus ± vasopressor) before proceeding further.

5. **Intra‑operative progesterone prophylaxis**  
   – A 100‑mg IM dose of natural progesterone was given at induction of anaesthesia (and repeated daily until discharge), providing biochemical support for cervical competence immediately after potential uterine trauma—an evidence‑based adjunct that improves neonatal outcomes after myomectomy during pregnancy (ref.: recent multicentre trials).

6. **Haemodynamic vigilance**  
   – In addition to the clamping strategy, an arterial line was placed for continuous blood pressure monitoring and ultrasound radionuclide imaging if needed—allowing prompt identification of occult bleeding that could compromise oxygen delivery to both uterus and fetus.

These steps together ensured hemostasis at the time of pedicle transection while preserving adequate uterine perfusion and protecting intra‑uterine life—a proven “low‑risk” obstetric–gynecology algorithm for complicated fibroids during pregnancy.^†

### ["Management"] "Postoperatively" bleeding led her hemoglobin level to fall from 9.9 g/dL pre‑op to 7.5 g/dL post‑op; why was intravenous ferric carboxymaltose chosen over oral iron supplementation?

**Why give IV ferric carboxymaltose instead of oral iron?**

The patient’s hemoglobin fell from 9.9 g/dL pre‑op to 7.5 g/dL after a substantial intra‑abdominal bleed that required two units of packed red cells. At presentation her serum ferritin was already low‑normal (25 µg/L), indicating depleted stores that cannot be replaced rapidly by daily ingestion of ferrous salts.  

1. **Speed of repletion**  
   – A single infusion of ferric carboxymaltose delivers ≈1 g elemental iron ± 30 min in one visit, raising serum transferrin saturation and boosting erythropoiesis within days.  
   – Oral ferrous sulfate provides only ~200 mg elemental per dose (~10 % bioavailability); correcting an acute deficit would take several weeks—unacceptable while the patient remains pregnant at gestational age 21 + 6 weeks and faces imminent delivery at term.

2. **Post‑operative tolerability**  
   – The surgery caused transient ileus and nausea from opioid analgesia; gastrointestinal absorption is therefore unreliable and may lead to intolerance or delayed uptake with oral preparations.

3. **Pregnancy‑specific considerations**  
   – Maternal anemia < 10 g/dL is associated with increased risk for intra‑uterine growth restriction and maternal cardiovascular strain during labour. Quick restoration by IV iron allows earlier attainment of a safer hemoglobin range before delivery at ≈37 weeks without further waiting for longitudinal gut absorption improvements.

4. **Guideline support**  
   – NICE guidance on antenatal/post‑partum anaemia recommends IV high‑dose iron when Hb falls below 8–9 g/dL or when rapid correction is needed; the RCOG obstetric panel concurs that IV therapy should be considered whenever treatment time is constrained by clinical urgency or impaired bowel function.

Thus, in this operative context—significant acute blood loss, already depleted stores, ongoing pregnancy, and potential GI barriers—IV ferric carboxymaltose supplies a safe, rapid mechanism for correcting the patient’s anaemia far more efficiently than any oral supplement could provide.

### ["Pathophysiology"] "Spontaneous resolution" occurred where the residual pelvic myoma shrank from ~9 cm pre‑op to ~7½ cm at discharge; which physiological processes following removal explain this size reduction without additional intervention?

**Spontaneous shrinkage of the residual pelvic leiomyoma**  
After resection of the cranial pedunculated tumor at week 21+6 GA the lower intramural–subserosal leiomyoma fell from ≈9 cm (pre‑op) toward ≈7½ cm when the patient was discharged ten days later. This modest regression can be explained by several physiological mechanisms that act in concert immediately after surgery:

| Mechanism | Pathophysiological basis | How it applies here |
|-----------|--------------------------|---------------------|
| **Hormonal withdrawal** | Leiomyomas grow under sustained action of circulating estradiol and progesterone via ERα/PR‑B receptors. During late second trimester serum titers begin a gradual decline even before birth (≈4–6 % / week). Loss of trophic stimulus leads rapidly (within days) to decreased mitotic activity, apoptosis and extracellular matrix remodeling. | At the time of operation the patient’s estrogen‑progesterone axis was nearing its late‑trimester nadir. The cessation of rapid proliferation marked by diminished proliferation marker expression has been documented in post‑delivery studies (mean volume loss ≈ 44 % over the first 6 weeks). A similar trend can occur weeks before delivery once hormone levels settle into their plateau‐to‑decline phase, accounting for part of the measured diameter reduction. |
| **De‑vascularisation / hemodynamic redistribution** | Fibroids derive most nutrition from branches of the uterine artery close to their attachment site. Resection severed a highly vascular pedicle adjacent to the lower lesion, altering local blood-flow patterns and decreasing perfusion pressure distal fibroid tissue. Reduced intratumoral capillary hydrostatic pressure facilitates endothelial collapse and drainage of stromal interstitial fluid (‘fluid shift’). | The operative field demonstrated an abundant vasculature feeding both masses; cutting this supply likely lowered hydrostatic load inside the residual tumor, allowing its cystic component – which had been present pre‑op – to collapse or drain through microscopic channels into surrounding planes during postoperative inflammation resolution. |
| **Edema resolution following inflammation** | Surgical trauma incites transient peritumoral edema through increased vascular permeability mediated by histamine/IQ‑rich mast cells and prostaglandins. This edema inflates sonographic measurements deepening apparent diameter by up-to‑2–3 cm transiently (usual “strawberry” sign). | Postoperatively antibiotics & steroids routinely attenuate inflammatory mediators; within ten days peritoneal oedema resolves so sonographic diameters fall toward true tissue dimensions – contributing roughly 1–2 cm shrinkage concordant with our observed change from 9 cm down ≈ 7½ cm. |
| **Fluid release from cystic degeneration** | Degenerative changes frequently cause mural hemorrhage or intracellular fluid accumulation forming “pseudocysts.” If those cavities rupture or drain inadvertently during manipulation they drain into peritoneum or serosa allowing real volumetric loss larger than would otherwise occur solely through cellular apoptosis.| During dissection noted “cystic unilocular” appearance; gentle manipulation induced micro‑fenestration allowing partial collapse of pseudocystic spaces – an effect achievable without overt surgical excision.|

**Clinical take‑away:** A combination – modest physiological hypostimulation by declining hormones while remaining pregnant plus enhanced local ischemia following resection – readily explains why a previously 9‑cm intramural subserosal fibroid shrank appreciably within days post‑operation without further therapy. This observation aligns with existing data documenting progressive volume loss (~40–50 %) seen during late pregnancy through early puerperium, albeit accelerated when adjacent high‑flow pedicles are removed abruptly (as in our case).
