Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Decoding the Diaphragm: Imaging Giant Masses in Pregnancy without the Gadolinium

## Why the Case Stuck With Us

When a 42‑year‑old, first‑pregnancy, obese patient arrives at the emergency department with a *steep* rise in left‑upper‑quadrant pain, tight left‑leg ankle swelling, and a sense of “throat‑tightening” at 21 weeks gestation, you’d start looking for the usual culprits – gestational hypertension, isolated placental abruption, or, of course, a placental site. What’s unusual here is that the sonographer’s display becomes dominated by two enormous, unilocular cystic structures that look more like “giant balloons” than any typical adnexal lesion. The larger one stretches from the left sub‑diaphragm down to the uterine fundus, and the smaller one sits tucked in the pouch of Douglas, apparently anchored to an intramural posterior myoma. This is the sort of image that forces the group (obstetrician, radiologist, and surgeon) to stop and ask: is this a decidualised endometrioma, a cystic leiomyoma with degenerative change, or something alarming like an ovarian carcinoma masquerading as pregnancy‑related fluid?

The intrigue lies in how the diagnostic ladder—physical exam, ultrasound, and then non‑contrast MRI—helps us sift through the possibilities without exposing the fetus to gadolinium. It’s a classic example of how clinical reasoning, imaging physics, and a safety‑first mindset converge in obstetric surgery cases that outsource the "diaphragm" of the diagnostic process to a powerful, safe imaging tool.

---

## The Workflow That Worked: From Bedside to Tube

1. **First‑line physical exam**  
   The bimanual pelvic exam provides the first tactile clue. A rigid, 9‑cm, non‑compressible mass “bulging” into the posterior fornix immediately raises alarm for a fixed intramural lesion rather than a freely mobile, cystic ovarian structure.

2. **Point‑of‑care ultrasound with Doppler**  
   With the uterus enlarged to a grandmother‑size frame, standard abdominal US is still the workhorse. In this patient, the technique revealed two large, low‑echoic unilocular cysts with peripheral blood flow, typical of a vascular pedicle or a vascularised cyst wall. The upper mass measured roughly 18 × 12 × 7 cm, covering almost the entirety of the left sub‑diaphragmatic space, while the lower mass sat behind the uterus, 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm, and displayed a thickened rim that seemed continuous with an adjacent intramural‑subserosal myoma.

3. **Non‑contrast MRI at 1.5 T**  
   Once the ultrasound leaves us with an atomic “mass in the belly” without decisive tissue characterization, MRI steps in. Because gadolinium is best avoided in pregnancy (counter‑measures like 23‑mg contrast risk fetal exposure, and the risks outweigh benefits for non‑urgent imaging), we lean on T1, T2, and fat‑saturated sequences. The machine spooled and produced images that showed the upper mass with a thickened 1.4‑cm wall, heterogeneous fluid (T2‑hypointense regions) and a few bright nodular spots on T1 (suggesting ectopic blood products). The lower mass blended into the posterior myoma, leaving an ambiguous “borderless” picture but no lymphadenopathy or evidence of extra‑uterine spread.

4. **Tumour markers as a guiding trend**  
   CA‑125 rose from 36 to 41.8 U/mL over two weeks—still marginal but consistent with a physiological trend seen in the third trimester. In isolation, this would be almost meaningless. When combined with imaging and the known pregnancy‑related estrogen surge, it nudges our thinking toward benign compromise rather than malignancy.

5. **Case‑team multidisciplinary decision**  
   Here, we had a patient with two huge, symptomatic masses that could threaten pulmonary function, cause uterine torsion, or even rupture. The only safe surgical strategy was to excise the pedunculated upper leiomyoma while leaving the lower, potentially hazardous mass in place—an approach that survived intra‑operative and post‑partum scrutiny.

---

## What Ultrasound Tells Us… And When It Fails

Ultrasound is our first‑line “eyes” inside the growing belly, but it has a few blind spots.

- **Mass size matters**: When a cyst spans a footprint comparable to a football field, the probe’s field of view shrinks and the depth of field expands. Acoustic attenuation can make the internal content look homogeneous even when it is not.
- **Colour Doppler is a lifesaver**: Peripheral vascularity suggests a pedicle still feeding the mass, whereas absent flow indicates a degenerating or necrotic core. In this patient, the pedicle on the upper mass was faint but visible; the lower mass had uniform peripheral flow, hinting at an ongoing vascular relationship with the posterior myoma.
- **Hardness on compression**: On digital compression (inside the probe), a cystic lesion yields to pressure and “fills,” while a fibrous or calcified wall resists. In our case, the upper mass was “teeth‑hard,” unmistakably fibrous.

**Limitations**: Large cystic masses obscure the deeper uterine wall, making it impossible to identify whether the echogenic rim is composed of uterine myometrium or nodule capsule. Likewise, adjacency to the fetus or placenta can distort the wall, causing “chocolate” appearance to be misread as blood‑filled fluid.

Therefore, a well‑performed ultrasound can expose anatomical relationships, but it rarely rules out the underlying pathology in the absence of definitive tissue characterization.

---

## The Pragmatic Value of the Bimanual Pelvic Examination

We might be inclined to treat ultrasound as if it is your diagnostic “oracle,” but medicine is not a machine. The in‑person bimanual assessment offers:

- **Direct feel of tissue consistency**: A smooth, compressible cyst is easily distinguished from a firmer, fixed mass. For our patient, the 9‑cm mass was rigid, non‑compressible, and projected against the posterior fornix—classic for an intramural, solid leiomyoma with cystic degeneration.
- **Assessment of mobility**: A freely mobile cyst will glide with pelvic manipulation, whereas a tethered mass will stay in place. The above mass had no mobility, ruling out a pedunculated ovarian cyst.
- **Anatomic orientation**: With a properly executed exam, you can determine if the mass is posterolateral (likely ovarian), anterior (intramural), or more central (possible uterine). In this case, the mass’s posterior curvature suggested its origin in the posterior wall.

It may seem old‑fashioned, but in a case about “mass” we never underestimate the value of simply placing a hand to the uterus.

---

## Non‑Contrast MRI: Shedding Light on Tissue Without the Radiolabel

### Safety Primer

- **Why no contrast?** Gadolinium crosses the placenta and can deposit in fetal tissues; the “risk” is not yet quantified but policy is to avoid it unless absolutely needed. In the second trimester, a 1.5‑T MRI without contrast is considered safe.
- **Why 1.5 T, not 3 T?** Higher field strengths can provide better signal‑to‑noise ratio but also increase the specific absorption rate (SAR), which raises concerns about localized heating and fetal effects. The literature supports 1.5 T as the gold standard for most obstetric MRI protocols.

### What MRI Adds

- **Tissue characterization**: Differentiation between simple fluid, protein‑rich fluid, fibrosis, or necrosis is easier. T1‑bright fluid usually indicates blood or proteinaceous “chocolate” fluid (classical for endometrioma), whereas T2 hypersignal can signal retention of fluid but may also represent a myxoid change in leiomyoma.
- **Spatial relationships**: The thick, 1.4‑cm wall on T2 sequences indicates a true capsule or fibrous interface in the upper mass. The lower mass’s lack of a clear cleavage plane but continuous signal to the uterus raised the suspicion that the cyst was an extension of a myoma undergoing degeneration.
- **You can’t see blood flow**: It isn’t a problem here; the pedicle was identified on ultrasound. In other cases, we might add phase‑contrast or spoiled gradient‑echo sequences to map flow without contrast.

In this patient, the MRI essentially confirmed the ultrasound’s suspicion: a pedunculated cystic leiomyoma and a second, more ambiguous, cystic degenerative process tethered to the uterus.

---

## Pitfalls in Interpretation—and How to Dodge Them

1. **“All cysts in the uterus are endometriomas”**: Clinicians sometimes default to assuming a cyst in a pregnant patient equals an endometrioma because endometriomas are notoriously estrogen‑sensitive. The reality is that cystic leiomyomas can easily masquerade as endometrioma, especially when decidualizing.

2. **Counting on CA‑125 alone**: A single CA‑125 value of 41 U/mL – slightly above the typical pregnancy‑grade threshold – can mislead you into thinking about malignancy. In practice, you should track the trend in CA‑125, compare it to trimester‑specific reference ranges, and always pair it with imaging.

3. **Missing pedicle geometry**: The look of a ventral extension that looks like a bulge can be a wall‑hypertrophy or a pedicle. A pedicle vulnerable to torsion needs to be addressed sooner rather than later. Color Doppler addresses this, but MRI is the final arbiter of pedicle presence.

4. **Ignoring the bubble “size” law**: Larger mass size often correlates with higher risk of complications (torsion, rupture, infection). The 18‑cm upper mass was a red flag that warrants immediate escalation to operative therapy.

---

## Putting the Pieces Together: Why the Upper Mass Was Excisable

The decision to resect the upper, pedunculated leiomyoma was predicated on a triad:

- **Mechanical compression**: The mass’s sheer size caused restrictive lung disease and acute pain.
- **Torsion + rupture risk**: The presence of a twisted, vascular pedicle—dead‑end on hysterosalpingography—creates a ticking time bomb.
- **Maternal‑fetal safety**: An uncomplicated laparotomy performed after verifying fetal well‑being, coupled with prophylactic progesterone and pre‑operative antibiotics, can preserve a near‑term pregnancy.

The lower mass, though still sizable, remained intact: it had a smooth surface, no obvious cease of vascularity, and was an intramural extension. Its removal could have jeopardized uterine integrity and increased the risk of preterm delivery or uterine rupture. Hence, we left the lower myoma in place, scheduling a planned cesarean at 37 weeks, avoiding a “dead‑end” surgery.

---

## Clinical Pearls & Take‑Home Messages

- **Always start with a tactile assessment** – a bimanual exam can quickly distinguish a fixed, fibrotic mass from a mobile cyst.
- **Ultrasound is your go‑to, but it’s limited by size** – large cysts can obscure their relationship to the uterus; look for peripheral vascularity and peripheral thickening.
- **Non‑contrast MRI is safe after the first trimester** – no gadolinium, use 1.5‑T, and rely on T1/T2 and fat‑sat to differentiate tissue components.
- **Interpret CA‑125 as a trend, not a snapshot** – pregnancy alone can raise the level; serial values and trimester‑specific cut‑offs improve specificity.
- **Mass size >15 cm, pedicle presence, or intractable pain → early surgery** – risk of torsion or rupture often outweighs operative risk in a stable patient.
- **Leave large intramural cystic masses in situ if they don’t threaten pregnancy**, and plan mode of delivery accordingly (e.g., cesarean near term if the mass impedes descent).

---

With this framework, we can “decode the diaphragm” of pregnancy‑imaging: ruling out malignancy, catching red‑flag symptoms before they turn into emergencies, and protecting both mother and fetus while still giving an opportunistic chance for surgical cure or conservative management. The next time you see a giant cystic mass on a pregnant patient’s US, remember the bimanual tactile clue, the ultrasound’s vascular pearls, and that an MRI without gadolinium can be the decisive witness—without the risk of a “diamagnetic drag” on the developing baby.



# The CA‑125 Puzzle: When Pregnancy Distorts Tumor Markers

## Why This Case Shouldn’t Make You Panic About CA‑125

A 41‑year‑old nulliparous woman, 21 + 6 weeks pregnant, presented with sharp left‑upper‑quadrant and pelvic pain that swelled to a Visual Analogue Scale of 5. Ultrasound showed two huge cystic abdominal masses: one spanning the left sub‑phrenic space to the uterine fundus, the other perched in the pouch of Douglas. A routine tumor‑marker panel sent a red flag: CA‑125 41.8 U/mL (up a few points from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier). Your gut instinct? Maybe ovarian cancer. But on lap‑open, the patient actually had a pedunculated, twisted, cystic myoma that was leaking and a massive intramural‑subserosal myoma that stayed put. The “tumor” was perfectly benign, the CA‑125 caveat was the issue.

The lesson? In pregnancy a spike in CA‑125 can be a physiological flare, not a gold‑standard for malignancy. Knowing the baseline fluctuations, interpreting serial values, and integrating them with imaging keeps you from unnecessary hysterectomies and maternal‑fetal compromise.

---

## Physiological CA‑125 Peaks Throughout Pregnancy

CA‑125 is a glycoprotein secreted by the peritoneum, endometrium, and, in pregnancy, the decidua. It is notoriously *fluctuating*.

| Phase | Median CA‑125 (U/mL) | Notes |
|-------|---------------------|-------|
| 1st trimester | ~23 | First peak; immune modulating milieu |
| 2nd trimester | ~14 | The “baseline” you would expect; lower than in the first |
| 3rd trimester | ~21 | Second, lower “peak”; still < 30 U/mL in most patients |

The 41.8 U/mL in the case was well above the 2nd‑trimester mean, but still far below the 2× baseline or the >70 U/mL more strongly associated with ovarian carcinoma. The rise was modest and isolated to CA‑125; the rest of the markers (CA‑19‑9, CEA, β‑2‑microglobulin, LDH) stayed in the green zone. This pattern echoes other reports: pregnancy itself can elevate CA‑125 by 20–30 U/mL, especially when the uterus enlarges significantly.

**Bottom line:** The normal reference range for CA‑125 shifts during pregnancy. A “rule‑of‑thumb” of 35 U/mL for malignancy is no longer valid when you’re dealing with a pregnant pelvis.

---

## Serial Markers > One‑Time Snapshots

A single CA‑125 measurement is akin to reading the temperature of a room one minute. If that minute falls during a spike of inflammation, you’d incorrectly label it a fever. Serial measurements give you a trend.

- **Stable or Declining** values across the gestation are reassuring, even if slightly above baseline.
- **Sustained rise** (for instance, >20 % step‑up over 2–4 weeks) warrants a re‑evaluation, but you still need imaging to cast doubt.
- **Plateau followed by abrupt spike** could be a red flag, especially >50 U/mL.

In the case, CA‑125 increased about 5 U/mL in 2 weeks – a 13 % rise. Though physiologic, it prompted further work‑up rather than dismissal, underscoring the need for a thoughtful, non‑drastic response.

---

## Imaging: The Sherlock Holmes to Your CA‑125

US and MRI are the *gold‑standard* for distinguishing benign from malignant adnexal or uterine masses in pregnancy. They provide structural, vascular, and sometimes functional data that no blood test can match.

1. **Ultrasound with Colour Doppler**  
   * Pros: Safety, real‑time, bedside.  
   * Cons: Limited by maternal size, field of view.  
   * Typical benign findings: unilocular cysts, smooth thick walls, modest vascularity.

2. **Non‑contrast MRI (1.5 T or less)**  
   * Pros: Excellent soft‐tissue contrast, no ionising radiation.  
   * Cons: Limited availability, cost, patient tolerance.  
   * Typical benign findings: T2‑hypointense stems for myomas, thick walls with focal hyperintense solid components for cystic degeneration.

In the patient, US and MRI identified two unilocular cystic lesions with thick walls and focal solid foci, suggesting possible myoma degeneration rather than ovarian cancer. Importantly, no discrete feeding vessel or stalk was visualised for the upper mass—again leaning away from a pedunculated cystic tumor.

---

## Decision‑Making: When Surgery Is Worth the Risk

Two factors tip the scale toward operative intervention in pregnancy:

1. **Symptomatic burden** – severe pain, dyspnoea, or signs of compromise (torsion, rupture, infection).  
2. **Risk of future complications** – sizeable growth, potential for necrosis or infection.

In our case, the patient had tachypnoea from mass effect, low‑grade fever (maybe from a leaking, twisted leiomyoma), and a rising CA‑125. After multidisciplinary discussion, a laparotomy was elected at 21 + 6 wks. The surgeons removed the upper pedunculated mass; the lower uterine myoma—larger and inseparable—was left in place to preserve the pregnancy and reduce operative morbidity.

The surgical approach underscored a key principle: *do what is necessary, avoid what is unnecessary.* The residual mass, identical to a typical intramural myoma, was monitored and subsequently its size fell post‑partum.

---

## Clinical Pearls

- **Gestation‑specific CA‑125 cut‑offs**: In pregnancy, use lower thresholds (e.g., 19 U/mL for myoma suspicion) rather than the standard 35 U/mL for malignancy.  
- **Serial trend over single value**: A modest rise may be normal; a steep, sustained increase demands imaging.  
- **Integrate imaging first**: US and non‑contrast MRI provide superior specificity; they are the appropriate partner for a suspicious CA‑125.  
- **Don’t let CA‑125 dictate surgical timing alone**: Consider size, location, symptoms, and uterine‑placental distance.  
- **Multidisciplinary board is essential**: Obstetrician, surgeon, radiologist, and, if needed, a medical oncologist—everyone in one room.

---

## Take‑Home Messages

- Pregnancy alters CA‑125 baselines; normal values shift throughout gestation.  
- Serial measurements establish trends that guide clinical action, but they are ineffective without imaging.  
- US and MRI remain the cornerstone for diagnosing and characterizing pelvic masses in pregnancy.  
- Management decisions should weigh symptoms, imaging findings, and obstetric risk—not the tumor marker alone.  
- Surgical intervention is reserved for symptomatic or complication‑prone masses; the goal is to preserve the pregnancy and minimise morbidity.



# Cutting Through Obstacles: Mid‑Trimester Myomectomy – When and How

## The Surprising Pivot

A 43‑year‑old first‑time mother‑to‑be walked into our emergency bay at 21 weeks + 6 days gestation with a 5‑point VAS upper abdominal and pelvic pain, breathing shortness, and a pulsatile abdominal lump so large it seemed to push the uterus toward the chest. Two unilocular cystic masses—one 18 × 12 × 7 cm above the left kidney and one 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm attached to a posterior intramural subserosal leiomyoma—were quickly identified on ultrasound and confirmed on a non‑contrast MRI. CA‑125 was mildly elevated and rising, and the patient had a low‑grade fever with a Klebsiella isolate from urine. In short, she had a huge, vascular‑looking, possibly necrotic myoma threatening torsion, infection, and compressive dyspnea. In our hands, the solution was clear: intervene early in the second trimester. 

The following case‑based guide distills that decision‑making journey into actionable pearls for every clinician who may encounter a “big, bad, pregnant” myoma.

## When Do We Cut?

**Indications for prenatal myomectomy**

| Symptom | Pathophysiology | Why surgery? |
|---------|-----------------|--------------|
| **Worsening pain or dyspnea** | Mass effect or torsion causing ischemia | Relief of compression, prevention of infarction |
| **Infection or necrosis** | Torsion of pedunculated or degenerating myoma | Remove the nidus, treat sepsis |
| **Mass growth** | Rapid increase in size (~6–7 % per week, even larger in first trimester) | Prevent future obstruction of uterus/fetal canal |
| **Prospective obstetric complications** | Posterior or intramural lesions that risk obstructing expulsive phase | Plan for safe delivery (e.g., elective CS) |
| **Suspicious oncologic features** | Inconclusive imaging, rising CA‑125, irregular margins | Rule out malignancy early |

In the highlighted case, the combination of mechanical dyspnea, fever, and a palpable fixed pelvic bulk tipped the scales toward operative management. A multidisciplinary team (OB‑GYN, gynecologic surgeon, anesthesiologist, and neonatologist) confirmed that the risk of spontaneous abortion or preterm delivery outweighed the operatory risks if the surgery was confined to the upper myoma, where a pedunculated stalk could be safely excised.

## Surgical Timing: The “Gold Window”

The second trimester (14–28 weeks) is recognized as the safest period for intra‑pregnancy myomectomy:

- **Fetal organogenesis is complete** – lower teratogenicity risk compared with first trimester.
- **Uterine size is still modest** – easier access for laparoscopic instruments or open approach.
- **Placental implantation** is usually mid‑uterine, distal from posterior masses, reducing the chance of inadvertent placental injury.
- **Physiologic uterine tone is high**, but preterm contractions are more likely only in the third trimester or beyond.

The case above exemplifies this window: the procedure at 21 + 6 weeks was radio‑compatible with the fetus, and careful intra‑operative fetal heart monitoring minimized risk to the baby.

## Laparotomy vs. Laparoscopy: Weighing the Sticks

| Feature | Laparoscopy | Laparotomy |
|---------|-------------|------------|
| **Maneuverability** | Excellent for limited, subserosal or pedunculated lesions | Better for large, intramural or multiform masses |
| **Blood loss** | Usually lower with minimal tissue disruption | Higher but manageable |
| **Recovery** | Faster, fewer wound complications | Longer hospitalization, larger incision |
| **Feasibility** | Depends on mass location, uterine distension, and surgeon skill | Universally applicable regardless of mass size |
| **Risk of uterine rupture** | Lower, fewer uterine wall breaches | Risk mitigated by careful fascial closure and avoiding deep myometrial cuts |

In the featured patient, the upper mass was pedunculated and oversized, fully accessible through a small midline incision. The cobbler of the mesentery demanded an open approach to safely clamp the stalk and prevent traumatic transection. For the lower intramural‑subserosal mass, surgery would have required an intramyometrial approach, steeply increasing the risk of uterine perforation and bleeding; leaving it in situ was the safer compromise.

**Key take‑away**: Whenever the mass is *non‑intramural, pedunculated, and large,* a laparoscopic approach is ideal. *Intramural or deeply situated lesions* usually warrant a classic open approach, especially if >6 cm or in proximity to the placenta.

## Preserving Uterine Integrity

- **Identify the pedicle early**: Use intra‑operative ultrasound (or 3‑D imaging) to locate the vascular stalk and clamp it with a non‑compressive device (e.g., double clamp) to maintain uterine wall orientation.
  
- **Avoid over‑excision**: Preserve the peritoneal covering of the remnant myoma and avoid creating a defect in the uterine serosa that could enlarge during late pregnancy.
  
- **Suture the base with absorbable material**: A single layer of 1–0 polydioxanone ensures hemostasis while leaving a minimal scar that can heal without compromising contractility.

- **Document the distance between myoma and placenta**: Although not invasive, we measured this via MRI – a thick (>1 cm) buffer zone is desirable when excising a myoma near the placental bed.

In our case, the cranial lesion’s pedicle was dissected gently, the base sutured, and the peritoneal lining left intact. The lower mass was deliberately spared to avoid compromising the posterior uterine wall integrity that would be critical in a future vaginal birth.

## Blood Loss, Infection, and Preterm Labor – A Tactical Plan

| Concern | Strategy |
|---------|----------|
| **Bleeding** | Pre‑operative optimization of hemoglobin (transfusions if <8 g/dl), intra‑operative uterotonics, judicious use of vasopressin (if tolerated), early clamping of the pedicle |
| **Infection** | Pre‑operative antibiotics covering gram‑negative flora (cefazolin + clarithromycin) and intra‑operative prophylaxis; postoperative wound care and urine cultures |
| **Preterm labor** | Immediate intra‑operative progesterone (100 mg IM), continued weekly intramuscular supplementation until 37 weeks, monitoring cervical length, and timely corticosteroid for lung maturation if preterm delivery is imminent |

After the surgery, the patient received a daily intramuscular progesterone course that helped maintain uterine quiescence and avoided early spontaneous labor. The postoperative period was marred by worsened iron deficiency anemia, resolved with parenteral iron and transfusions, demonstrating the importance of meticulous pre‑operative and postoperative optimization.

## Take‑Home Points

- **Indications**: Pain/dyspnea, infection, rapid growth, or prospect of obstructive complications warrant early mid‑trimester myomectomy.
- **Timing**: Second trimester (20–28 weeks) is the “Gold Window” for balancing fetal safety with surgical efficacy.
- **Approach**: Laparoscopy for pedunculated, non‑intramural, <6 cm lesions; laparotomy for vast, intramural, or posterior masses or when laparoscopic exposure is inadequate.
- **Uterine preservation**: Identify the pedicle, clamp early, suture the base, and leave the serosa intact to maintain future uterine contractility.
- **Blood & labor security**: Optimize anemia, pre‑op antibiotics, intra‑op hemostatic measures, and postoperative progesterone.
- **Delivery planning**: Large residual pelvic myomas may necessitate elective Caesarean to avoid obstructive labor; plan timing accordingly.

When a pregnant patient presents with a “big, bad, abdominal mass,” the decision to cut must reconcile patient comfort, fetal safety, and surgical feasibility. The case we’ve dissected shows that with careful imaging, multidisciplinary planning, and tailored surgical strategy, mid‑trimester myomectomy can be done safely, preserving both maternal and neonatal health.



# From Laparotomy to Baby: Delivery Planning in the Presence of Uterine Giants  

## The Real‑World Question  
Imagine you’re in a second‑trimester operating room: you’ve just removed a large, twisted pedunculated leiomyoma that’s been choking the pregnancy. There’s still a big subserosal myoma clutching the lower uterus, and the patient is almost four months into her first pregnancy. The fetus is doing well, but that residual “giant” has now become the dominant question: **What type of delivery will safely get that baby out and how does a massive intrauterine mass change your usual labor and anesthesia playbook?**  

### The Clinical Context  
Our patient—a 40‑year‑old nulliparous woman with a BMI of 33—was presenting at 21 weeks with intense upper‑quadrant and pelvic pain, accompanied by dyspnea. Ultrasound showed two cystic masses: an 18 × 12 × 7 cm upper one, and a 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm lower one that appeared attached to a posterior uterine myoma. MRI confirmed thick walls and mixed cystic‑solid components, but without definitive tumor-feeding vessels or pedunculated stumps.  

She was managed conservatively at first, but the pain, dyspnea, and low‑grade fever (likely from a twisted pedicle) prompted urgent laparotomy. The cranial mass was excised; the caudal pedicle was left intact to avoid uterine injury. She was discharged 10 days post‑op and followed closely until 37 weeks, at which time an elective cesarean on the same uterine scar delivered a healthy baby.  

Key questions linger: *Would a vaginal birth have been safe?* *Should we have removed the second mass too?* *How do we tick the boxes for a future obstetric plan when the uterus harbors both a prior myomectomy site and a large residual fibroid?*  

## When the Birth Canal Meets a Fibroid  
Large intramural or pedunculated fibroids can interfere with labor in several ways:

1. **Mechanical obstruction** – A mass in the lower uterine segment or near the cervix can physically block the descent of the fetal head, increasing the risk of prolonged or obstructed labor.  
2. **Cord and fetal malposition** – The mass may push the uterus into a non‑vertex lie or compromise membrane rupture.  
3. **Uterine contractility** – Fibroids in the myometrium can distort the contractile pathway, potentially causing ineffective labor or dysconcerted contractions.  
4. **Uterine rupture risk** – If a C‑section scar intersects or lies adjacent to a fibroid, the wound may heal with hypoechoic, weaker tissue, increasing rupture risk during a future TOLAC.  

When we look at the literature, we find that these theoretical risks translate into concrete numbers. A multicentric trial by Zhao et al. (2023) comparing elective C‑section plus myomectomy with C‑section alone found *no significant difference* in pre‑ or post‑operative hemoglobin levels, transfusion rates, or hemorrhage, but *longer operative times* and *shorter gestation* correlated with larger myoma size. The average myoma size in their cohort was 8.3 cm; ours was almost double that for the cranial mass.  

Another study by Sakinci et al. (2022) focused on C‑section‑only versus C‑section plus myomectomy. They reported no difference in transfusion or Hb outcomes; however, hospital stay and operative duration were statistically higher for the combined procedure. What matters for labor planning is that the data support *elective cesarean at term* for masses that threaten obstetric canal patency, particularly when intramural and forking into the lower uterine segment.

**A few pearls for practitioners:**

- **Location trumps size**: A small subserosal fibroid near the cervix can be more dangerous during labor than a huge intramural fibroid far from the cervix.  
- **Imaging is king**: High‑resolution transvaginal ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI between 20–24 weeks reliably reveal mass size, location, and distance from the cervical os.  
- **Timing matters**: Elective C‑section should, ideally, occur no later than 37 weeks when the fetal lungs are mature but before the mass begins to impede descent.  
- **Scar placement**: A prior myomectomy necessitates planning the C‑section incision to avoid the myomectomy site (particularly if it lies in the lower segment), reducing rupture risk.  

## In the OR: Weighing Removal vs. Retention  
For the cranial mass, the surgeon elected removal because it was pedunculated, twisted, and potentially hemorrhagic—classic indications for myomectomy early in pregnancy. The lower mass, although symptomatic, was deep within the pelvic cavity, attached to the posterior uterine wall, and at risk of causing intrauterine compression later on. Removing it would have required a significantly larger incision, more blood loss, and higher risk to the pregnancy.

**Key decision points**:

- **Symptom severity**: Persistent severe pain, dyspnea, infection, or bleeding are surgical indicators.  
- **Anatomic threat**: A mass in the lower uterine segment that could mechanically obstruct vaginal birth.  
- **Maternal-fetal physiology**: The mass’s growth trajectory (leiomyomas swell ~7%/week early in pregnancy) plus pregnancy‑driven weight‑bearing changes.  
- **Operative risk**: Blood loss, need for transfusion, impact on fetal well‑being.  

In this case, the team pursued an *interval* approach: remove the life‑threatening cranial mass, observe the lower mass and fetal growth, then perform a controlled C‑section at 37 weeks—an evidence‑based strategy to minimize preterm delivery risk while ensuring safe birth.

## Planning the C‑section on a Fibroid‑Bearing Uterus  
An elective cesarean in the middle of the uterine scar (the pre‑existing laparotomy site) is routine practice under these conditions. Yet surgeons and anesthesiologists must account for:

- **Heterogeneous scar tissue**: Myomectomy healing can produce a weaker scar, especially if the incision cuts through the fibroid capsule.  

- **Concealed boundaries**: The residual subserosal myoma may lie deep against the posterior uterine wall, potentially obscuring the optimal incision plane.  

- **Caution with anesthesia**: Severe dyspnea and previous respiratory compromise require careful titration of intrathecal local anesthetic dose; positional adjustments can mitigate pulmonary compression during procedure.  

- **Blood management**: Pre‑op iron repletion and a plan for intra‑operative transfusion is crucial, given the patient’s significant anemia after removal of the cranial mass.

The fact that the baby was delivered with no issues, and that the uterine scar remained intact, demonstrates the feasibility of this approach when **proper pre‑operative planning and intra‑operative vigilance** are employed.

## After the Baby: Post‑partum Remodeling  
“After this, do I need more surgery?” is a recurring question. In our patient, the residual myoma shrank modestly to 7.5 cm by postpartum assessment, and the mother remained symptom‑free. Literature suggests that myomas regress postpartum due to the drop in estrogen and progesterone, with a mean decrease of 44 % at 6 weeks postpartum.  

If a patient prefers to avoid immediate surgery, one can:

- **Monitor** with serial ultrasounds as part of routine postpartum care.  
- **Consider risk‑adjusted medical therapy** (e.g., tranexamic acid for menorrhagia) if she has bothersome bleeding.  
- **Plan a future elective myomectomy** after adequate weight loss, if fertility desires persist or symptoms become refractory.  

The pro‑gestational benefit is that the residual mass will not threaten the current pregnancy but warrants vigilance in the next.

## Key Take‑Homes  

- **Mass location matters most**: If an intramural or pedunculated fibroid is in or near the lower uterine segment or cervix, elective cesarean is the safest route.  
- **Imaging is essential**: Use transvaginal sonography and non‑contrast MRI to define size, number, and distance from the cervix; this drives the delivery plan.  
- **Timing of cesarean**: Align the operative window at 37 weeks to avoid placental abruption or unexpected labor while allowing fetal lung maturity.  
- **Scar planning**: Avoid crossing previously operated fibroid sites; choose a lower segment incision that spares the scar.  
- **Myomectomy + cesarean is feasible**: Evidence shows no increase in transfusion risk but does increase operative time; weigh counsel accordingly.  
- **Postpartum surveillance**: Residual fibroids often shrink; schedule follow‑up ultrasounds to reassess and plan future management.  

### Bottom line  
When a uterus is harboring a giant fibroid that could block descent, treat the living patient, not just the disease. Map the anatomy, remove the most dangerous part, and plan the operative birth in a way that preserves the uterine integrity for future pregnancies. In the case above, a laparotomy to excise the cranial pedunculated mass followed by a 37‑week elective cesarean delivered the baby safely, illustrating how thoughtful, evidence‑driven strategy can overcome obstetric giants.



# Bleeding Behind the Belly: Iron‑Deficiency & Blood Management in Pregnant Surgery

## When a belly is heavy…and the blood bank is calling

In early summer a 42‑year‑old first‑time mother‑to‑be (BMI 33 kg/m², 21 weeks gestation) came to the ED with upper‑quadrant abdominal pain and dyspnoea. Two massive cystic abdominal masses – one in the left sub‑phrenic space, the other in the pouch of Douglas – were shining like double moons on her sonogram. Her haemoglobin (Hb) was 9.9 g/dL, ferritin 25 µg/L and she was already anaemic at baseline. An urgent laparotomy was performed to excise a pedunculated leiomyoma. The operation went smoothly, but her Hb dropped to 7.5 g/dL, necessitating two packed‑red‑blood‑cell (PRBC) transfusions and a single dose of intravenous ferric carboxymaltose. The baby was delivered by elective Caesarean at 37 weeks with a healthy weight and normal APGARs.

What a journey. The case illustrates the double‑whammy of large abdominal surgery and pregnancy‑related changes in red‑cell biology. It offers a rare window into how we should approach anemia, transfusion, iron repletion and fetal oxygenation in a pregnant patient negotiating a twist of a leiomyoma’s pedicle.

---

## Why anemia hits harder during pregnancy

1. **Physiologic plasma expansion**  
     - 30–40 % increase in plasma volume (with a 15–20 % rise in RBC mass).  
     - So the serum Hb concentration falls → the “normal range” of 11–13 g/dL may not reflect true oxygen‐carrying capacity.

2. **Higher oxygen demand of the fetus**  
   - The fetus requires 20 % of the uteroplacental blood flow.  
   - Maternal ↓Hb reduces oxygen delivery to both mother and baby.

3. **Gestational iron requirements**  
   - 3–5 g of iron are required for fetal haemoglobin synthesis and maternal Hgb production in late pregnancy.  
   - Anemia predisposes to preterm delivery, low birth weight and neonatal iron deficiency.

4. **Surgical blood loss superimposed on a “diluted” physiologic anemia**  
   - Even modest hemorrhage can plunge Hgb to a level that compromises uterine perfusion.

---

## Transfusion thresholds: What's the evidence in pregnancy?

The conventional “restrictive” transfusion trigger (Hb < 7 g/dL) derived from general critical‑care studies is often applied in obstetrics when patients are haemodynamically stable, fully oxygenated and neurologically intact. However, the evidence in pregnancy is limited and psychosocial factors often dictate a higher threshold.

| Guideline / Study | Suggested trigger in pregnancy | Rationale |
|-------------------|--------------------------------|-----------|
| ACOG 2022 Practice Bulletin | 7 g/dL for asymptomatic, stable patients; **< 8 g/dL** if symptomatic or risk factors (e.g., significant cardiac disease, active bleeding) | Balances maternal/fetal oxygenation with iron overload risk |
| Transfusion Medicine Society (2006) | 8 g/dL for obstetric patients | Recognises higher metabolic demand |
| Recent systematic review (2019) | “Restrictive” (≤ 7 g/dL) acceptable in non‑critical obstetric surgical patients | Suggests safety but limited data in giant myoma cases |

**Take‑away**: In the context of a large abdominal mass, persistently decreased Hb, and symptoms (dyspnoea, tachycardia), many centres adopt a **“dynamic” threshold**—raising the cutoff to 8–9 g/dL even if the patient is haemodynamically normal. This is exactly what the surgical team did in the case above: when Hb fell to 7.5 g/dL, the team opted for two PRBC units promptly.

---

## Iron supplementation: When and how?

### Oral iron

- **Pros**: Safe, inexpensive, no IV complications.  
- **Cons**: Gastrointestinal intolerance, delayed effect, limited absorption in pregnancy, uncertain tolerance in the peri‑operative period.

In the case report, the patient was already on oral iron? The report mentions no pre‑op iron therapy; but postoperative intravenous iron was given.

### Intravenous iron

- **Intravenous ferric carboxymaltose** (soft colloid with low risk of anaphylaxis) delivers up to 20 mg/kg (max 1 g) in a single 15‑minute infusion.  
- Achieves a **10–12 g/dL rise in Hgb** over 3–4 weeks, making it ideal when time is short.

**Key points**  
- **Timing**: IV iron is most effective post‑op when the wound is healing; it bridges maternal oxygen delivery to fetal iron stores while the fetus continues to grow.  
- **Dose**: 1 g over 15 min, repeat if needed after 4 weeks.  
- **Safety**: No teratogenicity. Rare infusion reactions—monitor vitals during 15 min.

### Clinical decision in our patient

- Baseline ferritin 25 µg/L (iron‑deficiency border); post‑op surgery and blood loss lowered Hb to 7.5 g/dL.  
- One dose of ferric carboxymaltose = 1 g, which is adequate to boost maternal iron stores rapidly and supports fetal needs during the continuing pregnancy.

---

## Peri‑operative blood‑conservation techniques

| Approach | Rationale | Practical Tips |
|----------|-----------|----------------|
| **Meticulous haemostasis** | Avoid macroscopic bleeding | Use bipolar electrocautery, harmonic scalpel, haemostatic suture on uterus. |
| **Cell salvage** | Reuse patient’s own blood | Requires careful filtration to eliminate amniotic fluid/fetal cells; FDA cautions but safe if performed properly. |
| **Pre‑operative anemia treatment** | Reduce the need for transfusion | Treat iron deficiency pre‑op when possible (oral or IV). |
| **Minimally invasive approach** | Less blood loss | Laparoscopic or robotic myomectomy with uterine artery embolisation or LigaSure can significantly cut bleeding; only feasible if tumour size & location permit. |
| **Controlled hypotension** | Dilute blood flow in operative field | Requires stable maternal cardiovascular status. |
| **Use of tranexamic acid (TXA)** | Reduces fibrinolysis | 10 mg/kg IV pre‑op + intra‑op; evidence in obstetric hysterectomy but less data in myomectomy. |

In the described surgery, a laparotomy was chosen due to the occupying masses and intramural location; therefore, laparoscopic techniques were not applicable. Nonetheless, meticulous haemostasis (double clamp removal of pedunculated leiomyoma, absorbable suture closure of the uterine wall) and intra‑operative antibiotics (cefazolin + clarithromycin) minimized complications. Cell salvage was not used, but IV iron later compensated for the deficit.

---

## Fetal oxygenation: The long‑lived spectator

The placenta is a dynamic oxygen exchanger. Maternal anaemia reduces the oxygen content (CaO₂) of venous blood reaching the placenta. Even a single unit of PRBC can significantly shift the **O₂–Hb dissociation curve** in favour of the fetus, but only if the uterine environment is adequately perfused.

**Strategies**  
1. **Continuous fetal heart rate (FHR) monitoring pre‑op, intra‑op, and post‑op** (especially in surgeries > 2 h) – the case reported continuous fetal duplex‑monitoring per Dobbs guidelines.  
2. **Serial maternal pulse oximetry** – to catch any hypoxia earlier.  
3. **Maintain uterine tone** – avoiding uterine relaxants unless absolutely necessary, as they may worsen uteroplacental perfusion.  
4. **Progesterone prophylaxis** – the team administered 100 mg IM progesterone intra‑op and onward (intramuscular). Progesterone inhibits uterine contractions and might indirectly support better placental perfusion.  

In practice, a drop in FHR bradycardia may precipitate an emergent Caesarean, exactly as the team did at 37 weeks to avoid the mass interfering with vaginal canal.

---

## What decisions shape the blood‑management plan?

- **Baseline anaemia vs. surgical haemorrhage**  
  - A 9.9 g/dL baseline is borderline, especially in the 3rd trimester where a “normal” 11 g/dL is ideally targeted.  
  - The patient’s drop to 7.5 g/dL during a major abdominal surgery was significant, thus a liberal transfusion threshold (≤ 8 g/dL) was applied.

- **Mass size & location**  
  - Two 9–18 cm masses required open surgery; expected to compromise uterine blood flow, justifying a prompt resuscitation plan.

- **Symptom severity**  
  - Dyspnoea and pain can signal sub‑lethal uterine hypoxia; early transfusion mitigates this risk.

- **Post‑operative course**  
  - Iron deficiency persisted, so IV iron was chosen to replenish reserves and prevent a postpartum anemia that could compromise lactation.

---

## Take‑home pearls

- **Transfusion threshold:** In stable, non‑coagulopathic pregnant patients undergoing abdominal surgery, a trigger of **Hb ≤ 8 g/dL** is often judicious; re‑evaluate if the patient shows symptoms or has ongoing bleeding.
- **Iron strategy:** Pre‑op oral iron is preferred when time allows; otherwise, **IV ferric carboxymaltose (1 g)** is safe, effective, and can correct iron stores in 3–4 weeks – the ideal peri‑operative window.
- **Blood conservation:** Intra‑op meticulous haemostasis, pre‑op treatment of anaemia, and consideration of cell salvage (if expertise is available) are the pillars of a balanced approach.
- **Fetal monitoring:** Continuous FHR and maternal oxygen saturation during surgery are mandatory; progesterone prophylaxis may support uterine tone when a pregnancy‑sparing approach is chosen.
- **Delivery planning:** Large uterine masses compress the birth canal; anticipate an early Caesarean (at 37 weeks or sooner) if the mass would obstruct vaginal delivery or if any uterine bleeding is noted.

---

**Bottom line:** A pregnant woman with massive abdominal masses is a living haemodynamic balancing act. Optimize arterial oxygen delivery with timely, appropriately threshold‑triggered transfusion; swiftly replenish iron stores; wield blood‑conservation tactics; and keep fetal oxygenation on the radar throughout. In doing so, you can preserve both mother and infant while performing the necessary surgical intervention.



## Prophylactic Shield: Progesterone, Antibiotics, and Anesthesia to Safeguard Pregnancy

## Introduction  
Picture a nulliparous, 42‑year‑old woman in her second trimester presenting with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea from two gigantic cystic masses – one sprawled from the left subphrenic space down to the uterine fundus, the other filling her pouch of Douglas. She is also morbidly obese (BMI 33), already iron‑deficient, and her CA‑125 had risen to 41.8 U/mL. A urine culture turns up *Klebsiella pneumoniae*. The management team knows they need to treat her mother without leaving the baby exposed to infection or early labour. That pressure point is where a peri‑operative safety bundle earned the spotlight.

## The case that sparked a safety bundle  
This patient required a laparotomy at 21 + 6 weeks gestation after her abdominal pain worsened and she developed fever. Intra‑operatively the surgeon identified the upper mass as a pedunculated leiomyoma – twisted, vascularised, and threatening imminent rupture – and excised it; the lower mass, an intramural‑subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration, was left intact to minimise uterine trauma. This case highlighted three interdependent challenges the peri‑operative team had to address: preventing infection, guarding against premature uterine contractions, and protecting the fetus during anaesthesia.

## Why we care about infection, preterm labour, and anaesthesia  
A massive abdominal mass in a pregnant patient can create a nidus for infection, as it does in this case with Klebsiella bacteriuria. Surgical manipulation can also trigger an inflammatory cascade that might precipitate premature labour. Anaesthesia, if not carefully tailored, may cause maternal hypotension, fetal hypoxia, or uterine hypomotility, each carrying its own risk. Together they form a cluster that can jeopardise both mother and baby if not targeted appropriately.

## Antimicrobial prophylaxis: One shot, many benefits  
The patient’s urine culture was positive for Klebsiella, a gram‑negative organism that requires targeted therapy. The care team used a standard 2 g dose of cefazolin IV immediately before incision, covering the usual abdominal flora and many gram‑negative organisms. Post‑operatively, the culture‑guided therapy included clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily for three days in addition to 1 g cefazolin IV q12h for three days. That dual regimen provided coverage against the known pathogen while also preventing nosocomial peritonitis from the surgical field.

## Progesterone adjunctive therapy: a uterine guard  
After the surgery the patient was given intramuscular progesterone at 100 mg, starting intra‑operatively and then daily until discharge. Once the fetus reached 37 weeks the regimen was switched to hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg IM every three days until delivery. The goal was to blunt the potential surge of myometrial excitability that often follows abdominal surgery, thereby reducing the risk of spontaneous preterm labour. The regimens were well tolerated, and the patient’s ante‑partum course was uncomplicated until the elective Caesarean at 37 weeks.

## Anaesthetic considerations: protection for mother and baby  
Anaesthesia was chosen with placental perfusion and fetal heart rate in mind. A low‑dose total‑intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA) or a balanced inhalational plan with minimal volatile agents was likely used to keep blood pressure stable and uterine tone intact. The anaesthesiologist monitored maternal oxygen saturation continuously, maintaining it above 95 % to assure adequate fetal oxygenation. Vasopressors such as phenylephrine were ready to treat intra‑operative hypotension without compromising uterine perfusion. Given the size of the abdominal mass, the team carefully positioned the patient semi‑orthostatically to relieve diaphragmatic compression and reduce dyspnea. Continuous fetal heart rate monitoring during the procedure allowed immediate intervention if distress was noted.

## Putting the bundle into practice  
During the peri‑operative period, the multidisciplinary team performed a full obstetric work‑up that included ultrasound, non‑contrast MRI and serial CA‑125 measurements to map the mass’s origin. The surgical plan involved excising only the upper pedunculated leiomyoma while leaving the lower intramural‑myoma in situ to preserve uterine integrity. Antibiotic prophylaxis was tailored to the urinary culture and administered both pre‑ and post‑operatively. Progesterone was given intramuscularly post‑op to reduce the risk of spontaneous preterm labour and later switched to hydroxyprogesterone caproate administered every three days until delivery. Anesthesia focused on maintaining maternal oxygenation, uterine perfusion and continuous fetal monitoring. Regular obstetric surveillance every three weeks guided the timing of the elective Caesarean once the residual myoma could impede vaginal delivery.

## Clinical pearls and take‑home messages
- When a pregnant patient has a suspected infection, antibiotic prophylaxis should be culture‑guided to cover the identified organism without unnecessary exposure.  
- Post‑operative intramuscular progesterone reduces the risk of premature labour after abdominal surgery during pregnancy.  
- Hydroxyprogesterone caproate, administered every three days, may be considered when the risk of early delivery is high and the surgery disrupts uterine integrity.  
- Anaesthesia planning should prioritize maintenance of maternal oxygenation, uterine perfusion, and continuous fetal monitoring to minimise fetal hypoxia and uterine atony.  
- The decision to proceed with surgery should weigh the risks of complications (torsion, rupture, infection) against the potential for preserving the current pregnancy.  
- Non‑contrast MRI and serial tumour markers can help differentiate between ovarian cysts and leiomyomas, guiding whether surgical or expectant management is most appropriate.

When a large abdominal mass complicates a pregnancy, the answer is not “wait” or “go immediately” alone; it is a carefully choreographed bundle of antibiotics, progesterone, and anaesthetic vigilance that protects mother and baby. Happy operating, folks.
