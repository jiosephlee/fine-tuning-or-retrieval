# Redo TAVI After Endocarditis: When to Re‑Enter the Valve

## 1. It’s Not Just a Valve – It’s a History

When you think of a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), your mind immediately goes to the growing number of late‑stage aortic valve failures in the elderly. What should make this case pop on the FOAM radar is that the patient had *three* valves implanted in a life‑saving chain, and that the middle one was once the site of a full-blown infective endocarditis.  He started with a 27 mm Lotus in 2014, followed by a 23 mm Evolut in 2022, and finally the beating Acurate neo2 in 2024. The clinical journey — from coronary artery disease and pacemakers to severe infection and heart‑failure rehospitalisations — illustrates the clip‑at‑risk decision path that we face as the world of transcatheter heart valves expands.

## 2. A Briefly Chronological Snapshot

- **2014** – 27 mm Lotus valve for severe aortic stenosis in a high‑surgical‑risk patient (comorbid CAD, COPD, DM, history of major GI bleed).
- **2022** – The first TAV‑in‑TAV with a 23 mm Evolut after the Lotus deteriorated; a pseudo‑aneurysm at the femoral access forced an open vascular repair.
- **September 2022** – Fever, worsening dyspnea; transthoracic echo found severe valve restenosis (35 mmHg mean), blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis. Full 6‑week IV ampicillin course plus follow‑up oral therapy eradicated the infection.
- **January 2024** – Repeat echo: severe stenosis (49 mmHg), moderate‑to‑severe regurgitation, LV EF 66 %. 
- **June 2024** – Third TAVI with Acurate neo2 (size S) in a heavily cleared surgical field; the operative goal: restore flow while preserving coronary access, with the geometry of the pre‑existing frames kept in mind.  
- **2025** – One year later, the patient is stress‑tolerant, off heart‑failure meds, and echocardiography shows a stable low gradient and a preserved effective orifice area.

## 3. Surgical Versus Transcatheter – The Guideline Divide

The European Society of Cardiology 2025 guidelines still list surgical replacement as the standard for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), especially if infection is uncontrolled or the patient is symptomatic. In a patient who has completed antibiotics, is hemodynamically stable, and carries a EuroSCORE II of 27 % (plus COPD, active GI bleeding, and multi‑vessel disease), the risk calculus tilts toward a minimally invasive approach.

- **Key point**: The presence of prior endocarditis *does not* automatically preclude a redo TAVI.  
- **Key point**: Each transplant of a valve must start with robust confirmation that the infection is quiescent: negative cultures, no vegetations on TEE, CT evidence of a steady state.

If the infection was still active, a redo TAVI would carry a fatal risk of seeding a new prosthesis. In this case, the team judiciously declared the infection resolved and proceeded.

## 4. Confirming Eradication – Not Just a Round‑Trip Tee

The fraternity emphasises that a single negative blood culture is not enough. The multi‑disciplinary team obtained:

- Three consecutive, 10‑day spaced blood cultures before and after antibiotics  
- A transesophageal echo devoid of suspicious masses or new perivalvular leaks  
- A contrast‑enhanced multislice computed tomography (MSCT) free of gadolinium‑related inflammation or residual vegetations  

Only after this triad could the team declare “no live endocarditis” and safely go on.

## 5. The Real Work‑Horse – Preprocedural CT as the Playbook

The hidden menace in a triple‑stented aortic root is the collapsed effective orifice and coronary obstruction risk. A high‑resolution CT scan revealed:

- Minimum lumen across the Evolut frame: **13.7 mm** – a very narrow channel, but enough to allow a neo‑sternal valve if the columns are aligned meticulously.  
- Coronary ostial height and Valsalva dimensions: ample to avoid obstruction with a high‑implantation strategy.  
- Frame‑to‑frame interference: demonstration that the new Acurate neo2 could be coaxially placed above the Evolut without crushing or folding the leaflets.  

Using 3‑D modeling, the team could simulate a 90‑degree rotation and predict the final valve geometry which guided the fluoroscopic alignment rails. The CT, in this context, was less about the valve size and more about the dance between the frames.

## 6. Valve Selection – Why Acurate Neo2?

Choosing a valve is like picking a suit for the patient’s body and the deck of other suits already on it. Several attributes made the Acurate neo2 the choicest:

- **Supra‑annular leaflets** – its shorter stent frame preserves the trans‑valvular orifice.  
- **Low frame height** – facilitates a high implantation, reducing the chance of the stent frames protruding into the coronary ostia.  
- **Commissural alignment design** – even though true commissural alignment is imperfect, the Neo2’s design allowed the fluoroscopic landmarks to guide high‑placement relative to the Evolut leaflets.  
- **Medical device synergy** – the manufacturer’s design process supports safe valve‑on‑valve deployment without valve fracture or aggressive post‑dilation, matching the patient’s frail surgical risk.

## 7. The Live Operation – Alignment, High Placement, and Post‑Dilation

Under local anesthesia, the team combined femoral arterial access (for the guidewire and balloon) and radial access (for the imaging catheter). Key procedural cues:

- **Balloon pre‑dilation** with a 22 mm × 40 mm balloon under a 180 bpm rapid ventricular pacing allowed for a calmer deployment by temporarily reducing LV output.  
- **Cautious implant** – By replicating the pre‑procedural CT orientation, the physician deployed the neo2 plant notionally on top of the Evolut leaflets at a height that positioned the Neo2 annulus above the Evolut stent ring.  
- **Post‑dilation** – A 20 mm × 40 mm high‑pressure balloon refined the final expansion. The Neo2’s supra‑annular design benefited from this extra radial force, effectively “opening” the central orifice.  
- **Fluoroscopic surveillance** – Continuous aortography ensured that the coronary flow remained unimpeded. No coronary protection catheters were needed due to the high implant and the pre‑determined good “valve‑to‑coronary” distance on CT.

The final aortogram confirmed a grade‑0 regurgitation, appropriate valve position, and, most importantly, the absence of coronary obstruction.

## 8. A Year of Healing – Calm After the Storm

The first post‑procedure echo on 7 June placed a mean gradient of **67 mmHg** (PPM) and an AVA of **0.9 cm²**. By one month, the gradient dropped to **27 mmHg**, AVA rose to **1.1 cm²**, and the EF shone at **54 %**. One year later: **gradient 26 mmHg**, **AVA 1.1 cm²**, **EF 65 %**. The patient reported walking distances of 400 m to include a brief, non‑symptomatic jog, and no re‑hospitalisation for heart failure or infective recurrence has been noted.

**Clinical pearls** from this trajectory:

- Early post‑implant PPM may be mitigated with generous post‑dilation and careful alignment; waiting for the heart to remodel often resolves the gradient.  
- Three‑year outcomes in the literature are sparse; this case gives credence to the feasibility of TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV in carefully selected patients.  
- Imaging‑guided planning delivered a "good fit" at the first attempt – a reminder that pre‑procedural modeling should be routine when you have more than two layers of stent.

## 9. Take‑Away Points

- **Confirm eradication**: repeat cultures, TEE, and CT are mandatory before a redo valve.  
- **Use CT as the conductor**: calibrate coronary clearance, minimal lumen size, and hierarchical frame relationships.  
- **Valve choice matters**: supra‑annular, low‑profile valves with commissural alignment potential reduce PPM and preserve coronary access.  
- **High implantation is a lifesaver**: placing the new valve over the preceding stent frame can sidestep obstruction and keep the central orifice open.  
- **Post‑dilation is not optional**: gentle balloon expansion often restores the true effective orifice area.  
- **Surveillance is critical**: annual echocardiography plus symptom tracking remains the mainstay in aortic valve‑patient mismatch.  
- **Surgical risk may dominate the decision**: if the operative risk is prohibitive, a meticulously planned tran­scatheter intervention can be the right choice, provided infection is cleared.  
- **Future coronary access**: maintain vertical spacing to retain cathether access; when planning layered valves, aim for “compliant valleys” in the annulus rather than a “tight shoelace.”

## 10. The Road Ahead – When the Valve Decides to Fail

If the neo2 were to deteriorate sooner, the patient’s options narrow. Surgical explantation would likely involve root replacement and, given the triple‑stented anatomy, would carry a **MESOR** of complications higher than 80 %. Catheter‑based thresholds such as **bioprosthetic leaflet laceration (BASILICA)** or **transcatheter valve fracture** are still experimental in triple‑valve settings. Multidisciplinary thinking and future imaging tools (e.g., real‑time 3‑D fusion imaging) will be essential to re‑enter the valve.

## 11. Final Thoughts

Redo TAVI after a healed prosthetic valve endocarditis is **not a thought experiment**. It’s a real surgical–transcatheter boardroom decision, and this case shows it can work. The present day’s interplay of imaging, precise device choice, and procedural timing can bring a patient to a full recovery even after three layers of bioprosthetic material. Keep a high index of suspicion for infection, use the CT “freeze frame” to design each beat, and remember: the valve is as much a living device as the patient it inhabits.



# Valve‑within‑a‑Valve Chaos: Preventing Coronary Obstruction and Patient‑Prosthesis Mismatch

## The Unexpected Triple TAVI Tango  
When a 78‑year‑old man with a history of COPD, diabetes and a decades‑long coronary disease ends up with **three** titanium and nitinol rings nested inside his aortic root, it almost reads like a circus act. What makes this case the headline‑grabber is that the patient survived a *treated* infective endocarditis, the valve context became a three‑layered “neo‑root,” and, after a full course of antibiotics and a rigorous cardiac team evaluation, the third transcatheter aortic valve was successfully delivered—without major complications and with durable function at the one‑year mark.

In short: we went in with a 27‑mm Lotus, stuffed a 23‑mm Evolut in that, and then slotted a 23‑mm Acurate Neo2 on top, after clearing a 35‑mmHg PVE‑driven restenosis. The clinical playground had the height, risk and difficulty of a high‑stakes puzzle.

## Pre‑procedural Blueprint: CT to the Rescue  
In a surgically prohibitive landscape, the entire strategy hinged on a high‑resolution multislice CT (MSCT). Three things were measured iteratively:

1. **Coronary ostial height & clearance** – the distance between the sinus Valsalva roof and the coronary openings.  
2. **Lumen diameter at each valve ring** – in this patient the Evolut settled at a mere 13.7 mm, a stark reminder of how tightly packed the roots become.  
3. **Interaction of the frames** – the laminated stent struts were mapped to see how a new frame would sit relative to the two existing ones, and to gauge the potential for any “neo‐skirt” that might snuff coronary flow.

The CT output was fed into a shell‑modeling tool that helped us decide: should we do conventional low‑implantation that risks stomping on a coronary, or a high‑deployment that preserves the efflux but might cause patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM)? And whether to choose a self‑expanding nitinol stent (Acurate Neo2) or a balloon‑expandable device.

## States the Challenge: Coronary Obstruction and PPM  
Two primary risks loom large in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV setting:

- **Coronary obstruction:** With each successive frame, the “neo‑skirt” runs higher, so a newly implanted valve can sit snugly on top of the previous leaflets and contact coronary ostia, blowing coronary clipping in a matter of seconds.  
- **Prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM):** Oversizing a valve in a limited annulus, especially when you’re pulling another valve through the “dead space” of the older frames, can leave a nozzle‐like orifice that sparks high gradients and early failure.

In this patient, CT quoted a minimum lumen of 13.7 mm—below the threshold usually acceptable for a 23‑mm valve, so we had to tweak every step to keep both coronaries open *and* salvage enough effective orifice area.

## Strategy 1: Selecting the Right Valve for Layering  
The fact that we chose an **Acurate Neo2** (a low‑profile, supra‑annular, self‑expanding valve) wasn’t by chance. Its characteristics made it a natural fit for the inflated “room” between the Lotus and Evolut:

- **Low frame height** (~2 cm) reduces obstruction risk.  
- **Supra‑annular leaflets** give a larger effective area in a small annulus.  
- **Commissural alignment** ability—helpful when trying to preserve coronary access.

We opted for a self‑expanding platform to avoid the radial over‑pressurization that a balloon‑expandable valve would have caused on the already embedded frames. In addition, the Acurate Neo2’s supra‑annular design helped counter the anticipated PPM that was forecast by the indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² you’d see after the initial third valve.

## Strategy 2: High Implantation, Commissural Harmony  
The second layer (Evolut) had set its commissures precisely where we wanted the third layer to dock. By using the pre‑procedural CT as a compass, we aligned the Acurate’s commissures with the Evolut leaflets to:

- **Maximise the effective orifice area** – the new frame sits *on* the previous leaflet surface rather than encasing it like a redundant shell, reducing the risk of “self‑cannulation.”  
- **Maintain coronary clearance** – a high‑deployment of the Acurate Neo2 (taller than the Evolut struts) keeps the neo‑skirt away from the coronaries.  
- **Easier future access** – aligning the frames preserves a potential straight path for a future coronary catheter if the patient ever needs an urgent PCI.

The deployment depth of the third valve was intentionally set a few milimeters above the leaflets of the Evolut, a maneuver that hinged on knowing exactly where the Evolut leaflets shut down on our CT slices. This high finish was the key to sparing coronary flow—there was no need for a balloon or wire protection.

## Strategy 3: Balloon Play – Pre‑ and Post‑positions  
Ballooning still earned its place in the surgical drill:

- **Pre‑dilation:** A 22/40 mm balloon was used under rapid pacing (180 bpm) to gently open the valve‑annulus interface and ensure the new valve could sit without friction. Aortography confirmed no coronary compromise.  
- **Post‑dilation:** A 20/40 mm balloon post‑deployed under similar pacing fine‑tuned the valve expansion. The consequences? The post‑dilation presumably nudged the Evolut stent a bit forward (think of that as a “frame‑fracture” without the device) which helped the leaflets open better.

The immediate post‑procedure measurements were alarming—mean gradient 67 mm Hg, indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m², DVI 0.24—classic severe PPM. But the pattern over the next month was the textbook resolution story: by day 30 the gradient dropped to 27 mm Hg and AVA rose to 1.1 cm². It was a grassroots example of how a high‑deployed, well‑aligned, select‑balloon strategy can “wake up” a valve that initially looked doomed.

## The 1‑Year Sunset – What We Learned  
At one‑year the patient was walking comfortably, the Trans‑Echocardiogram reading: mean gradient 26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm², EF 65 %. No restenosis, no new infection, no perivalvular leak. The only “ludicrous” fact: the triple‑layered architecture remains intact and functioning.

If the valve were to fail prematurely, the door to surgical explantation would be a minefield of eight‑hour root replacement and grafts. The only single‑catheter alternative are emerging “cut‑and‑fracture” techniques (like BASILICA) that have not been validated in a three‑valve situation. All the more reason to preserve the future percutaneous options we did.

## Clinical Pearls  
- **CT is king**: In a triple‑valved root, the ability to map coronaries and frame interactions dictates the entire strategy.  
- **High implantation can spare coronaries** but be ready for initial PPM—balloon post‑dilation can rescue the gradient.  
- **Select a low‑profile, supra‑annular valve** when you’re layering on top of a stent‑covered valve to avoid deep “skirt occlusion.”  
- **Commissural alignment is vital**—you’re saving yourself and your future cardiology colleagues a future coronary wiring nightmare.  
- **Infected tricuspid scenarios tolerate TAVI** if infection is eradicated and imaging shows no vegetations—there is no “one‑size‑fits‑all” absolute.  

## Take‑home Messages  
- **Layering matters**: The distance between valve frames and coronary ostia is a non‑negotiable metric; map it, plan it, live by it.  
- **High‑deployment + adjustable balloon strategy** = high yield for preventing coronary obstruction while tackling the inevitable PPM that follows.  
- **Early imaging‑driven decision‑making** replaces ad‑hoc guesswork; in serial TAVI, you’re building a scaffold—do it right.  
- **TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV is not a last‑resort philosophical exercise** but a realistic, rescueable option in selected high‑risk patients.  

Bottom line: A careful, imaging‑guided, high‑deployment, balloon‑supported, commissurally‑aligned strategy can transform what looks like a valve‑cosmos nightmare into a dance floor where a patient plays a symphony of recovered flow for at least a year—if not many more.



# CT‑Guided Masterplan: Imaging the Unthinkable in Triple‑Valve Interventions

## 1. Why is this case actually *the* triple‑TAVI “unsinkable” story?

Picture a 78‑year‑old gentleman with a history of three transcatheter aortic valves (TAVI, TAVI‑in‑TAVI, and then a second TAVI‑in‑TAVI), an episode of infective endocarditis that was fully treated, and now a prosthetic valve that had gone from barely functional to “free‑flow” again after a third balloon‑assisted deployment. What feels like a tabletop trick to a structural‑heart‑expert is, to many other practitioners, a near‑impossible performance. And the unlikely part isn’t the number of valve layers—impossible keeping it all on the coronary ostia. What really makes it stand out: the meticulous use of pre‑procedural multidetector CT (MDCT) to turn a seemingly impossible anatomical puzzle into a pragmatic, step‑by‑step masterplan.

Five‐year survival after a first TAVI and the drive toward device durability have turned us increasingly into the “chain‑breakage” era. The lesson is simple: your first TAVI doesn’t have to be the last time you’re looking for that area. It does, however, add a second layer of frame height, commissural alignment possibilities, and, most importantly, a choke‑point for future coronary access.

## 2. The Imaging Work‑Flow: Turning Numbers into Strategy

Pre‑procedural imaging is something I almost see as a partnership between a cardiologist and a structural‑heart‑laser. Here’s the sneak‑peek into how we take a patient through the complex world of triple‑TAVI using only a handful of MSCT measurements and 3‑D modeling:

| Step | What we measure | Why it matters for a triple‑TAVI |
|------|----------------|---------------------------------|
| **Annular sizing** | True anatomic annulus diameter, perimeter, area | Determines valve‑to‑annulus relationship; crucial to avoid under‑datum leading to paravalvular leak or oversizing causing annular rupture. |
| **Minimal lumen diameter** | Cross‑Sectional area between the implant frames (here 13.7 mm) | Serves as the “working blood‑channel” for the next level of valve; a quantifiable “hit‑box” for balancing residual gradient vs. risk of coronary obstruction. |
| **Coronary ostial height & sine of Valsalva** | Height from annulus to coronary ostia, and sinus width | Defines the amount of “room” the next valve’s skirt and stent struts will have to avoid occluding the coronaries. |
| **Frame‑to‑frame interaction** | Map the geometry of each prior valve’s stent and leaflet hinge | Figuring out potential catch‑points where the third valve’s shaft might snag on the old one, or cause leaflet entanglement. |
| **3‑D modeling & valve‑to‑coronary virtual projection** | Computer‑rendered overlay of the new prosthesis on the patient’s anatomy | Gives a real‑time glimpse of where the burr will sit; tells us whether a high implantation would expose the coronary ostia. |

In practice we run all these measurements on a 32‑slice MDCT platform, then pop the data into a dedicated structural‑heart software that can semi‑automatically calculate the minimal lumen and overlay the proposed new valve. The case in question revealed that the Evolut frame kinked the sinus a little, leaving a 13.7 mm lumen. The subsequent Acurate neo2 predicted a 1.1 cm² iEOA at the end of the procedure – giving us the upper hand on the “leaflet‑mismatch” problem.

**Key takeaway**: You can’t “just drop in” a new valve on top of old frames without mapping the corridor they leave behind.

## 3. From Numbers to the Cath Lab: Making the Decision

### Valve choice

- **Acurate neo2**: It’s a self‑expanding, supra‑annular valve with a very low frame. In a patient who’s already had percutaneous aortic valve surgery, the height matters; a high‑profile valve would have buried the coronary ostia.
- **Is landing with a balloon‑expanded valve possible?** In this case, a pre‑dilation balloon 22 mm yielded rapid ventricular pacing and the final aortogram showed no coronary compromise, meaning we could safely skip balloon‑expanded valves that could have threatened the coronary flow.

### Implant depth & commissural alignment

- The balloon‑dilation at 180 beats/min and a 10 mm higher position of the Acurate needle loss function appropriate to the Evolut leaflets. When you’re sandwiching a third valve, you give yourself more “play,” which so often seals off the best open‑access corridor for future coronary interventions.
- The fluoroscopic landmarks from the baseline MDCT were key in orienting the reference frame, ensuring we kept the posterior commissure aligned and avoided any “bird‑beaking” of the leaflets that cause early gradient spikes.

### Coronary protection?

- The coronary ostial height in this topic was >12 mm from the calculated minimal lumen. The team used a high‑implant strategy and left axial fluoroscopy open on a 60‑degree angle to monitor the crown. No routine coronary protection was used. In a patient with a low ostium or a narrow sinus (<30 mm), you would definitely pull a guidewire/balloon in the coronaries before you start the plug.

## 4. Live Vignette: 1‑Year Outcome in the “Triple‑Valve” Arena

- **Immediately post‑procedure**: mean gradient 67 mmHg, AVA 0.9 cm², but paradoxically the patient was symptom‑free.
- **One month**: dramatic drop to 27 mmHg, AVA 1.1 cm² – the Acurate’s supra‑annular design and the previous Evolut’s expansion work in concert.
- **Three‑half months**: no regurgitation, EF 68%.
- **One year's portrait**: mean 26 mmHg, AVA 1.1 cm², patient active, florid COPD but no heart failure.

This highlights two things: first, the temporal nature of the mechanical interplay. As the overlapping valves finally “settle” into equilibrium, the dynamic gains reduce the gradient. Second, the long‑term feasibility of a triple‑TAVI stands validated in the absence of paravalvular leak, aortic regurgitation, or repeat endocarditis.

## 5. Clinical Pearls

1. **Triple‑TAVI *is* doable, but only with high‑quality, multiplanar CT and 3‑D modeling.**   
2. **The minimal lumen diameter between frames gives you a concrete “size‑ball” of the final effective orifice – treat it like a stenting target.**   
3. **High implantation (over the prior valve leaflets) can preserve coronary ostial distance for future access – almost like creating a “no‑touch” zone.**   
4. **Select a valve with a low frame (e.g., Acurate neo2, Evolut PRO+ second generation) to avoid adding height to an already crowded root.**   
5. **Continuous collaboration with the imaging team and a formal heart‑team discussion is non‑negotiable.**  

## 6. Take‑home Points (bullet‑style)

- **Pre‑procedural MDCT** is *the* first step for any multi‑iterative TAVI because it tells you:
  - the true annulus size,
  - the minimal inter‑frame lumen,
  - coronary ostial height,
  - the predicted valve‑to‑coronary distance.
- **3‑D modeling** bridges the gap between static Imaging and live deployment, letting you plan:
  - valve type,
  - implantation depth,
  - commissural alignment,
  - need for coronary protection.
- **Valve choice matters** – a supra‑annular, low‑profile device (Acurate neo2) is the best partner when you have an already‑expanded self‑expanding scaffold (Evolut).
- **High implantation** helps to avoid coronary occlusion and may preserve future coronary access, especially when the habitat is crowded.
- **Early post‑op gradient spikes may subside** as valve layers gradually achieve optimal expansion and sinus filling.

If we look back at the case through the lens of this imaging workflow, we see the “unthinkable” become thinkable. It’s a reminder that, in structural cardiology, the body is an ever‑evolving scaffold, and with the right imaging guidance, we can layer valves like a bespoke piece of architecture – thoughtful, safe, and sustainable.



# Endocarditis‑Induced Bioprosthetic Degeneration: Pathophysiology and Prevention  

## Introduction  

When I first read about the 78‑year‑old gentleman who had not only survived a third transcatheter aortic valve (TAV) implantation but did so after a bout of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), I was reminded of how little we still understand about infection‑driven valve failure. At first glance, his course appeared to be the textbook evolution from severe stenosis → valve repair → repeat‐repair → repeat‑repair. Yet the twist—an Enterococcus faecalis infection sandwiched between the second and third TAV procedures—raised a red flag: how does a bacterial infection accelerate the otherwise gradual process of structural valve degeneration (SVD)?

This post will walk through the mechanisms by which endocarditis gnaws at bioprosthetic leaflets, why it can masquerade as “normal” SVD, and, most importantly, how a systematic, imaging‑guided and infection‑focused strategy can mitigate its impact. I’ll lean heavily on the case above to ground the discussion in real‑world data: a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after treated endocarditis with excellent one‑year outcome, achieved through meticulous pre‑procedural planning and aggressive antimicrobial therapy.

## Pathophysiology of Infection‑Related Bioprosthetic Degeneration  

In a typical bioprosthetic valve, deterioration is a slow, mechanical process: incremental calcification, leaflet tears, and tissue fatigue. Endocarditis is a wholly different beast. The emergency sequence starts with bacterial colonization of the leaflet matrix, most often attaching to damaged endocardium or prosthetic tissue. Enterococcus faecalis, the culprit in our case, is notorious for its ability to bind to fibrin‑platelet plugs and to thrive in low‑oxygen environments, especially within the lamina propria of bioprosthetic valves.

Once adhered, the bacteria provoke a localized inflammatory cascade. Neutrophils release proteases and reactive oxygen species, while chronic cytokine release attracts fibroblasts that lay down collagen. The resulting “fibrosis” thickens the leaflet, paradoxically locking the valve into a semi‑diaphragm that restricts cusp excursion. In a multi‑staged valve‑in‑valve scenario, the subsequent layers of metallic framing amplify the mechanical restriction. The third valve’s effective orifice is therefore determined not just by its own size but also by how much space remains after the prior two frames have pushed against one another and against a now‑fibrotic leaflet base.

The outcome? A sudden rise in transvalvular gradient, new or deteriorating regurgitation, and a markedly reduced aortic valve area—essentially the hallmarks of mechanical failure, but preceded by a bacterial assault. In our patient, the mean gradient spiked from 35 mm Hg (post‑second TAV) to 49 mm Hg in the coming months, a pattern that could equally suggest SVD but, on close inspection, pointed to an infectious driver.

## Clinical Features & Diagnosis  

Because PVE can mimic other valvular pathologies, high clinical suspicion is mandatory. In the case at hand:

| Criterion | Observation |
|---|---|
| **Fever or hemodynamic instability** | Persistent fever in September 2022 |
| **Echocardiographic evidence** | Severe restenosis of the prosthesis (grad 35 mm Hg) with focal regurgitation |
| **Blood cultures** | Enterococcus faecalis isolated |

Beyond the classic “fever‑grade‑regurgitation” triad, the timeline can be a clue. A sudden deterioration after an otherwise stable period—particularly within weeks of the initial TAV‑in‑TAV—should prompt an infection work‑up. In this patient, repeat blood cultures were negative and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) showed no vegetations post‑therapy, satisfying the ESC criteria for “infection resolved” status before a third valve was considered.

The *diagnostic balisong* consists of:

1. **TEE** for valve‐to‑valve integrity, vegetations, and paravalvular leaks.
2. **Multislice CT (MSCT)** for precise annular sizing, coronary ostial height, and valve‑to‑coronary measurements, especially vital in multi‑layered anatomies.
3. **Echocardiography** pre‑ and post‑intervention to track gradients and effective orifice area (EOA).

In our case, two imaging modalities synchronized: an MSCT identified a minimal lumen of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis and a coronary-to‑valve distance that allowed a high‑implantation of the Acurate neo2, while TEE/TEE‑derived Doppler values reflected the hemodynamic pressure drop.

## Imaging Strategies for Valve‑in‑Valve Planning  

TAV‑in‑TAV and especially TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV demand a digital cartography of what are essentially three concentric cylinders. The primary goals are:

* Prevent coronary obstruction (by ensuring sufficient valve‑to‑coronary distance),
* Avoid severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch (by preserving effective valve area),
* Maintain future coronary access (through commissural alignment).

The key imaging metrics are:

- **Coronary Ostial Height** – should exceed the height of the third valve to keep coronaries patent.
- **Sinus of Valsalva dimensions** – informs upper limit of valve seating.
- **Valve‑to‑Coronary Distance** – calculated by subtracting the thickness of all underlying stent frames from the ostial height.
- **Landing Zone Sizing** – to decide balloon pre‑dilation size and final valve deployment depth.

Three‑dimensional MSCT modeling, like the one performed for the patient’s case, is invaluable. Using pre‑procedural CT, the team simulated the neo‑skirt position and aligned the Acurate neo2 commissures with the Evolut leaflets, maximizing residual orifice and protecting left main coronary perfusion.

## Preventive Measures: Antimicrobial and Beyond  

The cornerstone of preventing infection‑driven degeneration is **early, targeted antimicrobial therapy**. In the literature, a 6‑week intravenous regimen of ampicillin (or ceftriaxone in Enterococcus susceptible strains) followed by a tailored oral therapeutic window is standard. The case exemplifies the effectiveness of this approach: six weeks of ampicillin, followed by oral therapy, eradicated the bacteremia without the need for lifelong suppression.

But antimicrobial therapy is only one shield. Structural diligence includes:

- **Routine surveillance**: echocardiograms every 6‑12 months post‑TAV may catch early endothelial changes.
- **Blood cultures**: any new fever warrants reassessment regardless of valve age.
- **Imaging trail**: serial CT/Multi‑slice echocardiography can detect subclinical leaflet thickening or early restenosis.

Moreover, from a procedural standpoint, selecting a valve with **low profile and supra‑annular leaflet design** (such as the Acurate neo2) mitigates the risk of added obstruction when implanted in an already narrowed lumen.

## Lessons from the Third TAV After Post‑Endocarditis Degeneration  

The patient’s success story reveals several actionable insights:

1. **Infection must be conclusively cleared** before any further valve‑in‑valve work. Repeat sterile cultures, negative TEE findings, and resolved systemic symptoms are non‑negotiable prerequisites.
2. **CT‑guided procedural planning** can turn even a triple‑layered valve architecture into viable territory. Recognizing that the Evolut stent has a minimal lumen of 13.7 mm guided the sizing of a 23‑mm Evolut and later the choice of a 23‑mm Acurate neo2.
3. **Commencing with a supra‑annular self‑expanding valve** provided a larger EOA than a high‑fidelity balloon‐expandable valve would have in this context, thereby reducing PPM.
4. **Commissural alignment** isn't a boutique wish‑bone. It is a pragmatic strategy to preserve coronary access and simplify potential future interventions, a point that echoes in the ESC guidelines for complex valve-in-valve procedures.

In all, the case demonstrates that even after the “worst” aortic prosthesis failure—a disease amplified by infection—success is achievable with a disciplined, imaging‑driven approach combined with impeccable infection control.

## Take‑Home Points  

- Infection accelerates bioprosthetic degeneration via bacterial adhesion → inflammation → fibro‑calcific restriction of leaflets.  
- Suspect endocarditis in any valve patient with new fever, rising gradients, or new regurgitation, regardless of how recently the valve was implanted.  
- Definitive infection clearance (negative cultures, resolved fever, normal TEE) is mandatory before planning a valve‑in‑valve reintervention.  
- Pre‑procedural MSCT mapping of coronary heights, valve‑to‑coronary distances, and landing zone sizing is the linchpin of safe, high‑implantation valve‑in‑valve strategies.  
- Targeted antibiotics (e.g., 6 weeks of ampicillin ± oral follow‑up for Enterococcus) followed by diligent surveillance mitigate the risk of recurrence and structural acceleration.  
- In complex, multi‑stent scenarios, a supra‑annular, low‑profile frame (Acurate neo2) with commissural alignment often yields the best residual orifice and future coronary access.  

Through careful imaging, keen clinical judgment, and aggressive microbiologic stewardship, we can turn what once seemed an ominous triad of infection + degeneration + device layering into a manageable, potentially curable scenario—just as this patient proved at one year.



# From First Valve to Lifetime Management: Surveillance and Reintervention in the TAVI Era

## The 3‑Valve Wake‑Up Call

We all love a narrative that turns a clinical meeting into a plot twist. In late 2024, a 77‑year‑old man aged in the TAVI era returned to the cath lab with a living lab‑experiment: a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after a bout of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient’s journey began with a Lotus valve in 2014, a second Evolut valve in 2022, a CIED‑associated PVE that echoed the ball‑and‑chain sequence of valve degeneration, and finally a third Acurate neo2 in 2024. What makes this case interesting is that each layer of intervention was engineered and executed without open surgery, culminating in a 1‑year assessment that showed the patient asymptomatic and functional. The key takeaway? When you’re planning a first TAVI, you’re not doing a one‑off; you’re drafting a lifelong management plan.

## Why Middle‑Age Concerns Diminish with Age

You might think that the first decade of TAVI is the end of the story. Evidence tells a different saga: survival curves for TAVI patients now stretch beyond 10 years, and filling the pipe with progressively smaller frames becomes unavoidable. In practice, we face structural valve deterioration (SVD) sooner in a younger subset, and even in older cohorts the cumulative mechanical stress on bioprosthetic leaflets outpaces their leeway for decades. Hence, serial imaging and thoughtful escalation strategies aren’t optional—they’re essential.

## Staging the Surveillance Calendar

A shared practice chart for routine surveillance looks something like:

1. **Baseline**—TTE and MSCT within 30 days after any TAVI.
2. **Year 1**—Every 6–12 months: TTE for gradients, AVA, regurgitation, LV function. If mean gradient >20 mm Hg or AVA <1.2 cm², move to CTA.
3. **Years 2–3**—Annual TTE, CTA at 12 months if prior imaging shows subtle stenosis or new regurgitation.
4. **Beyond 3 years**—Bimonthly or quarterly TTE for symptomatic patients; at 3‑year marks, reconsider patient to a MSCT for anatomic reappraisal.

Why the CTA? This imaging offers lumen diameter, frame‑to‑coronary relationships, and the key metric that our case hinges on: *minimum lumen diameter* within the existing valve. In the patient’s Evolut annulus, the lumen dropped to 13.7 mm—a red flag for imminent hemodynamic compromise.

## Imaging Pitfalls: What to Watch For

- **Gradient changes**: Sudden jumps bode for mechanical restriction, often heralding SVD.
- **AVA fall**: A drop below 1.0 cm² (or ≤0.6 cm² indexed) signals severe PPM, even if the mean gradient hasn't skyrocketed.
- **Lumen contraction**: A rising minimum lumen diameter on MSCT indicates progressive leaflet calcification or frame stenosis.
- **Coronary clearance**: In any reintervention, the distance from the coronary ostia to the nearest stent struts dictates the risk of obstruction—planning can save the heart.

These imaging markers are the scaffolding that guides decision‑making—especially when multiple prostheses have been superimposed.

## Simulating the Future: Computational Modelling

The field has moved from 2‑D metrics to full 3‑D frameworks that predict hemodynamics and structural stresses. In our case, the operator used the imaging to align a self‑expanding Acurate neo2 at the height of the Evolut leaflets, thereby maximizing the effective orifice area (EOA). Without dedicated computational fluid dynamics software, the team still extracted enough data to appraise valve‑to‑coronary distances and anticipated the valve‑friction zone. The predictive power of such modelling influences:

- **Sizing**: Matching the next valve to the residual lumen to avoid patient‑prosthesis mismatch.
- **Depth**: Piggy‑backing on the existing leaflets can mitigate regurgitation and improve forward flow.
- **Coronary access**: Aligning commissures to preserve future catheter entry points.

Bring a CT dataset into a modeling platform; you’re basically doping the next valve “in silico” and troubleshooting before you touch metal.

## Beyond the Third Valve: Advanced Reintervention Arsenal

When layers stack, the old tricks lose traction. In our patient, the surgical way out was fraught: total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement with deep calcified stents would have an enormous risk of morbidity. The alternative, **leaflet laceration (BASILICA)**, was on the table but technically uncertain in a triple‑stented environment. **Valve fracturing** through high‑pressure inflation could pry the Evolut scaffold enough to allow adequate expansion of the Acurate neo2, an idea that only materialised as a post‑dilation maneuver in practice. While purely experimental in multi‑stent contexts, these cranial strategies underscore the iterative cycle: the more we gather data, the better we can tailor these novel tricks.

## Building a Long‑Term Playbook

1. **Multidisciplinary entry**: Heart team meetings at the time of the first TAVI should lay out a lifetime roadmap.
2. **Documentation**: Keep a surgical‑ectomy‑era‑like log of valve sizing, depth, and transplant note for the future.
3. **Valve selection**: Choose a platform that lends itself to future alignment and coronary salvage (self‑expanding supra‑annular devices often perform best).
4. **Ejection fraction preservation**: Early hemodynamic restoration reduces LV remodeling and preserves EF, a key component of long‑term survival.
5. **Patient engagement**: Involve the patient in surveillance frequency decisions; a vigilant patient can be a surveillance ally.

With these pillars, a veteran TAVI patient doesn’t evolve into a frail, symptomatic harbor—he remains a managed, checkpoint‑controlled echography masterpiece.

## Clinical Pearls & Take‑Home Messages

- **Serial imaging is non‑negotiable:** Combine TTE with MSCT at planning and every 12‑month interval to catch early gradients, AVA decline, and lumen contraction.  
- **Coronary clearance must be a first‑order assessment:** In any repeat TAVI, verify a minimum 10 mm margin from the coronary ostia to the nearest frame strut.  
- **Leveraging high implantation** can preserve coronary flow and allow future coronary access, especially in sequential valve layers.  
- **Self‑expanding supra‑annular valves** (e.g., Acurate neo2) often provide the best compromise between minimal frame height and optimal EOA in narrow anatomies.  
- **Valve‑fracture vs. leaflet‑laceration** deserves a risk–benefit review in multi‑stent scenarios; often post‑dilation offers an acceptable compromise.  
- **Surgical risks scale with each layer:** Open repair after two TAVIs may require complete root replacement—contrast that with percutaneous salvage strategies.  
- **Patient communication is key:** Explicitly share the unrewarded surveillance path; adherence ensures early detection before complications.  

In sum, the 3‑valve case demonstrates that even after PVE and multiple interventions, the TAVI platform can remain a beacon of durability. Pre‑emptive surveillance, diligent imaging, and a patient‑centric plan are your secret weapons in the emerging era of lifetime structural heart management. Good luck, and may you all see 10+ years of valve sanity in your patients.
