Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Workup and Imaging Strategy for Repeat Intervention

The patient’s presentation of progressively worsening exertional dyspnoea and fatigue, coupled with a marked rise in valve gradient (to 49 mm Hg) and moderate-to‑severe intraprosthetic regurgitation, triggered the workup for repeat valve intervention. The clinical picture was consistent with severe prosthetic valve stenosis (gradient > 35 mm Hg), significant regurgitation, symptomatic status and an improving left‑ventricular ejection fraction (from a nadir of 37 % after the second TAVI to 66 % at the time of re‑intervention), all of which qualify the patient for re‑intervention per current structural heart guidelines.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) provided the initial haemodynamic assessment. Using the continuity equation, the mean gradient of 49 mm Hg and an aortic valve area of 0.5 cm² (indexed 0.17 cm²/m²) were calculated, indicating severe stenosis. Doppler velocity integration and pulse‑wave Doppler of the left ventricular outflow tract determined the stroke volume and indexed effective orifice area (EOA). Two‑dimensional imaging also revealed grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, while three‑dimensional transoesophageal echocardiography (3‑D TEE) refined the valve geometry, excluded paravalvular leak, and confirmed the absence of residual vegetations or abscess formation. These data confirmed structural deterioration of the second transcatheter valve and ruled out ongoing infection.

Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) was paramount for procedural planning. The CT scan measured a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the previously implanted Evolut prosthesis, establishing that the subsequent valve could be accommodated at an appropriate depth. Sinus of Valsalva dimensions, coronary ostial height, and valve‑to‑coronary distances were all quantified, allowing assessment of coronary clearance and the risk of obstruction. Importantly, the CT images delineated the interface of the overlapping stent frames and facilitated a neo‑skirt prediction, ensuring that the third valve would not impinge upon coronary flow or compromise future coronary access. This comprehensive imaging strategy therefore allowed for precise valve sizing, optimal implantation depth, and avoidance of coronary obstruction without the need for coronary protection hardware.

Resolution of infective endocarditis was critical prior to the third intervention. Six weeks of intravenous ampicillin therapy (supplemented with bicarbonate‑based modulation) resulted in negative serial blood cultures. Repeat 3‑D TEE demonstrated no vegetations, abscesses, or paravalvular leaks, while a follow‑up CT scan revealed no residual paravalvular collections. These findings fulfilled the infection‑resolution criteria and justified proceeding with a transcatheter approach.

Surgical risk assessment further guided the decision. The EuroSCORE II of 27.36 % and a high Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) risk score reflected both the patient’s comorbid burden (COPD, diabetes, prior gastrointestinal bleeding) and the technical challenges of open surgery—namely, the need for extensive aortic root replacement due to multi‑layered stent fixation and proximity of prior prosthetic frames to the coronary ostia. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team deemed a third TAVI as the safest viable option, aligning with current ESC guidelines that recommend surgical valve replacement in PVE only when surgery is probable to be safe and uncomplicated.

In sum, the diagnostic workup integrated multi‑modality imaging and stringent infection‑resolution criteria to confirm severe prosthetic valve dysfunction, exclude active endocarditis, and verify procedural feasibility, thereby enabling a structured, patient‑centric approach to a high‑complexity repeat transcatheter aortic valve implantation.
