Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 3: ## Procedural Planning and Technological Considerations for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV

Performing a third transcatheter valve intervention within an already expanded bioprosthetic frame poses a series of interrelated technical challenges. Chief among these are the risk of coronary artery obstruction, the incremental loss of effective orifice area (EOA) with each new valve layer, and the potential for frame–frame interaction that can lead to under‑expansion or patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM). The case report demonstrates how meticulous imaging‑guided planning and a step‑wise procedural strategy can overcome these obstacles and yield durable hemodynamics.

### Valve choice
The chosen third prosthesis was an Acurate Neo2 self‑expanding valve (S‑size). Its design features—supra‑annular leaflet orientation, a short, low‑profile frame, and radiopaque commissural markers—directly address the critical concerns of a triple‑stented anatomy. Supra‑annular leaflets provide a larger effective orifice, reducing the likelihood of PPM in a constrained neo‑annulus. The low frame height limits the risk of coronary flow compromise, a vital consideration when the coronary ostia are already in close proximity to the existing Evolut struts.

### Sizing methodology
Pre‑procedural multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) routinely supplies the annular dimensions necessary for valve sizing. In this case, annular diameter and area were measured using standard orthogonal planes, while the *predicted neo‑annulus*—the diameter that would be available after deploying the Acurate within the Evolut—was simulated in a 3‑D reconstruction. The S‑size was selected to achieve an oversizing ratio of approximately 8–10 % relative to the measured neo‑annulus, a range that empirically balances adequate radial force against the risk of perforation or induced PPM. The MDCT also quantified the coronary–prosthesis gap to inform coronary protection decisions.

### High‑implant strategy
A central element of the procedural plan was to deliver the new valve *above* the leaflets of the previously implanted Evolut valve. Landing the Acurate above these leaflets preserves the lower landing zone free from strut interference, thereby minimizing neo‑skirt formation beneath the coronary ostia and allowing more rapid closure of the supra‑annular space. The planned depth was guided by CT‑derived measurements of the Evolut leaflet height (≈ 8 mm from the annulus) so that the Acurate’s frame would sit within a zone of clear radial expansion while not impinging the coronary ostia.

### Commissural alignment
The Acurate Neo2 includes radiopaque commissural markers that can be matched to the pre‑existing commissures on CT. A 3‑D CT reconstructions identified the orientation of the Evolut commissures, and these were projected onto the fluoroscopic screen. By aligning the new valve’s markers with the target angles during deployment, the team achieved commissural congruence, which not only improves hemodynamic performance but also ensures future coronary access by reducing the likelihood of stent strut obstruction.

### Coronary protection assessment
MDCT analysis revealed a coronary‑to‑prosthesis gap of > 4 mm at the lowest strut of the Evolut frame. Given this clearance and the high‑implant position of the Acurate, the team deemed it safe to forgo routine coronary protection—such as pre–placed balloon or guidewire “chimney” techniques. The left radial access remained available to deliver a rapid coronary angiogram should an occlusion be detected intra‑procedurally.

### Pre‑ and post‑dilation protocol
A 22 × 40 mm balloon was first inflated across the Evolut valve under rapid ventricular pacing (180 bpm) to open the neo‑annulus and to decompress the existing struts. This step enables smooth passage of the Acurate delivery sheath and prepares the landing zone for full expansion. After valve deployment, a 20 × 40 mm balloon was used for post‑dilation, once again under rapid pacing, to optimize radial expansion and to coax the Evolut frame into a more favorable orientation. Importantly, this post‑dilation was performed with a high‑pressure, low‑profile balloon to limit radial stress on the existing stents while maximizing the EOA of the new valve.

### Dual access and anesthesia
The procedure employed a dual‑access approach: a 7–9 Fr left femoral sheath for the Acurate delivery system and a 6 Fr left radial sheath for coronary angiography and rapid pacing support. Local anesthesia, rather than general sedation, minimized hemodynamic instability and accelerated recovery—an approach aligned with the patient’s high anesthetic risk profile. Rapid pacing was achieved via a femoral anesthetic pacing lead, allowing controlled LV unloading during valve deployment and dilation.

### Intra‑procedural imaging
During the operation, a combination of imaging modalities ensured real‑time confirmation of safety and success. Aortography provided a continuous visual of valve position, coronary flow, and transvalvular gradients. Fluoroscopy, calibrated with the CT‑derived commissural markers, guided precise deployment depth and vertical alignment. Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) offered high‑resolution assessment of leaflet motion, paravalvular sealing, and immediate detection of regurgitant jets. By cross‑referencing data from these modalities, the team confirmed correct valve seating and the absence of coronary obstruction before finalizing the intervention.

Through this integrated planning and execution framework—leveraging CT‑guided sizing and strategy, careful valve selection, a high‑implant technique, commissural alignment, judicious dilation, and robust intra‑procedural imaging—the third transcatheter valve was delivered safely without coronary compromise or significant PPM. The one‑year follow‑up data confirm sustained valve function and reinforce the viability of TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV as a feasible option for selected high‑risk patients with complex prosthetic valve failure.
