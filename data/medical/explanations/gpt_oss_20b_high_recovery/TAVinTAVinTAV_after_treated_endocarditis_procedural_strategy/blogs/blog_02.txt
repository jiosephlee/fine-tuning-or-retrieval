Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Valve‑within‑a‑Valve Chaos: Preventing Coronary Obstruction and Patient‑Prosthesis Mismatch

## The Unexpected Triple TAVI Tango  
When a 78‑year‑old man with a history of COPD, diabetes and a decades‑long coronary disease ends up with **three** titanium and nitinol rings nested inside his aortic root, it almost reads like a circus act. What makes this case the headline‑grabber is that the patient survived a *treated* infective endocarditis, the valve context became a three‑layered “neo‑root,” and, after a full course of antibiotics and a rigorous cardiac team evaluation, the third transcatheter aortic valve was successfully delivered—without major complications and with durable function at the one‑year mark.

In short: we went in with a 27‑mm Lotus, stuffed a 23‑mm Evolut in that, and then slotted a 23‑mm Acurate Neo2 on top, after clearing a 35‑mmHg PVE‑driven restenosis. The clinical playground had the height, risk and difficulty of a high‑stakes puzzle.

## Pre‑procedural Blueprint: CT to the Rescue  
In a surgically prohibitive landscape, the entire strategy hinged on a high‑resolution multislice CT (MSCT). Three things were measured iteratively:

1. **Coronary ostial height & clearance** – the distance between the sinus Valsalva roof and the coronary openings.  
2. **Lumen diameter at each valve ring** – in this patient the Evolut settled at a mere 13.7 mm, a stark reminder of how tightly packed the roots become.  
3. **Interaction of the frames** – the laminated stent struts were mapped to see how a new frame would sit relative to the two existing ones, and to gauge the potential for any “neo‐skirt” that might snuff coronary flow.

The CT output was fed into a shell‑modeling tool that helped us decide: should we do conventional low‑implantation that risks stomping on a coronary, or a high‑deployment that preserves the efflux but might cause patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM)? And whether to choose a self‑expanding nitinol stent (Acurate Neo2) or a balloon‑expandable device.

## States the Challenge: Coronary Obstruction and PPM  
Two primary risks loom large in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV setting:

- **Coronary obstruction:** With each successive frame, the “neo‑skirt” runs higher, so a newly implanted valve can sit snugly on top of the previous leaflets and contact coronary ostia, blowing coronary clipping in a matter of seconds.  
- **Prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM):** Oversizing a valve in a limited annulus, especially when you’re pulling another valve through the “dead space” of the older frames, can leave a nozzle‐like orifice that sparks high gradients and early failure.

In this patient, CT quoted a minimum lumen of 13.7 mm—below the threshold usually acceptable for a 23‑mm valve, so we had to tweak every step to keep both coronaries open *and* salvage enough effective orifice area.

## Strategy 1: Selecting the Right Valve for Layering  
The fact that we chose an **Acurate Neo2** (a low‑profile, supra‑annular, self‑expanding valve) wasn’t by chance. Its characteristics made it a natural fit for the inflated “room” between the Lotus and Evolut:

- **Low frame height** (~2 cm) reduces obstruction risk.  
- **Supra‑annular leaflets** give a larger effective area in a small annulus.  
- **Commissural alignment** ability—helpful when trying to preserve coronary access.

We opted for a self‑expanding platform to avoid the radial over‑pressurization that a balloon‑expandable valve would have caused on the already embedded frames. In addition, the Acurate Neo2’s supra‑annular design helped counter the anticipated PPM that was forecast by the indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² you’d see after the initial third valve.

## Strategy 2: High Implantation, Commissural Harmony  
The second layer (Evolut) had set its commissures precisely where we wanted the third layer to dock. By using the pre‑procedural CT as a compass, we aligned the Acurate’s commissures with the Evolut leaflets to:

- **Maximise the effective orifice area** – the new frame sits *on* the previous leaflet surface rather than encasing it like a redundant shell, reducing the risk of “self‑cannulation.”  
- **Maintain coronary clearance** – a high‑deployment of the Acurate Neo2 (taller than the Evolut struts) keeps the neo‑skirt away from the coronaries.  
- **Easier future access** – aligning the frames preserves a potential straight path for a future coronary catheter if the patient ever needs an urgent PCI.

The deployment depth of the third valve was intentionally set a few milimeters above the leaflets of the Evolut, a maneuver that hinged on knowing exactly where the Evolut leaflets shut down on our CT slices. This high finish was the key to sparing coronary flow—there was no need for a balloon or wire protection.

## Strategy 3: Balloon Play – Pre‑ and Post‑positions  
Ballooning still earned its place in the surgical drill:

- **Pre‑dilation:** A 22/40 mm balloon was used under rapid pacing (180 bpm) to gently open the valve‑annulus interface and ensure the new valve could sit without friction. Aortography confirmed no coronary compromise.  
- **Post‑dilation:** A 20/40 mm balloon post‑deployed under similar pacing fine‑tuned the valve expansion. The consequences? The post‑dilation presumably nudged the Evolut stent a bit forward (think of that as a “frame‑fracture” without the device) which helped the leaflets open better.

The immediate post‑procedure measurements were alarming—mean gradient 67 mm Hg, indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m², DVI 0.24—classic severe PPM. But the pattern over the next month was the textbook resolution story: by day 30 the gradient dropped to 27 mm Hg and AVA rose to 1.1 cm². It was a grassroots example of how a high‑deployed, well‑aligned, select‑balloon strategy can “wake up” a valve that initially looked doomed.

## The 1‑Year Sunset – What We Learned  
At one‑year the patient was walking comfortably, the Trans‑Echocardiogram reading: mean gradient 26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm², EF 65 %. No restenosis, no new infection, no perivalvular leak. The only “ludicrous” fact: the triple‑layered architecture remains intact and functioning.

If the valve were to fail prematurely, the door to surgical explantation would be a minefield of eight‑hour root replacement and grafts. The only single‑catheter alternative are emerging “cut‑and‑fracture” techniques (like BASILICA) that have not been validated in a three‑valve situation. All the more reason to preserve the future percutaneous options we did.

## Clinical Pearls  
- **CT is king**: In a triple‑valved root, the ability to map coronaries and frame interactions dictates the entire strategy.  
- **High implantation can spare coronaries** but be ready for initial PPM—balloon post‑dilation can rescue the gradient.  
- **Select a low‑profile, supra‑annular valve** when you’re layering on top of a stent‑covered valve to avoid deep “skirt occlusion.”  
- **Commissural alignment is vital**—you’re saving yourself and your future cardiology colleagues a future coronary wiring nightmare.  
- **Infected tricuspid scenarios tolerate TAVI** if infection is eradicated and imaging shows no vegetations—there is no “one‑size‑fits‑all” absolute.  

## Take‑home Messages  
- **Layering matters**: The distance between valve frames and coronary ostia is a non‑negotiable metric; map it, plan it, live by it.  
- **High‑deployment + adjustable balloon strategy** = high yield for preventing coronary obstruction while tackling the inevitable PPM that follows.  
- **Early imaging‑driven decision‑making** replaces ad‑hoc guesswork; in serial TAVI, you’re building a scaffold—do it right.  
- **TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV is not a last‑resort philosophical exercise** but a realistic, rescueable option in selected high‑risk patients.  

Bottom line: A careful, imaging‑guided, high‑deployment, balloon‑supported, commissurally‑aligned strategy can transform what looks like a valve‑cosmos nightmare into a dance floor where a patient plays a symphony of recovered flow for at least a year—if not many more.
