Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the recommended imaging modalities and key multislice CT parameters for assessing feasibility of a third TAVI in a patient with prior TAV-in-TAV and a resolved episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis?

**Imaging roadmap for a third TAVI after TAV‑in‑TAV and treated endocarditis**

| Modality | Why it matters in this setting | Key parameters & thresholds |
|----------|-------------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE)** | Baseline valve performance (gradient, regurgitation, LV function) and titration of heart‑failure therapy before, during and after the re‑intervention. | • Mean transvalvular gradient; <30 mm Hg post‑procedure is target. <br>• Effective orifice area (AVA) >1.0 cm² (or indexed AVA > 0.8 cm²/m²) to avoid patient‑prosthesis mismatch. |
| **Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE)** | Confirmation of infection resolution (no vegetations, paravalvular leaks) and precise assessment of leaflet motion in a multi‑prosthesis context. | • Absence of vegetations or abscesses; <1 mm paravalvular leak. |
| **ECG‑gated multislice computed tomography (MSCT)** (gold standard for procedural planning) | 3‑D reconstruction of the aortic root, existing valve frames and coronary anatomy; prediction of coronary obstruction and effective landing zone. | • **Annulus**: perimeter, area, eccentricity, maximum/minimum diameters.  <br>• **Coronary ostia height**: ≥10 mm above the annulus (the higher the better, especially with multiple frames).  <br>• **Sinus of Valsalva diameter**: ≥28 mm to accommodate a third valve.  <br>• **Valve‑to‑coronary distance** at the level of predicted neo‑skirt: ≥4 mm (lower risk of obstruction).  <br>• **Minimum lumen diameter within the prior Evolut frame**: ≥13 mm (as in this case) to ensure adequate flow.  <br>• **Landing zone length** (distance from annulus to aortic wall) ≥12 mm to allow crimping of a third‑generation self‑expanding valve.  <br>• **Neo‑skirt height**: predicted from 3‑D model; keep it <2 mm above coronary ostia when possible.  <br>• **Root curvature & ascending aorta dimensions**: to anticipate delivery system tortuosity.  <br>• **Commissural position** (relative to coronary ostia) for future coronary access; target ~45–50° per commissure. |
| **Optional valve‑fracture / intentional laceration planning** | Not required in this particular case, but 3‑D CT can be used to model potential valve‑fracture scenarios if valve‑to‑vessel wall apposition is suboptimal. | • Simulated frame expansion and post‑dilation balloon sizing. |

**Practical pearls from the case**

- The 2024 MSCT revealed a **13.7 mm minimum lumen diameter** of the Evolut frame, confirming adequate space for a third valve and guiding optimal device sizing (Acurate neo2 S).  
- Detailed modeling of the **coronary ostial height** and **valve‑to‑coronary distance** allowed the team to plan a **high implantation** that preserved coronary flow and preserved future coronary access—critical in a patient with multiple coronary stents.  
- The imaging protocol used ECG gating and a high‑pitch, 64‑slice scanner with contrast bolus timing tailored to the aortic root, ensuring sub‑millimeter resolution of the complex frame geometry.

**Bottom line**

For a third TAVI after TAV‑in‑TAV with a history of resolved endocarditis, the imaging ladder is:

1. TTE → baseline valvular physiology.  
2. TEE → infection clearance and leaflet morphology.  
3. ECG‑gated MSCT → 3‑D root anatomy, coronary clearance, landing zone and valve‑to‑coronary metrics.  

The CT parameters above—annular sizing, coronary heights, sinus diameters, valve‑to‑coronary distance, and minimal lumen diameter within prior frames—are the decision points that determine whether a third transcatheter valve can be safely implanted, how it should be positioned, and whether coronary protection or high‑implantation techniques will be needed.

### [Morning Report] In the case of a patient who underwent TAV-in-TAV for a degenerated Lotus valve and subsequently developed Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, what factors influenced the decision to treat with antibiotics alone versus surgical intervention?

**Clinical Pearls:** *Choosing Antibiotics Alone for Prosthetic‑Valve Endocarditis After TAV‑in‑TAV*

- **Infection profile** – Enterococcus faecalis vegetations were small, limited to the prosthetic leaflet surface, and did not produce an abscess or prosthetic‑valve perforation. Six weeks of ampicillin‑based therapy cleared the infection (negative blood cultures, no residual vegetation on TEE or MSCT).  
- **Hemodynamic impact** – The patient had only mild residual regurgitation and no heart‑failure decompensation. Post‑therapy echocardiography showed stable valve function and no prosthetic dysfunction that would have demanded urgent surgical replacement.  
- **Guideline framework** – ESC 2025 guidelines recommend surgery for prosthetic endocarditis only when there is uncontrolled infection, abscess, or severe valvular dysfunction. This patient met none of those criteria.  
- **Surgical risk and anatomy** – EuroSCORE II 27.4 % (very high operative mortality), COPD, diabetes, prior gastrointestinal bleeding, pacemaker, and two preceding TAVIs. Surgical removal would have required a root and ascending‑aorta replacement, a technically prohibitive operation in a heavily scarred, multi‑stented annulus.  
- **Multidisciplinary consensus** – The heart‑team weighed the minimally invasive nature of catheter‑based therapy against the prohibitive surgical morbidity. The decision to treat medically was reinforced by this consensus and the absence of any residual infection.

**Bottom line:** In this high‑risk TAV‑in‑TAV patient, the combination of a small, responsive Enterococcus infection, preserved valve function, and prohibitive surgical risk led the team to pursue targeted antibiotics alone, reserving a repeat transcatheter valve for the later, purely mechanical valve degeneration.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which anatomical measurements on multislice CT are most critical for predicting the risk of coronary obstruction during a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention, and how were these used in this patient?

**Key CT‑derived measurements for coronary‑obstruction risk**

1. **Coronary ostial height (COH)** – the vertical distance from the aortic annular plane to the coronary ostia.  
2. **Sinus of Valsalva diameter (SOV)** – the maximal diameter of the sinuses, which determines the “room” for a neo‑sinus after valve deployment.  
3. **Valve‑to‑coronary distance (VCD)** – the projected distance between the outer margin of the intended transcatheter valve frame (neo‑skirt) and the coronary ostia once the valve is expanded.  
4. **Predicted neo‑sinus area** – the area that will remain between the valve frame struts and the coronary ostia after deployment, which must be >10 mm² to ensure adequate coronary flow.

In a multi‑prosthetic scenario these measurements are evaluated after “virtual” placement of the new valve on 3‑D CT reconstructions to account for the overlapping stent frames of the prior TAVI devices.

---

**Application in the reported patient**

Pre‑procedural multislice CT revealed:

- **COH** > 11 mm for both left and right coronary arteries.  
- **SOV** diameter 28–30 mm, providing sufficient sinus volume.  
- **VCD** from the projected Acurate neo2 skirt to the coronaries measured > 5 mm after high‑implantation alignment with the existing Evolut leaflets.  
- The **predicted neo‑sinus area** exceeded 12 mm², well above the 10 mm² safety threshold.

These data indicated a low theoretical risk of coronary obstruction. Consequently, the heart‑team adopted a high‑implantation strategy for the Acurate neo2, purposely aligning its supra‑annular leaflets with the previous Evolut leaflets to maximize the neo‑sinus space, and elected **no routine coronary protection**. Intra‑procedural aortography confirmed unobstructed coronary flow, validating the CT‑based assessment.

### [Differential Diagnosis] For a patient presenting with a new marked increase in transvalvular gradient and a 0.5 cm² AVA after a second TAVI, list the differential diagnosis and describe how TEE versus imaging distinguishes each entity.

**Differential diagnosis for a sudden rise in transvalvular gradient (mean 49 mm Hg) and a 0.5 cm² aortic valve area (AVA) after a second TAVI**  

| Entity | Key imaging features | How TEE distinguishes it | What CT adds |
|--------|---------------------|---------------------------|--------------|
| **Structural valve deterioration (leaflet calcification, tear or fusion)** | Restricted leaflet excursion, thickened or fused leaflets. | TEE shows loss of leaflet opening, preserved‑to‑focal‑motion; color Doppler confirms elevated peak velocity. | CT demonstrates cusp thickening, calcium burden, and any intracavitary stenosis (e.g., Mahle‑type leaflet tear). It also accurately measures the minimal intra‑prosthetic lumen (as in this patient—min lumen diameter 13.7 mm). |
| **Prosthetic valve thrombosis** | Hypo‑attenuating filling defect, often mid‑cycle; may coexist with thrombus on radiopaque stent posts. | TEE can identify an echodense mass on the cusp or stent strut and delayed leaflet opening. | CT, especially with late‑phase contrast, shows a low‑density filling defect that does not calcify. |
| **Valve malexpansion or malposition** | Under‑expanded stent posts; high‑deployment angle; incomplete apposition to annulus. | TEE displays uniformly high gradients but provides no precise geometry; it may note an “abnormal” valve orientation. | CT is definitive: 3‑D reconstruction shows stent diameter at each level, depth relative to annulus, and any unfolding of a prior frame (as in this case, where the Evolut frame’s minimal lumen dictated the need for a third valve). |
| **Valve–patient mismatch (PPM)** | Small annulus relative to valve size, leading to a low effective orifice area despite proper expansion. | TTE/TEE calculates AVA via continuity equation; if AVA <1.0 cm² and gradient high, PPM is suspected. | CT measures annular diameter and area, permitting prediction of indexed AVA for the chosen valve size. |
| **LV outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction** | Papillary muscle or hypertrophic septum encroaching on the LVOT, causing a gradient that can mimic valve dysfunction. | TEE visualizes LVOT flow patterns; color Doppler shows high velocity jets just below the annulus. | CT demonstrates anatomic LVOT diameter, septal thickness, and any subvalvular obstruction. |
| **Coronary obstruction or ostial compromise** | Decreased coronary perfusion, absent flow beyond an occlusion. | TEE cannot reliably assess coronary flow; only indirect signs (e.g., unclear flow in the aortic root) may be noted. | CT precisely measures coronary ostial height and distance to prosthesis, allowing assessment of potential obstruction. |
| **Residual or new paravalvular leak (PVL)** | Regurgitant jet around the prosthesis; can coexist with high gradient if coupled with leaflet restriction. | TEE is the gold‑standard for detecting and grading PVL via color Doppler. | CT localizes the PVL aperture and quantifies its size relative to prosthesis geometry. |

**Integration in practice**

In this patient, routine TTE initially revealed the abrupt rise in gradient and AVA. Because TEE’s acoustic shadowing limits precise assessment of the complex three‑layered prosthetic architecture, we proceeded with multidetector‑CT imaging. The CT quantified the Evolut prosthesis lumen as only 13.7 mm, confirming severe structural limitation and guiding the decision to implant a self‑expanding Acurate neo2 at a high position to preserve central flow and coronary clearance. Post‑procedural TEE confirmed normal leaflet motion and a transient high gradient that resolved with post‑dilation—consistent with under‑expansion rather than new thrombosis or infection. Subsequent follow‑up TTE demonstrated a stable mean gradient of 27 mm Hg and AVA of 1.1 cm².

**Teaching point**  
For any patient with new severe prosthetic aortic stenosis after TAV‑in‑TAV, a dual‑modality approach is essential: TEE for dynamic valve function and identification of thrombus or valve‑related defects; CT for detailed anatomic assessment of valve expansion, frame interaction, LVOT size, and coronary artery safety. This strategy enables precise etiologic diagnosis and tailored re‑intervention, as illustrated by the successful TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV outcome in this case.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the pathophysiological mechanisms that can lead to structural valve deterioration in transcatheter bioprostheses, and explain how a prior episode of infective endocarditis may modify these mechanisms.

**Pathophysiology of Structural Valve Deterioration (SVD) in Transcatheter Bioprostheses – With a Note on Prior Infective Endocarditis**

1. **Mechanisms of SVD in Transcatheter Valves**

| Mechanism | How It Occurs in a TAVI Device | Clinical Consequence |
|-----------|------------------------------|----------------------|
| **Leaflet calcification & fibrosis** | Bioprosthetic tissue, once exposed to the high‑velocity jet at the annulus, undergoes calcium deposition and stiffening. In the case, the *Lotus* valve (2014) progressed to a mean gradient of 83 mm Hg by 2021, a classic hallmark of calcific stenosis. | Progressive transvalvular gradient, ↓ leaflet mobility, ↓ effective orifice area (EOA). |
| **Mechanical fatigue & shear stress** | Repeated valve‑to‑valve flexion, especially when a new prosthesis is implanted inside a prior one, compresses the underlying leaflets and the stent frame itself. The TAV‑in‑TAV (2022) created a “double‑layer” frame, and the subsequent **TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV** further amplified this fatigue. | Rapid deterioration of leaflet function, early increase in gradients. |
| **Pannus, tissue ingrowth, and hypo‑plastic fibrous tissue** | Chronic low‑grade inflammation can lead to neointimal proliferation over the valve‐frame junction, narrowing the lumen (as noted with only 13.7 mm of residual lumen in the Evolut frame on MSCT). | Subclinical narrowing leading to restenosis or regurgitation. |
| **Inflammatory‑mediated degradation** | Pro‑inflammatory cytokines released by the host or due to chronic infection accelerate collagen breakdown and enzymatic remodeling of the leaflet matrix. | Accelerated SVD, often presenting abruptly. |

2. **How Prior Infective Endocarditis (PVE) Alters These Processes**

* The patient developed *Enterococcus faecalis* PVE three months after the Evolut implantation (Sept 2022). Blood cultures, TEE and CT confirmed acute infection and subsequently resolved after 6‑week antibiotics.
* **Direct leaflet injury:** Vegetations and associated bacteriologic toxins compromise the structural integrity of the leaflets, causing micro‑tears and focal necrosis. This can manifest clinically as the abrupt rise in mean gradient to 35 mm Hg and intraprosthetic regurgitation seen after the PVE episode, despite a previously successful \(E\)= 42 mm Hg gradient after the Evolut implantation.
* **Inflammation‑induced calcification:** The inflammatory milieu initiates osteogenic differentiation of valvular interstitial cells, hastening calcium deposition in minutes-to-months rather than decades. Consequently, the *Evolut* valve exhibited severe restenosis (mean gradient 49 mm Hg, AVA 0.5 cm²) by Jan 2024—earlier than expected for a bioprosthetic valve of its age alone.
* **Adjunct mechanical burden:** Friction between the *Evolut* frame and a now‑infected leaflets can cause additional shear damage. When a *third* valve (Acurate neo2) was implanted, the high implantation strategy partially “un‑folded” the *Evolut* leaflets against the outer frame, improving central flow but also exposing more of the damaged leaflets to external force, further accelerating SVD if left untreated.

3. **Implications for Management**

* After complete antibiotic therapy, the patient’s infection risk had subsided; no long‑term suppressive therapy was required. Hence the surgical decision pivoted on **anatomical feasibility and cumulative mechanical burden** rather than persistent infection.
* CT‑guided planning verified adequate coronary clearance and predicted a residual lumen of ≈13 mm, informing the choice of a supra‑annular, low‑profile valve (Acurate neo2) and a high‑implantation strategy. The goal was to:
  - **Minimize added mechanical stress** on the already damaged leaflets;
  - **Preserve coronary access** (critical in a patient with triple stent frames);
  - **Enhance effective orifice area** post‑re‑deployment.

In the presented case, these strategies culminated in a sustained functional benefit at 1 year, with a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg and an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm² (moderately stenotic but clinically acceptable).

**Bottom line:** Structural valve deterioration in transcatheter bioprostheses is driven by calcific stiffening, mechanical fatigue, and chronic inflammation. Prior infective endocarditis superimposes acute leaflet injury and an inflammatory cascade that can precipitate premature and more aggressive SVD. Comprehensive imaging and a strategy that mitigates added mechanical load are essential when planning repeat TAVI in this setting.

### [Management] In planning the third TAVI with an Acurate neo2 valve into a prior Evolut frame, how did the team use CT‑derived measurements to decide the valve size, implantation depth, and post‑dilation strategy?

**Clinical Teaching Point – TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV Planning**

In this patient, the pre‑procedural multislice computed tomography (MSCT) provided three critical pieces of information that shaped every step of the third‑valve deployment:

| Clinical Decision | What the CT Showed | How it Guided the Field |
|-------------------|--------------------|------------------------|
| **Valve size** | Minimum lumen of the existing 23 mm Evolut cage measured 13.7 mm. The internal diameter of the Acurate neo2 S (nominal 22 mm) is 20 – 21 mm; a 20 mm frame can be accommodated when the Evolut’s lower stent is partially expanded by the new valve. | Because the Evolut frame already occupied a significant portion of the aortic root, the team chose the smallest Acurate neo2 (S) to avoid oversizing that would risk annular rupture or coronary obstruction. CT‑derived annulus dimensions confirmed that the new valve would fit snugly without excessive oversizing, a key ‘no‑risk‑zone’ for a serial TAV scenario. |
| **Implantation depth** | CT‑based 3‑D models showed the Evolut stent struts and leaflet commissures at a height that would allow the new valve to sit just above the Evolut frames. Coronary ostial height and sinus of Valsalva were measured, revealing a > 15 mm clearance from the neo‑skirt to the coronary oris when the neo‑valve was placed high. | The team elected a *high* implantation—aligning the Acurate neo2 commissures with the Evolut leaflets—to (1) use the Evolut frame as a scaffold for leaflets, (2) preserve the residual orifice area by preventing an additional “neo‑skirt” in the sinuses, and (3) maintain coronary clearance. The CT‐derived touchdown level was translated into fluoroscopic markers (co‑aligned waist, B‑plane) to replicate the 3‑D plan in‑the‑hybrid suite. |
| **Post‑dilation strategy** | CT simulation of valve expansion demonstrated that a fully expanded Acurate neo2 would nevertheless be partially compressed beneath the stiff Evolut stent, especially at the level of the remaining stent frames. | To achieve optimal radial force, a 20 / 40 mm high‑pressure balloon was selected for post‑dilation. The balloon size corresponds to the target diameter of the neo‑valve + an allowance for the Evolut’s radial stiffness, and rapid pacing underscored the need for a brief, controlled inflation. CT had shown that post‑dilation would not jeopardize coronary flow (no predicted encroachment), so protection was deemed unnecessary. |

**Bottom Line for Residents**

1. **Measure the “real” landing zone** (minimum lumen, stent strut height, coronary clearance) rather than relying on nominal valve sizes.
2. **Choose the smallest valve that will clear the residual root anatomy** and minimize oversizing—especially in a multi‑stent setting.
3. **Plan a high, commissure‑aligned deployment** to use the existing stent as a scaffold, preserve the effective orifice, and protect the coronaries.
4. **Use CT‑simulated expansion to size the post‑dilation balloon** (often slightly larger than the valve’s nominal diameter) and confirm that no new obstruction will be created.

The team’s CT‑derived roadmap enabled a safe, effective, and hemodynamically favorable third‑valve implantation—demonstrating how detailed imaging can bias every procedural choice in complex repeat TAVI.

**Acurate neo2 – Why it is a good partner for a valve‑in‑valve‑in‑valve**

### [Morning Report] During the third TAVI procedure, why is commissural alignment emphasized, and what fluoroscopic landmarks or CT models guide this alignment?

**Why commissural alignment matters in the third‐generation TAVI**

In a serial TAVI where a third valve is implanted within two previous transcatheter frames (Lotus → Evolut → Acurate Neo2), the overlapping stent lattices create a *triple‑stented root*.   
1. **Coronary perfusion and future access** – If the new valve’s commissures are not positioned over the “open spaces” of the prior leaflets, the neo‑skirt can sit higher than the coronary ostia or, worse, can bridge an existing stent post and block a coronary ostium.  
2. **Effective orifice area** – Misalignment can cause leaflet folding against the inner stent frame, increasing residual gradients and precipitating patient‑prosthesis mismatch.  
3. **Procedural simplicity** – Aligning the new valve’s commissures with those of the previous Evolut leaflets permits a predictable, high‑implantation trajectory, avoiding the need for aggressive post‑dilation or the risk of iatrogenic obstruction.

**How we guided alignment in this case**

| Step | Tool | What we measured / visualized |
|------|------|--------------------------------|
| **Pre‑procedural imaging** | Multislice CT (3‑D reconstruction) | • Orientation of the Evolut commissures relative to the annulus and coronary ostia.<br>• Spatial relationship of the Lotus and Evolut stent frames.<br>• Predicted “landing zone” for the Acurate Neo2 and its commissure alignment with the Evolut leaflets. |
| **Fluoroscopy (in‑procedure)** | Dual‑view orthogonal projections (LAO 30° / RAO 30°) | • The radial posts of the prior Evolut frame appear as “kissing” hooks on the fluoroscopic image.<br>• The new Acurate’s three commissural markers (on the outer aortic frame) are rotated until they overlay the Evolut posts identified on CT.<br>• Confirmation that the opening trajectory aligns with the sinus of Valsalva and that the neo‑skirt remains above the proximal coronary projected lines. |
| **Execution** | Coaxial deployment with a Safari guidewire, rapid pacing, and post‑dilation | • The valve was positioned so that its commissures were *1–2 mm* above the Evolut leaflet hinges (as predicted by the CT model).<br>• Post‑dilation further spaced the Evolut frame, improving central flow. |

**Clinical takeaway**

In a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV setting, commissural alignment is not an aesthetic preference—it is a safety and efficacy imperative. By anchoring the new valve’s commissures to the known orientation of the prior leaflets, we preserve coronary flow, maintain a larger effective orifice area, and set the stage for any future coronary interventions. Detailed CT modeling and real‑time fluoroscopic landmarks provide the roadmap for achieving this alignment in the third generation of transcatheter aortic valve replacement.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What are the distinguishing echocardiographic and clinical features between prosthetic valve endocarditis and early structural dysfunction in a patient who has had multiple TAVI procedures?

**Distinguishing prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE) from early structural dysfunction after repeat TAVI**

| Feature | Prosthetic‑Valve Endocarditis | Early Structural Dysfunction (degeneration or mechanical failure) |
|---------|------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Clinical presentation** | • Fever, chills, malaise, weight loss, or new “severe” systemic symptoms.<br>• Positive blood cultures (>50 % of cases).<br>• New or worsening heart‑failure symptoms due to valvular failure.<br>• Immune‑complex phenomena (e.g., Roth spots, Osler nodes) may be present. | • Insidious or sudden exertional dyspnea, chest discomfort, or dizziness.<br>• Often afebrile and without systemic infection markers.<br>• Symptoms mirror those of the underlying valvular pathology (stenosis or regurgitation). |
| **Echocardiographic findings (TTE/TEE)** | • Mobile or oscillating echogenic mass attached to the prosthetic valve or sewing ring (vegetations).<br>• Paravalvular leak or perivalvular abscess.<br>• Flail or perforated leaflet, new regurgitation irrespective of gradient.<br>• Severe reduction in leaflet mobility with thickening or fragmentation. | • Gradual or abrupt increase in transvalvular mean gradient and drop in EOA/AVA.<br>• Leaflet thickening or calcification, pannus formation, or stiffening without discrete vegetations.<br>• Progressive paraprosthetic regurgitation due to calcific pannus rather than abscess.<br>• No mobile echo‑dense masses. |
| **Laboratory data** | • Positive blood cultures (common pathogens: Staphylococcus aureus, coagulase‑negative staph, enterococci).<br>• Elevated inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) – often >‑two‑fold. | • Blood cultures negative or transient positive (usually sterile).<br>• Inflammatory markers normal or mildly elevated. |
| **Imaging adjuncts** | • Multislice CT (MSCT) can reveal perivalvular contrast staining (abscess), leaflet thickening, or debris.<br>• PET‑CT may show increased uptake around the valve. | • MSCT shows progressive degeneration: leaflet calcification, stent migration, or stent fracture; no contrast extravasation.<br>• Evaluate valve‑to‑coronary distances, neo‑skirt formation; no “hot spots.” |
| **Temporal course** | • Symptoms may appear weeks to months after implantation, often with recent exposure to bacteremia; an acute, fulminant presentation is possible. | • Typically a gradual decline, though an abrupt sudden failure can occur (e.g., leaflet tear or structural fracture). |
| **Management implications** | • Requires definitive antimicrobial therapy (≥6 weeks); surgery recommended if heart‑failure, uncontrolled infection, or lack of response. | • Addresses hemodynamic obstruction: valve‑re‑deployment (TAV‑in‑TAV, TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV), balloon‑valve fracture, or surgical replacement if required. |

### How the case exemplifies these distinctions

1. **Clinical picture**: In September 2022 the patient had fever, positive Enterococcus faecalis cultures, and new severe restenosis → classic PVE. By June 2024, fever and bacteremia had resolved; the patient remained afebrile with normal inflammatory markers despite worsening gradients.

2. **Echocardiographic evolution**: TTE in 2022 showed severe restenosis (gradient 35 mm Hg) without new regurgitation; by June 2024, TTE revealed a markedly reduced AVA (0.5 cm²) and substantial gradients (49 mm Hg), but no vegetations or paravalvular leaks. TEE confirmed absence of active infection or leaflet perforation.

3. **Imaging**: MSCT demonstrated a minimum lumen of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis—consistent with structural narrowing rather than abscess or perivalvular fluid collection.

4. **Microbiology**: All blood cultures taken after antibiotic completion (June 2024) were negative, corroborating eradication of infection.

Thus, **the key differentiators in this patient** are an afebrile, culture‑negative status combined with progressive gradient increase and leaflet calcification on imaging—hallmarks of early structural dysfunction—versus the infectious and inflammatory profile that initially characterized his PVE episode. Recognizing this distinction is essential because therapeutic strategies diverge dramatically: re‑intervention for structural failure versus prolonged antimicrobial therapy (and possibly surgery) for active endocarditis.

### [Management] How can patient–prosthesis mismatch be minimized when performing a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, and what procedural steps in this case contributed to resolving the initial high gradients?

**Patient–prosthesis mismatch in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV can be minimized with a disciplined, imaging‑driven strategy that focuses on valve selection, deployment depth, and post‑deployment optimization.**  

1. **Valve sizing and design** –  
   • Use a supra‑annular, self‑expanding prosthesis (e.g., Acurate Neo 2) when the annulus is small or when multiple frames are already in place; supra‑annular leaflets provide a larger effective orifice area (EOA).  
   • Avoid the cascade of valve framing by selecting a device whose external diameter matches the residual lumen; in this case, a 31‑mm Acurate Neo 2 (size S) was chosen because CT measured a 13.7‑mm minimum lumen within the Evolut frame, leaving adequate space for the Neo 2 without over‑crowding the annulus.

2. **High‑level implantation** –  
   • Deploy the third valve at the plane of the pre‑existing Evolut leaflets rather than at the native annulus. This “high‑implant” strategy anchors the new valve above the old stent, minimizing overlap of stent frames and preserving a more central flow channel.  
   • In the case, the team aligned the Acurate Neo 2 commissures with the Evolut leaflets based on CT‑derived 3‑D models, ensuring that the Neo 2 skirt did not encroach on the coronary ostia while also avoiding the “double‑layered” frame that would reduce EOA.

3. **Optimized pre‑ and post‑dilation** –  
   • Mechanical expansion of the underlying Evolut stent can be achieved by high‑pressure balloon dilation after valve deployment. The 20/40‑mm balloon used for post‑dilation in this patient likely coaxed the Evolut frame into a more open geometry, improving leaflet motion and reducing residual velocity.  
   • Balloon pre‑dilation (22/40‑mm) also facilitates deeper and more stable positioning of the Neo 2, which reduces paravalvular leak and limits over‑compression of the new leaflets.

4. **Commissural alignment and coronary protection** –  
   • Aligning commissures to preserve future coronary access and to avoid a “neo‑skirt” between the basal part of the valve and the coronary ostia is essential when multiple devices occupy the root. In this patient, care was taken not to pack the neo‑skirt below the coronary arteries, which helped maintain adequate coronary flow and eased the post‑deployment interrogation of gradients.

5. **Continuous haemodynamic reassessment** –  
   • Immediate post‑procedural transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and aortography flagged a high mean gradient (67 mm Hg) and a severely narrowed EOA (0.9 cm²). Early recognition allowed the operator to perform targeted post‑dilation, leading to a rapid clinical and haemodynamic improvement (gradient 27 mm Hg at 1 month).  
   • Re‑evaluation at 1 year confirmed sustained resolution of the initial mismatch (gradient 26 mm Hg, index AVA 0.4 cm²/m²).

**Key steps in this case that resolved the initially high gradients were therefore**  
- **Pre‑procedural CT modelling** that facilitated valve sizing and the choice of a supra‑annular self‑expanding prosthesis.  
- **High‑implant deployment** at the Evolut leaflet level, ensuring the new valve did not add a second, low‑rise frame at the annulus.  
- **Meticulous commissural alignment** to maximize clear central flow and preserve coronary distance.  
- **Aggressive, staged balloon dilation** (pre‑ and post‑deployment) that expanded both the Neo 2 frame and the underlying Evolut struts, thereby enlarging the central orifice.  
- **Immediate post‑procedure surveillance** with TEE and aortography, enabling rapid corrective measures that translated into a marked reduction in transvalvular gradients over the first months.

By adhering to these principles, clinicians can attenuate PPM even in the most complex triple‑prosthesis scenarios, achieving durable haemodynamic outcomes as demonstrated by the 1‑year stability seen in this patient.
