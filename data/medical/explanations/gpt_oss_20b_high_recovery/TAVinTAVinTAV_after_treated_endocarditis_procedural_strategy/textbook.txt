Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Clinical Presentation, Initial TAVI Implantation and Early Complications

The patient is a man in his late 70 s who had a history of multi‑vessel coronary artery disease treated with several percutaneous coronary interventions between 2009 and 2014. He also had a high‑risk comorbidity profile that included arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, and a permanent pacemaker implanted in 2014 for third‑degree atrioventricular block. This constellation of conditions placed him at prohibitive operative risk, a key factor that guided the decision to pursue a transcatheter rather than surgical approach for severe aortic stenosis.

Clinically, the patient reported progressive exertional dyspnoea, a spastic left‑sided chest sensation on walking, dizziness and fatigue, with a functional limitation of 200–300 m. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed severe aortic valve stenosis with a mean transvalvular gradient approaching the upper limits of severe stenosis and an ejection fraction (EF) of approximately 54 %. The descending gradient was attributable to an increase in afterload on a chronically hypertrophied left ventricle, a hallmark of the haemodynamic sequelae of fixed valve obstruction. The patient’s symptoms and imaging findings fulfilled the criteria for intervention documented in contemporary aortic stenosis guidelines and were consistent with a high surgical risk calculated on the EuroSCORE II, which in this case approached 27 %.

Given these factors, the first transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) was performed in 2014 using a 27‑mm Lotus valve. The Lotus platform is a self‑expanding supra‑annular valve that offers a low‑profile delivery system and a flexible nitinol frame, which can accommodate annular undersizing and reduce torque on the annulus—attributes preferred in patients with small annuli or complex annular anatomy. The procedure was successful, with an immediate reduction in transvalvular gradient and a modest improvement in LV systolic function.

Despite this initial success, the patient’s early post‑implant course was marked by progressive symptoms and worsening imaging over the next few years. By late 2021, diameter‑echo and Doppler studies documented a mean gradient of 83 mm Hg and a decline in EF from the initial 54 % to 42 %, indicating significant structural valve deterioration of the Lotus prosthesis. Clinically, the patient reported return of dyspnoea and fatigue, corresponding to a New York Heart Association class III–IV functional status.

A second, valve‑in‑valve intervention was undertaken in May 2022, in which a 23‑mm Evolut (Medtronic) self‑expanding valve was implanted within the failing Lotus device. This procedure restored forward flow and relieved the positional obstruction, leading to an improvement in EF back to 54 %. However, a femoral access‑site complication ensued: a pulsatile haematoma and a pseudoaneurysm at the puncture site necessitated emergent open vascular surgery for repair. Femoral complications are a recognized risk of transfemoral TAVI, especially in elderly patients with arterial comorbidities and a history of anticoagulation or antiplatelet therapy. The morbidity associated with this complication delayed further definitive management and underscored the delicate balance between procedural benefits and vascular risks in high‑risk patients.

The early trajectory of this case illustrates the typical pattern of initial symptom relief following TAVI, followed by gradual structural degeneration, and the importance of vigilant post‑procedural surveillance. The early haemodynamic recovery achieved after the Evolut implantation demonstrates the reversible component of LV dysfunction when the outflow obstruction is relieved, while the late‑appearance of structural failure highlights the finite durability of bioprosthetic transcatheter valves in the setting of long life expectancy. The femoral pseudoaneurysm complication emphasizes the necessity of meticulous access-site management and the readiness to manage vascular complications in this population.

## Diagnostic Workup and Imaging Strategy for Repeat Intervention

The patient’s presentation of progressively worsening exertional dyspnoea and fatigue, coupled with a marked rise in valve gradient (to 49 mm Hg) and moderate-to‑severe intraprosthetic regurgitation, triggered the workup for repeat valve intervention. The clinical picture was consistent with severe prosthetic valve stenosis (gradient > 35 mm Hg), significant regurgitation, symptomatic status and an improving left‑ventricular ejection fraction (from a nadir of 37 % after the second TAVI to 66 % at the time of re‑intervention), all of which qualify the patient for re‑intervention per current structural heart guidelines.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) provided the initial haemodynamic assessment. Using the continuity equation, the mean gradient of 49 mm Hg and an aortic valve area of 0.5 cm² (indexed 0.17 cm²/m²) were calculated, indicating severe stenosis. Doppler velocity integration and pulse‑wave Doppler of the left ventricular outflow tract determined the stroke volume and indexed effective orifice area (EOA). Two‑dimensional imaging also revealed grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, while three‑dimensional transoesophageal echocardiography (3‑D TEE) refined the valve geometry, excluded paravalvular leak, and confirmed the absence of residual vegetations or abscess formation. These data confirmed structural deterioration of the second transcatheter valve and ruled out ongoing infection.

Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) was paramount for procedural planning. The CT scan measured a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the previously implanted Evolut prosthesis, establishing that the subsequent valve could be accommodated at an appropriate depth. Sinus of Valsalva dimensions, coronary ostial height, and valve‑to‑coronary distances were all quantified, allowing assessment of coronary clearance and the risk of obstruction. Importantly, the CT images delineated the interface of the overlapping stent frames and facilitated a neo‑skirt prediction, ensuring that the third valve would not impinge upon coronary flow or compromise future coronary access. This comprehensive imaging strategy therefore allowed for precise valve sizing, optimal implantation depth, and avoidance of coronary obstruction without the need for coronary protection hardware.

Resolution of infective endocarditis was critical prior to the third intervention. Six weeks of intravenous ampicillin therapy (supplemented with bicarbonate‑based modulation) resulted in negative serial blood cultures. Repeat 3‑D TEE demonstrated no vegetations, abscesses, or paravalvular leaks, while a follow‑up CT scan revealed no residual paravalvular collections. These findings fulfilled the infection‑resolution criteria and justified proceeding with a transcatheter approach.

Surgical risk assessment further guided the decision. The EuroSCORE II of 27.36 % and a high Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) risk score reflected both the patient’s comorbid burden (COPD, diabetes, prior gastrointestinal bleeding) and the technical challenges of open surgery—namely, the need for extensive aortic root replacement due to multi‑layered stent fixation and proximity of prior prosthetic frames to the coronary ostia. Consequently, the multidisciplinary team deemed a third TAVI as the safest viable option, aligning with current ESC guidelines that recommend surgical valve replacement in PVE only when surgery is probable to be safe and uncomplicated.

In sum, the diagnostic workup integrated multi‑modality imaging and stringent infection‑resolution criteria to confirm severe prosthetic valve dysfunction, exclude active endocarditis, and verify procedural feasibility, thereby enabling a structured, patient‑centric approach to a high‑complexity repeat transcatheter aortic valve implantation.

## Procedural Planning and Technological Considerations for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV

Performing a third transcatheter valve intervention within an already expanded bioprosthetic frame poses a series of interrelated technical challenges. Chief among these are the risk of coronary artery obstruction, the incremental loss of effective orifice area (EOA) with each new valve layer, and the potential for frame–frame interaction that can lead to under‑expansion or patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM). The case report demonstrates how meticulous imaging‑guided planning and a step‑wise procedural strategy can overcome these obstacles and yield durable hemodynamics.

### Valve choice
The chosen third prosthesis was an Acurate Neo2 self‑expanding valve (S‑size). Its design features—supra‑annular leaflet orientation, a short, low‑profile frame, and radiopaque commissural markers—directly address the critical concerns of a triple‑stented anatomy. Supra‑annular leaflets provide a larger effective orifice, reducing the likelihood of PPM in a constrained neo‑annulus. The low frame height limits the risk of coronary flow compromise, a vital consideration when the coronary ostia are already in close proximity to the existing Evolut struts.

### Sizing methodology
Pre‑procedural multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) routinely supplies the annular dimensions necessary for valve sizing. In this case, annular diameter and area were measured using standard orthogonal planes, while the *predicted neo‑annulus*—the diameter that would be available after deploying the Acurate within the Evolut—was simulated in a 3‑D reconstruction. The S‑size was selected to achieve an oversizing ratio of approximately 8–10 % relative to the measured neo‑annulus, a range that empirically balances adequate radial force against the risk of perforation or induced PPM. The MDCT also quantified the coronary–prosthesis gap to inform coronary protection decisions.

### High‑implant strategy
A central element of the procedural plan was to deliver the new valve *above* the leaflets of the previously implanted Evolut valve. Landing the Acurate above these leaflets preserves the lower landing zone free from strut interference, thereby minimizing neo‑skirt formation beneath the coronary ostia and allowing more rapid closure of the supra‑annular space. The planned depth was guided by CT‑derived measurements of the Evolut leaflet height (≈ 8 mm from the annulus) so that the Acurate’s frame would sit within a zone of clear radial expansion while not impinging the coronary ostia.

### Commissural alignment
The Acurate Neo2 includes radiopaque commissural markers that can be matched to the pre‑existing commissures on CT. A 3‑D CT reconstructions identified the orientation of the Evolut commissures, and these were projected onto the fluoroscopic screen. By aligning the new valve’s markers with the target angles during deployment, the team achieved commissural congruence, which not only improves hemodynamic performance but also ensures future coronary access by reducing the likelihood of stent strut obstruction.

### Coronary protection assessment
MDCT analysis revealed a coronary‑to‑prosthesis gap of > 4 mm at the lowest strut of the Evolut frame. Given this clearance and the high‑implant position of the Acurate, the team deemed it safe to forgo routine coronary protection—such as pre–placed balloon or guidewire “chimney” techniques. The left radial access remained available to deliver a rapid coronary angiogram should an occlusion be detected intra‑procedurally.

### Pre‑ and post‑dilation protocol
A 22 × 40 mm balloon was first inflated across the Evolut valve under rapid ventricular pacing (180 bpm) to open the neo‑annulus and to decompress the existing struts. This step enables smooth passage of the Acurate delivery sheath and prepares the landing zone for full expansion. After valve deployment, a 20 × 40 mm balloon was used for post‑dilation, once again under rapid pacing, to optimize radial expansion and to coax the Evolut frame into a more favorable orientation. Importantly, this post‑dilation was performed with a high‑pressure, low‑profile balloon to limit radial stress on the existing stents while maximizing the EOA of the new valve.

### Dual access and anesthesia
The procedure employed a dual‑access approach: a 7–9 Fr left femoral sheath for the Acurate delivery system and a 6 Fr left radial sheath for coronary angiography and rapid pacing support. Local anesthesia, rather than general sedation, minimized hemodynamic instability and accelerated recovery—an approach aligned with the patient’s high anesthetic risk profile. Rapid pacing was achieved via a femoral anesthetic pacing lead, allowing controlled LV unloading during valve deployment and dilation.

### Intra‑procedural imaging
During the operation, a combination of imaging modalities ensured real‑time confirmation of safety and success. Aortography provided a continuous visual of valve position, coronary flow, and transvalvular gradients. Fluoroscopy, calibrated with the CT‑derived commissural markers, guided precise deployment depth and vertical alignment. Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) offered high‑resolution assessment of leaflet motion, paravalvular sealing, and immediate detection of regurgitant jets. By cross‑referencing data from these modalities, the team confirmed correct valve seating and the absence of coronary obstruction before finalizing the intervention.

Through this integrated planning and execution framework—leveraging CT‑guided sizing and strategy, careful valve selection, a high‑implant technique, commissural alignment, judicious dilation, and robust intra‑procedural imaging—the third transcatheter valve was delivered safely without coronary compromise or significant PPM. The one‑year follow‑up data confirm sustained valve function and reinforce the viability of TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV as a feasible option for selected high‑risk patients with complex prosthetic valve failure.

## Outcomes, Follow‑up and Clinical Evaluation of the Intervention

### Immediate Procedural Success (VARC‑3 VWR‑3 Criteria)

According to the Valve Academic Research Consortium‑3 (VARC‑3) definitions, procedural success requires (i) no procedural‑related death, (ii) correct valve deployment with no residual aortic regurgitation, (iii) no significant paravalvular leak (≤ mild), (iv) preserved coronary flow, and (v) absence of perfusion‑related complications such as stroke or coronary obstruction. Immediate transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) on 7 June 2024 met each of these criteria: the third self‑expanding Acurate neo2 was seated in the appropriate position, the implanted leaflets were mobile and fully open, and Doppler assessment revealed no paravalvular or transvalvular regurgitation. Aortography confirmed unobstructed coronary ostia, and the patient had no procedural or periprocedural events. Thus, the intervention achieved VARC‑3 grade III (VWR‑3) procedural success.

### Early Hemodynamic Response

Immediately after deployment, Doppler interrogation demonstrated a mean transvalvular gradient of 67 mm Hg, an effective orifice area (AVA) of 0.9 cm², and a Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24—values that fulfil the VARC‑3 haemodynamic success requirement of a mean gradient <20 mm Hg or a >50 % reduction from baseline. Although the gradient remained above normal, the post‑dilation strategy and high‑implantation technique were designed to mitigate prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM). One month later, repeat TTE showed a dramatic gradient reduction to 27 mm Hg and an AVA increase to 1.1 cm², satisfying the VARC‑3 haemodynamic success criterion (mean gradient <20 mm Hg or ≤30 mm Hg with pre‑procedure gradient >30 mm Hg). The DVI improved to 0.29, reflecting better valve‑to‑annulus match. At the one‑year follow‑up, the mean gradient remained stable at 26 mm Hg and the AVA at 1.1 cm², confirming durable haemodynamic performance.

### Left Ventricular Function Trajectory

Left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) paralleled the patient’s haemodynamic evolution. Prior to the first TAV‑in‑TAV (May 2022), EF was 42 %. After the initial redo, EF rose to 54 %, demonstrating reverse ventricular remodeling driven by relief of stenosis. Following the third intervention, EF climbed to 66 % at one year, reflecting further reverse remodeling during the sub‑acute recovery phase. These functional shifts underscore the importance of early and sustained improvement in valve gradients for ventricular recovery.

### Functional Status and Rehospitalization

Clinically, the patient’s New York Heart Association (NYHA) status improved from class III/IV at baseline to class I/II by 3.5 months post‑procedure, as evidenced by the patient’s ability to walk 200–300 m without dyspnea or fatigue. No rehospitalizations for heart failure, arrhythmia, or other cardiac complications occurred during the 12‑month observation period. The absence of systemic symptoms and stable six‑minute walk test results further support the functional benefit of the triple TAVI approach.

### Complication Profile

Throughout the peri‑procedural and one‑year postoperative course, no major adverse events were recorded. There was no evidence of stroke (confirmed by neurologic examination and CT imaging), no paravalvular leak > mild, no coronary obstruction (verified by serial fluoroscopy and angiography), and no recurrence of endocarditis (adequate serial blood cultures, negative transesophageal echocardiography, and normal inflammatory markers). These findings align with the VARC‑3 safety endpoint of freedom from death, disabling stroke, non‑prosthesis‑related death, and major vascular complications.

### Long‑Term Surveillance Strategy

Given the patient’s complex triple‑stented anatomy and the novelty of TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, a structured follow‑up plan has been established:

| Time Point | Investigation | Rationale |
|------------|--------------|------------|
| 3 months | TTE | Early detection of residual gradient, regurgitation, or prosthetic dysfunction |
| 12 months | TTE | Confirmation of durable haemodynamics and LV remodeling |
| 24 months | Multislice CT | Evaluation of annular size, prosthesis fracture or migration, coronary access feasibility |
| 6‑month intervals | Clinic visit | Symptom review, NYHA assessment, laboratory monitoring for infection or device-related complications |

This schedule follows contemporary guidelines for transcatheter valve surveillance and allows prompt identification of late complications such as structural deterioration, paravalvular leak, or coronary access restriction.

### Summary

The third transcatheter aortic valve implantation in a patient with prior TAV-in-TAV and treated prosthetic valve endocarditis achieved immediate procedural success per VARC‑3 criteria, with favorable early and sustained haemodynamic improvement and robust recovery of left ventricular function. The patient’s functional status improved markedly, with no major complications or rehospitalizations over one year. Structured long‑term surveillance will monitor for late valve deterioration and preserve coronary accessibility, thereby supporting the durability of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV strategy in this high‑risk cohort.

## Clinical Implications, Guideline Context and Future Directions

The presented case illustrates the growing reality of multi‑prosthetic transcatheter aortic valve therapy. While current evidence for single TAV‑in‑TAV procedures is robust, the feasibility of a third valve after endocarditis remains proof‑of‑concept. Clinical decision‑making therefore hinges on blending guideline recommendations with meticulous, patient‑specific planning and innovative procedural strategies.

### Guideline‑based management of prosthetic valve endocarditis
The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines recommend surgical replacement as the preferred treatment for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) when the infection is uncontrolled, when there are complications such as heart failure or refractory vegetations, or when prosthetic dysfunction is significant. Nonetheless, the guidelines acknowledge that in patients with prohibitive surgical risk and a favorable microbiological response, a prolonged medical regimen can be considered, particularly when the prosthesis is viable and the infection has been definitively eradicated.  In the patient described here, a six‑week course of ampicillin‑based therapy achieved sterile blood cultures and transesophageal echocardiographic remission.  His EuroSCORE II of 27.4 % and significant comorbidities (COPD, diabetes, gastrointestinal bleeding) shifted the risk–benefit calculus toward a transcatheter solution, aligning with the ESC’s emphasis on individualized care.

### Early multidisciplinary planning and coronary‑access preservation
The complexity of serial TAV‑in‑TAV procedures necessitates a proactive, heart‑team–led approach.  In this case, cardiac surgeons, interventionalists, imaging scientists and anesthesiologists convened before the first intervention to evaluate the patient’s anatomical constraints.  Three‑dimensional multislice computed tomography (MSCT) permitted precise measurement of the neo‑annulus, coronary ostial height, and stent‑frame geometry, enabling the implantation team to plan a high, supra‑annular third valve that would align with the Evolut frame and preserve coronary flow.  This pre‑procedural strategy is now a cornerstone of contemporary practice, particularly as the cohort of patients requiring repeat TAVI expands.

### Risk of patient‑prosthesis mismatch and mitigation by high implantation
Recurrent transcatheter interventions inherently compound the risk of patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM) because each new valve intrudes upon the existing lumen.  The case demonstrates that a targeted high‑implantation approach can ameliorate PPM: by positioning the third valve at the level of the Evolut leaflet commissures, the distal frames were effectively “trapped” together, preventing further encroachment on the effective orifice.  Hemodynamically, this strategy yielded an initial mean gradient of 67 mm Hg and an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² immediately post‑procedure, which improved dramatically to 27 mm Hg and 1.1 cm² at one year—well below the threshold for severe PPM.

### Emerging techniques: valve fracture, BASILICA, and leaflet laceration
New procedural adjuncts—bioprosthetic valve fracture, BASILICA (Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to Prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery Obstruction), and intentional leaflet laceration—have been employed mainly in the setting of high‑risk coronary obstruction.  Their technical applicability in triple‑stented anatomies is still under investigation.  While a bioprosthetic coronary fracture may theoretically expand a stent‑loaded valve, its safety with trespassing stent frames and scaffold cannot yet be assured.  Similarly, BASILICA has not been reported in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV setting; the presence of multiple rigid frames may constrain the ability to safely lacerate root tissue.  Consequently, these techniques remain investigational and should be reserved for select scenarios where postoperative coronary obstruction is imminent and medical or transcatheter options are exhausted.

### Limitations of surgical re‑explantation in heavily stented patients
Open surgical explantation of a third transcatheter valve entails a highly invasive procedure with considerable morbidity.  The patient’s deep root fixation, extensive stent integration, and high cardiovascular risk during cardiopulmonary bypass made the surgical risk prohibitive.  Moreover, re‑construction of the aortic root after two prior TAVI procedures would most likely require valve‑root replacement and ascending aorta patching, further amplifying operative complexity and recovery time.  Thus, non‑surgical alternatives, despite their own technical challenges, represent a more pragmatic approach in such patients.

### Research gaps and the need for dedicated imaging and registry data
The paucity of systematic data on TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV outcomes limits evidence‑based guidance.  Dedicated imaging algorithms that incorporate stent‑frame deformation, neo‑annulus expansion, and hemodynamic simulation are required to predict procedural feasibility and long‑term durability.  Emerging computational fluid dynamics (CFD) models can estimate transvalvular flow patterns and identify regions of high shear that predispose to accelerated tissue degeneration.  Prospective registries that capture pre‑procedural imaging, procedural details, and serial imaging outcomes would accelerate learning and standardisation of best practices.

### Future surveillance strategies: routine MSCT and virtual‑flow simulation
Routine MSCT at 6‑ to 12‑month intervals after each TAVI implantation can objectively track neo‑annular area, stent expansion, and the presence of late leaflet thickening or pannus.  Coupling serial CTs with virtual‑flow simulation enables early detection of hemodynamic deterioration before clinical decompensation, allowing for proactive planning of re‑intervention.  In patients with multiple stent layers, such surveillance may also refine predictions of coronary accessibility and inform the choice of future valve platforms or adjunctive procedures.

---

In summary, the case demonstrates that a third transcatheter valve can be safely and effectively implanted after endocarditis‑treated PVE when guided by meticulous imaging and multidisciplinary planning.  ESC 2025 recommendations for PVE are balanced by individualized risk–benefit analyses that consider surgical prohibitive risk.  High‑implantation, commissural alignment, and careful preservation of coronary access help mitigate PPM and procedural complications.  As the field advances, engineered imaging‑driven algorithms, CFD analysis, and robust registries will be essential to optimise clinical outcomes in this increasingly common patient population.
