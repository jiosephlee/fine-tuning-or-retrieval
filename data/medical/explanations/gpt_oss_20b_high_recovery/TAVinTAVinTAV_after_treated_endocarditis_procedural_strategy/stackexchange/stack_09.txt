Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Differential Diagnosis] What are the distinguishing echocardiographic and clinical features between prosthetic valve endocarditis and early structural dysfunction in a patient who has had multiple TAVI procedures?

**Distinguishing prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE) from early structural dysfunction after repeat TAVI**

| Feature | Prosthetic‑Valve Endocarditis | Early Structural Dysfunction (degeneration or mechanical failure) |
|---------|------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Clinical presentation** | • Fever, chills, malaise, weight loss, or new “severe” systemic symptoms.<br>• Positive blood cultures (>50 % of cases).<br>• New or worsening heart‑failure symptoms due to valvular failure.<br>• Immune‑complex phenomena (e.g., Roth spots, Osler nodes) may be present. | • Insidious or sudden exertional dyspnea, chest discomfort, or dizziness.<br>• Often afebrile and without systemic infection markers.<br>• Symptoms mirror those of the underlying valvular pathology (stenosis or regurgitation). |
| **Echocardiographic findings (TTE/TEE)** | • Mobile or oscillating echogenic mass attached to the prosthetic valve or sewing ring (vegetations).<br>• Paravalvular leak or perivalvular abscess.<br>• Flail or perforated leaflet, new regurgitation irrespective of gradient.<br>• Severe reduction in leaflet mobility with thickening or fragmentation. | • Gradual or abrupt increase in transvalvular mean gradient and drop in EOA/AVA.<br>• Leaflet thickening or calcification, pannus formation, or stiffening without discrete vegetations.<br>• Progressive paraprosthetic regurgitation due to calcific pannus rather than abscess.<br>• No mobile echo‑dense masses. |
| **Laboratory data** | • Positive blood cultures (common pathogens: Staphylococcus aureus, coagulase‑negative staph, enterococci).<br>• Elevated inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) – often >‑two‑fold. | • Blood cultures negative or transient positive (usually sterile).<br>• Inflammatory markers normal or mildly elevated. |
| **Imaging adjuncts** | • Multislice CT (MSCT) can reveal perivalvular contrast staining (abscess), leaflet thickening, or debris.<br>• PET‑CT may show increased uptake around the valve. | • MSCT shows progressive degeneration: leaflet calcification, stent migration, or stent fracture; no contrast extravasation.<br>• Evaluate valve‑to‑coronary distances, neo‑skirt formation; no “hot spots.” |
| **Temporal course** | • Symptoms may appear weeks to months after implantation, often with recent exposure to bacteremia; an acute, fulminant presentation is possible. | • Typically a gradual decline, though an abrupt sudden failure can occur (e.g., leaflet tear or structural fracture). |
| **Management implications** | • Requires definitive antimicrobial therapy (≥6 weeks); surgery recommended if heart‑failure, uncontrolled infection, or lack of response. | • Addresses hemodynamic obstruction: valve‑re‑deployment (TAV‑in‑TAV, TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV), balloon‑valve fracture, or surgical replacement if required. |

### How the case exemplifies these distinctions

1. **Clinical picture**: In September 2022 the patient had fever, positive Enterococcus faecalis cultures, and new severe restenosis → classic PVE. By June 2024, fever and bacteremia had resolved; the patient remained afebrile with normal inflammatory markers despite worsening gradients.

2. **Echocardiographic evolution**: TTE in 2022 showed severe restenosis (gradient 35 mm Hg) without new regurgitation; by June 2024, TTE revealed a markedly reduced AVA (0.5 cm²) and substantial gradients (49 mm Hg), but no vegetations or paravalvular leaks. TEE confirmed absence of active infection or leaflet perforation.

3. **Imaging**: MSCT demonstrated a minimum lumen of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis—consistent with structural narrowing rather than abscess or perivalvular fluid collection.

4. **Microbiology**: All blood cultures taken after antibiotic completion (June 2024) were negative, corroborating eradication of infection.

Thus, **the key differentiators in this patient** are an afebrile, culture‑negative status combined with progressive gradient increase and leaflet calcification on imaging—hallmarks of early structural dysfunction—versus the infectious and inflammatory profile that initially characterized his PVE episode. Recognizing this distinction is essential because therapeutic strategies diverge dramatically: re‑intervention for structural failure versus prolonged antimicrobial therapy (and possibly surgery) for active endocarditis.
