Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

The child was first brought to the outpatient department by his mother with a one‑week history of worsening right‑sided chest pain and shortness of breath that had begun gradually and had progressed to noticeable respiratory distress. The mother reported that the patient’s breathing had become increasingly labored over the last several days, prompting a formal medical assessment. On presentation, the initial assessment revealed a mildly tachypneic child (respiratory rate 20 /min, normal for his age) whose oxygen saturation measured 88–89 % on room air. The patient’s general appearance was otherwise unremarkable: alert, oriented, and without evident cyanosis or pallor.

On physical examination, the right hemithorax displayed classic signs of effusion. Percussion over the right subscapular, infra‑axillary, and axillary regions yielded a stony‑dull note, and vocal fremitus was markedly attenuated on the right compared with the left. Auscultation revealed diminished breath sounds in the same zones. Abdominal examination was unremarkable – the abdomen was soft, non‑tender, and without hepatosplenomegaly. Cardiovascularly, the child had a heart rate of 110 /min, consistent with mild tachycardia, and no murmurs were appreciated. The overall examination reaffirmed the presence of a right‑side pleural effusion superimposed on a stable systemic condition.

The patient was immediately transferred to the pediatric intensive care unit for further stabilization. A humidified oxygen mask delivering 5 L/min was instituted, improving oxygen saturations to the mid‑90s. Continuous monitoring of vital signs, cardiac rhythm, and oxygenation was commenced. Empiric antibacterial therapy with piperacillin‑tazobactam was started, and blood cultures were sent to rule out a potential infectious process. Routine laboratory investigations – complete blood count, basic metabolic panel, liver function tests, and inflammatory markers – were ordered to evaluate for underlying infection or systemic disease.

Rapid imaging work‑up was pursued. A plain post‑eroidal chest radiograph demonstrated a right‑sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle. This was complemented by a bedside lung ultrasound, which confirmed approximately one litre of fluid accumulating over the right hemithorax, predominantly around the subscapular and axillary fields. The ultrasound offered a swift confirmation of the effusion and guided the subsequent thoracocentesis.

An early therapeutic thoracentesis was performed under ultrasound guidance. Approximately one litre of dark‑red pleural fluid was aspirated, consistent with a haemorrhagic effusion. The removal of fluid resulted in rapid clinical improvement: the child’s tachypnea resolved, oxygen saturation returned to near‑normal levels (95–98 % on room air), chest discomfort abated rapidly, and the patient was weaned off supplemental oxygen within 24 hours. This dramatic response not only alleviated the acute respiratory compromise but also directed the diagnostic pathway toward further evaluation of a potential underlying thoracic‑abdominal pathology.

## Diagnostic Workup and Differential Diagnosis

In a child presenting with massive, recurrent, hemorrhagic pleural effusions, the diagnostic pathway must be broad yet systematic. The goal is to differentiate a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) from more common thoracic etiologies and to identify a secondary abdominal source when the initial fluid profile is ambiguous. The diagnostic approach in this case relied on serial pleural fluid analyses, serological enzyme measurements, and advanced cross‑sectional imaging, all of which converged to confirm PPF.

### Pleural fluid analysis – macroscopic appearance, protein, LDH, cell counts, cytology, and ADA

The aspirate was consistently a dark‑red, blood‑stained fluid. Macroscopic appearance alone can misleadingly suggest an exudative hemothorax. Therefore, quantitative analysis is essential. Using Light’s criteria – a pleural protein/serum protein ratio >0.5, pleural LDH/serum LDH >0.6, or pleural LDH > two‑thirds the upper limit of normal – would have classified this effusion as an exudate. Although the precise laboratory values are not provided in the report, the exudative nature is inferred from the high protein content typical of pancreatic leakages.

Cell counts usually show a mixture of neutrophils and lymphocytes; a neutrophil predominance would favor infection or trauma, whereas a lymphocytic shift could suggest tuberculosis. In this patient, the cell differential was not detailed, but the absence of a defensive neutrophilic exudate and normal peripheral counts excluded empyema or bacterial pleuritis.

Cytology was performed to exclude malignant causes of hemorrhagic effusion (e.g., lymphoma or metastatic disease), and no malignant cells were found. Adenosine deaminase (ADA) was mildly elevated, a laboratory feature that initially raised suspicion for tuberculous pleuritis. However, given the 1‑month history of abdominal trauma, the consistently high enzyme content in the fluid, and subsequent negative GeneXpert testing, tuberculosis was deemed unlikely. ADA levels above 40 U/L are commonly considered diagnostic of TB, yet in this case the value was modest and lacked corroborative microbiological evidence.

Collectively, pleural fluid characteristics ruled out common thoracic causes and pointed the evaluation toward an extrathoracic source.

### Serum and pleural fluid amylase/lipase concentrations and their diagnostic significance

The hallmark laboratory finding in PPF is an exceedingly elevated pleural fluid amylase. In this case, the amylase was 6,118 U/L and lipase 7,211 U/L, far above the typical thresholds (>1,000 U/L) that are considered pathognomonic for pancreatic leakage into pleural space. Serum amylase and lipase levels, while useful for confirming pancreatitis, are less specific because they can be normal or mildly elevated in many intra‑abdominal insults. The striking discrepancy – markedly high pleural enzyme levels versus only moderate serum elevation – is classic for a fistulous connection. 

In addition, differentiating pancreatic amylase from salivary amylase (the latter may rise in TB or malignancy) can be done by subfractionation, but this was not performed here. Nevertheless, the sheer magnitude of both enzymes, coupled with the clinical context, achieved sufficient diagnostic confidence.

### Imaging modalities – contrast‑enhanced CT chest and MRCP – sensitivity, specificity, and typical findings

Contrast‑enhanced computed tomography (CECT) of the chest was first employed to evaluate the recurrent effusion. While CECT is excellent at delineating pleural collections, lung parenchyma, and adjacent mediastinal structures, its sensitivity for detecting a pancreatopleural fistula is modest (≈33–47%). In this patient, CECT revealed a right‑sided hydropneumothorax and a multiloculated, hypodense, elongated collection extending from the mediastinum down to the pancreas tail, suggestive of a possible tract.

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) supersedes CECT for visualizing pancreatic ductal anatomy and fistulous ducts because of its high soft‑tissue contrast and multidetector capability. MRCP in this case demonstrated a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas, tracking retroperitoneally into the mediastinum, passing through the esophageal hiatus, and communicating with the pleural space—a classic anatomical pathway for a PPF. Typical MRCP sensitivity for detecting such ductal disruptions is reported as 70–80%, and specificity is high due to accurate ductal delineation. The imaging findings matched the laboratory evidence of pancreatic leakage and contextualized the trauma history.

### Differential diagnoses considered

Five principal alternative diagnoses were systematically evaluated:

1. **Tuberculous pleuritis** – Elevated ADA and contact history initially favored this. However, nonspecific radiologic findings, the absence of a purulent exudate, and a negative GeneXpert rendered it unlikely.
2. **Empyema** – The fluid’s dark‑red appearance could mimic a hemothorax or infected effusion. Nevertheless, only mild neutrophilia and the absence of fever or purulent characteristics argued against empyema.
3. **Malignancy (primary lung or metastatic disease)** – Hemorrhagic pleural effusions are seen in lymphoma or melanoma. Cytology was negative, peripheral blood counts were normal, and no constitutional symptoms were recorded, making malignancy improbable.
4. **Oesophageal perforation (Boerhaave’s syndrome)** – Produces amylase‑rich pleural fluid but is usually accompanied by severe chest pain, vomiting, or a history of forceful emesis. The patient’s history was of blunt abdominal trauma, and he denied vomiting or chest pain suggestive of a perforation.
5. **Diaphragmatic hernia** – Could allow abdominal viscera into the thoracic cavity, causing effusion. Imaging lacked visible abdominal organs in the chest; moreover, the pleural fluid would not have the high pancreatic enzyme content seen here.
6. **Hepatic hydrothorax** – The liver function tests were normal, abdominal imaging showed no cirrhotic changes or portal hypertension, and serology did not support chronic liver disease.

By systematically excluding each of these entities through a combination of laboratory, imaging, and historical data, the index of suspicion for PPF rose significantly.

### Diagnostic reasoning: integration of trauma history, fluid characteristics, enzyme elevation and imaging to confirm PPF

The diagnostic process ultimately hinged on the convergence of four pillars:

1. **Clinical context** – A history of a blunt abdominal injury from a bicycle handle one month prior, combined with the absence of fever, sick contacts, or typical TB symptoms, tilted consideration toward an abdominal source rather than a pulmonary process.
2. **Pleural fluid phenotype** – Consistently hemorrhagic, but non‑purulent, with no cytologic evidence of malignancy, narrowing the differential to rare etiologies that can “mask” as thoracic disease.
3. **Enzyme profiling** – The extraordinary elevation of amylase and lipase in pleural fluid (but not markedly in serum) was a decisive clue pointing to a pancreatic leak. A value ≥1,000 U/L is highly specific for PPF, providing an objective laboratory marker to steer the work‑up away from conditions like TB or malignancy that also raise ADA.
4. **Imaging confirmation** – CECT depicted a hypodense tract from the pancreatic tail to the right pleural space, while MRCP illuminated the ductal anatomy and offered a visual proof of a fistulous communication. These imaging findings, together with the laboratory data, formed a cohesive, evidence‑based explanation for the recurrent hemorrhagic effusions.

Once PPF was confirmed, empiric therapies for tuberculosis and suspected empyema were withdrawn, and specific conservative measures (nil per os, TPN, octreotide, and pancreatic enzyme replacement) were instituted. The drainage from the intercostal catheter progressively diminished, corroborating the functional closure of the fistula.

In summary, a methodical integration of pleural fluid analysis, enzyme measurements, imaging, and clinical history is essential for diagnosing a pancreatopleural fistula in a pediatric patient, especially when it masquerades as a common pulmonary pathology.

## Pathophysiology and Etiology of Pancreatopleural Fistula

A pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a dire consequence of pancreatic injury in which enzymatic pancreatic secretions leak from a ruptured duct or a pseudocyst and migrate through the retroperitoneal space, up the diaphragm, and into the pleural cavity. In the reported case, a previously healthy boy sustained blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle approximately one month before his first presentation. This single event precipitated posterior duct rupture, establishing the anatomical prerequisites for a PPF.

### Anatomical route of pancreatic fluid

The pancreas lies in the retroperitoneum, posterior to the stomach and anterior to the spine, with its ductal system (main pancreatic duct and the duct of Santorini) traversing its longitudinal axis. A rupture of the posterior portion of the main duct releases pancreatic juice into the retroperitoneal space. Because the retroperitoneum is a potential space, the fluid tracks along the least resistant path—posteriorly toward the diaphragm. Through the esophageal hiatal opening (or, less commonly, the aortic hiatus) the fluid gains access to the mediastinum and subsequently penetrates the pleural cavity. The fluid may dissect through the pleuro‑diaphragmatic fissure, producing a pleural effusion that is often massive, recurrent, and may be hemoptysis‑stained due to enzyme‑mediated vascular injury. In the child’s magnetic‑resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), the fistulous tract was seen extending from an intrapancreatic collection at the body/tail junction, through the esophageal hiatus, into a right‑sided pleural cavity, consistent with this classic anatomic pathway.

### Triggers of ductal disruption

Three principal insults drive ductal disruption in the pediatric population: blunt abdominal trauma, acute pancreatitis, and chronic pancreatitis.

1. **Blunt abdominal trauma** (as in this case) may cause laceration of the pancreas and direct rupture of the duct. The trauma can also generate a splenic or hepatic injury that complicates the inflammatory milieu, further predisposing the duct to dissection.

2. **Acute pancreatitis** leads to autodigestion and edema of the pancreatic parenchyma. The resulting interstitial or necrotic fluid can erode the duct wall, thereby creating a fistulous connection. The child’s high pleural fluid amylase and lipase levels reflect active enzymatic leakage typical of pancreatitis.

3. **Chronic pancreatitis** is marked by progressive fibrosis and irregular ductal obstruction. Repeated cycles of inflammation erode the ductal architecture and render it fragile, increasing the likelihood of fistula formation, especially after an acute insult such as trauma or recurrent acute pancreatitis.

In adults, alcohol-induced chronic pancreatitis commonly precipitates PPF. In children, however, hereditary predispositions are far more relevant.

### Genetic predispositions in children

A growing body of evidence implicates several genes in the pathogenesis of chronic pancreatitis and its complications in pediatric patients:

- **PRSS1** encodes cationic trypsinogen; mutations (e.g., R122H, N291S) produce a hyperactive enzyme that precipitates self‑activation and pancreatic autodigestion, weakening the ductal wall.
- **SPINK1** encodes a serine protease inhibitor that protects the pancreas from premature trypsin activation. Loss‑of‑function mutations or upregulation of the SPINK1 gene can reduce this protective effect, leading to ductal vulnerability.
- **CFTR** (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator) mutations alter the composition of pancreatic secretions, promoting ductal blockage and subsequent atrophy.
- **Chymotrypsin C** mutations, as well as other ductal structural genes, have also been implicated.

These genetic defects compromise pancreatic duct integrity and increase the risk that slight inflammation or mechanical stress (as seen with trauma) will culminate in ductal rupture and fistula formation. Even though the reported child was not genetically tested, the absence of a prior history of pancreatitis and the timing of the trauma strongly suggest that a subclinical or silent ductal defect was unmasked by the injury.

### Clinical presentation versus underlying abdominal pathology (“pulmonary mask” phenomenon)

PPF typically masquerades as a primary thoracic disorder. The child presented only with dyspnea, chest pain, and massive right‑sided pleural effusion, leading initially to repeated thoracocenteses and placement of an intercostal drain. Standard pulmonary investigations (X‑ray, ultrasound, ADA, GeneXpert) were pursued under the presumption of tuberculosis or empyema. The “pulmonary mask” refers to this deceptive presentation, in which the abdominal origin of the problem is overlooked until dramatic imaging (CT or MRCP) demonstrates the underlying pancreatic source. Recognizing that recurrent, hemorrhagic pleural effusions, particularly when high in amylase and lipase, often point to a pancreatic origin can prevent repeated invasive thoracic procedures.

### Pleural fluid amylase isoenzyme: pancreatic vs. salivary

In pleural fluid, elevated amylase can signal several processes—pancreatitis, tuberculosis, malignancy, or oesophageal rupture. Distinguishing the isoenzyme type helps narrow the differential:

- **Pancreatic amylase** predominates in PPF. Levels usually exceed 1,000 IU/L (often 3,000–10,000 IU/L), as seen in the child’s pleural fluid (≈6,000 IU/L).
- **Salivary amylase** dominates in tuberculous pleural effusion or malignancy, where levels may be elevated but rarely surpass the pancreatic threshold.
- A semi‑quantitative test can measure the proportion of pancreatic versus salivary isoforms, confirming a pancreatic source when the pancreatic fraction is markedly higher.

In this patient, the pleural fluid analysis revealed amylase and lipase far above the anticipated TB or empyema thresholds, and the MRCP confirmed a pancreatic origin, underscoring the diagnostic importance of amylase isoenzyme typing.

In sum, the pathophysiology of PPF consists of ductal rupture or pseudocyst leakage, retroperitoneal spread, diaphragmatic hiatus entry, and pleural cavity contact. Traction from trauma or pancreatitis precipitates this in children who may harbor genetic mutations that destabilize the ductal architecture. Clinically, the presentation as a massive, recurrent pleural effusion is misleading, necessitating a high index of suspicion and rapid imaging (CT/MRCP) to unveil the true abdominal etiology. Recognizing the characteristic amylase isoenzyme profile further streamlines differential diagnosis and prevents unnecessary thoracic therapy.

## Management of Pancreatopleural Fistula

A pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a rare but potentially life‑threatening complication of pancreatic disease, especially in children. Management is driven by the principles of the pediatric pancreatitis literature, but tailored to the fistula’s anatomical and clinical presentation. The therapeutic roadmap is usually three‑tiered: (1) conservative care, (2) endoscopic intervention, and (3) surgery when earlier measures fail. In the absence of formal, evidence‐based guidelines, a stepwise, individualized approach guided by clinical response and imaging is prudent.

### 1. Conservative Management

**Goal** – to reduce exocrine pancreatic output, collapse the fistula, and allow natural healing while preventing infection and nutritional compromise.

| Component | Rationale | Typical Practice (in the reported case) |
|---|---|---|
| **Nil per‑oral (NPO) & Bowel Rest** | Eliminates pancreatic stimulation from food intake, lowering enzyme secretion. | Patient placed NPO immediately upon diagnosis. |
| **Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN)** | Maintains caloric and micronutrient needs while the gut is bypassed; reduces enteric stimulation. | Started concurrently with NPO, supported until drainage resolved. |
| **Somatostatin analogue – Octreotide** | Suppresses pancreatic exocrine secretion and decreases fistula output. The 30 µg/kg/day dose (range 10–40 µg/kg/day) has the strongest evidence in pediatrics. | Administered 30 µg/kg/day, infused continuously; drip stopped once drainage ceased. |
| **Pancreatic Enzyme Replacement Therapy (PERT)** | Prevents malabsorption when the patient resumes oral feeding. | Provided after drainage had declined, allowing gradual re‑introduction of enteral feeds. |
| **Chest‑tube / Intercostal Drainage (ICD)** | Secures continuous removal of pleural fluid, preventing re‑accumulation and allowing monitoring of output. | Right ICD inserted; drainage observed over 10 days, with gradual reduction in volume. |

**Evidence & Outcomes**  
In pediatric series, medical therapy alone yields a modest 12 % success rate, reflecting the rarity of the condition and the variable ductal anatomy. Nevertheless, conservative care universally remains the first line due to its low morbidity and ease of implementation. In the presented case, a 10‑day course of octreotide, TPN, and PERT combined with daily drainage monitoring produced complete closure of the fistula, highlighting the potential for non‑invasive treatment.

**Monitoring of Drainage**  
- **Volume Trends**: A decline from >1 L/day to <100 mL/day within 5–7 days is reassuring.  
- **Character of Fluid**: A shift from amylase‑rich, hemorrhagic fluid to serosanguinous or serous loss indicates healing.  
- **Laboratory Normalization**: Pleural fluid amylase and lipase approaching normal ranges (<50 IU/L) within 2–3 weeks signals fistula closure.  

These criteria informed the decision to remove the ICD on day 10 in the case report.

### 2. Endoscopic Therapy

When conservative measures fail, or ductal obstruction is evident, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is the preferred next step.

**Indications**  
- Persistent pleural drainage >100 mL/day after 2–3 weeks of medical therapy.  
- Imaging evidence of ductal disruption or stenosis (e.g., narrowed main duct proximal to the fistula).  
- Failure of symptom resolution or recurrent effusions despite maximal medical care.

**Procedure**  
1. **Sphincterotomy** – facilitates drainage through the ampulla.  
2. **Trans‑papillary stenting** – place a plastic or metal stent across the disrupted duct to re‑establish ductal continuity and reduce intraductal pressure.  
3. **Optional balloon dilatation** – if stenosis limits stent passage.

**Success Rates & Timing**  
- In children, stent placement has a 48–100 % success rate, typically achieving closure within 2–3 weeks of stenting.  
- The stent usually remains for 4–6 weeks, after which it can be removed once imaging confirms ductal patency.

**Contingencies**  
If the fistula is remote from the ampulla or the ductal anatomy is unsuitable for stenting, consider bypass procedures (e.g., pancreaticojejunostomy) via surgery.

### 3. Surgical Options

Surgery is reserved for refractory cases, extensive ductal trauma, or when endoscopic stenting is not feasible.

| Procedure | Indication | Overview | Pediatric Outcomes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Distal Pancreatectomy** | Pancreatic tail injury, pseudocyst involving the tail, or lack of ductal continuity. | Resection of the diseased distal pancreas with preservation of the spleen when possible. | 93 % success, <5 % mortality, 11 % recurrence (based on pediatric series). |
| **Pancreaticojejunostomy (Puestow)** | Extensive ductal disruption needing internal drainage. | Opens a large pancreatic duct segment into a loop of jejunum, allowing continuous internal drainage. | Similar success to distal pancreatectomy with comparable morbidity. |
| **Cystojejunostomy** | Pseudocysts communicating with the fistula requiring external drainage. | Creates a fistulous tract between the cyst and jejunum for internal drainage. | Effective in controlling fluid collections; high success rates. |

**Operative Planning**  
- Provide detailed pre‑operative imaging (CT/MRCP) to delineate ductal anatomy.  
- Ensure multidisciplinary discussion (pediatric surgery, gastroenterology, pulmonology).  
- Post‑operative care involves monitoring drainage, ensuring adequate nutrition, and surveillance imaging.

### 4. Monitoring and Criteria for Fistula Closure

All management stages hinge on objective markers:

1. **Chest Drainage Volume**  
   - >1 L/day → urgent review.  
   - <100 mL/day for 48–72 h → consider removal (as in the case study).  
2. **Pleural Fluid Biochemistry**  
   - Amylase & lipase <100 IU/L, or a >90 % reduction from peak levels.  
3. **Imaging**  
   - Repeat MRCP or CT after 2–3 weeks of conservative care; closure defined by the disappearance of the fistulous tract or resolution of the collection.  
4. **Clinical Symptoms**  
   - Absence of chest pain, dyspnea, and fever.  
   - Resumption of normal oral intake without abdominal pain.

When these criteria are met consistently, the fistula is considered closed, and chest drainage can be discontinued.

### 5. Tailoring Therapy in the Absence of Formal Guidelines

Because pediatric PPF is exceedingly rare, recommendations largely derive from case reports and small series. Decision making should therefore be individualized, weighing:

- **Patient Age & Size** – Larger children may tolerate ERCP more readily.  
- **Anatomical Complexity** – MRCP should guide whether ductal disruption is amenable to stenting or requires surgical repair.  
- **Response to Initial Therapy** – Rapid decline in drainage favors continued conservative care; stagnation mandates escalation.  
- **Resource Availability** – In centers lacking ERCP expertise, early surgical consultation may be prudent.  
- **Patient Goals & Family Preferences** – Discuss risks of prolonged TPN or invasive procedures.

The described patient exemplifies a successful conservative pathway: after failing three episodes of thoracocentesis and empirical antitubercular therapy, a 10‑day regimen of octreotide and TPN, coupled with ICD drainage, led to resolution without further intervention. In other scenarios, escalating to ERCP or surgery may be necessary.

---

### Take‑Home Points

- **Start with conservative therapy**: NPO, TPN, octreotide 30 µg/kg/day, PERT, and chest drainage.  
- **Monitor output and labs closely**; a >90 % drop in pleural fluid amylase and <100 mL/day output heralds healing.  
- **Escalate to ERCP** (sphincterotomy + stent) if conservative care fails or ductal anatomy dictates.  
- **Consider surgery** (distal pancreatectomy, pancreaticojejunostomy, cystojejunostomy) when endoscopic therapy is not viable or fails after 3–4 weeks.  
- **Individualize the approach** in the absence of formal guidelines, guided by imaging, clinical response, and multidisciplinary expertise.  

By following this structured, evidence‑backed framework, pediatric clinicians can navigate the diagnostic and therapeutic complexities of pancreatopleural fistulas effectively.

## Outcome, Follow‑Up, and Clinical Significance

After the initiation of octreotide infusion (30 µg kg⁻¹ day⁻¹) and total parenteral nutrition, the child’s right intercostal drainage output fell steadily over a ten‑day period, eventually becoming negligible by day‑ten. A clear decline in pleural fluid volume is the hallmark of effective suppression of pancreatic exocrine secretion and of a decreasing fistulous leak, and it was precisely this pattern that prompted the clinicians to remove the chest tube on day‑eleven. The patient was then discharged home while still nil per‑oral, with a plan to transition to oral feeding and pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) once tolerability allowed.

Post‑hospital follow‑up consisted of a structured schedule of monthly clinic visits for the first six months, followed by quarterly reviews thereafter. At each visit, the clinician assessed for recurrence of respiratory symptoms, reviewed growth parameters, and recorded adherence to a balanced diet and regular exercise. No imaging was deemed necessary unless the patient exhibited clinical signs of re‑accumulation; the absence of new or recurrent symptoms obviated the need for routine repeat imaging.

Long‑term results were reassuring. At the latest outpatient review, six months after discharge, the patient remained free of chest pain, dyspnea, or signs of pleural effusion. He had resumed full‑time schooling and was able to engage in strenuous play without limitation. He had also adopted a healthier dietary pattern, moving away from processed, high‑calorie foods, and had initiated a regular exercise routine, both of which likely reduced metabolic demand on the pancreas and minimized the risk of future exocrine injury.

The prognosis for pediatric pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is relatively good when the fistula is successfully closed, whether medically or surgically. Literature data show that recurrent pleural effusions after conservative management occur in roughly 10–15 % of cases, whereas surgical series report recurrence rates as low as 8–11 %. Because this child’s fistula resolved with medical therapy alone, the risk of late recurrence is minimal, but ongoing vigilance remains essential given the propensity of pancreatic pathology to re‑activate in the setting of stress or injury.

Several key lessons emerge from this case that have broader clinical implications. First, a meticulous history that explicitly queries abdominal trauma can unveil a paradoxical presentation in which a young patient presents only with thoracic symptoms. Second, pleural fluid amylase markedly exceeding 1 000 IU L⁻¹ should alert the clinician to a pancreatic source and prompt immediate amylase‑typing to distinguish pancreatic from salivary isoform, thereby narrowing differential diagnoses. Third, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is superior to CT for delineating the fistulous tract and pancreatic duct morphology, and it should be pursued promptly after biochemical evidence of pancreatic fluid in the pleural space. Finally, the avoidance of empirical anti‑tubercular therapy not only spares the patient from unnecessary drug exposure but also reduces stigma and preserves limited antimicrobial resources. In sum, prompt recognition of the “pulmonary mask” of PPF, coupled with targeted biochemical and imaging investigations, allows timely, effective treatment, avoids misdiagnosis, and yields favorable long‑term outcomes.
