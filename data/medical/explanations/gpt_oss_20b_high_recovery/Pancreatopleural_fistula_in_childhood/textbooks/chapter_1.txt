Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

The child was first brought to the outpatient department by his mother with a one‑week history of worsening right‑sided chest pain and shortness of breath that had begun gradually and had progressed to noticeable respiratory distress. The mother reported that the patient’s breathing had become increasingly labored over the last several days, prompting a formal medical assessment. On presentation, the initial assessment revealed a mildly tachypneic child (respiratory rate 20 /min, normal for his age) whose oxygen saturation measured 88–89 % on room air. The patient’s general appearance was otherwise unremarkable: alert, oriented, and without evident cyanosis or pallor.

On physical examination, the right hemithorax displayed classic signs of effusion. Percussion over the right subscapular, infra‑axillary, and axillary regions yielded a stony‑dull note, and vocal fremitus was markedly attenuated on the right compared with the left. Auscultation revealed diminished breath sounds in the same zones. Abdominal examination was unremarkable – the abdomen was soft, non‑tender, and without hepatosplenomegaly. Cardiovascularly, the child had a heart rate of 110 /min, consistent with mild tachycardia, and no murmurs were appreciated. The overall examination reaffirmed the presence of a right‑side pleural effusion superimposed on a stable systemic condition.

The patient was immediately transferred to the pediatric intensive care unit for further stabilization. A humidified oxygen mask delivering 5 L/min was instituted, improving oxygen saturations to the mid‑90s. Continuous monitoring of vital signs, cardiac rhythm, and oxygenation was commenced. Empiric antibacterial therapy with piperacillin‑tazobactam was started, and blood cultures were sent to rule out a potential infectious process. Routine laboratory investigations – complete blood count, basic metabolic panel, liver function tests, and inflammatory markers – were ordered to evaluate for underlying infection or systemic disease.

Rapid imaging work‑up was pursued. A plain post‑eroidal chest radiograph demonstrated a right‑sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle. This was complemented by a bedside lung ultrasound, which confirmed approximately one litre of fluid accumulating over the right hemithorax, predominantly around the subscapular and axillary fields. The ultrasound offered a swift confirmation of the effusion and guided the subsequent thoracocentesis.

An early therapeutic thoracentesis was performed under ultrasound guidance. Approximately one litre of dark‑red pleural fluid was aspirated, consistent with a haemorrhagic effusion. The removal of fluid resulted in rapid clinical improvement: the child’s tachypnea resolved, oxygen saturation returned to near‑normal levels (95–98 % on room air), chest discomfort abated rapidly, and the patient was weaned off supplemental oxygen within 24 hours. This dramatic response not only alleviated the acute respiratory compromise but also directed the diagnostic pathway toward further evaluation of a potential underlying thoracic‑abdominal pathology.
