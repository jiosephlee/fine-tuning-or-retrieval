Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# From Bark to Blood: Spotting Capnocytophaga canimorsus in the ED

## The Unexpected Sepsis After a Dog Bite
A healthy woman in her late 40s came to the ED four days after her dog bit her on the upper lip. She received a tetanus booster and an oral prescription for phenoxymethylpenicillin, but never filled it. She was febrile (> 40 °C), hypoglycaemic, and complained of numbness and discoloration in her feet. By the time she reached the ED, she was septic, mottled, and had evidence of lactic acidosis, leukopenia, and DIC‑like coagulopathy. How did a seemingly minor household injury evolve into a life‑threatening infection that eventually required dialysis, plasma exchange, and eculizumab?

## DIC, Pancytopenia, and the Sepsis Work‑up
The triad of fever, marked inflammation, pancytopenia, and a bleeding tendency made septic DIC the most likely diagnosis on arrival. The differential remained broad: toxic shock, sepsis‑induced DIC, hemophagocytic syndrome, and, on the rarer end, a TMA. Broad‑spectrum piperacillin‑tazobactam was immediately started to cover gram‑negative rods frequently seen after dog bites, including Capnocytophaga canimorsus. Blood cultures later confirmed the culprit on day 7, underscoring the organism’s slow‑growing, fastidious nature.

The key point here is that early empiric coverage against C. canimorsus can change the trajectory when the identification of the pathogen takes days. Delayed therapy can let the infection spiral into multiorgan failure.

## The Heptomized Rise of Microangiopathic Hemolysis
After a period of sepsis control, the patient’s kidneys failed—she became anuric and required continuous renal replacement therapy. Eight days into admission she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin (111 → 68 g/L) with no bleeding source, and platelet counts remained low (≈ 30–40 × 10⁹/L). Haptoglobin was undetectable, LDH was markedly elevated, and a peripheral smear showed 15–20 % schistocytes. These findings signaled microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) superimposed on the sepsis‑induced DIC.

A high‑dose steroid trial was attempted after a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT), but the picture worsened. Repeat DAT was negative, and the ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5 %, ruling out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). Strep‑toxin‑producing E. coli HUS was excluded with a negative stool PCR. The remaining diagnosis—complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS)—was confirmed by renal biopsy showing tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and crescents consistent with TMA.

## Diagnosing CM‑HUS in the Setting of Active Sepsis
CM‑HUS is a clinic‑pathologic diagnosis of exclusion. Normal C3/C4 levels do not rule it out, because complement consumption may still be ongoing. In this patient, functional complement assays (alternative pathway activity) were markedly reduced (6 %) after plasma exchange and eculizumab induction, but the absence of pathogenic complement gene variants and anti‑factor H antibodies suggested an infection‑triggered episode rather than a heritable complement dysregulation.

The diagnostic “aha” moment was that the hemolysis appeared after improvement of septic parameters—a classic “second hit” pattern: the underlying pathogen (C. canimorsus) initiates complement activation, and the subsequent host response culminates in a TMA.

## Management – The Interplay of Antimicrobials, PEX, and Eculizumab
Once the diagnosis of CM‑HUS was entertained, a triple‑line attack began:

1. **Antimicrobials** – Piperacillin‑tazobactam was continued, then narrowed to intravenous ampicillin, and later switched to oral phenoxymethylpenicillin once cultures were clean. Until the blood cultures were negative, complement blockade was deferred to avoid compromising host defense against this encapsulated pathogen.

2. **Plasma Exchange (PEX)** – Bridging therapy to remove circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators. After three sessions, diuresis returned, but platelet counts remained sub‑optimal without definitive complement inhibition.

3. **Eculizumab** – Initiated once cultures cleared, after meningococcal vaccination and penicillin prophylaxis. A 4‑week induction schedule (900 mg weekly) was followed by maintenance dosing (1200 mg biweekly). Renal function partially recovered (creatinine 175 µmol/L), and hematologic remission was maintained.

The choice of eculizumab over other complement inhibitors was guided by the need for rapid, sustained blockade of the terminal pathway to halt endothelial injury.

## Why Capnocytophaga Should Be on Every Dog‑Bite Differential
- **Fastidious Growth** – Cultures may take up to a week; therefore, empiric coverage should not be withheld.
- **Sepsis Spectrum** – Can cause fulminant sepsis, DIC, multiorgan failure, and the rarer TMA.
- **Trigger of CM‑HUS** – In genetically unremarkable patients, C. canimorsus can act as the precipitating “second hit” for complement‑driven microangiopathy.
- **Empiric Regimens** – Piperacillin‑tazobactam or ampicillin‑sulbactam are preferred for dog‑bite wounds.

Keeping C. canimorsus in mind can prompt earlier therapy, reduce time to culture confirmation, and, crucially, allow timely initiation of complement blockade when TMA emerges.

## Clinical Pearls
- A dog bite with a **rapidly progressive fever, leukopenia, and coagulopathy** should raise suspicion for C. canimorsus.
- **Empiric coverage** with agents active against fastidious gram‑negative rods is essential; hold off on complement inhibition until cultures are negative.
- **Serum complement levels (C3/C4)** can be normal during CM‑HUS; rely on functional assays and ADAMTS13 activity for diagnosis.
- **Pneumococcal vaccination** and penicillin prophylaxis are mandatory before starting eculizumab due to the organism’s encapsulated nature.
- **Early eculizumab** (ideally within 7–10 days of TMA onset) improves renal and hematologic outcomes, even after a prolonged septic phase.

## Take‑Home Points
- **Recognize** the red‑flag pattern: dog bite → sepsis → DIC → sudden hemolysis with schistocytes that reappears after initial improvement.
- **Exclude** TTP and STEC‑HUS before diagnosing CM‑HUS; normal classical complement levels do not rule out the disease.
- **Treat early**: de‑escalate antibiotics only after cultures clear, then proceed with plasma exchange and prompt eculizumab initiation.
- **Monitor** renal function to decide on the duration of complement blockade; genetic testing can inform long‑term therapy decisions.
- **Educate** patients about the rare, but life‑threatening potential of a simple pet bite and the importance of completing antibiotic prophylaxis.

The trajectory from a simple dog bite to dialysis‑dependent kidney injury underscores the critical role of early recognition and aggressive, multidisciplinary management. In the era of complement‑targeted therapies, being vigilant for CM‑HUS in sepsis, especially when the source is an unusual gram‑negative rod, can truly change a patient’s outcome.
