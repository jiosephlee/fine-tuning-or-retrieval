Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Complement Inhibition Amid Bacteremia  
## When to Turn the Switch on Eculizumab  

---

### 1. The unexpected twist in a dog‑bite case

A 48‑year‑old woman arrives two days into a septic storm after a dog‑bit bite, already smelling like DIC, pancytopenia and anuria. Four days later her labs start mirroring a microangiopathic hemolysis – a classic HUS curve – but in a patient whose sepsis was *already* improving. The kicker? The culprit was Capnocytophaga *canimorsus*, an organism that hangs around a dog’s throat but can quickly become lethal in humans, and it triggered a complement‑mediated TMA that needed **eculizumab** – a drug that blocks the very arm of immunity the bacterium relies on.

This is a textbook example of the paradox: the same complement cascade that flags a gram‑negative, encapsulated bug is also the engine of a devastating TMA. Our challenge? When to flip the eculizumab switch while the battle against the bacterium is still raging.

---

## 2. Quick facts from the case

| Step | Key Clinical Feature | Lab/Imaging Highlight |
|------|----------------------|------------------------|
| **Day 0 – Bite** | Dog bite, oral Apocillin pending | Pain and hematoma |
| **Day 4 – ED** | Fever >40 °C, hypoglycemia, mottled extremities | ↑CRP, pancytopenia, DIC, AKI |
| **Day 7** | *C. canimorsus* isolated in blood | 100 % |
| **Day 12** | Acute drop in Hb, hepatitis of schistocytes | LDH ↑, haptoglobin <5 mg/dL |
| **Day 14** | ADAMTS13 43 %, negative TTP score | Platelets 30 ×10⁹/L, schistocytes 15–20 % |
| **Day 17** | Kidney biopsy: ATN + TMA | Fibrin thrombi in glomeruli |
| **Day 20** | Cultures negative, vaccinations administered | Menveo & Bexsero |
| **Day 21** | Start eculizumab (900 mg IV weekly) | |
| **Day 30** | Renal function improved, hemolysis resolved | Serum creatinine 175 µmol/L |

*The timeline underscores two truths: (1) Sepsis progressed to TMA after the systemic inflammatory response tapered; (2) Eculizumab was initiated only after the bacterium was eradicated and the patient was immunized.*

---

## 3. Why a complement blockade is life‑saving

- **CM‑HUS** is driven by dysregulated activation of the *alternative* complement pathway.  
- The complement cascade perpetuates endothelial injury, platelet activation, and microthrombi.  
- Early blockade with eculizumab (C5 inhibitor) stops the terminal pathway, halting ongoing hemolysis and preserving renal microvasculature.  
- Evidence consistently links **time‑to‑initiation** with renal recovery. A delay >3 weeks reduces the odds of nephrology independence by up to 30 %.

---

## 4. The risk: weakening the defense against a “friend of the complement”

Eculizumab depletes C5, **eliminating the ability to generate the membrane attack complex (MAC)**. Although the therapy narrows opsonization to surface markers, it leaves patients vulnerable to **encapsulated organisms**: *Neisseria*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*, and, important for our case, *Capnocytophaga*. In a patient with a recent bacteremia, this is no small issue.

- **Mortality risk** with untreated meningococcal infection rises sharply in the first few days of eculizumab therapy.  
- Even with vaccination, breakthrough infections can occur, especially if the vaccine isn’t fully immunogenic in the setting of ongoing infection.

---

## 5. Timing the switch – a real‑world strategy

| Scenario | Ideal Timing | Reasoning |
|----------|--------------|-----------|
| **Culture‑positive bacteremia** | **Delay** until cultures are clean (or near‑complet e.g. <48 h antibiotics with clinical improvement) | Avoids compromising bacterial clearance |
| **Quasi‑sterile cultures** (negative blood but ongoing antimicrobial therapy) | **Shortly after** negative swabs (within 24–48 h) | Balances urgency for TMA control vs infection risk |
| **Pre‑emptive** (high‑risk patient with known complement mutation and pending antibiotics) | **Within 24 h of antimicrobial start,** if cultures likely negative soon | Early control may prevent irreversible kidney injury |

In the case, eculizumab was started **21 days** after the bite, *after* clearance of the organism and *after* full meningococcal vaccination. This 3‑week gap is longer than ideal, but it was driven by the ethical calculus of avoiding catastrophic infection in a patient whose bacteremia was still potentially smoldering.

---

## 6. Vaccination strategy – the “3‑pillars”

1. **Menveo** (group A, C, Y, W)
2. **Bexsero** (serogroup B)
3. **Timing** – give the *first* dose at least **2 weeks before** starting eculizumab.  
   The second dose is optional but recommended approximately **8 weeks later**.  

- **Why 2 weeks?** Consensus guidelines (IDSA & EU) recommend a minimum 2‑week interval to ensure sufficient antibody titers.  
- **Why both vaccines?** Eculizumab dramatically amplifies risk to *all* meningococcal serogroups; coverage must be comprehensive.

In our patient, this vaccination schedule was followed, and the first dose was administered right before eculizumab induction.

---

## 7. Antibiotic prophylaxis – cover the capsule

- **Penicillin V (Apocillin)** was continued throughout the eculizumab course because *C. canimorsus* remains susceptible.  
- If a penicillin allergy is present, consider **azithromycin** or **rifampicin** (the latter used in complement‑inhibitor prophylaxis protocols).  
- Prophylaxis **must** be maintained at least until the antibody response to vaccination is considered adequate (typically 2–4 weeks post‑first dose).

The patient’s prophylactic Apocillin overlapped with eculizumab for 4 months – a duration longer than necessary in the absence of a confirmed infection, but safe given the high stakes.

---

## 8. Real‑world evidence: CM‑HUS triggered by *C. canimorsus*

- **Reviews** report a handful (~10) of *C. canimorsus*‑associated CM‑HUS cases, all treated with eculizumab.  
- Two key patterns emerge:
  1. **No underlying complement mutation** in *most* cases after exhaustive genetic work‑up (i.e., acquired CM‑HUS).  
  2. **Early** eculizumab initiation (median 2–4 days after TMA onset) correlates with partial renal recovery.

The case we’re dissecting illustrates this pattern perfectly: an immunocompetent adult, no pathogenic complement variants, who suffered a septic DIC‑driven shock only to develop a superimposed TMA after antibiotics took hold. The early anthier shock confers a “second hit” that unleashes the complement cascade.

---

## 9. Practical pearls & a quick check‑list

*Before you fire up eculizumab, snapshot the table below:*

- **Culture Status**
  - ❏ Blood cultures *negative* or on therapy with daily clearance?
- **Vaccination**
  - ❏ Menveo & Bexsero, 2 weeks before first dose?
- **Antibiotic Prophylaxis**
  - ❏ Penicillin V (or alternative) ongoing?
- **Complement Markers**
  - ❏ C3/C4 may still be normal → do not rely solely on them.
- **TMA Confirmation**
  - ❏ Schistocytes >10 % **and** LDH ↑, haptoglobin ↓, ADAMTS13 >10 %?  
  - ❏ Confirmed by biopsy if kidney function permits.
- **Timing**
  - ❏ Total delay from TMA onset to eculizumab **ideally <3 weeks**.
  - ❏ If delaying >3 weeks, counsel on higher risk of ESRD.

**Checklist Example:**  
**Day X** – Cultures asterisks (`*`) → **Stop** antibiotics? **Start** eculizumab?  
- **If** `*` + Vaccination + Prophylaxis → Proceed  
- **If** not, hold until all three are satisfied.

---

## 10. Bottom line for the frontline

- **CM‑HUS is often “hidden” beneath sepsis and DIC;‐sep** look for persistent thrombocytopenia, anemia drop, schistocytes, ADAMTS13 >10 % after sepsis improves.  
- **Complement inhibitors are double‑edged**; the same mechanism you’re blocking also prevents bacterial killing.  
- **Timing is a tightrope**: start eculizumab as soon as cultures are negative and vaccination cleared, but not while the bacterium is still brewing.  
- **Vaccination + antibiotic prophylaxis** are non‑negotiable pillars.  
- **Experience matters**: early inpatient eculizumab and close nephrology follow‑up yield the best chance of kidney recovery.

The dog‑bite induced CM‑HUS taught us that *some* infections can “open the door” to complement dysregulation even in the absence of inherited predisposition. As our diagnostics grow faster (real‑time nanopore sequencing, rapid ADAMTS13 assays) and our therapy arsenal expands (new complement inhibitors), the sooner we learn to read the clues early, the earlier we can mount that life‑saving switch.
