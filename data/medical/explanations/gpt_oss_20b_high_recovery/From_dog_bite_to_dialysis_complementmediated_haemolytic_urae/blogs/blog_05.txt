Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Plasma Exchange in CM‑HUS: That Temporary Help or the Core Treatment?

## The Unexpected Twist After a Dog Bite

A healthy woman in her late 40s presents with fever, hypotension and severe pancytopenia four days after a dog bite. Blood cultures grow *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a fastidious, encapsulated Gram‑negative rod that can trigger fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and, in rare cases, thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). In this patient, sepsis improved but a sudden drop in hemoglobin, rising lactate dehydrogenase and persistent thrombocytopenia spark thoughts of a complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). A kidney biopsy confirms microvascular thrombi, and ADAMTS13 activity is comfortably above 10 % ruling out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). The therapeutic question becomes: should we keep pumping plasma or jump straight to complement blockade with eculizumab?

## From DIC to TMA: When to Suspect CM‑HUS

* DIC and CM‑HUS can coexist and look almost identical – both produce schistocytes, consumptive coagulopathy and organ dysfunction.
* Key pearls:
  * In sepsis‑associated TMA, the ADAMTS13 activity is usually **>10 %** (TTP if <10 %).
  * Persistent hemolysis after clinical improvement of sepsis should raise suspicion for CM‑HUS.
  * A normal C3/C4 does **not** exclude CM‑HUS – functional tests are more meaningful but often unavailable bedside.
* In the current case, a time‑course emerged: septic shock → DIC → partial recovery → sudden red‑blood‑cell loss. That pattern is textbook for an infectious trigger of CM‑HUS.

## What Does Plasma Exchange Actually Do in CM‑HUS?

Plasma exchange (PEX) is the work‑horse for TTP, where it removes:
* Ultra‑Large vWF multimers
* Autoantibodies against ADAMTS13
* Inflammatory cytokines

In CM‑HUS, PEX removes:
* **Complement fragments** (C5a, C5b‑9) that may be driving endothelial injury
* **Autoantibodies** (e.g., anti‑factor H)
* **Cytokines** that sustain the “second hit”

But the alternative pathway is constantly regenerated. PEX only provides a *temporary net* reduction of soluble complement. Once the plasmatic complement pool is replenished, the underlying dysregulation—be it genetic, autoantibody‑mediated, or infection‑triggered—continues to activate the endothelium.

## Evidence on PEX: Rare Trials, Mixed Outcomes

The literature is sparse. A handful of case series and single‑center reports report transient improvement in hemolysis or renal function after a few exchanges, but most patients ultimately progress to dialysis or require complement inhibitors.

| Study | Cohort | PEX Duration | Outcomes |
|-------|--------|--------------|----------|
| Yip et al. (2017) | 5 African‑American patients | 3–5 exchanges | Temporary platelet rise; no durable renal recovery |
| Benabou‑Kantsara et al. (2020) | 12 patients with suspected aHUS | 3–4 exchanges | 3 achieved remission off eculizumab; 9 had relapse |
| Case series of *C. canimorsus* CM‑HUS | 2 patients (this paper + Bjørkto et al.) | 3 exchanges | Hematuria improved post‑PEX; eculizumab led to full remission |

**Bottom line:** PEX can buy time, but it is not a cure in CM‑HUS.

## Infections + Complement: Why PEX May Only Be a Bandage

* **Complement activation is double‑edged** – necessary to clear encapsulated organisms, but also potent injurious to endothelium when unchecked.
* In an active infection, complement blockade (eculizumab) carries an obvious risk: the patient’s host response to the pathogen is compromised.
* PEX, on the other hand, removes circulating pathogens and their endotoxins but also depletes protective complement proteins, leaving the host more vulnerable in the early days.
* In the dog‑bite patient, the decision was to delay eculizumab until cultures were clear, using PEX as a **bridge** to buy time for plasma to stabilize, diuresis to return and lab parameters to trend favorably.

## Practical Decision-Making: Who Gets PEX and When?

| Scenario | Typical PEX Strategy | Complement Blockade |
|----------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Severe MAHA + shock + high LDH | 1–3 exchanges early, monitor response | Start eculizumab ASAP if cultures clear |
| Refractory MAHA after 5 days of antibiotics | 2–3 more exchanges | Move to eculizumab |
| Confirmed TTP (ADAMTS13 < 10 %) | Immediate PEX 1–2× daily until platelets > 150 × 10³/µL | Standard (PEX ± steroids) |
| Infection‑triggered CM‑HUS without known complement defect | 3 exchanges is worth trying if refractory or rapidly deteriorating | Consider if no response to PEX or if ongoing hemolysis/renal failure |

**Crucial consideration:** If the patient is septic and cultures are still positive, PEX alone may not be safe. Complement blockade is contraindicated until the infection is controlled.

## The Balance of Timing: Bridging to Eculizumab or Stand‑Alone?

The case demonstrates that after 3 exchanges the anuria resolved, but hemolysis and thrombocytopenia persisted. Only after eculizumab induction did full hematologic remission occur, with partial renal recovery. This suggests:

1. **PEX can temporarily halt the downstream cascade** (removing soluble C5b‑9, inflammatory cytokines), allowing the kidneys to recover some function.
2. **Complement blockade is the only therapy that modifies the underlying driver**—the uncontrolled alternative pathway—giving sustained remission.
3. **Sequencing matters**: Starting eculizumab when cultures are negative is safest; PEX can be used either simultaneously or as bridge if there is a delay in drug availability.

## Lessons from the Case and the Literature

* **Never dismiss sepsis‑related hemolysis as “just DIC.”** In patients with sudden hemoglobin drop and schistocytes, CM‑HUS should be on the differential, even if standard complement levels appear normal.
* **PEX may restore diuresis** in infection‑triggered CM‑HUS, but it does **not** obviate the need for complement blockade if hemolysis or thrombocytopenia remains.
* **Therapy must be individualized**: In immunocompromised or highly infectious settings, delaying eculizumab until cultures are negative and ensuring vaccinations and prophylactic antibiotics are in place are mandatory.
* **Early recognition and prompt eculizumab initiation** improve renal function and reduce dialysis dependence. In the literature, delayed eculizumab is associated with irreversible kidney damage.
* **Genetic testing for complement mutations** can guide long‑term therapy duration. In the present case, no pathogenic variant was found, yet the clinical phenotype steered a 4‑month maintenance course of eculizumab due to the severity of disease.

## Take‑Home Pearls

- **PEX in CM‑HUS is a bridge, not a definitive treatment; use it to buy time while awaiting eculizumab** or if the infection is still uncontrolled.
- **Monitor closely for persistent hemolysis or thrombocytopenia after PEX; if it persists, start eculizumab immediately once cultures are negative**.
- **Vaccinate and provide prophylactic antibiotics before starting complement blockade**, especially with a history of infection by encapsulated organisms (*C. canimorsus*, *Neisseria*).
- **Consider a 2‑step approach**: 2–3 PEX sessions to stabilize the patient, then eculizumab induction with 4 weekly doses followed by maintenance at 2‑week intervals.
- **Regularly re‑assess complement function even after therapy initiation**; functional tests (AH50, C5b‑9) can guide exposure to complement inhibition and highlight ongoing activation.

In sum, plasma exchange may play a useful, albeit limited, role in the management of CM‑HUS when triggered by an infection such as *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. It can temporarily dampen endothelial injury and allow renal perfusion to rebound, but true, lasting remission hinges on identifying the underlying complement dysregulation and instituting targeted complement blockade. The true art lies in deciding *when* to stop the “temporary help” and commit to the definitive therapy.
