Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# When Genomics Fails the Tests: Understanding the Hidden ‘Second Hit’ in CM‑HUS

## The First Line of Inquiry: A Dog Bite, a Sepsis Storm, and a Stranger in the Lab  
Picture this: a 48‑year‑old woman waltzes into the ED with a dog bite on her upper lip, a temperature over 40 °C, and a surprisingly low glucose that won’t budge no matter how many boluses of IV sugar she gets. Within hours, her labs read like a checklist of organ failure – sepsis, DIC, leukopenia, and a creatinine in the 300 mM range. The culprit? Capnocytophaga canimorsus, a fastidious gram‑negative rod that takes days to grow on blood agar but, in the right hands, can be caught by real‑time nanopore sequencing.

Her course is hair‑line: septic shock, complete anuria, dialysis dependence, and—most intriguingly—sudden drop in hemoglobin paired with schistocytes. A picture of a microangiopathic hemolytic anemia that couldn’t be explained by DIC alone. The work‑up ruled out TTP (ADAMTS13 43 %, PLASMIC score 4), STEC (PCR negative), and autoimmune disease (ANA/ANCA negative). A renal biopsy confirmed TMA: thrombi in glomerular vessels, crescents, and acute tubular necrosis. Her narrative fit the textbook story of complement‑mediated HUS.

But the genetic panel whispered a different tale: no pathogenic variants in CFH, CFI, CD46, or other complement regulators. No anti‑factor H antibody. Complement levels (C3, C4) were within normal limits, functional assays were “normal” in the acute phase, and an extended assay of the alternative pathway performed after PEX and one dose of eculizumab showed only a reduced AH50 (~6 %). The message? There was no obvious inherited defect.

This was the head‑turning moment of the case: a patient with clinically definitive CM‑HUS, no identifiable genetic pre‑disposition, and a trigger in the form of a rare dog‑bite pathogen. The lesson? Genomics is a useful roadmap, but the genome does not always show the terrain you’re walking on.

## Why “No Mutation” Doesn’t Mean “No Problem”  
CM‑HUS traditionally is framed as a “genetic plus trigger” story. Mutations in regulators of the alternative complement pathway (CFH, CFI, CD46, CFB, and more) set the stage. Then, an external insult—usually an infection, pregnancy, or medication—turns the stage on fire. In the literature, 50–70 % of patients have a known genetic hit; the remainder are believed to carry unrecognized variants (intronic or regulatory) or to have acquired dysregulation of complement (antibodies, auto‑activation). Our patient represents the latter: an infectious “second hit” that unmasked a latent, functional complement vulnerability.

The dog bite pathogen itself is not innocently catapulted into the mix. Capnocytophaga canimorsus can activate the alternative complement pathway robustly. Some strains possess a capsule that confers partial evasion of complement; however, the host’s immune system still relies on complement for clearance. In a person whose complement regulation is subtly off (even if no mutation is caught), the hyperactivation triggered by the bacteria can spill onto the vasculature, leading to TMA.

One other report (Bjørkto et al.) described a patient with a heterozygous factor B variant who developed CM‑HUS after canine infection and responded happily to eculizumab. Our patient, in contrast, had no detectable variant. This highlights that a negative panel simply tells you “we didn’t find it” — it does not prove the complement system is intact in every possible way.

## The Classic “Diagnostic Gap” – Functional Tests vs. Genomics  
Genetic testing tends to focus on known coding mutations, but the pathobiology may lie elsewhere:

| **Diagnostic tool** | **What it tells** | **What it misses** |
|----------------------|-------------------|--------------------|
| Standard full panel (CFH, CFI, CD46, ADAMTS13, etc.) | Coding mutations, splicing defects | Non‑coding variants, epigenetics, deep intronic changes |
| Complement levels (C3, C4) | Overall consumption/repletion | Functional activity dynamics |
| Functional assays (AH50, sC5b‑9) | Global pathway activity | Pre‑activation states, dynamics post‑therapy |

In our patient, complement levels remained “normal” during the acute phase. Yet the alternative pathway activity (AH50) was dramatically low after eculizumab, reflecting therapy rather than underlying defect. The key point: a functioning complement system can still have a vulnerability that is revealed only when an external pathogen invades.

## How to Tackle the “Second Hit” – A Practical Workflow  
1. **Start with the basics.** Exclude TTP (ADAMTS13 < 10 %), STEC (Stx PCR), and autoimmune causes.  
2. **Consider the clinical context.** In a patient with recent infection, especially with an uncommon bacterium, a CM‑HUS picture should be kept on the radar.  
3. **Leverage functional complement testing early.** AH50, sC5b‑9, and C5b‑9, even in the setting of sepsis, can provide clues even when levels are normal.  
4. **Do not let a negative genetics panel lull you into complacency.** Proceed with eculizumab if clinical remission is otherwise uncertain or the course is escalating.  
5. **Initiate therapy after infection clears.** The risk of meningococcal (or Capnocytophaga) infection is amplified when complement is blocked. In this case, cultures were negative before therapy began.  

### Why Plasma Exchange?  
PEX is a mainstay in TTP, but for CM‑HUS it only offers a placebo‑like, temporary removal of complement fragments and inflammatory mediators. In the dog‑bite case, PEX helped restore diuresis, but complete recovery needed eculizumab.

### Vaccination and Prophylaxis  
The patient received Menveo and Bexsero before eculizumab induction. We continued penicillin prophylaxis until vaccination status was confirmed. Monitoring for meningococcal or other encapsulated infections remains essential while the patient is on eculizumab.

## When the Disease Ends?  
The dog‑bite CM‑HUS patient responded to a 4‑week induction of eculizumab (900 mg weekly) followed by 4 months of maintenance (1200 mg q 2 weeks). The decision to stop was hinged on:

- Clinical remission (platelets, hemoglobin, LDH, kidney function)  
- Normalization of kidney biopsy (no crescents/trabecular thrombi)  
- Extended complement genetic testing returning no pathogenic allele  

Because she had no underlying genetic driver, the institutional policy was to taper after 4 months with a plan for lifelong safety netting rather than indefinite therapy. However, the team kept a low threshold for restarting if relapse signs emerged (e.g., schistocytes, rising LDH, platelets falling).

## Family Counseling – The Genetics‑Free Discussion  
When a patient’s CM‑HUS is “infectious” and genetics come up empty, the take‑home message for relatives is still one of vigilance:

1. **No known inheritability**, but this does not preclude a rare variant we have not powered to detect.  
2. **Encourage family members to inform their clinicians if they develop a severe infection** (e.g., dog bite, sepsis) that is hard to manage.  
3. **Discuss the small but real risk of relapse**, especially if they have an episode that triggers complement.  
4. **Potential future genetic testing** may become more sensitive (whole‑genome, deep‑sequencing). They should keep the option open, especially if they consider pregnancy or eculizumab usage.

In summary, a negative panel should not give the illusion of safety – it is a cautionary flag to keep a patient in the diagnostic loop for CM‑HUS, independent of genetic data.

## Key Take‑Home Messages  
- **CM‑HUS can emerge in the absence of detectable complement gene mutations; an infectious “second hit” can unmask latent dysregulation.**  
- **Normal complement levels and “negative” genetic panels do not exclude CM‑HUS; functional assays and a high index of suspicion are required.**  
- **Eculizumab remains a lifesaving option even when genetics are negative—as long as infection has cleared and vaccination/prophylaxis are in place.**  
- **Family counseling should highlight that inherited risk may be minimal, but vigilance for relapse after infection is warranted.**  
- **Decision to stop eculizumab after remission should be individualized, incorporating clinical remission, functional complement rebound, and the absence of known pathogenic variants.**
