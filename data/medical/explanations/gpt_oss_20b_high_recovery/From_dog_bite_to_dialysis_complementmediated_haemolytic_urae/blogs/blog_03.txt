Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# CM‑HUS or TTP? A Practical Algorithm for the Acute Adult TMA  

## The case that got us talking  

A previously fit woman in her late 40s was bitten by her dog, failed to start the prophylactic penicillin she was dispensed, and four days later presented with high fever, hypoglycaemia, and mottled extremities. On arrival her labs were profound: pancytopenia with a platelet count of 32 × 10⁹/L, anuric acute kidney injury, coagulopathy fitting DIC, and a rising lactate.  

Broad‑spectrum antibiotics were started, and capnocytophaga spp. were suspected—indeed, cultures grew **Capnocytophaga canimorsus** after 7 days. The patient’s hemodynamics stabilized, but she became anuric, required continuous renal replacement therapy, and her skin developed ecchymoses and distal cyanosis suggestive of microthrombi. A week into admission a sudden, isolated drop in haemoglobin, persistent thrombocytopenia, and rising LDH revealed a microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA).  

Peripheral smear showed 20 % schistocytes, haptoglobin was undetectable, and a direct antiglobulin test was positive for IgG (later repeated as negative). ADAMTS13 activity happened to be 43.5 % (normal > 10 %, TTP < 10 %), steering us away from thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). A kidney biopsy demonstrated ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and crescent formation—classic T‑cell–mediated microangiopathy. Complement panels were unremarkable (normal C3/C4, alternative pathway activity 6 % after plasma exchange).

In short, this patient had **capnocytophaga–triggered, complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS)**, not TTP. She received plasma exchange first to clear inflammatory mediators, then started eculizumab once blood cultures cleared, with full haematological remission and partial renal recovery.  

Her dramatic course— sepsis and DIC masquerading as TMA, a later abrupt rise in LDH, a normal ADAMTS13, and a kidney biopsy confirming TMA—offers a perfect framework to outline a pragmatic algorithm for distinguishing TTP from CM‑HUS in the emergency setting.

## Untangling TMA: TTP vs CM‑HUS  

Both disorders present with **MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury**, so the differential hinges on subtle differences in underlying pathophysiology and lab data:

| Feature | TTP | CM‑HUS |
|---------|-----|--------|
| **Key driver** | Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10 %) → giant vWF multimers | Uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway |
| **Platelet count** | Often <30 × 10⁹/L | Variable, may be higher |
| **Coagulation tests** | Normal PT/aPTT | Usually normal (DIC may coexist in infection) |
| **Complement levels (C3/C4)** | Normal | Often normal, may be low but not diagnostic |
| **Triggers** | Drugs, pregnancy, autoimmune | Infections, pregnancy, drugs, autoimmunity |
| **Response to PEX** | Rapid, lifesaving | Transient, incomplete |
| **Preferred therapy** | PEX + steroids | Complement blockade (eculizumab or ravulizumab) |

The challenge is that **sepsis can produce DIC, pancytopenia, and MAHA**, making the early presentation identical to TTP. That is why a structured, evidence‑based algorithm is essential.

## Practical Algorithm for Adult Acute TMA

Below is a step‑by‑step guide based on the case experience and current guidelines. Think of it as your “TMA flow‐chart” when the door to the ICU opens.

### 1. Immediate bedside work‑up  
- CBC, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, reticulocytes  
- Peripheral smear for schistocytes  
- Coagulation panel (PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer)  
- Basic metabolic panel (renal function, electrolytes)  

**If** schistocytes > 5 % **or** LDH significantly ↑ **→** proceed to step 2.

### 2. Calculate the PLASMIC score  
| Criterion | Points |
|-----------|--------|
| Platelets < 30 × 10⁹/L | 1 |
| Hemolysis (high LDH, low haptoglobin) | 1 |
| No active cancer | 1 |
| No transplant history | 1 |
| INR < 1.5 | 1 |
| Sequestration? (no) | 0 |
| Creatinine < 2 mg/dL | 1 |
| – | – |

**Interpretation**  
- **High (≥ 6)** → **TTP likely** → *start plasma exchange immediately* (before definitive ADAMTS13 results).  
- **Intermediate (4–5)** → **Possible TTP** → *start plasma exchange empirically and send ADAMTS13*; if results <10 %, continue, if >10 % and improving, consider stopping.  
- **Low (≤ 3)** → **TTP improbable** → *focus on alternative diagnoses* (CM‑HUS, DIC, STEC‑HUS).

_In our case the PLASMIC was 4, pushing us into the “consider TTP” arm but with little evidence of a very low ADAMTS13, prompting aggressive diagnostics for CM‑HUS._

### 3. Immediate bedside interventions for all suspected TMAs  
- **Plasma exchange** (if PLASMIC high or intermediate) **OR** **start eculizumab** (if clinical suspicion for CM‑HUS high and infection not overt).  
- **High‑dose steroids** 1 mg/kg IV (as in TTP or severe auto‑immune hemolytic anemia).  
- **Antibiotics** if infection suspected or confirmed.  
- **Supportive care**: transfuse platelets, RBCs, fibrinogen, manage shock, renal replacement as needed.

### 4. Send definitive labs immediately  
- **ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor testing** (serum) – report typically after 1–2 days.  
- **Full complement panel**: C3, C4, functional assays (AH50, AP50), flow‑cytometry for CD46, CD55, CD59; factor H, I levels, antifactor H antibodies.  
- **Stool Shiga‑toxin PCR** (if gastrointestinal prodrome).  
- **Kidney biopsy** (if creatinine > 3 mg/dL or AKI persists > 48 h) to confirm microvascular injury.

### 5. Decision tree after lab results  

| Result | Action |
|--------|--------|
| ADAMTS13 < 10 % | **TTP** – continue PEX + steroids; consider caplacizumab. |
| ADAMTS13 10–30 % (borderline) | Re‑assess trend; if other features favour CM‑HUS, proceed to complement blockade. |
| ADAMTS13 > 30 % | **TTP unlikely** – focus on CM‑HUS. |
| Complement abnormalities (low AH50, factor H mutation, etc.) | **CM‑HUS** – start eculizumab (or ravulizumab) promptly. |
| Negative for complement dysregulation and ADAMTS13 normal | **Secondary TMA** (sepsis/DIC/other) – treat underlying cause, usually stop PEX. |
| Positive Shiga‑toxin PCR | **STEC‑HUS** – supportive care; no eculizumab. |

### 6. Treatment timeline in practice  

| Day | Intervention | Rationale |
|-----|--------------|-----------|
| 0‑1 | Broad‑spectrum antibiotics, IV fluids, blood products | Address sepsis/DIC |
| 0‑2 | Start plasma exchange (if PLASMIC 4–6) | Remove auto‑antibodies and soluble mediators |
| 2‑3 | Send ADAMTS13, complement, KDIGO labs | Narrow diagnosis |
| 3‑4 | If ADAMTS13 > 10 % and complement panel abnormal or kidney biopsy HUS, give **eculizumab induction** | Target terminal complement |
| 4‑8 | Continue eculizumab; complete plasma exchange series (2–3 sessions) | Stabilize hemolysis, platelets, kidney function |
| 8‑12 | Assess renal recovery, hemograms, repeat complement | Decide on maintenance eculizumab or taper |

In the case presented, the patient’s **ADAMTS13 was 43 %**, complement levels normal, but a kidney biopsy proved TMA, so she was started on **eculizumab after 3 plasma exchanges**. Her haematology responded, and partial renal recovery ensued.

## Key Take‑Home Points  

- **Don’t wait for ADAMTS13**: when the PLASMIC score is intermediate, begin plasma exchange while awaiting results—time is kidney life.  
- **ADAMTS13 > 10 % excludes TTP** and steers you toward CM‑HUS or secondary TMA.  
- **Complement panels aren’t definitive**: normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM‑HUS; functional assays (AH50) may help but can be altered by PEX or complement inhibitor therapy.  
- **Infection‑triggered CM‑HUS** may occur even in patients without known complement gene mutations (as shown in capnocytophaga bites).  
- **Start eculizumab early in CM‑HUS**; delayed therapy is associated with greater residual renal damage.  
- **Vaccinate and provide prophylaxis before complement blockade** if residual infection risk exists (e.g., meningococcal or encapsulated bacteria).  
- **Kidney biopsy can be decisive** if AKI persists and other causes have been excluded—histology can confirm TMA and rule out other glomerulopathies.  
- **Never stop PEX when data is uncertain**: a brief course can suffice to stabilize the patient while definitive treatment begins.  
- **Use a structured algorithm** rather than gestalt: this reduces diagnostic latency in the ICU and improves outcomes.

## A brief FOAM‑style reflection  

When a patient comes in with sepsis, DIC, and suddenly a drop in hemoglobin, the instinct is to think TTP. In reality, the immune system can blaze an even more destructive fire in the form of complement‑mediated HUS. The dog‑bite case reminds us that:

- **Infections can misuse the complement system** just as they can trigger TTP.  
- **Timing matters**: sepsis may mask the first signs of TMA; it may also hide a shift from DIC to true microangiopathy.  
- **There’s no silver bullet**—plasma exchange is lifesaving for TTP but only temporarily helpful for CM‑HUS.  
- **Layered diagnostics** (PLASMIC, ADAMTS13, complement, biopsy) provide a safety net against errors.
