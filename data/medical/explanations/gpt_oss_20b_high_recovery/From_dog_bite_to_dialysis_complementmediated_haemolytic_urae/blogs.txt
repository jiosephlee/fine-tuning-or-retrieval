Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# From Bark to Blood: Spotting Capnocytophaga canimorsus in the ED

## The Unexpected Sepsis After a Dog Bite
A healthy woman in her late 40s came to the ED four days after her dog bit her on the upper lip. She received a tetanus booster and an oral prescription for phenoxymethylpenicillin, but never filled it. She was febrile (> 40 °C), hypoglycaemic, and complained of numbness and discoloration in her feet. By the time she reached the ED, she was septic, mottled, and had evidence of lactic acidosis, leukopenia, and DIC‑like coagulopathy. How did a seemingly minor household injury evolve into a life‑threatening infection that eventually required dialysis, plasma exchange, and eculizumab?

## DIC, Pancytopenia, and the Sepsis Work‑up
The triad of fever, marked inflammation, pancytopenia, and a bleeding tendency made septic DIC the most likely diagnosis on arrival. The differential remained broad: toxic shock, sepsis‑induced DIC, hemophagocytic syndrome, and, on the rarer end, a TMA. Broad‑spectrum piperacillin‑tazobactam was immediately started to cover gram‑negative rods frequently seen after dog bites, including Capnocytophaga canimorsus. Blood cultures later confirmed the culprit on day 7, underscoring the organism’s slow‑growing, fastidious nature.

The key point here is that early empiric coverage against C. canimorsus can change the trajectory when the identification of the pathogen takes days. Delayed therapy can let the infection spiral into multiorgan failure.

## The Heptomized Rise of Microangiopathic Hemolysis
After a period of sepsis control, the patient’s kidneys failed—she became anuric and required continuous renal replacement therapy. Eight days into admission she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin (111 → 68 g/L) with no bleeding source, and platelet counts remained low (≈ 30–40 × 10⁹/L). Haptoglobin was undetectable, LDH was markedly elevated, and a peripheral smear showed 15–20 % schistocytes. These findings signaled microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) superimposed on the sepsis‑induced DIC.

A high‑dose steroid trial was attempted after a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT), but the picture worsened. Repeat DAT was negative, and the ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5 %, ruling out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). Strep‑toxin‑producing E. coli HUS was excluded with a negative stool PCR. The remaining diagnosis—complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS)—was confirmed by renal biopsy showing tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and crescents consistent with TMA.

## Diagnosing CM‑HUS in the Setting of Active Sepsis
CM‑HUS is a clinic‑pathologic diagnosis of exclusion. Normal C3/C4 levels do not rule it out, because complement consumption may still be ongoing. In this patient, functional complement assays (alternative pathway activity) were markedly reduced (6 %) after plasma exchange and eculizumab induction, but the absence of pathogenic complement gene variants and anti‑factor H antibodies suggested an infection‑triggered episode rather than a heritable complement dysregulation.

The diagnostic “aha” moment was that the hemolysis appeared after improvement of septic parameters—a classic “second hit” pattern: the underlying pathogen (C. canimorsus) initiates complement activation, and the subsequent host response culminates in a TMA.

## Management – The Interplay of Antimicrobials, PEX, and Eculizumab
Once the diagnosis of CM‑HUS was entertained, a triple‑line attack began:

1. **Antimicrobials** – Piperacillin‑tazobactam was continued, then narrowed to intravenous ampicillin, and later switched to oral phenoxymethylpenicillin once cultures were clean. Until the blood cultures were negative, complement blockade was deferred to avoid compromising host defense against this encapsulated pathogen.

2. **Plasma Exchange (PEX)** – Bridging therapy to remove circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators. After three sessions, diuresis returned, but platelet counts remained sub‑optimal without definitive complement inhibition.

3. **Eculizumab** – Initiated once cultures cleared, after meningococcal vaccination and penicillin prophylaxis. A 4‑week induction schedule (900 mg weekly) was followed by maintenance dosing (1200 mg biweekly). Renal function partially recovered (creatinine 175 µmol/L), and hematologic remission was maintained.

The choice of eculizumab over other complement inhibitors was guided by the need for rapid, sustained blockade of the terminal pathway to halt endothelial injury.

## Why Capnocytophaga Should Be on Every Dog‑Bite Differential
- **Fastidious Growth** – Cultures may take up to a week; therefore, empiric coverage should not be withheld.
- **Sepsis Spectrum** – Can cause fulminant sepsis, DIC, multiorgan failure, and the rarer TMA.
- **Trigger of CM‑HUS** – In genetically unremarkable patients, C. canimorsus can act as the precipitating “second hit” for complement‑driven microangiopathy.
- **Empiric Regimens** – Piperacillin‑tazobactam or ampicillin‑sulbactam are preferred for dog‑bite wounds.

Keeping C. canimorsus in mind can prompt earlier therapy, reduce time to culture confirmation, and, crucially, allow timely initiation of complement blockade when TMA emerges.

## Clinical Pearls
- A dog bite with a **rapidly progressive fever, leukopenia, and coagulopathy** should raise suspicion for C. canimorsus.
- **Empiric coverage** with agents active against fastidious gram‑negative rods is essential; hold off on complement inhibition until cultures are negative.
- **Serum complement levels (C3/C4)** can be normal during CM‑HUS; rely on functional assays and ADAMTS13 activity for diagnosis.
- **Pneumococcal vaccination** and penicillin prophylaxis are mandatory before starting eculizumab due to the organism’s encapsulated nature.
- **Early eculizumab** (ideally within 7–10 days of TMA onset) improves renal and hematologic outcomes, even after a prolonged septic phase.

## Take‑Home Points
- **Recognize** the red‑flag pattern: dog bite → sepsis → DIC → sudden hemolysis with schistocytes that reappears after initial improvement.
- **Exclude** TTP and STEC‑HUS before diagnosing CM‑HUS; normal classical complement levels do not rule out the disease.
- **Treat early**: de‑escalate antibiotics only after cultures clear, then proceed with plasma exchange and prompt eculizumab initiation.
- **Monitor** renal function to decide on the duration of complement blockade; genetic testing can inform long‑term therapy decisions.
- **Educate** patients about the rare, but life‑threatening potential of a simple pet bite and the importance of completing antibiotic prophylaxis.

The trajectory from a simple dog bite to dialysis‑dependent kidney injury underscores the critical role of early recognition and aggressive, multidisciplinary management. In the era of complement‑targeted therapies, being vigilant for CM‑HUS in sepsis, especially when the source is an unusual gram‑negative rod, can truly change a patient’s outcome.



# Between DIC and TMA: How Sepsis‑Triggered Coagulopathy Can Hide Microvascular Thrombosis

## Introduction  
A healthy woman in her late 40s presents to the ED four days after a dog bite. She is septic, her labs scream DIC, and she is anuric with impending multi‑organ failure. Antibiotics are started, vasopressors are added, and the picture improves – or so she and the team think. Weeks later, her hemoglobin plunges by 40 g/L, the platelet count stagnates near 30 × 10⁹/L, and a peripheral smear shows a striking 20 % schistocyte burden. The clinical mystery: was this merely a late consequence of sepsis or the harbinger of a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) that had begun in the shadows of DIC?

## DIC vs TMA: The clinical overlap  
Both DIC and TMA consume platelets and cause organ dysfunction, but the lab patterns diverge in subtle ways that can easily be buried by the acute sepsis backdrop. In classic DIC you see a profound prolongation of PT/INR and aPTT, a severe fall in fibrinogen, an explosive rise in D‑dimer, and a moderate drop in platelets. In TMA the coagulation times are usually preserved, fibrinogen may be normal or only slightly low, and the hallmark is microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) – high LDH, low haptoglobin, and a reticulocytosis that often lags behind the drop in hemoglobin. Crystallizing this distinction can be painfully difficult when a septic patient is on broad‑spectrum antibiotics and massive fluid resuscitation.

## The peripheral smear: the unsung hero  
The peripheral smear deserves a standing ovation. Even in the era of advanced coagulation panels, a smear’s visual read can be decisive. In this case, the 20 % schistocyte load – which would be dismissed as a random finding in a comatose septic patient – turned the diagnosis of TMA on its head. A smear also flags the presence of other clues such as spherocytes (suggesting autoimmune hemolysis) or Jackson–Lee lesions (artifact), thereby preventing unnecessary immunosuppression.

## Diagnostic cascade: choreographing the search  
When a septic patient shows unexplained anemia and thrombocytopenia after a DIC episode, the work‑up follows a concise choreography:

1. **Rule out TTP** – An ADAMTS13 activity <10 % is diagnostic. The PLASMIC score, while helpful, must be corroborated with a functional assay. In our patient the activity was 43 %, eliminating TTP.

2. **Exclude STEC‑HUS** – A stool PCR for Shiga toxin is fast and near‑certain; a negative result shifts the differential toward a complement‑mediated process.

3. **Complement work‑up** – Traditional C3/C4 may remain within reference ranges during an acute flare; functional assays such as AH50 better capture alternative‑pathway hyperactivity.

4. **Renal biopsy** – When the patient requires dialysis and the microangiopathy is clinically suspicious, a biopsy that demonstrates fibrin thrombi and crescents clinches the CM‑HUS diagnosis.

5. **Bridging plasma exchange** – While awaiting ADAMTS13 and complement results, plasma exchange removes circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, and potential autoantibodies.

Only after this cascade did the team feel confident to start eculizumab, the terminal complement inhibitor that has become the cornerstone of CM‑HUS therapy.

## Timing eculizumab: why minutes matter  
Eculizumab’s effectiveness in CM‑HUS is tightly linked to the time between clinical presentation and initiation of therapy. Each day’s delay has been shown to correlate with poorer renal recovery. In the case at hand, the first eculizumab infusion was delivered only after the blood cultures had turned negative, thereby balancing the risk of a fatal infection against the need for prompt complement blockade. The patient’s renal function began to recover after the second dose, and her hemolysis resolved quickly thereafter.

## Balancing infection risk vs. complement blockade  
Terminal complement inhibition is a double‑edge sword: it gives a patient (especially an immunocompetent host) a window into the septic bite’s pathogen (Capnocytophaga, a Class A capsule‑bearing bacterium). The standard of care is to provide meningococcal vaccination (Menveo® and Bexsero®) and broad‑spectrum antibiotics while on eculizumab. In this patient, the antibiotics were remained on piperacillin‑tazobactam until **Day 8** when cultures cleared, at which point eculizumab was started. The lesson? Vaccinate, pro‑state, and treat – no compromise on either front.

## Key take‑home messages  
- A sudden drop in hemoglobin and a high schistocyte count in a septic patient with resolved DIC should prompt an immediate TMA work‑up.  
- Normal PT, aPTT, fibrinogen and a negative ADAMTS13 do **not** rule out CM‑HUS.  
- Complement functional assays and renal biopsy are often required to identify infection‑triggered CM‑HUS.  
- Early eculizumab initiation is crucial for preserving renal function; start after cultures are cleared or documented bacteria are controlled.  
- Vaccinate against encapsulated bacteria and maintain adequate antibiotics when using terminal complement inhibitors.



# CM‑HUS or TTP? A Practical Algorithm for the Acute Adult TMA  

## The case that got us talking  

A previously fit woman in her late 40s was bitten by her dog, failed to start the prophylactic penicillin she was dispensed, and four days later presented with high fever, hypoglycaemia, and mottled extremities. On arrival her labs were profound: pancytopenia with a platelet count of 32 × 10⁹/L, anuric acute kidney injury, coagulopathy fitting DIC, and a rising lactate.  

Broad‑spectrum antibiotics were started, and capnocytophaga spp. were suspected—indeed, cultures grew **Capnocytophaga canimorsus** after 7 days. The patient’s hemodynamics stabilized, but she became anuric, required continuous renal replacement therapy, and her skin developed ecchymoses and distal cyanosis suggestive of microthrombi. A week into admission a sudden, isolated drop in haemoglobin, persistent thrombocytopenia, and rising LDH revealed a microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA).  

Peripheral smear showed 20 % schistocytes, haptoglobin was undetectable, and a direct antiglobulin test was positive for IgG (later repeated as negative). ADAMTS13 activity happened to be 43.5 % (normal > 10 %, TTP < 10 %), steering us away from thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). A kidney biopsy demonstrated ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and crescent formation—classic T‑cell–mediated microangiopathy. Complement panels were unremarkable (normal C3/C4, alternative pathway activity 6 % after plasma exchange).

In short, this patient had **capnocytophaga–triggered, complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS)**, not TTP. She received plasma exchange first to clear inflammatory mediators, then started eculizumab once blood cultures cleared, with full haematological remission and partial renal recovery.  

Her dramatic course— sepsis and DIC masquerading as TMA, a later abrupt rise in LDH, a normal ADAMTS13, and a kidney biopsy confirming TMA—offers a perfect framework to outline a pragmatic algorithm for distinguishing TTP from CM‑HUS in the emergency setting.

---

## Untangling TMA: TTP vs CM‑HUS  

Both disorders present with **MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury**, so the differential hinges on subtle differences in underlying pathophysiology and lab data:

| Feature | TTP | CM‑HUS |
|---------|-----|--------|
| **Key driver** | Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10 %) → giant vWF multimers | Uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway |
| **Platelet count** | Often <30 × 10⁹/L | Variable, may be higher |
| **Coagulation tests** | Normal PT/aPTT | Usually normal (DIC may coexist in infection) |
| **Complement levels (C3/C4)** | Normal | Often normal, may be low but not diagnostic |
| **Triggers** | Drugs, pregnancy, autoimmune | Infections, pregnancy, drugs, autoimmunity |
| **Response to PEX** | Rapid, lifesaving | Transient, incomplete |
| **Preferred therapy** | PEX + steroids | Complement blockade (eculizumab or ravulizumab) |

The challenge is that **sepsis can produce DIC, pancytopenia, and MAHA**, making the early presentation identical to TTP. That is why a structured, evidence‑based algorithm is essential.

---

## Practical Algorithm for Adult Acute TMA

Below is a step‑by‑step guide based on the case experience and current guidelines. Think of it as your “TMA flow‐chart” when the door to the ICU opens.

### 1. Immediate bedside work‑up  
- CBC, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, reticulocytes  
- Peripheral smear for schistocytes  
- Coagulation panel (PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer)  
- Basic metabolic panel (renal function, electrolytes)  

**If** schistocytes > 5 % **or** LDH significantly ↑ **→** proceed to step 2.

### 2. Calculate the PLASMIC score  
| Criterion | Points |
|-----------|--------|
| Platelets < 30 × 10⁹/L | 1 |
| Hemolysis (high LDH, low haptoglobin) | 1 |
| No active cancer | 1 |
| No transplant history | 1 |
| INR < 1.5 | 1 |
| Sequestration? (no) | 0 |
| Creatinine < 2 mg/dL | 1 |
| – | – |

**Interpretation**  
- **High (≥ 6)** → **TTP likely** → *start plasma exchange immediately* (before definitive ADAMTS13 results).  
- **Intermediate (4–5)** → **Possible TTP** → *start plasma exchange empirically and send ADAMTS13*; if results <10 %, continue, if >10 % and improving, consider stopping.  
- **Low (≤ 3)** → **TTP improbable** → *focus on alternative diagnoses* (CM‑HUS, DIC, STEC‑HUS).

_In our case the PLASMIC was 4, pushing us into the “consider TTP” arm but with little evidence of a very low ADAMTS13, prompting aggressive diagnostics for CM‑HUS._

### 3. Immediate bedside interventions for all suspected TMAs  
- **Plasma exchange** (if PLASMIC high or intermediate) **OR** **start eculizumab** (if clinical suspicion for CM‑HUS high and infection not overt).  
- **High‑dose steroids** 1 mg/kg IV (as in TTP or severe auto‑immune hemolytic anemia).  
- **Antibiotics** if infection suspected or confirmed.  
- **Supportive care**: transfuse platelets, RBCs, fibrinogen, manage shock, renal replacement as needed.

### 4. Send definitive labs immediately  
- **ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor testing** (serum) – report typically after 1–2 days.  
- **Full complement panel**: C3, C4, functional assays (AH50, AP50), flow‑cytometry for CD46, CD55, CD59; factor H, I levels, antifactor H antibodies.  
- **Stool Shiga‑toxin PCR** (if gastrointestinal prodrome).  
- **Kidney biopsy** (if creatinine > 3 mg/dL or AKI persists > 48 h) to confirm microvascular injury.

### 5. Decision tree after lab results  

| Result | Action |
|--------|--------|
| ADAMTS13 < 10 % | **TTP** – continue PEX + steroids; consider caplacizumab. |
| ADAMTS13 10–30 % (borderline) | Re‑assess trend; if other features favour CM‑HUS, proceed to complement blockade. |
| ADAMTS13 > 30 % | **TTP unlikely** – focus on CM‑HUS. |
| Complement abnormalities (low AH50, factor H mutation, etc.) | **CM‑HUS** – start eculizumab (or ravulizumab) promptly. |
| Negative for complement dysregulation and ADAMTS13 normal | **Secondary TMA** (sepsis/DIC/other) – treat underlying cause, usually stop PEX. |
| Positive Shiga‑toxin PCR | **STEC‑HUS** – supportive care; no eculizumab. |

### 6. Treatment timeline in practice  

| Day | Intervention | Rationale |
|-----|--------------|-----------|
| 0‑1 | Broad‑spectrum antibiotics, IV fluids, blood products | Address sepsis/DIC |
| 0‑2 | Start plasma exchange (if PLASMIC 4–6) | Remove auto‑antibodies and soluble mediators |
| 2‑3 | Send ADAMTS13, complement, KDIGO labs | Narrow diagnosis |
| 3‑4 | If ADAMTS13 > 10 % and complement panel abnormal or kidney biopsy HUS, give **eculizumab induction** | Target terminal complement |
| 4‑8 | Continue eculizumab; complete plasma exchange series (2–3 sessions) | Stabilize hemolysis, platelets, kidney function |
| 8‑12 | Assess renal recovery, hemograms, repeat complement | Decide on maintenance eculizumab or taper |

In the case presented, the patient’s **ADAMTS13 was 43 %**, complement levels normal, but a kidney biopsy proved TMA, so she was started on **eculizumab after 3 plasma exchanges**. Her haematology responded, and partial renal recovery ensued.

---

## Key Take‑Home Points  

- **Don’t wait for ADAMTS13**: when the PLASMIC score is intermediate, begin plasma exchange while awaiting results—time is kidney life.  
- **ADAMTS13 > 10 % excludes TTP** and steers you toward CM‑HUS or secondary TMA.  
- **Complement panels aren’t definitive**: normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM‑HUS; functional assays (AH50) may help but can be altered by PEX or complement inhibitor therapy.  
- **Infection‑triggered CM‑HUS** may occur even in patients without known complement gene mutations (as shown in capnocytophaga bites).  
- **Start eculizumab early in CM‑HUS**; delayed therapy is associated with greater residual renal damage.  
- **Vaccinate and provide prophylaxis before complement blockade** if residual infection risk exists (e.g., meningococcal or encapsulated bacteria).  
- **Kidney biopsy can be decisive** if AKI persists and other causes have been excluded—histology can confirm TMA and rule out other glomerulopathies.  
- **Never stop PEX when data is uncertain**: a brief course can suffice to stabilize the patient while definitive treatment begins.  
- **Use a structured algorithm** rather than gestalt: this reduces diagnostic latency in the ICU and improves outcomes.

---

## A brief FOAM‑style reflection  

When a patient comes in with sepsis, DIC, and suddenly a drop in hemoglobin, the instinct is to think TTP. In reality, the immune system can blaze an even more destructive fire in the form of complement‑mediated HUS. The dog‑bite case reminds us that:

- **Infections can misuse the complement system** just as they can trigger TTP.  
- **Timing matters**: sepsis may mask the first signs of TMA; it may also hide a shift from DIC to true microangiopathy.  
- **There’s no silver bullet**—plasma exchange is lifesaving for TTP but only temporarily helpful for CM‑HUS.  
- **Layered diagnostics** (PLASMIC, ADAMTS13, complement, biopsy) provide a safety net against errors.  

Feel free to share your own “dog‑bite‑to‑HUS” stories or other unusual triggers you’ve seen. Let’s teach ourselves how to keep the brain from crashing faster than the kidneys!



# Complement Inhibition Amid Bacteremia  
## When to Turn the Switch on Eculizumab  

---

### 1. The unexpected twist in a dog‑bite case

A 48‑year‑old woman arrives two days into a septic storm after a dog‑bit bite, already smelling like DIC, pancytopenia and anuria. Four days later her labs start mirroring a microangiopathic hemolysis – a classic HUS curve – but in a patient whose sepsis was *already* improving. The kicker? The culprit was Capnocytophaga *canimorsus*, an organism that hangs around a dog’s throat but can quickly become lethal in humans, and it triggered a complement‑mediated TMA that needed **eculizumab** – a drug that blocks the very arm of immunity the bacterium relies on.

This is a textbook example of the paradox: the same complement cascade that flags a gram‑negative, encapsulated bug is also the engine of a devastating TMA. Our challenge? When to flip the eculizumab switch while the battle against the bacterium is still raging.

---

## 2. Quick facts from the case

| Step | Key Clinical Feature | Lab/Imaging Highlight |
|------|----------------------|------------------------|
| **Day 0 – Bite** | Dog bite, oral Apocillin pending | Pain and hematoma |
| **Day 4 – ED** | Fever >40 °C, hypoglycemia, mottled extremities | ↑CRP, pancytopenia, DIC, AKI |
| **Day 7** | *C. canimorsus* isolated in blood | 100 % |
| **Day 12** | Acute drop in Hb, hepatitis of schistocytes | LDH ↑, haptoglobin <5 mg/dL |
| **Day 14** | ADAMTS13 43 %, negative TTP score | Platelets 30 ×10⁹/L, schistocytes 15–20 % |
| **Day 17** | Kidney biopsy: ATN + TMA | Fibrin thrombi in glomeruli |
| **Day 20** | Cultures negative, vaccinations administered | Menveo & Bexsero |
| **Day 21** | Start eculizumab (900 mg IV weekly) | |
| **Day 30** | Renal function improved, hemolysis resolved | Serum creatinine 175 µmol/L |

*The timeline underscores two truths: (1) Sepsis progressed to TMA after the systemic inflammatory response tapered; (2) Eculizumab was initiated only after the bacterium was eradicated and the patient was immunized.*

---

## 3. Why a complement blockade is life‑saving

- **CM‑HUS** is driven by dysregulated activation of the *alternative* complement pathway.  
- The complement cascade perpetuates endothelial injury, platelet activation, and microthrombi.  
- Early blockade with eculizumab (C5 inhibitor) stops the terminal pathway, halting ongoing hemolysis and preserving renal microvasculature.  
- Evidence consistently links **time‑to‑initiation** with renal recovery. A delay >3 weeks reduces the odds of nephrology independence by up to 30 %.

---

## 4. The risk: weakening the defense against a “friend of the complement”

Eculizumab depletes C5, **eliminating the ability to generate the membrane attack complex (MAC)**. Although the therapy narrows opsonization to surface markers, it leaves patients vulnerable to **encapsulated organisms**: *Neisseria*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*, and, important for our case, *Capnocytophaga*. In a patient with a recent bacteremia, this is no small issue.

- **Mortality risk** with untreated meningococcal infection rises sharply in the first few days of eculizumab therapy.  
- Even with vaccination, breakthrough infections can occur, especially if the vaccine isn’t fully immunogenic in the setting of ongoing infection.

---

## 5. Timing the switch – a real‑world strategy

| Scenario | Ideal Timing | Reasoning |
|----------|--------------|-----------|
| **Culture‑positive bacteremia** | **Delay** until cultures are clean (or near‑complet e.g. <48 h antibiotics with clinical improvement) | Avoids compromising bacterial clearance |
| **Quasi‑sterile cultures** (negative blood but ongoing antimicrobial therapy) | **Shortly after** negative swabs (within 24–48 h) | Balances urgency for TMA control vs infection risk |
| **Pre‑emptive** (high‑risk patient with known complement mutation and pending antibiotics) | **Within 24 h of antimicrobial start,** if cultures likely negative soon | Early control may prevent irreversible kidney injury |

In the case, eculizumab was started **21 days** after the bite, *after* clearance of the organism and *after* full meningococcal vaccination. This 3‑week gap is longer than ideal, but it was driven by the ethical calculus of avoiding catastrophic infection in a patient whose bacteremia was still potentially smoldering.

---

## 6. Vaccination strategy – the “3‑pillars”

1. **Menveo** (group A, C, Y, W)
2. **Bexsero** (serogroup B)
3. **Timing** – give the *first* dose at least **2 weeks before** starting eculizumab.  
   The second dose is optional but recommended approximately **8 weeks later**.  

- **Why 2 weeks?** Consensus guidelines (IDSA & EU) recommend a minimum 2‑week interval to ensure sufficient antibody titers.  
- **Why both vaccines?** Eculizumab dramatically amplifies risk to *all* meningococcal serogroups; coverage must be comprehensive.

In our patient, this vaccination schedule was followed, and the first dose was administered right before eculizumab induction.

---

## 7. Antibiotic prophylaxis – cover the capsule

- **Penicillin V (Apocillin)** was continued throughout the eculizumab course because *C. canimorsus* remains susceptible.  
- If a penicillin allergy is present, consider **azithromycin** or **rifampicin** (the latter used in complement‑inhibitor prophylaxis protocols).  
- Prophylaxis **must** be maintained at least until the antibody response to vaccination is considered adequate (typically 2–4 weeks post‑first dose).

The patient’s prophylactic Apocillin overlapped with eculizumab for 4 months – a duration longer than necessary in the absence of a confirmed infection, but safe given the high stakes.

---

## 8. Real‑world evidence: CM‑HUS triggered by *C. canimorsus*

- **Reviews** report a handful (~10) of *C. canimorsus*‑associated CM‑HUS cases, all treated with eculizumab.  
- Two key patterns emerge:
  1. **No underlying complement mutation** in *most* cases after exhaustive genetic work‑up (i.e., acquired CM‑HUS).  
  2. **Early** eculizumab initiation (median 2–4 days after TMA onset) correlates with partial renal recovery.

The case we’re dissecting illustrates this pattern perfectly: an immunocompetent adult, no pathogenic complement variants, who suffered a septic DIC‑driven shock only to develop a superimposed TMA after antibiotics took hold. The early anthier shock confers a “second hit” that unleashes the complement cascade.

---

## 9. Practical pearls & a quick check‑list

*Before you fire up eculizumab, snapshot the table below:*

- **Culture Status**
  - ❏ Blood cultures *negative* or on therapy with daily clearance?
- **Vaccination**
  - ❏ Menveo & Bexsero, 2 weeks before first dose?
- **Antibiotic Prophylaxis**
  - ❏ Penicillin V (or alternative) ongoing?
- **Complement Markers**
  - ❏ C3/C4 may still be normal → do not rely solely on them.
- **TMA Confirmation**
  - ❏ Schistocytes >10 % **and** LDH ↑, haptoglobin ↓, ADAMTS13 >10 %?  
  - ❏ Confirmed by biopsy if kidney function permits.
- **Timing**
  - ❏ Total delay from TMA onset to eculizumab **ideally <3 weeks**.
  - ❏ If delaying >3 weeks, counsel on higher risk of ESRD.

**Checklist Example:**  
**Day X** – Cultures asterisks (`*`) → **Stop** antibiotics? **Start** eculizumab?  
- **If** `*` + Vaccination + Prophylaxis → Proceed  
- **If** not, hold until all three are satisfied.

---

## 10. Bottom line for the frontline

- **CM‑HUS is often “hidden” beneath sepsis and DIC;‐sep** look for persistent thrombocytopenia, anemia drop, schistocytes, ADAMTS13 >10 % after sepsis improves.  
- **Complement inhibitors are double‑edged**; the same mechanism you’re blocking also prevents bacterial killing.  
- **Timing is a tightrope**: start eculizumab as soon as cultures are negative and vaccination cleared, but not while the bacterium is still brewing.  
- **Vaccination + antibiotic prophylaxis** are non‑negotiable pillars.  
- **Experience matters**: early inpatient eculizumab and close nephrology follow‑up yield the best chance of kidney recovery.

The dog‑bite induced CM‑HUS taught us that *some* infections can “open the door” to complement dysregulation even in the absence of inherited predisposition. As our diagnostics grow faster (real‑time nanopore sequencing, rapid ADAMTS13 assays) and our therapy arsenal expands (new complement inhibitors), the sooner we learn to read the clues early, the earlier we can mount that life‑saving switch.

# Plasma Exchange in CM‑HUS: That Temporary Help or the Core Treatment?

## The Unexpected Twist After a Dog Bite

A healthy woman in her late 40s presents with fever, hypotension and severe pancytopenia four days after a dog bite. Blood cultures grow *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a fastidious, encapsulated Gram‑negative rod that can trigger fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and, in rare cases, thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). In this patient, sepsis improved but a sudden drop in hemoglobin, rising lactate dehydrogenase and persistent thrombocytopenia spark thoughts of a complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). A kidney biopsy confirms microvascular thrombi, and ADAMTS13 activity is comfortably above 10 % ruling out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). The therapeutic question becomes: should we keep pumping plasma or jump straight to complement blockade with eculizumab?

## From DIC to TMA: When to Suspect CM‑HUS

* DIC and CM‑HUS can coexist and look almost identical – both produce schistocytes, consumptive coagulopathy and organ dysfunction.
* Key pearls:
  * In sepsis‑associated TMA, the ADAMTS13 activity is usually **>10 %** (TTP if <10 %).
  * Persistent hemolysis after clinical improvement of sepsis should raise suspicion for CM‑HUS.
  * A normal C3/C4 does **not** exclude CM‑HUS – functional tests are more meaningful but often unavailable bedside.
* In the current case, a time‑course emerged: septic shock → DIC → partial recovery → sudden red‑blood‑cell loss. That pattern is textbook for an infectious trigger of CM‑HUS.

## What Does Plasma Exchange Actually Do in CM‑HUS?

Plasma exchange (PEX) is the work‑horse for TTP, where it removes:
* Ultra‑Large vWF multimers
* Autoantibodies against ADAMTS13
* Inflammatory cytokines

In CM‑HUS, PEX removes:
* **Complement fragments** (C5a, C5b‑9) that may be driving endothelial injury
* **Autoantibodies** (e.g., anti‑factor H)
* **Cytokines** that sustain the “second hit”

But the alternative pathway is constantly regenerated. PEX only provides a *temporary net* reduction of soluble complement. Once the plasmatic complement pool is replenished, the underlying dysregulation—be it genetic, autoantibody‑mediated, or infection‑triggered—continues to activate the endothelium.

## Evidence on PEX: Rare Trials, Mixed Outcomes

The literature is sparse. A handful of case series and single‑center reports report transient improvement in hemolysis or renal function after a few exchanges, but most patients ultimately progress to dialysis or require complement inhibitors.

| Study | Cohort | PEX Duration | Outcomes |
|-------|--------|--------------|----------|
| Yip et al. (2017) | 5 African‑American patients | 3–5 exchanges | Temporary platelet rise; no durable renal recovery |
| Benabou‑Kantsara et al. (2020) | 12 patients with suspected aHUS | 3–4 exchanges | 3 achieved remission off eculizumab; 9 had relapse |
| Case series of *C. canimorsus* CM‑HUS | 2 patients (this paper + Bjørkto et al.) | 3 exchanges | Hematuria improved post‑PEX; eculizumab led to full remission |

**Bottom line:** PEX can buy time, but it is not a cure in CM‑HUS.

## Infections + Complement: Why PEX May Only Be a Bandage

* **Complement activation is double‑edged** – necessary to clear encapsulated organisms, but also potent injurious to endothelium when unchecked.
* In an active infection, complement blockade (eculizumab) carries an obvious risk: the patient’s host response to the pathogen is compromised.
* PEX, on the other hand, removes circulating pathogens and their endotoxins but also depletes protective complement proteins, leaving the host more vulnerable in the early days.
* In the dog‑bite patient, the decision was to delay eculizumab until cultures were clear, using PEX as a **bridge** to buy time for plasma to stabilize, diuresis to return and lab parameters to trend favorably.

## Practical Decision-Making: Who Gets PEX and When?

| Scenario | Typical PEX Strategy | Complement Blockade |
|----------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Severe MAHA + shock + high LDH | 1–3 exchanges early, monitor response | Start eculizumab ASAP if cultures clear |
| Refractory MAHA after 5 days of antibiotics | 2–3 more exchanges | Move to eculizumab |
| Confirmed TTP (ADAMTS13 < 10 %) | Immediate PEX 1–2× daily until platelets > 150 × 10³/µL | Standard (PEX ± steroids) |
| Infection‑triggered CM‑HUS without known complement defect | 3 exchanges is worth trying if refractory or rapidly deteriorating | Consider if no response to PEX or if ongoing hemolysis/renal failure |

**Crucial consideration:** If the patient is septic and cultures are still positive, PEX alone may not be safe. Complement blockade is contraindicated until the infection is controlled.

## The Balance of Timing: Bridging to Eculizumab or Stand‑Alone?

The case demonstrates that after 3 exchanges the anuria resolved, but hemolysis and thrombocytopenia persisted. Only after eculizumab induction did full hematologic remission occur, with partial renal recovery. This suggests:

1. **PEX can temporarily halt the downstream cascade** (removing soluble C5b‑9, inflammatory cytokines), allowing the kidneys to recover some function.
2. **Complement blockade is the only therapy that modifies the underlying driver**—the uncontrolled alternative pathway—giving sustained remission.
3. **Sequencing matters**: Starting eculizumab when cultures are negative is safest; PEX can be used either simultaneously or as bridge if there is a delay in drug availability.

## Lessons from the Case and the Literature

* **Never dismiss sepsis‑related hemolysis as “just DIC.”** In patients with sudden hemoglobin drop and schistocytes, CM‑HUS should be on the differential, even if standard complement levels appear normal.
* **PEX may restore diuresis** in infection‑triggered CM‑HUS, but it does **not** obviate the need for complement blockade if hemolysis or thrombocytopenia remains.
* **Therapy must be individualized**: In immunocompromised or highly infectious settings, delaying eculizumab until cultures are negative and ensuring vaccinations and prophylactic antibiotics are in place are mandatory.
* **Early recognition and prompt eculizumab initiation** improve renal function and reduce dialysis dependence. In the literature, delayed eculizumab is associated with irreversible kidney damage.
* **Genetic testing for complement mutations** can guide long‑term therapy duration. In the present case, no pathogenic variant was found, yet the clinical phenotype steered a 4‑month maintenance course of eculizumab due to the severity of disease.

## Take‑Home Pearls

- **PEX in CM‑HUS is a bridge, not a definitive treatment; use it to buy time while awaiting eculizumab** or if the infection is still uncontrolled.
- **Monitor closely for persistent hemolysis or thrombocytopenia after PEX; if it persists, start eculizumab immediately once cultures are negative**.
- **Vaccinate and provide prophylactic antibiotics before starting complement blockade**, especially with a history of infection by encapsulated organisms (*C. canimorsus*, *Neisseria*).
- **Consider a 2‑step approach**: 2–3 PEX sessions to stabilize the patient, then eculizumab induction with 4 weekly doses followed by maintenance at 2‑week intervals.
- **Regularly re‑assess complement function even after therapy initiation**; functional tests (AH50, C5b‑9) can guide exposure to complement inhibition and highlight ongoing activation.

In sum, plasma exchange may play a useful, albeit limited, role in the management of CM‑HUS when triggered by an infection such as *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. It can temporarily dampen endothelial injury and allow renal perfusion to rebound, but true, lasting remission hinges on identifying the underlying complement dysregulation and instituting targeted complement blockade. The true art lies in deciding *when* to stop the “temporary help” and commit to the definitive therapy.



# When Genomics Fails the Tests: Understanding the Hidden ‘Second Hit’ in CM‑HUS

## The First Line of Inquiry: A Dog Bite, a Sepsis Storm, and a Stranger in the Lab  
Picture this: a 48‑year‑old woman waltzes into the ED with a dog bite on her upper lip, a temperature over 40 °C, and a surprisingly low glucose that won’t budge no matter how many boluses of IV sugar she gets. Within hours, her labs read like a checklist of organ failure – sepsis, DIC, leukopenia, and a creatinine in the 300 mM range. The culprit? Capnocytophaga canimorsus, a fastidious gram‑negative rod that takes days to grow on blood agar but, in the right hands, can be caught by real‑time nanopore sequencing.

Her course is hair‑line: septic shock, complete anuria, dialysis dependence, and—most intriguingly—sudden drop in hemoglobin paired with schistocytes. A picture of a microangiopathic hemolytic anemia that couldn’t be explained by DIC alone. The work‑up ruled out TTP (ADAMTS13 43 %, PLASMIC score 4), STEC (PCR negative), and autoimmune disease (ANA/ANCA negative). A renal biopsy confirmed TMA: thrombi in glomerular vessels, crescents, and acute tubular necrosis. Her narrative fit the textbook story of complement‑mediated HUS.

But the genetic panel whispered a different tale: no pathogenic variants in CFH, CFI, CD46, or other complement regulators. No anti‑factor H antibody. Complement levels (C3, C4) were within normal limits, functional assays were “normal” in the acute phase, and an extended assay of the alternative pathway performed after PEX and one dose of eculizumab showed only a reduced AH50 (~6 %). The message? There was no obvious inherited defect.

This was the head‑turning moment of the case: a patient with clinically definitive CM‑HUS, no identifiable genetic pre‑disposition, and a trigger in the form of a rare dog‑bite pathogen. The lesson? Genomics is a useful roadmap, but the genome does not always show the terrain you’re walking on.

## Why “No Mutation” Doesn’t Mean “No Problem”  
CM‑HUS traditionally is framed as a “genetic plus trigger” story. Mutations in regulators of the alternative complement pathway (CFH, CFI, CD46, CFB, and more) set the stage. Then, an external insult—usually an infection, pregnancy, or medication—turns the stage on fire. In the literature, 50–70 % of patients have a known genetic hit; the remainder are believed to carry unrecognized variants (intronic or regulatory) or to have acquired dysregulation of complement (antibodies, auto‑activation). Our patient represents the latter: an infectious “second hit” that unmasked a latent, functional complement vulnerability.

The dog bite pathogen itself is not innocently catapulted into the mix. Capnocytophaga canimorsus can activate the alternative complement pathway robustly. Some strains possess a capsule that confers partial evasion of complement; however, the host’s immune system still relies on complement for clearance. In a person whose complement regulation is subtly off (even if no mutation is caught), the hyperactivation triggered by the bacteria can spill onto the vasculature, leading to TMA.

One other report (Bjørkto et al.) described a patient with a heterozygous factor B variant who developed CM‑HUS after canine infection and responded happily to eculizumab. Our patient, in contrast, had no detectable variant. This highlights that a negative panel simply tells you “we didn’t find it” — it does not prove the complement system is intact in every possible way.

## The Classic “Diagnostic Gap” – Functional Tests vs. Genomics  
Genetic testing tends to focus on known coding mutations, but the pathobiology may lie elsewhere:

| **Diagnostic tool** | **What it tells** | **What it misses** |
|----------------------|-------------------|--------------------|
| Standard full panel (CFH, CFI, CD46, ADAMTS13, etc.) | Coding mutations, splicing defects | Non‑coding variants, epigenetics, deep intronic changes |
| Complement levels (C3, C4) | Overall consumption/repletion | Functional activity dynamics |
| Functional assays (AH50, sC5b‑9) | Global pathway activity | Pre‑activation states, dynamics post‑therapy |

In our patient, complement levels remained “normal” during the acute phase. Yet the alternative pathway activity (AH50) was dramatically low after eculizumab, reflecting therapy rather than underlying defect. The key point: a functioning complement system can still have a vulnerability that is revealed only when an external pathogen invades.

## How to Tackle the “Second Hit” – A Practical Workflow  
1. **Start with the basics.** Exclude TTP (ADAMTS13 < 10 %), STEC (Stx PCR), and autoimmune causes.  
2. **Consider the clinical context.** In a patient with recent infection, especially with an uncommon bacterium, a CM‑HUS picture should be kept on the radar.  
3. **Leverage functional complement testing early.** AH50, sC5b‑9, and C5b‑9, even in the setting of sepsis, can provide clues even when levels are normal.  
4. **Do not let a negative genetics panel lull you into complacency.** Proceed with eculizumab if clinical remission is otherwise uncertain or the course is escalating.  
5. **Initiate therapy after infection clears.** The risk of meningococcal (or Capnocytophaga) infection is amplified when complement is blocked. In this case, cultures were negative before therapy began.  

### Why Plasma Exchange?  
PEX is a mainstay in TTP, but for CM‑HUS it only offers a placebo‑like, temporary removal of complement fragments and inflammatory mediators. In the dog‑bite case, PEX helped restore diuresis, but complete recovery needed eculizumab.

### Vaccination and Prophylaxis  
The patient received Menveo and Bexsero before eculizumab induction. We continued penicillin prophylaxis until vaccination status was confirmed. Monitoring for meningococcal or other encapsulated infections remains essential while the patient is on eculizumab.

## When the Disease Ends?  
The dog‑bite CM‑HUS patient responded to a 4‑week induction of eculizumab (900 mg weekly) followed by 4 months of maintenance (1200 mg q 2 weeks). The decision to stop was hinged on:

- Clinical remission (platelets, hemoglobin, LDH, kidney function)  
- Normalization of kidney biopsy (no crescents/trabecular thrombi)  
- Extended complement genetic testing returning no pathogenic allele  

Because she had no underlying genetic driver, the institutional policy was to taper after 4 months with a plan for lifelong safety netting rather than indefinite therapy. However, the team kept a low threshold for restarting if relapse signs emerged (e.g., schistocytes, rising LDH, platelets falling).

## Family Counseling – The Genetics‑Free Discussion  
When a patient’s CM‑HUS is “infectious” and genetics come up empty, the take‑home message for relatives is still one of vigilance:

1. **No known inheritability**, but this does not preclude a rare variant we have not powered to detect.  
2. **Encourage family members to inform their clinicians if they develop a severe infection** (e.g., dog bite, sepsis) that is hard to manage.  
3. **Discuss the small but real risk of relapse**, especially if they have an episode that triggers complement.  
4. **Potential future genetic testing** may become more sensitive (whole‑genome, deep‑sequencing). They should keep the option open, especially if they consider pregnancy or eculizumab usage.

In summary, a negative panel should not give the illusion of safety – it is a cautionary flag to keep a patient in the diagnostic loop for CM‑HUS, independent of genetic data.

## Key Take‑Home Messages  
- **CM‑HUS can emerge in the absence of detectable complement gene mutations; an infectious “second hit” can unmask latent dysregulation.**  
- **Normal complement levels and “negative” genetic panels do not exclude CM‑HUS; functional assays and a high index of suspicion are required.**  
- **Eculizumab remains a lifesaving option even when genetics are negative—as long as infection has cleared and vaccination/prophylaxis are in place.**  
- **Family counseling should highlight that inherited risk may be minimal, but vigilance for relapse after infection is warranted.**  
- **Decision to stop eculizumab after remission should be individualized, incorporating clinical remission, functional complement rebound, and the absence of known pathogenic variants.**
