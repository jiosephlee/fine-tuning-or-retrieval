Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Morning Report] A patient presents after a dog bite with fever, hypoglycaemia, and pancytopenia. Which laboratory abnormalities most strongly indicate the development of disseminated intravascular coagulation, and why is early recognition of DIC vital?

**Key laboratory clues that pointed to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)  
and the urgency of early recognition**

*In the presented patient, the diagnostic hallmarks that most strongly signalled the onset of DIC were:*

| Lab abnormality | Why it is a DIC marker | Evidence in this case |
|-----------------|-----------------------|-----------------------|
| **Severe thrombocytopenia** (platelets < 50 × 10⁹ /L) | Platelets are consumed in the widespread micro‑thrombi that characterize DIC. | Platelets fell to 30–40 × 10⁹ /L on admission, remained persistently low (30–40 × 10⁹ /L) despite transfusion. |
| **Prolonged clotting times** (↑ PT/INR and/or ↑ aPTT) | Fibrinogen and coagulation factors are depleted, and natural anticoagulants (protein C/S, antithrombin) are consumed. | The initial coagulation profile (Table 1) showed marked prolongation of PT/aPTT. |
| **Low fibrinogen** (< 1.5 g/L) | Fibrinogen is both consumed in clot formation and cleared by endothelial damage. | The coagulopathy noted on admission included markedly reduced fibrinogen levels. |
| **Elevated D‑dimer/FDP** (often > 10 µg/mL) | Reflects fibrinolysis of the excessive fibrin generated by DIC. | D‑dimer was markedly elevated in serial checks, confirming hyper‑fibrinolytic activity. |

These four abnormalities comprise the classic ISTH DIC score; in this patient the score exceeded the threshold for overt DIC (> 5), confirming the diagnosis.

---

### Why early recognition of DIC is vital

| Clinical Impact | Why it matters | How it was applied in this patient |
|-----------------|----------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Rapid escalation of supportive care** | DIC can progress from mild coagulopathy to massive bleeding or micro‑vascular thrombosis within hours. | Platelet and fibrinogen infusions were started immediately after the diagnosis was suspected, preventing hemorrhagic complications. |
| **Avoidance of inappropriate therapies** | Mislabeling DIC as thrombotic microangiopathy (e.g., TTP) can delay definitive source‑control antibiotics and lead to unnecessary plasma exchange. | The thrombocyte count, D‑dimer, and ADAMTS13 activity (43 %) ruled out TTP, guiding the team toward sepsis management rather than TTP‑specific therapy. |
| **Guiding infection control** | Early DIC alerts clinicians to a severe inflammatory state that often precedes multi‑organ failure and bacterial dissemination. | Prompt source control (antibiotic escalation to piperacillin‑tazobactam) and ICU transfer were undertaken once DIC was recognized. |
| **Facilitating early organ‑protective strategies** | DIC‑induced microthrombi can precipitate acute kidney injury (AKI) and other organ dysfunction. | Renal replacement therapy was initiated as soon as oliguria and anuria developed, limiting the duration of renal ischemia. |
| **Informing the need for prophylactic measures when later using complement blockade** | Use of eculizumab in a patient with active DIC and ongoing infection is risky; early clearance of the trigger reduces this risk. | Eculizumab was only started after blood cultures turned negative and DIC had begun to resolve, minimizing the danger of postoperative infectious complications. |

In short, the constellation of thrombocytopenia, prolonged coagulation times, low fibrinogen and a high D‑dimer unmistakably identifies DIC. Recognizing these derangements early catalyzes a cascade of life‑saving interventions—targeted transfusions, prompt antibiotics, and vigilant monitoring—that together reduce mortality from sepsis‑related DIC and prevent progression to irreversible organ damage.
