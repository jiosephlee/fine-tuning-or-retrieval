Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Differential Diagnosis] When a septic patient develops acute kidney injury, thrombocytopenia, schistocytes, and hemolysis, which entities must be considered in the differential diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy, and which laboratory studies help differentiate them?

**TMA in the septic ICU: when AKI, thrombocytopenia, schistocytes and hemolysis appear, consider:**

| Entity | Clinical clues that help isolate it | Key laboratory studies |
|--------|--------------------------------------|------------------------|
| **Sepsis‑associated disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)** | Very early, widespread activation; often improves with source control | Prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, high D‑dimer, circulating fibrin‑splitting products; thrombocytopenia; schistocytes usually are modest in number and resolve as the sepsis improves. |
| **Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)** | Sudden severe hemolysis, neuro‑psychiatric symptoms, “cold” plasma‑exchange responsiveness | **ADAMTS13 activity < 10 %** (or < 5 % in classic TTP); absence of Shiga toxin; PLASMIC score ≥ 6 favors TTP. In our patient, ADAMTS13 was 43.5 % with a PLASMIC of 4/7, ruling out TTP. |
| **Shiga‑toxin–producing E. coli HUS (STEC‑HUS)** | Often follows an episode of bloody diarrhoea or prodromal gastroenteritis | **Stool PCR or culture for Shiga toxin**; normal complement levels and ADAMTS13 activity. Our patient’s stool PCR was negative, excluding STEC‑HUS. |
| **Complement‑mediated (atypical) HUS (CM‑HUS)** | Usually in patients with a trigger (infection, pregnancy, drug) and often presents after the sepsis has begun to resolve; normal or low C3/C4 may be seen; ADAMTS13 normal; renal biopsy shows thrombotic lesions without immune deposition (our patient’s biopsy met these criteria). | **Complement functional tests** (alternative‑pathway AH50) and **genetic panel** for CFH, CFI, MCP, C3, CFHR1‑5, etc. C3/C4 may remain normal, as in this case. ↓AH50 (6 %) was noted after plasma exchange, but the clinical picture and response to eculizumab confirmed CM‑HUS. |
| **Drug‑induced TMA (e.g., penicillin, vancomycin, thrombopoietin receptor agonists)** | Temporal relationship to drug exposure; may show a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) or “pseudopositive” SAAG in penicillin‑treated patients | **DAT** (IgG, C3d), **drug review**, and often a normal complement panel; ADAMTS13 activity is usually > 10 %; renal biopsy not required if clinical picture fits. In this case, the initial positive IgG DAT was later found to be a false positive, and the time‑course suggested an infectious trigger rather than drug. |
| **Other rarer TMAs (e.g., pregnancy‑associated, mechanical‑heart‑valve, eculizumab‑mediated)** | Evaluate relevant history and exposures; usually accompany other specific clinical clues | Specific labs as per trigger; not applicable in the presented case. |

**Bottom line:** Begin with a standard DIC work‑up, then rapidly exclude TTP with ADAMTS13 activity and the PLASMIC score. If ADAMTS13 is normal, rule out STEC‑HUS with stool PCR. If both are negative, assess complement function and genetics to confirm CM‑HUS. Drug‑induced TMA is considered by a detailed medication review and DAT testing. In the index patient, the combination of persistent hemolysis, schistocytosis, normal ADAMTS13, normal Shiga toxin PCR, and a biopsy consistent with TMA led to the diagnosis of CM‑HUS, treated successfully with eculizumab.
