Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Management] Why is plasma exchange frequently initiated before eculizumab in patients with suspected complement‑mediated HUS who are still septic, and what strategies should be used to mitigate the risk of severe infection when starting terminal‑complement blockade?

**Key teaching point** – In patients with suspected complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) who still have active sepsis, plasma exchange (PEX) is often begun before the first dose of eculizumab. PEX stabilizes the patient, removes circulating pathogenic mediators, and buys time to control the infection and obtain definitive diagnosis. Once the infection is under control, eculizumab is instituted, preceded by meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis to minimize the risk of severe encapsulated‑bacteria infection that terminal‑complement blockade predisposes to.

---

### Why start PEX before eculizumab while the patient is septic?

| Reason | Why it matters in the case |
|--------|-----------------------------|
| **Immediate reduction of circulating complement fragments & cytokines** | The patient’s DIC and emerging TMA (schistocytes, MAHA, thrombocytopenia) were driven by massive complement activation. PEX can transiently lower C5b‑9, soluble MAC, and inflammatory mediators, improving hemolysis and platelet count as was seen after the third PEX in this woman. |
| **Psychologic stabilization of organ injury** | Kidney biopsy later confirmed microangiopathic injury. PEX can provide a window of renal perfusion (diuresis returned after the third session) while awaiting permanent therapy. |
| **Diagnostic “bridge”** | No prompt definitively diagnostic tests (ADAMTS13, extended complement, genetic studies) are immediately available. PEX allows clinicians to treat empirically for TTP/CM‑HUS while droppers off sepsis, and to observe response to therapy before committing to lifelong eculizumab. |
| **Avoiding premature complement blockade** | Eculizumab blocks C5 and converts a powerful host-defense mechanism into a liability against encapsulated organisms. In a patient with ongoing bacteremia, premature complement inhibition could allow uncontrolled pathogen growth. Starting PEX first allows the infection to be cleared or at least put under antibiotic control before complement is suppressed. |

In the reported case, the blood cultures had become negative and the patient was clinically improving before the first eculizumab dose. The temporary hemolysis and thrombocytopenia resolved only after the induction of eculizumab, underscoring that PEX provided only fleeting benefit while the patient was still septic.

---

### Strategies to mitigate severe infection risk when initiating terminal‑complement blockade

| Step | Practical implementation in this case |
|------|----------------------------------------|
| **Meningococcal vaccination** | Administer a quadrivalent conjugate vaccine (Menveo) and a serogroup B vaccine (Bexsero) at least 2 weeks before the first eculizumab dose, or immediately thereafter if vaccination is not feasible prior to therapy. In the case, vaccination was given before eculizumab induction. |
| **Antibiotic prophylaxis** | Begin a coverage regimen against encapsulated organisms (e.g., oral penicillin V or intravenous ampicillin) as soon as vaccination status is secured. The patient received prophylactic Apocillin after vaccination, reducing the risk of relapse of Capnocytophaga infection. |
| **Ensure infection clearance** | Obtain at least two consecutive negative cultures, and monitor clinical signs (fever, leukocytosis, organ dysfunction) to confirm resolution of sepsis before starting eculizumab. This patient’s cultures had cleared and inflammatory markers had trended down before therapy. |
| **Close monitoring for meningococcal disease** | Observe for rash, temperature spikes, or meningitic symptoms during and after the initial eculizumab cycle; have a low threshold for empiric ceftriaxone or vancomycin if suspicion arises. |
| **Refresh vaccination series** | Provide a second dose of meningococcal vaccine 6–8 weeks after the first dose, especially if the patient remains on maintenance eculizumab. In the case, a second vaccination series was planned for outpatient follow‑up. |
| **Patient education & contact prophylaxis** | Inform household contacts that they may need prophylactic penicillin for 7 days after exposure to a patient on eculizumab. |

---

**Take‑home message**: In septic patients with suspected CM‑HUS, start plasma exchange to transiently curb the complement‐driven microangiopathy and allow time to eliminate the infectious trigger. Only after culture clearance, adequate antibiotic therapy, and meningococcal immunization should eculizumab be introduced, with prophylactic antibiotics and vigilant monitoring to guard against the heightened risk of life‑threatening encapsulated‑bacteria infections.
