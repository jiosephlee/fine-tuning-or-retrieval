Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] Capnocytophaga canimorsus is frequently underdiagnosed in dog‑bite patients. What microbiologic and host‑related factors enable this organism to cause fulminant sepsis, and why might its detection in blood cultures be delayed compared with more common flora?

**Microbiology and host biology that favor fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection**

| Microbiologic factors | Host‑related factors |
|-----------------------|----------------------|
|• **Fastidious, encapsulated, gram‑negative rod** – requires enriched, anaerobic media and a prolonged lag phase. In the case report, blood cultures were positive only after 7 days, whereas typical pathogens (e.g., *Staphylococcus*, *Escherichia*) emerge within 12–24 h. |• **Early, vigorous innate immune activation** – lipopolysaccharide (LPS) triggers massive cytokine release and a cascade that culminates in disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), plate‑assisted thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury. The patient’s progressive leukopenia and coagulopathy exemplify this hyperinflammatory state. |
|• **Polysaccharide capsule** – confers resistance to serum complement and phagocytosis, allowing the organism to persist in the bloodstream until the host’s complement system is overwhelmed or dysregulated. |• **Complement dysregulation or reduced clearance** – even in “apparently normal” patients, transient complement activation can unmask idiopathic susceptibility. In the present case, the development of complement‑mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS) was likely precipitated by bacterial endotoxin‑induced complement over‑activation. |
|• **Low bacterial loads in early bacteremia** – because of its slow growth and the relative paucity of organisms in the blood, detection by routine culture is easily missed. |• **High inoculum from deep oral bite** – the dog bite to the upper lip introduced a substantial bacterial burden into vascular tissue, enabling rapid systemic spread once the host’s defenses were breached. |

**Why detection is delayed compared with more common flora**

1. **Slow in‑vitro growth** – *C. canimorsus* often requires 48–72 h of enrichment before colonies become visible; automated blood‑culture systems routinely stop monitoring after 5 days, and the organism may be missed during that window. The case report documents a 7‑day turnaround for positivity, a stark contrast to the 12–24 h detection of common pathogens.

2. **Fastidious media requirements** – Many standard labs do not routinely employ the enriched, anaerobic media that *C. canimorsus* needs, so organism recovery depends on institutional culture protocols.

3. **Low organism count** – Even if the bacterium is present systemically, its numbers may fall below the detection threshold if neutrophil‑mediated killing briefly reduces the load; by the time the patient presents with fulminant sepsis, the organism may already be exploding out of the culturing vessel.

4. **Concurrent antibiotic exposure** – The patient had been prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin but did not initiate therapy, potentially allowing a low‑dose or sub‑therapeutic antibiotic effect that may further suppress in‑culture growth until the bacterial burden builds.

Because of these microbiologic and host factors, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for *C. canimorsus* in any dog‑bite patient presenting with rapidly progressive sepsis, DIC, or unexplained shock, and consider expedited molecular diagnostics (e.g., 16S rRNA PCR, nanopore sequencing) while awaiting culture confirmation.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When a septic patient develops acute kidney injury, thrombocytopenia, schistocytes, and hemolysis, which entities must be considered in the differential diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy, and which laboratory studies help differentiate them?

**TMA in the septic ICU: when AKI, thrombocytopenia, schistocytes and hemolysis appear, consider:**

| Entity | Clinical clues that help isolate it | Key laboratory studies |
|--------|--------------------------------------|------------------------|
| **Sepsis‑associated disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)** | Very early, widespread activation; often improves with source control | Prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, high D‑dimer, circulating fibrin‑splitting products; thrombocytopenia; schistocytes usually are modest in number and resolve as the sepsis improves. |
| **Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)** | Sudden severe hemolysis, neuro‑psychiatric symptoms, “cold” plasma‑exchange responsiveness | **ADAMTS13 activity < 10 %** (or < 5 % in classic TTP); absence of Shiga toxin; PLASMIC score ≥ 6 favors TTP. | **Shiga‑toxin–producing E. coli HUS (STEC‑HUS)** | Often follows an episode of bloody diarrhoea or prodromal gastroenteritis | **Stool PCR or culture for Shiga toxin**; normal complement levels and ADAMTS13 activity. Our patient’s stool PCR was negative, excluding STEC‑HUS. |

**Bottom line:** Begin with a standard DIC work‑up, then rapidly exclude TTP with ADAMTS13 activity and the PLASMIC score. If ADAMTS13 is normal, rule out STEC‑HUS with stool PCR. If both are negative, assess complement function and genetics to confirm CM‑HUS. Drug‑induced TMA is considered by a detailed medication review and DAT testing. In the index patient, the combination of persistent hemolysis, schistocytosis, normal ADAMTS13, normal Shiga toxin PCR, and a biopsy consistent with TMA led to the diagnosis of CM‑HUS, treated successfully with eculizumab.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway causes endothelial injury in complement‑mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome, and explain why organisms such as Capnocytophaga canimorsus can trigger this process even in the absence of identifiable genetic mutations.

**Uncontrolled alternative‑pathway complement activation → endothelial injury and CM‑HUS**  
In complement‑mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS) the alternative complement pathway becomes dysregulated.  
**1. What the alternative pathway normally does**  
 The C3 convertase C3bBb is formed spontaneously on any surface.  On host cells it is rapidly inactivated by factor H and factor I with the co‑factor membrane cofactor protein (CD46).  These regulators keep the conversion of C3 to C3b—and subsequently the formation of the C5 convertase—at bay.  
**2. How dysregulation damages endothelium**  
When regulation fails (genetic loss of factor H/I, factor B gain‑of‑function, or autoantibodies), the C3 convertase lives longer, leading to an excess of C3b and the downstream C5 convertase.  Lysis is mediated by the terminal membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b‑9).  MAC deposits on endothelial cells (ECs) in the kidney, lung, and other microvasculature, producing sub‑lytic injury that up‑regulates von Willebrand factor, activates platelets, and promotes fibrin deposition.  Resultant microthrombi damage the glomerular capillaries, trigger mechanical hemolysis of red cells, and cause the consumptive thrombocytopenia seen in CM‑HUS.  This explains the triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury that the patient developed 8–12 days into her illness.  

**3. Infectious “second hits” can provoke the cascade even without a genetic defect**  
The patient had *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia.  This organism possesses a polysaccharide capsule and several surface proteins that engage the complement system.  Encapsulated bacteria are normally cleared by opsonization and MAC deposition; however, *C. canimorsus* can bind factor H and other regulators, hijacking or depleting them.  In the setting of overwhelming bacteremia, the local concentration of factor H on the luminal endothelium is out‑competed by the bacterial surface, allowing the alternative pathway to run unchecked at sites adjacent to the vessels.  Additionally, the high bacterial load and endotoxin stimulate complement activation systemically, transiently exhausting soluble regulators.  These “second‑hit” events de‑sensitize the endothelial regulatory shield without a pre‑existing genetic mutation, triggering the same pathogenic loop that is otherwise seen in hereditary CM‑HUS.  

**Case illustration**  
The patient’s blood counts, schistocytes, and renal biopsy all confirmed a TMA.  ADAMTS13 activity was normal, ruling out TTP.  Complement levels (C3/C4) were within normal limits during the sepsis phase—a common observation in CM‑HUS, as consumption can be transient or masked by acute‑phase production.  Extended testing after plasma exchange and the first eculizumab dose revealed marked reduction in AH50 activity, reflecting the therapeutic blockade rather than a pre‑existing deficit.  Importantly, no pathogenic complement gene mutations were detected, underscoring that *C. canimorsus* alone sufficed to ignite the alternative‑pathway storm.  

**Bottom line**  
Uncontrolled alternative‑pathway activation causes MAC‑mediated endothelial injury, platelet activation and microthrombi, producing the classic triad of CM‑HUS.  Encapsulated infections such as *C. canimorsus* can tip the balance by saturating or disabling complement regulators, allowing the host’s own complement cascade to become pathologic even in individuals with normal genetics.

### [Differential Diagnosis] A patient has a PLASMIC score of 4/7 and an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5 %. How would you interpret the likelihood of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura versus complement‑mediated HUS, and what are the clinical implications?

**Interpretation of the diagnostic indices**

| Test | Result | What it means for TTP | What it means for CM‑HUS |
|------|--------|-----------------------|---------------------------|
| PLASMIC score | 4/7 | Low pre‑test probability; <2 % chance of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency | Does **not** support TTP; leaves CM‑HUS as the leading alternative |
| ADAMTS13 activity | 43.5 % | Far above the diagnostic threshold (<10 %) → virtually excludes TTP | Compatible with complement‑mediated TMA; suggests either a primary or secondary HUS |

**Conclusion**  
With a low PLASMIC score and an ADAMTS13 activity well above 10 %, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura is highly unlikely. The patient’s presentation (MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI, persistent hemolysis, schistocytes, and a normal infectious work‑up) points toward complement‑mediated HUS. Negative stool PCR for Shiga toxin further excludes STEC‑HUS, consolidating CM‑HUS as the most probable diagnosis.

---

**Clinical implications**

1. **Therapy of choice** –  
   * **Complement blockade (eculizumab or ravulizumab)** should be initiated urgently. Early treatment correlates with superior renal and hematologic outcomes, as seen in the case report where eculizumab induced prompt remission.  
   * **Plasma exchange** is lifesaving in TTP but offers only transient benefit in CM‑HUS; it can be used as a bridging strategy until complement inhibition is feasible, especially if the patient is unstable or volume status is critical.

2. **Infection control** –  
   Complement inhibitors impair defense against encapsulated organisms (e.g., *Capnocytophaga*, *Neisseria*). Therefore, ensure that blood cultures are negative or the infection is adequately treated before initiating eculizumab. Administer appropriate vaccines (meningococcal, *C. canimorsus*‑specific if available) and consider prophylactic antimicrobials.

3. **Complement work‑up** –  
   Routine serum C3/C4 often remain normal in active CM‑HUS. Detailed functional assays (e.g., AH50, factor H/I/B levels, anti‑factor H antibodies) are useful for long‑term monitoring but are not required for the acute therapeutic decision.

4. **Follow‑up** –  
   Monitor hemoglobin, platelet count, LDH, haptoglobin, kidney function, and complement activity. Consider discontinuation of eculizumab only after sustained remission and evidence of normal complement regulation, often guided by genetic testing.  

**Bottom line**  
A PLASMIC score of 4/7 with an ADAMTS13 of 43.5 % virtually rules out TTP, directing the clinician to diagnose and treat CM‑HUS promptly, with attention to infection clearance and vaccination when using terminal complement blockade.

### [Pathophysiology] What laboratory assays should be performed to confirm complement‑mediated TMA, and how do the results of C3/C4, the alternative‑pathway AH50 assay, and ADAMTS13 activity guide both diagnosis and therapeutic decisions?

**Key laboratory work‑up to confirm complement‑mediated TMA**

1. **ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor**  
   – The gold standard for ruling out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP).  
   – Activity < 10 % = classic TTP → immediate plasma exchange (PEX).  
   – Activity > 10 % (as in the present case: 43.5 %) excludes severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and steers the diagnostic algorithm toward other TMAs.

2. **Shiga‑toxin screening**  
   – Stool PCR for *E. coli* verotoxin.  
   – Negativity (here: stool PCR negative) removes STEC‑HUS from the differential.

3. **Complement profiling**  
   - **C3, C4**: baseline levels; marked consumption supports active complement activation but *normal* values do **not exclude** complement‑mediated disease (as early in this patient).  
   - **CH50 (total hemolytic complement)**: assesses classical pathway; can be normal in CM‑HUS.  
   - **AH50 (alternative‑pathway activity)**: gold‑standard functional test for the alternative pathway.  
     – A markedly reduced AH50 (here 6 %) indicates dysfunction/consumption of the alternative pathway and is strongly suggestive of CM‑HUS.  
     – Note that AH50 can be artificially low after PEX or eculizumab; therefore, whenever possible, obtain the sample before any complement‑directed therapy.

4. **Anti‑factor‑H antibodies** (if genetic work‑up deferred or unavailable) – a positive result would provide additional evidence for secondary CM‑HUS.

5. **Complement‑regulatory gene panel** (factor H, factor I, factor B, MCP/CD46, C3, C4b‑binding protein) – typically reserved for patients with severe disease, relapses, or when considering discontinuation of eculizumab.

**Interpretation in the context of this case**

| Test | Result | Clinical inference |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| ADAMTS13 activity | 43.5 % | TTP excluded; no need for prolonged PEX targeting ADAMTS13 deficiency. |
| C3/C4 | within reference ranges (early) | Normal levels do NOT rule out CM‑HUS; consumption may be compensated or may develop later. |
| AH50 | 6 % (after 8 days of PEX and 1 dose of eculizumab) | Low AH50 reflects alternative‑pathway depletion or blockade; supports complement‑mediated TMA. Earlier pre‑treatment values would have been more definitive. |
| Other complement (CH50, anti‑factor H, genetics) | Not shown or normal | Supports absence of additive immunologic inhibition or genetic abnormality; reinforces diagnosis of infection‑triggered CM‑HUS. |

**Guidance for therapy**

* **If ADAMTS13 < 10 %** → immediate PEX; eculizumab unnecessary.  
* **If ADAMTS13 ≥ 10 % and AH50 markedly reduced** (even with normal C3/C4) → high probability of CM‑HUS.  
  – Start complement blockade (eculizumab or ravulizumab).  
  – Concomitant PEX may be used as a bridge while awaiting eculizumab effect, but it will not correct the underlying pathway dysfunction.  
* **If adjunctive findings (anti‑factor H, gene mutations) are present** → consider longer‑term complement blockade and more intensive monitoring.

**Take‑home points for residents**

1. **ADAMTS13 activity is the first, most decisive test.** Anything above 10 % basically rules out TTP.  
2. **Complement assays** (C3/C4, CH50, AH50) are supportive but not definitive alone; functional AH50 is the most sensitive indicator of alternative‑pathway involvement.  
3. **Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS.** Complement consumption can be transient or compensated; clinical context and AH50 are more informative.  
4. **Timing matters.** Measure complement functions before any PEX or eculizumab, because both can artificially lower AH50 and confound interpretation.  
5. **Treatment is driven by these labs.** In the reported patient, the ADAMTS13 result let clinicians safely forgo prolonged PEX, while the low AH50 confirmed complement dysregulation, prompting prompt eculizumab initiation that led to remission.

### [Management] Why is plasma exchange frequently initiated before eculizumab in patients with suspected complement‑mediated HUS who are still septic, and what strategies should be used to mitigate the risk of severe infection when starting terminal‑complement blockade?

**Key teaching point** – In patients with suspected complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) who still have active sepsis, plasma exchange (PEX) is often begun before the first dose of eculizumab. PEX stabilizes the patient, removes circulating pathogenic mediators, and buys time to control the infection and obtain definitive diagnosis. Once the infection is under control, eculizumab is instituted, preceded by meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis to minimize the risk of severe encapsulated‑bacteria infection that terminal‑complement blockade predisposes to.

---

### Why start PEX before eculizumab while the patient is septic?

| Reason | Why it matters in the case |
|--------|-----------------------------|
| **Immediate reduction of circulating complement fragments & cytokines** | The patient’s DIC and emerging TMA (schistocytes, MAHA, thrombocytopenia) were driven by massive complement activation. PEX can transiently lower C5b‑9, soluble MAC, and inflammatory mediators, improving hemolysis and platelet count as was seen after the third PEX in this woman. |
| **Psychologic stabilization of organ injury** | Kidney biopsy later confirmed microangiopathic injury. PEX can provide a window of renal perfusion (diuresis returned after the third session) while awaiting permanent therapy. |
| **Diagnostic “bridge”** | No prompt definitively diagnostic tests (ADAMTS13, extended complement, genetic studies) are immediately available. PEX allows clinicians to treat empirically for TTP/CM‑HUS while droppers off sepsis, and to observe response to therapy before committing to lifelong eculizumab. |
| **Avoiding premature complement blockade** | Eculizumab blocks C5 and converts a powerful host-defense mechanism into a liability against encapsulated organisms. In a patient with ongoing bacteremia, premature complement inhibition could allow uncontrolled pathogen growth. Starting PEX first allows the infection to be cleared or at least put under antibiotic control before complement is suppressed. |

In the reported case, the blood cultures had become negative and the patient was clinically improving before the first eculizumab dose. The temporary hemolysis and thrombocytopenia resolved only after the induction of eculizumab, underscoring that PEX provided only fleeting benefit while the patient was still septic.

### Strategies to mitigate severe infection risk when initiating terminal‑complement blockade

| Step | Practical implementation in this case |
|------|----------------------------------------|
| **Meningococcal vaccination** | Administer a quadrivalent conjugate vaccine (Menveo) and a serogroup B vaccine (Bexsero) at least 2 weeks before the first eculizumab dose, or immediately thereafter if vaccination is not feasible prior to therapy. In the case, vaccination was given before eculizumab induction. |
| **Antibiotic prophylaxis** | Begin a coverage regimen against encapsulated organisms (e.g., oral penicillin V or intravenous ampicillin) as soon as vaccination status is secured. The patient received prophylactic Apocillin after vaccination, reducing the risk of relapse of Capnocytophaga infection. |
| **Ensure infection clearance** | Obtain at least two consecutive negative cultures, and monitor clinical signs (fever, leukocytosis, organ dysfunction) to confirm resolution of sepsis before starting eculizumab. This patient’s cultures had cleared and inflammatory markers had trended down before therapy. |
| **Close monitoring for meningococcal disease** | Observe for rash, temperature spikes, or meningitic symptoms during and after the initial eculizumab cycle; have a low threshold for empiric ceftriaxone or vancomycin if suspicion arises. |
| **Refresh vaccination series** | Provide a second dose of meningococcal vaccine 6–8 weeks after the first dose, especially if the patient remains on maintenance eculizumab. In the case, a second vaccination series was planned for outpatient follow‑up. |
| **Patient education & contact prophylaxis** | Inform household contacts that they may need prophylactic penicillin for 7 days after exposure to a patient on eculizumab. |

---

**Take‑home message**: In septic patients with suspected CM‑HUS, start plasma exchange to transiently curb the complement‐driven microangiopathy and allow time to eliminate the infectious trigger. Only after culture clearance, adequate antibiotic therapy, and meningococcal immunization should eculizumab be introduced, with prophylactic antibiotics and vigilant monitoring to guard against the heightened risk of life‑threatening encapsulated‑bacteria infections.

### [Management] What is the recommended induction and maintenance dosing regimen for eculizumab in acute complement‑mediated HUS, and how does the timing of initiation affect renal and hematologic outcomes?

**Eculizumab in acute complement‑mediated HUS**

**Standard dosing**

| Phase | Dose | Frequency | Notes |
|-------|------|-----------|-------|
| **Induction** | 900 mg IV (adult weight ≥50 kg) | Once weekly for 4 weeks | Weight‑adjusted doses (e.g., 500 mg for <50 kg) are used in children. |
| **Maintenance** | 1200 mg IV | Every 2 weeks | Continue until hematologic remission and renal recovery; duration is individualized. |

The patient in the case–report received exactly this regimen: 900 mg weekly for 4 weeks followed by 1200 mg every 2 weeks for 4 months. This schedule is endorsed by current KDIGO and EMA guidelines for aHUS and is supported by phase‑2/3 trials.

**Timing of initiation and outcomes**

1. **Early initiation is critical**  
   - In the literature, starting eculizumab within 10–14 days of the first clinical signs of TMA predicts a >70 % chance of renal recovery and a lower incidence of permanent dialysis.  
   - In the present case the drug was begun after blood cultures cleared and before the patient’s renal function had reached a plateau of anuria; this timing allowed rapid hematologic remission (schistocytes resolved, creatinine fell from peak values) and partial reversal of AKI (return of spontaneous diuresis).  

2. **Delayed therapy worsens prognosis**  
   - Initiation >3 weeks after the onset of severe thrombocytopenia and hemolysis is associated with persistent microvascular injury, progression of tubular necrosis, and a higher risk of end‑stage kidney disease.  

3. **Balancing infection risk**  
   - Because C5 blockade impairs defense against encapsulated organisms, eculizumab is ideally started only after the septic source has been eradicated, as was done here (post‑negative cultures, prior meningococcal vaccination, and antibiotic prophylaxis).  
   - In cases where infection cannot be fully ruled out, the benefits of halting uncontrolled complement activation must be weighed against the risk of opportunistic infections.  

**Bottom line**

Use the 900 mg / week × 4 ‑induction followed by 1200 mg / 2 weeks maintenance. Start as soon as the diagnosis of CM‑HUS is secure (ideally within one to two weeks of presentation) and after active infection is addressed. Prompt therapy yields rapid hematologic resolution and preserves renal function; delays tend to portend incomplete recovery and higher dialysis rates.

### [Management] After commencing eculizumab therapy for complement‑mediated HUS, why are meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis mandatory, and what is the recommended timing and schedule of immunization?

**Why meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis are mandatory after starting eculizumab**

| Reason | Clinical basis |
|--------|----------------|
| **Loss of terminal complement** | Eculizumab blocks C5, abolishing the membrane‑attack complex (MAC). Encapsulated bacteria such as *Neisseria meningitidis* (MenACWY and MenB) rely on MAC for bactericidal activity. The patient in the case report was placed on eculizumab while still at risk of a severe, often fatal meningococcal outbreak. |
| **Inadequate humoral response without complement** | Complement is needed for efficient opsonisation and antibody‑mediated clearance. Even a normal antibody level may be ineffective if MAC is absent. Vaccination primes the adaptive immune system before complement inhibition, ensuring that enough functional antibody is produced once eculizumab is given. |
| **Increased incidence of meningococcal disease** | The FDA and EMA label for eculizumab lists meningococcal infection as a serious, life‑threatening adverse event; incidence is 400–500 times higher than in the general population. |
| **Legal and ethical protection** | Failure to provide vaccine/prophylaxis can lead to medicolegal exposure and patient harm. |

**Recommended timing & schedule**

1. **Vaccination before complement blockade**  
   - Give a single dose of **MenACWY** (Menveo® or Menactra®) *and* a single dose of **MenB** (Bexsero®) **≥ 2 weeks** before initiating eculizumab.  
   - In our patient, the first doses of both vaccines were administered right after the third plasma‑exchange session, *just before* the first eculizumab infusion (900 mg IV weekly). Because the inoculations occurred within 2 weeks of starting eculizumab, antibiotic prophylaxis was started (Apocillin®) to cover the vulnerable period.

2. **Second‑dose schedule**  
   - **MenACWY**: second dose at **2 weeks after the first dose** (or 8 weeks after the initial dose if a two‑dose series is planned).  
   - **MenB**: second dose at **4 weeks after the first dose** (or 8 weeks after the initial dose if a two‑dose series is planned).  
   - The case report followed a pragmatic schedule: second doses were planned 8 weeks later on an outpatient basis – a widely accepted alternative that balances immune maturation with logistical feasibility.

3. **Antibiotic prophylaxis**  
   - If vaccination is given within 2 weeks of eculizumab initiation, administer oral penicillin G/ amoxicillin (or Apocillin®) as a daily regimen for **at least 2 weeks** of eculizumab therapy, or until a clear antibody titre is documented.  
   - Continue prophylaxis throughout the entire maintenance eculizumab course in patients who have not yet completed the full meningococcal vaccine series or who have low post‑vaccine antibody titres.

4. **Serologic monitoring**  
   - Measure anti‑MenACWY and anti‑MenB IgG titres 4–6 weeks after the final dose. A titre ≥ 1.0 µg/mL (MenACWY) or ≥ 0.5 µg/mL (MenB) is considered protective. Repeat titration at least once per year if eculizumab is continued > 12 months. 

**Key take‑away for the day‑to‑day practice**

- **Give meningococcal vaccines at least 2 weeks before eculizumab.** If urgent eculizumab is needed and vaccinations cannot be fully completed, start daily penicillin prophylaxis for the full duration of the eculizumab course.  
- **Use a two‑dose series for both MenACWY and MenB** (2‑week and 4‑week intervals respectively) when possible, but an 8‑week interval is acceptable for practical reasons.
- **Confirm protective antibody levels** before ceasing prophylaxis.  
- **Explain the rationale** to patients: eculizumab impairs the MAC; the vaccine teaches the immune system to produce antibodies that will function even in the absence of complement.

The case report illustrates this approach—vaccines were given just before eculizumab induction, antibiotic prophylaxis was administered, and the second vaccine doses were scheduled 8 weeks later. This regimen protects the patient from the life‑threatening risk of meningococcal infection that accompanies terminal complement blockade.

### [Pathophysiology] Why might C3 and C4 levels remain normal during active complement‑mediated HUS, and how can functional alternative‑pathway testing still uncover complement dysregulation?

**Complement mediation in HUS – why C3/C4 can be deceptively normal and how functional testing discloses the real defect**

1. **Complement basics**  
   *C3* is a central convertase substrate activated by all three pathways; *C4* is specifically consumed during classical and lectin‑pathway activation.  
   The *alternative pathway* (AP) proceeds directly through C3 without C4 involvement, using factor B, H, I and properdin to amplify antigen‑independent activation.

2. **Why C3/C4 may stay “normal” in CM‑HUS**  
   - **Sub‑threshold consumption**: In early or modest AP activation, C3 is converted but the liver can up‑regulate synthesis, keeping serum C3 within reference ranges.  
   - **Temporal lag**: C3 consumption may trail clinical deterioration; serum levels often fall only after prolonged or very severe activation.  
   - **Pathway specificity**: Since the AP does not involve C4, a disease driven almost exclusively by the AP will not lower C4 at all.  
   - **Compensatory factors**: Release of control proteins (e.g., factor H, factor I) can buffer loss of C3, blunting the drop in measurable quantity.  
   Thus, routine C3/C4 measurements provide a coarse read‑out and can miss the subtle, pathway‑specific dysregulation seen in CM‑HUS.

3. **Functional assays reveal the hidden dysregulation**  
   The *alternative‑pathway hemolytic assay (AH50)* tests the capacity of a serum sample to lyse antibody‑coated rabbit erythrocytes in a complement‑rich milieu.  
   - **Low AH50** = impaired or over‑consuming AP activity.  
   - In the reported patient, baseline C3/C4 were normal and even the classical/lectin pathways (CH50, L-50) appeared intact.  
   - Eight days after plasma exchange and one dose of eculizumab, the AH50 dropped to 6 % (normal >60 %), confirming severe AP dysfunction that the routine panels had missed.

4. **Clinical implication**  
   An apparently normal C3/C4 panel should **not** preclude the diagnosis of CM‑HUS.  
   Early, specific functional testing (AH50, plus factor assays or inhibitor studies) is essential in patients with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI when TTP and STEC‑HUS are excluded.  
   The case illustrates that even in the setting of severe infection (Capnocytophaga canimorsus), AP dysregulation can erupt clinically while conventional complement levels remain unchanged—highlighting the diagnostic value of functional assays and justifying the use of complement blockade (eculizumab) once the defect is uncovered.
