Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 5: ## Therapeutic Strategy and Clinical Dilemmas

The management of complement‑mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS) arising in the setting of an active bacterial sepsis presents a diagnostic and therapeutic paradox. On the one hand, the underlying trigger—a disseminated infection with **Capnocytophaga canimorsus** in this patient—demands timely antimicrobial suppression; on the other hand, the endothelial injury and microvascular thrombosis characteristic of CM‑HUS require rapid modulation of the complement cascade. The therapeutic algorithm in this case was therefore built around three interlocked considerations: (1) the short‑term stabilisation afforded by plasma exchange, (2) the definitive but infection‑sensitive action of complement blockade with eculizumab, and (3) the prophylactic measures necessary to mitigate the heightened infectious risk of terminal complement inhibition.

### Plasma exchange: rationale and sequencing

Plasma exchange (PEX) removes circulating pathogenic mediators and replaces them with therapeutic plasma, thereby diminishing auto‑antibody titres, inflammatory cytokines, and complement fragments. In thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) the main driver is an acquired ADAMTS13 auto‑antibody; PEX removes the inhibitor and simultaneously delivers exogenous functional ADAMTS13, providing a clear therapeutic benefit. In CM‑HUS, PEX is not curative because disease is rooted in intrinsic dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway, but it can transiently lower soluble C5b‑9 and other downstream effectors. This can abort ongoing microvascular injury and may allow time for conventional antibiotics to eradicate the inciting pathogen.

In the present patient, PEX was commenced immediately after the clinical suspicion of TMA was confirmed—while the ADAMTS13 activity was still pending—to avoid irreversible renal ischemia. Three sequential exchanges with Octaplas were performed before the first dose of eculizumab. The patient’s diuresis returned shortly after the third PEX session, but full hematologic remission and stable kidney function were only achieved after complement blockade, underscoring the temporary benefit of plasma exchange for CM‑HUS.

The timing of PEX relative to eculizumab is therefore critical: PEX may be used in the acute phase when rapid control of microthrombosis is needed or when the diagnosis is still equivocal (e.g., a suspicion of TTP). Once a definitive diagnosis of CM‑HUS is established, eculizumab should be prioritized, as plasma exchange offers no durable disease control.

### Eculizumab dosing strategy

Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that binds C5 and prevents the assembly of the membrane attack complex (MAC), is the standard of care for CM‑HUS. In this patient, the dosing regimen followed the institutional protocol derived from the randomized trials: a 900‑mg induction course administered weekly for four weeks, giving a total of 3 600 mg, followed by a maintenance dose of 1 200 mg every two weeks. This schedule maintains trough concentrations of the drug sufficient to block terminal complement activation while limiting drug exposure.

The induction dose was initiated after the last PEX and once blood cultures were negative, thereby reducing the risk of potentiating an active infection. The subsequent maintenance phase is normally continued indefinitely in patients with underlying complement gene mutations; however, in patients with infection‑triggered CM‑HUS and no pathogenic complement variants, evidence suggests that therapy may be discontinued once the patient has remained in remission for an adequate period. In this case, maintenance eculizumab was planned for four months, with a planned reassessment based on serial laboratory monitoring and clinical status.

### Infectious risks of terminal complement inhibition

Inhibition of C5 abolishes the formation of the MAC, a key mechanism for opsonizing encapsulated bacteria such as **Neisseria meningitidis**, **Streptococcus pneumoniae**, **Haemophilus influenzae** type b, and **Capnocytophaga** species. Consequently, eculizumab therapy renders patients susceptible to life‑threatening meningococcal infections. The risk is highest during the first days of therapy, when complement blockade is established before the adaptive immune response can compensate.

Real‑world data in CM‑HUS patients treated with eculizumab show a markedly reduced incidence of meningococcal disease when patients receive pre‑emptive vaccination and appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis. The vaccine coverage covering serogroups A, C, Y, W, and B is essential because alternative complement activation remains intact against non‑meningococcal organisms.

### Vaccination strategy

The patient received the conjugate Menveo vaccine (serogroups A, C, Y, W) and the Bexsero vaccine (serogroup B) 2 weeks before the initiation of eculizumab. This pre‑emptive schedule conforms to CDC and NICE recommendations for patients receiving terminal complement inhibition. The timing maximized immune response to the polysaccharide antigens at the time C5 blockade would be operative. The second dose of each vaccine was planned for eight weeks after completing the induction phase, an interval at which the protective antibody titres are still robust but the drug exposure is lower.

### Antibiotic prophylaxis and monitoring of bacteremia

Because the terminal complement system plays a pivotal role in phagocytosis of encapsulated bacteria, eculizumab represents a relative contraindication to ongoing bacteremia. Here, antibiotic therapy was certainly effective, as repeat blood cultures turned negative before eculizumab initiation. To further safeguard against occult infection, the patient received prolonged oral penicillin V (Apocillin) prophylaxis throughout the eculizumab course, a regimen supported by the fact that prophylaxis with penicillins is standard for patients on complement inhibitors. Serial blood cultures were repeated during the first week after eculizumab induction to confirm the absence of persistent bacteraemia.

### Monitoring response and disease activity

The team used a tight monitoring schedule to gauge therapeutic response and to detect relapse promptly:

1. **Hematology** – Daily counts of haemoglobin, platelets, and white blood cells, coupled with repeat reticulocyte counts. A drop in haemoglobin without bleeding, a persistently low platelet count and the presence of schistocytes provide objective evidence of ongoing microangiopathy.

2. **Hemolysis markers** – Lactate dehydrogenase, haptoglobin, bilirubin, and indirect Coombs testing. Re‑negative DAT after the second test helped rule out simple autoimmune hemolysis.

3. **Renal function** – Serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate were measured twice weekly. Urine output was also tracked daily; the recovery of spontaneous diuresis after the final plasma exchange session was regarded as an early sign of improvement, but persistent anuria suggested that further complement blockade was warranted.

4. **Complement activity** – Functional assays of the alternative pathway (AH50) and levels of individual complement factors were monitored at intervals after the final PEX and eculizumab administrations. A decrease in AH50 to 6 % reflected the impact of the therapy, although this is expected and does not signify disease activity. Restoring alternative pathway activity is only associated with a return of disease activity rather than a baseline physiological level.

All these laboratory trends were cross‑referenced with the clinical picture to draw a composite picture of therapeutic efficacy and to inform decisions about maintenance therapy.

### Decision‑making regarding continuation or discontinuation

When CM‑HUS has no known genetic or auto‑inflammatory predisposition, the risk–benefit analysis for maintenance eculizumab hinges on renal recovery, persistent complement dysregulation, and the potential for relapse. In this patient, the partial improvement of renal function (creatinine 175 µmol/L) to near‑baseline levels, combined with normalization of hematologic parameters and the absence of any underlying complement mutations on extended testing, set the stage for a considerated taper.

Clinicians must weigh the probability of relapse against the cost, infusion burden, and infectious risk of ongoing eculizumab. The chosen strategy was to maintain the bi‑weekly infusion for four months while performing rigorous surveillance as outlined above. Should the patient remain stable, a step‑down protocol—reduced frequency or eventual cessation—could be followed, with a two‑month “watch‑and‑wait” period before deciding conclusively. Any signs of hemolysis, thrombocytopenia, or rising creatinine would prompt rapid resumption of therapy.

In sum, the therapeutic journey in this case underscores a dynamic interplay between plasma exchange and complement blockade, infection control, and vigilant serial monitoring. The nuanced decision‑making—balancing rapid complement inhibition against infection risk, a disease caused by an infection—offers a template for managing similarly complex presentations of CM‑HUS in an immunocompetent host.
