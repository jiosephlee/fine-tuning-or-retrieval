Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 2: ## Differential Diagnosis of Hematologic and Coagulopathic Findings

The patient’s clinical evolution—severe sepsis following a dog bite, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, acute kidney injury (AKI), and later a sudden fall in hemoglobin with schistocytes and ongoing thrombocytopenia—raised an array of possible etiologies. In an intensive‐care setting, the workup must proceed systematically: begin with the most common trigger for these laboratory abnormalities (sepsis‑related disseminated intravascular coagulation, DIC), then rule out the rare but potentially life‑threatening processes that can present with similar findings, namely acute leukemia, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), Shiga toxin–producing *E. coli* HUS (STEC‑HUS), complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) and other thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs), and finally autoimmune cytopenias such as immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP). The decisions that guide this hierarchy rest on the patient’s clinical course, laboratory data collected within 24–48 h, and evolving findings in the setting of improving infection.

### Sepsis‑associated DIC and multiorgan failure as baseline

Sepsis is the most frequent precipitant of DIC, and the interplay between bacterial toxins, massive cytokine release and intravascular coagulation explains the early burst of pancytopenia and coagulopathy. In this patient, admission labs showed thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, elevated D‑dimer, and evidence of organ dysfunction (acidosis, lactic acidosis, rising creatinine). These findings strongly supported diagnosis of sepsis‑associated DIC per the ISTH scoring system. Importantly, the DIC was “baseline” in that it mapped to the early septic phase, and the coagulopathy and low platelets improved after appropriate antibiotic therapy and supportive measures. However, as the septic shock resolved—evidenced by normalization of inflammatory markers, stable vitals, and return of stool leaks—the patient’s thrombocytopenia recurred without coagulopathic changes. This divergence suggested an alternate process superimposed on the initial DIC.

### Acute leukemia

Acute leukemia remains a primary differential for unexplained pancytopenia. The clinical clues include sustained severe neutropenia, blasts on peripheral smear, and a potential splenomegaly or hepatomegaly. In this case, peripheral smear showed no blasts or dysplastic cells. Flow cytometry of peripheral blood did not reveal any aberrant immunophenotype, and the patient’s bone marrow aspirate was ultimately reported normal—no increase in CD34⁺ blasts or abnormal lineage markers. These data effectively exclude an acute leukemic process despite the early overt cytopenia.

### Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH)

HLH is characterized by hyperinflammation, fever, pancytopenia, high ferritin, hypertriglyceridemia, hypofibrinogenemia, splenomegaly, and evidence of hemophagocytosis on bone marrow or lymph node biopsy. A formal HLH‑2004 score or HScore greater than 200 raises suspicion. The patient’s presentation (fever, cytopenias, low fibrinogen) initially could mimic HLH, but laboratory workup did not meet the threshold: ferritin was not markedly elevated, triglycerides remained within normal limits, and soluble IL‑2 receptor (sCD25) was not elevated (the latter was not measured, but would be abnormal in HLH). The resolution of fever with antibiotics and the lack of bone marrow hemophagocytosis further argued against HLH.

### Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)

TTP is an immune or hereditary deficiency of ADAMTS13, typically measured as activity <10 %, and presents with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, neurologic symptoms, renal dysfunction, and fever. The PLASMIC score (platelet count, hemolysis, absence of active malignancy, serologic evidence, etc.) aids pre‑test probability. In this case, the PLASMIC score was 4/7, indicating low pre‑test probability. Subsequent ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5 %, well above the cutoff that would implicate TTP. Therefore, TTP was ruled out, and plasma exchange (PEX) was pursued as a bridging strategy rather than definitive therapy for TTP.

### Shiga toxin–producing *E. coli* HUS (STEC‑HUS)

STEC‑HUS typically follows a diarrheal prodrome, often hemolytic, and is confirmed by stool PCR for Shiga toxin genes. The patient had no gastrointestinal symptoms and a negative stool PCR. The absence of this defining exposure and laboratory confirmation excluded STEC‑HUS.

### Complement‑mediated HUS and other thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs)

CM‑HUS (formerly atypical HUS) arises from dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway, often in a patient with an underlying genetic or acquired complement regulatory defect. The clinical hallmarks overlap with other TMAs—MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI—but differentiate by the absence of a Shiga toxin trigger and persistent complement activation despite resolution of the initial insult. In this patient, the sudden decline in hemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin, rising lactate dehydrogenase, and a smear showing 15–20 % schistocytes established ongoing MAHA. Blood cultures had cleared, and coagulation studies had normalized, reinforcing that the DIC had resolved. A kidney biopsy revealed acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and crescents—pathognomonic of TMA. Complement studies revealed normal C3/C4 levels (which may remain within normal limits in active CM‑HUS), normal classical, lectin, and alternative pathway functional assays initially, but later reduced alternative pathway activity after treatment, consistent with disease activity rather than a baseline defect. ADAMTS13 activity and exclusion of STEC-HUS directed the diagnosis toward complement‑mediated TMA. The rapid improvement in haematological parameters after initiation of eculizumab further corroborated this diagnosis.

### Autoimmune thrombocytopenia and other immune‑mediated cytopenias

Autoimmune cytopenias such as immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) and autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) can cause isolated platelet or red blood cell loss. In this case, an initial double‑tube Coombs test was positive for IgG, raising suspicion of AIHA; however, repeated testing was negative. The temporal pattern of hemolysis—appearing after the peak of infection—alongside persistent thrombocytopenia despite platelet transfusion also suggested a process other than isolated ITP. Serologic investigation for antinuclear antibodies and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies was negative, further reducing the likelihood of autoimmune hemolysis triggered by systemic infection or drug exposure.

---

**Integration and Conclusion**

Systematic evaluation of the patient’s hematologic and coagulation abnormalities began with the most common trigger—sepsis‑associated DIC. When the disease course deviated (persistent thrombocytopenia and hemolysis after the resolution of sepsis and coagulopathy), a targeted workup excluded acute leukemia, HLH, TTP, STEC‑HUS, and autoimmune cytopenias. Remaining evidence—schistocytosis, MAHA, persistent thrombocytopenia, renal biopsy consistent with TMA, and a negative ADAMTS13 assay—favored a diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS. Prompt initiation of complement blockade with eculizumab led to hematologic remission and partial renal recovery. This case illustrates the importance of a structured differential, the value of serial laboratory monitoring, and the necessity of correlating laboratory data with the evolving clinical picture to identify a treatable cause of TMA in the context of severe infection.
