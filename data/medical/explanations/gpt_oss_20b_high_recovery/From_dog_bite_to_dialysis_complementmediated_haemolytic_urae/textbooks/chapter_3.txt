Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup for Complement‑Mediated TMA

The patient’s clinical course crossed the threshold from an uncomplicated septic patient into a distinctive haematological syndrome when her haemoglobin fell sharply while invasive investigations and support were already underway. The ensuing diagnostic workup combined routine laboratory indices with targeted assays that distinguish complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (CM‑HUS) from other thrombotic microangiopathies—most notably thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and Shiga toxin–producing‑E coli HUS (STEC‑HUS). The sequence of investigations reflected the urgency of the situation and the overlap of clinical features among the TMA spectrum.

### Microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA)

The first indication of a TMA was a rapid decline in haemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days) alongside an absent bleed‑source. Immediate assessment focused on markers of intravascular haemolysis: lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) rose markedly, haptoglobin vanished, and indirect bilirubin increased. The hallmark of MAHA—a dislodged red‑cell fragment (schistocyte)—was identified on a peripheral smear, where 15–20 % of erythrocytes showed characteristic shards. The constellation of tachy‑bilirubinemia, hyper‑LDH, low haptoglobin, and schistocytosis established the diagnosis of schistocytic haemolysis, the first step toward excluding other causes of thrombocytopenia.

### Direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and false‑positive concerns

The initial DAT was positive for IgG anti‑erythrocyte antibodies. In the context of a recent penicillin prescription and blood transfusions, a false‑positive DAT is common, as penicillin induces IgG binding to red‑cell antigens and transfused red cells may carry allo‑antibodies. The DAT margin was therefore re‑evaluated by repeating the test after transfusions and after a wash in the setting of the patient’s current clinical state; the result returned negative, excluding autoimmune haemolytic anaemia as the source of haemolysis.

### ADAMTS13 activity threshold for TTP exclusion

Given the presence of schistocytes and thrombocytopenia, ruling out TTP is paramount, because the therapeutic approach differs radically. ADAMTS13 activity was measured immediately before the first plasma‑exchange session and found to be 43.5 %. In TTP, activity is almost universally <10 % (often <5 %). Because the patient’s value was significantly above this limit and his PLASMIC score was low (4/7), TTP was excluded, directing the work‑up toward complement‑mediated disease.

### STEC‑HUS exclusion via negative stool PCR

STEC‑HUS is common in the young but can occur in adults. A stool PCR panel targeting Shiga toxin genes was negative, and there was no history of diarrhoea; thus, the diagnosis of STEC‑HUS was excluded.

### Functional complement tests: C3/C4, AH50 alternative‑pathway activity

CM‑HUS results from dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway. Conventional quantification of C3 and C4 can be normal, as consumption does not always deplete these proteins, especially in an acute infection. In this patient, serum C3/C4 levels were within reference limits at the time of testing. To capture functional deficiency, an AH50 assay (measuring alternative‑pathway activity) was performed after the final plasma‑exchange session and the first dose of complement blockade. The alternative‑pathway activity was markedly reduced (6 %), compatible with either acute complement consumption or therapeutic suppression. Because the assay was performed after plasma removal and eculizumab, the pronounced reduction likely reflected therapy rather than intrinsic dysfunction; nevertheless, the abnormal result supported the diagnosis of a complement‑driven TMA.

### Kidney biopsy findings: acute tubular necrosis, intravascular fibrin thrombi, crescentic lesions consistent with TMA

Renal pathology is integral when TMA is suspected. The biopsy displayed severe acute tubular necrosis, a finding common in sepsis yet nonspecific, but was accompanied by intravascular fibrin thrombi within glomerular capillaries and scattered parietal epithelial cell crescents. Electron microscopy showed no evidence of immune‑complex deposition, and immunofluorescence was unremarkable, excluding other glomerular diseases. The combination of thrombi, crescents, and necrosis—without immune deposits—arched the diagnosis toward CM‑HUS.

### Algorithm for initiating plasma exchange and planning eculizumab

The diagnostic algorithm that guided management integrated these findings:

1. **Suspect TMA** when haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and organ dysfunction coexist.
2. **Order hemolysis panel, DAT, peripheral smear**. A positive DAT is reassessed if recent antibiotics or transfusion are present.
3. **Measure ADAMTS13 activity**; a value >10 % rules out TTP.
4. **Exclude STEC‑HUS** with stool PCR; a negative result and absence of diarrhoea diminish this diagnosis.
5. **Obtain serum complement** (C3/C4) and functional alternative‑pathway assay (AH50). Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS; a reduced AH50 is suggestive but may reflect therapy.
6. **If plasma exchange is feasible**, initiate it urgently in patients with severe organ failure or when TTP cannot be excluded. In this case, plasma exchange was started before the eculizumab infusion and while awaiting definitive ADAMTS13 results.
7. **Confirm CM‑HUS** with the convergence of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, renal biopsy findings consistent with TMA, and exclusion of TTP and STEC‑HUS.
8. **Begin eculizumab** (900 mg IV weekly for four weeks) only after the diagnosis of CM‑HUS is secured and, when the patient’s infection is controlled. In this patient, eculizumab was commenced following conversion to negative cultures, proper vaccination, and completion of at least two plasma‑exchange sessions.

This systematic approach ensured that the patient was not subjected to unnecessary plasma exchange or delayed complement blockade, thereby optimizing the potential for both haematologic remission and renal recovery.
