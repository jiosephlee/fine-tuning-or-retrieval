Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 4: ## Pathophysiology of CM‑HUS and the Role of Capnocytophaga

Complement activation is a central pillar of innate immunity, with the classical, lectin and alternative pathways converging on the cleavage of C3 and the eventual formation of the membrane‑attack complex (MAC). In the alternative pathway, surface‑bound C3b associates with factor B and, after cleavage by factor D, forms the C3 convertase C3bBb. This complex is rapidly amplified but is normally limited by regulatory proteins: factor H competes with factor B for C3b binding and recruits factor I to cleave and inactivate C3b, factor I itself degrades C3b and iC3b, and CD46 (membrane cofactor protein) serves as a cofactor for factor I on host cell surfaces. Adequate regulation prevents host cell membrane damage and permits controlled complement amplification in response to pathogens. Genetic variants in CFH, CFI, CFB, and CD46 have been identified in up to 70 % of patients with complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS); however, disease expression is incompletely penetrant and typically requires an external trigger—the “second hit”—such as infection, pregnancy or medication.

In CM‑HUS, a precipitating “second hit” overwhelms the already‑dysregulated alternative pathway, leading to excessive C3 convertase activity and uncontrolled downstream complement activation. The resulting release of anaphylatoxins (C3a, C5a), recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages, and deposition of MAC damage the endothelium of small vessels. Activated endothelial cells express pro‑coagulant and anti‑fibrinolytic factors, recruit platelets, and promote micro‑thrombus formation—manifesting clinically as microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia and acute kidney injury.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus, a gram‑negative rod residing in canine oral flora, can trigger this cascade. Its lipopolysaccharide and surface structures activate complement, but the bacterium’s polysaccharide capsule confers variable sensitivity: capsule‑expressing isolates may resist classical and mannose‑binding lectin pathways yet remain susceptible to alternative‑pathway attack. In most hosts, early complement activation facilitates bacterial clearance. However, in an under‑regulated alternative pathway, the robust complement response can paradoxically shift from a defensive reaction to a pathological one, precipitating endothelial injury and TMA. This dual nature explains why patients with C. canimorsus sepsis may develop DIC initially and later CM‑HUS once the infection subsides or antibiotic therapy dampens the primary inflammatory drive but leaves complement unchecked.

Clinical reports linking C. canimorsus to CM‑HUS are sparse but compelling. Our patient, for example, displayed classic features of CM‑HUS—persistent thrombocytopenia, schistocytosis, hemolytic anemia and severe renal failure—after a bout of Capnocytophaga bacteraemia. Comprehensive complement testing revealed normal C3/C4 levels and abolition of alternative‑pathway activity only after plasma exchange and eculizumab; extended genetic screening found no pathogenic variants in CFH, CFI, CFB or CD46. These findings support an infection‑driven “second hit” model rather than a pre‑existing hereditary complement disorder. In contrast, a handful of other cases have identified factor‑B or factor‑H variants, underscoring the heterogeneity of triggers and underlying susceptibility. Nonetheless, the recurrence of CM‑HUS in the setting of C. canimorsus infection, even when complement genetics are unremarkable, reinforces the necessity of considering this zoonotic pathogen as a cause of complement‑mediated TMA in otherwise healthy adults.
