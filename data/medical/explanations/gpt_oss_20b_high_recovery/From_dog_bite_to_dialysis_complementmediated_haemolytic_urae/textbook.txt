Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Sepsis Management

The index patient, a previously healthy woman in her late forties, presented to the emergency department (ED) with a rapidly evolving constellation of systemic illness that had begun four days after a dog bite. She had received tetanus prophylaxis on the day of the bite but, because the prescription for phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was not collected, no antimicrobial therapy was started. The patient was otherwise aster‑individual without chronic disease or significant medication use.

### Symptom evolution

On the fourth post‑bite day the patient noted a high fever (temperatures > 40 °C) followed by malaise and anorexia. Within 24 h she developed numbness and discoloration of the feet, prompting a visit to a primary‑care urgent‑care centre. There, she was found to be hypoglycaemic (glucose nadir 0.6 mmol/L) and refractory to both oral glucose and intramuscular glucagon. She was subsequently transferred to the tertiary ED, where her condition had progressed to overt sepsis with features of septic shock and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).

### Physical examination and early assessment

On arrival she was alert and oriented, yet she appeared acutely ill: her skin over the lower extremities was mottled and cool, suggestive of peripheral hypoperfusion. Vital signs indicated a rapid heart rate, a mildly reduced systolic blood pressure (< 100 mm Hg), a respiratory rate exceeding 22 breaths/min, and a temperature of > 40 °C. Her mental status remained normal, and peripheral pulses were palpable. Using the quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score, she had one positive criterion (either altered mentation, hypotension, or tachypnea) – a score of 1/3 – which prompted the recognition of possible septic shock.

### Initial laboratory profile

The first laboratory data set revealed profound systemic derangement. The complete blood count showed pancytopenia (anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia). Metabolic assessment from arterial blood gas demonstrated a pH of 7.27 indicative of metabolic acidosis and a markedly elevated lactate (5.7 mmol/L) – an established marker of tissue hypoxia and poor perfusion. Renal function was impaired, with an acute rise in serum creatinine, and liver enzymes (AST/ALT) were elevated, consistent with multiorgan involvement. Coagulation studies revealed a prolonged prothrombin time, elevated activated partial thromboplastin time, low fibrinogen, and a pronounced increase in D‑dimer level, hallmarks of the consumptive coagulopathy seen in sepsis‑induced DIC. Urinalysis identified proteinuria and hematuria, indicating renal endothelial injury. A full haemogram, electrolyte panel, and liver panel supported a picture of systemic inflammation with marrow suppression and endothelial activation.

### Early differential diagnosis

The constellation of fever, pancytopenia, DIC, and acute kidney injury raised several potential etiologies. The most pressing diagnosis was sepsis with multiorgan failure secondary to bacterial infection, a likely scenario given the recent dog bite. However, the presence of extreme leukopenia and profound thrombocytopenia also warranted a broadened differential that included acute leukemia, haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), and thrombotic microangiopathies such as thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) or hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS). Accordingly, early hematologic consultation was obtained to exclude malignant or immune‑mediated bone‑marrow disorders.

### Initial management of presumed septic shock

Immediate treatment was guided by Surviving Sepsis Campaign recommendations. The first priority was rapid fluid resuscitation: crystalloid boluses were administered to achieve adequate organ perfusion, as evidenced by a rising diuresis and the normalization of lactate in the early hours. Broad‑spectrum antimicrobial coverage was instituted with intravenous piperacillin‑tazobactam, a regimen that provides coverage for gram‑positive, gram‑negative, and anaerobic organisms commonly implicated in dog‑bite pharyngitis or soft‑tissue infections, including Capnocytophaga species.

Supportive therapy included stress‑dose intravenous corticosteroids (hydrocortisone 50 mg q6 h) to mitigate adrenal insufficiency that can accompany severe sepsis. In parallel, platelet and fibrinogen concentrates were transfused to correct consumptive coagulopathy, as the patient’s platelet count fell below critical thresholds and her fibrinogen level was low, risking uncontrolled bleeding. The patient also received intravenous glucose infusion to counter the severe hypoglycaemia, which is a frequent complication in septic patients due to hepatic dysfunction, increased glucose utilization, and dysregulation of counter‑regulatory hormones.

Throughout the first hours of care, she remained hemodynamically stable without the need for vasopressors; no sign of refractory hypotension emerged. Clinical observation and laboratory monitoring were intensified as the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer surveillance. At that point, her systemic inflammatory markers continued to rise, confirming the persistence of an infectious source.

The early recognition and aggressive treatment of septic shock and DIC not only stabilized the patient’s hemodynamic status but also allowed for the identification and isolation of the causative organism, later confirmed as Capnocytophaga canimorsus in blood culture. Subsequent antibiotic de‑escalation became possible once susceptibility data were available. The meticulous multidisciplinary approach, coupling early source control, hemodynamic support, and organ‑protective measures, set the stage for the subsequent critical steps in the patient’s complex course.

## Differential Diagnosis of Hematologic and Coagulopathic Findings

The patient’s clinical evolution—severe sepsis following a dog bite, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, acute kidney injury (AKI), and later a sudden fall in hemoglobin with schistocytes and ongoing thrombocytopenia—raised an array of possible etiologies. In an intensive‐care setting, the workup must proceed systematically: begin with the most common trigger for these laboratory abnormalities (sepsis‑related disseminated intravascular coagulation, DIC), then rule out the rare but potentially life‑threatening processes that can present with similar findings, namely acute leukemia, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), Shiga toxin–producing *E. coli* HUS (STEC‑HUS), complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) and other thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs), and finally autoimmune cytopenias such as immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP). The decisions that guide this hierarchy rest on the patient’s clinical course, laboratory data collected within 24–48 h, and evolving findings in the setting of improving infection.

### Sepsis‑associated DIC and multiorgan failure as baseline

Sepsis is the most frequent precipitant of DIC, and the interplay between bacterial toxins, massive cytokine release and intravascular coagulation explains the early burst of pancytopenia and coagulopathy. In this patient, admission labs showed thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, elevated D‑dimer, and evidence of organ dysfunction (acidosis, lactic acidosis, rising creatinine). These findings strongly supported diagnosis of sepsis‑associated DIC per the ISTH scoring system. Importantly, the DIC was “baseline” in that it mapped to the early septic phase, and the coagulopathy and low platelets improved after appropriate antibiotic therapy and supportive measures. However, as the septic shock resolved—evidenced by normalization of inflammatory markers, stable vitals, and return of stool leaks—the patient’s thrombocytopenia recurred without coagulopathic changes. This divergence suggested an alternate process superimposed on the initial DIC.

### Acute leukemia

Acute leukemia remains a primary differential for unexplained pancytopenia. The clinical clues include sustained severe neutropenia, blasts on peripheral smear, and a potential splenomegaly or hepatomegaly. In this case, peripheral smear showed no blasts or dysplastic cells. Flow cytometry of peripheral blood did not reveal any aberrant immunophenotype, and the patient’s bone marrow aspirate was ultimately reported normal—no increase in CD34⁺ blasts or abnormal lineage markers. These data effectively exclude an acute leukemic process despite the early overt cytopenia.

### Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH)

HLH is characterized by hyperinflammation, fever, pancytopenia, high ferritin, hypertriglyceridemia, hypofibrinogenemia, splenomegaly, and evidence of hemophagocytosis on bone marrow or lymph node biopsy. A formal HLH‑2004 score or HScore greater than 200 raises suspicion. The patient’s presentation (fever, cytopenias, low fibrinogen) initially could mimic HLH, but laboratory workup did not meet the threshold: ferritin was not markedly elevated, triglycerides remained within normal limits, and soluble IL‑2 receptor (sCD25) was not elevated (the latter was not measured, but would be abnormal in HLH). The resolution of fever with antibiotics and the lack of bone marrow hemophagocytosis further argued against HLH.

### Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)

TTP is an immune or hereditary deficiency of ADAMTS13, typically measured as activity <10 %, and presents with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, neurologic symptoms, renal dysfunction, and fever. The PLASMIC score (platelet count, hemolysis, absence of active malignancy, serologic evidence, etc.) aids pre‑test probability. In this case, the PLASMIC score was 4/7, indicating low pre‑test probability. Subsequent ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5 %, well above the cutoff that would implicate TTP. Therefore, TTP was ruled out, and plasma exchange (PEX) was pursued as a bridging strategy rather than definitive therapy for TTP.

### Shiga toxin–producing *E. coli* HUS (STEC‑HUS)

STEC‑HUS typically follows a diarrheal prodrome, often hemolytic, and is confirmed by stool PCR for Shiga toxin genes. The patient had no gastrointestinal symptoms and a negative stool PCR. The absence of this defining exposure and laboratory confirmation excluded STEC‑HUS.

### Complement‑mediated HUS and other thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs)

CM‑HUS (formerly atypical HUS) arises from dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway, often in a patient with an underlying genetic or acquired complement regulatory defect. The clinical hallmarks overlap with other TMAs—MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI—but differentiate by the absence of a Shiga toxin trigger and persistent complement activation despite resolution of the initial insult. In this patient, the sudden decline in hemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin, rising lactate dehydrogenase, and a smear showing 15–20 % schistocytes established ongoing MAHA. Blood cultures had cleared, and coagulation studies had normalized, reinforcing that the DIC had resolved. A kidney biopsy revealed acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and crescents—pathognomonic of TMA. Complement studies revealed normal C3/C4 levels (which may remain within normal limits in active CM‑HUS), normal classical, lectin, and alternative pathway functional assays initially, but later reduced alternative pathway activity after treatment, consistent with disease activity rather than a baseline defect. ADAMTS13 activity and exclusion of STEC-HUS directed the diagnosis toward complement‑mediated TMA. The rapid improvement in haematological parameters after initiation of eculizumab further corroborated this diagnosis.

### Autoimmune thrombocytopenia and other immune‑mediated cytopenias

Autoimmune cytopenias such as immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) and autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) can cause isolated platelet or red blood cell loss. In this case, an initial double‑tube Coombs test was positive for IgG, raising suspicion of AIHA; however, repeated testing was negative. The temporal pattern of hemolysis—appearing after the peak of infection—alongside persistent thrombocytopenia despite platelet transfusion also suggested a process other than isolated ITP. Serologic investigation for antinuclear antibodies and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies was negative, further reducing the likelihood of autoimmune hemolysis triggered by systemic infection or drug exposure.

**Integration and Conclusion**

Systematic evaluation of the patient’s hematologic and coagulation abnormalities began with the most common trigger—sepsis‑associated DIC. When the disease course deviated (persistent thrombocytopenia and hemolysis after the resolution of sepsis and coagulopathy), a targeted workup excluded acute leukemia, HLH, TTP, STEC‑HUS, and autoimmune cytopenias. Remaining evidence—schistocytosis, MAHA, persistent thrombocytopenia, renal biopsy consistent with TMA, and a negative ADAMTS13 assay—favored a diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS. Prompt initiation of complement blockade with eculizumab led to hematologic remission and partial renal recovery. This case illustrates the importance of a structured differential, the value of serial laboratory monitoring, and the necessity of correlating laboratory data with the evolving clinical picture to identify a treatable cause of TMA in the context of severe infection.

## Diagnostic Workup for Complement‑Mediated TMA

The patient’s clinical course crossed the threshold from an uncomplicated septic patient into a distinctive haematological syndrome when her haemoglobin fell sharply while invasive investigations and support were already underway. The ensuing diagnostic workup combined routine laboratory indices with targeted assays that distinguish complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (CM‑HUS) from other thrombotic microangiopathies—most notably thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and Shiga toxin–producing‑E coli HUS (STEC‑HUS). The sequence of investigations reflected the urgency of the situation and the overlap of clinical features among the TMA spectrum.

### Microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA)

The first indication of a TMA was a rapid decline in haemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days) alongside an absent bleed‑source. Immediate assessment focused on markers of intravascular haemolysis: lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) rose markedly, haptoglobin vanished, and indirect bilirubin increased. The hallmark of MAHA—a dislodged red‑cell fragment (schistocyte)—was identified on a peripheral smear, where 15–20 % of erythrocytes showed characteristic shards. The constellation of tachy‑bilirubinemia, hyper‑LDH, low haptoglobin, and schistocytosis established the diagnosis of schistocytic haemolysis, the first step toward excluding other causes of thrombocytopenia.

### Direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and false‑positive concerns

The initial DAT was positive for IgG anti‑erythrocyte antibodies. In the context of a recent penicillin prescription and blood transfusions, a false‑positive DAT is common, as penicillin induces IgG binding to red‑cell antigens and transfused red cells may carry allo‑antibodies. The DAT margin was therefore re‑evaluated by repeating the test after transfusions and after a wash in the setting of the patient’s current clinical state; the result returned negative, excluding autoimmune haemolytic anaemia as the source of haemolysis.

### ADAMTS13 activity threshold for TTP exclusion

Given the presence of schistocytes and thrombocytopenia, ruling out TTP is paramount, because the therapeutic approach differs radically. ADAMTS13 activity was measured immediately before the first plasma‑exchange session and found to be 43.5 %. In TTP, activity is almost universally <10 % (often <5 %). Because the patient’s value was significantly above this limit and his PLASMIC score was low (4/7), TTP was excluded, directing the work‑up toward complement‑mediated disease.

### STEC‑HUS exclusion via negative stool PCR

STEC‑HUS is common in the young but can occur in adults. A stool PCR panel targeting Shiga toxin genes was negative, and there was no history of diarrhoea; thus, the diagnosis of STEC‑HUS was excluded.

### Functional complement tests: C3/C4, AH50 alternative‑pathway activity

CM‑HUS results from dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway. Conventional quantification of C3 and C4 can be normal, as consumption does not always deplete these proteins, especially in an acute infection. In this patient, serum C3/C4 levels were within reference limits at the time of testing. To capture functional deficiency, an AH50 assay (measuring alternative‑pathway activity) was performed after the final plasma‑exchange session and the first dose of complement blockade. The alternative‑pathway activity was markedly reduced (6 %), compatible with either acute complement consumption or therapeutic suppression. Because the assay was performed after plasma removal and eculizumab, the pronounced reduction likely reflected therapy rather than intrinsic dysfunction; nevertheless, the abnormal result supported the diagnosis of a complement‑driven TMA.

### Kidney biopsy findings: acute tubular necrosis, intravascular fibrin thrombi, crescentic lesions consistent with TMA

Renal pathology is integral when TMA is suspected. The biopsy displayed severe acute tubular necrosis, a finding common in sepsis yet nonspecific, but was accompanied by intravascular fibrin thrombi within glomerular capillaries and scattered parietal epithelial cell crescents. Electron microscopy showed no evidence of immune‑complex deposition, and immunofluorescence was unremarkable, excluding other glomerular diseases. The combination of thrombi, crescents, and necrosis—without immune deposits—arched the diagnosis toward CM‑HUS.

### Algorithm for initiating plasma exchange and planning eculizumab

The diagnostic algorithm that guided management integrated these findings:

1. **Suspect TMA** when haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and organ dysfunction coexist.
2. **Order hemolysis panel, DAT, peripheral smear**. A positive DAT is reassessed if recent antibiotics or transfusion are present.
3. **Measure ADAMTS13 activity**; a value >10 % rules out TTP.
4. **Exclude STEC‑HUS** with stool PCR; a negative result and absence of diarrhoea diminish this diagnosis.
5. **Obtain serum complement** (C3/C4) and functional alternative‑pathway assay (AH50). Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS; a reduced AH50 is suggestive but may reflect therapy.
6. **If plasma exchange is feasible**, initiate it urgently in patients with severe organ failure or when TTP cannot be excluded. In this case, plasma exchange was started before the eculizumab infusion and while awaiting definitive ADAMTS13 results.
7. **Confirm CM‑HUS** with the convergence of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, renal biopsy findings consistent with TMA, and exclusion of TTP and STEC‑HUS.
8. **Begin eculizumab** (900 mg IV weekly for four weeks) only after the diagnosis of CM‑HUS is secured and, when the patient’s infection is controlled. In this patient, eculizumab was commenced following conversion to negative cultures, proper vaccination, and completion of at least two plasma‑exchange sessions.

This systematic approach ensured that the patient was not subjected to unnecessary plasma exchange or delayed complement blockade, thereby optimizing the potential for both haematologic remission and renal recovery.

## Pathophysiology of CM‑HUS and the Role of Capnocytophaga

Complement activation is a central pillar of innate immunity, with the classical, lectin and alternative pathways converging on the cleavage of C3 and the eventual formation of the membrane‑attack complex (MAC). In the alternative pathway, surface‑bound C3b associates with factor B and, after cleavage by factor D, forms the C3 convertase C3bBb. This complex is rapidly amplified but is normally limited by regulatory proteins: factor H competes with factor B for C3b binding and recruits factor I to cleave and inactivate C3b, factor I itself degrades C3b and iC3b, and CD46 (membrane cofactor protein) serves as a cofactor for factor I on host cell surfaces. Adequate regulation prevents host cell membrane damage and permits controlled complement amplification in response to pathogens. Genetic variants in CFH, CFI, CFB, and CD46 have been identified in up to 70 % of patients with complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS); however, disease expression is incompletely penetrant and typically requires an external trigger—the “second hit”—such as infection, pregnancy or medication.

In CM‑HUS, a precipitating “second hit” overwhelms the already‑dysregulated alternative pathway, leading to excessive C3 convertase activity and uncontrolled downstream complement activation. The resulting release of anaphylatoxins (C3a, C5a), recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages, and deposition of MAC damage the endothelium of small vessels. Activated endothelial cells express pro‑coagulant and anti‑fibrinolytic factors, recruit platelets, and promote micro‑thrombus formation—manifesting clinically as microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia and acute kidney injury.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus, a gram‑negative rod residing in canine oral flora, can trigger this cascade. Its lipopolysaccharide and surface structures activate complement, but the bacterium’s polysaccharide capsule confers variable sensitivity: capsule‑expressing isolates may resist classical and mannose‑binding lectin pathways yet remain susceptible to alternative‑pathway attack. In most hosts, early complement activation facilitates bacterial clearance. However, in an under‑regulated alternative pathway, the robust complement response can paradoxically shift from a defensive reaction to a pathological one, precipitating endothelial injury and TMA. This dual nature explains why patients with C. canimorsus sepsis may develop DIC initially and later CM‑HUS once the infection subsides or antibiotic therapy dampens the primary inflammatory drive but leaves complement unchecked.

Clinical reports linking C. canimorsus to CM‑HUS are sparse but compelling. Our patient, for example, displayed classic features of CM‑HUS—persistent thrombocytopenia, schistocytosis, hemolytic anemia and severe renal failure—after a bout of Capnocytophaga bacteraemia. Comprehensive complement testing revealed normal C3/C4 levels and abolition of alternative‑pathway activity only after plasma exchange and eculizumab; extended genetic screening found no pathogenic variants in CFH, CFI, CFB or CD46. These findings support an infection‑driven “second hit” model rather than a pre‑existing hereditary complement disorder. In contrast, a handful of other cases have identified factor‑B or factor‑H variants, underscoring the heterogeneity of triggers and underlying susceptibility. Nonetheless, the recurrence of CM‑HUS in the setting of C. canimorsus infection, even when complement genetics are unremarkable, reinforces the necessity of considering this zoonotic pathogen as a cause of complement‑mediated TMA in otherwise healthy adults.

## Therapeutic Strategy and Clinical Dilemmas

The management of complement‑mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS) arising in the setting of an active bacterial sepsis presents a diagnostic and therapeutic paradox. On the one hand, the underlying trigger—a disseminated infection with **Capnocytophaga canimorsus** in this patient—demands timely antimicrobial suppression; on the other hand, the endothelial injury and microvascular thrombosis characteristic of CM‑HUS require rapid modulation of the complement cascade. The therapeutic algorithm in this case was therefore built around three interlocked considerations: (1) the short‑term stabilisation afforded by plasma exchange, (2) the definitive but infection‑sensitive action of complement blockade with eculizumab, and (3) the prophylactic measures necessary to mitigate the heightened infectious risk of terminal complement inhibition.

### Plasma exchange: rationale and sequencing

Plasma exchange (PEX) removes circulating pathogenic mediators and replaces them with therapeutic plasma, thereby diminishing auto‑antibody titres, inflammatory cytokines, and complement fragments. In thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) the main driver is an acquired ADAMTS13 auto‑antibody; PEX removes the inhibitor and simultaneously delivers exogenous functional ADAMTS13, providing a clear therapeutic benefit. In CM‑HUS, PEX is not curative because disease is rooted in intrinsic dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway, but it can transiently lower soluble C5b‑9 and other downstream effectors. This can abort ongoing microvascular injury and may allow time for conventional antibiotics to eradicate the inciting pathogen.

In the present patient, PEX was commenced immediately after the clinical suspicion of TMA was confirmed—while the ADAMTS13 activity was still pending—to avoid irreversible renal ischemia. Three sequential exchanges with Octaplas were performed before the first dose of eculizumab. The patient’s diuresis returned shortly after the third PEX session, but full hematologic remission and stable kidney function were only achieved after complement blockade, underscoring the temporary benefit of plasma exchange for CM‑HUS.

The timing of PEX relative to eculizumab is therefore critical: PEX may be used in the acute phase when rapid control of microthrombosis is needed or when the diagnosis is still equivocal (e.g., a suspicion of TTP). Once a definitive diagnosis of CM‑HUS is established, eculizumab should be prioritized, as plasma exchange offers no durable disease control.

### Eculizumab dosing strategy

Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that binds C5 and prevents the assembly of the membrane attack complex (MAC), is the standard of care for CM‑HUS. In this patient, the dosing regimen followed the institutional protocol derived from the randomized trials: a 900‑mg induction course administered weekly for four weeks, giving a total of 3 600 mg, followed by a maintenance dose of 1 200 mg every two weeks. This schedule maintains trough concentrations of the drug sufficient to block terminal complement activation while limiting drug exposure.

The induction dose was initiated after the last PEX and once blood cultures were negative, thereby reducing the risk of potentiating an active infection. The subsequent maintenance phase is normally continued indefinitely in patients with underlying complement gene mutations; however, in patients with infection‑triggered CM‑HUS and no pathogenic complement variants, evidence suggests that therapy may be discontinued once the patient has remained in remission for an adequate period. In this case, maintenance eculizumab was planned for four months, with a planned reassessment based on serial laboratory monitoring and clinical status.

### Infectious risks of terminal complement inhibition

Inhibition of C5 abolishes the formation of the MAC, a key mechanism for opsonizing encapsulated bacteria such as **Neisseria meningitidis**, **Streptococcus pneumoniae**, **Haemophilus influenzae** type b, and **Capnocytophaga** species. Consequently, eculizumab therapy renders patients susceptible to life‑threatening meningococcal infections. The risk is highest during the first days of therapy, when complement blockade is established before the adaptive immune response can compensate.

Real‑world data in CM‑HUS patients treated with eculizumab show a markedly reduced incidence of meningococcal disease when patients receive pre‑emptive vaccination and appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis. The vaccine coverage covering serogroups A, C, Y, W, and B is essential because alternative complement activation remains intact against non‑meningococcal organisms.

### Vaccination strategy

The patient received the conjugate Menveo vaccine (serogroups A, C, Y, W) and the Bexsero vaccine (serogroup B) 2 weeks before the initiation of eculizumab. This pre‑emptive schedule conforms to CDC and NICE recommendations for patients receiving terminal complement inhibition. The timing maximized immune response to the polysaccharide antigens at the time C5 blockade would be operative. The second dose of each vaccine was planned for eight weeks after completing the induction phase, an interval at which the protective antibody titres are still robust but the drug exposure is lower.

### Antibiotic prophylaxis and monitoring of bacteremia

Because the terminal complement system plays a pivotal role in phagocytosis of encapsulated bacteria, eculizumab represents a relative contraindication to ongoing bacteremia. Here, antibiotic therapy was certainly effective, as repeat blood cultures turned negative before eculizumab initiation. To further safeguard against occult infection, the patient received prolonged oral penicillin V (Apocillin) prophylaxis throughout the eculizumab course, a regimen supported by the fact that prophylaxis with penicillins is standard for patients on complement inhibitors. Serial blood cultures were repeated during the first week after eculizumab induction to confirm the absence of persistent bacteraemia.

### Monitoring response and disease activity

The team used a tight monitoring schedule to gauge therapeutic response and to detect relapse promptly:

1. **Hematology** – Daily counts of haemoglobin, platelets, and white blood cells, coupled with repeat reticulocyte counts. A drop in haemoglobin without bleeding, a persistently low platelet count and the presence of schistocytes provide objective evidence of ongoing microangiopathy.

2. **Hemolysis markers** – Lactate dehydrogenase, haptoglobin, bilirubin, and indirect Coombs testing. Re‑negative DAT after the second test helped rule out simple autoimmune hemolysis.

3. **Renal function** – Serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate were measured twice weekly. Urine output was also tracked daily; the recovery of spontaneous diuresis after the final plasma exchange session was regarded as an early sign of improvement, but persistent anuria suggested that further complement blockade was warranted.

4. **Complement activity** – Functional assays of the alternative pathway (AH50) and levels of individual complement factors were monitored at intervals after the final PEX and eculizumab administrations. A decrease in AH50 to 6 % reflected the impact of the therapy, although this is expected and does not signify disease activity. Restoring alternative pathway activity is only associated with a return of disease activity rather than a baseline physiological level.

All these laboratory trends were cross‑referenced with the clinical picture to draw a composite picture of therapeutic efficacy and to inform decisions about maintenance therapy.

### Decision‑making regarding continuation or discontinuation

When CM‑HUS has no known genetic or auto‑inflammatory predisposition, the risk–benefit analysis for maintenance eculizumab hinges on renal recovery, persistent complement dysregulation, and the potential for relapse. In this patient, the partial improvement of renal function (creatinine 175 µmol/L) to near‑baseline levels, combined with normalization of hematologic parameters and the absence of any underlying complement mutations on extended testing, set the stage for a considerated taper.

Clinicians must weigh the probability of relapse against the cost, infusion burden, and infectious risk of ongoing eculizumab. The chosen strategy was to maintain the bi‑weekly infusion for four months while performing rigorous surveillance as outlined above. Should the patient remain stable, a step‑down protocol—reduced frequency or eventual cessation—could be followed, with a two‑month “watch‑and‑wait” period before deciding conclusively. Any signs of hemolysis, thrombocytopenia, or rising creatinine would prompt rapid resumption of therapy.

In sum, the therapeutic journey in this case underscores a dynamic interplay between plasma exchange and complement blockade, infection control, and vigilant serial monitoring. The nuanced decision‑making—balancing rapid complement inhibition against infection risk, a disease caused by an infection—offers a template for managing similarly complex presentations of CM‑HUS in an immunocompetent host.

## Outcomes, Prognosis, and Practice Implications

The patient’s recovery trajectory illustrates the critical impact of timely recognition and intervention in infection‑triggered complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). At the time of ICU discharge, she remained dialysis‑dependent, with significant oliguria and an elevated serum creatinine of >250 µmol/L. Following a course of plasma exchange (PEX) and the initiation of eculizumab, spontaneous diuresis returned and creatinine began to fall, settling at 175 µmol/L when she was discharged from the nephrology ward. The renal benefit was gradual; the patient still required intermittent dialysis during the first two weeks of rehabilitation, but by the end of the month her urine output had reached 1.2 L/day with residual proteinuria (2 g/24 h). Subsequent outpatient visits showed a plateau in creatinine at 170 µmol/L and a modest improvement in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR ≈ 30 mL/min/1.73 m²), indicating partial but incomplete renal recovery.

Eculizumab was started 12 days after admission, after negative blood cultures and following meningococcal vaccination. The induction regimen (900 mg IV weekly for four weeks) was followed by maintenance therapy (1200 mg IV every two weeks). She has received two additional maintenance doses and is scheduled for a reassessment of her disease activity and renal function after a total of four months on therapy. The decision to continue maintenance eculizumab was based on two key observations: (1) the absence of a pathogenic complement gene mutation or anti‑factor H antibody—extended testing revealed only a transient reduction in alternative pathway activity likely secondary to recent plasma exchange and first‐cycle eculizumab, rather than an intrinsic defect—and (2) the persistence of subclinical hemolysis and thrombocytopenia during the first month of maintenance therapy, suggesting that biochemical remission had not yet been achieved. The planned reassessment will consider the risk–benefit ratio of long‑term complement blockade, with renewal of therapy if renal function fails to stabilise or if any evidence of relapse emerges.

Early initiation of eculizumab has been consistently linked with improved renal outcomes in CM‑HUS. In this patient, the interval between diagnosis and start of complement blockade was less than 48 hours after the first evidence of MAHA (dramatic drop in hemoglobin and rising LDH). Although PEX temporarily improved platelet counts and kidney function, it did not halt the underlying complement activation. The rapid switch to eculizumab halted terminal complement amplification, allowing endothelial repair and a gradual recovery of tubular function. Had eculizumab been delayed, irreversible ischemic scarring of the nephrons could have progressed, potentially precluding dialysis independence. This aligns with contemporary studies demonstrating that earlier complement inhibition leads to a higher rate of complete renal recovery and reduced need for long‑term dialysis.

The complexity of this case underscores the necessity of multidisciplinary care. Infectious disease specialists guided antibiotic stewardship and monitoring of bacteremia clearance; nephrology managed dialysis and fluid balance; hematology directed TMA work‑up and supported the initiation of eculizumab; and pharmacy ensured proper dosing and monitoring of complement inhibitors. Effective communication among these specialties facilitated the rapid transition from a severe septic state to targeted TMA therapy while mitigating the risk of opportunistic infections associated with terminal complement blockade.

Screening for complement dysregulation has practical implications. In adults presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, early assessment for ADAMTS13 activity and complement function is warranted to rule out TTP and to identify CM‑HUS. Even in the absence of a pathogenic variant, a typical clinical phenotype—persistent hemolysis, schistocytes on smear, TMA on biopsy—justifies empiric complement inhibition, especially when the clinical course is severe or deteriorating. Routine genetic testing for complement regulators (CFH, CFI, MCP, C3, CFB) remains valuable for prognosis and long‑term management decisions, but its absence does not exclude an infection‑triggered episode, as illustrated here.

Rapid molecular diagnostics for *Capnocytophaga* spp. can alter the therapeutic timeline. Conventional cultures of *C. canimorsus* may take several days to weeks, delaying both appropriate antibiotic selection and recognition of an infection‑triggered TMA. Real‑time PCR or nanopore sequencing, performed on blood samples, can detect *C. canimorsus* within hours, enabling early de‑escalation of broad‑spectrum therapy and prompt consideration of complement blockade if TMA develops.

Clinically, this case reinforces three reminders. First, CM‑HUS should be considered in sepsis patients who develop persistent cytopenias and unexplained hemolysis, even when initial hematologic parameters are confounded by DIC or septic shock. Second, the presence of schistocytes on peripheral smear, persistently low haptoglobin, and rising LDH in the setting of a sepsis‑recovered patient signals the emergence of a TMA and should trigger a fulsome diagnostic work‑up (ADAMTS13, complement panels). Third, early, aggressive complement inhibition—ideally within 24–48 hours of TMA recognition—offers the best chance of renal salvage and reduced morbidity.

In summary, this patient illustrates that early complement blockade, coordinated multidisciplinary management, and judicious use of advanced diagnostics can yield partial renal recovery and hematologic remission in CM‑HUS triggered by a rare zoonotic pathogen. The course highlights that even in immunocompetent adults, severe infections can unmask or trigger complement dysregulation, and that vigilance for TMA in the setting of sepsis is essential for optimal outcomes.
