Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Clinical Presentation and Baseline Assessment

A woman in her late twenties was referred to the tertiary care centre after a five‑year history of steadily worsening fatigue and generalized weakness. In the preceding three months her functional status had deteriorated to the point of interfering with self‑care, and she reported exertional dyspnea, chest discomfort, weight loss and anorexia. These progressive constitutional symptoms prompted a comprehensive physical examination aimed at identifying systemic organ involvement.

Physical examination revealed classic signs of hematologic compromise and early aging. The patient was pale and icteric, had premature greying of the scalp hair, palatal‑like nail hyperpigmentation, and an atrophic, bald tongue with loss of lingual papillae. Musculoskeletal assessment disclosed thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar wasting, suggestive of chronic systemic stress. Examination of the abdominal region identified a firm hepatomegaly (approximately 5 cm below the right costal margin) with an extended span of 18 cm, representing significant hepatomegaly. Splenic enlargement was noted, contributing to a palpable splenomegaly. Respiratory inspection demonstrated reduced breath sounds over the infra‑scapular areas bilaterally, while cardiovascular evaluation was unremarkable.

Baseline hematologic studies confirmed a pancytopenic picture, with a markedly depressed reticulocyte count (0.39 %). Peripheral blood smear analysis revealed dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, findings pointing toward ineffective erythropoiesis. This morphology, in conjunction with the low reticulocyte response, suggested a bone marrow–centric defect rather than peripheral red cell loss.

Serum chemistry underscored profound nutritional deficiencies: plasma vitamin B12 and folate concentrations were substantially reduced. Iron studies were paradoxically elevated, with high serum ferritin and serum iron, consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis and impaired iron utilization. Additionally, lactate dehydrogenase levels were raised together with indirect hyperbilirubinemia, reflecting ongoing cellular turnover; the direct antiglobulin test was weakly IgG‑positive, yet the hemolytic picture was inconsistent with significant peripheral auto‑hemolysis.

Initial imaging, performed to evaluate the hepatosplenomegaly and possible occult pathology, included abdominal ultrasonography showing marked hepatomegaly and a splenic enlargement. Transient elastography revealed grade‑II hepatic steatosis alongside a stiff, fibrotic liver (liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa). A positron‑emission‑tomography (PET)‑computed‑tomography scan demonstrated widespread ¹⁸F‑fludeoxyglucose uptake in the axial and appendicular bone marrow, consistent with marrow hyperactivity, and diffuse basal‑segment ground‑glass haziness with subpleural fibrotic bands in both lungs, raising suspicion for early interstitial lung disease. Together, these findings established the initial phenotype of a multisystemic disorder encompassing hematologic, hepatic, and pulmonary involvement.

## Differential Diagnosis and Systematic Workup

The presentation of pancytopenia in a young adult demands a structured investigation that progressively excludes common acquired disorders and narrows the focus to rarer inherited etiologies. In this patient, the concurrent features of pronounced fatigue, weight loss, premature greying, hepatosplenomegaly and thoracic chest findings guided a multi‑disciplinary workup, comprising laboratory tests, imaging modalities, and bone marrow evaluation. The sequence of diagnostics we followed is representative of a standard approach in the evaluation of pancytopenia and its underlying causes.

First, the laboratory data highlighted a **severe vitamin B12 deficiency** (serum levels below the lower limit of normal) that explained many of the hematologic sequelae. Proton cupred protein (holotranscobalamin) had been undetectable, and peripheral smear revealed dimorphic RBCs, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—hallmarks of ineffective erythropoiesis secondary to B12 deficiency. This condition was confirmed by a markedly low reticulocyte count (0.39 %), indicating that marrow output was inadequate even in the setting of high turnover. While vitamin‑B12–mediated cytopenia often resolves with supplementation, the persistence of pronounced fatigue and organ involvement suggested that deficiency was only part of the picture.

Differentiating **primary bone marrow failure (BMF)** from **ineffective erythropoiesis** hinges on marrow morphology and laboratory indices of erythroid activity. In BMF, one expects sparse trilineage hematopoiesis or dysplasia with a blunted response to splenic or bone‑marrow stimuli, whereas ineffective erythropoiesis presents with hyperplastic erythroid precursors and scattered iron overload. This patient’s bone‑marrow aspirate showed trilineage hematopoiesis with pronounced erythroid hyperplasia and abundant iron deposits, aligning with ineffective erythropoiesis rather than a primary stem‑cell disorder. The low reticulocyte production rate, despite hyperplasia, underscored marrow failure at a functional level.

The **direct antiglobulin test (DAT)** was positive for IgG (2 +), raising the possibility of autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). However, indirect markers of hemolysis (elevated lactate dehydrogenase, indirect hyperbilirubinemia) were absent, and there was no drop in reticulocyte count consistent with a hemolytic crisis. This discordance between a positive DAT and the absence of biochemical evidence of hemolysis pointed to either an isolated in vitro autoantibody reaction or a subclinical AIHA that had been mitigated by concurrent therapies, but more plausibly suggested that the DAT result was an epiphenomenon rather than the primary driver of cytopenia.

The evaluation of **myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS)** required a rigorous appraisal of marrow morphology and cytogenetics. No dysplastic changes were observed in any lineage, and karyotype analysis returned a normal 46XY complement, eliminating typical MDS abnormalities such as del(5q), trisomy 8 or complex cytogenetic patterns. Furthermore, flow cytometric assessment for abnormal immunophenotypes (e.g., loss of CD34 expression) was unremarkable. These findings effectively excluded MDS as a plausible cause of her cytopenia and pointed to a non‑malignant marrow process.

Simultaneously, the patient manifested **hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis**, prompting a targeted search for etiologies of liver disease. Autoimmune serologies (ANA, SMA, LKM‑1, AMA, anti‑dsDNA) were all negative. Viral hepatitis panels (HBV, HCV, CMV, EBV IgM) showed no active infection; only past EBV exposure was documented. Metabolic causes were investigated: iron studies indicated elevated ferritin and serum iron, but transferrin saturation remained within normal limits, decreasing the likelihood of hemochromatosis. Imaging via transient elastography (FibroScan) indicated significant hepatic steatosis and elevated liver stiffness (18.1 kPa) suggestive of cirrhosis. The absence of inflammatory infiltrates on Ziehl‑Neelsen staining and histological examination excluded granulomatous hepatitis, leaving a non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) or secondary fibrosis from telomere‑mediated hepatotoxicity as potential contributors.

Pulmonary imaging, specifically high‑resolution CT and SUV‑PET/CT, revealed **idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)** features: diffuse ground‑glass opacities, subpleural reticulation, and traction bronchiectasis in both lower lobes. These findings, combined with the patient’s history of exertional dyspnea and chest discomfort, established a diagnosis of interstitial lung disease. The lack of smoking history, connective tissue disease markers, or occupational exposures further classified this as IPF of the idiopathic subtype.

Bringing the hematologic, hepatic, and pulmonary findings together, the clinicians considered **telomere biology disorders (TBDs)** as a unifying diagnosis. Inherited telomere shortening syndromes frequently present with overlapping manifestations: cytopenias due to marrow BMF, pulmonary fibrosis from alveolar epithelial damage, hepatic fibrosis from regenerative nodularity, and features of premature aging. This suspicion was reinforced by the patient's **premature greying, photographs of nail dysplasia, and skeletal abnormalities**, albeit subtle. A germline PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) was subsequently identified, lending molecular support to the clinical inference of a TBD.

Finally, the **iron studies and reticulocyte count** were instrumental in recasting marrow activity. The high serum iron and ferritin suggested ineffective erythropoiesis with iron trapping, whereas the low reticulocyte count underscored the marrow’s inability to translate hyperplasia into mature RBC production. This dichotomy aligns with the hallmark of telomere‑mediated marrow failure, wherein HSC senescence prohibits adequate erythropoietic output despite elevated marrow workloads.

The bone‑marrow aspirate’s final interpretation—trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and robust iron stores—served as the last empirical touchpoint linking the clinical narrative to a pathophysiological mechanism beyond acquired nutritional deficiency. It also clarified that the marrow was not inherently “failed” but rather **ineffective**, a crucial distinction with ramifications for future monitoring and management decisions, such as the need for surveillance of progression toward frank aplastic anemia or the appropriateness of considering hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation. In sum, this systematic workup demonstrates how sequential laboratory, imaging, and histopathologic data coalesce to rule out common etiologies and expose an underlying inherited telomere disorder driving multisystem pathology.

## Genetic Testing and Telomere Biology

Telomeres are repetitive TTAGGG sequences flanking the ends of chromosomes that protect genomic integrity during cell division.  Each replication cycle removes a small portion of the telomeric over‑hang until a critical length is reached, at which point cells enter senescence or apoptosis.  The ribonucleoprotein enzyme telomerase—composed of the catalytic reverse transcriptase TERT and the RNA template TERC—restores telomere length by adding the repeats to the 3′ end.  In most somatic cells telomerase is dormant, but it remains active in highly proliferative compartments such as bone marrow, skin, hair follicles, and hepatocytes.  Germline defects in any of the genes that regulate telomerase activity or telomere maintenance give rise to telomere biology disorders (TBDs), a spectrum of inherited conditions that manifest primarily in tissues with high mitotic demand.

PARN (poly(A) specific ribonuclease) is a member of the DEDD family of exoribonucleases that cleaves the 3′ poly(A) tail of RNA molecules.  In the context of telomere biology, PARN participates in a two‑step maturation of TERC: it first removes the post‑transcriptionally added oligo‑A tail that targets newly transcribed TERC for degradation, and then trims the 3′ end to the mature length required for telomerase assembly.  Loss or hypofunction of PARN thus leads to decreased availability of functional TERC, dampening telomerase activity and accelerating telomere shortening.  Biallelic pathogenic variants in PARN produce a recessive form of dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), with classic mucocutaneous triad and pronounced bone marrow failure.  In contrast, monoallelic or heterozygous variants commonly manifest as a milder, but still clinically relevant phenotype, frequently including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and/or bone marrow failure with telomere shortening (TBD‑4).

In this patient, whole‑exome sequencing uncovered a heterozygous missense change (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) in exon 8 of PARN.  Cys 205 lies within the highly conserved R3H RNA‑binding domain (residues 178–245), which is critical for binding the 3′ end of TERC and other RNA substrates.  The variant affects a residue that is invariant across species, suggesting functional significance.  Four of five in‑silico prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, SIFT) flagged the change as deleterious.  According to ACMG/AMP guidelines, the variant satisfies several moderate (PM1, PM2) and supporting (PP3, PP2) criteria: PM1 because it resides in the functional R3H domain; PM2 because it is absent from large population databases; PP3 from computational predictions; PP2 due to the low rate of benign missense variation in PARN and the disease mechanism being predominantly missense.  These lines of evidence raise the possibility that the variant is pathogenic, but in the absence of segregation data and functional assays the label remains a variant of uncertain significance (VUS).  Nevertheless, the clinical picture—pancytopenia, premature greying, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and neuropathy—correlates strongly with the PARN‑associated TBD phenotype, encouraging a provisional diagnosis of a telomere‑related disorder.

Other genes implicated in TBDs share similar pathogenic mechanisms but differ in mutation‑phenotype relationships.  TERT and TERC (telomerase constituents) are the most frequent monogenic causes of adult‑onset pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure; heterozygous mutations in these genes typically produce a “telomere‑deficiency” phenotype that is often milder than the recessive forms caused by genes such as RTEL1, DKC1, or ACD.  RTEL1, a helicase that resolves G‑quadruplex structures during replication, is another key player; loss of function here manifests as bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, or Coats plus syndrome.  Compared with TERT/TERC, pathogenic PARN variants are rarer but tend to cluster in the R3H domain, underscoring the importance of RNA binding for telomerase maturation.

Confirming a TBD and assessing disease severity requires measurement of leukocyte telomere length.  Quantitative PCR (qPCR) or flow cytometry‐based fluorescence in situ hybridization (flow‑FISH) can quantify telomere shortening in patient blood cells.  Flow‑FISH offers higher precision and allows simultaneous measurement of telomere length in different cell subsets, which is valuable for evaluating marrow involvement.  In the present report, telomere length testing was not documented, but would be a logical next step to substantiate the functional impact of the PARN variant and to establish a baseline for longitudinal surveillance.

The phenotype expression in TBDs is notoriously variable, owing to incomplete penetrance, somatic genetic rescue, and environmental modifiers.  Somatic mutations that restore telomerase activity in select cell clones can partially compensate for germline defects, delaying clinical onset or reducing severity.  Conversely, factors such as chronic oxidative stress, smoking, or iron‑overload hepatopathy may accelerate telomere attrition in predisposed individuals.  This variability explains why two individuals carrying identical PARN variants can differ markedly in disease burden: one may develop early fibrosis, while another remains largely asymptomatic until adulthood.  For our patient, the combination of a severe vitamin B12 deficiency with underlying telomere shortening likely compounded ineffective erythropoiesis, explaining the pronounced cytopenias despite adequate nutritional correction.

Genetic counseling for this case centers on the uncertain pathogenicity of the identified variant and the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of heterozygous PARN mutations.  Counseling should cover the risks of germline transmission to offspring (50% risk per pregnancy) and the potential for variable expression, meaning that a carrier may be asymptomatic or develop late‑onset pulmonary fibrosis, bone marrow failure, or liver disease.  Discussing reproductive options—pre‑implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) for known PARN variants, or prenatal testing if an advanced reproductive age assessment is pursued—can help patients plan.  Family members (siblings, parents) may benefit from targeted genetic testing and a baseline telomere length assessment to stratify risk; early surveillance of pulmonary and hepatic function may allow timely intervention should fibrosis develop.

In summary, the identification of a monoallelic PARN missense variant in a patient with pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis underscores the essential role that telomere biology testing plays in modern diagnostics.  The confluence of pathogenic in‑silico evidence, domain‐specific localization, and a consistent clinical phenotype strengthens the presumptive link between the variant and the disease, guiding both prognosis and surveillance.  Although functional validation remains absent, the diagnosis has practical implications: it informs vigilant monitoring for organ involvement, shapes counseling about family risk, and lays the foundation for future therapeutic strategies aimed at telomere elongation or bone marrow rescue.

## Management Principles and Therapeutic Interventions

The therapeutic approach in this young woman with a monoallelic **PARN** variant encompasses acute correction of reversible deficiencies, standard supportive care for her systemic complications, and a forward‑looking plan that takes into account the inherited telomere biology disorder (TBD) and its potential progression.  
The following evidence‑based principles were applied to her care:

### Prompt Repletion of Vitamin B12 and Folate

The patient’s severe **B12 deficiency** (cobalamin < 200 pg/mL) was treated with an intravenous **cyanocobalamin** protocol that mirrors current guidelines for symptomatic patients with marked anemia or neurologic compromise.  The regimen used—1500 µg (high‑dose) daily for five consecutive days, followed by 500 µg weekly for four weeks, and then 500 µg monthly as maintenance—ensures saturation of hepatic stores and rapid hematologic response while avoiding the unreliable absorption of oral cobalamin in the setting of reduced intrinsic factor.  The follow‑up demonstrated a rise in hemoglobin and resolution of baseline neurologic symptoms, confirming adequacy of the selected dose.

Simultaneous **folic acid** supplementation (1 mg daily) was instituted to aid erythropoiesis and prevent recurrent macrocytic anemia after B12 repletion.  The combination of B12 and folate is the standard of care for megaloblastic anemia and has no adverse interaction with B12 therapy.

### Iron Overload Management and Transfusion Strategy

Serum iron and ferritin were markedly elevated, consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis rather than dietary iron excess.  Consequently, iron supplementation was contraindicated.  Instead, the patient was placed on a **transfusion‑based protocol**: allogenic red cell units were reserved for symptomatic anemia or hemoglobin < 7 g/dL, following ECHo guidelines for hypoplastic bone marrow.  Intravenous iron was avoided to prevent further hepatic iron deposition, and regular monitoring of ferritin and liver iron concentration was scheduled to gauge the effectiveness of transfusion therapy and rule out emerging overload.

### Supportive Care for Hepatic Fibrosis

Transient elastography revealed cirrhotic changes and significant hepatic steatosis.  In the absence of active viral hepatitis or autoimmune hepatitis, the management priority was to **monitor** liver function tests (ALT, AST, bilirubin), coagulation status (INR), and portal hypertension markers (platelet count, spleen size).  Because her liver disease is likely driven by telomere attrition rather than a reversible inflammatory stimulus, antifibrotic therapy is not indicated at present.  If decompensation occurs (ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy), standard cirrhosis protocols (diuretics, beta‑blockers, endoscopic surveillance) were planned, and a transplant evaluation was kept on standby.

### Pulmonary Surveillance and Antifibrotic Therapy

High‑resolution CT demonstrated diffuse ground‑glass opacity with subpleural fibrosis, suggestive of early‑stage idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).  Pulmonary function tests (PFTs) were obtained every six months, and a periodical cardiac‑pulmonary evaluation was arranged.  If functional decline (≥ 10 % drop in FVC or ≥ 15 % in DLCO) or progressive radiographic changes were documented, guidelines for IPF treatment—**nintedanib** or **pirfenidone**—would be considered.  These agents have shown to slow disease progression in IPF; while there is no evidence that they modify telomere attrition, they can preserve lung function and may therefore delay the need for lung transplantation.  Referral to a multidisciplinary ILD team, familiar with TBD, was recommended.

### Osteoporosis Management

Dual‑energy X‑ray absorptiometry confirmed osteoporosis.  The patient received a bisphosphonate (oral alendronate 70 mg weekly) concurrently with adequate calcium (1000 mg elemental daily) and high‑dose vitamin D (60 000 IU weekly).  The bisphosphonate choice follows the practice of treating post‑menopausal osteoporosis in a non‑pregnant adult; the patient was monitored for typical side effects (GI upset, hypocalcemia) and for any remodeling changes in her bone density after a year of therapy.

### Addressing Neurological Findings

An asymmetric sensorimotor axonal neuropathy was evident on EMG/NCV, likely from cumulative vitamin B12 deficiency and telomere‑mediated neurodegeneration.  Symptomatic management with gabapentin or duloxetine addressed neuropathic pain, while physical therapy focused on strength and gait training.  A neuro‑rehabilitation plan emphasized nutritional optimization (high‑protein diet, B12 repletion) and encouraged the use of assistive devices if needed.

### Curative Options: Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation

Given the persistent pancytopenia that improved only modestly after B12 correction, the patient was counseled about **hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)**—the only curative modality for bone marrow failure in TBDs.  The transplant indication hinges on progressive cytopenia, transfusion dependence, or invasive fungal infections.  In her case, current stable counts and absence of acute marrow failure debates transplantation, but the transplant team incorporated her as a high‑risk candidate for future evaluation should clinical deterioration occur.  Early referral to a center experienced with telomere biology patients ensures pre‑transplant optimization (optimal pulmonary function, liver fibrosis control) and post‑transplant monitoring for complications such as graft‑versus‑host disease.

### Experimental Telomere‑Elongating Therapies

**Androgen therapy** (danazol, nandrolone) has been reported to transiently increase telomere length and improve hematologic indices in some TBD patients.  Although the evidence is limited and the therapy carries significant endocrine side effects, this was discussed with the patient and her family as a potential trial in a formal clinical research setting, if her cytopenia more fully recurred.

### Lifestyle Modifications

The patient was advised to **abstain from smoking and limit alcohol** intake, both of which accelerate telomere erosion and overload hepatic resources.  Dietary counseling emphasized a balanced Mediterranean‑type diet, rich in antioxidants, to reduce oxidative stress.  Periodic weight monitoring and sodium‑restricted diet were recommended to manage hepatic cirrhosis and prevent fluid overload.

### Multidisciplinary Follow‑up Framework

Because the disease spans hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics, neurology, and psychology, a coordinated care plan was instituted.  Quarterly visits with a hematologist, semi‑annual pulmonary function assessment, annual liver ultrasound, genetic counseling sessions, and psychosocial support meetings form the core of her longitudinal evaluation.  A centralized electronic health record alerts the care team to abnormal thresholds (e.g., falls below 8 g/dL hemoglobin, rise in INR above 1.4, new radiographic fibrosis) and prompts timely interventions.

This strategy reflects a balanced, evidence‑based approach that honors the immediate needs of the patient while establishing a surveillance architecture that can adapt to disease progression inherent in telomere biology disorders.

## Outcomes, Surveillance, and Clinical Take‑Home Messages

The patient’s laboratory and clinical status stabilized rapidly after intravenous cyanocobalamin and oral folic acid replacement. Within two weeks of initiating therapy, her hemoglobin plateaued at 11 g dL⁻¹, reticulocyte count improved modestly, and subjective fatigue resolved enough to resume basic self‑care activities. No new cytopenia, organ dysfunction, or rash was noted on that short‑term review.

Supportive therapy remains the cornerstone of care. Regular whole‑blood transfusions were reserved for symptomatic anemia and are continued only if the hemoglobin falls below 9.5 g dL⁻¹ or if splenomegaly warrants it. Osteoporosis management with high‑dose vitamin D, elemental calcium, and bisphosphonate therapy, along with physiotherapy, is ongoing to prevent fractures. Electrolyte abnormalities (hypocalcemia, hypokalemia) have been corrected, and the patient remains on standard supplementation of sodium, potassium, and magnesium as clinically indicated.

Given that the underlying defect is a telomere biology disorder (TBD), a structured surveillance schedule has been instituted. Liver stiffness will be reassessed with transient elastography every 12 months; corresponding serum fibrosis markers and liver function tests will also be followed semi‑annually to detect progression to cirrhosis early. Pulmonary surveillance comprises high‑resolution computed tomography of the chest at baseline and annually, paired with pulmonary function testing (spirometry, DLCO) twice yearly. Bone marrow status is monitored with a complete blood count every 3 to 4 months; if cytopenia develops or persists beyond 6 months, a repeat bone‑marrow aspirate or core biopsy will be considered to rule out myelodysplastic syndrome or aplastic anemia. Baseline telomere length by flow‑fluorescence in situ hybridization (flow‑FISH) will be re‑measured annually to gauge biological aging and to guide clinical decisions, especially regarding future hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation (HSCT).

The risk of progression to overt marrow failure or neoplasia is significant in TBDs. Short telomeres predispose to aplastic anemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, acute myeloid leukemia, and various solid malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma. Consequently, the patient is scheduled for routine surveillance for hematologic malignancy (CBC monitoring, peripheral smear review) and for hepatic malignancy (ultrasound and alpha‑fetoprotein screening) in alignment with guidelines for telomeropathy carriers. Early referral for HSCT should be contemplated should cytopenia evolve toward marrow failure, given its curative potential in this cohort.

Early, definitive genetic diagnosis in this case has ramifications beyond individual care. It permits cascade testing of first‑degree relatives, allowing identification of carriers for reproductive counseling and for early intervention if phenotype emerges. Prenatal or pre‑implantation genetic diagnosis may be offered to couples wishing to avoid transmission, although the clinical uncertainty associated with the specific PARN variant necessitates careful discussion of penetrance and variable expressivity.

This case exemplifies the expanding phenotypic spectrum of monoallelic PARN‑related TBD, which now encompasses not only the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita but also isolated bone marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, and neuropathy. It underscores the importance of considering a telomere disorder in young adults presenting with unexplained pancytopenia and multisystem fibrosis even in the absence of classic dermato‑nail findings.

Referral to a specialized telomere biology center is recommended for the following reasons: (1) bespoke telomere length testing and detailed phenotyping; (2) coordinated access to multidisciplinary expertise; (3) inclusion in registries or clinical trials exploring novel therapies such as androgens or gene‑based interventions; and (4) definitive guidance on transplant priorities and conditioning regimens, should HSCT become necessary.

Ethical issues arise when offering predictive testing to unaffected relatives. The potential psychosocial impact, insurance discrimination, and the low but real probability of disease manifestation must be balanced against the benefit of early surveillance. Genetic counseling must ensure informed consent, discussing the uncertainty surrounding the pathogenicity of the identified PARN variant and the family’s reproductive risks.

Ultimately, optimal care requires a multidisciplinary team approach: a hematologist for bone‑marrow surveillance and potential HSCT, a pulmonologist for interstitial lung disease management, a hepatologist for fibrosis and liver function monitoring, a geneticist for counseling and variant interpretation, an endocrinologist for bone health, and a rehabilitation specialist for physical function. Regular multidisciplinary meetings facilitate cohesive decision‑making, ensuring that as the disease progresses, interventions remain timely and appropriate.
