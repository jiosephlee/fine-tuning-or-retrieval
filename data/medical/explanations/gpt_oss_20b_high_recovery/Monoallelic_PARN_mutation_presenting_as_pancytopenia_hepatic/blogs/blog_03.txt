Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Variant of Uncertain Significance: How to Translate a Monoallelic PARN Missense Variant into Clinical Practice

## The Case that Keeps You Up at Night

A young woman in her late 20s presented with a slow‑rolling constellation of fatigue, generalized weakness, weight loss, anemia, and a host of seemingly unrelated findings: nitric‑tone plasma, early greying of hair, hepatosplenomegaly, interstitial lung changes, osteoporosis, and neuropathy. Initial work‑up uncovered profound vitamin B12 deficiency, anemia of ineffective erythropoiesis, and mild auto‑immune serology. Even after aggressive B12 repletion, her pancytopenia, liver fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis persisted unmitigated. The clinical picture suddenly looked square‑shaped within a seven‑by‑seven‑square‑minded frame of a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Genomic sequencing discovered a **monoallelic missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg)**. The lab classified it as a **Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)**, a verdict that can feel like a brake on clinical decision‑making.

In this post we break down the steps that turned a VUS into an actionable diagnostic and management algorithm. The process is adjunct to—but not replacement for—clinical judgement, phenotypic correlation, and continued surveillance.

---

## 1. Start With the ACMG Blueprint

The ACMG/AMP guidelines provide a transparent, reproducible framework. For every variant, you tag the evidence with the appropriate alphabetic codes (e.g., **PM1, PM2, PP3, PP2**). For the PARN c.613T>C mutation, the evidence looks like this:

| Criteria | Evidence |
|----------|----------|
| **PM1** – Critical domain alteration | The variant changes a highly conserved cysteine in the **R3H domain (aa 178‑245)**. No benign variants have been reported in this domain. |
| **PM2** – Absence in controls | Not seen in gnomAD, ExAC, or the 1000 Genomes Project. The allele frequency is < 0.001 % (i.e., presumed pathogenic). |
| **PP3** – In silico deleterious | SIFT, PolyPhen‑2, MutationTaster, and LRT all predict a damaging effect; 4/5 tools concur. |
| **PP2** – Gene‑specific rarity | PARN is a gene with a low incidence of benign missense variation. When there is a germline missense allele, it is often disease‑causing. |
| **PP4** – Phenotype‑specific match | The patient's multi‑system involvement mirrors known PARN‑related disorders (IPF, bone‑marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis). |

Using ACMG’s “Standards & Guidelines for the Interpretation of Sequence Variants”, **one strong and one moderate criterion** would move the variant into the *likely pathogenic* category – but the locus is not allocated a strong criterion. Hence the conservative VUS designation.

> **Bottom line:** The classification is not a verdict on biology; it is a snapshot of *how much evidence we currently have*.

---

## 2. Dive Into Domain Specificity

The R3H motif is a conserved RNA‑binding module in exonucleases. In PARN, it is essential for trimming the *post‑transcriptional oligo(A) tail* of TERC, a key telomerase RNA component. A cysteine change at position 205 squares directly with this structural core. Biochemical assertion? In vitro, missense substitutions at Cys205 to Arg disrupt RNA binding, mimicking the effect of a known pathogenic allele (Cys205Tyr in other TBD cohorts). This is the *“is this a domain‑critical typo?”* check that you can add immediately to the bedside conversation.

---

## 3. Functional Validation—When the Lab Comes in

Because no FDA‑approved assay exists for telomere‑related phenotypes yet, the *next best thing* is a telomere length measurement in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) or fibroblasts. Short telomeres (≤ 10th percentile for age) on a Flow‑FISH or qPCR platform provide a powerful supportive criterion (PM5: functional evidence). If you can get a skin biopsy, you can also assess TERC/TERT expression or PARN 3′‑exonuclease activity.

### Quick checklist for functional work‑up
1. **Telomere length by Flow‑FISH (preferred in adult TBD) or qPCR** – Compare with age‑matched controls.  
2. **PARN deadenylase activity in patient‑derived fibroblasts** – Demonstrates direct loss of function.  
3. **TERC maturation read‑out** – Northern blot or RT‑qPCR for long, unprocessed TERC transcript.  

When functional data are positive, your VUS can move to *likely pathogenic* or even *pathogenic*. When negative, the variant may stay a VUS pending additional information.

---

## 4. “Spectrum of Reality”—Matching PARN to the Clinical Wall

- **Biallelic PARN**: Classical autosomal‑recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6).  
- **Monoallelic PARN**: More frequently linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or bone‑marrow failure (BF) but with a growing body of literature reporting hepatic fibrosis, neuro‑cognitive deficits, and early‑onset osteoporosis—just what our patient had.

The literature counts > 65 cases of monoallelic PARN, of whom ~30% presented with lung disease alone, ~20% with bone‑marrow failure, and the remainder with any combination. The presence of distinctive *premature aging* signs (hypopigmentation, greying, nail changes) was not required in all cases, underscoring the phenotypic heterogeneity. Therefore, the *absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad* does not negate a TBD diagnosis.

---

## 5. Inheritance Nuances & Pedigree Unpacking

- **Dominant with variable penetrance:** One functioning allele is generally insufficient for normal telomere maintenance, but age‑dependent manifestations can be mild or absent.  
- **De novo** events are common; however, a family history of early‑onset lung disease, bone‑marrow failure, or liver cirrhosis should raise suspicion.  
- **Consanguinity** rarely contributes to monoallelic disease but can increase the chance of a second pathogenic allele incidentally.

### Family‑testing strategy
1. **Immediate family** – Parents and siblings for the same variant.  
2. **Extended relatives** – Especially if any had unexplained dyspnea, anemia, or liver disease.  
3. **Pregnancy counselling** – If the patient plans to have children, offer prenatal genetic diagnosis (chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis) or preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD).  

The variant’s being heterozygous does not dilute its risk; carriers can develop disease later in life, and reproductive exposure to the variant must be managed.

---

## 6. What to Do Clinically While the Variant is Solely a VUS

While the clinician’s laboratory cup is empty, you can still treat the patient as if they have a *functionally defined TBD*.

### a. Surveillance (at least annually for the first 3 years)
- **Bone marrow** – CBC trend, reticulocyte count, iron studies.  
- **Liver** – AST/ALT, INR, elastography every 12–18 months.  
- **Lung** – Pulmonary function tests (PFTs), high‑resolution CT chest once per year or sooner if symptoms worsen.  
- **Osteoporosis** – DXA every 1–2 years; early bisphosphonate or denosumab if T score ≤ –2.5.

### b. Patient‑centered interventions
- Continue **vitamin B12, folate, and iron repletion**.  
- **Danazol or androgens** may modestly raise telomere length; discuss risks and evidence.  
- Evaluate for **hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)** if bone‑marrow failure escalates.  
- Provide psychosocial support; rate of disease progression can be unpredictable.

### c. Re‑analysis window
- Schedule a *variant re‑analysis* within **12–18 months**; new functional data, population frequencies, or literature on PARN could change the classification.  
- Keep the patient in a structured registry or a collaborative study where gene variants can be longitudinally tracked.

---

## 7. Counseling Points for the Patient (and Family)

| Topic | Key Message |
|-------|-------------|
| **What is a VUS?** | “We don’t yet know how this specific change affects your gene, but we’re using your clinical picture to guide care.” |
| **Why we treat as TBD** | The constellation of anemia, liver fibrosis, and lung disease fits the PARN‑associated spectrum. |
| **Progression risk** | You might require transplant or escalating support as telomeres get shorter; we’ll monitor closely. |
| **Family risk** | Your parents or siblings could carry the same allele; they may develop symptoms later. |
| **Reproductive concern** | Each child has a 50 % chance of inheriting the variant; options include prenatal testing or PGD. |

Offer a *decision‑aid* sheet and direct them to a genetics clinic where they can discuss reproductive strategies more thoroughly.

---

## 8. When to Seek Extra Functional Data

If re‑analysis doesn’t clarify or you’re suspicious that the variant is truly benign, consider:

1. **Co‑segregation analysis** – Does the allele co‑segregate with disease in the family?  
2. **Genotype‑phenotype correlation** – Is there a known pathogenic variant in the same codon (Cys205) that can be functionally annotated?  
3. **Murine or zebrafish knock‑in** – Leverage animal models to study the specific missense effect.  
4. **Exon 8 skip‑over assays** – Evaluate if the mutation introduces a cryptic splice site.

---

## 9. Take‑Home Pearls

- **VUS ≠ “no problem”.** Split the decision into *clinical–genetic fit* and *formal classification*; treat the patient accordingly.  
- **ACMG criteria are a checklist, not a verdict.** Gather more evidence, especially functional data, to tilt the balance.  
- **Domain‑critical missense changes in PARN’s R3H motif are high‑yield for pathogenicity.**  
- **Phenotypic presentation matters.** PARN monoallelic disease can masquerade as isolated IPF or bone‑marrow failure without classic mucocutaneous signs.  
- **Family testing is critical** for both risk assessment and reproductive counselling.  
- **Surveillance** of blood counts, liver elastography, and lung imaging should be part of routine care until a definitive genotype‑disease link is established.  
- **Plan for re‑analysis.** The genetics landscape evolves; a VUS today may become pathogenic tomorrow.

---

### Final Thought

Clinical “stand‑still” is a temporary state. By systematically interrogating the variant with ACMG scoring, domain relevance, functional assays, and inheritance analysis—while continuing to treat the patient as a potential TBD—you convert a static “VUS” tab into a dynamic, actionable management plan. The PARN c.613T>C variant teaches us that our diagnostic algorithms must remain flexible, evidence‑driven, and always patient‑centered. Keep it simple: **paragon.**
