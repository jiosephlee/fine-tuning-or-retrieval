Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 2: ## Differential Diagnosis and Systematic Workup

The presentation of pancytopenia in a young adult demands a structured investigation that progressively excludes common acquired disorders and narrows the focus to rarer inherited etiologies. In this patient, the concurrent features of pronounced fatigue, weight loss, premature greying, hepatosplenomegaly and thoracic chest findings guided a multi‑disciplinary workup, comprising laboratory tests, imaging modalities, and bone marrow evaluation. The sequence of diagnostics we followed is representative of a standard approach in the evaluation of pancytopenia and its underlying causes.

First, the laboratory data highlighted a **severe vitamin B12 deficiency** (serum levels below the lower limit of normal) that explained many of the hematologic sequelae. Proton cupred protein (holotranscobalamin) had been undetectable, and peripheral smear revealed dimorphic RBCs, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—hallmarks of ineffective erythropoiesis secondary to B12 deficiency. This condition was confirmed by a markedly low reticulocyte count (0.39 %), indicating that marrow output was inadequate even in the setting of high turnover. While vitamin‑B12–mediated cytopenia often resolves with supplementation, the persistence of pronounced fatigue and organ involvement suggested that deficiency was only part of the picture.

Differentiating **primary bone marrow failure (BMF)** from **ineffective erythropoiesis** hinges on marrow morphology and laboratory indices of erythroid activity. In BMF, one expects sparse trilineage hematopoiesis or dysplasia with a blunted response to splenic or bone‑marrow stimuli, whereas ineffective erythropoiesis presents with hyperplastic erythroid precursors and scattered iron overload. This patient’s bone‑marrow aspirate showed trilineage hematopoiesis with pronounced erythroid hyperplasia and abundant iron deposits, aligning with ineffective erythropoiesis rather than a primary stem‑cell disorder. The low reticulocyte production rate, despite hyperplasia, underscored marrow failure at a functional level.

The **direct antiglobulin test (DAT)** was positive for IgG (2 +), raising the possibility of autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). However, indirect markers of hemolysis (elevated lactate dehydrogenase, indirect hyperbilirubinemia) were absent, and there was no drop in reticulocyte count consistent with a hemolytic crisis. This discordance between a positive DAT and the absence of biochemical evidence of hemolysis pointed to either an isolated in vitro autoantibody reaction or a subclinical AIHA that had been mitigated by concurrent therapies, but more plausibly suggested that the DAT result was an epiphenomenon rather than the primary driver of cytopenia.

The evaluation of **myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS)** required a rigorous appraisal of marrow morphology and cytogenetics. No dysplastic changes were observed in any lineage, and karyotype analysis returned a normal 46XY complement, eliminating typical MDS abnormalities such as del(5q), trisomy 8 or complex cytogenetic patterns. Furthermore, flow cytometric assessment for abnormal immunophenotypes (e.g., loss of CD34 expression) was unremarkable. These findings effectively excluded MDS as a plausible cause of her cytopenia and pointed to a non‑malignant marrow process.

Simultaneously, the patient manifested **hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis**, prompting a targeted search for etiologies of liver disease. Autoimmune serologies (ANA, SMA, LKM‑1, AMA, anti‑dsDNA) were all negative. Viral hepatitis panels (HBV, HCV, CMV, EBV IgM) showed no active infection; only past EBV exposure was documented. Metabolic causes were investigated: iron studies indicated elevated ferritin and serum iron, but transferrin saturation remained within normal limits, decreasing the likelihood of hemochromatosis. Imaging via transient elastography (FibroScan) indicated significant hepatic steatosis and elevated liver stiffness (18.1 kPa) suggestive of cirrhosis. The absence of inflammatory infiltrates on Ziehl‑Neelsen staining and histological examination excluded granulomatous hepatitis, leaving a non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) or secondary fibrosis from telomere‑mediated hepatotoxicity as potential contributors.

Pulmonary imaging, specifically high‑resolution CT and SUV‑PET/CT, revealed **idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)** features: diffuse ground‑glass opacities, subpleural reticulation, and traction bronchiectasis in both lower lobes. These findings, combined with the patient’s history of exertional dyspnea and chest discomfort, established a diagnosis of interstitial lung disease. The lack of smoking history, connective tissue disease markers, or occupational exposures further classified this as IPF of the idiopathic subtype.

Bringing the hematologic, hepatic, and pulmonary findings together, the clinicians considered **telomere biology disorders (TBDs)** as a unifying diagnosis. Inherited telomere shortening syndromes frequently present with overlapping manifestations: cytopenias due to marrow BMF, pulmonary fibrosis from alveolar epithelial damage, hepatic fibrosis from regenerative nodularity, and features of premature aging. This suspicion was reinforced by the patient's **premature greying, photographs of nail dysplasia, and skeletal abnormalities**, albeit subtle. A germline PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) was subsequently identified, lending molecular support to the clinical inference of a TBD.

Finally, the **iron studies and reticulocyte count** were instrumental in recasting marrow activity. The high serum iron and ferritin suggested ineffective erythropoiesis with iron trapping, whereas the low reticulocyte count underscored the marrow’s inability to translate hyperplasia into mature RBC production. This dichotomy aligns with the hallmark of telomere‑mediated marrow failure, wherein HSC senescence prohibits adequate erythropoietic output despite elevated marrow workloads.

The bone‑marrow aspirate’s final interpretation—trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and robust iron stores—served as the last empirical touchpoint linking the clinical narrative to a pathophysiological mechanism beyond acquired nutritional deficiency. It also clarified that the marrow was not inherently “failed” but rather **ineffective**, a crucial distinction with ramifications for future monitoring and management decisions, such as the need for surveillance of progression toward frank aplastic anemia or the appropriateness of considering hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation. In sum, this systematic workup demonstrates how sequential laboratory, imaging, and histopathologic data coalesce to rule out common etiologies and expose an underlying inherited telomere disorder driving multisystem pathology.
