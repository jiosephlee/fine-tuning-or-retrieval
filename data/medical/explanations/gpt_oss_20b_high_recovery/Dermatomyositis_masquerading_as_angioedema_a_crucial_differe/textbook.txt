Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Clinical Presentation and Early Differential Diagnosis

A male in his 40 s presented with a 3‑week history of progressive, painless swelling of the neck that had begun to limit swallowing and cause mild discomfort. The swelling was non‑tender, non‑fluctuant, and diffusely involved the anterior neck. He also reported proximal hip‑flexor weakness and mild myalgia, but denied fever, dysphonia, odynophagia, or constitutional symptoms. On examination the skin over the face was erythematous, particularly over the nasolabial folds, and the cervical soft tissues were visibly edematous yet non‑erythematous. Capillaroscopy revealed periungual erythema with avascular spots, and a focused musculoskeletal exam noted that hip‑flexor strength was 4/5. Of note, classic dermatomyositis rashes such as Gottron’s papules or heliotrope coloration were absent at that point.

Laboratory studies were notable for an elevated C‑reactive protein (55.6 mg/L) in the context of a normal white‑cell count (9.1 × 10^9 /L). This marked inflammatory marker with a preserved leukocyte profile suggested a non‑bacterial source of inflammation. Imaging by CT of the neck (performed after initial suspicion of a deep‑space infection) demonstrated diffuse soft‑tissue edema involving the strap muscles, with mild extension into the retropharyngeal space but no discrete abscess, gas, or fluid collection. Importantly, the airway lumen was preserved, and no bony destruction or enlarged glandular structures were identified.

In the setting of neck swelling with imaging evidence of non‑specific soft‑tissue edema, the early differential focused on the most common causes of severe submandibular‑space inflammation. Ludwig angina—polymicrobial bacterial phlegmon of the submandibular and sublingual spaces—remains the archetypal presentation, often accompanied by fever, marked leukocytosis, and rapid airway compromise. The absence of fever and leukocytosis, along with the lack of abscess on CT, made this diagnosis less likely but still considered due to its clinical urgency. A deep neck space infection, encompassing retropharyngeal or parapharyngeal phlegmon/abscess, similarly warranted exclusion; again, the neutropenia‑free, sterile imaging and unremarkable systemic signs argued against it.

Another provisional diagnosis at this juncture was idiopathic angioedema—a non‑histaminergic, non‑bradykinin‑mediated swelling that can localise to the neck. Angioedema typically presents with rapid, painless swelling that is often non‑tender and may involve the face, lips, tongue, or airway structures. The persistently progressive nature, lack of typical triggers (e.g., ACE inhibitors, DPP‑4 inhibitors), and the presence of extra‑cutaneous signs (periungual erythema) nonetheless prompted consideration of an underlying systemic process.

The constellation of subtle cutaneous findings, proximal muscle weakness, and a high‑grade CRP in the absence of overt infection, prompted the medical team to broaden the differential to include inflammatory myopathies. Though the classic dermatomyositis skin lesions were not yet visible, early nailfold changes and muscle weakness are recognised early features of the disease. The high creatine kinase level obtained subsequently would prove diagnostic, but at presentation the differential narrowly focused on Ludwig angina, deep neck space infection, and idiopathic angioedema, with an emerging suspicion that a systemic inflammatory myopathy could be the underlying driver of the neck oedema.

## Dermatomyositis and the Anti‑SAE1 Antibody  

Dermatomyositis is an organ‑specific autoimmune disease that predominantly involves the skin and proximal skeletal muscles but can affect the lungs, heart and gastrointestinal tract.  Its hallmark is a complement‑mediated microangiopathy, where deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) in perifascicular capillaries triggers a cascade of inflammation that ultimately leads to myofibre necrosis and the classic perifascicular atrophy seen on muscle biopsy.  The vascular injury is thought to be initiated by circulating autoantibodies that fix complement, and it is this process that underlies the proximal muscle weakness and the predilection for the neck and shoulder girdle that are typical of the disease.

Cutaneous disease in dermatomyositis is highly pathognomonic.  Classic manifestations include Gottron papules over the knuckles, a heliotrope rash around the eyelids, a shawl sign over the back, and a V‑neck sign on the chest.  In the patient described, the cutaneous signs were subtle – periungual erythema and mild facial erythema – underscoring that obvious skin lesions are not a prerequisite for diagnosis and that a high index of suspicion must be maintained when other organ systems are involved.

Myositis‑specific autoantibodies provide a biomarker framework that refines diagnostic certainty, informs prognosis, and suggests organ‑specific risks.  The spectrum includes anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑Jo‑1, anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑MDA‑5, anti‑NXP‑2, anti‑OJ, and, more recently, anti‑SAE1.  Each autoantibody is associated with a distinct phenotype.  In particular, anti‑SAE1 is an uncommon serotype that has been increasingly recognized and is linked to a severe, rapidly progressive clinical course.

Anti‑SAE1 antibodies target the small ubiquitin‑like modifier‑activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1), a component of the sumoylation pathway that regulates nuclear transport, transcriptional activity, and DNA repair.  The prevailing hypothesis is that binding of anti‑SAE1 antibodies disrupts the sumoylation machinery, leading to a pro‑inflammatory state that precipitates complement activation.  In the patient’s thigh biopsy, the characteristic findings were evident: necrotic and vacuolated fibres, scattered perimysial histiocytes, and robust deposition of C5b‑9 around perifascicular capillaries.  These histologic features mirror the underlying pathogenic sequence of complement fixation, endothelial injury, and subsequent muscle fibre necrosis.

The clinical phenotype of anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis is dominated by pronounced dysphagia and interstitial lung disease (ILD).  The case patient presented with severe pharyngeal dysphagia that necessitated nasogastric feeding, and imaging revealed peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration‑related inflammation.  Dysphagia and ILD are recognized as major risk factors for malignancy in dermatomyositis, and the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) algorithm classifies anti‑SAE1 carriers with dysphagia as high‑risk for underlying cancer.  Accordingly, the patient received comprehensive malignancy screening, including CT imaging and tumor‑marker panels, and will continue targeted surveillance.

In summary, anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis represents a distinct serotype of the disease, characterized by severe muscle inflammation, significant dysphagia, potential pulmonary involvement, and a subtle cutaneous presentation.  Recognition of its immunopathogenic signature – complement‑driven microangiopathy triggered by an antibody against a sumoylation enzyme – enables clinicians to initiate aggressive immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids, IVIG, rituximab) and to implement vigilant monitoring for organ damage and occult malignancy.  The case illustrates how a combination of subtle skin findings, markedly elevated creatine kinase, and biopsy confirmation of complement deposition can guide the timely diagnosis and tailored management of this rare but potentially serious antibody phenotype.

## Diagnostic Workup and Reasoning

The patient’s initial presentation—persistent neck swelling with no obvious infectious or structural cause—necessitated a systematic, multi‑modality assessment to delineate the underlying pathology. The diagnostic strategy combined basic laboratory surveillance, targeted autoantibody testing, advanced imaging, and definitive tissue diagnosis, all of which converged to identify anti‑SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis.

**Comprehensive laboratory workup** revealed a strikingly elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 16,184 U/L, far above the threshold commonly used to suspect inflammatory myopathy (>5 000 U/L). Concurrently, the hepatic panel showed markedly raised transaminases (ALT 143 U/L, AST 396 U/L) and an elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH 692 U/L). These laboratory abnormalities are hallmarks of muscle cell necrosis, indicating active myositis. The antinuclear antibody (ANA) screen was highly positive (titer 1:1280, speckled pattern), supporting an autoimmune process. A Euroimmun myositis antibody panel was performed, yielding a strongly positive anti‑small ubiquitin‑like modifier activating enzyme subunit‑1 (SAE1) antibody (signal intensity 173). The panel was negative for other myositis‑specific antibodies (anti‑Mi‑2α, anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑Jo‑1, anti‑OJ), narrowing the differential to dermatomyositis with a distinct serotype.

**Imaging studies** further mapped the extent of inflammatory involvement. A chest CT scan displayed diffuse ground‑glass opacities and several non‑cavitating nodules, suggesting interstitial inflammation rather than malignancy. MRI of the thighs demonstrated active myositis with perifascicular signal changes and atrophy, characteristics typical of dermatomyositis. Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG‑PET) revealed diffuse, abnormal uptake in multiple muscle groups—including the neck, shoulder girdle, and torso—consistent with widespread myositis rather than localized pathology. Finally, a video fluoroscopic swallow study (barium swallow) identified severe pharyngeal dysphagia with residue in the pyriform fossae and post‑swallow penetration of fluids, correlating with the patient’s clinical airway compromise.

**Muscle biopsy** provided definitive histopathological confirmation. The specimen showed moderate necrosis, vacuolated degenerated fibers, and regenerative changes in a perifascicular distribution. Perimysial inflammation consisted of isolated histiocytes, and immunohistochemistry highlighted complement deposition (C5b‑9) along the endomysial capillaries surrounding the perifascicular zone—an immunopathologic signature of dermatomyositis. The absence of rimmed vacuoles or inclusion bodies excluded inclusion body myositis, while the lack of widespread myofiber necrosis without inflammation argued against immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy.

**Exclusion of alternative diagnoses** was accomplished through a series of negative investigations: cultures (blood, sputum, and deep airway specimens) were sterile, ruling out bacterial deep‑neck space infection; C3, C4, and C1‑esterase levels were within normal ranges, excluding hereditary or acquired complement‑mediated angioedema; complement C5b‑9 deposition in muscle, but not in serum, distinguished a complement‑mediated pathogenic process localized to muscle from systemic complement consumption. Viral serologies (HIV, HBV, HCV, VZV, CMV) and tuberculosis testing were negative, eliminating infectious etiologies that could mimic myositis.

**Diagnostic reasoning** integrated these findings into a coherent diagnosis of anti‑SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis. The markedly elevated CK coupled with transaminitis and LDH confirmed muscle involvement severe enough to cause systemic release of cytosolic enzymes. The serologic profile—high‑titer ANA and specific anti‑SAE1 positivity—aligned with a known dermatomyositis autoantibody phenotype. Imaging demonstrated active, diffuse muscle inflammation involving the neck and throat, explaining the neck swelling and dysphagia. The biopsy’s classic cavernosal‑complement deposition and perifascicular necrosis provided the gold‑standard confirmation, distinguishing dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies. The synergy of serology, imaging, and biopsy, coupled with the systematic exclusion of infectious, vascular, and medication‑induced etiologies, established dermatomyositis as the single most plausible diagnosis, justifying the aggressive immunosuppressive regimen that followed.

## Multimodal Treatment and Airway Management

The treatment of this patient was deliberately structured to achieve rapid suppression of active inflammation while preventing long‑term tissue damage, and to mitigate the inherent risks of both the disease and its therapies. The choice and sequence of therapies were guided by the severity of the presentation (severe neck oedema threatening the airway, marked proximal weakness and very high creatine kinase), the presence of dysphagia, and the documented pulmonary involvement.

**Induction with high‑dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for 3 days)**  
Severe manifestations of dermatomyositis, particularly when airway structures are involved, warrant aggressive early control. High‑dose methylprednisolone delivers rapid steroid effect through non‑genomic mechanisms, inhibiting cytokine production, stabilising lysosomal membranes and reducing leukocyte migration. In this case, a 3‑day course produced a marked decrease in neck oedema and a rapid fall in CK from >16 000 U/L to within the normal range, thereby averting imminent airway compromise. The subsequent taper to oral prednisone (60 mg daily, then 5 mg every 2–3 weeks) allowed gradual withdrawal while the disease was kept under control by other agents.

**Adjunctive intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, 2 g/kg over 5 days, followed by monthly 2 g/kg infusions)**  
IVIG exerts multiple immune‑modulatory effects: Fc‑receptor blockade, neutralisation of pathogenic autoantibodies, modulation of complement activation, and inhibition of pro‑inflammatory cytokine release. Because anti‑SAE1 disease is antibody‑driven, a high initial dose was chosen to saturate pathogenic antibodies and complement components, followed by regular maintenance infusions (five additional monthly doses). This schedule corresponds to the standard practice for refractory inflammatory myopathies and provided sustained disease suppression while steroid doses were reduced.

**Steroid‑sparing immunosuppression: mycophenolate mofetil (1 g twice daily) and rituximab (1000 mg IV, two doses 2 weeks apart)**  
The high cumulative steroid dose required prolonged immunosuppression. Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) inhibits lymphocyte proliferation by blocking inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase; it is orally administered, well tolerated, and effective as a maintenance agent in dermatomyositis. Rituximab targets CD20‑positive B‑cells, depleting the source of autoantibody production. The combination of MMF and rituximab was chosen because: 1) MMF is predictable and convenient in a middle‑aged adult; 2) rituximab has evidence for steroid‑sparing in refractory myositis; and 3) together they target both T‑ and B‑cell pathways, which is valuable in a disease with a strong autoimmune signature. This approach allowed a successful taper of oral prednisone to discontinuation by 7 months, as noted in the follow‑up.

**Avoidance of methotrexate**  
Methotrexate is a common steroid‑sparing agent in dermatomyositis, but it carries a notable risk of interstitial lung disease (ILD). The patient had a baseline restrictive pattern with bibasilar nodules and peribronchial consolidation on CT, suggesting early pulmonary involvement. To avoid exacerbating ILD, methotrexate was deliberately omitted in favour of MMF and rituximab, which have a more favourable pulmonary safety profile.

**Opportunistic infection prophylaxis**  
High‑dose steroids plus MMF and rituximab create a profound immunosuppressive milieu that increases risk of *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia (PCP). The initial prophylactic regimen was TMP‑SMX (trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole), the standard first‑line agent. Two months into therapy, the patient developed haemolytic anaemia (notably in the absence of G6PD deficiency), necessitating a switch to nebulised pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks. Pentamidine remains effective for PCP prophylaxis and is better tolerated in patients who cannot remain on TMP‑SMX.

**Airway and nutritional management**  
The pronounced neck swelling and dysphagia posed a dual threat: airway compromise and inadequate caloric intake. ENT evaluation confirmed no mechanical obstruction of the airway, but the subclinical risk warranted close monitoring. Meanwhile, the severe pharyngeal dysphagia identified on barium swallow precluded safe oral feeding. A nasogastric tube was inserted to ensure reliable nutrition and hydration during the acute phase. Subsequent ENT follow‑up assessed swallowing recovery; progressive improvement allowed weaning from the tube after 2 months, with eventual return to a regular diet. The nasogastric route also provided a stable delivery path for oral medications (MMF) during this vulnerable period.

Altogether, the multimodal approach—rapid high‑dose steroids, targeted IVIG, judicious steroid sparing, infection prophylaxis, and airway‑supportive measures—ensured disease control, prevented complications, and facilitated a complete clinical remission with no residual dysphagia or airway sequelae.

## Prognosis, Follow‑up, and Malignancy Surveillance

The patient’s initial therapeutic response was strikingly rapid. Within three weeks of commencing high‑dose methylprednisolone and intravenous immunoglobulin, creatine kinase had fallen from 16 184 U/L to the normal range (37 U/L) and proximal muscle strength returned to full, while the diffuse neck‑soft‑tissue swelling had markedly subsided. By the time of discharge, the neck edema was no longer palpable, and the patient could open his mouth with minimal resistance. During a two‑month clinic review the tetraparesis had resolved completely and the patient was able to tolerate a soft‑solid diet after removal of his nasogastric tube. These findings underscore the fact that aggressive early immunosuppression can reverse even the most disfiguring manifestations of dermatomyositis.

The pulmonary sequelae also showed a favorable trajectory. Initial high‑resolution CT of the chest had revealed numerous non‑cavitating nodules and patchy peribronchial ground‑glass infiltrates, attributed in part to aspiration pneumonitis. At 11 months post‑diagnosis a repeat scan demonstrated near‑complete resolution of the previously noted opacities, leaving only a mild, focal residual airspace consolidation. Pulmonary function testing at the same time point revealed a modest improvement, with the restrictive defect now approximating the patient’s baseline spirometry metrics. The residual restriction is likely a feature of pre‑existing pulmonary physiology rather than ongoing disease activity, reinforcing the notion that the immunomodulatory regimen effectively contained the interstitial component of his dermatomyositis.

The International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) framework for malignancy screening in inflammatory myopathies places this patient squarely in the high‑risk category. Dermatomyositis itself is a recognised paraneoplastic marker; dysphagia contributes an additional weight; anti‑SAE1 antibodies, though less frequently associated with malignancy than other myositis specific antibodies, are still classified within the high‑risk stratum; and male sex further elevates the index. Consequently, the surveillance protocol reflects this compounded risk.

In line with current recommendations, the patient undergoes annual low‑dose chest CT scans to monitor for recurrence of nodular lesions or emergence of new pulmonary pathology. Abdominal imaging—either ultrasound or computed tomography—coupled with serial tumor markers (CEA, CA‑19‑9, CA‑125, PSA) is repeated yearly to screen for occult gastrointestinal or genitourinary malignancies. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and colonoscopy were completed at baseline and will be repeated when clinically indicated; this patient has yet to develop any symptoms suggestive of underlying neoplasia. In addition, routine surveillance of skin and nail changes, coupled with periodic laboratory reassessment of creatine kinase and inflammatory markers, ensures early detection of disease flare before systemic manifestations recur.

Several clinical pearls emerge from this case. First, dermatomyositis should be retained in the differential diagnosis of unexplained regional neck swelling, particularly when it fails to respond to typical ENT or infectious work‑up. Second, an early serum creatine kinase assessment together with a meticulous dermatologic and nailfold examination can pivot diagnostic reasoning toward an inflammatory myopathy rather than angioedema. Finally, the case illustrates that a strategic combination of high‑dose steroids, intravenous immunoglobulin, and steroid‑sparing agents (here, mycophenolate mofetil and rituximab) can induce remission of even uncommon manifestations such as pseudo‑angioedema, thereby preventing irreversible airway compromise. Continued vigilance for malignancy, guided by IMACS criteria, remains essential, as does structured long‑term follow‑up of pulmonary function and muscle strength to confirm sustained remission.
