Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Neck Swelling That Won’t Shrink: Dermatomyositis on the Radar  

---

## The “Ludwig’s” Mystery

A 47‑year‑old man falls into the ED with a three‑week history of a progressively enlarging, tender‑to‑the‑touch neck. The CT scan shows diffuse edema of the strap muscles and a little retropharyngeal “cloud” – a textbook spot for Ludwig’s angina. He’s afebrile, his oxygen sat is fine, and the white cell count is only a tad above normal. ENT is called; a naso‑endoscopy shows an unremarkable pharynx but a lot of secretions and coughing.

For a moment, we all think of dental infection, bacterial cellulitis, or even an idiopathic angioedema. The inflammatory markers are fine but a CRP of 55 mg/L nudges us to dig deeper. We screen for complement deficiency, C1‑esterase—everything ticks. And yet the swelling thrives.  

Then the big red‑flag appears: the patient’s neck is swollen, the face carries a diffuse low‑grade erythema over the nasolabial folds, and a tender but non‑pulsatile neck ball. A subtle, almost‑missable nail‑fold capillaroscopy shows peri‑ungual microhaemorrhages. Proximal hip flexor weakness is noted at the bedside (4/5). A CK at 16,184 U/L? The skin breaks...and the mystery dissolves.  

Dermatomyositis (DM) aloofly masquerades as angioedema. This case is a reminder that muscle inflammation can churn out diffuse soft‑tissue swelling that looks nothing like the classic “red, itchy, wave‑like” facial puff.

---

## When Neck Edema Looks “Wrong”

1. **Pain and transcription of a non‑infectious, non‑histamine process**  
   - No fever, no local warmth, and normal WCC.  
   - CT shows edema, not a collection or abscess.  
   - Labs: elevated CRP, not a neutrophilic surge.  

2. **Subtle cutaneous clues**  
   - Nail‑fold haemorrhages, pale avascular zones.  
   - Peri‑ungual erythema, a scattered rash over the thigh.  
   - No Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash yet—those can evolve or be subtle.  

3. **Muscle weakness and dysphagia come later**  
   - Proximal weakness in hip flexors is a mild early finding.  
   - Severe dysphagia on later swallow study; aspiration consolidation on chest CT.  

4. **High CK and transaminases**  
   - CK 16 kU/L, ALT > 140 U/L, AST ≈ 400 U/L, LDH ≈ 690 U/L.  
   - Classic myositis pattern.  

5. **Autoantibody profile**  
   - ANA 1:1280 speckled.  
   - Positive anti‑SAE1 antibody (strongly positive).  
   - Other myositis antibodies negative.  

6. **Complement normal, C1‑esterase normal**  
   - Ruling out complement‑mediated angioedema.  

7. **Biopsy points to DM**  
   - Perifascicular necrosis, vacuolation, endomysial complement C5b‑9 deposition.  

---

## Diagnostic Playbook for Unexplained Neck Swelling

1. **Get the CK early** if  
   - There is any proximal muscle weakness (even 4/5).  
   - The neck swelling is diffuse, non‑tender, non‑pulsatile, and persistent.  
   - There are subtle skin changes or nail‑fold abnormalities.  

2. **Look for dysphagia**—a silent sign of pharyngeal muscle involvement.  
   - Early video‑fluoroscopy can catch aspiration or oropharyngeal weakness.  

3. **Order a full myositis panel** for autoantibodies (ANA, myositis‑specific panel).  
   - Anti‑SAE1 is a red‑flag for dermatomyositis with a propensity for dysphagia and interstitial lung disease.  

4. **Complement, C1‑esterase** to rule out hereditary/idiopathic angioedema.  

5. **Imaging**  
   - MRI of thighs/arms shows muscle edema.  
   - PET‑CT can map active myositis and help rule out occult malignancy.  

6. **Muscle biopsy**—the “gold standard” for definitive DM diagnosis.  

7. **Screen for malignancy**—especially with high‑risk antibodies, dysphagia, young adult age.

## Treatment Snapshot

| Step | Modality | Rationale |
|------|----------|-----------|
| **Initial** | IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg × 3 days | Rapid relief of airway‑critical muscle inflammation. |
| **Adjunct** | Oral prednisone taper (60 mg → stop by 7 mo) | Sustain remission; careful taper to avoid flare. |
| **Steroid‑sparing** | Mycophenolate mofetil 1 g BID | Oral, well‑tolerated; avoids methotrexate‑fibrosis risk (patient had ILD). |
| **Escalation** | IVIG 2 g/kg over 5 days, repeat 5 cycles every 4 weeks | Plasmapheresis alternative; dampens autoantibody effect. |
| **Biologic** | Rituximab 1000 mg × 2 | Deplete B‑cells; used in refractory DM. |
| **Prophylaxis** | Initially TMP‑SMX → switched to nebulised pentamidine | Avoid hemolysis; prophylaxis for PCP in high‑dose steroid + biologic setting. |
| **Monitoring** | Serial CK, LFTs, pulmonary function, imaging | Gauge response, catch flare early, assess ILD progression. |

## Lessons You’ll Take Home

- **Dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema**—especially when muscle inflammation produces diffuse soft‑tissue swelling.  
- **Elevated CK is a golden clue** in the face of unexplained neck swelling; get it ASAP.  
- **Subtle cutaneous and nail‑fold findings matter**—they’re often the first hint of DM.  
- **Collaborate early**: ENT may first appreciate the swelling, but ENT, immunology, and rheumatology together unearth the diagnosis.  
- **Anti‑SAE1 antibodies signal a high‑risk DM phenotype** (disability, dysphagia, ILD, and malignancy).  
- **Early, aggressive immunosuppression improves outcomes** and the risk of irreversible dysphagia or respiratory compromise.  

---

## Quick‑Reference Take‑Home Points

- Neck (or facial) swelling that *doesn’t fit a classic infection*: Think **pseudo‑angioedema** secondary to dermatomyositis.  
- **CK + muscle exam + skin/nail review** are your first triage steps.  
- **Autoantibody panels**: a positive anti‑SAE1 in a patient with abnormal CK, dysphagia, and mild skin changes commands urgent treatment.  
- **Treat early** with high‑dose steroids; add IVIG or rituximab for refractory disease; tailor steroid‑sparing agents to avoid pulmonary toxicity.  
- **Screen for underlying malignancy** per IMACS consensus—high‑risk factors include dermatomyositis, dysphagia, SAE1 positivity, and male sex.  

---

#### Bottom line  
If you see a patient with neck swelling that refuses to go away and doesn’t match an obvious infection or vasculitic angioedema, pull out that CK tube, re‑examine the skin and nails, and consider an early myositis panel. Dermatomyositis may be hiding in plain sight—eyes (and a bit of detective work) open.
