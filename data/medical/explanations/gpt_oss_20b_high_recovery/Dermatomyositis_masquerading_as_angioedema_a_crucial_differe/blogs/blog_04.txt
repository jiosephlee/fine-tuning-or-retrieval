Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# From Steroids to Rituximab: Building a Stepwise Prescription for Dermatomyositis  

## Introduction  
When a 42‑year‑old man thumped into the emergency department with a three‑week history of neck swelling, it sounded like every textbook definition of Ludwig’s angina. A CT scan confirmed diffuse strap‑muscle oedema extending into the retropharyngeal space, yet he had no fever, no tachycardia, and a normal white‑cell count. ENT surgeons looked at the soft tissues and ruled out airway compromise or an abscess, yet the swelling persisted. Only after a nail‑fold capillaroscopy revealed periungual erythema and micro‑haemorrhages, and a creatine kinase drawn on the second day climbed into the tens of thousands, did the picture change dramatically: we were looking at a fulminant, anti‑SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema.  

Dermatomyositis is rare, and neck‐space inflammation even more so. In a review of 121 patients, only eight had any form of angioedema—most commonly facial, but rarely involving the neck at all. This case reminds us that the skin and muscle may hide in plain sight and can emulate an ENT emergency. The lesson? Keep the diagnosis of inflammatory myopathy on your differential when the presentation looks “angioedema” but the clues (proximal weakness, dysphagia, abnormal CK, nail changes) do not fit the classic picture.

## The Subtle Red Flags that Broke the Case  
The decision to pivot from a surgical work‑up to an immunologic one hinged on a few key findings. First, the neck swelling was disproportionate to the subtle facial erythema and the limited inflammatory markers (CRP 55 mg/L, WCC 9.1 × 10⁹/L). Second, the presence of proximal hip‑flexor weakness and a normal body temperature made infectious causes less likely. Third, a nail‑fold capillaroscopy showing avascular spots is a dermatologist’s fly‑paper for dermatomyositis. Finally, the CK not jumping into the high‑thousands was the “aha” moment—this was a muscle disease, not an allergic reaction or a vascular leak.  

When these findings line up, you should order a myositis‐autoantibody panel right away, no more than a day or two after initial presentation. In our patient the Euroimmun Euroline assay was strongly positive for anti‑SAE1 (signal intensity 173 U vs. <35 U normal), confirming the diagnosis.  

## Stepwise Algorithm: From a Steroid Kick‑off to Rituximab  
The cornerstone of treatment is a rapid but controlled anti‑inflammatory assault. Start with intravenous methylprednisolone 1 000 mg daily for three consecutive days; this “pulse” dose clears the most active muscle inflammation quickly, and in our patient that was essential for preserving swallowing function. Following the pulse, transition to oral prednisone at 60 mg daily and then taper by 5 mg every two to three weeks. The goal is to keep the dose high enough to keep inflammation in check, yet low enough to minimize long‑term steroid toxicity.  

Once steroid tapering begins, introduce a steroid‑sparing agent to halt the disease long term. In patients with interstitial lung disease (ILD) or those who may not tolerate hepatotoxic drugs, mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) 1 g BID is a safe, orally administered option; it was the first choice in our case. Azathioprine is another viable alternative, but our patient preferred MMF for its more predictable side‑effect profile. Avoid methotrexate in the presence of ILD, as it can worsen pulmonary fibrosis.  

If the patient remains active after four to six weeks of combined steroids and a steroid‑sparing agent, add either intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) or rituximab. In the patient under discussion, a course of IVIG 2 g/kg (split over five days) was administered weekly for five cycles before transitioning to monthly infusions. Rituximab was introduced only after confirming inadequate response to IVIG: two 1 000 mg IV doses 14 days apart. Rituximab offers a durable, B‑cell‑depleting platform that often reduces the need for high‑dose steroids in the long run.  

The total strategy can be summed up as a sequential stack:  
1. **High‑dose IV pulse methylprednisolone** →  
2. **Oral prednisone taper** →  
3. **Steroid‑sparing agent (MMF or azathioprine)** →  
4. **Add IVIG or rituximab for refractory disease**.  

## Infectious Prophylaxis and Safety Nets  
The heavy climbing of steroids and immunomodulators opens the door to opportunistic infections. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis with trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole (TMP‑SMX) is standard; however, our patient developed a brisk hemolytic anaemia, prompting a switch to nebulized pentamidine 300 mg quarterly. Regular CBCs, electrolytes, and liver enzymes must be monitored for drug toxicity. In patients receiving rituximab, vaccinate against pneumococcus, Haemophilus influenzae type b, and a high‑dose influenza prior to therapy, and consider a live vaccine only after B‑cell recovery.

## Pulmonary Guardrails  
In dermatomyositis, ILD is a frequent complication and an independent risk factor for mortality. The case report revealed an extrathoracic‑restrictive pattern on spirometry and CT chest nodules without fibrosis, but no definite ILD at baseline. Even a mild pulmonary involvement indicates that the patient may develop ILD later, so drug selection must be ILD‑wise. Methotrexate, the “go‑to” disease‑modifying agent in many myositis protocols, is contraindicated in ILD: its pulmonary toxicity is additive. Hence, we favored MMF, which has a more favorable ILD trajectory.

## Monitoring the Fulminance  
Long‑term remission depends on vigilant monitoring. Serial CK levels should be obtained every 1–2 weeks until normalization, then taper to monthly check‑ins; persistence or rebound of CK signals relapse. Imaging—MRI or FDG‑PET—for muscle activity isn’t routinely repeated unless clinical deterioration occurs, but it can be reassuring after a year of steady improvement. Pulmonary function testing every 3–6 months keeps the ILD sensor alert. Finally, nerve function—both peripheral and bulbar—should be retained in the endocrine (symptom diaries, dysphagia scales), akin to how we treat myasthenia gravis: early speech‑therapy consults often prevent silent aspiration.

## Special Considerations in SAE1 Dermatomyositis  
Anti‑SAE1 positivity is a distinct subtype: it carries high rates of dysphagia (over 40 %), ILD (≈ 44 %), and dysmorphisms in the muscle atrophy pattern. These patients are also at increased risk of malignancy—roughly 18 %. As a result, a bleeding‑risk screening (full-body MRI or CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, tumor markers including CEA, CA‑19‑9, CA‑125, PSA) and periodic surveillance via endoscopy or colonoscopy is warranted. Importantly, the anti‑SAE1 serology did not alter the pharmacologic plan in our case; it simply underscored the need for close monitoring of dysphagia and ILD, as well as a rigorous malignancy work‑up that included a PET scan and routine imaging.

## What the Patient’s Journey Shows  
The man went from potentially irreversible airway compromise and permanent dysphagia to a complete resolution of cervical swelling and a return to normal creatine kinase levels in nine months. The gradual taper of steroids, the sustained MMF regimen, the supportive IVIG, and the timely rituximab infusion crafted a treatment roadmap that worked for a disease that is, by nature, variable and often unpredictable. His case illuminates that dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema; that a thoughtful, staged immunosuppressive schedule can rescue function; and that early, comprehensive screening for malignancy is no longer a luxury—it’s a necessity.

## Take‑home Messages  
- **Think dermatomyositis when neck swelling is unexplained, especially if CK is dramatic and there are subtle skin or nail clues.**  
- **Start with a high‑dose IV methylprednisolone pulse, then taper prednisone over several weeks, always matching the taper to clinical activity.**  
- **Choose a steroid‑sparing agent that protects or does not harm the lungs; MMF is preferable to methotrexate in ILD‑at‑risk patients.**  
- **Add IVIG (2 g/kg over 5 days) or rituximab (1000 mg × 2, 14 days apart) for refractory or fulminant disease.**  
- **Prophylaxis for PJP (TMP‑SMX or nebulized pentamidine) and routine infection monitoring are essential with high‑dose steroids and B‑cell depletion.**  
- **Monitor CK, pulmonary function, and muscle imaging regularly; adjust immunosuppression accordingly.**  
- **Screen aggressively for malignancy (CT/PET, endoscopy) in anti‑SAE1–positive patients, especially if dysphagia or ILD is present.**
