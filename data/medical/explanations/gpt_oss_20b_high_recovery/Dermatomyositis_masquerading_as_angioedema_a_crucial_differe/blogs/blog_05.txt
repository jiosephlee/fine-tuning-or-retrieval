Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Skimming the Skin: Nailfold Capillaries to Gottron Papules, How to Spot Dermatomyositis Early  

---

## What makes this case a Head‑Lightning Alert  

I used to think “neck swelling in a young, otherwise healthy male” meant Ludwig’s angina, cellulitis or, if imaging cleared, idiopathic angioedema. When a patient in his early 40s came to our ED with a 3‑week march of neck oedema—diffuse strapping of the strap muscles, retropharyngeal fullness on CT, a mild rise in CRP (55 mg/L) and no fever—I was ready for a surgical consultation.  
What stuck with me long after he was scheduled for discharge was a pair of tiny spots on his nail folds that appeared “too big for the size” of the hand and a faint, non‑tender, eczema‑like erythema on his jaw.  Gottron papules?  Heliotrope?  No.  The skin was almost *quiescent*.  Yet the creatine kinase begged for something that would detonate the whole picture: 16 kU/L.  

That CK spike pushed us to think beyond angioedema.  A muscle biopsy showed perifascicular necrosis and complement deposition—classic for dermatomyositis (DM).  What made the case even more head‑long was that the neck swelling was an uncommon manifestation of DM.  Instead of empiric antibiotics or antihistamines, we gave high‑dose steroids, IVIG, and mycophenolate, and the patient’s CK normalized, dysphagia resolved, and the neck swelling receded—within weeks.  

You’re probably wondering: *How did those nail‑fold changes help us shortcut a correct diagnosis?*  That’s the heart of this post.

---

## Nailfold Capillaroscopy: The “Peek‑inside” Sniffer  

**Why it matters**  
Periungual telangiectasias, capillary dropouts, and micro‑hemorrhages are a hallmark of systemic autoimmune disease, and they’re often under‑seen in busy EDs or ENT sees. In DM, nailfold capillaroscopy can reveal:

* **Micro‑hemorrhages** – tiny bleeding sites around capillaries.  
* **Avascular “loops”** – missing or broken capillary loops.  
* **Periungual erythema** – a subtle warm red halo around the nails.  

In our patient, capillaroscopy was *not* a routine study; it was a physical‑exam trick. When I sulked over the hand, a magnifier and a custom “nail‑fold meter” turned the home‑cooked capillaroscope into a diagnostic tool.  

**Practical tip:**  
1. Use a 10× magnification (a smartphone camera or a simple dermoscope).  
2. Look at all fingers; count ≥3 micro‑hemorrhages or any avascular zones.  
3. A “shielding” erythema around the nail (the “Holstein sign” as we call it) is a subtle cue worth not ignoring.  

If the findings are suspicious, flag it on your differential and order a CK in parallel.  

---

## The Skin: From “No Petechiae” to “Gottron’s Papules”  

The spectrum of cutaneous signs in DM is broader than the classic “heliotrope + Gottron papules.”  In the literature, an estimated 50–70 % of dermatomyositis patients have periungual telangiectasias or nailfold changes.  Holsten‑sign (peri‑nail erythema), a blue‑violet eyelid rash (heliotrope), V‑sign (shawl‑like rash on the chest) and Gottron papules (red‑brown papules over the knuckles) are the “gold‑standard” but are *often subtle and missed in early disease*.

Our patient had **periungual erythema** (Holstein sign), a faint ochre erythema over the face, and a mild rash on the right lateral thigh.  No Gottron papules were visible, perhaps because they were yet to surface or were underreported in a pain‑dominant presentation.  

**Bottom line:**  
Pay attention to that “faint blush” around the nails or eyelids.  They can actually be more reliable than classic papules in the early stage.

---

## Neck Swelling – The “Pseudo‑Angioedema” That Is Actually Myositis  

When we first saw the patient’s neck, we’d default to “deep neck space infection,” then “angioedema.”  The literature calls this pseudo‑angioedema rare – 8 out of 121 DM patients in a large cohort – but it can happen.  The swelling originates not from histamine or bradykinin, but from **inflammatory edema of striated muscle** in the neck and pharynx.  

Key clues that hinted at DM rather than angioedema:

| Feature | DM | Angioedema |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | subacute, progressive | abrupt |
| Associated weakness | proximal, facial | often absent |
| Parallel erythema | subtle, diffuse | intense swelling |
| CK level | huge | normal |
| Complement levels | normal | low C4 (hI-1) |
| Nailfold changes | present | absent |

These distinctions remind us that a normal CK or a positive ANA can be the *tip of the iceberg* when interpreting neck swelling.

## A Converging Exam Checklist: “Nail‑Foot‑Popay”  

Some clinicians still rely on a long list of videos or papers to find DM clues.  Here’s how you can quickly screen for DM in one pass:

1. **Neck & Mouth:** palate, tongue, pharynx. Look for swelling, dysphagia.  
2. **Face:** heliotrope rash takes the top–right seat; arthritic cheeks for shawl sign.  
3. **Hands & Fingers:** thrill for Gottron papules over dorsum of index and middle fingers.  
4. **Peri‑Nail Assessment:** look for micro‑hemorrhages, telangiectasias, “Holstein” redness.  
5. **Legs:** subfascial erythema, especially the lateral thigh.  
6. **Labs:** CK, LDH, transaminases, ANA, anti‑dozens.  

The “popay” in the menstrual “Nail‑Foot‑Popay” is the cough of “Neck & Mouth + Nail + POPA (Peri‑Nail + opti‑CK)".  (Game of phonetics).  

Keep this in the back of your head: when you see swelling that *feels like edema yet it’s no histamine*, pull out a CK.  If it shoots, keep the capillary exam close.

## How the Story Unfolded – One Patient’s Timeline  

- **Day 0–3** – Neck swelling, mild myalgia, mild proximal weakness.  
- **Day 1** – CT neck shows diffuse strapping.  
- **Day 2** – ENT ruled out infection; flexible nasendoscopy normal body.  
- **Day 3** – Nailfold changes noted (micro‑hemorrhages).  
- **Day 4** – CK 16 184 U/L.  
- **Day 5** – ANA 1:1280, anti‑SAE1 positive.  
- **Day 7** – Muscle biopsy, C5b‑9 deposition.  
- **Day 10** – started IV methylpred, IVIG, mycophenolate.  
- **Day 21** – discharge with nasogastric tube.  
- **Month 3** – CK normalized, neck swelling resolved, pred discontinued.  

That’s the fastest route: early CK → autoantibody screen → muscle pathology → targeted therapy.

## Take‑Home Takeaways (Pavilions)  

- **Ask for CK when you see unexplained neck swelling or dysphagia, even if the patient has no obvious skin rash.**  
- **Perform a quick nail‑fold capillaroscopy; micro‑hemorrhages or avascular loops are a red flag for DM.**  
- **Remember the “Hallmark‑Pitfalls”:**  
  - “No contraction” but *subtle* facial erythema (Holstein sign).  
  - Lack of Gottron papules does *not* exclude DM.  
- **Workup order:** CT neck → ENT → CK → ANA + myositis antibody panel → muscular biopsy if CK remains high.  
- **Early aggressive immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids, IVIG, immunosuppressants) can reverse the neck swelling and prevent long‑term sequelae.**  
- **DM patients may also have “pseudo‑angioedema” – swelling that mimics histamine‑mediated forms but is truly inflammatory muscle edema.**  

---

## Bottom Line  

Dermatomyositis can be a silent masquerader, hiding behind a quiet neck in a 40‑year‑old male.  The trick is to look beyond the obvious and use *tiny* physical clues – micro‑hemorrhages in the nail fold, a faint peri‑nail redness – to trigger the right laboratory workup.  A failed initial diagnosis can lead to delayed immunosuppression, and that translates to worse outcomes.  

Next time you encounter a patient with unexplained neck swelling, keep the capilaroscopy in your pocket and the CK bottle under your wrist.  A quick glance at the nail line could be your best diagnostic cheat sheet.
