Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# When ENT Meets Immunology: A Multidisciplinary Playbook for Dermatomyositis in the ED

## The “angioedema” that was a myositis

I first saw the picture of a neck that “looked like Ludwig’s angina” when a 42‑year‑old man was brought into the ED after three weeks of progressive swelling. The CT showed diffuse edema in the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space, yet he was afebrile, saturating 99 % on room air, and had no signs of infection. An ENT consult followed the obvious logic: was this a deep neck space infection? The nasendoscopy was unremarkable other than a “noisy” airway with increased secretions – still nothing definitive.

What was truly surprising – and ultimately life‑saving – was a sharp rise in creatine kinase (CK) to 16,184 U/L the next day, along with a speckled ANA at 1:1280. Nailfold capillaroscopy revealed micro‑haemorrhages and avascular patches. A myositis screen was strongly positive for anti‑SAE1 antibodies. We suddenly had a patient whose neck swelling was not histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema at all, but rather inflammatory edema from a severe, SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis (DM).

This case showcases how skin, muscle, and airway can intersect in a way that gives ENT and emergency physicians a cue that “angioedema” may actually be “myositis masquerading as angioedema”.

---

## Recognising the red flags

| Red flag | Why it matters |
|----------|----------------|
| Neck swelling **without** fever or dental abscess | Think beyond infection → inflammatory myopathy |
| Elevated inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) **and** CK | CK >10× ULN is a hallmark of myositis |
| Nailfold changes or subtle facial erythema | Peri‑ungual telangiectasia or heliotrope‑like rash can be subtle |
| Weakness, especially proximal hip flexors or dysphagia | Dysphagia signals bulbar muscle involvement → airway risk |
| Absence of medication exposures that cause angioedema (ACEI, ibuprofen) | Excludes classic drug‑related paths |

The stepladder of suspicion begins with: **“If the neck swelling looks angioedema but the patient keeps having elevated CK and a rash or nail changes, get a rheumatology/immunology consult ASAP.”**

---

## The airway first checklist

1. **Rapid assessment of breathing** – tachypnoea, use of accessory muscles, stridor.  
2. **Image‑guided scope** – flexible nasendoscopy to rule out mass lesions or true airway compression.  
3. **Vital‑sign stability** – ensure O₂ sat >94 % on nasal cannula; if not, intubation (oropharyngeal).

If the patient has an impending airway compromise from muscular inflammation, early intubation can be lifesaving. In the case described, the patient had no stridor but a “noisy” airway for secretions. A nasendoscopy was useful in ruling out true mechanical obstruction before proceeding with steroids.

---

## Steroids in the ED: the 1000 mg pulse

High‑dose IV methylprednisolone (1 g/d) for 3 days is the standard for active dermatomyositis with airway compromise. In our patient, this pulse therapy reduced the inflammation in strap muscles enough to allow a safe taper off the ventilator and to start oral prednisone at 60 mg. *Remember*: In myositis, the more rapid the steroid suppression, the lower the risk of complications like dysphagia or aspiration.

---

## Beyond steroids: a rapid pharmacologic strategy

| Agent | Timing | Rationale in DM |
|-------|--------|-----------------|
| **IVIG** (2 g/kg over 5 days) | Weeks 1‑2 | Bystander anti‑inflammatory effect; helpful for severe disease or steroid‑resistant cases |
| **Mycophenolate mofetil** (1 g BID) | Start in ED or admission | Oral, good for long‑term steroid sparing; avoids methotrexate’s pulmonary toxicity in patients with ILD |
| **Rituximab** (1 g×2, 2 wk apart) | Weeks 4‑6 | B‑cell depleting; useful when rapid control of autoantibody drivers is needed |
| **Pneumocystis prophylaxis** | With prednisone >20 mg/d for >3 mo | Start TMP‑SMX; if hemolysis risk → nebulized pentamidine |

An important FOAM lesson: *“You don’t need to start a battery of antibiotics when the picture screams DM. Antibacterial stewardship matters even in the ED.”*

---

## Multidisciplinary coordination: a quick workflow

1. **Early ENT call** – to confirm airway safety and evaluate for compressive lesions.  
2. **Simultaneous immunology consult** – request CK, ANA, myositis panel, and complement levels.  
3. **Radiology loop** – repeat CT neck if swelling persists, chest CT if pulmonary symptoms; FDG‑PET if malignancy suspicion.  
4. **Rheumatology review** – start steroids and plan oral/IV therapy schedule.  
5. **Pharmacy & Infectious Disease** – review antibiotic necessity and give prophylaxis.  
6. **Admissions & Follow‑up Plan** – set up wheelchair‑controlled imaging and tumour screening.

Setting up this playbook takes skeleton crew time, but the outcome is a well‑coordinated team that can progress from empirical therapy to targeted immunosuppression in a matter of hours rather than days.

---

## Lessons from the literature

* **Anti‑SAE1 DM** is “the iceberg below the surface.” It tends to have prominent dysphagia (≈ 50 %) and interstitial lung disease (≈ 40 %) but can present with unexpected skin or soft‑tissue edema.  
* **Pseudo‑angioedema** is documented in ~ 8 of 121 DM cases – a remote but real possibility.  
* **Risk of malignancy** is higher in male, dysphagic, and anti‑SAE1 DM cases; a full CT chest‑abdomen‑pelvis and PET scan are warranted.  
* **Treatment heterogeneity** – no RCTs, so we rely on expert consensus: steroids first, then a steroid‑sparing agent (MPS or azathioprine), add IVIG if severe, rituximab in refractory cases.

---

## Take‑home pearls

- **Always order CK** in a patient with subacute neck swelling that looks angioedema but is disproportionate to classic triggers.  
- **Don’t over‑use antibiotics** when the inflammatory profile points to myositis; focus on immunosuppression.  
- **Perform a quick ENT airway assessment** before giving any high‑dose steroids to avoid precipitating airway edema or displacement of a mass.  
- **Initiate high‑dose IV steroids early** (methylprednisolone 1 g ×3 days) in DM with airway risk.  
- **Automate immunology clinact**: get ANA, anti‑myositis panel, complement and CK on the same blood draw.  
- **Screen for malignancy early** (CT chest/abdomen, PET if available) because a significant minority of anti‑SAE1 DM patients have occult cancers.  
- **Coordinate across teams**: ENT + ED + Immunology + Rheumatology + Pharmacy = fastest route to remission.

---

## Bottom line

Dermatomyositis can present with spectacular neck edema that looks exactly like Ludwig’s angina or cold‑mediated angioedema. In the ED, the key is: 1) look for the deeper clues (CK, ANA, nail changes, dysphagia), 2) secure the airway first, and 3) start high‑dose steroids immediately, followed by targeted immunosuppressive therapy. When ENT medicine meets immunology, the patient gets a swift, lifesaving, multidisciplinary plan—no one day should be spent on ineffective antibiotics or unnecessary imaging.
