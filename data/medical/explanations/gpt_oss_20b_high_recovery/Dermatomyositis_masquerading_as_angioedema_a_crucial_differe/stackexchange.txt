Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] When an adult presents with unexplained non‑tender cervical edema despite normal imaging for infection, what bedside investigation should raise alert toward an inflammatory myopathy?

**Bedside screening that should immediately raise the possibility of an inflammatory myopathy**  
– **Ask for a serum creatine kinase (CK) level (and related muscle enzymes such as LDH).**  

In the reported patient presenting with persistent, non‑tender neck swelling that was essentially “angiocentric” yet imaging showed only soft‑tissue edema, a single blood test was pivotal: his CK spiked to 16 184 U/L. That dramatic elevation tipped the clinical picture from presumed angioedema or deep‑neck infection to an inflammatory myopathy—in this case anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis.  

**Why CK?**  
- CK is released from damaged skeletal muscle and is the most sensitive early marker of muscle inflammation or necrosis.  
- It can be obtained at the bedside via point‑of‑care or routine serum assay and provides immediate clinical guidance.  
- In cases of “pseudo‑angioedema” where histamine/kinin pathways are ruled out, an elevated CK directs workup toward dermatomyositis or other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies.

Thus, when faced with unexplained cervical edema in a middle‑aged adult with normal infectious workup, obtain a serum CK (and LDH) at the bedside; a markedly raised enzyme level warrants urgent rheumatologic evaluation and exclusion of dermatomyositis as demonstrated in this case report.

### [Differential Diagnosis] "In differentiating bradykinin‑mediated angioedema from an inflammatory muscle disorder that manifests as cervical soft tissue swelling,
which laboratory finding best supports the latter?")

**Key laboratory clue favoring an inflammatory myopathy over bradykinin‑mediated angioedema**

A **markedly elevated serum creatine kinase (CK)** level is the most specific laboratory finding that points toward an inflammatory muscle disease—even when it presents with neck‑swelling mimicking angioedema.  

In this case, CK rose from a baseline range to **16 184 U/L** on admission (normal < 200 U/L), a > 80‑fold increase that is incompatible with benign plasma‑volume swelling but classic for active myositis.  By contrast, bradykinin‑mediated forms of angioedema do **not** elevate CK; they may exhibit normal or decreased complement pathways (e.g., low C4 or impaired C1‑esterase function), whereas this patient had **normal complement levels** (C3, C4 and C1‑esterase).  

Thus, a highly abnormal CK level is the laboratory hallmark that distinguishes dermatomyositis‑associated cervical edema from true bradykinin‑mediated angioedema and should prompt prompt referral for myopathic investigation and treatment.

### [Pathophysiology] "What pathological mechanism underlies complement deposition observed around perifascicular vessels in patients whose autoantibody targets SUMO‑activating enzyme subunit‐1?")

**Answer**

In anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis the perifascicular “red‑rim” deposit of membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) arises from **classical‑pathway complement activation triggered by IgG immune complexes along endomysial capillaries**.  

The small‑ubiquitin‑like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 protein is an intracellular antigen that becomes exposed or released when myofibers are damaged. SAE1‑specific IgG binds these antigens (or cross‑reacts with surface molecules upregulated during inflammation) and fixes C1q on the endothelial surface. Subsequent cleavage cascades produce a local burst of C3b/C4b‐complement fragments that assemble into a terminal convertase → cleavage of C5 → formation of the pathogenic MAC (C5b‑9). The MAC inserts into the endothelial membrane and disrupts perfusion at the endomysial level, producing ischemia preferentially at the periphery of muscle fascicles—hence the classic “perifascicular atrophy” seen in DM biopsies.[^1]  

The case report provides direct evidence: immunohistochemical staining demonstrated prominent C5b‑9 localized around perifascicular endomysial capillaries despite normal circulating complementary levels, supporting a focal intra‑muscular activation phenomenon rather than systemic depletion.^[Case Report] Thus, even though serum complements were normal, local antibody–complement engagement sufficed to cause vascular injury and peripheral muscle fiber necrosis in anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis.

### [Management] "This patient’s immunosuppressive regimen avoided methotrexate despite its common use in dermatomyositis.
What explains its exclusion?")

Methotrexate was omitted because it carries a well‑documented risk of causing or exacerbating interstitial lung disease—a concern amplified in this patient who already had impaired pulmonary status. The man’s baseline investigations revealed a restrictive ventilatory defect on spirometry coupled with focal ground‑glass consolidations on chest CT; both findings raised alarm for pre‑existing or potential drug‑induced pulmonary injury. The treating team therefore elected to avoid a medication that could worsen his respiratory compromise and chose mycophenolate mofetil instead, which has a more favorable safety profile in patients with active interstitial or fibrotic changes.

### ["Morning Report"] "The team eventually ordered serum creatine kinase only after persistent neck swelling
had been studied.
Which history clue could have prompted ordering CK sooner?")

**Key teaching point**

When a patient presents with unexplained cervical soft‑tissue swelling that is not otherwise amenable to imaging or infectious work‑up, **any associated proximal muscle weakness or myalgia should trigger an early serum creatine‑kinase (CK) test** to rule out an inflammatory myopathy such as dermatomyositis.

**Why this matters in the index case**

The 40‑year‑old man described in the report first reported “progressive neck swelling” together with *proximal* hip‑flexor weakness and *mild myalgia*. These are classic clues to an underlying disease process affecting skeletal muscle even before overt skin disease appears—dermatomyositis frequently presents with initial subclinical or minimal cutaneous signs while muscular inflammation is already underway. In patients who meet these criteria, a single bedside measurement of CK (often >10 × upper limit) can quickly confirm active muscle injury and steer management toward immunosuppression instead of prolonged infectious work‑up or repeated imaging for a presumed deep‑neck infection or idiopathic angioedema.

**Clinical application**

During histories for any “pseudo‑angioedema” presenting as localized facial or cervical edema:
- Ask specifically about recent or ongoing *weakness* in thighs, hips, shoulders, or neck muscles.
- Inquire about *bone‑pain* or *myalgias* disproportionate to physical activity.
- Remember that these subtle symptoms may be easy to overlook if attention focuses solely on airway compromise.
  
Prompt ordering of CK under these circumstances—alongside basic chemistry profiles—can identify dermatomyositis early, prevent unnecessary procedures (e.g., repeated CT scans), and initiate timely immunotherapy to avert complications such as dysphagia and critical–airway involvement.

### ["Clinical Pearls"] "Patients classified high‑risk according to current IMACS criteria who meet dermatologic criteria plus dysphagia plus anti‑SAE positivity
how frequently should chest CT scans be obtained following initial diagnosis?")

**Chest‑CT surveillance in dermatomyositis with anti–SAE + dysphagia**

*High‑risk definition.*  
According to the current International Myositis Assessment & Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) algorithm a patient who has **dermatomyositis skin findings + dysphagia + an autoantibody directed against an inflammatory‑myopathy phosphoprotein** (anti‑SAE 1 being an intermediate marker) scores ≥ 2 high‑risk points plus ≥ 1 intermediate point and is thus classified as *high risk* for occult malignancy.

*Baseline workup.*  
All high‑risk patients should receive an immediate contrast‑enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest—±abdomen/pelvis—as part of their initial cancer screen. This was followed in the present case by a mid‑to‑lower zone non‑cavitating pulmonary nodule complex which resolved after immune suppression without recurrence until > 11 months post‑diagnosis.

*Recommended frequency.*  
Evidence from retrospective DM cohorts and expert consensus suggests that most tumours occur within the first two years after onset. Consequently:
1. **Repeat a chest CT every **six to twelve months** during the first 2 years** from initial diagnosis—preferably sooner (≈6 months) if CK rises or new respiratory/dysphagia symptoms appear.
2. After two years of negative surveillance, further imaging may be ceased unless new clinical aberrations occur; routine annual scans thereafter provide minimal incremental benefit and expose patients unnecessarily to radiation.

*Practical take‑away.*  
In an anti‑SAE + dermatomyositis patient with dysphagia, perform a baseline thoracic CT immediately after immunology confirmation; then schedule a second scan between **6–12 months** post‑initiation and repeat yearly until month 24 unless clinically indicated otherwise. This schedule mirrors what was used successfully in the presented case—baseline imaging followed by a single follow‑up scan at ~11 months—which reinforced its utility without over‑imaging when disease remains quiescent.^[Case Report]

### ["Pathophysiology"] "What histologic characteristic distinguishes a dermatomyositis muscle biopsy from one showing inclusion body myopathy?")

**Histologic feature that distinguishes dermatomyositis (DM) from inclusion‑body myopathy (IBM) on muscle biopsy**

| Feature | Dermatomyositis | Inclusion‑body myopathy |
|---------|-----------------|--------------------------|
| **Pattern of muscle fiber change** | Predominantly *perifascicular* necrosis/regeneration with atrophy of fibers adjacent to the fascia | *Endomysial* involvement—diffuse fiber damage scattered throughout the fascicle |
| **Capillary changes** | Complement deposition (C5b‑9 MAC) along small endomysial capillaries adjacent to affected fascicles | No systematic complement deposition; capillaries are usually normal |
| **Inflammatory infiltrate** | Histogenesis is humoral; perimysial infiltrate rich in monocytes/macrophages; occasional CD4+ T cells | Cell–mediated immunity dominates; a dense infiltrate of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells |
| **Characteristic inclusions** | None – classic histopathology shows necrosis/regeneration without cytoplasmic bodies | *Rim‑shaped vacuoles* containing amyloid β or phosphorylated TDP‑43; intracytoplasmic “sarcolemmal” actin aggregates |
| **Immunohistochemistry** | Positive staining for C5b‑9 MAC in capillaries confirms immune‑mediated microangiopathy | Negative for complement MAC; positive for amyloid or TDP‑43 in vacuoles |

**Why this matters:**  
In our patient’s thigh biopsy (Figure 4), the authors described “foci of moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated degenerate, myophagocytosed and regenerative myofibres *in a perifascicular distribution*” and *immunohistochemistry showed membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) deposition* in perifascicular endomysial capillaries—hallmarks of DM. The absence of rimmed vacuoles or amyloid/TDP‑43 inclusions – features that are pathognomonic for IBM – rules out inclusion‑body pathology.

**Bottom line:** The presence of *perifascicular necrosis/atrophy coupled with C5b‑9 complement deposition on capillaries* uniquely identifies dermatomyositis, whereas *rimmed cytoplasmic vacuoles containing amyloid/TDP‑43* distinguish inclusion‑body myositis.

### ["Management"] "For patients on chronic glucocorticoids who develop hemolytic anemia during TMP‑SMX pneumocystis prophylaxis,
which alternative agent has comparable efficacy while sparing erythrocytes?")

**Answer**

Nebulised pentamidine (300 mg every 4 weeks) is the preferred alternative for PCP prevention in patients receiving chronic glucocorticoids who develop haemolytic anaemia while on TMP‑SMX.  

**Why it works**

1. **Comparable efficacy** – Randomised studies and guideline consensus (e.g., IDSA) show that weekly or monthly nebulised pentamidine confers protection against *Pneumocystis jirovecii* infection almost as effectively as daily TMP‑SMX in high‑risk immunosuppressed populations.
2. **Erythrocyte sparing** – Pentamidine has no oxidative effect on red cells and does not diquat‑metabolises into reactive intermediates; therefore it does not precipitate haemolysis even in patients lacking G6PD deficiency.
3. **Clinical confirmation** – In the present case (dermatomyositis patient on prolonged prednisone), haemolysis developed during TMP‑SMX prophylaxis (despite normal G6PD). When switched to weekly nebulised pentamidine, he tolerated therapy without further anaemia and maintained effective PCP prevention as evidenced by stable clinical status over the subsequent follow‑up period.

**Take‑home point**

When a steroid‑treated patient presents with haemolytic anaemia during TMP‐SMX, switch to weekly–monthly nebulised pentamidine at 300 mg/occasion – it offers equivalent protection against PCP while avoiding red‑cell injury.
