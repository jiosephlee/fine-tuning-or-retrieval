Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Neck Swelling That Won’t Shrink: Dermatomyositis on the Radar  

---

## The “Ludwig’s” Mystery

A 47‑year‑old man falls into the ED with a three‑week history of a progressively enlarging, tender‑to‑the‑touch neck. The CT scan shows diffuse edema of the strap muscles and a little retropharyngeal “cloud” – a textbook spot for Ludwig’s angina. He’s afebrile, his oxygen sat is fine, and the white cell count is only a tad above normal. ENT is called; a naso‑endoscopy shows an unremarkable pharynx but a lot of secretions and coughing.

For a moment, we all think of dental infection, bacterial cellulitis, or even an idiopathic angioedema. The inflammatory markers are fine but a CRP of 55 mg/L nudges us to dig deeper. We screen for complement deficiency, C1‑esterase—everything ticks. And yet the swelling thrives.  

Then the big red‑flag appears: the patient’s neck is swollen, the face carries a diffuse low‑grade erythema over the nasolabial folds, and a tender but non‑pulsatile neck ball. A subtle, almost‑missable nail‑fold capillaroscopy shows peri‑ungual microhaemorrhages. Proximal hip flexor weakness is noted at the bedside (4/5). A CK at 16,184 U/L? The skin breaks...and the mystery dissolves.  

Dermatomyositis (DM) aloofly masquerades as angioedema. This case is a reminder that muscle inflammation can churn out diffuse soft‑tissue swelling that looks nothing like the classic “red, itchy, wave‑like” facial puff.

---

## When Neck Edema Looks “Wrong”

1. **Pain and transcription of a non‑infectious, non‑histamine process**  
   - No fever, no local warmth, and normal WCC.  
   - CT shows edema, not a collection or abscess.  
   - Labs: elevated CRP, not a neutrophilic surge.  

2. **Subtle cutaneous clues**  
   - Nail‑fold haemorrhages, pale avascular zones.  
   - Peri‑ungual erythema, a scattered rash over the thigh.  
   - No Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash yet—those can evolve or be subtle.  

3. **Muscle weakness and dysphagia come later**  
   - Proximal weakness in hip flexors is a mild early finding.  
   - Severe dysphagia on later swallow study; aspiration consolidation on chest CT.  

4. **High CK and transaminases**  
   - CK 16 kU/L, ALT > 140 U/L, AST ≈ 400 U/L, LDH ≈ 690 U/L.  
   - Classic myositis pattern.  

5. **Autoantibody profile**  
   - ANA 1:1280 speckled.  
   - Positive anti‑SAE1 antibody (strongly positive).  
   - Other myositis antibodies negative.  

6. **Complement normal, C1‑esterase normal**  
   - Ruling out complement‑mediated angioedema.  

7. **Biopsy points to DM**  
   - Perifascicular necrosis, vacuolation, endomysial complement C5b‑9 deposition.  

---

## Diagnostic Playbook for Unexplained Neck Swelling

1. **Get the CK early** if  
   - There is any proximal muscle weakness (even 4/5).  
   - The neck swelling is diffuse, non‑tender, non‑pulsatile, and persistent.  
   - There are subtle skin changes or nail‑fold abnormalities.  

2. **Look for dysphagia**—a silent sign of pharyngeal muscle involvement.  
   - Early video‑fluoroscopy can catch aspiration or oropharyngeal weakness.  

3. **Order a full myositis panel** for autoantibodies (ANA, myositis‑specific panel).  
   - Anti‑SAE1 is a red‑flag for dermatomyositis with a propensity for dysphagia and interstitial lung disease.  

4. **Complement, C1‑esterase** to rule out hereditary/idiopathic angioedema.  

5. **Imaging**  
   - MRI of thighs/arms shows muscle edema.  
   - PET‑CT can map active myositis and help rule out occult malignancy.  

6. **Muscle biopsy**—the “gold standard” for definitive DM diagnosis.  

7. **Screen for malignancy**—especially with high‑risk antibodies, dysphagia, young adult age.  

---

## Treatment Snapshot

| Step | Modality | Rationale |
|------|----------|-----------|
| **Initial** | IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg × 3 days | Rapid relief of airway‑critical muscle inflammation. |
| **Adjunct** | Oral prednisone taper (60 mg → stop by 7 mo) | Sustain remission; careful taper to avoid flare. |
| **Steroid‑sparing** | Mycophenolate mofetil 1 g BID | Oral, well‑tolerated; avoids methotrexate‑fibrosis risk (patient had ILD). |
| **Escalation** | IVIG 2 g/kg over 5 days, repeat 5 cycles every 4 weeks | Plasmapheresis alternative; dampens autoantibody effect. |
| **Biologic** | Rituximab 1000 mg × 2 | Deplete B‑cells; used in refractory DM. |
| **Prophylaxis** | Initially TMP‑SMX → switched to nebulised pentamidine | Avoid hemolysis; prophylaxis for PCP in high‑dose steroid + biologic setting. |
| **Monitoring** | Serial CK, LFTs, pulmonary function, imaging | Gauge response, catch flare early, assess ILD progression. |

---

## Lessons You’ll Take Home

- **Dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema**—especially when muscle inflammation produces diffuse soft‑tissue swelling.  
- **Elevated CK is a golden clue** in the face of unexplained neck swelling; get it ASAP.  
- **Subtle cutaneous and nail‑fold findings matter**—they’re often the first hint of DM.  
- **Collaborate early**: ENT may first appreciate the swelling, but ENT, immunology, and rheumatology together unearth the diagnosis.  
- **Anti‑SAE1 antibodies signal a high‑risk DM phenotype** (disability, dysphagia, ILD, and malignancy).  
- **Early, aggressive immunosuppression improves outcomes** and the risk of irreversible dysphagia or respiratory compromise.  

---

## Quick‑Reference Take‑Home Points

- Neck (or facial) swelling that *doesn’t fit a classic infection*: Think **pseudo‑angioedema** secondary to dermatomyositis.  
- **CK + muscle exam + skin/nail review** are your first triage steps.  
- **Autoantibody panels**: a positive anti‑SAE1 in a patient with abnormal CK, dysphagia, and mild skin changes commands urgent treatment.  
- **Treat early** with high‑dose steroids; add IVIG or rituximab for refractory disease; tailor steroid‑sparing agents to avoid pulmonary toxicity.  
- **Screen for underlying malignancy** per IMACS consensus—high‑risk factors include dermatomyositis, dysphagia, SAE1 positivity, and male sex.  

---

#### Bottom line  
If you see a patient with neck swelling that refuses to go away and doesn’t match an obvious infection or vasculitic angioedema, pull out that CK tube, re‑examine the skin and nails, and consider an early myositis panel. Dermatomyositis may be hiding in plain sight—eyes (and a bit of detective work) open.



# The Anti‑SAE1 Story: Why One Autoantibody Can Change Your Plan  

## The Unexpected Swelling  

Picture a 43‑year‑old man who comes in with a three‑week history of progressive neck swelling. His CT neck shows diffuse soft‑tissue edema but no abscess or cellulitis. ENT rules out a structural problem, and you’re left with a patient who feels weak, is febrile‑free and has a high CRP. A “deep neck space infection” has been ruled out, and no allergy or medication trigger is apparent – yet the edema keeps growing.

The cue that turned the diagnostic dial was a modest, non‑tender erythema over the nasolabial folds and subtle nailfold changes on examination. A creatine kinase (CK) test was ordered out of habit, and the result was **16,184 U/L**. This massive CK elevation, paired with a few skin findings and proximal hip‑flexor weakness, raised the suspicion of an inflammatory myopathy rather than angioedema. An anti‑small ubiquitin‑like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibody came back strongly positive, and the patient’s biopsy confirmed dermatomyositis.  

What follows is a story of how one rare autoantibody can dictate everything from prognosis to the choice of steroid taper, adjunctive immunosuppression and malignancy surveillance.

---

## What Is Anti‑SAE1 and Where Does It Sit in the Dermatomyositis Spectrum?  

Anti‑SAE1 targets a protein involved in SUMO‑dependent post‑translational modification. It is one of the myositis‑specific autoantibodies most frequently associated with **dermatomyositis (DM)** rather than immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy or inclusion‑body myopathy. In the literature, SAE1 antibodies are seen in roughly **5–10 % of adult DM patients**.  

Three major clinical themes tie the antibody to the disease biology:  

1. **Severe disease burden** – patients often present with high CK and pronounced systemic involvement.  
2. **Dysphagia and ILD** – 46 % develop dysphagia, 43 % have interstitial lung disease (ILD), mirroring the case’s aspiration pneumonitis and pulmonary nodules.  
3. **Malignancy link** – about **18 %** of anti‑SAE1 DM cases have an associated malignancy, putting patients in a higher surveillance category.

These points surfaced in the case’s rapid escalation – from neck swelling to a pneumonitis‑driven hypoxemia that required high‑dose steroids and airway‑protective care.

---

## Clinical Hooks: When to Suspect SAE1‑Positive Dermatomyositis  

- **Persistent, non‑inflammatory neck swelling** that looks like angioedema but lacks a clear etiologic trigger.  
- **Elevated CK** in the absence of trauma, statin use or strenuous exercise.  
- **Subtle cutaneous clues** such as peri‑ungual erythema, heliotrope or Gottron lesions—even if not classic.  
- **Signs of proximal muscle weakness** (e.g., weakness of hip flexors, shoulder abductors) with or without dysphagia.  
- **Abnormal transaminases** or LDH out of proportion to the degree of muscle injury.  
- **Imaging that shows diffuse muscle inflammation** – MRI, FDG‑PET or CT chest for ILD.

If you see any combination of the above, pull a CK and a myositis antibody panel ASAP. In our case, the early CK spike helped route the patient from an ENT misdiagnosis to a rheumatology referral and ultimately life‑saving therapy.

---

## Diagnostic Pitfalls and Key Tests  

| Step | Why It Matters | Typical Finding in SAE1 DM |
|------|----------------|---------------------------|
| **History & Physical** | Quit medication triggers (ACE‑I, NSAIDs, DPP‑4 inhibitors) | Subtle skin changes, dysphagia |
| **CK, AST/ALT, LDH** | Rapidly assess muscle injury | 10,000–20,000 U/L CK |
| **Nailfold capillaroscopy** | Can reveal peri‑ungual telangiectasias | Nailfold microhaemorrhages |
| **Myositis antibody panel** | Detects SAE1 and other specificities | Anti‑SAE1 > ≥ 173 (Euroimmun scale) |
| **MRI (extremities)** | Visualizes active myositis | Hyperintensity in neck, shoulders, thighs |
| **FDG‑PET** | Assesses systemic muscle burden | Diffuse uptake in neck, trunk, limbs |
| **Biopsy** | Histopathology confirms DM | Perifascicular necrosis, C5b‑9 deposition |

---

## Therapeutic Landscape: From High‑Dose Steroids to Biologics  

| Strategy | Typical Regimen | Rationale in SAE1 DM |
|----------|-----------------|----------------------|
| **High‑dose IV methylprednisolone** | 1 g × 3 days | Protects airway, counteracts severe myositis |
| **Oral prednisone taper** | 60 mg daily → 5 mg every 2–3 weeks | Maintains disease control while we add immunosuppression |
| **Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)** | 1 g BID | Oral, well‑tolerated, favorable in DM with ILD |
| **Rituximab** | 1000 mg IV × 2 (2 weeks apart) | B‑cell depletion; used here due to severe disease |
| **IVIG (Gamunex® 10 %)** | 2 g/kg over 5 days, repeat 5 cycles 4 weeks apart | Rapid improvement in dysphagia, data limited in SAE1 DM |
| **Pneumocystis prophylaxis** | TMP‑SMX → switch to nebulised pentamidine if hemolysis | Needed because of steroid + MMF + rituximab |

The regimen that this patient tolerated gave him **remarkable improvement**: CK dropped to 37 U/L by 3 weeks, dysphagia resolved, and the neck swelling resolved by 3 months. The cornerstone was early aggressive steroid therapy to salvage the airway, followed by steroid‑sparing immunosuppression and IVIG for severe dysphagia.

---

## Surveillance & Prognosis  

1. **CK & muscle enzymes** – every 2–4 weeks while tapering steroids, then annually if stable.  
2. **Pulmonary function tests** – baseline, 3–6 months, then yearly; monitor for ILD progression.  
3. **High‑resolution CT chest** – baseline, 6 months, then yearly or sooner if symptoms.  
4. **Malignancy screening** – CT chest, abdomen, pelvis; tumor markers (CEA, CA‑19‑9); upper/lower endoscopy per guidelines.  
5. **Swallow assessment** – repeat VFSS or FEES when dysphagia improves.  
6. **Adverse‑event monitoring** – CBC, liver enzymes (TMP‑SMX), renal function.

Despite a high risk of malignancy with anti‑SAE1, this patient remained cancer‑free at 12 months and continues to be monitored.

---

## Take‑Home Pearls  

- **Neck swelling can be myositis‑related**; think dermatomyositis when infection and angioedema are ruled out.  
- **CK is the go‑to test** for unexplained edema or any suspicion of muscle inflammation.  
- **Anti‑SAE1 antibodies signal a high‑risk phenotype**—dysphagia, ILD, and a ~18 % chance of malignancy.  
- **Early high‑dose steroids + steroid‑sparers** (MMF, rituximab) plus IVIG can salvage airway and dysphagia.  
- **PCP prophylaxis is essential** and may need to be switched to nebulised pentamidine if hemolysis occurs.  
- **Rigorous malignancy surveillance** remains mandatory—CT, labs, and endoscopy.  
- **Follow CK, PFTs, and imaging** to gauge response and guide tapering timelines.  

In short, a single autoantibody can shift the entire management algorithm—from emergency airway measures to a long‑term multi‑drug plan and vigilant malignancy screening. Keep it up your differential list the next time you see a patient with unexplainable neck swelling.



# When ENT Meets Immunology: A Multidisciplinary Playbook for Dermatomyositis in the ED

## The “angioedema” that was a myositis

I first saw the picture of a neck that “looked like Ludwig’s angina” when a 42‑year‑old man was brought into the ED after three weeks of progressive swelling. The CT showed diffuse edema in the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space, yet he was afebrile, saturating 99 % on room air, and had no signs of infection. An ENT consult followed the obvious logic: was this a deep neck space infection? The nasendoscopy was unremarkable other than a “noisy” airway with increased secretions – still nothing definitive.

What was truly surprising – and ultimately life‑saving – was a sharp rise in creatine kinase (CK) to 16,184 U/L the next day, along with a speckled ANA at 1:1280. Nailfold capillaroscopy revealed micro‑haemorrhages and avascular patches. A myositis screen was strongly positive for anti‑SAE1 antibodies. We suddenly had a patient whose neck swelling was not histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema at all, but rather inflammatory edema from a severe, SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis (DM).

This case showcases how skin, muscle, and airway can intersect in a way that gives ENT and emergency physicians a cue that “angioedema” may actually be “myositis masquerading as angioedema”.

---

## Recognising the red flags

| Red flag | Why it matters |
|----------|----------------|
| Neck swelling **without** fever or dental abscess | Think beyond infection → inflammatory myopathy |
| Elevated inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) **and** CK | CK >10× ULN is a hallmark of myositis |
| Nailfold changes or subtle facial erythema | Peri‑ungual telangiectasia or heliotrope‑like rash can be subtle |
| Weakness, especially proximal hip flexors or dysphagia | Dysphagia signals bulbar muscle involvement → airway risk |
| Absence of medication exposures that cause angioedema (ACEI, ibuprofen) | Excludes classic drug‑related paths |

The stepladder of suspicion begins with: **“If the neck swelling looks angioedema but the patient keeps having elevated CK and a rash or nail changes, get a rheumatology/immunology consult ASAP.”**

---

## The airway first checklist

1. **Rapid assessment of breathing** – tachypnoea, use of accessory muscles, stridor.  
2. **Image‑guided scope** – flexible nasendoscopy to rule out mass lesions or true airway compression.  
3. **Vital‑sign stability** – ensure O₂ sat >94 % on nasal cannula; if not, intubation (oropharyngeal).

If the patient has an impending airway compromise from muscular inflammation, early intubation can be lifesaving. In the case described, the patient had no stridor but a “noisy” airway for secretions. A nasendoscopy was useful in ruling out true mechanical obstruction before proceeding with steroids.

---

## Steroids in the ED: the 1000 mg pulse

High‑dose IV methylprednisolone (1 g/d) for 3 days is the standard for active dermatomyositis with airway compromise. In our patient, this pulse therapy reduced the inflammation in strap muscles enough to allow a safe taper off the ventilator and to start oral prednisone at 60 mg. *Remember*: In myositis, the more rapid the steroid suppression, the lower the risk of complications like dysphagia or aspiration.

---

## Beyond steroids: a rapid pharmacologic strategy

| Agent | Timing | Rationale in DM |
|-------|--------|-----------------|
| **IVIG** (2 g/kg over 5 days) | Weeks 1‑2 | Bystander anti‑inflammatory effect; helpful for severe disease or steroid‑resistant cases |
| **Mycophenolate mofetil** (1 g BID) | Start in ED or admission | Oral, good for long‑term steroid sparing; avoids methotrexate’s pulmonary toxicity in patients with ILD |
| **Rituximab** (1 g×2, 2 wk apart) | Weeks 4‑6 | B‑cell depleting; useful when rapid control of autoantibody drivers is needed |
| **Pneumocystis prophylaxis** | With prednisone >20 mg/d for >3 mo | Start TMP‑SMX; if hemolysis risk → nebulized pentamidine |

An important FOAM lesson: *“You don’t need to start a battery of antibiotics when the picture screams DM. Antibacterial stewardship matters even in the ED.”*

---

## Multidisciplinary coordination: a quick workflow

1. **Early ENT call** – to confirm airway safety and evaluate for compressive lesions.  
2. **Simultaneous immunology consult** – request CK, ANA, myositis panel, and complement levels.  
3. **Radiology loop** – repeat CT neck if swelling persists, chest CT if pulmonary symptoms; FDG‑PET if malignancy suspicion.  
4. **Rheumatology review** – start steroids and plan oral/IV therapy schedule.  
5. **Pharmacy & Infectious Disease** – review antibiotic necessity and give prophylaxis.  
6. **Admissions & Follow‑up Plan** – set up wheelchair‑controlled imaging and tumour screening.

Setting up this playbook takes skeleton crew time, but the outcome is a well‑coordinated team that can progress from empirical therapy to targeted immunosuppression in a matter of hours rather than days.

---

## Lessons from the literature

* **Anti‑SAE1 DM** is “the iceberg below the surface.” It tends to have prominent dysphagia (≈ 50 %) and interstitial lung disease (≈ 40 %) but can present with unexpected skin or soft‑tissue edema.  
* **Pseudo‑angioedema** is documented in ~ 8 of 121 DM cases – a remote but real possibility.  
* **Risk of malignancy** is higher in male, dysphagic, and anti‑SAE1 DM cases; a full CT chest‑abdomen‑pelvis and PET scan are warranted.  
* **Treatment heterogeneity** – no RCTs, so we rely on expert consensus: steroids first, then a steroid‑sparing agent (MPS or azathioprine), add IVIG if severe, rituximab in refractory cases.

---

## Take‑home pearls

- **Always order CK** in a patient with subacute neck swelling that looks angioedema but is disproportionate to classic triggers.  
- **Don’t over‑use antibiotics** when the inflammatory profile points to myositis; focus on immunosuppression.  
- **Perform a quick ENT airway assessment** before giving any high‑dose steroids to avoid precipitating airway edema or displacement of a mass.  
- **Initiate high‑dose IV steroids early** (methylprednisolone 1 g ×3 days) in DM with airway risk.  
- **Automate immunology clinact**: get ANA, anti‑myositis panel, complement and CK on the same blood draw.  
- **Screen for malignancy early** (CT chest/abdomen, PET if available) because a significant minority of anti‑SAE1 DM patients have occult cancers.  
- **Coordinate across teams**: ENT + ED + Immunology + Rheumatology + Pharmacy = fastest route to remission.

---

## Bottom line

Dermatomyositis can present with spectacular neck edema that looks exactly like Ludwig’s angina or cold‑mediated angioedema. In the ED, the key is: 1) look for the deeper clues (CK, ANA, nail changes, dysphagia), 2) secure the airway first, and 3) start high‑dose steroids immediately, followed by targeted immunosuppressive therapy. When ENT medicine meets immunology, the patient gets a swift, lifesaving, multidisciplinary plan—no one day should be spent on ineffective antibiotics or unnecessary imaging.



# From Steroids to Rituximab: Building a Stepwise Prescription for Dermatomyositis  

## Introduction  
When a 42‑year‑old man thumped into the emergency department with a three‑week history of neck swelling, it sounded like every textbook definition of Ludwig’s angina. A CT scan confirmed diffuse strap‑muscle oedema extending into the retropharyngeal space, yet he had no fever, no tachycardia, and a normal white‑cell count. ENT surgeons looked at the soft tissues and ruled out airway compromise or an abscess, yet the swelling persisted. Only after a nail‑fold capillaroscopy revealed periungual erythema and micro‑haemorrhages, and a creatine kinase drawn on the second day climbed into the tens of thousands, did the picture change dramatically: we were looking at a fulminant, anti‑SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema.  

Dermatomyositis is rare, and neck‐space inflammation even more so. In a review of 121 patients, only eight had any form of angioedema—most commonly facial, but rarely involving the neck at all. This case reminds us that the skin and muscle may hide in plain sight and can emulate an ENT emergency. The lesson? Keep the diagnosis of inflammatory myopathy on your differential when the presentation looks “angioedema” but the clues (proximal weakness, dysphagia, abnormal CK, nail changes) do not fit the classic picture.

## The Subtle Red Flags that Broke the Case  
The decision to pivot from a surgical work‑up to an immunologic one hinged on a few key findings. First, the neck swelling was disproportionate to the subtle facial erythema and the limited inflammatory markers (CRP 55 mg/L, WCC 9.1 × 10⁹/L). Second, the presence of proximal hip‑flexor weakness and a normal body temperature made infectious causes less likely. Third, a nail‑fold capillaroscopy showing avascular spots is a dermatologist’s fly‑paper for dermatomyositis. Finally, the CK not jumping into the high‑thousands was the “aha” moment—this was a muscle disease, not an allergic reaction or a vascular leak.  

When these findings line up, you should order a myositis‐autoantibody panel right away, no more than a day or two after initial presentation. In our patient the Euroimmun Euroline assay was strongly positive for anti‑SAE1 (signal intensity 173 U vs. <35 U normal), confirming the diagnosis.  

## Stepwise Algorithm: From a Steroid Kick‑off to Rituximab  
The cornerstone of treatment is a rapid but controlled anti‑inflammatory assault. Start with intravenous methylprednisolone 1 000 mg daily for three consecutive days; this “pulse” dose clears the most active muscle inflammation quickly, and in our patient that was essential for preserving swallowing function. Following the pulse, transition to oral prednisone at 60 mg daily and then taper by 5 mg every two to three weeks. The goal is to keep the dose high enough to keep inflammation in check, yet low enough to minimize long‑term steroid toxicity.  

Once steroid tapering begins, introduce a steroid‑sparing agent to halt the disease long term. In patients with interstitial lung disease (ILD) or those who may not tolerate hepatotoxic drugs, mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) 1 g BID is a safe, orally administered option; it was the first choice in our case. Azathioprine is another viable alternative, but our patient preferred MMF for its more predictable side‑effect profile. Avoid methotrexate in the presence of ILD, as it can worsen pulmonary fibrosis.  

If the patient remains active after four to six weeks of combined steroids and a steroid‑sparing agent, add either intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) or rituximab. In the patient under discussion, a course of IVIG 2 g/kg (split over five days) was administered weekly for five cycles before transitioning to monthly infusions. Rituximab was introduced only after confirming inadequate response to IVIG: two 1 000 mg IV doses 14 days apart. Rituximab offers a durable, B‑cell‑depleting platform that often reduces the need for high‑dose steroids in the long run.  

The total strategy can be summed up as a sequential stack:  
1. **High‑dose IV pulse methylprednisolone** →  
2. **Oral prednisone taper** →  
3. **Steroid‑sparing agent (MMF or azathioprine)** →  
4. **Add IVIG or rituximab for refractory disease**.  

## Infectious Prophylaxis and Safety Nets  
The heavy climbing of steroids and immunomodulators opens the door to opportunistic infections. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis with trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole (TMP‑SMX) is standard; however, our patient developed a brisk hemolytic anaemia, prompting a switch to nebulized pentamidine 300 mg quarterly. Regular CBCs, electrolytes, and liver enzymes must be monitored for drug toxicity. In patients receiving rituximab, vaccinate against pneumococcus, Haemophilus influenzae type b, and a high‑dose influenza prior to therapy, and consider a live vaccine only after B‑cell recovery.

## Pulmonary Guardrails  
In dermatomyositis, ILD is a frequent complication and an independent risk factor for mortality. The case report revealed an extrathoracic‑restrictive pattern on spirometry and CT chest nodules without fibrosis, but no definite ILD at baseline. Even a mild pulmonary involvement indicates that the patient may develop ILD later, so drug selection must be ILD‑wise. Methotrexate, the “go‑to” disease‑modifying agent in many myositis protocols, is contraindicated in ILD: its pulmonary toxicity is additive. Hence, we favored MMF, which has a more favorable ILD trajectory.

## Monitoring the Fulminance  
Long‑term remission depends on vigilant monitoring. Serial CK levels should be obtained every 1–2 weeks until normalization, then taper to monthly check‑ins; persistence or rebound of CK signals relapse. Imaging—MRI or FDG‑PET—for muscle activity isn’t routinely repeated unless clinical deterioration occurs, but it can be reassuring after a year of steady improvement. Pulmonary function testing every 3–6 months keeps the ILD sensor alert. Finally, nerve function—both peripheral and bulbar—should be retained in the endocrine (symptom diaries, dysphagia scales), akin to how we treat myasthenia gravis: early speech‑therapy consults often prevent silent aspiration.

## Special Considerations in SAE1 Dermatomyositis  
Anti‑SAE1 positivity is a distinct subtype: it carries high rates of dysphagia (over 40 %), ILD (≈ 44 %), and dysmorphisms in the muscle atrophy pattern. These patients are also at increased risk of malignancy—roughly 18 %. As a result, a bleeding‑risk screening (full-body MRI or CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, tumor markers including CEA, CA‑19‑9, CA‑125, PSA) and periodic surveillance via endoscopy or colonoscopy is warranted. Importantly, the anti‑SAE1 serology did not alter the pharmacologic plan in our case; it simply underscored the need for close monitoring of dysphagia and ILD, as well as a rigorous malignancy work‑up that included a PET scan and routine imaging.

## What the Patient’s Journey Shows  
The man went from potentially irreversible airway compromise and permanent dysphagia to a complete resolution of cervical swelling and a return to normal creatine kinase levels in nine months. The gradual taper of steroids, the sustained MMF regimen, the supportive IVIG, and the timely rituximab infusion crafted a treatment roadmap that worked for a disease that is, by nature, variable and often unpredictable. His case illuminates that dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema; that a thoughtful, staged immunosuppressive schedule can rescue function; and that early, comprehensive screening for malignancy is no longer a luxury—it’s a necessity.

## Take‑home Messages  
- **Think dermatomyositis when neck swelling is unexplained, especially if CK is dramatic and there are subtle skin or nail clues.**  
- **Start with a high‑dose IV methylprednisolone pulse, then taper prednisone over several weeks, always matching the taper to clinical activity.**  
- **Choose a steroid‑sparing agent that protects or does not harm the lungs; MMF is preferable to methotrexate in ILD‑at‑risk patients.**  
- **Add IVIG (2 g/kg over 5 days) or rituximab (1000 mg × 2, 14 days apart) for refractory or fulminant disease.**  
- **Prophylaxis for PJP (TMP‑SMX or nebulized pentamidine) and routine infection monitoring are essential with high‑dose steroids and B‑cell depletion.**  
- **Monitor CK, pulmonary function, and muscle imaging regularly; adjust immunosuppression accordingly.**  
- **Screen aggressively for malignancy (CT/PET, endoscopy) in anti‑SAE1–positive patients, especially if dysphagia or ILD is present.**



# Skimming the Skin: Nailfold Capillaries to Gottron Papules, How to Spot Dermatomyositis Early  

---

## What makes this case a Head‑Lightning Alert  

I used to think “neck swelling in a young, otherwise healthy male” meant Ludwig’s angina, cellulitis or, if imaging cleared, idiopathic angioedema. When a patient in his early 40s came to our ED with a 3‑week march of neck oedema—diffuse strapping of the strap muscles, retropharyngeal fullness on CT, a mild rise in CRP (55 mg/L) and no fever—I was ready for a surgical consultation.  
What stuck with me long after he was scheduled for discharge was a pair of tiny spots on his nail folds that appeared “too big for the size” of the hand and a faint, non‑tender, eczema‑like erythema on his jaw.  Gottron papules?  Heliotrope?  No.  The skin was almost *quiescent*.  Yet the creatine kinase begged for something that would detonate the whole picture: 16 kU/L.  

That CK spike pushed us to think beyond angioedema.  A muscle biopsy showed perifascicular necrosis and complement deposition—classic for dermatomyositis (DM).  What made the case even more head‑long was that the neck swelling was an uncommon manifestation of DM.  Instead of empiric antibiotics or antihistamines, we gave high‑dose steroids, IVIG, and mycophenolate, and the patient’s CK normalized, dysphagia resolved, and the neck swelling receded—within weeks.  

You’re probably wondering: *How did those nail‑fold changes help us shortcut a correct diagnosis?*  That’s the heart of this post.

---

## Nailfold Capillaroscopy: The “Peek‑inside” Sniffer  

**Why it matters**  
Periungual telangiectasias, capillary dropouts, and micro‑hemorrhages are a hallmark of systemic autoimmune disease, and they’re often under‑seen in busy EDs or ENT sees. In DM, nailfold capillaroscopy can reveal:

* **Micro‑hemorrhages** – tiny bleeding sites around capillaries.  
* **Avascular “loops”** – missing or broken capillary loops.  
* **Periungual erythema** – a subtle warm red halo around the nails.  

In our patient, capillaroscopy was *not* a routine study; it was a physical‑exam trick. When I sulked over the hand, a magnifier and a custom “nail‑fold meter” turned the home‑cooked capillaroscope into a diagnostic tool.  

**Practical tip:**  
1. Use a 10× magnification (a smartphone camera or a simple dermoscope).  
2. Look at all fingers; count ≥3 micro‑hemorrhages or any avascular zones.  
3. A “shielding” erythema around the nail (the “Holstein sign” as we call it) is a subtle cue worth not ignoring.  

If the findings are suspicious, flag it on your differential and order a CK in parallel.  

---

## The Skin: From “No Petechiae” to “Gottron’s Papules”  

The spectrum of cutaneous signs in DM is broader than the classic “heliotrope + Gottron papules.”  In the literature, an estimated 50–70 % of dermatomyositis patients have periungual telangiectasias or nailfold changes.  Holsten‑sign (peri‑nail erythema), a blue‑violet eyelid rash (heliotrope), V‑sign (shawl‑like rash on the chest) and Gottron papules (red‑brown papules over the knuckles) are the “gold‑standard” but are *often subtle and missed in early disease*.

Our patient had **periungual erythema** (Holstein sign), a faint ochre erythema over the face, and a mild rash on the right lateral thigh.  No Gottron papules were visible, perhaps because they were yet to surface or were underreported in a pain‑dominant presentation.  

**Bottom line:**  
Pay attention to that “faint blush” around the nails or eyelids.  They can actually be more reliable than classic papules in the early stage.

---

## Neck Swelling – The “Pseudo‑Angioedema” That Is Actually Myositis  

When we first saw the patient’s neck, we’d default to “deep neck space infection,” then “angioedema.”  The literature calls this pseudo‑angioedema rare – 8 out of 121 DM patients in a large cohort – but it can happen.  The swelling originates not from histamine or bradykinin, but from **inflammatory edema of striated muscle** in the neck and pharynx.  

Key clues that hinted at DM rather than angioedema:

| Feature | DM | Angioedema |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | subacute, progressive | abrupt |
| Associated weakness | proximal, facial | often absent |
| Parallel erythema | subtle, diffuse | intense swelling |
| CK level | huge | normal |
| Complement levels | normal | low C4 (hI-1) |
| Nailfold changes | present | absent |

These distinctions remind us that a normal CK or a positive ANA can be the *tip of the iceberg* when interpreting neck swelling.

## A Converging Exam Checklist: “Nail‑Foot‑Popay”  

Some clinicians still rely on a long list of videos or papers to find DM clues.  Here’s how you can quickly screen for DM in one pass:

1. **Neck & Mouth:** palate, tongue, pharynx. Look for swelling, dysphagia.  
2. **Face:** heliotrope rash takes the top–right seat; arthritic cheeks for shawl sign.  
3. **Hands & Fingers:** thrill for Gottron papules over dorsum of index and middle fingers.  
4. **Peri‑Nail Assessment:** look for micro‑hemorrhages, telangiectasias, “Holstein” redness.  
5. **Legs:** subfascial erythema, especially the lateral thigh.  
6. **Labs:** CK, LDH, transaminases, ANA, anti‑dozens.  

The “popay” in the menstrual “Nail‑Foot‑Popay” is the cough of “Neck & Mouth + Nail + POPA (Peri‑Nail + opti‑CK)".  (Game of phonetics).  

Keep this in the back of your head: when you see swelling that *feels like edema yet it’s no histamine*, pull out a CK.  If it shoots, keep the capillary exam close.

## Take‑Home Takeaways (Pavilions)  

- **Ask for CK when you see unexplained neck swelling or dysphagia, even if the patient has no obvious skin rash.**  
- **Perform a quick nail‑fold capillaroscopy; micro‑hemorrhages or avascular loops are a red flag for DM.**  
- **Remember the “Hallmark‑Pitfalls”:**  
  - “No contraction” but *subtle* facial erythema (Holstein sign).  
  - Lack of Gottron papules does *not* exclude DM.  
- **Workup order:** CT neck → ENT → CK → ANA + myositis antibody panel → muscular biopsy if CK remains high.  
- **Early aggressive immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids, IVIG, immunosuppressants) can reverse the neck swelling and prevent long‑term sequelae.**  
- **DM patients may also have “pseudo‑angioedema” – swelling that mimics histamine‑mediated forms but is truly inflammatory muscle edema.**

## Bottom Line  

Dermatomyositis can be a silent masquerader, hiding behind a quiet neck in a 40‑year‑old male.  The trick is to look beyond the obvious and use *tiny* physical clues – micro‑hemorrhages in the nail fold, a faint peri‑nail redness – to trigger the right laboratory workup.  A failed initial diagnosis can lead to delayed immunosuppression, and that translates to worse outcomes.  

Next time you encounter a patient with unexplained neck swelling, keep the capilaroscopy in your pocket and the CK bottle under your wrist.  A quick glance at the nail line could be your best diagnostic cheat sheet.
