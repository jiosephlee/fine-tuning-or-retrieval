Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the clinical implications of persistent hiccups in a patient presenting with hyponatraemia, and what pathophysiological mechanisms can explain this manifestation?

**Clinical pearl**

**Persistent hiccups are a red‑flag symptom in hyponatraemic patients because they often herald severe, neurologically‑derived SIADH.** In the index case, hiccups appeared early in a patient with fever, headache and hyponatraemia, and were followed by the recognition of SIADH secondary to acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD). Persistent hiccups prompted a focused endocrine work‑up, which revealed a markedly low serum osmolality, inappropriate urine osmolality, and elevated ADH – the classic laboratory triad of SIADH.  

**Pathophysiology**

1. **Hyponatraemia‑induced neuronal swelling** – Low extracellular sodium reduces osmolality, causing water influx into neurons and glia, leading to cytotoxic edema. This edema and the accompanying ionic disturbance make the central pattern generator for the hiccup reflex (located in the medullary dorsal vagal complex, phrenic nucleus and the respiratory control centre) more excitable and prone to aberrant firing.  
2. **Inflammatory cytokine contribution** – CNS inflammation, as seen in aseptic meningitis or neuro‑BD, produces IL‑6 and other mediators that stimulate ADH release. The resultant water retention aggravates hyponatraemia, creating a vicious cycle that sustains the hiccup trigger.  
3. **Autonomic dysregulation** – Severe hyponatraemia can also tilt the balance of the vagal–phrenic reflex arc by impairing central inhibitory interneurons, further perpetuating the hiccup cycle.

**Clinical implications**

- **Early recognition**: Persistent hiccups should prompt rapid assessment for serum sodium, osmolality and urine indices, because they may precede overt neurological deficits or risk of cerebral oedema.  
- **Avoid rapid correction**: As in the present case, conservative fluid restriction and gradual sodium correction prevent osmotic demyelination and further neurological injury.  
- **Identify underlying CNS disease**: The coexistence of hiccups and hyponatraemia should raise suspicion for CNS inflammatory processes (e.g., meningitis, neuro‑BD) or central lesions, warranting neuroimaging and CSF analysis.  
- **Treatment strategy**: Address the root cause (in the case, colchicine therapy for acute neuro‑BD) to relieve inflammation, ADH excess, and consequently resolve both hyponatraemia and hiccups.

**Teaching point**

When a patient presents with persistent hiccups and hyponatraemia, consider SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation promptly, especially if other neurological signs (headache, vertigo) are present. Early hormone work‑up, careful fluid management, and treatment of the underlying inflammatory etiology can reverse both the electrolyte disturbance and the hiccup syndrome, as demonstrated here.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Among the various causes of euvolemic hyponatraemia, which laboratory criteria distinguish SIADH, and why does this patient meet those criteria?

**Answer**

In euvolemic hyponatraemia the most useful laboratory triad that pins down SIADH is:

1. **Low serum osmolality** (≤ 275 mOsm/kg)  
2. **Inappropriately concentrated urine** (urine osmolality > 100 mOsm/kg, often > 300–400, indicating the kidneys are not excreting free water)  
3. **High urinary sodium** (≥ 20–30 mmol/L, usually > 40 mmol/L) with no renal or adrenal cause

These criteria reflect the fundamental pathophysiology: ADH is secreted despite hypotonic plasma, causing renal water reabsorption, concentrated urine, and sodium excretion because the kidneys are free‑water overloaded.

**Why the patient satisfies SIADH**

| Criterion | Case‑specific findings |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Serum osmolality | 267 mOsm/kg (low) |
| Urine osmolality | 900 mOsm/kg (very high) |
| Urine sodium | 88 mmol/L (high) |
| Volume status | Clinically euvolemic (no edema, normal BP, no oliguria) |
| ADH level | 22.4 pg/mL (elevated) |
| Principals excluded | Normal thyroid, cortisol, renal function; no diuretics; no heart/failure signs |

The markedly low plasma osmolality with a concentrated, sodium‑rich urine demonstrates that the kidneys are not appropriately diluting the urine in the setting of euvolemia. The elevated ADH level confirms that the renal concentrating stimulus is centrally driven. Normal adrenal and thyroid axes rule out adrenal/thyroid disease, and the absence of diuretics or volume overload eliminates other causes. Therefore, the laboratory profile satisfies the diagnostic definition of SIADH and explains the patient’s profound hyponatraemia.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how interleukin‑6 released during CNS inflammation could contribute to the development of SIADH in acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease.

**Interleukin‑6 and the Cascade to SIADH in Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s Disease**  
- **IL‑6 is a key cytokine released by activated monocytes** in Behçet’s disease.  
  In the acute neuro‑phase, the brain’s inflammatory milieu elevates IL‑6 in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and periphery; the markedly heightened serum CRP in this patient is a surrogate marker for such an IL‑6–driven response.

- **Central non‑osmotic stimulation of ADH**.  
  IL‑6 crosses the blood–brain barrier and binds to IL‑6 receptors on neurons within the hypothalamus and posterior pituitary. Neuronal activation leads to increased corticotropin‑releasing hormone (CRH) release, which in turn stimulates the hypothalamic‑pituitary axis to produce antidiuretic hormone (ADH). Importantly, IL‑6 can directly sensitize ADH‑producing neurosecretory cells, bypassing osmotic regulation.

- **Peripheral amplification of water retention**.  
  IL‑6 enhances the renal response to ADH by up‑regulating aquaporin‑2 (AQP2) channel expression in the collecting ducts and increasing β‑cAMP signaling downstream of the vasopressin‑2 (V₂) receptor. This amplifies ADH’s antidiuretic effect even if plasma osmolality is low, producing the classic SIADH picture of euvolemic hyponatraemia with concentrated urine and inappropriately high ADH levels.

- **Connecting the dots in the case**.  
  The patient’s CSF showed mild pleocytosis and protein elevation—hallmarks of aseptic meningitis—alongside a dramatic rise in CRP. Although IL‑6 was not directly measured, these findings strongly imply central IL‑6 activity. The elevated ADH (22.4 pg/mL) and the sustained hyponatraemia (serum Na⁺ 124 mmol/L) are entirely consistent with IL‑6–mediated stimulation of ADH secretion and potentiation of its renal effect.

- **Therapeutic implications**.  
  Colchicine, which dampens monocyte activation and downstream IL‑6 production, coincided with rapid resolution of both neurological symptoms and hyponatraemia. This underscores the pivotal role of inflammation‑mediated IL‑6 in driving SIADH and suggests that anti‑inflammatory therapy can reverse the electrolyte disturbance by curtailing IL‑6 release.

In sum, IL‑6 released during CNS inflammation in acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease can both induce non‑osmotic ADH secretion from the hypothalamic–pituitary axis and exaggerate the antidiuretic response of the kidneys, thereby precipitating SIADH.

### [Pathophysiology] What are the typical neuroimaging findings in acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease, and how can a normal MRI still be compatible with the diagnosis?

**Typical neuro‑imaging of acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD)**  
In the acute phase, the brain MRI most often shows:

| MRI sequence | Typical finding | Location | Pathophysiology |
|--------------|-----------------|----------|-----------------|
| T2 / FLAIR   | Patchy, hyperintense lesions | Brainstem (pons, mid‑brain, medulla), cerebellar peduncles, occasionally basal ganglia or corticospinal tracts | Granulomatous inflammation and micro‑vasculitic ischemia cause focal demyelination/necrosis |
| Contrast‑enhanced T1 | Ring‑ or nodular‑enhancement of the same lesions | Brainstem or adjacent subcortical white matter | Blood‑brain barrier disruption from active inflammation |
| Diffusion‑weighted imaging | Restricted diffusion in acute lesions | Same regions | Cytotoxic edema from early ischemia |

These lesions are usually visualized as “brainstem punctate or ovoid hyperintensities” that may be subtle but become apparent once the inflammatory infiltrate has matured.

---

**Why a normal MRI can still be compatible with a diagnosis of acute neuro‑BD**

1. **Aseptic meningitis as the sole clinical presentation**  
   - Acute neuro‑BD can manifest as isolated meningeal inflammation without parenchymal involvement. In such cases, T2/FLAIR and contrast series may be completely normal, as was seen in the presented patient (MRI showed no abnormalities).

2. **Early imaging window**  
   - Micro‑inflammatory lesions may be too small or diffuse to reach the spatial resolution of routine MRI at the time of presentation. Serial imaging or higher‑resolution sequences (e.g., thin‑slice FLAIR, gradient‑echo) can later reveal lesions that were initially occult.

3. **CSF evidence of CNS inflammation**  
   - A mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, and negative cultures strongly suggest aseptic meningitis. In neuro‑BD, such CSF findings are pathognomonic even when the brain parenchyma looks normal.

4. **Clinical criteria and exclusion of other etiologies**  
   - The concurrent mucocutaneous manifestations (oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like rash, genital ulcer, HLA‑B51 positivity) satisfy the International Criteria for Behçet’s disease. Once infectious, neoplastic, and other autoimmune causes are excluded, the diagnosis of acute neuro‑BD can be made on clinical and CSF grounds alone.

In short, the presence of characteristic brainstem lesions on MRI is a hallmark of neuro‑BD, but the disease can first appear as isolated aseptic meningitis with a normal MRI. When the clinical picture and CSF findings are unmistakable, a normal neuro‑imaging study does **not** exclude the diagnosis.

### [Management] What is the standard therapeutic approach to acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease, and how does the evidence support using colchicine alone as opposed to corticosteroids?

**Standard treatment of acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD)**  
- **Acute phase**: high‑dose glucocorticoids are the cornerstone.  
  - Intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g/day for 3‑5 days (or oral prednisone 1–2 mg/kg/day) followed by a slow taper.  
  - For patients who are steroid‑intolerant, refractory, or have severe CNS lesions, conventional agents such as cyclophosphamide or azathioprine are added.  
- **Corticosteroids are rarely used alone** in a chronic setting; many clinicians combine them with a disease‑modifying agent to prevent relapse.

**Colchicine as a single agent in acute neuro‑BD**  
- **Evidence from the literature**: systematic reviews and case series consistently report that colchicine does not alter the course of acute neuro‑BD. It is effective for mucocutaneous disease but has no proven benefit in the CNS form.  
- **The present case** reports an unusual response: a 60‑year‑old man with fever, aseptic meningitis, and SIADH improved rapidly after colchicine monotherapy, with full neurologic and mucocutaneous resolution over weeks and no relapse for 4 years.  
  - This is a single‑patient observation; the improvement could represent spontaneous remission or an exceptionally mild CNS flare.  
  - Importantly, the patient received no steroids, antiepileptics, or other immunosuppressants, yet recovered fully—an anomaly rather than a paradigm shift.  

**Bottom line for practice**  
- **First‑line therapy** for acute neuro‑BD remains high‑dose steroids (± immunosuppressants).  
- **Colchicine monotherapy** is **not** supported by evidence for acute neuro‑BD and should be reserved for mucocutaneous disease or used as an adjunct when steroids are contraindicated or to reduce steroid exposure.  
- Clinicians should consider colchicine only in highly selected patients, and its use should be documented as an exception rather than standard care.

### [Management] Describe the fluid restriction regimen used to manage SIADH in this patient, including target intake and monitoring parameters.

**Fluid restriction for SIADH**

In this case the patient’s SIADH was treated with a **strict fluid restriction of less than 800 mL per day**. The goal was a gradual, safe rise in serum sodium—typically 4–6 mmol/L over 48–72 h (no more than 8–12 mmol/L over the first 24 h) to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome.  

**Monitoring protocol used**

1. **Daily serum sodium** – measured each morning to track the rate of correction.  
2. **Serum and urine osmolality** – checked when sodium rose >145 mmol/L or if clinical symptoms (e.g., nausea, confusion) appeared, to assess ongoing water restriction efficacy.  
3. **Clinical weight and volume status** – daily checks for euvolemia, ensuring no signs of dehydration or over‑restriction.  

The patient’s sodium rose from 124 mmol/L to 136 mmol/L by day 16, with ADH falling to normal levels and no need for hypertonic saline or desmopressin. This conservative approach, guided by the aforementioned monitoring, safely cured the hyponatremia associated with his acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease.

### [Management] Why is rapid correction of hyponatraemia hazardous in this setting, and what monitoring is required to prevent osmotic demyelination?

**Why a rapid rise in serum sodium can be fatal in this case**

SIADH produces a chronic, euvolemic hyponatremia that results, over days, in a brain‑cell adaptive response to a hypotonic environment: neurons and glia lower their intracellular osmolytes in order to prevent swelling. When serum sodium is increased too quickly, extracellular osmolality rises rapidly and water is drawn out of the already‑adapted cells. The abrupt loss of water causes a traumatic decrease in cell volume, leading to excitotoxic injury of oligodendrocytes and classical osmotic demyelination (central pontine or extrapontine myelinolysis). In a patient with neuro‑Behçet’s disease, where CNS inflammation may already compromise the blood‑brain barrier and neuronal integrity, the brain is even more vulnerable to osmotic stress. Thus, ameliorating hyponatremia is essential, but the pace of correction must be guarded.

**What to monitor to avoid osmotic demyelination – the practical approach**

| What | Frequency/Hold‑points | Rationale |
|------|----------------------|-----------|
| **Serum sodium** | Every 4–6 h until the patient is >135 mmol/L, then q12–24 h | The Endocrine Society recommends that the increment in the first 24 h not exceed 10–12 mmol/L. In acute settings (<24 h), most experts limit the increase to 8 mmol/L. |
| **Neurologic status** | Every 8–12 h (or sooner if symptoms change) | New or worsening dysarthria, gait changes, seizures, or altered mental status are red flags for osmotic demyelination.  |
| **Fluid intake/usage** | Document all fluids and insensible losses | Fluid restriction (<800 mL/day in this case) prevented over‑repletion. |
| **Desmopressin** | Prophylactic use if sodium >130 mmol/L or if the correction is backing off | Desmopressin can blunt a sudden rise in sodium by limiting water diuresis. |
| **Urine osmolality** | Baseline and after any interventions | Helps gauge ADH suppression and decide on fluid changes. |

The patient’s management exemplified these principles: an aggressive fluid restriction was employed, no hypertonic saline or vasopressin‑antagonist was used, and serum sodium rose from 124 to 136 mmol/L over 16 days—well below the 10 mmol/L per day threshold. Daily monitoring ensured that no correction spike appeared, averting osmotic demyelination.

**Bottom line**  
In SICDH associated with neuro‑Behçet’s disease, the brain is at increased risk of osmotic injury.  Correct the serum sodium slowly, no more than 10 mmol/L over 24 h and ideally 8 mmol/L in the first 48 h, and perform frequent sodium checks and bedside neurologic exams.  If the increase accelerates, administer desmopressin or halt fluid therapy to prevent catastrophic demyelination.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiological relationship between severe hyponatraemia and rhabdomyolysis, and outline how clinicians should monitor and manage this complication.

**Pathophysiological link between severe hyponatraemia and rhabdomyolysis**

*Low extracellular osmolality* (here, serum Na⁺ = 124 mmol/L) creates a gradient that draws water into the cytoplasm of all cells, including skeletal muscle fibres.  
• The resulting *cellular swelling* disrupts the sarcolemma, causing leakage of intracellular Ca²⁺.  
• Intracellular Ca²⁺ activates calcium‑dependent proteases and phospholipases, which damage the contractile apparatus and precipitate myocyte necrosis.  
• In addition, the heightened inflammatory milieu of acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (e.g., IL‑6–mediated cytokine storm) can impair mitochondrial function and amplify oxidative stress, rendering muscle cells even more vulnerable to osmotic injury.  
Thus, profound hyponatraemia can precipitate rhabdomyolysis by a direct osmotic mechanism, a phenomenon seen in the case where CK rose to 1 349 U/L alongside the low sodium.

**Monitoring strategy**

| Parameter | Timing | Rationale |
|-----------|--------|-----------|
| Serum Na⁺, plasma osmolality | q4‑6 h while Na⁺ < 120 mmol/L | Detect rapid correction or progression |
| Serum creatinine, BUN, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺ | q12‑24 h | Early detection of renal injury or electrolyte derangements |
| Serum CK | q12‑24 h until ↓ 30–50 % and < 1 000 U/L | Gauge extent of muscle injury |
| Urine output | hourly | Ensure ≥ 1 mL/kg/h to flush myoglobin |
| Urine colour & osmolality | Daily | Myoglobinuria → dark urine, low urine osmolality |
| Neurologic status (seizure, altered mental status) | Continuous | Hyponatraemic seizures can trigger secondary rhabdomyolysis |

In the reported case, serial monitoring of Na⁺, CK and renal function confirmed a gradual rise in sodium (to 136 mmol/L over 16 days) and a return of CK toward normal, without renal impairment.

**Clinical management**

1. **Hyponatraemia**  
   *Fluid restriction* (≤ 800 mL/day for euvolemic patients) is first‑line, as employed in this case.  
   *If severe symptoms* (seizure, coma) develop, a *low‑volume hypertonic saline* bolus may be used, but the rate of correction must not exceed 8–12 mmol/L in 24 h to avoid osmotic demyelination.  
   *Treat the underlying cause* – in this case colchicine suppressed CNS inflammation, allowing ADH secretion to normalize.

2. **Rhabdomyolysis**  
   *Isotonic saline* (200–250 mL h⁻¹) or *induced diuresis* (≥ 200 mL h⁻¹) may be given if urinary output is < 0.5 mL/kg/h or if CK is > 10 000 U/L; in moderate myoglobinuria (CK ≈ 1 300 U/L) aggressive fluid therapy is optional.  
   *Urine alkalinization* (bicarbonate 50 mEq L⁻¹) is reserved for markedly acidic urine (< 6.0) to prevent tubular precipitation of myoglobin.  
   *Avoid nephrotoxic agents* (NSAIDs, radiocontrast).  
   *Correct secondary electrolyte derangements* – hyperkalaemia, hypocalcaemia, or hypomagnesemia – as they can aggravate muscle injury or precipitate arrhythmias.

3. **Adjunctive care**  
   *Monitor for acute kidney injury* and consider dialysis if creatinine rises or volume overload occurs.  
   *Maintain balanced nutrition* and correct hypovolemia cautiously.  
   *Document all fluid input/output* and serial electrolyte values to guide titration.

In the presented patient, a conservative approach of fluid restriction combined with close lab surveillance appropriately resolved both the hyponatraemia and the rhabdomyolysis without the need for hypertonic saline or aggressive fluid resuscitation. The underlying inflammatory disease was controlled with colchicine, illustrating that managing the root cause can attenuate both SIADH and secondary muscle injury.

**Take‑home message** – In any patient with severe hyponatraemia, especially when SIADH is secondary to CNS inflammation, always screen for rhabdomyolysis (CK, urine colour). Monitor sodium and CK closely; correct sodium slowly while providing adequate renal perfusion to prevent myoglobin‑induced nephropathy. Treat the underlying trigger (e.g., inflammatory disease) to halt the vicious cycle of hyponatraemia and muscle breakdown.
