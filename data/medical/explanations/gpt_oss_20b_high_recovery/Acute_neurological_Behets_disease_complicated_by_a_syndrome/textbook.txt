Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Case Presentation & Initial Clinical Assessment

An Asian man in his 60 s was admitted to the emergency department after a 3‑day history of fever, headache, general malaise, loss of appetite, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia affecting the shoulders and ankles. He had an unremarkable occupational history (metalworking) and no recent travel, exposure to animals, or known infections. 
His medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, for which he had not been receiving treatment; his glycaemic status at presentation was abnormal (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L, HbA1c 8.2 %). No other chronic illnesses were recorded and he was otherwise healthy.

On examination he was alert but fatigued. Vital signs demonstrated a temperature of 38.1 °C, blood pressure 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate 119 bpm (regular), respiratory rate 19/min, and oxygen saturation 96 % on room air. The patient was euvolemic: there were no peripheral oedema, jugular venous distension, or third‑degree clubbing. Digital examination revealed multiple oral aphthous ulcers and facial and truncal folliculitis‑like eruptions (figure 1A–C). No focal neurological deficits or cranial nerve palsies were evident. Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were reassuring, and muscle strength was intact in all extremities.

Initial laboratory investigations identified a striking electrolyte disturbance: serum sodium was 124 mmol/L, indicating severe hyponatraemia. Total serum osmolality was 267 mOsm/kg (low), and urinary osmolality was 900 mOsm/kg (high). Urinary sodium was markedly increased at 88 mmol/L. Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was significantly elevated at 22.4 pg/mL. These findings satisfy the classic laboratory triad for the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH): hypo‑osmolar hyponatraemia, concentrated urine (high osmolality), and high urinary sodium while the patient remains euvolemic. 
Other pertinent labs revealed an inflammatory picture: C‑reactive protein (CRP) 180 mg/L and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 70 mm/h. Musculoskeletal enzymes were also strongly elevated: creatine kinase 1,349 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 77 U/L, and alanine aminotransferase 64 U/L. Renal function was preserved (serum creatinine and BUN within reference limits). Endocrine assessment found a normal thyroid‑stimulating hormone, free T4, and serum cortisol, ruling out hypothalamic‑pituitary‑adrenal axis dysfunction.

Baseline imaging consisted of a non‑contrast computed tomography (CT) scan of the head, as well as CT of the chest and abdomen, all of which were unremarkable; no intracranial mass, hydrocephalus, pulmonary infiltrates, or abdominal pathology could be identified. An abdominal chest X‑ray also showed no acute findings.

In light of the fever, neurological symptoms, and laboratory evidence of systemic inflammation and severe hyponatraemia, the initial working diagnosis encompassed a broad differential that included infectious meningitis (bacterial, viral, or tuberculous), other causes of aseptic meningitis, systemic inflammatory disorders, and drug‑induced SIADH. The combination of a clear inflammatory marker profile, a presumed cerebral focus due to the neurological complaints, and the biochemical picture of SIADH directed early empiric antimicrobial therapy (cefepime and acyclovir) while awaiting definitive diagnostic work‑up. 

This initial clinical assessment set the stage for a focused investigation of the underlying etiology of the neurological illness and the associated endocrine disturbance.

## Differential Diagnosis & Comprehensive Work‑up

The patient’s presentation with fever, headache, general malaise and hyponatraemia immediately raised a broad differential that required a methodical, step‑wise evaluation. The work‑up was structured around three primary domains: (1) the most common causes of fever and meningeal irritation, (2) the differential for hyponatraemia, and (3) investigations that could identify occult neoplasia or autoimmune disease. By systematically excluding each category, the clinicians were able to converge on the diagnosis of acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH).

### Central nervous system infection: the priority in fever, headache and irregular fluid balance

Bacterial meningitis, viral encephalitis (particularly HSV and VZV), and tuberculous meningitis are the foremost infectious processes that can cause fever, headache, and hyponatraemia. In bacterial meningitis, the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) typically shows a markedly neutrophilic pleocytosis, markedly elevated protein, low glucose, and a diagnostic Gram stain or culture. Viral encephalitides, such as HSV or VZV, usually present with a lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal or mildly elevated protein, and a normal opening pressure. Tuberculous meningitis is characterized by a chronic history, a lymphocytic pleocytosis, markedly increased protein, and often an elevated opening pressure. In this patient, the CSF opening pressure was only mildly raised (17 cm H₂O), protein slightly elevated to 0.97 g/L, and cell count showed only 11 lymphocytes/µL, all of which are more compatible with an aseptic process. The CSF cultures were sterile, PCR assays for HSV, VZV and Mycobacterium tuberculosis were negative, and fungal antigen testing was unremarkable. These findings effectively excluded bacterial meningitis, acute viral encephalitis, and tuberculous meningitis as the etiologies of the CNS findings.

### CSF analysis and its diagnostic relevance

The CSF profile – mildly increased opening pressure, modest protein elevation, and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis – is a classic pattern seen in aseptic meningitis. Importantly, the cytology demonstrated no malignant cells, ruling out lymphoma or metastatic disease within the CSF compartment. The absence of neutrophils, normal bacterial cultures, and negative viral PCR all argue against an infectious etiology. Consequently, the remaining possibilities were either an autoimmune or a vasculitic process affecting the CNS, a neuro‑BD manifestation, or a secondary effect of systemic inflammation on the blood–brain barrier.

### Neoplastic work‑up: imaging and tumour‑marker considerations

A brain MRI is the imaging modality of choice for detecting CNS lymphoma or metastatic disease. In this case, MRI revealed no focal lesions, destroying the most common imaging sign of central nervous system lymphoma. Serum soluble interleukin‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) was mildly elevated at 704 U/mL; however, sIL‑2R is a nonspecific marker that can be raised in any inflammatory state. In the context of normal MRI and the absence of malignant cells in the CSF, the evidence for a CNS lymphoma was minimal. Metastatic disease was also unlikely given the lack of primary tumour imaging on chest and abdomen CT and the absence of systemic signs of malignancy on physical examination. Thus, neoplasia was effectively ruled out.

### Autoimmune and connective‑tissue disease screening

Autoimmune diseases, particularly systemic vasculitides and neuro‑autoimmune disorders, can present with fever, headache, and hyponatraemia. An exhaustive serologic panel including antinuclear antibody (ANA) and extractable nuclear antigen (ENA) antibodies was negative, making systemic lupus erythematosus and related connective‑tissue diseases unlikely. Sarcoidosis, a granulomatous disorder that can involve the nervous system, was evaluated by serum angiotensin‑converting enzyme (ACE) and lysozyme levels, both of which returned within normal limits. Interferon‑γ release assays and other specific investigations were not required because the clinical picture did not suggest a sarcoid phenotype. Together, these results further narrowed the differential to a neuro‑inflammatory process of a different etiology, consistent with neuro‑BD.

### Endocrine causes of SIADH: ruling out hypothalamic dysfunction and ectopic hormone production

Hyponatraemia in this patient was discordant with normal renal function, normal adrenal cortisol levels, and normal thyroid hormone levels, but with a low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, and a high urine sodium. These laboratory features fit the classic criteria for SIADH. Investigation of potential central causes included MRI of the brain, specifically evaluating the hypothalamic and pituitary regions; no abnormalities were identified. Medication review excluded drugs known to provoke SIADH (e.g., carbamazepine, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors). A history was obtained that ruled out pulmonary disease, which can be an ectopic source of ADH. Finally, imaging of the chest and abdomen was negative for any malignancy that could secrete ADH ectopically. Thus, the SIADH was attributed to central CNS inflammation rather than an endocrine or ectopic source.

### Laboratory testing strategy

The diagnostic strategy began with a complete blood count and cultures to evaluate for systemic infection. Viral PCR panels for HSV, VZV, and enteroviruses were performed on CSF, complemented by fungal antigen assays and a tuberculin skin test or interferon‑γ release assay. Endocrine panels encompassed sodium, osmolality, cortisol, thyroid-stimulating hormone, free T4, and plasma ADH concentrations. Imaging included a non‑contrast CT head, chest radiograph, CT abdomen/pelvis, and an MRI brain with contrast. The CSF studies included opening pressure, cell differential, protein, glucose, culture, Gram stain, and PCR for common pathogens, along with cytology. The sequence of tests was designed to first exclude life‑threatening infections, then address emergent electrolyte derangements, and finally evaluate for occult neoplasia or connective‑tissue disease.

### Interpretation of negative findings and narrowing the differential

The cumulative negative findings in cultures, PCR, cytology, imaging, and serology provided a multi‑layered exclusion of the most common causes of fever, headache and hyponatraemia. The lack of evidence for bacterial, viral, tuberculous or fungal infection, combined with normal imaging of the brain and the absence of malignant cells in CSF, meant that a primary CNS infection or tumour could be reasonably ruled out. Likewise, a normal autoimmune serologic panel removed connective‐tissue disorders from consideration. The normal endocrine investigations except for the SIADH criterion, the absence of hypothalamic lesions on MRI, and the lack of ectopic tumour spread left CNS inflammation as the most plausible source of the clinical syndrome.

In the context of the patient’s strong mucocutaneous manifestations (oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like rashes, genital ulceration) and a HLA‑B51 allele, the clinical picture was most consistent with Behçet’s disease. The simultaneous neurological findings – aseptic meningitis on CSF analysis and the absence of parenchymal lesions on MRI – directed the diagnosis toward acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease. The associated SIADH was deemed a consequence of CNS inflammation, rather than a primary endocrine disorder or drug side‑effect. This systematic, exclusionary approach not only clarified the underlying pathology but also guided a targeted therapeutic strategy that ultimately avoided the use of high‑dose steroids and immunosuppressants, relying instead on colchicine.

## Diagnostic Reasoning & Confirmation of Behçet’s Disease with Neuro‑Behçet and SIADH

The patient’s constellation of mucocutaneous lesions, fever, headache and hyponatraemia prompted a systematic diagnostic work‑up that confirmed three interrelated entities: systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) with a definite International Criteria (IC) score, acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) manifested as aseptic meningitis, and a syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH).  

### International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease

The ICBD assigns point values to major clinical manifestations: oral aphthae (2 pt), genital ulcers (2 pt), skin lesions such as a folliculitis‑like rash (1 pt), ocular lesions (2 pt) and a positive pathergy test (1 pt). The patient presented with oral aphthae, genital ulcers and a characteristic folliculitis‑like rash, totaling 5 points. No ocular involvement or pathergy testing is required to reach the threshold of ≥4 points, which defines *definite* BD.  Thus, the cumulative skin and mucosal findings satisfied the ICBD without need for ancillary investigations.

### Recognition of Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s Disease

Neuro‑BD typically presents with meningoencephalitis, often centered in the brainstem. In this case, neuro‑MRI was unremarkable—a finding not unusual in early disease, as brainstem lesions may be too subtle for detection or absent altogether. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis supported aseptic meningitis: an opening pressure of 17 cm H₂O (mildly elevated), protein 0.97 g/L, and a lymphocyte‑dominant pleocytosis of 11 cells/µL. The CSF was culture‑negative, and panels for common viral pathogens (HSV, VZV), as well as fungal and mycobacterial organisms, were negative. These findings, taken together with the established BD diagnosis, led to the identification of acute neuro‑BD—the inflammation of the central nervous system that manifested as aseptic meningitis in the absence of focal lesions.

### Diagnosis of SIADH

The patient’s laboratory profile met the classic SIADH criteria: serum sodium of 124 mmol/L (hyponatraemia), serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg (hypo‑osmolality), and euvolemic status (no edema, normal blood pressure). Urine osmolality was markedly concentrated at 900 mOsm/kg, with a urinary sodium of 88 mmol/L—both exceeding the thresholds for inappropriate water reabsorption and sodium excretion. An ADH concentration of 22.4 pg/mL was supraphysiologic for the degree of hypo‑osmolality. Thyroid and adrenal axes were normal, precluding hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency as alternate explanations. These data definitively established SIADH.

### Role of HLA‑B51 and Exclusion of Alternative Diagnoses

HLA‑B51 positivity, detected during routine serology, is a recognized genetic association with BD but remains non‑specific and is not part of the diagnostic criteria. It thus functioned purely as supportive evidence rather than a definitive marker. A comprehensive differential excluded infectious meningitis (bacterial, viral, mycobacterial) through negative cultures, PCRs, and serologies; malignant CNS disease via normal imaging and negative CSF cytology; sarcoidosis through normal SERULINATION cytokine checks; and connective–tissue disorders via negative autoimmune panels. This exclusion of competing etiologies reinforces the conclusion that CNS inflammation stemmed from BD.

### Pathophysiologic Link Between CNS Inflammation and SIADH

Neuro‑inflammation, a hallmark of acute neuro‑BD, is known to elevate pro‑inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‑6 (IL‑6). IL‑6 can stimulate the hypothalamic–pituitary axis, provoking aberrant ADH release even in the absence of overt brain lesions. Although IL‑6 levels were not directly measured in this patient, the markedly raised serum C‑reactive protein (180 mg/L) underscores substantial systemic inflammation, plausibly mirroring CNS cytokine activity. Consequently, it is biologically credible that the aseptic meningitis driven by the BD inflammatory cascade led to SIADH through an IL‑6‑mediated pathway, rather than by drug toxicity or another comorbidity.

In sum, the careful integration of mucocutaneous criteria, CSF abnormalities, and hallmark hyponatraemic features, alongside rigorous exclusion of other causes, yielded a coherent and definitive diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease, acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease, and SIADH—each component logically corroborated by the others.

## Therapeutic Strategy and Management of Acute Neuro‑Behçet and SIADH  

Upon presentation, the patient’s acute febrile illness and meningeal–like symptoms prompted an empiric antibacterial approach. Cefepime, a broad‑spectrum fourth‑generation cephalosporin, was initiated immediately after blood cultures were obtained to cover potential Gram‑negative and Gram‑positive CNS pathogens commonly implicated in community‑acquired meningitis. When serial blood cultures and the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies remained sterile and the CSF picture evolved into a sterile, lymphocytic pleocytosis, the treating team discontinued cefepime on day 3, aligning with stewardship principles and the absence of an identified infectious agent.

During the same window of clinical deterioration, the headache and vertigo intensified, raising concern for viral encephalitis. Empiric acyclovir was therefore started on day 3, standard for suspected herpes simplex virus (HSV) or varicella‑zoster virus (VZV) encephalitis until PCR assays became available. Negative viral polymerase chain reaction (PCR) results for HSV, VZV and cryptococcal antigen led to the prompt cessation of acyclovir by day 4, underscoring the importance of rapidly narrowing differential diagnoses in the setting of CNS inflammation.

With the infectious work‑up negative, the focus shifted to the underlying inflammatory disorder. The clinical picture—oral and genital aphthae, folliculitis‑like rashes, elevated inflammatory markers, and CSF evidence of aseptic meningitis—corroborated a diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) with concurrent acute neuro‑BD. Conventional management of acute neuro‑BD often involves high‑dose corticosteroids; however, the benefits of steroid therapy must be weighed against risks, especially in a patient with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes and potential for steroid‑induced hyperglycaemia and infection. Furthermore, literature indicates that a subset of patients with meningoencephalitis‑type neuro‑BD experience spontaneous remission. Considering these factors, the clinicians elected to initiate colchicine monotherapy on day 8, an agent traditionally reserved for mucocutaneous manifestations but known to inhibit neutrophil chemotaxis, mitigate interleukin‑1β (IL‑1β) and interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) production, and suppress downstream inflammation. The rapid clinical improvement—resolution of fever, mucosal ulcers, vertigo, and normalization of serum sodium within two weeks—suggests that colchicine was sufficient to quell the inflammatory milieu driving both the neuro‑BD and SIADH.

The hyponatraemia profile was consistent with the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Endocrine evaluation revealed low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg) with high urinary sodium, and inappropriately elevated ADH (22.4 pg/mL). Feature of a euvolemic state and lack of adrenal or thyroid dysfunction excluded other causes. SIADH in the context of CNS inflammation is usually managed conservatively, especially when the serum sodium is only mildly to moderately low (serum Na⁺ < 135 mmol/L) and neurologic symptoms are not life‑threatening. Hence, the team implemented strict fluid restriction (<800 mL/day), directed by the principles of water balance correction derived from the "water restriction" paradigm. Daily monitoring of serum sodium and careful clinical assessment prevented rapid over‑correction, which could precipitate osmotic demyelination. Hypertonic saline was deemed unnecessary because the patient’s sodium never fell below 125 mmol/L and there were no indications of cerebral edema or severe neurologic compromise. Vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans) were likewise avoided; their use is typically reserved for refractory SIADH or when fluid restriction fails, and there is limited evidence supporting efficacy in acute neuro‑BD‑associated SIADH. The patient’s serum sodium normalized over the course of the stay without pharmacologic intervention beyond fluid restriction.

Adjunctive local treatments addressed the cutaneous manifestations directly. Topical corticosteroid ointment was applied to oral and genital aphthous lesions, providing rapid pain relief and reducing local inflammation. Silver sulfadiazine cream was used for the genital ulcer to prevent secondary infection and promote epithelialization. These local modalities complemented systemic colchicine therapy without introducing systemic immunosuppression.

During hospitalization, the patient experienced generalized malaise, arthralgia and a decline in functional status, likely secondary to the systemic inflammatory burden and immobilization. Early rehabilitation—including mobilization, physiotherapy and occupational therapy—was instituted to mitigate deconditioning, improve endurance and facilitate a full recovery. The short‑course, high‑dose antibiotics and antivirals had minimal impact on physical function; therefore, the focus on rehabilitation aimed to reduce hospital‑related complications such as deep‑vein thrombosis and muscle wasting.

In sum, this therapeutic strategy exemplified a stepwise, pathology‑driven approach: empiric antimicrobial coverage that was promptly narrowed; early cessation of antiviral therapy upon virology results; judicious use of colchicine as primary anti‑inflammatory therapy, thereby avoiding systemic steroids; a conservative, evidence‑based regimen for SIADH that prevented electrolyte overcorrection; targeted local care for mucocutaneous lesions; and proactive rehabilitation to preserve functional capacity. The case underscores that, in select patients with neuro‑Behçet’s disease, colchicine monotherapy combined with conservative SIADH management may yield satisfactory outcomes without the need for corticosteroids or more aggressive endocrine interventions.

## Outcome, Follow‑up, Clinical Lessons, and Implications for Practice  

After initiation of colchicine on day 8, the patient’s systemic inflammatory markers (C–reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate) and myogenic enzymes (creatine kinase) fell rapidly. Fever resolved the following day, and appetite returned within two days. Neurologic symptoms—headache, vertigo, and persistent hiccups—were markedly reduced after the second week, with complete resolution by day 16. Concomitantly, serum sodium rose from 124 mmol/L to 136 mmol/L, and plasma ADH fell from 22.4 to 1.8 pg/mL, indicating recovery from SIADH. These clinical and biochemical improvements occurred without the use of systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants.

The patient was discharged after a 28‑day inpatient stay, following a period of close monitoring for fluid balance and sodium levels. Colchicine was continued for approximately four years, during which there was no recurrence of mucocutaneous lesions, neurologic complaints, or hyponatraemia. During follow‑up, addition of a biguanide drug improved glycaemic control (HbA1c declined from 8.2 % to a therapeutic range), suggesting that stabilization of the inflammatory milieu in Behçet’s disease can favour better metabolic regulation.

Although the literature generally recommends corticosteroids for acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease, this case demonstrates that colchicine monotherapy can suffice when the disease presents with isolated aseptic meningitis, mild neurologic impairment, and no focal brain lesions. Colchicine’s anti‑neutrophil and anti‑cytokine effects likely mitigate the underlying inflammatory cascade without the adverse effects associated with high‑dose steroids. The absence of relapses over four years reinforces the therapeutic potential of colchicine in selected patients, particularly when steroid exposure is undesirable.

For clinicians, the key learning points are: (1) a high index of suspicion for neuro‑Behçet’s should be maintained in patients presenting with fever, headache, hyponatraemia, and mucocutaneous findings; (2) early recognition facilitates targeted therapy and reduces unnecessary antibiotic or antiviral prophylaxis; and (3) SIADH in the setting of CNS inflammation may surface through seemingly unrelated symptoms such as hiccups, vertigo, or rhabdomyolysis, warranting prompt electrolyte assessment.

To avoid a diagnostic cascade, focus testing should be directed toward markers that directly inform treatment. In this case, measurement of serum ACE and soluble interleukin‑2 receptor was confined to exclude sarcoidosis or lymphoma. Mild elevations in these parameters were interpreted in the context of the overall clinical picture rather than prompting additional invasive investigations. This pragmatic approach prevents overtreatment and conserves resources.

Recognition that SIADH can manifest with nonspecific signs—in particular persistent hiccups and muscle enzyme elevation—should prompt immediate evaluation of serum and urine osmolality, even when imaging is unremarkable. Early correction of hyponatraemia with fluid restriction and careful monitoring prevented neurological sequelae and likely accelerated neurologic recovery.

Finally, a multidisciplinary team—rheumatology, neurology, and endocrinology—was essential for integrated care. Rheumatology guided the decision to initiate colchicine, neurology monitored neurologic progress and CSF parameters, while endocrinology managed hyponatraemia and, later, glycaemic control. Such collaboration ensures that organ‑specific complications of Behçet’s disease are addressed holistically, improving long‑term outcomes.
