Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# SIADH in Neuro‑Behçet’s: The Silent Contributor to Hyponatraemia and Complications  

## Introduction  
You’ve probably seen a patient with fever, headache, and a rash, only to find a diagnosis that moves you in two directions at once. That was the case for a 63‑year‑old gentleman who presented to the emergency department (ED) with a fever, a severe headache, oral aphthae, and a “folliculitis‑like” rash. What made his case knotty (and what you’ll find in the next few paragraphs) was that he also had profound hyponatraemia and, later in his hospital stay, genital ulceration—hallmarks of Behçet’s disease (BD) and, intriguingly, a syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). What follows is a snapshot of what we learned from this patient and why SIADH should be on your radar when you’re dealing with neuro‑Behçet’s.

## The patient at a glance  
- **Age/Gender**: 63‑year‑old man  
- **Vascular risk**: Undiagnosed type 2 diabetes (glucose 14.2 mmol/L, HbA1c 8.2 %)  
- **Initial ED complaints**: 3 days of fever (38 °C), acute headache, general malaise, and persistent hiccups  
- **Dermatological findings**: Oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like facial rash, later scrotal ulcer  
- **Neurologic exam**: Alert, no focal deficits, normal neck‑stiffness  
- **Vital signs**: HR 119/min, BP 135/73 mm Hg, RR 19/min  

| Lab | Result | Interpretation |
|-----|--------|----------------|
| Na⁺ | 124 mmol/L | Hyponatraemia |
| Serum osmolality | 267 mOsm/kg | Low |
| Urine osmolality | 900 mOsm/kg | Inappropriately concentrated |
| Urine Na⁺ | 88 mmol/L | >20 mmol/L |
| ADH | 22.4 pg/mL (high) | Elevated |
| CRP | 180 mg/L | Marked inflammation |
| CK | 1,349 U/L | Rhabdomyolysis |
| Liver enzymes | Mildly elevated | Non‑specific inflammation |
| Brain MRI | Normal (no parenchymal lesions) | No gross disease |
| CSF | Mild pleocytosis (11 cells/µL), protein 0.97 g/L | Aseptic meningitis |

With these findings, the presumptive diagnosis was **acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) complicated by SIADH**. The concurrent development of genital ulcers and a positive HLA‑B51 confirmed definitive BD per the International Criteria (score 5/5).

## Why did SIADH show up?  
SIADH can result from virtually any CNS insult that stimulates non‑osmotic ADH release. In neuro‑BD, the pathophysiology is thought to be dominated by a cytokine storm:

- **IL‑6**, a key pro‑inflammatory mediator in BD, drives ADH secretion from the posterior pituitary and hypothalamus.  
- Even in the absence of overt lesions on MRI, the CSF of patients with acute neuro‑BD can contain elevated IL‑6, explaining how a “normal” scan doesn't rule out CNS inflammation.  
- In our patient, CRP was massively raised (180 mg/L), a surrogate marker of IL‑6 activity that may have led to the disorderly ADH surge.

Because SIADH elevates intracellular water, patients become **euvolemic** on the surface—no edema or tachycardia—so the hyponatraemia may fly under the radar unless checked.

### Paradoxes that lit the path to SIADH  

- **Hiccups**: A rare but sensitive sign of severe hyponatraemia (often <120 mmol/L). In this case, hiccups persisted right up until serum Na⁺ rose >130 mmol/L.  
- **Rhabdomyolysis (CK > 1,300 U/L)**: Severe hyponatraemia can precipitate skeletal‑muscle injury. While the exact trigger is unclear, we suspect the low sodium and systemic inflammation conspired here.  
- **Hyperglycaemia**: In diabetics, hyperglycaemia depresses serum Na⁺ by itself (≈ 4‑6 mmol/L per 5.5 mmol/L glucose). After the patient’s glucose dropped with caloric control, we fixed Na⁺ upward with fluid restriction and monitored for over‑correction.

## How we found the diagnosis – and why it matters  
1. **Clinical suspicion** – Fever, headache, hyponatraemia, and a rash raised the flame of “cNS inflammation” on the differential.  
2. **Biochemical triad for SIADH** – Low serum osmolality, concentrated urine, and high urinary sodium with preserved adrenal and thyroid function.  
3. **CSF work‑up** – Aseptic meningitis, no bacterial, viral, or malignant cells.  
4. **BD confirmation** – Oral and genital ulcers, skin rash, inflammatory markers, and positive HLA‑B51.

The lesson: *Do not dismiss SIADH when CSF shows aseptic meningitis and MRI is negative – do the sodium panel, otherwise you might miss a treatable complication.*

## Managing SIADH in the setting of neuro‑BD  
The cornerstone of SIADH is **fluid restriction**. For this patient, the team instituted <800 mL/day – a conservative but effective drop in free water intake.  

| Management step | Rationale | Caveats |
|-----------------|-----------|---------|
| Fluid restriction | Reduces euvolemic water load → raises Na⁺ | Need daily trend checks; avoid dehydration |
| Sodium monitoring | 2‑3 ×/day initially | Watch for rebound hyponatraemia |
| Hypertonic saline (3% NaCl) | Indicated if severe symptoms (seizures, altered mental status) | Rate‑limit to <6 mmol/kg/day |
| Vasopressin antagonists (tolvaptan) | Useful if fluid restriction fails | Not yet proven in neuro‑BD, but can be considered in refractory cases |
| Correct for hyperglycaemia | Adjust Na⁺ by +0.024 × (Glucose‑100) | Avoid over‑correction, monitor for osmotic demyelination |

In this case, the fluid restriction alone was sufficient; the sodium rose back to 136 mmol/L within ~2 weeks. No vasopressors or vaptans were required, illustrating that a simple, disciplined approach can rescue a patient even with significant comorbidities.

## The colchicine twist  
Traditionally, acute neuro‑BD is tackled with high‑dose steroids. However, this patient did **not** receive steroids. Instead, after empirical antibiotics and antivirals were ruled out, the team switched to oral colchicine (dosing not specified, but standard for mucocutaneous BD).  

Colchicine’s mechanism—neutrophil inhibition, down‑regulation of IL‑1β/IL‑6—may explain its rapid effect on both the rash and the CNS inflammation. The patient’s appetite, headaches, vertigo, and mucocutaneous lesions all improved within two weeks, and the SIADH resolved in tandem.  

While we should not generalize from a single case, it underscores that **colchicine can be a viable first‑line therapeutic **when steroids are contraindicated or the neuro‑BD is mild**. It certainly merits consideration, especially in patients wanting to avoid steroids’ side‑effects.

## Lessons & Clinical pearls for the ED & inpatient teams  

- **Hyponatraemia + fever + headache** in a man with oral/genital ulcers → think neuro‑BD + SIADH.  
- **Hiccups** and **rhabdomyolysis** are red‑flag signs of severe hyponatraemia even when the patient is otherwise medically “stable.”  
- If imaging is normal, don’t discard neuro‑BD. ACS (aseptic meningitis) can be the only MRI‑negative manifestation.  
- **Always correct for hyperglycaemia** when interpreting sodium.  
- **Fluid restriction** is the first‑line SIADH therapy; closely monitor serum sodium to avoid over‑correction.  
- In a steroid‑intolerant patient, colchicine may suffice for both mucocutaneous and neuro‑BD manifestations; more data are needed, but the evidence here suggests it’s a valid option.  
- Keep a high index of suspicion for systemic disease when you encounter chronic mucocutaneous lesions + acute CNS symptoms.

## Take‑home points  
- **SIADH is a real but under‑reported complication of acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease.**  
- **IL‑6‑mediated inflammation can drive inappropriate ADH release, even without radiographic lesions.**  
- **Hiccups and rhabdomyolysis alert to severe hyponatraemia; correct for hyperglycaemia before interpreting sodium levels.**  
- **Fluid restriction, vigilant sodium monitoring, and correction of underlying hyperglycaemia are cornerstone therapies.**  
- **Colchicine alone can sometimes reverse neuro‑BD and its SIADH, offering an alternative to steroids when appropriate.**  

In whatever case you encounter, the key takeaway is to keep the neuro‑BD + SIADH combo in your differential. Hyponatraemia might be the silent contributor behind a patient’s headaches, vertigo, or even skin lesions. With timely recognition, simple management with fluid restriction, and a tailored anti‑inflammatory strategy, you can restore both the brain’s water balance and the body’s inflammatory equilibrium.
