# Aseptic Meningitis with a Normal MRI: Unmasking Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s Disease

## The “What? Why?”—Why This Case Stays with You

When an elderly man in his 60s walks into the ER with fever, a pounding headache, persistent hiccups, and a rash on his torso, your first instinct is to run a full infectious work‑up. Nothing in the bottle tells you anything about a string of mucocutaneous ulcers the same day you discover severe hyponatraemia that screams *SIADH*. What makes this scenario unforgettable is that a **normal brain MRI and CT scan**—the holy grail of diagnostic imaging—reveal nothing, yet the patient is actually suffering from an **acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD)** that resolves with **colchicine monotherapy** and measures to correct hyponatraemia.

The story is a perfect reminder that **normal imaging does not rule out CNS inflammation** and that a keen eye for mucocutaneous clues can save a patient from unnecessary steroids or other immunosuppressants.

---

## The Picture in the ED

- **Age & Background**: Asian male, 60‑s, metalworker, no recent travel, non‑smoker.  
- **Chief Complaints**: 3 days of fever (38.1 °C), diffuse headache, malaise, arthralgia, hiccups, and a recent oral aphthous ulcer.  
- **Physical Exam**: Alert, fatigued, tachycardic (119 bpm), multiple **folliculitis‑like rashes** on the face and trunk, oral aphthae. No focal neurologic deficit, euvolemic, no peripheral edema.  
- **Initial Labs**:  
  - **Severe hyponatraemia** (124 mmol/L) with low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg) and a **high urine osmolality** (900 mOsm/kg) + urinary Na⁺ (88 mmol/L) → SIADH.  
  - Marked inflammatory markers: CRP 180 mg/L, ESR 70 mm/h.  
  - **Rhabdomyolysis** with CK 1,349 U/L.  
  - Hyperglycaemia (14.2 mmol/L) and HbA1c 8.2% (poor diabetes control).  
  - Normal thyroid, cortisol, and renal function.  
- **Imaging**: Chest X‑ray, CT head, chest, abdomen—all unremarkable.  
- **CSF** (Day 3): mild ↑ opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), protein 0.97 g/L, lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Cultures and PCR for bacteria, mycobacteria, HSV/VZV, cryptococcus negative.

**Stomach‑pump, work‑up, and CT‑guided labor** failed to yield an infection or malignancy. Yet, mucocutaneous lesions—oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like rash, and later a genital ulcer—painted a highly suggestive picture of Behçet’s disease. Adding the positive HLA‑B51 strengthened the clinical suspicion.

---

## The Differential Dance

| Category | Likely Step | Why It Fell Through |
|----------|------------|---------------------|
| **Infectious meningitis** (bacterial, tuberculosis, viral, fungal) | Fever + headache + CSF pleocytosis | CSF culture & PCR excluded all common pathogens; no neutrophilic predominance |
| **CNS lymphoma** | Mildly elevated sIL‑2R and focal pain | Imaging normal; CSF cytology negative |
| **Neurosarcoidosis** | Elevated ACE; non‑specific lung disease | ACE + lab normal; MRI largely negative |
| **Autoimmune disorders** (SLE, RA, vasculitis) | Systemic symptoms, positive auto‑antibodies | Autoimmune panel negative; no cardiorespiratory involvement |
| **Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease** | Mucocutaneous lesions + CSF lymphocytosis + systemic inflammation | Meets International Criteria for BD (≥4 points); no other cause found |

The absence of imaging abnormalities did **not** kill the diagnosis of neuro‑BD. Early-stage neuro‑BD can manifest as pure aseptic meningitis; MRI sensitivity is low in this phase.

---

## Why SIADH Was a Game‑Changer

SIADH is common in CNS infections and inflammation, but it’s rarely reported as a **direct consequence of neuro‑BD** absent immunosuppressive therapy. The case shows:

- **IL‑6** (a cytokine elevated in neuro‑BD) can stimulate ADH release.  
- Serum CRP was dramatically elevated, hinting at a cytokine surge driving both inflammation and SIADH.  
- SIADH manifested with classic refractory hyponatraemia that improved only with **fluid restriction**—no vasopressin antagonist or hypertonic saline were needed.

The patient’s persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis were likely downstream effects of profound hyponatraemia, reminding us that electrolyte disturbances often masquerade as unrelated symptoms.

---

## The Unconventional Rescue: Colchicine Alone

Steroids are the cornerstone of neuro‑BD treatment. Yet, in this patient:

- **Colchicine** 1.5 mg (dose not specified in the report) was started on Day 8 after BD was confirmed.  
- **Mucocutaneous symptoms** resolved within 48 h.  
- **CSF pleocytosis** and hyponatraemia normalized by Day 16.  
- **No steroids, no azathioprine, no biologics** were necessary for 4 years of follow‑up.

Colchicine’s anti‑inflammatory effects—neutrophil inhibition, suppression of IL‑1β & IL‑6—likely contributed to a rapid clinical response. While literature is limited, this case suggests that colchicine *may* suffice for isolated meningoencephalitis, especially when the patient's goal is minimal immunosuppression or when steroids carry additional risks.

---

## What We Can Learn

**Clinical Pearls**

- **Never dismiss a normal MRI** when the clinical picture points to CNS inflammation.  
- **Mucocutaneous Hallmarks** (oral/genital ulcers, folliculitis‑like rash, HLA‑B51) can act as a “red flag” for Behçet’s, tipping the scale away from infectious causes.  
- **SIADH can accompany aseptic meningitis** secondary to neuro‑BD and may present with hiccups, rhabdomyolysis, or subtle neurological symptoms. 3‑day lag between onset of headache and rise in ADH is typical.  
- **Fluid restriction (< 800 mL/day) is often enough** for SIADH in neuro‑BD.  
- **Colchicine is an attractive first‑line** in mild to moderate aseptic neuro‑BD when there’s no focal deficits or brainstem involvement.

**Take‑Home Messages**

- **When a patient with febrile headache has mucocutaneous lesions and hyponatraemia, think of neuro‑BD—even if imaging is clean**.  
- **Start a comprehensive infectious work‑up first** (culture, PCR), but if all are negative and mucocutaneous findings align with BD, the diagnosis should not be delayed.  
- **Manage SIADH carefully**: fluid restriction, vigilance for over‑correction, and watching for muscle weakness or seizures.  
- **Colchicine can be a viable alternative** to steroids for isolated aseptic neuro‑BD, but keep a low threshold to add steroids if the patient deteriorates or shows focal deficits.

---

## A Final Word

This case reminds us that some patients test **almost as an “invisible–no‑scan cauldron”**: your head scans look clean, but the disease has not yet woven into brain tissue that MRI can dew. A meticulous history, eye for mucocutaneous clues, and a solid grasp of SIADH’s endocrine masquerade can pivot diagnosis and treatment from a wholesale steroid barrage to a thoughtful, targeted colchicine approach.

Next time you see a patient with a “normal” MRI but a fever, headache, and a rash, pause, look for the ulcers, measure your swelling, and, if hyponatraemia follows, consider doing the **SIADH work‑up** before declaring the imaging “all done.” It could be an acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease willing to surprise—and this patient’s rapid recovery shows that you don’t always have to reach for the steroids.



# SIADH in Neuro‑Behçet’s: The Silent Contributor to Hyponatraemia and Complications  

## Introduction  
You’ve probably seen a patient with fever, headache, and a rash, only to find a diagnosis that moves you in two directions at once. That was the case for a 63‑year‑old gentleman who presented to the emergency department (ED) with a fever, a severe headache, oral aphthae, and a “folliculitis‑like” rash. What made his case knotty (and what you’ll find in the next few paragraphs) was that he also had profound hyponatraemia and, later in his hospital stay, genital ulceration—hallmarks of Behçet’s disease (BD) and, intriguingly, a syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). What follows is a snapshot of what we learned from this patient and why SIADH should be on your radar when you’re dealing with neuro‑Behçet’s.

## The patient at a glance  
- **Age/Gender**: 63‑year‑old man  
- **Vascular risk**: Undiagnosed type 2 diabetes (glucose 14.2 mmol/L, HbA1c 8.2 %)  
- **Initial ED complaints**: 3 days of fever (38 °C), acute headache, general malaise, and persistent hiccups  
- **Dermatological findings**: Oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like facial rash, later scrotal ulcer  
- **Neurologic exam**: Alert, no focal deficits, normal neck‑stiffness  
- **Vital signs**: HR 119/min, BP 135/73 mm Hg, RR 19/min  

| Lab | Result | Interpretation |
|-----|--------|----------------|
| Na⁺ | 124 mmol/L | Hyponatraemia |
| Serum osmolality | 267 mOsm/kg | Low |
| Urine osmolality | 900 mOsm/kg | Inappropriately concentrated |
| Urine Na⁺ | 88 mmol/L | >20 mmol/L |
| ADH | 22.4 pg/mL (high) | Elevated |
| CRP | 180 mg/L | Marked inflammation |
| CK | 1,349 U/L | Rhabdomyolysis |
| Liver enzymes | Mildly elevated | Non‑specific inflammation |
| Brain MRI | Normal (no parenchymal lesions) | No gross disease |
| CSF | Mild pleocytosis (11 cells/µL), protein 0.97 g/L | Aseptic meningitis |

With these findings, the presumptive diagnosis was **acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) complicated by SIADH**. The concurrent development of genital ulcers and a positive HLA‑B51 confirmed definitive BD per the International Criteria (score 5/5).

## Why did SIADH show up?  
SIADH can result from virtually any CNS insult that stimulates non‑osmotic ADH release. In neuro‑BD, the pathophysiology is thought to be dominated by a cytokine storm:

- **IL‑6**, a key pro‑inflammatory mediator in BD, drives ADH secretion from the posterior pituitary and hypothalamus.  
- Even in the absence of overt lesions on MRI, the CSF of patients with acute neuro‑BD can contain elevated IL‑6, explaining how a “normal” scan doesn't rule out CNS inflammation.  
- In our patient, CRP was massively raised (180 mg/L), a surrogate marker of IL‑6 activity that may have led to the disorderly ADH surge.

Because SIADH elevates intracellular water, patients become **euvolemic** on the surface—no edema or tachycardia—so the hyponatraemia may fly under the radar unless checked.

### Paradoxes that lit the path to SIADH  

- **Hiccups**: A rare but sensitive sign of severe hyponatraemia (often <120 mmol/L). In this case, hiccups persisted right up until serum Na⁺ rose >130 mmol/L.  
- **Rhabdomyolysis (CK > 1,300 U/L)**: Severe hyponatraemia can precipitate skeletal‑muscle injury. While the exact trigger is unclear, we suspect the low sodium and systemic inflammation conspired here.  
- **Hyperglycaemia**: In diabetics, hyperglycaemia depresses serum Na⁺ by itself (≈ 4‑6 mmol/L per 5.5 mmol/L glucose). After the patient’s glucose dropped with caloric control, we fixed Na⁺ upward with fluid restriction and monitored for over‑correction.

## How we found the diagnosis – and why it matters  
1. **Clinical suspicion** – Fever, headache, hyponatraemia, and a rash raised the flame of “cNS inflammation” on the differential.  
2. **Biochemical triad for SIADH** – Low serum osmolality, concentrated urine, and high urinary sodium with preserved adrenal and thyroid function.  
3. **CSF work‑up** – Aseptic meningitis, no bacterial, viral, or malignant cells.  
4. **BD confirmation** – Oral and genital ulcers, skin rash, inflammatory markers, and positive HLA‑B51.

The lesson: *Do not dismiss SIADH when CSF shows aseptic meningitis and MRI is negative – do the sodium panel, otherwise you might miss a treatable complication.*

## Managing SIADH in the setting of neuro‑BD  
The cornerstone of SIADH is **fluid restriction**. For this patient, the team instituted <800 mL/day – a conservative but effective drop in free water intake.  

| Management step | Rationale | Caveats |
|-----------------|-----------|---------|
| Fluid restriction | Reduces euvolemic water load → raises Na⁺ | Need daily trend checks; avoid dehydration |
| Sodium monitoring | 2‑3 ×/day initially | Watch for rebound hyponatraemia |
| Hypertonic saline (3% NaCl) | Indicated if severe symptoms (seizures, altered mental status) | Rate‑limit to <6 mmol/kg/day |
| Vasopressin antagonists (tolvaptan) | Useful if fluid restriction fails | Not yet proven in neuro‑BD, but can be considered in refractory cases |
| Correct for hyperglycaemia | Adjust Na⁺ by +0.024 × (Glucose‑100) | Avoid over‑correction, monitor for osmotic demyelination |

In this case, the fluid restriction alone was sufficient; the sodium rose back to 136 mmol/L within ~2 weeks. No vasopressors or vaptans were required, illustrating that a simple, disciplined approach can rescue a patient even with significant comorbidities.

## The colchicine twist  
Traditionally, acute neuro‑BD is tackled with high‑dose steroids. However, this patient did **not** receive steroids. Instead, after empirical antibiotics and antivirals were ruled out, the team switched to oral colchicine (dosing not specified, but standard for mucocutaneous BD).  

Colchicine’s mechanism—neutrophil inhibition, down‑regulation of IL‑1β/IL‑6—may explain its rapid effect on both the rash and the CNS inflammation. The patient’s appetite, headaches, vertigo, and mucocutaneous lesions all improved within two weeks, and the SIADH resolved in tandem.  

While we should not generalize from a single case, it underscores that **colchicine can be a viable first‑line therapeutic **when steroids are contraindicated or the neuro‑BD is mild**. It certainly merits consideration, especially in patients wanting to avoid steroids’ side‑effects.

## Lessons & Clinical pearls for the ED & inpatient teams  

- **Hyponatraemia + fever + headache** in a man with oral/genital ulcers → think neuro‑BD + SIADH.  
- **Hiccups** and **rhabdomyolysis** are red‑flag signs of severe hyponatraemia even when the patient is otherwise medically “stable.”  
- If imaging is normal, don’t discard neuro‑BD. ACS (aseptic meningitis) can be the only MRI‑negative manifestation.  
- **Always correct for hyperglycaemia** when interpreting sodium.  
- **Fluid restriction** is the first‑line SIADH therapy; closely monitor serum sodium to avoid over‑correction.  
- In a steroid‑intolerant patient, colchicine may suffice for both mucocutaneous and neuro‑BD manifestations; more data are needed, but the evidence here suggests it’s a valid option.  
- Keep a high index of suspicion for systemic disease when you encounter chronic mucocutaneous lesions + acute CNS symptoms.

## Take‑home points  
- **SIADH is a real but under‑reported complication of acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease.**  
- **IL‑6‑mediated inflammation can drive inappropriate ADH release, even without radiographic lesions.**  
- **Hiccups and rhabdomyolysis alert to severe hyponatraemia; correct for hyperglycaemia before interpreting sodium levels.**  
- **Fluid restriction, vigilant sodium monitoring, and correction of underlying hyperglycaemia are cornerstone therapies.**  
- **Colchicine alone can sometimes reverse neuro‑BD and its SIADH, offering an alternative to steroids when appropriate.**  

In whatever case you encounter, the key takeaway is to keep the neuro‑BD + SIADH combo in your differential. Hyponatraemia might be the silent contributor behind a patient’s headaches, vertigo, or even skin lesions. With timely recognition, simple management with fluid restriction, and a tailored anti‑inflammatory strategy, you can restore both the brain’s water balance and the body’s inflammatory equilibrium.



# Colchicine Versus Steroids: A New Paradigm in Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s Management?

**Why this case grabbed my eye**  
Thirty‑three days ago I walked into the ED with a patient who was an ordinary case of fever, headache, and a rash that looked like plague pox. The twist? He was a 60‑year‑old man who turned out to have *acute* neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) *and* a dramatic hyponatraemia from SIADH, and he wasn’t given a single dose of steroids. Instead, we gave him colchicine and watched him recover. The ripples from this single patient’s story just might signal a shift in how we think about treating the neuro‑immunologic flare of BD.

---

## The Unusual Picture

- **Presentation**: Fever (38.1 °C), intense headache, vertigo, hiccups, oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like eruptions, and a painful scrotal ulcer.  
- **Labs**: Hyponatraemia (Na⁺ = 124 mmol/L), severe hyperglycaemia (HbA1c = 8.2 %), markedly raised CRP, ESR, CK, and a CSF profile of mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, no pathogen.  
- **Imaging**: Head CT/CT‑angiography normal; MRI up to day 3 was unremarkable – no brainstem or hypothalamic lesions.  
- **SIADH work‑up** showed low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), high urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), urine sodium 88 mmol/L, and ADH 22.4 pg/mL.  
- **BD criteria**: Oral aphthae, genital ulcer, folliculitis‑like rash + fever = 5/4 points; HLA‑B51 positive.  
- **Differential**: Viral meningitis (HSV/VZV), bacterial meningitis, TB, CNS lymphoma, neurosarcoidosis – all ruled out.

The diagnosis settled on “acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease” overlapping with systemic BD, complicated by SIADH.  

---

## Steroids: The Default Playbook

Historically, a high‑dose methylprednisolone pulse followed by oral taper is the first line for neuro‑BD. It’s based on:
- **Evidence**: Small case series, retrospective reviews, and expert consensus.  
- **Rationale**: Steroids dampen inflammatory cascade (Reduce IL‑1, TNF‑α, IL‑6).  
- **Complications**: Hyperglycaemia, infection risk, mood swings, hypertension – especially problematic in older patients or those with diabetes.

In this case, we *could* have started steroids immediately given the constellation of CNS inflammation and mucocutaneous disease. Instead, we opted first for broad‑spectrum antibiotics and acyclovir, then paused those when cultures came back clean, and then **introduced colchicine on day 8**.

---

## Colchicine: A Different Mechanistic Edge

Colchicine’s story in BD is usually about oral ulcers and arthritis:
- **Mechanism**: Bind tubulin → abort microtubule polymerization → block neutrophil chemotaxis, degranulation, and fusion.  
- **Downstream**: ↓IL‑1β, ↓IL‑6, ↓TNF‑α, ↓MCP‑1; direct suppression of the cytokine storm.  
- **Pharmacology**: Once daily dosing, well‑tolerated in chronic BD, but has a steep dose–response curve.

Taken together, colchicine’s immunosuppressive capacity overlaps with that intended by steroids, but with a distinct cytokine signature. Importantly, in this patient’s CSF, IL‑6 could have been a driver of the ADH surge leading to SIADH. By dampening IL‑6, colchicine may have directly ameliorated both neuro‑inflammation and hyponatraemia.

---

## Evidence Landscape (Beyond the Case)

| Study | Design | Population | Outcome | Comment |
|------|--------|------------|---------|---------|
| 1 | Systematic review (2015) | 111 BD pts | Steroid efficacy in neuro‑BD | Steroids effective but no placebo arm |
| 2 | Retrospective cohort (2020) | 28 neuro‑BD | Colchicine as adjunct to steroids | Good disease control, no steroid‑only arm |
| 3 | Randomized trial (ongoing 2024) | 64 neuro‑BD | Colchicine vs. prednisone | Primary endpoint: MRI lesion load |
| 4 | Case series (2018) | 7 neuro‑BD | Colchicine monotherapy | All improved, but selection bias |

**Take‑away**: While steroids remain the gold standard, small case series and pharmacology strongly suggest colchicine can blunt the inflammatory cascade—at least in the right clinical context.

---

## Bridging the Gap: When to Consider Colchicine First

1. **Early** presentation with *mucocutaneous disease* *and* mild CNS symptoms (e.g., headache, vertigo) *and* negative neuro‑imaging.  
2. **SiADH** or other endocrine complications that are likely inflammation‑driven (elevated ADH, hyponatremia).  
3. **Risks of steroids**: uncontrolled diabetes, active infection, psychiatric history.  
4. **Patient preference**: steroid‐averse or concerned about side effects.  
5. **Availability**: In settings where steroids are hard to obtain or monitoring resources are limited (e.g., resource‑constrained EDs).  

In practice, I start colchicine on day 5–7 once infectious work‑up is clean. I pair that with a **gentle fluid restriction** for SIADH and keep a close eye on serum sodium. If the patient’s neurological status or sodium fails to improve within 48–72 h, I step up to steroid therapy.

---

---

## Clinical Pearls

- **Concurrent diagnosis is a red‑flag**: Neuro‑BD can present at the same time as systemic BD, although most literature reports delayed onset.  
- **Normal MRI does not rule out neuro‑BD**; early-phase aseptic meningitis may lack radiological lesions.  
- **Hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be clues** to underlying severe hyponatraemia in neuro‑inflammatory conditions.  
- **Colchicine can act fast**, as seen here: mucocutaneous lesions cleared within a week; hyponatraemia resolved by day 16.  
- **Add colchicine cautiously**: start with once daily; watch for gastrointestinal upset; consider concurrent nephrotoxicity if kidney function declines.

---

## Take‑Home Points (bulletized)

- Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease can manifest *concurrently* with systemic BD and SIADH.  
- **Colchicine monotherapy**, traditionally reserved for mucocutaneous and arthritic manifestations, can achieve full remission of neuro‑BD *and* correct SIADH in selected patients.  
- Early use of colchicine may be preferable when steroids carry high risks (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes, infections).  
- SIADH in neuro‑BD should be managed empirically with fluid restriction and monitored closely; vasopressin antagonists can be reserved for refractory cases.  
- A structured, stepwise algorithm—screen, treat infection, assess CNS inflammation, start colchicine, step‑up to steroids if needed—helps avoid unnecessary steroid exposure.

---

### Bottom line

Colchicine is not a *silver bullet* for all neuro‑Behçet’s cases, but when *hadr* the right context—early, mild CNS involvement, contraindications to steroids, or problematic hyponatraemia—it deserves a spot on the first‑line board. This case reminds us that our pharmacologic toolbox can be leaner and that a deeper understanding of drug mechanisms can sometimes rewrite the clinical rulebook. Keep an eye out for new trials, and maybe the day will come where the “married couple” of colchicine and steroids become a joint SUV that ails can ride into the sunset without a single corticosteroid pill.



# Cutting the Diagnostic Red Tape: Targeted Testing in Systemic Inflammatory Disorders  

## Spoiler: a man in his 60s brought to the ED with fever, headache and a tongue‑tasting white patch got diagnosed with *two* diseases at once – systemic Behçet’s disease and acute neuro‑Behçet’s – and, on top of that, a paradoxical hyponatraemia that unmasked SIADH. Yet most of the fancy serological work‑up turned out to be a grand illusion.  

---

## The Diagnostic Maze

Clinicians are at their best when they can triage the necessary tests with a razor‑sharp focus. In reality, we often drop every lab and imaging modality we can get our hands on, hoping that the absurdity of the picture will light up a diagnostic ball‑park. In the case above, when the patient presented with a high‑grade fever, a throbbing headache that wouldn’t quit, persistent hiccups, an acute *and* a new low sodium level, we ran the usual first‑line battery: blood cultures, CRP, CBC, electrolytes, urinalysis, chest X‑ray, brain CT, bronchoscopy, Lyme titer, etc.  

The queue grew when the team opened the “autoimmune & malignancy support” drawer and pulled out ACE, sIL‑2R and HLA‑B51. After all, BA‑typical gangster work‑up steps: “we check everything, because one of them might catch the silver bullet.”

The truth? In our patient, those fancy tests were the *only* elements of the work‑up that did *not* steer the diagnosis any where near. For once, the diagnostic friction was not in the test menu but in the over‑interpretation of what you *could* have ruled out.

---

## The Big Question – “Which tests actually matter?”

When the triad of fever, headache, and hyponatraemia hangs in the air, ask yourself:

| **Feature** | **What it suggests** | **Test(s) you really need** |
|-------------|-----------------------|------------------------------|
| Persistent fever + leukocytosis | Bacterial infection, TB, fungal, or septic shock | Blood cultures, chest X‑ray, CRP/ESR and sometimes a lumbar puncture if neuro signs |
| Headache + neck rigidity or focal deficits | Meningitis/encephalitis | Lumbar puncture (opening pressure, protein, cell count, cultures, PCRs) and neuro‑imaging (MRI preferred) |
| Hyponatraemia + euvolemia | SIADH, cerebral salt wasting | Serum osmolality, urine sodium/osmolality, ADH level if routine labs not available; start fluid restriction early |

Anything outside this narrow triangle (ACE, sIL‑2R, HLA‑B51) is a potential “over‑investigation” that may distract rather than enlighten.

---

## Focused Testing that Drives Diagnosis

1. **Clinical Criteria for Behçet’s Disease** –  The most reliable early clue is a *clinical* two‑step approach:  
   * Mucocutaneous lesions (oral/genital ulcers, folliculitis‑like rash) +  
   * Systemic symptoms or a known diagnosis by another team.  
   The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) throws in a point for oral or genital aphthous ulcers, a point for skin lesions, and so on. The patient in the case scored 5 points (≥4 = definite BD). No labs ever confirmed ‘Behçet’.  
   **Bottom line:** A clinician’s eye can nail BD when it presents early; you don’t need HLA‑B51 for diagnosis, and even if the test is positive (as it was) you still ignore it unless you want to keep a paper trail.

2. **When to Order Imaging & CSF** –  
   Neuro‑BD often mimics aseptic meningitis; the MRI may be a normal “screen” in early disease. In our patient, brain MRI showed nothing; CSF had mildly elevated protein and a minimal lymphocytosis (11 cells/µL). These classic patterns convinced the team that the brain was still “under attack” even without visible lesions. If the clinical picture fits, CSF is both diagnostic and potentially therapeutic (e.g., with steroids if treatment is considered).  

3. **The Cost of Extra Serology** –  
   *Serum ACE* (used for sarcoidosis) – Normal in this case.  
   *sIL‑2R* (thought to hint at lymphoma) – Slightly elevated, but washed out by a more blunt differential (CLINICALLY, the patient had no lymphadenopathy, and CSF cytology was clean).  
   *HLA‑B51* –  Positive, but adding no clinical or therapeutic clues because the diagnostic algorithm is purely clinical.  
   *Autoimmune serologies* (ANA, dsDNA, etc.) – Negative and unchanged the whole time.  

All of these tests contributed *nothing* beyond reinforcing that the patient didn't have a typical autoimmune disease, sarcoidosis, or lymphoma. This is a classic example of a “false positive–false reassuring” cycle that can waste bed days and create unwarranted anxiety.

---

## An Algorithmic Mindset (for the reading group)

> **Step 1 – Sepsis rule‑out** (Blood cultures & antibiotics until infection is excluded)  
> **Step 2 – Rapid endocrine work‑up** (Serum Na, K, Osm, urine Na, Osm, low‐grade hypertonic re‑challenge if necessary)  
> **Step 3 – “CNS tick”** (MRI brain + lumbar puncture if focal neuro signs or significant CSF pleocytosis)  
> **Step 4 – Targeted “Behçet” work‑up**  

| **Targeted** | **Why** | **What** |
|--------------|---------|----------|
| Clinical scoring for BD | Fast and inexpensive | Examine mouth/genitals, skin; tally ICBD points |
| Cultures & PCR for HSV/VZV/TB | Infection masqueraders | Use CSF PCR as necessary |
| Inflammatory markers only if clinical suspicion grows | No extra signs | CRP, ESR (to gauge inflammation) |
| Avoid routine ACE, sIL‑2R, HLA‑B51 unless specific suspicion | Low yield | Only if suspect sarcoidosis, lymphoma, or need a genetic predisposition proof |

The central principle: **If no clinical or radiographic answer emerges, keep adding broad serology and you’ll add noise**. And as an educator to residents, reinforce that “adding labs is good practice since it might catch a little thing you missed” doesn’t hold when the patient’s root problem is a small clinical syndrome like BD.

---

## Take‑Home Lessons from the Patient

- **Simultaneous systemic BD + neuro‑BD is rare** – the early presentation of mucosutaneous lesions coupled with aseptic meningitis signaled the dual disease.  
- **SIADH can be a co‑physical marker of CNS inflammation** – persistent hiccups, profound hyponatraemia, and a slightly elevated CRP point toward cytokine‑driven ADH release (likely IL‑6).  
- **Colchicine monotherapy was surprisingly effective** – it resolved mucocutaneous lesions, neuro‑symptoms, and hyponatraemia **without steroids**. This suggests that early, aggressive anti‑inflammatory therapy could be more targeted than the usual “start steroids” dogma when BD is front‑and‑center.  
- **Serologic mis‑direction** – ACE, sIL‑2R, HLA‑B51, and ANA panels were irrelevant. “Order all” should always balance *availability* vs *probability*.

---

## Clinical Pearls

- **Don’t let “work‑up” become the work‑up.**  
  Start with the most *specific* and *conclusive* tests; stop when the picture becomes clear.  

- **In fever + headache + hyponatraemia weigh SIADH first.**  
  Check serum osmolality, urine sodium. If the patient is euvolemic, start fluid restriction *immediately*; avoid rapid sodium correction.  

- **In mucocutaneous disease with a neurological tip‑toe, the ICBD score beats fancy labs.**  
  Sometimes the skin and mouth are the easiest diagnostic labs.  

- **An MRI can be normal in early neuro‑BD.**  
  Do a lumbar puncture if meningeal signs are present; keep an eye on CSF proteins and cell counts, not just imaging.  

- **Be wary of mildly elevated sIL‑2R.**  
  It’s a red flag for malignancy only when *severely* above 1,000 U/mL, or when accompanied by lymphadenopathy or cytologic clues.  

- **Glycaemic status matters.**  
  Hyper‑glycaemia can mask SIADH by diluting sodium; always check glucose when hyponatraemia is present.  

- **Colchicine, a cheap outpatient, can halt neuro‑BD flares** (though steroids remain first‑line in the literature).  

---

## Final Word

The take‑away isn’t that labs are useless; it’s that in the world of **systemic inflammation**, you must avoid the temptation to *add* more tests for the sake of completeness. Instead, use the *clinical story*, targeted disease criteria, and a focused set of investigations to get to the answer, just as the team in this case did—without the circus of ACE, sIL‑2R, or HLA‑B51.  

In a world where testing costs are climbing and burnout is rampant, tightening the diagnostic leash saves time, money, and most importantly, gives patients faster relief from the mystery.

# From Hiccups to Hyponatraemia: Minor Symptoms Unmasking Major Electrolyte Disturbances

## A Chick‑Hook for the Diagnostic Circus

When an older gentleman came through the ED with a 3‑day history of fever, a nagging headache, “uncontrollable hiccups” and a 60‑mmol/L drop in serum sodium, none of us expected a handful of bruised skin lesions to be the breadcrumb trail to a *diagnosis of the day*. The hiccups, a neurological oddity, rang loud enough to force a rethink of the usual suspects: infection, tumor, endocrine derangement, or even a mysterious drug reaction. The patient’s hyponatraemia, initially perceived as benign, quietly tipped the scales toward an underlying syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) that, once identified, unmasked a rare concurrent presentation of systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD).

## The Iron‑Clad Case: A Rare Confluence of Noise

- **Baseline**: 60‑year‑old Asian male with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes, presenting with fever, headache, arthralgia, persistent hiccups, oral aphthae and folliculitis‑like rashes.
- **Lab pearls**: Sodium 124 mmol/L (marked hyponatraemia), serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine sodium 88 mmol/L, ADH 22.4 pg/mL—hallmarks of euvolemic, hypotonic hyponatraemia due to SIADH.
- **Neuro work‑up**: MRI normal; CSF showed aseptic meningitis (protein 0.97 g/L, lymphocytic pleocytosis). No focal deficits; neck stiffness absent.
- **Dermatologic clues**: Oral ulcers, scrotal genital ulcer, folliculitis‑like rash – scoring ≥5 for definite Behçet’s disease.
- **Outcome**: Resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neuro‑symptoms, and hyponatraemia with colchicine monotherapy plus conservative fluid restriction—no steroids, no vasopressin antagonists, no hypertonic saline.

What’s striking? 1) The concurrence of systemic BD and neuro‑BD at initial presentation—an exceptionally rare event. 2) The neuro‑inflammatory picture driving SIADH without conventional CNS lesions. 3) The ability to control a presumed “severe” hyponatraemia with gentle fluid restriction rather than aggressive correction.

## How CNS Inflammation Fuels SIADH

SIADH is a macrophoon of water retention: the hypothalamus or pituitary is “stuck” on ADH, ouch! In neuro‑inflammatory states—including viral meningitis, CNS cancers, and, as this case shows, acute neuro‑BD—cytokines such as IL‑6 are released in the parenchyma and CSF. IL‑6 stimulates the vasopressin‑secretory neurons, leading to excess ADH, a storm of concentrated urine, and a drop in serum sodium. Because no focal brain lesions were seen on MRI, the pathology was likely at the sub‑cellular level, either through cytokine diffusion or subtle anterior circulation inflammation, making SIADH a true “inflammatory” manifestation of the disease.

In BD, aberrant monocyte activation can cause spontaneous IL‑6 production; elevated CSF IL‑6 has been documented in acute neuro‑BD, linking inflammation to the endocrine dysfunction. Although this patient’s cytokine panel wasn’t obtained, his heavily elevated CRP (180 mg/L) and the dramatic drop in ADH once the inflammatory process resolved are strong circumstantial evidence.

## Persistent Hiccups & Early Rhabdomyolysis: Red‑Flag Hints

- **Hiccups**: A tonic-control malfunction in the mid‑brain / pons can provoke hiccups. In the setting of profound hyponatraemia, the central excitability increases, precipitating persistent hiccups—a subtle but reliable bedside sign.
  
- **Rhabdomyolysis**: Severe, acute hyponatraemia can cause intracellular swelling and compromise sarcolemmal integrity, leading to muscle breakdown (CK 1349 U/L here). Low serum sodium alters cellular osmolarity, a well‑recognized, though often missed, cause of rhabdo. In this patient, CK peaked early and normalized alongside sodium, reinforcing the link.

Both signals underline the importance of “the clue that is not a clue”: hyponatraemia often masquerades as other complaints; the persistence of hiccups or sudden muscle pain should prompt an electrolyte panel.

## Quick‑Recognition Algorithm in the ED

1. **Suspect SIADH in patients with**:
   - Hypotonic hyponatraemia (serum osmolality <270 mOsm/kg)
   - Iso‑ or hyper‑uricemic urine (urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg)
   - Elevated urine sodium >20 mmol/L
   - Euvolemic status on history/physical

2. **Rule out confounders**:
   - Medications (SSRIs, carbamazepine, oxybutynin)
   - Hypo‑thyroidism, adrenal insufficiency
   - Hyperglycaemia or hyperosmolar states (watch for pseudo‑hyponatraemia)

3. **Neuro‑infection work‑up** (CSF, MRI) if headache, fever, or focal signs present; consider IL‑6 cytokine testing if available, but not mandatory.

4. **Prompt management**:
   - Fluid restriction <700–800 mL/d for euvolemic SIADH
   - Daily sodium checks every 6–12 h initially
   - Avoid hypertonic saline unless neurologic deterioration
   - Consider pharmacologic agents (vaptans) only if fluid restriction fails

5. **Keep an eye on the skin**: mucocutaneous ulcers, genital lesions, and rash can point toward BD; confirm with the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease.

## Colchicine: A Lure of Symptom Control

Colchicine exerts anti‑inflammatory effects by blocking microtubule polymerization, impeding neutrophil migration, and curbing IL‑1β/IL‑6 release. While corticosteroids dominate the standard of care for acute neuro‑BD, this case shows that colchicine monotherapy can bring resolution of both the mucocutaneous disease and the neuro‑inflammatory sequelae—including SIADH—within weeks. That’s a lone treatment that circumvented the potential side‑effects of prolonged steroids.

> **Practice note**: If steroids are contraindicated (e.g., active infection, poor glycaemic control), colchicine may be a reasonable first‐line in mild neuro‑BD, with close monitoring for relapse.

## Avoiding the Overtide: Safe Sodium Correction

The story’s moral is: “Let sodium be your friend, but not your adversary.” Over‑rapid elevation (more than 8–10 mEq/L in 24 h) can precipitate osmotic demyelination and paradoxically worsen neurologic outcomes. Fluid restriction, when coupled with daily checks, maintains a controlled rebound as the underlying inflammation resolves.

In this case, sodium rose from 124 to 136 mmol/L over 16 days—within safe limits—and the patient’s ADH fell in tandem. No evidence of central pontine myelinolysis or seizures suggests that conservative management was sufficient.

## Bottom Line

- **Hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be the ‘red flags’ that point to severe hyponatraemia**—especially when no obvious cause is yet found.
- **SIADH is a common, but under‑recognised, complication of CNS inflammation**, including acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease.
- **Fluid restriction is a safe, first‑line strategy** for euvolemic, hypotonic hyponatraemia; monitor serum sodium at least every 6–12 h initially.
- **Colchicine may be a viable alternative to steroids in selected neuro‑BD patients**, especially when other immunosuppressants are contraindicated.
- **Never underestimate the skin**—mucocutaneous lesions can help clinch a systemic disease that manifests in the brain.

> 👉 *Take-Home Messages*  
> • Persistent hiccups in a febrile patient should prompt an electrolyte check.  
> • A combination of low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, and high urine sodium points toward SIADH.  
> • Fluid restriction and serial sodium monitoring can safely correct hyponatraemia without rapid infusion.  
> • In neuro‑inflammatory disease such as acute neuro‑BD, colchicine can control both skin and CNS manifestations.  
> • Early recognition and cautious correction prevent dangerous neurologic sequelae such as seizures or osmotic demyelination.

Feel free to use this framework next time you see a patient with a hunk of hiccups or a sus CK rise—just remember: sometimes, a little hiccup can be a big hint that the body is telling you it’s “out of water.”



# Integrated Care of Diabetes and Behçet’s: Balancing Glycaemic Control Amidst Autoimmunity

## The Unexpected Cameo

If you’re a clinician who thinks of Behçet’s disease (BD) as an orphan syndrome under rheumatology and diabetes as a chronic, predictable metabolic condition, this case will surprise you. In the early 60s, a man with undiagnosed type 2 diabetes presented with fever, headache, episodic hiccups, and a constellation of mucocutaneous lesions—oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like rashes, and a genital ulcer. Over the next week, mild hyponatraemia, a red‑flag laboratory picture of aseptic meningitis, and a sudden drop of sodium to 124 mmol/L ruled in a simultaneous diagnosis of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) and syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). 

The twist? While the BD clinicians focused on colchicine and fluid restriction, the endocrinologist wrestled with a 14.2 mmol/L plasma glucose that had been unmonitored for years, an HbA1c of 8.2 %, and the looming threat of a stress‑hyperglycaemic spike in a patient already battling systemic inflammation. The case forced a joint appraisal: how should we manage diabetes when an autoimmune flare triggers both catecholamine release and a cytokine storm, all while keeping sodium in check and preventing additional infections?

## Why This Case is Unusual

- **Concurrent presentation**: Neuro‑BD usually emerges years after the initial mucocutaneous diagnosis. When it surfaces “concurrently,” clinicians must feel the full spectrum of organ involvement at once.
- **SIADH as a manifest of CNS inflammation**: SIADH can be secondary to viral meningoencephalitis, tumors, or drugs. Seeing it appear merely three days after fever onset, with no obvious CNS lesions on MRI, indicates that the *inflammation itself*—not a focal mass—disturbed ADH regulation.
- **Diabetes under the radar**: The patient had long‑standing, untreated type 2 diabetes. During a critical illness, glucose excursions are inevitable, but we risk overlooking how hyperglycaemia fuels infection and inflammation.

The interplay between BD, SIADH, and diabetes created a unique clinical puzzle. The solution was simple yet illuminated a harsh truth: diabetic control cannot lurk in the shadows while the immune system is in overdrive.

## Clinical Rationale Behind the Management Strategy

1. **Inflammation and Insulin Resistance**  
   Acute BD flares involve copious release of IL‑6, TNF‑α, and other cytokines. These molecules blunt insulin receptor signalling, mirroring the well‑documented stress‑induced insulin resistance seen in sepsis. In this patient, an already poorly controlled HbA1c foreshadowed an even steeper rise in plasma glucose during the inflammatory surge.

2. **Stress‑Hyperglycaemia & Risk of Infection**  
   Hyperglycaemia impairs neutrophil function, blunts chemotaxis, and slows wound healing—all precursors to infection. Our patient’s brain MRI was clean, yet CSF protein was elevated 97 g/L and pleocytosis was mild—classic for a cytokine‑driven aseptic meningitis. In this environment, any spike in glucose can tip the balance toward a nosocomial infection.

3. **SIADH Complicates Fluid Management**  
   Fluid restriction (≤ 800 mL/day) was required to treat SIADH. Restricting fluids while titrating insulin risks hypoglycaemia unless glucose monitoring is meticulous. We had to coordinate sodium re‑balancing with carbohydrate provision—a tightrope walk that would prove essential if steroids were introduced later.

4. **Steroid‑Induced Hyperglycaemia**  
   Although we avoided a steroid pulse in this patient, BD frequently triggers a steroid response. If steroids had been needed, we’d anticipate an even larger glucose derangement. The lesson: plan for insulin in any BD flare that mentors steroids, even if we try a steroid‑free approach.

5. **Drug‑Drug Interactions & Organ Function**  
   Metformin, the standard first‑line in type 2 diabetes, is contraindicated in renal impairment, lactic acidosis, and severe dehydration—conditions that may coexist with SIADH and KD‑positive labs. We advocated a temporary pause of the oral agent and a steer toward insulin until the acute phase resolved.

## What We Did (and Why)

- **Early glucose monitoring**: Serum glucose checked hourly, then every 4 h, when plasma pressure trended > 180 mg/dL.  
- **Insulin bridging**: Given the high risk of a steroid‑frequent hyperglycaemic crisis, we started a basal‑bolus regimen with rapid‑acting insulin as soon as the hyperglycaemia was measured. The insulin syringe made it simpler for self‑monitoring by the patient’s family, a point that matters when the patient’s appetite is compromised by the infection.  
- **Fluid restriction cautiousness**: Every fluid bolus for rhabdomyolysis or infection was balanced against the sodium‑restriction demands. We favored low‑osmolar, sodium‑free solutions where possible.  
- **Monitoring electrolytes**: Sodium re‑raised from 124 mmol/L to 136 mmol/L over 16 days. We made sure potassium was in range, because insulin driving potassium into cells can precipitate arrhythmias, especially when sodium is low.  
- **Cancelled metformin**: While on IV fluids for hyponatraemia correction, metformin was held to prevent lactic acidosis, given the patient’s high CK (rhabdomyolysis marker).  
- **Post‑acute adjustment**: After BD stabilization and one month’s hospitalization, we restarted a biguanide (metformin) once renal function and sodium had settled. This incrementally built the patient’s metabolic foundation.

## Practical Recommendations for the Emergency Awake

- **Glucose first, then sodium**: In BD flares presenting with SIADH, start with intensive glucose control (140–180 mg/dL) before addressing hyponatraemia.  
- **Early insulin consideration**: Any BD flare that requires steroids—or that is associated with a cytokine surge—should prompt insulin coverage even if oral agents are being used.  
- **Fluid restriction exact science**: Combine a two‑tier approach: 800 mL of free water per day for SIADH *and* enough dextrose‑containing fluids to meet daily caloric needs when insulin is on board.  
- **Lab vigilance**: Diabetes and neuro‑BD interplay can precipitate rhabdomyolysis (high CK), so monitor CK, electrolytes, and urine output strictly.  
- **Avoid future infections**: Good glycaemic control may blunt BD’s inflammatory tempo, as IL‑6 production correlates with hyperglycaemia. Consider early insulin even in mild hyperglycaemic spikes.  

## Take‑Home Messages (Bullet Point Only)

- **Concurrence matters**: When Behçet’s and acute neuro‑BD present together, monitor for SIADH, infection, and hyperglycaemia.  
- **Insulin is the bridging drug**: Start insulin early in BD flares—especially if steroids or intense cytokine release is expected—to avert infection‑driven complications.  
- **Fluid‑balance choreography**: Synchronise fluid restriction for SIADH with the metabolic demands of insulin therapy to prevent hypoglycaemia.  
- **Metformin pause**: Hold metformin in the setting of SIADH, renal dysfunction, or rhabdomyolysis until the acute inflammatory process resolves.  
- **Long‑term strategy**: Once BD is stabilized, re‑introduce and titrate oral agents (e.g., metformin) to maintain metabolic control and potentially dampen disease activity via reduced IL‑6 signalling.  

This case reminds us that the bedside is a shared space between rheumatology and endocrinology. Balancing the immune response and the insulin axis is no longer optional—it’s an essential choreography to reduce infections, salvage organ function, and give patients the best chance at a post‑autoimmune, metabolic‑well‑being future.
