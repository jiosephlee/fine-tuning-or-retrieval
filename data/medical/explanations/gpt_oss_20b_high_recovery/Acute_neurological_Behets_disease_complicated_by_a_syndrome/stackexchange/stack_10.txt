Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiological relationship between severe hyponatraemia and rhabdomyolysis, and outline how clinicians should monitor and manage this complication.

**Pathophysiological link between severe hyponatraemia and rhabdomyolysis**

*Low extracellular osmolality* (here, serum Na⁺ = 124 mmol/L) creates a gradient that draws water into the cytoplasm of all cells, including skeletal muscle fibres.  
• The resulting *cellular swelling* disrupts the sarcolemma, causing leakage of intracellular Ca²⁺.  
• Intracellular Ca²⁺ activates calcium‑dependent proteases and phospholipases, which damage the contractile apparatus and precipitate myocyte necrosis.  
• In addition, the heightened inflammatory milieu of acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (e.g., IL‑6–mediated cytokine storm) can impair mitochondrial function and amplify oxidative stress, rendering muscle cells even more vulnerable to osmotic injury.  
Thus, profound hyponatraemia can precipitate rhabdomyolysis by a direct osmotic mechanism, a phenomenon seen in the case where CK rose to 1 349 U/L alongside the low sodium.

**Monitoring strategy**

| Parameter | Timing | Rationale |
|-----------|--------|-----------|
| Serum Na⁺, plasma osmolality | q4‑6 h while Na⁺ < 120 mmol/L | Detect rapid correction or progression |
| Serum creatinine, BUN, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺ | q12‑24 h | Early detection of renal injury or electrolyte derangements |
| Serum CK | q12‑24 h until ↓ 30–50 % and < 1 000 U/L | Gauge extent of muscle injury |
| Urine output | hourly | Ensure ≥ 1 mL/kg/h to flush myoglobin |
| Urine colour & osmolality | Daily | Myoglobinuria → dark urine, low urine osmolality |
| Neurologic status (seizure, altered mental status) | Continuous | Hyponatraemic seizures can trigger secondary rhabdomyolysis |

In the reported case, serial monitoring of Na⁺, CK and renal function confirmed a gradual rise in sodium (to 136 mmol/L over 16 days) and a return of CK toward normal, without renal impairment.

**Clinical management**

1. **Hyponatraemia**  
   *Fluid restriction* (≤ 800 mL/day for euvolemic patients) is first‑line, as employed in this case.  
   *If severe symptoms* (seizure, coma) develop, a *low‑volume hypertonic saline* bolus may be used, but the rate of correction must not exceed 8–12 mmol/L in 24 h to avoid osmotic demyelination.  
   *Treat the underlying cause* – in this case colchicine suppressed CNS inflammation, allowing ADH secretion to normalize.

2. **Rhabdomyolysis**  
   *Isotonic saline* (200–250 mL h⁻¹) or *induced diuresis* (≥ 200 mL h⁻¹) may be given if urinary output is < 0.5 mL/kg/h or if CK is > 10 000 U/L; in moderate myoglobinuria (CK ≈ 1 300 U/L) aggressive fluid therapy is optional.  
   *Urine alkalinization* (bicarbonate 50 mEq L⁻¹) is reserved for markedly acidic urine (< 6.0) to prevent tubular precipitation of myoglobin.  
   *Avoid nephrotoxic agents* (NSAIDs, radiocontrast).  
   *Correct secondary electrolyte derangements* – hyperkalaemia, hypocalcaemia, or hypomagnesemia – as they can aggravate muscle injury or precipitate arrhythmias.

3. **Adjunctive care**  
   *Monitor for acute kidney injury* and consider dialysis if creatinine rises or volume overload occurs.  
   *Maintain balanced nutrition* and correct hypovolemia cautiously.  
   *Document all fluid input/output* and serial electrolyte values to guide titration.

In the presented patient, a conservative approach of fluid restriction combined with close lab surveillance appropriately resolved both the hyponatraemia and the rhabdomyolysis without the need for hypertonic saline or aggressive fluid resuscitation. The underlying inflammatory disease was controlled with colchicine, illustrating that managing the root cause can attenuate both SIADH and secondary muscle injury.

**Take‑home message** – In any patient with severe hyponatraemia, especially when SIADH is secondary to CNS inflammation, always screen for rhabdomyolysis (CK, urine colour). Monitor sodium and CK closely; correct sodium slowly while providing adequate renal perfusion to prevent myoglobin‑induced nephropathy. Treat the underlying trigger (e.g., inflammatory disease) to halt the vicious cycle of hyponatraemia and muscle breakdown.
