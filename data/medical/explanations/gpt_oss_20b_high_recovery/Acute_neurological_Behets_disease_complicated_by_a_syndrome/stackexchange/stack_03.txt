Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Among the various causes of euvolemic hyponatraemia, which laboratory criteria distinguish SIADH, and why does this patient meet those criteria?

**Answer**

In euvolemic hyponatraemia the most useful laboratory triad that pins down SIADH is:

1. **Low serum osmolality** (≤ 275 mOsm/kg)  
2. **Inappropriately concentrated urine** (urine osmolality > 100 mOsm/kg, often > 300–400, indicating the kidneys are not excreting free water)  
3. **High urinary sodium** (≥ 20–30 mmol/L, usually > 40 mmol/L) with no renal or adrenal cause

These criteria reflect the fundamental pathophysiology: ADH is secreted despite hypotonic plasma, causing renal water reabsorption, concentrated urine, and sodium excretion because the kidneys are free‑water overloaded.

**Why the patient satisfies SIADH**

| Criterion | Case‑specific findings |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Serum osmolality | 267 mOsm/kg (low) |
| Urine osmolality | 900 mOsm/kg (very high) |
| Urine sodium | 88 mmol/L (high) |
| Volume status | Clinically euvolemic (no edema, normal BP, no oliguria) |
| ADH level | 22.4 pg/mL (elevated) |
| Principals excluded | Normal thyroid, cortisol, renal function; no diuretics; no heart/failure signs |

The markedly low plasma osmolality with a concentrated, sodium‑rich urine demonstrates that the kidneys are not appropriately diluting the urine in the setting of euvolemia. The elevated ADH level confirms that the renal concentrating stimulus is centrally driven. Normal adrenal and thyroid axes rule out adrenal/thyroid disease, and the absence of diuretics or volume overload eliminates other causes. Therefore, the laboratory profile satisfies the diagnostic definition of SIADH and explains the patient’s profound hyponatraemia.
