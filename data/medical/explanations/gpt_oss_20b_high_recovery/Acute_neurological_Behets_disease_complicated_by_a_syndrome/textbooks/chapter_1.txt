Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 1: ## Case Presentation & Initial Clinical Assessment

An Asian man in his 60 s was admitted to the emergency department after a 3‑day history of fever, headache, general malaise, loss of appetite, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia affecting the shoulders and ankles. He had an unremarkable occupational history (metalworking) and no recent travel, exposure to animals, or known infections. 
His medical history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, for which he had not been receiving treatment; his glycaemic status at presentation was abnormal (plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L, HbA1c 8.2 %). No other chronic illnesses were recorded and he was otherwise healthy.

On examination he was alert but fatigued. Vital signs demonstrated a temperature of 38.1 °C, blood pressure 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate 119 bpm (regular), respiratory rate 19/min, and oxygen saturation 96 % on room air. The patient was euvolemic: there were no peripheral oedema, jugular venous distension, or third‑degree clubbing. Digital examination revealed multiple oral aphthous ulcers and facial and truncal folliculitis‑like eruptions (figure 1A–C). No focal neurological deficits or cranial nerve palsies were evident. Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were reassuring, and muscle strength was intact in all extremities.

Initial laboratory investigations identified a striking electrolyte disturbance: serum sodium was 124 mmol/L, indicating severe hyponatraemia. Total serum osmolality was 267 mOsm/kg (low), and urinary osmolality was 900 mOsm/kg (high). Urinary sodium was markedly increased at 88 mmol/L. Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) was significantly elevated at 22.4 pg/mL. These findings satisfy the classic laboratory triad for the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH): hypo‑osmolar hyponatraemia, concentrated urine (high osmolality), and high urinary sodium while the patient remains euvolemic. 
Other pertinent labs revealed an inflammatory picture: C‑reactive protein (CRP) 180 mg/L and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 70 mm/h. Musculoskeletal enzymes were also strongly elevated: creatine kinase 1,349 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 77 U/L, and alanine aminotransferase 64 U/L. Renal function was preserved (serum creatinine and BUN within reference limits). Endocrine assessment found a normal thyroid‑stimulating hormone, free T4, and serum cortisol, ruling out hypothalamic‑pituitary‑adrenal axis dysfunction.

Baseline imaging consisted of a non‑contrast computed tomography (CT) scan of the head, as well as CT of the chest and abdomen, all of which were unremarkable; no intracranial mass, hydrocephalus, pulmonary infiltrates, or abdominal pathology could be identified. An abdominal chest X‑ray also showed no acute findings.

In light of the fever, neurological symptoms, and laboratory evidence of systemic inflammation and severe hyponatraemia, the initial working diagnosis encompassed a broad differential that included infectious meningitis (bacterial, viral, or tuberculous), other causes of aseptic meningitis, systemic inflammatory disorders, and drug‑induced SIADH. The combination of a clear inflammatory marker profile, a presumed cerebral focus due to the neurological complaints, and the biochemical picture of SIADH directed early empiric antimicrobial therapy (cefepime and acyclovir) while awaiting definitive diagnostic work‑up. 

This initial clinical assessment set the stage for a focused investigation of the underlying etiology of the neurological illness and the associated endocrine disturbance.
