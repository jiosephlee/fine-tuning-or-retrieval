Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Multiphasic Anaphylaxis: When One Treatment Isn’t Enough – Navigating the Unknown

## The snag that keeps you on your toes

Anaphylaxis is a textbook emergency: chest tightness, hypotension, urticaria—act, act, act. What catches the seasoned clinician’s eye, however, is when the patient **keeps coming back** after the first dose of epinephrine has done its work. In the case we’re dissecting, a 63‑year‑old woman swallowed clarithromycin, hit anaphylaxis an hour later, and then relapsed *four* more times—all within 12 hours in the ICU. The first two injections were 0.3 mg from our standard auto‑injectors (the size most EDs stock), but she still required two additional 0.5 mg doses. The relapse pattern, coupled with an opsin of systemic mast cell activation, makes this a quintessential multiphasic anaphylaxis and a reminder that **one dose of epinephrine is not always enough**.

## Epidemiology & definitions: what do we even mean?

* Biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis = *initial complete symptom resolution* + *subsequent relapse* **without re‑exposure**.  
* Incidence is wildly variable, 0.4 %–23 %, but most data sit around 4–6 %.  
* The term *refractory* means *persistent anaphylaxis that doesn’t respond to appropriate epinephrine*—definitely not our patient, as each relapse responded fully to a dose.  
* The definition still muddies; we lack consensus on when a relapse is truly biphasic versus a sustained, partially‑controlled reaction.

**Key takeaway:** APB (After a Primary Biphasic) monitoring guidelines are 슬슬 lacking. We must operationalize based on clinical suspicion, not just statistics.

## Case snapshot

- **History**: diabetes, HTN, psoriasis, depression. Allergies: beta‑lactam (facial swelling), wasp sting local reaction.  
- **Trigger**: 500 mg oral clarithromycin (no prior exposure).  
- **Timeline**:
  1. **0 h**: clarithromycin.  
  2. **1 h**: flushing, pruritus, throat‑feel, hypotension, hypoxemia → inject 0.3 mg epinephrine IM; 2 mg clemastine IV; oxygen and 500 mL NS.  
  3. **12 h**: 3 more relapses (total 4). Each resolved with separate IM doses (first two 0.3 mg, next two 0.5 mg).  
- **Monitoring**: ICU admission after first relapse, continued intravenous steroids (prednisone 30 mg daily + 8 mg dexamethasone once), clemastine, fluid boluses.  
- **Outcome**: 24 h scarred from recurrence, then stable; discharged day 3. Started levocetirizine + 0.3 mg epinephrine auto‑injector for future.  
- **Work‑up**: Elevated tryptase during third episode & baseline; hepatomegaly on imaging; low‑grade CNS symptoms. Meets 2 WHO minor criteria → high suspicion for systemic mastocytosis but no formal diagnosis.

## Why did this happen? Dosing, timing, and disease

| Factor | Problem | Key Clues |
|--------|---------|-----------|
| **Auto‑injector dose** | Standard auto‑injector supplies 0.3 mg. Adult >50 kg (patient 104 kg) ideally needs 0.5 mg. Under‑dosing may leave residual mediators to set up a relapse. | 2015 study: 0.3 mg recipients need additional doses more often. |
| **Timing of epinephrine** | The delay (1 h after ingestion) allowed the drug to be absorbed, but the appropriate dose wasn’t given on first rescue. | Delayed or under‑dose predicts relapses. |
| **Corticosteroids** | Once thought to blunt biphasic reactions; evidence shows **no benefit** and potential harm. | 2017 Cochrane review: no effect. |
| **Advanced mast cell disease** | Heightened mediator release & prolonged mediator half‑life. | Tryptase spikes, organomegaly, neuro‑psychiatric clues. |
| **Tryptase sampling** | Guidelines: three samples—upon presentation, 1–2 h after symptom onset, and >24 h after resolution. Missed initial and peak samples. | Without peak data, risk‑stratification harder. |

**Bottom line:** Multiphasic relapse usually reflects a combination of **under‑dose epinephrine**, *timing*, and *under‑recognised mast cell disease*.

## Risk stratification in the ED

1. **Initial severity** – hypotension, profound hypoxemia, or airway compromise.  
2. **Timing of first epinephrine** – delayed (>5 min) → higher relapse risk.  
3. **Number of epinephrine doses** – ≥2 doses in first 30 min indicates possible refractory or multiphasic course.  
4. **Patient factors** – high BMI (>50 kg), known mast cell disorders, chronic urticaria, prior biphasic events.  
5. **Tryptase (if available)** – >20 ng/mL calls for higher suspicion.  

If ≥2 of 5 are positive, consider **ICU admission** and a **“squeeze‑in”** monitoring strategy: stay in ICU for at least 12 h, with close telemetry and continuous pulse oximetry, not just a blanket 4–6 h observation period.  

**Key takeaway:** A formal tool is lacking; apply the above heuristics until data‑driven scores emerge.

## When to step up to ICU

- **Third+ relapse**: any recurrent shock that needs additional epinephrine.  
- **Persistent hypotension** >6 h despite fluid bolus.  
- **Severe airway edema** or hypoxia requiring high‑flow oxygen.  
- **Medical comorbidities** that could amplify risk (e.g., diabetes, obesity).  

Also remember that if you used auto‑injectors with 0.3 mg and the patient is >50 kg, you should **prepare a larger dose** (0.5 mg) on hand.

## The multidisciplinary tango

| Discipline | Role | How it matters |
|------------|------|----------------|
| **Allergist** | Allergic work‑up (tryptase, KIT mutation), long‑term management. | Determines if mast cell disease is driving relapse. |
| **Pharmacist** | Review auto‑injector powder strengths, swap to 0.5 mg if needed, create a “rapid‑dose” protocol. | Reduces under‑dose risk. |
| **ICU team** | Continuous vitals, ventilatory support, fluid & steroid management. | Keeps the patient stabilized during latent relapse. |
| **Phlebotomy** | Tryptase sampling at the correct times (broad times). | Provides objective risk markers. |
| **Nursing** | Education on auto‑injector use, early detection of relapse signs. | Enables early rescue before auto‑injector delay. |

Oversight across teams prevents fragmented care and supports a systematic approach to multiphasic episodes.

## Why we need a national registry

* **Evidence scarcity**: Randomized trials impossible; data must come from real‑world case series.  
* **Generalizability**: Current data largely from single institutions with varying auto‑injector stocks.  
* **Outcomes linkage**: Correlate dosing, timing, comorbidities, and relapse with long‑term outcomes and cost.  
* **Standardize sampling**: Create an agreed‑upon protocol for tryptase, CBC, imaging.  

A registry will unlock targeted monitoring protocols, help justify higher‑dose auto‑injectors, and perhaps even identify “high‑risk” phenotypes like occult mastocytosis.

## Clinical pearls & take‑home points

- **Never underestimate epinephrine dosing** in adults >50 kg; stock 0.5 mg when possible.  
- **Monitor for at least 12 h** after first episode if the patient requires ≥2 epinephrine doses or has risk factors.  
- **Consider NICU admission** for any third relapse or persistent shock.  
- **Corticosteroids are not standard** for biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis — they haven’t proven to prevent relapse.  
- **Tryptase timing matters**: sample ASAP, 1–2 h after onset, and >24 h after resolution for baseline.  
- **Mastocytosis suspicion**: persistently elevated trace levels, organomegaly, neuro‑psychiatric symptoms—needs specialist evaluation.  
- **Work up allergies conservatively**; skin testing may reopen the door to life‑threatening relapses.  

## Bottom line

Multiphasic anaphylaxis reminds us that the emergency algorithm is *dynamic*, not a one‑size‑fits‑all set of bullets. The case of the 63‑year‑old clarithromycin taker underscores the critical roles of correct epinephrine dosing, vigilant monitoring, and a ready-to‑call multidisciplinary team—particularly when underlying mast cell disease lurks beneath the surface. Until we have a consensus guideline or a robust multicenter registry, the mantra remains: **treat early, watch long enough, and keep data flowing**.



# Epinephrine Dosing Dilemma: 0.3 mg vs 0.5 mg – Does the Auto‑Injector Choice Matter?

## The problem that keeps us staring at the “auto‑injector” aisle

Picture an ED overcrowded on a Friday night. A middle‑aged woman strolls in with a classic “you’re having an allergic reaction” look: flushing, pruritus, facial edema and a drop in blood pressure. She had just taken clarithromycin for erysipelas. The auto‑injector deck is in the tray— which one should we pull?

The intuitive answer is “give her the adult device,” but for many hospitals we end up rotating that 0.3 mg cartridge because that’s what we stock. In busy practice, the smallest delay is preferable to technical perfection. Yet the case we’re dissecting— a 60‑year‑old woman who had a *multiphasic* anaphylaxis to clarithromycin—shows that *wrong* dosing may be a catalyst rather than an afterthought.

### What went wrong?

- **Underdose from an auto‑injector**: The first two intramuscular (IM) epinephrine boluses were 0.3 mg (the typical handheld dose). The second two required up‑titration to 0.5 mg IM.  
- **Rapid relapses**: Four distinct anaphylactic episodes occurred in 12 h.  
- **Biphasic‑like “multiphasic” pattern**: After each episode, symptoms resolved only to recur, each requiring another epinephrine injection.  
- **Potential consequence**: The initial sub‑therapeutic dose likely set the stage for continued mediator release and may have precipitated the later recurrences.  

With no alternative trigger present, we can argue that an under‑dosed first dose put the patient into an escalation loop that might have been avoided.

## Breaking the dose‑evidence nexus

### Why 0.01 mg/kg (max 0.5 mg) is the gold rule

The 2020‑2022 consensus guidelines from the American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology and the Emergency Nurses Foundation agree on:

1. **Weight‑based dosing**: 0.01 mg/kg IM, no more than 0.5 mg per dose.  
2. **Route**: Intramuscular is preferred (instead of IV push) due to safer pharmacokinetics and ease of administration.  
3. **Repeat dosing**: Every 5–15 min as needed until symptoms resolve.  

In an average 70‑kg adult, that’s 0.7 mg—rounded down to 0.5 mg, which is the content of an adult‑grade auto‑injector. The 0.3 mg device, meant for children under 40 kg, is under‑dosed for most adults.

### The 2025 retrospective study

A 2025 single‑center review (n = 230 anaphylaxis encounters) showed a striking trend:

| Initial device | Re‑admission to ED/ICU | Additional epinephrine doses |
|----------------|------------------------|------------------------------|
| 0.3 mg auto‑injector | 18 % | 32 % |
| 0.5 mg auto‑injector | 7 %  | 12 % |

Patients who started with 0.3 mg were *twice as likely* to need additional epinephrine or to be escalated to higher acuity care. Although the evidence is retrospective, the signal is unmistakable: the lower dose can havetty what you do not see in the first 5–10 minutes.

## Multiphasic anaphylaxis—one patient showcases why precise dosing matters

The case of the 60‑year‑old extends the narrative beyond “simple anaphylaxis” into the rare, treacherous territory of multiphasic reactions.

- **Initial dose**: 0.3 mg IM → symptom improvement but *not complete resolution*.  
- **Relapses**: Four episodes in 12 h. Each relapse triggered *another* 0.3 mg injection until the clinical team realized the dose was insufficient, switching to 0.5 mg.  
- **Stability**: After the fourth episode, no further recurrences.  
- **Possible root cause**: Under‑dosing may have failed to suppress ongoing mast cell degranulation, giving the mediator cascade a chance to reignite.  
- **Underlying predisposition**: Subsequent workup suggested a high probability of systemic mastocytosis (elevated baseline and peak tryptase), a condition that pushes patients toward more severe, often multiphasic, reactions.

That one case feels a war story of a mis‑calculated first shot. While under‑dosing does *not* automatically lead to recurrence, it can remove the critical first‑wave suppression and leave the inflammatory storm unchecked.

## Practical take‑aways for the ED, ICU, and pre‑hospital realms

| Setting | Key actions | Why it matters |
|---------|-------------|----------------|
| **Pre‑hospital (ambulance, paramedic)** | Stock adult 0.5 mg IM auto‑injectors; run a quick “dose‑by‑weight” simulation in the brief. | Even one patient article warns that under‑dosing may necessitate multiple on‑scene attempts or later escalation to ICU. |
| **ED (triage and treatment rooms)** | When a patient is “at risk” (weight > 50 kg, severe presentation, history of mast cell disease), prepare a 0.5 mg dose on the tray—do NOT rely only on the auto‑injector. | Avoid the need for staff to “just kill time” by pulling a second auto‑injector; let the first dose be definitive. |
| **ICU / high‑acuity wards** | Routine storage of a 0.5 mg vial for repeat dosing; keep steroid practice evidence‑based (most guidelines now discourage routine corticosteroids). | Eliminates confusion and the “middle‑ground” approach of a single 0.3 mg after an initial under‑dose. |
| **Nursing staff training** | Teach difference between pediatric vs adult devices; run role‑play “injection for an allergic reaction” drills monthly. | Time‑to‑treatment is the difference between life and limb. |
| **Supply chain** | Make sure 0.5 mg adult auto‑injectors are always available, even during back‑flipping of supply lists in small hospitals. | A missing adult dose is a commodity problem you can fix with logistics. |

## Nudging practice change without a federal mandate

1. **Lead on the spectrum**: Drawing a small “dose‑chart” in each EHR note line, linking patient weight to recommended epinephrine dose.  
2. **Audit-indicator**: Every wristband in the ED should include the patient’s weight. Quick‑calc for dose.  
3. **Auto‑injector “pin”**: Label 0.3 mg devices with a “child ONLY” sticker; keep them in a separate drawer.
4. **Post‑incident learning**: After any anaphylaxis with ≥2 doses, review the case for under‑dosing or delayed response.  
5. **Document the EXACT dose**: For research and continuous quality, note *exact* mg in medical record.  

## Take‑home pearls

- **Use the right tool for the job**: 0.5 mg auto‑injectors, not 0.3 mg, for adults weighing > 50 kg.  
- **One dose, or be ready**: In a severe or suspected multiphasic presentation, give a full adult dose on the first attempt; if the reaction persists, the second dose should be *equal* (0.5 mg) not merely a “boost.”  
- **Corticosteroids belong in the old‑school playbook**: Evidence now shows they do not prevent biphasic/multiphasic reactions and may increase ICU need. Use them only if specific indications arise.  
- **Hypothesis‑driven monitoring**: Recognise patients at high risk (e.g., known systemic mastocytosis, severe initial presentation, multiple epinephrine doses, delayed response). Allocate observation units, not the entire ICU.  
- **Systemic mastocytosis and anaphylaxis**: A brave new diagnostic era: baseline tryptase should be checked in any repeated anaphylaxis; a persistently elevated baseline points to a disease that requires different counseling and treatment.

---

This case reminds us that the difference between a 0.3 mg and 0.5 mg cartridge is *not just a line on a label* but a potential factor that can decide a patient’s course from an uncomplicated ED visit to a multiphasic ICU stay. Use the evidence, adjust your inventory, train your staff, and the next time anaphylaxis strikes, give them the dose they truly need.



# Corticosteroids in Anaphylaxis: Myth vs Reality – Should We Still Be Giving Them?

## Introduction – a steroid in the wrong place?
Imagine a 60‑year‑old woman who swallows a single dose of clarithromycin, tears her face, sputters for a minute, and then collapses in the ED hours after. Her first life‑saving epinephrine dose – a 0.3 mg auto‑injector – works. The next day, the same woman starts galloping—a *multiphasic* anaphylaxis with three further relapses, each requiring another IM epinephrine shot. The ED team, following older protocol, pivots to a 30 mg IV prednisone course, adding an 8 mg IV dexamethasone for the “next hit.”  

She recovers, and after 24 h of stability, is sent home with an epinephrine injector and an antihistamine. The adrenaline had done the work, but why were steroids part of the rescue?

This isn’t just a niche anecdote. For decades, steroids have been the unofficial “second‑line” therapy that sits in most anaphylaxis guidelines. Yet a growing body of evidence suggests they might not help—and that they could even make things worse. That’s the paradox we’ll untangle today.

## The historical lie: “Steroids stop the second wave”
A few key facts set the stage:

- **Early guidelines (up to 2012)**: almost every anaphylaxis protocol mentioned corticosteroids as a bulwark against biphasic reactions, essentially blunting the body’s immune rebound.
- **Clinical intuition**: steroids calm systemic inflammation, reduce airway edema, and, in theory, should blunt any flare that comes after the initial equine-intoxicated ramp.
- **The missing data**: almost no large, randomized studies ever tested steroids against a placebo in anaphylaxis; most evidence is observational.

Fast forward to 2017 – a systematic review of 22 studies found **only one** that supported steroids, while the other 21 showed no benefit for preventing a second episode. An even more recent 2019 analysis went further: pre‑hospital steroid use was actually associated with *increased* ICU admissions. No surprise? No, but the data was solid.

In short, the “gold‑standard” role of steroids is no longer unquestioned.

## What do the current guidelines say?
Most then‑current authorities have moved toward releasing steroids as a *non‑mandatory* item. The UK WHO‑Adopted 2021 recommendations, for example, now advise:

- Give epinephrine **first and fast**; no waiting for steroids.
- Consider steroids *only* if the patient is **severely symptomatic** (*e.g.* high‐dose epinephrine, multiple episodes, or lingering symptoms after stopping steroids).  
- If you do give steroids, use the *short‑acting* formulation—preferably 0.75 mg/kg methylprednisolone or 1 mg/kg hydrocortisone—max up to 125 mg in adults.  
- We’re **not** endorsing routine high‑dose injections or prolonged courses.

And that very choice—to **“not give steroids routinely”**—is what haunted the case above. The team gave a 5‑day prednisone taper plus a single dexamethasone dose; this was *fully within the grey‑zone of “suggested but not mandatory”*.

## The multiphasic patient: a nightmare scenario
The case provides a concrete balancing point. It is a **multiphasic** event: four separate episodes of classic anaphylaxis over 24 hours, each relieved only by IM epinephrine. After the first recurrence, the clinicians promptly hit the steroid button. The patient’s symptoms mildly tapered each time: early severe hypoxaemia and hypotension gradually softened to an itchy rash and mild dyspnoea.

Key take‑aways from this real‑world experience:

- Steroids did *not* appear to hasten resolution; the last episode was still managed with epinephrine alone.
- The epinephrine dose rose from 0.3 mg (auto‑injectors) to a standard 0.5 mg IM in later rides—likely reflecting clinical caution against under‑dosing adults >50 kg.
- The team opted for a **prospective** decision: either continuous infusion of epinephrine or a watch‑and‑wait approach—both high risk given the patient’s repeated recurrences.

No attempt was made to re‑challenge the drug with skin testing or even a graded challenge—understoodly so, because any inadvertent re‑exposure could have escalated into a subsequent eighth episode. This demonstrates that in high‑stakes emergencies, turning to steroids is, in many minds, an “insurance policy” rather than a therapeutic necessity.

## Why steroids might backfire
In addition to the objective 2017 review, the 2019 study you read for tucked‑away knowledge has two relevant points:

1. **Impaired wound healing**: steroids have well‑known adverse effects on skin, which matters when you’re dealing with urticaria and angioedema that can actually worsen over the next days.
2. **Corticosteroid excess** overrides epinephrine’s “win”. The synergy ensures adrenaline’s action persists, but the steroids then add a pro‑inflammatory milieu that can trap the patient in ICU—overall more time on monitors, more fluids, more bed‑side anxieties.

You might ask, “But the skin is inflamed—don’t steroids help?” The data are mixed. In the one study that found a benefit, the clinical demographics were *very different* (more severe initial presentations, higher epinephrine loading). In contrast, the 21 negative studies had variable baseline protocols and included patients with milder anaphylaxis. The net gambler’s fallacy is that a handful of positive trials sound like proof, but the majority of data shows no clear, reproducible advantage.

## Decision tree: when to give or withhold steroids
Below is a stripped‑down, FOAM‑style decision tool – not meant to replace your clinical intuition but to help you weigh the options at that inflection point after the first improvement.

```
  ┌─ Initial anaphylaxis → Administer epinephrine 0.5 mg IM ± 0.3 mg auto‑injector
  │
  │  Recovered? – yes and no residual symptoms → Discontinue steroids
  │
  │  Recovered? – no or persistent symptoms (hypotension, dyspnoea, angioedema, dark‑red rash) → check later:
  │
  │   ┌─ Severe presentation? (≥2 vasopressors, airway compromise)
  │   │     └─ Give short‑acting steroid (methylprednisolone 1 mg/kg) *only if* high‑risk for rebound
  │   │
  │   └─ Mild to moderate presentation, no intra‑hospital recurrence → No steroid; plan observation ≥6 h
  │
  └─ Identify risk factors (systemic mastocytosis, high baseline tryptase, known anaphylaxis risk).
``` 

In practice, use the tree to decide at the *moment of first relief*. If you’re still swinging dizzily and cramping, a cheap steroid might mean trio of surcharges—worsening a patient’s course.

## Economic shockwave: ICU admissions and resource stewardship
Our case had a **short 24‑hour ICU stint**. But a 2019 audit quantifies the real cost of steroids: pre‑hospital steroids correlated with a 17 % rise in ICU admission rates. From the system perspective:

- **More ICU bed days** = higher costs.
- **More fluids, vasopressors, and monitoring** = longer hospital stays.  
- **Steroid side‑effects** (hyperglycemia, psychosis, secondary infections) can add additional treatments and readmissions.

In a world where a single 0.5 mg epinephrine improves odds for a quick, safe discharge, intravenous steroids add money to the ledger without clear evidence of saving lives. As clinicians who’re always balancing patient safety with cost, the decision to forgo steroids becomes an attractive trade‑off.

## Bottom line – what does the evidence really say?
*Steroids do not prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis.* A comprehensive systematic review in 2017 found that 22 studies showed no benefit, with 21 of them finding *no effect* and one suggesting harm. The 2019 pre‑hospital audit fizzled a strong association between steroid use and higher ICU admissions.

**What to do?**  
1. Start with epinephrine *only*—dosing by weight, not auto‑injector volume.  
2. Add steroids *only* for patients showing significant, unresolved distress after the first epinephrine dose (e.g., persistent hypotension, airway edema).  
3. Keep doses low (≤125 mg total for an adult).  
4. Observe all anaphylaxis patients for at least 6 h (4 h for mild, 6‑24 h for severe or high‑risk).  
5. Track baseline and post‑reaction tryptase levels for future phenotyping—vital if you suspect systemic mastocytosis or other mast‑cell disorders.

It’s the old “watchful waiting” philosophy in a modern, evidence‑driven framework—no more “just give everything” static packing.

## Take‑home points
- **Corticosteroids are *not* a proven method to prevent biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis.**  
- **Evidence from large systematic reviews and real‑world audits shows no benefit and potential harm, especially tied to ICU admissions.**  
- **Give steroids selectively**: when severe, ongoing symptoms persist after epinephrine, and the patient is at high risk (e.g., systemic mastocytosis, high baseline tryptase).  
- **Dose appropriately**: short‑acting regimens (methylprednisolone 1 mg/kg or hydrocortisone 50 mg IV) with a capped total dose.  
- **Monitor for at least 6 h** (up to 24 h for high‑risk or multi‑episode patients).  
- **Collect tryptase samples** at baseline, peak, and post‑recovery—especially if you suspect an underlying mast‑cell disease.  

The next time you see a patient with a burst of anaphylaxis, remember: rely on epinephrine, keep the observation vigilant, consider steroids *only* when warranted by the patient’s clinical picture, and never let tradition override evidence.



# Systemic Mastocytosis and Anaphylaxis: A Hidden Link You Need to Know

## The Unexpected Multiphasic Emergency

It’s one thing to see a patient suffer a single, dramatic anaphylactic episode. It’s another to watch a woman in her 60s collapse, then re‑collapse—not once, but three more times within a 24‑hour period—despite what we think of as “adequate” treatment. Her trigger? A single oral dose of clarithromycin, a macrolide antibiotic that is rarely implicated in anaphylaxis. The cascade was relentless: hypotension, hypoxaemia, diffuse urticaria, agitation, and then the fearless (but not life‑threatening) return of symptoms each time the epinephrine injections were given.

What was most striking was not that the patient had a severe episode but that she had a **multiphasic** one—four distinct anaphylactic events in less than a day, with progressively blunted severity. In other words, her mast cells decided to keep the fireworks going. A multiphasic presentation is the hallmark of an underlying mast cell disorder, often systemic mastocytosis (SM), even when the classic WHO criteria are not fully met.

## Why Should EM Residents Consider Mastocytosis in Multiphasic Episodes?

The clinical pattern is telling: **recurrent, robust anaphylaxis after a single exposure**. The literature estimates that because the spectrum of basophilic and mast cell activation disorders is broad, a subset of these patients have SM. Importantly, SM confers a predilection toward severe anaphylaxis because of an excess load of mast cells and heightened mediator release. In our case, a baseline tryptase level was elevated, and later repeat samples during the crisis surpassed 20 µg/L—an essential minor WHO criterion.

What does this mean for us bedside? Imagine giving a patient a single dose of 0.3 mg epinephrine (the ubiquitous auto‑injector in many EDs) and watching them wander back into the bathroom for a half‑hour‑later rush of flushing and dyspnoea. If SM is lurking under the surface, the first dose is likely under‑effective, and alarmingly, subsequent doses often miss the mark too. This is a textbook situation where the initial thermometers (baseline tryptase, imaging of the bone marrow) can transform a point‑in‑time crisis into a guided, long‑term plan.

## WHO Diagnostic Criteria in Context

The 2022 WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis are simple enough to remember but require a comprehensive work‑up:

| Major Criterion | Minor Criteria (≥2 qualify) |
|-----------------|------------------------------|
| >30 % atypical mast cells in bone‑marrow aspirate **or** distinct clusters on bone‑marrow biopsy | 1. Baseline serum tryptase >20 µg/L (or >24 µg/L with concurrent episode) |
| | 2. CD2 and CD25 co‑expression on mast cells (flow cytometry) |
| | 3. KIT D816V mutation in mast cells or lymphocytes |
| | 4. ≥25 % increase in tryptase in blood during crisis |
| | 5. Presence of >15 tryptase‑positive mast cells in a tissue biopsy |

In our patient, baseline tryptase and the high repeat values represented one minor criterion. We did not obtain a full bone‑marrow aspirate so the major criterion was unassessable, nor did we confirm CD2/CD25 positivity or the KIT mutation; thus, she falls into the “possible SM” category. Still, that is enough to warrant a heightened vigilance: prophylactic antihistamines, avoidance counseling, and a formal allergy referral.

## Proof‑of‑Concept: When Baseline and Peak Tryptase Matter

The urgency of measuring tryptase in anaphylaxis is rooted in guidelines: two to three samples at defined intervals. The first should be drawn *immediately* (or as soon as treatment does not delay a life‑saving intervention). The second 1–2 h after symptom onset, when tryptase peaks, and a third at least 24 h later, once the patient is clinically settled, to establish a baseline.

In many emergency rooms, we skip the baseline due to “too much blood loss” or because we think a single sample is enough. That ad‑hoc approach was precisely what happened in the case under discussion: the first tryptase came only after her third episode, 27 h after the last recurrence. By then the window to capture both an acute spike and the true baseline was narrowed, muddling our ability to gauge disease severity.

The takeaway for the ED is threefold:
1. **Treat first, test later**—blood can be drawn immediately after the first epinephrine dose without delaying care.
2. **Avoid delaying the earliest possible sample**—take it within minutes if physical exam allows.
3. **Document the delay**—if we’re not able to do the first sample promptly, record the timing so that follow‑up labs are interpreted correctly.

## Word on the Administration of Epinephrine

There is a small but critical disconnect between guideline‑recommended dosage and the reality of the auto‑injectors in most EDs. Adult epinephrine is 0.5 mg (1 mL of 1:1000 solution). However, auto‑injectors in many countries only provide 0.3 mg. The difference is not trivial: a 2015 retrospective review found that patients who received the 0.3 mg dose were significantly more likely to require escalation (repeat dosing, ICU transfer).

In this case, the first two attacks were treated with the 0.3 mg auto‑injector; the subsequent two required higher doses (0.5 mg). The transition to the higher dose correlated with a **subtly decreasing severity** of presentations—perhaps reflecting a combination of better induction, slower depletion of degranulation, and the patient’s own epinephrine tolerance.

**Bottom line**: if you suspect an underlying mast cell disorder that may predispose to a multiphasic course, aim for the adult epinephrine dose first and consider having a prepared 0.5 mg IM dose ready for the ED nursing team.

## Steroids: A Historical Footnote

For decades, it was standard to give a steroid after the first anaphylactic episode, based on the hope that it would curtail a biphasic reaction. The evidence, however, is unconvincing—most of the data come from small, poorly controlled studies that do not survive modern scrutiny.

A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found only one study supporting a benefit and 21 showing no effect. Even the 2019 study that highlighted harm, noting increased ICU admissions after pre‑hospital steroid use, remains under‑systematized and hasn't displaced the guidelines. Current consensus (2021 UK and 2022 WAA updates) advises against routine steroid use for biphasic prevention in the ED.

In the case at hand, steroids were administered after the first recurrence. It may have been a reflexive action and not necessarily contributive to preventing the next 3 episodes. The decision to withhold steroids in an acute setting, especially in an older patient with diabetes and hypertension, remains aligned with up‑to‑date guidance.

## Managing the Patient After the Crisis

Once the acute multiphasic schism ceases, the post‑anaphylactic strategy is paramount. For patients with possible SM—or even strongly suspected SM—they should receive:

1. **High‑dose antihistamine therapy**:  
   * H1 blockers at or above the usual prescribing: levocetirizine 10 mg PO BID or cetirizine 20 mg BID.  
   * H2 blockers: famotidine 20 mg PO QID or cimetidine 150 mg QID.  
   * Consider leukotriene receptor antagonists (montelukast 10 mg daily) if respiratory symptoms persist.

2. **Re‑education on epinephrine use**:  
   * Ensure she has a **0.5 mg adult auto‑injector** and that she (and any caregiver) can demonstrate proper intramuscular technique.  
   * Encourage carrying a **second device** for anaphylaxis‑high‑risk patients.  
   * Openly communicate that an under‑dosed 0.3 mg device is no longer the default standard for adults.

3. **Trigger avoidance map**:  
   * Clarithromycin, clindamycin, and other antibiotics (*macrolides* are less frequent overall but may trigger non‑IgE mediated pathways).  
   * Many other allergens were ruled out—component testing after ED stabilization can be considered.

4. **Follow‑up laboratory work‑up** –  
   * Repeat serum tryptase at least one month post‑moment, or sooner if new symptoms arise.  
   * Consider referral to an allergist/hematologist for definitive BM aspirate and CD2/CD25 testing if baseline tryptase remains >20 µg/L.

5. **Risk‑adjusted monitoring** –  
   * In hindsight, a 24‑ to 48‑hour ICU stay was a prudent yet valuable precaution given the multiphasic pattern.  
   * Some guidelines (UK 2021, Dutch NHG) allow a 4–6 h observation in low‑risk single‑episode cases, but in suspected SM, always trend to longer, continuous cardiac/breathing and blood pressure monitoring.

## The Take‑Home Messages

- Multiphasic anaphylaxis should trigger suspicion for systemic mastocytosis, especially when the patient has a high baseline or peak tryptase (>20 µg/L).  
- **Baseline tryptase** and **peak tryptase** (1–2 h after onset, then 24 h later) are paramount for diagnosing SM.  
- Adult epinephrine doses should default to **0.5 mg IM**; 0.3 mg auto‑injectors are inadequate for patients >50 kg.  
- Routine corticosteroids for biphasic prevention are not recommended; guidelines favor tight observation and epinephrine re‑dosing.  
- Post‑crisis management for suspected SM includes **high‑dose H1/H2 blockers, education on epinephrine, and avoidance strategies**.  
- A dedicated registry of multiphasic anaphylaxis cases would be a golden resource to refine risk stratification and monitoring protocols.  

If you encounter a patient who somehow “makes a comeback” after an anaphylactic gasp, pause to ask: “Could this be a mast cell disorder?” The answer will shape not just the next dose of epinephrine but the entire trajectory of care for that patient—and for others who may suffer the same multiphasic drama in your ED.



# Tryptase Timing and Interpretation: Lessons from a Multiphasic Anaphylaxis Case  

---

## Why this case matters at first glance  

Our patient—an older woman who’d had a single apparently uncomplicated anaphylactic episode after taking clarithromycin—returned to the ED **four times** with a full‑blown relapse, each time clutching the same epinephrine auto‑injector.  
What made the case truly eye‑opening was the *missing* data: we didn’t draw serum tryptase *until after her third recurrence*, and we never captured the peak level that so often characterizes anaphylaxis. In retrospect, the delayed testing not only robbed us of a clearer picture of her acute mast cell degranulation but also obscured a subtle clue that she may be a “mildly” systemic mastocytosis patient.  

---

## The pivotal role of serum tryptase  

Tryptase is the most useful serum marker of mast cell activation in anaphylaxis. A spuriously low or late‑taken value can lead clinicians astray—especially when you’re trying to decide whether to label a case as “classic” anaphylaxis or to screen for underlying mast cell disease.  

- **Peak window**: Tryptase usually rises within 1–2 hours of symptom onset and peaks around 2–4 hours.  
- **Resolution window**: Baseline levels should be measured ≥24 hours after the patient has completely recovered.  
- **Repeat sampling**: For biphasic or multiphasic courses, you may need a second “peak” sample with each new recalcitrant episode.  

---

## What went wrong in the reported case  

| Sampling time | What we had | What we should have had | Consequence |
|---------------|-------------|--------------------------|-------------|
| **Immediately after ED arrival** | None | First sample (≤ 30 min) | Missed the *true* peak |
| **1 hour post third episode** | First sample | 2nd sample 1–2 hrs post *initial* onset | Missed the early peak, data less reliable |
| **27 hrs after last recurrence** | 2nd sample | Baseline sample ≥24 hrs after full resolution | Baseline underestimated; not a true baseline |

Because the initial peak was missed, we didn't know how high tryptase truly spiked. The persistently *elevated* baseline (≈ > 20 µg/L) only trended upwards during follow‑up, raising the suspicion of systemic mastocytosis—something that remains an orphan diagnostic space in emergency medicine.

---

## Putting the guidelines into practice  

1. **Order as soon as possible**—you can sample *before* you give epinephrine; the two are not mutually exclusive.  
2. **Baseline**: take a repeat sample 24 hrs after complete symptom resolution; this is essential for interpreting subsequent elevations.  
3. **Multiphasic courses**: Each new surge of symptoms warrants a fresh “peak” sample (1–2 hrs after that surge).  

> **Tip**: If you are on a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto‑injector (the one most commonly stocked in many EDs), you’re under‑dosing adults >50 kg. While not directly related to tryptase, it’s a reminder that early, aggressive treatment can blunt the rise in tryptase simply by preventing further mast cell degranulation.

---

## Interpreting the numbers  

| Tryptase level | Interpretation in anaphylaxis | Interpretation in baseline |
|----------------|------------------------------|----------------------------|
| **< 11.5 µg/L** | Normal (no significant degranulation) | Normal baseline |
| **11.5–20 µg/L** | Mild to moderate mast cell activation | Possible baseline elevation—evaluate risk for mast cell disorders |
| **> 20 µg/L** | Significant mast cell activation | Persistent elevation > 20 µg/L strongly suggests systemic mastocytosis or mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS) |

In the reported case, the persistent baseline elevation above 20 µg/L led the allergology team to evaluate for systemic mastocytosis, even though bone‑marrow criteria were not fully met. The absence of a formal diagnosis underscores the fluid nature of mast cell disorder criteria; a persistent elevation is, at minimum, a *red flag* that warrants vigilant follow‑up.

---

## A step‑by‑step algorithm for tryptase in anaphylaxis  

1. **Suspect anaphylaxis** → Call for treatment.  
2. **Order first tryptase sample** *before or immediately after* epinephrine.  
3. **Treat** with epinephrine, supportive care.  
4. **If the patient deteriorates or has a biphasic/multiphasic course** →  
   * Repeat a “peak” sample 1–2 hrs after that recurrence.  
5. **Once symptom-free for ≥24 hrs** → Draw a baseline sample.  
6. **Interpret all values** in the context of the peak and baseline.  
7. **If baseline > 20 µg/L** → Refer to allergology/hematology for mast cell work‑up.  

---

## Persistent elevation = possible systemic mastocytosis  

- **Case reminder**: Baseline tryptase above 20 µg/L was seen even *after* clinical resolution.  
- **Why it matters**: Most patients with systemic mastocytosis have a “steady‑state” elevation > 20 µg/L; the figure isn’t a laboratory quirk.  
- **Next steps**: Look for clinical clues (e.g., hepatomegaly, unexplained flushing, unexplained pruritus, eosinophilia) and consider specialized tests (CD30‑positive mast cells, KIT mutation).  

---

## Practical take‑aways for the ED & ICU nurse/physician  

- **Urgency trumps precision**: Draw tryptase right away; delay with the thought of “we’ll order it later” kills the diagnostic yield.  
- **Timing is everything**: The 1–2 hour post‑onset window is the sweet spot for the peak concentration.  
- **Persistently high baseline is a red flag**: Even if the peak was modest, a baseline > 20 µg/L warrants referral to mast cell experts.  
- **Document clearly**: When you order the baseline, note that the patient was ≥24 hrs symptom‑free.  
- **Review local auto‑injector stock**: If you’re routinely using a 0.3 mg device for adults, consider switching to a 0.5 mg bottle or training staff to identify weight‑based dosing.  

---

## Key take‑home messages  

- **Don’t wait for epinephrine to be “finished”**—draw tryptase first, then treat.  
- **Peak sampling window is 1–2 hrs after symptom onset**; if missed, the sample won’t reflect mast cell burden.  
- **Baseline sampling ≥24 hrs after resolution** distinguishes an acute spike from a chronic elevation.  
- **Persistently elevated baseline tryptase (>20 µg/L)** should trigger mast cell disease work‑up, even if formal diagnostic criteria aren’t met.  
- **In a multiphasic scenario, repeat peak sampling** with each relapse to capture the true magnitude of ongoing degranulation.  

By weaving these simple, time‑sensitive steps into our standard anaphylaxis protocols, we not only improve acute care but also lay the groundwork for diagnosing potentially impactful chronic conditions like systemic mastocytosis. In a world of high‑stakes emergencies, a little extra blood now can save a lot of lives and reduce future crises.
