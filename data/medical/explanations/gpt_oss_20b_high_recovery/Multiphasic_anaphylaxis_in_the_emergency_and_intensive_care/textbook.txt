Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Initial Presentation and Emergency Stabilization

A woman in her sixties, weighing 104 kg, presented to the emergency department (ED) with a classic picture of systemic anaphylaxis. Her past medical history was notable for type‑2 diabetes mellitus (metformin 500 mg BID, gliclazide 80 mg daily), hypertension (hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg daily), psoriasis and depression (sertraline 100 mg daily). She had a known allergy to beta‑lactam antibiotics, manifesting only as facial swelling after amoxicillin in adolescence, and a strong local skin reaction to a wasp sting without systemic sequelae. Her only recent drug exposure was clarithromycin 500 mg taken orally, 5 days after a course of clindamycin had induced a rash interpreted as a late allergic reaction; the clarithromycin was started to avoid further clindamycin exposure. Ten‑one hundred minutes after ingestion she developed pruritus, flushing, profuse sweating, agitation and a sensation of throat swelling. She presented to the ED within an hour of onset, providing the clinical window in which anaphylaxis must be addressed promptly.

On arrival the patient was hypotensive (systolic BP 74 mm Hg), hypoxemic (SpO₂ 88 % on room air), and tachypneic (22 breaths min⁻¹). Physical examination revealed pronounced facial edema and diffuse erythema with urticarial wheals over the trunk and extremities. There was no tongue or laryngeal edema, and cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. Hemoglobin and leukocyte counts were not yet available; serum glucose was 12.7 mmol/L, and a sinus rhythm ECG excluded immediate cardiac compromise. The constellation of cutaneous, respiratory, and cardiovascular findings fulfilled the clinical criteria for anaphylaxis, where rapid onset and multisystem involvement define the diagnosis, independent of any confirmatory laboratory test.

Standard anaphylaxis treatment was immediately initiated: intramuscular (IM) epinephrine was administered using a 0.3 mg auto‑injector, followed by IV infusion of clemastine 2 mg for antihistamine coverage. Supplemental oxygen was delivered via high‑flow non‑rebreather mask at 15 L min⁻¹, and 500 mL of isotonic saline (0.9 % NaCl) was infused to counteract distributive shock. The epinephrine dose, though lower than the guideline‑recommended 0.5 mg (0.01 mg kg⁻¹ capped at 0.5 mg), produced rapid reversal of hypotension and a marked reduction in angioedema and urticaria, illustrating the life‑saving efficacy of adrenergic stimulation in anaphylactic shock. The patient’s oxygenation and hemodynamic parameters returned to baseline within minutes, confirming adequate initial stabilization.

However, the clinical course deviated from the typical monophasic pattern. Despite a complete apparent resolution, the patient experienced recurrent anaphylactic episodes within the first 12 hours of admission: four peaks in total, including the initial ED event. Each episode required IM epinephrine for resolution—first with repeat 0.3 mg auto‑injector doses, then with 0.5 mg IM injections prepared at the bedside. The recurrence of symptoms in the absence of further clarithromycin exposure, with intervals of several hours, fulfilled the definition of multiphasic anaphylaxis. Corticosteroids (prednisone 30 mg IV daily for five days and a single 8 mg IV dexamethasone dose) were introduced after the first relapse, reflecting traditional practice to dampen the late inflammatory phase, although contemporary evidence does not support routine use for biphasic prevention. Bilateral H1‑antihistamine therapy (clemastine 2 mg IV twice) continued to mitigate cutaneous mediator effects. Additional 500 mL NS were given during the second recurrence to maintain circulating volume.

The repeated anaphylactic episodes exhibited a progressive attenuation of severity: the first presentation involved profound hypotension, cyanosis, and severe dyspnea; subsequent episodes were dominated by hypoxemia, rash, and pruritus, with only mild cardiovascular compromise. This gradual diminution suggests a diminishing mediator burden or an evolving resolution phase, yet the persistence of symptom recurrence underscores the complexity of multiphasic anaphylaxis and the limits of standard epinephrine dosing, particularly when auto‑injector formulations supply only 0.3 mg. Notwithstanding these challenges, the patient ultimately remained free of further symptoms after 24 hours of observation, allowing transfer from the intensive care unit to a standard internal medicine ward and eventual discharge after three days. Post‑discharge management included initiation of levocetirizine and provision of a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto‑injector for self‑rescue, with no subsequent anaphylactic events reported. This case illustrates how prompt recognition, adherence to foundational anaphylaxis protocols, and vigilant monitoring are critical in managing even the most recalcitrant presentations.

## Diagnosis of Anaphylaxis with Biphasic/Multiphasic Course  

Establishing a diagnosis of anaphylaxis in the emergency department relies on both clinical presentation and the rapid application of validated diagnostic criteria. In the case presented, the patient’s immediate symptom onset after oral clarithromycin, the rapid development of hypotension, hypoxaemia, urticaria, facial angioedema and pruritus, and the swift reversal of these signs following intramuscular epinephrine, fulfill the American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology/International Consensus on Allergy and Clinical Immunology (AAACI/IHS) definition of anaphylaxis. The AAACI/IHS criteria require either (1) involvement of the skin or mucosal tissue (hives, pruritus, flushing, or swelling of lips/face/eyes) PLUS a second organ system (respiratory compromise, cardiovascular collapse, gastrointestinal symptoms), OR (2) hypotension (or a reduction in systolic blood pressure ≥ 30 mm Hg) or reduced cardiac output in a patient with known or possible IgE‑mediated sensitization, or (3) anaphylaxis that improves after epinephrine administration. The patient satisfied criterion 1 (cutaneous angioedema and urticaria with hypotension) and criterion 3 (prompt recovery after epinephrine). 

**Key features supporting anaphylaxis in this patient**

- **Temporal correlation**: Symptoms began 1 hour after the first clarithromycin dose, a time frame compatible with IgE‑mediated reactivity.
- **Multi‑system involvement**: Cutaneous (flushing, urticaria), cardiovascular (severe hypotension 74/50 mm Hg), and respiratory (hypoxaemia 88 % on room air) manifestations.
- **Exclusion of alternative causes**: No documented fever, rash of a different morphology, or bloody diarrhoea that would suggest other drug reactions; the patient’s background medications do not explain acute hypoxaemia and hypotension.
- **Positive response to epinephrine**: Rapid normalization of blood pressure and resolution of angioedema confirm the diagnosis of anaphylaxis and exclude a benign transient reaction.

**Definition and timing of biphasic and multiphasic reactions**

A biphasic reaction is defined as the recurrence of any anaphylactic symptoms after apparent clinical resolution, occurring without re‑exposure to the culprit allergen, and usually within 24 hours of the initial episode. Multiphasic reactions involve two or more such recurrences. In this case, after the first epinephrine dose and subsequent stabilization, the patient experienced three additional relapses over a 12‑hour period, meeting the criteria for a multiphasic reaction. All recurrences reproduced the same pattern—hypotension, flushing, urticaria, and dyspnoea—thus distinguishing them from an ongoing or partially treated episode.

**Differential diagnosis**

- **Sepsis**: Fever, WBC elevation, and a sustained source of infection are typically present. In this patient, blood cultures were pending and the patient was afebrile with normal CBC except for transient eosinophilia later in the stay.
- **Thyroid storm**: Requires recent thyroid disease and hallmark signs such as tachycardia, delirium, diarrhoea, and hyperthermia. The patient’s thyroid function tests were within normal limits, and her symptoms lacked hyperthermia or tachycardia.
- **Angioedema**: Isolated swelling of lips, tongue, or face without other systemic signs. The patient’s episode involved generalized urticaria, hypotension, and dyspnoea, which exceed isolated angioedema.
- **Drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS)**: A delayed reaction (≥ 2 weeks), with skin rash, eosinophilia, fever, and multi‑organ involvement. The rapid onset of symptoms and their recurrence pattern made DRESS unlikely.

These considerations strengthen the conclusion that the patient experienced anaphylaxis rather than an alternative severe drug reaction or systemic disease.

**Recognition of symptom recurrence and criteria for a true second phase**

Clinicians must differentiate a true biphasic rebound from transient physiological fluctuation or from complications of ongoing treatment. The following triad identifies a real second phase:

1. **Re‑emergence of at least one of the initial clinical manifestations** (e.g., facial angioedema, pruritic urticaria, hypotension).
2. **No new exposure to the triggering agent** and absence of a pharmacological cause for the relapse.
3. **Clinical response to standard anaphylaxis therapy** (second epinephrine dose) and subsequent resolution.

In this case, the patient developed new hypotension (70/45 mm Hg), pronounced flushing, and a diffuse rash approximately 5 hours after the first episode had resolved. Intramuscular epinephrine rapidly restored baseline haemodynamics, confirming a true biphasic event.

**Role and limitations of laboratory investigations**

**Serum tryptase** is the most specific laboratory marker for mast‑cell mediator release. Guidelines recommend three samples:  
- Within 1 hour of symptom onset (to capture peak).  
- 1–2 hours later (to confirm persistence).  
- ≥24 hours after full clinical resolution (for baseline).  

In this patient, the first tryptase measurement was taken 1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode, and a second at 27 hours after the last relapse. Although these values were markedly elevated and remain high at follow‑up, the lack of an early sample precludes definitive confirmation of peak tryptase, and thus limits its diagnostic precision for the original episode. Nonetheless, the persistently high baseline tryptase supports a mast‑cell disorder (systemic mastocytosis), which can predispose to severe and recurrent anaphylaxis.

**Complete blood count (CBC)** can reveal eosinophilia, a marker for allergic inflammation or DRESS, but in this patient only mild eosinophilia was observed during follow‑up and was not present during the acute episodes, arguing against DRESS.

**Liver function tests (LFTs)** are useful to rule out hepatic involvement in other severe drug reactions. Mild hepatomegaly on ultrasound suggested hepatic involvement, but LFTs remained within normal limits, making hepatic failure unlikely.

In summary, a swift application of the AAACI/IHS criteria, recognition of a biphasic/multiphasic pattern, systematic exclusion of other severe reactions, and judicious use of laboratory markers—especially tryptase—are essential for accurate diagnosis. These steps guided the clinicians in this case to appropriately identify a multiphasic anaphylaxis, tailor subsequent monitoring and treatment, and finally uncover an underlying predisposition (mild systemic mastocytosis) that explained the prolonged course.

## Therapeutic Management Strategies for Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

The case of the woman in her 60s illustrates the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges that arise when anaphylaxis is not a single, self‑limited event but recurs several times within the first 24 hours. In such multiphasic courses, clinicians must decide rapidly how to dose epinephrine, whether to rely on auto‑injectors or manually prepared syringes, how aggressively to use adjunctive drugs such as antihistamines and corticosteroids, and when to admit to the ICU versus a general ward. The following discussion outlines the current evidence‑based principles that informed the care in this case and provides a framework for decision‑making in similar high‑risk scenarios.

---

### Weight‑Based Epinephrine Dosing and Maximal Limits

The cornerstone of anaphylaxis treatment is intramuscular (IM) epinephrine. Guidelines from the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommend a weight‑based dose of **0.01 mg kg⁻¹** of epinephrine, with a maximum single dose of **0.5 mg** for adults. For a 104‑kg patient—such as the index patient—this translates to 1.04 mg; however, the practice standard caps the dose at 0.5 mg to avoid the higher cardiovascular side‑effect profile of supratherapeutic doses. This ceiling also aligns with the 0.5 mg recommendation in many emergency protocols.

The clinical reality is that many emergency departments and pre‑hospital providers default to the 0.3 mg dose contained in standard adult auto‑injectors. While convenient, this dose may be **sub‑therapeutic for patients weighing >50 kg**, potentially delaying full reversal and predisposing to reco­llapses. In the present case, the first two relapses were treated with 0.3 mg auto‑injectors, followed by 0.5 mg manually prepared doses that appeared to hasten resolution. This pattern underscores how weight‑based dosing should be adhered to, even when auto‑injectors are available.

---

### Auto‑Injector Potency (0.3 mg vs 0.5 mg): Implications for Adult Care

Auto‑injectors are prized for ease and speed; they standardise dosing, reduce medication error, and minimize hesitation in staff unfamiliar with epinephrine. Yet the potency differences between **0.3 mg** and **0.5 mg** formulations have important practice implications. The 0.3 mg auto‑injector is often marketed for “adult” use, but adult weight ranges vary widely; for patients in the inter‑weight band of 50–75 kg, 0.3 mg can be 40–60 % of the calculated dose. In patients >75 kg (common in Western practice), the under‑dosing is even more pronounced.

The 2025 retrospective analysis cited in the article confirmed that patients initially treated with a 0.3 mg dose were **significantly more likely to require repeated epinephrine and ICU admission**. Consequently, many European societies now endorse the use of **0.5 mg auto‑injectors for adults, or at least verify weight‑based dosing before deployment**. In the ICU setting, where rapid, repeated dosing may be required, the pragmatic choice is to prepare **injectable epinephrine solutions** (e.g., 1:10,000 or 1:1,000) rather than relying on auto‑injectors to ensure precise dosing.

---

### Adjunctive Medications: Antihistamines, Corticosteroids, and their Evidence Base

#### Antihistamines
Allergic histamine mediators are responsible for many cutaneous and gastrointestinal manifestations. Intramuscular or intravenous H1 antihistamines (e.g., clemastine, diphenhydramine) and H2 antagonists (ranitidine, famotidine) are typically added to stabilize skin and mucosal symptoms. In the case, 2 mg intravenous clemastine was repeated at the second relapse and again during later episodes, which likely contributed to the attenuation of pruritus and urticaria. No evidence suggests that antihistamines prevent recurrence; however, they remain useful for symptom control when administered early.

#### Corticosteroids
Corticosteroid use has long been taught as a prophylaxis against biphasic anaphylaxis. Yet systematic reviews from 2017 onward consistently show **no convincing benefit** in preventing recurrent episodes, and some studies even indicate an association with longer hospital stay or higher ICU admission rates. In the present patient, steroid use (30 mg prednisone orally for 5 days plus an IV dexamethasone 8 mg) was initiated after the first recurrence due to conventional practice. Subsequent observations showed a tapering intensity of episodes, but whether steroids contributed is uncertain. Current guidelines (e.g., EAACI, NICE) advise **reserved corticosteroid use**, mainly for chronic urticaria or severe systemic reactions refractory to epinephrine and antihistamines.

#### Other Adjuncts
The case did not include epinephrine intramuscular pre‑loading doses or pre‑emptive epinephrine pumps. In a multiphasic setting, the decision to initiate continuous intravenous (IV) epinephrine hinges on the stabilization of the patient, the presence of ongoing mediator release, and the feasibility of a continuous infusion (e.g., 0.05–0.10 µg kg⁻¹ min⁻¹). These infusions are associated with tachycardia and ventricular arrhythmias and should be delivered by trained intensivists under cardiac monitoring.

---

### ICU Admission Criteria and Specialized Monitoring Protocols

Multiphasic anaphylaxis is a prototypical indication for ICU care. The patient’s first recurrence required ICU admission following the development of profound hypotension and hypoxemia despite initial stabilization. ICU admission should be considered when:

1. **Recurrent circulatory collapse** despite at least two appropriately dosed IM epinephrine injections.
2. **Respiratory compromise** (e.g., persistent hypoxemia, requiring supplemental oxygen >6 L min⁻¹) or airway edema.
3. **Evidence of multiorgan involvement** (e.g., arrhythmias, CNS symptoms).
4. **Need for continuous cardiac telemetry**, advanced respiratory support, or repeated drug administration under observation.

The ICU team provides continuous fluid resuscitation, frequent vital sign checks, and ready availability of vasopressors, corticosteroids, antihistamines, and resuscitative fluids. Collaborative decision‑making between allergology and critical care teams can refine therapeutic goals.

---

### Continuous IV Epinephrine Infusion vs Expectant Treatment

In refractory or multiphasic anaphylaxis, continuous IV epinephrine infusion is theoretically attractive: it maintains a steady catecholamine level, potentially suppressing mast cell degranulation and preventing relapses. However, evidence remains sparse, and infusion carries increased risk of arrhythmias and hypertension. Clinical experience varies: some centers adopt a **“pre‑emptive infusion”** after two relapses, while others prefer an “expectant” approach—monitor closely and administer repeated IM epinephrine at the first sign of deterioration.

The decision depends on:

- **Severity of the most recent episode** (e.g., hypotension <90 mm Hg, need >500 mL fluid).
- **Time elapsed since last relapse** (the risk curve for multiphasic reactions peaks within 8–12 h but can extend up to 48 h).
- **Resource availability** (continuous infusion pumps, cardiac monitoring).
- **Patient comorbidities** (e.g., cardiac disease increases risk of catecholamine toxicity).

In an institutional protocol, it is reasonable to **reserve continuous infusion for patients with ≥3 recurrences within 12 h**, after multidisciplinary consensus. In the case report, the care team chose to *not* initiate infusion, preferring to observe with robust monitoring; the patient so turned over after 24 h of symptom‑free interval.

---

### Risk‑Stratified Observation Periods and Decision Algorithms

Traditional guidelines recommend a **4‑ to 6‑hour observation** period after full resolution of anaphylaxis. However, this interval fails to capture patients who develop recurrences beyond 12 h. Several risk factors—delayed epinephrine administration, severe initial presentation, requirement of multiple epinephrine doses, history of prior biphasic reaction—have been proposed to stratify patients into low, intermediate, or high risk for recurrence.

A practical algorithm might assign a score (e.g., **2 points** for each risk factor present). A cumulative score of ≥4 could trigger ICU admission and continuous observation >24 h, while a score of 1–3 might warrant extended high‑level monitoring (e.g., telemetry, 24 h) in a step‑down unit. In the reported case, the patient presented with multiple risk factors (age >60, weight >100 kg, history of severe reaction, multiple IM epinephrine doses), justifying ICU admission and a near‑24‑hour observation, which ultimately proved sufficient.

---

In summary, multiphasic anaphylaxis demands a nuanced approach to epinephrine dosing, a critical evaluation of adjunctive therapy, and a tailored observation strategy. Weight‑based epinephrine dosing remains essential, auto‑injector potency should be matched to body weight, corticosteroids have limited evidence for prophylaxis against recurrence, and ICU resources should be mobilised for high‑risk, multi‑recurrent cases. Continuous infusion is a potential yet unproven strategy, reserved for refractory situations. Applying a risk‑stratified observation model helps balance patient safety with efficient use of intensive care resources.

## Investigation of Underlying Pathology: Systemic Mastocytosis

Systemic mastocytosis is a clonal mast cell disorder in which the number of mast cells is markedly increased in multiple tissues, leading to an overproduction of the inflammatory mediators that drive anaphylaxis. Understanding the cellular basis of the disease, the formal criteria used to diagnose it, and the laboratory sequence required to capture its biochemical signature clarifies why the patient in this case experienced unusually severe and recurrent reactions.

The mast cell is a tissue-resident leukocyte that stores a repertoire of histamine, proteases, leukotrienes, prostaglandins and cytokines in secretory granules. Upon activation through IgE or other stimuli, mast cells rapidly degranulate, releasing mediators that cause vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, bronchospasm, and pruritus. In systemic mastocytosis the clonal expansion of mast cells, usually driven by a D816V KIT mutation, results in a much higher mast cell burden throughout the bone marrow, skin, liver, spleen, gut, and peripheral blood. The amplified mediator load explains both the typical cutaneous findings of urticaria pigmentosa and the propensity for life‑threatening anaphylaxis, particularly when external triggers provoke an already sensitized mast cell pool.

The World Health Organization (WHO) endorses a set of major and minor diagnostic criteria to classify systemic mastocytosis. The major criterion requires a dense infiltrate of mast cells or mast cell aggregates comprising at least 15 mast cells per square millimetre in bone marrow aspirate and immunohistochemistry. One minor criterion may suffice if the major criterion is absent: (1) increased basal serum tryptase at or above 20 ng/mL, (2) aberrant expression of CD25 or CD30 on mast cells, (3) the presence of a pathogenic KIT mutation (most commonly the D816V variant), and (4) a morphologic abnormality of mast cells (e.g., atypical spindle‑shaped cells). Diagnosis requires the major criterion and one minor, or at least three of the four minor criteria. In the case reported here, the patient’s serum tryptase consistently exceeded 20 ng/mL, and the bone marrow biopsy demonstrated an increased frequency of morphologically normal mast cells. However, the sample was limited and did not allow comprehensive immunophenotyping or mutational testing, leaving only two minor criteria fulfilled; consequently, the patient could not be assigned a formal WHO diagnosis, yet a high probability of systemic mastocytosis was entertained.

Objective assessment of tryptase is a cornerstone of mastocytosis evaluation and is especially pertinent to anaphylaxis. Tryptase, the mast cell protease highly released during degranulation, shows a predictable time course: an early peak typically occurs 1–2 hours after the onset of anaphylaxis. Guidelines recommend three serial samples— (1) as soon as possible after first symptoms to capture the initial peak, (2) 1–2 hours after to confirm or refine the peak, and (3) a baseline sample taken at least 24 hours after full resolution of symptoms to assess the patient’s resting tryptase level. In the present patient, the first sampling occurred 1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode, and the second 27 hours later. Because the timing did not correspond to the early peak of the initial reaction, the procedural sampling missed the most informative data, potentially underestimating the acute tryptase surge that would have underscored mast cell overactivation.

Bone marrow biopsy findings contextualize the biochemical data. In systemic mastocytosis, a dense mast cell infiltrate often demonstrates clusters of cells with morphological abnormalities. In this patient, the biopsy revealed a modest increase in mast cells that were predominantly histologically normal, a pattern consistent with an early or indolent form of mast cell disease. Absence of CD25 or CD30 staining and lack of documented KIT mutation preclude a definitive diagnosis, yet the histochemical evidence, combined with a high baseline and a reactive tryptase peak during an anaphylactic event, strongly supports a mast cell–driven process.

The clinical constellation accompanying the laboratory abnormalities offers additional clues. An abdominal ultrasound identified a mildly enlarged liver. Hepatomegaly is a recognized manifestation of organ infiltration by mast cells in systemic mastocytosis. The patient also reported chronic mood disorders and episodic memory impairment— neuro‑psychiatric symptoms that can result from widespread mediator release affecting the central nervous system. Moreover, the history of a cutaneous response to clarithromycin and previous mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin suggest a predisposition to drug triggers, which, when confronted with a mast cell–rich milieu, can precipitate exaggerated anaphylaxis.

Systemic mastocytosis makes patients especially vulnerable to severe, prolonged, or multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. The sheer quantity of mast cells means that each subsequent triggering event releases a larger volume of mediators, amplifying the clinical picture and extending the time required to return to baseline. Additionally, clonal mast cells may have altered kinetics of degranulation, leading to delayed secondary releases that underlie biphasic or multiphasic episodes. In this context, standard epinephrine dosing may be insufficient; the under‑dosed 0.3 mg auto‑injector used initially likely contributed to the need for several subsequent doses during the patient’s course. Corticosteroid use remains controversial, as evidence does not support a prophylactic benefit in preventing recurrences, yet clinicians often administer them in high‑risk settings such as the one described.

In sum, the corroborated findings of elevated tryptase, impaired bone marrow histology, hepatomegaly, and neuro‑psychiatric symptoms collectively suggest that the patient harbored underlying systemic mastocytosis, which contributed to the intensity and recurrence of her anaphylactic episodes. Recognizing this relationship informs both acute management—by prompting higher epinephrine doses and prolonged observation—and long‑term care, including the provision of a self‑injectable epinephrine device and avoidance of known mast cell activators.

## Follow‑Up, Secondary Prevention, and Clinical Lessons

After a multiphasic anaphylactic crisis has been controlled, the next priority is to convert the acute event into a long‑term safety plan. The disposition and counseling in this case illustrate several key principles that should be applied to all patients who have experienced anaphylaxis, particularly those with evidence suggestive of an underlying mast cell disorder.

### Discharge disposition and patient counseling

The patient was transferred from the intensive care unit to a regular medical ward after a 24‑hour period of symptom stability and was discharged, after a total of three days, with a clear plan for ongoing care. Counseling emphasized avoidance of the precipitating antigen (clarithromycin and, by implication, other macrolides that might cross‑react) and the importance of identifying any additional potential triggers. Because her initial allergic reaction had involved only a single macrolide exposure, it was recommended that she inform all future prescribers of her anaphylaxis history and carry an “allergy card” in addition to her epinephrine auto‑injector.

### Prescription and training for epinephrine auto‑injector usage

The patient was prescribed a 0.3‑mg epinephrine auto‑injector and trained in its use. In teaching the resident, it is vital to emphasize the pharmacologic basis for the 0.01‑mg/kg dose recommendation (maximum 0.5 mg for adults). The case demonstrates how an auto‑injector containing only 0.3 mg can be insufficient in a patient weighing 104 kg, thereby necessitating repeat injections and potentially prolonging the need for monitoring. Practical instruction covered:

1. **Recognition of early signs** – skin flushing, pruritus, dyspnea, or hypotension that indicate a re‑emergence of anaphylaxis.
2. **Injection technique** – proper positioning of the arm and ensuring a full intramuscular dose.
3. **Post‑injection follow‑up** – call the emergency department if symptoms recur within minutes or if the patient feels any residual discomfort.

In addition, the resident should be aware that many institutions now provide 0.5‑mg auto‑injectors for adults; when available, these are preferable for patients above 50 kg.

### Continuation with antihistamine therapy and monitoring schedules

Levocetirizine was initiated at discharge as a once‑daily, second‑generation antihistamine. The decision was based on the patient’s ongoing cutaneous manifestations and the desire to simplify the medication regimen for a 60‑year‑old man. Follow‑up included:

- **Clinical assessment** at 1‑week and 1‑month intervals to document residual urticaria or angioedema.
- **Monitoring of serum tryptase** when clinically indicated; the case showed persistently elevated baseline tryptase, a marker that guides the frequency of surveillance in suspected mast cell disease.
- **Education on medication adherence** and potential side effects such as drowsiness or anticholinergic effects.

### Long‑term surveillance strategies for systemic mastocytosis

Although the patient did not meet the full WHO criteria for systemic mastocytosis, the presence of two minor criteria and an elevated tryptase level warranted ongoing surveillance:

1. **Serial serum tryptase** measurements every 3–6 months to detect progressive increases suggestive of clonal mast cell expansion.
2. **Bone‑marrow evaluation**, if clinically indicated by new organ involvement (hepatic, neurological, or dermatologic symptoms).
3. **Regular clinic visits** with an allergist/immunologist in coordination with the primary care team.
4. **Patient‑reported symptom diary** to track triggers, mast cell mediator–related manifestations, and any new exposures.

Guidelines recommend that individuals with suspected systemic mastocytosis have age‑appropriate monitoring for disease‑related complications, allowing early intervention should mucosal or visceral intrusions occur.

### Current guideline recommendations for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis

Contemporary consensus documents (e.g., the 2021 World Allergy Organization guidelines and UK Resuscitation Council recommendations) outline a standard management algorithm:

- **Initial treatment**: Intramuscular epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg (max 0.5 mg) with subsequent doses as needed, accompanied by oxygen, IV fluids, antihistamines, and corticosteroids if judged clinically appropriate.
- **Observation**: A standard minimum of 4–6 h after symptom resolution; however, the consensus has not defined a universal duration, especially for high‑risk patients.
- **Risk stratification**: Clinical judgment should consider factors such as delayed epinephrine administration, severity of initial reaction, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and known mast cell disorders.

The guidelines explicitly state that corticosteroids lack robust evidence to prevent biphasic reactions but are still routinely used in clinical practice. They also note that epinephrine dosing recommendations are clear, yet multiple auto‑injector sizes remain in use, contributing to the under‑dosing risk observed in this case.

### Identified gaps in current knowledge and practice

1. **Epinephrine dosing heterogeneity** – The discrepancy between the 0.3‑mg auto‑injector used in the ED and the 0.5‑mg recommendation highlights a potential source of under‑dosing and delayed symptom control.
2. **Corticosteroid role** – While frequently administered, systematic reviews do not support a definitive benefit in preventing biphasic or multiphasic reactions; high‑risk patients may receive unnecessary exposure to glucocorticoid side effects.
3. **Risk stratification tools** – No validated score exists to predict which patients will develop a biphasic reaction, making it difficult to tailor observation periods.
4. **Observation duration** – The optimal length of monitoring after symptom resolution remains uncertain; evidence is limited to small cohort studies, leading to variability in practice.

These gaps underscore the need for a more precise, evidence‑based approach to the secondary care of patients after anaphylaxis, particularly those with complex presentations.

### Proposal for a multicentre registry to generate evidence‑based guidelines

To address these uncertainties, a prospective, multicentre registry should be established with the following core components:

- **Patient demographics and comorbidities** (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, chronic skin conditions).
- **Trigger identification and exposure details** – including drug class, dose, and route.
- **Clinical course** – timing of onset, peak severity, number of epinephrine doses, presence of repeated episodes, and exact interval between episodes.
- **Laboratory data** – baseline and peak serum tryptase, blood counts, and imaging if mast cell disease is suspected.
- **Treatment variables** – epinephrine dose, timing, route, corticosteroid administration, antihistamines, and supportive care.
- **Observation metrics** – duration of monitoring, place (ED, ward, ICU) and any adverse outcomes.

Analyses of registry data would enable estimation of the incidence of biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis across practice settings, evaluation of dosing practices and outcomes, identification of credible predictors of recurrence, and derivation of risk‑stratified observation protocols. Ultimately, a robust, data‑driven guideline could be published to reduce variability in treatment, improve patient safety, and optimize resource allocation in the emergency and intensive care environments.
