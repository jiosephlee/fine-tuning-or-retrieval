Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Pathophysiology] Why is a serial measurement of serum tryptase essential in anaphylaxis, what is the optimal timing of the samples, and what information do the results provide for diagnosing systemic mastocytosis?

**Why serial serum tryptase measurement is a cornerstone of the work‑up of anaphylaxis**

1. **Detection of mast‐cell degranulation** – Tryptase is released almost immediately following mast‑cell activation. A peak within 1–2 h after the onset of symptoms constitutes biochemical confirmation that the clinical picture is due to mast‑cell‑mediated anaphylaxis rather than an unrelated event (e.g., septic shock, cardiogenic hypotension, or drug reaction without mast‑cell involvement).

2. **Risk stratification for biphasic/refractory disease** – In the case report, the patient experienced four anaphylactic episodes over 12 h. A markedly elevated peak tryptase (> 20 ng/mL) and a rising trend have been reported to correlate with severity and the likelihood of biphasic or multiphasic courses. Knowing that the patient’s tryptase was already high at the third episode alerts the team that further recurrences were biologically plausible, justifying the ICU transfer.

3. **Identification of an underlying mast‑cell disorder** – Persistent elevation of *baseline* tryptase is almost pathognomonic for systemic mastocytosis (SM). In our patient, the baseline (post‑resolution) tryptase remained above 20 ng/mL, satisfying one of the WHO minor criteria. Serial sampling thus provides the objective data needed to trigger further evaluation (bone marrow, KIT D816V testing) even when formal diagnostic criteria could not be fulfilled in the acute setting.

---

**Optimal timing of the samples**

| Sample | Timing | Rationale |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| **1st (early)** | **As soon as possible after presentation, before or immediately after the first epinephrine dose** | Captures pre‑peak “baseline” levels and does not delay treatment. |
| **2nd (peak)** | **1–2 h after the onset of symptoms** (must be within 4 h) | Tryptase peaks within this window; a rise ≥ 2 ng/mL + 20 % of baseline or > 20 ng/mL is regarded as diagnostic of anaphylaxis. In the report, the second sample was taken 27 h after the last episode—an interval that missed the peak, highlighting the need for early sampling. |
| **3rd (baseline)** | **≥ 24 h after complete clinical resolution** (ideally 24–48 h) | Establishes the true “baseline” value. A persistently high baseline (> 20 ng/mL) or a chronic rise over several days points to SM. |

*Note:* For patients who survive anaphylaxis and are monitored, a third sample taken 48–72 h is acceptable if earlier sampling is not feasible.

---

**Information provided by the results**

1. **Confirmation of anaphylaxis** – A short‑lived rise of tryptase that peaks 1–2 h after symptom onset, with the ratio of peak to baseline meeting the WHO threshold, is a biochemical hallmark of mast‑cell‑mediated anaphylaxis.

2. **Assessment of severity** – Higher peaks and a larger delta reflect a vigorous mast‑cell response and correlate with hemodynamic collapse, the need for multiple epinephrine doses, and the occurrence of biphasic episodes—as observed in the patient’s four crisis events.

3. **Evaluation for systemic mastocytosis** – WHO minor criteria include a *baseline* serum tryptase > 20 ng/mL. In our patient, the sustained elevation met this criterion. Together with two other minor criteria (visceral organ involvement and molecular KIT mutation) it creates a high probability of SM even if the bone‑marrow biopsy did not meet the major criterion.

4. **Guiding long‑term management** – A high baseline tryptase prompts the clinician to counsel the patient about a chronic mast‑cell disorder, to prescribe chronic antihistamine therapy, and, where appropriate, to facilitate surveillance for organ dysfunction (e.g., hepatomegaly noted on ultrasound).

---

**Take‑home points for practice**

1. **Always draw a tryptase serum sample immediately on arrival, *before* therapy is started, when possible.**

2. **Get a second sample 1–2 h after symptom presentation to capture the peak.**

3. **Obtain a third sample 24 h after complete resolution to establish baseline.**

4. **Interpret results with the WHO criteria for anaphylaxis and SM: a peak > 20 ng/mL or a ≥ 2‑fold rise from baseline confirms anaphylaxis; a baseline > 20 ng/mL is a major clue for systemic mastocytosis.**

5. **In patients with persistently elevated baseline tryptase, refer for specialist evaluation (bone marrow, KIT testing) and initiate chronic antihistamine cover.**

In the case presented, the delay in tryptase sampling (first sample only 1 h after the third episode) prevented documentation of anaphylactic peak and delayed recognition of the underlying systemic mastocytosis. Adhering to the above timing would have allowed earlier diagnosis, potentially altering monitoring intensity and long‑term therapy.
