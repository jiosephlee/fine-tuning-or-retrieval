Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] Summarize the most recent evidence regarding the use of systemic corticosteroids for preventing biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis and explain why many guidelines now recommend against routine administration.

**Clinical Pearl**

**Systemic corticosteroids are no longer recommended as routine therapy for preventing biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis.**  
Recent evidence shows that steroids neither reduce the risk of recurrence nor shorten hospital stay, and in some circumstances may even increase the likelihood of ICU admission.

**Recent evidence**

1. **Systematic review (2017, 22 studies)** – Only one study reported a protective effect; the remaining 21 studies found no evidence that systemic corticosteroids decreased the incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis.  
2. **Prospective cohort (2019)** – Patients who received pre‑hospital steroids were significantly more likely to require ICU or inpatient admission, suggesting potential harm or at least no net benefit.  
3. **Ongoing observational data** – Subsequent small series and registry analyses have reproduced the lack of efficacy and highlighted common steroid‑related adverse effects (hyperglycaemia, delirium, infection risk).

These data collectively provide high‑level observational evidence that routine steroid use does not confer a preventative advantage.

**Why guidelines have moved away**

- **No proven benefit**: Meta‑analytic data do not support a reduction in recurrent reactions.  
- **Potential harm**: Steroids are associated with metabolic, psychological, and infectious complications that may outweigh any theoretical benefit, especially in the acute anaphylactic setting.  
- **Resource implications**: Prolonged hospitalization and ICU admission linked to steroid use place an unnecessary burden on healthcare systems.  
- **Lack of predictors**: Without reliable risk stratification tools, indiscriminate steroid administration offers no targeted benefit.  

Consequently, contemporary guidelines (EAACI, ACAAI, UK 2021) advise against routine systemic corticosteroid therapy in anaphylaxis, except in selected high‑risk or refractory cases.

**Case‑report tethering**

In the presented multiphasic anaphylaxis to clarithromycin, the patient received prednisone 30 mg IV daily and a single dose of dexamethasone after the first recurrence. She nonetheless experienced three additional relapses, albeit of decreasing severity, and ultimately recovered without further steroid escalation. This mirrors the evidence: steroids did not prevent recurrence and the clinical course resolved spontaneously.

**Bottom line**

Systemic corticosteroids should not be administered routinely to prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis; they should be reserved for carefully selected patients with severe, refractory, or high‑risk presentations.
