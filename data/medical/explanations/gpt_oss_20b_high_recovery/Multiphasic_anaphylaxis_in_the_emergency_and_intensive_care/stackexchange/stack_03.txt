Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how the presence of systemic mastocytosis modifies the pathophysiology of anaphylaxis, particularly in fostering a multiphasic or refractory course.

**Answer**

Systemic mastocytosis (SM) expands the number and reactivity of mast cells in every tissue.  In this setting the classic IgE‑mediated degranulation that underlies anaphylaxis is amplified in two key ways:

| Mechanism | Effect on anaphylaxis |
|-----------|-----------------------|
| • **High mast‑cell burden** (persistently elevated basal tryptase, often >20 µg/L) | A larger pool of liberated mediators (histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, prostaglandins) is available, so the initial insult produces a more massive, sustained response. |
| • **Intrinsic mast‑cell activation** (c‑KIT D816V mutation or other somatic alterations) | Mast cells degranulate more readily and even in the absence of allergen, maintaining or re‑initiating mediator release after the trigger has cleared. |

In the reported woman, baseline and peak tryptase levels were markedly increased, and the patient experienced four distinct anaphylactic episodes despite removal of clarithromycin and adequate epinephrine dosing.  The unusually high mediator load and residual systemic mast‑cell activation explain the multiphasic pattern: the first cycle of degranulation does not exhaust the enormous reservoir of mediators, and subsequent rounds of spontaneous or slowly‑clearing release drive recurrent shock, hypotension, and cutaneous symptoms.  The repeated demand for epinephrine may be partly due to the auto‑injector containing only 0.3 mg (below the 0.5 mg adult recommendation), but even with adequate dosing the sheer amount of histamine and other mediators can blunt the response, pushing the reaction toward refractoriness.

Thus, in SM the pathophysiology of anaphylaxis is shifted from a single, event‑driven mediator burst to a prolonged, “spill‑over” release that can unfold in multiple phases.  Recognizing SM—evidenced by increased baseline tryptase, organ‑infiltration symptoms, and minor WHO criteria—should raise suspicion for multiphasic or refractory courses and may warrant higher epinephrine dosing, prolonged monitoring, and consideration of early continuous infusion in refractory cases.
