Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] What essential education points should be covered with a patient who has had multiphasic anaphylaxis before discharge, including epinephrine auto‑injector instructions, trigger avoidance, and follow‑up planning?

**Clinical Pearl – Teaching a Patient after Multiphasic Anaphylaxis**

An 60‑year‑old woman who developed four episodes of anaphylaxis after a single dose of clarithromycin was discharged after 3 days with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto‑injector, a new antihistamine (levocetirizine) and a clear written action plan. The following points are essential when counseling such a patient:

| Core Message | What to Say | Why It Matters (Case Context) |
|--------------|-------------|--------------------------------|
| **Recognize early warning signs** | “If you feel itching, hives, swelling (especially of face/neck), tight‑ness of throat, difficulty breathing, dizziness, or sudden low blood pressure, tell someone right away.” | She had urticaria, facial swelling, and dyspnea within an hour of the first clarithromycin dose—classic precipitants that recur in multiphasic reactions. |
| **When to use the auto‑injector** | “Use it **as soon as you notice any of those symptoms**; do not wait for full collapse.” | In this case, all four episodes were treated with intramuscular epinephrine within minutes of symptom onset; delay would have meant prolonged hypotension and hypoxemia. |
| **How to use the device** | • Remove the safety cap.<br>• Aim for the outer thigh (avoid the upper arm, which can result in under‑delivery).<br>• Press firmly until the “click” stops and wait a few seconds before releasing.<br>• If symptoms persist or return, repeat the injection up to 3 × 30 seconds apart, but contact emergency services immediately. | The patient received 0.3 mg epinephrine from the auto‑injector—an amount below the guideline‑recommended 0.5 mg for someone who weighs 104 kg. Teaching her the proper technique maximizes each dose; repeating injections early can compensate for potential under‑dosage. |
| **Understanding the dosage** | “Because you weigh > 50 kg, the most effective adult dose is 0.5 mg. If you only have a 0.3 mg device, use it and immediately seek help—additional injections may be needed.” | The case highlights the real‑world issue of limited dosing. Patients who only have a 0.3 mg injector are at higher risk of needing extra doses and may stay on the wrong dose for longer if not told. |
| **Trigger avoidance** | • Avoid clarithromycin and other macrolides.<br>• Stay away from any confirmed beta‑lactam (clindamycin, amoxicillin, etc.) and known stinging insects.<br>• Ask your prescriber for non‑allergenic alternatives. | Her first anaphylaxis was to clarithromycin; a subsequent macrolide or any β‑lactam could precipitate recurrence, especially given the high baseline tryptase suggestive of systemic mastocytosis. |
| **Medication reminders** | “Start levocetirizine 5 mg daily; it reduces itching, hives and some wheal‑raising that may accompany an anaphylactic attack.” | The hospital started this antihistamine on discharge – chronic oral antihistamines are a safety net if inflammation boosts during future exposures. |
| **Medical “alert” and identification** | • **Wear a medical alert bracelet or card** that lists “Anaphylaxis – Clarithromycin/β‑lactam Allergy.”<br>• Bring a copy of this alert to any doctor or hospital visit. | In this patient’s case, the event was catastrophic; a medical alert could shave minutes of waiting for identification in a new emergency department. |
| **When to seek help** | • Call 911 (or local emergency number) if symptoms are serious or not fully controlled after the first injector.\n• If you are at home and symptoms are mild, try the antihistamine and monitor for 30–60 min, but always have a backup plan.\n• If you are abroad or far from an emergency department, have a local emergency number on hand. | Mexico alternate: The patient’s course was managed in ICU after the second relapse; early emergency call can prevent progression to shock. |
| **Follow‑up plan** | • **Allergy clinic assessment within 1–2 weeks** to confirm the culprit and discuss possible skin testing or oral challenge, weighing the high risk of recurrence. <br>• **Repeat tryptase** at least 24 h after full resolution to confirm baseline – a useful marker for systemic mastocytosis. <br>• **Consider a second auto‑injector** once the 0.5 mg device is available or written guidance from the prescriber. <br>• **Keep a daily log** of any new medications or exposures that begin. | The team later noted elevated tryptase and high suspicion of systemic mastocytosis; a structured follow‑up helps catch progression to overt disease or guide broadened allergen avoidance. |
| **Psychosocial support** | “It’s normal to feel anxious after a severe reaction. Consider counseling or a support group; depression or memory issues can help explain ongoing worries.” | The patient had depression and possible neuro‑cognitive symptoms likely linked to mastocytosis‑related inflammation. Highlighting mental health follow‑up can improve compliance with long‑term plans. |

### Take‑Home Script for the Patient

> “You’ve just had several episodes of anaphylaxis after clarithromycin. The auto‑injector you’re given has 0.3 mg, but because you weigh 104 kg, the recommended dose is 0.5 mg. Use it as soon as you detect any swelling, itching, breathing difficulty or dizziness. Aim for the outer thigh, press until the click stops, and wait a few seconds before removing the needle. If you feel symptoms return within 30 seconds, repeat the injection, but call emergency services immediately. Keep one device with you and a spare in your home. Avoid clarithromycin, other macrolides, and any β‑lactam antibiotics; always tell your doctor you’re allergic. Wear a medical alert bracelet, and bring a copy of this action plan to any visit. You’ll see an allergist in the next week; we’ll check your tryptase levels and decide if further testing or a different auto‑injector dose is needed. For routine care, start levocetirizine 5 mg daily to help control any hives if you’re exposed. If you ever feel anxious or notice mood changes, reach out for help. Remember, the most important thing is to act quickly and not wait for the reaction to worsen.”

This concise, evidence‑based teaching framework ensures that the patient understands **what** to watch for, **how** to intervene, **why** the treatment matters in the context of her specific presentation, and **when** to seek further care.
