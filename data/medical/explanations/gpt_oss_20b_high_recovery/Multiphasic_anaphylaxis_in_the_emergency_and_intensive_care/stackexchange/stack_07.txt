Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Management] After an initial anaphylactic event, what are the evidence‑based recommendations for the length of hospital observation, and how might these recommendations change in a patient who already experienced a biphasic or multiphasic reaction?

**Observation after an initial anaphylactic episode**

| Setting | Standard recommendation | Evidence |
|---------|--------------------------|----------|
| **General population after complete resolution** | 4 – 6 h of monitoring in a setting with continuous pulse‑oximetry and ready access to epinephrine | NICE 2018, European Academy of Allergy & Clinical Immunology 2020, Dutch NHG 2021. Observational studies show ~50 % of biphasic reactions occur within the first 6–12 h; a 6‑h window captures most uncomplicated recurrences. |
| **Patients with risk factors for a biphasic course (delayed epinephrine, severe presentation, ≥2 epinephrine doses, prior biphasic)** | ≥12 h, ideally overnight, with telemetry and IV access | UK 2021 “biphasic risk table” recommends prolonged observation of 12–24 h for those with two or more risk factors; observational data from 4475 anaphylaxis cases found 28 % of biphasic reactions in the 12‑24‑h window. |
| **Patients who have already experienced a biphasic or multiphasic reaction** | 24 h or longer, often ICU or high‑dependency unit monitoring depending on severity | The present case involved four distinct anaphylactic episodes within 12 h. German/UK consensus guidance suggests that patients with >1 recurrence in 24 h warrant 24‑h observation. In this patient, 24 h of zero‑recurrence allowed safe transfer to a normal ward. |

**Key practical points**

1. **Observe until objective improvement is durable.** After initial epinephrine, monitor until vital signs and symptoms remain stable for ≥2 h before deciding on discharge.  
2. **Risk stratification guides duration.**  
   * **Low risk:** no recurrence after 4 h → discharge home with an epinephrine auto‑injector (EAI) and written plan.  
   * **Moderate risk:** one risk factor (e.g., delayed epinephrine) → 4–6 h observation.  
   * **High risk:** ≥2 risk factors or any biphasic episode → ≥12 h; if ≥2 recurrences in 24 h → 24 h or ICU-level observation.  
3. **No proven benefit of routine corticosteroids to prevent biphasic or multiphasic recurrence.** Contemporary European, UK, and US guidelines now recommend against routine steroid use, consistent with the fact that most studies show no reduction in biphasic incidence and potential harms.  
4. **Manage recurrence as it appears.** Each new episode should be treated with a full dose of intramuscular epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg, capped at 0.5 mg), consider a continuous IV infusion of epinephrine only in ICU settings when multiple episodes occur while the patient remains symptomatic.  

**Applying the guidelines to the case**

- The patient’s initial event resolved but recurred four times in 12 h; she met the highest risk category.  
- ICU monitoring was warranted and indeed provided the patient with safety, continuous monitoring, and ready access to epinephrine.  
- After 24 h of no further recurrence, she was transferred to a normal ward and discharged after 3 days, aligning with recommendations for extended observation in multiphasic anaphylaxis.

**Bottom line**

Standard hospital observation after anaphylaxis is 4–6 h, but patients who develop one or more recurrences during that period – especially those with biphasic or multiphasic courses – should receive prolonged observation (≥12 h, often 24 h, sometimes ICU observation). This strategy balances the low but significant risk of recurrence against resource utilization, and is supported by the current evidence base and guideline recommendations.
