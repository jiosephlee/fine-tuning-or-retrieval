Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Differential Diagnosis] When a patient reports recurrent hypotension and urticaria several hours after initial anaphylaxis resolution, what differential diagnoses must be considered besides a biphasic reaction, and what clinical clues help differentiate a true biphasic event from sustained anaphylaxis with delayed resolution or residual allergen absorption?

**Recurrent hypotension and urticaria after apparent anaphylaxis resolution**

When a patient awakens a few hours after a “resolved” anaphylactic episode with new‑onset hypotension and urticaria, the clinician must broaden the differential beyond a classic biphasic reaction.  In the case of the 68‑year‑old woman who received a single 500‑mg dose of clarithromycin and experienced four separate hypotensive episodes in 24 h, the following possibilities should be considered (all compatible with the data in the case report):

| Differential | Core Clinical Clues | How it differs from a true biphasic reaction |
|--------------|----------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| **Biphasic anaphylaxis** | • Recurrent symptoms after complete symptom resolution.<br>• Onset usually within 4–12 h (but can be up to 78 h).<br>• Episodes are self‑contained and respond fully to a repeat epinephrine dose.<br>• No re‑exposure or source of the trigger. | This is the *definition* of a biphasic reaction.  Absence of a new trigger and a clean symptom-free interval support it. |
| **Sustained (refractory) anaphylaxis** | • Symptoms never fully abate; they fluctuate in intensity.<br>• Often requires multiple, escalating doses of epinephrine or continuous infusion.<br>• Incomplete response to treatment (e.g., only partial BP improvement).<br>• Can be accompanied by laboratory evidence of ongoing mast‑cell activation (persistent/ rising tryptase). | In the case, the patient’s hypotension **fully resolved** after each epinephrine dose before the next episode, making sustained anaphylaxis less likely. |
| **Residual allergen absorption** | • Initial dose of a drug with a long half‑life or formulation that permits slow release.<br>• Symptoms recur days after the last dose when the drug is still present in the circulation.<br>• Corroborated by plasma drug levels or knowledge of drug pharmacokinetics. | Clarithromycin was a single oral dose; its half‑life (3–7 h) and absence of further ingestion make residual absorption an unlikely contributor. |
| **Re‑exposure to the trigger** | • Inadvertent ingestion of the same drug or a cross‑reactive food.<br>• Documentation of a new exposure in the interval. | The patient had discontinued clarithromycin and had no further exposure; a repeat epinephrine dose after re‑exposure would also have early‑onset symptoms, not a delayed rebound. |
| **Underlying mast‑cell disorder (e.g., systemic mastocytosis)** | • Elevated baseline tryptase or rising tryptase during an episode.<br>• Organomegaly (hepatomegaly, splenomegaly) or other organ involvement.<br>• Multiple relapses or a multiphasic course. | The case report documents a **high and sustained baseline tryptase** and hepatomegaly, supporting a mast‑cell proliferation that can precipitate multiphasic anaphylaxis. |
| **Non‑allergic shock (sepsis, adrenal crisis, etc.)** | • Fever, leukocytosis, source of infection, cortisol deficiency tests.<br>• Lack of urticaria or cutaneous manifestations. | The patient’s episodes were classic anaphylaxis with urticaria and an eosinophilic‑mediated profile, making a septic or endocrine crisis unlikely. |

### Key clinical clues that favor a true biphasic event

1. **Clear, symptom‑free interval**: Full return to baseline vitals and quiet skin before the next episode (as seen after each of the patient’s four episodes).  
2. **Consistent, identical symptomatology**: New onset of hypotension, urticaria, and pruritus that mirrors the index reaction rather than broad, evolving signs.  
3. **Immediate, complete resolution after repeat epinephrine**: Each episode resolved after a single intramuscular dose, and the patient did not require escalating therapy or continuous infusion.  
4. **Absence of ongoing mediator/tryptase elevation**: Though the patient’s baseline tryptase was high (suggesting mastocytosis), tryptase levels during episodes did not markedly spike beyond the already‑elevated baseline, supporting a “refresh” of the same reaction rather than continuous activation.  
5. **No new exposure**: Clarithromycin was discontinued after the first dose and there was no evidence of subsequent ingestion or cross‑reactive exposure.  

### When it is **not biphasic**

- Persistent or worsening hypotension without full resolution after epinephrine (suggesting sustained anaphylaxis).  
- Recurrent symptoms that appear after a period of partial improvement but not complete resolution.  
- A pattern of fluctuating severity or new symptoms (e.g., sudden dyspnea, angioedema of the tongue) that accompany each relapse.  
- Laboratory evidence of uncontrolled mast‑cell degranulation (serial tryptase rise or sustained high lactate, etc.).  

**Bottom line:** In the presented case, the episodic nature with symptom‑free intervals and full responses to epinephrine support a **multiphasic (repeated biphasic) anaphylaxis** rather than sustained anaphylaxis or residual allergen absorption.  The underlying elevation of baseline tryptase and hepatomegaly point toward systemic mastocytosis as a predisposing factor and warrant longitudinal allergy follow‑up.
