Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 4: ## Investigation of Underlying Pathology: Systemic Mastocytosis

Systemic mastocytosis is a clonal mast cell disorder in which the number of mast cells is markedly increased in multiple tissues, leading to an overproduction of the inflammatory mediators that drive anaphylaxis. Understanding the cellular basis of the disease, the formal criteria used to diagnose it, and the laboratory sequence required to capture its biochemical signature clarifies why the patient in this case experienced unusually severe and recurrent reactions.

The mast cell is a tissue-resident leukocyte that stores a repertoire of histamine, proteases, leukotrienes, prostaglandins and cytokines in secretory granules. Upon activation through IgE or other stimuli, mast cells rapidly degranulate, releasing mediators that cause vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, bronchospasm, and pruritus. In systemic mastocytosis the clonal expansion of mast cells, usually driven by a D816V KIT mutation, results in a much higher mast cell burden throughout the bone marrow, skin, liver, spleen, gut, and peripheral blood. The amplified mediator load explains both the typical cutaneous findings of urticaria pigmentosa and the propensity for life‑threatening anaphylaxis, particularly when external triggers provoke an already sensitized mast cell pool.

The World Health Organization (WHO) endorses a set of major and minor diagnostic criteria to classify systemic mastocytosis. The major criterion requires a dense infiltrate of mast cells or mast cell aggregates comprising at least 15 mast cells per square millimetre in bone marrow aspirate and immunohistochemistry. One minor criterion may suffice if the major criterion is absent: (1) increased basal serum tryptase at or above 20 ng/mL, (2) aberrant expression of CD25 or CD30 on mast cells, (3) the presence of a pathogenic KIT mutation (most commonly the D816V variant), and (4) a morphologic abnormality of mast cells (e.g., atypical spindle‑shaped cells). Diagnosis requires the major criterion and one minor, or at least three of the four minor criteria. In the case reported here, the patient’s serum tryptase consistently exceeded 20 ng/mL, and the bone marrow biopsy demonstrated an increased frequency of morphologically normal mast cells. However, the sample was limited and did not allow comprehensive immunophenotyping or mutational testing, leaving only two minor criteria fulfilled; consequently, the patient could not be assigned a formal WHO diagnosis, yet a high probability of systemic mastocytosis was entertained.

Objective assessment of tryptase is a cornerstone of mastocytosis evaluation and is especially pertinent to anaphylaxis. Tryptase, the mast cell protease highly released during degranulation, shows a predictable time course: an early peak typically occurs 1–2 hours after the onset of anaphylaxis. Guidelines recommend three serial samples— (1) as soon as possible after first symptoms to capture the initial peak, (2) 1–2 hours after to confirm or refine the peak, and (3) a baseline sample taken at least 24 hours after full resolution of symptoms to assess the patient’s resting tryptase level. In the present patient, the first sampling occurred 1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode, and the second 27 hours later. Because the timing did not correspond to the early peak of the initial reaction, the procedural sampling missed the most informative data, potentially underestimating the acute tryptase surge that would have underscored mast cell overactivation.

Bone marrow biopsy findings contextualize the biochemical data. In systemic mastocytosis, a dense mast cell infiltrate often demonstrates clusters of cells with morphological abnormalities. In this patient, the biopsy revealed a modest increase in mast cells that were predominantly histologically normal, a pattern consistent with an early or indolent form of mast cell disease. Absence of CD25 or CD30 staining and lack of documented KIT mutation preclude a definitive diagnosis, yet the histochemical evidence, combined with a high baseline and a reactive tryptase peak during an anaphylactic event, strongly supports a mast cell–driven process.

The clinical constellation accompanying the laboratory abnormalities offers additional clues. An abdominal ultrasound identified a mildly enlarged liver. Hepatomegaly is a recognized manifestation of organ infiltration by mast cells in systemic mastocytosis. The patient also reported chronic mood disorders and episodic memory impairment— neuro‑psychiatric symptoms that can result from widespread mediator release affecting the central nervous system. Moreover, the history of a cutaneous response to clarithromycin and previous mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin suggest a predisposition to drug triggers, which, when confronted with a mast cell–rich milieu, can precipitate exaggerated anaphylaxis.

Systemic mastocytosis makes patients especially vulnerable to severe, prolonged, or multiphasic anaphylactic reactions. The sheer quantity of mast cells means that each subsequent triggering event releases a larger volume of mediators, amplifying the clinical picture and extending the time required to return to baseline. Additionally, clonal mast cells may have altered kinetics of degranulation, leading to delayed secondary releases that underlie biphasic or multiphasic episodes. In this context, standard epinephrine dosing may be insufficient; the under‑dosed 0.3 mg auto‑injector used initially likely contributed to the need for several subsequent doses during the patient’s course. Corticosteroid use remains controversial, as evidence does not support a prophylactic benefit in preventing recurrences, yet clinicians often administer them in high‑risk settings such as the one described.

In sum, the corroborated findings of elevated tryptase, impaired bone marrow histology, hepatomegaly, and neuro‑psychiatric symptoms collectively suggest that the patient harbored underlying systemic mastocytosis, which contributed to the intensity and recurrence of her anaphylactic episodes. Recognizing this relationship informs both acute management—by prompting higher epinephrine doses and prolonged observation—and long‑term care, including the provision of a self‑injectable epinephrine device and avoidance of known mast cell activators.
