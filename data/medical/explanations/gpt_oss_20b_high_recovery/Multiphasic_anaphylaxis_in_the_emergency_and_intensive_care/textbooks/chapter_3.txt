Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 3: ## Therapeutic Management Strategies for Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

The case of the woman in her 60s illustrates the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges that arise when anaphylaxis is not a single, self‑limited event but recurs several times within the first 24 hours. In such multiphasic courses, clinicians must decide rapidly how to dose epinephrine, whether to rely on auto‑injectors or manually prepared syringes, how aggressively to use adjunctive drugs such as antihistamines and corticosteroids, and when to admit to the ICU versus a general ward. The following discussion outlines the current evidence‑based principles that informed the care in this case and provides a framework for decision‑making in similar high‑risk scenarios.

---

### Weight‑Based Epinephrine Dosing and Maximal Limits

The cornerstone of anaphylaxis treatment is intramuscular (IM) epinephrine. Guidelines from the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommend a weight‑based dose of **0.01 mg kg⁻¹** of epinephrine, with a maximum single dose of **0.5 mg** for adults. For a 104‑kg patient—such as the index patient—this translates to 1.04 mg; however, the practice standard caps the dose at 0.5 mg to avoid the higher cardiovascular side‑effect profile of supratherapeutic doses. This ceiling also aligns with the 0.5 mg recommendation in many emergency protocols.

The clinical reality is that many emergency departments and pre‑hospital providers default to the 0.3 mg dose contained in standard adult auto‑injectors. While convenient, this dose may be **sub‑therapeutic for patients weighing >50 kg**, potentially delaying full reversal and predisposing to reco­llapses. In the present case, the first two relapses were treated with 0.3 mg auto‑injectors, followed by 0.5 mg manually prepared doses that appeared to hasten resolution. This pattern underscores how weight‑based dosing should be adhered to, even when auto‑injectors are available.

---

### Auto‑Injector Potency (0.3 mg vs 0.5 mg): Implications for Adult Care

Auto‑injectors are prized for ease and speed; they standardise dosing, reduce medication error, and minimize hesitation in staff unfamiliar with epinephrine. Yet the potency differences between **0.3 mg** and **0.5 mg** formulations have important practice implications. The 0.3 mg auto‑injector is often marketed for “adult” use, but adult weight ranges vary widely; for patients in the inter‑weight band of 50–75 kg, 0.3 mg can be 40–60 % of the calculated dose. In patients >75 kg (common in Western practice), the under‑dosing is even more pronounced.

The 2025 retrospective analysis cited in the article confirmed that patients initially treated with a 0.3 mg dose were **significantly more likely to require repeated epinephrine and ICU admission**. Consequently, many European societies now endorse the use of **0.5 mg auto‑injectors for adults, or at least verify weight‑based dosing before deployment**. In the ICU setting, where rapid, repeated dosing may be required, the pragmatic choice is to prepare **injectable epinephrine solutions** (e.g., 1:10,000 or 1:1,000) rather than relying on auto‑injectors to ensure precise dosing.

---

### Adjunctive Medications: Antihistamines, Corticosteroids, and their Evidence Base

#### Antihistamines
Allergic histamine mediators are responsible for many cutaneous and gastrointestinal manifestations. Intramuscular or intravenous H1 antihistamines (e.g., clemastine, diphenhydramine) and H2 antagonists (ranitidine, famotidine) are typically added to stabilize skin and mucosal symptoms. In the case, 2 mg intravenous clemastine was repeated at the second relapse and again during later episodes, which likely contributed to the attenuation of pruritus and urticaria. No evidence suggests that antihistamines prevent recurrence; however, they remain useful for symptom control when administered early.

#### Corticosteroids
Corticosteroid use has long been taught as a prophylaxis against biphasic anaphylaxis. Yet systematic reviews from 2017 onward consistently show **no convincing benefit** in preventing recurrent episodes, and some studies even indicate an association with longer hospital stay or higher ICU admission rates. In the present patient, steroid use (30 mg prednisone orally for 5 days plus an IV dexamethasone 8 mg) was initiated after the first recurrence due to conventional practice. Subsequent observations showed a tapering intensity of episodes, but whether steroids contributed is uncertain. Current guidelines (e.g., EAACI, NICE) advise **reserved corticosteroid use**, mainly for chronic urticaria or severe systemic reactions refractory to epinephrine and antihistamines.

#### Other Adjuncts
The case did not include epinephrine intramuscular pre‑loading doses or pre‑emptive epinephrine pumps. In a multiphasic setting, the decision to initiate continuous intravenous (IV) epinephrine hinges on the stabilization of the patient, the presence of ongoing mediator release, and the feasibility of a continuous infusion (e.g., 0.05–0.10 µg kg⁻¹ min⁻¹). These infusions are associated with tachycardia and ventricular arrhythmias and should be delivered by trained intensivists under cardiac monitoring.

---

### ICU Admission Criteria and Specialized Monitoring Protocols

Multiphasic anaphylaxis is a prototypical indication for ICU care. The patient’s first recurrence required ICU admission following the development of profound hypotension and hypoxemia despite initial stabilization. ICU admission should be considered when:

1. **Recurrent circulatory collapse** despite at least two appropriately dosed IM epinephrine injections.
2. **Respiratory compromise** (e.g., persistent hypoxemia, requiring supplemental oxygen >6 L min⁻¹) or airway edema.
3. **Evidence of multiorgan involvement** (e.g., arrhythmias, CNS symptoms).
4. **Need for continuous cardiac telemetry**, advanced respiratory support, or repeated drug administration under observation.

The ICU team provides continuous fluid resuscitation, frequent vital sign checks, and ready availability of vasopressors, corticosteroids, antihistamines, and resuscitative fluids. Collaborative decision‑making between allergology and critical care teams can refine therapeutic goals.

---

### Continuous IV Epinephrine Infusion vs Expectant Treatment

In refractory or multiphasic anaphylaxis, continuous IV epinephrine infusion is theoretically attractive: it maintains a steady catecholamine level, potentially suppressing mast cell degranulation and preventing relapses. However, evidence remains sparse, and infusion carries increased risk of arrhythmias and hypertension. Clinical experience varies: some centers adopt a **“pre‑emptive infusion”** after two relapses, while others prefer an “expectant” approach—monitor closely and administer repeated IM epinephrine at the first sign of deterioration.

The decision depends on:

- **Severity of the most recent episode** (e.g., hypotension <90 mm Hg, need >500 mL fluid).
- **Time elapsed since last relapse** (the risk curve for multiphasic reactions peaks within 8–12 h but can extend up to 48 h).
- **Resource availability** (continuous infusion pumps, cardiac monitoring).
- **Patient comorbidities** (e.g., cardiac disease increases risk of catecholamine toxicity).

In an institutional protocol, it is reasonable to **reserve continuous infusion for patients with ≥3 recurrences within 12 h**, after multidisciplinary consensus. In the case report, the care team chose to *not* initiate infusion, preferring to observe with robust monitoring; the patient so turned over after 24 h of symptom‑free interval.

---

### Risk‑Stratified Observation Periods and Decision Algorithms

Traditional guidelines recommend a **4‑ to 6‑hour observation** period after full resolution of anaphylaxis. However, this interval fails to capture patients who develop recurrences beyond 12 h. Several risk factors—delayed epinephrine administration, severe initial presentation, requirement of multiple epinephrine doses, history of prior biphasic reaction—have been proposed to stratify patients into low, intermediate, or high risk for recurrence.

A practical algorithm might assign a score (e.g., **2 points** for each risk factor present). A cumulative score of ≥4 could trigger ICU admission and continuous observation >24 h, while a score of 1–3 might warrant extended high‑level monitoring (e.g., telemetry, 24 h) in a step‑down unit. In the reported case, the patient presented with multiple risk factors (age >60, weight >100 kg, history of severe reaction, multiple IM epinephrine doses), justifying ICU admission and a near‑24‑hour observation, which ultimately proved sufficient.

---

In summary, multiphasic anaphylaxis demands a nuanced approach to epinephrine dosing, a critical evaluation of adjunctive therapy, and a tailored observation strategy. Weight‑based epinephrine dosing remains essential, auto‑injector potency should be matched to body weight, corticosteroids have limited evidence for prophylaxis against recurrence, and ICU resources should be mobilised for high‑risk, multi‑recurrent cases. Continuous infusion is a potential yet unproven strategy, reserved for refractory situations. Applying a risk‑stratified observation model helps balance patient safety with efficient use of intensive care resources.
