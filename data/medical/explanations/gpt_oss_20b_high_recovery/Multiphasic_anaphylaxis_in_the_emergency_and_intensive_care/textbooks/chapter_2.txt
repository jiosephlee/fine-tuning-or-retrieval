Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnosis of Anaphylaxis with Biphasic/Multiphasic Course  

Establishing a diagnosis of anaphylaxis in the emergency department relies on both clinical presentation and the rapid application of validated diagnostic criteria. In the case presented, the patient’s immediate symptom onset after oral clarithromycin, the rapid development of hypotension, hypoxaemia, urticaria, facial angioedema and pruritus, and the swift reversal of these signs following intramuscular epinephrine, fulfill the American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology/International Consensus on Allergy and Clinical Immunology (AAACI/IHS) definition of anaphylaxis. The AAACI/IHS criteria require either (1) involvement of the skin or mucosal tissue (hives, pruritus, flushing, or swelling of lips/face/eyes) PLUS a second organ system (respiratory compromise, cardiovascular collapse, gastrointestinal symptoms), OR (2) hypotension (or a reduction in systolic blood pressure ≥ 30 mm Hg) or reduced cardiac output in a patient with known or possible IgE‑mediated sensitization, or (3) anaphylaxis that improves after epinephrine administration. The patient satisfied criterion 1 (cutaneous angioedema and urticaria with hypotension) and criterion 3 (prompt recovery after epinephrine). 

**Key features supporting anaphylaxis in this patient**

- **Temporal correlation**: Symptoms began 1 hour after the first clarithromycin dose, a time frame compatible with IgE‑mediated reactivity.
- **Multi‑system involvement**: Cutaneous (flushing, urticaria), cardiovascular (severe hypotension 74/50 mm Hg), and respiratory (hypoxaemia 88 % on room air) manifestations.
- **Exclusion of alternative causes**: No documented fever, rash of a different morphology, or bloody diarrhoea that would suggest other drug reactions; the patient’s background medications do not explain acute hypoxaemia and hypotension.
- **Positive response to epinephrine**: Rapid normalization of blood pressure and resolution of angioedema confirm the diagnosis of anaphylaxis and exclude a benign transient reaction.

**Definition and timing of biphasic and multiphasic reactions**

A biphasic reaction is defined as the recurrence of any anaphylactic symptoms after apparent clinical resolution, occurring without re‑exposure to the culprit allergen, and usually within 24 hours of the initial episode. Multiphasic reactions involve two or more such recurrences. In this case, after the first epinephrine dose and subsequent stabilization, the patient experienced three additional relapses over a 12‑hour period, meeting the criteria for a multiphasic reaction. All recurrences reproduced the same pattern—hypotension, flushing, urticaria, and dyspnoea—thus distinguishing them from an ongoing or partially treated episode.

**Differential diagnosis**

- **Sepsis**: Fever, WBC elevation, and a sustained source of infection are typically present. In this patient, blood cultures were pending and the patient was afebrile with normal CBC except for transient eosinophilia later in the stay.
- **Thyroid storm**: Requires recent thyroid disease and hallmark signs such as tachycardia, delirium, diarrhoea, and hyperthermia. The patient’s thyroid function tests were within normal limits, and her symptoms lacked hyperthermia or tachycardia.
- **Angioedema**: Isolated swelling of lips, tongue, or face without other systemic signs. The patient’s episode involved generalized urticaria, hypotension, and dyspnoea, which exceed isolated angioedema.
- **Drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS)**: A delayed reaction (≥ 2 weeks), with skin rash, eosinophilia, fever, and multi‑organ involvement. The rapid onset of symptoms and their recurrence pattern made DRESS unlikely.

These considerations strengthen the conclusion that the patient experienced anaphylaxis rather than an alternative severe drug reaction or systemic disease.

**Recognition of symptom recurrence and criteria for a true second phase**

Clinicians must differentiate a true biphasic rebound from transient physiological fluctuation or from complications of ongoing treatment. The following triad identifies a real second phase:

1. **Re‑emergence of at least one of the initial clinical manifestations** (e.g., facial angioedema, pruritic urticaria, hypotension).
2. **No new exposure to the triggering agent** and absence of a pharmacological cause for the relapse.
3. **Clinical response to standard anaphylaxis therapy** (second epinephrine dose) and subsequent resolution.

In this case, the patient developed new hypotension (70/45 mm Hg), pronounced flushing, and a diffuse rash approximately 5 hours after the first episode had resolved. Intramuscular epinephrine rapidly restored baseline haemodynamics, confirming a true biphasic event.

**Role and limitations of laboratory investigations**

**Serum tryptase** is the most specific laboratory marker for mast‑cell mediator release. Guidelines recommend three samples:  
- Within 1 hour of symptom onset (to capture peak).  
- 1–2 hours later (to confirm persistence).  
- ≥24 hours after full clinical resolution (for baseline).  

In this patient, the first tryptase measurement was taken 1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode, and a second at 27 hours after the last relapse. Although these values were markedly elevated and remain high at follow‑up, the lack of an early sample precludes definitive confirmation of peak tryptase, and thus limits its diagnostic precision for the original episode. Nonetheless, the persistently high baseline tryptase supports a mast‑cell disorder (systemic mastocytosis), which can predispose to severe and recurrent anaphylaxis.

**Complete blood count (CBC)** can reveal eosinophilia, a marker for allergic inflammation or DRESS, but in this patient only mild eosinophilia was observed during follow‑up and was not present during the acute episodes, arguing against DRESS.

**Liver function tests (LFTs)** are useful to rule out hepatic involvement in other severe drug reactions. Mild hepatomegaly on ultrasound suggested hepatic involvement, but LFTs remained within normal limits, making hepatic failure unlikely.

In summary, a swift application of the AAACI/IHS criteria, recognition of a biphasic/multiphasic pattern, systematic exclusion of other severe reactions, and judicious use of laboratory markers—especially tryptase—are essential for accurate diagnosis. These steps guided the clinicians in this case to appropriately identify a multiphasic anaphylaxis, tailor subsequent monitoring and treatment, and finally uncover an underlying predisposition (mild systemic mastocytosis) that explained the prolonged course.
