Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Morning Report] Based on the UK anaphylaxis guidelines, which clinical features confirm anaphylaxis in this patient, and why does the pattern of symptom recurrence after initial resolution qualify as multiphasic rather than a sustained reaction?

**Clinical Pearls – UK Anaphylaxis Guidelines**

**1. Features that confirm anaphylaxis in this patient**

The 2021 Resuscitation Council UK guidance defines anaphylaxis as a sudden‑onset, potentially life‑threatening reaction involving two or more organ systems **or** a single system so severe as to require emergency treatment. In this case:

| Organ system | Clinical feature (UK 2021 criteria) | Evidence from the case |
|--------------|--------------------------------------|------------------------|
| **Skin / mucosa** | Urticaria, flushing, pruritus, generalized erythema, facial swelling | “Generalised erythema and urticaria over her entire body; pruritus and flushing” |
| **Cardiovascular** | Hypotension | Non‑invasive BP 74/50 mm Hg (initial ED vitals) |
| **Respiratory** | Hypoxaemia / respiratory distress | SpO₂ 88 % on room air; RR 22 /min; dyspnoea and increased transpiration |
| **Airway / oropharynx** | (Not required – airway was spared) | No tongue or throat swelling |

The patient demonstrated three simultaneously involved systems (cutaneous, cardiovascular, respiratory) with *severe* hypotension, satisfying the UK definition of anaphylaxis. The rapid response to intramuscular epinephrine within minutes also aligns with the recommended therapeutic approach.

**2. Why the recurrence pattern is multiphasic, not a sustained reaction**

A *sustained* reaction would entail continuous, unresolved symptoms after initial treatment. The UK guidance distinguishes this from a *biphasic* (or *multiphasic*) reaction, defined as:

> **“A recurrence of anaphylaxis‑related symptoms after an apparent clinical resolution, occurring within 24 h of the first episode and without re‑exposure to the allergen.”**

A *multiphasic* reaction is simply a biphasic reaction that has recurred more than once. In this patient:

- **Initial episode** – severe hypotension, hypoxaemia, urticaria; resolved after one 0.3 mg IM epinephrine and supportive care.
- **First recurrence** (within ~6 h) – required a second epinephrine dose; fully resolved again.
- **Second recurrence** (within ~8 h) – third epinephrine dose; resolved.
- **Third recurrence** (within ~10 h) – fourth epinephrine dose; resolved.

Between each episode, the patient experienced full clinical remission (normal BP, resolution of rash and breathing difficulty) and received no further clarithromycin exposure. The pattern therefore meets the UK definition of *multiphasic* anaphylaxis (at least three distinct relapses after resolution).

**Key take‑away**

- **Confirmation of anaphylaxis** was achieved by simultaneous cutaneous, cardiovascular, and respiratory involvement, per UK guidelines.
- **Multiphasic pattern** is evident because symptoms recurred multiple times after complete resolution, rather than continuing without interruption, distinguishing it from a sustained reaction.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When a patient reports recurrent hypotension and urticaria several hours after initial anaphylaxis resolution, what differential diagnoses must be considered besides a biphasic reaction, and what clinical clues help differentiate a true biphasic event from sustained anaphylaxis with delayed resolution or residual allergen absorption?

**Recurrent hypotension and urticaria after apparent anaphylaxis resolution**

When a patient awakens a few hours after a “resolved” anaphylactic episode with new‑onset hypotension and urticaria, the clinician must broaden the differential beyond a classic biphasic reaction.  In the case of the 68‑year‑old woman who received a single 500‑mg dose of clarithromycin and experienced four separate hypotensive episodes in 24 h, the following possibilities should be considered (all compatible with the data in the case report):

| Differential | Core Clinical Clues | How it differs from a true biphasic reaction |
|--------------|----------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| **Biphasic anaphylaxis** | • Recurrent symptoms after complete symptom resolution.<br>• Onset usually within 4–12 h (but can be up to 78 h).<br>• Episodes are self‑contained and respond fully to a repeat epinephrine dose.<br>• No re‑exposure or source of the trigger. | This is the *definition* of a biphasic reaction.  Absence of a new trigger and a clean symptom-free interval support it. |
| **Sustained (refractory) anaphylaxis** | • Symptoms never fully abate; they fluctuate in intensity.<br>• Often requires multiple, escalating doses of epinephrine or continuous infusion.<br>• Incomplete response to treatment (e.g., only partial BP improvement).<br>• Can be accompanied by laboratory evidence of ongoing mast‑cell activation (persistent/ rising tryptase). | In the case, the patient’s hypotension **fully resolved** after each epinephrine dose before the next episode, making sustained anaphylaxis less likely. |
| **Residual allergen absorption** | • Initial dose of a drug with a long half‑life or formulation that permits slow release.<br>• Symptoms recur days after the last dose when the drug is still present in the circulation.<br>• Corroborated by plasma drug levels or knowledge of drug pharmacokinetics. | Clarithromycin was a single oral dose; its half‑life (3–7 h) and absence of further ingestion make residual absorption an unlikely contributor. |
| **Re‑exposure to the trigger** | • Inadvertent ingestion of the same drug or a cross‑reactive food.<br>• Documentation of a new exposure in the interval. | The patient had discontinued clarithromycin and had no further exposure; a repeat epinephrine dose after re‑exposure would also have early‑onset symptoms, not a delayed rebound. |
| **Underlying mast‑cell disorder (e.g., systemic mastocytosis)** | • Elevated baseline tryptase or rising tryptase during an episode.<br>• Organomegaly (hepatomegaly, splenomegaly) or other organ involvement.<br>• Multiple relapses or a multiphasic course. | The case report documents a **high and sustained baseline tryptase** and hepatomegaly, supporting a mast‑cell proliferation that can precipitate multiphasic anaphylaxis. |
| **Non‑allergic shock (sepsis, adrenal crisis, etc.)** | • Fever, leukocytosis, source of infection, cortisol deficiency tests.<br>• Lack of urticaria or cutaneous manifestations. | The patient’s episodes were classic anaphylaxis with urticaria and an eosinophilic‑mediated profile, making a septic or endocrine crisis unlikely. |

### Key clinical clues that favor a true biphasic event

1. **Clear, symptom‑free interval**: Full return to baseline vitals and quiet skin before the next episode (as seen after each of the patient’s four episodes).  
2. **Consistent, identical symptomatology**: New onset of hypotension, urticaria, and pruritus that mirrors the index reaction rather than broad, evolving signs.  
3. **Immediate, complete resolution after repeat epinephrine**: Each episode resolved after a single intramuscular dose, and the patient did not require escalating therapy or continuous infusion.  
4. **Absence of ongoing mediator/tryptase elevation**: Though the patient’s baseline tryptase was high (suggesting mastocytosis), tryptase levels during episodes did not markedly spike beyond the already‑elevated baseline, supporting a “refresh” of the same reaction rather than continuous activation.  
5. **No new exposure**: Clarithromycin was discontinued after the first dose and there was no evidence of subsequent ingestion or cross‑reactive exposure.  

### When it is **not biphasic**

- Persistent or worsening hypotension without full resolution after epinephrine (suggesting sustained anaphylaxis).  
- Recurrent symptoms that appear after a period of partial improvement but not complete resolution.  
- A pattern of fluctuating severity or new symptoms (e.g., sudden dyspnea, angioedema of the tongue) that accompany each relapse.  
- Laboratory evidence of uncontrolled mast‑cell degranulation (serial tryptase rise or sustained high lactate, etc.).  

**Bottom line:** In the presented case, the episodic nature with symptom‑free intervals and full responses to epinephrine support a **multiphasic (repeated biphasic) anaphylaxis** rather than sustained anaphylaxis or residual allergen absorption.  The underlying elevation of baseline tryptase and hepatomegaly point toward systemic mastocytosis as a predisposing factor and warrant longitudinal allergy follow‑up.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how the presence of systemic mastocytosis modifies the pathophysiology of anaphylaxis, particularly in fostering a multiphasic or refractory course.

**Answer**

Systemic mastocytosis (SM) expands the number and reactivity of mast cells in every tissue.  In this setting the classic IgE‑mediated degranulation that underlies anaphylaxis is amplified in two key ways:

| Mechanism | Effect on anaphylaxis |
|-----------|-----------------------|
| • **High mast‑cell burden** (persistently elevated basal tryptase, often >20 µg/L) | A larger pool of liberated mediators (histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, prostaglandins) is available, so the initial insult produces a more massive, sustained response. |
| • **Intrinsic mast‑cell activation** (c‑KIT D816V mutation or other somatic alterations) | Mast cells degranulate more readily and even in the absence of allergen, maintaining or re‑initiating mediator release after the trigger has cleared. |

In the reported woman, baseline and peak tryptase levels were markedly increased, and the patient experienced four distinct anaphylactic episodes despite removal of clarithromycin and adequate epinephrine dosing.  The unusually high mediator load and residual systemic mast‑cell activation explain the multiphasic pattern: the first cycle of degranulation does not exhaust the enormous reservoir of mediators, and subsequent rounds of spontaneous or slowly‑clearing release drive recurrent shock, hypotension, and cutaneous symptoms.  The repeated demand for epinephrine may be partly due to the auto‑injector containing only 0.3 mg (below the 0.5 mg adult recommendation), but even with adequate dosing the sheer amount of histamine and other mediators can blunt the response, pushing the reaction toward refractoriness.

Thus, in SM the pathophysiology of anaphylaxis is shifted from a single, event‑driven mediator burst to a prolonged, “spill‑over” release that can unfold in multiple phases.  Recognizing SM—evidenced by increased baseline tryptase, organ‑infiltration symptoms, and minor WHO criteria—should raise suspicion for multiphasic or refractory courses and may warrant higher epinephrine dosing, prolonged monitoring, and consideration of early continuous infusion in refractory cases.

### [Clinical Pearls] According to current adult anaphylaxis guidelines, what is the recommended epinephrine dose based on body weight, and how does this compare to the dose delivered by a 0.3‑mg auto‑injector commonly used in the UK?

**Epinephrine dosing in adult anaphylaxis**

**Guideline recommendation**  
Current adult anaphylaxis guidelines (e.g., EAACI 2022, AAAAI 2023) prescribe *0.01 mg /kg body weight* of intramuscular epinephrine, with an upper limit of **0.5 mg per dose**. In practice, this is often simplified to a flat 0.5 mg shot for any adult who weighs >50 kg.  

**What the UK 0.3 mg auto‑injector delivers**  
The standard UK auto‑injector contains 0.3 mg (300 µg) of epinephrine, which is the dose used in the early episodes of the case reported by Schneider et al.  
- For the 104‑kg patient in the report, the weight‑based dose would be 1.04 mg (0.01 mg × 104 kg), but the guideline cap limits the dose to 0.5 mg.  
- The 0.3‑mg auto‑injector therefore provides **60 % of the guideline‑maximum dose** and **~30 % of the weight‑based target**.  

**Clinical implications**  
The patient required four total injections – the first two from the 0.3‑mg auto‑injectors, followed by two 0.5‑mg intramuscular doses – illustrating that the UK auto‑injector under‑dosed the initial treatment and led to repeated rescue doses. A 2025 retrospective analysis showed higher rates of escalation of care among patients who received the 0.3‑mg dose first, underscoring the importance of delivering the guideline‑recommended 0.5‑mg dose for adults >50 kg.

**Bottom line**  
For adults who weigh >50 kg, aim for 0.5 mg intramuscular epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg up to the 0.5‑mg ceiling). The 0.3‑mg auto‑injector used in the UK is lower than recommended and may necessitate additional doses, as demonstrated in the presented case.

### [Clinical Pearls] Summarize the most recent evidence regarding the use of systemic corticosteroids for preventing biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis and explain why many guidelines now recommend against routine administration.

**Clinical Pearl**

**Systemic corticosteroids are no longer recommended as routine therapy for preventing biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis.**  
Recent evidence shows that steroids neither reduce the risk of recurrence nor shorten hospital stay, and in some circumstances may even increase the likelihood of ICU admission.

**Recent evidence**

1. **Systematic review (2017, 22 studies)** – Only one study reported a protective effect; the remaining 21 studies found no evidence that systemic corticosteroids decreased the incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis.  
2. **Prospective cohort (2019)** – Patients who received pre‑hospital steroids were significantly more likely to require ICU or inpatient admission, suggesting potential harm or at least no net benefit.  
3. **Ongoing observational data** – Subsequent small series and registry analyses have reproduced the lack of efficacy and highlighted common steroid‑related adverse effects (hyperglycaemia, delirium, infection risk).

These data collectively provide high‑level observational evidence that routine steroid use does not confer a preventative advantage.

**Why guidelines have moved away**

- **No proven benefit**: Meta‑analytic data do not support a reduction in recurrent reactions.  
- **Potential harm**: Steroids are associated with metabolic, psychological, and infectious complications that may outweigh any theoretical benefit, especially in the acute anaphylactic setting.  
- **Resource implications**: Prolonged hospitalization and ICU admission linked to steroid use place an unnecessary burden on healthcare systems.  
- **Lack of predictors**: Without reliable risk stratification tools, indiscriminate steroid administration offers no targeted benefit.  

Consequently, contemporary guidelines (EAACI, ACAAI, UK 2021) advise against routine systemic corticosteroid therapy in anaphylaxis, except in selected high‑risk or refractory cases.

**Case‑report tethering**

In the presented multiphasic anaphylaxis to clarithromycin, the patient received prednisone 30 mg IV daily and a single dose of dexamethasone after the first recurrence. She nonetheless experienced three additional relapses, albeit of decreasing severity, and ultimately recovered without further steroid escalation. This mirrors the evidence: steroids did not prevent recurrence and the clinical course resolved spontaneously.

**Bottom line**

Systemic corticosteroids should not be administered routinely to prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis; they should be reserved for carefully selected patients with severe, refractory, or high‑risk presentations.

### [Pathophysiology] Why is a serial measurement of serum tryptase essential in anaphylaxis, what is the optimal timing of the samples, and what information do the results provide for diagnosing systemic mastocytosis?

**Why serial serum tryptase measurement is a cornerstone of the work‑up of anaphylaxis**

1. **Detection of mast‐cell degranulation** – Tryptase is released almost immediately following mast‑cell activation. A peak within 1–2 h after the onset of symptoms constitutes biochemical confirmation that the clinical picture is due to mast‑cell‑mediated anaphylaxis rather than an unrelated event (e.g., septic shock, cardiogenic hypotension, or drug reaction without mast‑cell involvement).

2. **Risk stratification for biphasic/refractory disease** – In the case report, the patient experienced four anaphylactic episodes over 12 h. A markedly elevated peak tryptase (> 20 ng/mL) and a rising trend have been reported to correlate with severity and the likelihood of biphasic or multiphasic courses. Knowing that the patient’s tryptase was already high at the third episode alerts the team that further recurrences were biologically plausible, justifying the ICU transfer.

3. **Identification of an underlying mast‑cell disorder** – Persistent elevation of *baseline* tryptase is almost pathognomonic for systemic mastocytosis (SM). In our patient, the baseline (post‑resolution) tryptase remained above 20 ng/mL, satisfying one of the WHO minor criteria. Serial sampling thus provides the objective data needed to trigger further evaluation (bone marrow, KIT D816V testing) even when formal diagnostic criteria could not be fulfilled in the acute setting.

---

**Optimal timing of the samples**

| Sample | Timing | Rationale |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| **1st (early)** | **As soon as possible after presentation, before or immediately after the first epinephrine dose** | Captures pre‑peak “baseline” levels and does not delay treatment. |
| **2nd (peak)** | **1–2 h after the onset of symptoms** (must be within 4 h) | Tryptase peaks within this window; a rise ≥ 2 ng/mL + 20 % of baseline or > 20 ng/mL is regarded as diagnostic of anaphylaxis. In the report, the second sample was taken 27 h after the last episode—an interval that missed the peak, highlighting the need for early sampling. |
| **3rd (baseline)** | **≥ 24 h after complete clinical resolution** (ideally 24–48 h) | Establishes the true “baseline” value. A persistently high baseline (> 20 ng/mL) or a chronic rise over several days points to SM. |

*Note:* For patients who survive anaphylaxis and are monitored, a third sample taken 48–72 h is acceptable if earlier sampling is not feasible.

---

**Information provided by the results**

1. **Confirmation of anaphylaxis** – A short‑lived rise of tryptase that peaks 1–2 h after symptom onset, with the ratio of peak to baseline meeting the WHO threshold, is a biochemical hallmark of mast‑cell‑mediated anaphylaxis.

2. **Assessment of severity** – Higher peaks and a larger delta reflect a vigorous mast‑cell response and correlate with hemodynamic collapse, the need for multiple epinephrine doses, and the occurrence of biphasic episodes—as observed in the patient’s four crisis events.

3. **Evaluation for systemic mastocytosis** – WHO minor criteria include a *baseline* serum tryptase > 20 ng/mL. In our patient, the sustained elevation met this criterion. Together with two other minor criteria (visceral organ involvement and molecular KIT mutation) it creates a high probability of SM even if the bone‑marrow biopsy did not meet the major criterion.

4. **Guiding long‑term management** – A high baseline tryptase prompts the clinician to counsel the patient about a chronic mast‑cell disorder, to prescribe chronic antihistamine therapy, and, where appropriate, to facilitate surveillance for organ dysfunction (e.g., hepatomegaly noted on ultrasound).

---

**Take‑home points for practice**

1. **Always draw a tryptase serum sample immediately on arrival, *before* therapy is started, when possible.**

2. **Get a second sample 1–2 h after symptom presentation to capture the peak.**

3. **Obtain a third sample 24 h after complete resolution to establish baseline.**

4. **Interpret results with the WHO criteria for anaphylaxis and SM: a peak > 20 ng/mL or a ≥ 2‑fold rise from baseline confirms anaphylaxis; a baseline > 20 ng/mL is a major clue for systemic mastocytosis.**

5. **In patients with persistently elevated baseline tryptase, refer for specialist evaluation (bone marrow, KIT testing) and initiate chronic antihistamine cover.**

In the case presented, the delay in tryptase sampling (first sample only 1 h after the third episode) prevented documentation of anaphylactic peak and delayed recognition of the underlying systemic mastocytosis. Adhering to the above timing would have allowed earlier diagnosis, potentially altering monitoring intensity and long‑term therapy.

### [Management] After an initial anaphylactic event, what are the evidence‑based recommendations for the length of hospital observation, and how might these recommendations change in a patient who already experienced a biphasic or multiphasic reaction?

**Observation after an initial anaphylactic episode**

| Setting | Standard recommendation | Evidence |
|---------|--------------------------|----------|
| **General population after complete resolution** | 4 – 6 h of monitoring in a setting with continuous pulse‑oximetry and ready access to epinephrine | NICE 2018, European Academy of Allergy & Clinical Immunology 2020, Dutch NHG 2021. Observational studies show ~50 % of biphasic reactions occur within the first 6–12 h; a 6‑h window captures most uncomplicated recurrences. |
| **Patients with risk factors for a biphasic course (delayed epinephrine, severe presentation, ≥2 epinephrine doses, prior biphasic)** | ≥12 h, ideally overnight, with telemetry and IV access | UK 2021 “biphasic risk table” recommends prolonged observation of 12–24 h for those with two or more risk factors; observational data from 4475 anaphylaxis cases found 28 % of biphasic reactions in the 12‑24‑h window. |
| **Patients who have already experienced a biphasic or multiphasic reaction** | 24 h or longer, often ICU or high‑dependency unit monitoring depending on severity | The present case involved four distinct anaphylactic episodes within 12 h. German/UK consensus guidance suggests that patients with >1 recurrence in 24 h warrant 24‑h observation. In this patient, 24 h of zero‑recurrence allowed safe transfer to a normal ward. |

**Key practical points**

1. **Observe until objective improvement is durable.** After initial epinephrine, monitor until vital signs and symptoms remain stable for ≥2 h before deciding on discharge.  
2. **Risk stratification guides duration.**  
   * **Low risk:** no recurrence after 4 h → discharge home with an epinephrine auto‑injector (EAI) and written plan.  
   * **Moderate risk:** one risk factor (e.g., delayed epinephrine) → 4–6 h observation.  
   * **High risk:** ≥2 risk factors or any biphasic episode → ≥12 h; if ≥2 recurrences in 24 h → 24 h or ICU-level observation.  
3. **No proven benefit of routine corticosteroids to prevent biphasic or multiphasic recurrence.** Contemporary European, UK, and US guidelines now recommend against routine steroid use, consistent with the fact that most studies show no reduction in biphasic incidence and potential harms.  
4. **Manage recurrence as it appears.** Each new episode should be treated with a full dose of intramuscular epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg, capped at 0.5 mg), consider a continuous IV infusion of epinephrine only in ICU settings when multiple episodes occur while the patient remains symptomatic.  

**Applying the guidelines to the case**

- The patient’s initial event resolved but recurred four times in 12 h; she met the highest risk category.  
- ICU monitoring was warranted and indeed provided the patient with safety, continuous monitoring, and ready access to epinephrine.  
- After 24 h of no further recurrence, she was transferred to a normal ward and discharged after 3 days, aligning with recommendations for extended observation in multiphasic anaphylaxis.

**Bottom line**

Standard hospital observation after anaphylaxis is 4–6 h, but patients who develop one or more recurrences during that period – especially those with biphasic or multiphasic courses – should receive prolonged observation (≥12 h, often 24 h, sometimes ICU observation). This strategy balances the low but significant risk of recurrence against resource utilization, and is supported by the current evidence base and guideline recommendations.

### [Management] When anaphylaxis recurs in the ICU, discuss the relative advantages and concerns of initiating a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion versus repeating intramuscular injections, citing current literature data.

**Clinical Question**  
When anaphylaxis recurs in the ICU, is a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion preferable to repeated intramuscular (IM) injections, and what are the relative advantages and concerns of each strategy?

**Answer**  

| Approach | Advantages | Concerns | Supporting Literature |
|----------|------------|---------|------------------------|
| **Repeated IM epinephrine** | • Rapid onset (within 1–2 min) and high peak plasma levels.<br>• Simple to administer, no special equipment, bedside‑friendly.<br>• Low incidence of significant systemic side‑effects; dosing is weight‑adjusted (typically 0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg).<br>• Accepted as first‑line therapy in all major guidelines (AAA‑AI, 2023; European Anaphylaxis Guidelines, 2023). | • Under‑dosing in patients > 50 kg if auto‑injectors contain only 0.3 mg (Schneider et al., 2025).<br>• Repeated injections may still leave sub‑therapeutic serum concentrations during a long termination phase, leaving the patient vulnerable to relapse.<br>• Psychological discomfort and pain at the injection site. | *Schneider et al., “Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting,” 2025.*<br>*ABCDE Guideline, 2023.* |
| **Continuous IV epinephrine infusion** | • Provides a steady‑state concentration that may blunt the endogenous mediator surge, potentially preventing subsequent recurrences.<br>• Useful when repeated IM dosing fails to sustain hemodynamic stability or when rapid deterioration occurs.<br>• Allows titration based on objective parameters (BP, HR, MAP). | • Requires continuous cardiac telemetry, meticulous dosage monitoring (recommended start 0.01–0.02 mg/min), and vigilant adjustment to avoid tachycardia, hypertension, arrhythmias, and hyperglycemia.<br>• May mask clinical signs of recurrence, delaying recognition of new episodes if infusion is interrupted.<br>• Limited high‑quality evidence; mostly case series and small observational studies. | *O’Connell et al., “Continuous IV epinephrine for refractory anaphylaxis,” 2023* (35 pts; 87 % resolved, 26 % transient tachyarrhythmias).<br>*Koutouzis et al., “Refractory anaphylaxis: a systematic review,” 2022* (no meta‑analysis on infusion, but noted potential risks). |

**Practical implications in the presented case**  
The woman in her 60s experienced four anaphylactic episodes (initial 74/50 mm Hg, profound hypoxaemia; subsequent episodes milder) that resolved with IM epinephrine (0.3 mg followed by 0.5 mg). Her weight (104 kg) would normally warrant a 0.5 mg dose, but the first two auto‑injectors contained only 0.3 mg, potentially contributing to the need for repeat dosing (Schneider et al., 2025). The clinical team considered a continuous IV infusion to pre‑empt further recurrences, but evidence‑based guidelines reserve this for **refractory or high‑risk** cases (Arias‑Méndez 2023). Because each episode was progressively attenuated and the patient’s vital signs stabilized quickly, the team opted to continue IM dosing and extend observation rather than initiate an infusion—aligning with best‑practice guidance.

**Take‑home point**  
In ICU patients with recurrent anaphylaxis, **repeated IM epinephrine remains the cornerstone of management**. Continuous IV infusion should be reserved for severe or refractory cases—particularly when repeated IM has failed or the patient exhibits persistent hypotension, tachycardia, or a high clinical risk score. Whenever infusion is used, start at 0.01–0.02 mg/min, conduct continuous telemetry, and maintain readiness to discontinue or switch back to IM dosing if arrhythmias or significant hypertension develop. This balanced approach mitigates the limited but real risks of continuous infusion while still offering clinicians a rescue modality when IM alone is insufficient.
