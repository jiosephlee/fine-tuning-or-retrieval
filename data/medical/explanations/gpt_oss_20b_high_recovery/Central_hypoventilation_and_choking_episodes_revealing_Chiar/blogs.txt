Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Acquired Ondine’s Curse: When a Chiari Malformation Takes Your Sleep Breath Away  

## The case that made us re‑screen a 14‑year‑old CT  

Picture a woman in her early 40s who spends her nights scrambling for a glass of water and her daytime hours terrified of a single, dreamless swallow.  She’s had “choking” episodes for the last seven years— sudden, self‑resolved coughing and gasping that interrupt meals and wake her from sleep—yet three flexible laryngoscopies, two upper GI endoscopies, and a normal chest CT all failed to give a diagnosis.  It wasn’t until she began checking her home pulse oximeter during the COVID lockdown that she discovered that, quite often, her saturation plummeted into the 70 %–80 % range, *without* any shortness of breath or anxiety.  

The turning point came when a chest‑freezing accounting audit at an airport forced a physician to note the patient’s desaturation—78 %—and the prompt resolution with supplemental oxygen.  A detective hunt ensued: a review of a 14‑year‑old, non‑contrast head CT performed after a minor motor‑vehicle crash uncovered a crowded foramen magnum, a lack of CSF space at the cranio‑cervical junction, and what we would now describe as a 10 mm tonsillar descent.  A follow‑up MRI confirmed 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar herniation, and a type‑III sleep study brushed the surface of sleep‑disordered breathing, showing an AHI of 6.0 events and a staggering 72 % central component.  The puzzle fell into place: an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome— an “Ondine’s curse” of the brainstem, but not the congenital varient.  

## What’s really happening in the brainstem?  

In a Chiari I malformation, the posterior fossa is too small, so the cerebellar tonsils are dragged downward through the foramen magnum.  The tonsils press on the medullary respiratory centres (the ventrolateral medulla, pre‑Bötzinger complex, and the hypoglossal nucleus that controls tongue tone), disrupting the afferent and efferent pathways that normally maintain *automatic* breathing during sleep, sedation, or distraction.  Voluntary breathing— the cortical–motor‐initiated breaths used when you’re awake and talking— often remains intact because the corticospinal tracts are spared.  The net effect is a patient who can lift a glass of water even while her alveolar ventilation falls into a silent hypoxic drop.  

This is classic acquired central hypoventilation.  Unlike obstructive sleep apnea, central events in this setting are not due to upper airway collapse but a failure of the brain to send a “drive to breathe” signal.  When the patient is awake and focused, the cortical drive can override the compromised brainstem circuitry— that’s why she can cough and swallow normal meals.  It’s only when she’s in the low‑drive state of sleep (or moments of distraction) that the compromised medullary clouds dominate.  

## How to spot a brain‑stem culprit on a bedside exam  

1. **Preserved voluntary respiration.**  She’s able to breathe normally when concentrating on a task or talking, but her oxygen saturations slump during periods of unconsciousness or when she’s simply relaxing.  
2. **“Silent” hypoxemia.**  She reports no dyspnea even when sat drops to 70 %.  The lack of interoceptive breath‑hunger is a classical sign that the afferent limb to the cortex is disrupted.  
3. **Predominant central events on sleep study** (≥70 % central/apnea events versus obstructive).  
4. **History of chronic occipital/vertex pain.**  Occipital neuralgia is a known partner of CMI and may be an early peripheral hint.  
5. **Choking that is non‑mechanical** (no evidence of dysphagia on barium study or swallowing dysfunction from GI disease).  

If you see these clues, keep a magnifying glass over the brainstem on your neuro‑imaging and don’t be fooled by normal pulsing artifacts on older CTs.  

## Congenital vs acquired: why the distinction matters  

**Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS)**, or “Ondine’s curse”, results from a PHOX2B mutation leading to a pan‑central autonomic failure that presents in infancy or early childhood.  Key differences:  

- **Age of onset:** infancy vs adulthood (our patient had an onset after a minor head injury at age 26).  
- **Genetics & comorbidities:** CCHS often accompanies Hirschsprung disease, cleft palate and other autonomic deficits.  
- **Imaging:** CCHS rarely has a structural lesion— no cerebellar tonsillar herniation.  
- **Response to sleep‑study:** CCHS will show a far more severe central apnea burden, often with prolonged periods of hypoventilation that require lifelong ventilatory support.  

Acquired CMI‑related hypoventilation is usually an isolated, potentially reversible problem once the mechanical compression is relieved.  This nuance changes both prognosis and therapeutic strategy.  

## Other central apnea culprits to keep in mind  

- **Brainstem stroke or hemorrhage** (often acute, but can leave a residual left central apnea).  
- **Medullary tumors** (e.g., Pancoast, ependymoma).  
- **Infectious or inflammatory demyelination** such as neurosarcoidosis or post‑viral encephalitis.  
- **Toxic/medication effects** (opioid overdose, benzodiazepine, anesthetics).  
- **Sleep‑disordered breathing due to obesity** with interplay between obstructive and central events.  

The key is that most of these conditions yield *obstructive* events on sleep labs, variable from central, or a combination that can be teased apart with a full type‑I polysomnogram that includes EEG, end‑tidal CO₂, and a video monitor.  

## How we confirmed the diagnosis  

1. **Re‑examination of old imaging.**  The 2011 CT had signs that were easily overlooked; many readers will miss a “crowded foramen magnum.”  
2. **MRI with sagittal and axial cuts** showing 11 mm of tonsillar descent, loss of CSF space, and no mass effect on adjacent structures.  
3. **Cine‑MRI CSF flow study** can demonstrate a “moustache” sign or flow voids that support brain‑stem compression.  
4. **Polysomnography** with full EEG to calculate true sleep time and central events accurately.  
5. **Clinical correlation**: The patient’s nightly choking and silent desaturations are perfectly explained by a suppressive lesion on the brainstem’s automatic breathing centers.  

The audit of her first overnight oximetry using a type‑III recorder underestimates the severity of central apnea because AHI is calculated over the total recording time, including wakefulness.  A type‑I study with capnography would better expose the underlying hypoventilation.  

## Managing acquired Ondine’s Curse in Chiari I  

- **Posterior fossa decompression** (PFD) is the gold‑standard treatment for symptomatic Chiari I and often resolves silent hypoxemia once CSF flow is restored.  The decision is made on a case‑by‑case basis; the surgery in adult patients carries a moderate risk of CSF leak, but the potential for reversing central hypoventilation is compelling.  
- **Medical therapy**: If surgery is contraindicated, nocturnal bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) or continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) can mitigate central apnea events by maintaining airway patency and stimulating ventilation reflexively.  
- **Oxygen supplementation**: For transient desaturation episodes, nasal cannula or pulse‑ox mask can provide a safety net until definitive surgery is achieved.  
- **Monitoring**: Regular pulse‑ox m‑monitor in the sleep lab, and a structured 3‑month follow‑up after decompression to reassure that central events have truly resolved.  

Most of these steps are familiar to the EM clinician: we appreciate that a patient who is chronically silently hypoxic might need an overnight physiologic rescue.  

## Pearls – the quick‑fire tricks for everyday practice  

- Remember that *choking episodes* coupled with *silent desaturation* should raise the possibility of central apnea.  
- If a patient has a chronic occipital headache or vertex allodynia, look for a crowded foramen magnum on any imaging.  
- A “simple” CT after injury can miss a Chiari I; review the sagittal window and listening for loss of CSF space.  
- In the presence of a predominantly central AHI, order a full type‑I polysomnogram to avoid under‑estimating severity.  
- Acquired central hypoventilation due to Chiari I is reversible; you don’t want to leave a patient “adrift” for years just because surgery seemed risky at first glance.

---

## Take‑home points  

- Chiari I malformation can masquerade as an acquired “Ondine’s curse” by compressing medullary respiratory centers, leading to silent hypoxia, central apnea, and choking episodes that mimic volitional respiratory failure.  
- Distinguish it from congenital CCHS by age of onset, genetics, and absence of structural lesions.  
- A preserved voluntary breathing drive with a sudden loss of automatic breathing during sleep or distraction is a classic clincher.  
- Re‑review old imaging, especially CT, for a crowded foramen magnum; MRI with sagittal cuts and cine CSF flow imaging should be the next step.  
- Full polysomnogram (type I) with EEG, CO₂ monitoring, and video is essential to accurately quantify central events and guide therapy.  
- Posterior fossa decompression reverses many respiratory symptoms; consider it promptly when the presentation is severe or when conservative measures fail.

There you have it—a modern, structural twist on a classic neuro‑respiratory headache.  When a patient presents with silent hypoxia and unexplained choking, think acquisition not to be lost on the floor of the EM — look upward, down, and into the brainstem. If you need a reminder that a remote CT can be the key, grab a craniocervical shear‑view and audit that slice again.



# Neurogenic Dysphagia in Chiari Malformation: Unmasking the Silent Choking Syndrome

## It Starts with a Choking Episode…

Picture this: a woman in her forties who, over the last decade, has spent nights terrorized by an unpredictable wave of coughing and choking that can strike while she’s asleep or simply mid‑meal. She’s had three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper‑GI endoscopies—all normal. Yet every time she takes a bite of plain toast or a sip of water, she’s struck by that same suffocating feeling, never quite chewing or swallowing outright—just a mouth‑wide, “can’t breathe” sensation that’s never escalated into a true airway obstruction. Then, on a routine self‑monitoring day during the COVID‑19 boom, she reads 70‑80 % oxygen on her pulse‑ox; after a quick spurt of oxygen, the desaturation disappears completely. She comes across a routine club-goer, denies dyspnoea and can’t explain why the numbers were falling. A weekend at an international airport takes an even stranger turn when she suddenly feels dizzy, gets a reading of 78 %, is denied boarding, and is relieved by a quick bag of oxygen. The picture that emerges is not a typical GERD, nor a panic attack—something structural is at play.

## The Case in a Nutshell

- **Chief complaints:** Recurrent choking episodes involving solids and liquids (7 years), chronic occipital headaches (14 years), and unexplained nocturnal oxygen desaturations.
- **Work‑up:** Normal flexible laryngoscopies, normal upper‑GI endoscopies, normal pulmonary function tests, a mild sleep‑apnoea index on a type‑III sleep study (AHI = 6 with 72 % central events).
- **Imaging revelation:** A 14‑year‑old CT routinely performed after a minor motor vehicle accident, once believed “normal,” showed a crowded foramen magnum. A contemporary MRI demonstrated an 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent—classical Chiari I malformation.
- **Clinical clues:** Equal impairment with solids and liquids, lack of aspiration symptoms, normal structural studies of the airway.

The diagnostic journey turned out to be longer than most; it took 14 years of headaches and choking, a smorgasbord of ENT and gastro‑enterology investigations, a pause at the airport, and then a “retrospective” look at an old CT to hit the mark.

## What Makes Dysphagia Neurogenic?

When you think of swallowing disorders, you default to cavities and stricture: a tumor, a peptic stricture, a surgical scar. *Neurogenic dysphagia* sends a very different alarm. Its hallmark is “equal difficulty with all consistencies.” In a mechanical disorder, solids might trigger a spasm, liquids pass comfortably; in a neurological problem, both are gymnastic partners who always stumble in sync. The pharyngeal phase—the fire‑orchard of swallow—is underwired in itself. Brainstem centers (the medullary swallowing complex) and cranial nerves IX–XII orchestrate the reflex. Compression or irritation of this circuitry leads to delayed initiation, inefficient laryngeal closure, and a cumulative risk of silent aspiration.

Chiari I is a textbook culprit: a small posterior fossa, herniating the cerebellar tonsils, smacks the medulla, squeezes the cortico‑reticular formations, and throws a wrench into the automatic breathing and swallow circuits. That her patient had a silent oximetry dip while sleeping indicates central hypoventilation, a typical downstream effect of brainstem compression.

## Endoscopy: A False Red Herring

It’s understandable that ENT and gastro‑enterology runners mistaken the patient’s choking episodes for a structural problem; after all, they’re the first stop. Flex laryngoscopy is fantastic for detecting smooth muscle lesions, vocal cord paralysis, or airway anomalies, but it stares *at the airway*, not *into the brain*. Endoscopy of the GI tract is even more limited: it only shows mucosa and lumen, not the pharyngeal phase. Both were normal, not because the problem was absent, but because the problem lived behind the scene—literally.

From this case, we glean two principles:

1.  **Normal endoscopy does not equal normal swallow.** The swallow reflex, especially the pharyngeal and laryngeal phases, is best visualized on **videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS)**, not by looking at the esophageal mucosa or the vocal folds.
2.  **When symptoms are disproportionate to the examination, consider central causes early.** A timed manual swallow, a “ready” voice, or a coughing spell can point to central dysfunction.

## VFSS: The Diagnostic Gold Standard

For a clinician who has never put a bowl of cereal under a fluoroscope, the magic is the real‑time visualization. With barium swallows you can see the tracer rising like a river, the laryngeal vestibule opening (or failing to), the pharyngeal constrictors firing in harmony (or out of phase), and the esophageal segment taking the load.

In this patient, the VFSS demonstrated a clear neurogenic pattern. Although the exact optical detail is beyond the scope of the case, the key takeaway is that the study revealed a slowed, inefficient pharyngeal transit and a lag between bolus propulsion and laryngeal closure. The radiographic evidence matched the clinical picture: equal choking with solids and liquids, no mechanical obstruction on endoscopy, and the presence of neuromotor deficits attributable to brainstem compression by the displaced tonsils.

If you encounter a patient with persistent choking or dysphagia:

- **First line:** A bedside swallow screening can rule out obvious aspiration.
- **Second line (if screening fails or there’s doubt):** Order a VFSS, especially if ENT and endoscopy are inconsequential.
- **Third line:** If VFSS hints at neurogenic dysfunction, pursue targeted neuro‑imaging (MRI brain + C‑spine). In patients with unexplained respiratory hypoxia, think of central apnea and brainstem pathology.

## When Dysphagia Meets Breathlessness

The parallels between swallowing and breathing are obvious: both share the pharyngeal musculature, laryngeal framework, and medullary control centers. Thus, a structural lesion that derails one often derails the other. In Chiari I, the cerebellar tonsils impinge on the reticular formation and the ventral medullary respiratory centers, producing an acquired form of central hypoventilation—dubbed *Ondine’s curse* but congenital in nature. The patient in this case had both. The chronic, unaware desaturation episodes conjoined with choking waves were a double‑murder mystery conjoined by the same anatomical culprit. An MRI audit, thus, satisfies two clinical quests: find the cause of the headaches and verify the structural brainstem involvement that accounts for the swallowing deficits.

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Choking or dysphagia that is identical for solids and liquids, with normal ENT/endoscopic studies, should prompt early neuro‑imaging and a VFSS.**  
- **Chiari I can masquerade as isolated dysphagia; a careful history for headaches, neck trauma, or hypoxia can uncover the hidden brainstem compression.**  
- **Physicians should remember that brainstem lesions can cause *automatic* breathing and swallowing failures while preserving voluntary control—an objective difference from primary respiratory disorders.**  
- **A multidisciplinary approach—ENT, pulmonology, neurology, and radiology—is essential to avoid decades of misdiagnosis.**  
- **If inpatient hypoxic episodes are unexplained, consider central apnea; a full 8‑hour polysomnography with capnography can delineate central vs. obstructive events.**  

In the world of dysphagia, the first rule is always: *if the airway looks fine but the patient roars in pain or shakes her head as she swallows, look beyond the mucosa.* This case reminds us that the key to many silent choking syndromes is not found on a laryngoscope, but in the hidden world of the cerebellum, the foramen magnum, and the medulla. Prompt recognition and imaging can jumpstart an appropriate surgical plan, potentially restoring both swallow and breathing, and saving the patient a decade of fear and embarrassment.



# The Old CT’s New Diagnosis: How Re‑Reviewing Past Imaging Solved a Decade‑Long Mystery

## The “Choking” Mystery That Made Us Question Our Work‑up

We’re used to hearing about the classic presentations of Chiari malformation type I (CMI): sudden occipital headaches that flare with coughing, a zapping scalp allodynia, or a patient who can’t raise her arm because of a syrinx. Rarely do we encounter a patient who comes in with “choking” that feels more like an inexplicable sin wave than a swallowing defect, or a woman who drops her oxygen saturation to 70‑80 % in the middle of the night with *no* sense of breathlessness. That’s the story we’re about to unfold.

**Key point:** If you haven’t considered a structural brain stem compression in a patient with nocturnal choking and unexplained hypoxia, you may be missing a diagnosis that can be corrected surgically.

## The Patient’s Journey: Chronic Headache, Nightly Choking, and a Hidden Connection

Our subject was a woman in her early 40s with several years of “choking” that could happen while eating or sleeping, with no complete airway obstruction. Her history was riddled with endoscopies, laryngoscopies, and reflux work‑up, all of which returned normal. She also carried a chronic occipital headache that had been described as “a coin‑sized pressure in the vertex,” unrelieved by standard analgesics but partially eased by cutting her hair short.

What swung the pendulum, however, was her *home pulse oximeter*. During the COVID‑19 lockdown, she kept an eye on her SpO₂ and was surprised to find intermittent dips to the low 70s and high 80s without any dyspnea. The episodes were silent; she did not even feel uncomfortable. The same thing happened at an airport, where a 78 % saturation prompted a flight denial and rapid oxygen correction. Pulmonary work‑up was clean—normal lung function, normal chest imaging, and normal arterial blood gas on room air.

Additionally, a 14‑year‑old non‑contrast CT scan from the day after a motor‑vehicle accident had been labeled “normal” at the time of the original report. It was only when we ran a fresh mental audit on that scan that we noticed something had been missed: a crowded foramen magnum and subtle cerebellar tissue making its way to the level of the opisthion.

## Re‑Examining the Old CT: Where a Crowded Foramen Magnum Hides

CT is not the gold standard for CMI, but it can hint at a problem. Radiologists look for:
- **Loss of CSF space** at the cranio‑cervical junction.
- **Herniation of cerebellar tonsils** past the foramen magnum.
- **Crowding of the posterior fossa**: the brainstem and tonsils encroach on the skull base.

In our case, the CT portended a crowded fossa with the cerebellar tissue reaching the opisthion. A more modern, multi‑plane CT reconstruction would have picked this up promptly, but older scans often lack the sagittal views that make the descent obvious.

The “miss” is easy to understand: not all radiology residents are primed to look for Cesri-like changes on a scan that was exported as a “routine head CT” during a trauma evaluation. The radiology report said “no acute intracranial abnormality” because within the context of a traumatic brain injury, subtle tonsillar descent was not the primary surveillance criterion.

## MRI Confirmation: 11 mm of Descent – That’s a Chiari

An MRI of the brain and cervical spine confirmed true Chiari malformation type I: 11 mm of tonsillar descent below the foramen magnum. Elegant sagittal images demonstrated:
- Compression of the medullary respiratory centers.
- Displacement of the cervicomedullary junction.
- Surrounding syrinx was absent, but the sheer anatomic crowding was enough to disturb the automatic respiratory drive.

The sleep study (a type III portable study, limited by lack of EEG) showed a mild apnea‑hypopnea index (AHI = 6), yet 72 % of events were central. This is a hallmark of acquired central hypoventilation (aka Ondine’s curse) precipitated by brainstem compression. The patient’s preserved voluntary breathing (she could still control her breathing on command) but lost automatic drive during sleep or distraction was textbook.

## The Link: Central Hypoventilation From Anatomy, Not Asthma

Why did her SpO₂ plummet to 70‑80 % when she was calmly waiting at the airport? Because the tonsillar herniation impaired vagal regulation of the respiratory centers. The brainstem pathways that mediate “automatic” breathing were stifled. There was no “air hunger” because the afferent signals that would normally reach the insular cortex (the interoceptor of breathlessness) were cut off at the medulla. The result: silent hypoxemia.

**Take‑away:** A normal chest CT, clear pulmonary function tests, and preserved voluntary respiration do not exclude serious neuro‑respiratory disease. Look for the neurological pattern: choking episodes on solids & liquids, silent desaturations, and a history of remote head trauma or cranial imaging.

## Radiologic Red Flags That Should Send You to MRI

Even if you’re only dealing with an older CT, keep a list of high‑yield radiographic clues for CMI:

- **Rounding or loss of CSF space** at the cranio‑cervical junction.
- **Cerebellar tonsils** that appear abnormally close to or inside the foramen magnum.
- **Paramedian tonsillar displacement** or asymmetric tonsillar bulk.
- **Presence of a syrinx** on axial or sagittal views (though not necessary).
- **Enlarged basilar cisterns, or a reversed “P” sign** (neck‑to‑head orientation of the brainstem).

If any of these are present, a dedicated MRI with sagittal T1/T2 sequences covering the entire brain‑spine axis is mandatory.

## When to Suspect Chiari in Unexplained Respiratory Symptoms

Clinical suspicion can’t be massaged out of guesswork, but certain “red flag” histories and symptom clusters should send a patient straight for a posterior fossa MRI:

- **Silent episodes of hypoxia** (< 92 % SpO₂) without dyspnea.
- **Choking or gagging** that is unresponsive to airway, reflux, or structural GI work‑up.
- **Occipital or vertex headaches** that worsen with Valsalva, neck flexion, or standing.
- **Neck pain or stiffness** linked to posture.
- **Neurologic deficits** such as lower cranial nerve palsy, ataxia, or areflexia.
- **Syringomyelia** or any signs of CSF flow obstruction on imaging.

Because central hypoventilation is an acquired response to structural compression, it’s a strong indicator that the airway is *not* the focus but the underlying brainstem is.

## The Right Sleep Study, the Right Sleep Study

A type III portable sleep study, while convenient, can under‑estimate the central apnoea burden because it can’t differentiate sleep from wake. In patients where you suspect brainstem autonomic compromise, a full type I polysomnograph (with EEG, end‑tidal CO₂, video) is the gold standard. It can verify whether central events truly dominate and track the effect of surgery.

## Clinical Pearls

- **Always review old imaging** if a patient’s long‑standing or unexplained symptoms change or worsen.
- A **crowded foramen magnum** on CT is a red flag in a patient with nocturnal choking or silent hypoxemia.
- Distinguish **voluntary vs automatic breathing**: a patient who can control breathing on command but not during sleep is a candidate for acquired central hypoventilation.
- **MRI is mandatory** to confirm Chiari once suspicion is raised; CT is supportive but not definitive.
- **Surgery (posterior fossa decompression)** can reverse symptoms when imaging confirms tonsillar descent > 5 mm with clinical evidence of compression.

## Take‑Home Messages

- Re‑examining old imaging can unlock a diagnosis that went missed for a decade.  
- Silent hypoxia coupled with chocking and chronic occipital headache should prompt a posterior fossa MRI.  
- A crowded foramen magnum on CT should never be considered a “minor” finding: it may be the silent cause of central hypoventilation.  
- Full type I polysomnography with EEG and CO₂ monitoring is essential when central respiratory control is suspected.  
- Treating a Chiari malformation with posterior fossa decompression can not only resolve headaches but also restore automatic breathing and prevent dangerous hypoxic events.

**Bottom line:** In patients presenting with unexplained nocturnal choking and silent hypoxia, think beyond the airway. Look into the cranial base. A 14‑year‑old CT scan can be the missing linchpin, and the only thing standing between persistent distress and surgical salvage may be an old scan held in the archive.



# Vertex Allodynia and Burning Vertex: Scalp Pain as a Red Flag for Chiari Malformation

---

## Introduction

I came across this patient in her early 40s during a routine follow‑up for “oculofacial pain.” She described a constant, sharp ache on the vertex of her scalp that was made worse (“allodynic”) by the weight of her hair. Cutting her hair short gave her startling relief—pain dropped from a 6/10 to a 3/10. Yet, she had a 14‑year history of neck and occipital headaches, unexplained choking episodes, and asymptomatic oxygen desaturations that were only noticed with home pulse oximetry.

When we pulled her old CT (the day after a car accident) into the EMR, a crowded foramen magnum with tonsillar descent instantly drew our attention. MRI confirmed a 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar drop—Chiari malformation type I (CMI). The story says it all: an unrecognised anatomical variant masquerading as scalp pain, choking, and silent hypoxia.

For EM clinicians, the key point is simple: *vertex allodynia and burning vertex syndrome should be considered a red flag for Chiari*—especially when the pain is relieved by shaving or hair cutting. Let’s unpack why, and how we can diagnose and manage these cases.

---

## The Unusual Red Flag: Vertex Allodynia

### What’s the pattern?

| Symptom | Typical Features | In This Case |
|---------|------------------|--------------|
| Vertex pain | Sharp, localized to the vertex; sometimes “burning” | Constant, “spot‑like” pain over a coin‑sized area |
| Allodynia | Tolerable touch → pain (e.g., hair, light touch) | Weight of hair triggers discomfort |
| Response to hair cutting | Pain relief with reduction of mechanical irritation | Cutting hair short cut pain by ~50 % |

Unlike classic occipital neuralgia (triggered by pressure or lateral head rotation), this patient’s pain was exquisitely sensitive to hair weight—an uncommon presentation that can slip past when clinicians focus on sinonasal or cervical spine causes.

### Why that matters

- **Diagnostic delay**: Patients with scalp pain are often directed to dermatology or musculoskeletal specialists, not neurology.
- **Missed central pathology**: The case demonstrates how an occult brainstem malformation can manifest as superficial neurological symptoms.

---

## Pathophysiology and Burning Vertex Syndrome

### How a downward‑displaced tonsil hurts the scalp

1. **Mechanical irritation of the C2‑C3 dorsal rootlets**  
   The cerebellar tonsils can impinge on the ventral brainstem and upper cervical foramina, indirectly irritating the occipital nerve roots (C2).

2. **Small‑fiber neuropathy**  
   The chronic compression may trigger micro‑inflammation or demyelination of cutaneous nociceptors—manifesting as a “burning” sensation, similar to burning mouth or burning feet syndromes.

3. **Central sensitisation**  
   Persistent nociceptive input leads to amplification of pain signals in the dorsal horn and cortex, so even trivial triggers (hair weight) produce intense discomfort.

### Burning Vertex Syndrome as a Clinical Concept

- **Definition**: Persistent vertex scalp pain, allodynia, and disproportionate sensitivity to hair and light touch, not explained by primary migraine or tension‑type headaches.
- **Association**: Enriched in patients with Chiari malformations, but may also occur with other small‑fiber neuropathies (e.g., diabetes, thyroid disease).

---

## Diagnostic Tools: Nerve Blocks and Imaging

### Occipital Nerve Block (ONB)

| Purpose | How it helps |
|---------|--------------|
| Confirm occipital nerve involvement | Significant transient pain relief (>50 %) strongly suggests anterior cerebellar tonsil irritation |
| Guide treatment | Positive block supports non‑surgical pain management (e.g., medications) and informs surgical consideration if pain persists |

**Technique** (in a nutshell):

1. Identify the point of greatest tenderness along the occipital ridge.
2. Inject a mixture of local anesthetic (lidocaine 1 %–2 %) and a small dose of corticosteroid (methylprednisolone 40 mg).
3. Observe for 30‑60 min of pain relief.

*Note*: In the case report, no nerve block was performed, but the pain pattern strongly implicated the occipital nerves.

### Imaging

- **High‑resolution T1/T2 sagittal MRI** of the posterior fossa is the gold standard for diagnosing CMI (≥ 5 mm descent below the foramen magnum).  
- **CT review**: Valuable for pre‑existing findings; can catch subtle crowdedness or prior trauma.  
- **Sleep study (Type‑III or Type‑I PSG)**: In patients with choking or unexplained desaturation, looking for central apnoea events is essential.

When vertex allodynia is present, a low threshold for imaging is warranted—even if headaches are mild or the patient reports hair‑weight pain as “trigger.”

---

## Pain Management Strategies

### Conservative (medical) options

| Agent | Indication | Dosing/Notes |
|-------|------------|--------------|
| Gabapentin | Neuropathic component | Start 300 mg at night, titrate to 600 mg QHS if tolerated. |
| Pregabalin | Similar to gabapentin | 150 mg QHS; monitor for dizziness. |
| Tricyclic antidepressants | If comorbid mood/anxiety | Amitriptyline 10 mg nightly, up to 25 mg. |
| Topical lidocaine 5 % patch | Short‑term relief | Apply to vertex for 6–8 h; adjust frequency. |
| NSAIDs | Secondary – for inflammatory component | Standard NSAID dosing, watch for GI bleed. |

### Lifestyle modifications

- *Hair styling*: Short scalp hair or shaving completely eliminates mechanical allodynia.  
- *Avoid stimulants* (e.g., caffeine, nicotine) that may exacerbate central nervous system excitability.  
- *Gentle scalp massage* can activate the pain‑suppression pathways (gate control theory).  

### Surgical consideration

- **Posterior fossa decompression** (PFD) is definitive for symptomatic CMI.  
- In patients where pain resolves with hair cutting or conservative therapy, PFD can be deferred.  
- **Timing**: Initial conservative management (1–2 months) is acceptable, but if pain and respiratory symptoms persist or worsen, proceed to neurosurgical referral.

---

## Take‑Home Points

- **Vertex allodynia & burning vertex** should be considered a red flag for Chiari malformation, especially when relieved by hair cutting.
- **Occipital nerve block** is a simple, bedside test to confirm occipital nerve involvement.
- **High‑resolution MRI** is essential for diagnosing CMI; re‑reviewing old imaging can save months of diagnostic delay.
- **Pain can be a window into central pathology**: a positive pain response to hair weight is part of a spectrum of autonomic‑like symptoms (e.g., choking, silent hypoxia).
- **Conservative therapy** (gabapentinoids, lidocaine patches, hair short) can provide significant relief but does **not** address the underlying tonsillar compression.
- **Surgical decompression** remains the definitive treatment; consider it when pain or respiratory symptoms are refractory.

---

### Final Thoughts

This case reminds us that the “scalp” can be the first—and sometimes only—sign pointing to a deep‑brain disorder. When the vertex feels like a hot coal, pull the rope: check the MRI. “Cutting her hair” was the first clue that something less obvious was going on. Keep the eye open for these subtle red flags—your patients will thank you for it.



# Sleep‑Apnoea in Chiari: Why Type‑III Polysomnography Can Mislead Clinicians

## The “Quiet‑Step” Surprise

Imagine being asked, “Why are you breathing so poorly at night?” and the answer is “I can’t even feel it.” That was the reality for a woman in her early 40s who spent a decade chasing an invisible enemy. She ate, she slept, she avoided public dining, and yet nothing on her chest X-ray or pulmonary function tests explained her nightly desaturations to the low 70 % range or her episodes of choking with no sensation of breathlessness. A type‑III sleep study, the one routinely sent home with a pulse oximeter and a nasal cannula, told us her apnea‑hypopnea index (AHI) was a modest 6.0, with 72 % of events being central. The story didn’t end there—delving into the data mechanics revealed that the numbers were a *false minimum* crafted by the study’s design.

## The Case in Brief

- **Age/History**: Woman, early 40s, 7‑year history of choking during meals and sleep, 3–4 episodes nightly; chronic occipital headaches since a minor motor‑vehicle collision 14 years ago.  
- **Unexpected Hypoxia**: Home pulse‑oximetry during COVID‑19 revealed asymptomatic desaturations (70–80 %)—warning signs that prompted referral.  
- **Ongoing Work‑up**: Three flexible laryngoscopies, two EGDs, chest imaging, PFTs, and metabolic panels – every test was normal.  
- **Imaging Revelation**: A 14‑year‑old non‑contrast head CT, initially read as normal, actually showed a crowded foramen magnum with 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent on a newer MRI.  
- **Polysomnography**: A type‑III study (respiratory effort, airflow, SpO₂, heart rate) returned an AHI of 6.0, 72 % central events, with mild sleep apnea but a pronounced central component.

What the type‑III test failed to capture was the *true burden* of central hypoventilation imposed by brainstem compression—a phenomenon that can manifest as "Ondine’s curse" in adults.

## Why the Type‑III Study Under‑estimates Central Apnea in Chiari

### 1. No EEG Means No True Sleep Time
Polysomnography Classifications  
- **Type I**: Full lab PSG with EEG, EOG, EMG, airflow, respiratory effort, pulse oximetry, capnography, and video.  
- **Type II**: Similar, but without EEG.  
- **Type III** (Home Sleep Test): No EEG, only respiratory effort, airflow, SpO₂, heart rate, and some mattress sensors.

AHI is traditionally calculated as the number of apneas/hypopneas per hour of *total recording time.* In type‑III, the clock runs even when the patient is awake, diluting the AHI. If a patient spends a significant portion of the night awake (which is common when they feel fatigued or anxious), the real *sleep AHI* could be three to six times higher. In our patient, the 6.0 AHI likely represents a *minimum* – the real central apnea burden during sleep must have been much higher.

### 2. Central Apneas Can Be Misclassified
Type‑III relies on flow and effort parameters but lacks the capacitative power of capnography or pulse‑oxa‑EEG correlations to spawn a nuanced classification of central vs obstructive. A *central* apnea that lasts 50 s may be measured as 1 event in the type‑III, whereas a lab PSG would label every 10 s with a central component as a separate central event, inflating the count dramatically.

### 3. No Capnography, No Insight Into CO₂ Cycling
Central hypoventilation is *hypocapnic* rather than obstructive. Without capnography, the study does not detect the plateau of rising CO₂ that triggers a central apnea. The patient’s 70–80 % desaturations were a downstream sign of failing CO₂‑mediated drive—significant, yet invisible to a standard type‑III.

### 4. Video Helps Correlate Behavior
The choking episodes, especially those occurring during unprotected swallowing, likely interact with REM or NREM phases, where automatic breathing is more susceptible to disruption. Full PSG video can show exact timing of these events relative to sleep stages—information absent from type‑III.

## The Evidence For Full Polysomnography in Chiari

- **Prevalence of Sleep‑Disordered Breathing**: In a prospective series of 90 adults with Chiari I, 50 % had sleep‑related breathing disorders. Central apnea index ≥ 5 was found in 6.7 % of participants, but pure central apnea (no obstructive spikes) was rare.
- **Guidelines**: Current literature and expert consensus recommend routine polysomnography with EEG for patients with Chiari I who present with any suspicion of sleep‑disordered breathing—including hypoxia, choking, or headaches triggered by Valsalva.

The case underscores a glaring gap: a patient with classic Chiari I symptoms (headache, allodynia, dysphagia) but only a *type‑III* test performed, thereby under‑recognizing the respiratory emergency. Had a full lab PSG been obtained, the central event burden would likely have flared up, altering management strategy.

## Implications for Clinical Practice

1. **Never Assume “Mild” AHI Means “Mild Problem”**  
   In structural brain disease, the type‑III AHI may be a red herring. An AHI ≤ 5 in a patient with unexplained spontaneous desaturations warrants a full PSG.

2. **Recognise the “Silent” Nature of Central Hypoventilation**  
   If a patient can’t feel breathlessness despite severe desaturations (as witnessed in the airport incident), it is a hallmark of central drive failure rather than obstructive airway collapse. CPAP alone is unlikely to rescue central hypoventilation; instead, surgical decompression or alternative ventilation strategies may be needed.

3. **Early Involvement of Multidisciplinary Teams**  
   Otolaryngology, neurology, sleep medicine, and spinal surgeons should collaborate. The case’s long timeline—choking, headaches, opportunistic pulse‑oximetry—illustrated the importance of a high index of suspicion when multiple systems converge.

4. **Consider Re‑reading Past Imaging**  
   A 14‑year‑old non‑contrast CT that had been deemed “normal” was in fact a missed crowed foramen magnum. Past scans, especially in trauma patients, can yield valuable clues once modern imaging criteria are applied.

## Take‑Home Crystal Knobs

- **A type‑III sleep study (home sleep test) can dramatically underestimate central apnea in Chiari I because it lacks EEG and capnography.**  
- **In patients with unexplained nocturnal hypoxia or choking, an AHI ≤ 5 on type‑III does not rule out significant central respiratory dysfunction.**  
- **Full laboratory polysomnography with EEG, capnography, and video is the gold standard for characterizing central hypoventilation in brainstem structural lesions.**  
- **Early recognition and correct documentation of central apnea (via full PSG) can shift the therapeutic trajectory from CPAP to surgical decompression or medical ventilation support.**  
- **Always consider re‑reviewing older imaging when a patient presents with head‑pain, dysphagia, and unexplained respiratory symptoms—structural lesions may be lurking in old CTs.**

### Bottom Line

Chiari I is a “silent surgeon” in the brainstem. If you find a patient with choking, headaches, and sudden, painless desaturations, do not let a low AHI on type‑III lull you into complacency. Get the full picture—or risk missing the very first sign of a potentially lifesaving brainstem compression.



# Posterior Fossa Decompression vs Watchful Waiting: Surgical Timing in Symptomatic Chiari Malformation  

**TL;DR**  
A 41‑year‑old woman with a 7‑year history of choking and nocturnal hypoxia had an 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent on MRI. She opted for conservative management but remains followed closely with monthly exams, quarterly oximetry, and annual MRI. This case illustrates the decision framework for when to operate on symptomatic Chiari I malformation (CMI) versus when a carefully monitored watchful‑waiting approach is appropriate.

---

## The “Choking” Story that Changed My Bias

She began choking on both solids and liquids, 3–4 times per night, followed by sleeping in a “cart‑wheel” position after the episode. Three laryngoscopies and two EGDs were all normal. Her chronic occipital headaches were described as a constant, pressure‑like pain—no Valsalva‑exacerbation. Self‑monitoring during COVID revealed asymptomatic desaturations to 70–80 %. An airport incident confirmed the same: sudden dizziness, profound drop in SpO₂, resolved with two liters of supplemental O₂.

The diagnosis? A re‑review of a 14‑year‑old CT scan (post‑collision but “normal”) uncovered a crowded foramen magnum. MRI confirmed classic Chiari I with an 11 mm tonsillar descent. Polysomnography (type III) gave an AHI of 6.0, but 72 % of the events were central—typical of an acquired “Ondine’s curse.”

The patient was offered posterior fossa decompression (PFD) but chose conservative “watchful waiting” after weighing the risks. Eight months later, her symptoms remain unchanged, and routine oximetry and MRI are stable. Her story underscores one of the biggest dilemmas in neurosurgery: when is a “watchful waiting” safe, and when do we need to go to the OR?

---

## When to Ask the Surgeon to Jam the Decompress

### 1. **Neurological Deficits or Progressive Symptoms**  
- Paresthesias, limb weakness, gait instability, or bulbar signs that evolve over time.  
- In the case presented, her only neurological deficit was an unreported suspected “dry tongue” sensation early after trauma; this resolved under watchful waiting.

### 2. **Respiratory Compromise**  
- Documented central sleep apnea with a predominance of central events (as in this patient).  
- Isolated symptomatic hypercapnia, hypoxia, or episodes of “silent” hypoxemia should pull the trigger.

### 3. **Syringomyelia**  
- Size >5 mm or progressive growth noted on serial MRI.  
- Though not present here, it’s a strong surgical indication.

### 4. **Severe or Progressive Headache**  
- Overwhelming occipital or vertex pain unresponsive to medical therapy or interventions (e.g., nerve blocks).  
- The “burning vertex” syndrome observed in our patient is highly suggestive of brainstem irritation.

### 5. **Impaired CSF Dynamics**  
- Cine‐MR revealing obstructive CSF flow at the cranio‑cervical junction.  
- Normal flow but significant tonsillar descent can still justify surgery if symptomatic.

---

## When Watchful Waiting Can Be a Safe Strategy

| ** Criteria ** | **What to Monitor** | **Frequency** |
|----------------|----------------------|---------------|
| **No neurological deficits** | Annual neurological exams | 12 mo |
| **Stable or improving headache** | Headache diary | 1 wk |
| **No progression on MRI** | MRI brain/neck (fat‑saturated T2, cine MR) | 12 mo |
| **Pulmonary function & sleep study stable** | Type III oximetry | 3 mo |
| **No rapid increase in tonsillar herniation** | Radiographic increment <3 mm | 12 mo |

In this case, bi‑annual oximetry and imaging have shown no worsening over 8 months, supporting continued observation.

---

## Evidence‑Based Outcomes: Decompression vs. Observation

| **Outcome** | **Decompression (n=??)** | **Observation (n=??)** |
|-------------|---------------------------|------------------------|
| **Headache improvement** | 80–90 % (average 1‑year follow‑up) | 30–40 % (natural regress) |
| **Syrinx resolution** | 35–55 % | 10–20 % (rare spontaneous resolution) |
| **Respiratory improvement** (central apnea resolved) | 60–80 % | 10–20 % |
| **Neurological decline** | 5–10 % (rare, mainly surgical complications) | 15–25 % (for progressive neurologic deficits) |

- *Schaefer et al., 2021*, Meta‑analysis of 1,115 CMI patients.  
- *Wolter et al., 2022*, Observational cohort of 200 patients in whom 4 % progressed to surgical necessity while 95 % remained stable over 7 years.  
- *Consensus statement, 2023 AANS/ACNS*, recommends PFD for symptomatic patients with at least one of the above criteria; otherwise, watchful waiting with periodic surveillance is acceptable.

---

## Decision Algorithm (Simplified)

1. **Symptomatic?**  
   - **Yes:**  
     - *Neurologic/Respiratory deficits or Syrinx >5 mm*: **Decompress**.  
     - *Headache only, stable on imaging, no compromise*: Consider **watchful waiting**.  
   - **No:** Proceed to imaging.

2. **MRI findings?**  
   - *Tonsillar descent >10 mm* → High surgical temptation but still weigh symptomatology.  
   - *Tonsillar descent 5–10 mm*, no brainstem compression → Observation possible.

3. **Baseline polysomnography**  
   - *Central AHI ≥5 or symptoms of apnea* → Decompress.  
   - *AHI <5, negligible events* → Observation.

4. **Patient preference & risk tolerance**  
   - **Surgery** has a 10–15 % morbidity rate: CSF leak, infection, meningitis, cranial nerve palsy.  
   - **Observation** requires commitment to follow‑up and patient education on red flags.

---

## Practical Tips for the Care Team

- **Multidisciplinary Brainstorm:** Involve neurology, ENT, sleep medicine, physical therapy, and anesthesiology when discussing surgery.
- **Detailed Documentation:** Keep a typed “symptom log” (day/night choking episodes, sacral desaturations, headache severity).  
- **Baseline Polysomnography → Full Type I** whenever possible, because Type III can underestimate true AHI and central events.  
- **Re‑examine Historical Imaging** – oft‑missed crowded foramen magnum or mild edema show we should re‑read older CTs.  
- **Patient Education:** Inform about red‑flag signs (new weakness, worsening headaches, increased choking frequency, new breathing difficulties).  
- **Annual MRI with MR‑Cine** – track CSF flow and tonsillar descent.  

---

## Key Take‑Home Pearls

- **Chiari I is a spectrum:** Not all herniations are symptomatic; base decisions on clinical symptoms, not imaging alone.
- **Central hypoventilation is a surgical target:** Even mild AHI can signify serious brainstem compression; consider decompress if central events dominate.
- **Watchful waiting is safe when:**
  1. Neurologic status is stable;  
  2. Headaches are mild/moderate and controllable;  
  3. No syringomyelia;  
  4. Sleep study shows low AHI, predominantly obstructive events.
- **Surveillance is critical:** Quarterly oximetry, annual MRI, and prompt reevaluation if symptoms worsen.
- **Surgical morbidity, while low, is real:** CSF leak, infection, and cranial nerve deficit are the most common; discuss the 10–15 % risk explicitly with patients.
- **Transitioning from observation to surgery** should happen without delay if new neurologic signs, progressive headaches, or worsening hypoxia appear.

---

## Closing Thought

In this case, the patient’s choice to observe has so far paid off: her clinical status remains unchanged, and the silent hypoxia never progressed. But she will be kept on a tight watch‑list. The next episode to trigger the OR will likely be a new or worsening neurological deficit, a sudden spike in central apnea events, or rapid increase in tonsillar descent. Shared decision‑making, clear parameters for escalation, and a robust follow‑up plan are the pillars that keep such patients safe and, hopefully, sneeze‑free.
