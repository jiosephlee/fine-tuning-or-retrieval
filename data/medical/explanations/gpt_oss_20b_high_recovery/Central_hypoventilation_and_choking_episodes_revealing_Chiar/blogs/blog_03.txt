Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# The Old CT’s New Diagnosis: How Re‑Reviewing Past Imaging Solved a Decade‑Long Mystery

## The “Choking” Mystery That Made Us Question Our Work‑up

We’re used to hearing about the classic presentations of Chiari malformation type I (CMI): sudden occipital headaches that flare with coughing, a zapping scalp allodynia, or a patient who can’t raise her arm because of a syrinx. Rarely do we encounter a patient who comes in with “choking” that feels more like an inexplicable sin wave than a swallowing defect, or a woman who drops her oxygen saturation to 70‑80 % in the middle of the night with *no* sense of breathlessness. That’s the story we’re about to unfold.

**Key point:** If you haven’t considered a structural brain stem compression in a patient with nocturnal choking and unexplained hypoxia, you may be missing a diagnosis that can be corrected surgically.

## The Patient’s Journey: Chronic Headache, Nightly Choking, and a Hidden Connection

Our subject was a woman in her early 40s with several years of “choking” that could happen while eating or sleeping, with no complete airway obstruction. Her history was riddled with endoscopies, laryngoscopies, and reflux work‑up, all of which returned normal. She also carried a chronic occipital headache that had been described as “a coin‑sized pressure in the vertex,” unrelieved by standard analgesics but partially eased by cutting her hair short.

What swung the pendulum, however, was her *home pulse oximeter*. During the COVID‑19 lockdown, she kept an eye on her SpO₂ and was surprised to find intermittent dips to the low 70s and high 80s without any dyspnea. The episodes were silent; she did not even feel uncomfortable. The same thing happened at an airport, where a 78 % saturation prompted a flight denial and rapid oxygen correction. Pulmonary work‑up was clean—normal lung function, normal chest imaging, and normal arterial blood gas on room air.

Additionally, a 14‑year‑old non‑contrast CT scan from the day after a motor‑vehicle accident had been labeled “normal” at the time of the original report. It was only when we ran a fresh mental audit on that scan that we noticed something had been missed: a crowded foramen magnum and subtle cerebellar tissue making its way to the level of the opisthion.

## Re‑Examining the Old CT: Where a Crowded Foramen Magnum Hides

CT is not the gold standard for CMI, but it can hint at a problem. Radiologists look for:
- **Loss of CSF space** at the cranio‑cervical junction.
- **Herniation of cerebellar tonsils** past the foramen magnum.
- **Crowding of the posterior fossa**: the brainstem and tonsils encroach on the skull base.

In our case, the CT portended a crowded fossa with the cerebellar tissue reaching the opisthion. A more modern, multi‑plane CT reconstruction would have picked this up promptly, but older scans often lack the sagittal views that make the descent obvious.

The “miss” is easy to understand: not all radiology residents are primed to look for Cesri-like changes on a scan that was exported as a “routine head CT” during a trauma evaluation. The radiology report said “no acute intracranial abnormality” because within the context of a traumatic brain injury, subtle tonsillar descent was not the primary surveillance criterion.

## MRI Confirmation: 11 mm of Descent – That’s a Chiari

An MRI of the brain and cervical spine confirmed true Chiari malformation type I: 11 mm of tonsillar descent below the foramen magnum. Elegant sagittal images demonstrated:
- Compression of the medullary respiratory centers.
- Displacement of the cervicomedullary junction.
- Surrounding syrinx was absent, but the sheer anatomic crowding was enough to disturb the automatic respiratory drive.

The sleep study (a type III portable study, limited by lack of EEG) showed a mild apnea‑hypopnea index (AHI = 6), yet 72 % of events were central. This is a hallmark of acquired central hypoventilation (aka Ondine’s curse) precipitated by brainstem compression. The patient’s preserved voluntary breathing (she could still control her breathing on command) but lost automatic drive during sleep or distraction was textbook.

## The Link: Central Hypoventilation From Anatomy, Not Asthma

Why did her SpO₂ plummet to 70‑80 % when she was calmly waiting at the airport? Because the tonsillar herniation impaired vagal regulation of the respiratory centers. The brainstem pathways that mediate “automatic” breathing were stifled. There was no “air hunger” because the afferent signals that would normally reach the insular cortex (the interoceptor of breathlessness) were cut off at the medulla. The result: silent hypoxemia.

**Take‑away:** A normal chest CT, clear pulmonary function tests, and preserved voluntary respiration do not exclude serious neuro‑respiratory disease. Look for the neurological pattern: choking episodes on solids & liquids, silent desaturations, and a history of remote head trauma or cranial imaging.

## Radiologic Red Flags That Should Send You to MRI

Even if you’re only dealing with an older CT, keep a list of high‑yield radiographic clues for CMI:

- **Rounding or loss of CSF space** at the cranio‑cervical junction.
- **Cerebellar tonsils** that appear abnormally close to or inside the foramen magnum.
- **Paramedian tonsillar displacement** or asymmetric tonsillar bulk.
- **Presence of a syrinx** on axial or sagittal views (though not necessary).
- **Enlarged basilar cisterns, or a reversed “P” sign** (neck‑to‑head orientation of the brainstem).

If any of these are present, a dedicated MRI with sagittal T1/T2 sequences covering the entire brain‑spine axis is mandatory.

## When to Suspect Chiari in Unexplained Respiratory Symptoms

Clinical suspicion can’t be massaged out of guesswork, but certain “red flag” histories and symptom clusters should send a patient straight for a posterior fossa MRI:

- **Silent episodes of hypoxia** (< 92 % SpO₂) without dyspnea.
- **Choking or gagging** that is unresponsive to airway, reflux, or structural GI work‑up.
- **Occipital or vertex headaches** that worsen with Valsalva, neck flexion, or standing.
- **Neck pain or stiffness** linked to posture.
- **Neurologic deficits** such as lower cranial nerve palsy, ataxia, or areflexia.
- **Syringomyelia** or any signs of CSF flow obstruction on imaging.

Because central hypoventilation is an acquired response to structural compression, it’s a strong indicator that the airway is *not* the focus but the underlying brainstem is.

## The Right Sleep Study, the Right Sleep Study

A type III portable sleep study, while convenient, can under‑estimate the central apnoea burden because it can’t differentiate sleep from wake. In patients where you suspect brainstem autonomic compromise, a full type I polysomnograph (with EEG, end‑tidal CO₂, video) is the gold standard. It can verify whether central events truly dominate and track the effect of surgery.

## Clinical Pearls

- **Always review old imaging** if a patient’s long‑standing or unexplained symptoms change or worsen.
- A **crowded foramen magnum** on CT is a red flag in a patient with nocturnal choking or silent hypoxemia.
- Distinguish **voluntary vs automatic breathing**: a patient who can control breathing on command but not during sleep is a candidate for acquired central hypoventilation.
- **MRI is mandatory** to confirm Chiari once suspicion is raised; CT is supportive but not definitive.
- **Surgery (posterior fossa decompression)** can reverse symptoms when imaging confirms tonsillar descent > 5 mm with clinical evidence of compression.

## Take‑Home Messages

- Re‑examining old imaging can unlock a diagnosis that went missed for a decade.  
- Silent hypoxia coupled with chocking and chronic occipital headache should prompt a posterior fossa MRI.  
- A crowded foramen magnum on CT should never be considered a “minor” finding: it may be the silent cause of central hypoventilation.  
- Full type I polysomnography with EEG and CO₂ monitoring is essential when central respiratory control is suspected.  
- Treating a Chiari malformation with posterior fossa decompression can not only resolve headaches but also restore automatic breathing and prevent dangerous hypoxic events.

**Bottom line:** In patients presenting with unexplained nocturnal choking and silent hypoxia, think beyond the airway. Look into the cranial base. A 14‑year‑old CT scan can be the missing linchpin, and the only thing standing between persistent distress and surgical salvage may be an old scan held in the archive.
