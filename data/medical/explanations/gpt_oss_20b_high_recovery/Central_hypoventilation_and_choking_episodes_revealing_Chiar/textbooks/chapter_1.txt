Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Comprehensive History

A woman in her early forties presented to her family‑medicine clinic with a 7‑year history of recurrent choking episodes. She described abrupt, distressing “bunching” events that occurred indiscriminately with solids and liquids, as well as periodically during sleep. The choking was unpredictable, involved violent coughing and gasping, yet surprisingly never progressed to complete airway obstruction. The symptom’s equal impact on both consistencies and its insidious onset distinguished it from the more familiar mechanical dysphagia caused by structural lesions, which generally affect solids or liquids more selectively.

During the COVID‑19 pandemic the patient began self‑monitoring oxygen saturation with a home pulse oximeter. Over several months she documented repeatedly asymptomatic drops to 70 %–80 %. These desaturations were often unrecognized until she checked the device, and she reported transient dizziness but no subjective breathlessness. The objective, unprovoked hypoxia meant that routine clinical encounters would have missed a critical physiological disturbance.

A separate, long‑standing neurological symptom—occipital and vertex pain—began 14 years earlier, after a motor‑vehicle collision that briefly caused a loss of consciousness. The pain was constant, pressure‑like, and localized to a coin‑sized area at the vertex. It was not exacerbated by coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers, but the weight of her hair produced marked allodynia. Multiple local scalp injections offered only transient relief, and cutting her hair short markedly reduced her discomfort, though this decision conflicted with cultural expectations and added to her emotional distress. This chronic vertex headache with allodynia, preceding the choking episodes by several years, suggested a neurogenic rather than peripheral source.

Despite extensive investigations aimed at ruling out mechanical causes of dysphagia, the patient’s diagnostic work‑up was unrevealing. Three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies documented entirely normal pharyngeal and esophageal anatomy. pH monitoring excluded gastro‑esophageal reflux as a trigger. Pulmonary function testing, chest imaging, and a comprehensive metabolic and autoimmune panel were all within normal limits. Thus, the recurrent choking could not be attributed to structural airway or esophageal abnormalities, reflux disease, or bulbar neuromuscular disorders such as myasthenia gravis, whose hallmark would have been fatigability, a fatigable bulbar weakness, and positive antibody testing.

The psychosocial impact of her symptoms was striking. She avoided dining out or social gatherings for fear of a public choking episode, and her anxiety and embarrassment grew over time. The need to keep hair short for pain relief conflicted with cultural norms and further undermined her self‑image. These facets of her life were inseparable from the clinical picture; heightened anxiety can amplify the subjective experience of choking, and social withdrawal can mask or exacerbate physiological symptoms.

Taken together, the patient’s history revealed key red flags for central or brainstem pathology. The simultaneous occurrence of choking across all consistencies, the nocturnal episodes of asymptomatic hypoxia, and the vertex headache with cutaneous allodynia point to a lesion affecting the craniocervical junction or brainstem. The antecedent head injury and subsequent emergence of both neurological pain and dysphagia further implicated a structural cause rather than a purely functional disorder. The normal swallowing studies, combined with normal reflux work‑up, strengthened this inference.

These elements of the history guided the subsequent imaging work‑up, which revealed cerebellar tonsillar descent and other findings diagnostic of Chiari malformation type I. The case illustrates how a comprehensive, methodical history—not merely a list of symptoms—can identify subtle patterns, eliminate common differentials, and direct clinicians toward uncovering an occult structural lesion that masquerades as central hypoventilation and chronic dysphagia.
