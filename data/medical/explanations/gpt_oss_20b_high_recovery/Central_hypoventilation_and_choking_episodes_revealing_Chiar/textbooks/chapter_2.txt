Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology of Chiari Malformation I and its Respiratory Manifestations

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is defined imaging‑wise by a caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal magnetic resonance imaging. In the adult population this anatomic variant is observed in approximately 1 % of routine scans, yet only about 0.1 % develop clinically significant symptoms. The condition exhibits a mild female predominance (≈1.3:1) and most symptomatic patients present in the third decade, although the spectrum extends from asymptomatic, incidental tonsillar descent to debilitating neurological illness. The recognition that many individuals harbor >5 mm of tonsillar herniation without complaint has shifted the approach from a binary “present/absent” classification to a symptom‑driven model, emphasizing the need to correlate the degree of descent with clinical features.

The developmental underpinning of CMI is a hypoplastic posterior fossa. This reduced space creates a volumetric mismatch between the intracranial contents—especially the cerebellum—and the available cranial cavity. When the posterior fossa volume is insufficient, the cerebellar tonsils are forced inferiorly, resulting in herniation through the foramen magnum. This caudal shift is not merely an anatomical curiosity; it can directly impede normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics. CSF outflow at the cranio‑cervical junction becomes obstructed, leading to a chronic “cushion” of pressure that exacerbates tonsillar descent and may provoke pulsatile syringomyelia. The combination of mechanical crowding and impaired CSF resorption constitutes the foundation for the diverse neurological sequelae observed in symptomatic CMI.

Central to the respiratory implications of CMI is the proximity of the descending tonsils to the medullary respiratory centers. The pre‑Bötzinger complex, located in the ventrolateral medulla, is the primary rhythm generator for the inspiratory phase of breathing. Adjacent to it lie the ventral respiratory group and the caudal dorsolateral nucleus, which integrate afferent input from the lung and pharyngeal stretch receptors. Compression of these nuclei by the herniated cerebellar tissue can disrupt the automatic (tonic) drive that maintains ventilation during quiet wakefulness, sleep, and states of distraction. In contrast, the volitional (cortically mediated) breathing pathway—reaching from the premotor cortex through the corticobulbar tracts to the phrenic nucleus—remains intact. This dichotomy explains why patients may cough or take rapid, conscious breaths during an episode of central hypoventilation but fail to generate spontaneous ventilation once the compensatory attentional drive wanes or the patient enters rapid‑eye‑movement (REM) or non‑REM sleep.

The classic illustration of this physiologic dissociation is the acquired “Ondine’s curse” or central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome secondary to CMI. In such patients, the brainstem’s automatic control is attenuated or lost, producing apnea or hypoventilation despite preserved consciousness. The patient’s sleep becomes a vulnerable period because the automatic rhythm is the primary driver of ventilation during the predominantly diaphragmatic pattern of slow‑wave sleep. In the presented case, the early‑morning choking episodes, nocturnal desaturations, and the frightening event at the airport where the woman briefly desaturated to 70 %–80 % without any accompanying sensation of breathlessness underscore this selective failure of automatic respiratory control.

Sleep‑related breathing disturbances form the next link in the pathophysiologic chain. While central apnea is the hallmark of the acquired hypoventilation syndrome, many CMI patients also exhibit central sleep apnea (CSA) of varying severity. In a prospective cohort of 90 adults with CMI, 50 % reported some form of sleep‑related breathing disorder; central apnoea indices (CAI) ≥ 5 were seen in 6.7 % of the cohort. The relative rarity of isolated CSA in CMI (only one case with CAI > 5 in that series) suggests that the more common presentation is a mixed sleep‑apnea profile where obstructive and central events coexist, likely because the behavioural components of sleep architecture (e.g., loss of muscle tone) are also perturbed by posterior fossa crowding. However, the presence of predominantly central events, as seen in the type III polysomnography of the index patient (72 % central), emphasizes that central mechanisms can dominate even when the overall apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) remains modest.

Beyond the respiratory centers, CMI can affect adjacent cranial nerve nuclei and descending pathways, providing a rationale for several non‑respiratory symptoms that are often interwoven with the respiratory picture. Compression of the lower cranial nerve nuclei can produce dysphagia, characterized by impaired coordination of pharyngeal and laryngeal musculature; this manifests as a failure to swallow both solids and liquids—an observation that matches the patient’s attempted swallowing studies. Loss of palatal or laryngeal sensation may also contribute to choking. Moreover, the cerebellar tonsils can impinge on the trigeminal or CN V fiber limb, producing occipital neuralgia—a painful, allodynic scalp phenomenon that the patient described as vertex pain relieved by hair trimming. Although the exact prevalence of such manifestations in CMI remains under‑studied, they can serve as red flags prompting neuroimaging in patients with nebulous or multifocal symptoms.

Imaging techniques elucidate the degree to which structural changes translate into functional impairment. The present case demonstrates the utility of careful review of older, non‑contrast CT scans. A previously missed crowded foramen magnum and loss of CSF space, unnoticed at the time of an acute head trauma, predicted the later recognition of a cerebellar tonsillar descent measured at 11 mm on MRI. Although the imaging findings were significant, the clinical interpretation—particularly of the hypoxic episodes without dyspnea—was delayed until the patient’s ongoing symptoms prompted a multidisciplinary work‑up. This underlines the importance of correlating radiographic severity with respiratory physiology, especially when evaluating the risk of central hypoventilation.

Finally, the association between CMI and central sleep apnea in adults calls for a high index of suspicion. In the absence of overt pulmonary pathology, unexplained nocturnal hypoxemia, recurrent choking, or dysphagia should trigger consideration of a posterior fossa disorder. Polysomnography that includes EEG, end‑tidal CO₂ monitoring, and video capture remains the gold standard for distinguishing central from obstructive events and for quantifying the severity of mixed sleep apnea. In the index patient, a type III study documented predominantly central events with a modest AHI, but the lack of EEG curtailed precise calculation of sleep duration and may have underestimated the severity. A full type I study would allow a more definitive appraisal of central apnea burden and guide therapeutic decisions, such as surgical decompression or ventilatory support.

In summary, Chiari malformation type I represents a spectrum of posterior fossa crowding that can lead to cerebellar tonsillar herniation, CSF outflow obstruction, and direct compression of medullary respiratory centers. The selective loss of automatic breathing while preserving volitional drive manifests clinically as central hypoventilation and the acquired “Ondine’s curse.” The coexistence of dysphagia, lower cranial nerve dysfunction, and occipital neuralgia further supports the multifocal impact of this structural disorder. Recognizing the pattern of respiratory disturbance—predominantly central events, especially during sleep—paired with imaging evidence of tonsillar descent should prompt consideration of CMI in the differential diagnosis of unexplained central hypoventilation syndromes.
