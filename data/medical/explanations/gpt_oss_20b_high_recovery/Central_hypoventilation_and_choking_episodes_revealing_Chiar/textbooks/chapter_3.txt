Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 3: ## Differential Diagnosis and Structured Diagnostic Workup

Evaluating a patient who presents with repeated choking episodes and unexplained periods of severe hypoxia requires a broad, organized approach that progressively narrows the possibilities from most frequent to less common causes. The case illustrates how a stepwise consideration of mechanical, functional, neurogenic, and reflux causes of dysphagia, coupled with systematic evaluation of the respiratory axis, can uncover a structural brainstem lesion—Chiari malformation type I (CMI)—that is rarely suspected when only respiration and dysphagia are described.

### 1. Choking: Differential of Mechanical, Functional, Neurogenic, and Reflux Etiologies

| Cause | Typical Features | Relevant Findings in the Case |
|-------|------------------|------------------------------|
| **Mechanical** (food bolus, anatomical narrowing, foreign body, laryngeal/oesophageal anomaly) | Sudden onset, often triggered by specific foods, a sense of “plug” in the throat, and clear airway obstruction. | Three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper‑GI endoscopies were all normal, with no evidence of structural lesions or strictures. |
| **Functional/psychogenic** | Often episodic, associated with anxiety or stress, may improve with distraction or reassurance. | Episodes occurred unpredictably, but the lack of psychosomatic features and the objective desaturations argued against a purely functional disorder. |
| **Neurogenic** (brainstem lesions, cranial‑nerve palsy, dysautonomia) | Bilateral dysphagia to both solids and liquids, often accompanied by other bulbar deficits, and a preserved voluntary swallow that fails simultaneously with automatic breathing. | Neurogenic dysphagia identified on videofluoroscopic swallow (equal difficulty with solids and liquids), and later imaging revealed cerebellar‑tonsillar herniation compressing the medulla. |
| **Reflux** (laryngopharyngeal reflux) | Suppurative cough, hoarseness, aspiration risk, acid‑related laryngeal changes, often relapsing with meals. | pH studies and esophageal exams were normal; no response to gold‑standard acid suppression therapy, making reflux unlikely. |

Systematically ruling out mechanical and reflux causes removed the most common etiologies of choking in an adult, shifting the attention toward neurogenic origins.

### 2. Unexplained Desaturation: Pulmonary, Cardiac, Vascular vs. Central Respiratory Failure

| Category | Key Diagnostic Tests | Case Findings |
|----------|----------------------|---------------|
| **Pulmonary** (COPD, ILD, asthma, pneumonia) | Chest radiograph, high‑resolution CT, spirometry, diffusion capacity. | Chest imaging was normal; spirometry was essentially normal (FEV₁ 98 % predicted, FVC 96 % predicted). |
| **Cardiac** (heart failure, conduction disease, pulmonary hypertension) | ECG, transthoracic echocardiogram, BNP. | No cardiac abnormalities noted; ABG showed normal PaCO₂. |
| **Vascular** (pulmonary embolism, atherosclerosis) | CT pulmonary angiography, Doppler studies. | No imaging evidence of embolism or vascular lesions. |
| **Central respiratory failure** (hypoventilation, central sleep apnea) | Polysomnography, brain imaging, assessment of autonomic respiratory control. | Type III sleep study showed an AHI of 6.0 with central events in 72 % of apneas; the patient’s ABG remained normal at baseline, indicating intact alveolar ventilation when awake. |

Thus, the absence of pulmonary, cardiac, or vascular disease directed the differential toward central mechanisms controlling ventilation.

### 3. Central Hypoventilation Syndromes: Congenital (PHOX2B) vs. Acquired

- **Congenital** (associated with PHOX2B mutations) is usually diagnosed in childhood or early adolescence, and patients typically have a family history and manifest profound hypoventilation from birth. No evidence of congenital anomaly or family history of hypoventilation was present in this patient.
- **Acquired** forms arise from destructive or compressive lesions of the brainstem or medulla that interrupt the automatic respiratory drive. The present case, with central apneas completely absent during sleep, preserved voluntary breathing, and a normal ABG on waking, fits the phenotype of an acquired hypoventilation syndrome (“Ondine’s curse”).

### 4. Imaging: Initial CT Review and Recognition of a Crowded Foramen Magnum

- A non‑contrast CT performed 14 years earlier—originally interpreted as normal—was re‑examined during the present assessment. The review revealed a **crowded foramen magnum** with loss of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space at the craniocervical junction and cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion. These findings were subtle and had been initially overlooked.
- An MRI of brain and cervical spine confirmed a **11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent**, exceeding the ≥5 mm threshold for CMI, and demonstrated tonsillar impingement on the lower medulla.

The imaging progression from an overlooked structural abnormality on old CT to definitive MRI evidence of CMI highlights the importance of correlating clinical suspicion with re‑review of prior imaging.

### 5. Polysomnography: Type III Methodology, AHI Calculation, Central Event Preponderance, EEG Limitations

- **Type III study** employed airflow (nasal pressure), respiratory effort belts, pulse oximetry, and heart rate. It lacks EEG, so the total recording time is often used as a surrogate for sleep time, potentially under‑estimating the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI).
- The patient’s AHI was 6.0/h, with **72 % central events** (i.e., the majority of apneas were central, not obstructive). The central predominance and profound oxygen desaturations (70–80 %) without dyspnea are hallmark features of central hypoventilation secondary to brainstem compression.
- Lack of EEG data precluded measurement of total sleep time and precise determination of the severity of central hypoventilation; nevertheless, the pattern of central events was sufficient to raise suspicion for an acquired central control disorder.

### 6. Swallow Studies: Assessing Neurogenic Dysphagia and Choking on Solids & Liquids

- A videofluoroscopic swallow study (modified barium swallow) was performed to evaluate bolus transfer and aspiration risk. The study revealed **neurogenic dysphagia** characterized by similar difficulty with solids and liquids—an important differentiator from mechanical dysphagia, which typically spares liquids. This finding correlated with her choking episodes during meals and reinforced the neurogenic nature of her dysphagia.

### 7. Laboratory Evaluation: Autoantibodies, Metabolic Panels, Pulmonary Function Tests

- **Autoimmune work‑up**: Anticholinergic receptor antibodies and anti‑muscle‑specific kinase antibodies were negative, excluding myasthenia gravis.
- **Metabolic panels**: Thyroid function, vitamin B12, and inflammatory markers were within normal limits, ruling out systemic metabolic or inflammatory contributors.
- **Pulmonary function tests**: Near‑normal spirometry and respiratory muscle pressures confirmed adequate pulmonary mechanics and diaphragmatic strength, supporting central rather than peripheral respiratory failure.

### 8. Diagnostic Algorithm: Integrating Clinical, Imaging, Sleep, and Swallow Data

1. **Clinical triage**: Document choking characteristics (solids vs. liquids, frequency, presence of overt airway obstruction).  
2. **ENT work‑up**: Perform flexible laryngoscopy and upper‑GI endoscopy. *If normal*, proceed.  
3. **Reflux assessment**: Esophageal pH monitoring and laryngoscopic assessment for LPR. *If negative*, proceed.  
4. **Pulmonary evaluation**: Chest radiography, CT thorax, spirometry, ABG. *If normal*, proceed.  
5. **Cardiac/vascular screens**: ECG, echo, BNP, CT angiography if indicated. *If normal*, proceed.  
6. **Swallow assessment**: Videofluoroscopic swallow study for neurogenic dysphagia.  
7. **Sleep evaluation**: Initiate Type III polysomnography. Note AHI, central vs. obstructive event distribution.  
8. **Neuroimaging**: Review previous CT; if abnormal or pending, obtain MRI of brain and cervical spine to assess tonsillar descent and posterior fossa crowding.  
9. **Synthesis**: Correlate central events, neurogenic dysphagia, and imaging of brainstem compression to establish a diagnosis of acquired central hypoventilation due to CMI.

This algorithm ensures that each potential etiology is explored and systematically excluded before attributing symptoms to an uncommon structural lesion.

### 9. Criteria for Diagnosing Acquired Central Hypoventilation in the Context of Brainstem Compression

| Criterion | How It Applied in This Case |
|-----------|-----------------------------|
| **Absence of pulmonary, cardiac, and vascular disease** | Normal chest imaging, spirometry, ECG, echo. |
| **Preserved voluntary breathing** | The patient could consciously ventilate when awake; desaturations were confined to times of sleep or distraction. |
| **Central events on polysomnography** | AHI of 6.0/h with 72 % central events. |
| **Evidence of brainstem compression** | MRI documentation of cerebellar tonsillar descent (11 mm) and crowded foramen magnum on CT. |
| **Normal autonomic and metabolic evaluations** | Negative for autoimmune neuromuscular disease; normal ABG, metabolic panels. |

When these elements converge, an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome secondary to brainstem compression can be reliably diagnosed, even in the absence of overt neurological deficits outside of dysphagia and respiratory compromise.

---

By following this structured diagnostic pathway, clinicians can avoid diagnostic delays and misattribution of symptoms to benign or functional disorders. In the presented case, integrating the patient’s choking history, unexplained desaturation, sleep‑study findings, swallow study, laboratory work‑up, and neuroimaging data led to the recognition of CMI as the unifying cause and appropriate referral for definitive treatment.
