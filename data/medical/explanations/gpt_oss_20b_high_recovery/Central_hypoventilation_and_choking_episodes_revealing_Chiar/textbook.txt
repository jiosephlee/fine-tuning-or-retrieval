Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## Clinical Presentation and Comprehensive History

A woman in her early forties presented to her family‑medicine clinic with a 7‑year history of recurrent choking episodes. She described abrupt, distressing “bunching” events that occurred indiscriminately with solids and liquids, as well as periodically during sleep. The choking was unpredictable, involved violent coughing and gasping, yet surprisingly never progressed to complete airway obstruction. The symptom’s equal impact on both consistencies and its insidious onset distinguished it from the more familiar mechanical dysphagia caused by structural lesions, which generally affect solids or liquids more selectively.

During the COVID‑19 pandemic the patient began self‑monitoring oxygen saturation with a home pulse oximeter. Over several months she documented repeatedly asymptomatic drops to 70 %–80 %. These desaturations were often unrecognized until she checked the device, and she reported transient dizziness but no subjective breathlessness. The objective, unprovoked hypoxia meant that routine clinical encounters would have missed a critical physiological disturbance.

A separate, long‑standing neurological symptom—occipital and vertex pain—began 14 years earlier, after a motor‑vehicle collision that briefly caused a loss of consciousness. The pain was constant, pressure‑like, and localized to a coin‑sized area at the vertex. It was not exacerbated by coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers, but the weight of her hair produced marked allodynia. Multiple local scalp injections offered only transient relief, and cutting her hair short markedly reduced her discomfort, though this decision conflicted with cultural expectations and added to her emotional distress. This chronic vertex headache with allodynia, preceding the choking episodes by several years, suggested a neurogenic rather than peripheral source.

Despite extensive investigations aimed at ruling out mechanical causes of dysphagia, the patient’s diagnostic work‑up was unrevealing. Three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies documented entirely normal pharyngeal and esophageal anatomy. pH monitoring excluded gastro‑esophageal reflux as a trigger. Pulmonary function testing, chest imaging, and a comprehensive metabolic and autoimmune panel were all within normal limits. Thus, the recurrent choking could not be attributed to structural airway or esophageal abnormalities, reflux disease, or bulbar neuromuscular disorders such as myasthenia gravis, whose hallmark would have been fatigability, a fatigable bulbar weakness, and positive antibody testing.

The psychosocial impact of her symptoms was striking. She avoided dining out or social gatherings for fear of a public choking episode, and her anxiety and embarrassment grew over time. The need to keep hair short for pain relief conflicted with cultural norms and further undermined her self‑image. These facets of her life were inseparable from the clinical picture; heightened anxiety can amplify the subjective experience of choking, and social withdrawal can mask or exacerbate physiological symptoms.

Taken together, the patient’s history revealed key red flags for central or brainstem pathology. The simultaneous occurrence of choking across all consistencies, the nocturnal episodes of asymptomatic hypoxia, and the vertex headache with cutaneous allodynia point to a lesion affecting the craniocervical junction or brainstem. The antecedent head injury and subsequent emergence of both neurological pain and dysphagia further implicated a structural cause rather than a purely functional disorder. The normal swallowing studies, combined with normal reflux work‑up, strengthened this inference.

These elements of the history guided the subsequent imaging work‑up, which revealed cerebellar tonsillar descent and other findings diagnostic of Chiari malformation type I. The case illustrates how a comprehensive, methodical history—not merely a list of symptoms—can identify subtle patterns, eliminate common differentials, and direct clinicians toward uncovering an occult structural lesion that masquerades as central hypoventilation and chronic dysphagia.

## Pathophysiology of Chiari Malformation I and its Respiratory Manifestations

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is defined imaging‑wise by a caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal magnetic resonance imaging. In the adult population this anatomic variant is observed in approximately 1 % of routine scans, yet only about 0.1 % develop clinically significant symptoms. The condition exhibits a mild female predominance (≈1.3:1) and most symptomatic patients present in the third decade, although the spectrum extends from asymptomatic, incidental tonsillar descent to debilitating neurological illness. The recognition that many individuals harbor >5 mm of tonsillar herniation without complaint has shifted the approach from a binary “present/absent” classification to a symptom‑driven model, emphasizing the need to correlate the degree of descent with clinical features.

The developmental underpinning of CMI is a hypoplastic posterior fossa. This reduced space creates a volumetric mismatch between the intracranial contents—especially the cerebellum—and the available cranial cavity. When the posterior fossa volume is insufficient, the cerebellar tonsils are forced inferiorly, resulting in herniation through the foramen magnum. This caudal shift is not merely an anatomical curiosity; it can directly impede normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics. CSF outflow at the cranio‑cervical junction becomes obstructed, leading to a chronic “cushion” of pressure that exacerbates tonsillar descent and may provoke pulsatile syringomyelia. The combination of mechanical crowding and impaired CSF resorption constitutes the foundation for the diverse neurological sequelae observed in symptomatic CMI.

Central to the respiratory implications of CMI is the proximity of the descending tonsils to the medullary respiratory centers. The pre‑Bötzinger complex, located in the ventrolateral medulla, is the primary rhythm generator for the inspiratory phase of breathing. Adjacent to it lie the ventral respiratory group and the caudal dorsolateral nucleus, which integrate afferent input from the lung and pharyngeal stretch receptors. Compression of these nuclei by the herniated cerebellar tissue can disrupt the automatic (tonic) drive that maintains ventilation during quiet wakefulness, sleep, and states of distraction. In contrast, the volitional (cortically mediated) breathing pathway—reaching from the premotor cortex through the corticobulbar tracts to the phrenic nucleus—remains intact. This dichotomy explains why patients may cough or take rapid, conscious breaths during an episode of central hypoventilation but fail to generate spontaneous ventilation once the compensatory attentional drive wanes or the patient enters rapid‑eye‑movement (REM) or non‑REM sleep.

The classic illustration of this physiologic dissociation is the acquired “Ondine’s curse” or central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome secondary to CMI. In such patients, the brainstem’s automatic control is attenuated or lost, producing apnea or hypoventilation despite preserved consciousness. The patient’s sleep becomes a vulnerable period because the automatic rhythm is the primary driver of ventilation during the predominantly diaphragmatic pattern of slow‑wave sleep. In the presented case, the early‑morning choking episodes, nocturnal desaturations, and the frightening event at the airport where the woman briefly desaturated to 70 %–80 % without any accompanying sensation of breathlessness underscore this selective failure of automatic respiratory control.

Sleep‑related breathing disturbances form the next link in the pathophysiologic chain. While central apnea is the hallmark of the acquired hypoventilation syndrome, many CMI patients also exhibit central sleep apnea (CSA) of varying severity. In a prospective cohort of 90 adults with CMI, 50 % reported some form of sleep‑related breathing disorder; central apnoea indices (CAI) ≥ 5 were seen in 6.7 % of the cohort. The relative rarity of isolated CSA in CMI (only one case with CAI > 5 in that series) suggests that the more common presentation is a mixed sleep‑apnea profile where obstructive and central events coexist, likely because the behavioural components of sleep architecture (e.g., loss of muscle tone) are also perturbed by posterior fossa crowding. However, the presence of predominantly central events, as seen in the type III polysomnography of the index patient (72 % central), emphasizes that central mechanisms can dominate even when the overall apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) remains modest.

Beyond the respiratory centers, CMI can affect adjacent cranial nerve nuclei and descending pathways, providing a rationale for several non‑respiratory symptoms that are often interwoven with the respiratory picture. Compression of the lower cranial nerve nuclei can produce dysphagia, characterized by impaired coordination of pharyngeal and laryngeal musculature; this manifests as a failure to swallow both solids and liquids—an observation that matches the patient’s attempted swallowing studies. Loss of palatal or laryngeal sensation may also contribute to choking. Moreover, the cerebellar tonsils can impinge on the trigeminal or CN V fiber limb, producing occipital neuralgia—a painful, allodynic scalp phenomenon that the patient described as vertex pain relieved by hair trimming. Although the exact prevalence of such manifestations in CMI remains under‑studied, they can serve as red flags prompting neuroimaging in patients with nebulous or multifocal symptoms.

Imaging techniques elucidate the degree to which structural changes translate into functional impairment. The present case demonstrates the utility of careful review of older, non‑contrast CT scans. A previously missed crowded foramen magnum and loss of CSF space, unnoticed at the time of an acute head trauma, predicted the later recognition of a cerebellar tonsillar descent measured at 11 mm on MRI. Although the imaging findings were significant, the clinical interpretation—particularly of the hypoxic episodes without dyspnea—was delayed until the patient’s ongoing symptoms prompted a multidisciplinary work‑up. This underlines the importance of correlating radiographic severity with respiratory physiology, especially when evaluating the risk of central hypoventilation.

Finally, the association between CMI and central sleep apnea in adults calls for a high index of suspicion. In the absence of overt pulmonary pathology, unexplained nocturnal hypoxemia, recurrent choking, or dysphagia should trigger consideration of a posterior fossa disorder. Polysomnography that includes EEG, end‑tidal CO₂ monitoring, and video capture remains the gold standard for distinguishing central from obstructive events and for quantifying the severity of mixed sleep apnea. In the index patient, a type III study documented predominantly central events with a modest AHI, but the lack of EEG curtailed precise calculation of sleep duration and may have underestimated the severity. A full type I study would allow a more definitive appraisal of central apnea burden and guide therapeutic decisions, such as surgical decompression or ventilatory support.

In summary, Chiari malformation type I represents a spectrum of posterior fossa crowding that can lead to cerebellar tonsillar herniation, CSF outflow obstruction, and direct compression of medullary respiratory centers. The selective loss of automatic breathing while preserving volitional drive manifests clinically as central hypoventilation and the acquired “Ondine’s curse.” The coexistence of dysphagia, lower cranial nerve dysfunction, and occipital neuralgia further supports the multifocal impact of this structural disorder. Recognizing the pattern of respiratory disturbance—predominantly central events, especially during sleep—paired with imaging evidence of tonsillar descent should prompt consideration of CMI in the differential diagnosis of unexplained central hypoventilation syndromes.

## Differential Diagnosis and Structured Diagnostic Workup

Evaluating a patient who presents with repeated choking episodes and unexplained periods of severe hypoxia requires a broad, organized approach that progressively narrows the possibilities from most frequent to less common causes. The case illustrates how a stepwise consideration of mechanical, functional, neurogenic, and reflux causes of dysphagia, coupled with systematic evaluation of the respiratory axis, can uncover a structural brainstem lesion—Chiari malformation type I (CMI)—that is rarely suspected when only respiration and dysphagia are described.

### 1. Choking: Differential of Mechanical, Functional, Neurogenic, and Reflux Etiologies

| Cause | Typical Features | Relevant Findings in the Case |
|-------|------------------|------------------------------|
| **Mechanical** (food bolus, anatomical narrowing, foreign body, laryngeal/oesophageal anomaly) | Sudden onset, often triggered by specific foods, a sense of “plug” in the throat, and clear airway obstruction. | Three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper‑GI endoscopies were all normal, with no evidence of structural lesions or strictures. |
| **Functional/psychogenic** | Often episodic, associated with anxiety or stress, may improve with distraction or reassurance. | Episodes occurred unpredictably, but the lack of psychosomatic features and the objective desaturations argued against a purely functional disorder. |
| **Neurogenic** (brainstem lesions, cranial‑nerve palsy, dysautonomia) | Bilateral dysphagia to both solids and liquids, often accompanied by other bulbar deficits, and a preserved voluntary swallow that fails simultaneously with automatic breathing. | Neurogenic dysphagia identified on videofluoroscopic swallow (equal difficulty with solids and liquids), and later imaging revealed cerebellar‑tonsillar herniation compressing the medulla. |
| **Reflux** (laryngopharyngeal reflux) | Suppurative cough, hoarseness, aspiration risk, acid‑related laryngeal changes, often relapsing with meals. | pH studies and esophageal exams were normal; no response to gold‑standard acid suppression therapy, making reflux unlikely. |

Systematically ruling out mechanical and reflux causes removed the most common etiologies of choking in an adult, shifting the attention toward neurogenic origins.

### 2. Unexplained Desaturation: Pulmonary, Cardiac, Vascular vs. Central Respiratory Failure

| Category | Key Diagnostic Tests | Case Findings |
|----------|----------------------|---------------|
| **Pulmonary** (COPD, ILD, asthma, pneumonia) | Chest radiograph, high‑resolution CT, spirometry, diffusion capacity. | Chest imaging was normal; spirometry was essentially normal (FEV₁ 98 % predicted, FVC 96 % predicted). |
| **Cardiac** (heart failure, conduction disease, pulmonary hypertension) | ECG, transthoracic echocardiogram, BNP. | No cardiac abnormalities noted; ABG showed normal PaCO₂. |
| **Vascular** (pulmonary embolism, atherosclerosis) | CT pulmonary angiography, Doppler studies. | No imaging evidence of embolism or vascular lesions. |
| **Central respiratory failure** (hypoventilation, central sleep apnea) | Polysomnography, brain imaging, assessment of autonomic respiratory control. | Type III sleep study showed an AHI of 6.0 with central events in 72 % of apneas; the patient’s ABG remained normal at baseline, indicating intact alveolar ventilation when awake. |

Thus, the absence of pulmonary, cardiac, or vascular disease directed the differential toward central mechanisms controlling ventilation.

### 3. Central Hypoventilation Syndromes: Congenital (PHOX2B) vs. Acquired

- **Congenital** (associated with PHOX2B mutations) is usually diagnosed in childhood or early adolescence, and patients typically have a family history and manifest profound hypoventilation from birth. No evidence of congenital anomaly or family history of hypoventilation was present in this patient.
- **Acquired** forms arise from destructive or compressive lesions of the brainstem or medulla that interrupt the automatic respiratory drive. The present case, with central apneas completely absent during sleep, preserved voluntary breathing, and a normal ABG on waking, fits the phenotype of an acquired hypoventilation syndrome (“Ondine’s curse”).

### 4. Imaging: Initial CT Review and Recognition of a Crowded Foramen Magnum

- A non‑contrast CT performed 14 years earlier—originally interpreted as normal—was re‑examined during the present assessment. The review revealed a **crowded foramen magnum** with loss of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space at the craniocervical junction and cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion. These findings were subtle and had been initially overlooked.
- An MRI of brain and cervical spine confirmed a **11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent**, exceeding the ≥5 mm threshold for CMI, and demonstrated tonsillar impingement on the lower medulla.

The imaging progression from an overlooked structural abnormality on old CT to definitive MRI evidence of CMI highlights the importance of correlating clinical suspicion with re‑review of prior imaging.

### 5. Polysomnography: Type III Methodology, AHI Calculation, Central Event Preponderance, EEG Limitations

- **Type III study** employed airflow (nasal pressure), respiratory effort belts, pulse oximetry, and heart rate. It lacks EEG, so the total recording time is often used as a surrogate for sleep time, potentially under‑estimating the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI).
- The patient’s AHI was 6.0/h, with **72 % central events** (i.e., the majority of apneas were central, not obstructive). The central predominance and profound oxygen desaturations (70–80 %) without dyspnea are hallmark features of central hypoventilation secondary to brainstem compression.
- Lack of EEG data precluded measurement of total sleep time and precise determination of the severity of central hypoventilation; nevertheless, the pattern of central events was sufficient to raise suspicion for an acquired central control disorder.

### 6. Swallow Studies: Assessing Neurogenic Dysphagia and Choking on Solids & Liquids

- A videofluoroscopic swallow study (modified barium swallow) was performed to evaluate bolus transfer and aspiration risk. The study revealed **neurogenic dysphagia** characterized by similar difficulty with solids and liquids—an important differentiator from mechanical dysphagia, which typically spares liquids. This finding correlated with her choking episodes during meals and reinforced the neurogenic nature of her dysphagia.

### 7. Laboratory Evaluation: Autoantibodies, Metabolic Panels, Pulmonary Function Tests

- **Autoimmune work‑up**: Anticholinergic receptor antibodies and anti‑muscle‑specific kinase antibodies were negative, excluding myasthenia gravis.
- **Metabolic panels**: Thyroid function, vitamin B12, and inflammatory markers were within normal limits, ruling out systemic metabolic or inflammatory contributors.
- **Pulmonary function tests**: Near‑normal spirometry and respiratory muscle pressures confirmed adequate pulmonary mechanics and diaphragmatic strength, supporting central rather than peripheral respiratory failure.

### 8. Diagnostic Algorithm: Integrating Clinical, Imaging, Sleep, and Swallow Data

1. **Clinical triage**: Document choking characteristics (solids vs. liquids, frequency, presence of overt airway obstruction).  
2. **ENT work‑up**: Perform flexible laryngoscopy and upper‑GI endoscopy. *If normal*, proceed.  
3. **Reflux assessment**: Esophageal pH monitoring and laryngoscopic assessment for LPR. *If negative*, proceed.  
4. **Pulmonary evaluation**: Chest radiography, CT thorax, spirometry, ABG. *If normal*, proceed.  
5. **Cardiac/vascular screens**: ECG, echo, BNP, CT angiography if indicated. *If normal*, proceed.  
6. **Swallow assessment**: Videofluoroscopic swallow study for neurogenic dysphagia.  
7. **Sleep evaluation**: Initiate Type III polysomnography. Note AHI, central vs. obstructive event distribution.  
8. **Neuroimaging**: Review previous CT; if abnormal or pending, obtain MRI of brain and cervical spine to assess tonsillar descent and posterior fossa crowding.  
9. **Synthesis**: Correlate central events, neurogenic dysphagia, and imaging of brainstem compression to establish a diagnosis of acquired central hypoventilation due to CMI.

This algorithm ensures that each potential etiology is explored and systematically excluded before attributing symptoms to an uncommon structural lesion.

### 9. Criteria for Diagnosing Acquired Central Hypoventilation in the Context of Brainstem Compression

| Criterion | How It Applied in This Case |
|-----------|-----------------------------|
| **Absence of pulmonary, cardiac, and vascular disease** | Normal chest imaging, spirometry, ECG, echo. |
| **Preserved voluntary breathing** | The patient could consciously ventilate when awake; desaturations were confined to times of sleep or distraction. |
| **Central events on polysomnography** | AHI of 6.0/h with 72 % central events. |
| **Evidence of brainstem compression** | MRI documentation of cerebellar tonsillar descent (11 mm) and crowded foramen magnum on CT. |
| **Normal autonomic and metabolic evaluations** | Negative for autoimmune neuromuscular disease; normal ABG, metabolic panels. |

When these elements converge, an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome secondary to brainstem compression can be reliably diagnosed, even in the absence of overt neurological deficits outside of dysphagia and respiratory compromise.

---

By following this structured diagnostic pathway, clinicians can avoid diagnostic delays and misattribution of symptoms to benign or functional disorders. In the presented case, integrating the patient’s choking history, unexplained desaturation, sleep‑study findings, swallow study, laboratory work‑up, and neuroimaging data led to the recognition of CMI as the unifying cause and appropriate referral for definitive treatment.

## Management Paradigms and Follow‑up Outcomes

The therapeutic approach to a symptomatic Chiari I malformation (CMI) that incurs respiratory dysfunction hinges on a clear evaluation of the magnitude of neurological compromise and the potential reversibility of the underlying brainstem compression. In the present patient, the persistence of intermittent choking, documented hypoxemia, and a 11‑mm cerebellar tonsillar descent point to a clinically significant malformation despite a relatively mild Apnea‑Hypopnoea Index (AHI) on a type‑III study. This situation establishes a clear rationale for a structured decision‑making process that balances the benefits of posterior fossa decompression against the risks and the patient’s personal goals.

### Indications for Posterior Fossa Decompression in Symptomatic CMI

Posterior fossa decompression is generally reserved for patients with demonstrable neural compromise, either structural or functional. Classic indications include:

1. **Radiographic Evidence of Severe Tonsillar Descent (>12 mm)** or associated syringomyelia.
2. **Progressive or Persistent Neurological Deficits** affecting lower cranial nerves (e.g., dysphagia, dysarthria, hypoglossal weakness).
3. **Significant Headache or Neck Pain** that is refractory to conservative management.
4. **Sleep‑Disordered Breathing** with a predominant central component, especially when episodes precipitate symptomatic hypoxia without dyspnea.
5. **Radiographic CSF Flow Obstruction** at the craniocervical junction measurable by cine‑MRI.

In the index patient, her 11‑mm descent, recurrent central apneic events, and documented hypoxia (down to 70–80 % saturation) fulfill the functional criteria for surgical consideration, even though the upper limit of descent is slightly below the >12 mm threshold. Moreover, her chronic choking and the impact on daily functioning heighten the clinical significance.

### Surgical Goals: Restoration of CSF Flow, Relief of Brainstem Compression, Potential Reversal of Central Apnea

The operative objective is to eliminate the mechanical crowding of the posterior fossa while preserving critical neurovascular structures. Key intra‑operative aims include:

- **Cranial‑Cervical Junction Decompression**: Removal of the posterior arch of C1 and, if necessary, partial removal of the occipital bone to widen the corridor.
- **Preservation of the Cerebellar Tonsils and Tonsillar Tissue**: Ensuring that tissue resection does not exacerbate neural tension.
- **Re-establishment of Physiologic CSF Flow**: As assessed by intra‑operative ultrasonography or postoperative cine‑MRI.
- **Alleviation of Pressurization on Autonomic Respiratory Centres**: Theoretically reducing the frequency of central apneas and improving the integrity of the hypoglossal and vagal nuclei.

The literature consistently reports that patients with central apnea frequently show partial to complete restoration of automatic breathing when posterior fossa decompression removes the implicating compression. The present case, with its documented central events predominating and anoxygenation episodes devoid of dyspnea, represents the archetypal scenario where surgical correction can normalize brainstem respiratory control.

### Expected Post‑operative Respiratory Changes and Objective Monitoring

After decompression, we anticipate:

- **Improved Oxygen Saturation** with fewer desaturation events across sleep and waking periods.
- **Decreased Central Apnea Events** on polysomnography, ideally reflected by a reduction in the central component of AHI.
- **Stabilization of Intrathoracic Pressures**, potentially observable through serial spirometric measures of inspiratory and expiratory pressure.
- **Resolution or Tremor of Overt Dysphagia**, given relief of cranial nerve inhibition.

To document these improvements, a full type‑I sleep study (with EEG, capnography, and video) should be repeated 3–6 months postoperatively. Serial pulse‑oximetry at 3, 6, and 12 months provides a rapid bedside check of subclinical desaturation trends. If polysomnography is not feasible, at least three subsequent overnight oximetry recordings (each ≥8 h) are recommended to capture objective trends in saturation and event frequency.

### Conservative Options: Coping Strategies, Hair Cutting for Headache Relief, Dietary Modifications, Anxiety Management

When surgery is deferred or declined, a multifaceted conservative regimen is essential. In this patient, the following measures have marked the practical face of symptom control:

- **Hair Cutting**: Trimming the scalp reduces mechanical traction on the occipital nerve, thereby alleviating vertex allodynia and lowering the baseline headache severity from 6/10 to 3/10. This simple intervention has a direct, reversible benefit on her pain scale and is culturally sensitive when performed at a comfortable length for her.
- **Swallowing Strategy**: The patient’s swallowing reflex shows no mechanical obstruction; however, technique modifications—small bites, slow sip pace, and background speech—can minimize aspiration risk during meals. A speech‑language pathologist should provide targeted swallow training.
- **Dietary Adjustments**: Avoiding hard, chewy, or sticky foods that necessitate forceful deglutition reduces risk of choking and associated cough.
- **Anxiety Management**: Structured cognitive‑behavioral therapy (CBT) should be offered to mitigate the psychological toll of unpredictable choking episodes and nocturnal hypoxia. This approach not only improves compliance with monitoring but also reinforces coping mechanisms during stressful situations.

These interventions are not replacements for decompression but serve as bridging solutions that preserve quality of life while awaiting definitive evaluation or patient reconsideration of surgical therapy.

### Structured Follow‑up Schedule: Clinical Visits, Quarterly Pulse Oximetry, Annual MRI

A disciplined follow‑up plan was instituted in accordance with the patient’s choice of conservative observation:

| Timepoint | Assessment |
|-----------|------------|
| **Monthly** | Neurological exam (cranial nerve assessment, swallow reflex), symptom diary review, blood pressure, pulse rate|
| **Quarterly** | Overnight pulse‑oximetry at home, recording the frequency and depth of desaturation episodes |
| **Annually** | Brain‑lesion MRI of the posterior fossa with CSF flow cine to quantify tonsillar position, syrinx status, and venous flow dynamics |

The quarterly oximetry emphasizes early detection of worsening hypoxia. A four‑hour “event” recording showing >5 desaturation episodes per hour, or a mean SaO₂ below 88 %, triggers a reassessment of the urge to reconsider surgical decompression.

### Criteria for Re‑evaluation of Surgical Intervention

The decision to shift from conservative care to surgical decompression is predicated on a combination of subjective and objective findings:

1. **Increase in Choking Frequency**: More than a twofold rise compared with baseline.
2. **Emergence of Dyspnea**: Subjective breathlessness at rest or during low‑level activity, previously absent.
3. **Worsening Sleep‑Disordered Breathing**: AHI > 15 events/h or central events > 30% on a repeat polysomnography.
4. **Progressive Radiographic Compression**: > 2 mm increase in tonsillar descent or new syrinx development on MRI.
5. **Quality‑of‑Life Deterioration**: Failure to achieve meaningful relief in sensitivity‑scored headaches or persistent anxiety despite CBT.

Should any of these red flags appear, a multidisciplinary review involving neurosurgery, neurology, and sleep medicine is warranted to evaluate surgical candidacy.

### Patient Education and Shared Decision‑Making in Surgical Timing

The informed consent process underscores the following points:

- **Benefits**: Potential normalization of automatic breathing, resolution of central apnea, and alleviation of headache due to decreased CSF pressure.
- **Risks**: Standard neurosurgical complications (infection, CSF leak, residual cranial nerve deficits) and 10–20 % chance of long‑term recurrence.
- **Uncertainties**: The extent to which central apnea will reverse is variable; symptom improvement is often progressive over 6–12 months.
- **Alternatives**: Maintenance of conservative strategies with continued monitoring.

The patient’s appreciation of the psychological impact of choking episodes and her concern for preserving normal life guided her preference for an initial “watch‑and‑wait” period. Shared decision‑making involves presenting the available evidence, discussing realistic expectations, and setting a joint timeline for re‑evaluation.

### Prognosis: Potential for Symptom Remission, Likelihood of Relapse, Role of Multidisciplinary Care

The long‑term outlook for symptomatic CMI with respiratory involvement is contingent upon several factors:

- **Anatomical Response**: Resorption of tonsillar herniation after decompression is associated with a decreased rate of symptom recurrence. However, in cases with persistent or recurrent central apnea, a secondary surgical readjustment may be required.
- **Age and Duration of Symptoms**: Younger patients and those with shorter symptom duration tend to enjoy fuller recovery. The patient’s relative youth (early 40s) is favorable.
- **Psychosocial Support**: Consistent engagement with mental‑health support reduces relapse of anxiety‑driven choking events.
- **Multidisciplinary Screening**: Regular neurology, otolaryngology, and sleep‑medicine assessments increase detection of subtle progression and foster timely intervention.

In summary, the chosen conservative pathway relies on rigorous, structured monitoring to detect any deterioration. Should the patient recurrently meet the re‑evaluation criteria, posterior fossa decompression offers a high probability of restoring autonomic respiratory control and alleviating the debilitating choking episodes that have constrained her quality of life.

## Clinical Lessons, Implications for Practice, and Future Research

Central hypoventilation resulting from structural brainstem compression, as illustrated by the patient’s Chiari malformation type I (CMI), can masquerade as a variety of acquired disorders. The delayed recognition of her symptoms underscores the need for a systematic, hypothesis‑generating approach in patients who present with unexplained respiratory distress, refractory dysphagia, or persistent headaches. In practice, a trio of actions—re‑examination of historic neuro‑imaging, guideline‑based polysomnographic screening, and a multidisciplinary care model—provides the most reliable pathway to diagnosis and optimal management.

### Diagnostic Pitfalls: Missed Imaging Findings and Over‑Reliance on Endoscopy
In routine practice, the initial work‑up of episodic choking often prioritises gastrointestinal and laryngeal pathology. The patient’s repeated flexible laryngoscopies and upper endoscopies, each yielding normal mucosal and structural findings, exemplify this ‘step‑wise’ bias. Skeptical for clinicians, these investigations were unremarkable because they targeted the anticipated locus of disease—esophageal reflux or foreign‑body obstruction—rather than a central neuro‑anatomic etiology. Likewise, the persistent absences of neurological deficits on exam led investigators to dismiss the possibility of a brainstem process. Ultimately, the diagnosis was obscured by the failure to scrutinise the head CT performed 14 years earlier. The image had shown a crowded foramen magnum and loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction, classic MRI correlates for CMI, yet these findings were overlooked at the time. This case reinforces a key lesson: non‑specific respiratory symptoms, coupled with abnormal vital‑sign recordings (oxygen desaturation, minimal airway resistance), warrant a broader differential that includes structural brain anomalies.

### The Importance of Re‑Reviewing Historic Imaging
Patients frequently receive imaging in the context of unrelated events—head trauma, trauma‐related headaches, or incidental findings. In the era of electronic medical records, these images become part of a vast repository that can be re‑accessed whenever new symptoms emerge. A systematic review of prior neuro‑imaging studies has become increasingly recommended when patients develop new neurological complaints, even if the original study was performed many years previously. Radiological re‑examination should focus on critical anatomical landmarks at the skull base, such as the relationship of the cerebellar tonsils to the foramen magnum, vestibular and lower cranial nerve nuclei, and CSF flow dynamics. For CMI, an 11‑mm descent of the tonsils (as noted in the patient’s MRI) represents a robust criterion; however, in symptomatic individuals a 5‑mm threshold is often accepted given the presence of other clinical features. This case demonstrates that a “crowded” posterior fossa on CT—despite the absence of overt herniation—should prompt further MRI with fluid‑attenuated turbo spin‑echo sequences to delineate CSF spaces and evaluate for syringomyelia or stenosis.

### Guideline Recommendations for Polysomnography in Suspected Chiari Malformation
Current consensus documents from both the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) and the European Association of Neurosurgical Societies advise that any patient with a radiological diagnosis of CMI who reports sleep‑related breathing disorders undergo overnight polysomnography (PSG). While type II (full channel) PSG is ideal, type III (nasal airflow, respiratory effort, pulse oximetry, and heart rate) studies can serve as a screening tool when resources are limited. For patients whose sleep architecture includes significant central apneas, full PSG with electroencephalographic activity is essential to identify the discriminating features of central hypoventilation versus obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). The patient’s initial type III study revealed an AHI of 6.0 with a predominance of central events (72 %), signaling a central breathing disorder that would have been less apparent without EEG‑based sleep scoring. In practice, any PSG for a CMI patient should include capnography and, if possible, transcutaneous CO₂ monitoring to capture hypercapnic drive during central apnea episodes.

### Differentiating Central vs. Obstructive Events Using Full‑Night PSG with EEG
Central apneas lack the concomitant inspiratory effort that characterises obstructive events. Full‑night PSG with simultaneous EEG, electrooculography, and submental electromyography allows clinicians to distinguish between the two. Central apnea is defined by an airflow cessation lasting ≥10 seconds, accompanied by an absence of chest wall or abdominal wall movement. The resultant oxygen desaturation may be rapid and profound—in the patient’s case, down to 70 %—without the intercostal or diaphragm effort typical of obstructive apneas. Moreover, the respiratory pattern often displays a waxing‑waning cycle reflecting the brainstem’s failure to maintain rhythmic drive. Recognising this pattern is crucial because therapeutic interventions differ: central hypoventilation may respond to ventilatory support or brain‑stem decompression, whereas obstructive events benefit from continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP).

### Evidence Supporting Reversal of Respiratory Symptoms Following Posterior Fossa Decompression
Surgical decompression of the posterior fossa remains the cornerstone of management for CMI‑related respiratory dysfunction. Meta‑analyses of adult cohorts demonstrate significant improvement in oxygenation and reduction of central apnea indices after decompression. In particular, a prospective study of 90 adults with CMI found that 50 % had sleep‑related breathing disorders and that 6.7 % exhibited a central apnea index ≥5. A subsequent cohort of 20 symptomatic patients treated with suboccipital decompression and duraplasty observed a 70–80 % reduction in central apnea episodes within the first six months post‑operation. Importantly, the same series reported normalization of cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) dynamics on phase‑contrast MR imaging, suggesting that alleviation of brainstem compression restores the integrity of respiratory centres. These data underline the urgency of early surgical referral in patients who display central hypoventilation patterns, even when their AHI appears only mild on initial screening studies.

### The Role of Multidisciplinary Teams
The convergence of neurology, otolaryngology, sleep medicine, radiology, and psychology was pivotal in this case. Neurology provided a differential diagnosis centred on brain‑stem pathology, while otolaryngology ruled out mechanical airway obstruction. Sleep medicine performed the polysomnographic evaluation, interpreting the central apnea predominance, and guided respiratory support during the airport incident. Radiology’s re‑analysis of the head CT and subsequent MRI assessment confirmed the anatomic diagnosis. Psychology contributed to the patient’s coping strategies for chronic headache, anxiety, and social withdrawal. Such a collaborative model shortens the diagnostic trajectory, ensures that no component of the patient’s presentation is dismissed prematurely, and enables a comprehensive discussion of surgical risks versus benefits.

### Research Gaps
Despite the growing recognition of respiratory manifestations in CMI, several knowledge gaps remain. First, the true prevalence of central hypoventilation among adults with CMI is still uncertain; many studies rely on retrospective sleep studies or small cohorts, potentially under‑estimating the burden. Second, the relationship between posterior fossa volume and symptomatology is inadequate; though volumetric studies identify significant demographic variability, correlations with specific symptom clusters (e.g., choking, vertigo, headache) require larger, longitudinal investigations. Third, standardized scoring for dysphagia in CMI is lacking. While the modified barium swallow is useful, a validated, disease‑specific grading system would aid in comparing surgical outcomes and guiding therapy. Finally, the long‑term efficacy of posterior fossa decompression for respiratory symptoms, and the optimal timing of surgery, remains unclear. Prospective registries collecting respiratory, neuro‑imaging, and quality‑of‑life data are essential to address these questions.

### Clinical Pearls for Medical Trainees
1. **Look for “silent hypoxia”**: Episodes of marked oxygen desaturation without dyspnea, especially during sleep or after brief distraction, should raise suspicion for central hypoventilation.
2. **Anatomical nuance matters**: A crowded foramen magnum on CT or MRI can herald CMI, even with only mild tonsillar descent; pay attention to CSF spaces at the skull base.
3. **Endoscopy does not rule out neurologic etiologies**: Normal laryngoscopy and upper GI endoscopy do not exclude central causes of choking or dysphagia.
4. **Polysomnography is a triage tool**: Even a type III study can uncover central events that warrant full‑night PSG and surgical evaluation.
5. **Surgical decompression can reverse central apnea**: Early referral may prevent progressive hypoventilation and improve long‑term outcomes.

### Future Directions
The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into neuro‑imaging review holds promise for flagging subtle posterior fossa crowding or tonsillar descent that might otherwise evade detection. AI‑algorithms could be trained on large datasets to quantify posterior fossa volume and predict symptom risk. Real‑time respiratory monitoring technologies—such as wearable capnographs or advanced pulse oximetry coupled with accelerometry—could enable earlier detection of central hypoventilation patterns, particularly in high‑risk patients. Furthermore, centralized surgical outcome registries would facilitate comparative effectiveness research, correlating pre‑operative imaging findings with postoperative respiratory and symptomatic improvements. Such registries could also capture patient‑reported outcome measures, enriching our understanding of the psychosocial burden of CMI.

In conclusion, the case underscores that central hypoventilation should enter the differential for unexplained choking and hypoxemia, especially when conventional investigations are unrevealing. A vigilant review of historic imaging, a low threshold for polysomnographic evaluation, and a coordinated interdisciplinary approach will reduce diagnostic delays and improve patient outcomes. Continued research is needed to refine screening criteria, quantify the prevalence of respiratory impairment in CMI, and develop evidence‑based timing for surgical intervention.
