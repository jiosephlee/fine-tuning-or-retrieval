Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Early Presentation and Diagnostic Evaluation

A 28‑year‑old man was brought to the endocrinology clinic during a routine health check for an incidental finding of profound hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. The patient’s BMI was 24.6 kg m⁻², blood pressure 112/70 mmHg, and heart rate 78 bpm – values within the normal range for someone of his age. He reported classic diabetes symptoms: increased thirst, excessive hunger and recent weight loss, though he denied any overt urinary or respiratory changes. Examination revealed no skin changes such as acanthosis nigricans, and his physical appearance was otherwise unremarkable.

Laboratory testing confirmed severe glycaemic derangement. His glycated haemoglobin was 10.2 % (88 mmol mol⁻¹), reflecting long‑term hyperglycaemia. Fasting plasma glucose measured 12.4 mmol l⁻¹, and a 75‑g oral glucose tolerance test yielded a 2‑hour glucose of 19.8 mmol l⁻¹—well above the threshold of 11.1 mmol l⁻¹ for diagnosing diabetes. These values indicated marked post‑prandial glucose excursions even as basal glucose was markedly elevated.

The lipid profile suggested a nascent metabolic syndrome: HDL cholesterol was 1.01 mmol l⁻¹ and triglycerides 4.31 mmol l⁻¹, both consistent with dyslipidaemia despite the absence of obesity or overt insulin resistance. This biochemical pattern is common in monogenic diabetes, where β‑cell dysfunction drives fasting and post‑prandial hyperglycaemia.

To assess residual β‑cell capacity, a glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide test was performed. Baseline C‑peptide was 0.39 nmol l⁻¹, rising to 0.83 nmol l⁻¹ after 6 minutes of glucagon. Values below 0.60 nmol l⁻¹ are generally interpreted as impaired insulin secretion; thus the modest rise corroborated β‑cell dysfunction rather than a complete failure. This result tempered the suspicion for type 1 diabetes, which typically shows markedly low endogenous insulin.

Autoimmune serology was unremarkable: glutamic acid decarboxylase (GADA) and insulinoma antigen‑2 (IA‑2A) antibodies were negative. Lack of islet autoantibodies further argues against type 1 diabetes. Renal screening was reassuring; microalbuminuria was absent, ruling out early nephropathy. However, a sudomotor assessment revealed severely reduced electrochemical skin conductance in the hands, indicating early autonomic neuropathy—a complication that can also develop in certain monogenic forms of diabetes such as HNF1A‑MODY.

The family history was striking: maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother who developed diabetes at age 20. This autosomal dominant pattern of early‑onset diabetes across multiple generations is a hallmark clinical cue for monogenic diabetes.

Finally, acute metabolic decompensation was excluded. The patient had no signs of ketoacidosis: his venous pH was within normal limits, and serum ketone bodies were undetectable. Hyperglycaemia, therefore, was attributable to β‑cell dysfunction rather than sudden metabolic crisis.

Taken together, the young age at presentation, relative lean physique, classic diabetes symptoms, marked fasting and post‑prandial hyperglycaemia, modest residual C‑peptide, absence of autoantibodies, and a strong family pedigree form a clinical constellation that highly favors a monogenic etiology—specifically a MODY phenotype over the more common type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus. This constellation guided the decision to pursue targeted genetic testing, which ultimately identified the novel HNF1A mutation responsible for the patient’s disease.

## Differential Diagnosis of Early‑Onset Diabetes

Early‑onset diabetes in a young adult who presents with hyperglycaemia and glycosuria can stem from several distinct pathophysiological mechanisms. Distinguishing these requires a systematic evaluation of clinical history, laboratory indices, and family pedigree. In the case presented—a 24‑year‑old man who was found to have impaired fasting and post‑prandial glucose with a markedly raised HbA1c and a negative autoimmune screen—several diagnoses were entertained before the definitive identification of HNF1A‑MODY.

**Autoimmune type 1 diabetes** is the most common cause of diabetes in adolescence and early adulthood. Its hallmark is a rapid loss of β‑cell mass due to T‑cell mediated autoimmunity, reflected by the presence of islet autoantibodies such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibody (GADA), insulinoma antigen‑2 antibody (IA‑2A), and zinc transporter‑8 antibody (ZnT8). Clinically, patients often present with polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, and a sudden rise in glucose levels. Laboratory findings typically include a low C‑peptide level soon after onset, and an elevated HbA1c when the disease is undiagnosed. In this patient, all three conventional autoantibodies tested negative, and the glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide remained low but not completely absent (0.83 nmol/L), arguing against the rapid β‑cell destruction that characterises type 1 diabetes. Moreover, the family history reveals a pattern of trans‑generational diabetes confined to the maternal line, a pattern more consistent with a hereditary monogenic form than the sporadic or polygenic autoimmune process of type 1.

**Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)** in young adults is increasingly common but typically co‑exists with insulin resistance, obesity, or metabolic syndrome. Physical examination usually finds increased visceral adiposity, acanthosis nigricans, or other signs of hyperinsulinaemia. C‑peptide values are normally preserved or elevated at presentation, reflecting excess insulin secretion. Our patient’s body mass index was 24.6 kg/m², below the threshold commonly used to define obesity, and there was no evidence of acanthosis nigricans. Despite an elevated fasting glucose (12.4 mmol/L) and marked post‑prandial excursion (19.8 mmol/L), his insulin secretion was not hyper‑secreted but partially impaired, inconsistent with the insulin‑resistant phenotype of T2DM. Accordingly, the diagnosis of type 2 diabetes was considered provisional only until other possibilities were excluded.

**Maturity‑onset diabetes of the young (MODY)** represents a spectrum of autosomal‑dominant, non‑autoimmune disorders caused by heterozygous mutations in genes that regulate pancreatic β‑cell development or function. HNF1A‑MODY, the most frequent subtype, usually presents before age 25 and features a strong family history of diabetes in at least two successive generations, typically from one parent’s side. Dysglycaemia in MODY is frequently characterised by isolated post‑prandial hyperglycaemia; patients often have a normal or near‑normal fasting glucose and a pronounced rise after mixed meals or an oral glucose tolerance test. The patient in this case displayed a strikingly high 2‑hour glucose value relative to fasting glucose, with a pattern strongly reminiscent of isolated post‑prandial hyperglycaemia, and a family tree that mirrors the classic autosomal‑dominant inheritance of MODY. Furthermore, the glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide—although reduced—was not low enough to be compatible with complete β‑cell failure, fitting the gradual decline of insulin secretion typical of HNF1A‑MODY. These clinical clues prompted genetic investigation, which confirmed a pathogenic heterozygous substitution in HNF1A.

**Neonatal and other rare monogenic diabetes forms** are typically diagnosed in infants (neonatal diabetes) or young children, often with an associated endocrine or systemic anomaly, and can be managed with sulfonylureas or insulin depending on the underlying gene. The adult age of onset, the absence of congenital anomalies, and a family history solely involving diabetic disease make neonatal or other rare monogenic forms implausible in this case.

**Secondary causes of diabetes** such as pancreatic disease, pancreatic endocrine tumours, exocrine insufficiency, or drug‑induced diabetes (e.g., from glucocorticoids or antiretroviral therapy) would be considered if imaging showed pancreatic atrophy or tumour, or if a medication history existed that predisposed to glucose intolerance. The patient had no pancreatic pathology on surveillance, no history of medication that could induce hyperglycaemia, and no signs of exocrine dysfunction, lowering the likelihood of a secondary cause.

The **Exeter MODY probability calculator** integrates age at diagnosis, BMI, family history, β‑cell reserve (C‑peptide), and serology to estimate the likelihood of MODY. Applying the calculator to this patient yielded a probability of 62 % (1 in 1.6). Although the tool was derived predominantly from a White European cohort and may over‑estimate MODY risk in Asian populations, the high probability paired with the clinical picture still supports a strong suspicion of MODY.

An **oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)** that reveals isolated post‑prandial hyperglycaemia—evidenced by a disproportionately high 2‑hour glucose relative to fasting glucose—is a classic diagnostic clue for HNF1A‑MODY. In this case, the 2‑hour value of 19.8 mmol/L exceeded the fasting level by a wide margin, reinforcing the MODY suspicion.

**Thresholds prompting genetic testing** typically involve the combination of early age of onset (<35 years), a strong family history of diabetes restricted to a single parental lineage, negative autoimmune serology, preserved or sub‑normal C‑peptide, and a BMI below the obesity threshold. When these criteria are met, a monogenic diabetes panel—especially covering the most common genes such as HNF1A, GCK, HNF1B, and HNF4A—should be ordered. In this patient, all four thresholds were present: age 24, maternal lineage family history, negative autoantibodies, moderate C‑peptide, and normal BMI; thus, genetic testing was warranted and ultimately confirmed the diagnosis.

In conclusion, early‑onset diabetes presents a diagnostic challenge because of overlapping clinical features across multiple etiologies. A structured approach that synthesises clinical presentation, laboratory markers, and family history allows a clinician to narrow the differential. In this case, the constellation of a non‑obese adolescent male with a strong maternal family history, isolated post‑prandial hyperglycaemia, and low‑to‑moderate insulin secretion, together with negative autoantibodies, made HNF1A‑MODY the most plausible diagnosis. Genetic confirmation not only clarified the etiology but also directed a more appropriate therapeutic strategy—transitioning from insulin to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist and subsequently simplifying treatment—highlighting the clinical impact of precision medicine in monogenic diabetes.

## Genetic Basis and Pathophysiology of HNF1A‑MODY  

Genetic testing in the case report confirmed a heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), in exon 4 of the **HNF1A** gene. Understanding how this gene and its protein product orchestrate glucose homeostasis, and how its disruption leads to the constellation of findings observed in the patient, is essential for interpreting the clinical presentation and tailoring therapy.

### Chromosomal location, exon structure, and protein domains of HNF1A  

The **HNF1A** locus resides on chromosome 12q24 and spans 23 790 bp, comprising 10 exons that are transcribed into a 631‑residue protein. Three functional domains are delineated: an N‑terminal dimerization domain, a central DNA‑binding domain (DBD) encoded mainly by exons 3–6, and a C‑terminal transactivation domain (TAD). The pathogenic variant in this patient (p.N270S) lies within the DBD, a hotspot for pathogenic missense changes that impede DNA interaction and target‑gene transcription. The amplicon (exon 4) encodes a tandem wing‑like motif essential for dimerization with its paralog HNF1B, underscoring the vulnerability of this segment to functional deficits.

### Regulatory role in pancreatic β‑cells (insulin, GLUT2, pyruvate kinase)  

In β‑cells, HNF1A acts as a master transcription factor, binding promoter regions of **INS**, **SLC2A2** (GLUT2), and **PKLR** (pyruvate kinase). Loss‑of–function mutations dampen transcription of these genes, thereby reducing intracellular insulin synthesis, glucose handling, and glycolytic flux. The patient’s glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide peak (0.83 nmol/L) reflects preserved but subnormal insulin secretory capacity—a hallmark of early HNF1A‑MODY. The impaired GLUT2 expression explains the large post‑prandial spike (19.8 mmol/L at 2 h OGTT) and undermines hepatic glucose sensing, commencing the progressive decline of β‑cell responsiveness noted in later stages.

### Liver and renal expression and its influence on glucose handling  

HNF1A is also expressed in hepatocytes and proximal tubular epithelial cells. In the liver, the transcription factor drives lipogenic and gluconeogenic enzymes, contributing to the patient’s dyslipidemia (triglycerides 4.31 mmol/L) and modest HDL reduction. In the kidneys, HNF1A regulates **SLC5A2** encoding the sodium‑glucose co‑transporter‑2 (SGLT2). The hypomorphic allele reported in this case reduces SGLT2 transcription, lowering the renal threshold for glucose. Consequently, the patient exhibited glycosuria without overt diabetic ketoacidosis—a feature that also correlates with the reported risk of dehydration and ketoacidosis when SGLT2 inhibitors are used in HNF1A‑MODY.

### Reduction of SGLT2 expression leading to glycosuria  

Reduced SGLT2 activity, as seen in HNF1A‑MODY, predisposes to glucose excretion during periods of hyperglycemia. The patient’s elevated HbA1c (10.2%) and fasting glucose (12.4 mmol/L) were accompanied by significant glycosuria, reflecting the kidneys’ inability to reclaim glucose that would normally be reabsorbed. This mechanism explains the striking post‑prandial glucose spikes and necessitates careful consideration of osmotic diuresis risks in therapy planning.

### Progressive decline in β‑cell function characteristic of MODY  

The trajectory of β‑cell impairment in HNF1A‑MODY is gradual. Initial phases present with post‑prandial hyperglycemia and normal fasting values, whereas later stages show diminished fasting insulin and C‑peptide levels, as illustrated by the patient’s ameliorated HbA1c after switching to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist. Loss of functional HNF1A diminishes the transcriptional amplification loop that sustains β‑cell mass and function, culminating in eventual insulin therapy in many patients, though our case demonstrates that GLP‑1 agonism can preserve endogenous insulin secretion long enough to obviate early insulin use.

### Comparison of HNF1A‑MODY phenotype with GCK‑MODY  

The clinical footprints of HNF1A‑MODY differ markedly from those of **GCK** (glucokinase) MODY. GCK mutations yield a near‑constant, mild fasting hyperglycemia with stable HbA1c (typically <7.5 %) and usually no progressive β‑cell impairment; therapy is often unnecessary. In contrast, HNF1A‑MODY presents with significant post‑prandial excursions, varying fasting glucose, and an age‑related decline in insulin secretion. Accordingly, HNF1A carriers benefit from sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 agonists, whereas GCK patients can be reassured of benign disease course. The patient’s pronounced post‑prandial spike, glycosuria, family history of early‑onset diabetes, and sustained C‑peptide deficits align with the HNF1A pattern rather than the inherited, plateaued hyperglycemia of GCK‑MODY.

--- 

The case thus illustrates how a single, pathogenic missense variant in the DNA‑binding domain of HNF1A disrupts transcriptional regulation of key glucose‑handling genes across pancreas, liver, and kidney, producing the high HbA1c, glycosuria, and progressive β‑cell dysfunction that characterized this young man’s diabetes. The mechanistic understanding directly informed the shift from insulin to GLP‑1 agonist therapy, underscoring the clinical utility of targeted genetic diagnosis.

## Genetic Testing Strategy and Variant Interpretation

The laboratory evaluation of suspected MODY follows a structured sequence: an initial biochemical screen (fasting glucose, HbA1c, autoantibodies, C‑peptide) is paired with a clinical assessment that identifies individuals most likely to harbor a monogenic etiology. Once the pre‑test probability is established, a genomic assay is selected—most commonly a next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panel that interrogates all known MODY‑associated loci or a targeted analysis focused on a single gene when the phenotype is highly specific. In this case, after the clinician noted the classic MODY features of early‑onset diabetes, a strong autosomal‑dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies and a preserved but modest C‑peptide response, the patient was referred for an NGS panel covering all 15 genes implicated in MODY. The panel delivered the sequence of HNF1A exon 4, revealing a heterozygous c.809A>G change that converted the codon for asparagine (N) to serine (S) at residue 270 (p.N270S).

Clinical criteria for selecting a patient for genetic work‑up are codified in several consensus guidelines and include: age of onset before 25 years, a first‑degree relative with diabetes diagnosed before 30 years, absence of obesity‑driven insulin resistance, lack of typical type‑1 autoantibodies and evidence of preserved endogenous insulin secretion (C‑peptide >0.6 ng/mL). When these elements converge, clinicians are urged to proceed with genetic testing, as accurate classification often informs therapy (e.g., sulfonylureas in HNF1A-MODY versus insulin in type‑1 diabetes) and provides valuable information for family counseling.

Interpretation of the HNF1A variant follows the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, which use a weighted combination of pathogenicity codes. For the novel p.N270S change, the following evidence codes were applied:

- **PM1** (Moderate): The variant lies in the DNA‑binding domain of HNF1A, a critical functional region free of benign variants, placing it within a recognized mutational hot spot.
- **PM2** (Moderate): The allele is absent from large population databases (gnomAD, ExAC), confirming its rarity in the general population.
- **PP3** (Supporting): Multiple in‑silico predictors, including AlphaMissense (score = 0.995), SIFT, PolyPhen‑2, and MutationTaster, unanimously indicate a deleterious effect on protein function.
- **PP1** (Supporting): Segregation analysis demonstrates cosegregation of the variant with diabetes in the patient’s mother, maternal grandmother, younger brother, and another first‑degree relative, meeting the threshold for evidence of co‑segregation in a small family pedigree.
- **PP2** (Supporting): Missense changes are a known disease‑causing mechanism for HNF1A, and the gene harbors numerous pathogenic missense variants.

Although a PVS1 “very strong” criterion cannot be invoked for a missense change, the accumulated evidence (two moderate, three supporting) meets or exceeds the ACMG threshold for a *pathogenic* classification. The absence of a benign phenotype in carriers in the unaffected pedigree members further strengthens the case.

The nucleotide alteration, c.809A>G, induces an amino‑acid change of Asparagine to Serine at codon 270 (p.N270S). This residue is situated immediately adjacent to known pathogenic positions (e.g., p.N267S), suggesting a disruption of DNA‑binding affinity or co‑activator recruitment. Functional prediction algorithms unanimously classify the variant as deleterious, with the AlphaMissense score of 0.995 indicating a near‑certain pathogenic effect.

Segregation analysis further corroborated disease linkage. When evaluated against the family pedigree, all affected individuals carried the variant while unaffected relatives lacked it, confirming cosegregation. This phenotype‑genotype concordance provides a high‑confidence link between the variant and the clinical presentation.

Importantly, the variant has never been cataloged in primary variant repositories (ClinVar, VarSome, HGMD), underscoring its novelty. The emergent nature of the mutation necessitates cautious interpretation; however, the convergence of domain‑specific location, functional predictions, population absence, and segregation argues decisively for pathogenicity, justifying its inclusion as a novel HNF1A-MODY variant.

From a counseling perspective, the identification of a pathogenic HNF1A mutation carries several actionable implications. The autosomal‑dominant inheritance pattern means that each first‑degree relative has a 50 % chance of harboring the mutation, warranting cascade testing. Knowledge of the mutation empowers therapeutic decisions: sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 receptor agonists are preferred over insulin in HNF1A-MODY, potentially avoiding overtreatment. Long‑term surveillance for complications (retinopathy, nephropathy) can be appropriately timed based on the genetic confirmation. Additionally, because the variant directly implicates HNF1A‑related pathways, clinicians should be vigilant for renal tubular abnormalities or early onset hypertension that occasionally arise in MODY patients. 

In sum, the genetic testing strategy illuminated a pathophysiological insight that reshaped both individual care and familial risk assessment. By integrating robust laboratory workflows, rigorous ACMG criteria, and meticulous pedigree analysis, clinicians can transform a vague diabetic phenotype into a precise monogenic diagnosis with direct therapeutic relevance.

## Precision Treatment Pathway and Clinical Outcome

At presentation, the patient’s glycemic profile (HbA1c 10.2 %) and the absence of ketoacidosis suggested a non‑type‑1 phenotype that could be approached with moderate insulin resistance. Basal insulin degludec (eight units daily) was initiated to achieve immediate glucose lowering, while metformin (1 500 mg daily) was added to address hepatic insulin resistance and to capitalize on its weight‑neutral properties. A dipeptidyl peptidase‑4 (DPP‑4) inhibitor, saxagliptin (5 mg daily), was also prescribed to augment post‑prandial insulin secretion via GLP‑1 potentiation, thereby providing a glucagon‑modulating effect with a low hypoglycemia risk in isolation. This combination represented a pragmatic, T2D‑oriented algorithm that could rapidly lower blood glucose while the diagnostic work‑up progressed.

One month after therapy initiation, repeat testing revealed a drop in HbA1c to 9.1 %, indicating effective acute glucose control. The residual insulin requirement remained modest: the patient continued to need only basal insulin, and further adjustments to dosage were not required. At this juncture, a heterozygous pathogenic mutation (p.N270S) in HNF1A was confirmed, reclassifying the disorder as HNF1A‑MODY. The preserved stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L) demonstrated that β‑cell reserve still existed, making it feasible to transition away from insulin while maintaining glycemic targets.

Following genetic confirmation, basal insulin was discontinued and replaced with the once‑weekly GLP‑1 receptor agonist dulaglutide (1.5 mg). GLP‑1 agonists provide glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, glucagon suppression, delayed gastric emptying, and modest weight loss—all advantages in HNF1A‑MODY where β‑cell dysfunction progresses gradually. Importantly, the risk of hypoglycemia with dulaglutide is considerably lower than with sulfonylureas, which historically have been used in HNF1A‑MODY to stimulate KATP channel closure but can precipitate hypoglycemia and contribute to β‑cell exhaustion. Additionally, the patient’s HNF1A mutation likely reduces renal SGLT‑2 expression; using SGLT‑2 inhibitors in such a setting carries heightened risks of dehydration and ketoacidosis, and thus this class was avoided.

The patient’s HbA1c trajectory reflected the progressive refinement of therapy: baseline 10.2 %, 9.1 % after one month of insulin, metformin and saxagliptin, and 6.0 % six months after initiation of dulaglutide combined with metformin and saxagliptin. Once glycemic control stabilized, the medication regimen was further simplified to metformin 1 500 mg daily and saxagliptin 5 mg daily, a strategy that preserved control while minimizing pill burden. Throughout follow‑up, HbA1c remained consistently below 6.5 %, with no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy. Annual screening for microvascular complications—renal function, urinary albumin excretion, retinal examinations, and autonomic nerve function testing—was instituted per guideline recommendations for monogenic diabetes.

Concurrent with pharmacologic adjustments, a comprehensive education program was implemented. The patient received individualized counseling on carbohydrate‑unit counting, regular physical activity, and balanced nutrition to support glycemic control and weight management. Hydration status was emphasized, particularly in the context of GLP‑1 therapy, to mitigate the possibility of volume depletion. The patient was also advised to avoid over‑use of over‑the‑counter sympathomimetics that could blunt glucose‑dependent insulin secretion. With these lifestyle modifications in place, the patient attained sustained metabolic control and avoided the adverse events commonly associated with insulin, sulfonylurea, or SGLT‑2 therapy, exemplifying the value of precision medicine guided by genetic diagnosis.

## Clinical Lessons, Implications, and Future Directions

The young man in this report underscores a growing reality in diabetes care: early‑onset hyperglycaemia does not equate automatically to type 1 or type 2 disease. His glycated haemoglobin of 10.2 %, coupled with glycosuria, nocturnal polyuria, and a relatively preserved fasting C‑peptide, initially suggested a non‑autoimmune phenotype. Yet the absence of insulin‑autoantibodies, the significant post‑meal glucose rise, and a striking family history prompted consideration of a monogenic etiology. The discovery of a novel heterozygous HNF1A mutation (p.N270S) not only clarified the diagnosis but also redirected therapy from basal insulin to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist, producing rapid and durable glycaemic control. This narrative demonstrates the indispensable value of genetic testing, the benefits of family‑based approaches, and the nuanced interplay of clinical judgment and genomic data.

### Essential Role of Genetic Diagnosis in Distinguishing MODY from Type 1 and Type 2 Diabetes

In the clinic, differentiating MODY from classic type 1 or type 2 diabetes relies on a constellation of clues: early age of onset, lack of autoimmune markers, modest obesity, and an intermediate fasting C‑peptide that sits between normoglycaemic and insulin‑deficient ranges. In this case, the negative GADA and IA‑2A panels and a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L would still allow uncertainty, as early type 1 diabetes may present with similar values. A molecular diagnosis provided unequivocal evidence that the defect lay in β‑cell transcriptional regulation rather than autoimmunity or insulin resistance. By confirming HNF1A‑MODY, the clinician was able to tailor therapy to the underlying pathophysiology, avoiding unnecessary insulin initiation and providing a drug class (GLP‑1‑RAs) that augments insulin secretion without precipitating hypoglycaemia.

### Benefits of Cascade Family Testing and Genetic Counseling

The family history of the index patient—grandmother, mother, and younger brother, all diagnosed before age 40—highlights the practicality of cascade testing. Genetic counselling guided the identification of at‑risk relatives, permitting early glucose screening and lifestyle interventions before the manifestation of overt hyperglycaemia. For families in which a pathogenic HNF1A variant is known, first‑degree relatives can undergo targeted testing at a relatively low cost, potentially averting severe complications through earlier pharmacologic modification. Moreover, this strategy alleviates the emotional burden of uncertainty for family members and informs reproductive planning by clarifying transmission risk.

### Limitations of MODY Probability Calculators in Non‑European Populations

The Exeter MODY probability calculator, a tool that integrates age at diagnosis, BMI, HbA1c, and antibody status, estimates a 62.4 % likelihood of MODY for our index patient. However, this model was derived from cohorts of predominantly White European in origin and may over‑estimate MODY prevalence in Asian populations, as cultural, genetic, and phenotypic variables differ markedly. Consequently, clinicians should treat calculator outputs as adjunctive rather than definitive and should not rely on them to exclude MODY in ethnically diverse settings. In practice, a high clinical suspicion, combined with a negative autoantibody profile and a family pedigree, justifies upfront genetic panel testing, especially when early‑onset diabetes presents with atypical features.

### Prevalence of MODY in Asian Cohorts and Reasons for Under‑diagnosis

Recent studies in China, Taiwan, and Japan have documented that roughly 1–2 % of patients presenting before age 35 with a diabetes phenotype otherwise consistent with type 2 disease carry actionable MODY mutations. Despite this, routine screening remains uncommon; clinicians frequently default to a type 2 management plan, as in the case’s initial insulin‑metformin‑saxagliptin regimen. Under‑diagnosis stems from overlapping metabolic syndrome presentations, misattribution of glycosuria to impaired renal threshold, and a lack of awareness regarding monogenic diabetes among general practitioners. Increasing use of next‑generation sequencing panels in young adults with a suggestive family history could unmask a substantial proportion of missed MODY cases, with significant implications for treatment sparing and complication prevention.

### Cost‑Benefit Considerations for Early NGS Screening Programs

Although genetic testing incurs upfront costs, early identification of MODY has demonstrated cost‑effectiveness through multiple pathways. First, targeted therapy reduces drug expenditures—our patient moved from daily insulin injections to once‑weekly GLP‑1 receptor agonist dosing, truncating the need for complex insulin regimens and associated supplies. Second, improved glycaemic control mitigates microvascular and macrovascular sequelae, lowering downstream inpatient and outpatient costs. Finally, cascade testing curtails the financial burden of untimely disease onset among relatives. Formal health‑economic analyses have shown that a one‑time NGS panel for early‑onset diabetes can generate savings exceeding the initial testing expense within three to five years, particularly when applied to high‑prevalence Asian cohorts.

### Potential Development of Standardized Screening Guidelines for MODY

The absence of universal screening protocols remains a barrier to widespread MODY recognition. Guidance from diabetes societies has begun to include recommendations—such as considering genetic testing in patients under 35 who display non‑autoimmune hyperglycaemia, a modest BMI, and a positive family history—but implementation lags. Developing region‑specific guidelines that incorporate ethnicity‑appropriate prevalence data, clinical calculators adapted for local populations, and cost‑effective testing pathways would streamline patient selection. Collaboration between endocrinology societies, geneticists, and health‑policy makers can facilitate consensus documents that provide clear criteria for initiating NGS panels.

### Emerging Pharmacologic Options and Personalized Therapy Approaches

Beyond sulfonylureas and GLP‑1 receptor agonists, recent advances are broadening therapeutic options for HNF1A‑MODY. In vitro assays have identified small‑molecule modulators that enhance HNF1A transcriptional activity, offering potential future treatment strategies. Meanwhile, DPP‑4 inhibitors, as employed in this case, confer modest glycaemic improvement and can be combined with GLP‑1‑RAs to minimize hypoglycaemic risk. Caution remains warranted regarding SGLT2 inhibitors; reduced renal reabsorption associated with HNF1A mutations could predispose patients to volume depletion and ketoacidosis. Personalized therapy thus demands a nuanced assessment of residual β‑cell function, insulin‑secretion capacity, and renal handling of glucose.

### Practical Recommendations for Clinicians Encountering Atypical Young‑Adult Diabetics

When faced with a young adult presenting with:

1. Hyperglycaemia or glycosuria > 110 mg/dL,
2. A family history of early‑onset diabetes,
3. Negative autoantibody panel,
4. Fasting C‑peptide > 0.3 nmol/L,

clinicians should not default to type 2 treatment but instead obtain a genetic test panel covering the major MODY genes. During the waiting period for results, basic metabolic panels and organ‑function testing should be performed as usual, with insulin therapy reserved for extreme hyperglycaemia or symptomatic episodes. Upon a confirmed HNF1A mutation, low‑dose sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 RA therapy should replace insulin, with careful monitoring of HbA1c and renal function.

### Enhancement of Global Variant Databases Through Collaborative Efforts

The novel p.N270S variant identified in this patient was absent from ClinVar and VarSome, illustrating the ongoing need for comprehensive variant annotation. Multicentre registries, particularly those pooling data from Asian populations, are essential for capturing the full spectrum of HNF1A alterations. Mandatory deposition of clinically relevant variants with functional evidence into global databases will improve the predictive accuracy of computational tools such as AlphaMissense and streamline future diagnostic interpretations. Training laboratories and clinicians in standardized reporting practices will accelerate the translation of novel findings into clinical care.

### Directions for Functional Validation Studies of Newly Discovered Mutations

To ascertain the pathogenicity of unexplained variants, functional validation remains indispensable. In vitro reporter assays that gauge HNF1A’s binding to the GLUT2 promoter, assessment of transcriptional activation using luciferase constructs, and CRISPR‑mediated knock‑in models in human β‑cell lines can illuminate the impact of amino‑acid substitutions. Complementary studies in induced pluripotent stem‑cell–derived β‑like cells allow for evaluation under physiological insulin‑secretion conditions. Sequencing the patient’s pancreatic tissue (where feasible) can verify co‑expression patterns and histologic changes. Integrating these functional data with bioinformatic predictions and family segregation strengthens the variant’s classification, thereby refining therapeutic choices.

In sum, this case of novel HNF1A‑MODY demonstrates the transformative power of genetic diagnosis, the far‑reaching benefits of cascade testing, and the necessity for culturally adapted screening tools and cost‑effective genomic sequencing strategies. As precision medicine continues to mature, clinicians must balance phenotypic insight with molecular evidence, leveraging both to deliver individualized care that optimises long‑term outcomes in young patients with early‑onset diabetes.
