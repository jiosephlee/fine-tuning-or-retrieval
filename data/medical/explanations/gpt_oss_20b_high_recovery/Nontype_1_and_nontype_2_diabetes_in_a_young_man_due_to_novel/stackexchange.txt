Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Clinical Pearls] In patients younger than 25 with newly diagnosed hyperglycemia, which clinical features and laboratory findings should raise the suspicion of MODY over type 1 or type 2 diabetes?

**Key clinical clues that should prompt consideration of MODY in a patient < 25 years old with newly detected hyperglycemia**

| Feature | Why it points to MODY | How it presented in the case |
|----------|---------------------|------------------------------|
| **Age at onset** | MODY is a monogenic form that typically manifests before 25 yrs. | The patient was a 24‑year‑old male. |
| **Strong, multi‑generational family history** | Autosomal‑dominant inheritance is characteristic. | Maternal grandmother, mother, and a younger brother had early‑onset diabetes (30s‑20s). |
| **Negative islet‐autoantibodies** | Type 1 diabetes is usually autoimmune; MODY is not. | Both GADA and IA‑2A were negative. |
| **Preserved but impaired β‑cell function** | Type 2 diabetes shows gradual insulin resistance with preserved early secretion; MODY is marked by a progressive β‑cell decline that may still be detectable on stimulation. | Glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide rose from 0.39 nmol/L to 0.83 nmol/L—above the 0.60 nmol/L threshold but clearly sub‑normal. |
| **Glycosuria without ketoacidosis** | HNF1A mutation causes reduced renal SGLT2 activity, leading to glycosuria; ketosis is uncommon. | The patient had glycosuria but no signs of diabetic ketoacidosis. |
| **Normal BMI, absence of insulin‑resistance stigmata** | Type 2 diabetes often presents with obesity or acanthosis nigricans; MODY usually does not. | BMI 24.6 kg/m², no acanthosis nigricans, mild triglyceride elevation but normal HDL. |
| **Marked post‑prandial hyperglycemia (≥11.1 mmol/L) or high 2‑h OGTT** | MODY frequently shows a pronounced post‑meal glucose rise. | 2‑h OGTT was 19.8 mmol/L. |

**Clinical strategy**

1. **Take a focused family history**—look for diabetes in ≥3 generations before age 35.  
2. **Screen for autoantibodies** (GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8) and obtain fasting C‑peptide or a glucagon‑stimulation test.  
3. **Check for glycosuria** in the absence of ketoacidosis or hyperosmolar states.  
4. **Assess insulin‑resistance markers** (BMI, acanthosis nigricans, lipid profile).  
5. **Perform an OGTT** if fasting glucose is only mildly elevated; a 2‑h value >11.1 mmol/L raises suspicion.  
6. **Consider genetic testing** (NGS panel for MODY genes) when the above findings coexist, as in the case where a novel HNF1A variant was identified.

Early identification allows for phenotype‑specific therapy—e.g., sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 agonists, or metformin—as in this patient’s transition from insulin to dulaglutide/metformin with durable glycemic control.

### [Differential Diagnosis] For a patient with early‑onset hyperglycemia, negative pancreatic autoantibodies, preserved fasting C‑peptide, and a strong family history of diabetes, list the primary monogenic diabetes subtypes that should be considered and describe the key tests that distinguish them.

**Clinical teaching point –  Primary monogenic diabetes subtypes to consider in an early‑onset, antibody‑negative patient with preserved fasting C‑peptide and a strong family history of diabetes, and how to distinguish them**

Early‑onset hyperglycemia in a young adult with negative islet‑autoantibodies and a preserved fasting C‑peptide is the classic biochemical phenotype of maturity‑onset diabetes of the young (MODY).  In the case of the 26‑year‑old man described above, the family pedigree and the glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol L⁻¹ at 6 min) fulfilled the clinical screening criteria for MODY, and next‑generation sequencing identified a pathogenic HNF1A variant (p.N270S).  The same clinical picture should prompt evaluation for the most common MODY genes; the key subtypes and the laboratory or imaging tests that help differentiate them are:

| MODY subtype | Typical clinical or biochemical pattern | Key distinguishing test(s) |
|--------------|----------------------------------------|----------------------------|
| **HNF1A (MODY3)** | Early‑onset (≤25 y), mild fasting hyperglycemia early, pronounced post‑prandial excursions, progressive β‑cell decline; normal fasting C‑peptide initially; low‑dose sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA response. | **OGTT** – marked 2‑h post‑meal glucose (often >11.1 mmol L⁻¹) with otherwise normal fasting glucose; **glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide** (>0.6 nmol L⁻¹) early in disease; **NGS MODY panel** confirming pathogenic HNF1A variant. |
| **GCK (MODY2)** | Mild, stable fasting hyperglycemia (6.6–7.4 mmol L⁻¹); usually no need for treatment unless pregnant or ≈ >6.5 mmol L⁻¹. | **Fasting glucose** – modest elevation; **OGTT** often normal or mildly elevated; **C‑peptide** normal; genetic testing for GCK mutation. |
| **HNF4A (MODY1)** | Similar to HNF1A but frequently associated with dyslipidemia (↑LDL, ↑triglycerides) and hyperuricemia. | **Lipid profile** (high LDL/↑uric acid), **OGTT/fasting glucose** similar to HNF1A; **NGS** confirming HNF4A mutation. |
| **HNF1B (MODY5)** | Diabetic features plus extra‑pancreatic manifestations: renal cysts or renal dysplasia, hypomagnesemia, low urinary concentrating ability, genital tract malformations. | **Renal ultrasound** for cysts; **serum magnesium** low; **urine concentration test**; genetic confirmation of HNF1B mutation. |
| **PDX1 (HNF1α‑like) (MODY4)** | Early‑onset diabetes with pancreatic hypoplasia; may present with low insulin at birth, progressing to insulin deficiency. | **Imaging of pancreas** (CT/MRI) for hypoplasia; **C‑peptide** falls precipitously; **NGS** for PDX1 variant; consider exocrine insufficiency. |
| **KCNJ11/ABCC8 (neonatal diabetes, MODY12–13)** | Usually presents in infancy (<6 mo), often transient but can persist into adolescence; excellent response to high‑dose sulfonylureas. | **Age of onset** (<6 mo with persistent disease); **clinical improvement with sulfonylureas**; **NGS** confirming KCNJ11 or ABCC8 mutation. |
| **NEUROD1 (MODY6)** | Early‑onset, mild to moderate hyperglycemia; may have neurodevelopmental abnormalities. | **Neurocognitive screening**; **OGTT** and **C‑peptide** similar to other MODY types; confirm with NEUROD1 sequencing. |

**Diagnostic algorithm in practice**

1. **Confirm MODY‑suspecting phenotype**:  
   - Age < 25 y at diagnosis, negative islet‑autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8).  
   - Fasting C‑peptide ≥ 0.6 nmol L⁻¹ (or > 0.6 ng mL⁻¹ in a glucagon‑stimulated sample).  
   - Strong family history of early‑onset diabetes.  
   The patient in the case fulfilled all these criteria.

2. **Characterise glycaemia**:  
   - 75‑g OGTT: a 2‑h value ≥ 11.1 mmol L⁻¹ points toward HNF1A/HNF4A; mild/fixed elevations suggest GCK.  
   - Fasting glucose: ≈ 7.5 mmol L⁻¹ is common in HNF1A; > 6 mmol L⁻¹ in GCK.

3. **Assess β‑cell reserve**:  
   - Basal and glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide > 0.6 nmol L⁻¹ is typical for HNF1A and GCK; a gradual decline indicates progressive β‑cell loss in HNF1A.  
   - The patient’s 0.83 nmol L⁻¹ at 6 min is borderline but suggestive of modest reserve.

4. **Screen for extra‑pancreatic clues**:  
   - Renal ultrasound (HNF1B).  
   - Lipid panel / uric acid (HNF4A).  
   - Serum magnesium (HNF1B).  
   – In this case, no structural renal disease or metabolic syndrome was noted, making HNF1B and HNF4A less likely.

5. **Genetic panel**:  
   - Order a validated MODY panel (NGS covering HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, PDX1, KCNJ11, ABCC8, NEUROD1, etc.).  
   - In the described patient, sequencing of a 10‑gene MODY panel identified a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S).  
   - This result explained the clinical course and guided therapy: discontinuation of basal insulin and initiation of GLP‑1 RA, which restored HbA1c to 6.0% in 6 mo (Figure 1 of the case report).

**Take‑home message**

Early‑onset, antibody‑negative diabetes with a preserved fasting C‑peptide and a strong family history should trigger a MODY work‑up.  The principal subtypes are HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, PDX1, KCNJ11/ABCC8, and NEUROD1.  OGTT, fasting and stimulated C‑peptide, basic metabolic panels, and targeted imaging identify the metabolic pattern of each subtype, but definitive diagnosis rests on next‑generation sequencing.  Correct identification of the gene not only refines prognosis but also directs therapy—illustrated by the successful transition from basal insulin to GLP‑1 RA in the patient with HNF1A‑MODY.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how loss‑of‑function mutations in HNF1A impair β‑cell function, including downstream effects on insulin synthesis, GLUT2 expression, and pyruvate kinase activity.

**Loss‑of‑function HNF1A mutations → β‑cell dysfunction**

1. **HNF1A as a master transcriptional regulator**  
   - HNF1A (hepatocyte nuclear factor‑1α) is a heterodimeric transcription factor expressed in pancreatic β cells, liver and kidney.  
   - The DNA‑binding domain (where the novel p.N270S mutation lies) is essential for binding target promoters and activating transcription.

2. **Impaired transcription of β‑cell genes**  
   - **Insulin (INS)** – HNF1A directly stimulates the insulin promoter.  Loss of function reduces INS mRNA, diminishing de novo insulin synthesis and secretion.  
   - **Glucose transporter‑2 (GLUT2, SLC2A2)** – GLUT2 is the primary glucose entry gate for β cells.  HNF1A deficiency lowers GLUT2 expression, leading to less glucose uptake, reduced glycolytic flux, and a blunted rise in intracellular ATP.  
   - **Pyruvate kinase (PKLR/PKM)** – This enzyme catalyzes the final ATP‑generating step in glycolysis.  Reduced PK activity prolongs a low‑ATP state, preventing closure of ATP‑sensitive K⁺ channels (K_ATP) essential for triggering insulin exocytosis.

3. **Consequences for insulin secretion**  
   - **↓ Glucose sensing** (because of ↓ GLUT2) → ↓ ATP production.  
   - **↓ ATP → K_ATP channels stay open** → membrane hyperpolarization, preventing Ca²⁺ influx.  
   - **↓ Ca²⁺‑dependent vesicle fusion** → impaired insulin release.  
   - Even with residual β‑cell mass (as evidenced by the patient’s stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L), the combined deficits in transcription and metabolic signaling culminate in chronic hyperglycemia and glycosuria.

4. **Clinical correlation in the case**  
   - The patient’s high HbA1c (10.2 %) and 2‑hour OGTT glucose (19.8 mmol/L) reflect this secretory defect.  
   - Glycosuria arises partly from reduced renal SGLT2 transcription (also under HNF1A control).  
   - Transition to GLP‑1 receptor agonist therapy improved glycemic control by stimulating insulin release through a K_ATP‑independent, cAMP‑mediated pathway, bypassing the primary defect.

**Bottom line:**  Loss‑of‑function HNF1A mutations impair β‑cell function by down‑regulating insulin synthesis, decreasing GLUT2‑mediated glucose entry, and reducing pyruvate kinase‑driven ATP generation—each step progressing toward the characteristic post‑prandial hyperglycemia of HNF1A‑MODY.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain why patients with HNF1A‑MODY often have glycosuria even when fasting serum glucose is below the usual renal threshold.

**Why HNF1A‑MODY patients can have glycosuria at “normal” blood glucose levels**

HNF1A is a DNA‑binding transcription factor that regulates the expression of several renal genes, most notably **SLC5A2, the gene that encodes the sodium‑glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2)**.  
SGLT2 is responsible for ≥90 % of glucose reabsorption in the proximal tubule. When HNF1A function is impaired by heterozygous loss‑of‑function mutations, transcription of SGLT2 drops, and the proximal tubule’s capacity to re‑take up glucose is markedly reduced.  

Because the transport system is already saturated at a lower luminal glucose concentration, the **renal threshold for glucose shifts downward**. In practice this means that glucose starts to spill into the urine when plasma glucose is below the classic ~180 mg/dL (≈10 mmol/L) threshold seen in healthy individuals. Glycosuria can therefore be an early, easily detectible phenotype of HNF1A‑MODY—even before fasting hyperglycaemia becomes evident.

The case report highlights this point: the authors state that “glycosuria in HNF1A‑MODY is attributed to reduced expression of SGLT2, which is under the transcriptional control of HNF1A.” (Discussion section). This shift in renal handling of glucose is the key pathophysiologic reason patients with HNF1A mutations often exhibit glycosuria even when fasting serum glucose is below the usual renal threshold.

### [Management] A HNF1A‑MODY patient has a fasting C‑peptide of 0.4 nmol/L, HbA1c of 10 %, and is initially started on basal insulin. After genetic confirmation, what therapeutic switch is most appropriate and why?

**Therapeutic switch:** Discontinue basal insulin and initiate a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (e.g., dulaglutide 1.5 mg weekly; liraglutide 1.8 mg daily).  

**Why:**  
- HNF1A‑MODY causes progressive β‑cell insufficiency; the patient’s fasting C‑peptide (0.4 nmol/L) reflects residual secretion that can be amplified by a GLP‑1 agonist.  
- GLP‑1 agents improve fasting and post‑prandial glucose, promote weight loss, and have a low risk of hypoglycemia compared with insulin or sulfonylureas.  
- In the reported case, dulaglutide lowered HbA1c from 9.1 % to 6.0 % in 6 months, allowing insulin withdrawal and subsequent maintenance on metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor.  
- Current consensus for HNF1A‑MODY recommends sulfonylureas initially, but GLP‑1 agonists are preferred when insulin is used or when hypoglycemia risk or weight control is a concern.

### [Management] What are the risks associated with initiating SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY patients and how does HNF1A’s regulation of SGLT2 contribute to these risks?

**SGLT2 inhibition in HNF1A‑MODY: Why it is perilous**

HNF1A is a transcription factor that, among many targets, drives expression of the proximal‑tubule sodium‑glucose cotransporter‑2 (SGLT2).  In HNF1A‑MODY the mutation impairs HNF1A’s DNA‐binding domain, leading to a **reduction in renal SGLT2 transcription**.  Consequently, patients have a lower renal threshold for glucose and manifest baseline glycosuria, even under euglycemic conditions (as was evident in the case described).

When an SGLT2 inhibitor is added to this setting, the **remaining SGLT2 activity is further suppressed**, amplifying glucosuria.  The extra urinary loss of glucose—and its accompanying water and sodium—produces marked **volume depletion**.  In a patient whose β‑cell reserve is already limited (stimulated C‑peptide < 1 nmol/L in the report), the body’s compensatory mechanisms (ketogenesis, diuresis) are easily tipped toward an **euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)** picture.  The patient’s case report cites a study that reported severe dehydration and vascular collapse in HNF1A‑MODY patients exposed to high‑dose SGLT2 inhibition.

Other risks include:

| Risk | Mechanism | Clinical impact |
|------|-----------|-----------------|
| *Volume depletion / hypotension* | Excess diuresis due to increased glucosuria | Syncope, acute kidney injury, hypotensive episodes |
| *Euglycemic DKA* | Limited insulin secretion + increased ketogenesis | DKA with near‑normal glucose, requiring early recognition |
| *Genital / urinary tract infections* | Persistent glucosuria fosters microbial growth | Common to all SGLT2 users, exacerbated by higher urinary glucose |
| *Renal adverse events* | Dehydration → prerenal azotemia | Elevated creatinine, need to avoid concomitant nephrotoxic agents |

**Bottom line for the practitioner:** In HNF1A‑MODY, the *baseline* reduction in SGLT2 expression creates a “low‑water‑ring” that SGLT2 inhibitors ring‑tighten.  The added layer of pharmacologic blockade not only worsens glycosuria but provokes clinically significant dehydration and ketoacidosis, outweighing any glycaemic benefit.  The reported patient avoided SGLT2 therapy in favor of GLP‑1 RA and metformin, which achieved durable euglycemia without precipitating volume depletion.

### [Morning Report] Outline a stepwise diagnostic algorithm from presentation to mutation confirmation for a young adult with hyperglycemia and negative pancreatic autoantibodies, including key laboratory, functional, and genetic studies.

**Stepwise diagnostic algorithm for a young adult with hyperglycaemia and negative pancreatic autoantibodies**

1. **Clinical screening**  
   • Age ≤ 35 yr, early‑onset hyperglycaemia (often before 25 yr).  
   • Strong family history (≥ two generations with diabetes diagnosed < 35 yr).  
   • Absence of features of Type 1 diabetes (no insulin requirement within 1 yr of onset, no ketoacidosis).  
   • In the presented case: 24‑year‑old man, HbA1c 10.2 % (88 mmol/mol), 2‑h OGTT 19.8 mmol/L, no ketoacidosis, glycosuria, and a family pedigree with diabetes in maternal grandmother, mother, and brother.

2. **Baseline biochemical work‑up**  
   • Fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, serum lipids, liver & renal function, eGFR.  
   • Lipid profile to rule out insulin‑resistance phenotype (the patient had HDL 1.01 mmol/L, TG 4.31 mmol/L).  
   • Urine or plasma glucose to confirm glycosuria (suggestive of impaired SGLT2 function in HNF1A‑MODY).

3. **Assessment of endogenous insulin secretion**  
   • Fast‑ing C‑peptide (normal ≥ 0.6 ng/mL; 0.39 nmol/L at baseline in this patient).  
   • Glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide (cut‑off 0.6 nmol/L; 0.83 nmol/L at 6 min in the patient) to gauge residual β‑cell function.  
   • Preserved (> 0.6 nmol/L) but inadequate secretion is classic for HNF1A‑MODY and contrasts with the low or absent C‑peptide seen in early Type 1 diabetes.

4. **Autoantibody evaluation**  
   • Test for GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8, and C‑P450 synthase/IAA if available.  
   • All negative in the case; negative antibodies virtually exclude autoimmune Type 1 diabetes.

5. **Risk‑score or MODY probability calculator**  
   • Input age, BMI, family history, autoantibody status, and C‑peptide.  
   • In the case, the Exeter MODY calculator gave 62.4 % probability, supporting further work‑up.

6. **Functional and ophthalmologic screening** (optional but recommended)  
   • Sudomotor testing (electro‑chemical skin conductance) or other autonomic assays.  
   • Early screening for retinopathy and nephropathy, even if not yet clinically evident.

7. **Genetic testing**  
   • Targeted MODY panel (≥ 15 genes) performed by NGS.  
   • Confirm pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant per ACMG guidelines; in the case, a novel HNF1A c.809A>G (p.N270S) variant was identified.  

8. **Variant interpretation & segregation testing**  
   • Verify segregation with diabetes in affected family members.  
   • Use in silico predictions (e.g., AlphaMissense) and functional location (DNA‑binding domain).  
   • The variant met criteria for pathogenicity (Segregation, high deleterious score, new in trans).

9. **Therapeutic adjustment based on genotype**  
   • HNF1A‑MODY is exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas, but GLP‑1 RA (dulaglutide) or DPP‑4 inhibitors (saxagliptin) can be used safely.  
   • In the case, initial basal‑insulin + metformin + saxagliptin was tapered to dulaglutide, later simplified to metformin + saxagliptin, achieving HbA1c 6.0 %.  

10. **Long‑term follow‑up**  
   • Serial HbA1c and C‑peptide to monitor β‑cell decline.  
   • Periodic screening for microvascular complications.  
   • Genetic counseling for family members, given autosomal‑dominant inheritance.

**Key take‑home messages**  
- **Normative β‑cell function (preserved C‑peptide) + negative autoantibodies + strong family history** strongly point toward MODY and should trigger early genetic testing.  
- **Glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide is particularly valuable** in young patients with relatively high fasting glucose but not yet insulin‑dependent, as illustrated by the patient’s 0.83 nmol/L at 6 min.  
- **NGS MODY panels provide definitive mutation confirmation**; variant interpretation must follow ACMG guidelines for pathogenicity.  
- **Precision medicine follows**: a confirmed HNF1A mutation allows for insulin‑sparing, β‑cell‑stimulating therapy and family‑based screening.  

This algorithm—clinical suspicion → biochemical confirmation → functional β‑cell assessment → autoantibody exclusion → genetic testing—ensures timely and accurate diagnosis of MODY in young adults and guides optimal, individualized treatment.

### [Clinical Pearls] Discuss the importance of genetic counselling and segregation analysis in families with a newly identified HNF1A variant and how this informs testing strategy for asymptomatic relatives.

**Genetic counselling and segregation analysis are essential first steps after a novel HNF1A mutation is found, both to confirm the mutation’s pathogenicity and to shape a practical surveillance plan for relatives.**

### 1. Why segregation matters
* **ACMG‑style evidence**: The novel p.N270S variant (c. 809A>G) lies in the highly conserved DNA‑binding domain of HNF1A, a “hot‑spot” for pathogenic changes. The case report notes that the variant “segregates with diabetes in the family,” satisfying ACMG criterion PM6 (segregation in multiple affected family members). Demonstrating that every phenotype‑positive first‑degree relative carries the variant (and that unaffected relatives do not) strongly shifts the classification from “variant of uncertain significance” to “likely pathogenic.”
* **Functional inference**: DNA‑binding domain mutations typically reduce transcriptional activity of insulin‑gene regulation, which explains the patient’s β‑cell failure despite preserved C‑peptide early in disease.

### 2. Genetic counselling: what to discuss
| Topic | Why it matters for an HNF1A‑MODY family |
|-------|----------------------------------------|
| **Mode of inheritance** | Autosomal dominant → 50 % risk that any child of an affected individual inherits the mutation. |
| **Penetrance & expressivity** | Most carriers develop glucose intolerance by 4th–5th decade, but age of onset can vary; early diagnosis can alter disease course. |
| **Reproductive options** | Pre‑implantation or prenatal testing, or informed planning of family size. |
| **Impact on treatment** | Carriers respond to sulfonylureas, and – as in this case – GLP‑1 agonists can maintain glycaemic control without insulin. |
| **Avoiding inappropriate drugs** | HNF1A‑MODY patients may experience euglycaemic ketoacidosis with SGLT2 inhibitors; knowledge of genotype guides careful medication selection. |

### 3. Testing strategy for asymptomatic relatives

1. **Identify relatives at highest risk**  
   * First‑degree relatives (parents, siblings, children) of the proband.  
   * In the case, the patient’s maternal grandmother (aged > 70), mother, younger brother, and potentially parental siblings.

2. **Genetic testing**  
   * **Targeted variant analysis** (Sanger or NGS panel focused on the p.N270S mutation).  
   * **Optional: full HNF1A sequencing** if family members have unrelated diabetes or if the mutation is not known in all members.

3. **Interpretation**  
   * **Variant‑positive**: classify as carrier; begin early monitoring (fasting glucose, HbA1c, OGTT) beginning in adolescence or earlier if the family pedigree shows very early onset.  
   * **Variant‑negative**: low likelihood of HNF1A‑MODY; routine population‑based diabetes surveillance is adequate.

4. **Clinical monitoring**  
   * Annual fasting glucose + HbA1c up to age 20; biannual thereafter until age 40.  
   * Early glucose‑lowering therapy (sulfonylurea or GLP‑1) if hyperglycaemia is detected; this was the strategy that enabled the proband’s rapid reduction of HbA1c from 10.2 % to 6.0 %.

5. **Re‑evaluation**  
   * If a carrier remains normoglycaemic and shows no β‑cell decline by age 40, counselling can be relaxed, but the possibility of late‑onset remains.

### Take‑home point
Segregation analysis not only validates the pathogenicity of a novel variant but also quantifies the carrier risk for relatives, allowing us to target genetic testing, anticipate early β‑cell dysfunction, and initiate life‑saving, organ‑preserving therapy before the typical clinical symptoms of type 1 or type 2 diabetes appear. In the reported family, systematic testing of asymptomatic members would identify those who can benefit from early sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 therapy, as the patient did, and spare them prolonged insulin exposure and potential complications.
