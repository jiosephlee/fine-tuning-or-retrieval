Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Clinical Pearls] In patients younger than 25 with newly diagnosed hyperglycemia, which clinical features and laboratory findings should raise the suspicion of MODY over type 1 or type 2 diabetes?

**Key clinical clues that should prompt consideration of MODY in a patient < 25 years old with newly detected hyperglycemia**

| Feature | Why it points to MODY | How it presented in the case |
|----------|---------------------|------------------------------|
| **Age at onset** | MODY is a monogenic form that typically manifests before 25 yrs. | The patient was a 24‑year‑old male. |
| **Strong, multi‑generational family history** | Autosomal‑dominant inheritance is characteristic. | Maternal grandmother, mother, and a younger brother had early‑onset diabetes (30s‑20s). |
| **Negative islet‐autoantibodies** | Type 1 diabetes is usually autoimmune; MODY is not. | Both GADA and IA‑2A were negative. |
| **Preserved but impaired β‑cell function** | Type 2 diabetes shows gradual insulin resistance with preserved early secretion; MODY is marked by a progressive β‑cell decline that may still be detectable on stimulation. | Glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide rose from 0.39 nmol/L to 0.83 nmol/L—above the 0.60 nmol/L threshold but clearly sub‑normal. |
| **Glycosuria without ketoacidosis** | HNF1A mutation causes reduced renal SGLT2 activity, leading to glycosuria; ketosis is uncommon. | The patient had glycosuria but no signs of diabetic ketoacidosis. |
| **Normal BMI, absence of insulin‑resistance stigmata** | Type 2 diabetes often presents with obesity or acanthosis nigricans; MODY usually does not. | BMI 24.6 kg/m², no acanthosis nigricans, mild triglyceride elevation but normal HDL. |
| **Marked post‑prandial hyperglycemia (≥11.1 mmol/L) or high 2‑h OGTT** | MODY frequently shows a pronounced post‑meal glucose rise. | 2‑h OGTT was 19.8 mmol/L. |

**Clinical strategy**

1. **Take a focused family history**—look for diabetes in ≥3 generations before age 35.  
2. **Screen for autoantibodies** (GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8) and obtain fasting C‑peptide or a glucagon‑stimulation test.  
3. **Check for glycosuria** in the absence of ketoacidosis or hyperosmolar states.  
4. **Assess insulin‑resistance markers** (BMI, acanthosis nigricans, lipid profile).  
5. **Perform an OGTT** if fasting glucose is only mildly elevated; a 2‑h value >11.1 mmol/L raises suspicion.  
6. **Consider genetic testing** (NGS panel for MODY genes) when the above findings coexist, as in the case where a novel HNF1A variant was identified.

Early identification allows for phenotype‑specific therapy—e.g., sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 agonists, or metformin—as in this patient’s transition from insulin to dulaglutide/metformin with durable glycemic control.
