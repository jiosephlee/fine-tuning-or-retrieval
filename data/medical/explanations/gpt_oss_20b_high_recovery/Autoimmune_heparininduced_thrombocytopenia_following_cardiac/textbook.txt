Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Background and Pathophysiology of Heparin‑Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) and Autoimmune HIT

Heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is an immune‑mediated disorder that paradoxically couples severe thrombocytopenia with a heightened prothrombotic state. In most patients who receive heparin—whether for cardiovascular surgery, percutaneous coronary procedures, or venous thromboembolism prophylaxis—platelet‑factor 4 (PF4) is released from α‑granules during platelet activation. PF4 is a small, cationic chemokine that rapidly binds circulating polyanions, especially unfractionated heparin (UFH), to form PF4/heparin polyanionic complexes. These complexes expose neo‑epitopes that are otherwise hidden on PF4 alone and become antigenic in a subset of individuals.

The adaptive immune system can generate serum IgG antibodies that are specific for these PF4/heparin complexes. When such antibodies bind the complexes, they create immune complexes that cross‑link Fcγ receptor type IIa (FcγRIIa) on platelet surfaces. This cross‑linking activates the platelets, triggering phosphatidylserine exposure, release of dense‑granule contents (ADP, thromboxane A₂), and up‑regulation of surface P‑selectin. Activated platelets are cleared from the circulation by the reticuloendothelial system, producing the characteristic drop in the peripheral platelet count. At the same time, activated platelets provide a catalytic surface for thrombin generation, predisposing patients to venous or arterial thrombosis, the hallmark complication of HIT.

Classic HIT typically manifests 5–10 days after the first exposure to therapeutic or prophylactic UFH or low‑molecular‑weight heparin (LMWH). In the perioperative cardiac‑surgery setting, this time course is preserved because the high doses of UFH used for cardiopulmonary bypass usually suffice to trigger antibody production earlier. When platelets are activated in this way, the clinical picture is generally a moderate thrombocytopenia (often a 50–75 % reduction from baseline with a nadir of 20–50k /µL) and a pronounced risk of arterial thrombosis or major venous occlusion.

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) represents a distinct immunologic variant. Patients with aHIT produce IgG antibodies that possess high affinity for PF4 and can bind PF4 alone or PF4/heparin at virtually all heparin concentrations, including the low therapeutic range and even in the absence of exogenous heparin. Consequently, antibody‑mediated platelet activation is largely heparin‑independent. The underlying mechanism appears to involve autoactivating anti‑PF4 antibodies that do not require the bridging role of heparin to form effective immune complexes. These aHIT antibodies can be generated after any recent heparin exposure, including pre‑operative catheterization, surgery, or therapeutic anticoagulation, and they often appear in the very early post‑operative period (sometimes before day 5).

Clinically, aHIT differs from classic HIT in several respects. First, platelet counts can fall to markedly lower nadirs (often <20k /µL) and can persist for longer periods. Second, the thrombotic sequelae can be more frequent and can involve unusual sites such as cerebral or coronary arteries. Third, the disease can begin earlier than expected because the triggering event (e.g., a pre‑operative catheterization) is “pre‑loaded” on the patient’s immune system. The combination of early onset, severe thrombocytopenia, and a pronounced hypercoagulable state provides a red flag for clinicians that classic HIT testing alone may be insufficient.

Diagnostic evaluation of HIT relies on a two‑step algorithm that integrates a highly sensitive screening test and a highly specific functional assay. The screening assay is typically a PF4/polyanion enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) that detects IgG antibodies against PF4/heparin complexes. Because many individuals have weakly positive results that are clinically irrelevant, a positive ELISA must be followed by a platelet‑activation test. The gold standard functional test is the serotonin‑release assay (SRA), in which donor platelets labeled with radiolabeled serotonin are incubated with patient serum and a range of heparin concentrations. In classic HIT, maximal serotonin release (>20 %) occurs at low heparin concentrations (typically 0.1–0.3 U/mL UFH) and is sharply reduced at high concentrations (≥100 U/mL), due to inhibition of complex formation. Addition of a blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) that occupies FcγRIIa almost always abolishes serotonin release in classic HIT, confirming the receptor‑mediated mechanism.

In aHIT, the SRA profile is altered. Because the antibodies can activate platelets in the absence of heparin, the test often shows strong serotonin release even at 0 U/mL UFH. Moreover, the heparin‑dependent peak may be blunted or absent, and, in some patients, the FcγRIIa blockade with IV.3 fails to inhibit the response, indicating that antibody‑mediated activation does not rely entirely on FcγRIIa cross‑linking. These laboratory nuances are critical: an SRA that is positive at zero heparin, or that is only partially inhibited by IV.3, shifts the diagnostic field toward aHIT, even when the ELISA is strongly positive. Recognizing these test patterns permits rapid differentiation between classic and autoimmune forms, guiding both anticoagulant choice and intensity of monitoring.

In summary, classical HIT and aHIT share the common trigger of PF4/heparin complex formation but diverge in antibody affinity, the requirement for heparin in platelet activation, and the clinical spectrum. A comprehensive understanding of these mechanistic differences is essential for interpreting laboratory results, anticipating disease severity, and steering therapeutic decisions—key lessons highlighted by the patient’s early postoperative thrombotic stroke and the atypical serologic findings described in this case.

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Workup

Aman, a 74‑year‑old man, presented with a long history of exertional dyspnea and chronic atrial fibrillation that had been managed with oral apixaban (5 mg twice daily). The patient had been discovered to have a new systolic murmur and underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterization six days before his scheduled operation. Because the apixaban had not been withheld, the interventionalist used a low dose of unfractionated Heparin (UFH) during the procedure (2000 U). The catheterization revealed a 60 % stenosis of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and a dilated ascending aorta. An operative plan was made for aortic valve replacement, ascending aortic replacement, coronary artery bypass, and left atrial appendage excision.

### Intra‑operative course and anticoagulation

The operation was performed on cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) with total systemic anticoagulation achieved using 45,000 U of UFH. The patient emerged from CPB without notable blood loss and without the need for transfusions. As is typical for patients undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB, postoperative thromboprophylaxis was initiated on postoperative day 1 (POD 1). Low‑molecular‑weight heparin (LMWH; 5 000 U dalteparin s.c.) was used on POD 1, and the dose was increased to 7 500 U on PODs 2–4. No intravenous anticoagulant was given on POD 5, when prophylactic‑dose fondaparinux (2.5 mg s.c.) was started.

### Post‑operative platelet dynamics

Initial postoperative counts were relatively reassuring: bed‑side platelet count on POD 1 was 155 × 10⁹ L⁻¹, down from the pre‑operative level of 210 × 10⁹ L⁻¹. In contrast, the platelet count declined steadily thereafter: 50 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ by the morning of POD 5, 26 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ at the time of a repeat morning count on POD 6, and 13 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ on POD 7. The nadir of 13 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ is markedly below the threshold that is typically considered thrombocytopenia of clinical importance after major surgery.

### Neurologic deterioration and cerebral infarct

The patient developed worsening postoperative delirium noted on POD 5 and worsened further on POD 6, accompanied by a sudden decline in platelet count to 26 × 10⁹ L⁻¹. Though no focal neurologic deficits were initially appreciated, the clinical suspicion of hemorrhagic or ischemic cerebral insult prompted diagnostic imaging. A non‑contrast computed tomography (CT) scan of the brain demonstrated an acute left parietal infarct measuring approximately 3.2 × 1.8 cm, consistent with a thrombotic stroke.

### Laboratory evaluation on POD 5

The laboratory data obtained on POD 5—when the platelet count was 50 × 10⁹ L⁻¹—were:

Additional studies—blood and urine cultures—returned negative, and chest radiography showed no acute pulmonary pathology. Retrospectively examining the peripheral smear (POD 7) revealed isolated thrombocytopenia with no platelet clumping, erythrocyte fragments (schistocytes), or abnormal white‑cell morphology.

The elevated fibrinogen, while an acute‑phase reactant, was consistent with the expected postoperative inflammatory response. Haptoglobin was elevated, arguing against active hemolysis. All coagulation parameters (PT, aPTT) were normal, and there was no evidence of consumptive coagulopathy or microangiopathic hemolysis in the initial smear.

### Exclusion of alternative causes of early postoperative thrombocytopenia

The pattern of decline and the absence of contributory findings steer the clinician toward a non‑hematologic, consumptive process rather than a supply‑side or mechanical cause:

1. **Bleeding or surgical loss** – No documented intra‑operative or post‑operative hemorrhage was noted. Hemoglobin remained stable (97 g L⁻¹) through the first five days, making ongoing blood loss unlikely. The absence of oropharyngeal or chest drainage and normal coagulation studies also argue against significant bleeding.

2. **Dilutional thrombocytopenia from CPB** – CPB circuits can dilute platelets, but this effect typically resolves within the first 24‑48 h. The persistent and progressive decline beyond POD 2 suggests a different mechanism.

3. **Drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia** – The only peri‑operative agents administered were the pre‑procedural UFH (2000 U), intra‑operative UFH (45,000 U), and post‑operative LMWH (dalteparin). No new drugs were introduced during the time course of the platelet fall. Moreover, the temporal profile of the decline aligns with known patterns for heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia rather than a classic drug‐induced immune response, which typically peaks 5–10 days after exposure.

4. **Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) or thrombotic microangiopathy (TTP)** – The coagulation panel was essentially normal; fibrinogen remained high, d‑dimer values were not strikingly elevated at this time. The peripheral smear displayed no schistocytes until day 9. Haptoglobin was not decreased, and bilirubin levels were normal—findings that are inconsistent with microangiopathic hemolysis.

5. **Infection‑related thrombocytopenia** – Blood and urine cultures were negative, and the leukocyte count remained within the normal range without an acute inflammatory response. Furthermore, the platelet decline preceded any clinical or laboratory evidence of infection.

6. **Post‑surgical thrombocytopenia due to transfusion reactions** – No blood transfusions were administered during this period; thus, transfusion‑related immune reactions are excluded.

Given the above, the remaining and most plausible explanation at this point is heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), most strongly suggested by the severe thrombocytopenia occurring in the context of recent heparin exposure (both intra‑operative CPB UFH and pre‑operative low‑dose UFH). The subsequent confirmation with a PF4/polyanion enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and the serotonin‑release assay (SRA) fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for HIT, while the early onset (before POD 5) underscored the influence of prior low‑dose pre‑operative heparin exposure. This initial presentation and workup set the stage for the nuanced distinction between classic HIT and the autoimmune subset that will be discussed later in the report.

## Differential Diagnosis and Clinical Reasoning for HIT

When a patient develops thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, the first step is to rapidly identify the most common and most dangerous causes. In our patient, the key differential diagnoses were shock‑associated thrombocytopenia, sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia (DITP), and transfusion‑related reactions. Each was examined against the clinical presentation, laboratory data, and timing of the platelet decline.

**Shock and critical illness** are frequently associated with platelet consumption. However, the patient remained haemodynamically stable, with normal blood pressures and cardiac output during the peri‑operative period. Coagulation parameters — specifically an elevated fibrinogen level, a normal prothrombin time and partial thromboplastin time, and normal D‑dimer concentrations early on — argue against the consumptive coagulopathy that accompanies shock.

**Sepsis** can cause platelet drop via cytokine‑mediated activation and consumption. Yet blood cultures and urine cultures stayed negative, and the white cell count remained within the reference range without a left shift. No clinical or biochemical evidence of infection was present. Thus, sepsis was unlikely.

**DIC** would manifest as a markedly low fibrinogen, prolonged PT and PTT, and widespread microvascular thrombosis with schistocytes. Our patient had an elevated fibrinogen (6.59 g/L) and a normal PTT, with no schistocytes on early peripheral smears. These findings are inconsistent with DIC.

**TTP** presents with microangiopathic hemolysis, schistocytes, and neurological abnormalities. Although the patient later developed a parietal infarct, early data revealed no schistocytes and a normal haptoglobin, effectively excluding TTP.

**Drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia** typically occurs several days after exposure to a causal drug. The platelet drop began within 24 hours post‑operatively; the only drugs administered during that window were cefazolin, vancomycin, tranexamic acid, and agents for heart rate control and diuresis, none of which are known to cause DITP on such an early time scale. Moreover, the patient had not received any vitamin K antagonists or heparins beyond the intraoperative dose until the pre‑operative coronary catheterisation.

**Transfusion reactions** are excluded because the patient did not receive any intra‑operative or post‑operative blood products, eliminating the possibility of alloimmune platelet destruction.

Having ruled out these common causes, attention turned to heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). The key clues supporting HIT were: (1) an early platelet decline that began after intraprocedural UFH exposure during catheterisation; (2) a thombbotic event—an acute ischemic stroke seen on day 6—consistent with the hypercoagulable state identical to that seen in HIT; and (3) a platelet count falling from 210 × 10⁹/L pre‑operation to 50 × 10⁹/L on day 5, a decrement of >50 % and well below 20 × 10⁹/L (the classic threshold for HIT severity).

The **4‑Ts score** was applied to quantify pre‑test probability. Thrombocytopenia severity (platelets <20 × 10⁹/L) earned 4 points; timing of the fall was early (within 5 days of heparin exposure) 1–2 points; a thrombotic complication (stroke) added 2 points; and the absence of other clear causes gave 2 points, yielding a total of 9–10, a high‑probability score. This score justifies prompt investigation.

Accordingly, the clinical team pursued HIT antibody testing: a PF4/polyanion ELISA and a serotonin‑release assay were chosen as they provide a sensitive screening test and a functional confirmation, respectively. The strongly positive ELISA (optical density 2.2, above the upper limit of 0.4) and the atypical serotonin‑release profile (strong activation at low and zero heparin concentrations) confirmed the diagnosis of autoimmune HIT.

Thus, by systematically excluding more common postoperative causes and recognizing the classic patterns of timing, magnitude, and thrombotic sequelae, clinicians could focus on HIT, leading to early diagnosis and targeted therapy that ultimately resulted in platelet recovery and neurological resolution.

## Laboratory Confirmation and Interpretation of Heparin‑Induced Thrombocytopenia Antibody Assays

Heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is diagnosed by a two‑step laboratory algorithm: a highly sensitive screening test that detects antibodies against platelet‑factor 4 (PF4)/heparin complexes, followed by a functional assay that confirms platelet activation.  In this patient, a strong positive PF4/polyanion enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and an atypical serotonin‑release assay (SRA) provided the serologic evidence that defined autoimmune HIT (aHIT).

### PF4/Polyanion ELISA

The PF4/polyanion ELISA is a solid‑phase immunoassay that detects circulating IgG antibodies that bind PF4 when it is complexed with any polyanion, most commonly heparin.  Its sensitivity exceeds 95 % for clinically relevant HIT antibodies, but its specificity is lower (≈ 30–40 %).  In the index case the quantitative optical density (OD) was 2.2 (reference < 0.4), well above the arbitrary threshold used by most laboratories.  This strong positive binding result identified robust anti‑PF4/heparin antibody production, setting the stage for a functional confirmation.

### Serotonin‑Release Assay Methodology

The SRA is regarded as the gold‑standard functional test for HIT.  It measures the ability of patient serum to trigger donor platelets, preloaded with radiolabeled serotonin, to release the transmitter in the presence of graded heparin concentrations (typically 0, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 5, and 100 U/mL).  Platelet activation by immune complexes engages FcγRIIa (CD32A), leading to serotonin release that is quantified as a percentage of total platelet content.  Because plasma gels directly produce serotonin only when the immune complexes efficiently cross‑link Fc receptors, a pattern of dose‑dependent activation is diagnostic.

### Classic HIT versus Autoimmune HIT SRA Patterns

In classic HIT, antibody‑dependent platelet activation is strictly heparin‑dependent: maximal serotonin release (≫ 20 %) occurs only at low to intermediate heparin concentrations (0.1–0.3 U/mL), whereas high heparin concentrations (≥ 50–100 U/mL) fail to generate immune complexes and the release falls back to < 20 %.  Autoimmune HIT, by contrast, features antibodies that retain a strong activating capacity even in the absence of heparin.  Consequently, patients with aHIT have serotonin release > 20 % at zero heparin, often accompanied by a classic heparin‑dependent peak.

### The Index Patient’s SRA Profile

The patient’s serum yielded 96–99 % serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, and virtually no release (0 %) at 100 U/mL.  This profile satisfies two hallmarks of aHIT:

1. **Heparin‑independent activation** – release > 50 % without heparin demonstrates antibody reactivity that does not require a heparin co‑factor to form a PF4/antibody complex.
2. **Classical heparin‑dependent peak** – the exceptional release at 0.1–0.3 U/mL mimics the pattern of classic HIT, indicating that the antibodies can also form potent complexes in the presence of heparin.

The absence of serotonin release at high heparin concentration confirms the specificity of the assay and rules out nonspecific platelet activation.

### Partial Inhibition by the IV.3 Anti‑FcγRIIa Monoclonal Antibody

The commonly used IV.3 monoclonal antibody blocks the FcγRIIa receptor, thereby preventing IgG‑mediated platelet activation.  In routine HIT, adding IV.3 to the SRA typically abolishes serotonin release (to < 20 %) at all heparin concentrations.  In this patient, IV.3 reduced release at 0.1 U/mL heparin to 76 % (rather than nearly complete suppression).  This partial blockade is remarkably unusual and suggests that the aHIT antibodies in this case possess a higher intrinsic affinity for FcγRIIa or can engage alternative platelet receptors, allowing residual activation even when FcγRIIa is partially saturated.  The novelty of this finding underscores the heterogeneity of aHIT sera and the need for careful interpretation of SRA inhibition studies.

### Serial SRA Testing and Serologic Evolution

Follow‑up SRA results obtained weeks later displayed a shift toward a more “classic” HIT pattern: modest serotonin release at 0 U/mL (≈ 15 %) and robust release at 0.3 U/mL, with full suppression by IV.3.  This evolution mirrors the clinical recovery of platelet counts and the waning of heparin‑independent antibody activity.  Serial testing thus not only confirmed the initial diagnosis but also provided a quantitative measure of antibody dynamics during the course of aHIT.

### Integration with Clinical Context to Confirm aHIT

The serologic data – a highly positive PF4 ELISA, SRA showing both strong heparin‑independent activation and classic heparin‑dependent peak, partial but incomplete FcγRIIa blockade, and subsequent serologic shift toward typical HIT – dovetail with the patient’s clinical presentation.  The precipitous fall in platelets within the first five postoperative days, the acute ischemic stroke, and the absence of alternative explanations for thrombocytopenia (no transfusion, no consumptive coagulopathy from other causes) all point to a hyperactive, heparin‑triggered platelet‑activating immune response.  The combined evidence decisively identifies the disorder as autoimmune HIT, with the observed atypical laboratory phenotype highlighting a severe, heparin‑independent subset of the disease.

## Therapeutic Management and Monitoring in Autoimmune HIT

Upon confirming the diagnosis of autoimmune HIT (aHIT)—a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA coupled with an atypical serotonin‑release assay (SRA) that showed pronounced, heparin‑independent platelet activation and only partial inhibition by FcγIIa blockade—the treating team terminated all heparin derivatives. Dalteparin, which had been used for postoperative thromboprophylaxis, and the prophylactic fondaparinux that was begun on POD 5 were stopped to eliminate the antigen that drives antibody‑mediated platelet activation.

### Anticoagulant options in HIT

HIT obliges the clinician to use non‑heparin anticoagulants. The mainstay is a direct thrombin inhibitor (argatroban, bivalirudin) or a factor Xa inhibitor that works independently of antithrombin (fondaparinux, danaparoid). Because the patient had suffered an acute cerebral infarct on POD 6, the choice favored an agent with a very short half‑life and predictable pharmacodynamics—argatroban—so that if bleeding were to occur the drug could be stopped immediately. The therapeutic dose of argatroban (0.75 µg kg⁻¹ min⁻¹) was titrated to maintain a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 55–85 s, the target range recommended for HIT management.

### Initial use of argatroban: safety in recent stroke, short half‑life

Argatroban’s two‑hour half‑life minimizes the risk of prolonged anticoagulation in the setting of recent cerebrovascular ischemia. Unlike heparin, it does not depend on platelet receptors, and its effects can be undone rapidly by stopping infusion, an essential consideration in this patient whose laboratory tests were already revealing ongoing consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer, emergent schistocytes). The team continued the argatroban infusion five days after the seizure of a cerebral infarct, confirming that the drug did not exacerbate neurological injury.

### High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin: dosing, expected platelet recovery kinetics

In aHIT, the pathogenic antibodies may bind platelets independent of heparin, creating a persistent activation signal that is refractory to standard anticoagulation. High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) can competitively occupy FcγIIa receptors on platelets, interrupting this cycle. The regimen chosen was 100 g IVIG on two consecutive days, followed by a third 100 g dose, as advocated for severe aHIT. Platelet recovery after IVIG in typical cases usually rises sharply within 24–48 h; however, in this patient the rise was modest, with a peak of 54 × 10⁹ L on POD 14 from a nadir of 13 × 10⁹ L a day earlier. The delayed response reflected the intensity of the heparin‑independent antibody activity documented by the SRA.

### Monitoring of fibrinogen, d‑dimer, schistocytes and platelet count

Serial assessment guided therapy because conventional platelet count alone could under‑represent the hypercoagulable activity. Fibrinogen fell progressively from 6.6 g L⁻¹ pre‑operatively to a nadir of 2.5 g L⁻¹ between POD 6 and 17, pointing to a consumptive process. d‑Dimer rose to a peak ≈12 µg mL⁻¹ (arbitrary units) around POD 12, indicating ongoing fibrinolysis secondary to thrombin generation. The peripheral smear revealed schistocytes from POD 9 to 16, consistent with microangiopathic hemolysis contributed by the antibody‑driven platelet cascade. These markers, together with the sluggish platelet count rise, prompted reassessment of the anticoagulation strategy.

### Criteria for switching from argatroban to fondaparinux

The decision to transition to fondaparinux on POD 14 rested on two interrelated criteria. First, administration convenience: fondaparinux is delivered subcutaneously once daily, allowing the patient to be managed in an inpatient setting without continuous intravenous infusion. Second, evidence of insufficient control of aHIT hypercoagulability while on argatroban—falling fibrinogen, rising d‑Dimer, schistocytosis. Fondaparinux, an anti‑Xa agent that relies on antithrombin, had been used off‑label as a therapeutic anticoagulant in HIT, and the team had confidence in its efficacy provided adequate antithrombin levels were guaranteed.

### Antithrombin supplementation before fondaparinux initiation

As HIT can lower circulating antithrombin (AT) by both consumption and antibody‑mediated clearance, the patient’s AT level on POD 14 was measured at 0.70 U mL⁻¹, slightly below the reference range. A 1,000‑U dose of pooled plasma‑derived AT concentrate was administered immediately before the first therapeutic‑dose fondaparinux (10 mg SC daily). This step ensured that the factor Xa inhibitor could exert maximal effect and avoided inadvertent sub‑therapeutic anticoagulation.

### Long‑term anticoagulation: re‑initiation of apixaban at 5 mg BID

After a prolonged period of platelet count normalization (platelets > 150 × 10⁹ L by POD 62) and clinical stability (no residual neurological deficits), fondaparinux was discontinued at POD 65. At that point the treating anticoagulation specialist felt that the risk of thrombotic recurrence was sufficiently low to allow transition to an oral direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC). Apixaban 5 mg twice daily was reinstated, an approach supported by the neutral evidence regarding DOAC use after aHIT. The patient’s final follow‑up showed a durable platelet count of 202 × 10⁹ L at POD 87, with repeated SRA studies indicating a shift to a predominantly heparin‑dependent antibody phenotype and no further evidence of hypercoagulability.

---

This case exemplifies the intricacies of managing autoimmune HIT in the postoperative cardiac surgery patient: immediate withdrawal of all heparin, selection of an anticoagulant based on pharmacokinetics and safety profile, adjunctive IVIG to counteract Fc‑mediated platelet activation, diligent monitoring of coagulation biomarkers beyond the platelet count, timely transition to a subcutaneous factor Xa inhibitor when thrombotic markers remain high, supplementation of antithrombin to secure drug efficacy, and finally, a structured plan to resume oral anticoagulation once the platelet count stabilizes and the risk of recurrence is adequately mitigated.

## Outcomes, Follow‑Up, and Clinical Lessons Learned

The patient’s laboratory and clinical course after the initial crisis illustrates a classic trajectory of severe autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) followed by recovery once appropriate therapy was instituted. Platelet counts fell to a nadir of 13 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ within a week of surgery, but began a steady upward slope after the initiation of argatroban and high‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin. By postoperative day 62 the platelets rebounded to 155 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ and further increased to 202 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ by day 87, a value within normal limits. Concurrently, repeat brain computed tomography, performed after the diagnosis of an acute left parietal infarct, demonstrated complete resolution of the hypodensity, and the patient’s neurologic status returned to baseline, with no residual deficits.

The hematologic recovery was accompanied by a marked shift in markers of coagulation activity. During the early argatroban phase (days 6–17) fibrinogen concentrations fell from 6.6 g L⁻¹ to a nadir of 2.5 g L⁻¹, while plasma D‑dimer levels rose to a peak around day 12, and peripheral blood films revealed circulating schistocytes from days 9 to 16. These changes reflect the consumptive coagulopathy and hypercoagulability that can accompany aHIT. Their resolution paralleled platelet recovery, underscoring the importance of serial coagulation profiling in patients with persistent thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery.

Serotonin‑release assay (SRA) results evolved predictably over several months. The index sample displayed a “aHIT” signature—robust serotonin release in the absence of heparin and only partial inhibition by the Fc‑a2b3 interfering monoclonal antibody IV.3. Subsequent assays, performed at weeks 4, 8, and 12, showed progressively diminished spontaneous release and a return to a classic heparin‑dependent profile, with maximal activation at 0.3 U mL⁻¹ UFH and near‑complete suppression at 100 U mL⁻¹ UFH. The final specimen, obtained after platelet recovery and after resumption of apixaban, had only 15 % spontaneous release, confirming waning aHIT and the dominance of a more typical HIT antibody population.

The early onset of thrombocytopenia in this case—first noted on POD 1—highlights the impact of preoperative UFH exposure. Although UFH had been given at a seemingly negligible dose (2000 U) during the catheterisation procedure, it was sufficient to sensitize the immune system, leading to the formation of platelet‑reactive antibodies that manifest well before the usual 5‑10 day postoperative window. Clinicians should, therefore, maintain a high index of suspicion for HIT even in the first days after cardiac surgery when platelet counts fall sharply.

The patient’s SRA profile demonstrated a previously unreported laboratory phenotype: strong heparin‑independent serotonin release combined with incomplete inhibition by IV.3. This partial blockade implies that the patient’s pathogenic antibodies may engage platelet Fc receptors in a manner that is only partially interfered with by monoclonal blockade, potentially due to higher affinity or alternative epitope recognition. Recognising this atypical pattern prevents the erroneous categorisation of the assay as indeterminate and prompts the clinician to pursue further confirmatory studies. Importantly, it supports the diagnosis of aHIT and informs therapeutic choice.

Hypercoagulability indicators—declining fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, and schistocytosis—served as real‑time surrogates for ongoing thrombo‑activational states. Their trajectory influenced the decision to transition from argatroban, a short‑half‑life direct thrombin inhibitor, to fondaparinux, allowing more convenient once‑daily subcutaneous dosing and possibly more reliable antithrombotic control in the setting of residual antibody activity. The patient’s spontaneous platelet recovery after fondaparinux, coupled with a stable anticoagulant effect, allowed a safe switch back to apixaban.

From a practical standpoint, the case underscores several evidence‑based recommendations for managing suspected aHIT after cardiac surgery. An early work‑up—including a PF4/polyanion ELISA and an SRA that tests both low‑ and high‑dose heparin—should be undertaken whenever a platelet drop >50 % occurs within the first 3 days post‑operation. When spontaneous serotonin release or partial IV.3 inhibition is detected, a diagnosis of aHIT should be considered. In patients with severe thrombocytopenia or recent hemorrhagic events, a direct thrombin inhibitor such as argatroban is preferable as first‑line therapy. Adjunctive high‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin can be employed, but clinicians should anticipate the possibility of a delayed platelet count response necessitating multiple dosing. Transition to a factor Xa inhibitor (fondaparinux) or low‑molecular‑weight heparin should be favoured once platelet counts rise above 50 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ and hypercoagulable markers normalize, with careful monitoring of antithrombin levels to ensure adequate anticoagulation. Finally, ongoing platelet and coagulation surveillance for at least 4–8 weeks, alongside periodic SRA testing, ensures that residual antibody activity does not provoke late complications.

In summary, this patient’s outcome demonstrates that even exceptionally severe and early‑onset aHIT can resolve fully with targeted therapy and vigilant monitoring. Recognising the subtle laboratory nuances of aHIT and aligning anticoagulant strategies accordingly can prevent thrombotic or hemorrhagic sequelae and ensure a favorable long‑term outcome.
