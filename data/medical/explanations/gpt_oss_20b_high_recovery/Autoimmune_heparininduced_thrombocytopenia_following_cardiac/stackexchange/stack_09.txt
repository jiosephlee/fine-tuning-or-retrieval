Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Clinical Pearls] The patient’s platelet count remained below 30x10^9/L for several weeks but without overt bleeding. What therapeutic strategies should be employed to mitigate the risk of further thrombosis while avoiding exacerbation of thrombocytopenia?

**Mitigating thrombotic risk in aHIT with persistent thrombocytopenia (≤ 30 × 10^9/L) yet no bleeding**

| Strategy | Rationale in this patient | Practical points |
|---------|---------------------------|------------------|
| **1. Continue non‑heparin anticoagulation** | Heparin‑independent HIT antibodies drive platelet activation regardless of the presence of UFH.  A drug that does **not** depend on PF4‑heparin complexes will block the downstream thrombin generation while avoiding further activation of the abnormal autoantibodies. | • **Direct thrombin inhibitors (argatroban, bivalirudin)** – Preferred when platelet counts are very low and a short half‑life is desirable (e.g., in the setting of recent stroke or potential bleeding). Target PTT 1.5–2.5× baseline. <br>• **Fondaparinux** – Factor‑Xa inhibitor that can be started once the patient is clinically stable and platelet count > 15–20 × 10^9/L.  Antithrombin can be supplemented if AT is < 0.6 U/mL. |
| **2. High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)** | In aHIT the antibody‑platelet interaction is Fc‑γ receptor mediated.  Saturation of these receptors with IVIG reduces platelet consumption and can modestly raise the count.  In our case, 100 g/d for 2 days + 100 g/d on day 3 produced a gradual rise (13 → 54 × 10^9/L). | • Initiate as soon as HIT is suspected or confirmed.  • Monitor for infusion reactions and thrombosis exacerbation. |
| **3. Avoid platelet transfusion** | Platelet transfusion supplies substrate for immune complexes, frequently worsening thrombosis in HIT.  Only give if active, life‑threatening bleeding or planned invasive procedure. | • Follow guidelines: do **not** transfuse on a routine basis for thrombocytopenia alone. |
| **4. Close laboratory surveillance** | Persistent low platelets with a rising d‑dimer, falling fibrinogen, and schistocytes (as seen Week 2–3 in the case) signify ongoing consumptive coagulopathy and hyper‑coagulability.  Adjust anticoagulation intensity accordingly. | • Daily platelet count and mean platelet volume. <br>• Anti‑Xa (if on fondaparinux) or PTT (if on argatroban). <br>• Fibrinogen, D‑dimer trend. |
| **5. Transition to oral anticoagulation once stable** | Once platelet count > 50 × 10^9/L and hyper‑coagulability markers normalize, switch to DOAC or warfarin to complete a 3‑month HIT therapy.  In the reported case apixaban was resumed 3 days after last fondaparinux dose (POD 65). | • Ensure no residual HIT‑antibody activity (SRA serial checks may help). <br>• DOAC dosing: maintain therapeutic level (e.g., apixaban 5 mg BID). |

**Key Take‑away from the case**  
The patient’s course illustrates that a severe autoimmune HIT can persist with platelet counts < 30 × 10^9/L yet remain non‑bleeding.  Initial management with argatroban + IVIG, followed by a switch to fondaparinux once platelet recovery and de‑hypercoagulability were demonstrated, led to full hematologic and neurologic recovery.  In practice, the same principles apply: use a non‑heparin anticoagulant that can be titrated to the bleeding risk, add IVIG to blunt antibody‑mediated platelet clearance, avoid platelet transfusion unless absolutely necessary, and monitor coagulation markers to guide therapy.
