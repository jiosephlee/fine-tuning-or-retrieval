Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Pathophysiology] In this case, the SRA showed strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin and only partial inhibition by the Fc receptor–blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3. What does this pattern indicate about the antibody’s characteristics, and why does it present a diagnostic challenge?

**Key point:**  
The SRA pattern with **strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin** plus **only partial inhibition with Fcγ IIa blocker IV.3** is the classic serologic hallmark of *autoimmune HIT (aHIT)* in which the antibody can activate platelets independently of heparin and through both Fc‑dependent and Fc‑independent mechanisms.  

**Why this pattern matters**

1. **Heparin‑independent activation** – The 96 % serotonin release without any heparin shows that the autoantibody binds PF4 (or another platelet ligand) alone and triggers platelet aggregation. This is unlike classic HIT, where platelet activation requires a PF4–heparin complex and is absent at 0 U/mL.  
2. **Partial Fc blockade** – IV.3 blocks the platelet Fcγ IIa receptor, which is the usual route for immune‑complex–mediated HIT activation. In our patient, IV.3 reduced but did not eliminate serotonin release (76 % remaining), indicating that the antibody also engages platelets through a second, Fc‑independent pathway or is so potent that ICs still recruit residual Fcγ IIa contacts.  
3. **Diagnostic gray zone** – Most confirmatory SRA protocols interpret “complete inhibition” by IV.3 as a hallmark of true HIT. Partial inhibition, especially when coupled with strong 0‑U release, can be misread as “indeterminate” or even “positive for a different process.” This creates a diagnostic pitfall that may delay recognition of severe aHIT.  

**Clinical implication** – Recognizing this atypical pattern is essential because it signals a high‑risk prothrombotic state and guides treatment (urgent discontinuation of heparin, use of direct thrombin or Xa inhibitors, and often high‑dose IV Ig). A careful blend of clinical suspicion, ELISA screening, and nuanced interpretation of functional assays is required to avoid misdiagnosis and ensure timely, appropriate therapy.
