Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Management] High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered in this patient. Explain the proposed mechanism by which IVIG improves platelet counts in aHIT and why it may sometimes fail to produce an immediate response.

**Mechanism of benefit**

High‑dose IVIG (≈ 1 g kg⁻¹ over 3 days) delivers a saturating pool of normal IgG.  
In autoimmune HIT (aHIT) pathogenic IgG antibodies bind PF4–heparin (or PF4 alone) and cross‑link FcγRIIa on platelets and monocytes, driving platelet activation, aggregation, and Fc‑mediated clearance.  
IVIG competes for FcγRIIa, thereby:

1. **Blocking receptor engagement** – the excess IgG on the IVIG molecule occupies FcγRIIa and prevents the pathogenic immune complexes from inducing platelet activation and destruction.  
2. **Neutralizing the antibodies** – anti‑idiotypic IgG in the IVIG preparations can bind the Fab portion of the HIT antibodies, reducing their functional activity.  
3. **Complement inhibition** – high‑dose IVIG inhibits complement activation that can contribute to platelet opsonization and clearance.  

The net effect is a rapid reduction in the rate of platelet consumption and a rebound in circulating platelet numbers.

**Why the response can be delayed**

- **High antibody burden and affinity** – In severe aHIT the serum contains very high titres of high‑affinity IgG. Saturating FcγRIIa may require prolonged IVIG infusion, delaying full blockade. The patient’s serotonin‑release assay showed partial inhibition by the Fc‑blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3), indicating that the antibodies possess a degree of Fc‑receptor resistance.
- **Ongoing hypercoagulability** – Until adequate anticoagulation (argatroban → fondaparinux) is achieved, continued platelet activation and consumption can blunt the short‑term rise in counts, as seen in this patient.
- **Delayed marrow recovery** – Severe thrombocytopenia induces transient marrow suppression; the platelet pool must be replenished, which naturally takes several days.
- **Heparin‑independent activation** – The patient’s antibodies were active in the absence of heparin; IVIG blocks FcγRIIa but does not remove the PF4‑targeting capacity of the antibodies, so residual activation can persist until the antibodies are cleared.

In this case, the patient required 3 days of 100 g IVIG before a modest rise (13 × 10⁹ → 54 × 10⁹ L⁻¹), and only after additional anticoagulant adjustment did the platelet count recover fully. Thus, while IVIG is a powerful adjunct in aHIT, its benefit may not appear immediately, especially in patients with intense antibody responses or partial Fc‑resistance.
