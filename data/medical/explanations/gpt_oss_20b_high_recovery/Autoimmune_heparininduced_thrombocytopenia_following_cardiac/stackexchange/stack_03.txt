Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Pathophysiology] What is the immunopathogenic difference between classic HIT and autoimmune HIT (aHIT) in terms of antibody specificity and platelet activation, especially regarding heparin dependence?

**Classic HIT vs. Autoimmune HIT (aHIT): Immunopathogenesis**

| Feature | Classic HIT | Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) |
|---------|-------------|-----------------------|
| **Antibody specificity** | IgG that binds *PF4–heparin* complexes. Antigens are formed only when heparin is present. | IgG that recognizes PF4 (often the same epitope) but can bind mono‑‐ or poly‑valently to PF4 without the need for exogenous heparin, or to “free” PF4 that is exposed in the circulation. |
| **Platelet activation** | Requires heparin to form the optimal antigenic complex. Activation is maximal at low–therapeutic heparin concentrations (≈0.1–0.3 U/mL) and is **abolished** by very high heparin (≥10 U/mL) or by FcγRIIA blockade (e.g., IV.3 monoclonal antibody). | Heparin‑independent platelet activation is possible – patients’ serum can trigger serotonin release even when *no* heparin is added (as seen in the case where 96 % release was seen at 0 U/mL). When heparin is present, the profile mirrors classic HIT (strong release at 0.1–0.3 U/mL, none at 100 U/mL), but the response is often less completely inhibited by Fc blockade. |
| **Clinical timing** | Platelet decline typically 5–10 days after first heparin exposure. | May onset earlier (≤5 days), especially after pre‑operative heparin or recent exposure, because the auto‑reactive antibodies are already primed (the case’s trough platelet count began falling by postoperative day 1). |
| **Therapeutic implications** | Platelet‐reactive IgG is removed by plasma‑exchange or high‑dose IVIG; direct thrombin inhibitors are preferred. | IVIG is often more effective (generally a brisk platelet rise) because it saturates FcγRIIA. However, as illustrated in the case, severe aHIT may show only partial response and may require prolonged, alternative agents (fondaparinux, argatroban). |

**Key points from the case**

1. **Strong ELISA OD (2.2) → PF4‑binding IgG present.**  
2. **SRA profile**:  
   • 96 % serotonin release **without heparin** → hallmark of aHIT.  
   • Robust release at 0.1–0.3 U/mL, none at 100 U/mL → consistent with classic heparin‑dependent activation.  
   • **Partial inhibition** by IV.3 (76 % release remains) → unusual, indicating that the IgG may engage platelet FcγRIIA with higher affinity or use additional platelet receptors.  
3. **Clinical course**: Early thrombocytopenia after pre‑operative catheterisation, severe thrombocytopenia, hypercoagulability (stroke, schistocytes), and only modest platelet rebound after IVIG, all signatures of a severe aHIT.

**Bottom line**  
Classic HIT is driven by heparin‑dependent PF4‑complex IgG that activates platelets only when heparin is present; aHIT involves IgG that can activate platelets independently of added heparin. The immunologic distinction is most readily seen in the serotonin‑release assay (activation at 0 U/mL) and the degree of inhibition by FcγRIIA blockade. Understanding this difference guides both diagnosis (look for zero‑heparin activation) and management (consider stronger or alternate anticoagulation and anticipate a more profound thrombocytopenia).
