Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Management] Given the patient’s recent ischemic stroke and severe thrombocytopenia, why was argatroban chosen initially over fondaparinux for anticoagulation? Discuss the pharmacologic and safety considerations.

**Why argatroban was started instead of fondaparinux**

1. **Urgent need for an immediately reversible anticoagulant**  
   The patient had just suffered an acute ischemic stroke (left parietal lobe infarct) and developed profound thrombocytopenia (13 × 10⁹ /L). In such circumstances the risk of intracranial hemorrhage is greatest if anticoagulation cannot be stopped quickly. Argatroban is a direct thrombin inhibitor with a short half‑life (≈ 45 min) that can be discontinued at any time; the anticoagulant effect dissipates within 1–2 h. Fondaparinux, by contrast, is a factor‑Xa inhibitor with a long half‑life (≈ 17–20 h) and is administered subcutaneously once daily, making it difficult to eliminate rapidly if bleeding starts.

2. **Bedside dose titration and monitoring**  
   Argatroban is given as a continuous IV infusion and its anticoagulant activity is monitored by activated partial thromboplastin time (PTT). The dose can be titrated to the therapeutic range (55–85 s) and adjusted promptly for changes in coagulation or kidney function. In this case the treating hematologist wanted tight control during the first week of therapy when the patient was at highest risk of hemorrhage.

3. **Avoidance of antithrombin dependence**  
   Fondaparinux requires an adequate antithrombin (AT) level for full activity. The patient’s AT was mildly reduced (0.70 U/mL) on the day of fondaparinux initiation; to compensate, AT concentrate was given, but the team still preferred the more reliable pharmacology of argatroban in a catechol‑dependent setting.

4. **Pharmacologic safety in severe thrombocytopenia**  
   While both agents are used off‑label for HIT, argatroban’s effect is less dependent on platelet count and is not affected by the high platelet‑activating antibody titers found in autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In practice, the very low platelets in this patient (13 × 10⁹ /L) could have potentiated the bleeding risk of a factor‑Xa drug that has a longer “clearance window” than a thrombin blocker. Argatroban also allows rapid down‑titration if signs of bleeding appear.

5. **Treatment sequence in the case**  
   After the diagnosis of aHIT and the stroke, the team stopped fondaparinux and started therapeutic‑dose argatroban with PTT monitoring. When evidence of residual hypercoagulability (falling fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer, schistocytosis) emerged after 14 days, they switched to fondaparinux—its once‑daily subcutaneous dosing being easier to manage once the acute bleeding risk had abated.

**Bottom line:** Argatroban was chosen initially because its intravenous, rapid‑onset, short‑duration, PTT‑monitorable action affords the safest, most flexible anticoagulation in a patient with a recent ischemic stroke, severe thrombocytopenia, and a confirmed but potentially fulminant aHIT. Fondaparinux was reserved for a later, more stable phase when the bleeding risk had markedly decreased and the convenience of once‑daily dosing became advantageous.
