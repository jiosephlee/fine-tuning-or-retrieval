Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Describe the diagnostic algorithm for HIT, including the roles of the PF4/polyanion ELISA and the serotonin release assay (SRA). How can the SRA help identify autoimmune HIT?

**Diagnostic algorithm for HIT**

1. **Clinical assessment**  
   • Determine the likelihood of HIT with the 4‑T score (thrombocytopenia, timing, thrombosis, other causes).  
   • In cardiac‑surgery patients a platelet fall < 5 × 10⁹ L/d on POD 5–10 is highly suspicious; an earlier fall, as in this case, points to prior heparin exposure (pre‑operative catheterisation).

2. **Screening immunoassay – PF4/polyanion ELISA**  
   • Detects IgG (and to a lesser extent IgM/IgA) that bind PF4–heparin complexes.  
   • High optical density (OD > 0.4) has high sensitivity; OD > 1.0 correlates with clinical HIT and enhances positive predictive value.  
   • In the present patient the ELISA was strongly positive (OD 2.2), indicative of a substantial antibody load.

3. **Confirmatory functional test – serotonin‑release assay (SRA)**  
   • Gold‑standard to prove that patient antibodies can activate platelets.  
   • Performed with donor platelets, patient serum and serial heparin concentrations (0, 0.1, 0.3 and 100 U/mL).  
   • Typical HIT pattern: ≥ 20 % serotonin release at low heparin (0.1–0.3 U/mL) and < 20 % at high heparin (100 U/mL).  
   • In this case the SRA met the classic profile (97 % release at 0.1 U/mL, 0 % at 100 U/mL) **and** showed strong release in the **absence of heparin** (96 % at 0 U/mL). This is the hallmark of *autoimmune HIT (aHIT)*.

4. **Interpretation of atypical SRA features**  
   • Baseline (0 U/mL) serotonin release indicates heparin‑independent platelet activation, confirming aHIT.  
   • Partial inhibition by Fc‑blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) suggests the antibodies may be unusually reactive or of mixed subclass—an observation that helped distinguish this case from classic HIT.  
   • Serial repeat SRAs can show evolution of the antibody phenotype (e.g., earlier aHIT profile transforming into a typical HIT pattern), as seen after the patient’s recovery.

5. **Management decision**  
   • A positive ELISA + classic SRA → treat as HIT with a non‑heparin anticoagulant.  
   • A positive ELISA + heparin‑independent SRA → diagnose aHIT; anticipate a more severe thrombocytopenia and hypercoagulability, and consider aggressive anticoagulation (e.g., argatroban, fondaparinux) plus adjunctive therapies such as high‑dose IVIG, as was required in this patient.

**Key point for residents:**  
The SRA is not just a confirmatory test; its pattern—particularly baseline platelet activation—reveals whether the antibodies are classic heparin‑dependent (HIT) or autoimmune (aHIT). In this case, the atypical SRA (strong release at 0 U/mL heparin and partial Fc inhibition) identified severe aHIT early, guiding prompt escalation of therapy and allowing the patient to recover fully.
