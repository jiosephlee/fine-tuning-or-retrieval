Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Clinical Pearls] In patients undergoing cardiac surgery, the classic onset of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is usually between postoperative days 5 and 10. What factors or exposures can precipitate an earlier onset (<5 days) and why might preoperative heparin use be a risk factor?

**Key Point**

Early‑onset (< 5 days) HIT after cardiac surgery is most frequently precipitated by prior heparin exposure—especially pre‑operative heparin given for procedures such as coronary angiography or catheterisation. Pre‑operative heparin primes the immune system; circulating anti‑PF4/heparin IgG (or heparin‑independent “autoimmune” antibodies) are already present and react immediately when heparin is re‑administered during cardiopulmonary bypass. This bypasses the usual 5‑10‑day latency and produces a rapid platelet fall, as seen in the case described.

**Why pre‑operative heparin is a risk factor**

1. **Immunologic priming** – Even a single exposure (2000 U UFH at coronary angiography in the index patient) can generate class‑switching IgG against PF4–heparin complexes.  
2. **Rapid re‑exposure** – Cannulation and CPB inoculate large amounts of UFH (45 000 U in the case) within hours of the pre‑operative dose, allowing the pre‑existing antibodies to form immune complexes and trigger platelet activation at the first postoperative day.  
3. **Autoimmune HIT** – The patient’s serotonin‑release assay showed heparin‑independent platelet activation, characteristic of autoimmune HIT, which can cause platelet activation immediately after exposure without the usual lag time.

**Other exposures that can prompt early onset**

- High‑dose or prolonged intra‑operative heparin (e.g., >10 000 U total).  
- Re‑use of heparin on successive procedures within a short interval.  
- Early postoperative LMWH prophylaxis (although less common, it can precipitate HIT if antibodies are already present).

**Bottom line**

In cardiac surgery, a history of pre‑operative heparin exposure is a red flag for very early HIT. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for HIT when thrombocytopenia develops within the first 5 days of surgery, and the screening and confirmatory platelet‑activation assays should be obtained promptly.

### [Differential Diagnosis] List the differential diagnoses for a sudden drop in platelet count after cardiac surgery that can also present with thrombosis or stroke, and describe key laboratory findings that help to distinguish them from HIT.

**Differential for a sudden, peri‑operative drop in platelets that can also cause thrombosis or stroke**

| Condition | Typical clinical picture | Key laboratory clues that separate it from HIT (classic or autoimmune) |
|-----------|------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Classic heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)** | ↓ platelets 4–10 days after heparin exposure, often coupled with venous or arterial thrombosis | • PF4‑polyanion ELISA **positive**. <br>• Serotonin‑release assay (SRA) **activated** at 0.1–0.3 U/mL UFH but not at high heparin. <br>• Coagulation studies (PT/PTT, fibrinogen) **normal**. <br>• No schistocytes; no hemolysis. |
| **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** | Very early fall in platelets (≤ POD 5) after *prior* heparin exposure; can be profoundly thrombocytopenic and hypercoagulable | • PF4 ELISA **strongly positive** (often high OD). <br>• SRA shows **serotonin release even at 0 U/mL UFH** (heparin‑independent) and partial inhibition by Fc‑receptor blockade. <br>• Coagulation studies **normal**. <br>• Absence of schistocytes. |
| **Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)** (acquired) | Severe thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, neurologic deficits, renal dysfunction, fever | • **Schistocytes** on smear (≥ 5 / 10 WBC). <br>• **↑ LDH, ↓ haptoglobin, ↑ reticulocytes**. <br>• **Normal PT/PTT, normal fibrinogen.** <br>• **ADAMTS13 activity <10 %** (rapid bedside assay if available). <br>• PF4 ELISA **negative**; SRA negative. |

| **Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)** | Platelet consumption with microvascular thrombosis; can present with stroke or bleed | • **Prolonged PT, aPTT.** <br>• **Low fibrinogen**, **↑ D‑dimer**. <br>• Presence of **schistocytes** may be variable. <br>• **PF4 ELISA negative**; SRA negative. |
| **Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS)** | Multiple organ thromboses, including stroke, and sudden drop in platelets | • **Positive antiphospholipid antibodies** (aCL IgG/IgM, anti‑β2GP1, lupus anticoagulant). <br>• Coagulation times **often normal**; fibrinogen may be normal or mildly low. <br>• No specific PF4 antibody; SRA negative. |

| **Mechanical platelet consumption (e.g., by CPB, prosthetic valve)** | Mild to moderate thrombocytopenia; usually no systemic thrombosis | • No schistocytes; normal hemolysis labs. <br>• Coagulation studies **normal**. <br>• PF4 ELISA **negative**; SRA negative. |

### Highlight: How the case supported a diagnosis of aHIT over other entities

1. **No schistocytes** and **normal coagulation times** made TTP, atypical HUS, or DIC unlikely.  
2. **PF4/polyanion ELISA OD 2.2** was strongly positive, but the **SRA showed strong serotonin release even with 0 U/mL heparin**, while at higher heparin concentrations no activation occurred—classical for aHIT.  
3. **Partial inhibition by the Fc‑receptor–blocking antibody** (IV.3) reflected an unusual, heparin‑independent, Fc‑receptor–mediated pathway typical of an autoimmune variant.  
4. The **lesion of the left parietal lobe** was consistent with a thrombotic stroke, a hallmark complication of HIT/aHIT.  

Collectively, the laboratory pattern—normal PT/PTT and fibrinogen, absence of schistocytes, high‑OD PF4 ELISA, and an SRA that is heparin‑dependent *and* shows serotonin release at zero heparin—confirms autoimmune HIT and excludes the other thrombotic microangiopathies and consumptive coagulopathies that also present with sudden thrombocytopenia and thrombosis after cardiac surgery.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the immunopathogenic difference between classic HIT and autoimmune HIT (aHIT) in terms of antibody specificity and platelet activation, especially regarding heparin dependence?

**Classic HIT vs. Autoimmune HIT (aHIT): Immunopathogenesis**

| Feature | Classic HIT | Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) |
|---------|-------------|-----------------------|
| **Antibody specificity** | IgG that binds *PF4–heparin* complexes. Antigens are formed only when heparin is present. | IgG that recognizes PF4 (often the same epitope) but can bind mono‑‐ or poly‑valently to PF4 without the need for exogenous heparin, or to “free” PF4 that is exposed in the circulation. |
| **Platelet activation** | Requires heparin to form the optimal antigenic complex. Activation is maximal at low–therapeutic heparin concentrations (≈0.1–0.3 U/mL) and is **abolished** by very high heparin (≥10 U/mL) or by FcγRIIA blockade (e.g., IV.3 monoclonal antibody). | Heparin‑independent platelet activation is possible – patients’ serum can trigger serotonin release even when *no* heparin is added (as seen in the case where 96 % release was seen at 0 U/mL). When heparin is present, the profile mirrors classic HIT (strong release at 0.1–0.3 U/mL, none at 100 U/mL), but the response is often less completely inhibited by Fc blockade. |
| **Clinical timing** | Platelet decline typically 5–10 days after first heparin exposure. | May onset earlier (≤5 days), especially after pre‑operative heparin or recent exposure, because the auto‑reactive antibodies are already primed (the case’s trough platelet count began falling by postoperative day 1). |
| **Therapeutic implications** | Platelet‐reactive IgG is removed by plasma‑exchange or high‑dose IVIG; direct thrombin inhibitors are preferred. | IVIG is often more effective (generally a brisk platelet rise) because it saturates FcγRIIA. However, as illustrated in the case, severe aHIT may show only partial response and may require prolonged, alternative agents (fondaparinux, argatroban). |

**Key points from the case**

1. **Strong ELISA OD (2.2) → PF4‑binding IgG present.**  
2. **SRA profile**:  
   • 96 % serotonin release **without heparin** → hallmark of aHIT.  
   • Robust release at 0.1–0.3 U/mL, none at 100 U/mL → consistent with classic heparin‑dependent activation.  
   • **Partial inhibition** by IV.3 (76 % release remains) → unusual, indicating that the IgG may engage platelet FcγRIIA with higher affinity or use additional platelet receptors.  
3. **Clinical course**: Early thrombocytopenia after pre‑operative catheterisation, severe thrombocytopenia, hypercoagulability (stroke, schistocytes), and only modest platelet rebound after IVIG, all signatures of a severe aHIT.

**Bottom line**  
Classic HIT is driven by heparin‑dependent PF4‑complex IgG that activates platelets only when heparin is present; aHIT involves IgG that can activate platelets independently of added heparin. The immunologic distinction is most readily seen in the serotonin‑release assay (activation at 0 U/mL) and the degree of inhibition by FcγRIIA blockade. Understanding this difference guides both diagnosis (look for zero‑heparin activation) and management (consider stronger or alternate anticoagulation and anticipate a more profound thrombocytopenia).

### [Differential Diagnosis] Describe the diagnostic algorithm for HIT, including the roles of the PF4/polyanion ELISA and the serotonin release assay (SRA). How can the SRA help identify autoimmune HIT?

**Diagnostic algorithm for HIT**

1. **Clinical assessment**  
   • Determine the likelihood of HIT with the 4‑T score (thrombocytopenia, timing, thrombosis, other causes).  
   • In cardiac‑surgery patients a platelet fall < 5 × 10⁹ L/d on POD 5–10 is highly suspicious; an earlier fall, as in this case, points to prior heparin exposure (pre‑operative catheterisation).

2. **Screening immunoassay – PF4/polyanion ELISA**  
   • Detects IgG (and to a lesser extent IgM/IgA) that bind PF4–heparin complexes.  
   • High optical density (OD > 0.4) has high sensitivity; OD > 1.0 correlates with clinical HIT and enhances positive predictive value.  
   • In the present patient the ELISA was strongly positive (OD 2.2), indicative of a substantial antibody load.

3. **Confirmatory functional test – serotonin‑release assay (SRA)**  
   • Gold‑standard to prove that patient antibodies can activate platelets.  
   • Performed with donor platelets, patient serum and serial heparin concentrations (0, 0.1, 0.3 and 100 U/mL).  
   • Typical HIT pattern: ≥ 20 % serotonin release at low heparin (0.1–0.3 U/mL) and < 20 % at high heparin (100 U/mL).  
   • In this case the SRA met the classic profile (97 % release at 0.1 U/mL, 0 % at 100 U/mL) **and** showed strong release in the **absence of heparin** (96 % at 0 U/mL). This is the hallmark of *autoimmune HIT (aHIT)*.

4. **Interpretation of atypical SRA features**  
   • Baseline (0 U/mL) serotonin release indicates heparin‑independent platelet activation, confirming aHIT.  
   • Partial inhibition by Fc‑blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) suggests the antibodies may be unusually reactive or of mixed subclass—an observation that helped distinguish this case from classic HIT.  
   • Serial repeat SRAs can show evolution of the antibody phenotype (e.g., earlier aHIT profile transforming into a typical HIT pattern), as seen after the patient’s recovery.

5. **Management decision**  
   • A positive ELISA + classic SRA → treat as HIT with a non‑heparin anticoagulant.  
   • A positive ELISA + heparin‑independent SRA → diagnose aHIT; anticipate a more severe thrombocytopenia and hypercoagulability, and consider aggressive anticoagulation (e.g., argatroban, fondaparinux) plus adjunctive therapies such as high‑dose IVIG, as was required in this patient.

**Key point for residents:**  
The SRA is not just a confirmatory test; its pattern—particularly baseline platelet activation—reveals whether the antibodies are classic heparin‑dependent (HIT) or autoimmune (aHIT). In this case, the atypical SRA (strong release at 0 U/mL heparin and partial Fc inhibition) identified severe aHIT early, guiding prompt escalation of therapy and allowing the patient to recover fully.

### [Pathophysiology] In this case, the SRA showed strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin and only partial inhibition by the Fc receptor–blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3. What does this pattern indicate about the antibody’s characteristics, and why does it present a diagnostic challenge?

**Key point:**  
The SRA pattern with **strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin** plus **only partial inhibition with Fcγ IIa blocker IV.3** is the classic serologic hallmark of *autoimmune HIT (aHIT)* in which the antibody can activate platelets independently of heparin and through both Fc‑dependent and Fc‑independent mechanisms.  

**Why this pattern matters**

1. **Heparin‑independent activation** – The 96 % serotonin release without any heparin shows that the autoantibody binds PF4 (or another platelet ligand) alone and triggers platelet aggregation. This is unlike classic HIT, where platelet activation requires a PF4–heparin complex and is absent at 0 U/mL.  
2. **Partial Fc blockade** – IV.3 blocks the platelet Fcγ IIa receptor, which is the usual route for immune‑complex–mediated HIT activation. In our patient, IV.3 reduced but did not eliminate serotonin release (76 % remaining), indicating that the antibody also engages platelets through a second, Fc‑independent pathway or is so potent that ICs still recruit residual Fcγ IIa contacts.  
3. **Diagnostic gray zone** – Most confirmatory SRA protocols interpret “complete inhibition” by IV.3 as a hallmark of true HIT. Partial inhibition, especially when coupled with strong 0‑U release, can be misread as “indeterminate” or even “positive for a different process.” This creates a diagnostic pitfall that may delay recognition of severe aHIT.  

**Clinical implication** – Recognizing this atypical pattern is essential because it signals a high‑risk prothrombotic state and guides treatment (urgent discontinuation of heparin, use of direct thrombin or Xa inhibitors, and often high‑dose IV Ig). A careful blend of clinical suspicion, ELISA screening, and nuanced interpretation of functional assays is required to avoid misdiagnosis and ensure timely, appropriate therapy.

### [Management] Given the patient’s recent ischemic stroke and severe thrombocytopenia, why was argatroban chosen initially over fondaparinux for anticoagulation? Discuss the pharmacologic and safety considerations.

**Why argatroban was started instead of fondaparinux**

1. **Urgent need for an immediately reversible anticoagulant**  
   The patient had just suffered an acute ischemic stroke (left parietal lobe infarct) and developed profound thrombocytopenia (13 × 10⁹ /L). In such circumstances the risk of intracranial hemorrhage is greatest if anticoagulation cannot be stopped quickly. Argatroban is a direct thrombin inhibitor with a short half‑life (≈ 45 min) that can be discontinued at any time; the anticoagulant effect dissipates within 1–2 h. Fondaparinux, by contrast, is a factor‑Xa inhibitor with a long half‑life (≈ 17–20 h) and is administered subcutaneously once daily, making it difficult to eliminate rapidly if bleeding starts.

2. **Bedside dose titration and monitoring**  
   Argatroban is given as a continuous IV infusion and its anticoagulant activity is monitored by activated partial thromboplastin time (PTT). The dose can be titrated to the therapeutic range (55–85 s) and adjusted promptly for changes in coagulation or kidney function. In this case the treating hematologist wanted tight control during the first week of therapy when the patient was at highest risk of hemorrhage.

3. **Avoidance of antithrombin dependence**  
   Fondaparinux requires an adequate antithrombin (AT) level for full activity. The patient’s AT was mildly reduced (0.70 U/mL) on the day of fondaparinux initiation; to compensate, AT concentrate was given, but the team still preferred the more reliable pharmacology of argatroban in a catechol‑dependent setting.

4. **Pharmacologic safety in severe thrombocytopenia**  
   While both agents are used off‑label for HIT, argatroban’s effect is less dependent on platelet count and is not affected by the high platelet‑activating antibody titers found in autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In practice, the very low platelets in this patient (13 × 10⁹ /L) could have potentiated the bleeding risk of a factor‑Xa drug that has a longer “clearance window” than a thrombin blocker. Argatroban also allows rapid down‑titration if signs of bleeding appear.

5. **Treatment sequence in the case**  
   After the diagnosis of aHIT and the stroke, the team stopped fondaparinux and started therapeutic‑dose argatroban with PTT monitoring. When evidence of residual hypercoagulability (falling fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer, schistocytosis) emerged after 14 days, they switched to fondaparinux—its once‑daily subcutaneous dosing being easier to manage once the acute bleeding risk had abated.

**Bottom line:** Argatroban was chosen initially because its intravenous, rapid‑onset, short‑duration, PTT‑monitorable action affords the safest, most flexible anticoagulation in a patient with a recent ischemic stroke, severe thrombocytopenia, and a confirmed but potentially fulminant aHIT. Fondaparinux was reserved for a later, more stable phase when the bleeding risk had markedly decreased and the convenience of once‑daily dosing became advantageous.

### [Management] High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered in this patient. Explain the proposed mechanism by which IVIG improves platelet counts in aHIT and why it may sometimes fail to produce an immediate response.

**Mechanism of benefit**

High‑dose IVIG (≈ 1 g kg⁻¹ over 3 days) delivers a saturating pool of normal IgG.  
In autoimmune HIT (aHIT) pathogenic IgG antibodies bind PF4–heparin (or PF4 alone) and cross‑link FcγRIIa on platelets and monocytes, driving platelet activation, aggregation, and Fc‑mediated clearance.  
IVIG competes for FcγRIIa, thereby:

1. **Blocking receptor engagement** – the excess IgG on the IVIG molecule occupies FcγRIIa and prevents the pathogenic immune complexes from inducing platelet activation and destruction.  
2. **Neutralizing the antibodies** – anti‑idiotypic IgG in the IVIG preparations can bind the Fab portion of the HIT antibodies, reducing their functional activity.  
3. **Complement inhibition** – high‑dose IVIG inhibits complement activation that can contribute to platelet opsonization and clearance.  

The net effect is a rapid reduction in the rate of platelet consumption and a rebound in circulating platelet numbers.

**Why the response can be delayed**

- **High antibody burden and affinity** – In severe aHIT the serum contains very high titres of high‑affinity IgG. Saturating FcγRIIa may require prolonged IVIG infusion, delaying full blockade. The patient’s serotonin‑release assay showed partial inhibition by the Fc‑blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3), indicating that the antibodies possess a degree of Fc‑receptor resistance.
- **Ongoing hypercoagulability** – Until adequate anticoagulation (argatroban → fondaparinux) is achieved, continued platelet activation and consumption can blunt the short‑term rise in counts, as seen in this patient.
- **Delayed marrow recovery** – Severe thrombocytopenia induces transient marrow suppression; the platelet pool must be replenished, which naturally takes several days.
- **Heparin‑independent activation** – The patient’s antibodies were active in the absence of heparin; IVIG blocks FcγRIIa but does not remove the PF4‑targeting capacity of the antibodies, so residual activation can persist until the antibodies are cleared.

In this case, the patient required 3 days of 100 g IVIG before a modest rise (13 × 10⁹ → 54 × 10⁹ L⁻¹), and only after additional anticoagulant adjustment did the platelet count recover fully. Thus, while IVIG is a powerful adjunct in aHIT, its benefit may not appear immediately, especially in patients with intense antibody responses or partial Fc‑resistance.

### [Management] During treatment with argatroban, the patient’s fibrinogen fell, d‑dimer rose, and schistocytes appeared on the peripheral smear. What do these laboratory findings signify about the hypercoagulable state associated with aHIT, and how should they guide therapy adjustment?

**Key laboratory clues**

| Lab change | What it tells us about aHIT | Clinical implication for anticoagulation |
|------------|-----------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **↓ fibrinogen** | Ongoing fibrin formation and consumption – a hallmark of uncontrolled thrombin generation. | Argatroban is a direct thrombin inhibitor, but the persistent consumption suggests that the therapeutic dose is not fully suppressing clotting. |
| **↑ d‑dimer** | Accelerated fibrinolysis secondary to extensive intravascular fibrin clot formation. | Rising d‑dimer identifies persistent thrombosis; it warns that the patient remains in a hypercoagulable state despite anticoagulation. |
| **Schistocytes** | Microangiopathic hemolysis from platelet‑rich microthrombi in post‑capillary venules—classic for HIT‑associated consumptive coagulopathy. | Presence of schistocytes indicates that the pathological platelet activation is still active and causing mechanical destruction of red cells. |

Together these findings reveal a sustained, heparin‑independent platelet‑activating antibody‑driven coagulopathy – the “hypercoagulable state” of autoimmune HIT. Even with a well‑monitored argatroban infusion, the patient remained thrombotically active.

---

### How to translate this into therapy

1. **Confirm that the defect is not a dosing issue.**  
   – Argatroban dosing is titrated to a PTT of 55–85 s. In this patient the trend of falling fibrinogen and rising d‑dimer was noted **within the therapeutic range**, indicating true pharmacologic failure rather than under‑dosing.

2. **Switch to an agent with more reliable suppression of the “trombus factory.”**  
   – In the case, the team switched to **fondaparinux 10 mg qd**.  
   – Fondaparinux is an indirect antithrombin‑dependent factor Xa inhibitor; in aHIT it has reliably reduced thrombin generation and, in this case, stabilized fibrinogen and d‑dimer levels.

3. **Correct antithrombin deficiency when using fondaparinux.**  
   – The patient had a modest AT level (0.70 U/mL); a single dose of 1000 U AT concentrate was given before starting fondaparinux, ensuring full drug efficacy.

4. **Continue close laboratory monitoring.**  
   – Repeat fibrinogen, d‑dimer, and peripheral smear after the switch. Resolution of these abnormalities supports adequate anticoagulation.  
   – If the trend persists (continued fibrinogen fall or rising d‑dimer), consider escalating to a newer non‑heparin direct factor Xa inhibitor (e.g., rivaroxaban or apixaban) once platelet counts are stable.

---

**Bottom line:**  
A falling fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer, and schistocytes during argatroban therapy signify a persistent, heparin‑independent hypercoagulable state typical of aHIT. They warrant an early switch to a more effective anticoagulant (fondaparinux in this case) and optimization of antithrombin activity, with vigilant laboratory monitoring to confirm restoration of a balanced coagulopathy.

### [Management] Upon platelet recovery, anticoagulation was switched to fondaparinux and later oral apixaban was restarted. What factors should guide the timing of resuming oral anticoagulation in a post‑HIT patient, particularly with a recent ischemic stroke, and why is antithrombin supplementation recommended before fondaparinux?

**Timing of oral anticoagulation after HIT with a recent ischemic stroke**

| Factor | Why it matters | How it was applied in this case |
|--------|----------------|---------------------------------|
| **Platelet count recovery** | In HIT the risk of recurrent thrombosis is highest until the platelet count has stabilized.  A plateau at ≥ 150 × 10⁹ /L is considered safe for transition. | The patient’s platelets climbed steadily to 155 × 10⁹ /L on POD 62; the thrombotic event (stroke) had been clinically stabilized by that time. |
| **Resolution of the acute hypercoagulable state** | Persistently elevated D‑dimer or falling fibrinogen suggests ongoing consumptive coagulopathy.  Anticoagulation should be withheld until these parameters normalize. | D‑dimer fell to near‑normal values and fibrinogen rose by POD 17–62, indicating resolution of HIT‑associated coagulopathy. |
| **Neurological stability / imaging** | Re‑initiation of anticoagulation in the setting of an ischemic stroke carries a bleeding‑transform risk.  Repeat CT/MRI should show no hemorrhagic conversion before the drug is resumed. | Repeat neuro‑imaging was not required because the infarct size was small (3.2 cm × 1.8 cm) and the patient had rapid neurological recovery, but the 3‑week bridging with fondaparinux allowed time for ischemic tissue to scar. |
| **Absence of active bleeding** | Overt bleeding or a high bleeding risk contraindicates any anticoagulant. | No bleeding complications were noted during the fondaparinux bridge. |
| **Bridging with a non‑heparin agent** | HIT mandates withdrawal of all heparin‑derived products.  Fondaparinux, a subcutaneous factor‑Xa inhibitor, provides a predictable anticoagulant that can be stopped quickly if hemorrhage develops. | Fondaparinux was given from POD 14 to POD 65, providing full coverage while platelet counts and coagulopathy improved. |
| **Patient‑specific thrombotic risk** | A history of atrial fibrillation, aortic valve replacement, and a recent cerebral thrombosis all increase thrombotic risk, favoring early resumption once hemostasis can be assured. | The patient had been on apixaban pre‑op; resuming it prevented recurrence of cardio‑embolic thrombus once the HIT‑related risk had been neutralized. |

**Key guideline point** – **Restart oral anticoagulation** only after (i) stable platelets ≥ 150 × 10⁹ /L, (ii) resolution of any acute stroke (evidence of no hemorrhagic conversion), (iii) no ongoing hypercoagulability, and (iv) completion of an adequate non‑heparin bridge.

---

**Why antithrombin (AT) supplementation is required before starting fondaparinux**

1. **Fondaparinux is an indirect antithrombin‑dependent Xa inhibitor** – its anticoagulant activity is achieved by binding to AT, which then blocks factor Xa.  
2. **Sub‑normal AT levels blunt fondaparinux’s effect** – current guidelines (ASH, ISTH) recommend AT supplementation when the plasma AT activity is < 70 % of normal (≤ 0.7 U/mL).  
3. **Failure to supplement risks sub‑therapeutic anticoagulation** – in a patient already prone to thrombosis (post‑HIT), inadequate inhibition of Xa could precipitate recurrent thrombus, including the existing cerebral infarct.  
4. **In this case** the pre‑infusion AT level was 0.70 U/mL (the lower limit of normal).  A 1 000 U AT concentrate was given to bring AT into the therapeutic range, ensuring that the fondaparinux dose of 10 mg QD achieved the expected peak anti‑Xa activity.

---

**Bottom line** – In a post‑HIT patient with recent ischemic stroke, oral anticoagulation (apixaban or another DOAC) should be deferred until platelets have recovered, the acute thrombosis is clinically and radiographically stable, and any hypercoagulable state has resolved.  A non‑heparin bridge with fondaparinux, given with antithrombin supplementation when AT is low, provides safe interim protection.  Once those milestones are achieved, as in this patient (platelets 155 × 10⁹ /L, no hemorrhagic change, D‑dimer normal), the DOAC can be restarted safely.
