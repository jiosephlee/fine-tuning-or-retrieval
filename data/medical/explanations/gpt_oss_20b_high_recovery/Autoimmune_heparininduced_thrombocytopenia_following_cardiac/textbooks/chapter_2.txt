Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 2: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Workup

Aman, a 74‑year‑old man, presented with a long history of exertional dyspnea and chronic atrial fibrillation that had been managed with oral apixaban (5 mg twice daily). The patient had been discovered to have a new systolic murmur and underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterization six days before his scheduled operation. Because the apixaban had not been withheld, the interventionalist used a low dose of unfractionated Heparin (UFH) during the procedure (2000 U). The catheterization revealed a 60 % stenosis of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and a dilated ascending aorta. An operative plan was made for aortic valve replacement, ascending aortic replacement, coronary artery bypass, and left atrial appendage excision.

### Intra‑operative course and anticoagulation

The operation was performed on cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) with total systemic anticoagulation achieved using 45,000 U of UFH. The patient emerged from CPB without notable blood loss and without the need for transfusions. As is typical for patients undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB, postoperative thromboprophylaxis was initiated on postoperative day 1 (POD 1). Low‑molecular‑weight heparin (LMWH; 5 000 U dalteparin s.c.) was used on POD 1, and the dose was increased to 7 500 U on PODs 2–4. No intravenous anticoagulant was given on POD 5, when prophylactic‑dose fondaparinux (2.5 mg s.c.) was started.

### Post‑operative platelet dynamics

Initial postoperative counts were relatively reassuring: bed‑side platelet count on POD 1 was 155 × 10⁹ L⁻¹, down from the pre‑operative level of 210 × 10⁹ L⁻¹. In contrast, the platelet count declined steadily thereafter: 50 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ by the morning of POD 5, 26 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ at the time of a repeat morning count on POD 6, and 13 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ on POD 7. The nadir of 13 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ is markedly below the threshold that is typically considered thrombocytopenia of clinical importance after major surgery.

### Neurologic deterioration and cerebral infarct

The patient developed worsening postoperative delirium noted on POD 5 and worsened further on POD 6, accompanied by a sudden decline in platelet count to 26 × 10⁹ L⁻¹. Though no focal neurologic deficits were initially appreciated, the clinical suspicion of hemorrhagic or ischemic cerebral insult prompted diagnostic imaging. A non‑contrast computed tomography (CT) scan of the brain demonstrated an acute left parietal infarct measuring approximately 3.2 × 1.8 cm, consistent with a thrombotic stroke.

The laboratory data obtained on POD 5—when the platelet count was 50 × 10⁹ L⁻¹—were:

Additional studies—blood and urine cultures—returned negative, and chest radiography showed no acute pulmonary pathology. Retrospectively examining the peripheral smear (POD 7) revealed isolated thrombocytopenia with no platelet clumping, erythrocyte fragments (schistocytes), or abnormal white‑cell morphology.

The elevated fibrinogen, while an acute‑phase reactant, was consistent with the expected postoperative inflammatory response. Haptoglobin was elevated, arguing against active hemolysis. All coagulation parameters (PT, aPTT) were normal, and there was no evidence of consumptive coagulopathy or microangiopathic hemolysis in the initial smear.

### Exclusion of alternative causes of early postoperative thrombocytopenia

The pattern of decline and the absence of contributory findings steer the clinician toward a non‑hematologic, consumptive process rather than a supply‑side or mechanical cause:

1. **Bleeding or surgical loss** – No documented intra‑operative or post‑operative hemorrhage was noted. Hemoglobin remained stable (97 g L⁻¹) through the first five days, making ongoing blood loss unlikely. The absence of oropharyngeal or chest drainage and normal coagulation studies also argue against significant bleeding.

2. **Dilutional thrombocytopenia from CPB** – CPB circuits can dilute platelets, but this effect typically resolves within the first 24‑48 h. The persistent and progressive decline beyond POD 2 suggests a different mechanism.

3. **Drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia** – The only peri‑operative agents administered were the pre‑procedural UFH (2000 U), intra‑operative UFH (45,000 U), and post‑operative LMWH (dalteparin). No new drugs were introduced during the time course of the platelet fall. Moreover, the temporal profile of the decline aligns with known patterns for heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia rather than a classic drug‐induced immune response, which typically peaks 5–10 days after exposure.

4. **Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) or thrombotic microangiopathy (TTP)** – The coagulation panel was essentially normal; fibrinogen remained high, d‑dimer values were not strikingly elevated at this time. The peripheral smear displayed no schistocytes until day 9. Haptoglobin was not decreased, and bilirubin levels were normal—findings that are inconsistent with microangiopathic hemolysis.

5. **Infection‑related thrombocytopenia** – Blood and urine cultures were negative, and the leukocyte count remained within the normal range without an acute inflammatory response. Furthermore, the platelet decline preceded any clinical or laboratory evidence of infection.

6. **Post‑surgical thrombocytopenia due to transfusion reactions** – No blood transfusions were administered during this period; thus, transfusion‑related immune reactions are excluded.

Given the above, the remaining and most plausible explanation at this point is heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), most strongly suggested by the severe thrombocytopenia occurring in the context of recent heparin exposure (both intra‑operative CPB UFH and pre‑operative low‑dose UFH). The subsequent confirmation with a PF4/polyanion enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and the serotonin‑release assay (SRA) fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for HIT, while the early onset (before POD 5) underscored the influence of prior low‑dose pre‑operative heparin exposure. This initial presentation and workup set the stage for the nuanced distinction between classic HIT and the autoimmune subset that will be discussed later in the report.
