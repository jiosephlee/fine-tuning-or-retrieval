Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# Argatroban, Fondaparinux, IVIG – Choosing the Right Anticoagulant in a Patient with Severe aHIT and Stroke

**Case snapshot**  
An octogenarian settles off an aortic-valve‑and‑ascending‑aorta replacement with a post‑op platelet count of 155 × 10⁹/L. Five days later he’s delirious, his platelets have plummeted to 50 × 10⁹/L, and CT scans reveal an acute left‑parietal infarct. A PF4/Heparin ELISA is strongly positive, but a serotonin‑release assay (SRA) shows both classic HIT‑pattern activation *and* strong, heparin‑independent serotonin release that is only partially blocked by an Fc‑receptor‑blocking antibody. The picture is that of **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** – a severe, early‑onset, heparin‑independent form of HIT that’s notoriously difficult to clear.

#### Why is this case interesting?
- **Early‑onset**: HIT usually manifests 5–10 days after exposure, but pre‑operative percutaneous coronary angiography with 2 000 U UFH explains the earlier onset in this surgical patient.  
- **Atypical serology**: Strong serotonin release even at *zero* heparin, with only partial inhibition by IV.3, a rare profile that may lead to a missed diagnosis if we’re only looking for textbook SRA results.  
- **Severe thrombocytopenia and consumptive coagulopathy**: Platelet nadir 13 × 10⁹/L, fibrinogen decline, d‑dimer rise, and schistocytes all testify to a pro‑thrombotic, immune‑mediated process rather than a bleeding diathesis.

---

## 1. Anticoagulation in the Midst of a Stroke + aHIT

| Anticoagulant | Mechanism | Advantages in ICU | Caveats | When to Use |
|---------------|-----------|------------------|---------|-------------|
| **Argatroban** | Direct thrombin inhibitor | Short half‑life (~35 min), no AT dependency, PTT‑guided dosing allows rapid titration | Requires IV infusion, not ideal for prolonged outpatient use, binds to plasma proteins | Early ICU period, when platelet counts <50 × 10⁹/L or fibrinogen low and we need tight control of acute hypercoagulability |
| **Fondaparinux** | Indirect AT‑dependent factor Xa inhibitor, SC | Once‑daily SC, no routine monitoring, lower bleeding risk once platelets recuperate | Dependent on AT (levels often low after cardiac surgery), off‑label use in HIT | Stabilized patient with rising platelets (>50 × 10⁹/L), no signs of ongoing hypercoagulability |

### Why Argatroban First?

1. **Immediate control** – With platelets below 15 × 10⁹/L and a recent cerebro‑vascular event, we need to shut down the platelet‑activating autoantibodies on a very short time‑scale.
2. **No AT requirement** – Post‑CPB patients often have transient low antithrombin; argatroban bypasses this.
3. **Titration Safety** – PTT monitoring allows us to keep the anticoagulation in a narrow therapeutic window, important when a patient is at risk of bleeding after a stroke.

### Why and When We Switch to Fondaparinux

- **Platelets recover** → We see a 54 × 10⁹/L count, disappearance of schistocytes, fibrinogen stabilizes, and d‑dimer falls. The hypercoagulability has less urgency, and outpatient safety becomes paramount.
- **Logistics** – Once daily sub‑cutaneous dosing is far easier in the ICU and for the transition to step‑down units than carrying an infusion pump.
- **Drug‑availability** – Fondaparinux is cheaper, easier to administer, and we have experience using it off‑label for HIT in our tertiary center.

Before starting, we give a 1 000‑U antithrombin bolus to correct for the falsely low AT levels seen in cardiac bypass patients.

---

## 2. High‑Dose IVIG: A Stop‑Gap Against Severe Thrombocytopenia?

**What does IVIG do?**  
- Saturates FcγRIIa on platelets, blocking HIT antibody‑mediated platelet aggregation.  
- By flooding the system with normal IgG, it outcompetes the autoantibody for Fc receptor binding and dampens inflammatory signaling.

**Evidence in aHIT**  
- Small case series and registry data show platelet count increases in 60–80 % of patients when high‑dose (≈1 g/kg) IVIG is combined with a non‑heparin anticoagulant.  
- Response varies widely; severe aHIT (nadir <30 × 10⁹/L) may be less responsive.

**Why we chose 100 g ×3 days**  
- To avoid fluid overload in a 70‑year‑old with recent surgery.  
- It has been shown in practice to achieve a rapid rise in platelet counts in many aHIT cases; when it fails, we can still continue anticoagulation safely.

**Clinical reality**  
In our patient, the platelet count rose from 13 × 10⁹/L to 54 × 10⁹/L over a week – modest compared with previous reports (median rise >200 × 10⁹/L). This might reflect the unusually intense antibody activity evidenced by partial Fc‑blocking resistance.

---

## 3. Monitoring the Balance: What to Watch for Each Day

| Parameter | Target (or trend) | Why |
|-----------|-------------------|-----|
| **Platelet count** | >50 × 10⁹/L before switching to fondaparinux | Reduces hemorrhagic risk; confirms platelet recovery |
| **Fibrinogen** | Stable or rising after 2 – 3 weeks | A drop suggests ongoing consumption, maybe residual aHIT activity |
| **d‑Dimer** | Falling trend | A marker of decreasing fibrin turnover; high levels continue to signal active clot formation |
| **Schistocytes** | Absent | Indicates resolved microangiopathic injury (thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura‑like) |
| **Anti‑Xyl** | No auto‑anti‑Xa testing needed | Fondaparinux slowly decreases AT binding; routine anti‑Xa levels are not recommended in our setting |

---

## 4. Practical Decision-Making Checkpoints

1. **Confirm aHIT** – PF4 ELISA strongly positive + SRA with heparin‑independent activity.  
2. **Rule out other causes** – No transfusion, no sepsis, no DIC signatures other than those associated with HIT.  
3. **Start argatroban** – dose titrate to PTT 55–85 s; adjust as needed.  
4. **Add IVIG** – if severe thrombocytopenia (nadir <30 × 10⁹/L) or poor platelet response to argatroban alone.  
5. **Re‑check labs (Platelet, Fibrinogen, d‑Dimer, Schistocytes) after 3–5 days** – assess trend.  
6. **If platelets >50 × 10⁹/L, stable fibrinogen, reduced d‑Dm** → Switch to fondaparinux after AT bolus.  
7. **Transition to DOAC/Oral anticoagulation** – safe 3–4 weeks after HIT symptom resolution, as seen in the patient’s 5‑month recovery phase.

---

## 5. Key Take‑Home Messages

- **Early-onset HIT in the post‑cardiac‑surgery patient can stem from preoperative UFH exposure**; always factor in all heparin exposures, not just those on bypass.  
- **Autoimmune HIT is harder to fight** – it can activate platelets without heparin and sometimes shows partial resistance to Fc‑blocking, leading to a dramatic “indeterminate” SRA result.  
- **Argatroban is the anticoagulant of choice in the ICU for acute aHIT** because it’s short‑acting, doesn’t need antithrombin, and is easily titratable.  
- **Switch to fondaparinux when the patient shows platelet recovery and decreasing markers of hypercoagulability**, furnishing a simpler outpatient regimen with lower bleeding risk.  
- **High‑dose IVIG can help restore platelet counts, but its effect depends on the severity of aHIT** and may be modest if the antibody is unusually potent.  

By appreciating the nuance of aHIT’s serology and clinical presentation, we tailor anticoagulation from an IV infusion that can be stopped at lightning speed to a once‑daily sub‑cutaneous dose that keeps the patient safely out of the hospital. In the end, the patient returned to baseline neurological function with platelets that recovered and an antithrombotic regimen that was both effective and practical.
