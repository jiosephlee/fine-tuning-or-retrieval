Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] What histologic finding most reliably distinguishes immune-mediated necrotising myopathy from polymyositis or dermatomyositis?

**Answer**

The hallmark that separates immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from polymyositis or dermatomyositis on muscle biopsy is **extensive myofiber necrosis with accompanying regeneration yet a severe paucity of interstitial lymphocytes**—in particular, minimal endomysial/perimy‑seal CD8⁺ T‑cell infiltration.

In our patient’s biopsy (Figure 3 A–B) we saw diffuse clusters of necrotic fibers surrounded by macrophages (acid phosphatase/CD68+) and newly formed regenerating fibers, but only sparse CD3⁺ T-cell “hot spots” (Figure 4 A). This pattern contrasts sharply with:

| Condition | Typical histology |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Polymyositis** | Endomysial invasion by sheet‑forming CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells, markedly active inflammation |
| **Dermatomyositis** | Perifascicular atrophy, perivascular capillary dropout/driven complement deposition; abundant B‑cell/T‑cell clusters |
| **IMNM** | Predominant fibre necrosis + regeneration; few or no interstitial lymphocytes; macrophage phagocytosis prominent |

Thus, a biopsy showing widespread fibre necrosis/regeneration **without substantial inflammatory infiltrate** is the most reliable morphologic discriminator for IMNM relative to polymyositis or dermatomyositis.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which laboratory result most effectively indicates that an elevated creatine kinase reflects muscular injury rather than liver damage?

**Key laboratory cue**  
A **markedly elevated serum creatine kinase (CK)**—often several thousand units per liter—coupled with **only mild or no elevation of the transaminases** (AST/ALT) signals skeletal‑muscle injury rather than hepatocellular damage.

**Why this works**  
- **CK** is essentially restricted to striated muscle and rises dramatically (> 10–15 × upper limit normal) when myocytes are damaged.  
- The **transaminases** are present in both liver and skeletal‑muscle tissue, but in a hepatic insult they rise proportionally more (often ≥ 5–10 × ULN), whereas in isolated myopathy they are usually only modestly increased or stay near normal.
- In our patient the CK peaked at **4 000 U/L** while **AST was 191 U/L and ALT 165 U/L**—only ~twofold over upper limits—an excellent example of a “CK‑dominant” pattern characteristic of immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy.

Thus, when faced with hyper‑CKemia, evaluate its ratio to serum transaminases; a large disconnect (high CK with mildly raised/aspectively unchanged AST/ALT) most reliably points to muscular origin rather than liver damage.

### [Management] In patients who develop proximal limb-girdle weakness several weeks after initiating a GLP-1 receptor agonist yet lack detectable anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP antibodies how should empiric treatment typically be started?

**Empiric management of seronegative immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) triggered by a GLP‑1 receptor agonist**

When a patient presents with progressive proximal limb‑girdle weakness that emerges several weeks after starting a GLP‑1 RA and tests negative for anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP antibodies—as in the semaglutide case reported by Lam et al.—treat as drug‑induced IMNM with high‑dose corticosteroids and consider early addition of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG).

1. **Discontinue the GLP‑1 RA** immediately upon suspicion of myopathy.  
2. **Initiate high‑dose glucocorticoids**:
   * **Intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g once daily** for 3–5 days (pulse therapy), *or*  
   * **Oral prednisone at ≥ 1 mg/kg/day** if IV access is not feasible.  
3. **Add IVIG if**  
   * Creatine kinase remains > 5–10× upper limit of normal, **or**  
   * Dysphagia/dysphonia or respiratory compromise are present—clinical hallmarks of severe IMNM—as occurred in the Lam et al case where IVIG (2 g/kg over four days) was required following pulse steroids.  
4. **Taper steroids over several weeks**, monitoring CK and strength weekly until normalization; maintain low dose prednisolone (> 30 mg/day) during transition off therapy if rapid improvement is seen, mirroring the successful tapering described in the report.

This algorithm—pulse steroids followed by IVIG in selected severe cases—mirrors the empiric regimen that led to full functional recovery in the referenced patient, demonstrating efficacy even when classic myositis autoantibodies are absent.

### [Morning Report] When proximal weakness evolves roughly four months after a single dose of semaglutide—with serial creatine kinase rising modestly initially then exceeding four thousand IU/L—what does this time course argue favor an autoimmune process rather than an acute toxic injury?

**Answer**

The 4‑month lag between the single semaglutide injection and the dramatic rise in creatine kinase (CK) together with insidious proximal weakness strongly favors an autoimmune necrotising myopathy rather than an acute drug‑toxicity picture.  

1. **Temporal pattern** – Acute toxic myopathies (e.g., statin‑induced rhabdomyolysis or tirzepatide‑associated rhabdomyolysis) typically present with a sudden spike in CK (often >10 000 IU/L) within hours or days of exposure, and CK falls rapidly once the offending agent is removed. In this case, CK was only mildly elevated (337 U/L) at the time of first semaglutide dose, and climbed slowly over 4 months to >4 000 U/L—consistent with a gradual immune response rather than immediate cell lysis.

2. **Clinical course** – The patient developed progressive proximal weakness and dysphagia over several weeks, not an abrupt loss of strength typical of toxin‑induced myonecrosis. The delayed nature of symptom onset mirrors that seen in drug‑triggered immune‑mediated necrotising myopathies where an initial trigger precipitates autoreactive T‑cell/B‑cell activation that takes days to weeks to produce muscle destruction.

3. **Biopsy findings** – Muscle histology revealed necrotic/regenerating fibres with macrophage infiltrates and rare T‑cells—a classic picture of IMNM rather than pure toxic myonecrosis, which would show diffuse fibre necrosis without organized inflammatory infiltrates.

4. **Response to immunosuppression** – Rapid clinical and biochemical recovery after high‑dose steroids plus intravenous immunoglobulin is characteristic of an immune mediated process; toxic injuries generally improve simply by stopping the drug or providing supportive care without such intensive therapy.

5. **Contrast with reported tirzepatide rhabdomyolysis** – In that case, CK spiked immediately to >57 000 U/L and normalized within days of withdrawal, highlighting that classic drug toxicity has a different cadence than the chronic rise seen here.

Thus, the protracted progression from modest CK elevation to severe disease over months argues for an autoimmune mechanism—an immune response triggered (but not solely caused) by semaglutide—rather than a straightforward acute toxic injury.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the cellular cascade—from antibody binding through complement activation—to precipitate macrophage-mediated ‘myofiber phagocytosis’ characteristic of seronegative immune mediated necrotising myopathy.

**Cell‑level cascade underlying macrophage‑mediated myofiber phagocytosis in seronegative IMNM**

1. **Muscle‑cell exposure of cryptic neo‑epitopes** – In the setting of semaglutide exposure (or another inciting trigger) muscle sarcolemmal proteins become aberrantly expressed or modified so that previously hidden epitopes are exposed or are presented in a pro‑inflammatory context (e.g., up‑regulated MHC class I).

2. **Binding of pathogenic IgG** – Autoantibodies—undetectable by routine panels (the patient was anti‑HMGCR‑negative and anti‑SRP‐negative)—recognise these altered epitopes and bind their Fc regions on damaged fibers. The intense local IgG deposition provides a scaffold for downstream effector pathways even if circulating levels fall below assay sensitivity.

3. **Classical complement activation** – IgG–Fc binds C1q → recruitment/activation of C1r/C1s → cleavage of C4 and C2 → formation of the classical convertase (C4b2a). This complex cleaves circulating C3 into C3a + C3b; surface‐adsorbed C3b attaches back onto myofibers giving an opsonic “coat” that is then amplified by alternative pathway components.

4. **Complement amplification & MAC generation** – Further cleavage yields additional convertases (C4b2a → C4b2a2b). This generates terminal factors that assemble into the membrane attack complex (C5b‐9), which deposits along damaged sarcolemmal membranes—explaining the positive MAC staining seen in this biopsy.

5. **Inflammatory mediator release** – Anaphylatoxins C3a/C5a recruit and activate innate cells, particularly MAC‑positive neutrophils and resident monocytes migrating into muscle interstitium via chemokine gradients such as MCP‑1/CCL2.

6. **Macrophage recruitment and activation** – Recruited CD68+ monocyte‐derived macrophages sense opsonized tissue fragments via CR3/CD11b×CD18 binding to iC3b/C3bi, while Fcγ receptors engage immune complexes directly released from injured fibers.

7. **Myophagocytosis** – Activated macrophages engulf entire necrotic or partially intact fibers (“myophagocytosis”), a phenomenon highlighted by acid phosphatase positivity surrounding affected units on histology—a hallmark signature identified in the patient’s muscle core section with concentric lysosomal activity around torn fibers.

8. **Secondary inflammatory amplification** – Phagocytosed material further stimulates interferon pathways within macrophages, perpetuating local tissue injury despite absence of visible lymphocytic infiltrates—a pattern that distinguishes immuno‑mediated necrotising myopathy from polymyositis or inclusion body myositis.

In summary, although routine serum autoantibody assays can be negative in seronegative IMNM—as occurred here—the underlying mechanism involves IgG-directed classical complement initiation culminating in MAC deposition, which both directly injures fibers and flags them for clearance by phagocytic macrophages—an effector loop perpetuating necrosis and regeneration observed clinically throughout this case.’

### [Pathophysiology] Why did pro-B-type natriuretic peptide rise markedly while left ventricular ejection fraction remained preserved during this illness episode?

**Why NT‑proBNP was markedly elevated while LVEF stayed normal**

In this patient the marked increase in NT‑proBNP (4 358 pg/mL vs. a reference ≤125 pg/mL) occurred in the setting of a brief episode of immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) precipitated by semaglutide use. **The key driver is subclinical myocardial inflammation** rather than frank systolic failure:

| Feature | Explanation |
|---------|-------------|
| **Cardiac imaging** | Echo demonstrated a preserved ejection fraction (65–70 %). Cardiac MRI showed mild hypokinesis of the mid‑anteroseptal/anterior wall with diffuse myocardial oedema but no late gadolinium enhancement—findings characteristic of *indeterminate* or *early* myocarditis / stress‑cardiomyopathy. |
| **Wall stress & stretch** | Even mild regional edema increases myocyte extracellular fluid volume and mechanical strain on ventricular walls. This local stretch stimulates atrial/ventricular myocytes to release natriuretic peptides independently of a drop in global LV systole. |
| **Neurohumoral activation** | The systemic inflammatory state—and transient tachyarrhythmia (paroxysmal atrial fibrillation) –augments sympathetic tone, raising preload and LV end‑diastolic pressure without immediate loss of contractility.|
| **Renal clearance** | The patient’s creatinine never rose above baseline thereafter, so decreased clearance does **not** account for the disproportionate NT‑proBNP rise (which would be only modest). |

Thus, *acute inflammatory edema* led to elevated intramyocardial pressure → heightened secretion of NT‑proBNP while overall pump function remained adequate until subsequent recovery. When the myocardium healed under immunosuppression, repeat imaging showed normalization of wall motion and resolution of BNP elevation, confirming reversible inflammatory involvement rather than permanent ventricular dysfunction.

### [Management] After symptom resolution treated empirically with steroids plus IVIG and permanent discontinuation of semaglutide is routine maintenance immunosuppression justified,and why does durable improvement often occur rapidly?

**Clinical Pearls – Management of Drug‑Induced Immune‑Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM)**  
*(Inspired by “Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment…”) *

**Key Question:**  
*After symptom resolution with corticosteroids + IVIG and permanent discontinuation of semaglutide, is routine maintenance immunosuppression justified? Why does durable improvement often occur so rapidly?*

---

### 1. Do we need routine maintenance therapy?

**Short‑answer:** **No – unless there is persistent disease activity (elevated CK, ongoing weakness).**

**Why?**

| Feature | Drug‑induced / seronegative IMNM | Idiopathic / HMGCR‑positive IMNM |
|---------|----------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Trigger** | Single exposure (semaglutide) | Chronic exposure (statins) or spontaneous |
| **Autoantibody profile** | Negative (SRP/HMGCR absent) | Positive |
| **Course after withdrawal + acute therapy** | Rapid remission (CK normalizes within weeks); no relapse for ≥8 months in the case report | Often requires prolonged immunotherapy (>6–12 months); relapse common |
| **Guideline stance** | UpToDate & EFNS reserve maintenance for ongoing activity | Recommend tapering steroids over 3–6 months + a steroid‑sparing agent if CK >2× ULN or persistent weakness |

In our patient:
- CK fell from >4000 U/L to <200 U/L within 2 weeks.
- Strength returned to baseline.
- She remained symptom‑free >8 months off prednisone/IVIG.
- Serial laboratory monitoring showed normal CK at all outpatient visits up to 10 months post‑discharge.

Thus continuing steroids or IVIG would expose her to unnecessary adverse events without clinical benefit. Routine monitoring (CK every 3–4 weeks during first 6 months, then every 3–6 months if stable) suffices.

---

### 2. Why does durable improvement often proceed rapidly?

1. **Removal of the inciting antigen eliminates driving force**
   - Semaglutide provides a unique structural trigger that likely induced transient epitope presentation on muscle fibers.
   - Once the drug was permanently stopped, antigenic stimulus disappeared → T/B‑cell activation waned.

2. **Complement‑mediated necrosis predominates**
   - Biopsy demonstrated MAC deposition but rare CD3⁺ T cells → minimal chronic inflammatory milieu.
   - Myofiber regeneration proceeds swiftly once necrotic driving factor is removed.

3. **Seronegativity indicates a “short‑lived” immune response**
   - Absence of HMGCR/SRP antibodies suggests the immune attack was not sustained by an autoantibody pathway.
   - In contrast, antibody‑mediated forms tend to persist even after trigger removal necessitating long‑term therapy.

4. **Effective acute suppression**
   - High‐dose methylprednisolone pulse + IVIG interrupts complement activation and stabilizes membranes.
   - Prompt decrease in circulating inflammatory mediators accelerates recovery trajectory seen in corticosteroid‐responsive myopathies such as polymyositis/dermatomyositis flare‐ups initiated by drug reaction.

---

### Practical Takeaway

- **Do not start routine long‑term immunosuppression** for patients who have fully recovered after acute steroid/IVIG treatment following single drug exposure (e.g., semaglutide).
- Monitor CK and strength serially; taper prednisone over ~4–6 weeks exclusively if repeat imaging/labs still abnormal.
- Rapid durability reflects two facts:
  1) The offending agent’s removal cuts off antigenic drive;
  2) The pathogenic mechanism (complement‐mediated necrosis without sustained autoantibodies) allows quick muscle regeneration once inflammation subsides.

> *“The resolution pattern observed here mirrors classic drug‑induced myopathies—activity dissipates almost entirely once the culprit compound no longer circulates.”*
