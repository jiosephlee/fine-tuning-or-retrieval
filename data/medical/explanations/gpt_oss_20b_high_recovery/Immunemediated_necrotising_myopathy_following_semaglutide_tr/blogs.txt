Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"





# Rhabdo or Autoimmunity? Decoding CK Dynamics in Drug‑Induced Myopathies

### *Hello fellow clinicians!*

The first time I read about this 60‑year‑old woman who got one shot of semaglutide and then eight months later found herself paralysed and on a taper of steroids, I had half a brain‑wave of deja vu. We’ve written thousands of posts about "drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis" in the classic sense—an acute surge in CK to >10 000 U/L, myoglobinuria, the urgency of IV fluids and the relief of a declining CK curve. We’ve also studied “immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy” (IMNM) as a rare, chronic autoimmune process that usually unspools over weeks, may be HMGCR‑positive, and requires immunosuppression. 

What’s astonishing here is that a single dose of a GLP‑1 receptor agonist triggered a TIME‑dependent, progressive immune response that masqueraded as disruptive myopathy. The CK trajectory, imaging, EMG, and biopsy paint a picture that begs a deeper look at how we interpret CK dynamics to pull apart *acute toxin* from *autoimmune*.

---

## The Unlikely Story of a Semaglutide‑Induced Myopathy

Picture this: a woman with obesity and fatty liver—she’s basically a patient we’d put on a GLP‑1 agonist for weight loss and glycaemic control. She gets the first injection. Hours later she’s nauseated, vomiting, and ends up in the ER with an elevated creatinine kinase (CK) of 337 U/L (only a bit above the upper limit). She recovers, is discharged, and then, three months later, she’s unable to stand, can’t swallow anything, and her CK rockets to 3 992 U/L. The narrative that unfolds is fascinating: there are no statins, no illicit drugs, no connective‑tissue disease jumping up her history—all clues steered the team toward infection, malignant myopathy, or even a drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis. The muscle biopsy, intriguingly, shows necrotic fibers, macrophage infiltration, a membrane attack complex, but only a handful of T‑cells. In short: classic immunopathology of IMNM, yet the patient has a *seronegative* autoimmune autoantibody panel.

The grave takeaway? A single exposure to semaglutide can be the spark that triggers an on‑going autoimmune assault—contrary to the textbook notion that drug‑induced myopathies are dose‑dependent or happen with long‑term exposure.

---

## CK Kinetics: The Pulse of the Pathology

### Rhabdomyolysis: The Spiking ECG

- **Rapid, massive rise**: CK climbs within 24 h, often exceeding 10 000 U/L.
- **Acute peak**: CK maximum typically by day 2‑3; linear decline with adequate hydration.
- **Clear systemic toxin**: Myoglobinuria, dark urine, acute kidney injury (AKI). 
- **Therapy**: IV fluids, correction of electrolytes; removal of the offending agent or exhaustion of the toxic trigger. Immunosuppression is rarely needed.

In our patient, the CK began at 337 U/L and climbed steadily over four months—a far cry from the classic rhabdo spike. The lack of classic rhabdo signs (no cola‑colored urine, no overt AKI beyond the initial dehydration episode) underscores that the muscle injury was gradual, not an acute toxic shock.

### IMNM: The Floor‑Elevator

- **Steady climb**: CK steadily rises over weeks to months often reaching 3 000‑7 000 U/L or higher.
- **Persistent elevation**: CK stays elevated as long as the immune assault continues; it can plateau if the immune response is dampened.
- **No myoglobinuria**: Because bigger necrotic areas are disrupted slowly, myoglobin release is less dramatic.
- **Therapy**: High‑dose steroids ± IVIG, sometimes longer regimens if autoantibody‑positive.

The 12‑fold rise from 337 U/L to ~4 000 U/L in the case is textbook for IMNM and not what you’d anticipate in a simple drug‑induced myotoxicity. CK kinetic patterns, when paired with clinical evolution, are the first guideline in suspecting immune‑mediated disease over pure toxin injury.

---

## Imaging and EMG: The Other Giants

**Muscle MRI** can give a powerful visual cue:

- **Symmetric edema** in proximal muscle groups (quadriceps, hamstrings, deltoids).
- **Subcutaneous edema** occasionally seen; indicates an inflammatory process.
- **No fatty replacement** in acute stages (for chronic disease you’d see fatty infiltration).

The MRI of this patient revealed diffuse, symmetrical edema of the vastus lateralis and some surrounding loose‑tissue swelling—typical for an inflammatory myopathy rather than a confined “burn” (as one might see in a toxin‑caused crush injury where focal necrosis is more common).

**Electromyography (EMG)** gives the electrophysiologic footprint:

- **Myopathic motor unit potentials**: small amplitude, short duration, early recruitment.
- **Fibrillation potentials** may be present, but are more frequent in active necrosis.
- **Rhomb**: In rhabdo, EMG changes may be minimal early on, then progress to fibrillations.

Although the EMG results weren’t detailed in the report, the presence of a myopathic pattern would be expected in IMNM, whereas rhabdo EMG could be variable but less specific.

---

## What’s Happening Inside?

Histology remains the gold standard. In this patient, the vastus lateralis biopsy showed:

- **Necrotic and regenerating fibers**.
- **Acid phosphatase** and **CD68** staining—hallmarks of macrophage phagocytosis of dead myofibers.
- **Membrane attack complex** (MAC) on fibers, an antibody‑mediated complement attack.
- **Rare T‑cells** (CD3), reflecting the paucity of mononuclear infiltrates in IMNM.

These findings echo “necrosis” as the central event, but importantly guarded by an immune mechanism. The somatic antibodies (HMGCR, SRP) were missing–an expanse of seronegative IMNM that foreshadows that the classic antibodies are not the whole picture. This also hammers across the notion that absence of an autoantibody should not bar you from suspecting an immune‑mediated process if the clinical context fits.

---

## The Decision Point: Ethics, Evidence, and the Clinical Pathway

1. **Recognise the CK pattern**: A gradual rise over weeks is a red flag.
2. **Couple CK with clinical features**: Proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia.
3. **Audit the drug pathway**: Identify any recent medication changes—GLP‑1 agonists, statins, supplements.
4. **Rule out other mimics**: Rhabdomyolysis, metabolic myopathies, myositis with systemic disease.
5. **Order MRI & EMG**: Distinguish inflammatory vs. toxic patterns.
6. **Consider biopsy** (and autoantibody panel) if diagnosis remains ambiguous.
7. **Treat aggressively if autoimmune**: Steroids, IVIG, tapering early; but don’t shy away from early immunosuppression if the pattern is unmistakable.
8. **Stop the inciting drug**: Withdrawal is critical for resolution; re‑challenge would be unethical in a known serious event.
9. **Monitor CK and strength**: A declining CK curve and improvement in function indicate success.

The case under discussion exemplifies this pathway. Once the rising CK timeline and weakness were appreciated, a swift initiation of high‑dose methylprednisolone, followed by a 2‑g/kg IVIG course, coupled with drug withdrawal, yielded an excellent outcome—functional recovery, normal CK, and no recurrence after eight months off steroids.

---

## Clinical Pearls

- **Slow, persistent CK rise + proximal weakness + dysphagia** strongly suggests IMNM over acute rhabdo.  
- **Single dose of a GLP‑1 agonist** (semaglutide) can provoke an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy; do not treat it as a straightforward drug toxicity.  
- **MRI shows symmetrical proximal edema**; EMG demonstrates a myopathic pattern—together they push you toward an inflammatory etiology.  
- **Seronegative IMNM is common**; a negative autoantibody panel does *not* rule out disease.  
- **Short, aggressive immunosuppression** (steroids + IVIG) can be curative if instituted early, and a drug halt often leads to lasting remission.  

When you see a patient with a creeping CK elevation, “back‑to‑back muscle aches” and a heavy history of metabolic medication use, take the time to let CK breathe and rewrite the story to consider autoimmunity. It can change management from a “give her fluids” script to a “start her steroids and stop the GLP‑1” trajectory, and ultimately save muscle function.



# Seronegative IMNM: How to Diagnose and Treat When Classic Autoantibodies Fail  

## 1. Why this case grabs your attention  

A 62‑year‑old woman with a history of obesity, NAFLD, and hypertension had a bizarre two‑stage disease after a *single* dose of semaglutide. The first episode was a spectacular GI flare with acute kidney injury and a modest CK rise (~330 U/L). Four months later, she presented with profound proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK that exploded to ~4 000 U/L. A muscle biopsy revealed necrotizing myopathy, yet the full‑panel myositis antibodies – HMGCR, SRP, and all the others – were negative. She responded rapidly to steroids and IVIG after semaglutide was held.  

This is the textbook scenario of **seronegative immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)**, plus a possible drug trigger that is not yet on the radar of many internists or endocrinologists.  

## 2. Clinical suspicion – the first “red flag”

- **Proximal muscle weakness** that is rapidly progressive and limits ADLs  
- **Marked CK elevation** (often >5× upper limit; here ≥4 000 U/L)  
- **Absence of extramuscular autoimmune features** (rash, arthralgias, sicca)  
- **Recent exposure to a potential trigger** (statins, biologics, GLP‑1 agonists)  

In our patient, the temporal relationship to semaglutide was striking. The initial GI symptoms were typical side effects, but the 4‑month rise in CK and the development of dysphagia strongly suggested a delayed drug‑induced autoimmune response.

## 3. Laboratory work‑up – what to check and why  

| Test | What it tells you | What the case showed |
|------|------------------|----------------------|
| **CK, AST/ALT, LDH** | Baseline muscle enzyme surge | CK 4 000 U/L; AST/ALT mildly elevated (likely hepatic) |
| **Full metabolic panel** | Rule out metabolic toxins | Normal electrolytes, renal function returned to baseline |
| **Autoimmune panel** (ANA, dsDNA, RF, anti‑Ro/La, anti‑SSB, complements, ESR/CRP) | Identify systemic lupus, RA, Sjögren, other myositis overlaps | ANA negative; isolated low‑titer SSB (+); CRP/ESR mildly up |
| **Myositis autoantibody panel** (HMGCR, SRP, Jo‑1, Mi‑2, ... ) | Detect pathogenic autoantibodies | All negative (seronegative) |
| **Creatinine kinase isoforms** (if available) | Distinguish muscle vs hepatic release | Not performed; CK peak 4 000 U/L strongly muscular |
| **Troponin, BNP** | Exclude myocarditis, cardiac strain | Troponin slightly elevated; BNP 4 358 pg/mL (stress cardiomyopathy) |
| **Hepatic panel with viral serologies** | Rule out viral myositis or hepatitis | Negative for hepatitis A–E, autoantibodies; biopsy confirmed steatosis |

> **Key point:** In seronegative IMNM, the enzyme elevation can be dramatic while autoantibodies remain undetectable. Comprehensive baseline labs are crucial to exclude other mimics.

## 4. Imaging – what you should look for on MRI  

**Muscle MRI** (preferably fat‑suppressed STIR or T2‑weighted) shows:

- **Diffuse or patchy edema** in iliopsoas, quadriceps, hamstrings, and sometimes neck extensors  
- **T2 hyperintensity** without significant fatty infiltration (helps differentiate from chronic myopathy)  
- In our patient: bilateral symmetric edema in proximal quadriceps and subcutaneous thigh tissue.  

Cardiac MRI was valuable to rule out myocarditis (mild myocardial edema, no LGE). Contrast‑enhanced CT was negative for ILD.  

> **Take‑away:** MRI is a non‑invasive “see‑first” tool that can guide biopsy sites and confirm active inflammation before you roll out IVIG.

## 5. Muscle biopsy – the ultimate “gold standard”

The biopsy is the linchpin in seronegative IMNM:

1. **Necrotic and regenerating fibers** with minimal lymphocytic infiltrate  
2. **Macrophage (CD68+) phagocytosis** of necrotic cores (myophagocytosis)  
3. **Complement deposition** (C5b-9/MAC) on viable fibers highlights an immune attack  
4. **Rare T‑cell presence (CD3+)** and perivascular inflammation (trouble‑makers? not the main culprits)  

The case’s vastus lateralis specimen fit this exact constellation. The presence of the MAC and macrophage activity differentiates IMNM from polymyositis, which is heavily T‑cell inflamed.  

> **Pro tip:** If you see MD hng. of "necrotic fibers with minimal inflammation", you already have a strong case for IMNM—even if autoantibodies are negative.

## 6. Differential diagnosis – a quick check‑list  

| Disease | Key distinguishing features | Why it was excluded |
|---------|------------------------------|---------------------|
| **Statin‑induced myopathy** | CK ≥ 10× ULN; usually improves with drug cessation | No statin exposure; CK rose long after semaglutide |
| **Rhabdomyolysis** | Acute CK spike >10 000; myoglobinuria; renal failure | CK was 4 000, no cola urine; renal function recovered with hydration |
| **Polymyositis** | T‑cell infiltrate, myositis antibodies (Jo‑1, Mi‑2) | Biopsy revealed necrosis > infiltration; antibodies negative |
| **Inclusion body myositis** | Young male, distal weakness, rimmed vacuoles | Patient female; proximal weakness; no biopsy vacuoles |
| **Drug‑induced myositis (e.g., anti‑TNF, methotrexate)** | Known drug history; often ANA or other autoantibodies | None other than semaglutide; serology negative |
| **Infectious myositis (CMV, adenovirus)** | Recent infection, other systemic signs | No evidence of infection, normal viral serologies |

## 7. Isolated SS‑B positivity – what does it mean?

An isolated low‑titer anti‑SS‑B (Ro52) is **not specific** for IMNM. It might be a benign epiphenomenon in an older patient with nonspecific B‑cell activation, especially given NAFLD and metabolic syndrome—a backdrop for low‑level autoimmunity.  

Importantly, the patient remained `ANA negative` and had no salivary gland disease, so the SS‑B positivity likely had no clinical relevance to her myopathy.  

**Bottom line:** Don’t read too much into a single weak autoimmune marker unless it fits a broader phenotype.

## 8. Drug‑induced vs. idiopathic – the “who, what, and when” puzzle

| Feature | Drug‑induced IMNM | Idiopathic IMNM |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Temporal pattern** | Rapid onset after drug; often within weeks | Variable onset; often months/years of progressive decline |
| **Additional systemic symptoms** | May have GI, renal, or cardiac side effects | Usually isolated to muscle |
| **Response to drug withdrawal** | Often rapid partial improvement | No effect |
| **Autoantibody status** | Seronegative often (but not always) | Seropositive if autoantibody present |
| **Biopsy** | Similar necrotic pattern; may have complement deposition | Same pattern; more variable |

In our patient, the CK rose **four months after** the single semaglutide dose – slower than typical drug reactions but still far shorter than idiopathic disease courses. Her dramatic recovery after drug withdrawal **and** aggressive immunosuppression argues strongly for a drug‑triggered autoimmune flare.

> **Clinical cue:** A “delayed” drug reaction is still within the realm of possibility, especially with agents that modulate the immune system (GLP‑1 agonists appear to do this).

## 9. Treatment algorithm for seronegative IMNM  

> *All treatment plans should be individualized; the following is a pragmatic, evidence‑derived workflow.*

1. **Confirm diagnosis** – CK, imaging, autoantibody panel, and muscle biopsy.  
2. **Remove the offending agent** – if a drug is implicated.  
3. **First‑line therapy**  
   - **High‑dose IV methylprednisolone** 1 g/day for 3–5 days  
   - **Oral prednisone** tapered from 1 mg/kg daily  
4. **Adjunctive therapy (if severe or refractory)**  
   - **IVIG** 2 g/kg over 4 days (as in this case)  
   - **Alternate immunosuppressants** (e.g., methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine) once steroids are tapered  
5. **Monitor response**  
   - **CK** every 2–3 days during induction, then weekly  
   - **Functional assessment** (MRC scale, grip strength)  
6. **Taper steroids**  
   - Gradual reduction over 6–12 months, depending on CK trends and muscle strength  
7. **Maintenance**  
   - Low‑dose prednisone + immunosuppressant until CK normalizes and strength improves ≥ 5/5 on MRC; then wean off all agents.  
8. **Long‑term follow‑up**  
   - Clinical visits every 3–6 months; repeat CK and autoantibody panels periodically.  

> **Real‑world note:** In this patient, IVIG was given once; further courses were avoided due to cost and normalizing labs.

## 10. Prognosis and follow‑up

Seronegative IMNM has a variable course. Early recognition and prompt treatment typically lead to remission, especially when the trigger is removed. The patient in the case regained baseline function and was off immunosuppression for 8 months after a brief IVIG course. Cardiac MRI abnormalities resolved, and renal function returned to baseline.  

**Key questions to assess** during follow‑up:

- Is CK normalized and stable?  
- Is muscle strength fully restored (≥ 5/5 on MRC)?  
- Has the offending drug been replaced or discontinued permanently?  
- Are there any new symptoms suggestive of relapse?

## 11. Take‑home messages 🥅

- **Seronegative IMNM is real, deadly, and treatable** – don’t get hung up on a negative antibody panel.  
- **Muscle biopsy is the gold standard**: look for necrosis + macrophage phagocytosis + minimal lymphocytes.  
- **MRI is invaluable** for locating active lesions and guiding biopsy.  
- **A temporal link to a new drug (e.g., semaglutide, tirzepatide) should raise suspicion** for drug‑induced IMNM even if the onset is delayed.  
- **Early high‑dose steroids + IVIG can rapidly control disease**, especially in seronegative patients, and may obviate the need for lifelong immunosuppression.  
- **Isolated low‑titer SS‑B in a patient with normal ANA** likely has no clinical significance in muscle disease.  
- **Removal of the suspected drug** is critical; reintroduction is contraindicated if the adverse event was severe.  
- **Monitor CK and strength closely**; taper steroids only after sustained remission.  

### Final thought

This case underscores that seronegative IMNM sits at the intersection of neurology, rheumatology, and pharmacology. When classic autoantibodies fail to give clues, let the muscle biopsy and the clinical narrative speak. And always keep an eye on the medications you’re prescribing, even the seemingly innocuous GLP‑1 receptor agonists that are taking over the obesity and diabetes space.  

Happy reading, and stay vigilant for the next kidney‑to‑muscle story that could land in your inbox!



# High‑Dose Steroids, IVIG, and the Art of Tapering: Managing a GLP‑1‑Induced Myopathy  

---

## Why This Case is a Wake‑Up Call

You’re a clinician who sees a 60‑year‑old woman with progressive proximal weakness. The first clue: a CK so high it screams “something’s wrong,” and a history of a single dose of semaglutide that triggered nausea, vomiting, and acute renal failure. A month later she’s struggling to sit upright, and CK spikes to almost 4,000 U/L. A muscle biopsy shows necrosis, minimal lymphocytes, and macrophages – textbook immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). The twist? All this after *one* semaglutide shot. It’s the first report that links a GLP‑1 receptor agonist to drug‑induced IMNM, and it gives us a clear window into how to treat such a rare but devastating adverse event.

---

## Quick Snapshot of the Patient

| Timeline | Key Events | Lab/Imaging |
|----------|------------|-------------|
| **Baseline** | NAFLD, hypertension, obesity (BMI ≈ 35 kg/m²) | ALT/AST up‑trending; negative auto‑panel |
| **Day 0** | Semaglutide 0.25 mg SC (first dose) | CK 337 U/L |
| **Day 2–3** | Hospitalised for GI symptoms, AKI; CK still mild | |
| **Month 4** | Presents with proximal weakness, dysphagia | CK 3,992 U/L; MRI femurs shows oedema |
| **Hospitalisation 1** | Pulse methylprednisolone 1 g ×5 days | |
| **Hospitalisation 2** | Oral prednisone 1 mg/kg + IVIG 2 g/kg over 4 days | CK falls rapidly |
| **Post‑discharge** | 12 days later readmissions, normal CK | |
| **10 months OK** | CK normal; off steroids 8 months | |

---

## Why Start With Pulse Corticosteroids?

1. **Aggressive histology** – a necrotising muscle with minimal inflammation signals a strong autoimmune drive.  
2. **Rapid response needed** – hyper‑CK leads to CK‑rhabdomyolysis, renal injury, and systemic inflammation.  
3. **Evidence base** – IMNM guidelines (EULAR/ACR 2023) recommend 1 g methylprednisolone IV for 3 days if severe disease, then a 1 mg/kg oral taper.

In practice, give 1 g × 5 days in ICU or hospital ward. Some centers opt for 3 days; the exact number is flexible – the goal is to blunt the cytokine surge that fuels myofibre necrosis.

---

## Where Does IVIG Fit In?

- **Why it helps** – IVIG (2 g/kg total, typically over 4 days) modulates Fc receptors, suppresses autoantibody‑mediated complement activation, and dampens macrophage‐driven myophagocytosis.  
- **When to add** – Admission CK > 3,000 U/L with functional decline or refractory to steroids after 48–72 h. In this patient’s case, a single 2 g/kg course bridged the gap while prednisone shipped the inflammation home.  
- **Cost & logistics** – A single dose may be enough if disease is truly drug‑induced; longer courses (≥ 2 weeks) are reserved for idiopathic IMNM that fails steroids.

**Early use is key.** Give IVIG while tapering steroids, not after you’ve already lost function.

---

## The Taper: Less Is More

The dogma has long been “longer steroid courses = fewer relapses.” That’s true for idiopathic IMNM, but when the culprit is a stopped drug (here, semaglutide), the immune system may reset faster.

**Typical taper (drug‑induced IMNM)**

| Week | Dose (or % reduction) | Notes |
|------|-----------------------|-------|
| 1 | 1 mg/kg (or 40 mg if <40 kg) | Hold until CK normal |
| 2 | ~0.75 mg/kg | If CK < 200 and strength stable |
| 3 | 0.5 mg/kg | Patient usually asymptomatic |
| 4 | 0.25 mg/kg | Large decrements of 10 mg/week if > 50 mg/day |
| 5 | 0 mg – stop | Confirm CK < 200 lifelong |

**In practice**: Many clinicians prefer to cut by 10 mg every 1–2 weeks for doses > 50 mg, as the patient in the case we followed down to 40 mg in 4 weeks and was never re‑stopped until 8 months off.

**Key point:** **Monitor**. Weekly CK and strength assessment keeps the taper on track.

---

## Watch the Watchful Eye: Infection & Relapse

| Issue | Why it matters | Practical tip |
|-------|----------------|---------------|
| **Infection risk** | High‑dose steroids + IVIG = immune suppression. | Check CBC, monitor for fever. Consider prophylactic TMP‑SMX if > 4 weeks of cumulative steroids. |
| **Relapse** | Even if you taper fast, a flare can occur months later if immune memory persists. | Patient education: report new weakness ASAP. |
| **Re‑exposure** | Controversial, but rechallenge is unethical once IMNM occurs. | Absolutely avoid GLP‑1 agonists; discuss alternatives for weight control/diabetes (e.g., SGLT2i, basal insulin). |

---

## Practical Roadmap for the ED or ICU

1. **Recognise**: Persistently rising CK, proximal weakness, prior exposure to potential myotoxins (statins, GLP‑1 agonist).  
2. **Confirm**: Muscle MRI or biopsy if time allows; serology for HMGCR/SRP and a comprehensive myositis panel.  
3. **Immediate Actions**:  
   - Stop offending agent (semaglutide).  
   - Start pulse methylprednisolone 1 g IV × 5 days.  
   - Begin oral prednisone 1 mg/kg once stable.  
   - If CK > 3,000 U/L or severe weakness, add IVIG 2 g/kg over 4 days.  
4. **Monitoring**:  
   - CBC, CMP, CK, AST/ALT daily first week, then every 3‑4 days.  
   - Functional checks: handgrip, 10‑second leg raise, 4‑meter walk.  
   - Watch for infection signs (fever, neutropenia).  
5. **Taper**: Weekly decrements per the table above, adjusting for CK trends and strength.  
6. **Discharge plan**:  
   - Outpatient follow‑up after 2 weeks, then monthly for 3 months.  
   - Continue home CK checks if available.  
   - Reinforce no GLP‑1 agonist; counsel alternative weight‑management strategies.  

---

## What If You Can’t Get IVIG?

In many centres, IVIG is a scarce or expensive resource. The evidence still supports a **high‑dose steroid first**. If steroid response is adequate, you can skip IVIG. If CK stays > 1,000 U/L after 48 h or weakness doesn’t improve, consider IVIG if possible. Alternatively, cyclophosphamide or a rapid‑acting biologic (e.g., rituximab) could be considered – but they bring higher infection risk and aren’t usually first‑line for drug‑induced IMNM.

---

## Briefly About the “Seronegative” Enigma

In this case, the myositis panel was clean: no HMGCR, SRP, or other known autoantibodies. Yet the histology was classic for IMNM. This underlines a crucial teaching: **seronegativity does not rule out clinically definite IMNM**. It also hints that GLP‑1 agonists may trigger a novel pathway of muscle autoimmunity – perhaps a post‐translational modification of mitochondrial proteins that the immune system sees as foreign. Until we know more, treat the clinical picture aggressively.

---

## Take‑Home Pearls

- **Do not underestimate a single drug exposure** – GLP‑1 analogues carry a small but real risk of drug‑induced IMNM.  
- **Aggressive induction matters** – Start with pulse methylprednisolone; add IVIG if CK > 3,000 U/L or weakness persists.  
- **Rapid taper is acceptable** when the offending agent is withdrawn and the biopsy shows a classic necrotising pattern with minimal inflammation.  
- **Close CK & functional monitoring** guides the taper and early detection of relapse.  
- **Infection vigilance** is essential; consider prophylaxis if steroids > 4 weeks.  
- **Never rechallenge** a patient who has had drug‑induced IMNM.

---

### Final Thought

This case reminds us that even “first‑in‑class” medications like semaglutide can have unforeseen knock‑on effects on skeletal muscle. As a frontline clinician, you can pivot quickly: identify, intervene with pulse steroids (and IVIG if warranted), taper thoughtfully, and keep the patient aware of the symptoms that signal a flare. In doing so, you’ll offer the safest possible path to recovery while safeguarding that patient’s metabolic health – just as the patient in this report eventually did, freed from both myopathy and the GLP‑1 agonist that started it all.



# From Semaglutide to Safety Signals: The Role of Pharmacovigilance in Rare Myopathies  

## Why This Case Sticks With Me  

Imagine a 66‑year‑old woman who gets a single intramuscular shot of semaglutide for obesity, feels a bout of nausea and vomiting, and is hospitalized for acute renal failure. She recovers, but over the next four months she begins to lose muscle power, almost can’t swallow, and her creatine kinase (CK) shoots from 300 U/L to almost 4,000 U/L. One course of high‑dose steroids and IVIG brings her back to baseline, and she never takes semaglutide again.  

To many of us, this pattern feels like “drug toxicity that turned into an autoimmune flare.” The clinical mystery is—was semaglutide simply a toxic kid on the wrong muscle, or did it trigger a true immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)? This question is not only pivotal for one patient; it has broader implications for prescribing a drug class that is climbing the ranks of modern diabetes and obesity therapy.  

## A Rapid‑Fire Clinical Snapshot  

| Timeline | Clinical Details | Labs/Imaging |
|----------|------------------|--------------|
| **Day of first dose** | Nausea, vomiting, acute renal failure, 2‑day ICU stay | CK 337 U/L (slightly above ULN) |
| **4 months later** | Progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia | CK 3,992 U/L; MRI: symmetric muscle edema |
| **Treatment** | 5 days IV methylprednisolone → 1 mg/kg oral prednisone → IVIG 2 g/kg over 4 days | Rapid CK fall; muscle biopsy: necrosis + macrophage engulfment; no lymphocytic infiltrate (classical for IMNM) |
| **Outcome** | Full strength recovery; off steroids/IVIG within 8 months; no recurrence | CK normalized; no other autoimmune antibodies (HMGCR, SRP, etc.) |

What makes this story rate‑up for discussion is that this patient used *only one dose* of semaglutide, had a clear temporal sequence, and then demonstrated a delayed but pronounced muscle injury that required strong immunosuppression. The biopsy, the lack of conventional autoantibodies, and the bouncy response to immune blockade all point toward a drug‑induced IMNM rather than a classic statin‑driven or idiopathic form.  

## Putting It Into the Pharmacovigilance Playbook  

### WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Framework

The WHO‑UMC framework is the gold standard for causality assessment of adverse drug reactions (ADRs). It grades an event as:

| Category | Key Features |
|----------|--------------|
| **Certain** | Clear temporal relationship, no alternative explanation, response to withdrawal, plausible mechanism |
| **Probable/likely** | Temporal relationship and response to withdrawal but alternative causes cannot be fully excluded |
| **Possible** | Temporal relationship present but alternative causes plausible |
| **Unlikely** | No clear temporal link, or explained by underlying disease |
| **Conditional/Unclassified** | Insufficient information |

For this patient, the elements line up with a **“probable”** causal relationship:  
1. **Time‑line**: Symptom onset lags 4 months after a single dose—acceptable for immune‑mediated processes.  
2. **Exclusion of other drugs**: No statin use, no other known precipitants (beet‑root, red yeast rice, etc.).  
3. **Response**: Symptom abatement after semaglutide discontinuation and immunosuppressive therapy.  
4. **Biologic plausibility**: GLP‑1 receptors are expressed on skeletal muscle; murine data suggest metabolic effects on myofibers, though direct immunologic mechanisms remain unexplored.  

The WHO standard guidance—**“Probable” requires ruling out alternative causes and considering temporal relationship**—line up with the data.  

### Why a Faster Report Matters  

1. **Label Updates**: Product monographs only mention GI intolerance, muscle cramps, or renal function monitoring but omit rare myopathy signals. Early collected data can push regulatory agencies to issue safety warnings.  
2. **Prescribing Guidelines**: Internal formulary committees can adjust black‑box warnings or advise baseline CK and periodic monitoring.  
3. **Clinical Vigilance**: Physicians entering a pharmacovigilance database may confront a pattern—would you then order a CK baseline for all GLP‑1 patients?  
4. **Research Direction**: Once a safety signal is flagged, mechanistic studies (cross‑sectional CK, autoantibody panels, genetic predispositions) can be funded and designed.  

In the case of tirzepatide, a recent published rhabdomyolysis case mirrored the quick CK peak and drug withdrawal response, but *didn’t* require immunotherapy. The semaglutide case extends the spectrum to immune‑mediated necrosis—widening the pool of clinicians who need to watch for myopathy after GLP‑1 use.  

## How to Get Your Voice Heard  

### **1. Identify a Suspected ADR**  
- Any unexplained CK rise >10× ULN  
- New proximal muscle weakness or dysphagia after starting a GLP‑1 agonist  
- Absence of metabolic disease flare, statin use, or other drug interactions  

### **2. Document Classic Information**  
- **Patient ID** (de‑identified for safety), age, sex  
- **Drug**: name, dose, route, start date, exposure  
- **Event**: clinical features, onset timing, lab & imaging data  
- **Outcome**: whether it resolved, improved after drug discontinuation, or needed extra therapy  

### **3. Choose the Reporting Channel**  

| Region | Reporting Tool | Steps |
|--------|----------------|-------|
| **USA** | FDA MedWatch (Adverse Event Reporting System) | Online form; can send via email, fax, or paper |
| **EU** | EudraVigilance | Web‑based; need a unique identifier |
| **Global** | WHO UMC Adverse Reaction Database | Standardized form; accessible via national centers (e.g., Canada, Australia) |
| **Japan** | PMDA Adverse Drug Reaction Reporting | Use the Japanese system’s online portal |

**Tip**: Check whether your institution has a pharmacovigilance lead or a drug safety officer (DSO). They can streamline submission and ensure data are sent to the right jurisdiction.

### **4. Keep the Cytokine of the Word**  

- **Use plain but complete language**: In the “Suspected Reaction” field, specify IMNM, CK elevation, proximal weakness, dysphagia.  
- **Attach supporting data**: CK trends, MRI or biopsy images (de‑identified), treatment courses.  
- **Declare any uncertainty**: If there’s a possibility of underlying predisposition, note it.

Once submitted, you’ll receive a confirmation code. If the agency senses a signal, they may contact you for further details.  

## Translating the Signal into Bedside Practice  

1. **Baseline CK**: Ideally prior to initiating a GLP‑1 agonist, especially in patients with obesity or a history of metabolic syndrome—CK may already be mildly elevated.  
2. **Periodic Re‑checks**: Repeat CK after 4–6 weeks, then quarterly if the patient shows symptoms.  
3. **Clinical Red Flags**: Any new proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, or unexplained fatigue warrants immediate evaluation and potential removal of the agent.  
4. **Early Specialist Input**: Rheumatology or neurology consult can rapidly orient modality (high‑dose steroids, IVIG).  
5. **Avoid Re‑challenge**: There’s no benefit in re‑exposure; if necessary, explore alternative agents (e.g., SGLT‑2 inhibitors, GLP‑1 analogues with a different receptor profile).  

## Take‑Home Points  

- **Semaglutide may trigger an immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy**, even with a single dose.  
- Baseline and serial CK monitoring can catch evolving muscle injury early.  
- Use the WHO‑UMC framework to assess causality; a “probable” relation is enough to generate a safety signal.  
- Report suspected IMNM via MedWatch, EudraVigilance, or WHO UMC—your case could influence labeling.  
- Prompt withdrawal of the drug plus steroids/IVIG is often curative; long‑term remission can be achieved with careful monitoring.
