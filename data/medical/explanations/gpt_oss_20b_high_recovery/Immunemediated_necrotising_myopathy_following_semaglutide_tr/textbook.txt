Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Background: GLP‑1 Receptor Agonists and Immune‑Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy  

Glucagon‑like peptide‑1 (GLP‑1) receptor agonists are a class of incretin‑based medicines that mimic the action of the endogenous gut hormone GLP‑1. They bind to the GLP‑1 receptor on pancreatic β‑cells, stimulating insulin secretion in a glucose‑dependent manner, while concurrently suppressing glucagon release and slowing gastric emptying. These pharmacologic effects translate clinically into improved glycaemic control and a marked reduction in body weight, which has led to their widespread use in type 2 diabetes and, more recently, in the treatment of obesity. Semaglutide (a long‑acting, once‑weekly subcutaneous analogue) and tirzepatide (a dual GLP‑1 and GIP receptor agonist) are the most frequently prescribed agents in this class.

Beyond the classic gastrointestinal adverse effects that prompt dose titration—nausea, vomiting, and diarrhoea—there have been sporadic reports of skeletal‑muscle de‑compensation. Weight loss, driven by appetite suppression, is an expected benefit, but several case reports have identified transient elevations in creatine kinase (CK) and, in some instances, frank rhabdomyolysis with peak CK values in the tens of thousands. These episodes tend to resolve rapidly after discontinuation of the drug and rehydration, suggesting a direct myotoxic mechanism rather than an immune‑mediated process. Nevertheless, the potential for a drug‑induced immunologic reaction has not been ruled out.

Immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct clinical entity within the spectrum of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies. It presents with rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness, markedly elevated CK, and minimal or absent systemic features of connective‑tissue disease. Muscle biopsy is the diagnostic cornerstone: it reveals clusters of necrotic and regenerating fibers surrounded by macrophages, often with abundant acid‑phosphatase activity, but with a paucity of T‑cell infiltrates—unlike polymyositis or dermatomyositis. The macrophage‑rich environment reflects humoral‑mediated injury rather than a classical cell‑mediated process.

Serological testing enriches the diagnostic ladder for IMNM. The most clinically relevant autoantibodies are directed against the enzyme 3‑hydroxy‑3‑methyl‑glutaryl‑coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR) and the signal recognition particle (SRP). Anti‑HMGCR antibodies are strongly associated with statin exposure, whereas anti‑SRP antibodies are often clinically accompanied by severe muscle weakness. Beyond these, the broader myositis panel can detect other myositis‑specific antibodies (e.g., Jo‑1, Mi‑2, MDA‑5), but these are less commonly implicated in IMNM. In roughly one‑third of patients, all autoantibody tests are negative; this seronegative subset poses a diagnostic challenge because the diagnosis must rely on the combination of clinical, histologic, and laboratory evidence.

The underlying immunopathogenesis of drug‑induced autoimmunity is not fully elucidated but is generally thought to involve one or more of three mechanisms. The hapten hypothesis posits that a small drug molecule covalently attaches to a self‑protein, creating a neo‑epitope that elicits an immune response. Molecular mimicry describes a scenario in which a drug or its metabolites share structural motifs with host proteins, leading to cross‑reactive autoantibodies. Epitope spreading occurs when the initial immune attack on a protein exposes additional proximal epitopes to the immune system, amplifying the autoimmune cascade. Statin‑associated IMNM, driven by anti‑HMGCR antibodies, exemplifies how a drug can initiate such a cascade.

In evaluating potential drug adverse events, the World Health Organization–Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO‑UMC) framework provides a systematic causality assessment. The assessment categorizes the relationship between a drug and an event into: certain, probable/likely, possible, unlikely, conditional/unclassified, and unassessable. It evaluates the temporal sequence, strength of evidence, the likelihood of alternative explanations, the response to de‑challenge, and, when available, the outcome of a rechallenge. In the case described, the close temporal relationship between the first semaglutide injection and the subsequent rise in CK, the absence of other plausible etiologies, and the clinical improvement after cessation of the drug and initiation of immunosuppression satisfy the criteria for a probable or likely causal link. This structured approach aids clinicians in distinguishing coincidental associations from genuine drug‑induced disease processes.

## 2. Clinical Course and Physical Findings

The patient, a woman in her 60s, was a known case of obesity (body mass index >30 kg/m²), non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism treated with levothyroxine, and a remote history of deep‑vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. None of these comorbidities typically predisposes to immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), although uncontrolled thyroid disease can mimic myopathy; her thyroid‑stimulating hormone was within the reference range, effectively excluding thyroid‑related muscle disease.

Semaglutide was started at 0.25 mg subcutaneously, the lowest incremental dose in its titration schedule. Within hours she developed marked nausea and vomiting—a common early adverse effect attributed to delayed gastric emptying. Persistent emesis led to poor oral intake, volume depletion, and acute renal failure complicated by metabolic acidosis. The inpatient work‑up revealed a slightly raised creatine kinase (CK) of 337 U/L (normal 12–191 U/L), mild transaminase elevations (AST = 191 U/L, ALT = 165 U/L), and a normal cardiac troponin, thereby ruling out concomitant myocardial infarction or overt rhabdomyolysis on admission. The mild CK elevation provided the first hint of a skeletal‑muscle component but was far below the threshold (≈5× upper limit) for clinical rhabdomyolysis.

No other systemic symptoms (rash, joint swelling, or fever) were present. She was discharged after five days of supportive care, with resolution of gastrointestinal symptoms and recovery of renal function. However, over the next four months she experienced a progressive decline: she lost the ability to stand or sit unaided, complained of difficulty swallowing liquids and solids (dysphagia) as well as hoarseness and throat discomfort (dysphonia). These findings suggested involvement of pharyngeal and laryngeal musculature, which are usually spared in early rheumatologic myopathies but become compromised as the disease advances.

On formal neurological examination upon admission to the tertiary hospital, the patient exhibited marked proximal muscle weakness. Strength testing—performed with the medical research Council (MRC) grading system—revealed 3/5 strength or worse in the quadriceps, deltoids, and hip abductors bilaterally, and 2/5 or less in the gastric and accessory swallowing muscles. The patient could not rise from a chair or stand without assistance and required bilateral hand‑rail support to mobilise. Importantly, the examination was otherwise unremarkable: skin was normal, no heliotrope rash or Gottron papules were seen, joints were painless without swelling or synovitis, and there were no ophthalmoplegia, facial diplegia, or sensory losses. This profile of isolated proximal muscle weakness without cutaneous or extra‑muscular manifestations is characteristic of an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy rather than a dermatomyositis or systemic connective‑tissue disease.

Overall, the clinical trajectory—from an initial, drug‑induced gastrointestinal and renal insult to a delayed, progressive proximal muscle weakness with dysphagia—highlights the importance of longitudinal observation in patients exposed to agents with potential myotoxic effects. The persistence and escalation of CK over the ensuing months strongly support an evolving necrotising myopathy rather than a self‑limited acute drug reaction.

## 3. Diagnostic Evaluation: Laboratory, Imaging, and Histopathology  

A systematic approach was required to define the patient’s progressive proximal weakness, to quantify ongoing muscle injury, and to differentiate an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from other causes of myopathy, including drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis, idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, and systemic autoimmune conditions. The diagnostic work‑up comprised serial creatine kinase (CK) measurements, liver function tests, thyroid studies, a comprehensive autoantibody panel, acute‑phase reactants, cardiac biomarkers, musculoskeletal and cardiac imaging, and ultimately, muscle and liver biopsies.  

### Serial Creatine Kinase Trend and Accompanying Transaminases  
CK elevation was the central laboratory finding that mirrored the clinical deterioration. Initial CK at the first hospitalisation was modest (337 U/L) and did not meet the threshold for rhabdomyolysis. Four months later, CK had surged to 3,992 U/L, a 12‑fold increase that persisted for days before commencing therapy. This pattern of a slow‑to‑peak but pronounced CK rise is characteristic of IMNM, contrasting with the abrupt, transient spike observed in drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis, where CK typically normalises within days of drug cessation.  

Simultaneous elevations in AST (191 U/L) and ALT (165 U/L) were noted. While transaminase rises can accompany myonecrosis, the moderate elevation together with a chronic hepatic steatosis diagnosed 3 years earlier made hepatic etiology unlikely. The liver biopsy confirmed steatohepatitis but excluded hepatocellular injury secondary to myopathic CK release.  

### Thyroid Function and Autoantibody Profile  
Thyroid studies, including TSH, were within reference limits, arguing against thyroid‑related myopathy, which usually presents with hyper‑ or hypothyroidism together with muscle weakness. An extensive myositis Autoantibody panel was negative for all anti‑mitochondrial, anti‑shr, anti‑Jo‑1, SRP, HMGCR, Mi‑2, and other disease‑specific antibodies. The only positive finding was a low‑titre SS‑B antibody (2.4, normal <1). In isolation, SS‑B positivity does not mandate a connective‑tissue disease and was interpreted as a possible underlying autoimmune predisposition rather than a pathogenic driver of the myopathy.  

The absence of anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP antibodies meant the case fell into the seronegative IMNM subset, which accounts for roughly a third of patients and necessitates histopathology for confirmation.  

### Inflammatory Markers  
Both ESR (22 mm/h) and CRP (22.8 mg/L) were elevated, reflecting a systemic inflammatory response. In the setting of a myopathy with minimal lymphocytic infiltration, modest elevation of acute‑phase reactants is common and supports an inflammatory process but is not specific for any one myositis subtype.  

### Cardiac Biomarkers and Imaging  
Cardiac troponin T was mildly elevated (62 pg/mL), and pro‑BNP was markedly increased at 4,358 pg/mL, suggesting subclinical myocardial stress. Cardiac MRI showed mild myocardial oedema (mid‑anteroseptal hypokinesis) without late gadolinium enhancement, while echocardiography revealed otherwise normal ventricular function. These findings are compatible with isolated myocarditis or heart‑strain but do not meet criteria for systemic inflammatory myopathies that involve significant cardiac compromise. Importantly, the absence of myocardial scarring or active inflammation on sequence made active myocarditis a less likely contributor to the myopathy.  

### Musculoskeletal Imaging  
MRI of the bilateral thighs demonstrated symmetric, diffuse subcutaneous and muscular oedema predominantly involving the vastus lateralis and quadriceps. Such a pattern is frequently seen in IMNM and reflects active muscle injury rather than chronic fatty infiltration. The absence of fatty replacement or rimmed vacuoles argues against inclusion‑body myositis, while lack of heterogenous enhancement or fascial involvement makes polymyositis less likely.  

### Muscle Biopsy  
The definitive diagnostic modality was the vastus lateralis muscle biopsy. Histology revealed numerous necrotic fibres and actively regenerating terminals, indicating ongoing myofibre injury. Acid phosphatase staining (positive) confirmed lysosomal enzyme activity within macrophage‑laden vacuoles, an entity called myophagocytosis. CD68 immunohistochemistry highlighted a dense macrophage infiltrate surrounding necrotic fibres. Complement deposition was visualised with C5b‑9 (membrane attack complex) staining on both necrotic and non‑necrotic myocytes, consistent with complement‑mediated cytotoxicity frequently described in IMNM.  

Crucially, T‐cell markers (CD3) were sparse, and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates were minimal. This paucity of lymphocytic inflammation distinguishes IMNM from polymyositis, which typically shows dense interstitial or endomysial CD8⁺ T‑cell infiltrates. The overall pathology—myonecrosis, macrophage‑rich phagocytosis, complement deposition, and few lymphocytes—issued a classic pattern for a necrotising myopathy.  

### Liver Biopsy  
Concurrent hepatic assessment was performed to confirm that the transaminase rise was not due to hepatotoxic injury from semaglutide or an occult hepatic inflammatory process. The liver specimen showed macrovesicular steatosis comprising ~70 % of the parenchyma and a NAFLD activity score of 5/8, consistent with the clinical picture of non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease. No interface hepatitis or significant portal inflammation existed, ruling out a hepatic source for the CK elevation.  

### Differential Exclusion  
1. **Statin‑induced myopathy** – The patient had never taken statins, and histology did not show persistent inflammation or pathology typical of statin‑induced necrosis.  
2. **Drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis** – The temporal profile (slow CK rise post‑single semaglutide dose), the need for immunosuppression, and histology rule out a pure toxic myopathy. Rhabdomyolysis would also typically present with myoglobinuria and renal injury not seen in this case.  
3. **Polymyositis** – Requires extensive T‑cell infiltrate and endomysial inflammation, absent in this biopsy.  
4. **Inclusion‑body myositis** – Age and gender are atypical, and biopsy lacks rimmed vacuoles; also shows degenerative lysosomal inclusions, which were not seen.  
5. **Myasthenia gravis** – Bilateral ocular or bulbar symptoms without fatigability, and normal repetitive nerve‑stimulus testing, discounted this.  
6. **Thyroid‑related myopathy** – Normal TSH and lack of overt thyroid dysfunction excluded this etiology.  

Collectively, the laboratory trajectory, imaging findings, and muscle histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy. The seronegative status and the temporal relationship to semaglutide use suggest a drug‑triggered autoimmune reaction, but the pattern clearly aligns with IMNM rather than statin‑induced toxicity or other inflammatory myopathies. The diagnostic work‑up therefore provided a robust framework for targeted immunosuppressive therapy and for safely discontinuing the suspected inciting agent.

## 4. Management and Therapeutic Rationale

**High‑dose intravenous methylprednisolone pulse (1000 mg/day for 5 days) and expected corticosteroid response**  
In immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), the pathogenesis is thought to involve antibody‑ or T‑cell‑mediated complement‑driven myofibre necrosis, with macrophage‑mediated phagocytosis of myofibres as a hallmark of injury. High‑dose intravenous methylprednisolone is the cornerstone of initial therapy because it rapidly suppresses inflammation, reduces complement activation, and dampens the antigen‑specific immune response. The 1000‑mg daily dose for 5 days is equivalent to a 1‑mg/kg per‑day regimen for a patient of roughly 70 kg and is adopted from standard protocols for severe idiopathic inflammatory myopathies. In this patient, the pulse therapy led to a notable decline in creatine kinase (CK) from a peak of 3992 U/L to 1237 U/L within a few days, accompanied by early improvement in proximal muscle strength and the resolution of dysphagia. The rapid biochemistry response is indicative of a cytokine‑driven, rather than purely metabolic, disease process that is susceptible to potent immunosuppression.  

**Transition to oral prednisone 1 mg/kg/day and taper protocol**  
After the initial burst, the patient was continued on oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg/day (approximately 60 mg/day in this case). This dose is consistent with the recommendations of the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) for glucocorticoid‑unresponsive or steroid‑dependent myositis and is designed to maintain suppression of the autoimmune cascade while allowing for dose reduction. A typical taper in IMNM involves a 2‑to‑3‑week interval between dose reductions by 5–10 mg, with a total taper duration of 6‑12 months; however, early sustained improvement in CK and clinical strength often permits an earlier completion. In this patient, a rapid taper over 8 weeks was achieved after normalization of CK and consisted of decreasing doses by 5 mg every 2 weeks, ultimately allowing cessation of steroids within 2 months of admission.  

**Indication for intravenous immunoglobulin: 2 g/kg over 4 days in severe cases**  
IVIG was added because the patient continued to exhibit severe proximal weakness, dysphagia, and a CK elevation that remained above 1000 U/L despite prednisolone. IVIG at a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days (0.5 g/kg/day), is considered a second‑line agent when high‑dose steroids alone do not rapidly restore strength or when the disease course is life‑threatening. The therapeutic mechanism in IMNM is presumed to involve neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies, inhibition of complement deposition, and modulation of Fcγ receptor expression on macrophages, thereby decreasing myofibre phagocytosis. In this patient, IVIG was administered after 5 days of pulse steroids and a short oral taper; CK fell below 400 U/L within two weeks, and the patient was able to maintain functional independence without further immunomodulation.  

**Monitoring of CK reduction, muscle strength, and potential steroid side effects**  
Serum CK was measured daily in the first week and twice weekly thereafter until values declined below 500 U/L, after which a monthly schedule sufficed. Clinical strength was quantified using the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale for proximal musculature, supplemented by timed functional tests (e.g., timed up‑and‑go, sit‑to‑stand). Early declines in CK usually correlated with improved MRC scores, providing reassurance that the inflammatory process was being arrested. Steroid side effects—including hyperglycaemia, hypertension, mood changes, and infection risk—were tracked through weekly blood pressure, random glucose and HbA1c (in a patient with type 2 diabetes) and regular complete blood count monitoring. Mild glucocorticoid‑induced hyperglycaemia was managed with adjustments to the patient’s antidiabetic regimen. No steroid‑induced diuresis or bone‑density loss was observed over the short course. IVIG was generally well tolerated; infusion reactions were mitigated with pre‑medication and slowed infusion rates, and no thromboembolic events were seen.  

**Role of GLP‑1 receptor agonist cessation and patient education to prevent rechallenge**  
Semaglutide was identified as the likely inciting agent, given its temporally proximal exposure and the absence of other recognized triggers (e.g., statins, mushrooms, or red yeast rice). Abrupt discontinuation was necessary to halt the underlying immune stimulation. Patients experiencing severe autoimmune sequelae to GLP‑1 agonists should receive formal counseling on the risks of rechallenge: the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) guidelines on severe drug reactions stipulate that re‑exposure is contraindicated when a strong temporal association and a plausible immunologic mechanism exist. Alternative anti‑obesity or antihyperglycaemic therapies (e.g., insulin, basal‑bolus, or sodium‑glucose co‑transporter 2 inhibitors) were discussed, and the patient’s weight and glucose control were monitored, demonstrating no regrowth of the obesity‑related comorbidity that originally prompted semaglutide use.  

**Management of complications: pseudomonal septic shock, urinary tract infection**  
During her hospitalization, the patient developed septic shock from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection. Management involved ICU support, broad‑spectrum antimicrobials (initial empiric coverage with a carbapenem and aminoglycoside, later tailored to culture susceptibilities), and aggressive fluid resuscitation. Sepsis was treated as an independent event; the acute management did not interfere with the immunosuppressive regimen, as studies in severe infections with concurrent steroid therapy show that high‑dose early steroids may reduce mortality in septic shock when initiated within the first 6 hours. No recurrence of elevated CK during or after the septic episode suggested that the complication did not trigger a new myopathic flare.  

**Follow‑up schedule: outpatient visits, CK monitoring, functional assessment**  
Following discharge, the patient attended a clinic visit 2 weeks after therapy initiation, during which CK was 180 U/L and her MRC score for upper and lower extremity proximal muscles had improved to 4/5. Subsequent visits occurred monthly for the first 3 months, then at 6 and 9 months. Serial CK measurements remained within normal limits, and functional assessments (e.g., 10‑meter walk, peg‑board test) showed complete recovery of baseline performance. Importantly, no evidence of relapse was seen up to 10 months post‑therapy, allowing cessation of all immunosuppressive medications and an extended period of drug‑free status. The follow‑up plan reinforces the principle that patients with presumed drug‑induced IMNM complete a finite course of immune modulation and then are monitored for recurrence via periodic CK and strength testing.  

In summary, this case highlights a pragmatic therapeutic pathway: a prompt high‑dose steroid pulse, transition to oral prednisone with a tailored taper, adjunctive IVIG in refractory or severe presentations, rigorous monitoring of biochemical and clinical parameters, and the elimination of the suspected inciting agent. The successful weaning off immunosuppression underscores that drug‑induced IMNM may resolve with brief, targeted therapy when the offending agent is eliminated and the autoimmune reaction is curbed.

## 5. Outcome, Prognosis, and Clinical Implications

At the 10‑month evaluation, the patient had fully returned to her pre‑illness level of function and was off prednisone for eight months, having been weaned successfully within two months after the initial course of steroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Her serum creatine kinase remained within the normal range throughout this interval, and no further elevations were noted, indicating a durable remission of the myopathic process. Cardiac imaging corroborated the clinical recovery; the myocardial edema and fractional wall motion abnormalities seen on the earlier cardiac MRI had resolved, and repeat echocardiography showed a normal ejection fraction with no persistent pulmonary hypertension. The absence of relapse after drug withdrawal and immunosuppressive therapy underscores the likely drug‑induced nature of the disorder rather than a chronic idiopathic myopathy that would typically require prolonged, intensive immunomodulation.

The causal relationship between semaglutide and the immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) was evaluated using the WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality framework, which classified the association as probable. The temporal proximity of symptom onset to the single semaglutide dose, the lack of alternative explanations (including statin exposure, nutritional supplements, or a new underlying connective‑tissue disease), and the clinical improvement following drug discontinuation and immunosuppressive therapy satisfy the criteria for a probable causality assessment. A formal rechallenge was contraindicated given the severity of the reaction, leaving the association to remain inferential yet strongly suggestive.

Several teaching points emerge from this case. First, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for drug‑induced myopathies in patients who develop unexplained proximal weakness, dysphagia, or marked CK elevation, especially when initiating medications with known metabolic effects such as GLP‑1 receptor agonists. A systematic work‑up—including comprehensive autoantibody panels, inflammatory markers, muscle imaging, and, when indicated, biopsy—facilitates timely diagnosis. Second, once a presumptive IMNM is established, prompt initiation of immunosuppression—here, high‑dose corticosteroids followed by IVIG—can lead to rapid biochemical and clinical recovery, averting extended disease morbidity. Third, the early tapering of steroids may be feasible when the offending agent is removed and the disease enters remission, as illustrated by the patient’s ability to discontinue prednisone within two months.

The underlying pathogenic mechanisms of GLP‑1 receptor agonist‑induced autoimmunity remain speculative. It is plausible that chronic activation of GLP‑1 receptors on immune cells or skeletal muscle may alter antigen presentation or induce low‑level autoimmunity in genetically or immunologically susceptible individuals. Alternatively, semaglutide’s influence on metabolic pathways could expose or generate novel antigens within muscle fibers, triggering a necrotising immune response. Future investigations should aim to delineate these mechanisms, perhaps through studies examining cytokine profiles, HLA typing, or the presence of T‑cell clones in affected patients.

Large, multicenter case series or pharmacovigilance databases will be essential to confirm the frequency of this adverse event and to identify predisposing factors, such as baseline autoantibody positivity (e.g., isolated SS‑B) or genetic markers. Prospective safety monitoring protocols for patients initiating GLP‑1 receptor agonists could include baseline CK and periodic surveillance in those reporting fatigue or myalgias. Reporting of similar episodes to drug adverse reaction databases will further refine signal detection and, ultimately, inform risk‑benefit assessments for these increasingly common agents.

**Take‑home lessons for clinicians:** (1) Recognise early signs of myopathy in patients on GLP‑1 receptor agonists; (2) obtain a comprehensive automated immunological work‑up and consider muscle imaging or biopsy when indicated; (3) discontinue the putative offending agent immediately and initiate immunosuppression promptly; (4) monitor CK closely, and be prepared to taper steroids rapidly in the setting of clinical stability and normal laboratory values; and (5) report such events to pharmacovigilance systems to contribute to the evolving safety profile of GLP‑1 receptor agonists.
