Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Rhabdo or Autoimmunity? Decoding CK Dynamics in Drug‑Induced Myopathies

### *Hello fellow clinicians!*

The first time I read about this 60‑year‑old woman who got one shot of semaglutide and then eight months later found herself paralysed and on a taper of steroids, I had half a brain‑wave of deja vu. We’ve written thousands of posts about "drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis" in the classic sense—an acute surge in CK to >10 000 U/L, myoglobinuria, the urgency of IV fluids and the relief of a declining CK curve. We’ve also studied “immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy” (IMNM) as a rare, chronic autoimmune process that usually unspools over weeks, may be HMGCR‑positive, and requires immunosuppression. 

What’s astonishing here is that a single dose of a GLP‑1 receptor agonist triggered a TIME‑dependent, progressive immune response that masqueraded as disruptive myopathy. The CK trajectory, imaging, EMG, and biopsy paint a picture that begs a deeper look at how we interpret CK dynamics to pull apart *acute toxin* from *autoimmune*.

---

## The Unlikely Story of a Semaglutide‑Induced Myopathy

Picture this: a woman with obesity and fatty liver—she’s basically a patient we’d put on a GLP‑1 agonist for weight loss and glycaemic control. She gets the first injection. Hours later she’s nauseated, vomiting, and ends up in the ER with an elevated creatinine kinase (CK) of 337 U/L (only a bit above the upper limit). She recovers, is discharged, and then, three months later, she’s unable to stand, can’t swallow anything, and her CK rockets to 3 992 U/L. The narrative that unfolds is fascinating: there are no statins, no illicit drugs, no connective‑tissue disease jumping up her history—all clues steered the team toward infection, malignant myopathy, or even a drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis. The muscle biopsy, intriguingly, shows necrotic fibers, macrophage infiltration, a membrane attack complex, but only a handful of T‑cells. In short: classic immunopathology of IMNM, yet the patient has a *seronegative* autoimmune autoantibody panel.

The grave takeaway? A single exposure to semaglutide can be the spark that triggers an on‑going autoimmune assault—contrary to the textbook notion that drug‑induced myopathies are dose‑dependent or happen with long‑term exposure.

## CK Kinetics: The Pulse of the Pathology

### Rhabdomyolysis: The Spiking ECG

- **Rapid, massive rise**: CK climbs within 24 h, often exceeding 10 000 U/L.
- **Acute peak**: CK maximum typically by day 2‑3; linear decline with adequate hydration.
- **Clear systemic toxin**: Myoglobinuria, dark urine, acute kidney injury (AKI). 
- **Therapy**: IV fluids, correction of electrolytes; removal of the offending agent or exhaustion of the toxic trigger. Immunosuppression is rarely needed.

In our patient, the CK began at 337 U/L and climbed steadily over four months—a far cry from the classic rhabdo spike. The lack of classic rhabdo signs (no cola‑colored urine, no overt AKI beyond the initial dehydration episode) underscores that the muscle injury was gradual, not an acute toxic shock.

### IMNM: The Floor‑Elevator

- **Steady climb**: CK steadily rises over weeks to months often reaching 3 000‑7 000 U/L or higher.
- **Persistent elevation**: CK stays elevated as long as the immune assault continues; it can plateau if the immune response is dampened.
- **No myoglobinuria**: Because bigger necrotic areas are disrupted slowly, myoglobin release is less dramatic.
- **Therapy**: High‑dose steroids ± IVIG, sometimes longer regimens if autoantibody‑positive.

The 12‑fold rise from 337 U/L to ~4 000 U/L in the case is textbook for IMNM and not what you’d anticipate in a simple drug‑induced myotoxicity. CK kinetic patterns, when paired with clinical evolution, are the first guideline in suspecting immune‑mediated disease over pure toxin injury.

## Imaging and EMG: The Other Giants

**Muscle MRI** can give a powerful visual cue:

- **Symmetric edema** in proximal muscle groups (quadriceps, hamstrings, deltoids).
- **Subcutaneous edema** occasionally seen; indicates an inflammatory process.
- **No fatty replacement** in acute stages (for chronic disease you’d see fatty infiltration).

The MRI of this patient revealed diffuse, symmetrical edema of the vastus lateralis and some surrounding loose‑tissue swelling—typical for an inflammatory myopathy rather than a confined “burn” (as one might see in a toxin‑caused crush injury where focal necrosis is more common).

**Electromyography (EMG)** gives the electrophysiologic footprint:

- **Myopathic motor unit potentials**: small amplitude, short duration, early recruitment.
- **Fibrillation potentials** may be present, but are more frequent in active necrosis.
- **Rhomb**: In rhabdo, EMG changes may be minimal early on, then progress to fibrillations.

Although the EMG results weren’t detailed in the report, the presence of a myopathic pattern would be expected in IMNM, whereas rhabdo EMG could be variable but less specific.

---

## What’s Happening Inside?

Histology remains the gold standard. In this patient, the vastus lateralis biopsy showed:

- **Necrotic and regenerating fibers**.
- **Acid phosphatase** and **CD68** staining—hallmarks of macrophage phagocytosis of dead myofibers.
- **Membrane attack complex** (MAC) on fibers, an antibody‑mediated complement attack.
- **Rare T‑cells** (CD3), reflecting the paucity of mononuclear infiltrates in IMNM.

These findings echo “necrosis” as the central event, but importantly guarded by an immune mechanism. The somatic antibodies (HMGCR, SRP) were missing–an expanse of seronegative IMNM that foreshadows that the classic antibodies are not the whole picture. This also hammers across the notion that absence of an autoantibody should not bar you from suspecting an immune‑mediated process if the clinical context fits.

## The Decision Point: Ethics, Evidence, and the Clinical Pathway

1. **Recognise the CK pattern**: A gradual rise over weeks is a red flag.
2. **Couple CK with clinical features**: Proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia.
3. **Audit the drug pathway**: Identify any recent medication changes—GLP‑1 agonists, statins, supplements.
4. **Rule out other mimics**: Rhabdomyolysis, metabolic myopathies, myositis with systemic disease.
5. **Order MRI & EMG**: Distinguish inflammatory vs. toxic patterns.
6. **Consider biopsy** (and autoantibody panel) if diagnosis remains ambiguous.
7. **Treat aggressively if autoimmune**: Steroids, IVIG, tapering early; but don’t shy away from early immunosuppression if the pattern is unmistakable.
8. **Stop the inciting drug**: Withdrawal is critical for resolution; re‑challenge would be unethical in a known serious event.
9. **Monitor CK and strength**: A declining CK curve and improvement in function indicate success.

The case under discussion exemplifies this pathway. Once the rising CK timeline and weakness were appreciated, a swift initiation of high‑dose methylprednisolone, followed by a 2‑g/kg IVIG course, coupled with drug withdrawal, yielded an excellent outcome—functional recovery, normal CK, and no recurrence after eight months off steroids.

---

## Clinical Pearls

- **Slow, persistent CK rise + proximal weakness + dysphagia** strongly suggests IMNM over acute rhabdo.  
- **Single dose of a GLP‑1 agonist** (semaglutide) can provoke an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy; do not treat it as a straightforward drug toxicity.  
- **MRI shows symmetrical proximal edema**; EMG demonstrates a myopathic pattern—together they push you toward an inflammatory etiology.  
- **Seronegative IMNM is common**; a negative autoantibody panel does *not* rule out disease.  
- **Short, aggressive immunosuppression** (steroids + IVIG) can be curative if instituted early, and a drug halt often leads to lasting remission.  

When you see a patient with a creeping CK elevation, “back‑to‑back muscle aches” and a heavy history of metabolic medication use, take the time to let CK breathe and rewrite the story to consider autoimmunity. It can change management from a “give her fluids” script to a “start her steroids and stop the GLP‑1” trajectory, and ultimately save muscle function.
