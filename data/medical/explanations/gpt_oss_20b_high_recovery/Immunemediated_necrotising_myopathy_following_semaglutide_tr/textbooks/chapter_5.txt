Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 5: ## 5. Outcome, Prognosis, and Clinical Implications

At the 10‑month evaluation, the patient had fully returned to her pre‑illness level of function and was off prednisone for eight months, having been weaned successfully within two months after the initial course of steroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Her serum creatine kinase remained within the normal range throughout this interval, and no further elevations were noted, indicating a durable remission of the myopathic process. Cardiac imaging corroborated the clinical recovery; the myocardial edema and fractional wall motion abnormalities seen on the earlier cardiac MRI had resolved, and repeat echocardiography showed a normal ejection fraction with no persistent pulmonary hypertension. The absence of relapse after drug withdrawal and immunosuppressive therapy underscores the likely drug‑induced nature of the disorder rather than a chronic idiopathic myopathy that would typically require prolonged, intensive immunomodulation.

The causal relationship between semaglutide and the immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) was evaluated using the WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality framework, which classified the association as probable. The temporal proximity of symptom onset to the single semaglutide dose, the lack of alternative explanations (including statin exposure, nutritional supplements, or a new underlying connective‑tissue disease), and the clinical improvement following drug discontinuation and immunosuppressive therapy satisfy the criteria for a probable causality assessment. A formal rechallenge was contraindicated given the severity of the reaction, leaving the association to remain inferential yet strongly suggestive.

Several teaching points emerge from this case. First, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for drug‑induced myopathies in patients who develop unexplained proximal weakness, dysphagia, or marked CK elevation, especially when initiating medications with known metabolic effects such as GLP‑1 receptor agonists. A systematic work‑up—including comprehensive autoantibody panels, inflammatory markers, muscle imaging, and, when indicated, biopsy—facilitates timely diagnosis. Second, once a presumptive IMNM is established, prompt initiation of immunosuppression—here, high‑dose corticosteroids followed by IVIG—can lead to rapid biochemical and clinical recovery, averting extended disease morbidity. Third, the early tapering of steroids may be feasible when the offending agent is removed and the disease enters remission, as illustrated by the patient’s ability to discontinue prednisone within two months.

The underlying pathogenic mechanisms of GLP‑1 receptor agonist‑induced autoimmunity remain speculative. It is plausible that chronic activation of GLP‑1 receptors on immune cells or skeletal muscle may alter antigen presentation or induce low‑level autoimmunity in genetically or immunologically susceptible individuals. Alternatively, semaglutide’s influence on metabolic pathways could expose or generate novel antigens within muscle fibers, triggering a necrotising immune response. Future investigations should aim to delineate these mechanisms, perhaps through studies examining cytokine profiles, HLA typing, or the presence of T‑cell clones in affected patients.

Large, multicenter case series or pharmacovigilance databases will be essential to confirm the frequency of this adverse event and to identify predisposing factors, such as baseline autoantibody positivity (e.g., isolated SS‑B) or genetic markers. Prospective safety monitoring protocols for patients initiating GLP‑1 receptor agonists could include baseline CK and periodic surveillance in those reporting fatigue or myalgias. Reporting of similar episodes to drug adverse reaction databases will further refine signal detection and, ultimately, inform risk‑benefit assessments for these increasingly common agents.

**Take‑home lessons for clinicians:** (1) Recognise early signs of myopathy in patients on GLP‑1 receptor agonists; (2) obtain a comprehensive automated immunological work‑up and consider muscle imaging or biopsy when indicated; (3) discontinue the putative offending agent immediately and initiate immunosuppression promptly; (4) monitor CK closely, and be prepared to taper steroids rapidly in the setting of clinical stability and normal laboratory values; and (5) report such events to pharmacovigilance systems to contribute to the evolving safety profile of GLP‑1 receptor agonists.
