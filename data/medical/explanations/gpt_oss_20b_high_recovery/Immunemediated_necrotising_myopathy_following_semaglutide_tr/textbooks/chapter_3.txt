Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 3: ## 3. Diagnostic Evaluation: Laboratory, Imaging, and Histopathology  

A systematic approach was required to define the patient’s progressive proximal weakness, to quantify ongoing muscle injury, and to differentiate an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from other causes of myopathy, including drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis, idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, and systemic autoimmune conditions. The diagnostic work‑up comprised serial creatine kinase (CK) measurements, liver function tests, thyroid studies, a comprehensive autoantibody panel, acute‑phase reactants, cardiac biomarkers, musculoskeletal and cardiac imaging, and ultimately, muscle and liver biopsies.  

### Serial Creatine Kinase Trend and Accompanying Transaminases  
CK elevation was the central laboratory finding that mirrored the clinical deterioration. Initial CK at the first hospitalisation was modest (337 U/L) and did not meet the threshold for rhabdomyolysis. Four months later, CK had surged to 3,992 U/L, a 12‑fold increase that persisted for days before commencing therapy. This pattern of a slow‑to‑peak but pronounced CK rise is characteristic of IMNM, contrasting with the abrupt, transient spike observed in drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis, where CK typically normalises within days of drug cessation.  

Simultaneous elevations in AST (191 U/L) and ALT (165 U/L) were noted. While transaminase rises can accompany myonecrosis, the moderate elevation together with a chronic hepatic steatosis diagnosed 3 years earlier made hepatic etiology unlikely. The liver biopsy confirmed steatohepatitis but excluded hepatocellular injury secondary to myopathic CK release.  

### Thyroid Function and Autoantibody Profile  
Thyroid studies, including TSH, were within reference limits, arguing against thyroid‑related myopathy, which usually presents with hyper‑ or hypothyroidism together with muscle weakness. An extensive myositis Autoantibody panel was negative for all anti‑mitochondrial, anti‑shr, anti‑Jo‑1, SRP, HMGCR, Mi‑2, and other disease‑specific antibodies. The only positive finding was a low‑titre SS‑B antibody (2.4, normal <1). In isolation, SS‑B positivity does not mandate a connective‑tissue disease and was interpreted as a possible underlying autoimmune predisposition rather than a pathogenic driver of the myopathy.  

The absence of anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP antibodies meant the case fell into the seronegative IMNM subset, which accounts for roughly a third of patients and necessitates histopathology for confirmation.  

### Inflammatory Markers  
Both ESR (22 mm/h) and CRP (22.8 mg/L) were elevated, reflecting a systemic inflammatory response. In the setting of a myopathy with minimal lymphocytic infiltration, modest elevation of acute‑phase reactants is common and supports an inflammatory process but is not specific for any one myositis subtype.  

### Cardiac Biomarkers and Imaging  
Cardiac troponin T was mildly elevated (62 pg/mL), and pro‑BNP was markedly increased at 4,358 pg/mL, suggesting subclinical myocardial stress. Cardiac MRI showed mild myocardial oedema (mid‑anteroseptal hypokinesis) without late gadolinium enhancement, while echocardiography revealed otherwise normal ventricular function. These findings are compatible with isolated myocarditis or heart‑strain but do not meet criteria for systemic inflammatory myopathies that involve significant cardiac compromise. Importantly, the absence of myocardial scarring or active inflammation on sequence made active myocarditis a less likely contributor to the myopathy.  

### Musculoskeletal Imaging  
MRI of the bilateral thighs demonstrated symmetric, diffuse subcutaneous and muscular oedema predominantly involving the vastus lateralis and quadriceps. Such a pattern is frequently seen in IMNM and reflects active muscle injury rather than chronic fatty infiltration. The absence of fatty replacement or rimmed vacuoles argues against inclusion‑body myositis, while lack of heterogenous enhancement or fascial involvement makes polymyositis less likely.  

### Muscle Biopsy  
The definitive diagnostic modality was the vastus lateralis muscle biopsy. Histology revealed numerous necrotic fibres and actively regenerating terminals, indicating ongoing myofibre injury. Acid phosphatase staining (positive) confirmed lysosomal enzyme activity within macrophage‑laden vacuoles, an entity called myophagocytosis. CD68 immunohistochemistry highlighted a dense macrophage infiltrate surrounding necrotic fibres. Complement deposition was visualised with C5b‑9 (membrane attack complex) staining on both necrotic and non‑necrotic myocytes, consistent with complement‑mediated cytotoxicity frequently described in IMNM.  

Crucially, T‐cell markers (CD3) were sparse, and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates were minimal. This paucity of lymphocytic inflammation distinguishes IMNM from polymyositis, which typically shows dense interstitial or endomysial CD8⁺ T‑cell infiltrates. The overall pathology—myonecrosis, macrophage‑rich phagocytosis, complement deposition, and few lymphocytes—issued a classic pattern for a necrotising myopathy.  

### Liver Biopsy  
Concurrent hepatic assessment was performed to confirm that the transaminase rise was not due to hepatotoxic injury from semaglutide or an occult hepatic inflammatory process. The liver specimen showed macrovesicular steatosis comprising ~70 % of the parenchyma and a NAFLD activity score of 5/8, consistent with the clinical picture of non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease. No interface hepatitis or significant portal inflammation existed, ruling out a hepatic source for the CK elevation.  

### Differential Exclusion  
1. **Statin‑induced myopathy** – The patient had never taken statins, and histology did not show persistent inflammation or pathology typical of statin‑induced necrosis.  
2. **Drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis** – The temporal profile (slow CK rise post‑single semaglutide dose), the need for immunosuppression, and histology rule out a pure toxic myopathy. Rhabdomyolysis would also typically present with myoglobinuria and renal injury not seen in this case.  
3. **Polymyositis** – Requires extensive T‑cell infiltrate and endomysial inflammation, absent in this biopsy.  
4. **Inclusion‑body myositis** – Age and gender are atypical, and biopsy lacks rimmed vacuoles; also shows degenerative lysosomal inclusions, which were not seen.  
5. **Myasthenia gravis** – Bilateral ocular or bulbar symptoms without fatigability, and normal repetitive nerve‑stimulus testing, discounted this.  
6. **Thyroid‑related myopathy** – Normal TSH and lack of overt thyroid dysfunction excluded this etiology.  

Collectively, the laboratory trajectory, imaging findings, and muscle histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy. The seronegative status and the temporal relationship to semaglutide use suggest a drug‑triggered autoimmune reaction, but the pattern clearly aligns with IMNM rather than statin‑induced toxicity or other inflammatory myopathies. The diagnostic work‑up therefore provided a robust framework for targeted immunosuppressive therapy and for safely discontinuing the suspected inciting agent.
