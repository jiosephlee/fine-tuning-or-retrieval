Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 4: ## 4. Management and Therapeutic Rationale

**High‑dose intravenous methylprednisolone pulse (1000 mg/day for 5 days) and expected corticosteroid response**  
In immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), the pathogenesis is thought to involve antibody‑ or T‑cell‑mediated complement‑driven myofibre necrosis, with macrophage‑mediated phagocytosis of myofibres as a hallmark of injury. High‑dose intravenous methylprednisolone is the cornerstone of initial therapy because it rapidly suppresses inflammation, reduces complement activation, and dampens the antigen‑specific immune response. The 1000‑mg daily dose for 5 days is equivalent to a 1‑mg/kg per‑day regimen for a patient of roughly 70 kg and is adopted from standard protocols for severe idiopathic inflammatory myopathies. In this patient, the pulse therapy led to a notable decline in creatine kinase (CK) from a peak of 3992 U/L to 1237 U/L within a few days, accompanied by early improvement in proximal muscle strength and the resolution of dysphagia. The rapid biochemistry response is indicative of a cytokine‑driven, rather than purely metabolic, disease process that is susceptible to potent immunosuppression.  

**Transition to oral prednisone 1 mg/kg/day and taper protocol**  
After the initial burst, the patient was continued on oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg/day (approximately 60 mg/day in this case). This dose is consistent with the recommendations of the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) for glucocorticoid‑unresponsive or steroid‑dependent myositis and is designed to maintain suppression of the autoimmune cascade while allowing for dose reduction. A typical taper in IMNM involves a 2‑to‑3‑week interval between dose reductions by 5–10 mg, with a total taper duration of 6‑12 months; however, early sustained improvement in CK and clinical strength often permits an earlier completion. In this patient, a rapid taper over 8 weeks was achieved after normalization of CK and consisted of decreasing doses by 5 mg every 2 weeks, ultimately allowing cessation of steroids within 2 months of admission.  

**Indication for intravenous immunoglobulin: 2 g/kg over 4 days in severe cases**  
IVIG was added because the patient continued to exhibit severe proximal weakness, dysphagia, and a CK elevation that remained above 1000 U/L despite prednisolone. IVIG at a cumulative dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days (0.5 g/kg/day), is considered a second‑line agent when high‑dose steroids alone do not rapidly restore strength or when the disease course is life‑threatening. The therapeutic mechanism in IMNM is presumed to involve neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies, inhibition of complement deposition, and modulation of Fcγ receptor expression on macrophages, thereby decreasing myofibre phagocytosis. In this patient, IVIG was administered after 5 days of pulse steroids and a short oral taper; CK fell below 400 U/L within two weeks, and the patient was able to maintain functional independence without further immunomodulation.  

**Monitoring of CK reduction, muscle strength, and potential steroid side effects**  
Serum CK was measured daily in the first week and twice weekly thereafter until values declined below 500 U/L, after which a monthly schedule sufficed. Clinical strength was quantified using the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale for proximal musculature, supplemented by timed functional tests (e.g., timed up‑and‑go, sit‑to‑stand). Early declines in CK usually correlated with improved MRC scores, providing reassurance that the inflammatory process was being arrested. Steroid side effects—including hyperglycaemia, hypertension, mood changes, and infection risk—were tracked through weekly blood pressure, random glucose and HbA1c (in a patient with type 2 diabetes) and regular complete blood count monitoring. Mild glucocorticoid‑induced hyperglycaemia was managed with adjustments to the patient’s antidiabetic regimen. No steroid‑induced diuresis or bone‑density loss was observed over the short course. IVIG was generally well tolerated; infusion reactions were mitigated with pre‑medication and slowed infusion rates, and no thromboembolic events were seen.  

**Role of GLP‑1 receptor agonist cessation and patient education to prevent rechallenge**  
Semaglutide was identified as the likely inciting agent, given its temporally proximal exposure and the absence of other recognized triggers (e.g., statins, mushrooms, or red yeast rice). Abrupt discontinuation was necessary to halt the underlying immune stimulation. Patients experiencing severe autoimmune sequelae to GLP‑1 agonists should receive formal counseling on the risks of rechallenge: the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) guidelines on severe drug reactions stipulate that re‑exposure is contraindicated when a strong temporal association and a plausible immunologic mechanism exist. Alternative anti‑obesity or antihyperglycaemic therapies (e.g., insulin, basal‑bolus, or sodium‑glucose co‑transporter 2 inhibitors) were discussed, and the patient’s weight and glucose control were monitored, demonstrating no regrowth of the obesity‑related comorbidity that originally prompted semaglutide use.  

**Management of complications: pseudomonal septic shock, urinary tract infection**  
During her hospitalization, the patient developed septic shock from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection. Management involved ICU support, broad‑spectrum antimicrobials (initial empiric coverage with a carbapenem and aminoglycoside, later tailored to culture susceptibilities), and aggressive fluid resuscitation. Sepsis was treated as an independent event; the acute management did not interfere with the immunosuppressive regimen, as studies in severe infections with concurrent steroid therapy show that high‑dose early steroids may reduce mortality in septic shock when initiated within the first 6 hours. No recurrence of elevated CK during or after the septic episode suggested that the complication did not trigger a new myopathic flare.  

**Follow‑up schedule: outpatient visits, CK monitoring, functional assessment**  
Following discharge, the patient attended a clinic visit 2 weeks after therapy initiation, during which CK was 180 U/L and her MRC score for upper and lower extremity proximal muscles had improved to 4/5. Subsequent visits occurred monthly for the first 3 months, then at 6 and 9 months. Serial CK measurements remained within normal limits, and functional assessments (e.g., 10‑meter walk, peg‑board test) showed complete recovery of baseline performance. Importantly, no evidence of relapse was seen up to 10 months post‑therapy, allowing cessation of all immunosuppressive medications and an extended period of drug‑free status. The follow‑up plan reinforces the principle that patients with presumed drug‑induced IMNM complete a finite course of immune modulation and then are monitored for recurrence via periodic CK and strength testing.  

In summary, this case highlights a pragmatic therapeutic pathway: a prompt high‑dose steroid pulse, transition to oral prednisone with a tailored taper, adjunctive IVIG in refractory or severe presentations, rigorous monitoring of biochemical and clinical parameters, and the elimination of the suspected inciting agent. The successful weaning off immunosuppression underscores that drug‑induced IMNM may resolve with brief, targeted therapy when the offending agent is eliminated and the autoimmune reaction is curbed.
