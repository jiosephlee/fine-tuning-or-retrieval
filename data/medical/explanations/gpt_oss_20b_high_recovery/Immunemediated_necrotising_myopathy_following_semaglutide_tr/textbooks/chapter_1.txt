Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 1: ## Background: GLP‑1 Receptor Agonists and Immune‑Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy  

Glucagon‑like peptide‑1 (GLP‑1) receptor agonists are a class of incretin‑based medicines that mimic the action of the endogenous gut hormone GLP‑1. They bind to the GLP‑1 receptor on pancreatic β‑cells, stimulating insulin secretion in a glucose‑dependent manner, while concurrently suppressing glucagon release and slowing gastric emptying. These pharmacologic effects translate clinically into improved glycaemic control and a marked reduction in body weight, which has led to their widespread use in type 2 diabetes and, more recently, in the treatment of obesity. Semaglutide (a long‑acting, once‑weekly subcutaneous analogue) and tirzepatide (a dual GLP‑1 and GIP receptor agonist) are the most frequently prescribed agents in this class.

Beyond the classic gastrointestinal adverse effects that prompt dose titration—nausea, vomiting, and diarrhoea—there have been sporadic reports of skeletal‑muscle de‑compensation. Weight loss, driven by appetite suppression, is an expected benefit, but several case reports have identified transient elevations in creatine kinase (CK) and, in some instances, frank rhabdomyolysis with peak CK values in the tens of thousands. These episodes tend to resolve rapidly after discontinuation of the drug and rehydration, suggesting a direct myotoxic mechanism rather than an immune‑mediated process. Nevertheless, the potential for a drug‑induced immunologic reaction has not been ruled out.

Immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct clinical entity within the spectrum of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies. It presents with rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness, markedly elevated CK, and minimal or absent systemic features of connective‑tissue disease. Muscle biopsy is the diagnostic cornerstone: it reveals clusters of necrotic and regenerating fibers surrounded by macrophages, often with abundant acid‑phosphatase activity, but with a paucity of T‑cell infiltrates—unlike polymyositis or dermatomyositis. The macrophage‑rich environment reflects humoral‑mediated injury rather than a classical cell‑mediated process.

Serological testing enriches the diagnostic ladder for IMNM. The most clinically relevant autoantibodies are directed against the enzyme 3‑hydroxy‑3‑methyl‑glutaryl‑coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR) and the signal recognition particle (SRP). Anti‑HMGCR antibodies are strongly associated with statin exposure, whereas anti‑SRP antibodies are often clinically accompanied by severe muscle weakness. Beyond these, the broader myositis panel can detect other myositis‑specific antibodies (e.g., Jo‑1, Mi‑2, MDA‑5), but these are less commonly implicated in IMNM. In roughly one‑third of patients, all autoantibody tests are negative; this seronegative subset poses a diagnostic challenge because the diagnosis must rely on the combination of clinical, histologic, and laboratory evidence.

The underlying immunopathogenesis of drug‑induced autoimmunity is not fully elucidated but is generally thought to involve one or more of three mechanisms. The hapten hypothesis posits that a small drug molecule covalently attaches to a self‑protein, creating a neo‑epitope that elicits an immune response. Molecular mimicry describes a scenario in which a drug or its metabolites share structural motifs with host proteins, leading to cross‑reactive autoantibodies. Epitope spreading occurs when the initial immune attack on a protein exposes additional proximal epitopes to the immune system, amplifying the autoimmune cascade. Statin‑associated IMNM, driven by anti‑HMGCR antibodies, exemplifies how a drug can initiate such a cascade.

In evaluating potential drug adverse events, the World Health Organization–Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO‑UMC) framework provides a systematic causality assessment. The assessment categorizes the relationship between a drug and an event into: certain, probable/likely, possible, unlikely, conditional/unclassified, and unassessable. It evaluates the temporal sequence, strength of evidence, the likelihood of alternative explanations, the response to de‑challenge, and, when available, the outcome of a rechallenge. In the case described, the close temporal relationship between the first semaglutide injection and the subsequent rise in CK, the absence of other plausible etiologies, and the clinical improvement after cessation of the drug and initiation of immunosuppression satisfy the criteria for a probable or likely causal link. This structured approach aids clinicians in distinguishing coincidental associations from genuine drug‑induced disease processes.
