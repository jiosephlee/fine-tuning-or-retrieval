Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical characteristics most strongly point toward maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) rather than type 1 or type 2 diabetes in a patient presenting before age 25?

**Clinical Pearls – When Early‑Onset Diabetes Is More Likely MODY Than Type 1 or Type 2**

The diagnosis of maturity‑onset diabetes of the young rests on recognizing a *pattern* of findings that diverges from classic type 1 (autoimmune β‑cell loss) and classic type 2 (obesity‑related insulin resistance). In the young adult described by Liu et al., several “red‑flag” characteristics pointed unequivocally toward HNF1A‑MODY:

| Feature | Why it favors MODY | How it appeared in the case |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------------------|
| **Strong autosomal‑dominant family history** | Multiple first‑degree relatives develop diabetes before age 30; transmission across generations is typical for MODY. | Maternal grandmother (30 s), mother (late 20s), younger brother (20) all diabetic. |
| **Absence of pancreatic autoantibodies** | Type 1 diabetes is almost always GAD-, IA‑2- or ZnT8‑positive; their absence makes autoimmune disease unlikely. | Both GADA and IA‑2A were negative. |
| **Preserved endogenous insulin secretion** | A stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L indicates viable β cells – inconsistent with the near‑absent secretion seen early in type 1. | Glucagon test: fasting C‑peptide 0.39 nmol/L → stimulated 0.83 nmol/L (adequate). |
| **Lack of marked insulin resistance** | Type 2 diabetes usually presents with obesity, high waist circumference, acanthosis nigricans, dyslipidaemia typical of metabolic syndrome. | BMI 24.6 kg/m², blood pressure normal, **no** acanthosis nigricans; triglycerides were high but overall phenotype was not classic metabolic syndrome. |
| **No ketosis or DKA at presentation** | Even with severe hyperglycaemia, MODY patients seldom develop ketoacidosis because enough insulin remains to suppress lipolysis. | The patient presented with hyperglycaemia (fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L) but no DKA. |
| **Disproportionate postprandial glucose rise & glycosuria** | HNF1A mutations lower the renal glucose threshold (SGLT2 down‑regulation); modest hyperglycaemia can produce glycosuria and striking post‑meal spikes. | OGTT 2‑h glucose 19.8 mmol/L; routine exam detected glycosuria despite fasting glucose only modestly elevated. |
| **Early age (<25) at diagnosis** | By definition MODY manifests before age 25; many type 2 patients are older at onset while type 1 often presents even earlier but with autoimmunity. | Patient was admitted as a “young adult” (<25). |

**Putting It All Together – Reasoning from the Case**

The constellation—*young age*, *strong vertical family history*, *negative autoantibodies*, *preserved C‑peptide*, *absence of clear insulin resistance*, *no DKA*, and *glycosuria disproportionate to fasting glucose*—gave a >60% probability on the Exeter MODY calculator for this patient. That high pretest probability justified definitive genetic testing, which uncovered a novel pathogenic heterozygous HNF1A variant (p.N270S). Confirmation allowed a rapid shift from basal insulin to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist + metformin regimen, achieving HbA₁c 6% within six months—a classic illustration of how accurate phenotypic recognition translates into precision therapy.

**Take‑Home Message for Residents**

When faced with a diabetic patient under 25 years old, ask yourself:

1. **Is there an autosomal‐dominant pedigree of early‐onset disease?**
2. **Are islet autoantibodies negative?**
3. **Is there measurable endogenous insulin (C‑peptide) despite hyperglycaemia?**
4. **Do anthropometrics/labs argue against significant insulin resistance?**
5. **Is there an absence of ketoacidosis?**
6. **Do you see glycosuria or exaggerated postprandial spikes out of proportion to fasting glucose?**

If ≥4–5 answers are “yes,” the pretest probability for MODY is high enough to proceed with targeted genetic testing—preferably an NGS panel covering HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, etc.—rather than assuming type 1 or type 2 disease and committing the patient to lifelong inappropriate therapy.

Recognizing these clues early averts unnecessary insulin exposure, guides drug choice (low‑dose sulfonylureas or GLP‑1RAs for HNF1A), informs family counseling, and ultimately improves long‑term outcomes through truly personalized medicine.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how loss-of-function mutations within the DNA-binding domain of HNF1A cause both impaired insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells and glycosuria.

**Pathophysiology of HNF‑1α DNA‑binding‑domain loss‑of‑function mutations – why they produce both β‑cell failure and glycosuria**

The hepatocyte nuclear factor‑1α (HNF‑1α) protein is a homodimeric transcription factor that binds a highly conserved “P‑box” motif in the promoters of dozens of genes expressed in liver, pancreas and kidney.  Its three functional regions are  

1. an N‑terminal **DNA‑binding domain (DBD)** (exons 1–4),  
2. a dimerisation domain, and  
3. a C‑terminal **transactivation domain**.  

In most HNF1A‑MODY cases – including the novel p.N270S variant described in Liu et al. – the pathogenic change resides in the DBD (exon 4).  The mutation replaces an asparagine that directly contacts DNA (position 270) with a serine.  This subtle substitution weakens DNA binding without destroying protein stability; the mutant allele therefore behaves as a **loss‑of‑function (haploinsufficient)** allele that cannot fully drive transcription of HNF‑1α target genes.

---

### 1️⃣ Impaired insulin secretion

In pancreatic β‑cells HNF‑1α regulates three critical pathways:

| HNF‑1α target | Role in β‑cell physiology | Consequence of DBD loss |
|--------------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| **INS** (insulin gene) | Direct transcription of pre‑proinsulin | ↓ insulin mRNA → ↓ stores for release |
| **GLUT2** (SLC2A2) | High‑capacity glucose transporter that senses extracellular glucose | ↓ GLUT2 → blunted glucose entry → inadequate rise in ATP/ADP ratio |
| **PKLR** (pyruvate kinase L/R) | Glycolytic enzyme that helps generate ATP after glucose uptake | ↓ PK activity → further reduction in ATP generation |

Because glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion hinges on the rise in intracellular ATP that closes K_ATP channels, any defect that diminishes glucose uptake (GLUT2) or ATP production (PKLR) translates into a **lower secretory response** even when β‑cell mass is preserved.  The glucagon stimulation test in the case report showed a modest rise in C‑peptide from 0.39 to 0.83 nmol/L, confirming that residual β‐cell function is present but suboptimal—a classic laboratory hallmark of HNF1A‐MODY.

---

### 2️⃣ Glycosuria – a renal phenotype

HNF‑1α is also expressed in the **proximal tubule** where it drives transcription of **SLC5A2**, the gene encoding the sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2).  SGLT2 reabsorbs ~90 % of filtered glucose; its activity sets the renal “threshold” (~180 mg/dL) above which glucose appears in urine.

A DBD mutation reduces SGLT2 mRNA and protein levels, lowering the transport capacity:

* **Reduced tubular reabsorption** → urinary glucose spill at plasma concentrations that would be normal for individuals with intact SGLT2.
* Clinically manifests as **glycosuria out of proportion to hyperglycemia**, as was observed in the patient’s routine health exam (urine dipstick positive despite only modestly elevated fasting glucose).

The authors explicitly note this mechanism: “glycosuria might be detected… attributed to a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption resulting from compromised glucose transport in renal tubules.”  Their case exemplifies it—early‐onset hyperglycemia plus unexplained glycosuria tipped them toward MODY testing.

---

### Integrated view

A single heterozygous missense change within the DBD produces *parallel* defects:

* **Pancreas** – blunted insulin gene expression and impaired glucose sensing → progressive β‐cell dysfunction ➜ postprandial hyperglycemia.
* **Kidney** – decreased SGLT2 transcription → low renal glucose threshold ➜ glycosuria at relatively low plasma glucose.

Because both organ systems rely on the same transcriptional regulator, loss of DNA binding yields a combined “diabetes + glycosuria” phenotype that distinguishes HNF1A‐MODY from type 1 or classic type 2 diabetes.

---

### Teaching pearls

| Point | Clinical relevance |
|------|--------------------|
| **DNA‑binding domain mutations → haploinsufficiency** | Dominant inheritance; one mutant allele suffices to lower target gene expression by ~50 % |
| **Low renal threshold = early glycosuria** | A bedside clue: positive urine glucose with only mild hyperglycemia should raise suspicion for HNF1A-MODY |
| **β‑cell response is blunted but not absent** | Stimulated C‑peptide is often >0.6 nmol/L early on—helps differentiate from type 1 diabetes |
| **Therapeutic implications** | Sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 agonists are preferred; SGLT2 inhibitors are relatively contraindicated because baseline SGLT2 activity is already reduced and may precipitate volume depletion or ketoacidosis |
| **Genotype–phenotype link** | The p.N270S variant sits at a highly conserved residue within the DBD—its pathogenicity is corroborated by ACMG criteria, family segregation, and functional predictions (AlphaMissense 0.995). |

In summary, loss-of-function mutations in the DNA-binding domain of HNF1A impair transcription of insulin‐related genes in β‑cells *and* SGLT2 in proximal tubular cells.  The resulting dual defect explains why patients like the young man described by Liu et al. present simultaneously with impaired insulin secretion and glycosuria—a hallmark that should prompt early genetic testing and precision therapy.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In evaluating a young adult with hyperglycaemia, which laboratory investigations help distinguish type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and HNF1A-MODY, and what result thresholds support each diagnosis?

**Laboratory work‑up that separates type 1 diabetes (T1D), type 2 diabetes (T2D) and HNF‑1α MODY in a young adult**

When a patient under 25 years presents with hyperglycaemia, the first laboratory step is a focused panel that tells us three things simultaneously: (1) whether an autoimmune attack on β‑cells is present, (2) how much endogenous insulin secretion remains, and (3) whether there are biochemical hallmarks of insulin resistance or of the renal‐threshold defect typical of HNF1A‑MODY.

| Test | How it discriminates | Thresholds that point toward each entity |
|------|----------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **Islet auto‑antibodies** – GAD65 (GADA), IA‑2A, ZnT8 | Presence = autoimmune β‑cell loss → T1D; Absence = non‑autoimmune → T2D or MODY | **Positive** (any titre ≥ 20 IU/mL for GADA or equivalent) → **T1D**<br>**Negative** (all three < 20 IU/mL) → **T2D/MODY** |
| **C‑peptide** – fasting & stimulated (glucagon or mixed meal) | Low values = little residual secretion → T1D;<br>Normal‑high values = retained β‑cell mass → T2D;<br>Intermediate or modest rise after stimulation suggests a genetically mediated secretory defect → MODY | **Fasting < 0.20 nmol/L** (< 0.5 ng/mL) or stimulated increase < 0.30 nmol/L → **T1D**<br>**Fasting ≥ 0.60 nmol/L** (> 1.5 ng/mL) and stimulated ≥ 1.00 nmol/L → **T2D**<br>**Fasting 0.30–0.60 nmol/L** with stimulated rise to **≈ 0.6–0.9 nmol/L** (as in the reported patient whose glucagon test rose from 0.39→0.83 nmol/L) → **HNF‑1α MODY** |
| **OGTT 2‑h plasma glucose** | All three disorders can exceed 11.1 mmol/L, but a disproportionate post‑prandial spike with relatively lower fasting glucose is classic for MODY | **Fasting ≥7 mmol/L + 2‑h ≥11.1 mmol/L** is required for diabetes in all groups; a pattern of “fasting modestly ↑ / post‑load very high” favours MODY when coupled with other labs |
| **Serum lipids & BMI** | Dyslipidaemia (high TG, low HDL) and obesity are markers of insulin resistance → T2D; normal lipids and lean stature are more common in MODY | **BMI ≥30 kg/m²**, triglycerides > 2·3 mmol/L, HDL < 1·03 mmol/L → supports **T2D**<br>**BMI <27 kg/m²**, normal TG/HDL – does not exclude T2D but leans toward **MODY** |
| **Urine glucose relative to serum glucose** | In HNF1A‐MODY the renal SGLT2 transporter is under‑expressed; glycosuria appears at lower plasma glucose than the usual renal threshold (~10 mmol/L). | Detectable urine glucose when serum glucose ≤9–10 mmol/L → suggestive of **HNF‑1α MODY** |

### How the presented case fits the algorithm

*Autoantibodies* were negative (GADA and IA‑2A < 20 IU/mL), steering away from T1D despite his age.<br>
*Stimulated C‑peptide* rose only to 0.83 nmol/L—below the range typically seen in insulin‑resistant T2D (> 1 nmol/L) yet above the “absent” zone of classic T1D—exactly the pattern described for HNF1A‐MODY.<br>
His *BMI* was 24.6 kg/m² and triglycerides were markedly elevated (4.31 mmol/L); while this could mimic metabolic syndrome of T2D, the absence of acanthosis nigricans and a striking autosomal dominant family history tipped the balance toward MODY.<br>
Finally, *glycosuria* was present despite no ketoacidosis and at a serum glucose that did not far exceed the normal renal threshold—a hallmark of reduced SGLT2 activity caused by HNF‑1α deficiency.<br>
When these laboratory clues converged—negative antibodies + modestly preserved but insufficient C‑peptide + early‐onset disease + family history—the team pursued NGS testing, which identified a pathogenic heterozygous p.N270S variant in HNF1A confirming HNF‑1α MODY.

### Bottom line

- **Negative islet antibodies + any measurable C‑peptide rules out classic T1D**, but you still need to know whether β‑cell reserve is robust (T2D) or only partially intact (MODY).  
- **Fasting C‑peptide ≈ 0.3–0.6 nmol/L with a stimulated rise to ≈ 0.6–0·9 nmol/L is the sweet spot for HNF1A‐MODY**, especially when accompanied by early onset, an autosomal dominant pedigree and glycosuria at relatively low plasma glucose.<br>
- **Elevated C‑peptide (> 0·8 nmol/L fasting; > 1·5 nmol/L after stimulation), obesity/metabolic syndrome features and negative antibodies support T2D**, whereas markedly low values (< 0·20 nmol/L fasting or < 0·3 nmol/L after stimulation) confirm T1D regardless of age.

These targeted investigations allow you to narrow the differential quickly enough to move on to definitive genetic testing when MODY is suspected—as illustrated by Liu et al.’s patient whose biochemical profile guided an accurate molecular diagnosis and an individualized treatment plan.​

### [Morning Report] The patient was initially started on basal insulin, metformin, and saxagliptin before genetic results returned. After confirming an HNF1A pathogenic variant his regimen was switched to a GLP-1 receptor agonist plus metformin while discontinuing insulin. Explain the clinical reasoning behind stopping insulin and choosing a GLP-1 agonist rather than reintroducing a sulfonylurea.

**Clinical Reasoning for Discontinuing Insulin and Selecting a GLP‑1 Receptor Agonist Instead of a Sulfonyl‑urea in HNF1A‑MODY**

**1. Goal of therapy after a definitive MODY diagnosis**  
When the pathogenic *HNF1A* variant was identified, the clinical picture changed from “unspecified early‑onset diabetes” to a monogenic disorder in which **β‑cell dysfunction is the primary defect** rather than insulin resistance or autoimmune destruction. The therapeutic aim therefore shifts from simply lowering glucose to:

* preserving what endogenous β‑cell reserve remains,
* avoiding agents that cause glucose‐independent insulin release,
* minimizing hypoglycaemia and weight gain,
* using drugs that exploit the residual incretin‑responsive pathway.

**2. Why stop basal insulin?**  

| Reason | Evidence from the case |
|--------|------------------------|
| **Insulin was only a bridge** – it provided rapid control while awaiting genetics (initial HbA₁c = 10.2 %). | “Initial treatment … basal insulin … reflecting a preliminary consideration of T2D.” |
| **β‑cell function was still present** – glucagon‐stimulated C‑peptide = 0.83 nmol/L (>0.60 nmol/L suggests preserved secretion). | “Glucagon stimulation test showed … 0.83 nmol/L at 6 min, indicating insufficient insulin secretion…” |
| **Long‐term exogenous insulin adds weight gain, injection burden and carries hypoglycaemia risk**, which are unnecessary when endogenous secretion can be leveraged. | No data suggest ongoing need; after one month HbA₁c fell to 9.1 % and genetics clarified the mechanism. |

Therefore, once glucose fell modestly and the mechanism was known, **insulin could be withdrawn safely**.

**3. Sulfonyl‑ureas are conventional first line in HNF1A‑MODY but were deliberately avoided**  

* **Mechanism of action:** Sulfonyl‑ureas close the ATP‑sensitive K⁺ channel independent of ambient glucose, forcing β‑cells to secrete insulin even when plasma glucose is low.
* **Risk profile in HNF1A deficiency:**  
  * The *HNF1A* mutation already lowers the renal threshold for glucose (glycosuria) and diminishes GLUT2 expression; adding a drug that drives glucose‑independent release heightens **hypoglycaemia susceptibility**, especially as β‑cell mass wanes.  
  * In this patient the stimulated C‑peptide was modest; further “push” by a sulfonyl‑urea could precipitate severe lows or accelerate β‑cell exhaustion.
* **Weight considerations:** Sulfonyl‑ureas tend to cause modest weight gain—undesirable in this young adult who already has features of metabolic syndrome (triglycerides = 4.31 mmol/L).  
* **Evidence from the report:** “GLP‑1 receptor agonist therapy presents a lower risk of mild hypoglycaemia compared with glimepiride” (Discussion). A small crossover trial cited in the article showed **equivalent glycaemic efficacy** between liraglutide and glimepiride but **greater safety** for the GLP‑1 agent.

Thus, while sulfonyl‑ureas remain an accepted option for many *HNF1A* carriers, this particular patient’s residual β–cell reserve, propensity for hypoglycaemia, and need for weight control made them less favourable.

**4. Why a GLP‑1 receptor agonist?**  

| Pharmacologic feature | How it matches HNF1A-MODY physiology |
|------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Glucose‑dependent amplification of insulin secretion** – acts only when plasma glucose is elevated | Protects against hypoglycaemia despite low β‑cell mass |
| **Suppression of inappropriate glucagon** | Counteracts postprandial excursions common in MODY |
| **Weight loss & improved lipid profile** | Addresses the patient’s mildly elevated triglycerides and BMI = 24.6 kg/m² |
| **Preservation of β‑cell function** | Incretin signalling may attenuate ongoing β‑cell decline versus chronic sulfonyl‑urea stimulus |
| **Convenient weekly dosing (dulaglutide)** | Facilitates adherence in a young adult after an intensive initial phase |

In this case, dulaglutide 1.5 mg weekly, added to metformin, drove HbA₁c from 9.1 % to 6.0 % within three months—a magnitude comparable to intensive sulfonyl‑urea therapy but without any documented hypoglycaemia episodes.

**5. Sequential de‐escalation strategy**

After stable control with dulaglutide + metformin, the treating team simplified therapy again to metformin + saxagliptin for maintenance—a regimen already part of his pre‐genetic workup. This stepwise approach illustrates:

* Use of an incretin‐based agent as a *bridge* when transitioning off insulin,
* Reassessment of β‐cell reserve before committing long‐term to sulfonyl‑urea,
* Application of precision medicine: tailoring drug class choice based on genotype‐specific pathophysiology rather than default algorithms.

---

### Take–Home Points

1️⃣ **Insulin can be safely discontinued once MODY is confirmed and modest glycaemic improvement is achieved**, because endogenous β‐cell function—though reduced—is still exploitable.

2️⃣ **Sulfonyl‑ureas remain effective in *HNF1A*-MODY but carry higher hypoglycaemia risk**, especially when residual β‐cell mass is limited (stimulated C–peptide ≈ 0.8 nmol/L) and when patients have metabolic derangements that make weight gain undesirable.

3️⃣ **GLP‑1 receptor agonists offer glucose‐dependent insulin augmentation, weight loss, cardiovascular benefit, and lower hypoglycaemia potential**, making them an excellent choice either as definitive therapy or as an interim bridge after stopping insulin.

4️⃣ The case exemplifies how rapid genetic confirmation converts an empiric T2D regimen into a precision approach—​discontinuing unnecessary insulin and substituting an agent that aligns with the underlying transcriptional defect in *HNF1A*.

### [Management] What is considered first-line pharmacotherapy for patients with confirmed HNF1A-MODY according to current guidelines, and what are the principal benefits and drawbacks relative to GLP-1 receptor agonists as beta-cell function declines?

**First‑line therapy for HNF1A‑MODY**  
Current consensus statements (e.g., ISPAD 2022, ADA/Endocrine Society 2023, NICE MODY pathway) designate **low‑dose sulfonylureas** as the preferred initial drug for patients with genetically confirmed HNF1A‑MODY. A typical regimen uses a thiazolidinedione‑sparing agent such as gliclazide 30–80 mg daily or glimepiride 0.5–2 mg daily—doses that are ½–¼ of those used in conventional type 2 diabetes because HNF1A patients are markedly hypersensitive to K_ATP‑channel blockade.

*Why sulfonylureas work* – HNF1A mutations impair transcription of the GLUT2 transporter and key glycolytic enzymes in β‑cells, blunting glucose‑stimulated insulin release. The surviving β‐cells retain an intact ATP‑sensitive potassium channel, so a modest sulfonylurea pulse can bypass the defective glucose sensor and trigger insulin secretion. In the case report the authors note this principle explicitly: “First‑line therapy involves low‑dose sulfonylureas…”.

**Principal benefits compared with GLP‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RA)**  

| Feature | Low‑dose Sulfonylurea | GLP‑1 RA |
|---|---|---|
| **Efficacy in early disease** | Immediate robust ↓ fasting & postprandial glucose; HbA1c reductions 2–3 % reported | Comparable glucose lowering when β‐cell reserve is still present (crossover trial cited in report) |
| **Cost & convenience** | Inexpensive oral tablet; no injection | Higher drug cost; weekly subcutaneous injection (e.g., dulaglutide) |
| **Weight effect** | Neutral to modest weight gain | Consistent weight loss (~2–4 kg) |
| **Hypoglycaemia risk** | Elevated once endogenous insulin output falls → non‐glucose‐dependent secretion | Minimal because secretion is glucose‑dependent |
| **β‑cell preservation** | No proven protective effect; chronic overstimulation may accelerate failure | Potentially β‑cell sparing via incretin signalling (still theoretical in MODY) |
| **Cardiovascular data** | None specific for MODY | CV benefit demonstrated for several agents in T2D (may be extrapolated) |

**Why GLP‑1 RA become attractive as β‐cell function declines**  
When stimulated C‐peptide falls toward ≤0.6 nmol/L (the patient’s value was 0.83 nmol/L), the absolute insulin reserve shrinks. Sulfonylureas then provoke disproportionate insulin release relative to ambient glucose, increasing the likelihood of mild–moderate hypoglycaemia—a concern highlighted in the case discussion (“…more effective alternative…particularly in individuals prone to hypoglycaemia”). GLP‑1 RAs preserve the glucose dependence of insulin secretion and avoid this pitfall while also offering weight loss and a favourable safety profile. In the reported case, after genetic confirmation the team discontinued basal insulin, initiated dulaglutide (weekly GLP‑1 RA), achieved an HbA₁c drop from 10.2 % to 6.0 % within three months, and later stepped down safely to metformin + saxagliptin.

**Drawbacks of GLP‑1 RA relative to sulfonylureas**  

* Injection burden and higher expense may limit long‐term adherence, especially in younger patients.
* Gastrointestinal adverse effects (nausea, vomiting) are common during titration.
* Evidence base in MODY remains limited—most data derive from small crossover trials or case series such as this one; thus they occupy a secondary position in guideline hierarchies.
* In advanced β‐cell failure (stimulated C‐peptide <0.3 nmol/L), even GLP‑1 RAs lose efficacy and insulin becomes unavoidable.

**Practical algorithm derived from the case and guidelines**

1. **Confirm HNF1A mutation** → proceed with precision therapy.
2. **If C‑peptide ≥0.6 nmol/L & HbA₁c modestly elevated (<8–9 %)** → start low‑dose sulfonylurea ± metformin.
3. **If C‑peptide borderline (0.4–0.6 nmol/L), HbA₁c >9 % or documented hypoglycaemia on sulfonylurea** → transition to a GLP‑1 RA (weekly formulation simplifies adherence).
4. **When C‑peptide <0.3 nmol/L or glycaemic control deteriorates despite oral agents**, introduce basal insulin while maintaining metformin for hepatic glucose suppression.
5. **Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors** in HNF1A‐MODY because reduced SGLT2 expression predisposes to dehydration and ketoacidosis—a point underscored by the authors’ discussion.

In short, **low‑dose sulfonylureas remain the guideline‑endorsed first line for HNF1A‑MODY**, capitalising on the mutation’s heightened drug sensitivity and low cost. As β‐cell capacity erodes, **GLP‑1 receptor agonists offer a safer glucose‑dependent alternative**, albeit at higher price and with injection requirements. The presented patient exemplifies this therapeutic shift—initial rapid control with a sulfonylurea-like approach was supplanted by dulaglutide once genetic confirmation clarified his underlying pathology and highlighted impending β‐cell decline.

### [Pathophysiology] Why might sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors pose an especially high risk of dehydration or ketoacidosis in individuals with HNF1A-MODY?

**Why SGLT‑2 inhibitors are especially hazardous in HNF1A‑MODY**

Patients with HNF1A‑MODY have a *primary defect in the transcription factor HNF‑1α*. One of the genes under direct control of HNF‑1α is **SLC5A2**, which encodes the renal sodium‑glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT‑2). Loss‑of‑function mutations in HNF1A therefore produce a *baseline reduction* in tubular SGLT‑2 expression. The clinical vignette illustrates this point: the young man presented with **spontaneous glycosuria** despite a normal renal threshold for glucose because his proximal tubules could not reabsorb glucose efficiently.

When an **SGLT‑2 inhibitor** is added on top of this genetically induced “partial knock‑down,” three intersecting mechanisms converge to precipitate severe dehydration and euglycaemic ketoacidosis (euDKA):

| Pathophysiologic element | What happens in HNF1A‑MODY | Effect of an SGLT‑2 inhibitor | Clinical consequence |
|---------------------------|----------------------------|------------------------------|----------------------|
| **Renal glucose handling** | ↓ SGLT‑2 expression → lower renal threshold → chronic glucosuria | Near‑complete block of any residual tubular glucose reabsorption | Massive osmotic diuresis → loss of water + Na⁺ → intravascular volume depletion |
| **Insulin secretion** | Progressive β‑cell dysfunction (stimulated C‑peptide ≈ 0.8 nmol/L) – modest residual insulin | No direct effect, but falling plasma glucose from osmotic diuresis reduces the insulin‐to‐glucose ratio | Relative insulinopenia drives peripheral lipolysis |
| **Counter‑regulatory hormones** | Baseline modest hyperglucagonemia (common in MODY) | SGLT‑2 inhibition itself raises glucagon and reduces endogenous insulin secretion via altered glucotoxicity | Enhanced hepatic ketogenesis despite only modest hyperglycaemia |
| **Autonomic reserve** | The case showed severely reduced sudomotor function → blunted thirst response | → Patient may not compensate by increasing fluid intake | Further predisposes to dehydration |

The *combined* osmotic loss of water + sodium plus an impaired ability to raise insulin quickly creates a perfect storm for **euDKA**:

* Plasma glucose may stay only modestly elevated (or even normal), masking the metabolic crisis.
* The patient’s limited β‑cell reserve cannot curb free fatty acid mobilisation.
* Elevated glucagon (both intrinsic to MODY and drug‑induced) shunts hepatic metabolism toward ketone production.
* Volume depletion activates catecholamines and RAAS, worsening peripheral perfusion and impairing renal clearance of ketones.

Hence, unlike typical type 2 diabetes where the kidneys retain full SGLT‑2 capacity, **HNF1A-MODY patients start life already “half‑inhibited.”** An additional pharmacologic blockade can push urinary glucose loss from mild (≈ 50–100 g/day) to **excessive (> 300 g/day)** amounts within hours, precipitating rapid dehydration, hypotension, and ketoacidosis even before marked hyperglycaemia appears.

**Take‑home pearls for the morning report**

1. **HNF1A regulates renal SGLT‑2 expression.** Loss‑of‑function mutations produce a low renal glucose threshold and baseline glycosuria.
2. **SGLT‑2 inhibitors act on already compromised transporters**, causing disproportionate osmotic diuresis and natriuresis.
3. **Beta‑cell dysfunction in MODY limits compensatory insulin secretion**, while SGLT‑2 inhibition raises glucagon—both drive lipolysis and ketogenesis.
4. **Autonomic dysfunction (e.g., reduced sudomotor activity) may blunt thirst**, accelerating dehydration.
5. **Clinical implication:** Avoid or use extreme caution with SGLT‑2 inhibitors in confirmed or strongly suspected HNF1A‑MODY; prefer agents that amplify endogenous insulin secretion (low-dose sulfonylureas) or enhance incretin signaling (GLP‑1 RAs), as illustrated by the successful transition from insulin to dulaglutide and metformin in this patient.

By understanding how a single transcription‐factor defect amplifies the pharmacologic actions of an SGLT‑2 inhibitor, we can prevent iatrogenic euDKA and preserve safe fluid balance in this genetically defined subgroup.

### [Differential Diagnosis] The identified variant c.809A>G p.N270S lies in exon 4 DNA-binding domain of HNF1A and is not catalogued in ClinVar or VarSome. List three ACMG criteria satisfied by this finding that justify its classification as pathogenic or likely pathogenic.

| **PP 3** *(supporting)* | **Multiple lines of computational evidence predict a deleterious effect** | In silico tools uniformly scored the change as damaging: an **AlphaMissense score of 0.995**, together with concordant predictions from REVEL, SIFT and PolyPhen‑2 (as reported by the authors). This fulfills the “multiple computational algorithms → deleterious” requirement. |

*(A fourth line of evidence—co‑segregation of the variant with diabetes in three affected first‑degree relatives—is mentioned in the report (“segregates with diabetes in the family”) and would satisfy **PP 1** (supporting); however only three criteria were asked for.)*

### Why these criteria matter
- **PM 1** tells us that disruption of the DNA‑binding interface is mechanistically plausible because this domain mediates transcriptional activation of insulin‑related genes (GLUT2, pyruvate kinase). Missense changes here have repeatedly proven pathogenic in HNF‑​1A‐MODY.
- **PM 2** eliminates the possibility that p.N270S represents a common benign allele—essential when working with rare monogenic disorders where background variation can be misleading.
- **PP 3** provides independent bioinformatic corroboration that the biochemical properties of Asn→Ser at position 270 likely impair protein folding or DNA interaction, reinforcing the functional inference drawn from its location.

### Putting it together
According to ACMG/AMP rules, **≥2 moderate + ≥1 supporting** → *Likely Pathogenic*.  
If future data show robust functional loss (e.g., EMSA demonstrating abolished DNA binding → **PS3**) or stronger segregation (**PP 1 Strong**) then an upgrade to *Pathogenic* would be justified—exactly what Liu et al. concluded when they labeled the allele “LP/P”.

---

**Take‑home point:**  
When you encounter a novel missense change in a well‑characterized gene such as **HNF‑​1A**, systematically evaluate (i) its position relative to known disease hotspots (**PM 1**), (ii) its presence/absence in population databases (**PM 2**) and (iii) concordant computational predictions (**PP 3**). Together these pillars often provide enough weight to classify an otherwise undocumented variant as clinically actionable—and guide precise therapy, as illustrated by this young man whose treatment was swiftly switched from insulin to GLP‑​1RA after genetic confirmation.

### [Morning Report] The glucagon stimulation test showed a fasting C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L rising to 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes. How does this result influence diagnostic certainty for MODY versus type 1 diabetes, and how does it guide therapeutic choice?

**Morning‑Report Pearls – Interpreting a Glucagon‑Stimulated C‑Peptide in a Young Adult with Early‑Onset Diabetes**

**Key Finding**  
Glucagon stimulation produced a fasting C‑peptide of **0.39 nmol/L** that rose to **0.83 nmol/L at 6 min**. In most laboratories a *stimulated* C‑peptide > 0.60 nmol/L is taken as evidence of **preserved endogenous insulin secretion**; values below this are regarded as indicating marked β‑cell failure.

---

### 1️⃣ What does the pattern tell us about **MODY vs. type 1 diabetes**?

| Condition | Typical fasting C‑peptide | Stimulated response | Expected clinical adjuncts |
|-----------|----------------------------|----------------------|----------------------------|
| **HNF1A‑MODY** (early–mid disease) | Low‑normal or mildly reduced (≈0.3–0.8 nmol/L) | **> 0.60 nmol/L** (often ~0.8–2 nmol/L) – modest rise | Negative autoantibodies, strong autosomal‑dominant family history |
| **Type 1 diabetes** (established) | Very low or undetectable (< 0.2 nmol/L) | Minimal change (< 0.05 nmol/L) – essentially flat | Positive GADA/IA‑2/ZnT8 antibodies, rapid decline in C‑peptide over months |

In the presented case the **stimulated level reaches 0.83 nmol/L**, crossing the preservation threshold despite a low fasting value. This pattern is *inconsistent* with advanced autoimmune β‑cell loss and *compatible* with **partial β‑cell dysfunction typical of HNF1A‐MODY**, where secretory capacity declines gradually rather than being abruptly abolished.

When paired with:

* **Negative islet autoantibodies** (GADA, IA‑2A),
* A **multigenerational early‑onset diabetes pedigree**, and
* Absence of ketoacidosis,

the glucagon test **raises the pre‑test probability for MODY from modest to high**, supporting proceeding to definitive genetic testing—as was done in the report (NGS uncovered a pathogenic p.N270S HNF1A variant).

---

### 2️⃣ How does this result shape **therapeutic choice**?

| Therapeutic option | Rationale based on C‑peptide results |
|--------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Insulin (continuous)** | Needed when β‐cell output is negligible (stimulated C‑peptide < 0.30 nmol/L) → typical of type 1 or long‐standing MODY with exhausted reserve |
| **Sulfonylurea (low dose)** | Exploits residual ATP‐sensitive K⁺ channel activity; effective when *any* endogenous secretion remains (stimulated C‑peptide > 0.60 nmol/L). Historically first line in HNF1A‐MODY |
| **GLP‑1 receptor agonist** (e.g., dulaglutide) | Enhances glucose‑dependent insulin release *and* reduces glucagon; useful when β‐cell reserve is modest but declines further—avoids hypoglycaemia seen with sulfonylureas in borderline patients |
| **Metformin ± DPP‑4 inhibitor** | Addresses hepatic gluconeogenesis & incretin tone without relying on β‐cell mass; safe adjunct once glycaemia is near target |

In this patient:

* The modestly preserved stimulated C‑peptide signaled that the pancreas could still respond to secretagogues.
* After confirming an HNF1A mutation, clinicians **discontinued basal insulin**, switched first to a GLP‑1RA (**dulaglutide**) to harness residual secretion while mitigating hypoglycaemia risk, and later simplified therapy to metformin + saxagliptin once HbA₁c fell to 6 %.
* Had the stimulated C‑peptide remained < 0.30 nmol/L despite negative antibodies, a decision to maintain insulin would have been justified, as genuine type 1 diabetes would be far more likely.

---

### Take‑Home Message

- A **glucagon‐stimulated C‑peptide > 0.60 nmol/L** argues strongly for *preserved β‑cell function*. In a young adult with negative autoantibodies and an autosomal dominant family history, this finding tips the balance toward **HNF1A‐MODY**, not type 1 diabetes.
- Demonstrated residual secretion enables **non‑insulin strategies**—low‐dose sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 receptor agonists—​which are both more physiologic and cost‐effective for HNF1A‐MODY.
- The test should be viewed as a *supportive biological filter*: it does not replace genetic confirmation but sharpens diagnostic certainty enough to justify molecular testing and tailor therapy away from lifelong insulin when appropriate.

> **Clinical pearl:** In any young adult with unexplained hyperglycaemia, obtain a glucagon stimulation test *early*. A stimulated C‑peptide above the preservation cutoff markedly raises suspicion for MODY and opens the door to precision therapy—​a classic illustration of “biology before branding.”

### [Clinical Pearls] Given an autosomal dominant pathogenic HNF1A mutation identified in an index case, outline an evidence-based cascade testing strategy for first-degree relatives and the appropriate follow-up monitoring if they test positive.

**Cascade Testing for a Pathogenic HNF1A Variant – A Practical “Morning‑Report” Pearl**

*Index case*: A young man was found to carry a heterozygous pathogenic HNF1‑A missense change (p.N270S).  His family history already included three affected relatives (maternal grandmother, mother and a younger brother).  The diagnosis enabled a rapid shift from basal insulin to a GLP‑1‑RA–based regimen with durable glycaemic control.  

**What we do next – systematic cascade testing of first‑degree relatives**

| Step | What we do | Why it matters |
|------|------------|----------------|
| **1️⃣ Pre‑test counseling** | A clinical geneticist or trained diabetes counselor meets each adult relative (and parents of minor children) to explain autosomal‑dominant inheritance (50 % transmission risk), variable age‑of‑onset (~70 % develop diabetes by age 25), possible treatment implications and psychosocial issues. | Informed consent is required under ACMG recommendations for cascade screening of dominantly inherited conditions[^1]. |
| **2️⃣ Identify the target population** | All **first‑degree** relatives: mother, father (if alive), siblings, and any children of these individuals. In this family the obvious candidates are (1) the mother – who already has early‑onset diabetes, (2) the father – who may be an obligate non‑carrier or silent carrier, and (3) the younger brother who was diagnosed at 20 y. | First‑degree contacts have the highest prior probability because each carries one allele from the proband’s parent[⁴]. |
| **3️⃣ Targeted genetic assay** | Instead of re‑running a full MODY panel, order a **single‑site PCR/ Sanger sequencing** (or a rapid targeted NGS assay) that interrogates the known c.809A>G (p.N270S) change. For distant relatives who cannot attend clinic in person, saliva kits can be mailed with identical assay design. | Cost‐effective (≈ US$50–100 per test) while preserving analytical sensitivity >99 % for a known variant[⁵]. |
| **4️⃣ Post‑test counseling & documentation** | • **Positive result** → confirm carrier status, discuss penetrance, outline personalized surveillance & treatment plan.<br>• **Negative result** → reassure no increased MODY risk but remind them that other forms of diabetes may still occur.<br>All results entered into the electronic health record with an alert flag for future clinicians. | Provides a clear “genetic flag” that drives downstream management decisions—as shown by the rapid de‑escalation from insulin in our proband once his genotype was known. |
| **5️⃣ Repeat cascade as family expands** | When a carrier has children or when additional siblings are identified, repeat step 3 automatically (“family‐wide” approach). | Early detection in younger generations prevents years of suboptimal therapy[⁶]. |

---

### Follow‑up Monitoring Once a Relative Tests Positive

Management splits into two pathways – **asymptomatic carriers** vs **carriers who already meet diabetes criteria**.

#### 1️⃣ Asymptomatic Carriers (no overt hyperglycaemia)

| Timing / Test | Rationale |
|---------------|-----------|
| **Baseline** – fasting plasma glucose (FPG), HbA₁c, 75 g OGTT ± C‑peptide<br>Renal panel & lipid profile | Establish current glycaemic status; OGTT most sensitive because HNF1A mutations often manifest as isolated postprandial spikes.[⁷] |
| **Every 12 months** – FPG + HbA₁c <br>If HbA₁c ≥ 5.7 % OR OGTT ≥ 11.1 mmol/L → proceed to treatment algorithm | Progressive β‑cell failure is expected; yearly labs catch conversion before complications develop.[⁸] |
| **Every 2–3 years** – repeat full OGTT </br>(or mixed meal tolerance test if OGTT not feasible) | Detect subtle deterioration in glucose tolerance that may not be reflected by HbA₁c alone owing to relatively low fasting glucose.[⁹] |
| **Annual review** – blood pressure, BMI, waist circumference, lipid panel | MODY patients have lower prevalence of metabolic syndrome than T2D but obesity may emerge over time; early lifestyle intervention reduces future cardiovascular risk.[¹⁰] |
| **Education & lifestyle** – advise low‐glycaemic diet, regular aerobic exercise and avoidance of excessive fructose intake which can unmask hyperglycaemia in HNF1A mutations.[¹¹] | Nonpharmacologic measures delay need for medication.^[12] |

#### 2️⃣ Carriers Who Meet Diabetes Criteria

| Intervention | Details & Evidence |
|--------------|-------------------|
| **First‑line pharmacotherapy** – low‑dose sulfonylurea (e.g., gliclazide 40–80 mg daily) OR GLP‑1 receptor agonist if hypoglycaemia risk high or β–cell reserve limited | Randomised crossover data show sulfonylureas and liraglutide produce comparable glucose lowering in HNF1A‑MODY—but sulfonylureas carry higher hypoglycaemia rates.[¹³] The index patient’s successful switch to dulaglutide illustrates how genotype‐directed therapy can spare insulin use and preserve β‐cell function.[¹⁴] |
| **If HbA₁c >9 % at diagnosis**, consider short course basal insulin until sulfonylurea/GLP‑1 titration stabilises glucose.| Mirrors initial approach taken in our case before genetic confirmation enabled de‑escalation.[¹⁵] |
| **Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors** unless compelling cardiovascular/kidney indication and careful hydration monitoring. | HNF1A loss reduces renal SGLT2 expression → baseline glycosuria; further inhibition risks marked dehydration and euglycaemic ketoacidosis – reported in MODY literature.[¹⁶] |
| **C‑peptide monitoring every 6–12 months** (fasting & stimulated) | Guides whether sulfonylurea dose remains effective or whether β‑cell reserve has waned enough to necessitate alternative agents.^[17] |
| **Standard ADA complication screening**, triggered after *5 years* of confirmed hyperglycaemia or at age ≥10 whichever comes first:<br>• Annual urine albumin/creatinine ratio<br>• Retinal exam every 2–5 years <br>• Foot exam if peripheral neuropathy present  | Although microvascular disease develops later than in type 1/2 diabetes, early onset means cumulative exposure can be significant.[¹⁸] The proband showed no microalbuminuria after six months—reassuring but still requires ongoing surveillance.|

---

### Putting It All Together – The Take‑Home Message

*Because an autosomal dominant HNF1A mutation confers a roughly 50 % chance per offspring and high penetrance by young adulthood,* systematic cascade testing of all first‑degree relatives is both evidence based and cost effective[^19]. Targeted genotyping eliminates unnecessary broad panels while delivering rapid results that directly inform management—just as it allowed our index patient’s therapy to be refined from insulin to a GLP‑1RA/metformin regimen within weeks.

For carriers without overt diabetes, annual fasting glucose/HbA₁c plus biennial OGTT provide an early warning system before β‑cell exhaustion becomes irreversible. Once diabetes manifests, low‐dose sulfonylureas—or increasingly preferred GLP‑1 receptor agonists—are the cornerstone therapies because they exploit residual β‐cell function without the hypoglycaemia risk associated with higher doses used in type 2 disease. Routine complication screening follows standard ADA timelines but should begin promptly given the often earlier age at onset characteristic of HNF1A‐MODY.

In short:

> **Identify every first-degree relative → offer targeted p.N270S testing → counsel both positive and negative results → monitor carriers annually with fasting glucose/HbA₁c + OGTT every 2–3 years → treat confirmed diabetes with genotype‐directed agents while avoiding SGLT2 inhibitors → institute complication surveillance on schedule.**

This algorithm converts an isolated genetic finding into a family‐wide precision health plan—a model that exemplifies modern “genomics in practice” discussed at morning report today.
