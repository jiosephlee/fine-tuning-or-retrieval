Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical characteristics most strongly point toward maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) rather than type 1 or type 2 diabetes in a patient presenting before age 25?

**Clinical Pearls – When Early‑Onset Diabetes Is More Likely MODY Than Type 1 or Type 2**

The diagnosis of maturity‑onset diabetes of the young rests on recognizing a *pattern* of findings that diverges from classic type 1 (autoimmune β‑cell loss) and classic type 2 (obesity‑related insulin resistance). In the young adult described by Liu et al., several “red‑flag” characteristics pointed unequivocally toward HNF1A‑MODY:

| Feature | Why it favors MODY | How it appeared in the case |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------------------|
| **Strong autosomal‑dominant family history** | Multiple first‑degree relatives develop diabetes before age 30; transmission across generations is typical for MODY. | Maternal grandmother (30 s), mother (late 20s), younger brother (20) all diabetic. |
| **Absence of pancreatic autoantibodies** | Type 1 diabetes is almost always GAD-, IA‑2- or ZnT8‑positive; their absence makes autoimmune disease unlikely. | Both GADA and IA‑2A were negative. |
| **Preserved endogenous insulin secretion** | A stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L indicates viable β cells – inconsistent with the near‑absent secretion seen early in type 1. | Glucagon test: fasting C‑peptide 0.39 nmol/L → stimulated 0.83 nmol/L (adequate). |
| **Lack of marked insulin resistance** | Type 2 diabetes usually presents with obesity, high waist circumference, acanthosis nigricans, dyslipidaemia typical of metabolic syndrome. | BMI 24.6 kg/m², blood pressure normal, **no** acanthosis nigricans; triglycerides were high but overall phenotype was not classic metabolic syndrome. |
| **No ketosis or DKA at presentation** | Even with severe hyperglycaemia, MODY patients seldom develop ketoacidosis because enough insulin remains to suppress lipolysis. | The patient presented with hyperglycaemia (fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L) but no DKA. |
| **Disproportionate postprandial glucose rise & glycosuria** | HNF1A mutations lower the renal glucose threshold (SGLT2 down‑regulation); modest hyperglycaemia can produce glycosuria and striking post‑meal spikes. | OGTT 2‑h glucose 19.8 mmol/L; routine exam detected glycosuria despite fasting glucose only modestly elevated. |
| **Early age (<25) at diagnosis** | By definition MODY manifests before age 25; many type 2 patients are older at onset while type 1 often presents even earlier but with autoimmunity. | Patient was admitted as a “young adult” (<25). |

**Putting It All Together – Reasoning from the Case**

The constellation—*young age*, *strong vertical family history*, *negative autoantibodies*, *preserved C‑peptide*, *absence of clear insulin resistance*, *no DKA*, and *glycosuria disproportionate to fasting glucose*—gave a >60% probability on the Exeter MODY calculator for this patient. That high pretest probability justified definitive genetic testing, which uncovered a novel pathogenic heterozygous HNF1A variant (p.N270S). Confirmation allowed a rapid shift from basal insulin to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist + metformin regimen, achieving HbA₁c 6% within six months—a classic illustration of how accurate phenotypic recognition translates into precision therapy.

**Take‑Home Message for Residents**

When faced with a diabetic patient under 25 years old, ask yourself:

1. **Is there an autosomal‐dominant pedigree of early‐onset disease?**
2. **Are islet autoantibodies negative?**
3. **Is there measurable endogenous insulin (C‑peptide) despite hyperglycaemia?**
4. **Do anthropometrics/labs argue against significant insulin resistance?**
5. **Is there an absence of ketoacidosis?**
6. **Do you see glycosuria or exaggerated postprandial spikes out of proportion to fasting glucose?**

If ≥4–5 answers are “yes,” the pretest probability for MODY is high enough to proceed with targeted genetic testing—preferably an NGS panel covering HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, etc.—rather than assuming type 1 or type 2 disease and committing the patient to lifelong inappropriate therapy.

Recognizing these clues early averts unnecessary insulin exposure, guides drug choice (low‑dose sulfonylureas or GLP‑1RAs for HNF1A), informs family counseling, and ultimately improves long‑term outcomes through truly personalized medicine.
