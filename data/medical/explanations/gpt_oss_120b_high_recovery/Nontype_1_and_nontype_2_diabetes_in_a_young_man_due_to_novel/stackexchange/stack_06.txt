Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Pathophysiology] Why might sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors pose an especially high risk of dehydration or ketoacidosis in individuals with HNF1A-MODY?

**Why SGLT‑2 inhibitors are especially hazardous in HNF1A‑MODY**

Patients with HNF1A‑MODY have a *primary defect in the transcription factor HNF‑1α*. One of the genes under direct control of HNF‑1α is **SLC5A2**, which encodes the renal sodium‑glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT‑2). Loss‑of‑function mutations in HNF1A therefore produce a *baseline reduction* in tubular SGLT‑2 expression. The clinical vignette illustrates this point: the young man presented with **spontaneous glycosuria** despite a normal renal threshold for glucose because his proximal tubules could not reabsorb glucose efficiently.

When an **SGLT‑2 inhibitor** is added on top of this genetically induced “partial knock‑down,” three intersecting mechanisms converge to precipitate severe dehydration and euglycaemic ketoacidosis (euDKA):

| Pathophysiologic element | What happens in HNF1A‑MODY | Effect of an SGLT‑2 inhibitor | Clinical consequence |
|---------------------------|----------------------------|------------------------------|----------------------|
| **Renal glucose handling** | ↓ SGLT‑2 expression → lower renal threshold → chronic glucosuria | Near‑complete block of any residual tubular glucose reabsorption | Massive osmotic diuresis → loss of water + Na⁺ → intravascular volume depletion |
| **Insulin secretion** | Progressive β‑cell dysfunction (stimulated C‑peptide ≈ 0.8 nmol/L) – modest residual insulin | No direct effect, but falling plasma glucose from osmotic diuresis reduces the insulin‐to‐glucose ratio | Relative insulinopenia drives peripheral lipolysis |
| **Counter‑regulatory hormones** | Baseline modest hyperglucagonemia (common in MODY) | SGLT‑2 inhibition itself raises glucagon and reduces endogenous insulin secretion via altered glucotoxicity | Enhanced hepatic ketogenesis despite only modest hyperglycaemia |
| **Autonomic reserve** | The case showed severely reduced sudomotor function → blunted thirst response | → Patient may not compensate by increasing fluid intake | Further predisposes to dehydration |

The *combined* osmotic loss of water + sodium plus an impaired ability to raise insulin quickly creates a perfect storm for **euDKA**:

* Plasma glucose may stay only modestly elevated (or even normal), masking the metabolic crisis.
* The patient’s limited β‑cell reserve cannot curb free fatty acid mobilisation.
* Elevated glucagon (both intrinsic to MODY and drug‑induced) shunts hepatic metabolism toward ketone production.
* Volume depletion activates catecholamines and RAAS, worsening peripheral perfusion and impairing renal clearance of ketones.

Hence, unlike typical type 2 diabetes where the kidneys retain full SGLT‑2 capacity, **HNF1A-MODY patients start life already “half‑inhibited.”** An additional pharmacologic blockade can push urinary glucose loss from mild (≈ 50–100 g/day) to **excessive (> 300 g/day)** amounts within hours, precipitating rapid dehydration, hypotension, and ketoacidosis even before marked hyperglycaemia appears.

**Take‑home pearls for the morning report**

1. **HNF1A regulates renal SGLT‑2 expression.** Loss‑of‑function mutations produce a low renal glucose threshold and baseline glycosuria.
2. **SGLT‑2 inhibitors act on already compromised transporters**, causing disproportionate osmotic diuresis and natriuresis.
3. **Beta‑cell dysfunction in MODY limits compensatory insulin secretion**, while SGLT‑2 inhibition raises glucagon—both drive lipolysis and ketogenesis.
4. **Autonomic dysfunction (e.g., reduced sudomotor activity) may blunt thirst**, accelerating dehydration.
5. **Clinical implication:** Avoid or use extreme caution with SGLT‑2 inhibitors in confirmed or strongly suspected HNF1A‑MODY; prefer agents that amplify endogenous insulin secretion (low-dose sulfonylureas) or enhance incretin signaling (GLP‑1 RAs), as illustrated by the successful transition from insulin to dulaglutide and metformin in this patient.

By understanding how a single transcription‐factor defect amplifies the pharmacologic actions of an SGLT‑2 inhibitor, we can prevent iatrogenic euDKA and preserve safe fluid balance in this genetically defined subgroup.
