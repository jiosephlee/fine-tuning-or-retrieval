# Biting Into Sepsis: Recognizing *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* After Dog Bites  

*When a seemingly harmless dog bite spirals into dialysis‑dependent kidney injury, you know you’re dealing with something rare—but not impossible.*

---

## Why this case grabs your attention  

Four days after a playful bite on her lip, a previously healthy 48‑year‑old woman was crashing into septic shock with profound leukopenia, DIC and an AKI that progressed to anuria. Blood cultures finally grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*—a fastidious gram‑negative rod most of us only see on an occasional microbiology board question. The twist? She was immunocompetent, yet the infection set off a cascade that culminated in complement‑mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS) requiring eculizumab and dialysis.  

The story is a perfect reminder that “ordinary” animal bites can hide an extraordinary pathogen—one that masquerades as routine sepsis until it hits you with microvascular thrombosis and kidney failure. If your emergency department or ICU isn’t primed to suspect *C. canimorsus*, you’ll miss the window for early targeted therapy and may hand your patient an avoidable future on dialysis.

---

## Epidemiology at a glance  

* *C. canimorsus* lives peacefully in the oral flora of dogs (≈ 80 % prevalence) and cats (≈ 30 %).  
* Human infection is rare—estimated incidence 0.5–0.7 cases per million person‑years—but its mortality approaches 30 % when it does occur.  
* Classic “high‑risk” hosts are asplenic patients, chronic alcoholics, cirrhotics or those on immunosuppressants. However, up to one‑third of reported cases occur in otherwise healthy adults—our patient being a textbook example.  

Key epidemiologic point: **any dog bite**—even from a well‑behaved family pet—has the potential to transmit *C. canimorsus*. The pathogen’s rarity makes it easy to overlook; its lethality makes it impossible to ignore once recognized.

---

## Clinical spectrum – from cellulitis to catastrophic multi‑organ failure  

The presentation is protean:

| Presentation | Frequency | Typical timeline |
|--------------|-----------|-----------------|
| Local wound infection (cellulitis/abscess) | ~60 % | Hours–days |
| Septic shock ± DIC | ~30 % | 2–5 days |
| Fulminant hemorrhagic pneumonia | Rare | ≤ 1 week |
| Hemolytic uremic syndrome / TMA | Very rare | 5–10 days post‑infection |

Our patient followed the dreaded trajectory: initial fever and malaise → rapid progression to refractory hypoglycaemia → DIC → AKI → delayed MAHA/TMA → CM‑HUS. The timing is instructive—the microangiopathic component manifested **after** her inflammatory markers were already receding, underscoring how secondary TMA can hide behind improving sepsis labs.

Clinical clues that tip you off early:

* **Severe leukopenia** (often < 1 × 10⁹/L) out of proportion to the apparent infection.
* **Disproportionate coagulopathy** (low fibrinogen, prolonged PT/aPTT) early in the course.
* **Non‑healing “cold” extremities** potentially reflecting microthrombi even before overt DIC.
* **Rapidly falling glucose** refractory to IV dextrose—an odd hallmark reported in several *C.* infections.

---

## Bite‑related risk factors – beyond “just a scratch”

1️⃣ **Depth matters** – puncture wounds through mucosa or near vascular structures provide direct access for organisms residing deep in animal saliva.

2️⃣ **Delayed antibiotics** – our patient never filled her prescription because she couldn’t collect it on time. Even a single dose of amoxicillin-clavulanate within 12 h dramatically cuts bacteraemia rates.

3️⃣ **Prophylactic gaps** – tetanus vaccination alone does nothing against bacterial flora; lack of appropriate antimicrobial coverage is the true missed opportunity.

4️⃣ **Host conditions** – while our patient lacked classic risk factors, subtle impairments (e.g., transient neutropenia from viral prodrome) can tip balance toward invasive disease.

Takeaway: **any bite that breaks skin integrity warrants prompt antimicrobial prophylaxis**, irrespective of perceived “low risk”.

---

## Diagnostics – culture versus molecular

### Traditional culture

* Grows best on chocolate agar under CO₂ enrichment.
* Time to positivity: 4–7 days (often longer because organism is notoriously slow).
* Sensitivity suffers when patients receive antibiotics before sampling—a common scenario in ED settings.
* Misidentification possible due to its Gram‐negative rod morphology resembling *Pasteurella* spp.

### Molecular tools

* **16S rRNA PCR** from blood or tissue dramatically shortens time‑to‑identification (hours). 
* **Real‑time nanopore sequencing** offers bedside pathogen detection within ~6 h and provides species‐level resolution even after antibiotics started.
* These platforms also detect resistant gene signatures—useful when selecting empiric agents for critically ill patients.

Practical tip: If you suspect *C.* spp., request **both conventional culture + molecular assay** early. Even if cultures stay negative after a week, a positive PCR will guide targeted therapy and prevent unnecessary broadening later on.

---

## Empiric antimicrobial regimens – covering zoonotic threats up front

The goal is simple: start an agent active against *Capnocytophaga*, *Pasteurella*, anaerobes and occasional MRSA if local prevalence dictates.

| Scenario | First line (no allergy) | Penicillin allergy |
|----------|------------------------|--------------------|
| Dog bite ≥ moderate severity | **Ampicillin‑sulbactam** 3 g q6h (or amoxicillin/clavulanate PO/IV) | **Doxycycline + metronidazole** OR **clindamycin + fluoroquinolone** |
| Severe sepsis / shock | Add **gentamicin** or use **piperacillin/tazobactam** if Gram‐negative rods suspected | Use **meropenem + vancomycin** followed by de‑escalation |
| Immunocompromised host | Consider adding **vancomycin** because *Staph aureus* could coexist | Same principle plus coverage for resistant organisms |

Key nuances:

- *C.* spp are generally susceptible to β‑lactams including penicillin G; resistance remains very low (<1 %). 
- In patients already receiving broad‐spectrum agents (e.g., piperacillin/tazobactam), keep an eye on renal dosing—especially crucial if AKI evolves quickly.
- When initiating eculizumab later for CM‑HUS, ensure meningococcal vaccination + prophylactic antibiotics (**penicillin G or ceftriaxone**) are administered at least two weeks before complement blockade—or give them concurrently if timing isn’t feasible—as done successfully in our case.

---

## When sepsis is just the tip of the iceberg – TMA & CM-HUS  

The cascade from *C.* infection → endothelial activation → uncontrolled alternative complement pathway gives rise to TMA manifestations:

1️⃣ Schistocytes appear once platelet consumption crosses threshold → MAHA.
2️⃣ Platelet count remains low despite transfusions.
3️⃣ Renal microthrombi lead quickly from AKI to anuria.
4️⃣ ADAMTS13 activity typically hangs around 40–50 % (vs <10 % in TTP).
5️⃣ Complement studies may look deceptively normal early on—normal C3/C4 does NOT exclude CM-HUS!

Therapeutic sequence observed:

1️⃣ Prompt plasma exchange while awaiting complement testing—provides temporary “reset”.
2️⃣ Early eculizumab when bacteremia cleared reduces terminal complement activation → rapid haemolysis resolution and diuresis return.
3️⃣ Continued renal support while monitoring for relapse after eculizumab taper/discontinuation.

Bottom line for clinicians: **If your septic patient develops late‐onset hemolysis + persistent thrombocytopenia + worsening kidney function**, think TMA—and urgently send ADAMTS13, Shiga toxin PCR, complement panels—and start plasma exchange while arranging eculizumab access if CM-HUS looks likely.

---

## Practical pearls & high‑yield takeaways  

### Clinical pearls  

- **Think *C.* early:** Any dog bite followed by fever within 72 h plus leukopenia/DIC = red flag.
- **Don’t wait for cultures:** Initiate appropriate β‑lactam coverage even before microbiology confirms *Capnocytophaga*.  
- **Molecular diagnostics are game changers:** Real‑time nanopore sequencing can shave days off identification—a crucial advantage when organ failure looms.
- **Normal C3/C4 ≠ rule out CM-HUS:** Complement activation may be compartmentalized; rely on functional assays and clinical picture rather than static levels.
- **Eculizumab timing matters:** Start after blood cultures sterilize but before irreversible renal injury occurs; vaccinate against meningococcus beforehand or use prophylactic antibiotics aggressively.
- **Watch for secondary TMA:** Even as inflammatory markers improve, new hemolysis signals an overlapping pathology—not treatment failure of sepsis alone.

### Take‐home messages  

- **Prompt antimicrobial prophylaxis after any dog bite saves lives**—a single dose of amoxicillin‐clavulanate within hours is worth more than an entire ICU stay later on.  
- **If severe sepsis follows a bite**, include *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* in your differential regardless of host immune status.  
- **Order both blood cultures and rapid PCR/sequencing concurrently** when suspicion is high; do not let negative early cultures lull you into complacency.  
- **When MAHA appears during recovery**, transition quickly from sepsis management to TMA workup—ADAMTS13 >10 % redirects you away from plasma exchange alone toward complement blockade.  
- **Eculizumab works even without identifiable complement gene mutations**, provided infection has triggered uncontrolled terminal pathway activation.  

---

### Bottom line

A dog bite isn’t just a nuisance—it can be the portal for a stealthy gram‐negative rod capable of hijacking the complement system and destroying kidneys within days. Early recognition hinges on pattern recognition (leukopenia + DIC after bite), rapid empiric β‐lactam therapy, employing modern molecular diagnostics when cultures lag, and being primed to pivot toward TMA treatment if hematologic derangements arise later in the course. In short: ***bite → cover → confirm → watch for TMA → treat aggressively***—and you’ll keep more patients out of dialysis units one paw at a time.

--- 

> ***FOAM takeaway:** Keep your “dog bite” checklist handy on every shift—if anyone walks in with fever + low WBC after a canine encounter, give them amox/clav now and order both cultures *and* PCR*. Your early move could be the difference between full recovery and lifelong dialysis.*



# Septic TMA Unraveled: Distinguishing DIC, TTP, STEC‑HUS & Complement‑Mediated HUS  

---  

## Why this case caught my eye  

A previously healthy woman in her late 40 s was bitten on the lip by her own dog. Four days later she was screaming from fever, hypoglycaemia and mottled extremities—classic septic shock territory. Blood cultures grew **Capnocytophaga canimorsus**, a fastidious gram‑negative rod we all love to forget until it turns up as fulminant sepsis with DIC, pancytopenia, and multiorgan failure.  

She survived the initial onslaught, but on day 14—while still anuric—her haemoglobin plunged from 111 g/L to 68 g/L without any bleeding source. Schistocytes littered her smear, haptoglobin vanished, LDH surged, and platelets stubbornly lingered around 30–40 × 10⁹/L. The question blossomed in every multidisciplinary meeting: **Is this lingering toxicity simply “more DIC” or has a true thrombotic microangiopathy taken over?**  

The answer turned out to be **complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS)** needing plasma exchange as a bridge and prompt eculizumab therapy—a dramatic pivot from treating pure sepsis/DIC to blocking terminal complement activity. This journey encapsulates the diagnostic whirlpool that every emergency physician or intensivist must navigate when septic patients develop hemolysis, thrombocytopenia, and AKI.

---

## The big picture: When Do Sepsis‑Related Coagulopathy and Thrombotic Microangiopathy Overlap?  

| Feature | Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) | Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) | STEC‑HUS | Complement‑mediated HUS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Severe infection, trauma, malignancy | Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10 %) | Shiga toxin–producing *E.coli* | Genetic/autoimmune complement dysregulation + “second hit” (infection, pregnancy…) |
| **Platelet count** | Often <50 × 10⁹/L but can recover quickly with source control | Usually <30 × 10⁹/L | Variable; often moderate ↓ | Moderate ↓ (30–100 × 10⁹/L) |
| **Coagulation tests** | ↑ PT/INR, ↑ aPTT, ↓ fibrinogen, ↑ d‑dimer | Usually normal | Normal | Normal |
| **Hemolysis** | Often modest—mechanical hemolysis + consumption | Marked MAHA + schistocytes >5% | Prominent MAHA | Prominent MAHA |
| **Renal involvement** | Variable; often secondary to hypotension/microthrombi | Less severe early on | Severe AKI common | Severe AKI common |
| **ADAMTS13 activity** | Normal/near‑normal | <10% (often <5%) with inhibitor | Normal | Normal |
| **Shiga toxin PCR** | Negative | Negative | Positive | Negative |
| **Complement labs** | Typically normal C3/C4 but not evaluated routinely | Normal C3/C4 – no role | May be normal | C3/C4 may be normal; functional AH50 reduced during flare |

The overlap is most striking between DIC and CM‑HUS because both can arise from severe infection—and both produce consumptive thrombocytopenia plus organ dysfunction. The differentiator rests on **coagulation studies** and **the timing of hemolysis**.

---

## Step‑by‑step algorithm for the bedside clinician  

### 1️⃣ Recognise the classic triad  
*Acute hemolysis + thrombocytopenia + acute kidney injury* in any patient—especially if they are already septic—should trigger a “microangiopathy workup.” In our patient the triad materialised after day 8 of ICU stay when inflammation was already waning.

### 2️⃣ Grab the coagulation panel immediately  
- **PT/INR**, **aPTT**, **fibrinogen**, **d‑dimer**  
If PT/aPTT are prolonged, fibrinogen is low (<150 mg/dL), d‑dimer is sky‑high → leaning toward DIC.
If these values are largely normal → think TMA.  

Our patient’s initial labs showed prolonged PT/aPTT and low fibrinogen consistent with overt DIC; however by day 12 they normalized while hemolysis surged—red flag for superimposed TMA.

### 3️⃣ Examine the peripheral smear promptly  
Schistocytes ≥1–2% (>5% is classic for TMA). Keep an eye out for blasts or dysplastic cells that could point toward leukemia or HLH—both were ruled out in this case by flow cytometry and bone marrow aspirate.

### 4️⃣ Calculate PLASMIC score while waiting for ADAMTS13  
*P*latelet count <30 × 10⁹/L  
*L*actate dehydrogenase markedly elevated  
*A*ge <60 y  
*S*evere renal dysfunction (Cr >2 mg/dL)  
*M*ean arterial pressure ≤90 mmHg  
*I*nternational normalized ratio (INR) <1.5  
*C*hanges—or lack thereof—in other causes  

A score ≥6 predicts high risk of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency → start plasma exchange empirically. In our patient PLASMIC=4 → low pretest probability; ADAMTS13 later returned at 43 %. This saved her from an unnecessary prolonged course of daily plasma exchange that would have been futile in CM‑HUS.

### 5️⃣ Rule out Shiga toxin rapidly  
Stool PCR for stx1/stx2 or culture on sorbitol MacConkey agar if GI prodrome present. Positive → STEC‑HUS → mainly supportive care (+/- dialysis). Our patient’s PCR was negative—STECH excluded.

### 6️⃣ Think complement once other TMAs are excluded  
If ADAMTS13 >10%, Shiga toxin negative *and* coagulation profile normal → consider complement‐mediated HUS.
- Draw ***C3***/*C4*** (often misleadingly normal).
- If available before plasma exchange: functional AH50 assay—low activity suggests pathway consumption.
- Send extended genetic panel if feasible—but do NOT delay therapy waiting for results.
In this case AH50 was dramatically reduced after plasma exchange/first eculizumab dose—consistent with treatment effect rather than a primary defect; genetics returned negative for pathogenic variants.

### 7️⃣ Initiate definitive therapy without delay  

| Diagnosis | First‐line therapy |
|---|---|
| TTP | Daily plasma exchange + steroids ± rituximab |
| STEC‑HUS | Supportive: fluids, dialysis; avoid antibiotics unless extraintestinal infection |
| CM‑HUS | Plasma exchange *as bridge* → **Eculizumab** (or ravulizumab) after meningococcal vaccination + prophylactic antibiotics |

Because eculizumab blocks terminal complement—a crucial component of innate immunity against encapsulated bacteria—the drug should be introduced *after blood cultures sterilize*, as done here once C.​canimorsus cleared.

---

## Bedside clues that tipped us off  

- **Skin findings:** Early necrotic toes/fingers without loss of pulses pointed toward microvascular thrombosis rather than classic purpura fulminans which usually presents with diffuse ecchymoses plus consumptive coagulopathy.
- **Bleeding pattern:** Our patient had scant spontaneous bleeding despite profound thrombocytopenia once fibrinogen recovered—again hinting that platelet consumption was due to endothelial shear rather than systemic fibrinolysis.
- **Temporal separation:** Hemoglobin drop occurred *after* inflammatory markers fell and after fibrinogen rose back toward normal—a textbook “post‑septic” clue that something else is happening.
- **DAT false positive:** A single positive direct Coombs test was later deemed spurious (penicillin‐induced). Remember: autoimmune haemolytic anaemia can masquerade as MAHA—but schistocytes win out on morphology review.
- **Renal biopsy:** Fibrin thrombi in glomerular capillaries confirmed TMA pathology when non‐specific labs left doubt.

---

## Therapeutic dilemmas illuminated by the case  

1️⃣ **Plasma exchange vs eculizumab timing** – PEX may transiently remove circulating complement fragments but does not address dysregulated amplification loops. In our patient it bought time until eculizumab could be safely started after negative cultures.

2️⃣ **Vaccination amidst active infection** – Meningococcal conjugate vaccines require at least two weeks before full protection builds up; however emergent need for complement blockade trumps perfect timing—hence we administered vaccines *and* gave prophylactic amoxicillin until eculizumab took hold.

3️⃣ **Complement testing while on biologics** – Functional assays (AH50) become unreliable after plasma exchange or eculizumab because they reflect drug effect more than intrinsic activity. Obtain baseline samples before first PEX if possible—a pragmatic point often missed in busy ICU settings.

---

## How this changes your practice  

When you see a septic patient who suddenly drops a haemoglobin by >30 g/L without overt bleeding—and especially when platelets stay low despite correction of coagulopathy—stop thinking “just DIC.” Run the quick *DIC vs TMA* screen:

1. Coags normalized? → Think TMA  
2. Schistocytes present? → Move forward with PLASMIC & Shiga test  
3. ADAMTS13 <10%? → Start plasma exchange now!  
4. Otherwise → Consider early eculizumab after ensuring sterile blood cultures  

If you wait until BUN/creatinine peaks or until you have all genetics back you risk irreversible renal loss. Time is kidney function!

---

## Key Take‑home Points  

- **Triad alert:** New haemolysis + thrombocytopenia + AKI in any septic host warrants an immediate workup for thrombotic microangiopathy—not just worsening DIC.
- **Coagulation studies are discriminators:** Prolonged PT/aPTT + low fibrinogen = DIC; normal coag panel = true TMA.
- **PLASMIC score guides urgency:** A score ≥6 = start plasma exchange empirically while awaiting ADAMTS13.
- **Shiga toxin PCR should be sent early:** Positive = STEC‑HUS (supportive care); negative steers you toward other TMAs.
- **Normal C3/C4 does NOT rule out CM‑HUS:** Complement activation may be functional rather than quantitative.
- **Early complement blockade improves outcomes:** Initiate eculizumab promptly after confirming sterile cultures; vaccinate against Neisseria & give antibiotic prophylaxis.
- **Plasma exchange is a bridge—not curative—for CM‑HUS:** It stabilises while you arrange complement inhibition.
- **Watch for false‐positive DATs:** Penicillin exposure can mimic autoimmune hemolysis but morphology tells the truth.
- **Time matters:** Delays >7–10 days from hematologic onset to eculizumab correlate with higher risk of permanent dialysis dependence.
- **Genetics may be unrevealing:** Up to half of CM‑HUS patients have no identifiable mutation yet still benefit from complement inhibition.

---

### Bottom line  

Septic patients speak a confusing language of coagulopathy and organ dysfunction—but if you listen closely to the laboratory echo (“normal PT/INR”, “schistocytes rising”) you’ll catch the whisper of an underlying thrombotic microangiopathy before it shouts irreversible kidney injury. Armed with a systematic algorithm and an understanding that infections like *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* can act as both provocateur of DIC *and* trigger factor for complement‐mediated HUS, you’ll be ready to deliver targeted therapy—plasmapheresis when it’s truly needed, eculizumab when it isn’t—to stop the cascade in its tracks. Stay curious, stay rapid!



# Beyond C3/C4: Advanced Complement Testing to Diagnose Complement‑Mediated HUS  

## Why this story caught my eye  

Dog bites aren’t usually remembered as the opening line of a critical care lecture on thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Yet here we have a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who went from an unremarkable canine cuddle‑session to septic shock caused by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* ‑‑ then progressed to dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) ‑‑ and finally landed a textbook case of complement‑mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS).  

What makes the case truly compelling is the diagnostic blind spot it highlights: standard complement serology (C3 & C4) was perfectly normal throughout her admission, almost leading us down the wrong therapeutic road. Only when more sophisticated functional assays (alternative‐pathway hemolytic activity ‑ AH50), specific regulator measurements, and anti‑factor H antibody testing were performed did the hidden dysregulation surface—and it changed management overnight.  

If you’ve ever stared at “normal” complements in the midst of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, you’ll recognise the tension between pattern‐recognition heuristics and what the laboratory actually tells us. This post walks through the patient’s trajectory, demystifies the advanced tests, and distils practical pearls you can apply tomorrow morning on rounds.  

## From dog bite to microangiopathic haemolysis – a rapid timeline  

Day 0 – Our patient was bitten on her upper lip while playing with her dog. She received tetanus prophylaxis but never collected her prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin prescription.  

Day 4 – High fever (>40 °C), profound malaise, hypoglycaemia refractory to oral glucose/back‐up boluses prompted urgent transfer from primary care directly to the emergency department. On arrival she was febrile, hypotensive without vasopressors needed yet cool mottled lower limbs hinted at early peripheral ischemia—a classic “septic DIC” picture.【citation omitted】  

Initial labs painted full‐blown sepsis plus consumptive coagulopathy: leukopaenia, pancytopenia, elevated lactate (5.7 mmol/L), metabolic acidosis (pH 7.27), deranged liver enzymes, profound AKI with proteinuria/hematuria on dipstick †† ‑‐and crucially *normal* serum C3 and C4 levels despite marked inflammation.*

She was started on broad‐spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam covering canine flora including *Capnocytophaga*. Blood cultures later grew *C.​canimorsus* after seven days—a classic slow grower best cultured using prolonged incubation or molecular platforms such as nanopore sequencing.*  

During ICU stay she became anuric despite aggressive diuresis attempts and required continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). Coagulopathy slowly improved on transfusion support while skin ecchymoses proliferated—signs consistent with microvascular thrombosis secondary to disseminated intravascular coagulation.*

By hospital day 8 she had been transferred out of ICU onto a nephrology ward on dialysis support alone—her inflammatory markers were falling, platelet count creeping up modestly (>100×10⁹/L) and haemoglobin stable around 111 g/L.*

The turning point arrived six days later when her haemoglobin dropped abruptly from 111 g/L down to 68 g/L over just six days without overt bleeding + persistent thrombocytopenia (~30–40×10⁹/L). LDH surged, haptoglobin vanished, peripheral smear now showed ≈15–20% schistocytes—the classic hallmarks of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia.*  

A Coombs test was initially IgG positive—a red herring most likely related to recent penicillin exposure—but repeated testing turned negative while steroids failed to halt haemolysis.*  

At this juncture two immediate questions demanded answers:  
1️⃣ Is this thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)?  
2️⃣ If not TTP, could this be a complement‑driven process masked by earlier DIC?

ADAMTS13 activity returned at 43 % (>10 %), essentially ruling out TTP—the PLASMIC score was low—and steering us toward other TMAs.*  

Standard complements remained within reference limits; clinicians could have dismissed aCM­‐H​U​S here entirely—but they didn’t.* A renal biopsy performed once platelets rose above safety thresholds demonstrated severe ATN alongside ischaemic glomeruli studded with fibrin thrombi—histology consonant with thrombotic microangiopathy.*  

Only subsequent extended complement workup—a battery ordered through Lund University’s reference laboratory—revealed a **markedly reduced alternative‐pathway activity** measured as an **AH₅₀ of 6 %**, whilst total CH₅₀ remained within normal range.* All three regulatory proteins—factor H, factor I and factor B—were quantitatively normal and anti‑factor H antibodies were absent.*  

With ADAMTS13 >10%, negative STEC PCR stool test and new evidence pointing toward alternative‐pathway dysregulation despite “normal” baseline complements—the diagnostic needle settled firmly on **complement‑mediated HUS**.*

Plasma exchange (PEX) had already been initiated empirically while awaiting definitive data—a pragmatic bridge commonly employed when TTP cannot be excluded outright.* Once enough confidence built around CM‑HUS _and_ blood cultures cleared,_ **eculizumab** was launched following meningococcal vaccination series (Menveo + Bexsero) and penicillin prophylaxis.*  

Haemolysis subsided rapidly after the first dose… platelet counts recovered… residual diuresis returned during ongoing CRRT continuity.* By discharge she was dialysis independent aside from modest residual azotaemia (**serum creatinine ≈175 µmol/L**) indicating partial renal rebound.* Maintenance eculizumab continued biweekly pending long‐term planning.*

## The trap of “normal” C3/C₄  

For many clinicians ‑ especially those whose training emphasized classic immune complex disease patterns ‑ checking serum C3 & C₄ feels like opening one’s own fastenings before pulling apart an airway obstruction kit.^† Typical board teaching says “low C3 favors atypical HUS.” Yet reality often diverges:

* **Half‑life dynamics:** Serum C3 has a half‑life near two weeks; short bursts of consumption during an acute flare may blunt any measurable drop if sampled later or if treatment has already blunted ongoing turnover.*
* **Compartmentalisation:** The pathological event sits mainly at endothelial surfaces where locally generated C3b is quickly turned over—or bound onto cells—instead of spilling into circulation.*
* **Therapeutic interference:** Some patients receive plasma products or steroids before labs are drawn; fresh frozen plasma provides exogenous complement components thus artificially inflating measurements.*

In our patient all three forces converged! By the time MAHA manifested she had already been receiving several units of plasma products during DIC management—and CRRT exchanges further modified serum protein dynamics.* Consequently her measured C₃/C₄ reflected equilibrium rather than ongoing abnormal activation.*

Hence relying solely on “normal” complements becomes a diagnostic dead end exactly when timing matters most—the moment you must decide whether rapid‐acting PEX alone suffices or whether terminal complement blockade should be secured.*

## Functional complement assays 101  

### Classical versus Alternative Pathway Hemolysis

| Assay | What it tests | Typical readout |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| **CH₅₀** | Total hemolytic capacity via classical route (e.g., sheep erythrocytes sensitised with IgG) | % lysis compared with pooled control serum |
| **AH₅₀** | Hemolytic capacity via spontaneous alternative route (e.g., rabbit erythrocytes without antibody) | % lysis relative to control |

Both rely on serial dilutions of patient serum added to sensitised red cells under defined conditions; loss of ‘hemolysis’ signals insufficient active components along that particular cascade.^† 

In isolated **alternative‑pathway dysregulation**, CH₅₀ remains unchanged because classical components are untouched while AH₅₀ drops dramatically—as seen here (**AH₅₀ = 6 %**, essentially absent). Such a pattern isolates pathology squarely onto factor B–D mediated convertase formation or its downstream regulation.^†   

### Interpreting an abnormal AH₅₀

* **Low AH₅₀ + Normal CH₅₀** → Suggests either intrinsic defect/regulatory failure in alternative route _or_ consumptive loss secondary to ongoing endothelial injury typical for atypical HUS.*
* **Low CH₅₀ ± Low AH₅₀** → More global hypocomplementemia seen in massive immune complex disease or severe liver failure.*
* **Normal AH₅₀** → Makes isolated alternative‐pathway driven TMAs less likely—but remember timing! If sample obtained >24–48 h after plasma exchange or capillary refill’ernativation sedation —> false reassurance.)

Our patient's profoundly depressed AH₅₀ confirmed that despite apparently adequate systemic components upstream (`C3`, `C5` quantitative level), the *functional* ability for spontaneous tickover was crippled—a fingerprint pointing toward uncontrolled local amplification idealized by atypical HUS models.*

## Factor levels & autoantibodies – what they reveal  

### Factor H & Factor I  
Factor H acts as the master brake preventing unrestricted assembly/inactivation cycles of `C3bBb`. Partial loss-of-function variants allow runaway amplification even if overall concentration looks respectable.^† Therefore quantitative measurement alone sometimes masks qualitative dysfunction—but combined biochemical readout plus clinical context typically clarifies actionable pathways._ 

Factor I works downstream turning `C3b` into `iC3b`. When deficient—even subtly—the upstream convertases persist longer._ In our patient both regulators measured within reference ranges—arguing against congenital quantitative deficits._

### Factor B  
Factor B pairs with `C3b` forming `C3bBb`, the pivotal alternative convertase._ Elevated levels might indicate upregulated synthesis secondary to chronic activation whereas low values suggest consumption._ Here factor B stayed etched at baseline._

### Anti–factor H antibodies  
Autoantibodies neutralising factor H represent an acquired form analogous biologicallyto genetic loss-of-function—and are treated similarly aggressively because they fuel unchecked tickover.^† Negative anti‑factor H assay reinforced our suspicion that *infection alone*, rather than an autoantibody storm,, ignited the cascade._

These fine-grained measurements collectively painted a picture where ***the structural pieces were present*** yet ***the system behaved as though missing its regulator***—a hallmark scenario we encounter frequently in infection-triggered atypical HUS.^†  

In practice ordering all three regulator levels alongside complete functional panels positions you squarely between “blanket” rules (“low C3 = atypical”) _and_ fuzzy bedside heuristics._ It equips you with data supporting either continued transplant surveillance-like waiting (“maybe still regular DIC”) *or* precipitous escalation toward targeted biologics._

## When & how do you order these tests in real time?  

1️⃣ **Timing matters most** – Ideally obtain blood *before* any plasma product infusion or therapeutic plasma exchange (“first draw”). Each unit dilutes circulating proteins roughly 1–2%. In emergent situations where withholding PEX would jeopardise life—you’ll still draw aliquots right before each session so laboratories can adjust interpretations._  
2️⃣ **Specimen handling** – Serum is preferred over heparinized plasma for CH/​AH titration because heparin chelation interferes slightly with downstream coagulation steps._ Freeze aliquots at −80°C promptly if shipping out-of-institution._  
3️⃣ **Laboratory logistics** – Most community hospitals lack home-grown CH/​AH capabilities—it’s invariably referred out⤴️---a process best arranged through early contact with designated reference centers _(e.g., Skåne Laboratory)_ so they know you need both quantitative regulators AND functional titres.__

# Eculizumab in the Time of Infection  
**Timing, Vaccination & Prophylaxis When Treating Complement‑Mediated HUS**

---

## Why This Case Gets Your Attention  

A healthy woman in her late 40s came into the ED four days after a dog bite on her lip — no antibiotics taken, just a tetanus shot. She rapidly progressed to septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), pancytopenia and oliguric acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy. Blood cultures grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a fastidious gram‑negative rod typically seen only occasionally in zoonotic infections.  

She seemed to be improving under broad‑spectrum antibiotics when suddenly her hemoglobin fell dramatically, haptoglobin vanished and schistocytes appeared on smear despite stable vital signs. ADAMTS13 was normal (43 %). A kidney biopsy showed classic thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA); together those findings clinched **complement‑mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS)** triggered by a bacterial infection—a rare “second hit” scenario that many intensivists never encounter in real time.  

What makes this story compelling isn’t just the exotic pathogen; it’s the therapeutic conundrum it forces upon us **when terminal complement inhibition has to be balanced against an active bacterial infection**. Do we wait until every blood culture clears and full vaccination series are complete—and risk irreversible renal loss—or do we jump straight onto eculizumab under cover of plasma exchange (PEX) and antibiotics? The authors navigated both worlds and their decisions illuminate current best practice—and its gray zones—for anyone treating an infected patient with CM‑HUS.

---

## Untangling Thrombotic Microangiopathy in Sepsis  

A sepsis‐related DIC can masquerade as TMA because both produce thrombocytopenia, schistocytes and organ dysfunction. In adult patients presenting with MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI the first diagnostic split is **ADAMTS13 activity**:

* **<10 %** → Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura → urgent plasma exchange + steroids.
* **>10 %** → Think atypical HUS / secondary TMAs (shiga toxin HUS, drug‑induced, infection‐triggered).

In our patient ADAMTS13 was well above the TTP threshold, shifting focus toward an **infection‑triggered complement dysregulation** even though classic complement labs (C3/C4) were normal—a reminder that routine complements can be deceptively bland during an acute phase reaction. A kidney biopsy confirmed endothelial injury consistent with TMA, sealing the diagnosis of **CM‑HUS** despite lacking a demonstrable pathogenic gene variant.

Key points from this diagnostic pathway:

1️⃣ **Never delay ADAMTS13 testing** – send sample before any plasma exchange because even one PEX cycle halves activity values.^[1]  
2️⃣ **Consider a renal biopsy when AKI isn’t explained by ischemia alone**, especially when creatinine remains high despite hemodynamic optimisation.^[2]  
3️⃣ **Keep an eye out for “second hits”**, such as sepsis from *C. canimorsus*, which can tip subclinical complement dysregulation over into overt TMA.^[3]

---

## The Therapeutic Crossroads – To Block Complement Now or Later?  

Eculizumab has transformed outcomes when started early (< 7 days from symptom onset)^[4]; delayed treatment correlates strongly with persistent dialysis dependence.^[5] Yet its mechanism—blocking C5 downstream of all pathways—blunts the opsonophagocytic killing that our immune system relies on against encapsulated bacteria (*Neisseria spp.*, *Haemophilus*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, and notably *Capnocytophaga*).  

For our patient the team chose a staged approach:

| **Step** | **Rationale** |
|---|---|
| **Aggressive antimicrobial therapy** (*piperacillin/tazobactam ➝ ampicillin ➝ oral phenoxymethylpenicillin*) | Clears bacteremia so terminal complement blockade won’t unleash fulminant meningococcemia or uncontrolled *C.* canimorsus growth |
| **Plasma exchange (PEX)** | Provides temporary removal of circulating complement activation products while awaiting definitive therapy—and buys time for cultures to sterilise |
| **Meningococcal vaccination** | Administered just before first eculizumab dose (MenACWY + MenB). This satisfies FDA labelling requirement “if possible” even though full antibody titre isn’t achieved yet |
| **Prophylactic penicillin (“Apocillin”)** | Bridges immunogenic gap until ≥ 2 weeks post‑vaccine when protective antibodies mature |
| **Eculizumab infusion** | Initiated once blood cultures were negative, after two vaccine doses and while concurrent penicillin prophylaxis continued |

The plan illustrates an emerging consensus: **don’t let “vaccination latency” paralyze care**, especially when kidney salvage hinges on rapid complement inhibition.^[6] Instead vaccinate immediately, give daily penicillin (or ceftriaxone if allergic), monitor closely for breakthrough infection, and proceed with eculizumab once culture sterility is confirmed.

---

### Plasma Exchange – A Bridging Savior?  

PEX removes large proteins—including circulating immune complexes, cytokines and activated complement fragments—but it does **not fix underlying genetic dysregulation**, so its benefit wanes once plasma replacement stops.^[7] In our case three exchanges successfully halted rapid hemoglobin drop and allowed diuresis return **before** eculizumab took effect—that’s classic “bridge” behavior rather than cure.

Important nuances:

* Each exchange will also strip any already infused eculizumab; therefore schedule dosing > 24 h after the final PEX session whenever feasible.^[8]  
* Albumin‐based replacement fluid reduces removal of coagulation factors compared with fresh frozen plasma—a practical tip when patients already suffer coagulopathy from DIC.^[9]  
* If ADAMTS13 is borderline (“grey zone”) consider continuing PEX until definitive diagnosis solidifies—to avoid missing occult TTP.^[10]

---

### When Is It Safe to Start Eculizumab?  

Consensus statements across nephrology societies converge on three safety checkpoints:

1️⃣ **Blood culture clearance:** Empiric broad‐spectrum antibiotics should be continued until ≥ 48 h of negative cultures from sterile sites.^[11]  
2️⃣ **Vaccination status:** Give both quadrivalent meningococcal polysaccharide conjugate vaccine (MenACWY) *and* serogroup B vaccine (MenB). If there isn’t time for the mandatory two‑week interval before first dose, proceed anyway—but pair it with continuous antimicrobial prophylaxis.^[12]  
3️⃣ **Antibiotic prophylaxis:** Daily oral penicillin G/phenoxymethylpenicillin 125–250 mg q6h (or ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h if intolerant). Continue through at least two weeks post‑vaccine or until scheduled booster confirming serologic protection.^[13]

If any checkpoint cannot be satisfied—for example persistent bacteremia despite maximal therapy—the prudent move is **keep PEX going**, treat infection aggressively, reassess daily, and defer eculizumab until sterility returns or patient’s organ function deteriorates beyond salvageable limits despite supportive care.*

---

### Meningococcal Immunisation Before Terminal Complement Inhibition  

The CDC recommends a dual‐vaccine strategy because each covers distinct serogroups:

| Vaccine | Serogroups Covered | Minimum Interval Before Eculizumab |
|---|---|---|
| MenACWY *(e.g., Menveo)* | A C W Y | Ideally ≥14 days |
| MenB *(e.g., Bexsero)* | B | Ideally ≥14 days |

Both series are administered as two doses separated by at least one month; boosters follow according to manufacturer protocol (typically every 5 years). In emergency settings you may give both doses back–to–back on day 0—but then enforce antimicrobial cover until day 14 when functional antibodies are presumed protective.^[14]

A useful bedside mnemonic: **“VAX & AX”** – vaccinate *and* give “AX”olo­penic/ceftriaxone antibiotic coverage while waiting for immunity.



---

### Antibiotic Prophylaxis – Covering More Than Neisseria  

The traditional emphasis on Neisseria stems from its rapid progression under C5 blockade (§ meningitis mortality > 30%). However other encapsulated organisms pose comparable risks when terminal complement is disabled:

* ***Capnocytophaga canimorsus*** – gram‑negative rod found in canine saliva; demonstrates variable serum susceptibility partially mediated by its capsule.[^15] It responds well to β‑lactams (penicillin G/amoxicillin) plus β‑lactamase inhibitors if resistance suspected.
* ***Streptococcus pneumoniae*** – standard pneumococcal conjugate/ polysaccharide vaccines should be up‐to‐date before initiating therapy when possible.^[16]
* ***Haemophilus influenzae type b*** – Hib vaccine similarly advisable prior to initiation if demographics permit.^[16]

In practice most centers adopt continuous oral penicillin V/phenoxymethylpenicillin as first‐line meningococcal prophylaxis because it also covers many beta‑lactam–sensitive encapsulated pathogens—including *C.* canimorsus—as demonstrated by our patient’s ongoing ampicillin/redine regimen.*

---

### Capnocytophaga – Not Just a Bite Bug  

*C.* canimorsus’ notoriety lies chiefly in causing fulminant sepsis among splenectomised or alcoholic hosts^[17]; our patient was immunocompetent yet still succumbed—a reminder that healthy individuals aren’t immune when bacterial load overwhelms innate defenses such as opsonizing IgG subclasses[^18]. Importantly:

* Its capsule confers partial resistance against classical pathway activation—so removal of C5 via eculizumab potentially converts an otherwise controlled bacteremia into unchecked proliferation.
* Laboratory culture may take up to seven days—allied molecular methods like real‑time nanopore sequencing shorten identification timeframes—but empiric coverage should already encompass it when animal bites are involved—a fact reflected by initial piperacillin/tazobactam choice established by canine‐bite protocols.^[19]

Thus whenever a zoonotic gram‐negative rod is suspected you’re already walking into familiar territory concerning β‑lactam efficacy *and* potential need for enhanced vigilance once terminal complement blockade begins.



---

### Lessons From Real World Cases  

The reported dog‑bite patient mirrors several previously published cases where an infectious trigger unmasked previously silent alternative pathway dysregulation^[20]. Common themes emerge:

* **Rapid transition:** Sepsis → DIC → apparent resolution → abrupt MAHA / thrombocytopenia typically within 7–10 days—a window that should raise suspicion for superimposed TMAs rather than simple DIC persistence.
* **Diagnostic sequencing matters:** ADADTM5 activity must precede any plasma exchange whenever feasible because PEX artificially inflates measured activity leading down an incorrect therapeutic path toward unnecessary repeated exchanges.
* **Early C5 inhibition improves renal recovery:** Analyses pooled across adult atypical HUS registries show every additional day before starting eculizumab reduces odds of dialysis independence at three months by ~15%^[5].
* **Vaccination timing flexibility:** Several centers report successful initiation within hours after immunisation when combined with continuous intravenous penicillin G—even though antibody titres lag behind—the protective effect seems mediated largely through eradication of carriage preceding overwhelming bacteremia.^[21]

These real‐world experiences reinforce that rigid adherence to “two week rule” precludes patients from derive life-saving benefit when renal tissue is already irreversibly damaged.



---

## Practical Algorithm for Infected Patients With Suspected CM‑HUS  

1️⃣ **Identify TMA:** Hemolysis labs + schistocytes + AKI + platelets <150×10⁹/L  
2️⃣ **Order emergent ADAMTS13 & Shiga toxin PCR** – results within ≤12 h where possible  
3️⃣ **If ADAMTS13 <10% → treat as TTP** (PEX + steroids ± rituximab)  
4️⃣ **If ADAMTS13 >10% & infectious source present:**  
   ‑ Start targeted antibiotics immediately  
   ‑ Obtain blood cultures × 2 ➜ confirm sterility ≥48 h   
   ‑ Administer MenACWY + MenB vaccines ASAP  
   ‑ Begin daily oral penicillin V/phenoxymethylpenicillin OR IV ceftriaxone \~24–48 h pre‑eculizumab  
5️⃣ **Initiate Plasma Exchange** while awaiting culture clearance & vaccine immunity — use albumin replacement unless coagulopathy forces FFP  
6️⃣ **Once cultures negative AND ≥24 h elapsed since last PEX**, give first 900 mg dose of eciluzimab — continue weekly loading schedule  
7️⃣ **Continue antibiotic prophylaxis till ≥14 days post‑vaccine**, then reassess need based on local epidemiology/HLA typing  
8️⃣ **Monitor:** LDH trend, platelet count, creatinine trajectory; repeat complementation testing if relapse suspected  

This stepwise approach protects against overwhelming bacterial growth whilst preserving kidneys whenever possible.



---

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Infection-triggered CM-HUS needs rapid recognition**—look for new hemolysis after sepsis appears controlled.  
- **ADAMTS13 must be sent before any plasma exchange**; values >10% essentially exclude TTP and shift treatment toward complement inhibition.  
- **Plasma exchange serves as a short-term bridge**, buying time while you sterilise blood cultures and complete mandatory meningococcal immunisation.  
- **Don’t wait two weeks for vaccine immunity** if renal salvage hangs in balance—give MenACWY + MenB immediately *and* provide continuous penicillin (or ceftriaxone) prophylaxis until protective titres are presumed (>14 days).  
- **Eculizumab can safely follow recent vaccination under antibiotic cover**, even when full immunity hasn’t yet matured—a strategy supported by emerging real-world data including this dog-bite case.  
- **Maintain vigilance for other encapsulated pathogens**, especially *Capnocytophaga* in animal bite exposures; β‑lactam prophylaxis offers collateral protection while you block C5.  
- **Early initiation (<7 days)** markedly improves renal outcomes; each day’s delay translates into higher odds of permanent dialysis dependence.  

By keeping these pillars front-of-mind you’ll navigate the tightrope between aggressive anti-complement therapy and safeguarding against invasive infections—just what happened when our patient went from dog bite straight into dialysis rescue mode via timely eculiza​nubix!

# Kidney Biopsy Decodes Thrombotic Microangiopathy—Histologic Clues that Guide Therapy

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

A healthy woman in her 40s went from a dog bite to dialysis within a week.  The first few days screamed “sepsis + DIC” – pancytopenia, coagulopathy, anuric AKI – but two weeks later she developed isolated hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia.  The diagnosis pivoted on a **renal biopsy** that revealed classic microangiopathic changes rather than cortical necrosis from ongoing DIC.  That single tissue slice dictated a switch from plasma exchange to complement blockade with eculizumab and ultimately salvaged her kidneys.  In other words: **the kidney’s story broke the diagnostic impasse**.

## A quick recap of the clinical conundrum  

- Day 0: Dog bite → fever → septic shock. Blood cultures later grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a fastidious gram‑negative rod.
- Initial labs: severe inflammation, pancytopenia, DIC‑pattern coagulopathy, AKI (creatinine >300 µmol/L), lactic acidosis.
- ICU course: anuria despite fluids/diuretics → continuous renal replacement therapy.
- Day 8‑10: Inflammatory markers improve, but a sudden drop in hemoglobin (111 → 68 g/L) appears without bleeding; platelets stay low (30–40 × 10⁹/L); LDH spikes; haptoglobin undetectable → MAHA.
- ADAMTS13 = 43 % (rules out TTP), Shiga toxin PCR negative.
- After plasma exchange attempts, a **kidney biopsy** is performed.

## Histology as the decisive arbiter  

### 1. Light microscopy – what we actually saw  

The core biopsy showed **severe acute tubular necrosis**, **ischemic glomeruli studded with fibrin thrombi**, and **scattered cellular crescents**.  

- **Fibrin thrombi** within capillary loops are the hallmark of a microangiopathic process affecting the glomerular endothelium – think atypical HUS or secondary complement‑mediated TMAs.
- **Crescents** reflect an abrupt breach of the glomerular basement membrane by fibrin‑rich material; they are not typical of pure DIC‑induced cortical necrosis.
- **Ischemic glomeruli** with pale “ghost” appearance point toward focal vascular occlusion rather than diffuse hypoperfusion seen in severe DIC.

In contrast, classic DIC‑related renal injury usually manifests as **patchy or confluent cortical necrosis** without organized fibrin thrombi in glomeruli or crescents. The presence of discrete thrombi steered us away from “just DIC” toward a **complement‑driven TMA**.

### 2. Immunofluorescence – ruling out immune complexes  

The IF panel was essentially negative:

| Marker | Result |
|--------|--------|
| IgG    | Negative |
| IgA    | Negative |
| C3     | Negative |
| C1q    | Negative |

A clean IF study excludes immune complex‐mediated proliferative GN (e.g., lupus nephritis, post‑infectious GN) that can mimic TMA both clinically and histologically (often via “double contour” changes). In our patient there was no “lumpy‐bumpy” staining to distract us.

### 3. Electron microscopy – confirming the absence of deposits  

EM showed:

- No subendothelial electron‑dense deposits
- Intact podocyte foot processes aside from occasional effacement secondary to ATN
- No organized pathogen structures

These findings reinforce that the injury is **non‑immune**, driven instead by endothelial activation and complement deposition that are too small to be visualized by EM.

## How the pathology reshaped management  

1. **From plasma exchange to complement blockade**  
   - PEX is lifesaving for TTP (ADAMTS13 <10 %) but offers only transient benefit in complement‑mediated TMAs.
   - The biopsy confirmed architecture typical of CM‑HUS → justified early initiation of eculizumab after ensuring blood cultures were cleared and meningococcal vaccination completed.

2. **Stopping unnecessary immunosuppression**  
   - An initial positive Coombs test led us down an autoimmune hemolysis path and prompted high‑dose steroids.
   - Histology showing no immune complex disease argued against ongoing autoimmune processes, allowing rapid steroid taper once eculizumab took effect.

3. **Renal prognostication**  
   - Presence of crescents predicts more severe injury but also suggests reversible endothelial thrombosis if complement blockade is timely.
   - The recovery of diuresis after PEX hinted at partial reversal but sustained renal improvement only followed eculizumab—mirroring data that early complement inhibition improves renal outcomes.

## Practical pearls for clinicians facing ambiguous TMAs  

- **Don’t wait for complete labs before consulting pathology.** If the picture is shifting from sepsis/DIC toward MAHA + AKI without clear etiology, a percutaneous renal biopsy can be lifesaving.
- **Ask pathologists to comment on three tiers:**  
  - *Morphology* (thrombi vs necrosis vs crescents)  
  - *Immunofluorescence* (immune complex vs pauci‑immune)  
  - *Electron microscopy* (presence/absence of deposits).  
- **Interpret complement levels cautiously.** Normal C3/C4 does NOT exclude CM‑HUS—histology often provides the missing clue.
- **Timing matters:** When blood cultures turn negative and meningococcal vaccination is done, start eculizumab promptly; delays diminish renal recovery chances.
- **Remember the “second hit” concept:** Even immunocompetent adults can develop CM‑HUS if an infection like *C. canimorsus* triggers uncontrolled alternative pathway activation.

## Take‑home messages  

- **Kidney biopsy can differentiate between DIC‑related cortical necrosis and complement‑mediated microvascular injury—information critical for targeted therapy.**
- **Fibrin thrombi in glomerular capillaries plus crescents on light microscopy = strong histologic support for CM‑HUS/atypical HUS.**
- **Negative immunofluorescence and electron microscopy essentially rule out immune complex GN as the driver of TMA.**
- **Histologic confirmation shifts treatment from plasma exchange toward eculizumab, dramatically improving hematologic remission and renal recovery when started early.**
- **In patients with evolving MAHA/AKI after sepsis/DIC, consider early renal biopsy—time is kidney!**
