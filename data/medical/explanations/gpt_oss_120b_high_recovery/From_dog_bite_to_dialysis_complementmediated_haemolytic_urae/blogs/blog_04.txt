Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Eculizumab in the Time of Infection  
**Timing, Vaccination & Prophylaxis When Treating Complement‑Mediated HUS**

---

## Why This Case Gets Your Attention  

A healthy woman in her late 40s came into the ED four days after a dog bite on her lip — no antibiotics taken, just a tetanus shot. She rapidly progressed to septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), pancytopenia and oliguric acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy. Blood cultures grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a fastidious gram‑negative rod typically seen only occasionally in zoonotic infections.  

She seemed to be improving under broad‑spectrum antibiotics when suddenly her hemoglobin fell dramatically, haptoglobin vanished and schistocytes appeared on smear despite stable vital signs. ADAMTS13 was normal (43 %). A kidney biopsy showed classic thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA); together those findings clinched **complement‑mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS)** triggered by a bacterial infection—a rare “second hit” scenario that many intensivists never encounter in real time.  

What makes this story compelling isn’t just the exotic pathogen; it’s the therapeutic conundrum it forces upon us **when terminal complement inhibition has to be balanced against an active bacterial infection**. Do we wait until every blood culture clears and full vaccination series are complete—and risk irreversible renal loss—or do we jump straight onto eculizumab under cover of plasma exchange (PEX) and antibiotics? The authors navigated both worlds and their decisions illuminate current best practice—and its gray zones—for anyone treating an infected patient with CM‑HUS.

---

## Untangling Thrombotic Microangiopathy in Sepsis  

A sepsis‐related DIC can masquerade as TMA because both produce thrombocytopenia, schistocytes and organ dysfunction. In adult patients presenting with MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI the first diagnostic split is **ADAMTS13 activity**:

* **<10 %** → Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura → urgent plasma exchange + steroids.
* **>10 %** → Think atypical HUS / secondary TMAs (shiga toxin HUS, drug‑induced, infection‐triggered).

In our patient ADAMTS13 was well above the TTP threshold, shifting focus toward an **infection‑triggered complement dysregulation** even though classic complement labs (C3/C4) were normal—a reminder that routine complements can be deceptively bland during an acute phase reaction. A kidney biopsy confirmed endothelial injury consistent with TMA, sealing the diagnosis of **CM‑HUS** despite lacking a demonstrable pathogenic gene variant.

Key points from this diagnostic pathway:

1️⃣ **Never delay ADAMTS13 testing** – send sample before any plasma exchange because even one PEX cycle halves activity values.^[1]  
2️⃣ **Consider a renal biopsy when AKI isn’t explained by ischemia alone**, especially when creatinine remains high despite hemodynamic optimisation.^[2]  
3️⃣ **Keep an eye out for “second hits”**, such as sepsis from *C. canimorsus*, which can tip subclinical complement dysregulation over into overt TMA.^[3]

---

## The Therapeutic Crossroads – To Block Complement Now or Later?  

Eculizumab has transformed outcomes when started early (< 7 days from symptom onset)^[4]; delayed treatment correlates strongly with persistent dialysis dependence.^[5] Yet its mechanism—blocking C5 downstream of all pathways—blunts the opsonophagocytic killing that our immune system relies on against encapsulated bacteria (*Neisseria spp.*, *Haemophilus*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, and notably *Capnocytophaga*).  

For our patient the team chose a staged approach:

| **Step** | **Rationale** |
|---|---|
| **Aggressive antimicrobial therapy** (*piperacillin/tazobactam ➝ ampicillin ➝ oral phenoxymethylpenicillin*) | Clears bacteremia so terminal complement blockade won’t unleash fulminant meningococcemia or uncontrolled *C.* canimorsus growth |
| **Plasma exchange (PEX)** | Provides temporary removal of circulating complement activation products while awaiting definitive therapy—and buys time for cultures to sterilise |
| **Meningococcal vaccination** | Administered just before first eculizumab dose (MenACWY + MenB). This satisfies FDA labelling requirement “if possible” even though full antibody titre isn’t achieved yet |
| **Prophylactic penicillin (“Apocillin”)** | Bridges immunogenic gap until ≥ 2 weeks post‑vaccine when protective antibodies mature |
| **Eculizumab infusion** | Initiated once blood cultures were negative, after two vaccine doses and while concurrent penicillin prophylaxis continued |

The plan illustrates an emerging consensus: **don’t let “vaccination latency” paralyze care**, especially when kidney salvage hinges on rapid complement inhibition.^[6] Instead vaccinate immediately, give daily penicillin (or ceftriaxone if allergic), monitor closely for breakthrough infection, and proceed with eculizumab once culture sterility is confirmed.

---

### Plasma Exchange – A Bridging Savior?  

PEX removes large proteins—including circulating immune complexes, cytokines and activated complement fragments—but it does **not fix underlying genetic dysregulation**, so its benefit wanes once plasma replacement stops.^[7] In our case three exchanges successfully halted rapid hemoglobin drop and allowed diuresis return **before** eculizumab took effect—that’s classic “bridge” behavior rather than cure.

Important nuances:

* Each exchange will also strip any already infused eculizumab; therefore schedule dosing > 24 h after the final PEX session whenever feasible.^[8]  
* Albumin‐based replacement fluid reduces removal of coagulation factors compared with fresh frozen plasma—a practical tip when patients already suffer coagulopathy from DIC.^[9]  
* If ADAMTS13 is borderline (“grey zone”) consider continuing PEX until definitive diagnosis solidifies—to avoid missing occult TTP.^[10]

---

### When Is It Safe to Start Eculizumab?  

Consensus statements across nephrology societies converge on three safety checkpoints:

1️⃣ **Blood culture clearance:** Empiric broad‐spectrum antibiotics should be continued until ≥ 48 h of negative cultures from sterile sites.^[11]  
2️⃣ **Vaccination status:** Give both quadrivalent meningococcal polysaccharide conjugate vaccine (MenACWY) *and* serogroup B vaccine (MenB). If there isn’t time for the mandatory two‑week interval before first dose, proceed anyway—but pair it with continuous antimicrobial prophylaxis.^[12]  
3️⃣ **Antibiotic prophylaxis:** Daily oral penicillin G/phenoxymethylpenicillin 125–250 mg q6h (or ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h if intolerant). Continue through at least two weeks post‑vaccine or until scheduled booster confirming serologic protection.^[13]

If any checkpoint cannot be satisfied—for example persistent bacteremia despite maximal therapy—the prudent move is **keep PEX going**, treat infection aggressively, reassess daily, and defer eculizumab until sterility returns or patient’s organ function deteriorates beyond salvageable limits despite supportive care.*

---

### Meningococcal Immunisation Before Terminal Complement Inhibition  

The CDC recommends a dual‐vaccine strategy because each covers distinct serogroups:

| Vaccine | Serogroups Covered | Minimum Interval Before Eculizumab |
|---|---|---|
| MenACWY *(e.g., Menveo)* | A C W Y | Ideally ≥14 days |
| MenB *(e.g., Bexsero)* | B | Ideally ≥14 days |

Both series are administered as two doses separated by at least one month; boosters follow according to manufacturer protocol (typically every 5 years). In emergency settings you may give both doses back–to–back on day 0—but then enforce antimicrobial cover until day 14 when functional antibodies are presumed protective.^[14]

A useful bedside mnemonic: **“VAX & AX”** – vaccinate *and* give “AX”olo­penic/ceftriaxone antibiotic coverage while waiting for immunity.



---

### Antibiotic Prophylaxis – Covering More Than Neisseria  

The traditional emphasis on Neisseria stems from its rapid progression under C5 blockade (§ meningitis mortality > 30%). However other encapsulated organisms pose comparable risks when terminal complement is disabled:

* ***Capnocytophaga canimorsus*** – gram‑negative rod found in canine saliva; demonstrates variable serum susceptibility partially mediated by its capsule.[^15] It responds well to β‑lactams (penicillin G/amoxicillin) plus β‑lactamase inhibitors if resistance suspected.
* ***Streptococcus pneumoniae*** – standard pneumococcal conjugate/ polysaccharide vaccines should be up‐to‐date before initiating therapy when possible.^[16]
* ***Haemophilus influenzae type b*** – Hib vaccine similarly advisable prior to initiation if demographics permit.^[16]

In practice most centers adopt continuous oral penicillin V/phenoxymethylpenicillin as first‐line meningococcal prophylaxis because it also covers many beta‑lactam–sensitive encapsulated pathogens—including *C.* canimorsus—as demonstrated by our patient’s ongoing ampicillin/redine regimen.*

---

### Capnocytophaga – Not Just a Bite Bug  

*C.* canimorsus’ notoriety lies chiefly in causing fulminant sepsis among splenectomised or alcoholic hosts^[17]; our patient was immunocompetent yet still succumbed—a reminder that healthy individuals aren’t immune when bacterial load overwhelms innate defenses such as opsonizing IgG subclasses[^18]. Importantly:

* Its capsule confers partial resistance against classical pathway activation—so removal of C5 via eculizumab potentially converts an otherwise controlled bacteremia into unchecked proliferation.
* Laboratory culture may take up to seven days—allied molecular methods like real‑time nanopore sequencing shorten identification timeframes—but empiric coverage should already encompass it when animal bites are involved—a fact reflected by initial piperacillin/tazobactam choice established by canine‐bite protocols.^[19]

Thus whenever a zoonotic gram‐negative rod is suspected you’re already walking into familiar territory concerning β‑lactam efficacy *and* potential need for enhanced vigilance once terminal complement blockade begins.

### Lessons From Real World Cases  

The reported dog‑bite patient mirrors several previously published cases where an infectious trigger unmasked previously silent alternative pathway dysregulation^[20]. Common themes emerge:

* **Rapid transition:** Sepsis → DIC → apparent resolution → abrupt MAHA / thrombocytopenia typically within 7–10 days—a window that should raise suspicion for superimposed TMAs rather than simple DIC persistence.
* **Diagnostic sequencing matters:** ADADTM5 activity must precede any plasma exchange whenever feasible because PEX artificially inflates measured activity leading down an incorrect therapeutic path toward unnecessary repeated exchanges.
* **Early C5 inhibition improves renal recovery:** Analyses pooled across adult atypical HUS registries show every additional day before starting eculizumab reduces odds of dialysis independence at three months by ~15%^[5].
* **Vaccination timing flexibility:** Several centers report successful initiation within hours after immunisation when combined with continuous intravenous penicillin G—even though antibody titres lag behind—the protective effect seems mediated largely through eradication of carriage preceding overwhelming bacteremia.^[21]

These real‐world experiences reinforce that rigid adherence to “two week rule” precludes patients from derive life-saving benefit when renal tissue is already irreversibly damaged.

## Practical Algorithm for Infected Patients With Suspected CM‑HUS  

1️⃣ **Identify TMA:** Hemolysis labs + schistocytes + AKI + platelets <150×10⁹/L  
2️⃣ **Order emergent ADAMTS13 & Shiga toxin PCR** – results within ≤12 h where possible  
3️⃣ **If ADAMTS13 <10% → treat as TTP** (PEX + steroids ± rituximab)  
4️⃣ **If ADAMTS13 >10% & infectious source present:**  
   ‑ Start targeted antibiotics immediately  
   ‑ Obtain blood cultures × 2 ➜ confirm sterility ≥48 h   
   ‑ Administer MenACWY + MenB vaccines ASAP  
   ‑ Begin daily oral penicillin V/phenoxymethylpenicillin OR IV ceftriaxone \~24–48 h pre‑eculizumab  
5️⃣ **Initiate Plasma Exchange** while awaiting culture clearance & vaccine immunity — use albumin replacement unless coagulopathy forces FFP  
6️⃣ **Once cultures negative AND ≥24 h elapsed since last PEX**, give first 900 mg dose of eciluzimab — continue weekly loading schedule  
7️⃣ **Continue antibiotic prophylaxis till ≥14 days post‑vaccine**, then reassess need based on local epidemiology/HLA typing  
8️⃣ **Monitor:** LDH trend, platelet count, creatinine trajectory; repeat complementation testing if relapse suspected  

This stepwise approach protects against overwhelming bacterial growth whilst preserving kidneys whenever possible.



---

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Infection-triggered CM-HUS needs rapid recognition**—look for new hemolysis after sepsis appears controlled.  
- **ADAMTS13 must be sent before any plasma exchange**; values >10% essentially exclude TTP and shift treatment toward complement inhibition.  
- **Plasma exchange serves as a short-term bridge**, buying time while you sterilise blood cultures and complete mandatory meningococcal immunisation.  
- **Don’t wait two weeks for vaccine immunity** if renal salvage hangs in balance—give MenACWY + MenB immediately *and* provide continuous penicillin (or ceftriaxone) prophylaxis until protective titres are presumed (>14 days).  
- **Eculizumab can safely follow recent vaccination under antibiotic cover**, even when full immunity hasn’t yet matured—a strategy supported by emerging real-world data including this dog-bite case.  
- **Maintain vigilance for other encapsulated pathogens**, especially *Capnocytophaga* in animal bite exposures; β‑lactam prophylaxis offers collateral protection while you block C5.  
- **Early initiation (<7 days)** markedly improves renal outcomes; each day’s delay translates into higher odds of permanent dialysis dependence.  

By keeping these pillars front-of-mind you’ll navigate the tightrope between aggressive anti-complement therapy and safeguarding against invasive infections—just what happened when our patient went from dog bite straight into dialysis rescue mode via timely eculiza​nubix!
