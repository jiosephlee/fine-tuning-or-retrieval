Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Differential Diagnosis] On ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI performed in the second trimester, which specific morphological features favor a diagnosis of a cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma rather than a decidualised endometrioma?

**Cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma versus decidualised endometrioma – imaging pearls in the second trimester**

In a pregnant patient the distinction rests almost entirely on morphology because tumour markers (e.g., CA‑125) are unreliable and gadolinium cannot be used.  In the present case the two large unilocular cysts showed a constellation of findings that pointed toward a leiomyomatous origin rather than an ovarian endometrioma.

---

### 1. Ultrasound clues

| Feature | Typical for cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma | Typical for decidualised endometrioma |
|--------|-------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Relationship to uterus** | *Continuity* with the uterine serosa or myometrium; often a visible stalk or broad‑based attachment (the “uterine‐myometrial continuity sign”). In our patient the lower mass was felt fixed in the pelvis and US showed it adherent to a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma. | Usually arises from the ovary and has no demonstrable connection to uterine tissue. |
| **Wall thickness** | Thick, sometimes irregular capsule ≥ 10 mm (the case showed walls 1.4 cm). | Thin wall ≤ 5 mm; if present it is smooth. |
| **Internal echo pattern** | Heterogeneous echogenicity reflecting mixed fluid‑necrotic content; may contain internal septa or floating debris. | Homogeneous low‑level “ground‑glass” echoes (“steady echo pattern”) typical of an endometrioma. |
| **Vascularity on colour Doppler** | Peripheral vessels that branch from uterine arteries and may traverse a pedicle; occasional feeding vessel seen at the stalk. The case demonstrated peripheral vascularisation of both lesions consistent with uterine supply. | Peripheral flow may be present but is usually modest; central flow is absent because the lesion is avascular fibrosis/hemo‑debris. |
| **Mobility** | Limited mobility when attached to uterus; may be tethered or fixed (“fixed mass”). The caudal lesion was fixed at the posterior fornix on bimanual exam. | Usually mobile separate from uterus unless torsed. |

---

### 2. Non‑contrast MRI pearls

| Feature | Leiomyoma with cystic degeneration | Decidualised endometrioma |
|--------|-----------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Signal of the capsule** | Thick hypointense rim (fibrous pseudocapsule) ≈ 1–1.5 cm on both T1‑fat sat and T2‑fat sat – exactly what was described (“thick walls 1.4 cm”). | Thin hypointense rim only if present; wall usually ≤ 5 mm. |
| **Cystic content** | Heterogeneous high signal on T2 (fluid) often mixed with areas of low/intermediate signal from proteinaceous debris; no classic “shading.” The case showed *inhomogeneous* fluid on both T1‐fat sat and T2‐fat sat images. | Classic “T2 shading”: progressive loss of signal intensity on T2 due to repeated hemorrhage (“dark spot”), often accompanied by uniformly bright T1 signal from blood products – not seen here. |
| **Solid component** | Iso‑ or mildly hyperintense nodules that are isointense to adjacent myometrium on both sequences (“focal hyperintense T1–T2 solid components”). The caudal mass displayed such foci adjoining a known intramural myoma, confirming continuity with uterine muscle. | If decidualisation occurs the lesion may become more heterogeneous but retains overall high T1 intensity without discrete solid nodules that follow myometrial signal intensity. |
| **Continuity sign** | Sagittal or coronal planes reveal uninterrupted low‑signal junctional zone between lesion and uterine wall—pseudocapsule merges with uterine serosa/myometrium (“junctional continuity”). In this patient MRI suggested that the caudal cyst was *in continuation* with a posterior intramural–subserosal fibroid measuring 58 × 71 mm. | No such junctional continuity; ovarian origin remains separate from uterus. |
| **Vascular pedicle** *(if present)* | Even without gadolinium one can infer a pedicle by following enlarged vessels emerging from uterine vasculature into the mass; absent in pure ovarian lesions. The upper mass lacked an obvious stalk because of its size but its peripheral vessels were clearly uterine in origin on Doppler correlates. |

---

### How these features guided management in the reported case

- **Thick (>10 mm) irregular wall** and **heterogeneous fluid** argued against the uniform “ground‑glass/shading” of an endometrioma.
- **Clear continuity** between the caudal mass and a known posterior intramural/subserosal fibroid—as shown by both US (fixed pelvic mass) and MRI (adjacent solid component sharing signal intensity with myometrium)—favoured a cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma.
- **Peripheral vascularisation arising from uterine vessels** further supported a uterine source rather than ovarian.
- The combination of these imaging signs outweighed the modest rise in CA‑125 and prompted surgical excision of the pedunculated upper lesion while leaving the lower intramural component in situ.

---

#### Bottom line

When facing large unilocular adnexal‐appearing cysts in pregnancy:

- **Look for a thick (>10 mm) fibrous capsule**, heterogeneous fluid without classic T2 shading, and any *junctional zone continuity* with uterus—these point toward cystic degeneration of a leiomyoma.
- **Absence of continuity**, thin smooth walls, homogeneous ground‑glass echo pattern on US, and characteristic low‑signal “shading” on T2 favour decidualised endometrioma.

These morphologic hallmarks allow clinicians to discriminate between two entities that otherwise share overlapping clinical presentations and modest tumor‐marker elevations, thereby steering appropriate obstetric–surgical decision‑making as illustrated by this case.
