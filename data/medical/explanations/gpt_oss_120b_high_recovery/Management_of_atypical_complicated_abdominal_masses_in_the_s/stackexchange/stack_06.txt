Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Morning Report] When a symptomatic pedunculated leiomyoma requires excision at 21–22 weeks gestation, what are the key patient‑ and tumor‑related factors that guide the decision between a laparoscopic versus an open laparotomic approach?

**Morning Report Pearls – Surgical Management of a Symptomatic Pedunculated Leiomyoma in Mid‑Second Trimester**

When a pregnant woman presents with a painful pedunculated myoma that must be removed at ≈ 21–22 weeks gestation, the choice between a minimally‑invasive (laparoscopic) versus an open (laparotomic) myomectomy hinges on a short list of *patient* and *tumour* variables that determine whether a safe operative field can be created without jeopardising fetal viability or maternal safety.

---

### 1. Patient‑related determinants  

| Factor | Why it matters | Practical threshold used in practice |
|--------|----------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Gestational age / uterine size** | By week 20–22 the gravid uterus reaches the umbilicus and occupies most of the abdominal cavity, limiting trocar placement, reducing working space, and raising the risk of accidental uterine injury when establishing pneumoperitoneum. | ≤ 14 weeks (early 2nd trimester) favours laparoscopy when other criteria are met; ≥ 20 weeks often pushes toward laparotomy unless the lesion is very small and clearly accessible. |
| **Maternal habitus (BMI & abdominal wall thickness)** | Obesity makes blind Veress entry hazardous and shortens instrument reach. In our patient (BMI 33) trocar insertion would have required excessive angles through thick pannus – a recognised cause of conversion or intra‑op complications. | BMI < 30 kg/m² generally acceptable for standard laparoscopy; BMI ≥ 30 often prompts careful Hasson entry or conversion to open access when combined with a large uterus. |
| **Comorbidities that impair CO₂ tolerance** | Laparoscopy requires Trendelenburg positioning and CO₂ insufflation; patients who already have compromised diaphragmatic mechanics (dyspnoea from a massive sub‑diaphragmatic mass) may not tolerate the additional intra‑abdominal pressure or shoulder‑tip pain without worsening maternal oxygenation. | Presence of significant respiratory compromise → favour laprotomy. |
| **Fetal considerations** | Continuous fetal heart‑rate monitoring is mandatory before, during and after any non‑obstetric surgery (Zhao et al.). With an enlarged uterus the abdomen is often too crowded to place trans‑abdominal dopplers reliably during laparoscopy; an open field allows direct visualisation of the uterus and rapid response to any emergent fetal distress. | Need for reliable intra‑op fetal monitoring → laparotomy when uterine contour hinders doppler placement. |
| **Surgeon/centre expertise** | Even experienced minimally‑invasive surgeons defer to an open approach when workspace is limited or tumour characteristics predict uncontrolled bleeding. A multidisciplinary team comfortable with intra‑op obstetric ultrasound can safely proceed minimally invasively only if they are accustomed to pregnant patients (> 15 weeks). | Institutional capability influences final decision more than any single numeric cut‑off.|

---

### 2. Tumour‑related determinants  

| Variable | Impact on surgical strategy |
|----------|-----------------------------|
| **Maximum diameter** | Large masses (> 10–12 cm) are difficult to extract through ≤ 10‑mm ports without morcellation – which is contraindicated when malignancy cannot be excluded or when necrotic tissue is present (risk of disseminating infected material). An 18‑cm pedunculated myoma therefore mandates an incision large enough for intact removal → laparotomy. |
| **Pedicle length & accessibility** | A short thick stalk embedded deep in the serosa makes secure clipping/snaring cumbersome laparoscopically; a long slender stalk protruding into the peritoneal cavity is ideal for minimally invasive excision. In this case the pedicle was short, twisted, and surrounded by abundant vessels – best controlled under direct vision through an open field. |
| **Vascularity / necrosis** | Highly vascular tumours increase intra‑op blood loss; necrotic tissue may bleed unpredictably after detorsion. Open surgery permits rapid suture ligation of the uterine defect and immediate haemostasis (“double‑clamp” technique used here). |
| **Location relative to uterus & surrounding organs** | Anterior low‑lying lesions are often reachable by laparoscopy; posterior‐high lesions that extend into the left sub‑phrenic space put vital structures (diaphragm, splenic flexure) at risk during trocar insertion or specimen extraction. The reported myoma stretched from the subphrenic region down to the fundus – a location unfavourable for safe port placement.<br>|
| **Number & concomitant masses** | Multiple bulky leiomyomas reduce available “free” peritoneal space needed for camera manipulation; they also increase the likelihood that traction on one will disturb another, precipitating uterine irritability or premature labour.<br>|
| **Radiologic suspicion for atypia/malignancy** | Non‑contrast MRI showed thick walls (1.4 cm), heterogeneous signal, and solid components – features that raised oncologic concern despite benign pathology on frozen section later. When imaging cannot definitively exclude sarcoma, morcellation must be avoided → open extraction preferred.|

---

### 3. Synthesis into an algorithm  

1️⃣ **Indication confirmed** – acute pain unresponsive to analgesia + evidence of torsion/necrosis → surgery required.  
2️⃣ **Assess gestational age & uterine height** – ≤ 14 weeks = consider laparoscopy if other criteria favourable; ≥ 20 weeks = strongly consider laparotomy unless tumour is tiny (< 8 cm) and clearly anterior low‑lying.  
3️⃣ **Evaluate BMI & abdominal wall thickness** – BMI ≥ 30 often pushes toward Hasson/open entry rather than Veress port placement in mid‑trimester pregnancy.  
4️⃣ **Review imaging** – Determine maximal diameter, stalk characteristics, vascular pattern, relationship to placenta/uterus ­– large (>12 cm), short/thick pedicle®, high vascularity → open excision.<br>  
5️⃣ **Check respiratory reserve & Trendelenburg tolerance** – Significant dyspnoea or diaphragmatic compression = avoid prolonged steep Trendelenburg/laparoscopic insufflation pressures (>12 mmHg).  
6️⃣ **Confirm ability for continuous fetal monitoring** – If Doppler probe cannot be reliably placed because uterus fills abdomen → open approach provides unobstructed access.“  
7️⃣ **Decision point:** When ≥ 2 “high‑risk” tumour factors *or* ≥ 2 patient factors exist → default to laparotomy; otherwise minimally invasive technique may be pursued with an experienced team and intra‑op obstetric support.

---

### 4. Application to the presented case  

- **Gestational age:** 21 w+6 d – uterus already occupying most abdominal cavity.
- **BMI:** 33 kg/m² – thick abdominal wall limited safe percutaneous entry.
- **Mass characteristics:** 18 × 12 × 7 cm pedunculated leiomyoma extending into left subphrenic space; twisted stalk with active bleeding/ischemic necrosis evident intra‑operatively.
- **Vascularity:** Highly vascularised stalk required immediate haemostatic control.
- **Respiratory status:** Pre‑operative dyspnoea from mechanical diaphragmatic compression made Trendelenburg intolerable.
- **Fetal monitoring:** Open field allowed reliable transabdominal Doppler throughout operation per Zhao et al.’s recommendation.
- **Oncologic uncertainty:** Thickened walls on MRI warranted removal without morcellation.

Given these converging patient‐(mid‐second trimester, obesity, respiratory compromise) and tumour‐related cues (size >15 cm, short/twisted pedicle, high vascularity), the multidisciplinary team appropriately elected a longitudinal xipho–pubic laparotomy rather than a laparoscopic route—exactly as described in the case report.

---

### 5. Take‑home messages  

1️⃣ **Gestational timing matters more than “pregnancy itself.”** After ~14–16 weeks uterine bulk limits safe port placement; this is amplified by obesity or high cervical location.
2️⃣ **Mass size >10–12 cm or a short thick pedicle = poor fit for laparoscopy**, because extraction without morcellation becomes impossible and haemostatic control is less reliable.
3️⃣ **Respiratory mechanics dictate tolerability of pneumoperitoneum/Trendelenburg**—dyspnoea from large supradiaphragmatic lesions tips the balance toward open surgery.
4️⃣ **Continuous fetal heart‐rate surveillance must be feasible throughout**; loss of signal during minimally invasive work should trigger conversion.
5️⃣ **Never sacrifice oncologic safety for minimally invasive convenience:** any suspicion of atypia mandates intact specimen removal—most safely accomplished via laparotomy in pregnancy.

In summary, deciding between laparoscopic versus open myomectomy at 21–22 weeks rests on a pragmatic assessment of maternal physiologic reserve, uterine dimensions, body habitus, tumor size/pedicle anatomy, vascularity/necrosis risk, and ability to monitor the fetus continuously—all illustrated by this patient’s successful open excision of an enormous torsed pedunculated leiomyoma while preserving pregnancy outcome.*
