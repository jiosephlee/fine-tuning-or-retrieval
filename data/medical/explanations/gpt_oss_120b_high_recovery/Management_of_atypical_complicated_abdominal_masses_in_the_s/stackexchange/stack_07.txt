Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Morning Report] During intra‑operative myomectomy performed in the second trimester, what fetal monitoring protocol should be employed before, during, and after surgery, and what is the rationale for administering peri‑operative progesterone?

**Intra‑operative Myomectomy in the Second Trimester – Fetal Monitoring & Peri‑operative Progesterone**

*Key teaching point*: Whenever a pregnant uterus is entered surgically, fetal viability must be documented before the skin incision, protected throughout the operative interval, and reassessed promptly after closure. Because uterine manipulation provokes a temporary functional “withdrawal” of progesterone activity, supplemental progestins are given peri‑operatively to preserve uterine quiescence and reduce the risk of pre‑term labour.

---

### Fetal Monitoring Protocol

| Phase | What to do | Why it matters |
|-------|------------|----------------|
| **Pre‑operative (baseline)** | • Perform a formal obstetric assessment in the operating suite or pre‑op holding area.<br>• Obtain a **non‑stress test (NST) / continuous cardiotocography (CTG)** or at minimum a **transabdominal Doppler** recording of fetal heart rate (FHR) for ≥5 min.<br>• Document fetal tone/movement and amniotic fluid status by bedside ultrasound.<br>• Confirm maternal hemodynamics (BP ≥ 100 mm Hg mean arterial pressure, oxygen saturation ≥ 95 %). | Establishes a reference FHR pattern against which any intra‑operative changes can be judged; identifies occult distress before anesthesia exposure. |
| **Intra‑operative** | • **Continuous external CTG** is preferred when the abdominal incision permits placement of the transducer above the uterus (most feasible during a midline laparotomy).<br>• If the surgical field blocks the transducer, switch to **intermittent Doppler/ultrasound** checks **every 5–10 min**, with an immediate read after any uterine handling (e.g., myoma pedicle clamping, suturing).<br>• Record baseline rate, variability, decelerations and any arrhythmias.<br>• Maintain maternal normothermia, oxygenation (SpO₂ > 95 %), normovolemia and avoid deep neuromuscular blockade that could mask uterine irritability.<br>• Alert the obstetric consultant whenever a persistent deceleration (>30 sec) or loss of variability occurs; be prepared to halt manipulation or give rescue agents (e.g., IV phenoxybenzamine or terbutaline). | Continuous surveillance detects acute hypoxia or vagal responses caused by uterine traction, anesthetic agents, or hemorrhage. Intermittent checks are acceptable when technical constraints exist, as demonstrated in this case where “intermittent transvaginal ultrasounds monitored fetal viability throughout the operation.” |
| **Immediate post‑operative** | • Within **15 min of skin closure**, re‑establish **continuous CTG** (or Doppler if not possible) while the patient is still in the recovery area.<br>• Confirm stable baseline FHR with reassuring variability and no late decelerations.<br>• Document maternal vitals and analgesia level because pain‑induced catecholamine surge can precipitate contractions. | Guarantees that any delayed ischemic insult or uterine irritability induced by suture line tension is caught early. |
| **Early post‑op surveillance** | • Keep **continuous CTG for at least 2 h** in recovery.<br>• If stable, transition to **intermittent bedside FHR auscultation/handheld Doppler every 4–6 h** for the first 24 h.<br>• Follow with daily obstetric ultrasound (growth scan & amniotic fluid index) as part of routine prenatal care. | Allows detection of evolving uterine irritability or subclinical pre‑term labour that often manifests hours after surgery. |

*Evidence base*: Zhao et al., a multicenter series of pregnant women undergoing non‑obstetric surgery, mandated documentation of FHR before incision, continuously during surgery and again after closure—a practice now endorsed by ACOG Committee Opinion on “Nonobstetric Surgery During Pregnancy” (2023).

---

### Rationale for Peri‑operative Progesterone

1. **Progesterone is the principal “uterine relaxant.”**  
   - In pregnancy it maintains decidualization and suppresses myometrial contractility via activation of the PR‑B isoform and inhibition of calcium influx.  
   - Surgical stress triggers a surge in inflammatory cytokines (IL‑6, TNF‑α) and prostaglandin synthesis (PGF₂α), which functionally antagonize progesterone’s quiescent effect—a phenomenon described as “functional progesterone withdrawal.”

2. **Exogenous progestins blunt this cascade.**  
   - Intramuscular **hydroxyprogesterone caproate** provides sustained serum levels (~10–20 µg/mL) that counteract operatively induced PG release.  
   - Short‑acting **100 mg IM micronised progesterone** given intra‑operatively rapidly raises circulating levels while anesthesia suppresses endogenous secretion.

3. **Clinical data supporting this strategy:**  
   - Randomized trials in women with prior spontaneous pre‑term birth have shown a ≈30 % reduction in delivery before 34 weeks when receiving weekly hydroxyprogesterone caproate from 16–20 weeks onward (Meis et al., NEJM 2003).   
   - Observational series of myomectomy during pregnancy report lower rates of post‑surgical uterine contractions when peri‑operative progesterone was administered (e.g., De Nola et al., current case).

4. **Specific dosing used in this case:**  
   - **100 mg IM micronised progesterone** intra‑operatively + daily until discharge → ensures an immediate rise in serum hormone during the critical window when inflammation peaks.<br>
   - Followed by **341 mg hydroxyprogesterone caproate IM every 3 days** (as per package insert) continued through pregnancy → maintains quiescence through the remainder of gestation when the residual pelvic myoma could otherwise act as a mechanical irritant.

5. **Safety profile:**  
   - Both formulations are Category B/unknown but have been used extensively in pregnancy without teratogenicity.<br>
   - No increase in maternal thromboembolic events has been reported when appropriate thromboprophylaxis is employed as part of standard peri‑operative care.

---

### Bottom Line for Residents

- **Baseline NST/CTG → continuous or frequent intermittent FHR monitoring throughout surgery → immediate post‑op reassessment**—this triad safeguards fetal oxygenation whenever you breach the uterus.
- **Give peri‑operative progesterone:** a single intra‐operative dose followed by daily low‐dose IM supplementation (or weekly hydroxyprogesterone caproate thereafter) to offset surgically induced “functional” progesterone withdrawal and markedly lower the odds of iatrogenic pre‑term labor.
- Document all readings meticulously; involve your obstetric consultant early if any non‑reassuring pattern appears—prompt intervention can convert a potential catastrophic outcome into an uneventful continuation of pregnancy, as illustrated by this case’s excellent neonatal result.
