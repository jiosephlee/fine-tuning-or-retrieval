Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# Heparin Before the Knife: How Pre‑Procedural UF Heparin Can Trigger Early Post‑Cardiac Surgery HIT  

*Why a “tiny” UF heparin bolus given a week before bypass can set off a fulminant autoimmune HIT that masquerades as routine post‑op thrombocytopenia – and what you can do about it today.*

---

## The Hook – When Platelets Crash Earlier Than Expected  

A few weeks ago an otherwise fit septuagenarian came off cardiopulmonary bypass without any hiccup—perfect valve prosthesis, clean anastomoses, smooth separation from pump. The next morning his platelets were down to 155 × 10⁹/L (a normal postoperative dip), but by **post‑op day 5** they were hovering around **50 × 10⁹/L**, and by day 6 they had plummeted under **30 × 10⁹/L** – concurrent with an acute left‐parietal stroke.  

The classic textbook teaching is that *heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia* after cardiac surgery shows up between **post‑op day 5–10**. Here the trajectory started **before day 5**, prompting us to ask: *Was something lurking before the sternotomy?*  

The answer turned out to be a **low‑dose unfractionated heparin (UFH) exposure during a pre‑operative cardiac catheterisation**—only **2 000 U**, delivered via the arterial sheath just seven days earlier. That single bolus primed an immune response that erupted as **autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT)** once the massive intra‑operative UFH dose was administered on bypass.  

That cascade was dramatic enough that it produced:

* Platelet nadir **13 × 10⁹/L** – one of the lowest post‑cardiac surgery counts ever reported.
* A large cerebral infarct despite therapeutic LMWH prophylaxis.
* A serotonin‑release assay (SRA) that was “indeterminate” because antibodies activated platelets **even without heparin** and were only partially blocked by Fc‑receptor inhibition—a pattern not seen in ordinary HIT.

If you’ve ever dismissed an early platelet dip as “just dilutional” after bypass, this case forces you to rethink your differential—and your peri‑operative checklist.

---

## Classic vs Early‑Onset HIT After Cardiac Surgery  

| Feature | Classic HIT (POD 5–10) | Early‐Onset HIT (< POD 5) |
|---|---|---|
| Typical incidence after CPB | **0.5–1 %**<sup>1</sup> | ≈ **10 %** of all post‑cardiac‐surgery HIT cases<sup>2</sup> |
| Common trigger | Post‑op UFH/LMWH prophylaxis | Recent *pre‑operative* UFH exposure (catheter labs, angiography) |
| Platelet nadir | Usually >30–50 × 10⁹/L | Can fall <20–30 × 10⁹/L (as low as 13 × 10⁹/L) |
| Thrombosis risk | Relative risk ≈15<sup>3</sup> | Even higher when antibodies are *autoimmune* |

The “early” phenotype has been recognized for over a decade but remains underappreciated because clinicians often focus on intra‑operative heparin exposure alone. In reality, any UFH dose given within about **7–10 days** before CPB can act as a *priming* event—especially when followed by the massive bolus required for bypass (**≈45 000 U** in our patient).

---

## The Priming Effect — Small Doses Set Off Big Fires  

Why does a modest **2 000 U** bolus matter?  
1️⃣ **Immune Complex Formation:** Even low concentrations of UFH allow PF4 released from activated platelets or endothelial cells during catheterisation to bind forming PF4/heparin complexes—the nidus for anti‑PF4 antibodies.<br>
2️⃣ **Sensitisation:** In susceptible individuals—often those with previous heparin exposures or underlying autoimmune tendencies—the first encounter triggers B‐cell activation without overt thrombocytopenia.<br>
3️⃣ **Amplification on CPB:** The subsequent high‐dose UFH creates an overwhelming load of PF4/heparin complexes that crosslink IgG FcγIIa receptors on platelets, leading either to classic HIT or, in some cases, bypasses the heparin requirement altogether (**autoimmune HIT**) where antibodies directly activate platelets independent of any added heparin.<br>

In our patient’s serum, the SRA showed **96 % serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin**, confirming that his antibodies could trigger platelet activation *without* exogenous heparin—classic aHIT behavior likely accelerated by prior sensitisation.  

What’s crucial is that this phenomenon isn’t limited to catheter labs. Any procedure requiring short bursts of UFH—vascular access placement, peripheral angiography, even intra‐arterial line placement with flushing—can serve as the primer if it occurs close enough to definitive cardiac surgery.

---

## Autoimmune HIT – When Antibodies Go Rogue  

Traditional HIT hinges on **heparin‐dependent** IgG anti‑PF4 antibodies that cause platelet activation only at therapeutic heparin concentrations (0.1–0.3 U/mL) and are inhibited at supratherapeutic levels (>100 U/mL).  

In contrast, *autoimmune* HIT demonstrates:

* Platelet activation at **zero** added heparin.
* Persistent activation even when heparin is present at therapeutic levels.
* Variable response to FcγIIa blockade—our case showed only **partial inhibition** by monoclonal antibody IV.3 (76 % release remained), which led the reference laboratory initially to label the SRA “indeterminate.”  
* A more severe clinical picture: profound thrombocytopenia, consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer), thrombotic complications despite anticoagulation.



Because functional assays vary between labs, clinicians should explicitly ask the performing laboratory whether they inspected **zero‑heparin wells** and **Fc‐blockade panels** when evaluating suspected early HIT after cardiac surgery. A missed aHIT diagnosis can delay appropriate therapy and increase morbidity—as happened here until argatroban plus high‑dose IVIG were initiated.

---

## Laboratory Clues You Might Have Overlooked  

1️⃣ **PF4 ELISA OD > 2** → high pretest probability  
2️⃣ **SRA pattern:**  
   * ↑Serotonine release at 0 U/mL → suggests aHIT  
   * Partial inhibition by IV.3 → signals unusually potent antibodies  
   * Lack of inhibition at supratherapeutic heparin → classic hallmark remains present  

If you see these patterns—or receive an “indeterminate” SRA—it’s worth pursuing repeat testing after a few days or obtaining an alternative functional assay (e.g., HIPA) rather than dismissing it as false positive/negative.

---

## How We Should Screen & Monitor After Cardiac Surgery  

Given the potential for early priming:

| Step | Timing | Who’s Covered |
|---|---|---|
| **Baseline CBC & PF4 ELISA** | Pre‑op clinic (ideally ≤24 h before cath) | Any patient scheduled for cardiac surgery who will receive any UFH ≤7 days prior |
| **Daily CBC** | POD 1–7 *or until platelet count stabilises above 150×10⁹/L* | Patients with any recent UFH exposure *or* who receive post‑op LMWH/UFH |
| **Thresholds prompting workup** | • ≥30 % fall from baseline <150×10⁹/L <br>• Absolute count <100×10⁹/L <br>• New thrombus/neurologic change | Same group above |
| **Rapid HIT algorithm** | As soon as threshold met | Initiate non‑heparin anticoagulation while awaiting labs |

A practical way to embed this into ICU workflow is adding a simple checkbox on the daily “post‑cardiac surgery” bundle: “Any UFH ≤7 days before bypass? □ Yes □ No”. If “Yes,” order daily CBCs and keep a low threshold for sending PF4 ELISA even before day 5 when classical teaching would say “too early.”

---

## Treatment Pearls From This Case  

1️⃣ **Non‐heparin anticoagulation ASAP:** Argatroban was chosen initially because its short half-life allowed rapid reversal if bleeding occurred around his recent stroke. When fibrinolysis markers suggested ongoing consumption despite argatroban (` falling fibrinogen`, `rising d-dimer`), we switched to therapeutic fondaparinux plus AT concentrate—a strategy supported by emerging series showing good control of both classic and autoimmune HIT when factor Xa inhibition is reliable.<br>

2️⃣ **High‑dose IVIG:** Administered 100 g daily ×3 gives rapid neutralization of FcγIIa receptors but may be less effective when antibodies are exceptionally strong—as reflected by only modest platelet rebound (`13 → 54 ×10⁹/L`). Nonetheless it remains one of the few adjuncts available and should be considered early when platelet count <20 × 10⁹/L or there’s evidence of consumptive coagulopathy.<br>

3️⃣ **Monitoring beyond platelets:** Serial fibrinogen and D‑dimer trends provide indirect insight into whether hypercoagulability is being tamed—the falling fibrinogen noted after argatroban signified ongoing consumption requiring regimen change.<br>

4️⃣ **Transition back to oral anticoagulation:** Once platelets recovered ≥150 ×10⁹/L and functional assays normalized (~POD 65 in our patient), apixaban was reintroduced safely—a reminder that DOACs remain viable long-term options after hit resolution provided there’s no residual renal/hepatic dysfunction.<br>

---

## Bottom Line – Make Early Priming Part of Your Checklist  

When you see an unexplained drop in platelets within the first four postoperative days after CPB—even if it seems modest—you should ask yourself:

*Did this patient receive any unfractionated heparin in the week preceding surgery?*  

If yes:

* Expect potential **early-onset**, possibly autoimmune TIPA (“THROMBOCYTOPENIA IN PATIENT WITH CARDIAC SURGERY”), which may behave more aggressively than classic postoperative thrombocytopenia.
* Actively pursue CFAT testing—don’t let “too early” deter you.
* Initiate non-hep anticoagulation promptly.
* Consider IVIG especially if nadir <20 × 10⁹/L or there’s evidence of consumptive coagulopathy.


### Take-Home Messages

- A single low‑dose UFH bolus given during pre‐operative catheterisation can prime an immune response that erupts as severe early‐onset HIT after cardiopulmonary bypass.
- Approximately **10 %** of post‑cardiac surgery HIT cases present before POD 5—and most have antecedent UFH exposure within the preceding week.
- Autoimmune HIT may show platelet activation even without added heparin and may be only partially inhibited by Fc‐receptor blockade; request these specific SRA controls when suspicion is high.
- Daily platelet surveillance through POD 7 (or longer if any recent UFH exposure) coupled with a low threshold for workup (>30 % fall or <150 ×10⁹/L) improves detection.
- In severe cases start argatroban (or another direct thrombin inhibitor) promptly; consider IVIG and transition to fondaparinux/AT concentrate if consumptive coagulopathy persists.
- Incorporate a simple “pre‑procedure UFH?” checkbox into perioperative handoffs—preventable priming events become visible hazards rather than hidden surprises.
