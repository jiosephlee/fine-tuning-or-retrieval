Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Hidden Mast Cell Disease: When Systemic Mastocytosis Triggers Unusual Anaphylaxis  

*Why a “simple” drug allergy can hide a lifelong mast‑cell disorder – and what to do about it.*

---

## Why This Case Stands Out

If you’ve ever managed an anaphylactic shock, you know the typical script: rapid epinephrine, fluids, antihistamine, observation, discharge. What makes the case of a 60‑year‑old woman who received a single dose of clarithromycin so striking is that she didn’t just have one episode – she suffered **four distinct anaphylactic attacks** within 12 hours despite appropriate epinephrine dosing and cessation of the offending drug.  

Even more intriguing is that the repeat reactions were not driven by lingering drug exposure; they appear to stem from **hyper‑responsive mast cells** caused by occult systemic mastocytosis. The diagnosis fell just shy of WHO criteria – two minor criteria met, no major criteria because a full bone‑marrow work‑up was limited – yet her baseline tryptase remained markedly elevated, flagging a hidden mast‑cell disease that transformed a routine antibiotic reaction into a multiphasic anaphylactic nightmare.

---

## A Brief Recap

| Feature | Details |
|---------|----------|
| **Trigger** | First dose of oral clarithromycin 500 mg (no prior exposure). |
| **Initial presentation** | Pruritus, flushing, agitation, hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxaemia (SpO₂ 88 %). |
| **Acute treatment** | IM 0.3 mg epinephrine auto‑injector ×2 + IV clemastine 2 mg + O₂ + 500 mL NS. |
| **Course** | After initial improvement she was admitted to the ward; four total anaphylactic episodes (primary + three relapses) over ≈12 h, each resolving with additional IM epinephrine (first two doses via auto‑injector, later two doses with manually drawn 0.5 mg). |
| **Adjuncts** | Corticosteroids (prednisone 30 mg IV daily ×5 + one dose dexamethasone) were added after the first relapse despite growing evidence against routine steroid use. |
| **Outcome** | No further episodes after ~24 h; discharged after three days on levocetirizine and a new 0.3 mg epinephrine auto‑injector. |
| **Mast‑cell work‑up** | Elevated baseline tryptase (> 20 ng/mL); minor WHO criteria met (elevated tryptase & possibly unrelated organomegaly); bone marrow biopsy insufficient for major criteria. |

## Mastocytosis Meets Anaphylaxis – Pathophysiology in Plain Language

Systemic mastocytosis is essentially **a clonal expansion of tissue mast cells** that carry activating KIT mutations (most often D816V). These extra mast cells sit primed to degranulate, releasing histamine, tryptase, prostaglandins, leukotrienes and platelet‑activating factor at lower thresholds than in a person with normal mast‑cell numbers.

When any IgE‐mediated trigger arrives—be it drug antigen or even a physical stimulus—those mast cells can release **massive bursts of mediators** in a cascade that overwhelms physiologic compensatory mechanisms:

1. **Vasodilation & capillary leak → hypotension & hypoxaemia**
2. **Bronchoconstriction & mucus → dyspnoea**
3. **Urticaria/angioedema → skin and upper airway swelling**
4. **Late‐phase mediator release → biphasic or multiphasic reactions**

Because the reservoir of releasable mediators is larger in systemic mastocytosis, the “second wave” can surface hours after the initial insult even if the trigger has been cleared—a classic recipe for biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis.

## Spotting Hidden Mast‑Cell Disease in Real Time

### 1️⃣ Baseline Tryptase — Your First Red Flag

* **What to measure:** Serum total tryptase at two time points—once during acute reaction (within 1–4 h of symptom onset) and again ≥24 h after full resolution for baseline.
* **Interpretation:**  
   * Normal: <11 ng/mL in most labs.  
   * Elevated baseline (>11–13 ng/mL) prompts consideration of clonal mast‑cell disease; >20 ng/mL carries a high positive predictive value for systemic mastocytosis.
* **Case lesson:** In our patient baseline tryptase was ≥20 ng/mL on follow‑up; unfortunately no sample was taken during the first attack which would have helped delineate peak vs baseline values.

### 2️⃣ WHO Diagnostic Criteria – Major vs Minor

| Criterion | Requirement |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Major** | Multifocal dense infiltrates of >15 mast cells in bone marrow or other extracutaneous tissue.</b>|
| **Minor #1** | Serum tryptase >20 ng/mL |
| **Minor #2** | KIT D816V mutation detected in peripheral blood or bone marrow |
| **Minor #3** | CD25/CD2 expression on mast cells |
| **Minor #4** | >25 % atypical spindle‐shaped or atypical morphology in infiltrates |

A diagnosis requires the major criterion *plus* one minor OR at least three minor criteria. In our patient only two minors were fulfilled (elevated tryptase + possible organomegaly neuropsychiatric features suggesting clonal disease), falling short of definitive WHO labeling but still signaling a “probable” clonal mast‑cell disorder.

### 3️⃣ Bone Marrow Biopsy – The Gold Standard With Caveats

*Biopsy provides direct histology & molecular testing.* However:
- It may be technically limited if marrow aspirate material is insufficient.
- Rarely performed emergently; timing can delay definitive labeling.
- In cases where suspicion is high but biopsy is non‑diagnostic, clinicians may still treat as “probable systemic mastocytosis” if baseline tryptase remains high and clinical course is severe—just like our patient.

### 4️⃣ Clinical Clues Beyond Labs

- Prior unexplained severe reactions (e.g., wasp sting with intense local swelling).
- Unexplained hepatomegaly or splenomegaly.
- Neuropsychiatric complaints such as depression or memory lapses—some registries link these to chronic mediator release.

When these converge with elevated tryptase—even without full WHO confirmation—you should adopt a high‐risk management plan akin to confirmed systemic mastocytosis.

## Multiphasic Anaphylaxis: Risk Factors Highlighted by This Case

| Potential predictor | Evidence from case |
|---------------------|---------------------|
| Severe initial presentation (hypotension & hypoxaemia) | Present |
| Delay in epinephrine? – No; administered promptly | N/A |
| Multiple epinephrine doses needed | Four episodes → repeated dosing |
| Under‑dosed auto‑injector (0.3 mg) in adult >50 kg | Yes – first two doses were suboptimal per guideline recommendation of 0.5 mg IM |
| Underlying mast‑cell disease | Highly probable |

While no single factor reliably predicts biphasic recurrence, combining severity markers with suspicion for clonal mast-cell disease should *lower* your threshold for extended observation and more aggressive prophylaxis.

---

## Treatment Pearls Extracted from the Story

1. **Epinephrine dose matters** – For adults >50 kg aim for 0.5 mg IM per dose (0.01 mg/kg). A 0.3 mg auto‑injector may be inadequate; consider drawing up additional epinephrine from vials if feasible.
2. **Rapid delivery beats perfect dosing** – In emergent settings where staff are uncomfortable handling ampoules, using an auto‑injector first is pragmatic provided you’re ready to give supplemental doses promptly.
3. **Corticosteroids are optional at best** – The patient received prednisone/dexamethasone because institutional protocols differed between wards and ICU; however contemporary data show no clear benefit in preventing biphasic reactions and they may increase ICU stay rates.
4. **Fluids matter especially when hypotension recurs** – A modest bolus (500–1000 mL NS) helped stabilize blood pressure during each relapse.
5. **Observation Duration** – Given that half of biphasic reactions occur within ≤12 h and our patient had three relapses within that window, a minimum *24‑hour* monitored stay is advisable for high‐risk patients.
6. **Early allergology input** – Engaging specialists early allowed discussion about continuous IV epinephrine versus watchful waiting—though neither was ultimately needed once symptom intensity waned.
7. **Discharge planning:** prescribe an H1 antihistamine (levocetirizine) plus educate on urgent use of an appropriate‐strength auto‑injector and arrange hematology/allergy follow‑up before discharge.

## A Pragmatic Follow‑Up Pathway for Suspected Systemic Mastocytosis After Anaphylaxis

1. **Within the first week**
   - Obtain serum total tryptase levels *as soon as possible* (ideally before steroids are started).
   - Repeat at peak window (1–3 h after symptom onset) if feasible.
   - Schedule baseline sample ≥24 h after full clinical resolution.

2. **Specialist referral**
   - Refer simultaneously to allergy/immunology *and* hematology/oncology who are familiar with mast cell neoplasms.
   - Provide them with all lab values, medication exposures and a detailed timeline of episodes.

3. **Diagnostic workup**
   - Peripheral blood KIT D816V PCR assay – minimally invasive screening.
   - If positive or if baseline tryptase remains >20 ng/mL despite negative PCR, arrange bone marrow biopsy with immunophenotyping for CD2/CD25 expression.
   - Imaging if organomegaly suspected (abdominal US/CT).

4. **Risk stratification & prophylaxis**
   - High baseline tryptase (>20 ng/mL) → start daily H1 antihistamine ± H2 blocker.
   - Consider leukotriene receptor antagonist if bronchospasm history exists.
   - For documented systemic mastocytosis: discuss prophylactic MC stabilizers (cromolyn sodium), vitamin D optimization and avoidance strategies for known triggers (certain NSAIDs, radiocontrast).

5. **Patient empowerment**
   - Train on recognizing early signs of mediator release (“flushing‐pruritus‐tight chest”).
   - Ensure they carry two appropriately dosed epinephrine auto‑injectors (~0.5 mg each) at all times.
   - Provide written emergency action plan linking any future reaction directly back to specialist team.

6. **Long‐term monitoring**
   - Reassess serum tryptase annually or sooner if new reactions occur.
   - Screen for anemia, osteoporosis or gastrointestinal infiltration—known complications of systemic mastocytosis.

---

## Take‑Home Messages

- **Hidden clonal mast cell disease can turn an otherwise ordinary drug allergy into multiphasic anaphylaxis**, even when standard acute care appears adequate.
- **Baseline serum tryptase is your gatekeeper:** values >11–13 ng/mL merit repeat testing and specialist referral; >20 ng/mL raises strong suspicion for systemic mastocytosis.
- **WHO diagnostic criteria require either one major + one minor or three minor elements**; when tissue data are incomplete but clinical severity aligns with elevated tryptase, treat as “probable” disease pending further workup.
- **Epinephrine dosing matters:** adult IM dose should be ~0·5 mg; rely on auto‑injectors only as bridging tools—be ready to give supplemental doses promptly.
- **Corticosteroids are not proven to prevent biphasic reactions** and should no longer be routine; focus on epinephrine, fluids and close monitoring instead.
- For high‑risk patients (*severe initial presentation*, *multiple epinephrine doses*, *elevated baseline tryptase*), aim for at least *24 h observation* post‐resolution before discharge.
- Early involvement of allergy/immunology and hematology ‑ combined with systematic tryptase sampling ‑ paves the way toward definitive diagnosis and tailored prophylaxis.

---

Understanding that systemic mast cell disorders often lurk beneath “ordinary” allergic presentations empowers us to anticipate complicated courses like multiphasic anaphylaxis—and ultimately saves lives through smarter observation windows, correct epinephrine dosing, and targeted follow-up care. Keep your eyes on that serum tryptase number—it’s more than just a lab value; it’s often the first clue that something more profound is driving the storm inside your patient’s immune system.
