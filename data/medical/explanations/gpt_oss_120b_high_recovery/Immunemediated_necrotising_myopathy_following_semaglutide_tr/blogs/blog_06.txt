Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Pharmacovigilance in the Era of Obesity Therapeutics: Building Safe Prescribing Practices for Semaglutide  

---

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

The diabetes/obesity armamentarium has exploded over the last few years thanks to glucagon‑like peptide‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs). Semaglutide sits at the top of that wave because it delivers impressive weight loss and glycaemic control while most prescribers expect mainly gastrointestinal side effects. The story below turned that expectation on its head—a single injection was followed several months later by an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). This isn’t simply another episode of rhabdomyolysis that resolves with fluids—it required steroids + IVIG and finally led the patient back to her baseline only after stopping semaglutide permanently. When a drug that millions are now taking shows up on an autoimmune muscle‐disease radar, every clinician who writes a prescription should pause and ask what we can do now to keep our patients safe while the signal matures.

---

## Clinical vignette recap  

A woman in her early sixties presented three years ago with non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and class II obesity. Her past medical history also included hypertension, hypothyroidism (TSH normal on assessment), remote deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism and no prior exposure to statins or other agents commonly linked to necrotising myopathies.  

Because her steatohepatitis persisted despite lifestyle measures, she was started on semaglutide 0.25 mg subcutaneously—the approved low‐dose initiation step. Within hours she developed severe nausea/vomiting leading to hypovolaemic acute kidney injury, metabolic acidosis and brief non‑invasive ventilation support two days later. At admission her creatine kinase (CK) was modestly raised at 337 U/L—far below levels seen in classic toxin‑driven rhabdo—and there were no pigmenturia or overt muscle pain reported at that time. She recovered renal function after aggressive hydration and was discharged five days later still feeling deconditioned but otherwise stable.  

Over the ensuing four months she noted insidious proximal limb weakness that progressed rapidly enough that she could no longer stand unaided and developed dysphagia/dysphonia affecting both liquids and solids. On re‑presentation her CK had peaked at 3 992 U/L accompanied by mildly elevated transaminases (AST 191 U/L, ALT 165 U/L) consistent with concurrent hepatic steatosis rather than pure muscle breakdown. A comprehensive autoimmune screen was essentially negative—including anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR, Mi‑2, Jo‑1 and other idiopathic inflammatory myopathy antibodies—though a low titre SS‑B antibody was present (2.4 × upper limit). 

MRI of both thighs showed symmetric oedema chiefly in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps regions without focal mass effect—a pattern compatible with an inflammatory process rather than isolated trauma or disuse atrophy. Definitive tissue diagnosis came from a right vastus lateralis biopsy revealing scattered necrotic fibers undergoing regeneration intermixed with abundant CD68⁺ macrophages phagocytosing debris (“myophagocytosis”), complement membrane attack complex deposition on sarcolemma and rare CD3⁺ T cells around vessels—all hallmarks of immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy despite negative canonical autoantibodies (seronegative IMNM).  

Therapy consisted initially of five days intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily followed by oral prednisone ≈ 1 mg/kg/day coupled with a total IVIG dose 2 g/kg spread over four days because her weakness remained strikingly profound despite steroids alone. Within two weeks her CK fell dramatically toward normal ranges and she began regaining functional strength enough to ambulate independently again. After an intercurrent pseudomonal urinary sepsis episode she was tapered off prednisone over roughly two months without relapse; she never required repeat IVIG owing both to clinical improvement and cost constraints surrounding continued therapy. Ten months after presentation she remained off all immunosuppressants, maintained normal CK values, regained pre‑illness functional status and avoided any further exposure to GLP‑1 RAs—including semaglutide—by choice.

## Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy—a quick primer  

IMNM occupies its own niche among inflammatory myopathies: rapid onset proximal weakness often accompanied by dysphagia/dysphonia, markedly elevated serum CK (typically > 1000 U/L), histology dominated by fiber necrosis/regeneration together with macrophage infiltrates rather than dense CD8⁺ T‐cell dominated endomysial inflammation seen in polymyositis. In roughly one third of patients an autoantibody target is identified—most commonly anti–HMGCR (often statin‐related) or anti–SRP—but seronegative disease still accounts for a substantial proportion where triggers remain cryptic until a medication exposure becomes temporally linked—as happened here with semaglutide.

---

## Differentiating immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy from classic rhabdomyolysis  

The familiar picture of drug­induced rhabdomyolysis features an abrupt surge in CK frequently exceeding ten thousand units per litre shortly after exposure followed within days by rapid decline once the offending agent is stopped and hydration administered; renal failure may ensue secondary to pigment nephropathy yet recovery usually occurs without immunosuppressive therapy. By contrast:

* Onset here was delayed—a single injection precipitated symptoms that intensified over weeks-to-months rather than hours.
* Peak CK rose gradually during out­patient follow up instead of crashing immediately.
* Muscle biopsy showed organized necrosis coupled with complement deposition—a signpost toward an antibody‐driven process rather than pure cytotoxic injury.
* Clinical trajectory responded robustly only after corticosteroids ± IVIG were added—not simply after aggressive fluids.
  
These distinctions matter because treating presumed rhabdo alone would miss an opportunity to modulate an evolving autoimmune attack amenable to early immunotherapy.

## Practical prescribing considerations  

When initiating any GLP‑1 RA today it remains reasonable simply to obtain baseline chemistry panels focusing on renal function + liver enzymes—as recommended in product labeling—but emerging anecdotal evidence argues that adding serum creatine kinase deserves serious consideration in selected individuals:

* Patients whose obesity coexist with hepatic steatosis/NASH already demonstrate chronic low‐grade inflammation—a milieu possibly priming aberrant immune responses.
* Those who harbor prior unexplained elevations in CK—even if modest—or who are taking concomitant agents known to affect skeletal muscle merit extra vigilance before adding another biologically active peptide.
* Thyroid dysfunction is another well recognized contributor to neuromuscular symptoms; confirming euthyroid status helps isolate the drug’s effect when weakness emerges unexpectedly soon after injection.

If baseline CK turns out markedly elevated (>5× upper limit), obtaining additional history directed toward cumulative exercise load, recent infections or latent inflammatory disease allows clinicians either deferred initiation pending clarification or close serial monitoring once treatment begins.

---

## Engaging patients – what they should watch out for  

Patients receiving semaglutide already expect gastrointestinal upset during titration week­ends—but they rarely anticipate progressive limb weakness or swallowing difficulty weeks down the road. A brief counselling script runs smoothly:

> “If you notice difficulty climbing stairs, rising from a chair without using your hands, trouble swallowing liquids versus solids—or even hoarseness that worsens—you should call us right away.”  
  
Encouraging adequate oral intake during titration helps prevent dehydration‐related renal insults that could confound subsequent laboratory interpretation (“was this just prerenal AKI versus evolving muscle injury?”). Offering concrete symptom checkpoints—in addition to standard glucose/BMI goals—creates a shared safety net where clinicians catch abnormal trajectories before irreversible damage accrues.

---

## How do we make sure these rare events get heard?  

Spontaneous adverse event reporting remains our primary conduit between bedside observation and regulatory action:

* In the United States practitioners file through **FDA MedWatch** via its web portal or downloadable Form 3500A; essential elements include product name/dose/date administered, succinct narrative describing the event chronology,
lab results—including peak CK,
diagnostic imaging,
biopsy findings,
and outcomes following drug discontinuation/therapy.
* European prescribers channel similar information through **EudraVigilance**, selecting “Suspected Adverse Reaction” followed by free‐text description mirroring MedWatch expectations.
* Institutional pharmacovigilance committees can streamline this workflow by flagging all outpatient lab results exceeding threefold ULN for CK among patients currently prescribed GLP‑1 RAs—and automatically prompting a brief electronic questionnaire sent backto the ordering provider regarding symptom context.
  
Documenting detailed investigations—even negative antibody panels—is crucial because aggregation tools such as WHO’s **VigiBase** rely on comprehensive data fields when performing disproportionality analyses that surface unexpected drug–event pairings across borders.

---

## Turning sporadic anecdotes into regulatory action  

When isolated instances accumulate enough statistical weight—in other words when disproportional reporting ratios climb above predefined thresholds—regulators launch formal signal assessments that may culminate in updated prescribing information handbooks (“monitor serum CK periodically when initiating therapy”) or black box warnings targeting specific high‐risk populations (“avoid use in known autoimmune inflammatory myopathies”). Historical precedent exists: repeated reports linking high‐intensity statins with anti–HMGCR antibodies propelled routine pre‑treatment antibody screens into cardiovascular practice guidelines decades ago. Likewise robust capture of GLP‑1 RA–associated IMNM could implant similar safeguards tomorrow—maybe even prompting trials evaluating whether alternative weight loss strategies bypass muscular autoimmunity altogether.

---

### Key Take‑Home Messages  

- **Baseline screening:** Think about obtaining serum creatine kinase before prescribing semaglutide—or any GLP‑1 receptor agonist—to individuals who carry obesity plus NAFLD/NASH, have had unexplained prior CK elevations or are concurrently on known myotoxic agents such as statins.
- **Patient empowerment:** Explicitly educate patients on early signs of proximal weakness, dysphagia or dysphonia and urge them to seek prompt evaluation rather than attributing these symptoms solely to deconditioning or weight loss fatigue.
- **Diagnostic vigilance:** When new muscular symptoms arise after starting a GLP‑1 RA obtain repeat CK promptly; consider MRI ± muscle biopsy early because seronegative IMNM will demand immunosuppression whereas simple toxin‐induced rhabdomyolysis generally resolves with hydration alone.
- **Therapeutic response:** Immediate cessation of the suspected agent combined with a short course of high-dose steroids +/- IVIG can lead back-to-baseline function when an immune mechanism is identified—in contrast to prolonged immunosuppressive regimens typically required for idiopathic IMNM.
- **Report relentlessly:** Use FDA MedWatch (or EudraVigilance abroad) to submit detailed adverse event reports—including biopsies—and encourage your health system’s pharmacovigilance team to build automated alerts around abnormal post‑initiation CK values.
- **Signal maturation:** Aggregated reports transform single anecdotes into actionable safety signals that ultimately shape labeling updates and guide future prescribing standards—your single contribution matters enormously toward protecting millions who will receive semaglutide worldwide.
