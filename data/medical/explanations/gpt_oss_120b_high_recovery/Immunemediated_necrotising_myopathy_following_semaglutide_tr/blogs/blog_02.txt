Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Rhabdomyolysis or Autoimmune Necrotising Myopathy? A Practical Diagnostic Roadmap After GLP‑1 Therapy  

## Why This Case Grabs Your Attention  

GLP‑1 receptor agonists have exploded onto the obesity/diabetes scene—and most clinicians think of nausea, vomiting, and weight loss when something goes wrong. The first published case linking a GLP‑1 agonist (semaglutide) to **immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)** flips that script upside down. The distinction isn’t academic: toxic rhabdomyolysis dissolves with fluids, whereas IMNM requires high‑dose steroids ± IVIG and a prolonged immunosuppressive strategy. Missing the diagnosis can condemn a patient to months of debility or expose them unnecessarily to aggressive immunotherapy when simple hydration would have sufficed.  

The story also reminds us how easy it is to chalk a rising creatine kinase (CK) up to “drug‐induced” toxicity without looking deeper at pattern, exam findings, or pathology. Let’s walk through how you can dissect that knot when a patient presents after starting—or even taking a *single* dose of—a GLP‑1 agonist.

## The Index Patient in Brief  

* Female, mid‑60s with obesity, NAFLD, hypertension & hypothyroidism  
* Initiated **semaglutide 0·25 mg SC** for weight loss → hours later N/V → hospitalized 2 days later for metabolic acidosis, AKI & mild CK rise (**337 U/L**, ULN ≈ 190).  
* Discharged after five days once volume status corrected ­– GI symptoms resolved but she remained deconditioned.  
* Over **four months** she developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia and an alarming surge in CK (**peak ≈ 4 000 U/L**) coupled with marked elevations of AST/ALT that tracked her underlying steatosis rather than muscle breakdown alone.  
* Autoimmune serology was negative for HMGCR, SRP and all classic myositis antibodies; low‐titre SS‑B (+2·4) was an isolated finding.  
* MRI of thighs showed symmetric STIR hyperintensity in proximal quadriceps—a pattern more suggestive of inflammatory myopathy than pure toxin‐induced injury.  
* Quadriceps biopsy demonstrated necrotic/regenerating fibres with CD68⁺ macrophages, membrane attack complex deposition on sarcolemma and sparse CD3⁺ T cells — classic **IMNM** histology despite seronegativity.  
* Treated promptly with pulse methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone **(≈ 1 mg/kg)** plus one course of IVIG (**2 g/kg over four days**).  
* After ~4 weeks inpatient rehab she returned toward baseline strength; steroids were tapered over two months and she has remained off immunosuppression ≥ 8 months while avoiding all GLP‑1 agonists entirely.

## What We Know About GLP‑1 Agonists & Muscle Injury  

* **Toxic rhabdomyolysis** has been reported occasionally (e.g., tirzepatide–associated cases where CK rose into “the thousands” rapidly after continuous dosing). These patients typically improve within days once hydration restored volume status and the offending drug was stopped.  
* **Skeletal muscle mass loss** has been observed chronically on imaging studies among long‐term users; mechanisms may involve altered protein synthesis pathways rather than overt necrosis.  
* The current semaglutide‐IMNM report appears mechanistically distinct—it presents *delayed*, shows *progressive* weakness plus *bulbar involvement*, harbours *biopsy evidence of immune activation*, and improves only after targeted immunosuppression—not merely fluid resuscitation.

Understanding these divergent phenotypes guides our workup when faced with an elevated CK after GLP‑1 exposure.

## Toxic Rhabdomyolysis vs Immune‐Mediated Necrotising Myopathy  

| Feature | Toxic Rhabdomyolysis | Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy |
|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | Minutes–hours after exposure or intense exertion | Days–weeks after exposure; may be delayed even after a *single* dose |
| **CK trajectory** | Abrupt rise (>10× ULN) → peak within ≤48 h → rapid fall once the toxin removed + aggressive fluids | Steady upward trend over days–weeks (+12× ULN possible) despite drug cessation unless immunotherapy instituted |
| **Typical symptoms** | Muscle pain/tenderness, dark urine (“cola”), generalized fatigue | Proximal weakness predominates; dysphagia/dysphonia common when cricopharyngeal muscles involved |
| **Renal impact** | Acute tubular necrosis common secondary to myoglobinuria | May develop AKI secondary to volume depletion or comorbidities but not driven by massive myoglobin release |
| **Lab clues** | Elevated serum potassium/phosphate/additional metabolic derangements common | Mildly elevated ESR/CRP possible; transaminases may rise secondary to muscle breakdown—but patterns often mirror hepatic disease |
| **Biopsy** | Diffuse fibre necrosis without significant inflammatory infiltrate; minimal complement deposition | Necrotic/regenerating fibres + macrophage infiltration (CD68⁺), MAC positivity on sarcolemma; sparse T cells supportive |

These distinctions frame each decision node below.



## Step‑by‑Step Diagnostic Roadmap  

### Step 1 – Document Exposure & Initial Presentation  

Ask specifically about ***any*** GLP‑1 agonist taken within the past weeks—including “one‐off” trials under research protocols or insurance pre‑authorizations that result in asymptomatic dosing errors. Capture associated gastrointestinal upset or hypovolaemia events—as happened here—with nausea/vomiting leading directly to AKI.


### Step 2 – Chart Your Patient’s CK Kinetics  

Serial measurements are essential:

* **Acute spike >10× ULN within <48 h:** Lean toward classic toxic rhabdo → prioritize volume resuscitation.
* **Progressive rise over ≥72 h despite hydration:** Triggers suspicion for an immune process especially if accompanied by worsening strength.<br>
In our patient initial `337 U/L` rose stealthily over four months culminating near `4000 U/L`. That trend alone demanded further workup beyond fluids.



### Step 3 – Perform a Targeted Neuromuscular Exam  

Look beyond generalized aches:

* Rapidly evolving proximal weakness (`≥MRC grade ≤3` shoulders/hips) suggests inflammatory myopathy.
* Bulbar signs (`dysphagia`, `dysphonia`) are atypical for pure toxin injury yet hallmark features of many IMNMs—including HMGCR/SRP seropositive disease.
* Absence of overt tenderness may help differentiate—rhabdo often feels sore when pressed.



### Step 4 – Order Baseline Lab Adjuncts  

Even though seronegative IMNMs exist (~⅓), start broad screening:

* **Myositis panel:** Include HMGCR & SRP antibodies—it’s cheap enough now that ruling them out still leaves room for seronegative disease.
* **Inflammatory markers:** ESR/CRP modestly uptrending strengthen an immune hypothesis.
* **Thyroid profile:** Exclude hypothyroid myopathy—a low-hanging fruit.
* **Renal panel & electrolytes:** Needed regardless—for AKI monitoring.



### Step 5 – Image the Muscles Early  

A thigh MRI performed within days serves two purposes:

* STIR/T2 hyperintensity *symmetrically* involving proximal musculature aligns best with inflammatory pathology.
* Focal eccentric hyperintensity confined solely to one compartment would fit compartment syndrome/rhabdo sequelae better.<br>
Our patient’s MRI showed diffuse edema across vastus lateralis/quadriceps together with superficial subcutaneous changes—a pattern fitting necrotising inflammation.



### Step 6 – Decide Whether You Need an Immediate Muscle Biopsy  

Biopsy indications are clear when any *red flag* persists:

* Rising CK >6–8 weeks despite drug withdrawal & aggressive fluid resuscitation.
* Negative autoantibody panel combined with progressive functional decline.
* Concerning bulbar involvement suggesting systemic disease.<br>
A prompt open or needle biopsy will differentiate “pure” necrosis from immune cell infiltrates—the former steering away from prolonged steroids whilst confirming an autoimmune process when appropriate.



### Step 7 – Initiate Management Tailored to Etiology  

| Scenario | First Line |
|---|---|
| **Toxic Rhadomyolysis** | Aggressive isotonic fluid bolus ➜ goal urine output ≥200–300 mL/hr <br>Consider bicarbonate infusion if severe acidosis/high risk of calcium phosphate precipitation.<br>Discontinue offending drug permanently—or pause if benefits outweigh risks.* |
| **Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy** | Immediate cessation of GLP‑1 agent <br>High‐dose IV methylprednisolone (`1000 mg ×5 d`) followed by oral prednisone (~`1 mg/kg`) <br>IVIG (`2 g/kg` split over four days) especially when rapid functional recovery needed <br>If relapse/tapering challenges arise → add steroid-sparing agents (azathioprine/methotrexate/mycophenolate).<br>Supportive care includes physical therapy early since deconditioning worsens outcome.|

*(Rechallenge is unethical once an adverse immune reaction has occurred.)*

### Step 8 – Follow-Up & Taper Strategy  

Monitor serum CK *weekly* until normalization then space out testing every month during steroid tapering—a pragmatic cadence gleaned from our patient’s outpatient course where an early plateau allowed rapid prednisone withdrawal over two months without rebound elevation.


### Step 9 – Document Causality For Pharmacovigilance  

Applying WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre criteria can help institutions flag similar cases prospectively:

| Criterion | Our Case |
|---|---|
| Temporal relationship | Symptoms began within hours of first semaglutide dose + delayed progressive weakness thereafter |
| Alternative explanations excluded | No statins/resident supplements/relevant infections preceding onset |
| Dechallenge plausible response | Weakness improved markedly once drug stopped + immunotherapy instituted |
| Rechallenge avoided ethically appropriate |

Labeling this as “probable” raises awareness among prescribers who might otherwise dismiss future similar presentations as benign side effects.



## Key Take‑Home Messages  

- **Don’t stop at “drug‐induced.”** A rising CK over weeks plus proximal/bulbar weakness mandates assessment for *immune-mediated necrotising myopathy,* even when classic autoantibodies are negative.  
- **Serial CK trends beat single values.* A modest elevation soon after exposure may mask an evolving autoimmune process that peaks later.*  
- **MRI does more than rule out structural lesions.* Symmetric proximal STIR hyperintensity points toward inflammatory myopathy versus focal edema seen in trauma/rhabdo.*  
- **Muscle biopsy remains definitive.* When autoantibodies are absent and clinical suspicion persists, obtain tissue before committing patients to long‐term immunosuppression.*  
- **High-dose steroids ± IVIG can reverse disease quickly.* Our patient’s functional recovery occurred within weeks following pulse methylprednisolone/IVIG—a reminder that early immunotherapy changes outcomes.*  
- **Avoid re-exposure outright.* Once an adverse immune event has manifested you cannot ethically rechallenge—the risk outweighs any metabolic benefit.*  
- **Use causality frameworks.* Labeling reactions as “probable” helps collect safety data critical amid expanding GLP‑1 agonist use.*  
- **Plan follow-up rigorously.* Weekly CK checks during acute treatment followed by monthly surveillance during taper guard against relapse.*

---

When you see an elderly obese patient walk through your door clutching their throat while their labs scream “CK ∼4000,” remember that not every peak comes from dehydration alone—and occasionally a single injection enough triggers a full-blown autoimmune showdown backstage. Spotting it early leads straight into life-changing therapy rather than an endless loop of hydration cycles.\
