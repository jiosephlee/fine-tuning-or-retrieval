Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# GLP‑1 Receptor Agonists Unmasked: First Report of Semaglutide‑Associated Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy  

## Why This Case Turns Heads  
When you think “GLP‑1 receptor agonist,” nausea, vomiting and mild weight loss are the first red flags that spring to mind—not a severe autoimmune myopathy that lands a patient in the ICU and forces you into high‑dose steroids plus IVIG. This report describes exactly that scenario—a single subcutaneous dose of semaglutide was followed weeks later by progressive dysphagia, dysphonia and crippling proximal weakness culminating in a creatine kinase (CK) surge close to 4 000 U/L. Muscle biopsy revealed classic necrotising changes with macrophage infiltrates—immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). The patient recovered after a brief course of immunosuppression and strict avoidance of all GLP‑1 agonists. It is the first documented link between semaglutide and IMNM and forces us to broaden our safety net when prescribing these agents.

## Semaglutide & Its Usual Side‑Effect Profile  
Semaglutide (commercially Wegovy®/Ozempic®) has become one of the most prescribed glucagon‑like peptide‐1 receptor agonists worldwide—for type 2 diabetes and obesity alike. Its efficacy is undeniable; real‑world data show prescriptions now numbering in the millions globally each year. The safety profile that most clinicians wrestle with includes:

* **Gastrointestinal upset** – nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea (often dose‐related).  
* **Pancreatic concerns** – rare reports of pancreatitis or elevated amylase/lipase.  
* **Gallbladder disease** – increased cholelithiasis risk especially during rapid weight loss.  
* **Thyroid tumours** – rodent‐specific medullary thyroid carcinoma signal leads to black‐box warnings in humans.  
* **Lean mass reduction** – modest loss of skeletal muscle has been observed secondary to calorie restriction, not a primary toxic effect.  

These events are well catalogued in product monographs and pharmacovigilance databases. What’s missing is any notion that these drugs could spark an autoimmune attack on muscle fibers—until now.

### Muscle Toxicity Goes Beyond Lean Mass Loss  
Two distinct entities have been reported in association with GLP‑1–based therapies:

| Entity | Typical Presentation | Temporal Pattern | Key Laboratory Feature |
|--------|----------------------|------------------|------------------------|
| **Drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis** | Acute muscle pain + dark urine | Hours–days after exposure | Explosive CK rise (often >10 000 U/L) that falls rapidly once the offending agent is stopped |
| **Immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy** | Progressive proximal weakness ± dysphagia/dysphonia | Days–months after exposure | Sustained CK elevation (often >1000 U/L) despite drug withdrawal; requires steroid/IVIG therapy |

A recent tirzepatide case highlighted classic rhabdomyolysis—CK spiked past 57 000 U/L and normalized within days after hydration and drug cessation. By contrast, our semaglutide patient displayed an insidious rise from a modest 337 U/L on admission up to nearly 4000 U/L over four months—a pattern more compatible with an immune process rather than simple toxicity.

## The Case In Detail  

A woman in her early sixties with obesity, non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), hypertension and hypothyroidism received her inaugural subcutaneous semaglutide injection (0.25 mg). Within hours she vomited repeatedly, developed metabolic acidosis and acute kidney injury requiring brief ICU stay for non‑invasive ventilation. Initial labs showed a modest CK elevation (337 U/L) without myoglobinuria or rash; troponin was normal despite an episode of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

After discharge she appeared deconditioned but stable—until four months later when she could no longer sit upright without assistance and complained of trouble swallowing liquids and solids. Admission labs now revealed a striking CK elevation (~3992 U/L). Neurology initiated pulse methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily ×5 days) followed by oral prednisone (~1 mg/kg/day). Rheumatology added intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at a total dose of 2 g/kg split over four days because her proximal weakness remained severe despite steroids alone.

A comprehensive workup showed:

* **Serology:** Negative ANA, anti‑SSA/A, anti‐DNA, ANCA, anti‐mitochondrial antibodies; full myositis panel negative for SRP, HMGCR and all other canonical autoantibodies; low‐titer isolated anti‐SSB positivity (<3× upper limit).
* **Inflammatory markers:** Mildly elevated ESR (22 mm/h) and CRP (22.8 mg/L).
* **Cardiac workup:** Slight troponin rise (62 pg/mL) and BNP elevation (~4350 pg/mL); cardiac MRI showed fleeting myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement—compatible with mild myocarditis versus stress cardiomyopathy.
* **Imaging:** Bilateral thigh MRI demonstrated symmetric edema involving proximal vastus lateralis/quadriceps muscles plus subcutaneous edema.
* **Biopsy:** Vastus lateralis specimen exhibited scattered necrotic/regenerative fibers punctuated by CD68⁺ macrophages with complement membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotic and intact fibers—a hallmark pattern consistent with IMNM rather than pure toxic myopathy.
* **Liver:** Repeat biopsy confirmed progression of steatosis but no new inflammatory component—suggesting persistently hepatic transaminase elevations were unrelated to muscle injury.

The patient’s CK fell dramatically over two weeks after steroids+IVIG initiation. She was discharged after almost four weeks inpatient care, completed a brief taper of prednisone over two months, avoided further IVIG due to cost considerations—and remained off all glucagon‑like peptide‐1 agents throughout recovery. Ten months later she returned to her pre‑illness functional baseline without any immunosuppressive therapy or CK rebound.

## Applying WHO–Uppsala Causality Criteria  

The World Health Organization–Uppsala Monitoring Centre framework classifies adverse drug reactions into six categories ranging from certain → unlikely → unassessable.^[WHO–UMC] For this patient:

| Criterion | Assessment |
|-----------|------------|
| **Temporal relationship** | Symptoms began hours after drug exposure; progressive myopathy manifested over subsequent months—a plausible latency for immune activation |
| **Dechallenge** | Discontinuation of semaglutide coincided with clinical improvement once immunosuppression was instituted |
| **Alternative explanations** | No statin exposure, no relevant supplements; exhaustive autoantibody panel negative; imaging ruled out structural causes |
| **Rechallenge** | Ethically prohibitive given severity |
| **Dose–response relationship** | Not applicable—the reaction occurred after a single low dose |
| **Previous reports** | Prior tirzepatide rhabdomyolysis exists; this is the inaugural semaglutide–IMNM description |

Collectively these elements satisfy WHO–UMC’s “probable” causality category—strong enough to alert prescribers while acknowledging absolute certainty remains elusive without rechallenge data.

## Management Strategy That Worked  

The therapeutic approach hinged on three pillars:

1️⃣ **Immediate cessation** of any GLP‑1 agonist—eliminates continued antigenic stimulation if one exists.  
2️⃣ **High‑dose glucocorticoids** – pulse methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone slowed necrosis while allowing regeneration on histologic level.^[EM]  
3️⃣ **Adjunctive IVIG** – administered early because sarcolemmal complement deposition suggested antibody‐mediated complement activation.^[JDM]  

Remarkably, once steroids were tapered off within two months—and IVIG limited to one course—the patient never required long‐term immunomodulators such as azathioprine or rituximab—a pattern more reminiscent of drug‐triggered forms of IMNM rather than idiopathic seronegative disease which often mandates sustained multidrug regimens.^[NEJM]

The brief septic episode secondary to pseudomonal UTI post-discharge did NOT provoke a CK flare—supporting remission rather than ongoing active disease at that point.

## Clinical Pearls & Bottom Line  

Below are actionable takeaways you can slot into your daily practice when prescribing GLP‑1 receptor agonists:

### Key Take‑Home Points
- **Think beyond GI upset** – New-onset proximal weakness or dysphagia weeks–months after starting any GLP‑1 agonist warrants prompt evaluation.
- **Check a baseline CK** before initiating therapy in patients who already have risk factors for myopathy (e.g., prior statin use, autoimmune predisposition).
- **Recheck CK** if unexplained muscle symptoms develop; persistent elevation (>1000 U/L) should trigger neurology/rheumatology referral.
- **Distinguish rhabdomyolysis vs IMNM** – Rapidly rising CK (>10 000) with pain/urine discoloration leans toward toxic rhabdo; gradual rise plus marked weakness suggests an immune process.
- **Muscle biopsy remains gold standard** when IMNM is suspected—look for necrosis plus macrophage infiltration with complement membrane attack complex deposition.
- **Stopping the offending agent can be curative**, often combined with a short steroid taper ± one course of IVIG.
- **Document & report** any suspected drug‐induced myopathy through FDA MedWatch or your national pharmacovigilance system—early signals guide future labeling updates.
- **Stay vigilant as usage explodes** – With millions receiving semaglutide annually worldwide, rare adverse events become clinically relevant sooner rather than later.

In short, while semaglutide continues reshaping diabetes care and weight management paradigms, it may also have a hidden capacity to incite autoimmune muscle injury in susceptible individuals. Early recognition saves patients from prolonged disability—and reminds us that even celebrated drugs can harbor surprising dark sides when viewed through an immunologic lens. Keep your differential broad, your labs handy, and your pharmacovigilance reflex sharp!
