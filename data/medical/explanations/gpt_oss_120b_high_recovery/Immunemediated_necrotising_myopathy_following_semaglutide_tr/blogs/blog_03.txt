Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Seronegative Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy: When Muscle Biopsy Takes Center Stage  

---

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

We all know GLP‑1 receptor agonists like semaglutide as the “new darling” of obesity and type‑2 diabetes therapy. Nausea, vomiting and occasional gallstone formation are the textbook side‑effects you warn patients about—​muscle disease isn’t on most checklists. Yet here we have a 60‑year‑old woman who received **one single dose** of semaglutide and subsequently spiralled into a classic picture of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). Her autoantibody work‐up was completely negative—no HMGCR, no SRP, nothing—and yet her muscle **biopsy** lit up with the hallmarks of an autoimmune attack.  

The bottom line? When you’re faced with a seronegative myopathy that refuses to resolve on hydration alone, **muscle biopsy** isn’t just an option—it’s the decisive test that prevents misdiagnosis, mistreatment, and potentially permanent disability.  

---

## A quick refresher on IMNM  

Immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy sits at the intersection of classic inflammatory myopathies and pure toxic rhabdomyolysis:

* **Clinical phenotype** – Subacute–acute proximal weakness (often severe), dysphagia/dysphonia, CK often > 5 × ULN – occasionally reaching > 10 000 U/L.
* **Serology** – About two thirds of patients harbour either anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP antibodies. The remaining ~30 % are **seronegative**, making diagnosis tougher.
* **Pathology** – Patchy necrosis with regenerating fibres, brisk macrophage infiltrate (CD68⁺), complement membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition on intact and necrotic fibres, and only **sparse CD3⁺ T cells**.
* **Triggers** – Statins are the classic culprit in anti‑HMGCR disease; other drugs—including now GLP‑1R agonists—are emerging suspects.
* **Outcome** – Those who receive early immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids ± IVIG) generally recover better than those treated as “pure toxic” rhabdomyolysis where steroids are rarely used.

## The patient’s story in chronological snapshots  

| Timeline | Key events |
|----------|------------|
| **3 years pre‑presentation** | NAFLD diagnosed on liver biopsy → lifestyle counseling only |
| **2½ years later** | Initiated semaglutide 0.25 mg SC |
| **Hours after first dose** | Nausea/vomiting → dehydration → AKI & metabolic acidosis → ICU admission briefly |
| **Admission labs** | CK 337 U/L (mildly elevated), AST/ALT ~2×ULN |
| **Discharge** | GI symptoms resolved, deconditioned baseline |
| **Months 0–4 post‑dose** | Progressive proximal weakness → dysphagia/dysphonia → CK surges to 3 992 U/L |
| **Hospitalization 1** | Pulse methylprednisolone → oral prednisone 1 mg/kg → IVIG 2 g/kg over 4 days → MRI‐guided vastus lateralis biopsy confirms IMNM |
| **Post‐discharge course** | Rapid CK decline, functional recovery despite brief steroid taper; no further IVIG needed |
| **10 months later** | Baseline functional status restored; off immunosuppression for > 7 months |

No prior statin use or exposure to other known “myotoxic” supplements was identified—the temporal relationship with semaglutide remains strikingly tight.

---

## Lab workup & imaging: Helpful but not decisive  

* **CK trajectory:** modest rise during the initial AKI episode (337 U/L) followed by a dramatic increase months later (≈ 4000 U/L). This biphasic pattern argues against simple drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis where CK spikes acutely then falls rapidly after hydration.
* **Autoantibodies:** Negative comprehensive myositis panel—including HMGCR, SRP, Jo‐1 etc.—leaving us in the seronegative zone.
* **MRI thigh:** Symmetric edema in proximal vastus lateralis/quadriceps guided us toward a well‐vascularized biopsy site—critical because patchy involvement can lead to false‐negative pathology if sampling error occurs.
* **Cardiac workup:** Mild myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement suggested transient stress cardiomyopathy rather than overt myocarditis—a useful reminder that systemic inflammation can involve more than skeletal muscle.
* **Liver enzymes:** Persistently modest AST/ALT elevations were attributed to underlying steatohepatitis rather than ongoing muscle breakdown.

Overall these studies painted an incomplete picture until we turned to histology.

---

## Muscle biopsy – where the diagnosis materialised  

The right vastus lateralis sample showed several microscopic clues that together form the *textbook* pattern of IMNM:

1. **Necrotic & regenerating fibers** scattered throughout—typical of an active destructive process rather than chronic atrophy.
2. **CD68⁺ macrophages** coating necrotic fibers (“myophagocytosis”). This macrophage sea is one of the most reliable pillars distinguishing IMNM from polymyositis.
3. **Membrane attack complex (C5b–9) deposition** along sarcolemmal membranes both on necrotic and apparently healthy fibers—evidence of complement‐mediated injury which is absent in pure toxic rhabdomyolysis.
4. Only *rare CD3⁺ T cells* perivascularly—contrasting sharply with polymyositis where dense endomysial CD8⁺ infiltrates invade non‑necrotic fibers.
5. *Patchy inflammatory architecture*: focal perivascular cuffs but no endomysial lymphocytic “conspiracy”.

These findings clinch an autoimmune process despite negative serology—a perfect illustration that pathology can be your decisive ally when blood tests are silent.

---

## How does seronegative IMNM differ from its mimics?  

| Feature | Seronegative IMNM | Polymyositis | Drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis / Toxic myopathy |
|--------|-------------------|--------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| Onset | Subacute–acute over weeks–months | Insidious over months | Typically within days of exposure |
| CK peak | Often >5× ULN but may be moderate | Usually <10× ULN | Can exceed 50× ULN (>40 000 U/L) |
| Histology | Necrosis + macrophages + MAC • Sparse T cells | Endomysial CD8⁺ T cells invading non‑necrotic fibers • No MAC | Massive necrosis ± minimal inflammation • No MAC deposits |
| Response to hydration alone | Poor | Irrelevant (immune) | Excellent—CK normalises within days |
| Immunosuppression needed? | Yes – usually high‑dose steroids ± IVIG/other agents | Yes – steroids +/- steroid-sparing agents | Generally not indicated |

In other words:  
*If you see MAC on sarcolemma plus a sea of CD68⁺ macrophages while your T cells are huddled in corners—that’s IMNM waving its flag.*  

Conversely, a “pure toxic” picture will lack complement deposition and will resolve quickly once the offending drug is stopped—and muscle weakness usually recovers without steroids.  

---

## Treatment pearls from this case  

* **Induction:** Pulse methylprednisolone 1 g/day × 3–5 days followed by oral prednisone ~1 mg/kg/day is standard first line even when antibodies are absent.
* **IVIG:** Adding 2 g/kg split over 4–5 days can accelerate functional recovery especially when dysphagia or respiratory involvement is present—or when rapid steroid taper is desired due to infection risk.
* **Monitoring:** Serial CK every 1–2 weeks during induction helps gauge biochemical response; MRI can be repeated if clinical suspicion persists despite normalized CK.
* **Taper strategy:** Because this was likely a drug‐triggered phenomenon rather than idiopathic chronic IMNM, an aggressive taper (over 6–8 weeks) was feasible once CK normalized and strength returned.
* **Avoid rechallenge:** Ethical considerations preclude reexposing patients to presumptive culprits—in this scenario that meant permanently discontinuing all GLP‑1R agonists.
* **Adjunctive agents:** In seropositive cases refractory to steroids/IVIG one might bring in methotrexate or rituximab; however many seronegatives resolve with steroids/IVIG alone once the trigger is withdrawn.

---

## Practical takeaways for clinicians  

1️⃣ When a patient develops subacute proximal weakness *after initiating any new medication*, think beyond direct toxicity—especially if CK trends don’t follow the classic rapid rise/fall curve seen with rhabdomyolysis.  

2️⃣ Order an MRI early if you suspect an inflammatory myopathy—it will locate active edema and guide you toward an optimal biopsy site while sparing you from blind sampling errors.  

3️⃣ If autoantibody panels come back negative yet clinical suspicion remains high, proceed directly to muscle biopsy—don’t let a “seronegative” label lull you into diagnostic inertia.  

4️⃣ Remember that GLP‑1R agonists are now widely prescribed for obesity weight loss—not just diabetes—and emerging post‑marketing surveillance suggests they could act as novel triggers for immune-mediated muscle injury. Reporting such events through MedWatch or your national pharmacovigilance system helps build an evidence base faster than waiting for another case report three years down the line.  

5️⃣ Early escalation to steroids ± IVIG—even before definitive pathology returns—can prevent irreversible loss of swallowing function or respiratory compromise while you await tissue confirmation.  

6️⃣ Finally, if your patient responds dramatically after a short course of immunosuppression *and* withdrawal of the suspect drug, you’re likely dealing with drug-associated IMNM rather than classic idiopathic disease—which often means a shorter overall treatment duration and better prognosis.

---

## Key Take‑Home Points  

- Seronegative immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy can present after just one dose of a GLP‑1 receptor agonist such as semaglutide—high vigilance is required now that these drugs are ubiquitous.  
- A normal autoantibody panel does *not* rule out IMNM; muscle biopsy remains the gold standard when clinical suspicion persists.  
- Histopathologic hallmarks = necrotic/regenerating fibers + abundant CD68⁺ macrophages + membrane attack complex deposition + only sparse CD3⁺ T cells—a pattern distinct from polymyositis and pure toxic rhabdomyolysis.  
- MRI should be used early to identify edematous muscle for targeted biopsy and to assess disease extent (including cardiac involvement).  
- Prompt immunosuppression (high‐dose steroids ± IVIG) coupled with removal of the offending agent yields rapid biochemical and functional recovery in drug‐associated cases—and often allows a much shorter taper than idiopathic seropositive IMNM requires.  
- Always report suspected drug‐induced autoimmune myopathies → pharmacovigilance data will shape future prescribing safety nets for increasingly popular agents like semaglutide.  

Stay curious, keep your biopsies ready, and remember: when the labs stay quiet but the muscles scream louder than ever, it’s time to let pathology do the talking!
