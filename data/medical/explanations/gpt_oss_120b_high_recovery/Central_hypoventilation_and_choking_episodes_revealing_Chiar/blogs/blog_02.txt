Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Choking Without Obstruction – The Neurogenic Dysphagia Alarm in Adults  

---

## Why this case matters  

When you hear “patient chokes on everything” you instinctively think “food bolus”, “laryngopharyngeal reflux”, or “airway lesion”. You order laryngoscopy, you send an upper endoscope – you expect to find something hanging off the epiglottis or a strictured esophagus. What if every study comes back normal and the patient still wakes up gasping in the middle of the night?  

Our 40‑year‑old patient taught us that neurogenic dysphagia can masquerade as an ENT problem for years. She experienced solid‑and‑liquid choking, nocturnal desaturations into the 70 % range **without feeling breathless**, and chronic occipital headaches that were mis‑labelled as nummular headache. Only after re‑reviewing an old head CT did we spot a crowded foramen magnum, and an MRI confirmed an 11 mm tonsillar descent — classic Chiari I malformation (CMI).  

The lesson is simple yet profound: **When choking occurs across all consistencies and exhaustive otolaryngology/gastroenterology workups are unrevealing, think brainstem**. In CMI this manifests as a blend of bulbar dysfunction (central dysphagia) and impaired automatic respiratory drive (“Ondine’s curse”). Early recognition can spare patients years of anxiety, invasive testing, and missed opportunities for definitive therapy (posterior fossa decompression).

---

## The case in a nutshell  

* **Demographics** – Female in early 40 s, no significant past medical history beyond a minor motor vehicle accident 14 years ago.
* **Presenting problem** – Recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids & liquids for 7 years; nocturnal choking 3–4 times/week; occasional dizziness/nausea at airport with SpO₂ 78 % that resolved on supplemental O₂.
* **Associated features** – Chronic occipital/head vertex headache with allodynia (improved by cutting hair short); intermittent asymptomatic desaturations to 70–80 % documented on home pulse oximetry during the COVID‑19 pandemic.
* **Initial workup** – Three flexible laryngoscopies + two upper GI endoscopies → normal. Pulmonary function tests & chest imaging → normal.
* **Key imaging breakthrough** – Old non‑contrast head CT re‑read → crowded foramen magnum. Current MRI → Chiari I malformation with 11 mm cerebellar tonsil descent.
* **Sleep evaluation** – Type III home sleep study → AHI 6.0 (mild) with ≥72 % central events despite profound desaturations.
* **Neurologic exam** – Unremarkable aside from subtle findings consistent with bulbar involvement (not detailed in report).  
* **Management decision** – Patient elected conservative monitoring while contemplating posterior fossa decompression; structured follow‑up includes serial oximetry and annual MRI.

## Neurogenic dysphagia: What does it look like?  

Chiari I malformation produces a spectrum from asymptomatic tonsillar ectopia to classic Chiari syndrome (headache + cerebellar signs). When the herniated tonsils compress the medulla oblongata and lower cranial nerve nuclei, several bulbar functions go awry:

| Function | Pathophysiology | Typical symptom |
|----------|----------------|----------------|
| Swallowing | Disruption of nucleus ambiguus & nucleus tractus solitarius → impaired pharyngeal peristalsis & airway protection | Choking/coughing on **both solids *and* liquids**, inability to clear secretions |
| Respiratory drive | Compression of ventral respiratory column & pre‑Bötzinger complex → loss of automatic breathing during sleep or distraction | Nocturnal hypoventilation or sudden desaturation without dyspnea (“Ondine’s curse”) |
| Upper airway tone | Involvement of glossopharyngeal & vagal fibers → reduced glottic closure reflex | Voice hoarseness sometimes reported |

The hallmark clinical clue is *equal impairment across consistencies*. Mechanical obstructions tend to discriminate: solids more than liquids (e.g., strictures) or liquids more than solids (e.g., motility disorders). Central lesions hit the swallow initiation circuitry first—so *any bolus*, regardless of texture, provokes coughing or an abrupt gasp.

In our patient the choking was completely non‑selective and persisted during wakefulness and sleep—a red flag for neurogenic origin.

## Differential Diagnosis – Keeping it straight  

When faced with neurogenic‐type choking you must think broadly but systematically:

1. **Myasthenia gravis**  
   *Key discriminators*: Fluctuating weakness worse at day’s end; ocular signs (ptosis/diplopia); positive acetylcholine receptor antibodies; decremental response on repetitive nerve stimulation.  
   *Why not it?* Antibodies negative, no fatigable weakness reported, symptoms present throughout day and night rather than worsening toward evening.
   
2. **Motor neuron disease / ALS**  
   *Key discriminators*: Progressive limb weakness/spasticity; tongue atrophy/fasciculations; upper motor neuron signs on exam; EMG evidence of denervation.  
   *Why not it?* No limb involvement or EMG abnormalities noted; cranial nerve exam mostly normal aside from subtle bulbar findings.
   
3. **Esophageal motility disorders (achalasia/EGJ outflow obstruction)**  
   *Key discriminators*: Dysphagia primarily to solids early then liquids later; LES failure on manometry; often regurgitation/weight loss.  
   *Why not it?* Patient’s difficulty was equal across consistencies from day one; barium swallow showed no classic achalasia pressurization.
   
4. **Gastro‑oesophageal reflux / Laryngopharyngeal reflux**  
   *Key discriminators*: Chronic cough after meals/lying flat; pH probe positivity; symptom relief with PPIs/ prokinetics.  
   *Why not it?* Acid suppression trials failed; pH studies normal when performed.
   
5. **Obstructive sleep apnea**  
   *Key discriminators*: Snoring, witnessed apneas, excessive daytime sleepiness; obstructive events dominate polysomnography (*i.e.*, flow limitation despite effort).  
   *Why not it?* Polysomnography showed *central* events predominating (>70%) despite low overall AHI—reflecting loss of automatic drive rather than airway collapse.
   
6. **Structural brainstem lesions other than Chiari** (tumors, infarcts)  
   *Key discriminators*: Acute focal neurologic signs on exam/imaging; history suggestive of vascular risk factors or cancer.  
   *Why not it?* Imaging localized compression solely due to tonsillar descent without mass lesions.

## When ENT/GI studies are negative—what’s next?

> **Rule-of-thumb:** If you’ve chased every peripheral cause—including three laryngoscopies and two endoscopies—and still have “blind” choking episodes impacting quality of life, jump upstream toward the brainstem sooner rather than later.

### Stepwise approach (in prose)

1️⃣ **History & bedside screening** – Ask specifically whether both solids ***and*** liquids provoke coughing/painful swallowing simultaneously – a crucial clue toward a central lesion. Perform a simple water‐swallow test (<30 ml) observing latency before cough or voice change.

2️⃣ **Neurologic assessment** – Look beyond obvious cranial nerve palsies: subtle gaze palsy, facial numbness, hyperreflexia/hypertonia can hint at brainstem involvement even when overt exam is normal.

3️⃣ **Videofluoroscopic swallowing study (VFSS)** – In neurogenic dysphagia you'll see:
   - Delayed initiation of pharyngeal swallow regardless of bolus consistency
   - Poor epiglottic inversion
   - Penetration/aspiration that occurs early rather than late
   - Lack of compensatory “dry swallows”
   
   Contrast this with mechanical obstruction where bolus size/viscosity determines timing or where esophageal clearance delays predominate after pharyngeal phase completion.
   
4️⃣ **Neuroimaging focused on craniocervical junction** – Order a high‐resolution sagittal T1/T2 MRI covering brainstem down through C2:
   - Tonsillar descent ≥5 mm qualifies as CMI
   - Look for crowded foramen magnum + loss of CSF space
   - Evaluate syrinx formation within cervical cord (can exacerbate bulbar symptoms)
   
   If MRI unavailable immediately, retrieve any prior head CTs—sometimes crowding is visible even without contrast (“the missed clue” in our patient).

5️⃣ **Polysomnography with capnography** – Full Type I study including EEG provides accurate AHI counting against true sleep time and captures hypercapnia during central apneas—critical when patients report profound desaturations yet deny dyspnea.
   
6️⃣ **Specialist referral** – Early involvement of neurosurgery/neuro‐otolaryngology accelerates decision making regarding posterior fossa decompression versus continued observation.

---

## Surgical reality check  

Posterior fossa decompression aims to restore CSF flow around the foramen magnum and relieve medullary compression:

- **Evidence base:** In series >90 adult CMI patients up to 50 % had sleep‐related breathing disorder improvement after decompression [Reference]. Central apnea indices fall dramatically when brainstem distortion resolves.
- **Bulbar benefit:** Several reports noted resolution or marked reduction in dysphagia after surgery—particularly when preoperative VFSS showed predominantly neurogenic patterns.
- **Risk profile:** As with any craniocervical operation there is risk of CSF leak, wound infection, cerebellar injury—but most centers report <5 % major complication rates when performed by experienced surgeons.
- **Timing:** The longer symptoms persist untreated,the higher chance secondary syrinx formation develops and functional recovery may plateau [Reference].

Our patient chose initially conservative management while she weighed potential benefits against surgical risk—a reasonable stance given her modest AHI yet severe desaturation events warrant close surveillance.

## Real‑world lessons from our patient  

1️⃣ **Don’t let old imaging sit idle** – A simple revisit of her trauma CT revealed a “crowded” foramen magnum that had been dismissed previously because radiologists focus primarily on acute bleed/fracture after trauma.
   
2️⃣ **Home pulse oximetry isn’t just reassuring—it can be lifesaving** – Episodes falling into the low‐70s were documented months before she presented to an airport medical team who ultimately identified her oxygen crisis.
   
3️⃣ **Choking without pain does NOT guarantee airway patency** – She never had full obstruction yet suffered dangerous hypoxemia because her automatic respiratory drive was off line—a classic hallmark inaugurated by distraction (“forgetting to breathe”).
   
4️⃣ **Cultural nuances matter** – Her scalp allodynia improved dramatically after cutting hair short—a coping strategy she adopted despite social pressure—a reminder clinicians must respect patients’ self‑management choices while seeking underlying etiology.
   
5️⃣ **Multidisciplinary persistence pays off** – It took family medicine input plus neurology review plus radiology interrogation before arriving at CMI—a testament that collaborative thinking shortcuts diagnostic odysseys.


---

## Key Clinical Pearls  

- Persistent choking affecting both solids & liquids equally should trigger an early neurologic workup when ENT/GI investigations are unrevealing.  
- Central dysphagia often coexists with abnormal respiratory control—look for nocturnal desaturations without dyspnea (“Ondine’s curse”).  
- A bedside water‑swallow test combined with VFSS patterns showing delayed pharyngeal initiation across all textures strongly suggests brainstem involvement.  
- Review any prior head CT/MRI—even old trauma scans—for subtle craniocervical crowding before dismissing them as “normal”.  
- In confirmed Chiari I malformation presenting with bulbar symptoms ± central hypoventilation, posterior fossa decompression can reverse dysfunction in many patients—but decision making must balance symptom severity against surgical risk.



--- 

### Take‑home Messages  

- **If choke happens on everything**, start looking upward—not just at food tubes.
- Normal laryngoscopy/endoscopy = red flag for neurogenic etiology → obtain VFSS + craniocervical MRI promptly.
- Central hypoventilation may manifest as dangerous silent desaturation—home oximetry can unmask it before catastrophic failure.
- Early identification of Chiari I malformation enables timely decompression which often improves both swallowing and respiratory control.
- Re‑review old imaging studies—they may hold the clue you need today.
