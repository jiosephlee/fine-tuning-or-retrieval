Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Neck Swelling Without Infection: Recognizing Pseudo‑Angioedema as a Manifestation of Dermatomyositis  

## Why this case caught our eye  
A healthy man in his 40s walked into the emergency department with a three‑week history of progressive neck swelling that looked exactly like Ludwig’s angina on CT. The ENT team cleared him of deep‑neck infection and the immunology service was consulted for “idiopathic angioedema.” What made the story twist was the discovery of a CK level >16 000 U/L and subtle nailfold changes—clues that pointed straight toward inflammatory myopathy rather than histamine or bradykinin‐mediated angioedema. In short, dermatomyositis was masquerading as an ENT emergency.  

## The vignette in brief  
The patient presented with diffuse non‑tender neck edema extending into the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space on contrast‐enhanced CT. He was afebrile, normoxic and lacked airway compromise, yet his CRP was modestly raised (≈56 mg/L). Physical exam revealed peri‑ungual microhaemorrhages on capillaroscopy, a faint erythematous rash over the right lateral thigh (“holster sign”), facial erythema over the nasolabial folds and mild hip flexor weakness (4/5). Laboratory workup showed CK = 16 184 U/L, elevated AST/ALT/LDH, ANA 1:1280 speckled pattern and a strongly positive anti‑SAE1 antibody (>170 IU). Complement levels and C1 inhibitor function were normal—making hereditary or acquired angioedema unlikely. MRI of the thighs demonstrated active myositis in multiple hip adductors and obturator muscles, while FDG‑PET highlighted diffuse hypermetabolism throughout head/neck musculature consistent with inflammatory edema rather than infection. Muscle biopsy confirmed perifascicular necrosis with C5b‑9 deposition—a hallmark of dermatomyositis.  

He received three days of intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g/day followed by an oral prednisone taper, IVIG (2 g/kg split over five days then monthly), mycophenolate mofetil 1 g BID and two doses of rituximab 1000 mg spaced two weeks apart. Dysphagia required nasogastric feeding for two months but resolved by three months post‑discharge after his cervical swelling fully subsided and CK normalized (<40 U/L).  

## Neck swelling: common versus uncommon culprits  
When faced with a “tight” neck we instinctively think infection (Ludwig’s angina), allergic angioedema or vascular obstruction (SVC syndrome). Less often considered are autoimmune processes that generate non‑histaminergic edema—dermatomyositis being a prime example despite its rarity (<10/million/year). In large series only ~6% of dermatomyositis patients develop overt facial or cervical swelling (“pseudo‑angioedema”), making it an easy pitfall if you’re only looking for pus or airway obstruction.  

## Red flags that tilt you away from infection or classic angioedema  

* **Insidious evolution** – swelling progressed over weeks rather than hours/days typical of infectious cellulitis or anaphylaxis.  
* **Absence of systemic inflammatory signs** – afebrile, normal white count despite CRP elevation modestly above baseline suggests sterile inflammation.  
* **Lack of airway compromise** – no stridor or hypoxia despite large soft tissue mass argues against life‑threatening infection needing emergent drainage.  
* **Concurrent muscle symptoms** – subtle proximal weakness (hip flexors) and dysphagia were present early on but might have been missed without targeted exam.  
* **Cutaneous clues** – peri‑ungual telangiectasias/microhaemorrhages on nailfold capillaroscopy and faint “holster” rash point toward an underlying connective tissue disease even when classic Gottron papules are absent.  

These cues should prompt you to look beyond ENT pathology before committing a patient to surgery or empiric antibiotics.  

## A focused bedside work‑up that can change the game  

The moment you suspect an inflammatory myopathy masquerading as angioedema, draw a basic metabolic panel plus CK—and if it’s markedly raised (as it was >16 000 U/L), you’ve essentially solved the mystery at once. While you’re waiting for serologies:

1️⃣ Perform a thorough skin exam—look for heliotrope discoloration, V‑neck/ shawl signs, holster rash or subtle erythema on trunk/extremities.^  
2️⃣ Inspect fingernail beds under magnification—peri‐ungual erythema or capillary loops with microhaemorrhages are highly suggestive of dermatomyositis.^  
3️⃣ Nailfold capillaroscopy (if available) quickly visualizes those microscopic changes without delay.^  
4️⃣ Order a limited myositis antibody panel—anti‑SAE1 positivity is strongly linked to cutaneous edema and dysphagia.^  
5️⃣ Consider MRI of involved muscle groups if clinical weakness is focal — it differentiates true inflammation from simple soft tissue congestion.^  

All these steps can be completed within hours in most tertiary EDs, sparing patients unnecessary invasive ENT procedures and allowing immunosuppression to start early.  

## How dermatomyositis produces “pseudo‑angioedema”  

Unlike histamine/bradykinin‐mediated angioedema that results from increased vascular permeability secondary to mast cell degranulation or C1 esterase inhibition failure, pseudo‐angioedema in DM stems from immune‐mediated inflammation within skeletal muscle fibers themselves:

* **Perifascicular capillary injury** – complement C5b‑9 deposits damage endothelium → leakage into interstitial space around neck muscles → soft tissue swelling visible on CT/MRI.^  
* **Cytokine storm** – infiltrating CD4⁺ T cells and macrophages release IL‐6, TNFα, interferon γ which promote local edematous response without cellulitic signs.^  
* **Sumoylation dysregulation** – anti‑SAE1 antibodies target the SUMO‐activating enzyme involved in post‐translational modification; experimental data suggest this pathway modulates inflammatory gene expression within muscle cells.^  

Thus what looks like “dark rind” neck edema on imaging is actually inflamed muscle compartments expanding into subcutaneous tissue—a crucial distinction because antibiotics won’t help while high‐dose steroids will rapidly reverse it.  

## When you suspect dermatomyositis—hit the right serologies  

A standard myositis panel includes Mi‑2α/β, Jo‑1 (anti‐histidyl tRNA synthetase), PL‐7/12 (anti–threonyl/tyrosyl tRNA synthetase), OJ (anti–isoleucyl tRNA synthetase), TIF1γ, MDA5 and SAE1 among others. In our patient:

* Anti‑SAE1 IgG index 173 (>35 cutoff) → strong association with cutaneous edema & dysphagia.^  
* ANA speckled pattern at high titer supports autoimmune background but isn’t specific.^  
* Absence of anti–MDA5 or anti–TIF1γ reduces—but does not eliminate—the risk profile for rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease or malignancy respectively.^  

Including SAE1 in your ordered panel is critical when facial/neck swelling appears out of proportion to any infectious trigger because traditional “DM” panels may miss this autoantibody if you rely only on older kits lacking it.  

## Imaging pearls & pitfalls  

Contrast‐enhanced neck CT is often the first study ordered when airway compromise is feared—it will show strap muscle enlargement and retropharyngeal thickening just as Ludwig’s angina does—but look carefully:

* **No focal fluid collection** – true abscesses usually have peripheral rim enhancement & occasionally gas formation.*  
* **Symmetric involvement** – infections tend to be unilateral or heavily lobulated whereas DM produces diffuse homogeneous edema across multiple muscle groups.*  
* **Absence of bony erosion** – helps rule out chronic osteomyelitis or neoplastic infiltration.*  

FDG-PET adds valuable context—global muscular hypermetabolism signals active inflammation rather than malignancy when coupled with negative tumor markers and clean PET elsewhere—as illustrated by our patient’s diffusely avid musculature without focal hypermetabolic masses outside head/neck/thorax/pelvis.^  

## Treatment principles – act fast before airway collapses  

I’m going back to basics here because time matters:

* **Pulse steroids** – methylprednisolone 1000 mg IV daily ×3 days curtails cytokine release instantly; most clinicians transition within 24–48 h to oral prednisone ≈0.8–1 mg/kg/day.^  
* **Steroid sparing agents** – mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) has excellent tolerability data in DM involving lung or esophagus; selected early here because methotrexate carries pulmonary fibrosis risk especially given underlying lung nodules.^  
* **IVIG** – provides immunomodulation via Fc blockade & neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies—particularly useful when rapid functional improvement is needed for swallowing muscles.^  
* **Rituximab** – B cell depletion can accelerate remission in refractory cases or when autoantibody titers are high (e.g., SAE1); used here synergistically after initial steroid burst.^  
* **Supportive care** – nasogastric feeds until dysphagia resolves prevents aspiration pneumonia; speech therapy accelerates safe oral intake return.^  
* **Prophylaxis** – PCP prophylaxis mandatory when high-dose steroids plus additional immunosuppressants are employed; our patient switched from TMP–SMX due to hemolytic anemia onto nebulised pentamidine successfully.^  

When faced with cervical swelling threatening airway patency—even if hemodynamically stable—early multidisciplinary coordination between ENT, rheumatology/immunology and critical care dramatically improves outcomes versus proceeding directly to surgical drainage which would have been fruitless here.  

## Follow up & malignancy vigilance  

Adult dermatomyositis carries an inherent malignancy risk (~15–20%). Heightened risk factors include male sex, dysphagia at presentation and certain autoantibodies such as anti‑SAE1 (intermediate risk relative to anti–TIF1γ which carries higher risk). According to IMACS cancer screening guidelines our patient warranted baseline whole-body imaging plus targeted tumor markers—which were all negative—and ongoing surveillance including periodic CT chest/abdomen/pelvis plus upper/lower GI endoscopy planned during follow up years later.^  

Serial CK measurement proved invaluable: falling from >16 000 U/L down <40 U/L correlated tightly with clinical resolution of both muscle strength deficits and cervical edema—reinforcing CK as both diagnostic anchor and therapeutic marker in this context.^  

## Take‑home messages  

- **Think inflammatory myopathy when neck swelling persists without fever, purulence or rapid progression despite standard therapy** → obtain CK promptly.  
- **Look beyond classic skin signs:** peri‑ungual telangiectasias/microhaemorrhages on nailfold capillaroscopy are often the first dermatologic clue in pseudo‑angioedema cases.  
- **Anti‑SAE1 positivity flags a propensity for cervical edema & dysphagia**, so request a full myositis antibody panel early if DM is suspected.  
- **CT may show “Ludwig’s”‐type edema**, but absence of fluid collection/rim enhancement plus symmetric muscular thickening should raise suspicion for DM rather than infection.  
- **High-dose steroids + IVIG ± rituximab can reverse life-threatening airway edema rapidly**; add MMF as steroid-sparing agent once acute phase settles.  
- **Malignancy screening is mandatory:** adult DM patients with dysphagia/right sex & SAE1 antibodies sit in a higher risk tier—baseline PET/CT plus age-appropriate endoscopic workup are essential.  
- **Early multidisciplinary communication (ENT ↔ Rheumatology ↔ Critical Care)** prevents unnecessary surgical interventions and accelerates initiation of disease-modifying therapy._



# Anti‑SAE1 Antibodies Unveiled: Clinical Phenotype, Prognosis & Malignancy Risk  

*What do you call a neck that looks like an allergic reaction but isn’t? A dermatomyositis that’s masquerading as angioedema.*  

When an otherwise healthy man in his 40s walked into the ED with a three‑week history of progressive neck swelling, the first thoughts were infection or “classic” angioedema. A CT showed diffuse strap‑muscle edema extending into the retropharyngeal space—no dental source or abscess was found. He was shunted between ENT surgeons and immunologists before a simple creatine kinase (CK) draw tipped the scale toward inflammatory myopathy. The final diagnosis? Anti‑small ubiquitin‑like modifier activating enzyme‐1 (anti‑SAE1) positive dermatomyositis presenting as pseudo‑angioedema.  

This story is a perfect reminder that **autoimmune muscle disease can hide behind what looks like an allergic reaction**, and it offers a concise primer on the emerging phenotype associated with anti‑SAE 1 antibodies.

---

## Anti‑SAE1 Antibodies – A Quick Primer  

Anti‑SAE antibodies target the SUMO‑activating enzyme complex involved in protein sumoylation—a process increasingly recognized as a driver of immune dysregulation in inflammatory myopathies. In contemporary myositis panels they sit alongside classic markers like Mi‑2, Jo‑1, TIF1‑γ and MDA5. While still relatively uncommon (<10 % of adult DM cases), SAE 1 positivity carries a distinctive clinical fingerprint that clinicians should learn to recognize early:

| Feature | Typical Frequency* |
|--------|--------------------|
| Dysphagia | ~46 % |
| Interstitial lung disease (ILD) | ~43 % |
| Malignancy association | ~18 % |
| Pseudo‑angioedema/soft tissue edema | <7 % (8/121 patients in a large cohort) |

\*Numbers derived from large retrospective series cited in the index case report and related literature reviews.

The takeaway? **If you see any two of these red flags—especially dysphagia + unexplained soft tissue swelling—consider ordering an extended myositis antibody panel even if classic Gottron papules are absent**.

---

## Phenotypic Hallmarks of Anti‑SAE1 Dermatomyositis  

### Skin clues you might miss  

* **Holster/V‑neck sign** – A violaceous rash over the anterior chest/upper back mimicking a “holster” was present in our patient (“right lateral thigh rash” plus facial erythema). It often predates overt muscle weakness.
* **Periungual erythema & nailfold capillary changes** – Subtle microhemorrhages or telangiectasias can be the *only* skin footprint early on. In this case nailfold capillaroscopy revealed avascular areas and microhemorrhages—findings that point toward an underlying vasculopathic process typical of DM.
* **Absence of heliotrope / Gottron’s does NOT exclude DM** – Classic rashes are helpful but not required; anti‑SAE patients may have minimal cutaneous disease beyond V‑neck or periungual changes.

### Musculoskeletal picture  

* **CK skyrocket** – Values can climb beyond 10 000 U/L (our patient hit >16 000 U/L). Such magnitude should trigger immediate rheumatology referral regardless of pain severity.
* **Neck muscle edema masquerading as angioedema** – Inflammation of strap muscles infiltrates subcutaneous tissue → non‑histaminergic swelling resistant to epinephrine or antihistamines.
* **Dysphagia is common** – Pharyngeal‐esophageal muscle involvement leads to aspiration pneumonitis, as reflected by our patient’s barium swallow findings and patchy consolidation on chest CT.
* **ILD pattern** – Restrictive physiology plus basilar ground glass opacities are seen in almost half of SAE+ cases; early recognition allows preemptive treatment before fibrosis sets in.

### Systemic involvement  

Beyond muscles and skin, SAE 1 positives may have:

* Elevated transaminases & LDH reflecting broad myocyte injury
* Positive ANA often at high titer (our patient 1:1280 speckled)
* Small‐vessel complement deposition on muscle biopsy (C5b–9)

---

## Malignancy Risk – How High Should We Be Worried?  

Dermatomyositis itself confers an innate oncologic vigilance requirement. The International Myositis Assessment & Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) malignancy‐screening algorithm stratifies patients based on clinical features:

| Risk factor | Weight |
|-------------|--------|
| **DM diagnosis** | High |
| Dysphagia | High |
| Anti‑SAE1 positivity | Intermediate |
| Male sex | Intermediate |

Our patient accrued **two high + two intermediate risk factors**, tipping him into the *high‐risk* category per IMACS recommendations. This justified an aggressive workup:

* Whole‐body CT (chest/abdomen/pelvis) — negative for solid tumors
* PET scan — no hypermetabolic foci outside inflamed musculature
* Tumor markers — CEA, CA19‑9, CA125, PSA all normal
* Planned upper/lower GI endoscopy — pending at 12 months

**Practical rule of thumb:** every newly diagnosed DM patient should have baseline age–appropriate cancer screening *plus* modality tailored by individual risk factors (e.g., PET for high‐risk serologies). Repeat surveillance should be symptom‐driven but maintain at least annual imaging for the first three years when warranted.

---

## Putting It Into Practice – When to Order a Myositis Panel  

A stepwise approach can prevent mislabeling an inflammatory myopathy as allergic edema:

1️⃣ **Red flag assessment at bedside**  
   *Rapidly progressive neck/facial swelling without identifiable infection or drug exposure.*  
   *Concomitant proximal weakness or unexplained fatigue.*  
   *Any cutaneous clue—even subtle periungual changes.*

2️⃣ **First lab grab**  
   - Serum CK + AST/ALT + LDH  
   - Basic inflammatory markers (CRP often modestly raised)  
   - Complement C3/C4 and C1 esterase inhibitor if bradykinin‐mediated angioedema still plausible

3️⃣ **If CK >1000 U/L or any skin/muscle clue present → send comprehensive myositis antibody panel**  
   - Panels usually include Mi‑2α/β, Jo‑1, PL‑7/12, SRP, TIF1γ, MDA5, NXP2 & SAE antibodies among others.
   - Positive SAE = strong suspicion for DM even if classic rashes missing.

4️⃣ **Imaging adjuncts**  
   - MRI of neck/thorax when edema localizes → distinguishes fluid collection from inflammatory infiltrate  
   - FDG‑PET useful both for assessing disease burden *and* cancer screening when indicated

5️⃣ **Fast‐track referral**  
   - Early rheumatology/immunology involvement shortens time to immunosuppression initiation—a key determinant of outcomes in airway‐compromising disease.

## Therapeutic Pearls from Our Case  

| Intervention | Rationale |
|--------------|-----------|
| **IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg ×3 days** | Rapid suppression of cytokine‐driven muscle inflammation preventing airway collapse |
| **Prednisone taper starting at 60 mg** | Bridge therapy while steroid‐sparing agents gain effect |
| **Mycophenolate mofetil 2 g daily** | Favoured over methotrexate given concurrent ILD concerns |
| **IVIG 2 g/kg divided over 5 days**, repeated monthly | Addresses refractory muscle inflammation & improves dysphagia |
| **Rituximab 1000 mg ×2 doses two weeks apart** | B cell depletion helpful in seropositive myositis refractory to steroids alone |
| **PJP prophylaxis** → Switched from TMP–SMX → Nebulised pentamidine after hemolysis | Highlights need for vigilance regarding drug toxicities during intensive immunosuppression |

Outcome metrics were striking: CK normalized from >16 000 U/L to <40 U/L within three weeks; cervical edema resolved completely by three months; dysphagia cleared by seven months; pulmonary opacities regressed on serial CTs; no malignancy detected after one year follow up.   

The combination of **high‐dose steroids**, **early IVIG**, and a thoughtful steroid-sparing strategy appears particularly effective in SAE+ patients presenting with severe soft tissue edema and airway compromise.

---

## Bottom Line  

- Dermatomyositis can masquerade as idiopathic angioedema when inflammation inflames strap muscles—don’t let a “swollen neck” steer you straight into ENT unless you’ve checked CK first.  
- Anti‑SAE1 antibodies highlight a phenotype packed with *holster/V‑neck rash*, *periungual capillary changes*, *frequent dysphagia*, *moderate ILD*, and an *elevated cancer risk*.  
- A systematic approach—clinical clue hunting → CK → extended myositis serology → targeted imaging → early multidisciplinary treatment—turns what looks like an intimidating airway emergency into a treatable autoimmune disease.  

---

### Key Take‑Home Points  

- **Any unexplained cervical/facial swelling without obvious infection or drug trigger = check CK ASAP**; consider inflammatory myopathy even if histamine/bradykinin labs are normal.  
- **Look beyond classic Gottron papules:** periungual erythema/nailfold hemorrhages and V‑neck/Holster rash are valuable early clues for SA­E+ dermatomyositis.  
- **Anti‑SAE1 positivity predicts:**  
  • Dysphagia ≈46 %  
  • ILD ≈43 %  
  • Malignancy ≈18 % → merit full cancer screening per IMACS guidelines.  
- **Management tip:** Pulse steroids followed by rapid escalation to steroid-sparing agents (MMF ± IVIG ± rituximab) yields quicker resolution of edema/dysphagia than steroids alone.  
- **Screening cadence:** Baseline whole-body imaging + age/gender appropriate cancers; repeat targeted studies annually for at least three years if high-risk features persist.  

Stay curious—when your ENT colleagues say “looks like Ludwig’s angina,” ask whether there’s an autoantibody hidden under those swollen straps!



# When ENT Meets Immunology: A Multidisciplinary Algorithm for Unexplained Deep Neck Space Swelling  

*By [Your Name], MD – Emergency Medicine & Rheumatology Enthusiast*  

---

## Why This Case Grabs Your Attention  

A previously healthy man in his forties strolls into the ED with three weeks of **progressive neck swelling** and mild proximal hip weakness. The CT looks “Ludwig‑angina‑like” – diffuse edema of the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space – yet he’s afebrile, white count is normal and there’s **no obvious source of infection**. ENT puts him on the “deep neck space infection” track while immunology is consulted for suspected idiopathic angioedema because his presentation resembles a non‑histaminergic swell‑up.  

The twist? A **creatine kinase of >16 000 U/L** and **positive anti‑SAE1 antibodies** reveal **dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema**. The swelling isn’t from histamine or bradykinin at all; it’s inflammatory oedema within inflamed muscle fibres (“pseudo‑angioedema”).  

If you ever see unexplained cervical soft‑tissue swelling that doesn’t dance to the usual infectious or allergic tune, this case reminds us to **think muscle** early – and to bring immunology into the fold before you cut into it surgically.

---

## Quick Recap of the Patient

| Feature | What It Looked Like | What It Actually Was |
|---|---|---|
| **Neck swelling** | Likely Ludwig’s angina / deep neck infection | Diffuse inflammatory oedema from myositis |
| **Systemic labs** | Elevated CRP (55 mg/L) → infection | Normal WCC (9 × 10⁹/L) → non‑infectious inflammation |
| **Airway assessment** | Concern for imminent obstruction | No airway compromise after steroids |
| **Complement studies** | Normal C3/C4 & C1‑INH → low suspicion for hereditary/acquired angioedema | Consistent |
| **CK** | Not ordered initially | 16 184 U/L → massive muscle injury |
| **Autoantibodies** | – | Positive ANA (1:1280 speckled) & anti‑SAE1 |
| **Physical exam clues** | “Just a swollen neck” | Peri‑ungual erythema/micro‑haemorrhages, subtle thigh rash (“holster sign”), facial erythema |

The crux was that **early CK measurement**, prompted by a thoughtful skin & nail exam, turned a diagnostic dead‑end into a treatable inflammatory myopathy within hours rather than days.

---

## The Bigger Picture – How Often Does This Happen?  

* Dermatomyositis has an incidence of ~10 per million.
* Pseudo‑angioedema is *rare*: ≤7 % of DM patients develop subcutaneous edema; neck involvement is even scarcer.
* Anti‑SAE1 antibodies are usually associated with classic cutaneous findings (heliotrope rash, Gottron papules). Edematous neck swelling has been reported in only ~3 % of SAE1‑positive cases.

Because it’s so infrequent, it slips through routine differential lists—until it doesn’t.

---

## A Stepwise Algorithm for Unexplained Deep Neck Swelling  

Below is a practical flowchart you can keep on your wall or embed in your EMR order set. It integrates **ENT imaging**, **infectious work‑up**, **complement testing**, and an early **rheumatology/immune work‑up** when red flags appear.  

> **Note:** The algorithm assumes the patient is hemodynamically stable and airway protected at presentation. If there are signs of airway compromise (stridor, voice change, rapidly expanding mass), bypass this pathway and secure the airway immediately per standard protocol.

### Step 0 – Immediate Airway & Hemodynamic Safety  

* Assess airway patency (talking, stridor, drooling).  
* Give high‑flow O₂ if needed; have emergent intubation equipment ready.  
* If airway threatened → ENT + anesthesia involvement → consider fiberoptic intubation or surgical airway before labs/imaging delay.

### Step 1 – Structured History & Physical  

| Element | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Medication review (ACEi, ARBs, DPP‑4 inhibitors) | Histamine‐independent angioedema often drug‑triggered |
| Recent infections or dental work | Classic deep neck space infections often odontogenic |
| Personal/family history of angioedema or autoimmunity | Guides complement vs rheumatologic workup |
| Detailed skin exam (Gottron papules, heliotrope rash), nailfold capillaroscopy | Subtle DM clues may be missed on cursory exam |
| Dysphagia / voice changes / odynophagia | Suggests muscle involvement beyond superficial tissue |

If any *muscular* clues appear—proximal weakness, dysphagia—even subtly—jump ahead to Step 4 (early rheumatology input).

### Step 2 – Baseline Laboratory Panel  

Order *simultaneously*:  

* CBC with diff  
* CRP & ESR  
* BMP (renal & electrolytes)  
* Liver function panel (AST/ALT/LDH) – often elevated in myositis  
* Creatine kinase (CK) – **critical!**  
* Serum complement C4 & C1 esterase inhibitor level/function  

Interpretation hints:

| Result Pattern | Likely Etiology |
|---|---|
| ↑ WBC + ↑ CRP + fever → suggests bacterial infection |
| Normal WBC + ↑ CRP ± mild AST/ALT ↑ → inflammatory/non‑infectious |
| Low C4 / low functional C1-INH → hereditary/acquired bradykinin angioedema |
| Extremely high CK (>5 000 U/L) → Active myopathy/muscle breakdown |

In our patient all infections markers were modest while CK was astronomic—pointing straight toward an inflammatory myopathy rather than an abscess.

### Step 3 – Imaging – CT Neck First  

* Contrast‑enhanced CT of the neck with thin cuts through the level of the hyoid is the go‐to study.* Look specifically for:

* Gas formation → necrotizing fasciitis / deep space abscess  
* Fluid collections with rim enhancement → abscess needing drainage  
* Diffuse soft‐tissue edema without focal collection → raises suspicion for non‐infectious processes (e.g., inflammatory myositis)  

If CT shows **diffuse strap muscle enlargement without rim enhancement**, consider moving forward to Step 4 even if CRP modestly elevated—it may be “pseudo‐angioedema”.

### Step 4 – Early Rheumatology / Immunology Consultation  

Trigger points for automatic consult:

1. CK ≥ 5 000 U/L OR CK rising rapidly on repeat draws  
2. Nailfold capillary changes / periungual telangiectasia / subtle rashes  
3. Dysphagia disproportionate to obvious structural lesions  
4. Normal complement studies *and* no drug trigger identified  

Rheumatology typically orders:

* Expanded myositis autoantibody panel (including anti‑SAE1, anti-Mi2α, anti-MDA5 etc.)  
* ANA with titer/pattern reporting  
* Possibly MRI of involved musculature if diagnosis remains elusive after labs

In our case the anti‑SAE1 IgG was strongly positive (>173 signal intensity), clinching dermatomyositis despite lack of classic Gottron papules.

### Step 5 – Decision Node – Medical Management vs Surgical Exploration  

| Scenario | Recommended Action |
|---|---|
| Imaging shows well-defined abscess *and* infectious labs positive → consult ENT/oS for incision & drainage plus antibiotics |
| Diffuse muscle edema + CK high + negative infectious workup → start high‑dose IV glucocorticoids *before* any operative plan |
| Complement low ⇒ bradykinin-mediated angioedema → give C1‑INH concentrate/icatibant per protocol |

If steroids are initiated early (<24 h), many patients experience rapid reduction in swelling—often obviating need for surgical debridement altogether.

### Step 6 – Ongoing Monitoring & Multidisciplinary Follow‑up  

* Daily bedside swallow assessment & speech therapist input when dysphagia present   
* Repeat CK every 48–72 h until trending downwards  
* TPM/IVIG schedule as per rheumatology plan

## Where This Algorithm Saves You Time (and Patients)

* **Rapid CK ordering** spotlights inflammatory myopathy in <24 h—avoids days of broad-spectrum antibiotics or unnecessary operative trips.
* **Complement testing early** rules out bradykinin pathways before you waste costly epinephrine or icatibant doses on a non–bradykinin process.
* **ENT imaging first** clarifies anatomy—if no focal collection exists you can safely defer any invasive drainage attempts.
* Involving rheumatology early streamlines autoantibody ordering—most hospitals now have a “myositis panel” that returns within 48 h once requested.
* The entire pathway can be built into an EMR order set so that once “unexplained deep neck swelling” is flagged you get all labs/imaging automatically ordered—and a paging template alerts immunology/rheumatology teams concurrently.


---

## Pearls & Pitfalls  

**Pearls**

1. *Always check CK when deep cervical soft tissue swelling is not explained by infection.* Even mildly elevated CK can hint at myositis; massive elevations (>10k) seal the diagnosis.
2. Subtle skin findings matter—periungual telangiectasias or a faint “holster sign’’ on thighs are easy to miss unless you specifically look.
3. Normal complement levels essentially rule out hereditary/acquired C1‐INH deficiency–mediated angioedema; proceed down the rheumatologic track instead.
4. High-dose IV methylprednisolone can dramatically reduce intramuscular edema within hours—request burst steroids once myositis suspected even before muscle biopsy results return.
5. In steroid‐sparing strategies for dermatomyositis with significant lung involvement avoid methotrexate due to its potential pulmonary toxicity—mycophenolate mofetil becomes first-line adjunct in many centres now.

**Pitfalls**

* Assuming every neck cellulitis/abscess is bacterial just because CRP is mildly raised—muscle inflammation also drives CRP up.
* Forgetting medication review—ACE inhibitors remain the most common cause of bradykinin-mediated angioedema in emergency settings.
* Delaying rheumatology input waiting for confirmatory biopsy—you can start empiric therapy based on CK + antibody profile while awaiting histopathology if clinical picture fits.


---

## Take–Home Messages

- **Unexplained deep neck swelling ≠ infection automatically**; keep inflammatory myopathy on your differential when labs don’t fit an infectious pattern.
- **Creatine kinase is your fastest gatekeeper**—order it alongside routine labs whenever you encounter atypical cervical edema without clear source.
- A brief nailfold capillaroscopy exam (+ targeted skin exam) can unmask dermatomyositis before serologies return.
- Early ***multidisciplinary*** engagement—ENT for imaging/airway expertise; Immunology/Rheumatology for autoimmunity workup—slashes diagnostic delay and spares patients needless surgery.
- The proposed algorithm provides a reproducible roadmap that integrates imaging, basic labs, complement studies and rheumatologic screening into one cohesive flow chart—fit it into your department protocol and watch diagnostic confidence rise!



# Beyond Steroids: Tailoring Immunosuppression for Severe Dermatomyositis With Airway Involvement  

## Why this case is worth a second look  

When a middle‑aged man walks into the ED with a “swollen neck” we immediately think Ludwig’s angina or an allergic angioedema—both potentially airway‑threatening emergencies that demand antibiotics or antihistamines/Bradykinin blockers. In this report the CT showed diffuse strap‑muscle oedema extending into the retropharyngeal space, but there was no fever, no dental source and no response to standard airway protocols. The clue that tipped the team off was **the combination of neck swelling with proximal muscle weakness and subtle nailfold changes**—features that don’t belong on a classic angioedema checklist. A single CK of >16 000 U/L later anchored the diagnosis: dermatomyositis masquerading as pseudo‑angioedema.  

This “angi‑myo‐edema” presentation is rare (≈6 % of dermatomyositis patients develop oedema at any site), but it is a red flag for **airway‑muscle involvement** that can progress to life‑threatening dysphagia or respiratory failure if treatment is delayed. The case forces us to rethink our default “steroids first” paradigm and consider how best to layer immunosuppression safely when the airway is on the line.

## Pathophysiology in a nutshell  

Dermatomyositis (DM) is an immune‑mediated microangiopathy targeting capillaries in skin and muscle. The classic perivascular complement C5b‑9 deposition you see on biopsy triggers fibrin deposition and necrosis—hence rising CK and muscle pain. In some patients, especially those with anti‑SAE1 antibodies, inflammation extends into subcutaneous tissue causing **non‑histaminergic oedema** (“pseudo‑angioedema”). The neck’s strap muscles are thin and richly vascularised; inflammation here can create dramatic swelling without infection and impair pharyngeal musculature → dysphagia and aspiration risk.

Anti‑SAE1 antibodies are not just serologic curiosities—they’re linked to prominent skin disease and frequently dysphagia (≈46 % in large series). Their presence steers you toward aggressive early therapy because they often herald severe systemic involvement.

## Diagnostic pearls – find the DM hiding behind the puffiness  

| Finding | Why it matters |
|---------|----------------|
| **CK >5 000 U/L** | Confirms active myositis; distinguishes inflammatory oedema from true angioedema |
| **Nailfold capillary microhaemorrhages / periungual erythema** | Easy bedside clue that skin microangiopathy is brewing |
| **Subtle skin signs** – Holster sign, facial erythema | Dermatomyositis cutaneous stigmata are often patchy or early |
| **Negative C1‑esterase inhibitor & normal complement** | Rules out hereditary/acquired bradykinin angioedema |
| **Anti‑SAE1 positivity** | Predicts more aggressive disease course & may guide immunotherapy choice |

If you see any combination of these, order CK and a myositis panel *before* launching antihistamines or antibiotics.

## Building an immunosuppressive regimen – stepwise but pragmatic  

### 1️⃣ Pulse methylprednisolone: hit hard, fast  

- **Dose:** 1 g IV daily × 3 days (as used in this patient).  
- **Rationale:** Massive glucocorticoid burst dampens complement‑mediated microvascular injury within hours—critical when airway muscles are inflamed.  
- **Practical tip:** Administer with anti‑emetics and monitor glucose; in diabetics consider insulin sliding scale from day 1.  

After pulsing, transition to oral prednisone 0.75–1 mg/kg/day (~60 mg) with a slow taper (~5 mg reduction every 2–3 weeks) guided by CK trend and clinical exam rather than fixed timelines.

### 2️⃣ Early IVIG loading – when “extra” muscle protection matters  

- **Indication:** Severe or rapidly progressive muscle involvement *or* airway/ dysphagia threatening nutrition/airway protection despite steroids alone.  
- **Regimen used:** 2 g/kg divided over 5 days (≈35 g/day for a 75 kg adult), then repeat cycles every 4–6 weeks until disease quiescent.  
- **Mechanism:** IgG saturates Fcγ receptors, blocks complement activation, and modulates B‑cell autoreactivity—especially helpful in antibody‐driven subtypes like SAE1+.  
- **Safety notes:** Pre‑infusion hydration, monitor for hemolysis (particularly with high‐dose steroids) and watch for thromboembolic events if patient has hypercoagulable risk factors.

### 3️⃣ Choosing a steroid‐sparing agent – mycophenolate mofetil vs methotrexate  

| Drug | Pro | Con |
|------|-----|-----|
| **Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)** | • Oral twice daily → adherence friendly<br>• Proven efficacy in ILD associated with DM<br>• Minimal pulmonary toxicity<br>• No folate antagonism (important if patient on folic acid supplementation) | • GI upset common<br>• Myelosuppression at higher doses |
| **Methotrexate** | • Long track record as first‐line DM agent<br>• Weekly dosing reduces pill burden | • Can precipitate or worsen interstitial lung disease<br>• Requires folic acid rescue<br>• Hepatotoxicity risk especially with alcohol or pre‑existing liver enzymes elevation |

In this patient the CT chest showed non‑cavitating nodules & mild restrictive physiology without frank fibrosis—a red flag for MTX pulmonary toxicity. MMF was therefore favoured despite its slower onset because it offered lung safety while still delivering robust disease control when combined with IVIG and rituximab.

Typical MMF start: 500 mg BID → titrate to 1000–1500 mg BID as tolerated; check CBC & LFTs every 2–4 weeks initially then monthly.

### 4️⃣ Rituximab – B cell depletion when autoantibodies drive the storm  

- **When to add:** In anti‑SAE1+ disease where rapid antibody suppression may hasten remission, or if CK fails to drop <5 × ULN after 4–6 weeks of steroids + MMF ± IVIG.  
- **Regimen used:** Two infusions of 1000 mg spaced two weeks apart (standard rheumatoid arthritis protocol). Some clinicians use weekly 375 mg/m² × 4 doses—pick whichever fits your infusion capacity.  
- **Evidence bite:** Small case series suggest rituximab yields faster CK normalization and fewer relapses in refractory DM; B‑cell depletion also reduces long‐term steroid need.  
- **Screening before infusion:** Hepatitis B surface Ag & core Ab screen (reactivation risk), baseline IgG levels (monitor for hypogammaglobulinemia), vaccination status up to date (pneumococcal & influenza).

### 5️⃣ Prophylaxis & toxicity monitoring  

| Issue | Recommended strategy |
|-------|----------------------|
| **PJP prophylaxis** | First line TMP‑SMX 80/400 mg daily × 3–4 weeks then thrice weekly if tolerated.<br>In case of sulfa intolerance or hemolysis (as occurred here), switch to inhaled pentamidine 300 mg q4wks or atovaquone PO if renal safe. |
| **Steroid side effects** | Bone protection: calcium + vitamin D + bisphosphonate if >3 months high dose.<br>Screen glucose daily while on pulses; taper quickly once disease controlled.<br>Consider stress‐dose steroids peri‐procedurally while tapering below ~10 mg prednisone equivalent. |
| **MMF toxicity** | CBC/chemistry q2wks → qmonthly after stable.<br>Hold dose if ANC <1.0 or platelets <50 ×10⁹/L.<br>Check CMV PCR if lymphopenic (<500). |
| **Rituximab adverse events** | Premedicate with acetaminophen + diphenhydramine ± methylpred 100 mg SC.<br>Watch for infusion reactions during first hour.<br>Repeat CD19 count at month 3–6 to gauge B‑cell reconstitution; retreat if CD19 rises before clinical flare.<br>Hepatitis B reactivation surveillance every 3 months for at least 12 months post‐infusion. |
| **IVIG safety** | Ensure adequate hydration (>2 L/day during infusion).<br>Consider low‐dose aspirin or enoxaparin prophylaxis if patient has thrombotic history.<br>Check baseline serum IgG level; repeat after several cycles if infections recur → may need dose escalation or subcutaneous replacement.|

## Putting it together – an algorithm you can run at bedside  

1. **Suspect DM** when neck/face swelling is non‑fluctuant, unilateral analgesic response absent, CK >5× ULN OR nailfold capillary changes present → order CK + myositis panel *immediately*.  
2. Start *pulse methylpred* ×3 days while arranging urgent ENT assessment (airway security).  
3. Transition to oral prednisone + initiate *IVIG loading* (2 g/kg over 5 days) if any of:
   - Dysphagia requiring NG tube,
   - Rapid CK rise,
   - Impending airway compromise.
4. Choose *MMF* as first steroid-sparing agent unless contraindicated; begin at 500 mg BID → titrate.
5. Add *rituximab* early (<6 weeks) in anti‑SAE1+/severe phenotype or if CK plateau >5× ULN after step #3.
6. Implement *PJP prophylaxis* proactively; monitor labs per above schedule.
7. Reassess weekly: CK trend, neck circumference/genial symmetry, swallow study from speech pathology.
8​ .Taper prednisone gradually once CK <500 U/L on two consecutive readings *and* clinical strength has returned ≥4/5 across proximal groups.
9​ .Long-term surveillance for malignancy per IMACS guidelines—annual CT chest + age‐appropriate cancer screening given high‐risk serology (+SAE1).

## Take‑home messages  

- **Neck edema + proximal weakness = think dermatomyositis**, not just infection or histamine/bradykinin angioedema.
- Prompt CK measurement can prevent weeks of ineffective anti-allergy therapy.
- Pulse methylprednisolone remains the cornerstone for airway involvement but must be paired quickly with steroid‑sparing drugs.
- Early IVIG loading is justified when dysphagia threatens nutrition/airway—its immunomodulatory effect complements glucocorticoids especially in antibody‐driven subtypes.
- Mycophenolate mofetil is preferable over methotrexate when any lung involvement is present; its safety profile aligns better with subclinical ILD.
- Rituximab provides rapid B cell depletion useful in SAE1+ disease and can shorten steroid courses.
- PJP prophylaxis should start alongside high‐dose steroids even if planned taper is rapid—monitor closely for drug intolerance and have inhaled pentamidine as an alternative.
- Vigilant laboratory surveillance (CK trend, CBC/LFT/eGFR) guides dosing adjustments and prevents avoidable toxicities.

By keeping an eye out for “pseudo‑angioedema” and employing a layered immunosuppressive strategy—high-dose steroids ➜ IVIG ➜ MMF ➜ rituximab—you can protect both the airway and long-term muscle function while minimizing steroid burden in severe dermatomyositis cases like this one.



# Cancer Surveillance in Dermatomyositis: Applying IMACS Guidelines to Anti‑SAE1 Positive Patients  

## The Hook – When “Angioedema” Was Really Myositis  
A previously healthy man in his 40s walked into the ED with three weeks of progressive neck swelling that looked just like Ludwig’s angina or idiopathic angioedema. CT showed diffuse edema of the strap muscles and the retropharyngeal space, but there was no abscess, no dental source and he was afebrile. The ENT team cleared him of infection; an immunology consult was called because his swelling didn’t behave like classic histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema.  

A quick glance at his nailfold capillaroscopy revealed peri‑ungual telangiectasias; a subtle rash on his thigh was noted; CK shot up to > 16 000 U/L. The final piece fell into place when his myositis panel returned strongly positive for anti‑small ubiquitin‑like modifier activating enzyme 1 (SAE1) antibodies. A muscle biopsy confirmed dermatomyositis (DM).  

What makes this case worth writing about isn’t just that DM can masquerade as angioedema – it’s that **anti‑SAE1–positive DM carries a non‑trivial malignancy risk** (≈ 18 % in large series). The patient’s presentation forced us to ask: **How aggressively should we screen this guy (and others like him) for cancer?**  

That question lands squarely on the shoulders of every emergency physician, ENT surgeon, rheumatologist and immunologist who sees a DM patient with high‑risk features. Below we walk through the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) recommendations, map them onto real‐world practice, and give you a pragmatic surveillance algorithm that balances thoroughness with resource stewardship.  

---

## Dermatomyositis & Malignancy – A Quick Refresher  
Dermatomyositis is one of the classic paraneoplastic autoimmune syndromes. Across cohorts the prevalence of an associated cancer ranges from **15–30 %**, most often within the first two years of DM onset. Classic red flags include:

* Rapidly evolving skin rash (heliotrope eruption or Gottron’s papules) together with systemic symptoms  
* New or worsening dysphagia  
* Unexplained weight loss or night sweats  
* Persistent elevation of inflammatory markers despite immunosuppression  

While any DM patient warrants some level of cancer screening, several variables tilt the scale toward a more intensive workup:

| High‑risk feature | Effect on screening intensity |
|-------------------|--------------------------------|
| Classic DM skin disease **plus** dysphagia | Classifies as *high* risk |
| Certain myositis‐specific antibodies (anti‐TIF1‑γ, anti‐NXP2, anti‐MDA5) | Pushes toward *high* intensity |
| Anti‑SAE1 positivity | Considered *intermediate* but adds weight when combined with other factors |
| Male sex & age >50 y | Adds another *intermediate* point |

IMACS recommends counting **two “high” or three “intermediate” factors** as *high cancer risk*, prompting an aggressive baseline assessment plus structured follow‑up. Our patient met this threshold (DM + dysphagia = high; anti‑SAE1 = intermediate; male sex = intermediate).  

---

## Anti‑SAE1 Antibodies – Not Just a Lab Oddity  
Anti‑SAE1 antibodies target the SUMO activating enzyme complex involved in protein post‑translational modification. Clinically they are associated with:

* Prominent cutaneous disease – V‑neck sign, shawl sign, “holster” erythema  
* Dysphagia in nearly half of cases  
* Interstitial lung disease (~43 %)  
* **Malignancy in ≈ 18 %**, similar to other “cancer‐associated” antibodies  

Thus an SAE1‐positive finger on your serology panel should automatically raise your index of suspicion for an occult neoplasm—especially when coupled with dysphagia or male gender.  

---

## IMACS Cancer‐Screening Recommendations – The Blueprint  

**Step 1 – Risk Stratification**  
Assign each patient to *low*, *intermediate*, or *high* cancer risk based on:

| Category | Criteria |
|----------|----------|
| **High** | Classic DM **plus** any one of: dysphagia, rapid weight loss/B symptoms, anti‑TIF1γ/anti‐NXP2/anti‐MDA5 antibodies |
| **Intermediate** | Classic DM alone *or* presence of an intermediate antibody (e.g., anti‑SAE1) without other high-risk features |
| **Low** | Amyopathic DM without systemic features or antibodies linked to cancer |

**Step 2 – Baseline Evaluation (within first 3 months)**  

| Modality | Indication |
|----------|------------|
| **CT chest/abdomen/pelvis** (contrast unless contraindicated) | All patients ≥ intermediate risk |
| **FDG‑PET/CT** | Preferred for *high-risk* patients OR if CT shows equivocal lesions |
| **Age–appropriate cancer screens** (colonoscopy ≥50 y or earlier if family history; mammography/PAP for women; PSA for men >50 y) | Mandatory for all adults |
| **Tumor markers** (CEA, CA19‑9, CA125, PSA) | Optional adjuncts; useful when PET unavailable |
| **Baseline labs** (CBC, CMP, ESR/CRP) | Helpful for future trend comparison |

**Step 3 – Follow‑up Schedule**

* **High‐risk:** Repeat whole‐body imaging (CT or PET) at 6 months and then annually for at least 3–5 years if prior studies are negative.
* **Intermediate‐risk:** Repeat imaging at 12 months; thereafter only if new red flags arise.
* **Low‐risk:** Annual age‐appropriate screening only; no routine repeat cross‑sectional imaging unless clinically indicated.

All patients should have CK monitoring every 3–6 months—not because CK predicts cancer but because spikes may herald disease flare that could obscure cancer signals on imaging.  

---

## Putting It Into Practice – A Tiered Imaging Algorithm  

Below is a practical flowchart you can keep on your phone or EMR cheat sheet when you encounter a DM patient:

```
                ┌───────────────┐
                │ Classify Risk │
                └───────┬───────┘
                        │
      ┌─────────────────┼─────────────────┐
      │                 │                 │
   Low Risk          Intermediate      High Risk
      │                 │                 │
   Age-specific      Baseline CT       Baseline CT +
   screening          chest/abd/pelvis   + FDG-PET/CT
      │                 │                 │
   No routine       Repeat CT @12mo    Repeat CT/FDPET @6mo,

```

### How This Played Out in Our SAE1 Case  
*Baseline*: Whole‐body CT chest/abdomen/pelvis plus tumor markers were all negative; FDG‑PET was obtained primarily to look for occult malignancy and incidentally mapped active myositis throughout his neck muscles—confirming that PET can serve dual purposes in DM.  
*Follow‐up*: Because he met *highrisk* criteria he had a repeat FDG‑PET at six months (still negative), followed by annual CTs up to five years while remaining on immunosuppression. His gastrointestinal tract is now slated for both upper endoscopy and colonoscopy despite no GI symptoms—an approach aligned with IMACS recommendations given his male sex and anti‑SAE1 status.  

---

## Red Flags That Demand an Immediate Reassessment  

Even after an exhaustive baseline workup you must stay vigilant. Prompt reimaging is warranted if any of these develop:

* Unexplained weight loss >5 % body weight over <6 months  
* New night sweats or persistent fever  
* Rapidly progressive dysphagia or odynophagia unrelated to known myositis activity  
* Palpable lymphadenopathy or new cutaneous nodules/masses  
* Sudden rise in inflammatory markers out of proportion to muscle disease control  
* Radiographic changes suggestive of new solid organ lesions on routine follow-up scans  

When any red flag surfaces, jump straight to a contrast-enhanced CT followed by targeted PET if lesions are equivocal—don’t wait for the next scheduled annual scan.  

---

## Balancing Thoroughness With Resources – When Is PET Worth It?  

FDG-PET delivers whole‐body metabolic imaging in one study but comes with higher cost and radiation dose than plain CT. The IMACS framework suggests reserving PET primarily for:

1. **High‐risk patients** where standard CT is either negative but suspicion remains high OR where you want simultaneous assessment of inflammatory burden.
2. **Equivocal findings** on CT that need functional clarification.
3. **Patients where radiation exposure from repeated CT is a concern**, such as younger adults—PET may replace multiple regionally focused CTs.

In low-to-intermediate risk individuals an initial contrast CT plus age-appropriate endoscopic workups usually suffice. 

---

## Take Home Messages – Your Checklist for DM Cancer Surveillance  

- **Risk Stratify Every DM Patient** using clinical features + myositis antibody profile; anti‑SAE1 adds an intermediate point toward “high” risk when combined with dysphagia or male sex.  
- **Baseline Imaging:** For intermediate/high risk obtain contrast CT chest/abdomen/pelvis; add FDG-PET/CT for high-risk patients or inconclusive CT findings.  
- **Age‑Appropriate Endoscopies & Screening:** Colonoscopy ≥50 y (or earlier per family history), upper endoscopy if GI symptoms/risk factors present; add mammography/PAP/PSA as indicated by gender/age.  
- **Follow-Up Schedule:** High-risk → repeat cross-sectional imaging at 6 months then annually ×3–5 years; intermediate → repeat at 12 months then only if red flags appear; low → routine age-specific screens only.  
- **Red Flag Vigilance:** New weight loss, night sweats, progressive dysphagia or unexplained lab spikes should trigger immediate imaging regardless of scheduled interval.  
- **Resource Stewardship:** Reserve FDG-PET for high-risk cases or equivocal CTs—most intermediate patients can be safely followed with serial contrast CTs plus standard cancer screens.

By threading these steps into your routine workflow you’ll catch occult malignancies early while avoiding unnecessary radiation and expense—a win–win that aligns perfectly with the FOAM spirit: share knowledge openly so every clinician can practice smarter!
