Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Clinical Pearls] How should CA-125 be interpreted during pregnancy: what are the expected trimester-specific trends, common benign causes of elevation (for example, endometriosis, leiomyoma, infection, menstruation), and why are serial measurements more informative than a single value when malignancy is a concern?

Direct answer: CA‑125 is of limited specificity in pregnancy and must be interpreted in the context of gestational age, symptoms and imaging. Pregnancy produces physiologic changes in CA‑125 (peaks in the first and third trimesters), and many benign gynecologic and inflammatory conditions commonly raise CA‑125; therefore a single mildly elevated value should not be taken as evidence of malignancy. Serial measurements (and correlation with ultrasound/MRI and the clinical course) are far more informative for distinguishing benign, transient or inflammatory causes from a progressive malignant process.

Why — trimester‑specific trends
- Population studies show a biphasic pattern in pregnancy: a relative peak in the first trimester (median ≈23 U/mL), lower values in the second trimester (median ≈14 U/mL) and a smaller rise again in the third trimester (median ≈21 U/mL). These medians are below the conventional nonpregnant ovarian‑cancer cut‑off (≥35 U/mL), but physiologic elevations do occur, especially early and late in pregnancy.
- Thus a value that would be abnormal outside pregnancy may be within the expected physiologic range for a given trimester, and the magnitude of any elevation must be judged against trimester norms.

Common benign causes of CA‑125 elevation and mechanisms
- Decidualised endometriosis/endometrioma and adenomyosis: CA‑125 is produced by coelomic/Müllerian epithelium; active endometriotic tissue or decidualisation increases serum levels.
- Uterine leiomyomas (especially degenerating, necrotic or torsed pedunculated myomas): myoma degeneration or torsion causes local inflammation and peritoneal irritation that raise CA‑125.
- Menstruation: transient peritoneal irritation during menses raises CA‑125.
- Pelvic or systemic infection/inflammation (PID, pelvic abscess, urinary infection with peritoneal reaction, peritonitis): inflammatory cytokines and mesothelial activation increase CA‑125.
- Non‑gynecologic causes: pleural disease, pericarditis and other serosal inflammation.
In short, any process that irritates serosal or Müllerian epithelium can raise CA‑125.

Why serial measurements are more informative than a single value
- Overlap: CA‑125 values for benign conditions (including normal pregnancy) and for early malignancy overlap substantially. A single mildly elevated value (for example the patient’s 41.8 U/mL in the second trimester) cannot reliably distinguish benign from malignant causes.
- Kinetics matter: malignant ovarian tumors tend to produce a persistent and often faster rise in CA‑125; benign or physiologic elevations are often static or fall over time as the triggering stimulus resolves (for example, after treatment of infection or resolution of torsion/degeneration).
- Clinical correlation: trends should be interpreted together with serial imaging (US/MRI) and the clinical picture (pain, fever, rapid growth, ascites, adenopathy). Algorithms developed for nonpregnant women (RMI, ROMA) are not validated in pregnancy.
- Practical consequence illustrated by this case: the patient’s CA‑125 rose from 36 to 41.8 U/mL over two weeks—an incremental rise that raised concern but was non‑diagnostic. The decision to operate was driven by symptoms, imaging features (large vascularised mixed cystic/solid masses, unclear cleavage), and risk of acute complications. Final pathology showed ischemic, degenerating/torsed leiomyoma (a benign explanation for the CA‑125 rise), underscoring that a single elevated value would have been misleading without serial data and clinical/imaging context.

Clinical pearl (takeaway)
- Always interpret CA‑125 in pregnancy by trimester and in the context of the patient’s symptoms and imaging. Mild elevations are common and nonspecific; serial CA‑125 measurements (short interval, paired with repeat ultrasound/MRI when indicated) help distinguish transient inflammatory or physiologic elevations from a progressive pattern more suggestive of malignancy, and thus avoid unnecessary interventions while allowing timely surgery when clinical or imaging features mandate it.
