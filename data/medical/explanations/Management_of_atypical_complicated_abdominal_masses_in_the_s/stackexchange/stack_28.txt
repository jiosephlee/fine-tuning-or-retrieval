### [Management] During exploration of a large cystic abdominal mass with uncertain origin in a pregnant patient, what intraoperative steps (e.g., peritoneal washings, identification of pedicle, assessment of uterine continuity) help establish diagnosis while preserving pregnancy?

When you open the abdomen for a large, indeterminate cystic mass in a pregnant patient your goal is simultaneous diagnosis and maximum preservation of the pregnancy — shorter, safer, definitive steps that avoid unnecessary hysterectomy or cavalrous exploration.

Start with targeted sampling and documentation: inspect and record fetal heart activity before incision and continue intermittent auscultation or Doppler monitoring intra‑ and immediately post‑op; obtain peritoneal washings for cytology and microbiology early (before manipulating the mass) so you do not contaminate specimens. Systematically explore the abdomen to identify organ of origin — follow the mass to its base. Demonstrating a discrete pedicle or stalk arising from the uterine serosa, ovarian surface, or mesentery is the single most useful intraoperative diagnostic finding; if a pedicle is present, clamp, ligate and divide it with care, control bleeding promptly, and close the uterine or serosal defect with absorbable sutures to restore uterine continuity. If the lesion is clearly contiguous with myometrium (no discrete stalk) or inseparable from uterine wall, avoid aggressive excision that would enter the endometrial cavity or substantially weaken the uterus; leave such intramural masses in situ unless they are causing life‑threatening complications. Avoid cyst rupture and spillage when malignancy cannot be excluded.

Obtain targeted specimens for histology — excise only what you need for diagnosis (the pedunculated mass, biopsy of suspicious solid areas) rather than wide resection; send fresh tissue for frozen section only if the result would change immediate management and pathology support is available. Minimize uterine manipulation and operative time, use meticulous hemostasis (topical agents or uterotonic measures as indicated), and employ prophylactic measures to reduce preterm labor risk (continuous fetal monitoring, and center‑specific tocolaxis or progesterone protocols when indicated). Finally, document the anatomic relationship of residual masses and counsel that staged postpartum surgery is often safer if the pregnancy can be preserved.

These steps — peritoneal washings before manipulation, careful identification of a pedicle, limited excision with secure closure of the uterine defect, avoidance of cavity entry or extensive myometrial resection, targeted tissue sampling (with frozen section only when necessary), fetal monitoring, and minimization of operative time and uterine trauma — together maximize diagnostic yield while protecting the pregnancy.
