Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Differential Diagnosis] A pregnant patient at 22 weeks presents with a large unilocular cystic mass adjacent to the gravid uterus and mild CA-125 elevation. What are the leading diagnostic considerations (for example, decidualized endometrioma, cystic degeneration of a uterine leiomyoma, epithelial ovarian neoplasm), and which clinical or imaging features push you toward one diagnosis versus another?

Direct answer: The leading considerations for a large unilocular cystic mass adjacent to a gravid uterus at 22 weeks are (1) decidualized ovarian endometrioma, (2) cystic (degenerating or red‑degenerating) uterine leiomyoma (including pedunculated/subserosal myoma), and (3) a cystic epithelial ovarian neoplasm. Distinguishing features come from history/exam, ultrasound/Doppler, non‑contrast MRI signal patterns, CA‑125 magnitude/trend, and dynamic behavior (pain, fever, torsion).

How to weigh each diagnosis (practical discriminators, with reference to the case):

1) Decidualized endometrioma
- What favors it:
  - Typical patient history of endometriosis (may be absent).
  - Ultrasound: unilocular cyst with internal low‑level echoes and a vascularized mural nodule that appears “polypoid.”
  - MRI: intrinsic T1 hyperintensity from blood products and the classic “T2 shading” (progressive T2 hypointensity) for chronic blood; mural decidual nodules can be T2 hyperintense and may enhance (gadolinium is usually avoided in pregnancy).
  - CA‑125 can be mildly elevated (but is nonspecific in pregnancy).
- What argues against it in this case:
  - MRI and US showed a mass that appeared continuous with the uterus (especially the caudal lesion), and no prior history of endometriosis reported.
  - The clinical picture of acute pain, low‑grade fever and intraoperative finding of a pedicle/torsion are more typical of a uterine myoma complication.

2) Cystic or degenerating uterine leiomyoma (including pedunculated/subserosal)
- What favors it:
  - Bimanual exam and imaging showing contiguity with the uterus or a mass at the uterine surface; presence of a connecting stalk or a “bridging vessel” on Doppler strongly supports uterine origin.
  - MRI: leiomyomas classically low T2 signal, but cystic or red degeneration produces inhomogeneous fluid signal (high T2), thick walls, areas of hemorrhage/necrosis, and lack of fatty/blood‑product pattern specific to endometrioma.
  - Clinical: acute, localized pain, low‑grade fever, anemia and tenderness are classic for red degeneration or torsion of a pedunculated fibroid.
  - Perioperative confirmation: pedunculated, highly vascular mass with twisted stalk—as was found in this case.
- In the case: the caudal mass had clear continuity with a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma on MRI, both masses were highly vascularized, the patient had acute pain and fever, and surgery demonstrated a pedunculated fibroid with torsion and ischemic necrosis—all pointing to leiomyoma with complication (cystic/red degeneration and pedicle torsion).

3) Cystic epithelial ovarian neoplasm
- What favors it:
  - Complex cystic mass with thick irregular septations, papillary excrescences or nodular solid components on US/MRI; bilateral adnexal involvement, ascites, peritoneal implants or adenopathy increase suspicion.
  - Markedly elevated CA‑125 (though pregnancy raises CA‑125 and moderates specificity).
  - Lack of clear continuity with the uterus on exam or imaging; an ovary separate from the uterus on transvaginal sonography.
- What argues against it in this case:
  - No ascites or adenopathy on MRI, tumour markers other than CA‑125 were normal, and MRI suggested uterine contiguity for the lower mass; the upper mass turned out intraoperatively to be pedunculated from the uterus. CA‑125 was only mildly elevated (41.8 U/mL) and rising modestly—nonspecific in pregnancy.

Practical imaging and exam tips to discriminate:
- Localize origin first: transvaginal/transabdominal ultrasound with careful search for a connecting stalk or “bridging vessel” to the myometrium; a mass that moves with the uterus or deforms uterine contour suggests uterine origin.
- Doppler: vascularized mural nodules occur both in decidualized endometriomas and in complicated fibroids; pattern and relation to uterus are key.
- MRI without gadolinium in pregnancy: T1 hyperintensity that persists on fat‑sat sequences → blood product (endometrioma or hemorrhagic degeneration); T2 shading → endometrioma; heterogeneous high T2 with thick walls, areas of hemorrhage/necrosis and loss of normal low‑T2 fibroid signal → cystic or red‑degenerating leiomyoma. Continuity with myometrium on MRI favors leiomyoma.
- CA‑125: interpret cautiously in pregnancy—mild elevations are common; trending and magnitude help but are not definitive.

Bottom line applied to the case: the mix of acute pain, fever, anemia, imaging evidence of uterine continuity (caudal lesion), marked vascularity and the intraoperative finding of a twisted pedunculated mass all point to uterine leiomyomas with cystic/red degeneration and torsion rather than decidualized endometrioma or ovarian malignancy. Surgical intervention was justified by symptom severity and risk of acute complication.
