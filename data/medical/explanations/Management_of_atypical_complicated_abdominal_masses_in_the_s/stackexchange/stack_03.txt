### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiologic mechanisms of cystic degeneration and torsion in subserosal pedunculated uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy and how these processes produce pain, low-grade fever, and inflammatory markers.

During pregnancy leiomyomas are exposed to a hormonal and hemodynamic milieu that predisposes them to both degenerative change and mechanical complications. The explanation for the findings in this case rests on two linked pathophysiologic processes: (1) growth-related ischemic degeneration of a myoma and (2) torsion of a pedunculated subserosal myoma with subsequent ischemia/necrosis and peritoneal inflammation.

Pregnancy increases circulating estrogen, progesterone and uterine blood flow, which commonly cause leiomyomas to enlarge. Rapid enlargement — especially of a subserosal nodule that projects on a narrow stalk — can outstrip the lesion’s vascular supply. When perfusion is inadequate relative to size or when venous/lymphatic outflow is impaired, the myoma undergoes ischemic change. That process may progress to red (hemorrhagic) or cystic degeneration as smooth muscle cells die, intratumoral extracellular fluid accumulates, and hemorrhage and liquefaction occur. On MRI and ultrasound this produces inhomogeneous, hyperintense T1/T2 signal and unilocular cystic appearance, as seen here.

A pedunculated subserosal myoma is mechanically vulnerable to torsion about its stalk. Torsion first obstructs venous and lymphatic drainage, causing congestion and swelling, then arterial inflow becomes compromised and true ischemic infarction follows. Ischemia and secondary hemorrhage breach tissue integrity, and necrotic debris and blood products can leak into the peritoneal cavity.

Those tissue events explain the clinical syndrome. Acute ischemia/necrosis produces sudden, localized visceral and somatic pain from stretch of the tumor capsule and irritation of adjacent peritoneum. Necrotic tissue and blood are potent triggers of innate immunity: damaged cells release damage‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that activate resident macrophages and mesothelial cells to secrete cytokines (IL‑1, IL‑6, TNF‑α). Cytokine signaling raises the hypothalamic set point (low‑grade fever) and stimulates hepatic synthesis of acute‑phase reactants (CRP) and bone‑marrow neutrophil mobilization with relative leukocytosis. Peritoneal irritation and increased production of glycoproteins from mesothelial cells also raise CA‑125 modestly. These inflammatory signals can mimic or coexist with infection; torsion with necrosis can be aseptic yet produce fever and elevated inflammatory markers identical to early pelvic infection.

In this patient the intraoperative finding of a twisted, vascularized pedunculated leiomyoma with adjacent ischemic necrosis explains the acute pain and low‑grade fever and accounts for the modest CRP and neutrophilia. The concurrent urinary Klebsiella infection may have contributed to the systemic inflammatory response, but the local peritoneal inflammation and tissue necrosis from torsion were sufficient to produce the full constellation of signs and imaging abnormalities observed. Clinically, the distinction matters because degeneration/torsion is a surgical indication when symptoms or mass effect are severe, whereas uncomplicated degeneration can often be managed expectantly.
