### [Management] What perioperative strategies (team composition, incision choice, operative time minimisation, haemostasis planning, fetal assessment protocol) are key to safely performing mid-trimester myomectomy when indicated?

When mid‑trimester myomectomy is being considered, the intra‑operative plan must prioritise maternal haemodynamic stability and uteroplacental perfusion while keeping the operation as short and blood‑sparing as possible. The strategy used in the presented case illustrates the key principles.

Assemble a multidisciplinary team beforehand: an experienced obstetric surgeon comfortable with myomectomy and uterine reconstruction, anaesthesiology with obstetric expertise, a neonatologist available (even if preterm delivery is unlikely), interventional radiology on call if uterine artery embolisation or balloon occlusion might be needed, and immediate access to blood‑bank and cell‑salvage. Nursing and instrumentation staff should be briefed and primed for rapid instrument and transfusion needs.

Choose an incision that gives rapid, extensible exposure and minimises uterine manipulation — a vertical midline (xipho‑pubic) incision is usually preferred in the mid‑trimester for very large or multiple lesions because it allows quick access, easy extension and better vascular control. Smaller lesions in favourable positions can be approached via Pfannenstiel or laparoscopy, but not when exposure, haemostasis or foetal monitoring would be compromised.

Minimise operative time by meticulous preoperative planning: review high‑quality ultrasound/MRI to define which lesions require removal and which can safely be left in situ, map vascular pedicles, rehearse the steps with the team, have sutures and haemostatic agents ready, and limit the operation to the therapeutic minimum (remove only the lesion(s) causing symptoms or suspected malignancy). Use the most experienced surgeon available to shorten operative and anaesthesia time; avoid prolonged, radical dissections that add little benefit and increase risk of bleeding and uterine irritability.

Plan haemostasis proactively rather than reactively. Anticipate heavy vascularity in pregnant uteri: use temporary measures (double clamps on pedicles, uterine tourniquet or vessel loops when anatomically feasible) and meticulous, layered uterine closure with absorbable sutures. Have advanced haemostatic tools ready — vascular clamps, bipolar energy, vessel‑sealing devices, topical haemostatic agents, oxidised cellulose, and sutures for uterine artery ligation if needed. Ensure adequate cross‑matched blood and consider intraoperative cell salvage (with appropriate filters) in centres where available and acceptable. Avoid routine use of systemic agents that might cause uterine tetany before delivery; uterotonics are reserved for the postpartum setting or after delivery.

Protect the fetus through perioperative fetal assessment and obstetric readiness. Document fetal heart activity before incision, arrange for intermittent or continuous fetal heart rate assessment during the case when feasible (Doppler or transabdominal monitoring), and document immediately after the procedure. Assign a dedicated obstetrical provider or sonographer to monitor fetal status so the surgical team can focus on haemostasis. Have a plan and thresholds for conversion to urgent delivery (gestational age, non‑reassuring tracing and maternal instability) and ensure neonatology presence if there is any realistic chance of delivery. Consider pharmacologic measures to reduce uterine irritability when indicated: perioperative progesterone or tocolysis can be considered on a case‑by‑case basis per institutional protocol, recognising evidence is limited. Maintain maternal oxygenation, normotension and euvolemia in collaboration with anaesthesia to preserve uteroplacental perfusion.

In short: multidisciplinary planning, an incision that permits rapid exposure, a tightly scripted, time‑efficient operative plan that removes only what is necessary, proactive haemostasis with blood products immediately available, and continuous attention to fetal monitoring and obstetric contingencies are the elements that make mid‑trimester myomectomy as safe as possible.
