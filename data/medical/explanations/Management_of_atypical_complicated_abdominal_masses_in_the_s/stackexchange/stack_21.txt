### [Clinical Pearls] What intraoperative techniques reduce bleeding risk during myomectomy in pregnancy (e.g., vasoconstrictors, uterotonics timing, devascularisation, suturing strategies), and which are contraindicated?

Key principle: reduce blood loss by minimizing uterine blood flow and promoting local hemostasis while avoiding interventions that provoke sustained uterine contraction or placental/fetal hypoperfusion. In pregnancy the margin for error is smaller — techniques that are routine in non‑pregnant myomectomy must be adapted for fetal safety.

Practical intraoperative strategy (what to do)
Start with optimisation before the cut: correct anemia if possible; have cross‑matched blood and cell‑salvage available; continuous fetal heart monitoring when gestation and logistics allow.

Mechanical devascularisation is the cornerstone. For a pedunculated myoma, clamp and ligate the pedicle early and remove the mass quickly; that alone often controls bleeding. For intramural lesions, temporary reduction of uterine inflow with a uterine tourniquet (Rubber or Foley tourniquet around the lower uterine segment) or bilateral temporary uterine artery occlusion (clips or vascular clamps) is effective and reversible — it lowers blood loss without causing systemic maternal vasospasm. Permanent internal iliac or uterine artery ligation can be reserved for uncontrolled hemorrhage but is more morbid and may compromise future uterine perfusion.

Local vasoconstriction: dilute vasopressin injected into the myometrium around the myoma (small volumes, low concentration) is commonly used to reduce bleeding. In pregnancy it must be used cautiously because systemic absorption can cause maternal hypertension, bradycardia and reflex uterine vasospasm with fetal heart rate changes; therefore use the lowest effective dose, aspirate before injection, monitor maternal vitals and fetal heart rate, and have anaesthesia ready to treat hemodynamic events.

Energy and topical adjuncts: careful bipolar electrocautery for focal coagulation is preferable to wide, high‑energy diathermy; excessive thermal spread risks myometrial damage. Topical haemostatic agents (oxidized regenerated cellulose, gelatin sponges, fibrin sealants) are useful adjuncts, particularly in oozing surfaces or when avoiding deep sutures.

Suturing technique for durable hemostasis and uterine integrity: multilayer closure of the myometrial defect with full‑thickness, delayed‑absorbable sutures to obliterate dead space and re‑approximate contractile tissue reduces bleeding and lowers risk of postoperative uterine rupture. Deep interrupted figure‑of‑eight or mattress sutures for the myoma bed followed by an outer seromuscular layer is standard. Avoid excessive intramural suture mass that compromises blood supply. Tie sutures securely but avoid very tight strangulation of tissue.

Adjuncts and haemostatic measures: topical tranexamic acid locally or systemically may reduce bleeding; systemic tranexamic acid is acceptable when needed. In centers with experience, intraoperative cell salvage with leukocyte‑depletion filters is reasonable in obstetric/myoma surgery.

What to avoid (contraindications or approaches to use with caution)
Avoid giving uterotonics (oxytocin bolus, methylergometrine, prostaglandins — carboprost, misoprostol) before the fetus is delivered or in cases where the uterus must remain relaxed; these agents cause strong uterine contraction and can precipitate fetal hypoxia, placental abruption, or compromise the surgical field by rapid contraction and tearing. Ergot alkaloids and prostaglandins are particularly contraindicated intraoperatively in a pregnant uterus unless the fetus is delivered or postpartum hemorrhage mandates their use.

Systemic high‑dose vasoconstrictors (eg, large boluses of vasopressin, terlipressin, unmonitored epinephrine) are risky because of maternal hypertension and secondary uteroplacental vasospasm; use only small, local, carefully titrated vasopressin with monitoring. Extensive use of unipolar diathermy across the myometrium should be avoided because it can cause thermal injury and impair healing.

Finally, prolonged, vigorous uterine exteriorisation and manipulation should be minimized because they provoke contractions and fetal stress; keep the operation as efficient and expeditious as safe.

Bottom line: in pregnant myomectomy prioritize reversible mechanical devascularisation (tourniquet or temporary uterine artery occlusion), cautious local vasopressin injection with monitoring, meticulous multilayer suturing and topical haemostatic adjuncts. Do not use systemic uterotonics or large systemic vasoconstrictor doses before delivery; avoid excessive thermal injury. Plan the procedure, monitor the fetus, and have blood/blood‑saving strategies ready.
