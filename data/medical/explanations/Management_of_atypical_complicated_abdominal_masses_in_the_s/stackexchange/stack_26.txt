Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Clinical Pearls] Under what limited circumstances might gadolinium-enhanced MRI be considered during pregnancy, and what fetal risks drive the general recommendation to avoid it?

Gadolinium chelates cross the placenta, enter the fetal circulation, and are excreted into and can accumulate in the amniotic fluid; fetal re‑uptake from swallowed amniotic fluid can prolong exposure. Animal and human observational data raise concerns about gadolinium deposition in fetal tissues and an association (in some retrospective series) with higher rates of stillbirth, neonatal death, and rare neonatal/inflammatory or rheumatologic conditions. Because the absolute risk is uncertain but plausibly real, major guidelines advise avoiding gadolinium in pregnancy unless the diagnostic information cannot be obtained by safer means and would materially change management.

In practice, gadolinium‑enhanced MRI in pregnancy should be considered only in tightly limited situations: when non‑contrast MRI and ultrasound are insufficient, when the result is urgent and would alter immediate care for a life‑threatening maternal or fetal condition (for example, characterization or staging of a suspected aggressive maternal malignancy where contrast is necessary to guide therapy, or rare acute surgical dilemmas in which contrast would change operative planning), and when the benefits to the mother (or fetus) clearly outweigh the potential fetal risks. If used, choose a single dose of a macrocyclic agent, document informed consent after discussion of uncertain fetal risks, avoid first‑trimester exposure when feasible, use the lowest field strength consistent with diagnostic needs (commonly 1.5 T), and limit repeat exposures. In most situations—including the case you describe—high‑quality ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI provide adequate information and gadolinium is best avoided.
