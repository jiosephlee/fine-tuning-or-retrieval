Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Differential Diagnosis] What factors during pregnancy increase the risk of misclassifying a large uterine fibroid as an adnexal mass on imaging, and which targeted ultrasound maneuvers can help establish uterine origin?

Teaching point: during pregnancy large uterine leiomyomas often masquerade as adnexal masses because the gravid uterus, the myomas’ degeneration and the patient’s body habitus conspire to break the usual sonographic clues that establish uterine origin. Recognising the specific factors that produce this diagnostic error and using a few targeted ultrasound maneuvers will usually allow you to demonstrate continuity with the myometrium and avoid misclassification.

Why the mistake happens
Large size and “steric bulk” of the pregnant uterus displace normal pelvic anatomy so the uterus, ovaries and adnexa no longer occupy their expected locations. Pedunculated subserosal myomas are particularly liable to look adnexal because a narrow stalk may be invisible on routine scans. Cystic or red‑degeneration, necrosis or haemorrhage change the expected solid appearance of a fibroid and can mimic ovarian cysts or decidualised endometriomas. Posterior or fundal location (and isthmic lesions during uterine enlargement) hides the myometrial cleavage plane. Maternal obesity and an elevated, distended uterus limit acoustic windows. Pregnancy itself raises CA‑125 and causes vascular hypertrophy, which may mimic hypervascular adnexal tumours. Finally, torsion/acute degeneration can alter mobility and vascular patterns and obscure a feeding stalk.

Ultrasound maneuvers that help establish uterine origin
Always look for continuity with the myometrium and for a bridging blood supply before labelling an adnexal mass. Practically, follow the mass in multiple planes with both transabdominal and transvaginal probes and attempt to trace an echogenic band or capsule continuous with the uterine wall. Use the transvaginal probe (high‑frequency) to inspect the uterine serosa–myometrium interface and the posterior uterus when the transabdominal view is limited. Apply graded pressure with the transducer and observe whether the mass moves with the uterus (“moves with uterus” sign) or whether a cleavage plane separates it (sliding sign). Change maternal position or ask for a full bladder to alter organ relationships and reveal continuity. Use colour Doppler to identify bridging vessels arising from the uterine circulation or a visible pedicle (the “bridging vessel” or “pedicle” sign) rather than ovarian arterial supply. If available, 3‑D sonography can help demonstrate the stalk or the interface between mass and myometrium. Correlate the ultrasound findings with careful bimanual pelvic examination: a fixed pelvic mass contiguous with the uterine contour suggests uterine origin.

Clinical principle and implication
If you can demonstrate a myometrial interface or uterine feeding vessels, treat the lesion as a fibroid; if these maneuvers are inconclusive and management hinges on the diagnosis, proceed to non‑contrast MRI or multidisciplinary discussion rather than assuming an adnexal tumour. Establishing uterine origin matters because it changes surveillance, operative planning and delivery decisions in pregnancy.
