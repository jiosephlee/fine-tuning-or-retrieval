Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Morning Report] A pregnant patient with large abdominal masses develops low-grade fever and neutrophilia. How would you differentiate infectious causes (e.g., urinary tract infection) from red degeneration or torsion of a fibroid, and what tests guide you?

Key teaching point: low‑grade fever and neutrophilia in a pregnant patient with large pelvic/abdominal masses can reflect either infection (most commonly a urinary tract infection) or a mass complication (red degeneration, torsion, hemorrhage/necrosis). Distinguishing them relies on clinical pattern, targeted laboratory testing, focused imaging (especially Doppler US and non‑contrast MRI), and early microbiology — not on a single abnormal white count or a mildly elevated CRP or CA‑125, all of which can be altered in pregnancy or by degenerating fibroids.

Clinical reasoning and practical approach
Begin with history and exam aimed at source. UTI typically produces urinary symptoms (dysuria, frequency, urgency), suprapubic tenderness and sometimes low‑grade fever; systemic toxicity is unusual unless pyelonephritis develops. Red degeneration of a fibroid or torsion of a pedunculated myoma classically causes sudden focal severe pain (often localized to the mass), a firm tender adnexal/uterine mass, sometimes low‑grade fever, and progressive focal signs rather than urinary symptoms. Torsion may cause intermittent severe pain if the pedicle twists and untwists; red degeneration is ischemic pain often more constant and associated with localized uterine tenderness and possible localized peritoneal irritation.

Laboratory testing to order and how to interpret
Obtain urinalysis with dipstick and microscopy and a urine culture early. A positive nitrite/leukocyte esterase and pyuria plus a pathogen on culture confirm UTI and explain fever/ neutrophilia. Blood tests should include CBC and inflammatory markers (CRP). Mild leukocytosis and mild CRP elevation can accompany both infection and degeneration and are nonspecific in pregnancy; trends over time matter more than a single value. If febrile or systemically unwell, obtain blood cultures before antibiotics. Measure renal function (to guide antibiotic choice). Tumor markers (CA‑125) are of limited value in pregnancy and should not be relied on to separate infection from degeneration.

Imaging that guides diagnosis
Start with transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound with color Doppler. For torsion of a pedunculated myoma you may see an enlarged, heterogeneous mass with absent or reduced internal blood flow and a twisted pedicle; for red degeneration you may see a heterogeneous, often cystic or inhomogeneous fibroid with peripheral vascularity and internal hemorrhagic changes. Doppler is most useful to demonstrate loss of flow to a mass (suggests torsion/ischemia) versus preserved flow (less likely torsion). If ultrasound is inconclusive, perform a non‑contrast MRI (safe after the first trimester) — red degeneration shows high signal on T1 (hemorrhage) and heterogeneous T2 signal with surrounding edema; torsion/ischemia shows signs of venous congestion, edema, hemorrhage and abrupt change at the pedicle. MRI will also help identify ovarian vs uterine origin of a mass.

When to rely on microbiology or invasive testing
A positive urine culture that correlates clinically is sufficient to treat as UTI. If sepsis is a concern, blood cultures are required. If surgery is undertaken (because imaging suggests torsion, rupture, or the diagnosis remains uncertain with worsening symptoms), obtain intra‑operative peritoneal fluid and tissue cultures and send tissue for pathology; these are definitive for infection versus ischemic necrosis. Persistent fever after appropriate antibiotics or progressive focal signs despite conservative care should lower the threshold for operative exploration.

Management implications
If urinalysis/culture confirm UTI and clinical features point to an uncomplicated urinary source, treat with pregnancy‑safe antibiotics and observe; fever and neutrophilia should improve within 48–72 hours. If imaging shows absent flow or MRI features of ischemia/hemorrhage in a pedunculated mass, or the pain is severe and focal or the patient is deteriorating, surgical management is indicated (myomectomy/detorsion) because ischemia/necrosis risks peritonitis and sepsis; intra‑operative findings and pathology then confirm red degeneration versus torsion. Empiric antibiotics are appropriate if infection is suspected, but do not delay imaging or surgical consultation when torsion/necrosis is plausible.

In short: correlate symptoms (particularly urinary vs focal abdominal), get urinalysis + culture first, obtain targeted labs and blood cultures if febrile, use Doppler ultrasound then non‑contrast MRI to look for loss of vascularity or hemorrhagic changes, and proceed to operative evaluation with intra‑operative cultures and pathology when imaging/clinical course suggests torsion or necrosis or when the patient worsens despite conservative care.
