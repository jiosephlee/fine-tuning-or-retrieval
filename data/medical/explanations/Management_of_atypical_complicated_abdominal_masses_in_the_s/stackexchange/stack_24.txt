### [Management] What is the role, if any, for perioperative tocolytics or progesterone support around non-obstetric surgery in mid-pregnancy, and what does current evidence suggest about efficacy and indications?

Short answer: There is no evidence to support routine administration of perioperative tocolytics or empiric progesterone solely because a pregnant patient undergoes non‑obstetric surgery in mid‑pregnancy. Management should be individualized: monitor the fetus, treat uterine activity or threatened preterm birth if it occurs, and apply established obstetric interventions (tocolysis, antenatal corticosteroids, magnesium neuroprotection, or maternal progesterone) only when their standard obstetric indications are met.

Why: Non‑obstetric abdominal surgery can increase the theoretical risk of uterine irritability and preterm labour, but randomized data are essentially absent and observational series do not demonstrate that prophylactic tocolysis or perioperative progesterone reduces miscarriage or preterm birth after surgery. Professional guidance (obstetric society statements and reviews of the literature) therefore does not recommend routine prophylactic tocolytics or routine perioperative progesterone for all pregnant patients undergoing surgery.

How this translates into practice (clinical reasoning): before surgery, assemble a multidisciplinary team (surgery, obstetrics/MFM, anesthesia, neonatology). Document fetal heart activity and gestational age. During and after the procedure, monitor for uterine contractions and signs of preterm labour. If contractions consistent with threatened preterm labour occur, treat them according to obstetric principles — calcium‑channel blockers (nifedipine) are commonly preferred over beta‑agonists because of a better side‑effect profile; avoid agents that carry disproportionate maternal risks in the perioperative setting. If preterm delivery is imminent (per standard obstetric criteria), give antenatal corticosteroids for fetal lung maturation and consider magnesium sulfate for neuroprotection when appropriate. Use progesterone prophylaxis only when the patient already meets established indications (prior spontaneous preterm birth, short cervix), not merely because of surgery.

Safety caveats: prophylactic tocolytics are not benign — beta‑agonists can cause maternal tachycardia, hyperglycaemia and pulmonary oedema (especially with intravenous fluids/oxytocics); nifedipine can cause hypotension; atosiban is not universally available. Progesterone (vaginal or intramuscular) is generally well tolerated but its efficacy in preventing surgery‑related preterm birth is unproven; using it for reassurance is common in some centers but should be framed as empiric rather than evidence‑based.

Applying this to the reported case: the team’s decision to monitor fetal heart rate intra‑/postoperatively and reserve interventions for signs of uterine activity was appropriate. The intraoperative and prolonged intramuscular progesterone the team administered reflects a pragmatic, prophylactic approach used by some centers, but it should be recognized as lacking high‑quality evidence for preventing surgery‑related preterm birth. The clearer, evidence‑based actions in such cases are careful fetal surveillance, prompt treatment of documented contractions, and preparation to give steroids and neonatal support if preterm delivery becomes likely.
