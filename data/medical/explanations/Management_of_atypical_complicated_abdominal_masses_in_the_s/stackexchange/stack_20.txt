Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Management] A 7–9 cm posterior intramural–subserosal fibroid occupies the lower uterine segment at term. How would you plan delivery (route, timing, uterine incision strategy), and what intrapartum risks require preparation?

You have a term patient with a 7–9 cm posterior intramural–subserosal fibroid occupying the lower uterine segment. The practical plan is guided by whether the leiomyoma actually obstructs the birth canal and by the balance between avoiding hemorrhage and achieving safe delivery.

Route and timing
If the fibroid materially occupies the lower uterine segment and would block descent (as in your scenario), plan an elective cesarean delivery at term (≥37 weeks) rather than expectant trial of labour. Elective timing minimizes the chance of intrapartum emergency, allows optimal staffing and blood product availability, and avoids obstructed labour and fetal compromise from an unplanned arrest. If fetal or maternal indications require earlier delivery, proceed when risks of continuation outweigh prematurity.

Uterine incision strategy
Aim for a lower uterine segment transverse (LUS) incision if feasible because it carries the lowest maternal morbidity and the best future-uterine-rupture profile. Pre‑operative imaging and palpation at operation determine feasibility. If the fibroid lies directly beneath the intended LUS incision and cannot be shifted aside, options (in order of preference) are: 1) make the transverse incision through the thinnest area of lower segment lateral to the fibroid; 2) extend the transverse incision laterally (J or T) to deliver the fetus without cutting deeply into leiomyoma; 3) if the LUS is obliterated by the myoma, consider a low vertical uterine incision (classical isthmic/low vertical) to avoid transecting the fibroid; reserve a classical (midline upper-segment vertical) incision only if lower-segment and low-vertical incisions are impossible because classical incisions increase hemorrhage and future rupture risk. If a subserosal or pedunculated fibroid is encountered at delivery, selective myomectomy at the time of cesarean can be considered by an experienced team; avoid routine enucleation of a firmly intramural lower-segment fibroid because of high hemorrhage risk. In short: prefer LUS transverse; if obstructed, choose a low-vertical or alternative incision rather than cutting blindly through a vascular myoma.

Intrapartum preparations and risks to anticipate
Anticipate and prepare for significant hemorrhage. Ensure preoperative cross‑matched blood available, large-bore IV access, cell salvage if available, tranexamic acid on hand, uterotonics, uterine compression sutures, balloon tamponade, and readiness for peripartum hysterectomy. Position senior obstetrician and anesthesiologist present, and involve gynaecologic surgeons and interventional radiology on standby if possible. Expect difficult access to the lower uterine segment, possible need for extension of skin/uterine incision, prolonged operative time, and higher likelihood of postpartum hemorrhage from atony or from bleeding at the myoma bed if inadvertently incised. Be prepared for retained‑placenta issues or placenta delivered from an altered lower segment. Counsel and document in advance the potential need for intraoperative myomectomy versus deferral, and the small but real possibility of hysterectomy.

Broader principle
Plan delivery based on mechanical obstruction and safety rather than fibroid size alone. Preserve the lower-segment transverse approach when possible to minimize future rupture risk; when the lower segment is effectively obliterated by the myoma, choose the uterine incision that achieves safe fetal extraction and hemorrhage control, with full preparation for massive obstetric hemorrhage.
