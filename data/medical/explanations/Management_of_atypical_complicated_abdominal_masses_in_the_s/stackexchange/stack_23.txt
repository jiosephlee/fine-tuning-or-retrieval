Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Differential Diagnosis] What MRI features support ovarian torsion (e.g., twisted vascular pedicle, ovarian enlargement, stromal oedema) and how do these differ from torsion of a pedunculated uterine fibroid?

Key MRI clues that point to ovarian torsion are related to the ovary’s change in size, internal structure and vascular supply; in contrast, torsion of a pedunculated subserosal fibroid shows continuity with the uterus and tissue characteristics of a leiomyoma rather than an adnexal organ. In practice you must combine imaging signs with the clinical picture (sudden or progressive unilateral pain, nausea/fever, leukocytosis) and remember that Doppler and contrast enhancement may be misleading or unavailable in pregnancy.

For ovarian torsion, MRI features that support the diagnosis are an enlarged ovary (often >4 cm) with stromal edema producing increased T2 signal centrally, multiple small peripheral follicles displaced to the rim (“follicular peripheralization”), and heterogeneous ovarian signal if haemorrhage or necrosis is present (T1 hyperintense areas for blood). The twisted vascular pedicle may be seen as the “whirlpool” or beaded tubular structure of engorged, spiraled vessels extending from the adnexa; associated findings include peri‑ovarian and pelvic free fluid and lack of normal venous/lymphatic drainage signs. On non‑contrast studies you may infer vascular compromise by progressive stromal T2 hyperintensity, T1 hyperintensity from haemorrhage, and progressive heterogeneity. Importantly, arterial flow on Doppler (or preserved flow on MRI vascular sequences) does not exclude torsion because venous outflow obstruction can precede arterial compromise.

For torsion of a pedunculated uterine fibroid, MRI shows a mass contiguous with the uterine serosa with a visible or inferable stalk or “bridging vessels” to the uterus; the lesion’s internal signal reflects leiomyoma tissue (typically low T2 signal for compact fibroid) but may show high T2 signal or cystic areas if there is red/cystic degeneration. A torsed pedunculated myoma demonstrates acute ischemic change: central or diffuse high T2 signal from oedema, heterogeneous high T1 if haemorrhagic necrosis is present, and peripheral or patchy internal heterogeneity. Crucially, the ovary is typically separate and identifiable — you will not see peripheralized ovarian follicles within the mass. In pregnancy, you cannot rely on gadolinium to show nonenhancement, so continuity with the uterine wall/stalk and identification of feeding vessels are the most helpful differentiators.

Put simply: an enlarged, edematous adnexal structure with peripheral follicles and a whirlpool vascular pedicle = ovarian torsion; a mass anatomically contiguous with the uterus, showing leiomyomatous signal characteristics (with secondary degeneration/necrosis) and a pedicle or bridging vessels to the uterine serosa = torsion of a pedunculated fibroid. Always synthesize these MRI signs with the ultrasound data and clinical course, because imaging can be equivocal in pregnancy and management hinges on symptoms and maternal–fetal risk.
