### [Pathophysiology] Why can large uterine leiomyomas in pregnancy mimic adnexal cystic masses on ultrasound, and how does uterine enlargement alter the perceived anatomic relationships?

Large uterine leiomyomas — especially subserosal or pedunculated ones that undergo cystic degeneration — can look like adnexal cysts on sonography because the imaging features and the anatomic relationships that normally let us tell “uterine” from “ovarian” are distorted.

Pathophysiology and imaging appearance: leiomyomas commonly outgrow their blood supply in pregnancy and develop hyaline/cystic degeneration and areas of necrosis. Degenerated leiomyomas can become unilocular, fluid‑filled, and avascular or rim‑vascularized on Doppler — features that overlap with ovarian cysts (including decidualized endometriomas). A pedunculated subserosal myoma may be attached to the uterine surface by a narrow stalk; if it twists or enlarges, the stalk can be thin or obscured and the tumor looks like an isolated adnexal mass. Thickened walls, internal septations or solid nodules from degeneration or infarction can further mimic complex ovarian lesions.

How pregnancy alters perceived anatomy: the gravid uterus enlarges, rising out of the pelvis and changing the spatial relationships of pelvic organs. The ovaries and adnexa are displaced laterally, superiorly or posteriorly; the pouch of Douglas and lateral gutters become occupied by shifted uterine tissue or by large masses. The stretched peritoneal reflections, overlying bowel, and increased abdominal fat or uterine contour can obscure the normal uterine outline and the uterine–ovarian cleavage plane on ultrasound, so a uterine mass may appear separable from the uterus. Large masses also displace vascular pedicles and feeding vessels, making it harder to demonstrate continuity with the myometrium on colour Doppler. Finally, gravid myometrial and decidual changes (and the physiologic rise in CA‑125) confound tumor markers and imaging interpretation.

Clinical implications: when a large complex “adnexal” mass is found in pregnancy, always think first about a uterine origin. Correlate with bimanual exam (fixed pelvic mass, relation to cervix/uterus), look carefully for a stalk or myometrial continuity on multiple ultrasound planes and with colour Doppler, and use non‑contrast MRI when US is equivocal because MRI better shows myometrial continuity and the distance between lesion and endometrial cavity. If imaging remains uncertain, manage according to symptoms and complication risk — conservative observation when stable, and surgery reserved for torsion, rupture, infection, or severe mass effect.
