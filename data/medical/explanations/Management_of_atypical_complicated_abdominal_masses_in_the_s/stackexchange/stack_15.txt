Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Management] Which antibiotic regimens are appropriate in pregnancy for a culture-proven Klebsiella urinary tract infection, and how do you coordinate perioperative prophylaxis if abdominal surgery is planned?

In a pregnant patient with a culture‑proven Klebsiella urinary tract infection you treat based on the organism’s susceptibility while prioritising antibiotics known to be safe in pregnancy and using an adequate duration for a complicated UTI. Beta‑lactams are first‑line: penicillins and cephalosporins (for example amoxicillin/clavulanate if susceptible, or a second/third‑generation cephalosporin such as cefuroxime/ceftriaxone when Klebsiella is sensitive) are effective, bactericidal, and well‑tolerated in pregnancy. Nitrofurantoin is an option for lower UTI in pregnancy (avoid near term because of neonatal haemolysis risk) but is less reliable for complicated infections or systemic disease. Aminoglycosides (e.g., gentamicin) can be used for Gram‑negative coverage when necessary but carry fetal/neonatal ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity risks and are usually reserved for severe infections where alternatives are unsuitable. Fluoroquinolones and trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole are generally avoided in pregnancy (trimethoprim in the first trimester and near term; TMP‑SMX also increases neonatal kernicterus risk). Macrolides: azithromycin is preferred when macrolide therapy is required; clarithromycin has less favourable pregnancy safety data and is not preferred.

Practical approach for this case and perioperative coordination
1) Base inpatient therapy on the antibiogram. If Klebsiella is highly susceptible to cephalosporins, IV ceftriaxone (1–2 g daily) or cefuroxime is an appropriate systemic choice for a complicated UTI in pregnancy. Duration is typically at least 7–14 days depending on clinical response, imaging, and whether bacteremia or urologic source control issues exist. Obtain a test‑of‑cure urine culture before stopping therapy when feasible.

2) Perioperative prophylaxis for abdominal surgery in pregnancy: standard single‑dose IV cefazolin given within 60 minutes before skin incision (2 g; 3 g if patient weight ≥120 kg) is the recommended prophylactic regimen for most abdominal and gynecologic procedures because it covers common skin organisms and many Enterobacterales. If the patient has a known urinary isolate of Klebsiella that is cephalosporin‑sensitive, cefazolin provides reasonable perioperative coverage; if the isolate requires a broader agent (for example an extended‑spectrum beta‑lactamase producer), tailor prophylaxis to include an agent active against the isolate (eg, ertapenem perioperatively for ESBL organisms) in consultation with microbiology/infectious diseases.

3) Timing and sterilisation: for elective surgery it is ideal to sterilise the urine preoperatively (document a negative urine culture after appropriate therapy). If surgery cannot be delayed, give appropriate IV, bactericidal, culture‑directed antibiotics at induction and continue post‑operatively as indicated until cultures are controlled and the planned treatment course is complete.

4) Allergy and alternatives: in severe beta‑lactam allergy, alternative prophylaxis should be chosen based on susceptibility and pregnancy safety (vancomycin plus aztreonam or clindamycin plus gentamicin combinations, with ID input).

5) Safety monitoring and teamwork: coordinate with obstetrics, anaesthesia and ID/microbiology. Monitor maternal response, fetal status (antepartum fetal heart monitoring when indicated during and after surgery), and obtain follow‑up cultures to ensure eradication.

Comment on the regimen reported in the case: perioperative IV cefazolin 2 g given before incision is appropriate. Continuing culture‑directed IV cephalosporin post‑op is reasonable for a cephalosporin‑sensitive Klebsiella. Use of clarithromycin is suboptimal because clarithromycin has less favourable pregnancy safety data and is not needed for Klebsiella; azithromycin would be the preferred macrolide if a macrolide were required.
