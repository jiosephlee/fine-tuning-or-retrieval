### [Morning Report] Imaging cannot identify a feeding vessel or stalk for a massive upper abdominal cystic mass in a 21-week pregnancy. How should this diagnostic uncertainty be incorporated into preoperative counselling and surgical planning?

When imaging in pregnancy cannot show a feeding vessel or stalk for a large upper abdominal cystic mass, you must treat that uncertainty as a central operative risk — not a minor diagnostic curiosity — and fold it explicitly into counselling, consent and the surgical plan.

Tell the patient and document that preoperative imaging (US ± non‑contrast MRI) cannot definitively distinguish an adnexal cyst from a pedunculated uterine mass, and explain the practical implications. If the mass is ovarian, the operation may be adnexectomy or cystectomy; if uterine and pedunculated, excision (myomectomy) with uterine wall repair may be required; if intramural or broad‑based myoma, removal may not be possible without substantial uterine incision and haemorrhage. Each scenario carries different risks to the pregnancy (procedure‑related blood loss, need for transfusion, preterm labour, possible pregnancy loss) and to future delivery (uterine scar).

Frame the consent around likely intraoperative decision points rather than a single predicted procedure. Specifically, preoperatively discuss and obtain consent for: exploration and excision of the mass if technically feasible; conversion of planned conservative procedure to myomectomy or ovarian excision as required; control of bleeding including use of uterotonics, direct uterine repair, temporary vascular control if available; blood transfusion; possible need to leave a second mass in situ to preserve the pregnancy; and, rarely, hysterectomy if uncontrollable haemorrhage occurs. Reassure that the team will attempt the least-invasive, pregnancy‑sparing approach compatible with maternal safety.

Operational planning should mirror that consent. Convene a multidisciplinary team (obstetrics, gynecologic surgery, anaesthesia, neonatology on standby) and prepare for worst‑case contingencies while aiming to minimize intervention. Ensure large‑bore IV access, cross‑matched blood available, cell‑saver if institutionally appropriate, and readiness for rapid transfusion. Plan intraoperative fetal monitoring when feasible (pre‑, intra‑ and post‑op fetal heart checks) and medical measures to support the pregnancy (prophylactic progesterone or tocolytics per local practice). Positioning and incision should permit extension if a uterine repair or unexpected hysterectomy is required. If the mass is unexpectedly pedunculated and torsed, be prepared to excise its stalk and repair the uterine defect with absorbable sutures; if a broad‑based intramural lesion is encountered, weigh immediate resection against leaving it in situ and deferring definitive management postpartum to avoid excessive bleeding and risk to the fetus.

Finally, discuss the implications for delivery: leaving large pelvic myomas in place may mandate elective cesarean delivery and influence timing. Make a documented plan for postoperative monitoring (hemoglobin trends, infection surveillance) and close obstetric follow‑up.

In short, convert diagnostic uncertainty into explicit, staged decision‑making: inform the patient about the plausible intraoperative findings and their consequences, obtain broad but specific consent for reasonable escalations, assemble the multidisciplinary team and blood‑management resources, and plan the operation to permit the least invasive, pregnancy‑preserving intervention compatible with maternal safety.
