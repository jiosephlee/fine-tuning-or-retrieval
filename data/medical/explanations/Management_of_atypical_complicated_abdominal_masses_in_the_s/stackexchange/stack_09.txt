Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiology of pain, low-grade fever, and laboratory changes when a pedunculated uterine fibroid undergoes torsion with ischemic necrosis and cystic degeneration during pregnancy, and why these processes can mimic infection or malignancy on imaging and tumor markers.

Direct answer
When a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma twists on its stalk the primary event is vascular compromise. Loss of venous and lymphatic outflow produces congestion and edema, which quickly progresses to arterial insufficiency, ischemia and then hemorrhagic/ischemic necrosis. Liquefactive change of necrotic tissue gives cystic degeneration and can leak blood and debris into the peritoneal cavity. The local tissue injury provokes a robust sterile inflammatory response that produces the clinical (pain, low‑grade fever) and laboratory (neutrophilia, raised CRP, modest rise in CA‑125) findings that can be indistinguishable from infection or neoplasm on initial testing and imaging.

Pathophysiologic sequence (linked to the case)
- Mechanical torsion → vascular outflow obstruction: the twisted pedicle in this patient produced venous congestion and impaired lymphatic drainage of the leiomyoma. Pathology confirmed ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle.
- Ischemia → cell death and hemorrhage → cystic degeneration: ischemic myoma tissue undergoes coagulative/hemorrhagic necrosis and liquefaction, producing a unilocular cystic mass with inhomogeneous fluid and focal blood products (explains the inhomogeneous T1/T2 signal and focal hyperintense areas seen on MRI and the taut‑elastic cystic appearance at surgery).
- Necrosis and debris → sterile inflammation: damaged cells release damage‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that recruit neutrophils and macrophages and trigger cytokine release (IL‑1, TNF, IL‑6). That inflammatory cascade causes low‑grade fever, an acute‑phase response with elevated CRP, and neutrophilic leukocytosis—the patient had mild fever, CRP 1.39 mg/dL and WBC 11.3×103/µL with neutrophilia.
- Local bleeding and chronic iron deficiency/pregnancy physiology → anemia: the patient’s iron deficiency anemia was present before surgery and worsened postoperatively; acute blood loss from the leaking/twisted mass and hemodilution of pregnancy contribute.
- Peritoneal irritation and marker release → elevated CA‑125: CA‑125 is produced by coelomic epithelium and is upregulated by peritoneal irritation, inflammation, decidual changes and some benign uterine conditions. In this case CA‑125 rose from 36 to 41.8 U/mL—above the usual second‑trimester median—reflecting sterile inflammation and tumor degeneration, not necessarily malignancy.

Why this mimics infection or malignancy on imaging and tumor markers
- Imaging appearance: cystic degeneration with hemorrhage produces complex cystic lesions with thickened walls, inhomogeneous fluid content and focal solid components. Peripheral neovascularization or hypertrophied vessels around a necrotic myoma can simulate the rim enhancement or peripheral vascularity seen with abscesses or ovarian neoplasms. In pregnancy the gravid uterus and large masses obscure a pedicle or feeding vessel, so a pedunculated myoma can be misinterpreted as an adnexal (ovarian) cyst or decidualised endometrioma—the same confusion seen in this patient’s ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI.
- Laboratory overlap: sterile necrosis generates an inflammatory response (CRP, neutrophilia, fever) similar to infection. CA‑125 is nonspecific—it physiologically rises in pregnancy and is released with peritoneal inflammation and some benign gynecologic disease—so modest elevations can be seen with degenerating fibroids as well as ovarian malignancy.
- Clinical confounders: secondary infection of a necrotic myoma is possible and would enhance the infectious picture; in this case a urinary Klebsiella added another possible source of fever, further complicating interpretation.

Clinical teaching points
- In pregnancy, interpret CA‑125 and inflammatory markers cautiously; modest rises can reflect sterile degeneration and pregnancy physiology rather than malignancy.
- Sudden localized pain, a tense cystic uterine mass and signs of peritoneal irritation should raise suspicion for torsion with ischemic degeneration of a pedunculated myoma. Doppler (looking for absent flow in the stalk/whirlpool sign) and non‑contrast MRI features of hemorrhage/cystic change are helpful but may be non‑diagnostic.
- Management is individualized: torsed, symptomatic, or leaking pedunculated myomas may require myomectomy despite pregnancy—this case illustrates that surgery confirmed the diagnosis and relieved mass effect with good maternal‑fetal outcome.

Keeping these pathophysiologic links in mind helps reconcile the clinical, imaging and laboratory overlap between degenerating torsed fibroids, infection and malignant disease.
