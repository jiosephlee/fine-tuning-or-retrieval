Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Morning Report] In a 21–22 week pregnancy with worsening mass-related pain, restrictive dyspnoea, and two large vascular abdominal masses, what factors would push you toward operative intervention instead of expectant management?

The decision to operate in the second trimester pivots on a risk–benefit balance: operative risk to the pregnancy (miscarriage, preterm labour, haemorrhage) versus imminent or likely maternal (and ultimately fetal) harm from the mass. In a patient at 21–22 weeks with worsening pain, restrictive dyspnoea and two large vascular abdominal masses, I would be pushed toward operative intervention when objective and reproducible findings indicate an unacceptable short‑term risk if managed expectantly.

Specifically, surgery is favored when the mother has progressive symptoms that do not respond to conservative therapy (analgesia, positioning, treatment of infection) and are impairing function or physiology—for example persistent or escalating pain despite treatment, respiratory compromise from diaphragmatic compression (restrictive dyspnoea) or any evidence of cardiorespiratory compromise. Evidence of an acute or evolving complication (torsion of a pedunculated mass, rupture, active bleeding, infected/necrotic degeneration with fever and rising inflammatory markers, or a leaking/twisted pedicle at risk of ongoing ischemia) mandates operative management because these events can rapidly deteriorate maternal status and precipitate fetal loss.

Imaging and laboratory features that raise the pretest probability of an urgent problem also tip the balance toward surgery. Thick walls, solid nodules or inhomogeneous signal on MRI, prominent peripheral vascularity, rapid increase in size, or a rising tumour marker (eg, CA‑125 trend) that makes neoplasm or complicated degeneration likely create diagnostic uncertainty that cannot safely be deferred until after delivery. When a mass is large enough to prevent further uterine expansion or is anatomically likely to obstruct labour (for future delivery) and is expected to continue growing with gestation, elective removal in the second trimester can be the lesser risk.

Finally, failure of conservative management together with multidisciplinary assessment (obstetrics, gynecologic oncology, anesthesiology) and informed consent from the patient are prerequisites. If these factors are present, the second trimester is the preferred window for abdominal surgery because uterine size allows access, and the risks of teratogenicity and spontaneous abortion are lower than in the first trimester and lower risk of preterm labour than in the third.

Conversely, absent persistent symptoms, acute complications, worrisome imaging/lab features, or diagnostic uncertainty, expectant management with close surveillance is the preferred approach.
