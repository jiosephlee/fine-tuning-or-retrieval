Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Morning Report] At 22 weeks’ gestation, imaging suggests an 18-cm pedunculated subserosal mass with torsion and a second large intramural posterior fibroid. What operative strategy would you choose (extent of resection, laparotomy versus laparoscopy, whether to leave the intramural lesion in situ), and what factors drive that decision?

Direct answer
- Remove the pedunculated, torsed 18‑cm subserosal myoma (myomectomy) and leave the large posterior intramural/subserosal fibroid in situ.  
- Approach by midline laparotomy rather than laparoscopy in this case.  

Why — clinical reasoning tied to the case
1. Indications for operative intervention in pregnancy
   - Operate when there is a clear complication or failed conservative care: acute painful torsion/necrosis, active infection, leak/hemorrhage, respiratory compromise from mass effect, or unresolved diagnostic/oncologic uncertainty. In this case the patient had severe pain, dyspnoea from mass effect, low‑grade fever, imaging suggesting torsion/leak and a small rise in CA‑125 — all reasonable indications to intervene in the second trimester.

2. Extent of resection: remove the torsed pedunculated lesion; leave the intramural lesion
   - Pedunculated subserosal myomas with torsion have a high and immediate risk of ischemia, infection, sepsis and worsening maternal compromise; excision removes that risk and is relatively straightforward because the stalk localizes the vascular pedicle. In the report the cranial mass was twisted, leaking and highly vascularized and was safely excised with stump ligation.  
   - Intramural (and broad subserosal) fibroids that are large but fixed and not actively necrotic carry a much higher operative risk for hemorrhage, uterine trauma, pregnancy loss, preterm labour and future scar complications if removed. The posterior intramural/subserosal 58×71 mm lesion in this patient was fixed and would have required more extensive dissection; leaving it in situ minimized obstetric risk while preserving pregnancy — and this is what was done successfully.

3. Choice of laparotomy versus laparoscopy
   - Laparotomy is preferred when the mass is very large (>10–15 cm), highly vascular, leaking/torsed, multiple, or when exposure and rapid hemorrhage control are anticipated to be difficult. The 18‑cm, highly vascular, leaking, torsed mass in this case and the imperfect preoperative localization made laparotomy the safer option; the team used a longitudinal xipho‑pubic incision for maximal exposure.  
   - Laparoscopy can be considered for smaller pedunculated lesions, earlier gestation, experienced laparoscopic teams, and when adequate visualization and prompt control of bleeding are feasible (open Hasson entry, low pneumoperitoneum pressures, readiness to convert). It was not optimal here because of size, vascularity and diagnostic uncertainty.

Perioperative measures and planning (key principles)
- Multidisciplinary planning (obstetrics, gynecologic surgery, anesthesia, neonatology), informed consent discussing risk of fetal loss/preterm labour and need for transfusion.  
- Continuous fetal monitoring (document fetal heart rate before, during and after procedure) when possible for gestational age, and obstetrical support in theatre. The team documented fetal cardiac activity intraoperatively in the case.  
- Minimize operative time and uterine manipulation; secure hemostasis at the pedicle (double clamp and suture closure as used), avoid extensive myometrial dissection.  
- Have blood products and uterotonics/tocolytics/progesterone available per local practice — the team used intraoperative progesterone prophylaxis and had transfusion when postoperative anaemia developed.  
- Perioperative antibiotics guided by infection findings (urine culture in this patient showed Klebsiella and antibiotics were chosen accordingly).  
- Plan delivery mode postpartum based on residual myoma size/location; a large posterior myoma obstructing the birth canal may necessitate elective cesarean (as occurred at 37 weeks in the report).

Bottom line
- In a 22‑week pregnancy with an 18‑cm torsed, highly vascular pedunculated subserosal myoma and a second large posterior intramural fibroid, the safest and most appropriate strategy is open myomectomy of the torsed pedunculated mass via laparotomy and conservative management (leave in situ) of the intramural lesion. The decision is driven by symptom severity, immediate complication risk (torsion/necrosis/infection), lesion size and vascularity, gestational age, technical feasibility, and the overriding goal of preserving the pregnancy while minimizing maternal morbidity.
