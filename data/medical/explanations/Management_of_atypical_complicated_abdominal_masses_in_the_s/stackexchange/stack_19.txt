### [Morning Report] Non-contrast MRI in mid-pregnancy shows a unilocular cyst with thickened wall, inhomogeneous fluid, and focal T1/T2 hyperintense nodular areas plus hypertrophied vessels. How does this pattern shape your differential, and what additional information would you seek preoperatively?

This MRI pattern—unilocular cyst with a thickened wall, inhomogeneous (T1/T2–bright) fluid and focal T1/T2–hyperintense nodular areas together with hypertrophied vessels—narrows the differential by pointing to two competing diagnostic families and one urgent management consideration.

First, the combination of T1 hyperintensity and inhomogeneous fluid strongly suggests blood‑ or protein‑rich contents (eg, endometrioma with repeated haemorrhage or a haemorrhagic cyst). Focal T1/T2‑bright mural nodules in a cystic adnexal lesion raise concern for decidualized endometrioma in pregnancy (benign, nodular decidual tissue can be bright and mimic solid tumor). Classically, endometriomas show the “shading” sign on T2 and T1 hyperintensity from blood products.

Second, the presence of hypertrophied feeding vessels and a thickened, sometimes pedunculated wall points toward a uterine origin — a pedunculated cystic or degenerating leiomyoma. Pedunculated fibroids can undergo cystic degeneration, torsion and haemorrhage and may have an identifiable vascular pedicle on Doppler or even MRI. In pregnancy the decidualized endometrioma versus cystic degenerated leiomyoma distinction is particularly important because management differs (expectant versus possible myomectomy if complicated).

Less likely but still on the list are cystic ovarian neoplasms with mural nodules (borderline or malignant cystadenomas), tubo‑ovarian abscess (if infectious signs dominate), and simple haemorrhagic cysts. The lack of adenopathy and normal other tumor markers lowers, but does not exclude, malignancy.

Because contrast MRI is avoided in pregnancy and that limits assessment of true mural enhancement (a key discriminator), the preoperative information I would seek is both diagnostic and pragmatic. Diagnostically, I would obtain a targeted transvaginal ultrasound with high‑resolution imaging and colour Doppler to look specifically for a vascular pedicle or stalk connecting the lesion to the uterus, assess the cleavage plane, and characterise mural nodules (vascularity, mobility) — finding a feeding vessel or clear continuity with uterine myometrium would favour a pedunculated leiomyoma. I would review T1 fat‑saturated sequences (to distinguish fat from blood/protein), look for the T2 “shading” sign of endometrioma, and add diffusion‑weighted imaging if available (restricted diffusion in solid malignant nodules). I would repeat CA‑125 and consider serial measurements rather than a single value to see trend, and check inflammatory markers and cultures if infection is suspected.

From a perioperative planning standpoint I would ensure fetal monitoring documentation and multidisciplinary agreement (obstetrics, anesthesia, gynecologic surgery), obtain blood type and crossmatch and optimize hemoglobin (anticipate possible transfusion), plan intraoperative fetal monitoring and short operative time, have uterotonics and tocolysis protocols ready, and counsel the patient about the possibility of conversion to more extensive surgery (eg, myomectomy vs greater uterine repair vs hysterectomy) and neonatal implications.

In short: the imaging pattern narrows the differential mainly to decidualized endometrioma versus cystic/haemorrhagic, pedunculated degenerating leiomyoma (and less likely cystic ovarian neoplasm). Because gadolinium is contraindicated, a focused transvaginal US with Doppler to look for a uterine pedicle, review of specific MRI sequences (T1 fat‑sat, T2 shading, DWI), serial CA‑125 and inflammatory markers, and thorough preoperative maternal–fetal planning are the key next steps to guide management.
