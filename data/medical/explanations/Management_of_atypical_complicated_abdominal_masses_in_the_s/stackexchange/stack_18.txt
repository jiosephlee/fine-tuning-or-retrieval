Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Clinical Pearls] How should CA-125 and other tumour markers (CEA, CA 19-9, LDH) be used when evaluating adnexal masses in pregnancy, and when would they meaningfully change management?

Tumour markers in pregnancy are adjuncts, not diagnostic tests. Pregnancy alters many marker levels and common benign gravid conditions (decidualisation, endometriosis, leiomyomas, placental release, inflammation) cause false‑positives, so interpretation must be anchored to trimester, imaging, and the clinical picture.

CA‑125: rises physiologically in the first trimester and again near term; second‑trimester levels are usually low (median ≈14 U/mL), so a modest isolated elevation in pregnancy (eg, 36–50 U/mL as in this case) is nonspecific and cannot by itself diagnose malignancy. Serial measurements that show a clear upward trend, very high absolute values, or concordant suspicious imaging (complex solid components, papillary projections, septations, ascites, nodal disease) increase concern and may tip management toward definitive diagnosis or surgery. In practice, CA‑125 meaningfully changes decisions when levels are markedly and persistently abnormal (eg, well above typical pregnancy peaks, often hundreds of U/mL) and when the marker amplifies worrisome sonographic/MRI features or a deteriorating clinical course.

CEA, CA 19‑9: these markers are rarely useful in pregnancy for primary adnexal evaluation. Mild elevations are nonspecific; very high values that fit an imaging pattern of mucinous ovarian tumors or an extracolonic process can support concern, but they are not independent indications for operative intervention.

LDH and germ‑cell markers: LDH (dysgerminoma), AFP and β‑hCG (yolk sac, choriocarcinoma, other germ‑cell tumours) have a clearer role because certain germ‑cell tumours have characteristic marker profiles. Interpretation is complicated by pregnancy physiology: maternal AFP and β‑hCG are increased by the pregnancy itself, so only very marked deviations from gestational norms or the presence of a tumour‑specific pattern (eg, extremely high LDH in the appropriate imaging context) are actionable. When a marker profile is strongly suggestive of a germ‑cell tumour, earlier surgical management is more justifiable because these tumours are often chemo‑sensitive and timely treatment affects maternal outcomes.

Practical approach: use markers as an adjunct to detailed ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI, obtain pregnancy‑adjusted reference points, and repeat markers if the initial result is borderline. Do not operate for a mildly elevated CA‑125 alone. Proceed to surgery during pregnancy when clinical signs (pain, torsion, rupture, infection), rapid mass growth, or concordant imaging plus markedly abnormal or rising tumour markers raise a genuine suspicion of malignancy — or when delay would threaten maternal or fetal health. Always make the decision in a multidisciplinary setting and prioritize fetal monitoring and minimally sufficient surgical intervention to address the immediate risk.
