Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Management] During non-obstetric surgery in the second trimester, what fetal monitoring is recommended before, during, and after the procedure, and what measures can be used to maintain uterine quiescence (for example, positioning, analgesia, judicious tocolysis or progesterone), including the strength of evidence supporting each?

Short answer
- Before surgery: document fetal viability and gestational age; if fetus is potentially viable and delivery would be possible should a catastrophe occur, involve obstetrics/neonatology and obtain baseline fetal heart rate (FHR) tracing or ultrasound cardiac activity.  
- During surgery: if fetus is at or beyond the institution’s threshold of viability and an emergent cesarean is feasible, continuous external fetal monitoring (CTG or Doppler) is recommended. If the fetus is previable or continuous monitoring is not feasible (early second trimester, surgical field, sterility constraints), intermittent assessment of fetal cardiac activity with ultrasound (transabdominal or transvaginal) is acceptable.  
- After surgery: re-document fetal heart activity immediately and monitor for uterine activity/late fetal compromise for an appropriate interval (commonly several hours; longer if contractions, bleeding, or maternal instability).  
- Measures to maintain uterine quiescence: left lateral tilt (or at least 15° left uterine displacement), minimization of uterine manipulation and operative time, good maternal oxygenation, normotension and normocapnia, adequate perioperative analgesia/anesthesia; reserve tocolytics for documented uterine contractions or threatened preterm labour (nifedipine first-line where appropriate); routine prophylactic tocolysis is not supported. Progesterone as routine perioperative prophylaxis is not evidence-based and should be individualized (considered by some teams by analogy with preterm-prevention strategies, but data are limited to case reports/series). Overall strength of evidence is low (observational data and expert consensus).

Why — clinical reasoning and evidence
- The objective of fetal monitoring around non‑obstetric surgery is to detect fetal compromise that would prompt intervention and to document fetal well‑being. Whether intraoperative monitoring should be continuous depends fundamentally on two things: (1) gestational age/viability and (2) whether an immediate delivery would be possible and beneficial. Professional guidance and large series therefore recommend baseline documentation before surgery and post‑operative documentation afterward, with intraoperative continuous monitoring reserved for fetuses at/near viability when a cesarean could be undertaken emergently. This is the practical teaching point emphasized by Zhao et al. and reiterated in reviews and committee opinions (ACOG/other societies) — the evidence base is observational and consensus-based, not randomized.  
- In the early–mid second trimester (as in this case at 21 + 6 weeks), the fetus is usually below most centres’ threshold for neonatal survivability. Continuous CTG in that setting rarely changes management and is often technically difficult during abdominal surgery; intermittent ultrasound assessment of cardiac activity (transabdominal or transvaginal) is therefore a pragmatic approach. That is exactly what the surgical team did in the reported case: serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasounds to verify fetal viability during the procedure, with documentation of fetal cardiac activity at exploration. The authors cite the Zhao multicentre study and state that checking and documenting FHR before, during and after surgery is mandatory.  
- If the fetus is ≥threshold of viability and delivery is a realistic option, continuous monitoring provides the best chance to detect sudden compromise and is therefore recommended. If continuous monitoring shows sustained nonreassuring tracings during surgery and delivery is feasible, emergent cesarean should be considered.

Maintaining uterine quiescence — pathophysiology and practical measures
- Uterine activity is driven by maternal hemodynamics, oxygenation, pain/stress and direct uterine manipulation. Interventions that reduce maternal sympathetic activation and preserve uteroplacental perfusion reduce the risk of stimulation of contractions. Practical measures with biologic plausibility and some supporting observational data:
  - Positioning: left lateral tilt or manual uterine displacement (≥15°) reduces aortocaval compression, preserves cardiac output and uteroplacental blood flow — recommended in all pregnant surgical patients. In the reported case, semi‑orthostatic positioning was used pre‑op to relieve diaphragmatic compression by the uterus and masses.
  - Anesthesia/analgesia: provide adequate analgesia, avoid maternal hypoxia and hypotension, and maintain normocapnia — all reduce uterine irritability. Choice of neuraxial versus general anesthesia is dictated by surgery; volatile anesthetics at sufficient concentrations relax uterus (may be helpful intraoperatively) but do not replace other measures. Short, efficient surgery minimizes uterine stimulation.
  - Minimize uterine manipulation and ischemia: gentle handling, limit traction and minimize operative time; the case illustrates limiting the operation to the symptomatic mass and leaving the adherent pelvic mass in situ to reduce obstetric risk.
  - Tocolytics: routine prophylactic tocolysis at the time of non‑obstetric surgery is not supported by RCT data. If there are documented contractions or cervical change, tocolysis can be considered to delay delivery. Agents used in practice (evidence largely from preterm labour literature, not perioperative trials) include nifedipine (commonly used, oral, good tolerated), atosiban where available, and indomethacin (use limited by gestational age and fetal ductus concerns). Beta‑agonists are less favored because of maternal side effects. Use should be directed by obstetric consultation.
  - Progesterone: progesterone reduces recurrent spontaneous preterm birth in selected high‑risk women, but there are no RCTs supporting routine perioperative progesterone to prevent surgery‑associated preterm birth. The team in this report administered intramuscular progesterone prophylactically intraoperatively and continued it — an approach supported only by low‑level evidence (case series and expert opinion). If used, it should be individualized and documented in the consent discussion.
  - Other supportive measures: perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis when indicated, transfusion and correction of anemia (as in this patient) to preserve oxygen delivery to the fetus.

Strength of evidence summary
- Recommendations rest mostly on observational studies, case series, expert consensus and professional committee opinions rather than randomized trials. Key points with stronger consensus:
  - Document fetal heart activity before and after non‑obstetric surgery — strong consensus (low level of evidence).  
  - Continuous intraoperative fetal monitoring when the fetus is viable and immediate delivery is a practical option — consensus/expert opinion (low-quality evidence).  
  - Positioning, maintaining maternal oxygenation and hemodynamics, minimizing uterine manipulation and providing adequate anesthesia/analgesia — biologically plausible with observational support (low–moderate level evidence).  
  - Routine prophylactic tocolysis or routine perioperative progesterone is not evidence‑based; use should be selective and individualized (low-quality evidence).

Practical takeaways for teams planning non‑obstetric surgery in the second trimester
- Multidisciplinary planning (obstetrics, anesthesia, surgery, neonatology) before the operation. Document gestational age and viability, discuss the threshold for emergent delivery, and obtain baseline fetal heart monitoring or ultrasound.  
- If fetus is previable (as in this case at ~21–22 weeks), intermittent ultrasound assessment of cardiac activity during the procedure is reasonable and was effectively used here. If fetus is potentially viable (≥institutional threshold), arrange for continuous fetal monitoring and immediate cesarean capability.  
- Use left lateral tilt, maintain maternal oxygenation and blood pressure, provide adequate analgesia/anesthesia, minimize uterine handling and operative time. Reserve tocolytics for documented uterine contractions. Consider progesterone only on a case‑by‑case basis; recognize that evidence for routine prophylaxis is lacking.  
- Document all monitoring and the plan in the operative record; ensure close post‑op obstetrical follow‑up.

In the presented case these principles were followed: fetal viability was documented before and during laparotomy with intermittent transvaginal ultrasound, the team minimized intervention by removing only the symptomatic pedunculated myoma, and they used intraoperative progesterone prophylaxis (an approach based on low‑level evidence and multidisciplinary judgment). The favorable maternal and neonatal outcome illustrates that careful, individualized multidisciplinary management is appropriate when non‑obstetric surgery is necessary in the second trimester.
