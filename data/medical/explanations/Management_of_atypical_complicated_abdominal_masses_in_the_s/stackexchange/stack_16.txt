### [Pathophysiology] Describe the typical trajectory of uterine leiomyoma size across trimesters and the early postpartum period, and how this natural history informs the timing of intervention.

Uterine leiomyomas do not change size in a simple linear way during pregnancy; their natural history is driven by early, hormone‑mediated enlargement followed by relative plateauing and then rapid postpartum involution. Quantitatively, cohort data show the greatest proportional growth occurs early — roughly a 6% weekly increase between the first and second trimesters (overall about 6.9%/week early on), slowing to approximately 3%/week between the second and third trimesters. After delivery many leiomyomas regress substantially: mean size reductions of about 40–50% are reported by six weeks postpartum (wide individual variation).

Understanding that trajectory explains clinical decision‑making. Because growth is most rapid in the first trimester and early second trimester, mass effect and degenerative complications (pain from red degeneration, torsion of pedunculated fibroids, infection, compression) are more likely to present in mid‑pregnancy — but elective removal in the first trimester carries a high miscarriage risk. Therefore, when intervention is necessary for acute complications (torsion, rupture, intractable pain, hemodynamic compromise, or clear oncologic concern) it is reasonable to operate emergently regardless of gestation; when surgery can be timed, the second trimester is the preferred window because maternal‑fetal risks (miscarriage, preterm labour, anesthesia) are lowest. Conversely, if symptoms are manageable and there is no immediate threat to the pregnancy, expectant management is usually preferred because many myomas will stop growing and often shrink substantially after delivery, allowing safer definitive treatment in the postpartum period.

Apply these principles to the presented case: the patient had a highly vascular, twisted pedunculated myoma with leakage and symptomatic mass effect — a complication that justifies intra‑pregnancy myomectomy. The team limited surgery to the symptomatic pedunculated lesion and left the large intramural/subserosal myoma in place because it was stable and likely to regress postpartum; indeed the residual myoma decreased in size on follow‑up. In practice, therefore, intervene urgently for complications irrespective of trimester, prefer the second trimester for nonurgent necessary procedures, and plan definitive management after delivery when feasible because of frequent postpartum involution.
