# The Adnexal Mass That Wasn’t: Pedunculated Cystic Fibroid Mimicking Endometrioma in Pregnancy

What makes this case interesting? At 21 weeks a nulliparous woman in her 40s arrived with dyspnea and abdominal pain and ultrasound showed two large cystic masses — one in the left subphrenic space and one filling the pouch of Douglas. CA-125 was mildly elevated. MRI without contrast showed large unilocular cysts with inhomogeneous contents and thick walls. The team planned surgery for worsening symptoms and potential complications, and intra‑operatively the upper “adnexal” lesion turned out to be a pedunculated, highly vascular cystic leiomyoma — twisted and leaking — not an ovarian endometrioma. The lower pelvic cyst was a cystic-degenerated posterior myoma left in situ to protect the pregnancy; delivery was by planned CS at term with a great maternal and neonatal outcome.

Why this matters: pregnant patients get a limited diagnostic toolbox. Mislabeling a pedunculated subserosal or cystic-degenerated fibroid as an adnexal mass changes counseling, risk estimates and surgical planning. Know the bedside and imaging clues that point back to the uterus.

Clinical context — what made the team consider surgery
- Severe pain localized to upper quadrant and pelvis plus mild dyspnea from mass effect.
- Rapidly enlarging, highly vascular masses with mixed solid/cystic components and a small, rising CA‑125 during the second trimester.
- Concern for torsion, rupture, infection and restriction of uterine growth as pregnancy progressed.
- Symptom control was inadequate with conservative therapy, so laparotomy with intraoperative fetal monitoring was chosen.

Practical pearls for distinguishing decidualized endometrioma vs cystic or pedunculated leiomyoma in pregnancy

Bimanual and bedside exam
- Don’t skip the bimanual vaginal exam. A fixed, posterior pelvic mass that bulges into the posterior fornix favors uterine origin (or at least close adherence). In this case the caudal mass was fixed and bulging posteriorly, a hint it was connected to the uterus.
- Assess mobility. Pedunculated subserosal lesions may be mobile on palpation if on a long stalk, but once torsed or adherent they can feel fixed.

Ultrasound clues (real-world, high-yield)
- Look for the “stalk sign”: a discrete pedicle connecting a mass to the uterine serosa. It may be thin and missed without careful scanning, especially with large gravid uteri. If you find it, you’ve got a uterine lesion.
- Bridging vessels on color Doppler: vessels that run from the uterus into the mass suggest uterine vascular supply (leiomyoma) rather than ovarian origin. These vessels often course along the myometrium into the lesion.
- Internal echotexture: endometriomas classically show ground-glass homogenous low-level internal echoes; decidualization in pregnancy can add vascular mural nodules. Cystic degeneration in fibroids can produce unilocular/ multilocular cystic areas with variable internal echoes and sometimes septations or thick walls.
- Peripheral vascularity and hypertrophied feeding vessels often point to a fibroid, especially when the mass is large and vascularized.
- Always scan systematically: assess uterine contour, adnexal regions, and the relationship between masses and uterus in multiple planes.

Non-contrast MRI — what to look for (safely after first trimester)
- Continuity with uterine wall: the clearest MRI sign a mass is uterine in origin is loss of a clear cleavage plane and visual continuity with myometrium. The caudal mass in this case blended with a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma on MRI.
- Signal characteristics: leiomyomas commonly show low T2 signal, but cystic degeneration increases T1/T2 signal variability. Hemorrhage or proteinaceous material can be T1 hyperintense. Endometriomas often have high T1 signal (blood products) and variable T2 shading; decidualized nodules may enhance with gadolinium — but in pregnancy we avoid gadolinium.
- Wall thickness and mural nodules: thick walls and focal mural solid components can occur in both entities; use the relationship to the uterus and bridging vessels to tip the scale.
- No contrast → rely on morphology, signal patterns and anatomical continuity. Remember: gadolinium is avoided in pregnancy unless absolutely necessary.

Tumor markers in pregnancy — don’t overinterpret
- CA‑125 can be physiologically elevated in pregnancy (peaks in first and third trimesters). A modest elevation (like 40 U/mL) is nonspecific. Serial rises are informative but not diagnostic.
- Use markers as an adjunct, not a determinant. In this case a rising CA‑125 nudged the team towards surgical exploration given clinical deterioration, but was not definitive.

When to operate in pregnancy
- Surgery is generally avoided if possible because of risks (bleeding, preterm labor, pregnancy loss). But operative management is indicated for:
  - Torsion of a pedunculated fibroid
  - Rupture or active hemorrhage
  - Severe, refractory pain or progressive mass effect (e.g., respiratory compromise)
  - Uncertain diagnosis with oncologic suspicion despite safe imaging
- If surgery is necessary, minimize duration and uterine manipulation; have fetal monitoring and a multidisciplinary team (obstetrics, anesthesia, surgery). Intraoperative and postoperative progesterone prophylaxis was used in this case, and fetal heart monitoring before/during/after surgery is mandatory.

Intraoperative decision-making matters
- Remove the life/uterus‑threatening lesion and leave adherent lesions that pose high obstetric risk if excised. In this patient the upper twisted pedunculated myoma was removed; the posterior intramural cystic myoma was deliberately left to avoid compromising the pregnancy.
- Control the pedicle carefully and suture the uterine defect. Anticipate and manage blood loss (this case required transfusion and IV iron).

Take-home messages
- Large cystic masses in pregnancy can be deceptive: pedunculated or cystic-degenerated leiomyomas may mimic adnexal endometriomas on imaging.
- Key distinguishing features:
  - Bimanual exam: fixed posterior mass bulging into the fornix → think uterine origin.
  - Ultrasound: look for the stalk sign and bridging vessels on color Doppler.
  - MRI (non-contrast): assess continuity with myometrium and cleavage planes; interpret T1/T2 signals in clinical context.
  - CA‑125 is nonspecific in pregnancy — interpret trends, not single values.
- Operate only for clear indications (torsion, rupture, infection, refractory symptoms, or strong oncologic suspicion). When you do, remove the lesion threatening the pregnancy and consider leaving adherent lesions to minimize obstetric risk.
- Multidisciplinary planning, intraoperative fetal monitoring and careful postpartum follow-up are essential.

This case is a great reminder: in pregnant patients the origin of a “pelvic/abdominal cyst” is as much about anatomy and vascular connections as it is about signal intensities. Look for the stalk and bridging vessels first — they will often save you from mislabeling a uterine fibroid as an adnexal tumor.



# CA-125 in Pregnancy: Don’t Take the Bait

This case is a great reminder that tumor markers can lie — or at least mislead — when pregnancy is in the picture. A woman in her 40s at 21 weeks gestation had two massive, cystic abdominal lesions and a CA-125 that drifted from 36 to 41.8 U/mL. That small bump—above the conventional 35 U/mL cutoff—pushed the team to pursue MRI and, ultimately, laparotomy. Intra‑op surprise: the “ovarian” upper mass was a pedunculated, cystic, twisted leiomyoma; the lower lesion was a cystic-degenerate posterior myoma left in situ. Pathology: benign leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis. Mother and baby did well.

Why this matters: CA-125 stirs anxiety. In nonpregnant patients it contributes to risk stratification for ovarian malignancy, but in pregnancy its baseline and biologic behavior change. Using an adult cutoff without context risks overcalling cancer and triggering unnecessary imaging or surgery that can harm the pregnancy.

What CA-125 does in pregnancy — the facts you need
- CA-125 normally fluctuates during pregnancy. Median values are higher in the first trimester, drop in the second, and rise again in the third. Typical second‑trimester medians are much lower than the arbitrary 35 U/mL cutoff used for nonpregnant adults.
- Transient mild elevations are common and nonspecific: physiologic changes, decidualization, endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic infection, and even large uterine leiomyomas can push levels up.
- A single CA-125 value in pregnancy has low specificity and positive predictive value for malignancy. Serial trends are more informative than one-off numbers, but even trends must be interpreted alongside clinical and imaging data.
- CA-125 is not a reliable discriminator between benign decidualized endometrioma and malignant transformation during pregnancy.

Why single cutoffs mislead
- The 35 U/mL threshold was validated outside pregnancy and performs poorly during gestation. A value slightly above 35 in the second trimester is neither diagnostic nor necessarily alarming.
- Pregnancy itself produces false positives. Relying on the cutoff can create cognitive bias: a borderline elevation may prime clinicians to interpret imaging through a malignancy lens.
- Tumor marker algorithms (e.g., Risk of Malignancy Index) incorporate CA-125 and ultrasound features, but these were not developed or validated in pregnant populations. Applying them blindly can overestimate cancer risk.

How to integrate CA-125 into evaluation of pelvic masses in pregnancy
- Start with a careful clinical exam and high-quality ultrasound (transabdominal + transvaginal when feasible). Ultrasound is the first-line tool for characterizing adnexal vs uterine lesions, vascularity, septations, solid components, and relationships to the uterus.
- Use non-contrast MRI (≤1.5 T if possible) when ultrasound is inconclusive, when anatomy is distorted by gravid uterus and large masses, or when you need better assessment of tissue planes and myometrial continuity.
- Treat CA-125 as an adjunct, not a driver. Consider:
  - Gestational age (first and third trimesters commonly higher).
  - Magnitude of elevation (mild—near cutoff—versus markedly high).
  - Trend over time (rising, stable, or falling).
  - Clinical features (pain, fever, torsion risk, mass growth, systemic symptoms).
  - Imaging appearance (clear ovarian corpus vs continuity with myometrium, solid enhancing nodules — although gadolinium is generally avoided).
- If CA-125 is mildly elevated but imaging suggests uterine origin (e.g., pedunculated or subserosal myoma), manage conservatively unless there are complications (torsion, rupture, infection, severe mass effect). This case shows how imaging misled initially but definitive surgery found a leiomyoma — the take-home is to synthesize all data, not overreact to a number.

When to operate
- Indications for surgical intervention in pregnancy remain: acute complications (torsion, hemorrhage, rupture), persistent severe symptoms unresponsive to conservative therapy, or honest concern for malignancy that cannot be clarified noninvasively.
- If surgery is required, plan it with a multidisciplinary team (obstetrics, gynecology, anesthesia, neonatology, radiology). Document fetal heart rate before, during, and after, keep the operation as short as feasible, and avoid gadolinium.
- Remember that conservative management is often safest for the fetus; but delayed surgery may be reasonable only if maternal risk remains acceptable.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t let CA-125 alone push you toward aggressive management in pregnancy. Context is everything.
- A small rise above 35 U/mL in the second trimester is common and nonspecific — combine tumor markers with serial imaging and clinical course.
- Use ultrasound first; reserve non-contrast MRI when ultrasound cannot answer the key questions (origin, relationship to uterus, solid vs cystic components).
- If imaging suggests myoma (continuity with myometrium, pedicle), treat as such unless other red flags exist.
- Salvage surgery for complications is appropriate; prophylactic surgery to “rule out malignancy” based solely on a borderline CA-125 is usually not.

Take-home messages
- CA-125 is unreliable as a standalone malignancy screen in pregnancy; avoid applying nonpregnant cutoffs rigidly.
- Interpret CA-125 trends, not isolated values, and always integrate with ultrasound/MRI and clinical status.
- Multidisciplinary decision-making and conservative management are the default unless clear complications or strong, corroborated imaging signs of malignancy exist.
- When in doubt, prioritize maternal and fetal safety: manage symptoms, monitor closely, and operate for complications — not for numbers.

This case nailed the key lesson: a small bump in CA-125 can be a red herring. Let imaging, serial assessments, and the clinical picture lead the way — not a single lab value.



# When Dyspnea Isn’t PE: Mass‑Effect Respiratory Failure from Giant Fibroids

What if your pregnant patient with new dyspnea doesn’t have pulmonary embolism, pneumonia or heart failure — but two massive uterine masses turning her chest into a piston? That’s exactly what happened in this case: a woman in her 40s at 21 weeks’ gestation presented with upper‑left abdominal pain and dyspnoea. Oxygenation and vital signs were reassuring, but bedside exam showed an 18‑cm subphrenic taut mass and a 9–10 cm pelvic mass. Ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI eventually revealed large cystic, vascularised uterine leiomyomas; the cranial lesion was a pedunculated, twisted, highly vascular fibroid that was resected at laparotomy. Dyspnoea was mechanical — diaphragmatic splinting and restricted lung expansion from “steric bulk” — not primary cardiopulmonary disease.

This is a useful diagnostic pitfall to remember: pregnant patients can develop symptomatic restrictive physiology from large intra‑abdominal masses, and the ED’s reflexive PE/pathology workup can miss the cause if you don’t add a quick abdominal assessment.

## Why this case matters
- Dyspnoea in pregnancy has a broad differential. PE will always be high on the list, but not every breathless pregnant patient needs an immediate CT pulmonary angiogram.
- Large uterine masses — especially when pedunculated, vascular and occupying the subphrenic space — can cause significant mechanical dyspnoea without hypoxaemia or abnormal cardiopulmonary testing.
- Management decisions must balance maternal symptoms, fetal viability and the risks of surgery in pregnancy. Multidisciplinary input is essential.

## How to think about dyspnoea in pregnancy — add “mass effect” to the checklist
Initial approach in a pregnant patient with dyspnoea should still prioritize life‑threatening causes: PE, pneumothorax, cardiac failure, severe pneumonia, sepsis. But the bedside exam is crucial and can shortcut investigations.

Quick bedside pearls:
- Look at the abdomen. Is the uterine fundus larger than expected for dates? Palpable, tense masses or an asymmetric abdomen are clues.
- Assess oxygenation and work of breathing. In this case SpO2 was 97%, HR 74, BP 100/60 — reassuring despite subjective dyspnoea.
- Simple maneuvers: does dyspnoea improve with upright or semi‑recumbent positioning? Positional relief suggests mechanical diaphragmatic restriction rather than intrinsic pulmonary disease.
- Auscultation and limited chest imaging (CXR or bedside ultrasound) can exclude major pulmonary pathology quickly if PE is less likely.
- Don’t forget vaginal bimanual exam in the obstetric setting — a fixed pelvic mass felt on exam may point to uterine/myometrial origin.

## When to image the chest — and when to image the abdomen
- If the patient has hypoxia, haemodynamic instability, pleuritic chest pain, or high pretest probability for PE, proceed with targeted cardiopulmonary imaging (CTPA or V/Q as per pregnancy protocols).
- If O2 saturations are normal, vitals stable, and bedside exam demonstrates an abdominal mass or positional improvement, prioritize pelvic/abdominal ultrasound as the next diagnostic step.
- Use non‑contrast MRI if US is inconclusive and you need better characterisation of adnexal vs uterine masses — avoid gadolinium in pregnancy unless absolutely necessary.
- Consider portable chest x‑ray (with abdominal shielding) if you need to rapidly exclude lobar consolidation or effusion and the suspicion for chest disease is low–moderate.

In the presented case, normal oxygenation and a large palpable abdominal mass prompted ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI rather than an immediate chest CT. That was the right diagnostic leap.

## Bedside management and temporizing measures
When the dyspnoea is due to mechanical restriction from large abdominal masses, early conservative measures can be very effective:
- Positioning: encourage semi‑upright/sitting posture to reduce diaphragmatic splinting. Trendelenburg/lying flat will usually worsen symptoms.
- Analgesia: effective pain control reduces respiratory splinting. Paracetamol is safe in pregnancy and helped this patient (IV paracetamol reduced pain VAS 5→1).
- Oxygen only if hypoxaemic. Avoid reflexive oxygen therapy if saturations are normal.
- Treat reversible contributors: correct anaemia (iron or transfusion as indicated), treat infections (this patient had a Klebsiella UTI).
- Continuous fetal monitoring during interventions when gestational age and maternal status warrant it.

## Red flags that push you from conservative care to surgery
Surgery in pregnancy is not benign and is usually avoided if possible. But several features should prompt operative management:
- Progressive or severe maternal respiratory compromise (significant work of breathing, dependence on supplemental oxygen, rising PaCO2).
- Signs of mass complication: torsion (severe focal pain), rupture, active bleeding or infection (fever, leukocytosis, positive cultures).
- Rapid mass growth or imaging features suspicious for malignancy (solid components, irregular thick walls, rising tumour markers with worrisome trend) — balanced against the pregnancy‑related variability of CA‑125.
- Mechanical obstruction of adjacent organs (urinary retention, bowel obstruction) or obstruction of the birth canal that will render vaginal delivery impossible.
- Failure of conservative measures with ongoing maternal risk.

In this case, the patient developed worsening pain, low‑grade fever, a slightly rising CA‑125, and imaging that suggested a vascular, twisted pedunculated lesion — these features justified laparotomy. The surgical team removed the cranial fibroid and left the pelvic myoma in situ to minimise obstetric risk. Multidisciplinary consent and intraoperative fetal monitoring were used, and perioperative progesterone was administered.

## Practical tips for emergency and obstetric clinicians
- Always include an abdominal inspection and palpation in the dyspnoeic pregnant patient.
- A “normal” SpO2 and ECG do not exclude clinically significant mechanical dyspnoea from an abdominal mass.
- Use ultrasound early — it’s safe, quick, and can identify large adnexal or uterine masses at the bedside.
- Reserve gadolinium MRI for exceptional circumstances; non‑contrast MRI (≤1.5 T) is a good problem‑solving tool after ultrasound.
- Coordinate early with obstetrics, maternal‑fetal medicine, radiology and surgical teams when a large mass is found.
- Document fetal heart rate before, during and after any surgery in pregnancy; obtain informed consent that includes obstetric risks.
- Manage reversible contributors (anaemia, infection, pain) promptly — they can markedly improve breathlessness.

## Clinical pearls and take‑home points
- Mechanical dyspnoea from intra‑abdominal mass effect is an underrecognised cause of breathlessness in pregnancy — think beyond PE.
- Rapid bedside abdominal exam and point‑of‑care ultrasound can change the diagnostic pathway and avoid unnecessary CTs.
- Positional improvement with sitting/semi‑upright and preserved oxygenation point toward restrictive mechanics rather than primary pulmonary disease.
- Red flags for operative management: torsion/rupture, infection, progressive respiratory compromise, suspicion for malignancy, or obstruction of labour.
- Non‑contrast MRI is the best second‑line imaging for complex pelvic masses in pregnancy; avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary.
- Multidisciplinary planning, intraoperative fetal monitoring, and perioperative obstetric measures (eg, progesterone prophylaxis in this case) improve maternal–fetal outcomes.

This case is a reminder: listen to the abdomen. A breathless pregnant patient with a disproportionately big‑looking belly may not need a CTPA — she may need an ultrasound and a surgical consult.



# Operating in the Second Trimester: Indications, Timing, and Tactics for Pelvic Masses

What makes this case interesting
A woman in her 40s at 21+6 weeks gestation presented with two very large abdominal masses and symptoms—upper-quadrant pain, pelvic pain and dyspnoea. Imaging (US + non-contrast MRI) could not definitively distinguish adnexal vs uterine origin. At laparotomy the “upper” lesion proved to be a pedunculated, twisted, highly vascular uterine leiomyoma (cystic degeneration, leaking) and was removed; the pelvic (caudal) mass that was adherent to the uterine posterior wall was left in situ. Pregnancy was preserved, she received perioperative progesterone, recovered after transfusion/iron, had an elective CS at 37 weeks and both mother and baby did well.

This case highlights the common real-world dilemma: large, symptomatic abdominal/pelvic masses in mid-pregnancy that pose immediate maternal risk (pain, torsion, infection, mass effect on respiration) but where intervention risks the pregnancy. It shows pragmatic multidisciplinary decision-making: operate for maternal safety/diagnosis while attempting to preserve the pregnancy and avoid overtreatment.

Clinical framing — when to consider surgery in pregnancy
Pregnancy is not an absolute contraindication to non-obstetric abdominal surgery. Expectant management remains the default for most asymptomatic masses, but intervene when the balance shifts toward maternal risk or diagnostic uncertainty that could hide malignancy.

Indications to strongly consider surgical intervention in pregnancy (illustrated by this case)
- Acute, uncontrolled pain despite conservative measures (analgesia, positioning)
- Signs of torsion, rupture, active leaking or necrosis (fever, inflammatory markers, peritoneal signs)
- Infection of the mass or patient (sepsis risk, positive cultures)
- Significant mass effect (respiratory compromise, urinary obstruction)
- Rapid growth with concern for degeneration or malignancy and inconclusive imaging/markers
- Inability to exclude malignancy by safe imaging modalities or sampling

Timing: why the second trimester is often preferred
- Second trimester (14–28 weeks) is the “sweet spot”: organogenesis is complete, uterine size still manageable for abdominal access, and risk of preterm labour is lower than third trimester. In this case, progressive mass effect and diagnostic uncertainty at 21+6 warranted surgery.
- First-trimester surgery increases miscarriage risk; third-trimester surgery carries higher preterm delivery risk and technical difficulty due to uterine size.

Imaging strategy before committing to surgery
- Ultrasound (with Doppler) is first-line: assess size, vascularity, relation to uterus, presence of solid components.
- Non-contrast MRI (preferably ≤1.5 T if practical) is safe after the first trimester and adds value for posterior/intramural lesions and to map distance from uterine cavity or placenta. Gadolinium should be avoided unless absolutely necessary.
- Tumour markers (eg, CA‑125) are of limited discriminatory value in pregnancy — values fluctuate with gestation and benign conditions (endometriosis, fibroids, infection) can raise them. Use trends and context, not single values.

Surgical approach: laparoscopy vs laparotomy
- Laparoscopy advantages: less postoperative pain, shorter stay, quicker recovery, lower adhesion formation. It can be considered in pregnancy for small-to-moderate masses early in pregnancy with experienced surgeons and appropriate equipment.
- Laparotomy advantages: better exposure for very large masses, multiple masses, highly vascular lesions, suspected malignancy, or when adhesions/torsion/degeneration make minimally invasive approach unsafe. In this case the large, vascular, and initially twisted pedunculated mass justified a midline laparotomy.
- Practical selection factors:
  - Mass size and number (large/giant masses → laparotomy)
  - Location (posterior/intramural, lower uterine segment involvement)
  - Gestational age and uterine size
  - Suspicion of malignancy or need for wide exposure
  - Team experience with advanced laparoscopy in pregnancy

Intraoperative and perioperative tactics
- Multidisciplinary planning: obstetrics, gynecologic surgery, anesthesia, neonatology should be involved.
- Positioning: left lateral tilt (15°) to avoid aortocaval compression; semi‑upright positioning can sometimes reduce diaphragmatic compression from large masses and the gravid uterus.
- Fetal heart rate monitoring: document fetal heart rate before, during (intermittent if continuous is impractical), and after surgery whenever possible. Continuous intraoperative monitoring may be difficult during laparotomy but should be attempted or at least serially checked.
- Anesthesia: gravid physiology mandates attention to airway (risk of difficult intubation), aspiration prophylaxis, reduced functional residual capacity, and oxygenation. Use uterine relaxation sparingly; avoid hypotension. Maintain normothermia and good perfusion.
- Blood loss preparedness: anticipate heavy bleeding with myomectomy; have crossmatched blood available and be ready for transfusion (this patient required transfusion and iron).
- Surgical strategy: limit operative time, minimize uterine manipulation, control pedicle before cutting, ensure watertight uterine repair if myometrial incisions are made. If possible, stage procedures (remove only the lesion causing acute risk and leave others).
- Antibiotics: treat concurrent infections guided by culture; infection in this case (Klebsiella UTI) influenced perioperative management.
- Progesterone/tocolysis: commonly used in practice but evidence is mixed.
  - This case used intraoperative and then ongoing IM progesterone (hydroxyprogesterone caproate regimen). Current guidelines do not mandate routine prophylactic tocolytics or progesterone for intraoperative uterine manipulation—decision is often individualized.
  - Tocolytics (eg, nifedipine, beta‑agonists) are used for postoperative uterine irritability or threatened preterm labour, not as standardized prophylaxis for all procedures. Discuss risks/benefits with obstetric team.

Pathology and delivery planning
- Expect possible degenerative changes in fibroids; pedunculated myomas can twist and necrose—this explains fever and CRP rise here.
- Residual large myomas can obstruct labour—plan mode of delivery based on location, size, and number of uterine masses (this patient had elective CS at 37 weeks due to a posterior isthmic mass).
- Postpartum regression: many leiomyomas shrink after delivery, which influences whether to schedule additional surgery.

Clinical pearls from this case
- Large, highly vascular, mixed solid-cystic pelvic masses in pregnancy may mimic adnexal pathology (eg, decidualised endometrioma) but can still be uterine in origin—keep pedunculated and degenerating leiomyoma high on the differential.
- CA‑125 can be modestly elevated in pregnancy and with benign conditions; trend and clinical context matter.
- When maternal risk (pain, torsion, infection, respiratory compromise) is high and imaging is inconclusive, timely surgery in the second trimester can preserve both maternal health and the pregnancy.
- Tailor the approach: remove the lesion causing immediate risk and leave stable lesions in situ when possible to minimize obstetric complications.
- Multidisciplinary planning, readiness for hemorrhage, attentive fetal heart assessment, and appropriate perioperative support are essential for successful outcomes.

Take-home messages
- Expectant management is preferred for most pelvic masses in pregnancy; operate when maternal risk or diagnostic uncertainty outweighs surgical risk.
- Second trimester is the optimal window for non-obstetric abdominal surgery when possible.
- Use transvaginal/transabdominal US and non-contrast MRI for preoperative planning; avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary.
- Choose laparoscopy for appropriately sized and located masses with experienced teams; opt for laparotomy for very large, multiple, highly vascular, or suspicious lesions.
- Document fetal heart rate before, during (if feasible), and after surgery; involve obstetrics/neonatology and anesthesia early.
- Prophylactic progesterone/tocolysis is common but remains controversial — individualize decisions and follow institutional practice and obstetric input.

This case is a useful reminder: prioritize maternal safety, use safe imaging, and adopt a pragmatic surgical plan—remove what must be removed, leave what can safely wait, and aim to preserve the pregnancy whenever possible.



# Myomectomy During Pregnancy: Take One, Leave One

What makes this case worth pausing for? A woman in her 40s at 21+ weeks gestation presented with two very large abdominal cystic masses and respiratory compromise from sheer space-occupying effect. Imaging and tumour markers were inconclusive. At laparotomy the “upper” mass — thought possibly ovarian or decidualised endometrioma — turned out to be a pedunculated, twisted, highly vascular uterine leiomyoma that was leaking and likely the source of fever and pain. The team resected that lesion and deliberately left a second large intramural-subserosal myoma in situ to preserve the pregnancy. Mother and baby did well — a tidy example of targeted, risk-balanced surgery in pregnancy.

This case illustrates the core decision-making we face with symptomatic pelvic masses in pregnancy: when to operate, what to remove, and how to plan for the worst while aiming for the best obstetric outcome.

## Why not remove both? The rationale for "take one, leave one"
- One lesion was pedunculated, twisted, leaking, highly vascular and producing acute symptoms (pain, low‑grade fever) plus restrictive dyspnoea from mass effect. These factors raised the immediate risk of torsion, necrosis, infection and further deterioration — reasonable indications for surgical intervention in pregnancy.
- The caudal mass was an adherent intramural‑subserosal leiomyoma fixed to the uterine torus and likely to interfere with labour if left large — but it was not acutely complicated. Removing it would have prolonged the operation, increased uterine manipulation and bleeding risk, and increased chance of pregnancy loss. Leaving it reduced operative time and obstetric risk.
- The operative plan prioritized maternal stability and fetal viability: fix the imminent problem (twisted pedunculated myoma), minimise uterine trauma, and accept staged management postpartum if needed.

That risk‑stratified approach — remove the lesion that threatens mother/fetus now, defer the rest — is the practical essence of “take one, leave one.”

## Key practical points from the case (operative planning and execution)
- Multidisciplinary prep is essential. This team included obstetrics, gynecology, surgery and anesthesiology — coordinate blood availability, neonatal team notification, and agree fetal monitoring plan.
- Imaging: use targeted ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI in pregnancy to characterise masses. Avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary. In this case MRI without contrast helped establish cystic nature and relationships.
- Indications for surgery in pregnancy remain acute symptoms (intractable pain), complications (torsion, rupture, infection, bleeding), diagnostic uncertainty suggesting malignancy, or when mass effect threatens maternal respiratory or circulatory status. This patient had pain, dyspnoea, fever and imaging uncertainty — reasonable grounds for laparotomy.
- Choose approach to minimise operative time and uterine handling: in large, multiple, posterior or intramural lesions and at mid‑pregnancy, open laparotomy may be safer than laparoscopy. Laparoscopy can be considered for smaller, pedunculated or accessible lesions in early gestation.
- Hemorrhage planning:
  - Have cross‑matched blood available and transfusion plan ready. In this case the patient required post‑op transfusion (and IV iron).
  - Prepare uterotonics and surgical hemostatic adjuncts. Use efficient uterine repair (suture the pedicle root promptly and securely after excision).
  - Use techniques to reduce bleeding: double clamps on the pedicle, prompt suture closure of the implantation site, judicious electrosurgery or topical hemostatics as indicated.
- Uterine repair: use absorbable sutures to close the root of the excised stalk; keep sutures neat and minimal to reduce nidus for scar dehiscence while achieving hemostasis. Minimise deep myometrial disruption where possible.
- Intraoperative fetal assessment: document fetal heart rate before, during (intermittently) and after the procedure. Use intraoperative ultrasound if available to confirm fetal cardiac activity and position; continuous fetal monitoring isn’t always possible in mid‑pregnancy so document intermittently.
- Perioperative medications: the team used progesterone prophylaxis intraoperatively then until discharge, and continued with hydroxyprogesterone caproate until elective delivery. Antibiotic prophylaxis tailored to culture results was used. (Follow local protocols — evidence on routine progesterone in this context is limited but was used here as a precautionary measure.)
- Postoperative care: monitor for anaemia, infection and uterine activity. This patient needed IV iron and two transfusions for postoperative Hb nadir. Schedule close obstetric follow‑up with serial ultrasounds for fetal growth and for assessment of the residual myoma.
- Delivery planning: plan the mode of delivery based on residual myoma size, location and effect on the birth canal. In this case the residual posterior isthmic myoma was large and could obstruct labour, so an elective CS at term via the same midline incision was chosen.

## Pearls from this case
- Mass effect (dyspnoea, restricted diaphragmatic excursion) is a valid reason to consider operative intervention in pregnancy when conservative measures fail.
- CA-125 can be mildly elevated in pregnancy and with benign conditions (endometriosis, degenerating myoma, inflammation). Trends, not single values, help. A modest rise alone should not drive radical decisions.
- MRI without gadolinium at 1.5T is a useful adjunct when ultrasound is inconclusive. Avoid contrast agents in pregnancy whenever possible.
- If one mass is acutely compromised (torsed, leaking, infected), resect it alone where feasible. Limiting the procedure lowers operative time and bleeding risk and preserves pregnancy.
- Prepare for blood loss — plan for transfusion, have cell‑saver if available and acceptable, and use hemostatic strategies (clamps, timely suturing, topical agents).
- Document fetal status before, during and after surgery. Use intraop ultrasound when continuous electronic monitoring isn’t practical at mid‑gestation.
- Expectant management of non‑acute myomas is often appropriate; many regress postpartum. Avoid overtreatment.

## Take‑home messages
- Operate in pregnancy only for clear indications (persistent severe symptoms, torsion/rupture/infection, diagnostic uncertainty suggesting malignancy, or mass effect threatening maternal/fetal health).
- Targeted surgery — treat the lesion that is acutely dangerous and leave stable lesions in situ — is often the safest strategy to preserve pregnancy.
- Multidisciplinary planning, blood and hemorrhage preparedness, efficient uterine repair, and intraoperative fetal assessment are the pillars of safe myomectomy in pregnancy.
- Use ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI for characterization; interpret tumour markers cautiously in pregnancy.
- Post‑op follow‑up and delivery planning should be individualized based on residual myoma size/location and obstetric considerations.

This case is a neat reminder: in pregnancy the best surgery is the least surgery that adequately treats the immediate threat. Cut what must be cut, leave what can safely wait, and plan for both the obstetric and surgical consequences.



# Planning the Delivery with Big Fibroids: Mapping the Road to Cesarean

What makes this case worth reading? A woman in her 40s at 21 weeks’ gestation presented with two giant, atypical abdominal masses causing pain and dyspnoea. Imaging and tumor markers were inconclusive; at laparotomy the upper mass turned out to be a hugely vascular, pedunculated cystic leiomyoma (twisted and leaking), which was excised. The lower pelvic mass — adherent to the posterior uterine wall and potentially obstructive for the fetal head — was intentionally left in situ. The pregnancy continued uneventfully to an elective cesarean at 37 weeks with an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome.

This vignette highlights three practical problems we face when planning delivery with large leiomyomas: 1) how imaging and clinical findings should drive delivery mode decisions, 2) when (and when not) to operate antenatally, and 3) how to balance maternal surgical risk with the obstetric need for a safe route of delivery while remembering that many myomas regress postpartum.

## Think first: size, number, location — not just “big vs small”

When counselling mode of delivery, don’t treat all fibroids the same. The factors that most strongly affect the risk of labour obstruction and therefore favour cesarean are:

- Location: lower uterine segment, cervical, isthmic or anterior/lateral submucosal/intramural lesions near the internal os are most likely to physically block the presenting part. Posterior fundal myomas may be less obstructive unless very large or low.
- Number and configuration: a single large, well‑placed myoma can be more obstructive than multiple small ones. A bulky mass in the pelvis (e.g., filling the pouch of Douglas or utero‑cervical region) behaves like a mechanical pelvimetry problem.
- Size and bulk: there’s no absolute size cut‑off, but masses >5–8 cm — and especially those that occupy the pelvic brim or distort the lower uterine segment — raise concern for obstructed labour.
- Mobility and attachment: pedunculated subserosal myomas can move and sometimes be less obstructive, but they carry torsion/necrosis risk. Fixed intramural masses are more likely to obstruct and to complicate labour.

In the reported case, the residual caudal 7.5–9 cm cystic intramural-subserosal myoma in the posterior isthmic/torus region posed a real risk to head engagement — driving the multidisciplinary choice of elective cesarean.

## How to map the problem: pragmatic imaging strategy

Safe, focused imaging is essential for delivery planning — especially in pregnancy:

- Start with high‑quality transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound. Document:
  - Size in three dimensions, relationship to cervix and internal os
  - Distance from uterine cavity and placenta
  - Location (fundal vs anterior vs posterior vs lower segment)
  - Vascularity and any cystic/degenerative change
- Use non‑contrast MRI when ultrasound is inconclusive or anatomy is distorted by gravid uterus or multiple masses. Non‑contrast 1.5T MRI after first trimester is reasonably safe and clarifies:
  - Number and boundaries of myomas
  - Relation to uterine cavity and lower segment
  - Adherence to adnexa or pelvic structures
- Tumor markers (CA‑125) are of limited use in pregnancy: they can be physiologically elevated (peaks in 1st and 3rd trimesters) and give false reassurance or alarm. Serial trends are more informative than a single value.

Document findings clearly in the chart and use them to map the anticipated path of the presenting part — this is the core of delivery planning.

## When to consider antenatal myomectomy

General rule: avoid antenatal myomectomy unless there is clear maternal benefit or imminent threat to the pregnancy.

Consider antenatal surgery if:
- There is torsion/necrosis of a pedunculated subserosal myoma causing persistent pain, fever, sepsis risk (as in this case).
- There is evidence of complication (hemorrhage, bowel obstruction, severe mass effect causing respiratory compromise).
- Conservative care fails and the mass is likely to worsen dramatically with uterine growth.

Key operative principles if you proceed antenatally:
- Multidisciplinary team approach (obstetrics, anaesthesia, surgical backup, neonatology).
- Keep the operation as short and blood‑sparing as possible (clamp pedicle, minimal uterine incision; do not attempt radical resections that prolong the case).
- Continuous fetal monitoring where feasible; document fetal heart rate before, during and after surgery.
- Use prophylactic measures (e.g., perioperative progesterone was used in the case report) as per local practice to reduce perioperative risk of preterm labour. Antibiotic therapy guided by cultures if infection suspected.
- Avoid gadolinium contrast MRI and prefer non‑contrast imaging.

In the case, the team removed only the twisted pedunculated mass and left the lower pelvic myoma in situ — a pragmatic compromise that relieved acute symptoms and preserved the pregnancy while acknowledging the obstetric problem would be managed at delivery.

## Timing and planning the cesarean after antenatal myomectomy

If you leave a potentially obstructive myoma in place, plan delivery thoughtfully:

- Elective cesarean timing: aim for term (≥37 weeks) if maternal and fetal conditions allow. In the case, delivery at 37 weeks minimized preterm risks while avoiding late-term complication of uterine scar.
- Incision planning: previous laparotomy scar and the location of the myoma guide the skin and uterine incision. Many teams re‑use the same skin incision to limit additional scar tissue and adhesions.
- Anticipate distorted anatomy: large posterior or lower segment myomas may require a classical, fundal or J‑extension uterine incision to safely deliver the baby. Have blood products available — myomectomy or delivery with large fibroids can bleed substantially.
- Be prepared for myomectomy at cesarean only when team is comfortable and bleeding risk is controlled. Some centres perform CS myomectomy selectively; others avoid adding myomectomy to cesarean if the myoma is adherent, intramural, or if haemorrhage risk is high. Evidence suggests CS myomectomy can be safe in selected patients, but it increases OR time and blood loss in some series.
- Counsel about future pregnancies and risk of uterine rupture: whenever significant myometrial incisions or myomectomy have been performed, record details (location and depth of uterine incisions, suturing technique) and plan delivery mode in subsequent pregnancies accordingly.

In the presented case, the team elected cesarean at term through the same longitudinal scar and avoided intra‑cesarean myomectomy of the deep posterior mass, which had already partially regressed postpartum.

## Use postpartum regression to defer definitive surgery when appropriate

Remember that many myomas shrink after delivery and with lactation. This means:

- If the myoma is not causing ongoing symptoms, consider postponing definitive surgery (myomectomy or hysterectomy) to the postpartum period to allow natural involution.
- Reassess at 6–12 weeks postpartum with imaging — some masses reduce significantly in size (the case reported partial regression to ~7.5 cm and symptom resolution).
- Deferred surgery may be preferable in women who want more children or who can tolerate an expectant approach.

## Practical clinical pearls from the case

- Large pelvic masses in pregnancy can masquerade as adnexal lesions on imaging; combine careful bimanual exam with targeted US and non‑contrast MRI for best preoperative mapping.
- CA‑125 elevations in pregnancy are nonspecific; interpret trends, not single values.
- Antenatal myomectomy is reasonable for complications (torsion, infection, intractable pain, mass effect) but should be conservative — remove only what is necessary to treat the acute problem and preserve the pregnancy.
- When a potentially obstructive pelvic myoma remains, plan an elective cesarean at term, prepare for altered anatomy and increased bleeding risk, and involve a multidisciplinary team.
- Postpartum reassessment is valuable; many leiomyomas regress and definitive surgery can often be deferred safely.

Take‑home messages

- The delivery mode with leiomyomas should be individualized based on size, number, location and mobility — not solely on diameter.
- High‑quality ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI are your friends for surgical planning; use them early.
- Antenatal surgery is reserved for complications; when performed, keep it focused and time‑efficient to preserve the pregnancy.
- If obstruction risk persists, schedule an elective cesarean at term with appropriate blood and surgical backup.
- Consider postpartum regression — reassess before committing to definitive surgery.

This case is a neat reminder that “big fibroid” is not an automatic cesarean label nor an absolute indication for antenatal hysterectomy — careful mapping, selective intervention, and staged management can safely navigate mother and baby through delivery.



## Pregnant, Infected, and Anemic: Perioperative Housekeeping That Changes Outcomes

What makes this case worth your attention? A woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, presenting with two very large abdominal masses, mild dyspnea from mass effect, a urinary tract infection with Klebsiella, iron deficiency anemia, and rising inflammatory markers. The team faced a classic set of interacting problems that frequently complicate decision-making in pregnant patients who need non‑obstetric surgery: how to treat infection safely in pregnancy, choose perioperative antibiotics, interpret inflammatory labs that are physiologically altered by pregnancy, correct iron deficiency in a timely and safe way, decide transfusion thresholds and targets, and put in place postpartum follow-up that protects both mother and fetus.

Below are practical takeaways distilled from the case and current practice — the stuff that prevents avoidable morbidity.

## Key perioperative infectious disease housekeeping

- Treat the infection, but treat it with pregnancy-safe agents and tailor therapy to culture sensitivities.
  - In this case the urine culture grew Klebsiella highly sensitive to cephalosporins and clarithromycin. They used cefazolin perioperatively and continued cefazolin + clarithromycin per sensitivity — a defensible plan.
  - General principles:
    - Use beta-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins) when possible — they are safe in pregnancy and cover most uropathogens.
    - Avoid fluoroquinolones (tendinopathy, cartilage concerns) and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole in the first trimester (folate antagonism) and near term (risk of kernicterus, unless absolutely necessary).
    - Nitrofurantoin is commonly used for UTI in pregnancy (avoid close to term in G6PD deficiency and with jaundice risk).
    - Macrolides: azithromycin preferred if needed; clarithromycin is used but azithromycin has a more established safety profile in pregnancy.
  - Always switch to targeted therapy once culture/antibiogram returns.

- Perioperative prophylaxis in pregnancy follows the same evidence-based rules as in non-pregnant patients, with pregnancy-specific dose adjustments:
  - Cefazolin remains the antibiotic of choice for routine surgical prophylaxis in obstetric/gynecologic surgery.
  - Dose: 2 g IV pre-incision for most adults; consider 3 g for very obese patients (≥120 kg). Re-dose intraoperatively if the procedure exceeds 3–4 hours or blood loss is major.
  - Time administration to within 60 minutes before skin incision.

## Interpreting inflammatory markers in pregnancy — trend over numbers

- Pregnancy changes the baseline.
  - WBC: physiologically higher (especially neutrophils) — a mild leukocytosis is common.
  - CRP: can be mildly elevated; there’s no pregnancy‑specific “normal” CRP but small rises occur.
  - Tumor markers (e.g., CA‑125): fluctuate with gestational age — CA‑125 can be elevated in early and late pregnancy and with benign gynecologic disease.
- Practical approach:
  - Use trends, not single values. Rising CRP/WBC or a change from baseline plus clinical signs (fever, focal pain, hemodynamic instability) should guide action.
  - Combine labs with imaging and clinical exam. In this case, fever, leukocytosis, a suspected leaking/twisted vascular pedicle and inhomogeneous MRI supported proceeding to surgery.

## Correcting iron deficiency pre- and post-op: timing and options

- Iron deficiency is common in pregnancy and matters perioperatively — anemia worsens oxygen delivery to mother and fetus and increases transfusion risk.
- Options:
  - Oral iron: first-line for nonurgent correction, but slow and often poorly tolerated.
  - IV iron: fast and effective when rapid repletion is needed or when oral iron fails or surgery is imminent. Preparations such as ferric carboxymaltose allow 1,000 mg doses and are increasingly used in pregnancy with reassuring safety data.
- In the case: Hb 9.9 g/dL preop, dropped to 7.5 g/dL post-op → IV iron (1 g ferric carboxymaltose) plus two units transfused, with Hb improving to 10 g/dL. That’s pragmatic: IV iron to rebuild stores and transfusion to get immediate oxygen-carrying capacity.

## Transfusion thresholds and targets around non‑obstetric surgery in pregnancy

- There is no absolute number that fits every situation — balance maternal symptoms, fetal needs, ongoing bleeding risk, and hemodynamics.
  - For stable, nonbleeding patients, a restrictive threshold (transfuse at Hb <7 g/dL) is commonly used in general surgery. Pregnancy often prompts a more liberal approach because fetal oxygenation depends on maternal oxygen delivery.
  - Reasonable practical targets:
    - If symptomatic (tachycardia, hypotension, chest pain, dyspnea) or ongoing bleeding: transfuse sooner.
    - Many clinicians aim for Hb ≥8–9 g/dL in the perioperative pregnant patient; in selected cases (cardiac disease, severe fetal compromise), higher targets may be reasonable.
- Documentation and shared decision-making are critical — discuss maternal and fetal risks with the team and the patient.

## Intra‑ and post‑operative fetal protection and monitoring

- Timing and type of surgery: second trimester is the safest window for non‑obstetric surgery when possible; in this case the team operated at ~22 weeks.
- Fetal monitoring:
  - Document fetal heart rate before, during (if feasible), and after the procedure. Continuous intraoperative monitoring is not always possible (gestational age, position), but intermittent checks or ultrasound verification help.
  - Consult obstetrics and neonatology early.
- Pharmacologic adjuncts:
  - Short-course progesterone was used here intra- and post-op as a prophylactic measure — practices vary, and there’s limited high‑quality evidence, but it’s reasonable in select cases aiming to reduce the risk of threatened preterm labor.
- Thromboprophylaxis:
  - Pregnancy is hypercoagulable. Assess VTE risk postoperatively; consider mechanical and pharmacologic prophylaxis unless contraindicated.

## Practical postoperative follow-up that matters

- Ensure culture-guided antibiotics are continued for the appropriate duration; stop unnecessary broad-spectrum agents to avoid microbiome harm and resistance.
- Reassess Hb and iron indices before discharge and plan for follow-up iron repletion if IV iron was given.
- Schedule early obstetric follow-up (serial growth scans / AFI) — large masses, surgery and perioperative anemia can affect fetal growth.
- Document the residual mass(es) and plan delivery mode considering myoma size/location — as in this case, the residual posterior myoma made elective cesarean reasonable at term.
- Mental health and breastfeeding support — major surgery in pregnancy is stressful; screen and support.

## Clinical pearls / take-home messages

- Infected pregnant patients who need surgery are not rare — treat infection promptly with pregnancy-safe, culture-directed antibiotics and give surgical prophylaxis as you would in non‑pregnant patients, adjusting dose for weight and timing appropriately.
- Pregnancy alters baseline labs — focus on trends and clinical context, not single CRP/WBC/CA‑125 numbers.
- If rapid correction of iron deficiency is needed around surgery, IV iron (e.g., ferric carboxymaltose) is effective and increasingly supported in pregnancy; transfuse based on symptoms, hemodynamics, and fetal considerations — many teams use a higher transfusion threshold in pregnancy than in non‑pregnant adults.
- Second trimester remains the preferred timing for non‑obstetric surgery when feasible; coordinate obstetric monitoring and neonatal planning early.
- Good perioperative “housekeeping” — appropriate antibiotic selection and dosing, targeted infection control, iron repletion, thoughtful transfusion strategy, and structured follow-up — changes outcomes for both mother and baby.

This case reminds us that the technical surgical decision is only part of the story. The quieter, systematic pieces — right antibiotic, right iron strategy, careful interpretation of labs, and structured fetal surveillance — are what make surgery in pregnancy safe and successful.
