Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

### [Clinical Pearls] In a second-trimester pregnant patient with large symptomatic abdominopelvic masses, which imaging modalities are preferred for characterization, what safety considerations apply (including recommended magnet strength and the use of gadolinium), and what added value can non-contrast MRI provide beyond ultrasound?

Direct answer
- First-line imaging for large symptomatic abdominopelvic masses in the second trimester is transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound with colour Doppler; non‑contrast MRI is the preferred second‑line study for further characterisation. CT and contrast studies are avoided when possible because they use ionizing radiation or intravenous gadolinium.
- Safety considerations: MRI without gadolinium is considered acceptably safe in pregnancy and was appropriately used in this case. Most centres prefer a 1.5‑T magnet in pregnant patients (the team in the report specifically avoided >1.5 T). Intravenous gadolinium crosses the placenta and should be avoided in pregnancy unless the diagnostic information cannot be obtained any other way and the benefit outweighs the theoretical fetal risk; routine gadolinium is not recommended.
- Added value of non‑contrast MRI beyond ultrasound: superior tissue characterisation, precise anatomic mapping (number, size, intramural vs subserosal vs pedunculated), evaluation of degeneration/haemorrhage/necrosis, detection of cleavage planes and continuity with the uterus, assessment of relationship to the uterine cavity and placenta, and improved surgical planning — all without ionizing radiation or gadolinium exposure.

Clinical reasoning and pathophysiology (with reference to the case)
- Start with ultrasound: it is bedside, fast, safe and excellent for initial triage (solid vs cystic, vascularity with Doppler, approximate size and pelvic origin). In the case report ultrasound identified two large unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularisation and suggested the caudal lesion was adherent to a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma.
- When ultrasound is limited by body habitus, gravid uterus, large lesion size, posterior location or indeterminate tissue features, non‑contrast MRI adds important information. MRI sequences (T1, T2, fat‑suppressed, diffusion‑weighted imaging) allow characterisation of internal contents (blood products, proteinaceous/decidualised material, cystic degeneration, solid components) and depict thickened walls, focal solid nodules, and vascular pedicles. In this patient MRI showed thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid content and focal T1/T2 hyperintense solid components and suggested continuity of the caudal mass with the posterior leiomyoma — findings that narrowed the differential between decidualised endometrioma and cystic degenerating leiomyoma and guided management.
- MRI also clarifies surgical anatomy: it can show whether a mass is pedunculated (risk of torsion), whether it communicates with the uterine wall (myoma vs adnexal), and absence/presence of adenopathy or invasive features. These data informed the team’s decision to remove the twisted pedunculated uterine mass and leave the adherent pelvic myoma in situ to minimise obstetric risk.

Safety details to remember
- Timing: MRI can be performed in any trimester when clinically indicated; many teams perform non‑contrast MRI after the first trimester when practicable, but urgent indications may warrant earlier imaging.
- Magnet strength: 1.5 T is the customary and conservative choice in pregnancy; routine avoidance of higher field strengths (3 T) is common practice in many centres because of more limited safety data and higher specific‑absorption rates.
- Gadolinium: crosses the placenta and is retained longer in the fetal compartment; observational data suggest a small association with adverse neonatal outcomes in some cohorts. Use only when essential for management and when no alternative sequence or imaging will provide the diagnosis; if used, document informed consent and use the lowest effective dose and preferred agents per local policy.
- No ionizing radiation: MRI avoids radiation exposure and is preferable to CT when soft‑tissue characterisation is required.

Practical clinical pearl
- Use ultrasound first for rapid assessment and Doppler evaluation; proceed to non‑contrast MRI (1.5 T, tailored T1/T2/fat‑sat/DWI sequences) when ultrasound is inconclusive or when precise anatomic mapping will change management. Avoid gadolinium in pregnancy unless there is a compelling, documented indication and the benefit clearly outweighs risk. The case illustrates how non‑contrast MRI added decisive information (inhomogeneous contents, solid nodularity, uterine continuity) that helped distinguish cystic degenerating leiomyoma from adnexal pathology and guided a safe, conservative surgical approach.

### [Clinical Pearls] How should CA-125 be interpreted during pregnancy: what are the expected trimester-specific trends, common benign causes of elevation (for example, endometriosis, leiomyoma, infection, menstruation), and why are serial measurements more informative than a single value when malignancy is a concern?

Direct answer: CA‑125 is of limited specificity in pregnancy and must be interpreted in the context of gestational age, symptoms and imaging. Pregnancy produces physiologic changes in CA‑125 (peaks in the first and third trimesters), and many benign gynecologic and inflammatory conditions commonly raise CA‑125; therefore a single mildly elevated value should not be taken as evidence of malignancy. Serial measurements (and correlation with ultrasound/MRI and the clinical course) are far more informative for distinguishing benign, transient or inflammatory causes from a progressive malignant process.

Why — trimester‑specific trends
- Population studies show a biphasic pattern in pregnancy: a relative peak in the first trimester (median ≈23 U/mL), lower values in the second trimester (median ≈14 U/mL) and a smaller rise again in the third trimester (median ≈21 U/mL). These medians are below the conventional nonpregnant ovarian‑cancer cut‑off (≥35 U/mL), but physiologic elevations do occur, especially early and late in pregnancy.
- Thus a value that would be abnormal outside pregnancy may be within the expected physiologic range for a given trimester, and the magnitude of any elevation must be judged against trimester norms.

Common benign causes of CA‑125 elevation and mechanisms
- Decidualised endometriosis/endometrioma and adenomyosis: CA‑125 is produced by coelomic/Müllerian epithelium; active endometriotic tissue or decidualisation increases serum levels.
- Uterine leiomyomas (especially degenerating, necrotic or torsed pedunculated myomas): myoma degeneration or torsion causes local inflammation and peritoneal irritation that raise CA‑125.
- Menstruation: transient peritoneal irritation during menses raises CA‑125.
- Pelvic or systemic infection/inflammation (PID, pelvic abscess, urinary infection with peritoneal reaction, peritonitis): inflammatory cytokines and mesothelial activation increase CA‑125.
- Non‑gynecologic causes: pleural disease, pericarditis and other serosal inflammation.
In short, any process that irritates serosal or Müllerian epithelium can raise CA‑125.

Why serial measurements are more informative than a single value
- Overlap: CA‑125 values for benign conditions (including normal pregnancy) and for early malignancy overlap substantially. A single mildly elevated value (for example the patient’s 41.8 U/mL in the second trimester) cannot reliably distinguish benign from malignant causes.
- Kinetics matter: malignant ovarian tumors tend to produce a persistent and often faster rise in CA‑125; benign or physiologic elevations are often static or fall over time as the triggering stimulus resolves (for example, after treatment of infection or resolution of torsion/degeneration).
- Clinical correlation: trends should be interpreted together with serial imaging (US/MRI) and the clinical picture (pain, fever, rapid growth, ascites, adenopathy). Algorithms developed for nonpregnant women (RMI, ROMA) are not validated in pregnancy.
- Practical consequence illustrated by this case: the patient’s CA‑125 rose from 36 to 41.8 U/mL over two weeks—an incremental rise that raised concern but was non‑diagnostic. The decision to operate was driven by symptoms, imaging features (large vascularised mixed cystic/solid masses, unclear cleavage), and risk of acute complications. Final pathology showed ischemic, degenerating/torsed leiomyoma (a benign explanation for the CA‑125 rise), underscoring that a single elevated value would have been misleading without serial data and clinical/imaging context.

Clinical pearl (takeaway)
- Always interpret CA‑125 in pregnancy by trimester and in the context of the patient’s symptoms and imaging. Mild elevations are common and nonspecific; serial CA‑125 measurements (short interval, paired with repeat ultrasound/MRI when indicated) help distinguish transient inflammatory or physiologic elevations from a progressive pattern more suggestive of malignancy, and thus avoid unnecessary interventions while allowing timely surgery when clinical or imaging features mandate it.

### [Differential Diagnosis] A pregnant patient at 22 weeks presents with a large unilocular cystic mass adjacent to the gravid uterus and mild CA-125 elevation. What are the leading diagnostic considerations (for example, decidualized endometrioma, cystic degeneration of a uterine leiomyoma, epithelial ovarian neoplasm), and which clinical or imaging features push you toward one diagnosis versus another?

Direct answer: The leading considerations for a large unilocular cystic mass adjacent to a gravid uterus at 22 weeks are (1) decidualized ovarian endometrioma, (2) cystic (degenerating or red‑degenerating) uterine leiomyoma (including pedunculated/subserosal myoma), and (3) a cystic epithelial ovarian neoplasm. Distinguishing features come from history/exam, ultrasound/Doppler, non‑contrast MRI signal patterns, CA‑125 magnitude/trend, and dynamic behavior (pain, fever, torsion).

How to weigh each diagnosis (practical discriminators, with reference to the case):

1) Decidualized endometrioma
- What favors it:
  - Typical patient history of endometriosis (may be absent).
  - Ultrasound: unilocular cyst with internal low‑level echoes and a vascularized mural nodule that appears “polypoid.”
  - MRI: intrinsic T1 hyperintensity from blood products and the classic “T2 shading” (progressive T2 hypointensity) for chronic blood; mural decidual nodules can be T2 hyperintense and may enhance (gadolinium is usually avoided in pregnancy).
  - CA‑125 can be mildly elevated (but is nonspecific in pregnancy).
- What argues against it in this case:
  - MRI and US showed a mass that appeared continuous with the uterus (especially the caudal lesion), and no prior history of endometriosis reported.
  - The clinical picture of acute pain, low‑grade fever and intraoperative finding of a pedicle/torsion are more typical of a uterine myoma complication.

2) Cystic or degenerating uterine leiomyoma (including pedunculated/subserosal)
- What favors it:
  - Bimanual exam and imaging showing contiguity with the uterus or a mass at the uterine surface; presence of a connecting stalk or a “bridging vessel” on Doppler strongly supports uterine origin.
  - MRI: leiomyomas classically low T2 signal, but cystic or red degeneration produces inhomogeneous fluid signal (high T2), thick walls, areas of hemorrhage/necrosis, and lack of fatty/blood‑product pattern specific to endometrioma.
  - Clinical: acute, localized pain, low‑grade fever, anemia and tenderness are classic for red degeneration or torsion of a pedunculated fibroid.
  - Perioperative confirmation: pedunculated, highly vascular mass with twisted stalk—as was found in this case.
- In the case: the caudal mass had clear continuity with a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma on MRI, both masses were highly vascularized, the patient had acute pain and fever, and surgery demonstrated a pedunculated fibroid with torsion and ischemic necrosis—all pointing to leiomyoma with complication (cystic/red degeneration and pedicle torsion).

3) Cystic epithelial ovarian neoplasm
- What favors it:
  - Complex cystic mass with thick irregular septations, papillary excrescences or nodular solid components on US/MRI; bilateral adnexal involvement, ascites, peritoneal implants or adenopathy increase suspicion.
  - Markedly elevated CA‑125 (though pregnancy raises CA‑125 and moderates specificity).
  - Lack of clear continuity with the uterus on exam or imaging; an ovary separate from the uterus on transvaginal sonography.
- What argues against it in this case:
  - No ascites or adenopathy on MRI, tumour markers other than CA‑125 were normal, and MRI suggested uterine contiguity for the lower mass; the upper mass turned out intraoperatively to be pedunculated from the uterus. CA‑125 was only mildly elevated (41.8 U/mL) and rising modestly—nonspecific in pregnancy.

Practical imaging and exam tips to discriminate:
- Localize origin first: transvaginal/transabdominal ultrasound with careful search for a connecting stalk or “bridging vessel” to the myometrium; a mass that moves with the uterus or deforms uterine contour suggests uterine origin.
- Doppler: vascularized mural nodules occur both in decidualized endometriomas and in complicated fibroids; pattern and relation to uterus are key.
- MRI without gadolinium in pregnancy: T1 hyperintensity that persists on fat‑sat sequences → blood product (endometrioma or hemorrhagic degeneration); T2 shading → endometrioma; heterogeneous high T2 with thick walls, areas of hemorrhage/necrosis and loss of normal low‑T2 fibroid signal → cystic or red‑degenerating leiomyoma. Continuity with myometrium on MRI favors leiomyoma.
- CA‑125: interpret cautiously in pregnancy—mild elevations are common; trending and magnitude help but are not definitive.

Bottom line applied to the case: the mix of acute pain, fever, anemia, imaging evidence of uterine continuity (caudal lesion), marked vascularity and the intraoperative finding of a twisted pedunculated mass all point to uterine leiomyomas with cystic/red degeneration and torsion rather than decidualized endometrioma or ovarian malignancy. Surgical intervention was justified by symptom severity and risk of acute complication.

### [Clinical Pearls] On ultrasound or MRI, what specific signs help localize a large pelvic mass to uterine versus ovarian origin in pregnancy (for example, bridging vessels from the uterine artery on Doppler/MRI, continuity with myometrium or a pedicle, identification of a normal ipsilateral ovary, the ovarian crescent sign), and how would each influence management?

Bottom line
- On pregnancy ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI, the single most useful clues that a large pelvic mass is uterine (leiomyoma) rather than ovarian are: direct continuity with the myometrium (claw sign/“bulging” myometrium), a pedicle or stalk, and vascular connections to the uterine arterial system (bridging‑vessel or vascular pedicle sign on color Doppler/MRI). Findings that favour ovarian origin are visualization of the normal ipsilateral ovary separate from the mass and the ovarian crescent sign (rim of ovarian tissue abutting a cyst). Each sign changes preoperative planning and management (expectation of hemorrhage, likelihood of conservative management, timing and indication for surgery).  

Key imaging signs and what they mean for management
- Continuity with myometrium (claw sign, myometrial “cleavage”): when the mass appears to be part of or continuous with the uterine wall on ultrasound or MRI, a uterine origin is very likely. Clinical consequence: most intramural/subserosal leiomyomas are managed conservatively in pregnancy unless they cause severe pain, torsion, infection, obstruct delivery, or necrosis; if surgery is required, anticipate a myometrial repair rather than adnexal resection and prepare for potential bleeding and fetal monitoring.

- Pedicle/stalk (visible on US or MRI): a pedunculated subserosal fibroid often demonstrates a discrete stalk attaching to the uterus. Clinical consequence: pedunculated fibroids can torsion, become ischemic/infected and often mandate surgical removal in pregnancy if symptomatic (as in this case, where the cranial mass proved to be a twisted, pedunculated leiomyoma). Knowing a stalk exists helps plan a focused myomectomy with control of the root.

- Bridging vessels / vascular pedicle sign on color Doppler or MR angiography sequences: vessels running between uterus and mass (feeding from uterine arteries) strongly favour uterine origin. Clinical consequence: confirms uterine supply → anticipate heavy vascularity at surgery, consider cross‑matched blood, hemostatic strategies and uterine‑sparing repair; helps differentiate from ovarian masses which are usually supplied by ovarian vessels.

- Myoma MRI signal pattern: classic leiomyomas are T2‑hypointense relative to myometrium; degenerating/cystic fibroids have mixed signal but retain continuity with uterus. Clinical consequence: signal patterns plus continuity support conservative management unless complications occur.

- Visualization of a normal ipsilateral ovary separate from the mass: seeing the ovary distinct from the lesion makes ovarian origin likely. Clinical consequence: ovarian cysts in pregnancy that are simple and small can be observed; large, complex, symptomatic, torsed or suspicious lesions require surgical management (adnexectomy or cystectomy) with oncologic precautions when indicated.

- Ovarian crescent sign: a rim or crescent of ovarian tissue around a cystic lesion supports ovarian origin. Clinical consequence: if benign‑appearing, can often be observed; if complex or growing, surgical intervention is more likely.

Practical caveats illustrated by the case
- Imaging can be limited in pregnancy by uterine enlargement and mass bulk. In this patient the large cranial lesion was mischaracterized preoperatively as likely ovarian because a pedicle and feeding vessel were not identified on imaging; intraoperatively it was a pedunculated, twisted uterine leiomyoma. That teaches that absence of a visible stalk/bridging vessel does not exclude uterine origin when anatomy is distorted.  
- The caudal mass showed apparent continuity with a posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma on imaging and was left in situ to avoid obstetric risk—an example of how demonstrating uterine continuity can push toward expectant management during pregnancy.

Imaging strategy in pregnancy
- Start with targeted transvaginal and transabdominal ultrasound with color Doppler: seek a pedicle, bridging vessels, a claw of myometrium, and visualize ovaries.  
- Use non‑contrast MRI (1.5 T, no gadolinium) when ultrasound is inconclusive to demonstrate myometrial continuity, stalks, and tissue signal characteristics.  
- Do not rely solely on CA‑125 or tumor markers in pregnancy; they are often non‑specific.

How findings change management
- Uterine origin with continuity/pedicle/bridging vessels → usually conservative; intervene surgically only for complications (torsion, severe degeneration/infection, refractory pain, obstruction of labour). If surgery is done, prepare for uterine bleeding and fetal monitoring.  
- Ovarian origin with separate normal ovary/ovarian crescent → small, simple cysts may be observed; complex, large, torsed or suspicious lesions require timely surgical management with considerations for oncologic staging if features suggest malignancy.  
- Indeterminate imaging + symptoms or signs of complication (fever, persistent severe pain, respiratory compromise from mass effect) → multidisciplinary discussion and early surgical exploration may be required, accepting that intraoperative findings may differ from preop imaging (as in this report).

Clinical pearl
- Look first for continuity with the uterus (claw sign/pedicle) and for bridging vessels on Doppler; these two findings most reliably point to a leiomyoma and change the risk profile (vascular uterine supply, myometrial repair, obstetric implications). If neither is demonstrable and the ovary is seen apart from the lesion or an ovarian crescent is present, consider ovarian origin and manage according to ovarian‑mass principles. Always integrate imaging with symptoms and multidisciplinary risk assessment when deciding on surgery in pregnancy.

### [Management] What are the accepted indications to operate on a pelvic mass or leiomyoma during pregnancy (for example, torsion, rupture, infection, intractable pain, obstructive symptoms, or strong oncologic suspicion), and what timing and operative principles minimize maternal–fetal risk?

Short answer
Operate in pregnancy only for true indications: acute complications (torsion, rupture, hemorrhage), uncontrolled infection of the mass, severe intractable pain or cardiorespiratory/urinary obstruction from mass effect, or strong oncologic concern that cannot be resolved non‑operatively. When surgery is required, the second trimester (mid‑trimester) is the usual window for elective procedures; emergencies are managed regardless of gestation. Minimize maternal–fetal risk by multidisciplinary planning, limiting the procedure to what is necessary, choosing the safest surgical approach for the size/location, meticulous haemostasis, intra‑ and postoperative fetal monitoring, and perioperative measures to preserve uterine quiescence and hemodynamic stability.

Clinical reasoning and principles (with points illustrated by the case)
1) Accepted indications to operate
- Acute complications: torsion of a pedunculated mass, rupture with haemorrhage, or infected/necrotic leiomyoma that risks sepsis — these are accepted urgent indications. In the case, the cranial mass was a highly vascular, twisted (torsed) pedunculated leiomyoma with leakage and features consistent with ischemic necrosis and infection; that justified resection.
- Intractable symptoms or organ compromise: severe, progressive pain, restrictive dyspnoea or urinary/intestinal obstruction that cannot be controlled expectantly. The patient’s worsening pain and dyspnoea from mass effect were important drivers for intervention.
- Strong oncologic suspicion or diagnostic uncertainty that cannot be resolved with safe imaging and would change management—surgery may be required for diagnosis and cure. In this case a rising CA‑125 and mixed solid/cystic imaging features contributed to diagnostic uncertainty and factored into the decision.
- Elective myomectomy for asymptomatic leiomyomas is generally deferred to the postpartum period because of risks (bleeding, preterm labour, pregnancy loss). The multidisciplinary team in this report therefore removed only the dangerous pedunculated lesion and left the adherent pelvic myoma in situ.

2) Timing
- Elective procedures that can be delayed are usually scheduled in the second trimester (classically 14–28 weeks) because organogenesis is complete and the risk of spontaneous abortion is lower than in the first trimester, and the uterus is not yet at its maximal size as in the third trimester. The team operated at 21+6 weeks, a typical mid‑trimester timing for an elective/urgent procedure.
- Emergency surgery (torsion, rupture, severe infection, uncontrolled haemorrhage) must be performed regardless of gestational age.

3) Preoperative evaluation and imaging
- Use pelvic/abdominal ultrasound with Doppler as first line; add non‑contrast MRI if further characterization is needed. Avoid gadolinium unless the benefit clearly outweighs fetal risk. In the case, ultrasound followed by non‑contrast MRI clarified the anatomy without contrast exposure.
- Laboratory evaluation (CBC, blood type and crossmatch) and selective tumour markers can inform the probability of malignancy, but markers such as CA‑125 are physiologically altered in pregnancy and interpreted cautiously; the slight CA‑125 rise in this patient contributed to concern but was not diagnostic.

4) Operative approach and intraoperative principles to minimize risk
- Multidisciplinary team: obstetrics, gynecologic surgery, anesthesia, and neonatology must be involved. Obtain informed consent that includes risk of pregnancy loss, preterm labour and need for transfusion or obstetric interventions.
- Choose approach based on size/location and surgeon experience: laparoscopy can be safe early in pregnancy for small, accessible lesions (Hasson/open entry preferential), but large, multiple, posterior or adherent masses frequently necessitate laparotomy — as in this report where the large, vascular masses prompted a longitudinal laparotomy.
- Limit the operation to what is necessary: remove the offending lesion(s) only. In this case the team excised the torsed pedunculated myoma and left the large adherent pelvic myoma in situ to avoid undue uterine trauma.
- Minimize uterine manipulation and incision length; keep operative time short to reduce fetal stress. Use careful technique for pedicle control (double clamp or ligation), and close the uterine or stalk defect with absorbable suture to restore integrity.
- Meticulous haemostasis and blood preparedness: crossmatch blood, have cell salvage or transfusion capability. Be prepared for significant bleeding during myomectomy.
- Anesthesia goals: maintain uteroplacental perfusion (avoid hypotension), position the patient with left lateral tilt, and avoid teratogenic drugs. Document fetal heart activity preoperatively, intra‑operatively as feasible, and postoperatively — the team followed this principle per cited recommendations.
- Tocolysis/prophylaxis: routine prophylactic tocolytics are not universally recommended, but many teams give perioperative progesterone or tocolytics selectively. The team used intramuscular progesterone intra‑ and post‑operatively until delivery.
- Antibiotics: perioperative prophylaxis and targeted therapy for documented infection (the patient had Klebsiella UTI and received cefazolin and clarithromycin guided by cultures).

5) Postoperative care and delivery planning
- Monitor for preterm labour, fetal growth, and maternal anaemia/infection. Correct anaemia promptly as the team did with IV iron and transfusion.
- Decide delivery mode based on residual myomas’ size/location and obstetric considerations. A large residual myoma that obstructs the birth canal may mandate elective cesarean; here the residual pelvic myoma predicted obstructed labour and the team planned an elective CS at 37 weeks via the same scar, with good outcome.

Bottom line
Surgery in pregnancy is justified for acute complications (torsion, rupture, infection), severe mass effect not amenable to conservative care, or compelling oncologic concern. When required, aim to operate in the safest window (mid‑second trimester if elective), limit the operation to the minimum necessary, use appropriate imaging (US and non‑contrast MRI), plan with a multidisciplinary team, secure haemostasis and fetal monitoring, and individualize perioperative measures (antibiotics, progesterone/tocolysis) to preserve pregnancy and reduce maternal and fetal risk. The presented case exemplifies these principles: targeted resection of a torsed, leaking pedunculated leiomyoma in the mid‑second trimester preserved the pregnancy and led to a favorable maternal and neonatal outcome.

### [Morning Report] At 22 weeks’ gestation, imaging suggests an 18-cm pedunculated subserosal mass with torsion and a second large intramural posterior fibroid. What operative strategy would you choose (extent of resection, laparotomy versus laparoscopy, whether to leave the intramural lesion in situ), and what factors drive that decision?

Direct answer
- Remove the pedunculated, torsed 18‑cm subserosal myoma (myomectomy) and leave the large posterior intramural/subserosal fibroid in situ.  
- Approach by midline laparotomy rather than laparoscopy in this case.  

Why — clinical reasoning tied to the case
1. Indications for operative intervention in pregnancy
   - Operate when there is a clear complication or failed conservative care: acute painful torsion/necrosis, active infection, leak/hemorrhage, respiratory compromise from mass effect, or unresolved diagnostic/oncologic uncertainty. In this case the patient had severe pain, dyspnoea from mass effect, low‑grade fever, imaging suggesting torsion/leak and a small rise in CA‑125 — all reasonable indications to intervene in the second trimester.

2. Extent of resection: remove the torsed pedunculated lesion; leave the intramural lesion
   - Pedunculated subserosal myomas with torsion have a high and immediate risk of ischemia, infection, sepsis and worsening maternal compromise; excision removes that risk and is relatively straightforward because the stalk localizes the vascular pedicle. In the report the cranial mass was twisted, leaking and highly vascularized and was safely excised with stump ligation.  
   - Intramural (and broad subserosal) fibroids that are large but fixed and not actively necrotic carry a much higher operative risk for hemorrhage, uterine trauma, pregnancy loss, preterm labour and future scar complications if removed. The posterior intramural/subserosal 58×71 mm lesion in this patient was fixed and would have required more extensive dissection; leaving it in situ minimized obstetric risk while preserving pregnancy — and this is what was done successfully.

3. Choice of laparotomy versus laparoscopy
   - Laparotomy is preferred when the mass is very large (>10–15 cm), highly vascular, leaking/torsed, multiple, or when exposure and rapid hemorrhage control are anticipated to be difficult. The 18‑cm, highly vascular, leaking, torsed mass in this case and the imperfect preoperative localization made laparotomy the safer option; the team used a longitudinal xipho‑pubic incision for maximal exposure.  
   - Laparoscopy can be considered for smaller pedunculated lesions, earlier gestation, experienced laparoscopic teams, and when adequate visualization and prompt control of bleeding are feasible (open Hasson entry, low pneumoperitoneum pressures, readiness to convert). It was not optimal here because of size, vascularity and diagnostic uncertainty.

Perioperative measures and planning (key principles)
- Multidisciplinary planning (obstetrics, gynecologic surgery, anesthesia, neonatology), informed consent discussing risk of fetal loss/preterm labour and need for transfusion.  
- Continuous fetal monitoring (document fetal heart rate before, during and after procedure) when possible for gestational age, and obstetrical support in theatre. The team documented fetal cardiac activity intraoperatively in the case.  
- Minimize operative time and uterine manipulation; secure hemostasis at the pedicle (double clamp and suture closure as used), avoid extensive myometrial dissection.  
- Have blood products and uterotonics/tocolytics/progesterone available per local practice — the team used intraoperative progesterone prophylaxis and had transfusion when postoperative anaemia developed.  
- Perioperative antibiotics guided by infection findings (urine culture in this patient showed Klebsiella and antibiotics were chosen accordingly).  
- Plan delivery mode postpartum based on residual myoma size/location; a large posterior myoma obstructing the birth canal may necessitate elective cesarean (as occurred at 37 weeks in the report).

Bottom line
- In a 22‑week pregnancy with an 18‑cm torsed, highly vascular pedunculated subserosal myoma and a second large posterior intramural fibroid, the safest and most appropriate strategy is open myomectomy of the torsed pedunculated mass via laparotomy and conservative management (leave in situ) of the intramural lesion. The decision is driven by symptom severity, immediate complication risk (torsion/necrosis/infection), lesion size and vascularity, gestational age, technical feasibility, and the overriding goal of preserving the pregnancy while minimizing maternal morbidity.

### [Management] During non-obstetric surgery in the second trimester, what fetal monitoring is recommended before, during, and after the procedure, and what measures can be used to maintain uterine quiescence (for example, positioning, analgesia, judicious tocolysis or progesterone), including the strength of evidence supporting each?

Short answer
- Before surgery: document fetal viability and gestational age; if fetus is potentially viable and delivery would be possible should a catastrophe occur, involve obstetrics/neonatology and obtain baseline fetal heart rate (FHR) tracing or ultrasound cardiac activity.  
- During surgery: if fetus is at or beyond the institution’s threshold of viability and an emergent cesarean is feasible, continuous external fetal monitoring (CTG or Doppler) is recommended. If the fetus is previable or continuous monitoring is not feasible (early second trimester, surgical field, sterility constraints), intermittent assessment of fetal cardiac activity with ultrasound (transabdominal or transvaginal) is acceptable.  
- After surgery: re-document fetal heart activity immediately and monitor for uterine activity/late fetal compromise for an appropriate interval (commonly several hours; longer if contractions, bleeding, or maternal instability).  
- Measures to maintain uterine quiescence: left lateral tilt (or at least 15° left uterine displacement), minimization of uterine manipulation and operative time, good maternal oxygenation, normotension and normocapnia, adequate perioperative analgesia/anesthesia; reserve tocolytics for documented uterine contractions or threatened preterm labour (nifedipine first-line where appropriate); routine prophylactic tocolysis is not supported. Progesterone as routine perioperative prophylaxis is not evidence-based and should be individualized (considered by some teams by analogy with preterm-prevention strategies, but data are limited to case reports/series). Overall strength of evidence is low (observational data and expert consensus).

Why — clinical reasoning and evidence
- The objective of fetal monitoring around non‑obstetric surgery is to detect fetal compromise that would prompt intervention and to document fetal well‑being. Whether intraoperative monitoring should be continuous depends fundamentally on two things: (1) gestational age/viability and (2) whether an immediate delivery would be possible and beneficial. Professional guidance and large series therefore recommend baseline documentation before surgery and post‑operative documentation afterward, with intraoperative continuous monitoring reserved for fetuses at/near viability when a cesarean could be undertaken emergently. This is the practical teaching point emphasized by Zhao et al. and reiterated in reviews and committee opinions (ACOG/other societies) — the evidence base is observational and consensus-based, not randomized.  
- In the early–mid second trimester (as in this case at 21 + 6 weeks), the fetus is usually below most centres’ threshold for neonatal survivability. Continuous CTG in that setting rarely changes management and is often technically difficult during abdominal surgery; intermittent ultrasound assessment of cardiac activity (transabdominal or transvaginal) is therefore a pragmatic approach. That is exactly what the surgical team did in the reported case: serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasounds to verify fetal viability during the procedure, with documentation of fetal cardiac activity at exploration. The authors cite the Zhao multicentre study and state that checking and documenting FHR before, during and after surgery is mandatory.  
- If the fetus is ≥threshold of viability and delivery is a realistic option, continuous monitoring provides the best chance to detect sudden compromise and is therefore recommended. If continuous monitoring shows sustained nonreassuring tracings during surgery and delivery is feasible, emergent cesarean should be considered.

Maintaining uterine quiescence — pathophysiology and practical measures
- Uterine activity is driven by maternal hemodynamics, oxygenation, pain/stress and direct uterine manipulation. Interventions that reduce maternal sympathetic activation and preserve uteroplacental perfusion reduce the risk of stimulation of contractions. Practical measures with biologic plausibility and some supporting observational data:
  - Positioning: left lateral tilt or manual uterine displacement (≥15°) reduces aortocaval compression, preserves cardiac output and uteroplacental blood flow — recommended in all pregnant surgical patients. In the reported case, semi‑orthostatic positioning was used pre‑op to relieve diaphragmatic compression by the uterus and masses.
  - Anesthesia/analgesia: provide adequate analgesia, avoid maternal hypoxia and hypotension, and maintain normocapnia — all reduce uterine irritability. Choice of neuraxial versus general anesthesia is dictated by surgery; volatile anesthetics at sufficient concentrations relax uterus (may be helpful intraoperatively) but do not replace other measures. Short, efficient surgery minimizes uterine stimulation.
  - Minimize uterine manipulation and ischemia: gentle handling, limit traction and minimize operative time; the case illustrates limiting the operation to the symptomatic mass and leaving the adherent pelvic mass in situ to reduce obstetric risk.
  - Tocolytics: routine prophylactic tocolysis at the time of non‑obstetric surgery is not supported by RCT data. If there are documented contractions or cervical change, tocolysis can be considered to delay delivery. Agents used in practice (evidence largely from preterm labour literature, not perioperative trials) include nifedipine (commonly used, oral, good tolerated), atosiban where available, and indomethacin (use limited by gestational age and fetal ductus concerns). Beta‑agonists are less favored because of maternal side effects. Use should be directed by obstetric consultation.
  - Progesterone: progesterone reduces recurrent spontaneous preterm birth in selected high‑risk women, but there are no RCTs supporting routine perioperative progesterone to prevent surgery‑associated preterm birth. The team in this report administered intramuscular progesterone prophylactically intraoperatively and continued it — an approach supported only by low‑level evidence (case series and expert opinion). If used, it should be individualized and documented in the consent discussion.
  - Other supportive measures: perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis when indicated, transfusion and correction of anemia (as in this patient) to preserve oxygen delivery to the fetus.

Strength of evidence summary
- Recommendations rest mostly on observational studies, case series, expert consensus and professional committee opinions rather than randomized trials. Key points with stronger consensus:
  - Document fetal heart activity before and after non‑obstetric surgery — strong consensus (low level of evidence).  
  - Continuous intraoperative fetal monitoring when the fetus is viable and immediate delivery is a practical option — consensus/expert opinion (low-quality evidence).  
  - Positioning, maintaining maternal oxygenation and hemodynamics, minimizing uterine manipulation and providing adequate anesthesia/analgesia — biologically plausible with observational support (low–moderate level evidence).  
  - Routine prophylactic tocolysis or routine perioperative progesterone is not evidence‑based; use should be selective and individualized (low-quality evidence).

Practical takeaways for teams planning non‑obstetric surgery in the second trimester
- Multidisciplinary planning (obstetrics, anesthesia, surgery, neonatology) before the operation. Document gestational age and viability, discuss the threshold for emergent delivery, and obtain baseline fetal heart monitoring or ultrasound.  
- If fetus is previable (as in this case at ~21–22 weeks), intermittent ultrasound assessment of cardiac activity during the procedure is reasonable and was effectively used here. If fetus is potentially viable (≥institutional threshold), arrange for continuous fetal monitoring and immediate cesarean capability.  
- Use left lateral tilt, maintain maternal oxygenation and blood pressure, provide adequate analgesia/anesthesia, minimize uterine handling and operative time. Reserve tocolytics for documented uterine contractions. Consider progesterone only on a case‑by‑case basis; recognize that evidence for routine prophylaxis is lacking.  
- Document all monitoring and the plan in the operative record; ensure close post‑op obstetrical follow‑up.

In the presented case these principles were followed: fetal viability was documented before and during laparotomy with intermittent transvaginal ultrasound, the team minimized intervention by removing only the symptomatic pedunculated myoma, and they used intraoperative progesterone prophylaxis (an approach based on low‑level evidence and multidisciplinary judgment). The favorable maternal and neonatal outcome illustrates that careful, individualized multidisciplinary management is appropriate when non‑obstetric surgery is necessary in the second trimester.

### [Management] How do the size, number, and location of residual uterine leiomyomas guide delivery planning—including the need for cesarean delivery, choice of uterine incision, and timing near term—particularly when a large posterior lower-segment mass could obstruct descent?

Bottom line
- Size, number and—most important—the location of leiomyomas determine whether vaginal birth is reasonable or whether a planned cesarean is the safer option. A fixed, large mass in the lower uterine segment or pelvis that occupies the pelvic outlet (as in this case) is a mechanical obstruction to descent and is an indication to plan a cesarean delivery rather than attempt labour.

Why these factors matter (clinical reasoning)
- Mechanical obstruction: a large posterior or isthmic myoma that projects into the pelvic cavity can physically block engagement and the expulsive phase of labour. In the case presented the residual “caudal” myoma was adherent to the posterior uterine torus and measured on follow‑up ~7.5 cm; the team judged it would impede fetal head progression and therefore elected an early-term CS.
- Uterine contractility and malpresentation: multiple or large intramural myomas distort uterine geometry and may impair effective contractions and lead to malpresentation or labour dystocia; this increases the likelihood of intrapartum cesarean.
- Size dynamics in pregnancy: myomas often enlarge in early pregnancy (growth greatest in 1st–2nd trimester) but may stabilize or decrease in the late third trimester/postpartum. That potential for partial regression must be weighed against the risk of spontaneous labour and acute complications (torsion, degeneration, haemorrhage).

How to evaluate pre‑delivery
- Map size, number and exact relation to the uterine cavity and lower uterine segment with transvaginal/transabdominal ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI when needed—this was done in the case to define continuity with the posterior wall. Bimanual exam also helps assess pelvic obstruction.
- Reassess near term: imaging close to delivery is useful because sizes can change during pregnancy and the operative plan (incision choice, need for myomectomy) depends on current anatomy.

Implications for mode and timing of delivery
- Vaginal delivery can be attempted when myomas are small, not in the lower segment, not distorting the pelvis or presenting as fixed obstructing masses, and when there are no other obstetric contraindications.
- Plan an elective cesarean when a myoma is:
  - Fixed in the lower uterine segment or pelvis and likely to obstruct descent (as in this report), or
  - Multiple/large and causing malpresentation, significant haemorrhage risk, or placenta-related problems.
- Timing: arrange delivery once fetal maturity is achieved (often 37 weeks) rather than waiting for spontaneous labour if obstruction is likely—this minimizes emergency surgery and the risk of scar rupture or intrapartum complications. The team in the case scheduled CS at 37 weeks for these reasons.

Choice of uterine incision and intraoperative considerations
- If the lower uterine segment is unobstructed, a low transverse incision is preferred for least morbidity and lower risk of uterine rupture in future pregnancies.
- If a lower-segment myoma occupies the planned incision site, options are:
  - Perform a transverse or classical vertical (midline) uterine incision above the myoma (classical incision carries higher subsequent rupture risk), or
  - Remove the myoma (cesarean myomectomy) to allow a low transverse incision—this is feasible but requires experienced surgeons, good haemostatic technique, readiness for transfusion, and patient counselling about risks. The literature cited in the report suggests CS myomectomy can be done safely in select patients but increases operative time and hospital stay.
- Prepare for blood loss: crossmatched blood available, uterotonics, cell‑salvage if available, and experienced surgical team. The report notes the team considered bleeding risk and chose a staged approach (left the posterior myoma in situ) to minimise obstetric risk.

Counselling and multidisciplinary planning
- Discuss with the patient the likelihood of cesarean vs trial of labour, the possible need for intraoperative myomectomy, the potential for increased bleeding, and implications of different uterine incisions for future pregnancies.
- Multidisciplinary planning (obstetrics, gynecologic surgery, anesthesia, blood bank) is essential. In the case, that approach guided the decision to leave the caudal myoma in place and to schedule an elective CS at term, balancing maternal/fetal safety with avoidance of overtreatment.

Application to this case
- The team removed the pedunculated upper myoma at 21+6 weeks for symptom control and diagnostic certainty but left the fixed caudal posterior myoma in situ because it was adherent and removal would increase obstetric risk. They monitored the residual mass (which regressed slightly to ~7.5 cm) and elected an elective cesarean at 37 weeks because the mass was anticipated to obstruct head descent—an individualized, evidence‑based plan that led to an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome.

Practical checklist before delivery in similar patients
- Repeat imaging (US ± MRI) near term to map current anatomy.
- Decide mode of delivery based on location/size: lower‑segment/pelvic obstruction → plan cesarean.
- Decide planned uterine incision and whether removal of the myoma at CS is intended.
- Ensure blood products and multidisciplinary team availability.
- Counsel patient about risks, alternatives, and implications for future pregnancies.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiology of pain, low-grade fever, and laboratory changes when a pedunculated uterine fibroid undergoes torsion with ischemic necrosis and cystic degeneration during pregnancy, and why these processes can mimic infection or malignancy on imaging and tumor markers.

Direct answer
When a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma twists on its stalk the primary event is vascular compromise. Loss of venous and lymphatic outflow produces congestion and edema, which quickly progresses to arterial insufficiency, ischemia and then hemorrhagic/ischemic necrosis. Liquefactive change of necrotic tissue gives cystic degeneration and can leak blood and debris into the peritoneal cavity. The local tissue injury provokes a robust sterile inflammatory response that produces the clinical (pain, low‑grade fever) and laboratory (neutrophilia, raised CRP, modest rise in CA‑125) findings that can be indistinguishable from infection or neoplasm on initial testing and imaging.

Pathophysiologic sequence (linked to the case)
- Mechanical torsion → vascular outflow obstruction: the twisted pedicle in this patient produced venous congestion and impaired lymphatic drainage of the leiomyoma. Pathology confirmed ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle.
- Ischemia → cell death and hemorrhage → cystic degeneration: ischemic myoma tissue undergoes coagulative/hemorrhagic necrosis and liquefaction, producing a unilocular cystic mass with inhomogeneous fluid and focal blood products (explains the inhomogeneous T1/T2 signal and focal hyperintense areas seen on MRI and the taut‑elastic cystic appearance at surgery).
- Necrosis and debris → sterile inflammation: damaged cells release damage‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that recruit neutrophils and macrophages and trigger cytokine release (IL‑1, TNF, IL‑6). That inflammatory cascade causes low‑grade fever, an acute‑phase response with elevated CRP, and neutrophilic leukocytosis—the patient had mild fever, CRP 1.39 mg/dL and WBC 11.3×103/µL with neutrophilia.
- Local bleeding and chronic iron deficiency/pregnancy physiology → anemia: the patient’s iron deficiency anemia was present before surgery and worsened postoperatively; acute blood loss from the leaking/twisted mass and hemodilution of pregnancy contribute.
- Peritoneal irritation and marker release → elevated CA‑125: CA‑125 is produced by coelomic epithelium and is upregulated by peritoneal irritation, inflammation, decidual changes and some benign uterine conditions. In this case CA‑125 rose from 36 to 41.8 U/mL—above the usual second‑trimester median—reflecting sterile inflammation and tumor degeneration, not necessarily malignancy.

Why this mimics infection or malignancy on imaging and tumor markers
- Imaging appearance: cystic degeneration with hemorrhage produces complex cystic lesions with thickened walls, inhomogeneous fluid content and focal solid components. Peripheral neovascularization or hypertrophied vessels around a necrotic myoma can simulate the rim enhancement or peripheral vascularity seen with abscesses or ovarian neoplasms. In pregnancy the gravid uterus and large masses obscure a pedicle or feeding vessel, so a pedunculated myoma can be misinterpreted as an adnexal (ovarian) cyst or decidualised endometrioma—the same confusion seen in this patient’s ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI.
- Laboratory overlap: sterile necrosis generates an inflammatory response (CRP, neutrophilia, fever) similar to infection. CA‑125 is nonspecific—it physiologically rises in pregnancy and is released with peritoneal inflammation and some benign gynecologic disease—so modest elevations can be seen with degenerating fibroids as well as ovarian malignancy.
- Clinical confounders: secondary infection of a necrotic myoma is possible and would enhance the infectious picture; in this case a urinary Klebsiella added another possible source of fever, further complicating interpretation.

Clinical teaching points
- In pregnancy, interpret CA‑125 and inflammatory markers cautiously; modest rises can reflect sterile degeneration and pregnancy physiology rather than malignancy.
- Sudden localized pain, a tense cystic uterine mass and signs of peritoneal irritation should raise suspicion for torsion with ischemic degeneration of a pedunculated myoma. Doppler (looking for absent flow in the stalk/whirlpool sign) and non‑contrast MRI features of hemorrhage/cystic change are helpful but may be non‑diagnostic.
- Management is individualized: torsed, symptomatic, or leaking pedunculated myomas may require myomectomy despite pregnancy—this case illustrates that surgery confirmed the diagnosis and relieved mass effect with good maternal‑fetal outcome.

Keeping these pathophysiologic links in mind helps reconcile the clinical, imaging and laboratory overlap between degenerating torsed fibroids, infection and malignant disease.

### [Management] How should perioperative iron-deficiency anemia in mid-pregnancy be managed when urgent non-obstetric surgery is needed—what are the roles of intravenous iron versus red blood cell transfusion, and what thresholds and targets balance maternal symptoms, fetal oxygen delivery, and procedural risk?

Direct answer
In a pregnant patient needing urgent non‑obstetric surgery, treat iron‑deficiency anemia by balancing the time available before operation, the immediacy of oxygen‑carrying capacity required, and fetal oxygenation. Use intravenous (IV) iron when there is time to correct stores or to accelerate postoperative recovery; use red‑cell transfusion when immediate improvement in oxygen delivery is required (hemodynamic instability, severe or symptomatic anemia, or anticipated major blood loss). Aim pragmatically for a post‑treatment hemoglobin (Hb) that relieves maternal symptoms and avoids perioperative hypoxia—usually ≥8 g/dL, higher (≈9–10 g/dL) when there is ongoing bleeding, cardiopulmonary disease, or concern for fetal compromise.

Why this approach (clinical reasoning and physiology)
- Pregnancy increases plasma volume more than red‑cell mass, so physiologic dilutional anemia occurs, but true iron‑deficiency reduces maternal oxygen content and impairs fetal oxygen delivery if severe. Fetal oxygenation depends on maternal oxygen content and uterine perfusion; severe maternal anemia risks fetal hypoxia.
- IV iron replenishes iron stores and allows erythropoiesis, but it is not an immediate correction of oxygen‑carrying capacity. Reticulocytosis begins within days; clinically meaningful Hb rises usually take 1–2 weeks (though some rise may be seen earlier).
- Transfusion provides immediate, predictable increase in oxygen‑carrying capacity (one adult unit ≈ +1 g/dL Hb), and is therefore indicated when rapid correction is necessary for maternal stability or to mitigate fetal risk during/after surgery. Transfusion carries infectious, immunologic and volume risks and should be used selectively.

Practical thresholds and targets
- Stable pregnant patient, no active bleeding, short delay possible: if Hb ≥10 g/dL, proceed with careful monitoring; if Hb 8–10 g/dL and iron deficiency documented, give IV iron pre‑op if >3–7 days to surgery (or intra/post‑op if earlier) and consider proceeding; transfuse only if symptomatic or hemodynamically unstable.
- Hb <7 g/dL (or <8 g/dL with symptoms, cardiac disease, or ongoing bleeding): transfuse red cells to achieve an Hb ≥8 g/dL, with an individualized higher target (≈9–10 g/dL) if there is major blood loss risk or concern for fetal oxygenation.
- For planned transfusion, estimate number of units using desired Hb increment (each unit ≈ +1 g/dL) and body size; reassess after each unit.
- Use IV iron (ferric carboxymaltose, iron sucrose) in the second and third trimesters as standard therapy for iron‑deficiency during pregnancy to restore stores and reduce subsequent transfusions; typical single dosing (eg, ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) is effective and was used safely in the reported case.

How this applied in the case
- The patient presented pre‑operatively with iron‑deficiency anemia (Hb 9.9 g/dL, ferritin 25 mcg/L) and required urgent laparotomy at 21+ weeks. Because immediate correction of oxygen carrying capacity was not required pre‑op, surgery proceeded with intraoperative fetal monitoring and hemodynamic surveillance.
- Postoperative Hb fell to 7.5 g/dL with ongoing iron deficiency; clinicians appropriately combined RBC transfusion (two units) to quickly restore oxygen delivery and IV ferric carboxymaltose 1 g to replenish iron and support erythropoietic recovery. The combined strategy raised Hb to 10 g/dL and the mother and fetus did well—illustrating the complementary roles of transfusion (immediate correction) and IV iron (rapid restoration of iron and sustained Hb recovery).

Monitoring, safety and team processes
- Document maternal hemodynamics and fetal heart rate before, during and after surgery. Discuss transfusion risks and alternatives with the patient and obtain consent.
- Screen for iron deficiency preoperatively with Hb, ferritin and, if available, transferrin saturation; if surgery can be delayed, give IV iron to avoid transfusion.
- Be prepared for transfusion if intraoperative or postoperative bleeding occurs. Use multidisciplinary care (obstetrics, anesthesia, surgery, hematology/blood bank) to individualize thresholds.

Clinical pearl
When time is limited, IV iron is an important tool to reduce later transfusion needs and speed recovery, but it does not replace transfusion when immediate oxygen‑carrying capacity is required; integrate both strategies based on Hb level, symptoms, hemodynamics, bleeding risk and fetal considerations, as in the presented case.
