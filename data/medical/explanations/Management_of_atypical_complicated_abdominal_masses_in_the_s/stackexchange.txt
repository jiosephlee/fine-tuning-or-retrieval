### [Morning Report] A 21-week pregnant patient presents with acute left upper quadrant and pelvic pain plus dyspnoea. Ultrasound shows two large unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularity, one subphrenic (18 cm) and one in the pouch of Douglas (9–10 cm). What is your first-pass differential for the pain and dyspnoea, and which life-threatening obstetric and non-obstetric causes must be excluded early?

Think first about what is causing the acute pain and the new dyspnoea, then prioritise exclusion of immediate threats that would require urgent intervention.

Clinical reasoning and first‑pass differential
The combination of acute severe left upper‑quadrant and pelvic pain in the second trimester with two large abdominal cystic masses points to mass‑related mechanical and ischemic processes first: torsion (pedunculated myoma or adnexal torsion), acute haemorrhage into a mass, and red (ischaemic) degeneration or infarction of a leiomyoma. These explain focal pain, local peritoneal irritation and fever. The pelvic mass adherent to a posterior myoma makes red degeneration or cystic degeneration and torsion of a pedunculated component especially likely. Infective complication (infected cyst or myoma with sepsis) is also possible — the low‑grade fever and positive urine culture support infection as a cofactor.

Dyspnoea in this setting is most often mechanical: massive upward displacement of the diaphragm by large intra‑abdominal masses and uterine enlargement limiting tidal volume. Other causes that must be considered are anaemia from acute or chronic blood loss (noted Hb 9.9 → 7.5 g/dL postop in this case), septic physiology, and true cardiopulmonary disease (pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, pleural effusion, cardiogenic failure). In pregnancy, PE remains a high‑risk diagnosis even when mechanical causes seem likely.

Life‑threatening obstetric and non‑obstetric causes to exclude early
You should rapidly exclude conditions that necessitate immediate intervention: massive intra‑abdominal haemorrhage or rupture of a mass (myoma or ovarian cyst), ovarian/adnexal torsion with infarction, severe infection progressing to sepsis, and pulmonary embolism. Less likely but important to think about are strangulating bowel obstruction, splenic rupture (if left subphrenic pain is prominent), and acute cardiopulmonary collapse (peripartum cardiomyopathy is rare at 21 weeks but consider cardiogenic causes if exam suggests it).

Practical early work‑up and decision points
Begin with rapid bedside assessment: continuous maternal vitals and fetal heart rate, focused abdominal exam, oxygen saturation, ECG. Obtain STAT labs: CBC with differential, type & screen, coagulation profile, blood gas/lactate if hypotension or severe dyspnoea, CRP, blood cultures if febrile. Send urine culture and treat urinary sepsis promptly if suspected. Perform targeted imaging: expedite transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound with colour Doppler to look for ovarian/adnexal torsion, active bleeding, free fluid, and mass vascularity; if ultrasound is inconclusive and diagnosis will change management, non‑contrast MRI is appropriate in the second trimester to clarify origin and complications. For dyspnoea with concern for PE, do not rely on D‑dimer alone — proceed to imaging (ventilation‑perfusion scan or CTPA), balancing radiation but prioritising maternal life.

Management priorities
If haemodynamic instability or peritoneal signs develop, proceed to surgical exploration. If torsion, rupture, or infected/necrotic mass is likely and symptoms are progressive, timely surgical management (ideally after multidisciplinary planning) is indicated even in the second trimester. Treat sepsis aggressively with pregnancy‑appropriate antibiotics and supportive care. If PE is confirmed, start anticoagulation while balancing risk of recent or planned surgery.

Takeaway
Acute pain with large pelvic/abdominal masses in pregnancy is most commonly due to torsion, haemorrhage into a mass or ischaemic (red) degeneration of a myoma; infection can coexist and large masses can cause restrictive dyspnoea. Rapidly exclude life‑threatening haemorrhage/rupture, ovarian torsion, sepsis, and pulmonary embolism with focused clinical assessment, urgent ultrasound (±MRI), appropriate labs and, where indicated, thoracic imaging. Management should prioritise maternal stabilisation and definitive treatment of the complication while preserving the pregnancy when safe to do so.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Which ultrasound and MRI features help distinguish a decidualised endometrioma from a cystically degenerating uterine leiomyoma in mid-pregnancy?

The practical distinction between a decidualised endometrioma and a cystically degenerating uterine leiomyoma in mid‑pregnancy rests less on a single “pathognomonic” sign and more on a pattern of imaging relationships and tissue signals that point to ovarian versus uterine origin and to blood‑product versus myomatous degeneration.

On ultrasound, start by establishing the site of origin. An endometrioma is classically adnexal and the ovary can often be identified separately; it usually appears as a unilocular or pauciloculated cyst with “ground‑glass” homogeneous low‑level internal echoes. Decidualisation of an endometrioma produces vascularised mural nodules or papillary projections within that cystic mass; these nodules are typically smooth, round, show internal vascularity on Doppler, and may mimic a solid tumor. By contrast, a cystically degenerating leiomyoma is contiguous with the uterine wall: look for loss of a clear cleavage plane between mass and myometrium, the bridging‑vessel sign (vessels running from the myometrium into the mass), a visible pedicle in pedunculated myomas, or direct continuity with a known uterine fibroid on bimanual exam or transvaginal scan. Degenerating fibroids more often have heterogeneous echotexture with cystic areas and a relatively thick, irregular wall; the surrounding myometrium and a pseudocapsule may be seen.

MRI adds tissue‑characteristic information that helps separate blood‑product cysts from myomatous degeneration even without contrast. Endometriomas commonly show intrinsic T1 hyperintensity (blood products) and the classic “T2 shading” sign (progressive T2 signal loss from concentrated old blood). Decidualised mural nodules inside an endometrioma may be intermediate on T2 and can be mistaken for solid tumor, but persistence of T1 high signal in the cyst fluid supports an endometrioma. Cystic degeneration of a leiomyoma typically produces central high T2 signal fluid within a mass whose solid components are low T2 signal like normal myometrium; the mass will often have a low‑signal rim and demonstrate clear continuity with uterine tissue. Identification of a pedicle or bridging vessels on MRI strongly favors uterine origin. Absence of T1 blood‑product signal and presence of myometrial‑type low T2 signal solid tissue argue for degenerating leiomyoma.

In short: carefully define origin (ovarian versus uterine) using cleavage plane, pedicle/bridging vessels and relation to ovary; on US, ground‑glass internal echoes with mural vascularised nodules favour decidualised endometrioma, whereas heterogeneous cystic change contiguous with uterine wall favors degenerated myoma; on MRI, T1 hyperintense blood products and T2 shading point to endometrioma, whereas central T2‑bright cystic change within a low‑T2 myometrial mass and clear continuity with uterus point to cystic degeneration of a leiomyoma. Use the whole clinical picture (symptoms, CA‑125 trend, prior history of fibroids or endometriosis) and proceed to surgery only when diagnosis remains uncertain or the mass is symptomatic or complicated, as in this case.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiologic mechanisms of cystic degeneration and torsion in subserosal pedunculated uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy and how these processes produce pain, low-grade fever, and inflammatory markers.

During pregnancy leiomyomas are exposed to a hormonal and hemodynamic milieu that predisposes them to both degenerative change and mechanical complications. The explanation for the findings in this case rests on two linked pathophysiologic processes: (1) growth-related ischemic degeneration of a myoma and (2) torsion of a pedunculated subserosal myoma with subsequent ischemia/necrosis and peritoneal inflammation.

Pregnancy increases circulating estrogen, progesterone and uterine blood flow, which commonly cause leiomyomas to enlarge. Rapid enlargement — especially of a subserosal nodule that projects on a narrow stalk — can outstrip the lesion’s vascular supply. When perfusion is inadequate relative to size or when venous/lymphatic outflow is impaired, the myoma undergoes ischemic change. That process may progress to red (hemorrhagic) or cystic degeneration as smooth muscle cells die, intratumoral extracellular fluid accumulates, and hemorrhage and liquefaction occur. On MRI and ultrasound this produces inhomogeneous, hyperintense T1/T2 signal and unilocular cystic appearance, as seen here.

A pedunculated subserosal myoma is mechanically vulnerable to torsion about its stalk. Torsion first obstructs venous and lymphatic drainage, causing congestion and swelling, then arterial inflow becomes compromised and true ischemic infarction follows. Ischemia and secondary hemorrhage breach tissue integrity, and necrotic debris and blood products can leak into the peritoneal cavity.

Those tissue events explain the clinical syndrome. Acute ischemia/necrosis produces sudden, localized visceral and somatic pain from stretch of the tumor capsule and irritation of adjacent peritoneum. Necrotic tissue and blood are potent triggers of innate immunity: damaged cells release damage‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that activate resident macrophages and mesothelial cells to secrete cytokines (IL‑1, IL‑6, TNF‑α). Cytokine signaling raises the hypothalamic set point (low‑grade fever) and stimulates hepatic synthesis of acute‑phase reactants (CRP) and bone‑marrow neutrophil mobilization with relative leukocytosis. Peritoneal irritation and increased production of glycoproteins from mesothelial cells also raise CA‑125 modestly. These inflammatory signals can mimic or coexist with infection; torsion with necrosis can be aseptic yet produce fever and elevated inflammatory markers identical to early pelvic infection.

In this patient the intraoperative finding of a twisted, vascularized pedunculated leiomyoma with adjacent ischemic necrosis explains the acute pain and low‑grade fever and accounts for the modest CRP and neutrophilia. The concurrent urinary Klebsiella infection may have contributed to the systemic inflammatory response, but the local peritoneal inflammation and tissue necrosis from torsion were sufficient to produce the full constellation of signs and imaging abnormalities observed. Clinically, the distinction matters because degeneration/torsion is a surgical indication when symptoms or mass effect are severe, whereas uncomplicated degeneration can often be managed expectantly.

### [Management] In the second trimester, which imaging modalities are preferred and considered safe for characterising complex adnexal or uterine masses, and which should be avoided unless benefits clearly outweigh fetal risks?

In the second trimester the cornerstone for evaluating adnexal or uterine masses is transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound with colour Doppler; it gives real‑time anatomic detail, vascularity and dynamic relationships to the gravid uterus without ionising radiation and is the first‑line test. When ultrasound is inconclusive for complex, large, posterior or multiple lesions, non‑contrast MRI is the preferred second‑line study because it provides superior soft‑tissue characterisation and multiplanar anatomy without ionising radiation. Current practice is to use non‑contrast MRI at 1.5 T (avoid routine use of higher field strengths) and to avoid gadolinium unless a life‑threatening maternal diagnosis cannot be established otherwise and the information would change management.

Studies and guidelines therefore recommend avoiding imaging modalities that expose the fetus to ionising radiation or transplacental contrast unless the maternal benefit clearly outweighs fetal risk. CT scans (especially of the abdomen/pelvis), PET/CT and diagnostic studies requiring iodinated or gadolinium contrast should not be used routinely in pregnancy. Gadolinium crosses the placenta and has been associated with rare fetal/neonatal adverse effects; iodinated contrast can affect fetal thyroid function. Plain radiographs or limited CT for critical, time‑sensitive indications may be acceptable with dose minimisation and shielding, but they should be an exception after multidisciplinary discussion and informed consent.

Clinical principle: use the least risky test that will answer the clinical question. Start with ultrasound, proceed to non‑contrast 1.5‑T MRI when needed for anatomical clarification, and reserve contrast‑enhanced MRI, CT or PET only for situations where diagnostic uncertainty poses a greater threat to mother or fetus than the imaging risks.

### [Clinical Pearls] How should a CA-125 level of 42 U/mL in the second trimester be interpreted in a patient with large adnexal/uterine masses, and what is the value of serial trends versus single measurements during pregnancy?

A CA‑125 of 42 U/mL in the second trimester must be interpreted in context, not as a standalone “cancer” signal. CA‑125 normally varies with pregnancy (peaks in the first and again in the third trimester) and is influenced by many benign processes—including leiomyomas, endometriosis, pelvic inflammation, necrosis, torsion and even peritoneal irritation. The usual oncologic cut‑off of 35 U/mL loses positive predictive value in pregnancy and in the presence of large pelvic masses.

The clinical reasoning therefore is: a single, modest elevation (eg, 36 → 42 U/mL) in the second trimester is non‑specific and by itself is insufficient to diagnose malignancy or mandate radical intervention. What matters more is the trend and concordance with the rest of the picture. A steadily rising CA‑125 over days–weeks, especially when accompanied by new pain, fever, inflammatory markers or imaging showing complex, solid components, thick walls, increased vascularity or loss of cleavage planes, increases concern for complication (degeneration, torsion, infection) or, less commonly, neoplasm. Conversely, a stable, mildly elevated value with reassuring ultrasound/MRI features and benign clinical course argues for expectant management.

Practically, use CA‑125 as an adjunctive, serial test rather than a single diagnostic threshold in pregnancy. Repeat measurements at short interval (eg, 1–2 weeks) to define a trend, and always interpret them with careful clinical assessment and high‑quality imaging. In the presented case the modest but rising CA‑125 combined with worsening pain, fever and complex, highly vascularised masses on imaging appropriately raised concern and supported the decision to operate; the final pathology (degenerating, torsed pedunculated leiomyoma) is a classic benign explanation for the CA‑125 change.

### [Morning Report] In a 21–22 week pregnancy with worsening mass-related pain, restrictive dyspnoea, and two large vascular abdominal masses, what factors would push you toward operative intervention instead of expectant management?

The decision to operate in the second trimester pivots on a risk–benefit balance: operative risk to the pregnancy (miscarriage, preterm labour, haemorrhage) versus imminent or likely maternal (and ultimately fetal) harm from the mass. In a patient at 21–22 weeks with worsening pain, restrictive dyspnoea and two large vascular abdominal masses, I would be pushed toward operative intervention when objective and reproducible findings indicate an unacceptable short‑term risk if managed expectantly.

Specifically, surgery is favored when the mother has progressive symptoms that do not respond to conservative therapy (analgesia, positioning, treatment of infection) and are impairing function or physiology—for example persistent or escalating pain despite treatment, respiratory compromise from diaphragmatic compression (restrictive dyspnoea) or any evidence of cardiorespiratory compromise. Evidence of an acute or evolving complication (torsion of a pedunculated mass, rupture, active bleeding, infected/necrotic degeneration with fever and rising inflammatory markers, or a leaking/twisted pedicle at risk of ongoing ischemia) mandates operative management because these events can rapidly deteriorate maternal status and precipitate fetal loss.

Imaging and laboratory features that raise the pretest probability of an urgent problem also tip the balance toward surgery. Thick walls, solid nodules or inhomogeneous signal on MRI, prominent peripheral vascularity, rapid increase in size, or a rising tumour marker (eg, CA‑125 trend) that makes neoplasm or complicated degeneration likely create diagnostic uncertainty that cannot safely be deferred until after delivery. When a mass is large enough to prevent further uterine expansion or is anatomically likely to obstruct labour (for future delivery) and is expected to continue growing with gestation, elective removal in the second trimester can be the lesser risk.

Finally, failure of conservative management together with multidisciplinary assessment (obstetrics, gynecologic oncology, anesthesiology) and informed consent from the patient are prerequisites. If these factors are present, the second trimester is the preferred window for abdominal surgery because uterine size allows access, and the risks of teratogenicity and spontaneous abortion are lower than in the first trimester and lower risk of preterm labour than in the third.

Conversely, absent persistent symptoms, acute complications, worrisome imaging/lab features, or diagnostic uncertainty, expectant management with close surveillance is the preferred approach.

### [Management] What fetal monitoring is recommended before, during, and after non-obstetric surgery in the second trimester, and how does this change by gestational age?

Before any non‑obetric surgery in pregnancy you must first establish the presence and baseline status of the fetus and document a plan that matches gestational age and the team's ability to act on fetal findings. Practically, that means a preoperative fetal assessment (hand‑held Doppler or bedside ultrasound) to confirm fetal cardiac activity, baseline heart rate and gestational age, and a discussion with obstetrics, anesthesia and neonatology about the threshold for intervention if fetal distress occurs.

During surgery the monitoring strategy depends on fetal maturity and the feasibility of continuous assessment. For a non‑viable fetus (generally <23–24 weeks) continuous intraoperative fetal monitoring is neither feasible nor actionable; intermittent confirmation of cardiac activity before and after the procedure (and intraoperative ultrasound if there is concern) is appropriate. For a potentially viable fetus (approximately ≥23–24 weeks) you should provide continuous electronic fetal heart rate monitoring during the procedure if the surgical field and sterility permit and if the obstetric and neonatal teams are prepared to respond to fetal distress (convert to cesarean delivery if indicated). If continuous electronic monitoring cannot be reliably performed, use intermittent auscultation plus intraoperative ultrasound as available, and ensure clear contingency plans.

After surgery monitor the fetus for signs of compromise and the mother for uterine activity or bleeding. For early gestations (<23–24 weeks) re‑confirm fetal cardiac activity and then follow routine obstetrical surveillance; for viable gestations provide continuous or intermittent electronic monitoring for a period after surgery (commonly at least several hours, longer if there were intraoperative concerns, uterine irritability, significant blood loss, maternal hypotension, or preterm labor symptoms). Any new abnormal fetal tracing, uterine contractions, bleeding, or maternal instability should trigger immediate obstetric reassessment with readiness for delivery if the fetus is viable.

Two additional practical points: optimize maternal physiology to protect the fetus (left lateral tilt, normoxia, normocapnia, hemodynamic stability, adequate hemoglobin) and document all assessments and the agreed plan preoperatively — the decision to monitor continuously hinges not only on gestational age but on whether the team is prepared to act on the information.

### [Management] During exploratory laparotomy at 22 weeks, a large pedunculated cystic mass is found to be a twisted, leaking, highly vascular fibroid. What surgical steps minimise uterine manipulation and haemorrhage while safely resecting the pedicle?

When you open the abdomen at 22 weeks and find a large pedunculated, torsed, leaking and highly vascular leiomyoma, the priorities are to remove the source of sepsis/bleeding quickly while disturbing the gravid uterus as little as possible and securing haemostasis. The operative plan should therefore focus on isolating and controlling the pedicle, not on extensive uterine dissection.

First, avoid routine manipulation or exteriorisation of the uterus — leave the uterus in situ and handle only the pedunculated mass with atraumatic forceps. Identify the pedicle at its uterine implantation and expose a length of stalk sufficient to apply control. Inject dilute vasopressin into the base of the stalk (if not contraindicated) to produce local vasoconstriction and reduce bleeding. Place two atraumatic vascular clamps across the pedicle (one proximal, one distal) and then ligate the base with a secure absorbable transfixing suture (figure‑of‑eight or transfixing ligature) close to the uterine wall; divide the stalk between clamps. If the pedicle is broad-based, apply a purse‑string or interrupted figure‑of‑eight suture to include myometrium and serosa and reinforce the uterine side. Use bipolar cautery for any small bleeding points; avoid extensive myometrial resection.

Keep the procedure short. Maintain cell‑saving and blood products available and monitor fetal heart rate before, during and after. Minimise uterine contractions by avoiding uterotonics intraoperatively (unless indicated for haemorrhage postpartum) and by gentle tissue handling. If the lesion is very vascular or the pedicle cannot be readily controlled, consider temporary uterine artery occlusion (clips or vessel loops) or a local tourniquet placed at the lower uterine segment to reduce inflow while you ligate the stalk. Close any myometrial defect with an absorbable single‑layer, inverted or imbricating suture to reapproximate serosa and prevent bleeding.

In short: expose and control the pedicle with local vasoconstriction, double clamp and transfixing ligature placed close to the uterus, divide between clamps, secure uterine side with absorbable sutures, use bipolar coagulation for oozing, avoid uterine exteriorisation and prolonged manipulation, and keep operative time and blood loss to a minimum while having blood products and fetal monitoring ready.

### [Morning Report] Why might a second, fixed posterior intramural–subserosal fibroid be left in situ during mid-pregnancy myomectomy, and what risks does its removal pose at that gestation?

Because the second lesion was an intramural–subserosal fibroid that was broad‑based, fixed to the posterior uterine wall and not acutely compromised, the team chose to leave it in place to avoid the predictable maternal and fetal hazards of trying to excise it in mid‑pregnancy.

The clinical reasoning is straightforward: removing a fixed intramural/subserosal myoma requires a deep uterine incision and extensive dissection into the myometrium. In the mid‑second trimester that almost always produces brisk bleeding from a highly vascular uterus, risks entry into or disruption of the uterine cavity, and creates a large myometrial defect that is technically difficult to repair under time pressure. Those intraoperative problems translate into serious obstetric risks — hemorrhage requiring transfusion or hysterectomy, premature rupture of membranes, uterine irritability or preterm labor, direct fetal compromise from maternal instability or anesthesia, and an increased chance of pregnancy loss. In addition, making a substantial myometrial scar in the mid‑trimester raises the theoretical risk of later uterine rupture, and it may complicate placentation or fetal growth. 

By contrast, pedunculated or torsed myomas that are the source of acute pain, infection, leaking or necrosis can be safely removed because they can often be excised with minimal myometrial disruption and controlled haemostasis. A fixed posterior intramural mass without those complications is better managed expectantly: shorter operative time, less blood loss, continuous fetal monitoring, perioperative tocolaxis/progesterone as used here, and the option to defer definitive treatment until after delivery (or at the time of planned cesarean). This balances maternal benefit against fetal risk and is why the caudal mass was left in situ.

### [Pathophysiology] Why can large uterine leiomyomas in pregnancy mimic adnexal cystic masses on ultrasound, and how does uterine enlargement alter the perceived anatomic relationships?

Large uterine leiomyomas — especially subserosal or pedunculated ones that undergo cystic degeneration — can look like adnexal cysts on sonography because the imaging features and the anatomic relationships that normally let us tell “uterine” from “ovarian” are distorted.

Pathophysiology and imaging appearance: leiomyomas commonly outgrow their blood supply in pregnancy and develop hyaline/cystic degeneration and areas of necrosis. Degenerated leiomyomas can become unilocular, fluid‑filled, and avascular or rim‑vascularized on Doppler — features that overlap with ovarian cysts (including decidualized endometriomas). A pedunculated subserosal myoma may be attached to the uterine surface by a narrow stalk; if it twists or enlarges, the stalk can be thin or obscured and the tumor looks like an isolated adnexal mass. Thickened walls, internal septations or solid nodules from degeneration or infarction can further mimic complex ovarian lesions.

How pregnancy alters perceived anatomy: the gravid uterus enlarges, rising out of the pelvis and changing the spatial relationships of pelvic organs. The ovaries and adnexa are displaced laterally, superiorly or posteriorly; the pouch of Douglas and lateral gutters become occupied by shifted uterine tissue or by large masses. The stretched peritoneal reflections, overlying bowel, and increased abdominal fat or uterine contour can obscure the normal uterine outline and the uterine–ovarian cleavage plane on ultrasound, so a uterine mass may appear separable from the uterus. Large masses also displace vascular pedicles and feeding vessels, making it harder to demonstrate continuity with the myometrium on colour Doppler. Finally, gravid myometrial and decidual changes (and the physiologic rise in CA‑125) confound tumor markers and imaging interpretation.

Clinical implications: when a large complex “adnexal” mass is found in pregnancy, always think first about a uterine origin. Correlate with bimanual exam (fixed pelvic mass, relation to cervix/uterus), look carefully for a stalk or myometrial continuity on multiple ultrasound planes and with colour Doppler, and use non‑contrast MRI when US is equivocal because MRI better shows myometrial continuity and the distance between lesion and endometrial cavity. If imaging remains uncertain, manage according to symptoms and complication risk — conservative observation when stable, and surgery reserved for torsion, rupture, infection, or severe mass effect.

### [Differential Diagnosis] List benign and malignant etiologies for two large, unilocular cystic abdominal masses detected in the second trimester, and provide one or two imaging features for each that help in differentiation.

When confronted with two large, unilocular cystic abdominal masses in the second trimester, the differential must explicitly include pathologies of ovarian/adnexal origin, cystic uterine lesions (including degenerating myomas), inflammatory collections, and malignant cystic neoplasms. Imaging features on ultrasound (grayscale + Doppler) and non‑contrast MRI that point toward one diagnosis over another are crucial because management in pregnancy balances maternal/fetal risk with the low but real possibility of malignancy.

Benign etiologies

1) Ovarian serous cystadenoma (simple adnexal cyst)
Reasoning and key imaging features: usually unilocular, thin-walled and anechoic on US with no solid components and minimal or absent internal vascularity. On MRI it is homogeneously fluid signal (low T1, high T2) without mural nodules or thick septa.

2) Endometrioma (including decidualised endometrioma in pregnancy)
Reasoning and key imaging features: classic T1 hyperintense “blood‑product” signal with T2 shading on MRI; on US often homogeneous low‑level internal echoes. Decidualisation can produce vascularized mural nodules during pregnancy — look for vascularized papillary solid components that may mimic malignancy but usually lack irregular invasive borders and diffuse peritoneal disease.

3) Cystic degeneration of uterine leiomyoma (including pedunculated cystic leiomyoma)
Reasoning and key imaging features: continuity with the uterine myometrium or a visible pedicle/stalk (bridging vessel sign on Doppler) and location abutting the uterus point to myoma origin. MRI shows a lesion contiguous with uterine tissue, often heterogeneous T2 signal with areas of cystic change; lack of true enhancing papillary nodules favors benign degeneration.

4) Paraovarian/paratubal cyst
Reasoning and key imaging features: thin‑walled unilocular cyst separate from the ovary on careful transvaginal US (ovary visualized separately) and simple fluid signal on MRI without solid components.

5) Tubo‑ovarian abscess or infected cyst
Reasoning and key imaging features: clinically febrile/raised inflammatory markers; imaging shows thick irregular walls, internal debris or septations, surrounding inflammatory fat stranding on MRI and restricted diffusion on DWI sequences. Peripheral hyperemia on Doppler supports inflammation.

Malignant (or borderline) etiologies

1) Ovarian epithelial malignancy / cystadenocarcinoma
Reasoning and key imaging features: multiloculated or unilocular cysts with mural nodules or papillary projections, thick/irregular septations, solid components with internal vascularity on Doppler; MRI demonstrates solid T2‑intermediate nodules within cyst, heterogeneous signal, and often associated ascites or peritoneal implants.

2) Borderline ovarian tumor
Reasoning and key imaging features: papillary excrescences or nodules on the inner cyst wall but often less invasive appearance than frank carcinoma; nodules are vascularized but peritoneal invasion is absent or limited.

3) Cystic ovarian metastasis (Krukenberg‑type or other)
Reasoning and key imaging features: often bilateral, solid nodular components or multiple nodules, associated peritoneal disease and lymphadenopathy; MRI/US show mixed cystic–solid masses with marked vascularity.

4) Cystic degeneration in a uterine sarcoma (e.g., leiomyosarcoma with necrosis)
Reasoning and key imaging features: rapid interval growth, irregular margins, heterogeneous signal with hemorrhagic/necrotic areas (high T1 signal foci), and irregular solid components that on Doppler show chaotic internal vascularity. True sarcoma is rare in pregnancy but suspect when rapid growth plus irregular enhancing solid tissue is present.

How to apply this to the presented case
The imaging that matters here is (A) continuity with the uterus or a visible pedicle — strongly favors leiomyoma/cystic degeneration or pedunculated myoma rather than primary ovarian tumor, and (B) presence of vascularized solid mural nodules or papillary projections — raises concern for decidualised endometrioma or neoplasm. The case’s features — large unilocular cysts, one contiguous with a posterior myoma, peripheral hypertrophied vessels, inhomogeneous fluid and focal T1/T2 hyperintense solid components — narrow the differential to cystic degenerated leiomyoma (or pedunculated cystic myoma) versus decidualised endometrioma; the absence of ascites, nodes, and diffuse peritoneal disease and normal other tumor markers argued against ovarian carcinoma, while intraoperative identification of uterine stalks confirmed benign myomata.

### [Management] When is laparoscopy versus laparotomy appropriate for management of adnexal or uterine masses in pregnancy, and what patient- and mass-specific factors determine the choice?

The choice between laparoscopy and laparotomy for adnexal or uterine masses in pregnancy is not arbitrary; it follows the same clinical logic as in non‑pregnant patients but must be adapted to gestational physiology, fetal safety and obstetric consequences. Laparoscopy is preferable when it can safely achieve the diagnostic or therapeutic goal with less maternal morbidity, but laparotomy remains the appropriate option when the mass, the patient or the intraoperative risks make minimally invasive access unsafe or unlikely to succeed.

Practically, favor laparoscopy for presumed benign, mobile adnexal lesions or pedunculated subserosal myomas that are relatively small (generally up to ~8–12 cm depending on surgeon experience and trocar placement), are not densely adherent to the uterus, and when the gestation is in the first or early second trimester. The benefits—reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay and fewer adhesions—are real and supported by series in pregnancy. To minimize fetal risk use an open (Hasson) entry, place trocars high and lateral to the uterus, limit insufflation pressures (10–12 mmHg), tilt the patient left, and monitor the fetal heart rate before and after the procedure. Experienced laparoscopic teams are essential; conversion should be anticipated.

Choose laparotomy when mass‑specific or patient‑specific factors increase the risk of incomplete resection, major bleeding, prolonged operative time, or uterine injury with consequent pregnancy loss. Those factors include large size that limits working space (masses filling the abdomen), multiple lesions, intramural or posterior isthmic myomas adherent to uterine wall, highly vascular or necrotic/degenerating masses, unclear anatomy with concern for malignancy, evidence of torsion with haemodynamic compromise, infection, or need for extensive reconstruction. Later gestational age (mid‑late second and third trimesters) also favors laparotomy because the enlarged fundal height impedes safe trocar placement and instrument manipulation. Patient factors—obesity, prior extensive abdominal surgery with adhesions, cardiopulmonary disease that makes pneumoperitoneum high risk, and limited surgeon experience—also push toward open surgery.

Decision making must balance three goals: preserve the pregnancy, address maternal risk from the mass (pain, torsion, bleeding, infection, malignancy), and avoid overtreatment. That balance explains why a multidisciplinary team elected laparotomy in this case: two very large, vascularized, atypical masses occupying the abdomen and pelvis with worsening pain, dyspnoea from mass effect, and MRI features that did not clearly separate adnexal from uterine origin. The intraoperative finding of a twisted, highly vascular pedunculated leiomyoma justified removal; leaving the adherent pelvic myoma in situ minimized obstetric risk. Had the lesion been smaller, mobile and clearly pedunculated earlier in pregnancy, a laparoscopic approach (open entry, low pressure, careful uterine handling) could reasonably have been attempted.

Finally, whatever approach is chosen, prepare for obstetric contingencies: document fetal heart tones before, during (as feasible) and after surgery, have blood products available, use uterine‑protective measures (avoid unnecessary uterotonics; consider perioperative progesterone or tocolysis based on local practice), and plan delivery mode guided by residual myomas and obstetric anatomy rather than by the incision alone. The surgical approach should be individualized, prioritizing fetal–maternal safety and the likelihood of definitive, timely management.

### [Clinical Pearls] What obstetric complications are associated with performing myomectomy during pregnancy, and what are the accepted indications that justify proceeding despite these risks?

Myomectomy during pregnancy is feasible but not benign. The principal obstetric risks stem from the gravid uterus’s increased vascularity and the intimate relationship of leiomyomas to the uterine wall: intraoperative hemorrhage (sometimes massive), need for blood transfusion or even emergency hysterectomy; pregnancy loss (miscarriage or fetal demise); induction of preterm labor; postoperative infection; uterine dehiscence or rupture later in pregnancy or during labour; and fetal compromise from intraoperative hypoxia or haemodynamic instability. These risks increase with intramural location, large size, multiple myomas, proximity to the placental implantation site, and later gestational age.

Because of those risks, myomectomy in pregnancy is reserved for clear, compelling indications where conservative management is more dangerous than surgery. Accepted indications include a necrotic, torsed, or pedunculated myoma producing acute, unrelenting pain or sepsis; a rapidly enlarging or highly vascular mass with concern for degeneration, rupture, or haemorrhage; a mass causing severe organ compression (for example respiratory compromise, urinary obstruction) or intestinal obstruction; inability to distinguish a uterine myoma from an adnexal neoplasm where malignancy cannot be reasonably excluded; and when a myoma is obstructing the pelvic outlet sufficiently to preclude vaginal delivery and cannot be managed expectantly. In all cases the decision should be individualized, multidisciplinary, and informed by gestational age, fetal status, imaging (US/MRI), tumor markers, and the mother’s hemodynamic status.

When surgery is undertaken, the team should minimize operative time and uterine trauma, ensure meticulous hemostasis, provide continuous fetal monitoring when feasible, arrange blood products and experienced anesthesia/surgical support, and preferentially remove only the lesion(s) responsible for the acute problem while leaving asymptomatic myomas in situ. This balanced approach — intervene for compelling maternal or fetal indications, otherwise defer — is the guiding principle illustrated by the case you presented.

### [Morning Report] A pregnant patient with large abdominal masses develops low-grade fever and neutrophilia. How would you differentiate infectious causes (e.g., urinary tract infection) from red degeneration or torsion of a fibroid, and what tests guide you?

Key teaching point: low‑grade fever and neutrophilia in a pregnant patient with large pelvic/abdominal masses can reflect either infection (most commonly a urinary tract infection) or a mass complication (red degeneration, torsion, hemorrhage/necrosis). Distinguishing them relies on clinical pattern, targeted laboratory testing, focused imaging (especially Doppler US and non‑contrast MRI), and early microbiology — not on a single abnormal white count or a mildly elevated CRP or CA‑125, all of which can be altered in pregnancy or by degenerating fibroids.

Clinical reasoning and practical approach
Begin with history and exam aimed at source. UTI typically produces urinary symptoms (dysuria, frequency, urgency), suprapubic tenderness and sometimes low‑grade fever; systemic toxicity is unusual unless pyelonephritis develops. Red degeneration of a fibroid or torsion of a pedunculated myoma classically causes sudden focal severe pain (often localized to the mass), a firm tender adnexal/uterine mass, sometimes low‑grade fever, and progressive focal signs rather than urinary symptoms. Torsion may cause intermittent severe pain if the pedicle twists and untwists; red degeneration is ischemic pain often more constant and associated with localized uterine tenderness and possible localized peritoneal irritation.

Laboratory testing to order and how to interpret
Obtain urinalysis with dipstick and microscopy and a urine culture early. A positive nitrite/leukocyte esterase and pyuria plus a pathogen on culture confirm UTI and explain fever/ neutrophilia. Blood tests should include CBC and inflammatory markers (CRP). Mild leukocytosis and mild CRP elevation can accompany both infection and degeneration and are nonspecific in pregnancy; trends over time matter more than a single value. If febrile or systemically unwell, obtain blood cultures before antibiotics. Measure renal function (to guide antibiotic choice). Tumor markers (CA‑125) are of limited value in pregnancy and should not be relied on to separate infection from degeneration.

Imaging that guides diagnosis
Start with transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound with color Doppler. For torsion of a pedunculated myoma you may see an enlarged, heterogeneous mass with absent or reduced internal blood flow and a twisted pedicle; for red degeneration you may see a heterogeneous, often cystic or inhomogeneous fibroid with peripheral vascularity and internal hemorrhagic changes. Doppler is most useful to demonstrate loss of flow to a mass (suggests torsion/ischemia) versus preserved flow (less likely torsion). If ultrasound is inconclusive, perform a non‑contrast MRI (safe after the first trimester) — red degeneration shows high signal on T1 (hemorrhage) and heterogeneous T2 signal with surrounding edema; torsion/ischemia shows signs of venous congestion, edema, hemorrhage and abrupt change at the pedicle. MRI will also help identify ovarian vs uterine origin of a mass.

When to rely on microbiology or invasive testing
A positive urine culture that correlates clinically is sufficient to treat as UTI. If sepsis is a concern, blood cultures are required. If surgery is undertaken (because imaging suggests torsion, rupture, or the diagnosis remains uncertain with worsening symptoms), obtain intra‑operative peritoneal fluid and tissue cultures and send tissue for pathology; these are definitive for infection versus ischemic necrosis. Persistent fever after appropriate antibiotics or progressive focal signs despite conservative care should lower the threshold for operative exploration.

Management implications
If urinalysis/culture confirm UTI and clinical features point to an uncomplicated urinary source, treat with pregnancy‑safe antibiotics and observe; fever and neutrophilia should improve within 48–72 hours. If imaging shows absent flow or MRI features of ischemia/hemorrhage in a pedunculated mass, or the pain is severe and focal or the patient is deteriorating, surgical management is indicated (myomectomy/detorsion) because ischemia/necrosis risks peritonitis and sepsis; intra‑operative findings and pathology then confirm red degeneration versus torsion. Empiric antibiotics are appropriate if infection is suspected, but do not delay imaging or surgical consultation when torsion/necrosis is plausible.

In short: correlate symptoms (particularly urinary vs focal abdominal), get urinalysis + culture first, obtain targeted labs and blood cultures if febrile, use Doppler ultrasound then non‑contrast MRI to look for loss of vascularity or hemorrhagic changes, and proceed to operative evaluation with intra‑operative cultures and pathology when imaging/clinical course suggests torsion or necrosis or when the patient worsens despite conservative care.

### [Management] Which antibiotic regimens are appropriate in pregnancy for a culture-proven Klebsiella urinary tract infection, and how do you coordinate perioperative prophylaxis if abdominal surgery is planned?

Use of clarithromycin is suboptimal because clarithromycin has less favourable pregnancy safety data and is not needed for Klebsiella; azithromycin would be the preferred macrolide if a macrolide were required.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the typical trajectory of uterine leiomyoma size across trimesters and the early postpartum period, and how this natural history informs the timing of intervention.

Uterine leiomyomas do not change size in a simple linear way during pregnancy; their natural history is driven by early, hormone‑mediated enlargement followed by relative plateauing and then rapid postpartum involution. Quantitatively, cohort data show the greatest proportional growth occurs early — roughly a 6% weekly increase between the first and second trimesters (overall about 6.9%/week early on), slowing to approximately 3%/week between the second and third trimesters. After delivery many leiomyomas regress substantially: mean size reductions of about 40–50% are reported by six weeks postpartum (wide individual variation).

Understanding that trajectory explains clinical decision‑making. Because growth is most rapid in the first trimester and early second trimester, mass effect and degenerative complications (pain from red degeneration, torsion of pedunculated fibroids, infection, compression) are more likely to present in mid‑pregnancy — but elective removal in the first trimester carries a high miscarriage risk. Therefore, when intervention is necessary for acute complications (torsion, rupture, intractable pain, hemodynamic compromise, or clear oncologic concern) it is reasonable to operate emergently regardless of gestation; when surgery can be timed, the second trimester is the preferred window because maternal‑fetal risks (miscarriage, preterm labour, anesthesia) are lowest. Conversely, if symptoms are manageable and there is no immediate threat to the pregnancy, expectant management is usually preferred because many myomas will stop growing and often shrink substantially after delivery, allowing safer definitive treatment in the postpartum period.

Apply these principles to the presented case: the patient had a highly vascular, twisted pedunculated myoma with leakage and symptomatic mass effect — a complication that justifies intra‑pregnancy myomectomy. The team limited surgery to the symptomatic pedunculated lesion and left the large intramural/subserosal myoma in place because it was stable and likely to regress postpartum; indeed the residual myoma decreased in size on follow‑up. In practice, therefore, intervene urgently for complications irrespective of trimester, prefer the second trimester for nonurgent necessary procedures, and plan definitive management after delivery when feasible because of frequent postpartum involution.

### [Management] What analgesic strategies are preferred for fibroid-related pain in pregnancy, and which medications should be avoided or limited by gestational age due to fetal risks?

Acetaminophen (paracetamol) is the first‑line analgesic for fibroid‑related pain in pregnancy because it is effective for moderate pain, has a favorable safety profile for the fetus at usual doses, and was the drug used successfully in this case. Nonpharmacologic measures — pelvic rest, positional changes (semi‑upright to reduce diaphragmatic compression), abdominal support, heat, and iron repletion if anaemia is present — should be applied concurrently because they reduce analgesic needs and maternal physiologic stress.

If acetaminophen alone is inadequate, short, judicious use of other agents may be considered with attention to gestational age and fetal risks. Nonsteroidal anti‑inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) can provide superior analgesia for myoma pain or degeneration but must be used with caution: avoid routine or prolonged NSAID use in pregnancy and especially avoid NSAIDs after mid‑pregnancy because of well‑documented fetal risks (oligohydramnios from decreased fetal renal perfusion and premature closure of the ductus arteriosus). Opioids are acceptable for severe, refractory pain for short courses, using the lowest effective dose and monitoring the mother and fetus; prolonged or high‑dose opioid exposure in late pregnancy risks neonatal respiratory depression at delivery and neonatal withdrawal. Avoid chronic opioid therapy during pregnancy whenever possible.

Practical approach: begin with acetaminophen and supportive measures; if breakthrough pain before mid‑pregnancy, a short NSAID course may be used sparingly with informed consent; after ~20 weeks, avoid NSAIDs and, if needed, use short opioid courses with obstetric and neonatal planning. Always weigh maternal benefit against fetal risk and coordinate management with obstetrics, anaesthesia and (if needed) neonatology.

### [Clinical Pearls] How should CA-125 and other tumour markers (CEA, CA 19-9, LDH) be used when evaluating adnexal masses in pregnancy, and when would they meaningfully change management?

Tumour markers in pregnancy are adjuncts, not diagnostic tests. Pregnancy alters many marker levels and common benign gravid conditions (decidualisation, endometriosis, leiomyomas, placental release, inflammation) cause false‑positives, so interpretation must be anchored to trimester, imaging, and the clinical picture.

CA‑125: rises physiologically in the first trimester and again near term; second‑trimester levels are usually low (median ≈14 U/mL), so a modest isolated elevation in pregnancy (eg, 36–50 U/mL as in this case) is nonspecific and cannot by itself diagnose malignancy. Serial measurements that show a clear upward trend, very high absolute values, or concordant suspicious imaging (complex solid components, papillary projections, septations, ascites, nodal disease) increase concern and may tip management toward definitive diagnosis or surgery. In practice, CA‑125 meaningfully changes decisions when levels are markedly and persistently abnormal (eg, well above typical pregnancy peaks, often hundreds of U/mL) and when the marker amplifies worrisome sonographic/MRI features or a deteriorating clinical course.

CEA, CA 19‑9: these markers are rarely useful in pregnancy for primary adnexal evaluation. Mild elevations are nonspecific; very high values that fit an imaging pattern of mucinous ovarian tumors or an extracolonic process can support concern, but they are not independent indications for operative intervention.

LDH and germ‑cell markers: LDH (dysgerminoma), AFP and β‑hCG (yolk sac, choriocarcinoma, other germ‑cell tumours) have a clearer role because certain germ‑cell tumours have characteristic marker profiles. Interpretation is complicated by pregnancy physiology: maternal AFP and β‑hCG are increased by the pregnancy itself, so only very marked deviations from gestational norms or the presence of a tumour‑specific pattern (eg, extremely high LDH in the appropriate imaging context) are actionable. When a marker profile is strongly suggestive of a germ‑cell tumour, earlier surgical management is more justifiable because these tumours are often chemo‑sensitive and timely treatment affects maternal outcomes.

Practical approach: use markers as an adjunct to detailed ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI, obtain pregnancy‑adjusted reference points, and repeat markers if the initial result is borderline. Do not operate for a mildly elevated CA‑125 alone. Proceed to surgery during pregnancy when clinical signs (pain, torsion, rupture, infection), rapid mass growth, or concordant imaging plus markedly abnormal or rising tumour markers raise a genuine suspicion of malignancy — or when delay would threaten maternal or fetal health. Always make the decision in a multidisciplinary setting and prioritize fetal monitoring and minimally sufficient surgical intervention to address the immediate risk.

### [Morning Report] Non-contrast MRI in mid-pregnancy shows a unilocular cyst with thickened wall, inhomogeneous fluid, and focal T1/T2 hyperintense nodular areas plus hypertrophied vessels. How does this pattern shape your differential, and what additional information would you seek preoperatively?

This MRI pattern—unilocular cyst with a thickened wall, inhomogeneous (T1/T2–bright) fluid and focal T1/T2–hyperintense nodular areas together with hypertrophied vessels—narrows the differential by pointing to two competing diagnostic families and one urgent management consideration.

First, the combination of T1 hyperintensity and inhomogeneous fluid strongly suggests blood‑ or protein‑rich contents (eg, endometrioma with repeated haemorrhage or a haemorrhagic cyst). Focal T1/T2‑bright mural nodules in a cystic adnexal lesion raise concern for decidualized endometrioma in pregnancy (benign, nodular decidual tissue can be bright and mimic solid tumor). Classically, endometriomas show the “shading” sign on T2 and T1 hyperintensity from blood products.

Second, the presence of hypertrophied feeding vessels and a thickened, sometimes pedunculated wall points toward a uterine origin — a pedunculated cystic or degenerating leiomyoma. Pedunculated fibroids can undergo cystic degeneration, torsion and haemorrhage and may have an identifiable vascular pedicle on Doppler or even MRI. In pregnancy the decidualized endometrioma versus cystic degenerated leiomyoma distinction is particularly important because management differs (expectant versus possible myomectomy if complicated).

Less likely but still on the list are cystic ovarian neoplasms with mural nodules (borderline or malignant cystadenomas), tubo‑ovarian abscess (if infectious signs dominate), and simple haemorrhagic cysts. The lack of adenopathy and normal other tumor markers lowers, but does not exclude, malignancy.

Because contrast MRI is avoided in pregnancy and that limits assessment of true mural enhancement (a key discriminator), the preoperative information I would seek is both diagnostic and pragmatic. Diagnostically, I would obtain a targeted transvaginal ultrasound with high‑resolution imaging and colour Doppler to look specifically for a vascular pedicle or stalk connecting the lesion to the uterus, assess the cleavage plane, and characterise mural nodules (vascularity, mobility) — finding a feeding vessel or clear continuity with uterine myometrium would favour a pedunculated leiomyoma. I would review T1 fat‑saturated sequences (to distinguish fat from blood/protein), look for the T2 “shading” sign of endometrioma, and add diffusion‑weighted imaging if available (restricted diffusion in solid malignant nodules). I would repeat CA‑125 and consider serial measurements rather than a single value to see trend, and check inflammatory markers and cultures if infection is suspected.

From a perioperative planning standpoint I would ensure fetal monitoring documentation and multidisciplinary agreement (obstetrics, anesthesia, gynecologic surgery), obtain blood type and crossmatch and optimize hemoglobin (anticipate possible transfusion), plan intraoperative fetal monitoring and short operative time, have uterotonics and tocolysis protocols ready, and counsel the patient about the possibility of conversion to more extensive surgery (eg, myomectomy vs greater uterine repair vs hysterectomy) and neonatal implications.

In short: the imaging pattern narrows the differential mainly to decidualized endometrioma versus cystic/haemorrhagic, pedunculated degenerating leiomyoma (and less likely cystic ovarian neoplasm). Because gadolinium is contraindicated, a focused transvaginal US with Doppler to look for a uterine pedicle, review of specific MRI sequences (T1 fat‑sat, T2 shading, DWI), serial CA‑125 and inflammatory markers, and thorough preoperative maternal–fetal planning are the key next steps to guide management.

### [Management] A 7–9 cm posterior intramural–subserosal fibroid occupies the lower uterine segment at term. How would you plan delivery (route, timing, uterine incision strategy), and what intrapartum risks require preparation?

You have a term patient with a 7–9 cm posterior intramural–subserosal fibroid occupying the lower uterine segment. The practical plan is guided by whether the leiomyoma actually obstructs the birth canal and by the balance between avoiding hemorrhage and achieving safe delivery.

Route and timing
If the fibroid materially occupies the lower uterine segment and would block descent (as in your scenario), plan an elective cesarean delivery at term (≥37 weeks) rather than expectant trial of labour. Elective timing minimizes the chance of intrapartum emergency, allows optimal staffing and blood product availability, and avoids obstructed labour and fetal compromise from an unplanned arrest. If fetal or maternal indications require earlier delivery, proceed when risks of continuation outweigh prematurity.

Uterine incision strategy
Aim for a lower uterine segment transverse (LUS) incision if feasible because it carries the lowest maternal morbidity and the best future-uterine-rupture profile. Pre‑operative imaging and palpation at operation determine feasibility. If the fibroid lies directly beneath the intended LUS incision and cannot be shifted aside, options (in order of preference) are: 1) make the transverse incision through the thinnest area of lower segment lateral to the fibroid; 2) extend the transverse incision laterally (J or T) to deliver the fetus without cutting deeply into leiomyoma; 3) if the LUS is obliterated by the myoma, consider a low vertical uterine incision (classical isthmic/low vertical) to avoid transecting the fibroid; reserve a classical (midline upper-segment vertical) incision only if lower-segment and low-vertical incisions are impossible because classical incisions increase hemorrhage and future rupture risk. If a subserosal or pedunculated fibroid is encountered at delivery, selective myomectomy at the time of cesarean can be considered by an experienced team; avoid routine enucleation of a firmly intramural lower-segment fibroid because of high hemorrhage risk. In short: prefer LUS transverse; if obstructed, choose a low-vertical or alternative incision rather than cutting blindly through a vascular myoma.

Anticipate and prepare for significant hemorrhage. Ensure preoperative cross‑matched blood available, large-bore IV access, cell salvage if available, tranexamic acid on hand, uterotonics, uterine compression sutures, balloon tamponade, and readiness for peripartum hysterectomy. Position senior obstetrician and anesthesiologist present, and involve gynaecologic surgeons and interventional radiology on standby if possible. Expect difficult access to the lower uterine segment, possible need for extension of skin/uterine incision, prolonged operative time, and higher likelihood of postpartum hemorrhage from atony or from bleeding at the myoma bed if inadvertently incised. Be prepared for retained‑placenta issues or placenta delivered from an altered lower segment. Counsel and document in advance the potential need for intraoperative myomectomy versus deferral, and the small but real possibility of hysterectomy.

Broader principle
Plan delivery based on mechanical obstruction and safety rather than fibroid size alone. Preserve the lower-segment transverse approach when possible to minimize future rupture risk; when the lower segment is effectively obliterated by the myoma, choose the uterine incision that achieves safe fetal extraction and hemorrhage control, with full preparation for massive obstetric hemorrhage.

### [Clinical Pearls] What intraoperative techniques reduce bleeding risk during myomectomy in pregnancy (e.g., vasoconstrictors, uterotonics timing, devascularisation, suturing strategies), and which are contraindicated?

Key principle: reduce blood loss by minimizing uterine blood flow and promoting local hemostasis while avoiding interventions that provoke sustained uterine contraction or placental/fetal hypoperfusion. In pregnancy the margin for error is smaller — techniques that are routine in non‑pregnant myomectomy must be adapted for fetal safety.

Practical intraoperative strategy (what to do)
Start with optimisation before the cut: correct anemia if possible; have cross‑matched blood and cell‑salvage available; continuous fetal heart monitoring when gestation and logistics allow.

Mechanical devascularisation is the cornerstone. For a pedunculated myoma, clamp and ligate the pedicle early and remove the mass quickly; that alone often controls bleeding. For intramural lesions, temporary reduction of uterine inflow with a uterine tourniquet (Rubber or Foley tourniquet around the lower uterine segment) or bilateral temporary uterine artery occlusion (clips or vascular clamps) is effective and reversible — it lowers blood loss without causing systemic maternal vasospasm. Permanent internal iliac or uterine artery ligation can be reserved for uncontrolled hemorrhage but is more morbid and may compromise future uterine perfusion.

Local vasoconstriction: dilute vasopressin injected into the myometrium around the myoma (small volumes, low concentration) is commonly used to reduce bleeding. In pregnancy it must be used cautiously because systemic absorption can cause maternal hypertension, bradycardia and reflex uterine vasospasm with fetal heart rate changes; therefore use the lowest effective dose, aspirate before injection, monitor maternal vitals and fetal heart rate, and have anaesthesia ready to treat hemodynamic events.

Energy and topical adjuncts: careful bipolar electrocautery for focal coagulation is preferable to wide, high‑energy diathermy; excessive thermal spread risks myometrial damage. Topical haemostatic agents (oxidized regenerated cellulose, gelatin sponges, fibrin sealants) are useful adjuncts, particularly in oozing surfaces or when avoiding deep sutures.

Suturing technique for durable hemostasis and uterine integrity: multilayer closure of the myometrial defect with full‑thickness, delayed‑absorbable sutures to obliterate dead space and re‑approximate contractile tissue reduces bleeding and lowers risk of postoperative uterine rupture. Deep interrupted figure‑of‑eight or mattress sutures for the myoma bed followed by an outer seromuscular layer is standard. Avoid excessive intramural suture mass that compromises blood supply. Tie sutures securely but avoid very tight strangulation of tissue.

Adjuncts and haemostatic measures: topical tranexamic acid locally or systemically may reduce bleeding; systemic tranexamic acid is acceptable when needed. In centers with experience, intraoperative cell salvage with leukocyte‑depletion filters is reasonable in obstetric/myoma surgery.

What to avoid (contraindications or approaches to use with caution)
Avoid giving uterotonics (oxytocin bolus, methylergometrine, prostaglandins — carboprost, misoprostol) before the fetus is delivered or in cases where the uterus must remain relaxed; these agents cause strong uterine contraction and can precipitate fetal hypoxia, placental abruption, or compromise the surgical field by rapid contraction and tearing. Ergot alkaloids and prostaglandins are particularly contraindicated intraoperatively in a pregnant uterus unless the fetus is delivered or postpartum hemorrhage mandates their use.

Systemic high‑dose vasoconstrictors (eg, large boluses of vasopressin, terlipressin, unmonitored epinephrine) are risky because of maternal hypertension and secondary uteroplacental vasospasm; use only small, local, carefully titrated vasopressin with monitoring. Extensive use of unipolar diathermy across the myometrium should be avoided because it can cause thermal injury and impair healing.

Finally, prolonged, vigorous uterine exteriorisation and manipulation should be minimized because they provoke contractions and fetal stress; keep the operation as efficient and expeditious as safe.

Bottom line: in pregnant myomectomy prioritize reversible mechanical devascularisation (tourniquet or temporary uterine artery occlusion), cautious local vasopressin injection with monitoring, meticulous multilayer suturing and topical haemostatic adjuncts. Do not use systemic uterotonics or large systemic vasoconstrictor doses before delivery; avoid excessive thermal injury. Plan the procedure, monitor the fetus, and have blood/blood‑saving strategies ready.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain how large abdominal and uterine masses in mid-pregnancy can cause restrictive ventilatory symptoms despite normal oxygen saturation and ECG, and how patient positioning can mitigate symptoms.

Large intra‑abdominal and uterine masses produce dyspnoea in pregnancy by a purely mechanical, restrictive process rather than by primary lung or cardiac disease. The masses (plus the gravid uterus) push the diaphragm cephalad, reduce the resting volume of the thoracic cavity and limit chest wall excursion. That lowers functional residual capacity, expiratory reserve volume and total lung capacity; the patient must breathe more rapidly and shallowly to maintain minute ventilation, increasing the work of breathing and producing the sensation of breathlessness even though gas exchange at the alveolar level remains adequate. Because ventilation–perfusion relationships are often preserved, peripheral oxygen saturation and a resting ECG can be normal despite significant symptomatic restriction.

Two additional factors commonly amplify symptoms in pregnancy: physiologic increases in oxygen demand and cardiac output, and any coexisting anaemia or low‑grade infection (both present in this case) which raise ventilatory drive so the mechanical limitation becomes clinically apparent. Likewise, a pedunculated, torsed or highly vascular mass can produce discomfort and reflex splinting that further restricts tidal expansion.

Patient positioning mitigates symptoms by changing the relation of the abdominal contents to the diaphragm. Sitting upright or semi‑orthostatic and lateral decubitus positions allow gravity to pull the uterus and masses away from the diaphragm, increasing diaphragmatic excursion and FRC, lowering work of breathing and relieving dyspnoea. Left lateral tilt is especially useful in pregnancy because it also optimises venous return and uteroplacental perfusion. These are simple, effective bedside measures while definitive evaluation (imaging, multidisciplinary planning) proceeds.

Clinical takeaway: dyspnoea from large intra‑abdominal masses in pregnancy is usually restrictive and mechanical—expect normal SpO2/ECG early on. Management focuses first on supportive measures (oxygen if needed, correct anaemia/infection) and on positioning to relieve diaphragmatic compression, and then on targeted imaging and multidisciplinary planning for intervention only when maternal or fetal risk or complications warrant surgery.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What MRI features support ovarian torsion (e.g., twisted vascular pedicle, ovarian enlargement, stromal oedema) and how do these differ from torsion of a pedunculated uterine fibroid?

Key MRI clues that point to ovarian torsion are related to the ovary’s change in size, internal structure and vascular supply; in contrast, torsion of a pedunculated subserosal fibroid shows continuity with the uterus and tissue characteristics of a leiomyoma rather than an adnexal organ. In practice you must combine imaging signs with the clinical picture (sudden or progressive unilateral pain, nausea/fever, leukocytosis) and remember that Doppler and contrast enhancement may be misleading or unavailable in pregnancy.

For ovarian torsion, MRI features that support the diagnosis are an enlarged ovary (often >4 cm) with stromal edema producing increased T2 signal centrally, multiple small peripheral follicles displaced to the rim (“follicular peripheralization”), and heterogeneous ovarian signal if haemorrhage or necrosis is present (T1 hyperintense areas for blood). The twisted vascular pedicle may be seen as the “whirlpool” or beaded tubular structure of engorged, spiraled vessels extending from the adnexa; associated findings include peri‑ovarian and pelvic free fluid and lack of normal venous/lymphatic drainage signs. On non‑contrast studies you may infer vascular compromise by progressive stromal T2 hyperintensity, T1 hyperintensity from haemorrhage, and progressive heterogeneity. Importantly, arterial flow on Doppler (or preserved flow on MRI vascular sequences) does not exclude torsion because venous outflow obstruction can precede arterial compromise.

For torsion of a pedunculated uterine fibroid, MRI shows a mass contiguous with the uterine serosa with a visible or inferable stalk or “bridging vessels” to the uterus; the lesion’s internal signal reflects leiomyoma tissue (typically low T2 signal for compact fibroid) but may show high T2 signal or cystic areas if there is red/cystic degeneration. A torsed pedunculated myoma demonstrates acute ischemic change: central or diffuse high T2 signal from oedema, heterogeneous high T1 if haemorrhagic necrosis is present, and peripheral or patchy internal heterogeneity. Crucially, the ovary is typically separate and identifiable — you will not see peripheralized ovarian follicles within the mass. In pregnancy, you cannot rely on gadolinium to show nonenhancement, so continuity with the uterine wall/stalk and identification of feeding vessels are the most helpful differentiators.

Put simply: an enlarged, edematous adnexal structure with peripheral follicles and a whirlpool vascular pedicle = ovarian torsion; a mass anatomically contiguous with the uterus, showing leiomyomatous signal characteristics (with secondary degeneration/necrosis) and a pedicle or bridging vessels to the uterine serosa = torsion of a pedunculated fibroid. Always synthesize these MRI signs with the ultrasound data and clinical course, because imaging can be equivocal in pregnancy and management hinges on symptoms and maternal–fetal risk.

### [Management] What is the role, if any, for perioperative tocolytics or progesterone support around non-obstetric surgery in mid-pregnancy, and what does current evidence suggest about efficacy and indications?

Short answer: There is no evidence to support routine administration of perioperative tocolytics or empiric progesterone solely because a pregnant patient undergoes non‑obstetric surgery in mid‑pregnancy. Management should be individualized: monitor the fetus, treat uterine activity or threatened preterm birth if it occurs, and apply established obstetric interventions (tocolysis, antenatal corticosteroids, magnesium neuroprotection, or maternal progesterone) only when their standard obstetric indications are met.

Why: Non‑obstetric abdominal surgery can increase the theoretical risk of uterine irritability and preterm labour, but randomized data are essentially absent and observational series do not demonstrate that prophylactic tocolysis or perioperative progesterone reduces miscarriage or preterm birth after surgery. Professional guidance (obstetric society statements and reviews of the literature) therefore does not recommend routine prophylactic tocolytics or routine perioperative progesterone for all pregnant patients undergoing surgery.

How this translates into practice (clinical reasoning): before surgery, assemble a multidisciplinary team (surgery, obstetrics/MFM, anesthesia, neonatology). Document fetal heart activity and gestational age. During and after the procedure, monitor for uterine contractions and signs of preterm labour. If contractions consistent with threatened preterm labour occur, treat them according to obstetric principles — calcium‑channel blockers (nifedipine) are commonly preferred over beta‑agonists because of a better side‑effect profile; avoid agents that carry disproportionate maternal risks in the perioperative setting. If preterm delivery is imminent (per standard obstetric criteria), give antenatal corticosteroids for fetal lung maturation and consider magnesium sulfate for neuroprotection when appropriate. Use progesterone prophylaxis only when the patient already meets established indications (prior spontaneous preterm birth, short cervix), not merely because of surgery.

Safety caveats: prophylactic tocolytics are not benign — beta‑agonists can cause maternal tachycardia, hyperglycaemia and pulmonary oedema (especially with intravenous fluids/oxytocics); nifedipine can cause hypotension; atosiban is not universally available. Progesterone (vaginal or intramuscular) is generally well tolerated but its efficacy in preventing surgery‑related preterm birth is unproven; using it for reassurance is common in some centers but should be framed as empiric rather than evidence‑based.

Applying this to the reported case: the team’s decision to monitor fetal heart rate intra‑/postoperatively and reserve interventions for signs of uterine activity was appropriate. The intraoperative and prolonged intramuscular progesterone the team administered reflects a pragmatic, prophylactic approach used by some centers, but it should be recognized as lacking high‑quality evidence for preventing surgery‑related preterm birth. The clearer, evidence‑based actions in such cases are careful fetal surveillance, prompt treatment of documented contractions, and preparation to give steroids and neonatal support if preterm delivery becomes likely.

### [Management] How do you approach iron-deficiency anaemia in mid-pregnancy when complicated by acute blood loss or surgery: thresholds for IV iron, transfusion triggers, and post-operative targets?

Short answer: treat the physiology and the clinical context, not a single number. In pregnant patients with iron-deficiency anaemia complicated by acute blood loss or surgery you should prioritize rapid iron repletion when bleeding is controlled and reserve red‑cell transfusion for hemodynamic instability, ongoing major blood loss, or severe/symptomatic anemia. Use a restrictive transfusion strategy (Hb threshold ~7 g/dL in stable patients) with higher individualized thresholds (≈7.5–8 g/dL or higher) for symptomatic women, cardiovascular disease, or major obstetric concerns. Give intravenous iron (e.g. ferric carboxymaltose) when oral iron is inadequate or too slow — typically when Hb is modestly low and rapid repletion is needed (Hb ≈8–11 g/dL with ferritin <30 µg/L) or after transfusion to restore iron stores. Aim post‑operative Hb in the low normal range for pregnancy (about 8–11 g/dL) and restore ferritin to >30–50 µg/L to prevent relapse.

Why: pregnancy increases plasma volume and oxygen requirements; the fetus depends on maternal oxygen-carrying capacity, so anemia risks maternal symptoms and fetal compromise. However, liberal transfusion exposes mother to alloimmunisation, transfusion reactions, and infection risk and does not improve outcomes if Hb is acceptable. Trials and guidelines outside pregnancy support restrictive transfusion thresholds (Hb 7 g/dL) in stable patients; the same principles apply in pregnancy but with a lower tolerance for symptoms and comorbidity. Acute hemorrhage with hemodynamic instability requires immediate transfusion and surgical control.

How to apply this to the case
Before surgery the patient had iron‑deficiency anemia (Hb 9.9 g/dL, ferritin 25). That is a setting where IV iron is appropriate if oral therapy is unlikely to be effective before the planned intervention or if symptoms/progression suggest imminent need. After operative blood loss her Hb fell to 7.5 g/dL with ongoing iron deficiency; the team appropriately gave IV ferric carboxymaltose (rapid depot repletion) and transfused two units to rapidly restore oxygen carrying capacity and allow safe continuation of pregnancy. That approach balances the need for immediate Hb correction (transfusion for symptomatic or low Hb after acute loss) with definitive iron repletion to prevent recurrent anemia.

Practical thresholds and targets
- Indications for IV iron in pregnancy: failure/intolerance of oral iron; need for faster correction (surgery, late pregnancy, symptomatic anemia); Hb roughly 8–11 g/dL with ferritin <30 µg/L. IV iron (ferric carboxymaltose or iron sucrose) is effective and generally safe in second and third trimesters. Dosing: weight- and deficit-based (many regimens use 1,000 mg ferric carboxymaltose single dose if needed).
- Transfusion triggers: hemodynamic instability/ongoing bleeding → transfuse immediately. For hemodynamically stable patients use restrictive threshold: Hb <7 g/dL generally indicates transfusion; consider transfusion at Hb 7–8 g/dL if symptomatic, evidence of cardiac disease, or obstetrical concerns. In pregnancy you may choose a slightly higher threshold (≈7.5–8 g/dL) in symptomatic women or when fetal concerns exist.
- Post‑operative targets: restore Hb to a level that relieves symptoms and supports pregnancy — commonly aim for Hb 8–11 g/dL. More clinically: if transfused to rapidly correct oxygen delivery, follow with IV iron to replenish iron stores and target ferritin >30–50 µg/L (some aim for >50–100 µg/L for robust repletion).

Additional points
- Always individualize decisions by hemodynamics, ongoing blood loss, symptoms, comorbidities, gestational age, and fetal status. Document fetal heart rate before/during/after surgery.
- Use IV iron early when time to oral correction is insufficient; IV iron reduces need for transfusion when given preop or soon after blood loss.
- When transfusing, consider single‑unit transfusion followed by reassessment in stable patients rather than automatic 2‑unit transfusions.
- Monitor Hb and ferritin after therapy (within 2–4 weeks) and continue iron supplementation as needed through pregnancy and postpartum.

Bottom line: in mid‑pregnancy with surgical blood loss you treat acute instability with transfusion, use restrictive transfusion thresholds in stable patients (≈7 g/dL, higher if symptomatic), and give IV iron when rapid or reliable iron repletion is needed — then aim to restore Hb to the low‑normal pregnancy range and replenish ferritin to prevent recurrence.

### [Clinical Pearls] Under what limited circumstances might gadolinium-enhanced MRI be considered during pregnancy, and what fetal risks drive the general recommendation to avoid it?

Gadolinium chelates cross the placenta, enter the fetal circulation, and are excreted into and can accumulate in the amniotic fluid; fetal re‑uptake from swallowed amniotic fluid can prolong exposure. Animal and human observational data raise concerns about gadolinium deposition in fetal tissues and an association (in some retrospective series) with higher rates of stillbirth, neonatal death, and rare neonatal/inflammatory or rheumatologic conditions. Because the absolute risk is uncertain but plausibly real, major guidelines advise avoiding gadolinium in pregnancy unless the diagnostic information cannot be obtained by safer means and would materially change management.

In practice, gadolinium‑enhanced MRI in pregnancy should be considered only in tightly limited situations: when non‑contrast MRI and ultrasound are insufficient, when the result is urgent and would alter immediate care for a life‑threatening maternal or fetal condition (for example, characterization or staging of a suspected aggressive maternal malignancy where contrast is necessary to guide therapy, or rare acute surgical dilemmas in which contrast would change operative planning), and when the benefits to the mother (or fetus) clearly outweigh the potential fetal risks. If used, choose a single dose of a macrocyclic agent, document informed consent after discussion of uncertain fetal risks, avoid first‑trimester exposure when feasible, use the lowest field strength consistent with diagnostic needs (commonly 1.5 T), and limit repeat exposures. In most situations—including the case you describe—high‑quality ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI provide adequate information and gadolinium is best avoided.

### [Morning Report] Imaging cannot identify a feeding vessel or stalk for a massive upper abdominal cystic mass in a 21-week pregnancy. How should this diagnostic uncertainty be incorporated into preoperative counselling and surgical planning?

When imaging in pregnancy cannot show a feeding vessel or stalk for a large upper abdominal cystic mass, you must treat that uncertainty as a central operative risk — not a minor diagnostic curiosity — and fold it explicitly into counselling, consent and the surgical plan.

Tell the patient and document that preoperative imaging (US ± non‑contrast MRI) cannot definitively distinguish an adnexal cyst from a pedunculated uterine mass, and explain the practical implications. If the mass is ovarian, the operation may be adnexectomy or cystectomy; if uterine and pedunculated, excision (myomectomy) with uterine wall repair may be required; if intramural or broad‑based myoma, removal may not be possible without substantial uterine incision and haemorrhage. Each scenario carries different risks to the pregnancy (procedure‑related blood loss, need for transfusion, preterm labour, possible pregnancy loss) and to future delivery (uterine scar).

Frame the consent around likely intraoperative decision points rather than a single predicted procedure. Specifically, preoperatively discuss and obtain consent for: exploration and excision of the mass if technically feasible; conversion of planned conservative procedure to myomectomy or ovarian excision as required; control of bleeding including use of uterotonics, direct uterine repair, temporary vascular control if available; blood transfusion; possible need to leave a second mass in situ to preserve the pregnancy; and, rarely, hysterectomy if uncontrollable haemorrhage occurs. Reassure that the team will attempt the least-invasive, pregnancy‑sparing approach compatible with maternal safety.

Operational planning should mirror that consent. Convene a multidisciplinary team (obstetrics, gynecologic surgery, anaesthesia, neonatology on standby) and prepare for worst‑case contingencies while aiming to minimize intervention. Ensure large‑bore IV access, cross‑matched blood available, cell‑saver if institutionally appropriate, and readiness for rapid transfusion. Plan intraoperative fetal monitoring when feasible (pre‑, intra‑ and post‑op fetal heart checks) and medical measures to support the pregnancy (prophylactic progesterone or tocolytics per local practice). Positioning and incision should permit extension if a uterine repair or unexpected hysterectomy is required. If the mass is unexpectedly pedunculated and torsed, be prepared to excise its stalk and repair the uterine defect with absorbable sutures; if a broad‑based intramural lesion is encountered, weigh immediate resection against leaving it in situ and deferring definitive management postpartum to avoid excessive bleeding and risk to the fetus.

Finally, discuss the implications for delivery: leaving large pelvic myomas in place may mandate elective cesarean delivery and influence timing. Make a documented plan for postoperative monitoring (hemoglobin trends, infection surveillance) and close obstetric follow‑up.

In short, convert diagnostic uncertainty into explicit, staged decision‑making: inform the patient about the plausible intraoperative findings and their consequences, obtain broad but specific consent for reasonable escalations, assemble the multidisciplinary team and blood‑management resources, and plan the operation to permit the least invasive, pregnancy‑preserving intervention compatible with maternal safety.

### [Management] During exploration of a large cystic abdominal mass with uncertain origin in a pregnant patient, what intraoperative steps (e.g., peritoneal washings, identification of pedicle, assessment of uterine continuity) help establish diagnosis while preserving pregnancy?

When you open the abdomen for a large, indeterminate cystic mass in a pregnant patient your goal is simultaneous diagnosis and maximum preservation of the pregnancy — shorter, safer, definitive steps that avoid unnecessary hysterectomy or cavalrous exploration.

Start with targeted sampling and documentation: inspect and record fetal heart activity before incision and continue intermittent auscultation or Doppler monitoring intra‑ and immediately post‑op; obtain peritoneal washings for cytology and microbiology early (before manipulating the mass) so you do not contaminate specimens. Systematically explore the abdomen to identify organ of origin — follow the mass to its base. Demonstrating a discrete pedicle or stalk arising from the uterine serosa, ovarian surface, or mesentery is the single most useful intraoperative diagnostic finding; if a pedicle is present, clamp, ligate and divide it with care, control bleeding promptly, and close the uterine or serosal defect with absorbable sutures to restore uterine continuity. If the lesion is clearly contiguous with myometrium (no discrete stalk) or inseparable from uterine wall, avoid aggressive excision that would enter the endometrial cavity or substantially weaken the uterus; leave such intramural masses in situ unless they are causing life‑threatening complications. Avoid cyst rupture and spillage when malignancy cannot be excluded.

Obtain targeted specimens for histology — excise only what you need for diagnosis (the pedunculated mass, biopsy of suspicious solid areas) rather than wide resection; send fresh tissue for frozen section only if the result would change immediate management and pathology support is available. Minimize uterine manipulation and operative time, use meticulous hemostasis (topical agents or uterotonic measures as indicated), and employ prophylactic measures to reduce preterm labor risk (continuous fetal monitoring, and center‑specific tocolaxis or progesterone protocols when indicated). Finally, document the anatomic relationship of residual masses and counsel that staged postpartum surgery is often safer if the pregnancy can be preserved.

These steps — peritoneal washings before manipulation, careful identification of a pedicle, limited excision with secure closure of the uterine defect, avoidance of cavity entry or extensive myometrial resection, targeted tissue sampling (with frozen section only when necessary), fetal monitoring, and minimization of operative time and uterine trauma — together maximize diagnostic yield while protecting the pregnancy.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How would an elevated LDH in the setting of a rapidly enlarging uterine mass influence suspicion for leiomyosarcoma, and how is oncologic concern balanced with fetal considerations in mid-pregnancy?

An isolated laboratory abnormality never makes the diagnosis, but an elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) in the context of a rapidly enlarging uterine mass should raise the index of suspicion for a malignant smooth‑muscle neoplasm and prompt more urgent, multidisciplinary assessment.

Why LDH matters here: LDH is a nonspecific marker of cell turnover and tissue necrosis. Rapid tumor growth with central ischemic necrosis—features that can produce a high LDH—are characteristic of sarcomas (including leiomyosarcoma) but can also be seen with degenerating benign fibroids, torsion of a pedunculated myoma, infarction, infection, or even pregnancy‑related changes. Thus an elevated LDH increases pretest probability for malignancy when seen together with other worrying features: marked and persistent rapid growth despite pregnancy, new focal solid components or irregular enhancement on MRI, heterogeneous signal with hemorrhage/necrosis, new severe pain or systemic symptoms, and atypical vascular patterns on Doppler. By itself LDH has poor specificity and modest sensitivity — it is a clue, not a confirmation.

How to act in mid‑pregnancy: balance oncologic urgency against fetal maturity and the maternal/fetal risks of surgery or delayed diagnosis. The clinical approach is therefore individualized and governed by three principles: confirm the level of suspicion, minimize harm to the pregnancy when reasonable, and expedite definitive care when concern for malignancy is substantial.

If suspicion is low–moderate (e.g., mildly elevated LDH but imaging consistent with degenerating fibroid, stable fetal status, and symptoms controlled): obtain targeted, non‑contrast MRI if not already done; repeat imaging and tumour markers in short interval; increase surveillance frequency; treat complications conservatively; involve gynecologic oncology in planning. Avoid percutaneous biopsy of presumed uterine mass during pregnancy because sampling is often nondiagnostic and may risk bleeding or dissemination.

If suspicion is high (rapid, unabated growth; markedly elevated LDH; MRI showing large necrotic solid components; systemic signs): counsel the patient that the benefit of prompt surgical resection—both to establish diagnosis and to achieve tumor‑free margins—may outweigh the fetal risks. In the second trimester (as in this case) surgical myomectomy or excisional biopsy via laparotomy can be performed with acceptable fetal and maternal outcomes when indicated, because the uterus is less irritable than in the third trimester and teratogenic risks are lower after organogenesis. Intraoperative continuous fetal monitoring, perioperative steroids if preterm delivery is a possibility, and a plan for fetal resuscitation are essential. If malignancy is confirmed, further management (completion hysterectomy, staging, adjuvant therapy) must be timed according to gestational age, the extent of disease, and maternal wishes; chemotherapy in pregnancy is feasible in some situations but requires oncologic expertise.

In all scenarios the discussion must be shared and documented: explain the rarity of uterine sarcoma, the imperfect predictive value of LDH and CA‑125, the risks of waiting (progression, rupture, hemorrhage, missed window for cure) versus the risks of surgery to the fetus (preterm labor, fetal loss), and the alternatives, including deferred surgery after fetal viability. The multidisciplinary team should include obstetrics, gynecologic oncology, maternal–fetal medicine, anesthesia, and neonatology to tailor timing and the surgical plan to both oncologic safety and fetal maturity.

Applied to the presented case: had LDH been elevated in addition to the clinical picture of pain, fever, mixed solid–cystic mass and rapid enlargement, the threshold to operate at 21 weeks would have been lower—both to relieve symptoms and to obtain tissue diagnosis—while preserving the pregnancy when possible, as the team ultimately did.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What factors during pregnancy increase the risk of misclassifying a large uterine fibroid as an adnexal mass on imaging, and which targeted ultrasound maneuvers can help establish uterine origin?

Teaching point: during pregnancy large uterine leiomyomas often masquerade as adnexal masses because the gravid uterus, the myomas’ degeneration and the patient’s body habitus conspire to break the usual sonographic clues that establish uterine origin. Recognising the specific factors that produce this diagnostic error and using a few targeted ultrasound maneuvers will usually allow you to demonstrate continuity with the myometrium and avoid misclassification.

Why the mistake happens
Large size and “steric bulk” of the pregnant uterus displace normal pelvic anatomy so the uterus, ovaries and adnexa no longer occupy their expected locations. Pedunculated subserosal myomas are particularly liable to look adnexal because a narrow stalk may be invisible on routine scans. Cystic or red‑degeneration, necrosis or haemorrhage change the expected solid appearance of a fibroid and can mimic ovarian cysts or decidualised endometriomas. Posterior or fundal location (and isthmic lesions during uterine enlargement) hides the myometrial cleavage plane. Maternal obesity and an elevated, distended uterus limit acoustic windows. Pregnancy itself raises CA‑125 and causes vascular hypertrophy, which may mimic hypervascular adnexal tumours. Finally, torsion/acute degeneration can alter mobility and vascular patterns and obscure a feeding stalk.

Ultrasound maneuvers that help establish uterine origin
Always look for continuity with the myometrium and for a bridging blood supply before labelling an adnexal mass. Practically, follow the mass in multiple planes with both transabdominal and transvaginal probes and attempt to trace an echogenic band or capsule continuous with the uterine wall. Use the transvaginal probe (high‑frequency) to inspect the uterine serosa–myometrium interface and the posterior uterus when the transabdominal view is limited. Apply graded pressure with the transducer and observe whether the mass moves with the uterus (“moves with uterus” sign) or whether a cleavage plane separates it (sliding sign). Change maternal position or ask for a full bladder to alter organ relationships and reveal continuity. Use colour Doppler to identify bridging vessels arising from the uterine circulation or a visible pedicle (the “bridging vessel” or “pedicle” sign) rather than ovarian arterial supply. If available, 3‑D sonography can help demonstrate the stalk or the interface between mass and myometrium. Correlate the ultrasound findings with careful bimanual pelvic examination: a fixed pelvic mass contiguous with the uterine contour suggests uterine origin.

Clinical principle and implication
If you can demonstrate a myometrial interface or uterine feeding vessels, treat the lesion as a fibroid; if these maneuvers are inconclusive and management hinges on the diagnosis, proceed to non‑contrast MRI or multidisciplinary discussion rather than assuming an adnexal tumour. Establishing uterine origin matters because it changes surveillance, operative planning and delivery decisions in pregnancy.

### [Management] What perioperative strategies (team composition, incision choice, operative time minimisation, haemostasis planning, fetal assessment protocol) are key to safely performing mid-trimester myomectomy when indicated?

When mid‑trimester myomectomy is being considered, the intra‑operative plan must prioritise maternal haemodynamic stability and uteroplacental perfusion while keeping the operation as short and blood‑sparing as possible. The strategy used in the presented case illustrates the key principles.

Assemble a multidisciplinary team beforehand: an experienced obstetric surgeon comfortable with myomectomy and uterine reconstruction, anaesthesiology with obstetric expertise, a neonatologist available (even if preterm delivery is unlikely), interventional radiology on call if uterine artery embolisation or balloon occlusion might be needed, and immediate access to blood‑bank and cell‑salvage. Nursing and instrumentation staff should be briefed and primed for rapid instrument and transfusion needs.

Choose an incision that gives rapid, extensible exposure and minimises uterine manipulation — a vertical midline (xipho‑pubic) incision is usually preferred in the mid‑trimester for very large or multiple lesions because it allows quick access, easy extension and better vascular control. Smaller lesions in favourable positions can be approached via Pfannenstiel or laparoscopy, but not when exposure, haemostasis or foetal monitoring would be compromised.

Minimise operative time by meticulous preoperative planning: review high‑quality ultrasound/MRI to define which lesions require removal and which can safely be left in situ, map vascular pedicles, rehearse the steps with the team, have sutures and haemostatic agents ready, and limit the operation to the therapeutic minimum (remove only the lesion(s) causing symptoms or suspected malignancy). Use the most experienced surgeon available to shorten operative and anaesthesia time; avoid prolonged, radical dissections that add little benefit and increase risk of bleeding and uterine irritability.

Plan haemostasis proactively rather than reactively. Anticipate heavy vascularity in pregnant uteri: use temporary measures (double clamps on pedicles, uterine tourniquet or vessel loops when anatomically feasible) and meticulous, layered uterine closure with absorbable sutures. Have advanced haemostatic tools ready — vascular clamps, bipolar energy, vessel‑sealing devices, topical haemostatic agents, oxidised cellulose, and sutures for uterine artery ligation if needed. Ensure adequate cross‑matched blood and consider intraoperative cell salvage (with appropriate filters) in centres where available and acceptable. Avoid routine use of systemic agents that might cause uterine tetany before delivery; uterotonics are reserved for the postpartum setting or after delivery.

Protect the fetus through perioperative fetal assessment and obstetric readiness. Document fetal heart activity before incision, arrange for intermittent or continuous fetal heart rate assessment during the case when feasible (Doppler or transabdominal monitoring), and document immediately after the procedure. Assign a dedicated obstetrical provider or sonographer to monitor fetal status so the surgical team can focus on haemostasis. Have a plan and thresholds for conversion to urgent delivery (gestational age, non‑reassuring tracing and maternal instability) and ensure neonatology presence if there is any realistic chance of delivery. Consider pharmacologic measures to reduce uterine irritability when indicated: perioperative progesterone or tocolysis can be considered on a case‑by‑case basis per institutional protocol, recognising evidence is limited. Maintain maternal oxygenation, normotension and euvolemia in collaboration with anaesthesia to preserve uteroplacental perfusion.

In short: multidisciplinary planning, an incision that permits rapid exposure, a tightly scripted, time‑efficient operative plan that removes only what is necessary, proactive haemostasis with blood products immediately available, and continuous attention to fetal monitoring and obstetric contingencies are the elements that make mid‑trimester myomectomy as safe as possible.
