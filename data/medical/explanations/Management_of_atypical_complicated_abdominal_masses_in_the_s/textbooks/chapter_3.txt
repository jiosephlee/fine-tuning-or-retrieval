Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

Chapter 3: ## Differential Diagnosis of Large Cystic Abdominopelvic Masses in the Second Trimester

Large cystic or mixed abdominopelvic masses encountered in the second trimester present a diagnostic challenge because the gravid uterus alters anatomy, imaging windows are restricted, and serum markers have limited specificity in pregnancy. In this situation the clinician must integrate the clinical presentation (pain, fever, mass effect), focused ultrasound with colour Doppler, and—when ultrasound is non-diagnostic—non‑contrast MRI to separate lesions of uterine origin from adnexal or peritoneal pathology. The case presented (two large cystic masses in a 21‑week pregnancy, one ultimately shown at surgery to be a twisted pedunculated cystic leiomyoma) illustrates the overlapping appearances and the importance of searching specifically for features that discriminate uterine leiomyomas (including degenerating or pedunculated subserosal types) from ovarian cysts, decidualized endometriomas, adnexal torsion, and other rarer entities.

Approach and priorities
- First, establish clinical urgency. Acute severe pain, peritoneal signs, fever suggest torsion, infarction/necrosis, superinfection or rupture and prioritise urgent intervention even if imaging is imperfect. Mass effect causing respiratory compromise or compression of urinary tract or the uterus also pushes toward intervention. In the presented patient, worsening pain, dyspnoea from mass effect and signs consistent with infection/necrosis were the drivers to operate.
- Second, perform systematic imaging: transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound with Doppler is the first-line test in pregnancy. If ultrasound cannot confidently determine origin or exclude malignancy, proceed to non‑contrast MRI (preferably 1.5 T) which better delineates relationships between mass and myometrium, evaluates internal contents and defines vascular/pedicle anatomy without gadolinium exposure.
- Third, use tumor markers judiciously. CA‑125 and other markers can be altered by pregnancy, endometriosis and benign conditions; an isolated mild CA‑125 elevation is not diagnostic. Serial trends may add information but should not trump convincing imaging or clinical findings.

Common diagnostic considerations and distinguishing features

Degenerating leiomyoma (cystic degeneration)
- Context: Uterine fibroids are common and may undergo degeneration during pregnancy as blood supply becomes insufficient to rapidly enlarging fibroid tissue. Cystic degeneration produces large fluid-filled spaces within a fibroid.
- Imaging: Ultrasound shows a generally uterine‑contiguous mass with cystic areas and well‑defined margins; Doppler may show peripheral vascularity with relatively avascular cystic core. On MRI the degenerated areas are variable: acute haemorrhagic degeneration may be T1 hyperintense, whereas chronic cystic degeneration is T2 hyperintense; fibroid capsule and surrounding myometrium often identifiable. The “beak” or “claw” sign (myometrium curving around the lesion) and direct continuity with the uterine wall support leiomyomatous origin.
- Clinical clues: Localised pain (especially with red degeneration), low‑grade fever and raised inflammatory markers may occur. Large lesions can cause mass effect (dyspnoea, urinary frequency).

Pedunculated subserosal leiomyoma with torsion
- Context: A pedunculated fibroid may twist on its stalk, compromising vascular supply and producing acute, often severe, focal abdominal pain, fever and necrosis.
- Imaging: If visible, a pedicle connecting the mass to the uterus clinches the diagnosis. Doppler may show absence or reduced flow in the pedicle or in the mass if torsion has led to infarction. MRI can show a twisted vascular pedicle (whirlpool) and areas of haemorrhagic infarction (T1 hyperintensity). In pregnancy, a pedicle may be displaced or obscured by the enlarged uterus and bowel.
- Clinical clues: Acute, focal severe pain, systemic inflammatory response or fever, and a previously mobile mass that becomes fixed favor torsion; surgical management is often necessary.

Decidualized endometrioma
- Context: Endometriomas (ovarian “chocolate” cysts) may undergo decidualization in pregnancy, producing vascularised mural nodules that can mimic malignancy.
- Imaging: Classic non‑pregnant endometrioma shows T1 hyperintensity and T2 shading (progressive signal loss on T2), reflecting blood products. In pregnancy, decidualized mural nodules are typically vascular on Doppler and may enhance on imaging (contrast not used in pregnancy), making distinction from neoplasm difficult. Identification of ovarian stroma adjacent to the cyst, and a plane separating the cyst from the uterus, supports ovarian origin.
- Clinical clues: History of known endometriosis, cyclical pain prior to pregnancy, and typical MRI signal characteristics favour endometrioma; however, decidualized nodules may require close follow‑up or surgical evaluation if suspicious.

Benign ovarian cysts (serous/mucinous)
- Imaging: Serous cystadenomas are usually unilocular, thin‑walled, anechoic on ultrasound. Mucinous cystadenomas may be multilocular with septations. An ovary seen separately from the cyst or visualisation of ovarian tissue adjacent to the lesion supports ovarian origin. Absence of solid papillary projections or suspicious mural nodules and lack of ascites favour benign cysts.
- Clinical clues: Typically asymptomatic unless large; torsion is a complication if pedunculated.

Paraovarian cyst and hydrosalpinx
- Paraovarian cyst: Grows in the mesosalpinx/mesovarium; ultrasound shows a simple cyst located adjacent to but separate from the ovary with a plane of cleavage. The ipsilateral ovary can generally be identified next to the cyst.
- Hydrosalpinx: Tubular, elongated cystic structure with incomplete septations and the “beads on a string” sign on ultrasound. Often bilateral or associated with pelvic inflammatory disease history.

Peritoneal inclusion cyst
- Imaging: Multiloculated fluid collection that molds around ovary and pelvic organs; the ovary is typically seen within or at the periphery of the cyst (“ovary within a cyst” sign). Often associated with prior abdominal surgery, endometriosis or pelvic inflammatory disease.
- Clinical clues: Longstanding pelvic pain and prior surgery/adhesions.

Ovarian torsion
- Imaging and clinical features: Enlarged, oedematous ovary with peripheral displaced follicles on ultrasound and absent or reduced Doppler flow. The whirlpool sign (twisted vascular pedicle) is diagnostic when seen. Torsion is a surgical emergency.

Neoplastic processes (primary ovarian cystadenomas, borderline tumors, cystic carcinomas)
- Imaging: Complex cystic masses with papillary projections, solid enhancing components, nodularity, thick irregular septations, or ascites raise concern for neoplasia. In pregnancy the absence of gadolinium complicates assessment of enhancement, but morphology and vascularity of mural nodules on Doppler help risk‑stratify.
- Clinical clues: Advanced maternal age, rapidly growing complex cyst, or markedly elevated tumor markers raise suspicion though markers are imperfect in pregnancy.

Imaging signs that help determine uterine versus adnexal origin
- Continuity with myometrium: Direct visualization of the mass arising from or indenting the myometrium is the most reliable sign of uterine origin.
- Beak/claw sign: Myometrium forming a beak or claw around the mass suggests uterine origin.
- Bridging vessel sign: Vessels seen on colour Doppler or MRI running between the uterus and the mass favour a fibroid or subserosal myoma with pedicle.
- Identification of ovarian stroma: Visualization of normal ovarian tissue adjacent to the lesion (follicles or stromal echotexture) supports adnexal origin.
- Absence of ipsilateral ovary: If the ipsilateral ovary is not identified separately, an adnexal origin is less likely—though gravid anatomy can obscure the ovary.

Interpretation of serum tumor markers in pregnancy
- CA‑125 and other tumor markers are of limited utility because pregnancy, endometriosis and benign pelvic pathology alter concentrations. CA‑125 typically rises in the first and third trimesters and may be mildly elevated in benign conditions. An isolated mild elevation (for example low‑forties as in the case) is non‑diagnostic; serial measurements showing rising trend can prompt closer surveillance, but imaging and clinical context carry more weight in decision‑making.

Putting it together in practice
- Use a stepwise algorithm: (1) Assess clinical stability and need for urgent surgery. (2) Perform targeted ultrasound (transvaginal when feasible) with Doppler looking for pedicle, bridging vessels, ovarian stroma, papillary projections and haemorrhagic signal. (3) If origin remains uncertain or the lesion is large/complex, obtain non‑contrast MRI to define relation to myometrium, detect haemorrhagic infarction and assess mural nodules. (4) Interpret tumor markers cautiously and in serial context. (5) Convene a multidisciplinary discussion (obstetrics, gynaecology, radiology, anaesthesia) to weigh conservative management against surgery—indications for operation include torsion, rupture, uncontrolled pain, infection/sepsis, significant mass effect or reasonable concern for neoplasm.
- Recognise limitations: The gravid uterus may displace, compress or hide pedicles and planes of cleavage; decidualized endometriomas can look malignant; and non‑contrast MRI cannot provide contrast enhancement patterns that sometimes help differentiate benign from malignant mural nodules.

Clinical application to the case
In the reported patient, ultrasound showed two large unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularity and the caudal cyst appearing adherent to a posterior myoma; MRI revealed thick walls and inhomogeneous T1/T2 contents but did not clearly demonstrate a myometrial stalk for the cranial lesion. The mild CA‑125 elevation supported but did not prove an endometriotic origin. Because acute worsening pain, dyspnoea from mass effect, evidence of infection and imaging uncertainty were present, surgical exploration was appropriate. Intraoperative inspection identified a pedunculated, twisted cystic leiomyoma for the upper lesion—explaining fever, vascular appearance on imaging and the operative necessity—and a separate large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma in the pelvis that was left in situ to preserve the pregnancy. This outcome highlights a few practical lessons: (1) large cystic leiomyomas can mimic adnexal cysts on imaging, particularly when gravid anatomy obscures bridging signs; (2) clinical deterioration should prompt surgical management even with imperfect preoperative characterization; and (3) decision making must balance maternal–fetal risks, weighing the risks of intervention against those of expectant management.

Summary
A methodical combination of clinical assessment, targeted ultrasound with Doppler, and non‑contrast MRI when needed will usually differentiate the common causes of large cystic abdominopelvic masses in pregnancy. Look specifically for continuity with the myometrium, bridging vessels, the beak/claw sign and the presence or absence of ovarian stroma. Interpret tumor markers cautiously and prioritise urgent surgery for torsion, rupture, infection or significant mass effect rather than pursuing exhaustive preoperative tissue diagnosis when the maternal–fetal balance mandates timely intervention.
