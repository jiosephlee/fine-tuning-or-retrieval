Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

When a pregnant patient in the second trimester presents with acute abdominal pain and dyspnea, the immediate priority is the same as in any acute presentation: assess and stabilize airway, breathing and circulation while simultaneously considering pregnancy-specific physiologic changes and fetal wellbeing. In this clinical context, however, the evaluation must also be directed at distinguishing obstetrical and gynecologic causes (torsion, degeneration, rupture, infection of an adnexal lesion or myoma, placental problems) from primary cardiopulmonary disease. A succinct but systematic approach reduces delay in diagnosis and allows early multidisciplinary planning.

Begin with a focused yet rapid physiologic assessment. Check pulse oximetry, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate and temperature; in pregnancy, maintain a high index of concern for hypoxaemia because maternal oxygenation directly affects fetal oxygen delivery (aim for an SpO2 that preserves adequate fetal oxygenation — many teams use a target ≥95% when possible). Document any chest symptoms, and obtain an ECG if there is chest pain or concern for cardiac pathology. Routine chest radiography (with abdominal shielding) or CT should not be withheld if clinically indicated to evaluate life‑threatening pulmonary processes (for example, suspected pulmonary embolus or pneumonia), but consultation with radiology and obstetrics is appropriate to weigh risks and optimize technique. In the absence of cardiopulmonary findings, dyspnea in a woman with very large abdominopelvic masses is commonly mechanical: diaphragmatic elevation, reduced functional residual capacity and limited chest wall excursion can produce subjective shortness of breath without primary lung or heart disease, as occurred in the case patient whose SpO2 and vitals were initially normal.

A careful abdominal and pelvic examination provides high-yield information and should be performed early. Note overall abdominal size and compare the measured or palpated fundal height with the expected gestational age; an abdomen that is “too large” for dates suggests an additional intra‑abdominal process such as a large cystic mass, ascites, multiple leiomyomas, or polyhydramnios. Palpate the abdomen for localized tenderness, masses, peritoneal signs (guarding, rigidity, rebound), and for signs of free fluid. Characterize any palpable lesion for location (quadrant, midline), size, contour, consistency, mobility and tenderness. Perform a bimanual pelvic examination when feasible and acceptable: a pelvic mass that is fixed in the pelvis and bulges into the posterior fornix (as in this case) suggests a pelvic origin or uterine-adherent lesion, whereas a mobile, high, upper-abdominal mass may be pedunculated from the uterine fundus or ovarian in origin. Documentation of the relationship of masses to the uterine fundus and cervix (fundal height, whether the mass moves separately from the uterus) helps guide the next diagnostic steps. Throughout the examination remain mindful of the comfort and positioning needs of a pregnant patient—left lateral tilt and semi‑upright positioning can reduce aortocaval compression and diaphragmatic compromise.

Initial laboratory testing should be broad and directed at the clinical possibilities: a complete blood count, blood group and antibody screen, ferritin and iron indices, basic metabolic panel, liver enzymes, coagulation studies if bleeding is suspected, C‑reactive protein, and urinalysis with culture. Interpret results in the context of pregnancy. Pregnancy commonly produces a mild physiologic leukocytosis and neutrophilia (white-cell counts are often higher than the non‑pregnant range, particularly in the second and third trimesters), and CRP can be modestly elevated; therefore small deviations from non‑pregnant reference ranges should not be overinterpreted as infection without corroborating clinical or microbiologic evidence. Nevertheless, rising leukocytes, a high CRP, fever, or a positive urine culture — as in this patient with a Klebsiella urinary tract infection — point to an infectious process that requires treatment. Early urine culture is essential since urinary infection is a frequent and treatable contributor to fever and systemic inflammation in pregnancy.

Assessment for anemia is essential. Many pregnant women have iron deficiency; ferritin <30 mcg/L and low hemoglobin support that diagnosis. Acute or subacute blood loss from a ruptured or degenerating mass, haemorrhagic degeneration of a leiomyoma, or chronic inflammatory anaemia can coexist. Clinical severity and gestational considerations determine management: symptomatic anemia or very low hemoglobin (institutional thresholds vary, but many teams use Hb ≤7–8 g/dL as a transfusion trigger in hemodynamically unstable or symptomatic patients) often prompts transfusion and iron repletion (intravenous iron is commonly used in pregnancy for moderate–severe iron deficiency). In this case the patient’s Hb fell postoperatively to 7.5 g/dL and required IV iron and transfusion, illustrating the need to anticipate and plan for possible blood loss when masses are large or surgery is undertaken.

Early, pregnancy‑safe symptomatic treatment eases discomfort and avoids precipitating iatrogenic problems. Paracetamol (acetaminophen) is first‑line for acute pain in pregnancy and was effective in the presented case. Opioids may be used for short‑term, severe pain under supervision; nonsteroidal anti‑inflammatory drugs should generally be avoided in late pregnancy because of risks to the fetus (ductus arteriosus constriction, oligohydramnios), although earlier use may be considered cautiously. Non-pharmacologic measures can be helpful: semi‑orthostatic or semi‑Fowler positioning and left lateral tilt reduce diaphragmatic elevation and aortocaval compression by the gravid uterus and large masses, often improving dyspnea and venous return. Supplemental oxygen should be given only when indicated by hypoxaemia.

Concurrent evaluation for the source and nature of the mass(es) should be rapid and multidisciplinary. Transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasonography with color Doppler is the first‑line imaging modality in pregnancy: it defines cystic versus solid features, vascularity, relationship to the uterus, and the presence of free fluid. If sonographic findings are inconclusive and further anatomic detail is required, non‑contrast MRI (preferably ≤1.5 T) provides valuable soft‑tissue characterization and can delineate the distance between lesions and the uterine cavity without ionizing radiation; gadolinium contrast should be avoided unless the potential benefit clearly outweighs the fetal risk. Consult radiology early to tailor imaging. In most centers, CT is reserved for scenarios in which MRI is not available and CT findings would materially alter acute management; fetal shielding and radiology consultation are essential.

Multidisciplinary coordination should begin at presentation. Obstetricians and gynecologists should direct maternal‑fetal assessment and manage obstetrical interventions. Radiology interprets imaging strategy and findings. Anesthesiology should be involved early when there is any potential for operative intervention so that airway and haemodynamic planning and blood‑product availability are anticipated. If malignancy is a concern, gynecologic oncology should be included. Neonatology should be aware of any possibility of preterm delivery so that steroid and tocolysis considerations, as well as neonatal preparedness, are addressed. In the presented case, such coordination led to timely imaging, operative planning, intraoperative fetal monitoring and appropriate postoperative obstetrical follow‑up.

Finally, triage decisions must balance maternal risks (hemodynamic instability, peritonitis, sepsis, ischemia from torsion or degeneration) and fetal risks (preterm birth, fetal compromise during maternal hypotension or anesthesia). Indications for urgent surgical exploration in pregnancy include haemodynamic instability, peritonitis, evidence of ovarian torsion or ruptured hemorrhagic mass, sepsis not controlled by medical therapy, or progressive respiratory compromise from space‑occupying intra‑abdominal lesions. When surgery is elected, steps to protect the pregnancy include continuous fetal monitoring when feasible, minimizing operative time and blood loss, and involvement of an experienced obstetric anesthesia team — measures that were followed in the reported patient.

In summary, the initial assessment of a second‑trimester pregnant woman with acute abdominal pain and dyspnea focuses first on stabilization, then on a targeted history and physical examination (noting discrepancy between abdominal size and gestational age and bimanual findings), appropriate laboratory and imaging investigations tuned for pregnancy, early detection and management of infection and anemia, symptom control with pregnancy‑safe measures, and early multidisciplinary coordination to guide imaging and determine the urgency and extent of intervention. The case illustrates these principles: careful clinical assessment identified large, symptomatic masses producing mechanical respiratory compromise and localized signs of infection, targeted imaging with ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI refined the differential, and a coordinated operative plan prioritized maternal stabilization while preserving the ongoing pregnancy.
