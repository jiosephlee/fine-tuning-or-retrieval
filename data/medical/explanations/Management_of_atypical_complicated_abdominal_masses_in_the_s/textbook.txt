# Atypical Abdominal Masses in Mid-Pregnancy: Distinguishing Degenerating Leiomyomas from Adnexal Tumors and Managing Complications

A nulliparous woman in her 40s presented at 21 weeks’ gestation with acute upper left quadrant abdominal pain and dyspnea. Ultrasound revealed two large, unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularity: a cranial lesion spanning the left subphrenic space to the uterine fundus and a caudal pelvic lesion abutting a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma. CA-125 was mildly elevated, and non-contrast MRI showed thick-walled cystic lesions with inhomogeneous fluid and focal T1/T2 hyperintensity. Laparotomy identified the cranial mass as a torsed, leaking pedunculated cystic leiomyoma, which was excised; the fixed caudal lesion was left in situ. The pregnancy progressed under close surveillance to an elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks due to an obstructing residual fibroid, with excellent maternal and neonatal outcomes.

This case illustrates the diagnostic ambiguity posed by atypical, largely cystic pelvic masses in pregnancy and highlights a pragmatic, pregnancy-preserving approach: careful clinical assessment, judicious use of ultrasound and non-contrast MRI, individualized operative decision-making for symptomatic or complicated masses, and delivery planning based on anatomic considerations.

## Clinical Approach to Abdominal Masses and Pain in Pregnancy

Acute abdominal pain and a palpable mass in pregnancy demand a structured, obstetric-centered evaluation. The initial priorities are maternal stabilization and rapid, nonionizing imaging to define the lesion(s) while monitoring fetal well-being. Pregnancy-specific physiology and anatomy complicate both presentation and interpretation:

- The enlarging uterus displaces adnexa and bowel, obscuring organ of origin and producing atypical pain localization.
- Pregnancy-associated vascular changes engorge pelvic tissues and tumors, predisposing to torsion, venous congestion, and degeneration within leiomyomas.
- Biomarkers such as CA-125 have altered baselines, limiting their specificity.

In this patient, acute pain, dyspnea, and a firm pelvic mass on bimanual examination raised concern for a large, space-occupying process with potential for torsion, rupture, or degeneration. Dyspnea warranted cardiopulmonary assessment to exclude thromboembolism or cardiac disease; normal oxygenation, examination, and ECG made restrictive mechanics from mass effect the leading explanation.

The differential diagnosis for large cystic pelvic/abdominal masses in the second trimester includes:

- Ovarian and paraovarian cysts: endometriomas (which can decidualize in pregnancy), cystadenomas, dermoids (typically with fat/fluid levels), functional cysts (usually first trimester), and torsion of an adnexal mass.
- Uterine leiomyomas with degeneration: subserosal pedunculated fibroids can mimic adnexal masses, and cystic or red (carneous) degeneration can impart complex echotexture.
- Peritoneal inclusion cysts, mesenteric or omental cysts, and less commonly splenic or retroperitoneal cystic lesions.
- Malignancy is uncommon but must remain on the differential when imaging suggests solid components, papillary projections, nodularity, ascites, or suspicious nodes.

Findings that narrow the diagnosis include the relationship to the uterus on imaging and examination (“claw sign” of myometrium wrapping a mass, or “bridging vessels” from the uterus to the tumor), ovarian visualization separate from the mass, and Doppler flow patterns. In this case, the pelvic mass appeared continuous with a posterior myoma, suggesting uterine origin, whereas the cranial mass lacked an obvious pedicle on imaging due to uterine displacement and sheer size.

## Pathophysiology: Leiomyomas in Pregnancy and Cystic Change

Uterine leiomyomas are monoclonal smooth muscle tumors arising from myometrial cells, frequently harboring mutations (e.g., MED12, HMGA2) that drive proliferation and extracellular matrix accumulation. Growth is hormonally modulated: estrogens and progesterone (and their receptors) promote cellular hypertrophy and matrix deposition, and pregnancy augments tumor vascularity via uterine artery remodeling.

Leiomyoma behavior in pregnancy is nonlinear. Many enlarge in the first and early second trimester and then stabilize or regress later. Several forms of degeneration occur when growth outpaces blood supply:

- Hyaline degeneration (most common) produces homogeneous echogenic change and T2 hypointensity.
- Cystic degeneration results from liquefactive transformation of hyaline degeneration, creating unilocular or multilocular cystic cavities with thin or thick walls; on MRI these are T2 hyperintense with variable T1 signal depending on proteinaceous content.
- Red (carneous) degeneration is hemorrhagic infarction due to venous thrombosis within the fibroid or along a pedicle; classically presents with acute pain and low-grade fever, and MRI often shows a peripheral low T2 rim and T1 hyperintensity from methemoglobin.
- Torsion of a pedunculated subserosal fibroid compromises arterial inflow and venous outflow, leading to ischemia, leakage, inflammation, and pain. Vascular engorgement in pregnancy increases torsion risk and intraoperative bleeding potential.

Large pedunculated and subserosal fibroids can ascend into the upper abdomen, compress the diaphragm, and contribute to restrictive respiratory symptoms. In the presented case, a torsed, leaking pedunculated fibroid with cystic degeneration accounted for the fever, pain, and dyspnea, while a second intramural–subserosal cystic fibroid occupied the cul-de-sac and would later obstruct labor.

## Diagnostic Workup in Pregnancy: Tools, Signals, and Pitfalls

The goals of evaluation are to localize the mass, characterize its nature, assess complications, and estimate malignancy risk while protecting the pregnancy.

- Clinical examination: Abdominal palpation and bimanual examination can suggest a fixed pelvic mass and its relation to the uterus. In pregnancy, however, mass mobility and organ of origin are often ambiguous.

- Ultrasound with color Doppler: First-line and safe in all trimesters. Fibroids typically appear as solid, hypoechoic or heterogeneous masses with peripheral vascularity (pseudocapsule). Cystic degeneration yields anechoic or complex cystic areas with septations and variable echo densities. Endometriomas often have homogeneous, “ground-glass” internal echoes and peripheral vascularity can be seen when decidualized. Transvaginal views help define pelvic relationships. In this case, both lesions were largely unilocular cystic with peripheral flow, mimicking decidualized endometriomas.

- MRI without gadolinium: When ultrasound is indeterminate—especially with multiple, large, posterior, or intramural lesions—MRI refines tissue characterization and origin. Non-contrast MRI is considered safe in pregnancy; gadolinium should be avoided unless the diagnostic benefit is compelling, as it crosses the placenta and has been associated with rare but concerning neonatal outcomes. Imaging clues to uterine origin include the “claw sign” (myometrial tissue partially engulfing the mass), signal similar to myometrium on non-degenerated portions, and “bridging vessels” (best appreciated with contrast or Doppler; sometimes implied by flow voids). Degenerated fibroids display mixed T1/T2 signals depending on hemorrhage and fluid complexity. In the presented patient, thick walls and inhomogeneous T1/T2 hyperintense contents were consistent with cystic/hemorrhagic degeneration; contiguity with a posterior intramural–subserosal fibroid supported a myomatous origin for the caudal mass.

- Laboratory testing: CBC may reveal pregnancy-associated hemodilution or iron deficiency; inflammation from degeneration can mildly elevate CRP and leukocytes. Tumor markers are of limited value in pregnancy. CA-125 can be modestly elevated in the first and third trimesters and in many benign gynecologic conditions (endometriosis, adenomyosis, leiomyomas), limiting specificity. Serial trends can aid follow-up, but risk-of-malignancy algorithms and ovarian malignancy indices are not validated in pregnancy. In this case, a second-trimester CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL mildly exceeded typical medians yet remained within a range compatible with benign conditions.

- Fetal assessment: Confirm fetal cardiac activity and viability at presentation. Continuous intraoperative monitoring is generally not feasible before viability; pre- and post-procedure fetal heart rate documentation is recommended, and intermittent transvaginal checks can be used intraoperatively if logistics permit.

CT is rarely necessary and is generally avoided given ionizing radiation; if essential for maternal indications, tailored protocols can minimize fetal dose.

## Deciding When and How to Operate

Most fibroids identified during pregnancy are managed expectantly. Indications for surgery are restricted to specific scenarios where maternal or fetal risk is high or malignancy is suspected:

- Acute complications: torsion of a pedunculated fibroid, rupture, or hemoperitoneum.
- Intractable pain from red degeneration or rapid enlargement unresponsive to conservative therapy (rest, analgesia, hydration).
- Obstructive symptoms (bowel, ureter, or severe diaphragmatic compression) or infection within a degenerating mass.
- Concerning features for malignancy (rare), recognizing the poor positive predictive value of “rapid growth” alone.

Timing: If surgery is necessary, the second trimester (generally 16–24 weeks) minimizes the risks of miscarriage compared with the first trimester and preterm labor compared with the third. This patient underwent surgery at 21+6 weeks due to worsening pain and restrictive symptoms, concern for torsion/rupture, and the anticipated progression of uterine size exacerbating mass effect.

Approach: Laparoscopy can be considered in early to mid–second trimester for appropriately selected cases (smaller, accessible, non-intramural lesions) using pregnancy-adapted techniques (open/Hasson entry, left uterine displacement, lower insufflation pressures, capnography). Laparotomy is often chosen for very large, multiple, intramural, or posterior lesions, where exposure, hemostasis, and expediency are critical. Intraoperative principles include:

- Maternal positioning with left lateral tilt to reduce aortocaval compression.
- Gentle uterine handling, avoidance of vasopressors that might compromise uteroplacental perfusion, and maintenance of maternal oxygenation and normocapnia.
- Meticulous hemostasis; consider uterotonic-sparing techniques and avoidance of vasoconstrictors directly into the myometrium during pregnancy.
- Pre- and post-procedure documentation of fetal heart rate; intraoperative intermittent checks can be considered where feasible. Continuous monitoring becomes practical only near or after viability.

Tocolysis is not routinely indicated prophylactically. If uterine irritability occurs postoperatively, short courses of nifedipine or indomethacin (before 32 weeks) may be used judiciously. Routine prophylactic progesterone to prevent contractions lacks robust evidence in this setting; practice patterns vary. Standard antibiotic prophylaxis is administered per surgical protocol, with therapeutic antibiotics tailored to intercurrent infections, as done for the patient’s Klebsiella urinary tract infection.

Surgical decision-making should aim for the narrowest effective intervention. In the presented case, removal of the torsed pedunculated fibroid resolved the acute problem while leaving a fixed intramural–subserosal cystic fibroid in situ to minimize obstetric risk.

## Obstetric Sequelae of Fibroids and Delivery Planning

Leiomyomas are common in pregnancy (approximately 0.3–10%), and adnexal masses are identified in 1–6%. Most are asymptomatic. When large or strategically located, fibroids confer increased risks of:

- Miscarriage and early bleeding (particularly with submucosal lesions)
- Pain crises from degeneration (classically in the second trimester)
- Malpresentation, growth restriction (inconsistently), preterm labor, and placental abruption
- Obstructed labor, dysfunctional labor, and higher cesarean rates
- Postpartum hemorrhage and subinvolution

Delivery planning is individualized. Vaginal delivery is possible for most patients with fibroids unless a mass obstructs the cervix or lower uterine segment, there is significant distortion of the cavity, or there is a prior myomectomy with a high risk of rupture (e.g., full-thickness fundal incisions entering the endometrial cavity). Pedunculated subserosal myomectomy without cavity entry carries a low rupture risk; route of delivery is more often dictated by residual obstructing fibroids. In this case, the persistent large posterior pelvic fibroid posed a mechanical barrier to descent, and an elective cesarean at 37 weeks optimized maternal and neonatal outcomes while minimizing risks of labor and scar dehiscence.

Intraoperative myomectomy at cesarean can be considered for pedunculated subserosal lesions with favorable anatomy, acknowledging increased operative time and potential blood loss. Intramural myomectomy at cesarean is more controversial and generally avoided unless necessary for delivery or closure.

## Postoperative and Antepartum Care

After non-obstetric surgery in pregnancy, monitor for preterm labor, hemorrhage, and infection. Pain control should balance efficacy with fetal safety; acetaminophen is first line, and NSAIDs are used with caution and generally avoided after 32 weeks due to ductus arteriosus constriction risk and oligohydramnios. Early ambulation and venous thromboembolism prophylaxis are important given pregnancy-associated hypercoagulability.

Anemia is common and may be exacerbated by perioperative blood loss or chronic inflammation. Iron deficiency should be corrected with oral or intravenous iron; intravenous preparations such as ferric carboxymaltose are effective and safe in the second and third trimesters. Transfusion decisions are individualized; thresholds typically consider symptoms and hemodynamics, with many obstetric protocols transfusing at hemoglobin levels around 7 g/dL or higher if symptomatic or unstable.

Most fibroids regress postpartum as hormonal support wanes; meaningful size reductions often occur over the third trimester and early puerperium. As in this patient, deferred interval myomectomy is often unnecessary if symptoms abate and significant involution occurs. Uterine artery embolization is contraindicated during pregnancy but can be considered postpartum in suitable candidates who have completed childbearing.

## Imaging Pearls: Distinguishing Degenerating Fibroid from Ovarian Mass

- Identify the ovaries apart from the mass, if possible.
- Look for the “claw sign”: myometrium partially enveloping the mass suggests uterine origin.
- Seek “bridging vessels”: flow from the uterine artery to the mass on Doppler; contrast MRI enhances this sign but is typically avoided in pregnancy.
- Degenerating fibroids may be largely cystic with thick walls and peripheral vascularity; red degeneration imparts T1 hyperintensity and a peripheral low T2 rim.
- Decidualized endometriomas in pregnancy can show intracystic papillary projections with marked vascularity, mimicking malignancy; correlation with bilaterality, “ground-glass” echoes, and clinical context helps.
- Risk-of-malignancy scoring systems and most serum biomarkers are not validated in pregnancy; interpret CA-125 with caution.

## Putting It Together in the Presented Case

The patient’s two large cystic masses with peripheral vascularity, one appearing contiguous with a posterior myoma, and MRI features of thick-walled, inhomogeneous cystic contents localized the process to degenerating leiomyomas despite the initial impression of adnexal cysts. Worsening pain, dyspnea from diaphragmatic compression, and concern for torsion/rupture were accepted indications for mid-trimester surgery. Laparotomy revealed a torsed, leaking pedunculated fibroid, which was excised with careful hemostasis, fetal heart checks, and standard antibiotic prophylaxis; the fixed intramural–subserosal pelvic fibroid was left in place. Postoperative management addressed anemia with transfusion and intravenous iron and treated a concurrent urinary tract infection. The remainder of the pregnancy proceeded uneventfully under surveillance, with an elective cesarean at 37 weeks to circumvent mechanical obstruction. The benign pathology with ischemic necrosis confirmed the clinical diagnosis, and postpartum regression of the residual fibroid supported expectant future management.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points

- In pregnancy, large subserosal and pedunculated leiomyomas can mimic adnexal masses; cystic and red degeneration produce complex imaging appearances that challenge standard pattern recognition.
- Ultrasound with color Doppler is first-line; non-contrast MRI is a valuable adjunct for large, multiple, posterior, or indeterminate lesions. Gadolinium is generally avoided in pregnancy.
- CA-125 and ovarian malignancy risk indices are unreliable in pregnancy; modest elevations of CA-125 are common and nonspecific.
- Operative intervention for fibroids during pregnancy is exceptional and reserved for clear indications: torsion, rupture/hemoperitoneum, intractable pain from degeneration, obstructive symptoms, or strong oncologic concern. When needed, the second trimester is the safest window.
- Choose the least extensive effective surgery. Pedunculated subserosal myomectomy can be performed safely in selected patients; intramural myomectomy carries higher risks of bleeding and pregnancy loss and is generally deferred.
- Pre- and post-procedure fetal heart rate documentation is recommended; continuous intraoperative monitoring is usually impractical before viability.
- Routine prophylactic tocolysis or progesterone surrounding non-obstetric surgery is not evidence-based; treat uterine irritability if it arises.
- Plan delivery around the anatomic realities: obstruction of the lower uterine segment or cervix by a fibroid, significant distortion of the cavity, or specific myomectomy histories may necessitate cesarean birth.
- Most fibroids involute postpartum; defer definitive treatment if symptoms abate and avoid overtreatment during pregnancy.
- Always anchor decisions in multidisciplinary discussions that balance maternal symptom control, obstetric safety, and the rarity of malignancy in this context.
