Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy

## Case Presentation and Initial Obstetric Assessment

The patient was a nulliparous woman in her 40s with obesity (BMI 33) who presented at 21 weeks’ gestation. Routine first‑ and second‑trimester screening, including non‑invasive prenatal testing, had been reassuring with normal fetal morphology. Her advanced maternal age and obesity are relevant contextual factors because they influence baseline obstetric risk and can make physical examination and imaging more challenging.

She complained of acute, localized pain in two regions: the left upper quadrant of the abdomen and the pelvis, with a reported Visual Analogue Scale score of 5 in both locations, and associated mild dyspnoea. Acute focal abdominal pain in mid‑pregnancy warrants prompt assessment because it can reflect obstetric complications (e.g., degenerated myoma, placental abruption), non‑obstetric intra‑abdominal pathology (e.g., adnexal torsion, cyst rupture), or mechanical effects of large pelvic masses. The concomitant dyspnoea raised concern for cardiopulmonary causes but was also plausibly related to mechanical diaphragmatic compression from the gravid uterus and large abdominal masses.

Initial bedside cardiopulmonary assessment was reassuring: oxygen saturation was normal (≈97%), heart rate and blood pressure were within expected limits (median HR 74 bpm, BP 100/60 mmHg), and the ECG showed no acute abnormalities. Normal vital signs and ECG argue against acute cardiopulmonary collapse or major arrhythmia as the cause of dyspnoea, making a mechanical cause more likely. Nonetheless, normal resting observations do not exclude clinically significant mass effect, and serial monitoring is indicated.

Abdominal and pelvic examination provided key localizing information. The maternal abdomen appeared larger than expected for gestational age, and palpation revealed a taut‑elastic, tender mass in the left upper quadrant measuring approximately 18 cm. In addition, bimanual vaginal examination identified a fixed, rounded, hard mass of about 9 cm occupying the lesser pelvis and bulging the posterior fornix. These findings localize one lesion to the upper abdomen/subphrenic region and a second to the deep pelvis adjacent to the posterior uterine wall. A fixed pelvic mass palpable on bimanual exam that bulges the posterior fornix favors a uterine or adnexal origin in the pelvis (for example, a posterior myoma or an adnexal cyst), and the combination of two large masses raises the possibility of multiple uterine fibroids, adnexal lesions, or one uterine lesion plus an adnexal pathology. Obesity can limit the sensitivity of abdominal palpation, so the discrepancy between uterine size by dates and examination heightens suspicion for pathology rather than simple uterine enlargement.

Initial management prioritized symptom control and maternal–fetal stability. Analgesia with intravenous paracetamol (1 g twice daily) was administered; paracetamol is the first‑line analgesic in pregnancy for moderate pain because of its favorable safety profile. The patient was positioned semi‑orthostatically (upright at an angle) to reduce diaphragmatic compression by the gravid uterus and abdominal masses, a simple noninvasive measure that can relieve dyspnoea and discomfort without fetal risk. These measures reduced pain from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale and improved symptoms, supporting the hypothesis that at least some symptoms were due to mechanical factors rather than ongoing ischemic or septic shock.

During the hospital stay the patient developed a low‑grade fever. Given the pandemic context and the obstetric setting, SARS‑CoV‑2 testing was appropriately performed and was negative. Urine culture identified Klebsiella pneumoniae, providing an explanation for the fever and a potentially treatable source of infection; urinary tract infections are common in pregnancy and can present atypically. Laboratory results showed iron deficiency anemia (hemoglobin 9.9 g/dL, ferritin 25 μg/L), a mildly elevated C‑reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL), and a neutrophilic leukocytosis (WBC 11.29 × 10^3/μL, neutrophils 9.27 × 10^3/μL). Interpretation of inflammatory markers in pregnancy requires caution because pregnancy itself can cause modest leukocytosis and CRP changes; nevertheless, the combination of fever, leukocytosis and a positive urine culture supports a concurrent urinary infection contributing to the clinical picture and justifies targeted antimicrobial therapy.

Putting these findings together, the clinical problem that required escalation was a mid‑trimester pregnancy complicated by two large abdominal–pelvic masses producing pain and restrictive symptoms and of uncertain origin. The priorities at this stage were to stabilize the patient (analgesia, positioning, treat infection if present), confirm fetal well‑being, and proceed rapidly to focused imaging (ultrasound and, if needed, non‑contrast MRI) and multidisciplinary planning to define the nature of the masses and their risks (torsion, rupture, infection, progressive mass effect) in order to determine whether conservative management or timely surgical intervention would be safer for mother and fetus.

## Pregnancy-Safe Diagnostic Workup: Imaging and Laboratory Interpretation

When a pregnant patient presents with large, atypical abdominal masses, the diagnostic workup must balance diagnostic accuracy with fetal safety. In this case the team used a stepwise approach beginning with targeted ultrasound with colour Doppler, followed by non‑contrast MRI after the first trimester, and selective laboratory tests for tumour markers. Each modality contributed specific information and had known limitations that influenced the decision to proceed to surgery.

Ultrasound is the first-line imaging study in pregnancy because it is widely available, safe, and excellent for characterizing cystic versus solid adnexal or uterine lesions. In this patient, transabdominal and bimanual sonography identified two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularisation. The upper lesion measured 18 × 12 × 7 cm in the left subphrenic region and the lower cyst measured about 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm and appeared adherent to a posterior intramural–subserosal leiomyoma with cystic degeneration (58 × 71 mm). Colour Doppler added useful information by demonstrating peripheral/hypertrophied vessels, which can indicate a vascularised pedicle (as with pedunculated myomas), decidualized endometriomas, or neoplastic neovascularity. In this case the peripheral vascularity raised concern that at least one lesion was highly vascular and therefore at risk for torsion, bleeding or degeneration.

Laboratory tumour markers were used as adjuncts rather than diagnostic tests. CA 19‑9, CA 15‑3, CEA, β2‑microglobulin and LDH were within normal limits; CA‑125 was mildly elevated at 41.8 U/mL and had risen from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier. Interpretation of CA‑125 in pregnancy requires caution: physiologic elevations commonly occur in the first and third trimesters, and mild increases in the second trimester are not specific for malignancy. A single mildly raised CA‑125 in isolation cannot establish an ovarian cancer diagnosis, but an upward trend in the setting of atypical imaging and symptoms increases concern and informs the risk–benefit discussion. The normal values of the other tumour markers in this case lowered the likelihood of some epithelial malignancies but did not exclude pathology, so tumour markers were used to refine risk rather than to make a definitive diagnosis.

Non‑contrast MRI after the first trimester was the next step to improve tissue characterisation and to define relationships to the gravid uterus. In this patient MRI (performed without gadolinium and on a machine not exceeding 1.5 T, as recommended after the first trimester) showed thick‑walled cysts (wall ≈1.4 cm) with inhomogeneous fluid signal on T1 and T2 fat‑sat sequences and focal hyperintense solid components on both T1 and T2. Importantly, no pathological adenopathy was identified. These MRI features are nonspecific: thick walls and heterogenous T1 signal may reflect haemorrhagic or proteinaceous content (which occurs with endometriomas and with degenerating leiomyomas), while focal solid signal can represent decidualized tissue in an endometrioma or viable myometrial tissue in a cystic degenerating fibroid. The lack of nodal enlargement reduced but did not eliminate the suspicion of malignancy. MRI also suggested the caudal mass had an unclear cleavage plane and appeared continuous with the posterior myoma, favouring a myomatous origin for that lesion.

The choice to avoid gadolinium and CT reflects safety principles. Ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI provide the needed diagnostic information in most cases; gadolinium crosses the placenta and has been associated with fetal exposure and a small but real potential for adverse outcomes, so it is reserved for situations where the benefit clearly outweighs the risk. CT involves ionizing radiation and is likewise avoided unless a life‑threatening maternal indication requires it. In this case the imaging available without contrast was sufficient to guide management decisions while minimising fetal risk.

Imaging findings also helped explain the patient’s dyspnoea. Normal oxygen saturation, ECG and cardiac exam argued against intrinsic cardiopulmonary disease; the large upper mass occupying the left subphrenic space, together with the gravid uterus, supported a mechanical restrictive physiology—elevation of the diaphragm, reduced functional residual capacity and impaired diaphragmatic excursion—rather than primary pulmonary pathology. This correlation between anatomy on imaging and clinical exam supported conservative respiratory management and informed timing of intervention.

Finally, it is important to recognise the limitations of preoperative imaging in pregnancy, illustrated by this case. The “steric bulk” of the gravid uterus and very large adjacent masses can obscure small connecting structures such as pedicles or feeding vessels on both ultrasound and MRI; gravid‑related decidual changes may increase vascularity and create solid‑appearing areas within cysts that mimic neoplasm; and non‑contrast MRI has lower specificity for vascular invasion or subtle tissue planes than contrast studies. These limitations explain why a lesion that appeared adnexal preoperatively proved to be a pedunculated, highly vascularised uterine leiomyoma at surgery. Given these constraints, imaging must be integrated with serial clinical assessment (symptom progression, laboratory trends) and multidisciplinary risk assessment to decide whether conservative management or timely surgical intervention is indicated.

## Focused Differential Diagnosis and Pathophysiology

Large cystic-appearing abdominal masses in pregnancy present a diagnostic challenge because pregnancy-related tissue changes blur some of the usual imaging distinctions. In this case two unilocular, well‑circumscribed cystic lesions with peripheral vascularity were identified by ultrasound, and non‑contrast MRI showed inhomogeneous fluid with focal solid T1/T2‑hyperintense components. These imaging findings can fit either decidualised endometriomas or cystic/degenerating leiomyomas; distinguishing between them relies on a combination of morphologic clues, vascular patterns, demonstration of continuity with the uterus, and the clinical context.

Decidualised endometrioma versus cystic/degenerating leiomyoma
- Endometrioma classically contains blood products. On MRI a true endometrioma is typically hyperintense on T1‑weighted sequences because of subacute blood and often shows the “shading” sign (low signal) on T2 due to concentrated blood products. During pregnancy, endometriomas may undergo decidualisation: the cyst wall or internal surface develops vascularised papillary or solid‑appearing nodules (decidualised nodules) that can be echogenic on ultrasound and show internal Doppler flow. These mural nodules may mimic malignant mural vegetations because they are vascular and solid on imaging; contrast enhancement would help differentiate but gadolinium is generally avoided in pregnancy.
- Degenerating leiomyomas demonstrate different imaging patterns. Cystic degeneration results from progressive hyaline change and liquefaction within a fibroid, producing well‑circumscribed cystic areas that are typically of simple fluid signal unless complicated by hemorrhage. Red (hemorrhagic) degeneration—more common in pregnancy—produces heterogeneous T1 and T2 signal because of blood products and can give the lesion a complex cystic appearance. A crucial distinguishing feature is continuity with the myometrium: leiomyomas usually show a broad-based relationship to the uterine wall, a pseudocapsule, or an identifiable stalk for pedunculated subserosal tumours. On ultrasound, leiomyomas often have peripheral or rim vascularity whereas decidualised nodules within an endometrioma may show internal neovascularisation.

How these apply to the present case
- The caudal lesion was seen on both ultrasound and MRI to be continuous with a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma (cystic degeneration), which favors a myomatous origin. The upper mass lacked a demonstrable stalk on imaging because the gravid uterus and the lesion’s size distorted anatomy, so it mimicked an adnexal cyst or endometrioma. The MRI description of inhomogeneous fluid and focal T1/T2 hyperintense solid components is compatible with haemorrhagic/cystic degeneration of a fibroid and is also the pattern that can be seen with blood‑filled endometriomas or decidualised mural nodules—hence the diagnostic uncertainty.

Pedunculated subserosal leiomyoma: torsion, ischemia and fever
Pedunculated subserosal fibroids are at particular risk of torsion because their blood supply runs through a stalk that can twist. Torsion causes acute venous outflow obstruction, ischemia and eventually infarction; clinically this produces sudden, localized severe pain, local peritoneal irritation, systemic inflammatory signs and sometimes fever. Intraoperative findings in this patient—a twisted, highly vascular pedunculated lesion with areas of leakage and ischemic necrosis on pathology—explain the acute left‑upper quadrant pain and the peri‑operative fever. Red degeneration (hemorrhagic infarction) during pregnancy can have a similar presentation but is usually intramural; torsion is a classic explanation for an otherwise well‑circumscribed pedunculated mass becoming acutely tender and inflamed.

Cystic degeneration of leiomyomas: mechanism and imaging
Cystic degeneration represents liquefaction of previously hyalinised fibroid tissue. It tends to produce unilocular or multilocular fluid‑filled spaces within a myoma, with thin to thick walls depending on the degree of necrosis and hemorrhage. On ultrasound such areas appear cystic and softer to palpation; on MRI they are variable on T1 (depending on blood content) and usually hyperintense on T2 unless chronic blood products are present. Thickened walls, heterogeneous internal signal and peripheral vascularity can indicate recent hemorrhage or infarction within the fibroid rather than a simple benign ovarian cyst. In this patient the MRI thick walls and heterogeneous signals were consistent with degenerative change and, retrospectively, with ischemic damage from a twisted pedicle.

Interpreting CA‑125 in pregnancy
CA‑125 is nonspecific and is affected by pregnancy. Physiologic rises are common in the first trimester and near term; values that would be suspicious outside pregnancy may be only mildly elevated in a pregnant woman. CA‑125 is also raised in benign conditions such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammation and sometimes large fibroids. In this case a CA‑125 of 41.8 U/mL (slightly above the non‑pregnant cutoff of 35) and a modest upward trend increase concern but lack specificity; the absence of other worrisome features (markedly complex multilocularity, ascites, adenopathy, markedly elevated other tumour markers) lowers the likelihood of ovarian malignancy but does not entirely exclude it. Serial measurements and correlation with imaging and clinical course are therefore more informative than a single value.

Leiomyoma size dynamics in pregnancy and puerperium
Fibroid growth during pregnancy is not linear. Many fibroids enlarge early in gestation—likely hormonally mediated—then tend to stabilise during the second and third trimesters; a substantial proportion regress in the postpartum period. This predictable tendency explains why conservative management is usually chosen when the lesion is asymptomatic: many fibroids will not continue to expand and may shrink after delivery. In the present case, however, the combination of acute symptoms and features suggesting ischemia/complication made surgical intervention reasonable despite the general tendency toward expectant management.

Symptom generation: dyspnoea and pelvic outlet obstruction
Large upper abdominal masses add to the mechanical effect of the gravid uterus: they can elevate the diaphragm, reduce lung volumes, and produce a restrictive pattern of dyspnoea and orthopnoea that is mechanical rather than cardiopulmonary. In this patient the upper mass’s size and position plausibly explained the mild dyspnoea without hypoxaemia or cardiac dysfunction. Conversely, a large posterior lower uterine myoma occupying the pouch of Douglas or the pelvic outlet can mechanically obstruct the fetal head during labor; a fixed, bulky posterior myoma in the isthmic area is a classic indication to plan cesarean delivery if it is expected to block descent, which is what guided delivery planning here.

Oncologic risk estimation in pregnancy
Certain imaging and clinical features lower the suspicion of malignancy: unilocular configuration, regular smooth margins, absence of ascites, normal lymph nodes on imaging, and normal/near‑normal additional tumour markers. Pregnancy itself complicates interpretation because decidualisation can mimic malignant mural nodules. The decision pathway in this case appropriately combined the limited increase in CA‑125, unilocular morphology, lack of adenopathy or ascites, and the acute clinical picture suggesting torsion/degeneration to favour a benign myomatous process, while still pursuing surgical exploration because of symptoms, risk of further complications, and the need for definitive diagnosis.

Takeaway linked to the case
In summary, overlapping imaging features of hemorrhagic endometrioma, decidualised mural nodules, and degenerating fibroid create real diagnostic uncertainty in pregnancy. Demonstration of continuity with the uterus, presence of a stalk, vascular pattern, and the clinical scenario—acute pain, fever, and signs of ischemia—are the most useful discriminators. In this patient the combination of imaging ambiguity and acute inflammatory signs ultimately pointed to a complicated pedunculated leiomyoma (torsion/degeneration), which was confirmed at surgery and by pathology.

## Maternal–Fetal Management Strategy: Indications, Operative Choices, and Peripartum Planning

This case illustrates how a stepwise, pregnancy‑preserving strategy balances maternal symptoms, obstetric risks and diagnostic uncertainty when large abdominal masses appear during the second trimester. The decision to operate, how to operate, and how to plan follow-up and delivery were driven by a few concrete clinical imperatives: escalating maternal symptoms with respiratory compromise, a real risk of acute mass complications (torsion, leak/rupture, infection), uncertainty about the lesions’ nature, and the expectation that uterine and mass growth would progressively reduce safe options.

Indications for surgery
Surgery in pregnancy is not routine and is reserved for compelling maternal or fetal reasons. In this patient the indications were cumulative: severe, progressive abdominal pain and dyspnoea from mass effect; imaging showing large, vascular, mixed cystic–solid lesions with an unclear origin; a clinical picture suggestive of complication (the cranial mass was twisted and leaking and the patient had fever and bacteriuria); and the foreseeable mechanical problem of further uterine enlargement worsening maternal respiratory function and increasing risk of necrosis, rupture or torsion. These indications follow the standard principle that operative intervention during pregnancy is justified when conservative care fails, when there is a significant risk of acute complication, or when diagnostic uncertainty cannot be safely resolved without tissue diagnosis.

Timing: second‑trimester rationale
When surgery is indicated in pregnancy, the second trimester (generally 14–28 weeks) is preferred because organogenesis is complete, the uterus is still of a size that permits reasonable surgical access, and the risks of perioperative miscarriage and preterm labour are lower than in the first and third trimesters respectively. In this report the operation was performed at 21+6 weeks to limit the interval during which the maternal respiratory compromise and mass‑related complications could worsen, while still operating in a window that minimizes teratogenic risk and optimizes surgical exposure.

Approach selection: laparotomy versus laparoscopy
Choice of surgical approach must reflect lesion size, number, location, gestational age and the need for rapid hemorrhage control. Laparoscopy has advantages in early pregnancy for smaller, accessible lesions, but becomes impractical and potentially unsafe when masses are very large, multiple, posteriorly located or intramural—situations in which visualization, instrument manipulation and vascular control are limited and conversion risk is high. In this patient the cranial mass measured 18 cm, the caudal mass was adherent to a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma, and the uterus was gravid at 21 weeks. Those factors favored a midline longitudinal (xiphoid–pubic) laparotomy to allow wide exposure, direct vascular control and the capacity to manage unanticipated hemorrhage while minimizing operative time and fetal stress.

Key intraoperative steps and tactics to preserve pregnancy
Operative tactics in pregnant patients aim to (1) secure maternal safety, (2) minimize uterine manipulation and duration of surgery, and (3) protect the fetus. The team followed several recommended steps:

- Pre‑ and intraoperative fetal assessment: documentation of fetal cardiac activity before incision and continued intermittent checks during and after the procedure (as emphasized in obstetric surgery guidance) to confirm ongoing fetal viability.
- Diagnostic peritoneal sampling: collection of free peritoneal fluid for culture and cytology provided information to exclude peritoneal infection or malignancy without increasing uterine risk.
- Prompt identification and controlled excision of the problematic lesion: intraoperative inspection revealed the cranial lesion to be a highly vascular, pedunculated cystic leiomyoma that was twisted and leaking. The surgeons applied a double clamp on the pedicle, excised the mass with rapid hemostasis and closed the uterine stump with absorbable suture. This technique minimizes blood loss and uterine trauma and is appropriate for pedunculated myomas when myometrial disruption can be limited.
- Minimize operative time and uterine handling: the team limited dissection to what was necessary, leaving adherent tissue undisturbed when appropriate.

Conservative management of the caudal mass
The pelvic (caudal) leiomyoma was fixed, adherent to the uterine torus and consistent with an intramural–subserosal lesion. Attempting removal would have prolonged surgery, increased the risk of hemorrhage, and jeopardized the pregnancy. Given these risks, the team deliberately left the caudal mass in situ. This conservative choice reflects a fundamental principle in obstetric surgery: remove lesions only when benefits outweigh added operative/obstetric risk. Leaving a large myoma intact is appropriate when it is not actively compromised and when resection would likely cause more harm than benefit.

Perioperative uterine management: progesterone prophylaxis
Perioperative uterotonic/tocolytic strategies were used to reduce uterine irritability and the theoretical risk of preterm labour following abdominal surgery. In this case the team administered intramuscular progesterone starting intraoperatively and continuing until delivery. While routine use of progesterone for every obstetric surgery is not universally mandated, progesterone is commonly used in practice to stabilize the uterus in women at increased risk for preterm labour; it was used here as a pregnancy‑preservation measure given the intraoperative manipulation and gestational age. The goal was to reduce uterine contractility and the risk of pregnancy loss or preterm birth while the pregnancy matured.

Infection control: prophylaxis and targeted therapy
Standard perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis was given (cefazolin intravenously), which is widely accepted in pregnancy for surgical prophylaxis due to good safety and efficacy. The patient also had Klebsiella pneumoniae bacteriuria; postoperative antibiotic therapy was tailored to culture sensitivities and included cefazolin and clarithromycin as reported. Choosing antibiotics during pregnancy requires balancing efficacy against fetal safety and the antibiogram; in this case targeted therapy aimed to treat the urinary pathogen identified and to reduce the risk of postoperative or pelvic infection that could threaten the pregnancy.

Hematologic support after surgery
Significant blood loss and preexisting iron‑deficiency anaemia required active management. The patient’s postoperative Hb fell to 7.5 g/dL, prompting intravenous iron (ferric carboxymaltose) and two packed red blood cell transfusions, after which hemoglobin rose to a safer level. Optimizing maternal oxygen‑carrying capacity is critical for fetal well‑being; transfusion thresholds are individualized in pregnancy but treating symptomatic or severe anemia is standard. The prompt correction of iron deficiency and anemia facilitated recovery and ongoing obstetric surveillance.

Postoperative surveillance and obstetric follow‑up
After discharge the patient underwent close obstetric follow‑up at roughly three‑week intervals with serial ultrasound assessments of fetal growth and amniotic fluid, monitoring for signs of preterm labour, infection or lesion progression. The remaining pelvic myoma was reassessed by ultrasound; its partial postpartum regression and absence of symptoms supported continued conservative management. Close surveillance balances the need to detect complications early with the desire to avoid repeat surgery.

Delivery planning: elective cesarean at term
Delivery mode was determined by the anatomy and potential for labour obstruction. The posterior intramural–subserosal myoma in the isthmic region was judged likely to obstruct the fetal head during active labour, so an elective cesarean at 37 weeks was planned. Reusing the existing midline laparotomy scar for the cesarean minimized additional abdominal wall insult and avoided creating a secondary incision. Timing at 37 weeks balances fetal maturity with the desire to minimize the chance of spontaneous labour and potential intrapartum complications. The plan exemplifies individualized delivery planning: cesarean to prevent labour dystocia and potential emergency surgery versus trial of labour that could become complicated by obstructive myoma.

Clinical teaching points
- Operative intervention in pregnancy should be reserved for clear maternal/fetal indications (progressive symptoms, risk of acute mass complications, diagnostic uncertainty that affects management).
- When surgery is required, the second trimester is the preferred window when practical.
- Approach choice (laparoscopy versus laparotomy) must be individualized; very large, posterior, multiple or highly vascular lesions and a gravid uterus often favour an open approach for rapid control.
- Intraoperative measures to preserve pregnancy include fetal monitoring, minimal uterine manipulation, prompt hemostasis, collecting peritoneal samples when indicated, and limiting procedures to lesions that can be safely excised.
- Conservative management of adherent intramural lesions can reduce maternal and fetal risk when removal would markedly increase operative morbidity.
- Perioperative progesterone, targeted antibiotics, and aggressive correction of anemia may be employed to support pregnancy recovery; all interventions should be tailored to maternal status, culture results and gestational age.
- Delivery planning must incorporate the residual anatomy—obstructive myomas often mandate elective cesarean at term to avoid labour dystocia and emergency interventions.

In this patient the chosen strategy—timely second‑trimester laparotomy limited to excision of the acutely compromised pedunculated leiomyoma, conservative preservation of the adherent pelvic myoma, perioperative measures to protect the pregnancy and close longitudinal obstetric follow‑up—achieved the dual goals of resolving the immediate maternal complication while preserving the pregnancy and producing an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome.

## Outcomes, Pathology, and Practice-Changing Lessons

Pathology and cytology from this case provided definitive reassurance. The excised cranial lesion proved to be a benign uterine leiomyoma with areas of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to a twisted pedicle, explaining the acute pain, fever and the intraoperative finding of a leaking, highly vascular stalk. Peritoneal fluid sent for culture and cytology was negative, and there was no histological evidence of malignancy. These findings converted the preoperative diagnostic uncertainty into a clear, benign diagnosis and justified the decision to limit the operation to the symptomatic, high‑risk mass.

Maternal recovery and neonatal outcome were excellent. Removal of the pedunculated, torsed myoma produced prompt symptom relief. The patient required treatment for postoperative anaemia (intravenous iron and two transfusions) but otherwise recovered without surgical complications and was discharged on postoperative day 10. Fetal well‑being was maintained throughout follow‑up; growth remained satisfactory and an elective caesarean at term produced a healthy neonate. The residual pelvic myoma behaved conservatively: its size decreased modestly to about 7.5 cm postpartum, the patient remained asymptomatic while breastfeeding, and she elected expectant management rather than immediate resection.

Several practical, case‑based lessons emerge for managing large atypical abdominal masses in pregnancy. First, diagnostic sequencing matters: focused transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound with colour Doppler is the initial modality of choice to characterise size, cystic versus solid components, vascularity and relationship to the uterus. When sonographic features are indeterminate or when precise anatomical detail is required, non‑contrast MRI (avoiding gadolinium) provides superior soft‑tissue characterisation and clarification of uterine versus adnexal origin. Tumour markers such as CA‑125 must be interpreted cautiously in pregnancy; physiologic rises occur and an isolated, mildly elevated second‑trimester value (as in this patient) is not diagnostic of malignancy, although serial trends can add context.

Surgical intervention should be reserved for well‑defined indications: acute complications (torsion, rupture, infection), intractable symptoms that do not respond to conservative measures, significant mass effect that threatens respiratory or uterine space for fetal growth, or substantial diagnostic uncertainty with a reasonable concern for malignancy. When surgery is necessary, the second trimester is a preferred window when feasible because it balances maternal surgical risk and fetal maturity. Intraoperative management should prioritize fetal safety — document fetal heart rate before, during (as feasible), and after the procedure — and minimize operative time and uterine trauma. In this case the team used intraoperative fetal monitoring and prophylactic progesterone and limited resection to the lesion most likely to cause acute harm, leaving a large but stable pelvic myoma in situ to avoid undue obstetric risk.

Decisions about mode of delivery should be individualized and driven by anatomy rather than diagnosis alone. Residual pelvic or lower‑segment myomas that obstruct the birth canal or are likely to impede descent of the fetal head are valid indications to plan a caesarean delivery; conversely, multiple non‑obstructing myomas do not mandate Caesarean delivery by themselves. The management here — elective caesarean at term through the same abdominal scar because the remaining isthmic/posterior myoma posed an obstacle to labour — illustrates this principle.

Finally, multidisciplinary planning is central to safe, patient‑centred care for these complex presentations. Obstetricians, gynaecologic surgeons, anaesthetists, radiologists and neonatologists should collaborate to weigh risks and benefits, decide imaging strategy, plan surgical approach and anticipate perioperative fetal and maternal needs. A practical, case‑informed algorithm is: symptomatic abdominal/pelvic mass in pregnancy → targeted US with Doppler → if indeterminate or anatomy unclear, obtain non‑contrast MRI → perform risk assessment for malignancy versus complication (using imaging, clinical course and serial tumour markers) → if stable and low risk, manage expectantly with close follow‑up; if torsion/rupture/infection, intractable symptoms, significant mass effect or compelling oncologic concern, plan timely surgery optimally in the second trimester with intraoperative fetal monitoring and measures to minimize uterine disruption → plan delivery mode according to residual anatomy and obstruction risk. This case underscores that conservative, pregnancy‑preserving decisions guided by careful imaging, multidisciplinary judgment and targeted surgery when indicated can produce excellent maternal and neonatal outcomes while avoiding overtreatment.
