# CA-125 in Pregnancy: Don’t Take the Bait

This case is a great reminder that tumor markers can lie — or at least mislead — when pregnancy is in the picture. A woman in her 40s at 21 weeks gestation had two massive, cystic abdominal lesions and a CA-125 that drifted from 36 to 41.8 U/mL. That small bump—above the conventional 35 U/mL cutoff—pushed the team to pursue MRI and, ultimately, laparotomy. Intra‑op surprise: the “ovarian” upper mass was a pedunculated, cystic, twisted leiomyoma; the lower lesion was a cystic-degenerate posterior myoma left in situ. Pathology: benign leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis. Mother and baby did well.

Why this matters: CA-125 stirs anxiety. In nonpregnant patients it contributes to risk stratification for ovarian malignancy, but in pregnancy its baseline and biologic behavior change. Using an adult cutoff without context risks overcalling cancer and triggering unnecessary imaging or surgery that can harm the pregnancy.

What CA-125 does in pregnancy — the facts you need
- CA-125 normally fluctuates during pregnancy. Median values are higher in the first trimester, drop in the second, and rise again in the third. Typical second‑trimester medians are much lower than the arbitrary 35 U/mL cutoff used for nonpregnant adults.
- Transient mild elevations are common and nonspecific: physiologic changes, decidualization, endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic infection, and even large uterine leiomyomas can push levels up.
- A single CA-125 value in pregnancy has low specificity and positive predictive value for malignancy. Serial trends are more informative than one-off numbers, but even trends must be interpreted alongside clinical and imaging data.
- CA-125 is not a reliable discriminator between benign decidualized endometrioma and malignant transformation during pregnancy.

Why single cutoffs mislead
- The 35 U/mL threshold was validated outside pregnancy and performs poorly during gestation. A value slightly above 35 in the second trimester is neither diagnostic nor necessarily alarming.
- Pregnancy itself produces false positives. Relying on the cutoff can create cognitive bias: a borderline elevation may prime clinicians to interpret imaging through a malignancy lens.
- Tumor marker algorithms (e.g., Risk of Malignancy Index) incorporate CA-125 and ultrasound features, but these were not developed or validated in pregnant populations. Applying them blindly can overestimate cancer risk.

How to integrate CA-125 into evaluation of pelvic masses in pregnancy
- Start with a careful clinical exam and high-quality ultrasound (transabdominal + transvaginal when feasible). Ultrasound is the first-line tool for characterizing adnexal vs uterine lesions, vascularity, septations, solid components, and relationships to the uterus.
- Use non-contrast MRI (≤1.5 T if possible) when ultrasound is inconclusive, when anatomy is distorted by gravid uterus and large masses, or when you need better assessment of tissue planes and myometrial continuity.
- Treat CA-125 as an adjunct, not a driver. Consider:
  - Gestational age (first and third trimesters commonly higher).
  - Magnitude of elevation (mild—near cutoff—versus markedly high).
  - Trend over time (rising, stable, or falling).
  - Clinical features (pain, fever, torsion risk, mass growth, systemic symptoms).
  - Imaging appearance (clear ovarian corpus vs continuity with myometrium, solid enhancing nodules — although gadolinium is generally avoided).
- If CA-125 is mildly elevated but imaging suggests uterine origin (e.g., pedunculated or subserosal myoma), manage conservatively unless there are complications (torsion, rupture, infection, severe mass effect). This case shows how imaging misled initially but definitive surgery found a leiomyoma — the take-home is to synthesize all data, not overreact to a number.

When to operate
- Indications for surgical intervention in pregnancy remain: acute complications (torsion, hemorrhage, rupture), persistent severe symptoms unresponsive to conservative therapy, or honest concern for malignancy that cannot be clarified noninvasively.
- If surgery is required, plan it with a multidisciplinary team (obstetrics, gynecology, anesthesia, neonatology, radiology). Document fetal heart rate before, during, and after, keep the operation as short as feasible, and avoid gadolinium.
- Remember that conservative management is often safest for the fetus; but delayed surgery may be reasonable only if maternal risk remains acceptable.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t let CA-125 alone push you toward aggressive management in pregnancy. Context is everything.
- A small rise above 35 U/mL in the second trimester is common and nonspecific — combine tumor markers with serial imaging and clinical course.
- Use ultrasound first; reserve non-contrast MRI when ultrasound cannot answer the key questions (origin, relationship to uterus, solid vs cystic components).
- If imaging suggests myoma (continuity with myometrium, pedicle), treat as such unless other red flags exist.
- Salvage surgery for complications is appropriate; prophylactic surgery to “rule out malignancy” based solely on a borderline CA-125 is usually not.

Take-home messages
- CA-125 is unreliable as a standalone malignancy screen in pregnancy; avoid applying nonpregnant cutoffs rigidly.
- Interpret CA-125 trends, not isolated values, and always integrate with ultrasound/MRI and clinical status.
- Multidisciplinary decision-making and conservative management are the default unless clear complications or strong, corroborated imaging signs of malignancy exist.
- When in doubt, prioritize maternal and fetal safety: manage symptoms, monitor closely, and operate for complications — not for numbers.

This case nailed the key lesson: a small bump in CA-125 can be a red herring. Let imaging, serial assessments, and the clinical picture lead the way — not a single lab value.
