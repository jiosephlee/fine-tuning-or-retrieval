# Operating in the Second Trimester: Indications, Timing, and Tactics for Pelvic Masses

What makes this case interesting
A woman in her 40s at 21+6 weeks gestation presented with two very large abdominal masses and symptoms—upper-quadrant pain, pelvic pain and dyspnoea. Imaging (US + non-contrast MRI) could not definitively distinguish adnexal vs uterine origin. At laparotomy the “upper” lesion proved to be a pedunculated, twisted, highly vascular uterine leiomyoma (cystic degeneration, leaking) and was removed; the pelvic (caudal) mass that was adherent to the uterine posterior wall was left in situ. Pregnancy was preserved, she received perioperative progesterone, recovered after transfusion/iron, had an elective CS at 37 weeks and both mother and baby did well.

This case highlights the common real-world dilemma: large, symptomatic abdominal/pelvic masses in mid-pregnancy that pose immediate maternal risk (pain, torsion, infection, mass effect on respiration) but where intervention risks the pregnancy. It shows pragmatic multidisciplinary decision-making: operate for maternal safety/diagnosis while attempting to preserve the pregnancy and avoid overtreatment.

Clinical framing — when to consider surgery in pregnancy
Pregnancy is not an absolute contraindication to non-obstetric abdominal surgery. Expectant management remains the default for most asymptomatic masses, but intervene when the balance shifts toward maternal risk or diagnostic uncertainty that could hide malignancy.

Indications to strongly consider surgical intervention in pregnancy (illustrated by this case)
- Acute, uncontrolled pain despite conservative measures (analgesia, positioning)
- Signs of torsion, rupture, active leaking or necrosis (fever, inflammatory markers, peritoneal signs)
- Infection of the mass or patient (sepsis risk, positive cultures)
- Significant mass effect (respiratory compromise, urinary obstruction)
- Rapid growth with concern for degeneration or malignancy and inconclusive imaging/markers
- Inability to exclude malignancy by safe imaging modalities or sampling

Timing: why the second trimester is often preferred
- Second trimester (14–28 weeks) is the “sweet spot”: organogenesis is complete, uterine size still manageable for abdominal access, and risk of preterm labour is lower than third trimester. In this case, progressive mass effect and diagnostic uncertainty at 21+6 warranted surgery.
- First-trimester surgery increases miscarriage risk; third-trimester surgery carries higher preterm delivery risk and technical difficulty due to uterine size.

Imaging strategy before committing to surgery
- Ultrasound (with Doppler) is first-line: assess size, vascularity, relation to uterus, presence of solid components.
- Non-contrast MRI (preferably ≤1.5 T if practical) is safe after the first trimester and adds value for posterior/intramural lesions and to map distance from uterine cavity or placenta. Gadolinium should be avoided unless absolutely necessary.
- Tumour markers (eg, CA‑125) are of limited discriminatory value in pregnancy — values fluctuate with gestation and benign conditions (endometriosis, fibroids, infection) can raise them. Use trends and context, not single values.

Surgical approach: laparoscopy vs laparotomy
- Laparoscopy advantages: less postoperative pain, shorter stay, quicker recovery, lower adhesion formation. It can be considered in pregnancy for small-to-moderate masses early in pregnancy with experienced surgeons and appropriate equipment.
- Laparotomy advantages: better exposure for very large masses, multiple masses, highly vascular lesions, suspected malignancy, or when adhesions/torsion/degeneration make minimally invasive approach unsafe. In this case the large, vascular, and initially twisted pedunculated mass justified a midline laparotomy.
- Practical selection factors:
  - Mass size and number (large/giant masses → laparotomy)
  - Location (posterior/intramural, lower uterine segment involvement)
  - Gestational age and uterine size
  - Suspicion of malignancy or need for wide exposure
  - Team experience with advanced laparoscopy in pregnancy

Intraoperative and perioperative tactics
- Multidisciplinary planning: obstetrics, gynecologic surgery, anesthesia, neonatology should be involved.
- Positioning: left lateral tilt (15°) to avoid aortocaval compression; semi‑upright positioning can sometimes reduce diaphragmatic compression from large masses and the gravid uterus.
- Fetal heart rate monitoring: document fetal heart rate before, during (intermittent if continuous is impractical), and after surgery whenever possible. Continuous intraoperative monitoring may be difficult during laparotomy but should be attempted or at least serially checked.
- Anesthesia: gravid physiology mandates attention to airway (risk of difficult intubation), aspiration prophylaxis, reduced functional residual capacity, and oxygenation. Use uterine relaxation sparingly; avoid hypotension. Maintain normothermia and good perfusion.
- Blood loss preparedness: anticipate heavy bleeding with myomectomy; have crossmatched blood available and be ready for transfusion (this patient required transfusion and iron).
- Surgical strategy: limit operative time, minimize uterine manipulation, control pedicle before cutting, ensure watertight uterine repair if myometrial incisions are made. If possible, stage procedures (remove only the lesion causing acute risk and leave others).
- Antibiotics: treat concurrent infections guided by culture; infection in this case (Klebsiella UTI) influenced perioperative management.
- Progesterone/tocolysis: commonly used in practice but evidence is mixed.
  - This case used intraoperative and then ongoing IM progesterone (hydroxyprogesterone caproate regimen). Current guidelines do not mandate routine prophylactic tocolytics or progesterone for intraoperative uterine manipulation—decision is often individualized.
  - Tocolytics (eg, nifedipine, beta‑agonists) are used for postoperative uterine irritability or threatened preterm labour, not as standardized prophylaxis for all procedures. Discuss risks/benefits with obstetric team.

Pathology and delivery planning
- Expect possible degenerative changes in fibroids; pedunculated myomas can twist and necrose—this explains fever and CRP rise here.
- Residual large myomas can obstruct labour—plan mode of delivery based on location, size, and number of uterine masses (this patient had elective CS at 37 weeks due to a posterior isthmic mass).
- Postpartum regression: many leiomyomas shrink after delivery, which influences whether to schedule additional surgery.

Clinical pearls from this case
- Large, highly vascular, mixed solid-cystic pelvic masses in pregnancy may mimic adnexal pathology (eg, decidualised endometrioma) but can still be uterine in origin—keep pedunculated and degenerating leiomyoma high on the differential.
- CA‑125 can be modestly elevated in pregnancy and with benign conditions; trend and clinical context matter.
- When maternal risk (pain, torsion, infection, respiratory compromise) is high and imaging is inconclusive, timely surgery in the second trimester can preserve both maternal health and the pregnancy.
- Tailor the approach: remove the lesion causing immediate risk and leave stable lesions in situ when possible to minimize obstetric complications.
- Multidisciplinary planning, readiness for hemorrhage, attentive fetal heart assessment, and appropriate perioperative support are essential for successful outcomes.

Take-home messages
- Expectant management is preferred for most pelvic masses in pregnancy; operate when maternal risk or diagnostic uncertainty outweighs surgical risk.
- Second trimester is the optimal window for non-obstetric abdominal surgery when possible.
- Use transvaginal/transabdominal US and non-contrast MRI for preoperative planning; avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary.
- Choose laparoscopy for appropriately sized and located masses with experienced teams; opt for laparotomy for very large, multiple, highly vascular, or suspicious lesions.
- Document fetal heart rate before, during (if feasible), and after surgery; involve obstetrics/neonatology and anesthesia early.
- Prophylactic progesterone/tocolysis is common but remains controversial — individualize decisions and follow institutional practice and obstetric input.

This case is a useful reminder: prioritize maternal safety, use safe imaging, and adopt a pragmatic surgical plan—remove what must be removed, leave what can safely wait, and aim to preserve the pregnancy whenever possible.
