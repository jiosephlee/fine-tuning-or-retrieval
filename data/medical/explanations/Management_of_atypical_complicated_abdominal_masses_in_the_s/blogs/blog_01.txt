# The Adnexal Mass That Wasn’t: Pedunculated Cystic Fibroid Mimicking Endometrioma in Pregnancy

What makes this case interesting? At 21 weeks a nulliparous woman in her 40s arrived with dyspnea and abdominal pain and ultrasound showed two large cystic masses — one in the left subphrenic space and one filling the pouch of Douglas. CA-125 was mildly elevated. MRI without contrast showed large unilocular cysts with inhomogeneous contents and thick walls. The team planned surgery for worsening symptoms and potential complications, and intra‑operatively the upper “adnexal” lesion turned out to be a pedunculated, highly vascular cystic leiomyoma — twisted and leaking — not an ovarian endometrioma. The lower pelvic cyst was a cystic-degenerated posterior myoma left in situ to protect the pregnancy; delivery was by planned CS at term with a great maternal and neonatal outcome.

Why this matters: pregnant patients get a limited diagnostic toolbox. Mislabeling a pedunculated subserosal or cystic-degenerated fibroid as an adnexal mass changes counseling, risk estimates and surgical planning. Know the bedside and imaging clues that point back to the uterus.

Clinical context — what made the team consider surgery
- Severe pain localized to upper quadrant and pelvis plus mild dyspnea from mass effect.
- Rapidly enlarging, highly vascular masses with mixed solid/cystic components and a small, rising CA‑125 during the second trimester.
- Concern for torsion, rupture, infection and restriction of uterine growth as pregnancy progressed.
- Symptom control was inadequate with conservative therapy, so laparotomy with intraoperative fetal monitoring was chosen.

Practical pearls for distinguishing decidualized endometrioma vs cystic or pedunculated leiomyoma in pregnancy

Bimanual and bedside exam
- Don’t skip the bimanual vaginal exam. A fixed, posterior pelvic mass that bulges into the posterior fornix favors uterine origin (or at least close adherence). In this case the caudal mass was fixed and bulging posteriorly, a hint it was connected to the uterus.
- Assess mobility. Pedunculated subserosal lesions may be mobile on palpation if on a long stalk, but once torsed or adherent they can feel fixed.

Ultrasound clues (real-world, high-yield)
- Look for the “stalk sign”: a discrete pedicle connecting a mass to the uterine serosa. It may be thin and missed without careful scanning, especially with large gravid uteri. If you find it, you’ve got a uterine lesion.
- Bridging vessels on color Doppler: vessels that run from the uterus into the mass suggest uterine vascular supply (leiomyoma) rather than ovarian origin. These vessels often course along the myometrium into the lesion.
- Internal echotexture: endometriomas classically show ground-glass homogenous low-level internal echoes; decidualization in pregnancy can add vascular mural nodules. Cystic degeneration in fibroids can produce unilocular/ multilocular cystic areas with variable internal echoes and sometimes septations or thick walls.
- Peripheral vascularity and hypertrophied feeding vessels often point to a fibroid, especially when the mass is large and vascularized.
- Always scan systematically: assess uterine contour, adnexal regions, and the relationship between masses and uterus in multiple planes.

Non-contrast MRI — what to look for (safely after first trimester)
- Continuity with uterine wall: the clearest MRI sign a mass is uterine in origin is loss of a clear cleavage plane and visual continuity with myometrium. The caudal mass in this case blended with a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma on MRI.
- Signal characteristics: leiomyomas commonly show low T2 signal, but cystic degeneration increases T1/T2 signal variability. Hemorrhage or proteinaceous material can be T1 hyperintense. Endometriomas often have high T1 signal (blood products) and variable T2 shading; decidualized nodules may enhance with gadolinium — but in pregnancy we avoid gadolinium.
- Wall thickness and mural nodules: thick walls and focal mural solid components can occur in both entities; use the relationship to the uterus and bridging vessels to tip the scale.
- No contrast → rely on morphology, signal patterns and anatomical continuity. Remember: gadolinium is avoided in pregnancy unless absolutely necessary.

Tumor markers in pregnancy — don’t overinterpret
- CA‑125 can be physiologically elevated in pregnancy (peaks in first and third trimesters). A modest elevation (like 40 U/mL) is nonspecific. Serial rises are informative but not diagnostic.
- Use markers as an adjunct, not a determinant. In this case a rising CA‑125 nudged the team towards surgical exploration given clinical deterioration, but was not definitive.

When to operate in pregnancy
- Surgery is generally avoided if possible because of risks (bleeding, preterm labor, pregnancy loss). But operative management is indicated for:
  - Torsion of a pedunculated fibroid
  - Rupture or active hemorrhage
  - Severe, refractory pain or progressive mass effect (e.g., respiratory compromise)
  - Uncertain diagnosis with oncologic suspicion despite safe imaging
- If surgery is necessary, minimize duration and uterine manipulation; have fetal monitoring and a multidisciplinary team (obstetrics, anesthesia, surgery). Intraoperative and postoperative progesterone prophylaxis was used in this case, and fetal heart monitoring before/during/after surgery is mandatory.

Intraoperative decision-making matters
- Remove the life/uterus‑threatening lesion and leave adherent lesions that pose high obstetric risk if excised. In this patient the upper twisted pedunculated myoma was removed; the posterior intramural cystic myoma was deliberately left to avoid compromising the pregnancy.
- Control the pedicle carefully and suture the uterine defect. Anticipate and manage blood loss (this case required transfusion and IV iron).

Take-home messages
- Large cystic masses in pregnancy can be deceptive: pedunculated or cystic-degenerated leiomyomas may mimic adnexal endometriomas on imaging.
- Key distinguishing features:
  - Bimanual exam: fixed posterior mass bulging into the fornix → think uterine origin.
  - Ultrasound: look for the stalk sign and bridging vessels on color Doppler.
  - MRI (non-contrast): assess continuity with myometrium and cleavage planes; interpret T1/T2 signals in clinical context.
  - CA‑125 is nonspecific in pregnancy — interpret trends, not single values.
- Operate only for clear indications (torsion, rupture, infection, refractory symptoms, or strong oncologic suspicion). When you do, remove the lesion threatening the pregnancy and consider leaving adherent lesions to minimize obstetric risk.
- Multidisciplinary planning, intraoperative fetal monitoring and careful postpartum follow-up are essential.

This case is a great reminder: in pregnant patients the origin of a “pelvic/abdominal cyst” is as much about anatomy and vascular connections as it is about signal intensities. Look for the stalk and bridging vessels first — they will often save you from mislabeling a uterine fibroid as an adnexal tumor.
