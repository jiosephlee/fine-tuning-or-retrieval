Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Avoiding Patient–Prosthesis Mismatch in Redo TAVR: Device Choice and Implantation Technique  

---

## Why this case matters  

When you hear “third transcatheter valve in the same aortic root,” you probably imagine an anatomy that’s already screaming “no more metal.” Yet our 70‑year‑old gentleman survived a **Lotus → Evolut → Acurate neo2** sequence without surgical explantation, infection relapse, or catastrophic coronary obstruction. The real star of the story is not just that we managed a triple‑stented valve—but that we turned an initially **mean gradient of > 60 mm Hg** after the third implant into a durable **< 30 mm Hg** result by tweaking device selection and implantation technique. If you’re grappling with structural valve deterioration (SVD) after a TAV‑in‑TAV, this case offers a roadmap for dodging severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

---

## The anatomy of stacked valves – why PPM happens  

Every time we add a new frame we shrink the residual effective orifice area (EOA). In this patient:

* **Lotus (2014)** – first generation intra‑annular device with a relatively low radial force.
* **Evolut (2022)** – self‑expanding supra‑annular frame sitting inside the Lotus frame.
* **Acurate neo2 (2024)** – third frame placed inside both previous prostheses.

The **minimum lumen diameter** inside the Evolut was only **13.7 mm**, already borderline for an adult aortic annulus. When you cramp another valve into that space you risk two things:

1. **Geometric narrowing** – the “neo‑skirt” created by overlapping stent struts reduces central flow.
2. **Leaflet restriction** – each successive prosthesis leaves less room for leaflet excursion, pushing the Doppler velocities up and the calculated EOA down.

Clinically this manifests as severe PPM: high transvalvular gradients despite normal left ventricular ejection fraction. In our patient’s immediate post‑deployment echo after the third valve, the mean gradient spiked to **67 mm Hg**, AVA dropped to **0.9 cm²**, and Doppler velocity index was only **0.24** — textbook severe mismatch.

---

## Device selection: supra‑annular vs intra‑annular  

When you already have multiple frames occupying the annulus, you need every millimetre of flow area you can muster:

| Feature | Intra‑annular devices (e.g., original Lotus) | Supra‑annular devices (e.g., Evolut, Acurate neo2) |
|---------|---------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| Leaflet position | At annular plane → limited EOA | Above stent frame → larger effective orifice |
| Frame height | Shorter but often more bulky at annulus | Lower frame height at sinus level; higher leaflet position |
| Coronary clearance | Dependent on frame struts | Often better if commissural alignment achieved |
| Suitability for narrow lumen | Poor when inner diameter <14 mm | Superior because leaflets open into sinus rather than through constrained annulus |

Our patient’s third implant needed a valve that could *reach over* the existing stents without adding bulk underneath them. The **Acurate neo2** was chosen precisely because:

* It is **supra‑annular**, so its leaflets sit above the existing frames and can use whatever sinus space remains.
* Its frame is relatively low profile, mitigating further luminal encroachment.
* It offers built‑in tools for **commissural alignment**, which is crucial when you have already stacked two previous commissural orientations.

Had we stuck with another intra‑annular device, we’d have been forcing leaflets through an ever tighter aperture—likely cementing severe PPM forever.

---

## Implantation depth – high versus low  

Depth matters twice: it determines where the new stent sits relative to previous leaflet planes and it dictates how much neoskirt will sit below the coronary ostia.

**High implantation strategy** (our approach) placed the neo2 frame so its inflow edge aligned roughly with the leaflets of the Evolut prosthesis rather than plunging deeper into its lumen. The benefits were:

1. **Maximised residual EOA** – By “sandwiching” the Evolut leaflets against its own frame you preserve their opening area while still creating a central flow channel.
2. **Reduced neo‑skirt downstream** – Less metal protrudes into the sinus below the coronaries, preserving coronary perfusion and future access.
3. **Facilitated commissural alignment** – Aligning onto existing leaflet orientation gave us predictable inflow/outflow geometry.

If you implant low (deep), you create an additional skirt below all previous frames—a recipe for higher gradients and jeopardized coronary flow, especially when coronary heights are modest (<12 mm). In our CT workup we confirmed adequate coronary ostial heights (>10 mm) and that high deployment would leave >6 mm clearance from any stent struts—a safe buffer without needing prophylactic coronary protection wires.

---

## Balloon strategies – pre‑dilation + post‑dilatation  

Even a perfectly sized valve can suffer from incomplete expansion when forced into an already rigid scaffold:

* **Pre‑dilation** with a 22 × 40 mm balloon under rapid pacing “softened” any residual tissue/fibrosis from prior implants and gave us a clear landing zone.
* **Post‑dilatation** using a slightly smaller 20 × 40 mm balloon under rapid pacing achieved two things:
  - *Radial expansion* of the previously crimped Acurate neo2 frame to its intended diameter.
  - *Fracture/expansion* of portions of the underlying Evolut stent—although we didn’t intentionally perform BASILICA or intentional Bioprosthetic Valve Fracture (BVF), high‐pressure inflation likely nudged its nitinol struts outward enough to improve leaflet motion.

The immediate post‐implant echo still showed severe gradients (>60 mm Hg), which taught us that *visual inspection alone is insufficient*. We repeated angiography after post‐dilatation and noted improved forward flow with no new coronary compromise. Over the next few weeks gradients fell dramatically—by one month mean gradient was down to **27 mm Hg**, AVA up to **1.1 cm²**, cementing the notion that aggressive yet controlled ballooning can rescue an otherwise mismatched valve stack.

---

## Putting it together – our stepwise plan for a triple‐valve patient  

1. **Comprehensive CT Planning**  
   - Measure inner lumen diameters of all existing frames (13–14 mm in our case).  
   - Map coronary ostial heights & sinus dimensions; calculate “coronary clearance” after predicted implantation depth.  
   - Simulate virtual implantation using vendor software or open‐source modelling tools to anticipate neoskirt position.
2. **Device Selection Tailored to Geometry**  
   - Choose a supra‐annular valve with low frame height if inner diameter <15 mm—Acurate neo2 fits nicely here because it restores supra‐annular flow without excessive radial force.
3. **High‐Implant Strategy Aligned With Prior Leaflets**  
   - Use fluoroscopic landmarks from CT overlay to align commissures before deployment; aim for inflow edge at level of preceding prosthesis leaflets.
4. **Balloon Management**  
   - Pre‐dilate generously under rapid pacing to “prepare” prior stents and reduce risk of malpositioning.  
   - After deployment, perform careful post‐dilation—start low pressure, titrate up while watching hemodynamics on fluoroscopy & invasive pressure tracings.
5. **Intraoperative Assessment**  
   - Perform full angiography before committing final dilation—to verify no coronary obstruction even if CT suggests clearance is adequate.
   - Use an immediate post‑procedural TEE or rapid echo sweep if feasible; especially valuable where gradients are unexpectedly high.
6. **Post‐procedure Surveillance**  
   - Echo at discharge, 30 days, 3 months, then yearly—track mean gradient trend; expect initial overshoot in gradients that may settle once frames fully expand and leaflet dynamics normalize.
   - Keep an eye on coronary access pathways—document any limitations early if future PCI might be needed.
7. **Team Communication & Shared Decision Making**  
   - Multidisciplinary heart team review especially critical when infective endocarditis history exists—ensure infection eradicated before any reintervention.
   - Discuss realistic expectations about residual gradients with patients given layered prostheses; emphasize that improvement over time is common when proper balloon tactics are used.

---

## Take‑home pearls  

- **Supra‑annular valves win** when you’re reusing an annulus already narrowed by earlier frames; they preserve EOA by moving leaflets above existing metal scaffolds.
- **Higher implantation depth aligned with prior leaflet planes** maximises central flow area while protecting coronaries—avoid deep deployment unless anatomy forces you.
- **Aggressive yet measured balloon post-dilatation** can dramatically lower gradients even after an apparently failed implant; watch hemodynamics closely during inflation.
- **CT is your GPS**—measure inner diameters, coronary heights, sinus dimensions, and simulate virtual valve positioning before ever touching catheters.
- **Commissural alignment matters twice:** first for immediate haemodynamics, second for future coronary access—a non‑negotiable goal when multiple frames are present.
- Even severe early PPM (**mean gradient >60 mm Hg**) does not always spell doom—strategic ballooning plus time can bring gradients down into acceptable range (**<30 mm Hg**) as seen at one year in this case.
- When prosthetic valve endocarditis has been adequately treated (negative cultures, no vegetations on TEE/CT), redo TAVR becomes a viable option even after two prior transcatheter implants, provided surgical risk remains prohibitive.

---

Redo TAVR is moving from “exceptional circumstance” toward being part of ordinary lifetime management for structural valve disease. Mastering device choice, implantation depth strategy, and precise balloon techniques will keep patient–prosthesis mismatch at bay—even when your patient’s aorta looks like a layered cake! Keep your CT workstation hot and your balloons ready—you’ll thank yourself when those mean gradients finally drop below that scary threshold on day one follow‑up echo.
