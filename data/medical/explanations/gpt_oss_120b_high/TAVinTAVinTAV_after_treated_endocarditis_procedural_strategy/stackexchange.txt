Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] A man in his late 70s with a prior Lotus 27 mm valve implanted in 2014 and an Evolut 23 mm valve implanted in 2022 now presents with severe prosthetic stenosis (mean gradient 49 mm Hg) and grade 2–3 intraprosthetic regurgitation. His EuroSCORE II is 27 % and he has multiple comorbidities—including COPD, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease stage III, prior gastrointestinal bleeding episodes, three‑vessel coronary artery disease treated percutaneously, and a history of treated Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic‑valve endocarditis now resolved. Surgical explantation would require extensive aortic root reconstruction. Which clinical factors most strongly favor proceeding with a third transcatheter aortic‑valve implantation rather than surgical redo?

**Clinical Pearls – When a third transcatheter aortic‑valve implantation (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) outweighs surgical redo**

The patient described – a man in his late‑70s with two previously implanted transcatheter valves (Lotus 27 mm in 2014, Evolut 23 mm in 2022) – now presents with severe prosthetic stenosis (mean gradient 49 mm Hg) and moderate‑to‑severe intraprosthetic regurgitation.  His EuroSCORE II is 27 % and he carries multiple organ comorbidities (COPD, CKD‑III, diabetes, chronic GI bleeding) plus a history of treated prosthetic‑valve endocarditis.  Open surgical explantation would require extensive aortic root reconstruction because the existing stent frames are already incorporated into the wall.  

Below are the clinical elements that most strongly tip the balance toward a **third percutaneous valve** rather than an operative redo:

| Factor | Why it favors TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV | Evidence from the case |
|--------|--------------------------------|------------------------|
| **Very high predicted surgical mortality** – EuroSCORE II ≈ 27 % | A EuroSCORE II >20 % is conventionally regarded as “high‑risk” and predicts >20 % peri‑operative death. In such patients, even experienced cardiac surgeons cannot reliably achieve acceptable outcomes. | The heart team explicitly cited an EuroSCORE II of 27 % as prohibitive for conventional replacement. |
| **Multisystem comorbidity profile** – severe COPD (risk of prolonged ventilation), CKD‑III (higher likelihood of renal failure after cardiopulmonary bypass), diabetes (wound infection risk), repeated GI bleeding (higher peri‑operative bleeding risk) | Each comorbidity independently raises operative mortality and morbidity; combined they make sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass particularly hazardous. |
| **Prior percutaneous experience with excellent haemodynamic response** – EF rose from 42 % to 54 % after the first TAV‑in‑TAV | Demonstrates that this patient tolerates catheter‑based valve expansion and derives functional benefit without the physiologic stress of open surgery. |
| **Resolution of infective endocarditis** – negative blood cultures, no vegetations on repeat TOE/CT after full antimicrobial course | Active prosthetic infection is a strict indication for surgery; once infection is cleared the patient can be managed as any other structural valve deterioration case. |
| **Anatomical feasibility on multidetector CT** – minimum lumen diameter within Evolut frame = 13.7 mm, coronary ostial heights and sinus dimensions adequate to avoid obstruction; anticipated neo‑skirt well above coronaries | Detailed CT planning showed that a third valve could be implanted at a high depth, preserving coronary flow and leaving enough residual effective orifice area. This makes a percutaneous approach technically safe despite three overlapping frames. |
| **Requirement for complex root reconstruction if operated** – embedded stent struts would need removal, likely mandate total root/ascending‐aorta replacement and prolonged circulatory arrest | Surgical explantation would be technically demanding and would substantially increase cross‑clamp time, bleeding risk, and postoperative morbidity—risks that cannot be offset by any potential benefit over a catheter technique. |
| **Procedural invasiveness** – third TAVR performed under local anesthesia via femoral/radial access | Avoids general anesthesia and mechanical ventilation—critical in severe COPD—and reduces ICU stay, transfusion requirements, and postoperative delirium in elderly patients. |
| **Guideline‐driven individualisation** – ESC 2025 VHD/IE recommendations stress surgery for active PVE but allow transcatheter solutions when infection is cured and surgical risk prohibitive | The heart team’s decision aligns with contemporary guidance: once infection resolves, treatment hinges on operative risk assessment rather than defaulting to surgery. |

**Bottom line:**  
In this scenario the *dominant* considerations are **excessive surgical risk** (EuroSCORE II ≈ 27 %), **severe comorbid disease**, **technical impossibility of safe root replacement**, **absence of active infection**, and **favourable computed tomography anatomy that permits safe valve deployment**. Together these factors justify proceeding with a third transcatheter valve implantation—the only realistic option that offers symptomatic relief while respecting the patient’s frailty profile.

**Take‐away message for trainees:**  
When evaluating repeat interventions for failed transcatheter valves, always frame your decision around:

1. **Quantified surgical risk** (EuroSCORE II / STS score).  
2. **Patient‐specific organ dysfunction** that amplifies peri‑operative hazards.  
3. **Current infection status**—active endocarditis mandates surgery; cured infection allows percutaneous strategies if anatomy permits.  
4. **Anatomic feasibility on MSCT** (annular dimensions, coronary heights, sinus size).  
5. **Technical burden of surgical explantation** versus minimally invasive percutaneous access.

If three or more of these domains point toward prohibitive surgery—as they do here—a carefully planned “valve‑in‑valve‑in‑valve” becomes not only an option but often *the only* viable therapeutic pathway.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiologic mechanisms by which prosthetic‑valve endocarditis accelerates structural degeneration of bioprosthetic transcatheter valves leading to leaflet fibrosis, calcification and functional stenosis.

**Pathophysiology – How prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE) fast‑tracks bioprosthetic degeneration**

Prosthetic‐valve endocarditis adds a potent inflammatory insult on top of the inevitable wear‑and‑tear that limits the durability of transcatheter bioprostheses. In the present patient, severe restenosis recurred within three months of an adequately treated episode of **Enterococcus faecalis** PVE, illustrating how infection can convert a slowly deteriorating valve into one that fails acutely. The accelerated failure results from several inter‑related mechanisms:

1. **Bacterial adherence and bio‑film formation on pericardial leaflets**  
   Transcatheter valves lack a surgical suture line but possess metallic stent struts and glutaraldehyde‑cross‑linked polymeric leaflets that are incompletely endothelialised. These surfaces are readily colonised by organisms such as *E. faecalis*, which produce extracellular polymeric substances that shield bacteria from antibiotics and host immunity. The biofilm becomes a nidus of chronic low‑grade inflammation even after systemic cultures clear.

2. **Proteolytic injury and extracellular‑matrix breakdown**  
   Both bacterial proteases (collagenases, gelatinases) and host‐derived matrix metalloproteinases are up‑regulated within the vegetations. They cleave collagen and elastin fibers that give the pericardial tissue its pliability. Loss of matrix integrity stiffens the leaflets and predisposes them to micro‑tears during each cardiac cycle.

3. **Inflammatory cytokine surge driving fibro‑proliferation**  
   Infiltrating neutrophils, macrophages and lymphocytes release IL‑1β, TNF‑α, IL‑6 and TGF‑β. These cytokines stimulate resident fibroblasts and myofibroblasts to lay down excess collagen (“leaflet fibrosis”). In a bioprosthesis this fibrosis does not remodel intelligently; instead it creates uniformly thickened, less mobile leaflets that cannot fully open.

4. **Osteogenic signaling leading to rapid calcification**  
   The same pro‑inflammatory milieu induces osteogenic transcription factors (BMP‑2, Runx2) in valvular interstitial cells that survive within the pericardial scaffold. Calcium phosphate crystals nucleate on damaged collagen fibers much faster than in sterile degeneration, producing dense nodules that further immobilise the leaflets.

5. **Mechanical amplification in a previously stenosed geometry**  
   By the time PVE occurs this patient already had two overlapping frames (Lotus → Evolut). The confined annular space raises transvalvular gradients and increases shear stress on each leaflet edge. Infection‑induced stiffening therefore translates into disproportionately higher pressure gradients—explaining why his mean gradient rose from 35 mm Hg (early post‑PVE) to 49 mm Hg despite successful antimicrobial therapy.

6. **Irreversible remodeling despite eradication of infection**  
   Once fibrotic scar tissue and calcium deposits have formed they persist after antimicrobial cure; antibiotics eliminate live bacteria but do not dissolve biofilm or reverse matrix remodeling. Hence the valve continues its trajectory toward functional stenosis even when blood cultures are sterile—exactly what was observed in this case when repeat echo at three months showed severe restenosis without any residual vegetation on TEE or CT.

**Clinical take‑away**

PVE imposes an accelerated “double hit”: it introduces direct microbial injury plus a sustained inflammatory cascade that invokes fibrosis and calcification on an already vulnerable bioprosthetic substrate. In patients with prior TAV‑in‑TAV procedures—where effective orifice area is already limited—this can precipitate clinically significant stenosis within weeks to months, as illustrated by our patient’s rapid gradient rise after *E. faecalis* infection.

Recognising this pathophysiology prompts early imaging (TTE/TEE + multislice CT) when PVE is diagnosed on any prosthetic valve, even if infection appears controlled, because structural deterioration may be imminent rather than delayed. It also underscores the importance of meticulous procedural planning for redo transcatheter interventions: selecting supra‑annular devices with low frame height (e.g., Acurate neo2), aligning commissures to preserve coronary access, and aiming for high implantation—all strategies employed here to maximize residual effective orifice area once accelerated degeneration has occurred.

### [Clinical Pearls] When planning a third transcatheter valve inside two previously implanted frames (Lotus + Evolut), which multidetector computed‑tomography measurements are most predictive of coronary‑obstruction risk, and what threshold values define a high‑risk anatomy?

**Clinical Pearl – CT predictors of coronary‑obstruction when implanting a third TAV inside two prior frames (Lotus → Evolut → new valve)**  

When you are faced with a “TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV” scenario, the single most important step before ever touching a catheter is a meticulous multidetector computed‑tomography (MDCT) analysis focused on *coronary clearance*.  In the case reported by Merkulov et al., pre‑procedural MDCT was used exactly for this purpose—their imaging showed enough space between the anticipated neo‑skirt of the third prosthesis and both coronary ostia that they could proceed **without any prophylactic coronary protection** (BASILICA, balloon‐snorkel, etc.).  

Below is a compact checklist of the CT measurements that have been shown to predict iatrogenic coronary obstruction, together with the thresholds that separate “low‑risk” from “high‑risk” anatomy.  These are the same parameters that were evaluated in the present case.

| **CT Parameter** | **How it is obtained** | **High‑risk threshold* ** | **Why it matters for a third TAV** |
|------------------|------------------------|---------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Coronary ostial height** | Straight line from the basal plane of the *virtual* annulus (i.e., inner edge of the newly implanted frame) to the lowest point of each coronary ostium. In a TAV‑in‑TAV setting you measure from the *inner surface* of the **existing** transcatheter frame because that will become your new landing zone. | ≤ 10 mm (some centers use ≤ 12 mm) | A low ostium leaves little room for displaced leaflets or stent struts; even small movements can occlude flow. |
| **Sinus of Valsalva diameter / area** | Maximal cross‑sectional diameter (or cross‑sectional area) at mid‑sinus level on axial reconstructions. | Diameter ≤ 30 mm **or** area ≤ 700 mm² (≈ 28 mm diameter) | Small sinuses cannot accommodate both native/post‑implant leaflet bulk and any “neo‐sinus” created by the new device; obstruction occurs when leaflets occupy ≥ 80% of sinus volume. |
| **Virtual Valve‑to‑Coronary distance (VTC)** | Shortest perpendicular distance from the *inner surface* of the intended prosthetic frame/sealing skirt (“neo‐skirt”) to each coronary ostium on reconstructed 3‑D images. | ≤ 4 mm | This directly predicts whether an implanted leaflet or skirt will sit against an ostium. A VTC < 4 mm has been associated with >50% incidence of acute obstruction in large registries. |
| **Leaflet length vs sinus depth** (“cusp‐overhang” ratio) | Total projected length of all displaced leaflets (sum of prior prosthetic leaflets + planned new leaflets) measured from their hinge point to tip; compared with sinus depth measured from annular plane to sinus floor. Ratio = Leaflet length ÷ Sinus depth. | Ratio > 1  (i.e., leaflets longer than sinus depth) | When leaflets exceed available space they flop into each other and into the sinuses, sealing off an ostium even if heights appear acceptable. |
| **Frame overlap & mesh burden at commissural level** | Qualitative/quantitative assessment on multiplanar reconstructions showing how many struts will lie directly above each commissure after implantation, especially important when multiple self‑expanding frames are stacked. | >2 struts directly over an ostium or predicted *commissural misalignment* >30° relative to native cusps | Multiple meshes can physically block catheter passage later and can create a “shelf” that redirects displaced leaflets toward an ostium. |

\*The exact numeric cut‐offs vary slightly among institutions, but all major guideline documents (ESC/EAPCI 2025 TAVI recommendations) endorse these ranges as practical “high‐risk” flags.

---

### How these measurements guided the present case

* **What was measured:** The authors explicitly list “coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve‑to‑coronary distance and interaction between the frames” as part of their MDCT plan.
* **What they found:** Although exact numbers are not provided in the manuscript, they reported that these assessments “allowed the intervention to be performed without coronary protection.” By inference:
  * Coronary heights were comfortably above ~12 mm,
  * Sinus diameters exceeded ~30 mm,
  * Predicted VTC was > 5–6 mm for both left main and right coronary,
  * The summed leaflet length after placement of an Acurate neo2 was well within each sinus depth,
  * Commissural alignment modelling showed minimal mesh overlap over either ostium.
* **Result:** This favorable anatomy permitted a “high implantation” strategy aimed at keeping the neo‑skirt above both coronaries while aligning commissures for future access—key decisions directly derived from those CT metrics.

---

### Take‑home points for your own “third valve” cases

1. **Never rely on a single metric** – combine ostial height, sinus size and VTC; discordance often uncovers hidden risk.
2. **Measure from the *inner edge* of existing frames**, because those become your new annular reference once you implant another valve.
3. **Apply strict cutoffs**:  
   * Ostial height ≤10 mm → consider BASILICA or protective wire/balloon placement.  
   * Sinus diameter ≤30 mm → same; may require balloon fracturing or selection of a low‐profile device.  
   * VTC ≤4 mm → mandate protection strategy despite otherwise normal heights.
4. **Model leaflet stack** – use dedicated software or manual overlay techniques to estimate total leaflet bulk after three layers; if projected bulk exceeds sinus depth ⇒ high risk even with adequate heights.
5. **Commissural alignment matters more than ever** – in triple stenting every degree off alignment translates into additional mesh crossing over an artery; plan implantation depth accordingly.
6. **If any one parameter crosses its high‑risk threshold**, have rescue options ready before you start: BASILICA, balloon‐snorkel grafts, surgically prepared bailouts, or bioprosthetic fracture for self‐expanding frames when feasible.

By treating these MDCT measurements as a checklist rather than an afterthought, you can predict—and prevent—the dreaded sudden coronary occlusion that historically limits repeat TAVI procedures. The Merkulov et al.^1 experience shows that careful CT planning makes even a third transcatheter valve technically achievable with low procedural risk when anatomical thresholds are respected.

---

**Reference**

1. Merkulov EV et al., *TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome.* JACC Cardiovasc Interv 2024; forthcoming.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Three months after his second TAVI the patient develops an increase in mean transvalvular gradient from ~20 mm Hg to >50 mm Hg together with new intraprosthetic regurgitation on echocardiography. List the principal differential diagnoses for prosthetic aortic‑valve dysfunction in this setting and indicate the key diagnostic test(s) that help discriminate each entity.

**Prosthetic‑aortic‑valve dysfunction 3 months after a second TAVI**  
*Mean gradient ↑ from ~20 mm Hg to >50 mm Hg + new intraprosthetic regurgitation*  

The acute haemodynamic deterioration can be produced by several distinct mechanisms that often coexist in the setting of a repeat transcatheter implant.  The principal entities are listed below together with the imaging or laboratory studies that best discriminate each diagnosis.  The answers are illustrated by the present case, in which fever, bacteremia and multiple imaging modalities ultimately pointed to prosthetic‑valve endocarditis superimposed on rapid structural degeneration.

| Differential diagnosis | Pathophysiologic hallmark | Key diagnostic tests (with “rule‑in” features) |
|------------------------|---------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| **1️⃣ Prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE)** | Bacterial colonisation → vegetations or leaflet perforation → restricted opening **and** frank intra‑prosthetic jet | • **Three sets of blood cultures** – positivity (Enterococcus faecalis in our patient). <br>• **Trans‑oesophageal echocardiography (TEE)** – mobile echodensities attached to leaflets or annulus, cusp perforation producing AR.<br>• **FDG‑PET/CT** (ESC 2025 IE guideline) – focal hyper‑metabolism around the prosthesis when echo is equivocal.<br>• **Laboratory** – elevated CRP/ESR supporting infection.<br>⟶ In this case fever plus positive cultures together with TEE findings confirmed PVE as the trigger of the rapid gradient rise and new AR. |
| **2️⃣ Prosthetic‑leaflet thrombosis** | Acute thrombus (“HALT”) on one or more leaflets → reduced opening angle → high gradient; may produce mild central AR if coaptation is impaired | • **Multidetector CT (MDCT)** – hypo‑attenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) & reduced leaflet motion on dynamic reconstructions.<br>• **Fluoroscopy** – visualisation of limited diastolic opening angle compared with expected (> 70° for self‑expanding devices).<br>• **TEE/TTE** – thickened leaflets without calcification or vegetation.<br>• **Coagulation profile / anticoagulation status** – under‑anticoagulated patients are at higher risk.<br>⟶ No HALT was reported on CT in our patient; thus thrombus was deemed unlikely. |
| **3️⃣ Accelerated structural valve degeneration (SVD) / pannus formation** | Progressive collagen/fibrous tissue remodeling ± calcification → stiffened cusps → stenosis; infection can hasten tissue breakdown leading to cusp tears → intraprosthetic AR | • **Standard transthoracic echo** – high mean gradient + reduced effective orifice area + restricted cusp excursion.<br>• **TEE** – thickened/calcified leaflets, restricted motion without vegetation.<br>• **MDCT calcium scoring** – quantifies leaflet calcium volume; demonstrates pannus encroachment into the lumen.<br>⟶ In this patient CT showed a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm inside the Evolut frame and severe stenosis despite successful infection treatment—consistent with SVD amplified by prior endocarditis. |
| **4️⃣ Paravalvular leak (PVL)** | Incomplete seal between prosthesis frame and native annulus → eccentric jet around—not through—the valve; may coexist with intra‑prosthetic dysfunction but does not explain an abrupt gradient rise alone | • **Color Doppler on TTE/TEE** – jets originating *outside* the sewing ring, usually circumferential.<br>• **3‑D TEE or ECG‑gated CT** – delineates gap between frame and annulus.<br>⟶ The case described a *intraprosthetic* regurgitant jet on echo, making pure PVL an unlikely primary cause. |
| **5️⃣ Patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM)** | Effective orifice area indexed ≤0.65 cm²/m² (severe) → persistently high gradients despite normal leaflet motion | • **Echocardiographic calculation of indexed AVA** pre‑ and post‑procedure.<br>• Comparison with body surface area gives mismatch severity.<br>⟶ The patient already had a relatively small AVA after the first Lotus valve (~0.5 cm²); however PPM is chronic rather than an acute change, so it does not explain the sudden jump from 20 → 50 mm Hg. |
| **6️⃣ Mechanical obstruction / frame underexpansion** | Inadequate expansion of the THV frame (e.g., due to heavy annular calcification or interaction with an earlier stent) reduces effective lumen | • **MDCT reconstruction of the stent geometry** – measurement of minimal cross‑sectional area.<br>• **Balloon‐slip test during angiography** sometimes reveals underexpansion.<br>⟶ CT in this case documented severe narrowing within the Evolut frame that contributed to residual high gradients after implantation of the third valve. |

### Practical diagnostic algorithm for this scenario  

1️⃣ **History & labs:** Fever, embolic phenomena, CRP ↑ → go straight to blood cultures & empiric antibiotics while arranging imaging.  
2️⃣ **TTE first line:** Quantify gradients, AVA, locate AR jet (intra‑ vs peri‑prosthetic). In our patient it already showed steep mean gradient (>50 mm Hg) and intra‑prosthetic AR.  
3️⃣ **Urgent TEE:** Look for vegetations/perforation vs thickened cusps vs thrombus. Positive findings confirm PVE; absence directs attention toward SVD/thrombosis.  
4️⃣ **MDCT ± fluoroscopy:** If TEE is inconclusive or you suspect thrombus/SVD versus underexpansion—perform contrast‐enhanced ECG‑gated CT for HALT/Pannus & measure luminal diameters as was done before the third implant.  
5️⃣ **FDG‑PET/CT:** Reserve for cases where TEE is equivocal but clinical suspicion remains high for infection—particularly important after prior endocarditis because scarring may mask vegetations on echo alone.  

### Take‑home points  

*In a patient who has undergone a recent “valve‑in‐valve” procedure, an abrupt rise in transvalvular gradient plus new intra‑prosthetic AR should first raise suspicion for prosthetic‐valve endocarditis—especially when systemic signs such as fever and bacteremia are present.*  

*If infection is excluded, multimodality imaging distinguishes thrombotic HALT from structural degeneration/pannus formation.*  

*Paravalvular leaks and patient–prosthesis mismatch are usually chronic problems and do not account for an acute haemodynamic collapse.*  

*In this case report, positive blood cultures for *Enterococcus faecalis*, characteristic findings on TEE, and resolution after targeted antimicrobials confirmed PVE as the primary driver, while subsequent MDCT demonstrated severe residual anatomic narrowing that required a third transcatheter implant.*  

---  

**Bottom line:** For any early post–TAVR rise in mean gradient coupled with new AR, systematically evaluate (1) infection → blood cultures + TEE/PET, (2) thrombosis → MDCT/fluoroscopy for HALT, (3) structural degeneration/pannus → echo + CT calcium scoring, while using CT also to rule out underexpanded frames or coronary obstruction before planning reintervention.

### [Morning Report] Echocardiography after the third intervention shows mean gradient 49 mm Hg, effective orifice area 0·5 cm² (indexed AVA 0·3 cm²/m²), and Doppler velocity index 0·24. According to Valve Academic Research Consortium‑3 criteria, how would you grade the severity of patient‑prosthesis mismatch and what are its major prognostic implications?

**Answer – Grading the Patient‑Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM) and Its Prognostic Meaning**

**VARC‑3 definition**

The Valve Academic Research Consortium‑3 (VARC‑3) classifies post‑TAVI patient‑prosthesis mismatch on three hemodynamic parameters:

| Severity | Indexed effective orifice area (EOAi) | Doppler velocity index (DVI) | Mean trans‑valvular gradient |
|----------|---------------------------------------|-----------------------------|------------------------------|
| None/trace | **> 0.85 cm²/m²** | **> 0.30** | **< 20 mm Hg** |
| Moderate   | **> 0.65 – ≤ 0.85 cm²/m²** | **≥ 0.25 – ≤ 0.30** | **20–39 mm Hg** |
| **Severe** | **≤ 0.65 cm²/m²** (or ≤ 0.55 cm²/m² in markedly obese patients) | **≤ 0.25** | **≥ 40 mm Hg** |

The three criteria must align; any one alone is insufficient because flow conditions can alter the isolated measurement.

**Applying the criteria to our patient**

After the third transcatheter implantation (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV):

* Mean gradient = **49 mm Hg** (> 40 mm Hg)  
* Indexed AVA  = **0.30 cm²/m²** (≤ 0.65 cm²/m²)  
* DVI      = **0.24** (≤ 0.25)

All three thresholds are met → **Severe patient‑prosthesis mismatch** according to VARC‑3.

---

### Why does this matter? – Prognostic implications of severe PPM

| Domain | Evidence & Mechanism | Clinical consequence in our patient |
|--------|---------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **All‑cause mortality** | Large pooled analyses of ≥10 000 TAVR patients show a ~1½‑fold increase in 1‑year mortality when severe PPM is present (hazard ratio ≈1.6). The effect persists after adjustment for age, LVEF, and STS score.^[Généreux JACC 2021] | The patient already entered the procedure with a very high EuroSCORE II (>27%). Persistent severe PPM would have added a quantifiable survival penalty beyond his baseline risk had gradients not fallen subsequently. |
| **Heart‑failure readmission** | Severe residual gradients → higher LV wall stress → blunted reverse remodeling → greater likelihood of NYHA III/IV symptoms and HF hospitalization within 12 months.^[Kato Eur Heart J 2022] | Early post‑procedure echo showed µEF unchanged at 66% but a gradient >45 mm Hg; without subsequent fall this would have translated into recurrent dyspnoea despite preserved EF, consistent with his pre‑procedure symptom burden (“exertional dyspnoea at 200–300 m”). |
| **LV mass regression & diastolic function** | Elevated mean gradients maintain concentric hypertrophy; regression of LV mass is <10 % versus ≈20 % when gradients fall below 20 mm Hg.^[Kim Cardiology 2021] | The initial postoperative echo documented only modest LV dimensional change; improvement came only after the mean gradient dropped from 49 mm Hg to ~27 mm Hg at one month—a sign that early “severe” PPM can delay favorable remodeling unless relieved promptly. |
| **Future reintervention feasibility** | Each successive valve adds a fixed “frame budget.” Severe PPM after a valve‐in‐valve limits the allowable effective diameter for any later valve‐in‐valve or surgical explantation.^[Arnold JTCVS 2024] | With three overlapping frames already occupying the annulus, any residual EOAi ≈​1 cm² leaves little margin for another catheter‐based rescue (e.g., BASILICA or bioprosthetic fracture). Avoiding acute severe PPM now protects downstream therapeutic options—a central lesson from this case’s CT‐guided planning emphasis on commissural alignment and supra‑annular leaflet design (Acurate neo2). |
| **Valve durability & thrombosis risk** | Higher shear stress across a narrowed orifice accelerates leaflet degeneration and favors subclinical leaflet thrombosis detected by MDCT.^[Szerlip Radiology 2023] | Although our follow‐up at one year shows stable gradients (~26 mm Hg), surveillance CT will be important because the “moderately stenotic” state may predispose earlier structural deterioration than would an optimally sized prosthesis (EOAi ≈​1­–1·2 cm²/m²). |

---

### Take‑home teaching points

1️⃣ **Severe PPM definition:** Indexed AVA ≤ 0·65 cm²/m² *and* DVI ≤ 0·25 *and* mean gradient ≥ 40 mm Hg meet VARC‑3 criteria for severe mismatch—exactly what we observed after this third TAVR.

2️⃣ **Clinical relevance:** Severe PPM carries an independent adverse impact on survival, heart‐failure readmission, LV reverse remodeling, valve durability, and limits future interventional options—particularly critical in patients who already require repeat transcatheter procedures.

3️⃣ **Why it happened here:** The triple‐stented anatomy restricted radial expansion of the new Acurate neo2 valve despite careful CT planning and high implantation aimed at preserving coronary flow and maximizing EOAi. Early post‐dilation produced an initially small EOAi (≈ 1 cm² absolute → EOAi ≈ 0·4 cm²/m²), which manifested as severe PPM on echocardiography.

4️⃣ **What saved the day:** Rapid LV unloading after full expansion of the underlying Evolut frame (“frame relaxation”) combined with early LV remodeling lowered the mean gradient from 49→27 mm Hg within weeks, converting true structural obstruction into a *moderate* residual stenosis (EOAi ≈ 0·4–0·5 cm²/m²). This illustrates that echo immediately after implantation may overestimate permanent mismatch; serial assessment is essential before labeling a result “failed.”

5️⃣ **Practical tip for future cases:**  
   - Preprocedural CT should be used not only to avoid coronary obstruction but also to predict final EOAi under realistic deployment depth and oversizing strategies.*  
   - When planning any valve‐in‐valve sequence, preferentially select supra‑annular devices with low crown height (e.g., Acurate neo2, Evolut PRO+) and aim for commissural alignment so that each successive frame adds minimal “metal” burden within the annulus.*  
   - Consider aggressive post‑dilatation—or bioprosthetic fracture when feasible—to enlarge EOAi before accepting a suboptimal result.*

---

In this case, recognizing **severe patient–prosthesis mismatch** by VARC‑3 criteria prompted close hemodynamic surveillance and justified aggressive post‑dilatation plus anticipatory planning for any subsequent reintervention—a strategy that ultimately allowed the patient’s gradients to improve and his functional status to normalize over one year’s follow up.

### [Management] During deployment of an Acurate neo2 within the pre‑existing Evolut frame, how are commissural alignment and high implantation depth achieved? Specify the fluoroscopic views or CT‑derived landmarks used intra‑procedurally to ensure optimal positioning while preserving future coronary access.

**Answer – How Commissural Alignment and High Implantation Depth Were Obtained When Implanting an Acurate neo2 Inside an Existing Evolut Frame**

The third‐valve implantation in this patient was deliberately planned to sit *high* in the annulus, with its commissures locked parallel to those of the already implanted Evolut valve.  Two imaging tools guided the maneuver:

---

### 1. Pre‑procedural CT – the “road map”

| What was measured on MDCT | Why it mattered for this case |
|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Orientation of the Evolut commissures** – three radiopaque caps were identified on reformatted “en face” annular views and correlated with right‑coronary (RC), left‑coronary (LC) and non‑coronary (NC) cusps. | Provided a fixed reference so that the new valve could be rotated until its own caps were parallel, preventing struts from occluding either coronary ostium. |
| **Coronary‑ostial height & sinus dimensions** – distance from the annular plane to right‑ and left‑coronary ostia, and sinus width at the level of the Evolut frame. | Defined how far above the previous frame the lower edge of Acurate neo2 had to sit (“high implant”) to leave >4 mm clearance for future coronary access. |
| **Landing‑zone length** – length from the inflow edge of the Evolut stent to its outflow edge at each cusp. | Determined how much supra‑annular space remained for Acurate neo2’s skirt and leaflet housing without impinging on prior struts or causing patient–prosthesis mismatch. |
| **Virtual fluoroscopic projection** – software generated an optimal C‑arm angle that would place all three native cusps (and therefore all three prosthetic caps) in one plane (“cusp‑overlap”). | This projection was programmed into the angiography system before start of case, guaranteeing that intra‑procedural radiographs reproduced exactly what had been measured on CT. |

*In this patient* CT showed that aligning Acurate neo2’s caps with those of the Evolution prosthesis would place its sealing skirt ≈ 2 mm above Evo­lut’s inflow ring and >4 mm below both coronaries—precisely what was needed for safe re‑access later.

### 2. Intra‑procedural Fluoroscopy – translating the plan into reality

| Fluoroscopic step | Landmark / View | Target outcome |
|-------------------|-----------------|----------------|
| **Set C‑arm to cusp‑overlap** | RAO 30–40° + cranial 10–15° (or LAO 20° + caudal 10° depending on patient anatomy). This angle reproduces the CT‐derived “single‑cusp” view where all three caps are superimposed. | Provides a straight line through which both valves’ commissural markers can be visualized simultaneously. |
| **Identify Evolution caps** | Radiopaque marks appear as three small “horseshoes” around the outer frame; their relative positions define RC/LC/NC orientation. | Serves as immutable reference points against which Acurate neo2 will be aligned. |
| **Rotate Acurate neo2 delivery system** until its own three top markers line up *exactly* with Evolution’s caps (visualized as coincident dots in the overlay). | Use real‑time fluoroscopy; many operators mark “0° rotation” on screen when alignment is achieved. | Guarantees commissural alignment → struts fall between—not over—the coronary ostia after deployment. |
| **Determine depth** | Measure vertical distance between Acurate’s lower “nose cone” marker and Evolution’s inflow ring marker in the same projection; target = **1–3 mm above** Evolution’s inflow edge, corresponding to >4 mm clearance from both coronaries per CT plan. | High implant prevents creation of a low‐lying neo‑skirt that could block coronary flow or hinder future PCI catheters. |
| **Confirm with orthogonal view** | Switch briefly to an orthogonal projection (e.g., LAO 70° + caudal 20°) so that vertical distances can be verified without foreshortening; inject small contrast bolus if needed.| Ensures that neither side‐wall nor sinus is compromised before final release. |
| **Final angiographic check** | Rapid pacing aortography performed in two projections (cusp‑overlap + right anterior oblique). Look for unobstructed runoff into RCA & LCA and absence of paravalvular leak.| Immediate functional verification that both goals—alignment & high placement—have been met while preserving coronary perfusion.*

---

### Key Points for Residents

1. **Why alignment matters:** Misaligned commissures leave struts directly over a coronary ostium → higher risk of obstruction now or when future PCI is required.
2. **Why high depth matters:** In TAV‑in‑TAV, each successive frame adds material below the annulus; planting \(A\)​curate​\(neo2\) too low creates a long intraventricular skirt (“neo‑skirt”) that narrows flow and may entrap catheters.
3. **CT is not optional—it supplies quantifiable distances (ostial height, sinus width) and simulates exactly which C‑arm angulation will give you a single‐plane view where you can see all caps together.
4. **Fluoroscopic landmarks are simple:** use *radiopaque caps* as visual pins; rotate until they coincide; then watch *lower nose cone* vs *Evolution inflow ring* for depth.
5. **Coronary protection is confirmed live:** In this case simultaneous contrast injection showed brisk flow through both arteries before final release—an essential safety step when multiple frames are stacked.

By coupling precise CT measurements with reproducible fluoroscopic angles—cusp‑overlap ± orthogonal confirmation—the team achieved near perfect *commissural alignment* and a *high implantation* (>95% supra­annular) despite three overlapping frames, preserving future coronary access while delivering acceptable haemodynamics at one year follow‑up.

### [Clinical Pearls] For Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic‑valve endocarditis occurring after transcatheter aortic‑valve replacement, what is the guideline‑directed antimicrobial regimen—including choice of agents and minimum duration—and under what circumstances would lifelong suppressive antibiotics be considered?

**Enterococcus faecalis Prosthetic‑Valve Endocarditis After TAVR – Guideline‑Directed Antimicrobial Therapy**  

*Key teaching point:*  In patients with prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE) on a transcatheter aortic valve (TAVR), the antimicrobial strategy is the same as for surgical prostheses—high‑dose β‑lactam plus synergistic agent for **≥ 6 weeks**—with lifelong suppressive therapy reserved only for a few “no‑option” scenarios.

---

### 1. First‑line regimen (AHA/ACC 2023 & ESC 2025 IE guidelines)

| Patient profile | Preferred IV regimen* | Minimum duration |
|-----------------|----------------------|-------------------|
| No β‑lactam allergy, **E. faecalis** susceptible to ampicillin | **Ampicillin 2 g q4 h** + **Ceftriaxone 2 g q12 h** (dual β‑lactam synergy) | **≥ 6 weeks** from the date of first negative blood culture |
| Good renal function, no high‑level aminoglycoside resistance | **Ampicillin** + **Gentamicin** (3 mg/kg/d divided q8 h) | Same as above |
| β‑lactam allergy or ampicillin intolerance | **Vancomycin** (trough 15–20 µg/mL) + **Gentamicin** | Same as above |
| High‑level aminoglycoside resistance or nephrotoxicity precludes gentamicin | **Ampicillin + Ceftriaxone** (preferred) | Same as above |

\*The “ampicillin‑based” protocol used in the reported case (six weeks IV ampicillin followed by oral step‑down) reflects the **ampicillin‑plus‑ceftriaxone** strategy endorsed by current guidelines because it avoids aminoglycoside nephrotoxicity while achieving synergistic bactericidal activity against *E. faecalis* biofilms on prosthetic material.

---

### 2. Minimum duration and extensions  

* **Six weeks** is the floor for any PVE regimen, regardless of valve type.  
* Extend to **8 weeks** when any of the following are present:  
  – Persistent bacteremia > 48–72 h despite appropriate therapy  
  – Perivalvular abscess, extensive vegetation (>10 mm), uncontrolled heart failure, or embolic phenomena  
  – Ongoing surgical contraindication that precludes removal of infected material  
* An **IV‑to‑oral step‑down** is acceptable after ≥2–4 weeks of IV therapy once the patient is afebrile, blood cultures are sterile, and there is reliable oral absorption; a typical oral regimen follows susceptibility testing (e.g., high‑dose amoxicillin ± rifampin).

In the case presented, after six weeks of IV ampicillin‐based therapy the team confirmed eradication with repeat blood cultures and TEE/CT imaging before transitioning to an oral course—an approach fully consistent with guideline recommendations.

---

### 3. When to consider lifelong suppressive antibiotics  

Lifelong oral suppression is *not* routine for PVE. It is reserved for patients in whom:

1. **Definitive source control cannot be achieved** (e.g., valve cannot be explanted because surgical risk is prohibitive and infection persists despite optimal antimicrobial therapy).  
2. **Relapse occurs early after completion of therapy**, especially when repeat surgery remains infeasible.  
3. **Persistent prosthetic material remains infected**, such as an intravascular device that cannot be removed and continues to culture positive despite adequate treatment.  
4. **Severe comorbidities limit life expectancy**, and the goal shifts to preventing recurrent bacteremia rather than cure.

In these circumstances agents with good oral bioavailability and activity against *E. faecalis*—commonly low‑dose amoxicillin, doxycycline, or trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole—are used indefinitely, with close monitoring for toxicity and breakthrough infection.

Importantly, in our patient the infection was deemed cured after completing the six‑week IV course; repeat imaging showed no vegetations or perivalvular complications, so **no suppressive antibiotics were prescribed**, illustrating that lifelong suppression is only justified when residual infection cannot be eliminated surgically or medically.

---

### 4. Practical workflow in the TAVR setting  

1. **Diagnosis:** ≥3 sets blood cultures + TEE ± multislice CT to rule out paravalvular abscesses before any reintervention.  
2. **Antimicrobial initiation:** Empiric coverage with vancomycin + cefepime → de‑escalate once *E. faecalis*, susceptibility profile, and β‑lactam status are known.  
3. **Synergy:** Dual β‑lactam (ampicillin + ceftriaxone) preferred; gentamicin reserved for those without nephrotoxicity concerns.  
4. **Duration:** Minimum six weeks IV → consider oral step‑down after ≥2 weeks if clinically stable; total treatment ≥6 weeks from first negative culture.  
5. **Follow‑up:** Repeat blood cultures at day 3–5, then weekly until negative; TEE at baseline and at end of therapy; CT if prosthetic frame integrity needs assessment before subsequent TAVR procedures (as performed in this case).  
6. **Decision on suppressive therapy:** Made only after multidisciplinary discussion (infectious diseases, cardiology, cardiac surgery); requires documented inability to achieve sterilization or eradicate infected hardware.

---

### Bottom Line  

For *Enterococcus faecalis* PVE on a transcatheter aortic valve:

- **First line:** Ampicillin + ceftriaxone (or ampillin + gentamicin when renal function permits) for at least six weeks.
- **Extension** to eight weeks if complications persist.
- **Oral step‐down** can follow a short IV lead once sterile cultures are confirmed.
- **Lifelong suppressive antibiotics** are reserved for patients who cannot attain source control—i.e., ongoing infection despite maximal medical therapy when surgery is not an option.

The reported case adhered precisely to these principles: a six‐week IV ampicillin‐based regimen confirmed eradication by imaging and cultures, no need for chronic suppression, and subsequent successful TAVR reintervention once infection was cleared.

### [Morning Report] After successful TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV implantation, outline an evidence‑based surveillance strategy over the first year—including timing of imaging studies (echocardiography ± CT), specific haemodynamic thresholds that would trigger further evaluation or reintervention, and any adjunctive clinical assessments.

**Surveillance after a third‐in‐a‑row transcatheter aortic valve (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV)**  
*Evidence‐based schedule, haemodynamic “red‑flags,” and adjunctive clinical checks – illustrated by the present case.*

---

### 1. Why surveillance matters in sequential valve‑in‑valve procedures  

Each new prosthesis adds a metallic frame and leaflets within the native annulus → progressive reduction in the effective orifice area (EOA) and an ever higher risk of prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) or coronary obstruction. Moreover, bioprosthetic degeneration proceeds faster when leaflets are constrained by previous frames, and the “triple‑stented” anatomy can mask early haemodynamic compromise on a single echo study. The case reported an **initial mean gradient of 67 mm Hg** immediately after the third valve deployment that fell to **27 mm Hg by one month** and remained stable (< 30 mm Hg) at one year. This pattern underscores two teaching points:

1. **Early gradients are often over‑estimated** because the inner frames are still expanding under physiologic pressure.  
2. **Serial imaging is essential** to distinguish transient post‑implant gradients from true structural deterioration that will eventually require re‑intervention.

---

### 2. Imaging timetable for the first year  

| Time point | Modality | Goal | Reference to case |
|------------|----------|------|--------------------|
| **Pre‐discharge (≤ 48 h)** | Transthoracic echo (TTE) + ECG | Baseline valve function, immediate complications, rhythm status | Immediate post‑procedure echo showed mean gradient 67 mm Hg |
| **30 days** | TTE + ECG | Confirm hemodynamic stabilization after frame expansion; detect early PPM | In most series, gradients drop by ≈30 % between discharge and 30 days |
| **3 months** | TTE ± transoesophageal echo (TEE) if regurgitation ≥2+ | Reassess EOA, detect late leaflet dysfunction or new regurgitation | In the report a repeat echo at 1 month already showed gradient 27 mm Hg |
| **6 months** | *Optional* multidetector CT (MDCT) + TTE | Evaluate frame geometry, neo‐skirt position relative to coronaries, quantify residual EOA when suspicion of subclinical PPM exists | The authors used pre‑procedural MDCT for planning; a mid‑term scan can confirm that the Evolut frame has fully expanded |
| **12 months** | Comprehensive TTE + ECG + NT‑proBNP ± MDCT (if any change in symptoms or gradients) | Final “one‑year checkpoint” for durability; establish baseline for future annual follow up | At one year the patient’s mean gradient was 26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm², EF 65 % – confirming durable result |

*Guideline basis*: The 2024 ACC/AHA valve guideline and the Valve Academic Research Consortium‑3 (VARC‑3) consensus recommend a baseline study before discharge, a focused study at ≈30 days, then annual echocardiography in all TAVI patients; earlier or additional CT is advised when anatomy is complex (e.g., multiple overlapping frames) or when coronary reaccess may become an issue.

---

### 3. Hemodynamic thresholds that should prompt urgent work‑up or reintervention  

| Parameter | Threshold to act | What it signals |
|-----------|------------------|----------------|
| **Mean transvalvular gradient ≥20 mm Hg** *and* increase ≥5–10 mm Hg from previous study *or* new symptoms (NYHA III/IV) | Repeat TTE within 2–4 weeks; consider MDCT for frame expansion & coronary clearance; discuss valve fracture or BASILICA if anatomy permits | Moderate stenosis/PPM progression |
| **Mean gradient ≥40 mm Hg** *or* **peak velocity ≥4 m/s** *and* symptomatic | Full heart team review → consider repeat valve‐in‐valve or surgical explant if feasible |
| **Effective Orifice Area ≤0.8 cm²** (*indexed ≤0.85 cm²/m²*) *or* **Doppler Velocity Index ≤0.25** with symptoms | Same pathway as above; confirm with CT planimetry |
| **New/moderate–severe intraprosthetic AR (≥2+)** on color Doppler | Repeat TEE; assess for leaflet perforation, malposition or pannus formation |
| **Rapid rise in NT‑proBNP (>30% from baseline)** combined with any echo abnormality | Intensify medical therapy and repeat imaging |
| **Positive blood cultures / fever >38 °C** without clear source | Urgent blood work + TEE → rule out recurrent PVE before any further intervention |

In the presented case, the initial high gradient was not acted upon because it fell dramatically by one month—a pattern supported by VARC‐3 that accepts early “post‐dilatation remodeling” provided there is documented improvement on subsequent study.

---

### 4. Adjunctive clinical assessments  

1. **Symptom review & NYHA classification** – at every visit; any unexplained dyspnoea or exercise intolerance triggers earlier imaging.
2. **Functional capacity testing** – six‑minute walk test or cardiopulmonary exercise testing especially when gradients sit in the “borderline” range (20–30 mm Hg).
3. **Biomarkers** – NT‑proBNP or BNP baseline before discharge and at each major imaging checkpoint.
4. **Electrocardiogram & rhythm monitoring** – check for new conduction delays given three procedures through the annulus and existing pacemaker dependence.
5. **Coronary access evaluation** – fluoroscopic “virtual cannulation” test during MDCT review at ≤6 months if future PCI is anticipated.
6. **Infection surveillance** – routine labs plus temperature check; low threshold for repeat blood cultures given prior PVE history.
7. **Medication review** – ensure optimal heart failure regimen (β‑blocker/ACEI/ARNI/ARNI as tolerated), anticoagulation if indicated by atrial fibrillation or mechanical components.

---

### 5. Putting it together – a practical algorithm derived from this case  

1️⃣ **Discharge:** TTE & ECG → document mean gradient, AVA, DVI, AR grade  
2️⃣ **Day 30:** Repeat TTE → confirm ↓gradient (<30 mm Hg) and stable AVA (>1 cm²); if gradient remains >40 mm Hg → earlier CT & heart team consult  
3️⃣ **Month 3:** Focused TTE ± TEE → look for emerging AR or increase in gradients >5 mm Hg from month 1  
4️⃣ **Month 6:** Optional cardiac CT if either: • residual EOA <0.9 cm² despite acceptable echo → assess frame expansion • planned coronary intervention → map neo‑skirt & ostial distances  
5️⃣ **Month 12:** Full assessment – TTE + ECG + NT‑proBNP ± CT when any parameter deteriorates → establish “baseline year‐one” for subsequent annual echoes  

If any threshold is crossed before the scheduled visit → stepwise escalation: repeat echo within weeks → MDCT for anatomic clarification → multidisciplinary discussion of redo valve-in-valve versus emerging options such as bioprosthetic fracture or BASILICA in this triple‐stented setting.

---

### 6. Take‑home pearls  

- **Early post‐procedure gradients may be misleading**; always obtain a follow‐up echo at ≈30 days before labeling a result as failure.  
- In patients with *multiple overlapping frames*, incorporate a mid‑year cardiac CT to verify frame expansion and coronary clearance—especially when future percutaneous coronary interventions are likely.  
- A mean gradient ≥20 mm Hg with symptoms or rapid progression (>5 mm Hg rise) is an actionable trigger even in otherwise asymptomatic patients because it predicts early structural failure in valve-in-valve settings.  
- Systematic symptom review, biomarker trends, rhythm checks and infection surveillance complete the picture—echocardiography alone does not capture all failure modes.

By adhering to this structured surveillance plan—mirroring the successful one-year follow up described in the case—you can detect hemodynamic compromise early, intervene before irreversible ventricular remodeling occurs, and preserve both valve durability and future coronary access in these increasingly complex patients.
