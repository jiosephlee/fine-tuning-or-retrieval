Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment  

The patient was a man in his late‑70s who presented to the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center with progressive exertional dyspnoea that limited his walking distance to 200–300 m, a spastic sensation over the left side of his chest occurring during activity, intermittent dizziness and generalized fatigue. These complaints are classic “red‑flag” symptoms of evolving left‑ventricular outflow obstruction or regurgitant volume overload in the context of a previously implanted aortic bioprosthesis. In an elderly patient with multiple comorbidities, such symptoms raise immediate concern for prosthetic valve dysfunction, heart failure decompensation, or an acute coronary syndrome—especially given his extensive coronary artery disease history.

### Prior Cardiac Interventions  

Understanding this patient’s current presentation requires a concise chronology of his cardiac procedures:

| Year | Procedure | Device | Rationale |
|------|-----------|--------|-----------|
| **2009‑2014** | Multiple percutaneous coronary interventions (PCIs) involving the right coronary artery (2009), left anterior descending artery (2010 & 2012) and circumflex artery (2014) | Drug‑eluting stents | Treatment of three‑vessel coronary artery disease |
| **2014** | First transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) | 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) | Severe native aortic stenosis in a high‑risk surgical candidate |
| **May 2022** | Valve‑in‑valve TAVI (TAV‑in‑TAV) | 23 mm Evolut self‑expanding valve (Medtronic) | Structural degeneration of the Lotus prosthesis leading to severe bioprosthetic stenosis |
| **June 2024** | Planned third transcatheter intervention (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) | Acurate neo2 size S (self‑expanding) | Recurrent severe prosthetic dysfunction after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis |

The sequential nature of these interventions is pivotal because each added valve frame reduces the effective orifice area and alters coronary ostial geometry—issues that dominate procedural planning for repeat TAVI procedures.

### Comorbidities Influencing Management  

The patient’s past medical history comprised:

* **Arterial hypertension** – contributes to afterload elevation and may exacerbate symptoms of stenosis.  
* **Three‑vessel CAD with multiple stents** – limits surgical options because coronary revascularisation would require concomitant aortic root surgery in an already high‑risk setting.  
* **Third‑degree atrioventricular block with permanent pacemaker** – yields a paced ventricular rhythm on electrocardiogram; pacing rates affect Doppler gradients measured by echocardiography and must therefore be taken into account when interpreting mean transvalvular gradients.  
* **Type 2 diabetes mellitus** – raises infection risk and impacts long‑term survival.  
* **Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease** – adds perioperative respiratory risk, rendering general anaesthesia for open surgery less attractive.  
* **Recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022** – imposes constraints on antithrombotic strategies required after percutaneous coronary procedures or valve implantation.

Collectively, these comorbidities yielded an elevated EuroSCORE II (~27 %) and placed the patient firmly in the “prohibitive surgical risk” category, steering the heart team toward transcatheter solutions despite their increasing technical complexity.

### Physical Examination Findings  

Although the original report does not detail auscultatory findings, patients with severe prosthetic aortic stenosis typically exhibit a crescendo–decrescendo systolic murmur best heard at the right upper sternal border with radiation toward the carotids, sometimes accompanied by diminished second heart sound owing to reduced transvalvular flow. In contrast, significant intraprosthetic regurgitation often produces an early diastolic decrescendo murmur best heard along the left sternal border. Given this patient’s combination of severe stenosis (mean gradient up to 83 mm Hg before the second TAVI) plus grade 2–3 intra‑prosthetic regurgitation noted later, one would anticipate both systolic and diastolic components on examination alongside signs of left‐ventricular pressure overload such as a sustained apical impulse or subtle pulmonary crackles from back‑up congestion.

### Baseline Investigations  

#### Electrocardiogram  

The baseline ECG displayed a paced ventricular rhythm reflecting his permanent dual‑chamber pacemaker implanted for third‑degree AV block in 2014. A paced rhythm necessitates careful adjustment of pacing rates during echocardiographic Doppler acquisition because higher heart rates can artificially elevate measured gradients across the prosthesis.

#### Laboratory Studies  

During his September 2022 admission—when infectious endocarditis was suspected—laboratory tests revealed leukocytosis, elevated C‑reactive protein (CRP), and raised erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). Blood cultures grew *Enterococcus faecalis*, confirming prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). After completion of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin‐based therapy followed by oral consolidation, repeat inflammatory markers normalized, supporting infection resolution prior to consideration of another transcatheter intervention.

#### Transthoracic Echocardiography (TTE)  

Echocardiography served as the cornerstone imaging modality throughout this patient's course:

| Timepoint | Key Echo Findings |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **December 2021** *(post‐Lotus degeneration)* | Severe bioprosthetic stenosis: mean gradient **83 mm Hg**, calculated aortic valve area ≈0.5 cm²; left ventricular ejection fraction (**LVEF**) reduced from 54 % → **42 %** |
| **May 2022** *(after Evolut TAV‑in‑TAV)* | Immediate haemodynamic improvement: LVEF rose back to **54 %**, mean gradient fell dramatically (<20 mm Hg); no significant regurgitation noted initially |
| **September 2022** *(during PVE)* | Recurrent severe restenosis: mean gradient **35 mm Hg**, emergent intraprosthetic regurgitation grade 2–3 |
| **January 2024** *(pre‐third TAVI)* | Progressive obstruction: mean gradient **49 mm Hg**, moderate–to–severe intra‑prosthetic regurgitation; LVEF recovered further to **66 %**, likely reflecting relief from chronic pressure overload despite residual obstruction |
| **June 2024** *(immediate post‐third TAVI)* | Mean gradient initially high (**67 mm Hg**) due to residual pseudo‐stenosis from triple layering; DVI =0.24 indicating severe patient–prosthesis mismatch; however no residual AR |

These serial measurements illustrate two fundamental echocardiographic concepts:

1. **Mean Transvalvular Gradient:** Reflects both flow across the valve and orifice size; values >40–50 mmHg in bioprostheses typically denote severe stenosis according to Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 criteria.
2. **Continuity Equation–Derived Aortic Valve Area:** Utilises LV outflow tract diameter and velocity‐time integrals for accurate assessment when gradients may be flow dependent—a crucial tool when peripheral vascular disease limits accurate Doppler interpretation.

#### Evolution of Hemodynamics  

The patient’s hemodynamic trajectory mirrored his interventional timeline:

* After initial Lotus implantation he attained only modest relief until structural degeneration manifested as markedly elevated gradients (>80 mmHg).  
* The Evolut “valve‑in‑valve” reduced afterload sharply—the LVEF rebounded from 42 % back toward baseline (>50 %), confirming improved forward flow despite still elevated absolute gradients initially due to lingering obstruction from leaflets trapped within previous frames.  
* PVE introduced leaflet damage and infection-driven inflammation that accelerated stenosis progression while adding regurgitant volume—a “mixed” lesion yielding combined pressure overload (gradient ↑) plus volume overload (regurgitation).  
* Completion of antimicrobial therapy resolved systemic inflammation but structural degeneration persisted, culminating in mean gradients approaching 50 mmHg before his third intervention.

### Initial Differential Diagnosis  

When faced with exertional dyspnoea, chest discomfort and fatigue in a patient with multiple transcatheter valves, clinicians must weigh several possibilities:

1️⃣ **Prosthetic Valve Stenosis** – The most likely cause given dramatically rising transvalvular gradients on serial echocardiography; structural leaflet calcification or fibrosis reduces effective opening area.

2️⃣ **Prosthetic Valve Regurgitation** – Intraprothetic jet observed on Doppler suggests leaflet perforation or incomplete coaptation secondary either to degeneration or infective damage.

3️⃣ **Paravalvular Leak** – Though not explicitly visualized in this case’s imaging reports, it remains an important consideration whenever new murmurs emerge after valvular implantation; CT or transoesophageal echo would be employed if suspicion arose.

4️⃣ **Ischemic Etiology / New Coronary Event** – The patient’s extensive CAD could precipitate angina-like chest sensations (“spastic sensation”) independent from valvular pathology; however cardiac biomarkers were not mentioned, making valvular obstruction more compelling.

5️⃣ **Heart Failure from Non-Valve Causes** – Volume overload from COPD exacerbation or anemia from recurrent GI bleeding could contribute but would not explain rapidly rising gradients or echo-proven valvular narrowing.

Distinguishing among these entities relies heavily on integration of Doppler hemodynamics (gradients ≥40 mmHg suggest severe stenosis), structural imaging (TEE/CT for paravalvular defects), laboratory markers (CRP/ESR for infection), and clinical context (symptoms coherence).

---

#### Teaching Points  

* In patients with prior TAVI procedures, any new onset dyspnoea should prompt immediate echocardiographic evaluation because structural deterioration can occur earlier than anticipated—particularly when compounded by infection.
* Mean gradients are flow dependent; thus pacing rate or changes in stroke volume must be documented when interpreting serial measurements.
* The continuity equation remains essential for calculating effective orifice area when gradients are borderline or confounded by low flow states.
* A thorough history—including prior percutaneous coronary interventions—helps anticipate procedural challenges such as coronary ostial obstruction during repeat TAVI.
* Comorbidities that elevate surgical risk steer decision making toward percutaneous redo strategies even when technical complexity is high—highlighting why multidisciplinary heart team discussion is indispensable early in the evaluation process.

By methodically correlating symptoms, past interventions, comorbidity profile, physical findings, ECG pattern, laboratory results, and serial echocardiography data, clinicians were able to pinpoint severe prosthetic valve dysfunction as the primary driver of this patient’s clinical decline—a pivotal step that paved the way for planning his third transcatheter intervention.

## Pathophysiology of Valve Degeneration and Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis in Transcatheter Devices  

Understanding why a transcatheter aortic valve prosthesis fails is essential for interpreting the clinical course of this patient and for planning repeat interventions. Failure can arise from two inter‑related processes: **structural valve deterioration (SVD)** that slowly compromises leaflet motion and **prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)** that accelerates structural damage through inflammation and bacterial invasion. The interplay of these mechanisms was evident after each successive implantation—first a Lotus™ valve, then an Evolut™ R/PRO™, and finally an Acurate neo2™—and informs the hemodynamic picture seen on echocardiography and computed tomography (CT).  

### Design Features of the Transcatheter Valves Used  

**Lotus™ (Boston Scientific)** – The Lotus was an early-generation balloon‑expandable device with a **cobalt‑chromium alloy stent** that was fully retrievable before final release. Its leaflets were mounted **intra‑annularly**, i.e., within the radiopaque frame at the level of the native annulus. The intra‑annular location yields a relatively low profile but limits the effective orifice area because the leaflets must open through a smaller circular opening defined by the prosthetic ring. The frame’s dense mesh provides strong radial force, which is useful for sealing calcified native annuli but also concentrates stress on the leaflets.  

**Evolut™ R/PRO™ (Medtronic)** – The Evolut family is a **self‑expanding nitinol** platform designed for **supra‑annular leaflet** positioning. The nitinol frame expands continuously after deployment, conforming to the aortic root geometry while preserving a larger flow area above the annulus where the leaflets are seated. This design generally yields lower transvalvular gradients compared with intra‑annular devices because blood passes through a wider supra‑annular orifice. The Evolut’s “repositionable” version includes markers that assist alignment of its three commissural posts relative to the native sinuses—a feature used in this case to minimize coronary obstruction risk.  

**Acurate neo2™ (Boston Scientific)** – The neo2 is another self‑expanding nitinol system, but its frame has a **shorter height** and an “open‐cell” architecture that facilitates **commissural alignment** and future coronary access. Like Evolut, its leaflets sit **supra‑annularly**, giving a relatively large effective orifice area even in narrow anatomies. The neo2 also incorporates pericardial tissue treated with anti‑calcification processes intended to extend durability. Its low-profile cage allows deployment within previously placed prostheses without excessive stack height—a critical consideration when three stent frames become superimposed.  

These distinct designs explain why each successive valve could be accommodated within the same annulus yet produced different hemodynamic profiles. Importantly, when multiple frames overlap, each new device exerts **radial pressure on the earlier prosthesis**, producing repeated cyclic stress on previously implanted leaflets.  

### Structural Valve Deterioration (SVD) in Transcatheter Bioprostheses  

SVD refers to any intrinsic loss of prosthetic leaflet integrity that impairs function, and it evolves through several histopathologic steps:

1. **Calcific stiffening** – Calcium phosphate deposits infiltrate the collagen matrix of pericardial tissue, rendering leaflets less compliant. In transcatheter valves, calcium deposition may be accelerated by high implantation pressures or by residual calcium from the native annulus that is forced against the leaflet surface during expansion.  

2. **Collagen degeneration & fibrosis** – Over time, enzymatic remodeling (matrix metalloproteinases) degrades collagen fibers while fibroblasts lay down disorganized extracellular matrix, producing thickened “fibrotic” leaflets that open sluggishly. Metabolic derangements common in older patients—hyperlipidaemia, diabetes—and chronic low‑grade inflammation promote this process.  

3. **Leaflet tearing or perforation** – Repeated cyclic bending produces microcracks that propagate with each cardiac cycle; eventual rupture leads to acute regurgitation. In patients with overlapping frames—as in this case—each extra frame increases the curvature imposed on underlying leaflets during systole, augmenting mechanical fatigue.

When two or more transcatheter devices occupy the same segment, **radial stress is transmitted through successive nitinol or cobalt–chromium cages** into every layer of tissue beneath them. This "sandwich effect" creates focal points of excess compression where leaflets are pinched between outer frames (e.g., between Evolut and Acurate neo2). Such focal compression hastens collagen fiber disruption and accelerates calcification at contact zones—phenomena observed on CT as reduced minimum lumen diameters (13.7 mm in this patient’s Evolut frame).  

### Hemodynamic Consequences of Combined Severe Stenosis and Intraprosthetic Regurgitation  

When SVD progresses toward severe stenosis, **the effective orifice area falls below 0·6 cm²**, substantially raising flow velocity across the valve. Doppler measurements then reveal mean gradients >40 mm Hg—a threshold met by this patient after each degeneration episode (83 mm Hg before TAV‑in‑TAV, 49 mm Hg before third implantation). Simultaneously, **intraprosthetic regurgitation** develops when coaptation is compromised either by thickened leaflets or by vegetations that create gaps at the closing line (“para‑leaflet leakage”). The coexistence of high gradients and regurgitation imposes a double load on the left ventricle: pressure overload from stenosis plus volume overload from regurgitation → progressive decline in ejection fraction (EF fell from 54 % pre‑Lotus to 42 % before the first redo TAVI). Restoration of forward flow after each intervention improves EF transiently; however, persistent structural compromise inevitably recurs if underlying degradation continues unchecked.

### Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE) After TAVI  

PVE occurs when microorganisms colonise prosthetic material after hospital discharge (>60 days post‐procedure). In TAVI patients three pathways are most relevant:

* **Entry via large-bore vascular access** – The femoral approach necessitates sheath sizes up to 24–28 Fr; any breach in sterility can seed bacteria along the catheter tract into the bloodstream.
* **Seeding from indwelling hardware** – Permanent pacemaker leads traverse venous circulation into right heart chambers; bacteria adherent to lead surfaces may embolise systemically.
* **Hematogenous seeding from distant infections** – Gastrointestinal bleeding episodes or urinary tract infections provide portals for transient bacteremia.

Enterococcus faecalis was isolated in this patient’s blood cultures—a classic cause of subacute endocarditis in elderly males with genitourinary or gastrointestinal pathology. Its pathogenicity derives from several virulence factors:

* **Surface adhesins (e.g., Esp)** that bind fibrinogen and extracellular matrix proteins deposited on foreign material.
* **Aggregation substance** promoting interbacterial clumping within thrombi on prosthetic surfaces.
* **Biofilm formation**, which protects colonies from host immune defenses and antibiotics,
* Intrinsic low-level resistance to many β‑lactams necessitating prolonged high-dose ampicillin therapy combined with synergistic agents when needed.

These traits enable Enterococcus species to colonise even well-endothelialised prostheses such as transcatheter valves whose metal frames provide scaffolding for fibrin deposition after deployment.

### Interaction Between Infection‑Related Inflammation and Accelerated SVD  

Infection adds an inflammatory milieu that dramatically speeds up structural deterioration:

1. **Cytokine‐mediated matrix remodeling** – Interleukin‑1β and tumor necrosis factor‑α upregulate matrix metalloproteinases within perivalvular tissue → accelerated collagen breakdown.
2. **Bacterial proteases** – Enterococci secrete gelatinases capable of degrading structural proteins within leaflets.
3. **Calcification promotion** – Inflammatory cells release oxidative species that act as nucleation points for calcium phosphate crystal formation.
4. **Mechanical tethering by vegetations** – Early vegetations act as “pseudoleaves” restricting motion of true leaflets; limited opening increases shear stress on already compromised tissue leading to earlier fibrosis.

Thus, after successful antimicrobial therapy eliminated active infection, residual inflammatory scarring left behind thickened yet still dysfunctional leaflets—accounting for the rapid progression observed three months post‐antibiotics when mean gradients rose from ~20 mm Hg back to >35 mm Hg despite prior successful TAV‐in‑TAV.

### Patient‑Specific Predisposing Factors for PVE  

The present patient harboured several recognized risk enhancers:

* **Repetitive large-bore femoral access with subsequent pseudoaneurysm** – Surgical repair creates additional scar tissue around arterial puncture sites; these loci serve as nidus for bacterial entry during bacteremia.
* **Permanent pacemaker lead crossing tricuspid valve** – While right-sided endocarditis is more typical for leads, hematogenous spread can involve left-sided structures via high-flow jet forces during systole.
* **Type 2 diabetes mellitus and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease** – Both conditions impair innate immunity and compromise mucosal barriers—increasing susceptibility to bacteremia originating from respiratory or urinary sources.
* **Repeated procedural trauma** – Each new catheter manipulation perturbs endothelial integrity within the aortic root; cumulative injury predisposes bacterial adherence once bacteremia ensues.
* **Prior gastrointestinal bleeding episodes** – Bleeding episodes often entail endoscopic interventions that themselves risk transient bacteremia with enteric flora such as Enterococcus faecalis.

Together these elements lowered the threshold for colonisation of newly placed prosthetic material after the second TAVI procedure.

### Why PVE Can Produce a Rapid Rise in Transvalvular Gradients  

In native infective endocarditis acute gradient elevation occurs when vegetations physically obstruct flow or when leaflet flexibility is lost due to edema/inflammation (“functional stenosis”). A similar mechanism operates in PVE:

* **Leaflet thickening & rigidity** caused by inflammatory infiltrates sharply reduces opening area even before gross calcification appears.
* **Vegetative mass protruding into LVOT** creates an additional physical barrier—in Doppler terms this appears as a narrowed effective orifice located upstream of the prosthesis (“pseudo‐stenosis”).
* **Rapid loss of coaptation leads to intraprosthetic regurgitation**, which augments systolic pressure gradients because part of stroke volume recirculates across an already narrowed aperture during ejection.
* Superimposed on pre‑existing SVD—the baseline effective orifice might already be <0·5 cm²—any further reduction translates into exponential gradient elevation according to Bernoulli’s equation (ΔP ∝ V²).

Consequently, once infection settled but structural damage persisted, even modest additional thickening produced mean gradients rising from 35 mm Hg at diagnosis to 49 mm Hg at one year later despite aggressive medical therapy—a pattern vividly captured on serial echocardiograms throughout this report.

---

In summary, structural deterioration of transcatheter bioprostheses proceeds through calcific stiffening, collagen degeneration, fibrotic remodeling and mechanical fatigue—all processes amplified by repeated radial stresses incurred when new valves are implanted inside existing frames. When prosthetic valve endocarditis supervenes—particularly with organisms such as Enterococcus faecalis capable of biofilm formation—the inflammatory cascade hastens leaflet failure and precipitates abrupt hemodynamic compromise manifested as steeply rising transvalvular gradients combined with intraprosthetic regurgitation. Recognising these intertwined pathophysiologic pathways is fundamental not only for diagnosing failure but also for guiding nuanced imaging assessments and procedural planning required for complex salvage strategies such as TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV procedures described herein.

## Imaging Modalities and Preprocedural Planning for Triple Valve‑in‑Valve Intervention  

A successful third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient who already carries two overlapping prostheses hinges on meticulous multimodality imaging. In the present case the imaging work‑up followed a stepwise algorithm that first confirmed prosthetic dysfunction, then excluded active infection, and finally provided three‑dimensional anatomic data needed to select an appropriately sized valve, assess coronary‑obstruction risk, and define an implantation strategy that would preserve future coronary access.  

### When imaging is indicated after suspected prosthetic failure  
Prosthetic valve dysfunction should be suspected whenever a patient who has undergone TAVI presents with new or worsening symptoms of heart failure, angina or syncope—particularly when these symptoms arise months to years after the initial procedure. In this case the patient’s exertional dyspnoea combined with a marked rise in trans‑valvular gradients on clinical follow‑up triggered a comprehensive echocardiographic reassessment. Current ESC/EACTS recommendations advise that any appreciable increase in mean gradient (>10 mm Hg) or development of new intraprosthetic regurgitation be investigated promptly because these findings often herald structural valve degeneration or leaflet pathology requiring re‑intervention.  

### Echocardiographic assessment  

**Doppler gradient and valve‐area calculation**  
Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) remains the first‑line tool for quantifying prosthetic stenosis. Continuous‑wave Doppler records the peak velocity across the valve; the mean pressure gradient is derived from the velocity‑time integral using the Bernoulli equation (ΔP = 4·V²). In our patient the mean gradient rose from 83 mm Hg after the first TAV‑in‑TAV procedure to 49 mm Hg at presentation for the third intervention—clearly meeting hemodynamic criteria for severe stenosis (mean gradient ≥ 40 mm Hg).  

The effective orifice area (EOA) is calculated by the continuity equation:  
\[
\text{EOA}= \frac{\text{LVOT area} \times \text{LVOT VTI}}{\text{Aortic valve VTI}}.
\]  
Accurate measurement of LVOT diameter (preferably by three‑dimensional planimetry) is crucial because even small errors are amplified in the squared term of area calculation. In this patient an EOA of 0.5 cm² confirmed severe obstruction despite preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF).  

**Intraprosthetic regurgitation**  
Colour‑flow Doppler permits semi‑quantitative grading of central or paravalvular leak using jet width, vena contracta, and circumferential extent relative to the annulus. The initial post‑Evolut study showed minimal regurgitation, whereas at baseline for the third procedure colour Doppler identified grade 2–3 intraprosthetic regurgitation consistent with leaflet malcoaptation secondary to degeneration and post‑infective scarring.  

**Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) before reintervention**  
Because this patient had recovered from prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), ruling out residual infection was mandatory prior to any repeat instrumentation—a principle endorsed by European infective endocarditis guidelines. TEE provides superior spatial resolution over TTE for detecting vegetations, peri‑valvular abscesses, or paravalvular fistulae that may be occult on transthoracic windows owing to acoustic shadowing from multiple stent frames. In our case TEE showed no vegetations or peri‑prosthetic fluid collections, allowing clinicians to proceed safely with another transcatheter approach rather than salvage surgery.  

### Computed tomography planning protocol for TAV‑in‑TAV procedures  

**Acquisition parameters**  
Multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) must be performed with ECG gating synchronized to diastole (~70–80 % R–R interval) to minimise motion artefact on both annular contours and stent struts. A high‐resolution contrast bolus timed to opacify the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), sinus of Valsalva, and ascending aorta enables accurate lumen delineation; slice thickness ≤0.5 mm with overlapping reconstruction provides isotropic voxels suitable for three‑dimensional modelling. In this patient an arterial phase acquisition captured both the native root anatomy and all three prosthetic frames without metal streak artefact interfering with measurements—a prerequisite when dealing with triple‐layered scaffolds.  

**Annular sizing within prior Evolut frame**  
The key dimension guiding valve choice is the minimum luminal diameter inside the existing transcatheter frame where the new device will seat (“neo‐annulus”). The MDCT software automatically renders orthogonal cross sections through each frame level; manual measurement confirmed a narrowest internal diameter of 13.7 mm within the second‐generation Evolut stent—a critical constraint because any subsequently implanted frame must expand without exceeding this limitation lest it cause undue radial force on adjacent structures or fail to achieve full expansion.  

**Sinus‐of‐Valsalva dimensions & coronary ostial heights**  
For each sinus one measures maximal transverse dimension and vertical height from annular plane to sinus roof. Coronary ostia are traced from their origin at the sinus wall to their tip; heights ≥10–12 mm are generally considered safe from iatrogenic occlusion when deploying another self‑expanding valve inside an existing one because there remains room for “neo‐skirt” tissue displacement. In our patient both left main and right coronary ostia were ≥11 mm above the annular plane and >4 mm from any metallic strut after simulated placement of a low‑profile Acurate neo2—values that reassured operators that coronary protection devices would not be required intra‑procedurally.  

### Coronary obstruction risk assessment  

The principal mechanism of sudden coronary occlusion during TAV‐in‐TAV procedures is displacement of native leaflets—or previously implanted leaflets—against the sinus wall creating a “neo‐skirt” that blocks ostial inflow. Virtual THV‐to‐coronary distance (VTC) quantifies this risk by measuring from the projected outer edge of the new frame (“skirt”) to each ostium on simulated deployment models generated by dedicated software (e.g., HeartNavigator). A VTC <4 mm traditionally predicts high obstruction risk and prompts preemptive protection strategies such as chimney stenting or BASILICA laceration. For this case simulation yielded VTC values >7 mm bilaterally after positioning an Acurate neo2 at high implantation depth aligned with Evolut leaflets—rendering protective wiring unnecessary while preserving procedural simplicity despite three overlapping frames.  

### Virtual implantation simulations  

Beyond static measurements, dynamic simulation incorporates anticipated self-expansion forces and predicts final device geometry relative both to existing stents and coronary ostia. The team generated a virtual model where the commissures of an Acurate neo2 size S were rotated so that they coincided with those of the previous Evolut valve (“leaflet alignment”). This alignment maximises residual effective orifice area by avoiding channeling flow through multiple intersecting struts—a common cause of patient–prosthesis mismatch when commissures are randomised across layers. The simulation also allowed visualization of ‘frame stack height’ ensuring that none of the proximal neo2 crown would protrude beyond previously placed frames into restrictive sinotubular junctions—a scenario associated with increased post-dilatation pressures required later during inflation cycles.

### Planning high implantation depth  

In usual TAV‐in‐TAV cases operators aim for “mid” depth ~3–5 mm below native annulus so that leaflets above previous frame can fully expand (“valve-in-valve anchor”). However with triple layering one must balance two competing goals: keeping sufficient distance from coronaries while avoiding excessive embedment into prior stent struts that could prevent adequate expansion—and thereby worsen PPM—of the new prosthesis itself. By positioning Acurate neo2 so its inflow cuff sat just above—or flush against—the Evolut leaflets (“high” implantation), surgeons achieved several effects:

1️⃣ The Neo2 skirt rested on already expanded metal rather than within restrictive calcified native annulus, facilitating larger final EOA;  
2️⃣ The supra‐annular leaflet position stayed clear of earlier frames’ leaflet hinges preventing residual pinching;  
3️⃣ Coronary ostia remained protected because no additional material extended deeper into sinuses where they originate.

Thus high implantation was deliberately selected after confirming via CT that sufficient radial space existed between Evolut leaflets and sinus walls.

### Valve selection algorithm based on CT dimensions  

At this stage all pertinent measurements converge into a straightforward algorithmic decision:

1️⃣ **Maximum feasible external diameter:** Must be ≤13–14 mm given measured internal diameter inside Evolut → excludes most balloon-expandable valves which require larger landing zones (>16 mm).  
2️⃣ **Effective-orifice location:** Supra‑annular design desirable because it functions beyond limits imposed by internal ring size—hence self‑expanding devices preferred over intra‑annular counterparts in narrow anatomies.<br>3️⃣ **Frame profile & height:** Low.frame height reduces cumulative stack thickness; Acurate neo2’s inflow cage (~12 mm) plus short crown avoided encroachment upon sinotubular junction.<br>4️⃣ **Commissural alignment capability:** The neo2 platform allows deliberate orientation during deployment using fluoroscopic markers—a decisive feature when trying to align more than one layer’s commissures.<br>5️⃣ **Coronary clearance:** Simulated VTC>7 mm confirmed safety even at higher placement.<br>Given these constraints, a size S Acurate neo2 (nominal outer dimension ~23 mm at inflow but collapsible down to fit 13–14 mm internal lumen when seated high) was selected as it met all five criteria while providing supra‑annular leaflet flow beneficial in small anatomic corridors.

### Integration into clinical decision making  

The multimodality imaging strategy described above directly informed each step taken during this patient’s third transcatheter intervention—from confirming severe hemodynamic deterioration on TTE through exclusion of active infection on TOE, through precise sizing & risk quantification on MDCT, culminating in a preplanned high implant aligned with prior commissures using an appropriately low-profile supra-annular device—all without resorting to provisional coronary protection techniques. This integrated approach underscores how contemporary cardiac imaging has evolved from diagnostic adjuncts into essential procedural roadmaps that enable even complex “valve-in-valve-in-valve” therapies to be performed safely and durably in high-risk patients.

--- 

*Key take‑away*: In patients undergoing repeat transcatheter interventions within previously placed prostheses—especially when multiple layers already exist—the combination of quantitative echocardiography and detailed ECG-gated MDCT offers an evidence-based framework that converts anatomical complexity into actionable procedural plans while safeguarding against catastrophic complications such as coronary occlusion or severe patient–prosthesis mismatch.*

## Procedural Strategy and Technical Execution of TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV  

When a patient has already received two transcatheter aortic valves and subsequently develops severe prosthetic dysfunction, the decision to proceed with a third valve implantation must balance the very high operative risk of open surgery against the technical feasibility of another percutaneous approach. In this case the multidisciplinary heart team—comprising interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, cardiac imagers, anesthesiologists, and an infectious disease specialist—performed a structured risk–benefit analysis that ultimately guided every step of the procedure.  

### Heart‑team evaluation  

The first item on the agenda was an objective appraisal of surgical risk. Using contemporary European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE II) calculations the patient’s predicted 30‑day mortality was 27 % (EuroSCORE II = 27 %); this estimate incorporated his advanced age, severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney dysfunction (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²), multi‑vessel coronary disease with prior percutaneous interventions, permanent pacemaker dependency and recent gastrointestinal bleeding. The cardiac surgeon emphasized that redo surgical aortic root replacement would require explantation of two deeply embedded self‑expanding frames and likely replacement of the ascending aorta—a technically demanding operation with an anticipated need for prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass time far beyond what could be justified given the calculated mortality.  

Conversely, interventional cardiology colleagues pointed out that detailed multislice computed tomography (MSCT) demonstrated an adequate ilio‑femoral vessel diameter for transfemoral delivery (minimum lumen > 7 mm), sufficient distance between the most cranial edge of the existing Evolut frame and both coronary ostia (>6–8 mm), and a predictable “neo‑skirt” location when a third supra‑annular valve is positioned high in the prior frame. The infectious disease team confirmed sterilisation of the previous prosthetic infection by documenting three consecutive negative blood cultures, sterile transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) images without vegetations, and normal inflammatory markers (>4 weeks after completion of six weeks’ ampicillin therapy). Because no active infection persisted and all anatomic criteria were met, consensus was reached that a third percutaneous valve could be performed safely under local anesthesia with meticulous imaging guidance.  

### Rationale for selecting the Acurate neo2®  

Choosing a device capable of delivering an acceptable effective orifice area (EOA) in an already constrained annulus was crucial. The Acurate neo2® was favored for three specific reasons:

1. **Low frame height** – The nickel–titanium stent measures <20 mm from inflow edge to outflow apex. In a “triple‐stented” configuration this limits additional material projecting into the sinus of Valsalva and preserves space for future coronary access.
2. **Supra‑annular leaflet position** – The leaflets sit above the metallic frame; consequently even when anchored within a narrow residual lumen (the Evolut stent measured only 13–14 mm on MSCT) the neo2 can achieve a larger EOA than a purely intra‑annular device.
3. **Commissural alignment capability** – The neo2’s orientation marks enable intentional alignment of its commissures with those of the pre‑existing Evolut prosthesis according to preprocedural three‑dimensional planning software. Alignment reduces leaflet interference, mitigates patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM), and improves chances for unobstructed coronary reaccess in later procedures.

Alternative options such as balloon‐expandable Sapien 3 were deemed less attractive because their taller frames would increase obstruction risk at the level of the coronary ostia and could exacerbate PPM by further reducing the residual annular area already compromised by two prior frames.

### Access strategy  

The procedural plan called for primary left femoral arterial access using a 14–16 French sheath compatible with the neo2 delivery system. Although previous femoral complications (a pseudoaneurysm after the second TAVI) had occurred on the right side, MSCT showed bilateral iliac arteries were suitable; therefore left‐sided puncture was selected to avoid repetition on a previously damaged vessel segment while still capitalising on optimal coaxial alignment with the ascending aorta.  

A secondary left radial arterial catheter was placed solely as an angiographic conduit for selective contrast injections into each coronary sinus during key steps (baseline aortography before dilation, post‑dilation checks). This “dual access” configuration allowed real-time visualisation without manoeuvring large sheaths through small radial arteries—a consideration especially important after multiple previous catheterisations that had placed scar tissue in both femoral sites.

Local anesthesia was preferred over general anesthesia given his frailty, pulmonary disease, and moderate chronic kidney disease (to avoid prolonged haemodynamic swings caused by positive pressure ventilation). Conscious sedation combined with meticulous analgesia provided sufficient patient comfort while maintaining spontaneous ventilation throughout rapid ventricular pacing periods.

### Guidewire choice and positioning  

Crossing three overlapping self‑expanding frames demands both flexibility to negotiate tight interstices and enough stiffness to support subsequent device delivery. The Safari™ extra stiff guidewire fulfilled both criteria: its hydrophilic tip permitted smooth passage through sinusoidal gaps between frames without dislodging previously implanted prostheses; once across into the left ventricular cavity it supplied enough columnar support to maintain coaxiality during balloon inflations and valve deployment.

The wire was advanced under fluoroscopic guidance using orthogonal cusp overlap views: first into the left ventricular outflow tract past all three valves then looped securely against the interventricular septum tip-to-tip width (“ventricular loop” technique). Positioning distal tip at least 5–6 cm below the aortic annulus prevented inadvertent entrapment within any residual leaflet tissue as well as minimized risk of wire perforation during rapid pacing bursts.

### Rapid ventricular pacing protocol  

During any balloon expansion or valve deployment it is essential to dampen forward flow so that transient forces do not displace devices within an already constrained annulus whose geometry is dictated by two prior frames. Rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute was employed via external pacing pads placed on anterior thoracic wall positions yielding capture thresholds <10 mA. Pace bursts lasted 8–10 seconds—long enough to achieve systolic arterial pressure drop below 30 mmHg yet brief enough to avoid profound hypotension given his limited cardiac reserve (EF >55% at this stage). This protocol proved effective both during pre‑dilatation with a 22 × 40 mm semi-compliant balloon and later during post‑dilatation using a smaller 20 × 40 mm balloon.

### Implantation technique   

Preprocedural CT modelling suggested optimal depth at which the new neo2 should sit so that its inflow edge aligns just cranial to the Evolut leaflets (“high implantation”). This positioning accomplishes three goals:

1. **Fixes preexisting leaflets against their metal cage**, preventing them from prolapsing into central flow pathways.
2  **Keeps the neo’s “skirt” well above** both coronary ostia thereby preserving their patency.
3  **Maximises EOA** by ensuring that most blood passes through supra‐annular leaflets rather than being squeezed between multiple mesh layers.

During fluoroscopy’s right‐cusp/left‐cusp overlap view—chosen because it provides maximal silhouette separation between commissures—the delivery system was slowly retracted until radiopaque markers indicated that implantation depth matched CT predictions (~4–5 mm below native annular plane). Commissure orientation marks on neo2 were rotated until they coincided with those projected from Evolut’s radiopaque struts visible on live cineangiography—a maneuver facilitated by prior identification of reference angles on reconstructed CT images downloaded onto workstation software linked directly to angiographic system overlay function.

Once satisfactory depth alignment was confirmed visually and through coaxial marker position relative to vertebral levels (~C7/T1 zone), rapid pacing commenced while valve deployment began by unsheathing across all three frames simultaneously (self‐expansion occurs gradually over several seconds). Immediate fluoroscopic cues—such as disappearance of top flare ring into sinotubular junction—signaled proper expansion without impinging upon sinuses or hepatic venous structures noticeable posteriorly.

### Intra‑procedural imaging verification   

Baseline angiography performed via left radial catheter injected contrast selectively into right coronary artery (RCA) followed by left main showed unobstructed flow before any manipulation—a critical reference point because even slight obstruction may evolve insidiously during deployment when proximal neo skirt collapses onto ostia due to oversizing or low implantation depth.

After pre‐dilatation—and again after final valve release—a repeat contrast injection secured visual confirmation that both coronaries filled promptly without delay or washout abnormalities suggestive of partial obstruction (“coronary blush test”). Simultaneously side‐by‐side AP/RAO cranial angulation allowed detection of any subtle “pinching” near sinus edges caused by mesh overlaying ostia—the lack thereof validated our earlier decision not to employ prophylactic wiring (“coronary protection”) across either ostium.

### Post‑dilatation  

The immediate haemodynamic readout following initial deployment showed an unexpectedly elevated mean gradient (~67 mmHg) despite correct positioning—a finding consistent with residual scaffold rigidity imparted by three overlapping frames limiting full expansion of neo2 leaflets at this early stage. To optimise final geometry without overstressing older metal cages we performed low‐pressure post‐dilatation using a noncompliant 20 × 40 mm balloon inflated to nominal pressures (~6 atm) under another brief rapid pacing run at 180 bpm. This maneuver compressed lingering infolded struts from both Evolut and Lotus prostheses while allowing neo2's supra‑annular leaflets vectors to open more fully toward centre streamlines → measured mean gradient dropped subsequently from >60 mmHg intraoperatively to <30 mmHg on early transthoracic echo performed one hour later—a clinically acceptable value given anticipated functional improvement over days as myocardial relaxation occurred.

The key nuance here is respecting frame integrity: excessive overinflation (>8 atm) could fracture earlier stents causing uncontrolled embolisation or jeopardising structural anchoring points already integrated into ascending wall tissue—an event we explicitly avoided by adhering strictly to manufacturer recommended maximal inflation pressures suited for composite multi-valve anatomy.

### Coronary protection plan  

Despite routine practice in many “valve-in-valve” cases involving low‐lying coronaries—including wiring one or both ostia or placing undeployed “chimney” stents as bailouts—the team elected not to place protective wires prophylactically because:

* MSCT had demonstrated L/R coronary heights (>12 mm) well above expected neo2 skirt position;
* Virtual simulation predicted clearances exceeding safety thresholds (>4–5 mm), thus reducing likelihood that any mesh movement would encroach upon ostial lumens;
* The added hardware could increase procedural complexity given limited femoral access space already occupied by multiple delivery devices;
* Readily available backup—an arterial catheter already positioned radially—allowed instantaneous selective contrast reinjection if any doubt about perfusion arose during real time fluoroscopy rather than committing preemptive hardware placement which might itself precipitate mechanical injury or thromboembolic events within fragile vasculature customarily encountered in octogenarian patients.

Nevertheless contingency measures were prepared: small PTCA balloons sized appropriately for each ostium were kept sterilely ready inside cath lab inventory so darting them into position could be executed within seconds should angiography reveal even modest compromise following final deployment or post‐dilation run.

### Procedural success according to VARC‑3 criteria  

The Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 (VARC‑3) defines technical success as successful vascular access without major complications, correct positioning (“≤5% deviation from intended landing zone”), delivery system retrieval without need for additional surgery or reintervention ≤30 days post-procedure, presence < moderate prosthetic regurgitation (<grade 2+), mean gradient <20 mmHg after any necessary post-dilatation **or** appropriate haemodynamic improvement if baseline gradients exceed this threshold due only transiently in early measurements supported by subsequent echocardiographic trends.... In this case:

* Vascular entry points healed uneventfully ; no major bleeding nor need for surgical conversion.
* Fluoroscopic landmarks confirmed valve placement at intended high depth aligning commissurally within ≤3 mm deviation.
* After post-dilation acute mean gradient fell rapidly from >60 mmHg intraoperatively down to ~27 mmHg at discharge—a value consistent with moderate stenosis yet judged acceptable given historic triple-valve anatomy where ideal values are unattainable.
* Aortic regurgitation reduced from grade 2–3 preprocedure down to trace/none immediately after deployment—and remained grade 0 throughout follow-up → meeting VARC definition for ≤moderate AR.
* No periprocedural myocardial infarction occurred; troponin rise remained <5× upper reference limit consistent with acceptable procedural myocardial injury threshold set by VARC §15.
* Patient survived hospitalization without stroke or conduction system deterioration beyond his baseline pacemaker dependence — satisfying early safety endpoint definitions (<30 days).

Accordingly all technical success components outlined in VARC‑3 were fulfilled despite procedural complexity inherent in performing TAV‐in‐TAV‐in‐TAV under conscious sedation alone. Follow-up echocardiograms confirmed sustained haemodynamic benefit at one year supporting durable endpoint achievement expected under VARC long-term performance criteria (*i.e.*, freedom from structural valve deterioration defined as >10% increase in mean gradient compared with discharge values).

Collectively this case demonstrates how systematic heart-team deliberations rooted in objective risk scores complemented by high-resolution CT planning can shape a coherent procedural blueprint—even when confronting triple-valve architecture compounded by prior infective endocarditis recovery. Each technical element—from route selection respecting earlier femoral complications through deliberate high-depth supra-annular implantation—to vigilant intraoperative angiographic surveillance ensures preservation of coronary perfusion while achieving satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes documented against contemporary VARC standard benchmarks.^
