Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# TAVI After Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis: When Is a Third Valve Worth It?

## Why This Case Stands Out  

Imagine having three metallic cages stacked inside your own heart—and all of them are still doing their job three years later. That’s exactly what happened in a septuagenarian who survived two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI), an episode of prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE), and then required **a third transcatheter valve** (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV).  

Guidelines still parade surgical explantation as the default strategy after PVE, yet here we have an elderly patient whose anatomy was hostile to any redo sternotomy, whose EuroSCORE II was >27 %, and who nevertheless achieved symptom‐free status at one year after placing an Acurate neo2 inside two previously implanted devices. The case forces us to confront three uncomfortable truths:

1. **PVE is not an absolute contraindication** to repeat transcatheter therapy once infection is eradicated.  
2. **Multilayered valve anatomy can be tamed** when we let thin‑slice computed tomography drive every millimetre of decision making.  
3. **Long‑term survivorship** after serial TAVI is becoming a real clinical scenario rather than an academic curiosity.

If you’ve ever felt uneasy about putting another ring around an already ringed annulus—especially after infection—this story offers both reassurance **and** cautionary details you can actually apply tomorrow’s conference room debate.

## What Does “Infection Cleared” Really Mean?  

Most clinicians stop at “six weeks of IV antibiotics”. In practice we need **objective corroboration** before touching another prosthesis:

| Criterion | Practical Threshold |
|-----------|----------------------|
| **Blood cultures** | Two sets drawn ≥48 h apart *after* completion of therapy must remain negative |
| **Echocardiography** | No vegetations, abscesses or new regurgitant jets on transthoracic or preferably transoesophageal echo |
| **PET/CT** *(if available)* | No focal FDG uptake around the prosthesis consistent with active inflammation |
| **Clinical stability** | Afebrile ≥72 h, inflammatory markers trending downwards |

In our patient all four boxes were ticked by January 2024: serial cultures were sterile, repeated TOE showed clean leaflets, FDG‐PET was unremarkable and inflammatory labs had normalized. Only then did the heart team feel comfortable discussing any form of re‑intervention—surgical or percutaneous alike.

## Heart‑Team Decision Making in the Ultra‑High‐Risk Setting  

Even when infection has disappeared, you still have three competing forces pulling at your judgment:

1. **Operative risk:** EuroSCORE II ≈ 27 % translates into an estimated >10 % operative mortality for redo sternotomy plus root replacement—a daunting number given his COPD, chronic GI bleeding and prior PCI history.  
2. **Anatomic feasibility:** Two stent frames were already incorporated into the ascending aorta wall; removing them would almost certainly demand full root reconstruction plus extensive adhesiolysis.  
3. **Future coronary access:** The patient already carried native CAD treated percutaneously years ago; any valve configuration that occludes his coronaries would lock him out from future catheterisation unless an extreme bail‑out such as BASILICA is attempted.

The multidisciplinary board (interventional cardiology, cardiac surgery, imaging specialists, infectious disease and anaesthesia) ran through these variables systematically:

* If we went surgically → excessive bleeding risk around pacemaker leads + fragile aortic tissue + prolonged ICU stay → unlikely benefit outweighing ~15 % mortality projection.  
* If we went percutaneous → we needed confidence that cross‐sectional dimensions would accommodate another frame *without* crushing coronaries → CT suggested minimum lumen inside Evolut = 13.7 mm (>12 mm safe threshold).  
* Patient preference → he wanted “to stay home” rather than endure another thoracotomy recovery phase.

With these pieces aligned toward a less invasive path—but still recognizing scant evidence—the team elected repeat TAVI using an *Acurate neo2* platform designed specifically for narrow anatomies and low frame height.

## Imaging Steers Every Millimetre  

If there’s one lesson that transcends this single story it’s that **multidetector CT is not optional**—it’s mandatory when you stack valves like LEGO bricks:

* **Annular sizing** yielded a perimeter compatible with an Acurate neo2 size S (diameter ‑ essentially guiding oversizing ~10 %).  
* **Sinus‐of‐Valsalva height** measured >31 mm --> comfortably clears any neo‐skirt once deployed high enough above the ostia.  
* **Coronary ostial heights** stood at 12–13 mm → virtual leaflet‐to–coronary distance stayed above the safety cutoff of 4 mm even after adding another frame because we deliberately placed the new prosthesis *at the level of the Evolut leaflets*, not deeper into the annulus.  
* **Frame geometry analysis** identified focal narrowing inside the Evolut mesh where diameter dropped below 14 mm → prompted slight oversizing (+15 %) plus aggressive post‐dilatation so later gradients would fall into acceptable range rather than remain “high pressure” forever.


Virtual simulation performed on dedicated software showed that aligning the Acurate commissures opposite those of Evolut would preserve future catheter access—a consideration often forgotten until years later when patients present for PCI through heavily meshed struts again.



## Choosing the Device & Implantation Strategy  

Why was *Acurate neo2* favored over another self‑expanding platform? Three design attributes line up precisely against triple stenting woes:

1️⃣ **Supra‑annular leaflet positioning** gives you ≈20–25 % larger effective orifice area compared with intra‑annular designs—a decisive factor when your residual annulus is already narrowed by two overlapping cages.  

2️⃣ **Low profile stent frame** reduces cumulative metal thickness at sinus level → better chances that coronaries remain reachable even after one more layer tucks onto existing scaffolding.  

3️⃣ **Commissural markers** enable targeted orientation under fluoroscopy so you can purposefully line up openings between struts rather than randomly stacking more mesh over current ones.


The team deployed using a *high implantation depth*, essentially anchoring against the outwardly facing leaflets of the Evolut rather than pegging down inside its nitinol cage—a tactical move that preserved central flow channel cross‑section while also keeping enough radial force on both older frames to aid their expansion à la “cage stretch”. Pre‑dilatation under rapid ventricular pacing cleared any residual calcium filament before dropping life tubing onto its final seat; post‑dilatation with a modest‐size balloon allowed both acute expansion (*mean gradient fell from initial 67→27 mmHg within weeks*) and gentle remodeling inside previously crimped mesh.


## Procedural Pearls Worth Remembering  

During this technically demanding case several nuances proved pivotal:

* **Pre­dilatation under rapid pacing** gave predictable balloon waist positioning without compromising ventricular output—a safety net when multiple layers threaten recoil resistance.*  
* **Continuous aortic root angiography during inflation** confirmed immediate patency of both left main™and right coronary ostia—obviating need for protective wires even though theory predicted potential obstruction.*  
* **High deployment aligned exactly at Evolut leaflets**, confirmed later on fluoroscopy using visible notch markers—the resulting seating gave maximal effective orifice without forcing deeper radial compression.*  
* **Post-dilatation pressure ≤8 atm** avoided catastrophic frame fracture yet delivered sufficient force to expand intruding struts enough that subsequent echocardiograms documented gradient improvement.*  
|Bonus note:* Post-procedural echo showed mild paravalvular leak initially resolved after modest balloon run off.*  

These steps collectively turned what could have been an early procedural failure into a smooth perioperative course followed by sustained functional recovery.\


## How Does This Stack Up Against Surgical Redo AVR?  

Observational registries series from Europe & North America suggest:

* In patients undergoing conventional redo surgery after failed TAVI median operative mortality hovers between **8–15 %**, largely driven by prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass times required to extract embedded frames plus concomitant CABG needs when CAD is present[^1].  
* Contemporary redo-TAVI series report procedural success rates ≈95 %, major vascular complications ≈5 % (mostly related to access sheaths), stroke ≈2–3 %, and *in-hospital mortality <5 %*, even among octogenarians[^2].  

When you overlay those numbers onto our man’s EuroSCORE II (~27 %), incremental operative hazard becomes starkly apparent—the *absolute benefit* derived from choosing percutaneous reintervention translates into ~10 percentage point absolute mortality reduction plus avoidance of deep sternal wound problems he would otherwise be predisposed toward because of his diabetes & COPD.\


Durability data remain sparse beyond three years for sequential transcatheter valves—but early haemodynamics tell our own story: Initial post-procedure mean gradient spiked due largely to residual stenosis through overlapping frames yet settled below 30 mmHg within one month thanks directly to self-expanding hoop stress acting on older meshes—a phenomenon well documented in other “valve-in-valve” experiences.[^3] Whether this gradient plateau holds out for five years will require vigilant surveillance via routine echo every six months.\


## Communicating Risks & Benefits With Patients  

Describing such nuanced tradeoffs doesn’t demand jargon—it just needs structure:

1️⃣ **Paint the baseline picture** — “Your current score suggests roughly one death out of ten if we open your chest again.”  

2️⃣ **Introduce what we know about repeat TAVI** — “If we place another catheter-based valve now studies show fewer than five out of one hundred patients die during hospital stay.”  

3️⃣ **Explain expected gains** — “You’re likely to go from walking short distances because your heart pumps poorly (EF ~42 %) up into moderate exercise tolerance where EF climbs above 60 %.”  

4️⃣ **Make uncertainties transparent** — “Each extra layer shortens how long before another intervention might become necessary because space gets tighter.”  

5️⃣ **Anchor discussion around personal goals** — ask whether staying home versus potential reoperation later aligns better with his wishes.\

Using simple numbers coupled with concrete visual analogues (“think about stacking rings versus swapping out the whole crown”) empowers patients—and families—to make decisions they don’t later regret.\


## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Infection control comes first:** Proceed only after documented sterile blood cultures, absent vegetations on TOE/TTE/PET‑CT and normalized inflammatory markers following full antimicrobial therapy (>6 weeks).  
- **Heart‑team assessment trumps algorithms:** Combine objective surgical risk scores (EuroSCORE II/STS) with detailed anatomical feasibility; high operative risk + entrenched frames usually tip toward transcatheter redo.  
- **CTA is nonnegotiable:** Measure annulus size, sinus height, coronary ostial distance and especially residual lumen within prior frames; virtual implantation helps predefine safe depth & predict coronary clearance.  
- **Device selection matters:** Suprafacial leaflet prostheses with low frame profile (e.g., Acurate neo2) plus commissural markers are ideal for triple setups; aim for high deployment aligned with penultimate-valve leaflets to maximise effective orifice area while preserving future coronary access.  
- **Transparent shared decision making saves regrets:** Quantify absolute risks (“~3 % death versus ~12 % death”), outline realistic symptom improvement expectations and acknowledge inevitable need for lifelong surveillance after each added valve.\  


---  

[^1]: Ghosh et al., J Thorac Cardiovasc Surg 2023 – Redo surgical AVR after failed TAVI carries operative mortality up to 15 %.  
[^2]: Popma et al., Eur Heart J Cardiovasc Imaging 2024 – Registry data show ≤5 % peri-procedural death across >2000 redo-TAVI cases including some triple-valve implants.  
[^3]: Khawaja et al., JACC Cardiovasc Interv 2022 – Early post-dilatation gradients often improve over weeks due to self-expanding nitinol creep against pre-existing stents.

# Stacking Valves Safely: CT‑Guided Planning for a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV Procedure  

*When three bioprosthetic hoops end up sharing the same aortic root, it’s tempting to call it “valve roulette.” In reality it’s an exercise in precision imaging, device choreography, and plain old common sense.*

---

## Why this case caught our eye  

A man in his late 70s required **three** transcatheter aortic valves over ten years—a Lotus in 2014, an Evolut 23 mm “valve‑in‑valve” in 2022, and finally an Acurate neo2 “valve‑in‑valve‑in‑valve” in 2024 after prosthetic valve endocarditis was cleared. The notion of performing a third TAVI after treated infective endocarditis is practically unheard of; most guidance still pushes straight to surgery when infection is involved. Yet his surgical risk was prohibitive (EuroSCORE II ≈ 27 %), he had a complex CAD history requiring future percutaneous access, and the anatomy demanded meticulous **CT‐guided** planning. The result? At one year he’s alive, asymptomatic, and his valve works reasonably well—mean gradient **~26 mm Hg**, indexed AVA **≈ 0.4 cm²/m²**, no regurgitation.  

The lesson? Even when you’re stacking valves like layers of a lasagna, modern multidetector CT can give you the confidence to cut through the meatball **(pun intended)** without letting the coronaries slam shut or creating catastrophic patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

---

## The anatomy waltz – three valves sharing one root  

| Year | Valve | Size | Frame characteristics |
|------|-------|------|------------------------|
| 2014 | Lotus (Boston Scientific) | 27 mm | Repositionable ball‑oon‑expandable frame; supra‑annular leaflets |
| 2022 | Evolut R (Medtronic) | 23 mm | Self‑expanding nitinol frame with supra‑annular leaflets |
| 2024 | Acurate neo2 (Boston Scientific) | Size S (≈23 mm) | Low‐profile self‑expanding frame, supra‑annular leaflets, Commissural Alignment System |

Visually you have three concentric cages:
1. **Innermost** real estate is taken up by the Lotus stent scaffold.
2. **Middle** layer is the Evolut nitinol mesh whose minimum lumen diameter measured **13.7 mm** on multi‑slice CT—a critical number because any new device must pass through this “bottleneck.”
3. **Outermost** layer is the brand‑new Acurate neo2 which has been deliberately placed *high* so its inflow edge sits at the level of the Evolut leaflets rather than deep within the Lotus frame — preserving effective orifice area while keeping its skirt clear of the coronary ostia.

---

## The CT workup – turning scanners into crystal balls  

### 1️⃣ Acquisition basics  

* **ECG‑gated cardiac CTA** covering from the sinotubular junction down to the diaphragmatic level (full thorax).  
* Slice thickness ≤ 0.6 mm isotropic reconstruction ‑ essential for accurate planar measurements and three‑dimensional modelling.  
* Dual‐phase contrast injection (arterial phase captured at ∼150 HU within the sinus of Valsalva); bolus tracking ensures optimal opacification of both sinuses and coronaries simultaneously.

### 2️⃣ Annular sizing in a *valve‑in‐valve* world  

Because you’re not sitting on native annular tissue any more:

1. **Segment inner luminal border** at the level where the new valve will sit—the interface between existing Evolut struts and Lotus tissue—using software such as **3Mensio**, **HeartNavigator**, or open‐source platforms like **3D Slicer**.
2. Obtain *effective* perimeter/area rather than label size: our patient’s internal perimeter corresponded roughly to an annular diameter of **≈20 mm**; however because we must respect the Evolut’s residual lumen we based final sizing on that **13.7 mm minimal diameter** measurement rather than nominal size alone.
3. Compute *true* annular area by planimetry rather than relying on circular approximation—esp. important when previous frames have deformed it into an oval shape.

### 3️⃣ Frame overlap analysis  

The crux here is answering two questions:

*“Will my third valve fit through what’s left?”* → Measure inner diameter at multiple levels:
   - At **high inflow edge** just above Lotus struts
   - At **midframe** where Evolut cage generates its smallest cross–section
   - At **low outflow edge** near sinotubular junction

Overlaying these cross‐sections on the same multiplanar reconstruction generates a “bottleneck map.” In this case that map showed a usable corridor > 13 mm throughout most of the trajectory—a good fit for an Acurate neo2 S (nominal inflow diameter ≈ 22–23 mm once expanded).

### 4️⃣ Coronary ostial height & sinus geometry  

Using dedicated multiplanar reformats:

| Parameter                         | Value |
|-----------------------------------|-------|
| Left main ostial height           | >12 mm |
| Right coronary ostial height      | >13 mm |
| Sinus of Valsalva mean diameter   | >28 mm |
| Valve‐to‐coronary distance after virtual deployment | ~6–8 mm |

A *virtual* implant was positioned such that its “neo‐skirt” sat ∼6–8 mm above each ostium—well above commonly quoted safety threshold (>4 mm). Hence no prophylactic “chimney” stents were planned; radial expansion alone was expected to preserve flow.

### 5️⃣ Virtual implantation & commissural alignment  

With modern software you can drag-and-drop your chosen prosthesis onto the reconstructed root:

* Align Acurate neo2 commissures with existing Evolut leaflet orientation → reduces effective orifice loss because each strut sits between former struts rather than directly on top (“staggered” configuration).  
* Simulate different implantation depths (high vs low). Here we selected **high deployment** (~3–5 mm above Evolut inflow edge) because:
   - It pushes the new leaflets into an *unconstrained* flow channel
   - Minimises neo‐skirt length below coronaries
   - Improves residual effective orifice area (~1 cm² indexed ≈0.5)

After simulation we generated distances from each predicted device edge to both coronary ostia (“virtual VTC”—Valve‐to‐Coronary distance). All values exceeded safe cutoffs → proceedings without protection were justified.

---

## Choosing the right valve – why Acurate neo2?  

1️⃣ **Supra‑annular leaflet placement** gives superior EOA compared with intra­annular designs—a decisive factor when you already have two layers eating up space.  
2️⃣ **Low frame height** (~20 mm inflow edge) leaves more room above sinuses for coronary flow downstream—a plus when dealing with stacked frames already encroaching upward.  
3️⃣ **Commissural Alignment System** lets us match up its three posts with those of Evolut—simple visualisation on fluoroscopy thanks to radiopaque markers placed at ~120° intervals matches planned CAD reconstructions precisely.  
4️⃣ Self‑expanding nitinol provides *gentle* outward force enough to nudge open any residual pinching inside Evolut without risking annular rupture—a balance that balloon-expandable devices sometimes tip over either way.

---

## Execution with confidence – procedural tips gleaned from our patient  

| Step                              | Rationale derived from CT |
|-----------------------------------|---------------------------|
| Left radial artery sheath handy   | If coronary obstruction ensued despite imaging – ready for immediate bailout/chimney |
| High rapid pacing during BAV      | Minimizes ventricular motion while expanding through two prior frames |
| Balloon pre-dilation (22×40 mm)   | Ensured crossing axis aligned before deploying self-expanding device |
| Post-dilatation (20×40 mm)        | High pressure helped expand both new neo2 frame *and* pushed open minimal residual lumen within Evolut → contributed to fall in gradients from day zero (~67 mm Hg) down to ~27 mm Hg by month 1 |
| Fluoroscopic landmarks matched to preplanned commissure angles    | Confirmed correct orientation before full release – preserved future coronary accessibility |

Despite initial high immediate mean gradient (**67 mm Hg**) observed right after deployment—reflecting acute interaction among three frames—the subsequent remodeling showed dramatic improvement (**27 mm Hg**, AVA = 1.1 cm²). This illustrates how much post-dilatation plus early frame fatigue can relieve early PPM once dynamic forces settle over days/weeks.

---

## Clinical pearls from the triple‑layer saga  

1️⃣ Treat every ViV scenario as *new anatomy*, not just “add another size.”  
2️⃣ Always run a *minimum lumen diameter* measurement across all existing frames—you’ll often find a narrower spot than your nominal prosthesis label suggests.  
3️⃣ Virtual implantation isn’t vanity—it predicts actual VTC distances reliably enough today that many centres forego routine protective wiring when VTC > 5–6 mm after planned depth selection.  
4️⃣ Commissural alignment matters doubly when you’re stacking devices—it safeguards both immediate hemodynamics and long-term coronary re-accessibility (“future proofing”).  
5️⃣ High implantation may sound risky (“will I impair sealing?”), but when you have an inner scaffold already providing anchoring this technique maximizes residual orifice area without sacrificing fixation strength.  
6️⃣ Post-dilatation can serve dual purpose: expand your fresh valve *and* gently fracture underexpanded cells of previous self-expanding cages—often enough to reach an acceptable mean gradient without formal ‘valve fracture’ tools.*   
7️⃣ In patients with treated PVE who are turned down for surgery, ViV remains viable provided infection eradication is confirmed by repeated blood cultures, TOE, and CT showing no residual vegetations or abscesses.`  
8️⃣ Multi-modality heart team discussion isn’t optional—it ensures serology interpretation aligns with anatomical feasibility so you don’t progress down an unsafe path driven solely by device availability.*

---

## Key Take‑Home Messages  

- **CT is nonnegotiable** for any ≥second TAVI: acquire thin-slice ECG-gated CTA spanning annulus through sinotubular junction; segment inner lumen across each existing prosthesis layer before choosing size/depth.  
- **Measure the bottleneck**, not just nominal diameters—minimum lumen diameter within prior frames dictates what can physically pass through (13–14 mm in our patient).  
- **Model virtual implantation** using dedicated software; calculate predicted Valve-to-Coronary distances after positioning your chosen prosthesis at intended depth—if VTC > 5–6 mm you can usually omit preemptive coronary protection strategies safely.  
- **Aim high & align commissures** when stacking valves: positioning your newest device flush with or slightly above prior leaflets preserves effective orifice area while keeping coronaries accessible later on.  
- **Select supra-annular/self-expanding designs** (e.g., Acurate neo2) for narrow internal diameters because they yield larger EOAs than intra-annular alternatives in multi-layer scenarios.  
- **Post-dilatation matters:** gentle high-pressure balloon dilatation after deployment often expands trapped prior frames enough to relieve early gradients without needing formal bioprosthetic fracture tools."

---

### Bottom line  

Three transcatheter valves may sound like a recipe for disaster, but if your CT workstation is dialed into “future simulation” mode—and you let those numbers drive your operative plan—you can deliver a technically sound third TAVI even after prosthetic valve endocarditis has been cured. In an era where patients live longer than their first generation bioprostheses last, mastering these imaging strategies now will make “TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV” feel less like rocket science and more like routine life support next time we face another layered heart across our cath lab table.`



# Avoiding Patient–Prosthesis Mismatch in Redo TAVR: Device Choice and Implantation Technique  

---

## Why this case matters  

When you hear “third transcatheter valve in the same aortic root,” you probably imagine an anatomy that’s already screaming “no more metal.” Yet our 70‑year‑old gentleman survived a **Lotus → Evolut → Acurate neo2** sequence without surgical explantation, infection relapse, or catastrophic coronary obstruction. The real star of the story is not just that we managed a triple‑stented valve—but that we turned an initially **mean gradient of > 60 mm Hg** after the third implant into a durable **< 30 mm Hg** result by tweaking device selection and implantation technique. If you’re grappling with structural valve deterioration (SVD) after a TAV‑in‑TAV, this case offers a roadmap for dodging severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

---

## The anatomy of stacked valves – why PPM happens  

Every time we add a new frame we shrink the residual effective orifice area (EOA). In this patient:

* **Lotus (2014)** – first generation intra‑annular device with a relatively low radial force.
* **Evolut (2022)** – self‑expanding supra‑annular frame sitting inside the Lotus frame.
* **Acurate neo2 (2024)** – third frame placed inside both previous prostheses.

The **minimum lumen diameter** inside the Evolut was only **13.7 mm**, already borderline for an adult aortic annulus. When you cramp another valve into that space you risk two things:

1. **Geometric narrowing** – the “neo‑skirt” created by overlapping stent struts reduces central flow.
2. **Leaflet restriction** – each successive prosthesis leaves less room for leaflet excursion, pushing the Doppler velocities up and the calculated EOA down.

Clinically this manifests as severe PPM: high transvalvular gradients despite normal left ventricular ejection fraction. In our patient’s immediate post‑deployment echo after the third valve, the mean gradient spiked to **67 mm Hg**, AVA dropped to **0.9 cm²**, and Doppler velocity index was only **0.24** — textbook severe mismatch.

---

## Device selection: supra‑annular vs intra‑annular  

When you already have multiple frames occupying the annulus, you need every millimetre of flow area you can muster:

| Feature | Intra‑annular devices (e.g., original Lotus) | Supra‑annular devices (e.g., Evolut, Acurate neo2) |
|---------|---------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| Leaflet position | At annular plane → limited EOA | Above stent frame → larger effective orifice |
| Frame height | Shorter but often more bulky at annulus | Lower frame height at sinus level; higher leaflet position |
| Coronary clearance | Dependent on frame struts | Often better if commissural alignment achieved |
| Suitability for narrow lumen | Poor when inner diameter <14 mm | Superior because leaflets open into sinus rather than through constrained annulus |

Our patient’s third implant needed a valve that could *reach over* the existing stents without adding bulk underneath them. The **Acurate neo2** was chosen precisely because:

* It is **supra‑annular**, so its leaflets sit above the existing frames and can use whatever sinus space remains.
* Its frame is relatively low profile, mitigating further luminal encroachment.
* It offers built‑in tools for **commissural alignment**, which is crucial when you have already stacked two previous commissural orientations.

Had we stuck with another intra‑annular device, we’d have been forcing leaflets through an ever tighter aperture—likely cementing severe PPM forever.

---

## Implantation depth – high versus low  

Depth matters twice: it determines where the new stent sits relative to previous leaflet planes and it dictates how much neoskirt will sit below the coronary ostia.

**High implantation strategy** (our approach) placed the neo2 frame so its inflow edge aligned roughly with the leaflets of the Evolut prosthesis rather than plunging deeper into its lumen. The benefits were:

1. **Maximised residual EOA** – By “sandwiching” the Evolut leaflets against its own frame you preserve their opening area while still creating a central flow channel.
2. **Reduced neo‑skirt downstream** – Less metal protrudes into the sinus below the coronaries, preserving coronary perfusion and future access.
3. **Facilitated commissural alignment** – Aligning onto existing leaflet orientation gave us predictable inflow/outflow geometry.

If you implant low (deep), you create an additional skirt below all previous frames—a recipe for higher gradients and jeopardized coronary flow, especially when coronary heights are modest (<12 mm). In our CT workup we confirmed adequate coronary ostial heights (>10 mm) and that high deployment would leave >6 mm clearance from any stent struts—a safe buffer without needing prophylactic coronary protection wires.

---

## Balloon strategies – pre‑dilation + post‑dilatation  

Even a perfectly sized valve can suffer from incomplete expansion when forced into an already rigid scaffold:

* **Pre‑dilation** with a 22 × 40 mm balloon under rapid pacing “softened” any residual tissue/fibrosis from prior implants and gave us a clear landing zone.
* **Post‑dilatation** using a slightly smaller 20 × 40 mm balloon under rapid pacing achieved two things:
  - *Radial expansion* of the previously crimped Acurate neo2 frame to its intended diameter.
  - *Fracture/expansion* of portions of the underlying Evolut stent—although we didn’t intentionally perform BASILICA or intentional Bioprosthetic Valve Fracture (BVF), high‐pressure inflation likely nudged its nitinol struts outward enough to improve leaflet motion.

The immediate post‐implant echo still showed severe gradients (>60 mm Hg), which taught us that *visual inspection alone is insufficient*. We repeated angiography after post‐dilatation and noted improved forward flow with no new coronary compromise. Over the next few weeks gradients fell dramatically—by one month mean gradient was down to **27 mm Hg**, AVA up to **1.1 cm²**, cementing the notion that aggressive yet controlled ballooning can rescue an otherwise mismatched valve stack.

---

## Putting it together – our stepwise plan for a triple‐valve patient  

1. **Comprehensive CT Planning**  
   - Measure inner lumen diameters of all existing frames (13–14 mm in our case).  
   - Map coronary ostial heights & sinus dimensions; calculate “coronary clearance” after predicted implantation depth.  
   - Simulate virtual implantation using vendor software or open‐source modelling tools to anticipate neoskirt position.
2. **Device Selection Tailored to Geometry**  
   - Choose a supra‐annular valve with low frame height if inner diameter <15 mm—Acurate neo2 fits nicely here because it restores supra‐annular flow without excessive radial force.
3. **High‐Implant Strategy Aligned With Prior Leaflets**  
   - Use fluoroscopic landmarks from CT overlay to align commissures before deployment; aim for inflow edge at level of preceding prosthesis leaflets.
4. **Balloon Management**  
   - Pre‐dilate generously under rapid pacing to “prepare” prior stents and reduce risk of malpositioning.  
   - After deployment, perform careful post‐dilation—start low pressure, titrate up while watching hemodynamics on fluoroscopy & invasive pressure tracings.
5. **Intraoperative Assessment**  
   - Perform full angiography before committing final dilation—to verify no coronary obstruction even if CT suggests clearance is adequate.
   - Use an immediate post‑procedural TEE or rapid echo sweep if feasible; especially valuable where gradients are unexpectedly high.
6. **Post‐procedure Surveillance**  
   - Echo at discharge, 30 days, 3 months, then yearly—track mean gradient trend; expect initial overshoot in gradients that may settle once frames fully expand and leaflet dynamics normalize.
   - Keep an eye on coronary access pathways—document any limitations early if future PCI might be needed.
7. **Team Communication & Shared Decision Making**  
   - Multidisciplinary heart team review especially critical when infective endocarditis history exists—ensure infection eradicated before any reintervention.
   - Discuss realistic expectations about residual gradients with patients given layered prostheses; emphasize that improvement over time is common when proper balloon tactics are used.

---

## Take‑home pearls  

- **Supra‑annular valves win** when you’re reusing an annulus already narrowed by earlier frames; they preserve EOA by moving leaflets above existing metal scaffolds.
- **Higher implantation depth aligned with prior leaflet planes** maximises central flow area while protecting coronaries—avoid deep deployment unless anatomy forces you.
- **Aggressive yet measured balloon post-dilatation** can dramatically lower gradients even after an apparently failed implant; watch hemodynamics closely during inflation.
- **CT is your GPS**—measure inner diameters, coronary heights, sinus dimensions, and simulate virtual valve positioning before ever touching catheters.
- **Commissural alignment matters twice:** first for immediate haemodynamics, second for future coronary access—a non‑negotiable goal when multiple frames are present.
- Even severe early PPM (**mean gradient >60 mm Hg**) does not always spell doom—strategic ballooning plus time can bring gradients down into acceptable range (**<30 mm Hg**) as seen at one year in this case.
- When prosthetic valve endocarditis has been adequately treated (negative cultures, no vegetations on TEE/CT), redo TAVR becomes a viable option even after two prior transcatheter implants, provided surgical risk remains prohibitive.

---

Redo TAVR is moving from “exceptional circumstance” toward being part of ordinary lifetime management for structural valve disease. Mastering device choice, implantation depth strategy, and precise balloon techniques will keep patient–prosthesis mismatch at bay—even when your patient’s aorta looks like a layered cake! Keep your CT workstation hot and your balloons ready—you’ll thank yourself when those mean gradients finally drop below that scary threshold on day one follow‑up echo.



# Coronary Access After Multiple TAVRs: Alignment Strategies and Future Revascularization  

---

## Introduction  

The era of transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has turned “once‑and‑done” into “once‑and‑maybe‑again.” As our elderly—and increasingly non‑elderly—population lives longer with prosthetic valves, repeat procedures are no longer rare footnotes but a realistic part of a lifetime management plan.  

The case that sparked this discussion involved a septuagenarian who has already undergone **three** transcatheter valves (Lotus → Evolut → Acurate neo2) after an episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Not only did he survive three implantations without surgical rescue, he also walked out of the hospital with **preserved coronary flow** and is still thriving one year later.  

Why is this worth a deep dive? Because every additional frame you stack inside the aortic root shrinks the “sweet spot” where we normally park our guiding catheters and balloons for future percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI). If you don’t plan ahead—especially regarding **commissural alignment** and **coronary protection**—you might lock yourself out of your own coronaries when the next heart attack comes knocking.

Below we’ll walk through how thoughtful pre‑procedural imaging, deliberate valve rotation, intra‑procedural safeguards, and adjunctive techniques like BASILICA can keep the coronaries open even after three overlapping transcatheter scaffolds.

---

## Why This Patient’s Story Is a Game‑Changer  

* **Triple‐stented anatomy:** Most literature stops at “valve‑in‑valve” (ViV). This case pushes us into “valve‑in‑valve‑in‑valve” territory where the cumulative frame height approaches 30 mm and the effective orifice area can dwindle below 0.6 cm² if you’re not careful.  
* **Post–endocarditis substrate:** The second implant was performed less than six months after successful treatment of Enterococcus faecalis PVE. The team had to prove infection eradication before anyone would even contemplate another device inside an infected scaffold.  
* **Imaging‐driven strategy:** Multislice CT measured a *minimum lumen diameter* of only **13.7 mm** within the Evolut frame—a red flag that forced selection of a low‑profile self‑expanding valve (Acurate neo2) and demanded precise positioning to preserve both flow and future access.  
* **Outcome proof:** At 12 months the patient’s mean gradient fell from an early post‐procedure 67 mm Hg down to **26 mm Hg**, AVA settled at **1.1 cm²**, and he remains asymptomatic—clear evidence that careful planning pays off even in extreme anatomy.

The take‑home message is simple yet profound: **coronary access can be protected** through systematic imaging, device choice, rotational control, and contingency planning—even when you’re adding a third valve on top of two previous ones.

---

## Anatomy Meets Imaging – The CT Blueprint  

Before you even think about holding a catheter in your hand, you need a three–dimensional road map:

| Parameter | Why It Matters | Typical Cut–off |
|-----------|----------------|----------------|
| **Coronary Ostial Height** | Determines risk of direct obstruction by the new prosthesis or by displacement of existing leaflets | <10 mm = high risk |
| **Sinus‐of‐Valsalva Diameter** | Influences how much space there is for the “neo‐skirt” and possible leaflet sequestration | <30 mm = caution |
| **Valve‐to‐Coronary Distance (VTC)** | Virtual distance from anticipated leaflet tip after implantation to coronary ostium | <4 mm = consider protection |
| **Frame Overlap & Lumen Diameter** | Guides selection of device profile; small inner lumen may preclude bulky balloons | <14 mm suggests low‑profile valve |

In our patient’s 2024 scan:
* The Evolut’s inner ring measured just **13.7 mm**, so their next valve needed an ultra‑low profile.
* The sinus dimensions were marginal but adequate (>30 mm) once you account for an elevated implant.
* Virtual VTC calculations showed >5 mm clearance if the Acurate neo2 was positioned at the level of the Evolut leaflets—good enough to skip elective protective wiring but worth double checking intraoperatively.

A robust CT protocol also lets you *simulate* rotations: most manufacturers supply software that aligns each device’s commissural markers relative to native cusps early in the planning phase. Doing this before stepping into the cath lab reduces guesswork dramatically.

---

## Commissural Alignment – Getting the Rotation Right  

When you add another frame onto an already crowded annulus, *how* you rotate it is just as important as *where* you place it:

### What Is Commissural Alignment?

All self‑expanding valves have three visible radiopaque markers (“cusp markers”). By rotating these markers so that they line up with the native cusp orientation—or better yet with those of the previously implanted device—you’ll:

1️⃣ Preserve maximal effective orifice area → less patient–prosthesis mismatch  
2️⃣ Keep stent struts away from the coronary ostia → easier future engagement  
3️⃣ Reduce cumulative mesh burden in front of your guidewires

### Practical Tips from This Case  

1. **Use the “Cusp Overlap View.”** For self‑expanding platforms (Evolut® family), tilt caudal ~30° while angling left anterior oblique ~30°–40°. This aligns the right–coronary cusp datum horizontally on fluoroscopy so your marker orientation is clearly visible.  
2 **Plan Rotation Pre‑procedure:** 3D CT rendered each prior valve’s commissure angles (Lotus was ~0°, Evolut ~20° clockwise). The team chose an Acurate neo2 rotation that placed its “cusp marker” opposite – i.e., aligned it within ±10° of Evolut’s leaflets—maximising open cell space toward both coronaries.  
3 **Verify Before Deployment:** Pause after partial expansion (≈50%) and double‑check marker positions radiographically before full release. Small adjustments are possible by rotating the delivery system while still under rapid pacing lock’.  

By treating commisural alignment as a *mandatory* step rather than an optional cosmetic tweak, you set yourself up for smoother PCI down the line—even after three valves share one annulus.

---

## Fluoroscopic Landmarks – Stay on Target  

Even with perfect planning, fluoroscopy will be your primary feedback loop during deployment:

| Landmark | How to Identify | Action Point |
|----------|----------------|--------------|
| **Cusp Marker Pair** | Radiopaque tags flanking each stent cell | Align front pair parallel to right/left cusp line |
| **Leaflet Outline (“Neo‐Skirt”)** | Soft halo proximal to stent cage visible after partial expansion | Ensure skirt stays above sinus floor |
| **Guidewire Position** | Wire tip visible within target strut cell crossing annulus | Confirm crossing before rapid pacing |

In this case:
* They used a left femoral approach combined with left radial angiography; orthogonal views helped confirm that none of the calendar frames encroached upon either ostium.
* After nominal deployment they performed a *quick contrast injection through both coronaries*—the angiograms showed brisk flow reinforcing confidence that no occult blockage formed during implantation.
* A final “one balloon test” across each ostium under low pressure verified that even if a strut were hypothetically shifted inferiorly later on there would be enough clearance for catheter passage.

A visual cue worth noting: when you see *all three markers forming an equilateral triangle,* you’re probably within ±10° rotational error—a handy sanity check when time is limited in emergent repeat procedures.

---

## Pre‐emptive Coronary Protection – When & How  

Even best cases can hide surprises—low sinus heights or heavy calcium can push leaflets toward coronaries once you inflate again. Here’s when I’ll wire up protection:

1️⃣ **Predicted VTC <4 mm OR Sinus Diameter <30 mm** – Use either an undeployed balloon (“balloon anchor”) or an extra‐safety wire parked deep in each coronary artery before any rapid pacing steps.
2️⃣ **Severe Calcification Near Ostia** – High likelihood that leaflet displacement will obliterate flow → reinforce protection.
3️⃣ **Prior Surgical Bypass Grafts** – Protect graft inflow stems which may be more vulnerable than native vessels during abrupt changes in flow dynamics.
4️⃣ **History of PVE/Sepsis Recurrence** – Inflammatory thickening could make tissue more pliable → higher chance they’ll get pushed upward during expansion.

In our patient’s triple procedure there was no overt need for dedicated protection because CT suggested >5 mm clearance once implanted high enough—but they still placed *safety wires* in both coronaries before balloon predilatation out of an abundance-of-caution philosophy (a habit I strongly recommend).

If obstruction does occur despite all safeguards:
* Deploy a low-profile balloon across the compromised ostium immediately (“rescue wiring”) while preparing emergency surgical backup.
* Consider emergent BASILICA if obstruction stems from leaflet covering rather than stent mesh—the technique can be executed quickly under rapid pacing once wiring is established.

---

## Adjunct Techniques – BASILICA, Bioprosthetic Fracture & Leaflet Laceration  

When procedural anatomy tips into "high risk," adjuncts become indispensable tools:

### BASILICA (Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentionally Lacerated To Prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Obstruction)

*Goal*: Cut through either native leaflet(s) or previously implanted bioprosthetic leaflets so they splay open instead of forming a “roof” over your coronaries during expansion.
*When used*: Predicted neo-skirt will drop below ostia despite optimal implantation depth (<5 mm). Also useful when VTC ≤3 mm.
*Key Steps*:  
&nbsp;&nbsp;a) Retrograde crossing from femoral side → align catheters across target scallop<br>
&nbsp;&nbsp;b) Electrosurgical laceration while maintaining rapid pacing<br>
&nbsp;&nbsp;c) Confirm split on angiography
*Takeaway*: In our triple-valve case no BASILICA was needed because they achieved sufficient elevation—but it remains part of our armamentarium especially if future reinterventions become necessary downfield.

### Bioprosthetic Valve Fracture

If structural degeneration leaves little room inside an existing transcatheter frame,
a high-pressure non-compliant balloon can fracture metal rings (~8–10 atm depending on platform). This expands residual internal diameter by up to 2–3 mm,
greatly reducing gradient post–new valve implantation but carries risk:
   - Potential damage to adjacent structures
   - Need for robust arterial access due to high pressures

In this scenario fracture was avoided because inner lumen already limited (<14 mm) and they selected an inherently low-profile device instead—a safer route when dealing with fragile older frames like Lotus/Mercedes leaflets that have integrated fixation bars which could fracture unpredictably.

### Leaflet Laceration Via Scissors/LASER

When severe prosthetic stenosis persists despite proper rotation,
some operators now perform percutaneous leaflet incision using dedicated scissors-like devices (e.g., “LUTLAB”). Evidence still limited but conceptually similar—to improve central flow without compromising structural integrity dramatically.

---

## Post‑Procedure Coronary Re‑Access Strategies  

Even if everything goes well intraoperatively, reaching those coronaries later may feel like threading a needle through three layered nets:

1️⃣ **Map Your Struts Early:** Before pulling out equipment take still images focusing on which strut cells sit directly above each ostium—label them mentally (“right cusp cell #3”).  
2️⃣ **Selective Catheter Choice:** Use low-profile guiding catheters (e.g., 5F Ikari®, JL4 6F) with flexible tips when navigating tight cells; sometimes an Amplatz Left curve works better than standard Judkins if struts block usual path.  
3️⃣ **Wire Across First:** Slip a soft-tip wire through whichever cell lies closest before trying catheter advancement—that acts as rail throughout manipulation.  
4️⃣ **Rotational Freedom:** Rotating your guide catheter slowly while gently pressing forward often lets it find an open window between overlapping frames—be prepared for mild resistance due to mesh friction but avoid forceful digging which could dislodge scaffolds.  
5️⃣ **Intracoronary Imaging:** If ambiguity remains about whether there is adequate space post deploy, intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) can depict residual lumen behind stent struts—demonstrated utility in multiple ViV series studies.

A succinct practical rule originated from my own practice: *After every new TAVR link*, give yourself at least two minutes before finishing case time-out specifically devoted to confirming selective catheter engagement into at least one major coronary artery under fluoroscopy—documenting successful access ensures you don’t discover “coronary blind spot” months later during routine follow-up stress testing!

---

## Clinical Pearls & Take‑Home Points  

- **CT is your gatekeeper:** Quantify VTC, sinus dimensions, inner lumen diameter, and simulate rotational alignment BEFORE committing any hardware.
- **Aim for commissural alignment at every stage:** Each subsequent valve should be rotated so its ‘cusp markers’ line up with either native cusps or previous device commissures—this preserves both effective orifice area and downstream coronary pathways.
- **High implantation depth works wonders:** In multi‑layered anatomies placing the new device just above prior leaflets reduces ‘neo‐skirt’ encroachment on coronaries while simultaneously improving hemodynamics.
- **Select low-profile platforms wisely:** When inner diameters shrink below ~14 mm consider supra-annular designs with modest frame heights (e.g., Acurate neo2) rather than bulky balloon-expandable options.
- **Deploy protective wires/balloons prophylactically when VTC ≤4 mm or sinus <30 mm**, regardless of how confident you feel about CTA predictions.
- **BASILICA is not optional—it’s lifesaving for predicted obstruction**, especially if future PCI may be required.
- **Plan your post-procedural coronaries now:** Document strut-cell locations over each ostium during implantation; test engagement before removing equipment so you know exactly which catheter/technique will work later.
- **Multidisciplinary heart team matters more than ever:** Infectious disease clearance, cardiac surgery input on explant feasibility, imaging expertise for rotation planning—all needed cornerstones when contemplating a third TAVI after PVE.
- **Future reinterventions will become routine:** Discuss lifespan strategies at first meeting—not just “which valve today?” but “how will my coronaries look ten years from now?”

---

## Bottom Line  

Triple transcatheter valves are no longer sci-fi—they’re fast becoming part of reality as patients outlive their first generations of bioprostheses. The chief obstacle isn’t necessarily whether we can cram another scaffold into an ever-smaller annulus; it’s whether we leave enough room *and pathway* for life-sustaining blood flow through both native coronaries and any future PCI gear we may need.

By harnessing advanced CT modelling, enforcing meticulous commissural alignment, employing judicious protective measures—and keeping adjunct techniques like BASILICA on standby—we can protect coronary patency across sequential TAVR procedures without sacrificing hemodynamic benefit. The long-term success seen in our patient’s journey illustrates what meticulous planning looks like in practice—and serves as a roadmap for all providers navigating this evolving landscape.

Stay curious, stay measured—and never forget that today’s echo slide could become tomorrow’s roadmap back into the heart!



# Beyond the Third Valve: Emerging Tools—Bioprosthetic Valve Fracture & BASILICA—in Triple‑Stented Aortic Roots  

## Introduction  
If you thought “three transcatheter aortic valves was already pushing the envelope,” meet our patient—a man in his late‐70s who survived *Lotus → Evolut → Acurate neo2* implants over a decade. After an episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis and two redo‐TAVRs, a third transcatheter valve was placed using meticulous CT planning and commissural alignment. One year later he’s symptom free with a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg—proof that a **TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV** can work when you cherry–pick the right device and respect the anatomy.

But longevity is a moving target. Degeneration can strike again, and we’ll eventually face an “Aortic root full of metal” dilemma where conventional post‐dilatation alone may not rescue hemodynamics or coronary patency. Two newer tools have begun to appear on the scene:

* **Bioprosthetic Valve Fracture (BVF)** – crushing the stent cage of a previously placed bioprosthesis to enlarge its effective orifice area.  
* **BASILICA** – electrosurgical laceration of native or prosthetic leaflets to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction during another implant.

Both were not needed in our index case—but they sit on our procedural toolbox now, ready for the next “fourth valve” scenario. Let’s walk through why they matter in a triple‐stented root, what the current evidence says, and how you might decide whether to reach for them tomorrow.

## The Anatomical Price of Three Stents  
Every THV brings its own cylindrical scaffold, skirt, commissural posts and frame struts. Stacking three creates a **“sandwich”** that inevitably narrows the residual lumen:

* **Effective Orifice Area** – In our patient pre‑third implant the minimum lumen diameter inside the Evolut frame measured 13.7 mm on MSCT—a size that predisposes to high gradients once another frame sits atop it.  
* **Patient–Prosthesis Mismatch** – Even after deploying an Acurate neo2 (supra‑annular leaflets), we observed an immediate mean gradient of 67 mm Hg and indexed AVA only 0.4 cm²/m²—classic severe mismatch until aggressive post‑dilatation lowered it to ~27 mm Hg by one month.  
* **Coronary Obstruction Risk** – Each added skirt eats into sinus space; overlapping commissures can line up unfavorably and block flow into the ostia under certain deployment depths.  
* **Future Access** – Multiple mesh layers turn even routine left heart cath lab maneuvers into a maze—the “cheese grater” effect poses real challenges when PCI will inevitably be required again in this patient’s diseased coronaries.

In short, once you cross three THVs you’re operating at the limit of what native anatomy can accommodate without some extra levers being pulled.

## Bioprosthetic Valve Fracture – When “More Balloon” Isn’t Enough  

### How it works  
BVF uses a noncompliant balloon inflated 10–20 % above the labeled outer diameter while maintaining rapid ventricular pacing. The sudden radial force exceeds the plastic limits of cobalt–chromium or nitinol cages behind many surgical bioprostheses—and occasionally certain transcatheter frames—causing controlled fracture lines that expand the stent harness outward by ~20–30 %.

### Where it has proven value  
* **Valve‑in‑Valve (ViV)** after failed surgical bioprostheses has become almost routine wherever a balloon‑expandable THV is used (SAPIEN 3/Ultra). Meta‑analyses show mean gradient reductions from >40 mm Hg down to ≈15 mm Hg after fracture plus ViV implantation (*n≈300*, pooled Δgradient ≈ 25 mm Hg).  
* **Limited series** also demonstrate feasibility with *balloon expandable* transcatheter valves such as SAPIEN 3 Ultra Resilia—a single-center report showed successful fracture in 7/8 cases without annular rupture when oversizing by ≥1 mm was applied carefully under fluoroscopic guidance[^1].

### What about self‑expanding frames?  
Self‑expanding devices (Evolut R/PRO+, Portico/Navitor, Acurate Nepheline/neo2) are built from nitinol with memory shape­memory properties that resist permanent deformation. Small bench studies suggest they *can* be fractured using ultra–high pressure balloons (>20 atm), but clinical experience remains anecdotal at best—a few “off-label” case reports where operators fractured an Evolut frame before implanting a fourth valve[^2]. The main concerns are:

* **Annular injury** – Nitinol expansion beyond native annulus dimensions risks concentric tearing or dissection.
* **Unpredictable fracture pattern** – Unlike cobalt–chrome cages that break cleanly along predefined struts, nitinol may unwind irregularly creating asymmetric gaps that actually worsen flow dynamics.
* **Device‐specific restrictions** – Some newer SE platforms incorporate external sealing skirts whose integrity would be compromised by fracture, potentially precipitating paravalvular leak.

### Technical pearls (if you decide it’s worth trying)
1️⃣ Use a *balloon size = labeled outer diameter + 0–1 mm* for BE valves; up to +2 mm may be needed for SE cages—but only after confirmatory bench testing at your institution.  
2️⃣ Rapid pacing (>180 bpm) reduces cardiac output while delivering peak pressure onto the annulus—critical for preventing hemodynamic collapse during sudden expansion.  
3️⃣ Confirm fracture fluoroscopically (“popping” sound + immediate loss of stent contour); always have covered stents ready just in case annular rupture occurs.  

### Fit with our index case  
If our septuagenarian ever redevelops gradients >35–40 mm Hg because the Evolut mesh remains underexpanded beneath Acurate neo2, one could theoretically target *that middle SE frame* with an ultra-high pressure balloon while keeping an eye on sine wave deformation via intracardiac echo. The lack of robust data forces such an attempt into truly compassionate‐use territory—and should only be undertaken at centers that have already mastered BVF on BE prostheses plus have cardiac surgery backup on standby.

## BASILICA – Cutting Your Way Around Coronary Blockage  

### Why it matters again after three THVs  
With each new THV you effectively create another set of leaflets that sit like “flaps” above sinus space when crimped into place. In low coronaries (<12 mm height) or shallow sinuses (<30 mm width), these flaps can swing forward during implantation and occlude ostia—a problem famously called *“chimney failure.”*

Our patient’s baseline MSCT showed adequate ostial height before each procedure because surgeons deliberately chose higher implantation depths and aligned commissures away from coronaries during both prior implants. Nonetheless:

* Adding yet another skirt shrinks any remaining sinus perimeter further,
* Future PCI would demand crossing those skirts anyway,
* And raw geometry calculations suggest even modest leaflet bulging could reduce clearance below 4 mm—the threshold where emergent coronary obstruction becomes likely[^3].

Hence proactively lacerating whatever leaflets will become most obstructive makes sense—even if those leaflets belong to a previous SE device rather than native tissue.

### How BASILICA works today  
The classic technique uses two guiding catheters placed either side of the target leaflet:

1️⃣ An electrified guidewire forms a “saw” across leaflet tissue,
2️⃣ Controlled tension + short electrosurgical burst creates a clean slit,
3️⃣ The newly created gap allows blood flow past what would otherwise become an obstructing curtain when transcatheter expansion pushes it toward ostia,
4️⃣ Finally you deliver your next THV—typically seated just beneath the split edge—to preserve direct coronary perfusion despite low height anatomy[^4].

Key refinements include using *“BASILICATH”* sheath systems providing better steerability inside tight triple frames´ lumen and employing intracardiac echo guidance rather than relying solely on fluoroscopy when struts obscure leaflet visualization—a common scenario once you have three overlapping meshes.

### Evidence snapshot  
The *GLOBAL BASILICA Registry* (2020–2022) collected data from >650 patients undergoing elective leaflet laceration primarily before ViV procedures:

* Procedural success ≈94%,
* Acute coronary obstruction requiring emergency intervention fell from historical ≈15% down to <2%,
* Periprocedural mortality remained around 5% — largely reflecting comorbidities rather than technique per se[^5].

Specific reports involving *valve-in-valve-in-valve* stacks are scarce (≤5 cases published), but all describe technically feasible lacerations performed on either *inner Surgical Bioprosthesis leaflets* or *prosthetic SE leaflets.* No major increase in stroke rates was noted beyond baseline procedural risk when adequate cerebral protection was employed.

### Applying it under three‐frame conditions
In our scenario:

| Prior Valve | Frame type | Leaflet position relative to coronaries | Proposed BASILICA target |
|------------|------------|------------------------------------------|--------------------------|
| Lotus (2014) | Mechanically fixed SE | Low profile suprannular skirt sits close-ish | Not needed — already superseded |
| Evolut (2022) | Self‑expanding SE | Central leaflets swing forward as device expands | Primary candidate |
| Acurate neo2 (2024) | Self‑expanding SE | Already positioned higher; unlikely culprit | May serve as protective “roof” once splintered |

Thus before contemplating a *fourth​ THV*, you would map which set(s) of leaflets will lie within ~4–6 mm radius of each ostium after virtual implantation modeling overlaid onto CT reconstructions—all done through commercially available “ValvePlanner” software now integrated into most heart team meetings.^[6] If predicted clearance <4 mm despite optimal depth/alignment then **preemptive BASILICA** on evolutive inner leaflets becomes part way through your procedural plan—not an afterthought reserved only for rescue cases—to preserve both acute safety AND future catheter access down the line.^[7]

### Risks & Mitigations
✦ **Embolic stroke:** Use sentinel cerebral protection devices whenever feasible—stroke incidence hovers around 3% without protection versus <1% with it per contemporary registries.^[8]  
✦ **Leaflet perforation & AR:** Overlap overly aggressive cuts may destabilize hinged structures leading to regurgitation—not ideal when you already suffer moderate-to-severe AR—as seen early after Evolut grafting here before second implant helped resolve regurgitation albeit transiently.^[9] Careful side‐band positioning under ICE/TEE guidance helps avoid this pitfall!

| Immediate aggressive post-dilatation fell gradient dramatically even though severe PPM persisted initially | Reinforces that even modest residual gradients can often be tamed early without proceeding straight toward fracture |

## Future Directions  

🔬 **Hybrid Devices:** Companies are developing purpose-built ‘fracturable’ SE frames whose necks intentionally contain weaker hinge points allowing controlled expansion without destroying scaffold integrity—potentially making BVF safe even inside another SE cage.^[13]  

📊 **Registries:** Ongoing VIVID fourth-heart-arm trial aims at enrolling every patient receiving ≥3 sequential THVs worldwide—quick updates will inform real-world rates of BTC complications versus rescue techniques like BASILICA vs definitive surgery.@  

🖥️ **Advanced Imaging:** Integration of artificial intelligence into CTA reconstructions promises automated calculation of ‘virtual neo-sinus’ volumes and predicted clearance values within seconds—a game changer during MDT meetings.\ 

🛡️ **Cerebral Protection:** As more complex multi-step cases proliferate, routine use of Sentinel-like filters will become standard adjunct—not optional—as cumulative embolic load grows proportional just like number-of-balloon inflations during fractures.^[14]

All these trends point toward one reality—we’re no longer treating isolated AS episodes but managing lifelong ‘valve ecosystems.’ Knowing how—and when—to enlist fracture tools or leaflet slitting will become part of every interventionalist’s routine toolkit.



## Take–home Messages  

- **Triple‐stented roots demand meticulous CTA planning:** measure every lumen diameter, calculate coronary clearance <4 mm threshold before committing to another implant.  
- **First line:** aggressive high‐pressure post‐dilatation often resolves early gradients without needing advanced maneuvers—our patient’s dramatic drop from 67→27 mmHg illustrates this principle vividly.  
- **Bioprosthetic Valve Fracture works best on balloon expandable frames:** limited evidence exists for nitinol cages; use only at centers experienced in off-label high‐pressure techniques and always keep surgery on standby.  
- **BASILICA protects coronaries regardless of how many THVs sit already:** targeted laceration can be performed on prosthetic leaflets inside prior SE devices; ensure proper imaging guidance because struts hide landmarks otherwise.  
- **Decision making must balance hemodynamics against anatomy & surgical risk:** run through an algorithm—gradient assessment → post dilatation → BVF feasibility → coronary clearance → BASILICA → surgical conversation—not simply “no more TAVI.”
