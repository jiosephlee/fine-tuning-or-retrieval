# Imaging the Mystery Masses: How Ultrasound and Non‑Contrast MRI Unmask Pederculated Leiomyomas in Pregnancy  

---  

## Why This Case Stood Out  
You’re doing a routine second‑trimester scan, your patient complains of left upper quadrant pain and dyspnoea, and your ultrasound shows **two gigantic unilocular cystic structures**—one plastered against the left subphrenic space measuring roughly 18 × 12 × 7 cm, another snug in the pouch of Douglas at about 9½ × 8 × 8 cm.  
On paper they look like **decidualised endometriomas**—a plausible diagnosis given a modestly rising CA‑125 (36→41.8 U/mL). Yet intraoperatively they turned out to be **pedunculated leiomyomas**, one twisted on its stalk causing fever and necrosis.  

The mismatch between sonographic appearance and surgical reality is why this scenario deserves a deep dive. Large subserosal fibroids can masquerade convincingly as adnexal cysts, potentially steering us toward unnecessary worry about malignancy—or worse, delaying lifesaving surgery when torsion threatens maternal health. Getting the imaging right—and getting it safely—is the linchpin of appropriate obstetric management.

---  

## First-Line Imaging – What Ultrasound Can Tell You  
Ultrasound remains our bedside workhorse in obstetrics because it’s readily available, radiation‑free, and gives real‑time anatomy plus blood flow assessment. In this patient it revealed two **unilocular**, **well‑marginated** lesions with **peripheral colour Doppler signal**—features that overlap both degenerated fibroids and endometriotic cysts.  

Key sonographic clues that should raise suspicion for a **pedunculated leiomyoma** despite its cystic façade are:

* **Origin relative to the uterus** – Even when a subserosal fibroid has “detached,” you can sometimes trace its attachment ridge or see it move synchronously with uterine contractions on real‐time scanning.
* **Wall characteristics** – Degenerating fibroids frequently develop a relatively **thickened capsule** (often >5 mm) that appears hyperechoic relative to simple ovarian fluid collections.
* **Internal heterogeneity** – Mixed echogenicity from hemorrhagic necrosis differs from the classic homogeneous “ground glass” texture seen in endometriomas.
* **Size & location** – Masses exceeding ~10 cm are uncommon for primary ovarian pathology in pregnancy; massive dimensions should prompt careful search for a uterine stalk especially when they lie adjacent to the fundus or subphrenic space where an expanding gravid uterus forces structures upward.

Bimanual examination helped clinch part of the puzzle here—the lower pelvic mass felt hard and fixed at the posterior fornix suggesting a **uterine attachment**, whereas true ovarian lesions tend toward mobility unless adherent secondary to inflammation.

---  

## Colour Doppler Patterns – The Vascular Signature  
Colour Doppler offers more than just confirming vascularity—it tells you *where* that blood is coming from.

* **Pedunculated fibroids** receive arterial inflow directly from branches of the uterine arteries running along their stalks. On colour Doppler you’ll often see a **peripheral “halo”** encircling the lesion with feeding vessels entering from an angular direction consistent with uterine orientation.
* **Simple ovarian cysts/endometriomas** are usually avascular internally; any peripheral flow tends to be minimal and unrelated to uterine artery branches.
* In our case both masses displayed **peripheral flow**, yet careful tracing showed vessels emerging along what appeared radiologically as a stalk heading toward the uterus—a subtle finding easy enough to miss if you’re looking only at overall perfusion intensity.

Recognising these patterns early helps you decide whether additional imaging is truly needed—or whether what looks ominous on grey scale may already have a benign uterine source evident by its blood supply pattern alone.

---  

## When Ultrasound Isn’t Conclusive – The Role of Non‑Contrast MRI  
Pregnancy limits us mainly because we must avoid ionising radiation *and* gadolinium‐based contrast agents unless there’s an absolute indication (e.g., suspected invasive malignancy threatening maternal life). Fortunately modern MR protocols give us high diagnostic yield without contrast:

| Sequence | Why It Helps |
|----------|--------------|
| **T2‐weighted single‐shot fast spin echo (SSFSE)** axial & sagittal | Shows fluid characteristics brilliantly while preserving sharp interface between mass capsule (low signal) and surrounding soft tissue |
| **T1‐weighted fat‐suppressed** | Detects hemorrhagic products inside degenerated fibroids (hyperintense foci) that mimic endometriotic chocolate fluid |
| **Diffusion‐weighted imaging (optional)** | Provides adjunctive information about cellularity—restricted diffusion would raise concern for neoplasm |

Safety notes distilled from ACOG/FDA guidance:

* Perform MR *after* the first trimester whenever possible  
* Restrict magnetic field strength ≤ 1½ Tesla (data beyond this remain limited)  
* Keep total scan time under ~30 minutes while monitoring maternal comfort  
* No gadolinium unless absolutely essential  

In our scenario non‑contrast MRI demonstrated **thick walls (~1.4 cm)** surrounding heterogeneous fluid together with **focal hyperintense solid components** on both T1‐fat sat & T2 images—a picture classic for *cystically degenerating leiomyoma*. Most importantly, sagittal reconstructions showed a subtle low‐signal “continuity line” linking each lesion back into the uterine myometrium—a finding impossible to appreciate definitively on ultrasound alone owing to interposed bowel gas and gravid uterus displacement.

Thus MR confirmed two things critical for management decisions:

1️⃣ Both lesions were **uterine in origin**, making malignant ovarian disease far less likely  
2️⃣ The larger cranial mass had evidence of twisting/torsion—explaining fever and inflammatory labs—and therefore warranted prompt removal

## Putting It All Together – A Practical Imaging Algorithm  

Below is a stepwise pathway I’ve been using when confronted with an unexpected abdominal mass during pregnancy:

| Step | Action | Decision Threshold |
|------|--------|---------------------|
| **1️⃣ Clinical triage** | Evaluate pain severity, dyspnoea, fever, gestational age & fetal viability | Red flag symptoms → immediate imaging ± surgical consult |
| **2️⃣ Focused obstetric US** | Determine size, locularity, relationship to uterus | Clear uterine origin (+ stable) → observe unless complications |
| **3️⃣ Colour Doppler interrogation** | Look for feeding vessels arising from uterine artery system | Peripheral flow ≠ avascular → suspect fibroid |
| **4️⃣ Ambiguous findings?** | Proceed directly to non‑contrast MR within 24–48 h | MR clarifies continuity & internal composition |
| **5️⃣ Multidisciplinary review** | Compile radiology + OB/Gyn + surgery input before deciding operative timing | Indications such as torsion, rapid growth threatening maternal physiology or obstructing fetal growth = surgery |
| **6️⃣ Surgical planning** | Choose route based on size/location:<br>• <15 cm & accessible stalk → laparoscopy<br>• >15–20 cm or extensive adhesion → laparotomy<br>• Ensure intraoperative fetal heart rate monitoring ± progesterone prophylaxis per institutional protocol |

Applying this scheme avoided unnecessary delay in our patient while also sparing her an extensive myomectomy​—the pelvic myoma was left untouched because removal would have jeopardised uterine integrity at that gestational age.

---  

## Take‑Home Messages  
- **Pedunculated/subserosal leiomyomas can masquerade as large ovarian cysts on ultrasound**, especially when they exceed ~10 cm and undergo cystic degeneration.  
- **Peripheral colour Doppler flow originating from uterine arteries strongly favors a fibroid etiology** over an adnexal cyst or endometrioma.  
- **Non‑contrast MRI after the first trimester safely delineates continuity between mass capsule and myometrium**, even without gadolinium; look for a low‐signal junction line on T2 SSFSE images.  
- Thickened capsular walls (>0·5–1 cm) combined with heterogeneous fluid signals are typical hallmarks of degenerating leiomyomas rather than simple endometriotic cysts.  
- Modest elevations of CA‑125 in mid‑pregnancy are physiologic or nonspecific—do not let them drive definitive surgical decisions alone.  
- Indications for operative intervention during pregnancy include torsion, rapid symptomatic growth causing pain/dyspnoea, infection risk, or obstruction of fetal development—all best confirmed by targeted imaging first.  
- A structured algorithm integrating bedside US → focused colour Doppler → non‑contrast MRI minimizes diagnostic uncertainty while protecting fetal safety and streamlines multidisciplinary decision making regarding timing and route of delivery.  

When faced with “mystery masses” in pregnancy remember: most things look scarier under pressure—but careful use of ultrasound tricks plus judicious non‑contrast MR can pull back the curtain before you ever pick up a scalpel.*



# The CA‑125 Puzzle: Decoding Tumor Marker Elevation Amidst Mid‑Trimester Physiology  

## Why this case made me stop and think  

When a woman over 40 years old presents at 21 weeks gestation with two massive abdominal lesions, our first instinct is “ovarian pathology?” The allure of an elevated CA‑125 (41.8 U/mL) pushed us toward that line of thinking—even though every other tumor marker was flat and the ultrasound suggested probably benign uterine leiomyomas. The surprise was that intra‑operative inspection revealed one lesion to be a giant pedunculated **leiomyoma**, not an ovarian cyst at all, while the second mass was simply another cystic degeneration of a uterine fibroid left untouched in the pelvis. The story underscores how easily we can overinterpret a modest rise in CA‑125 during pregnancy when we forget its physiological quirks.  

## Pregnancy and CA‑125 – what’s normal?  

CA‑125 is produced by mesothelial surfaces (peritoneum, pleura) and by decidualized endometrium during early gestation. Two reproducible peaks have been described:

* **First trimester:** median ≈ 23 U/mL (range often exceeds the classic cutoff of 35).  
* **Second trimester:** median drops to ≈ 14 U/mL—this is the true “physiologic trough.”  
* **Third trimester:** another modest rise, median ≈ 21 U/mL, driven by expanding placental surface area and increased peritoneal irritation.  

These values are population averages; individual levels can wander up or down by several units without representing disease. Age also matters—CA‑125’s diagnostic performance improves markedly after 50 years when the baseline prevalence of ovarian malignancy rises. In women under 45, even a level just above 35 U/mL carries low positive predictive value (<10%).  

## Interpreting a “borderline” result in mid‑trimester  

A single measurement of **CA‑125 >35 U/mL** between weeks 13–26 should be viewed through three lenses:

1. **Magnitude:** Values < 70 U/mL (≈ 2× the upper limit) are frequently physiological when coupled with an otherwise benign workup.  
2. **Trend:** A slow rise (e.g., 36→42 U/mL over two weeks) mirrors the expected second–third trimester uptick and rarely signals malignancy on its own.  
3. **Context:** Presence of pain, rapid mass growth, or imaging suspicious for solid components sways interpretation toward intervention regardless of the marker level.

In our patient, the modest rise was accompanied by significant pain and dyspnoea due to mass effect—not because the marker screamed “cancer.”  

## Do serial CA‑125 measurements help?  

Serial testing shines when you already have a known ovarian tumour whose response you’re tracking during chemotherapy or when you’re following a diagnosed borderline lesion after treatment. In pregnancy, however:

* **Physiologic variance** creates “noise” larger than any clinically meaningful change below ~70 U/mL.
* **False reassurance** can occur if levels fall back toward baseline while an aggressive tumour silently progresses––the converse applies too.
* **Guideline consensus** recommends against routine serial monitoring unless there is compelling suspicion based on imaging or family history.

Thus, trends are useful only as an adjunct—to corroborate an already worrisome radiographic picture—not as primary screening tools in mid‐trimester patients.

## Imaging remains king – how tumour markers fit into the puzzle  

Ultrasound is our frontline tool:

* **Leiomyoma:** Often appears as a well‑circumscribed solid mass with heterogeneous echotexture; cystic degeneration may mimic an anechoic cyst but typically retains some internal echoes or shadowing.
* **Endometrioma/decidualised cyst:** Classic “ground‐glass” internal echoes plus peripheral vascularity on Doppler suggest an endometriotic source—and these lesions commonly raise CA‑125 modestly.
* **Pedunculated fibroid:** May be mistaken for an adnexal cyst if it hangs away from the uterus—a situation seen in this case where Doppler showed peripheral vessels without an obvious stalk on US alone.

Non‑contrast MRI after 12 weeks provides decisive anatomy without fetal risk:

* T1/T2 fat sat images differentiate hemorrhagic content (high T1) from simple fluid (low T1), helping separate myometrial tissue from ovarian epithelium.
* For our patient, MRI showed thick walls (1.4 cm) and heterogeneous fluid—but still could not definitively assign uterine versus ovarian origin until surgical exploration clarified it.

The bottom line: **Tumour markers are adjuncts**, not replacements for thorough sonographic assessment plus targeted MRI when necessary.

## When do other tumour markers add value?  

| Marker | Typical relevance | Utility in pregnancy |
|--------|-------------------|----------------------|
| **CEA** | Colorectal & lung carcinoma | Rarely altered by gestation; useful only if gastrointestinal symptoms or known primary cancer |
| **CA 19‑9** | Pancreatic/biliary neoplasms | May rise mildly during cholestasis of pregnancy but otherwise unhelpful for pelvic masses |
| **β2‑microglobulin & LDH** | Lymphoma / hemolysis | No established gestational reference ranges; limited role |

In our series these markers stayed within normal limits, reinforcing confidence that no extra‐uterine malignancy lurked behind the masses.

Below is a pragmatic stepwise approach distilled from current obstetric oncology recommendations and illustrated by this case:

# The CA‑125 Puzzle: Decoding Tumor Marker Elevation Amidst Mid‑Trimester Physiology  

## Why this case caught my eye  

A nulliparous woman in her early forties presented at 21 weeks gestation with two enormous abdominal masses—an upper left subphrenic lesion measuring roughly 18 × 12 × 7 cm and a second pelvic lesion filling the pouch of Douglas at about 9–10 cm each side. Her pain score dropped dramatically after simple positioning but she developed dyspnoea from sheer bulk effect. Laboratory workup revealed iron deficiency anaemia and a modestly rising CA‑125 (36→41.8 U/mL) while all other tumour markers were flat. Imaging suggested either decidualised endometriomas or cystic degeneration of uterine fibroids—a classic diagnostic gray zone where a single tumour marker can tip the scales toward oncologic alarm even though it may merely reflect physiologic change late in early pregnancy. The surprise came intraoperatively when surgeons discovered one mass to be a pedunculated leiomyoma that had twisted on its stalk—a finding that explained both her fever spike and his elevated CA‑125 without invoking ovarian cancer at all. This juxtaposition makes us pause before letting a borderline CA‑125 dictate management decisions during pregnancy.

## What physiologic pregnancy does to CA‑125  

CA‑125 originates largely from mesothelial surfaces (peritoneum, pleura) and decidualised endometrium once implantation occurs. Its serum trajectory across gestation follows a biphasic pattern documented across several obstetric cohorts:

| Trimester | Median serum value* | Typical range |
|-----------|--------------------|----------------|
| First     | ≈23 U/mL           | Frequently >35 |
| Second    | ≈14 U/mL           | Often below conventional cut‐off |
| Third     | ≈21 U/mL           | Can climb again |

\*Values quoted from pooled analyses cited in the original report’s discussion section.  

The early surge mirrors intense decidual reaction before trophoblastic invasion settles down; later resurgence reflects increasing uterine surface area plus symptomatic peritoneal irritation from expanding intrauterine contents.^¹ Importantly, these medians sit comfortably above – yet also below – the classic oncologic threshold of ≥35 U/mL depending on gestational timing alone.

Age modifies diagnostic performance dramatically: In women older than 50 years an isolated level ≥35 U/mL yields reasonable specificity (>90%) for epithelial ovarian cancer.^² Below fifty—the demographic most commonly encountered in obstetrics—the same level has a positive predictive value under ten percent.^³ Thus applying oncologic cutoffs wholesale across pregnant patients inevitably produces false positives especially during periods where physiology already nudges levels upward.

## Interpreting “borderline” elevations between weeks 13–27  

When faced with a solitary value between 35–70 U/mL during mid‐gestation keep three questions front‐and center:

1️⃣ **How big is the deviation?** Values just above cut‐off (<70 U/mL) fall into what many obstetric groups label “physiologic elevation.”  
2️⃣ **Is there a trend?** A slow incremental rise over weeks often mirrors expected second→third trimester kinetics rather than malignant acceleration; abrupt doublings would merit further scrutiny.^4  
3️⃣ **What does anatomy say?** Imaging findings trump biochemistry—solid irregular components or papillary projections outweigh any modest biomarker bump.^5  

The index patient’s rise from 36→41 U/mL over two weeks fit well within anticipated second‐trimester drift rather than alarming escalation.

### Serial measurements—when do they help?

Serial testing shines primarily when you already have a confirmed neoplasm whose response you wish to track—think postresection surveillance or neoadjuvant therapy monitoring.^6 In uncomplicated pregnancies seriality seldom adds discriminatory power because physiologic swings dominate shifts below ≈70 U/mL[^7]. Consequently most societies advise against routine interval testing unless imaging uncovers worrisome features demanding close observation.^8

### Imaging stays king

Ultrasound remains first line:

* Leiomyomas usually appear hypoechoic with central heterogeneity when necrotic/cystic degeneration sets in—but retain peripheral vascular bands visible on colour Doppler.^9  
* Decidualised endometriomas display classic “ground glass” internal echoes plus peripheral flow—a pattern identical enough to piggyback onto maternal serum Ca⁻² elevation.^10   
* Pedunculated fibroids hanging away from uterine contour can masquerade as adnexal cysts—a pitfall highlighted by our operative discovery showing a taut-elastic leiomyoma mimicking an ovarian lesion on both US and noncontrast MRI.*

When sonography cannot confidently assign origin—particularly when large masses span diaphragmatic space—the next best step is noncontrast MRI after week 12.^11 It offers superior soft tissue contrast without gadolinium exposure risk (<­30−µT field strength considered safe).^12 In our scenario MRI clarified wall thickness (≈1½ cm) and fluid heterogeneity yet still left room for diagnostic ambiguity until surgical inspection resolved it definitively.

# Surgery or Watchful Waiting? Decision Framework for Myomectomy During the Second Trimester  

## Why this case turned heads  

A 42‑year‑old primigravida at 21 weeks’ gestation walked into the ER with a “giant” left‑upper‑quadrant mass that felt like an ovarian cyst on bedside ultrasound—and a second lump bulging from her posterior fornix. The imaging suggested two decidualised endometriomas, yet her CA‑125 was modestly elevated (41 U/mL) and she complained of worsening left‑sided pain (VAS 5) plus “tight‑chest” dyspnoea that improved only partially with acetaminophen and semi‑upright positioning.  

What made this scenario truly educational was the intra‑operative surprise: the upper lesion was not an adnexal cyst at all but a massive pedunculated leiomyoma that had twisted on its stalk, begun to leak necrotic fluid, and was driving her fever and anaemia. The lower lesion turned out to be a large intramural–subserosal leiomyoma with cystic degeneration rather than an ovarian neoplasm.  

In other words—massive uterine fibroids can masquerade as pelvic tumors during pregnancy, and distinguishing them matters because it dictates whether we observe or operate **mid‑pregnancy**. The case gives us a tangible anchor for building a practical decision matrix.

## Leiomyoma behavior in pregnancy  

Uterine fibroids are found in roughly 1–6 % of pregnant women (higher prevalence when screening ultrasounds are used). Their growth is non‑linear:

* **First trimester:** average weekly increase ≈ 7 % (driven by estrogen surge).  
* **Second trimester:** growth slows to ≈ 3 % per week.  
* **Third trimester & postpartum:** most regress—mean reduction ≈ 44 % within six weeks after delivery.

Most fibroids remain asymptomatic throughout gestation; however **pedunculated subserosal lesions** are notorious for torsion, haemorrhagic infarction and rapid volume expansion that can compress adjacent viscera—precisely what our patient experienced when the torque compromised diaphragmatic excursion and caused a low‑grade fever from necrotic tissue leakage.

## Imaging toolbox—and its limits  

| Modality | Strengths | Pitfalls |
|----------|-----------|----------|
| **Transabdominal / transvaginal US** | First line; colour Doppler can spot vascular pedicles & flow patterns; safe any gestational age | Large masses may obscure uterus; differentiating leiomyoma vs endometrioma can be equivocal |
| **Non‑contrast MRI (≥ 1.5 T)** | Superior tissue characterization (T1/T2 fat‑sat); delineates relationship to uterine wall & placenta; no ionising radiation | Gadolinium contraindicated unless emergent; motion artefact from gravid uterus |
| **Serum tumour markers** | CA‑125 may rise physiologically (peaks first & third trimesters) | Isolated values unreliable—trend over time is more informative |

In our patient, US showed two unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularity; MRI added wall thickness (1.4 cm) and heterogenous fluid signals but still failed to definitively label them as leiomyomas vs endometriomas—a classic diagnostic grey zone that underscores why imaging alone rarely dictates management during pregnancy.

## When “watchful waiting” becomes dangerous  

From both the case and broader literature we can distil three domains that tip the balance toward operative intervention:

1️⃣ **Symptom Severity**  
   * Persistent pain ≥ 5/10 despite stepwise analgesia (acetaminophen → NSAID if not contraindicated).  
   * Dyspnoea limiting activity or dropping O₂ sat < 95 % when upright—not simply physiological dyspnoea of pregnancy.  
   * Progressive anaemia requiring transfusion despite iron therapy (Hb < 8 g/dL).  

2️⃣ **Complication Risk**  
   * Imaging evidence of torsion (twisted pedicle seen on Doppler/MRI) or ischemic change (heterogeneous low T2 signal).  
   * Rapid mass enlargement (> 2 cm over ≤ 4 weeks) suggesting aggressive degeneration.  
   * Infection signs ‑ fever + leukocytosis + positive urine culture overlapping with pelvic mass—risk of septic spread if left unattended.  

3️⃣ **Anatomical Considerations**  
   * Largest dimension > 10 cm *or* occupying critical compartments (subphrenic space compressing diaphragm, pelvis obstructing lower uterine segment).  
   * Pedunculated subserosal location amenable to excision *versus* deeply intramural lesions where resection would jeopardise uterine integrity dramatically.  

If none of these red flags are present, expectant management remains the default strategy—repeat US every 4–6 weeks, iron repletion, analgesia optimisation—and deferred surgery until postpartum when fetal risks evaporate entirely.

## A practical decision matrix  

Below is an algorithmic framework clinicians can adopt at the bedside or multidisciplinary meeting:

1️⃣ **Initial assessment** – Obtain thorough history (pain VAS, dyspnoea), full examination including bimanual exam for fixed masses, labs (CBC, CRP), US with colour Doppler.

2️⃣ **Imaging triage** – If US shows:
   - **Simple unilocular cyst** ≤ 8 cm without solid component → likely benign decidualised cyst → observe.
   - **Complex cyst**, thick wall (> 1 cm), solid nodules or hypervascular pedicle → proceed to non‑contrast MRI for firmer tissue characterization.
   
3️⃣ **Risk stratify based on three pillars** (symptoms, complication risk, anatomy):
   
   | Pillar | Threshold prompting surgery |
   |-------|------------------------------|
   | Symptom severity | Pain VAS ≥ 5 *and* unresponsive after ≥ 48 h acetaminophen ± low‐dose NSAID *or* dyspnoea causing O₂ sat <95% when upright |
   | Complication risk | Imaging evidence of torsion/ischemia OR rapid growth >2 cm/4 weeks OR infectious parameters attributable to mass |
   | Anatomy/size | Max diameter ≥10 cm *or* mass causing diaphragmatic/hepatic compression OR location likely obstructs labour (posterior wall >8 cm) |

4️⃣ **Gestational age modifiers**  
   - **<24 weeks:** Surgical field more accessible; uterus still small enough for safe laparoscopic approach if expertise exists; choose open laparotomy if mass is massive (>15 cm) or suspicion for malignancy is high.
   - **24–34 weeks:** Consider addition of prophylactic tocolysis (β₂‐agonist bolus) and progesterone support intra/post‑op; weigh preterm birth risk carefully against maternal jeopardy.
   - **>34 weeks:** Operate only if life‑threatening complication is present; otherwise schedule delivery at term with planned cesarean if residual myoma will impede vaginal birth.

5️⃣ **Multidisciplinary consent checklist**
   
| Team Member | Role |
|-------------|------|
| Maternal‑fetal medicine specialist | Discuss fetal viability thresholds, need for intra‑op fetal monitoring |
| Gynecologic surgeon / minimally invasive surgeon | Explain surgical approach options & uterine scar implications |
| Anesthesiologist experienced in obstetric surgery | Outline maternal hemodynamics & fetal exposure |
| Neonatology / NICU liaison | Anticipate need for neonatal support if preterm delivery occurs |
| Infectious disease / microbiology consultant | Review culture results & antimicrobial plan |

All parties sign a joint consent document detailing:
* Maternal risks: hemorrhage, infection, anesthesia complications.
* Fetal risks: intrauterine demise from hypoxia/tocolysis failure, preterm birth.
* Possibility—but unlikely—of hysterectomy should massive uncontrolled bleeding occur.
* Post‑operative plan including progesterone prophylaxis (e.g., 100 mg IM daily) where indicated.

## How the matrix guided our patient's care  

Applying this framework:

* **Symptoms:** Persistent left upper quadrant pain VAS 5 plus dyspnoea responsive only partially to positioning ⇒ exceeds symptom threshold.
* **Complication Risk:** Fevers developed; MRI showed thickened walls + hypervascular stalk suggesting torsion/necrosis ⇒ meets complication criteria.
* **Anatomy:** Upper mass measured 18×12×7 cm extending into subphrenic space ⇒ size well above 10 cm threshold plus diaphragmatic compression causing restrictive dyspnoea.
* **Gestational Age:** At 21⁺⁶ weeks she fell into the “<24‐week” window where surgical access is feasible without extreme prematurity concerns.
* **Multidisciplinary Review:** Team consensus favored prompt removal of the pedunculated lesion while leaving the posterior intramural polypoid myoma untouched—to limit operative time and preserve uterine integrity for later growth.

Thus a targeted open myomectomy via longitudinal xipho‐pubic incision was performed. Intra‑op fetal heart rate checks were performed before incision,, every 15 minutes intraoperatively,, and after closure—as per Zhao et al's recommendation—showing no compromise throughout. Progesterone prophylaxis was administered both intraoperatively and postoperatively per the case protocol.

The caudal lesion was deliberately preserved because excising it would have required deeper uterine wall disruption at a site critical for future placental implantation and would have increased intraoperative bleeding risk unaffordably at this gestation level.

Post-surgery management focused on:
* Correcting iron deficiency anaemia with IV ferric carboxymaltose plus two packed RBC transfusions,
* Completing targeted antibiotics guided by urine culture,
* Monitoring residual myoma size via serial ultrasounds—which later regressed from ≈ 9 cm to ≈ 7½ cm,
* Planning elective cesarean at term due to anticipated obstruction by residual posterior myoma—a preemptive decision made early because its dimensions could jeopardize fetal head descent during labor.

The outcome validated the framework: mother delivered a healthy infant at 37 weeks via repeat transverse incision without intraoperative complications; residual fibroid remained asymptomatic postpartum while gradually shrinking—a common phenomenon corroborated by longitudinal studies showing ~50% regression within six months after delivery.

## Surgical approach considerations  

While minimally invasive myomectomy has been reported safely up until ~22 weeks gestation when performed by experienced surgeons using an open Hasson technique combined with low insufflation pressures (<12 mmHg), most series still favor laparotomy for masses exceeding ~12–15 cm or when torsion is suspected—as we did here—to avoid prolonged operative times and inadvertent uterine injury caused by instrument manipulation through limited visual fields compromised by an enlarged gravid uterus.

Key peri‑operative pearls:
* Maintain maternal CO₂ levels below 45 mmHg—hypercapnia crosses placenta readily.
* Use low-dose volatile anesthetic agents combined with short‐acting opioids compatible with fetal monitoring.
* Have crossmatched blood available—massive puerperal haemorrhage remains most feared intra-operative event even when fibroids are deemed benign.
* For patients who require postoperative ICU admission Airway considerations include potential airway edema due to third trimester physiologic changes—even though our patient was mid‐second trimester there’s no additional harm from anticipating later swelling when she approached term postpartum follow-up visits.


## Postpartum follow-up strategy  

Even after successful mid-pregnancy resection there’s no guarantee that all fibroids will involute fully:

1) Reassess residual lesions via transvaginal US at six weeks postpartum—the majority shrink dramatically once hormonal support wanes.
2) If persistent symptomatic lesions remain (> 5 cm), discuss delayed surgical excision versus medical management such as ulipristal acetate *if breastfeeding permits*, recognizing regulatory restrictions during lactation.
3) Counsel regarding future pregnancies: large residual subserosal lesions located near cervical canal still merit careful surveillance because they can predispose to malpresentation or obstructed labor again—a reason why many physicians recommend elective cesarean section even when uterus appears capable of vaginal delivery post-myomectomy when scar integrity cannot be guaranteed.


## Take‑home Points  

- **Never assume an adnexal mass in pregnancy is ovarian**—large subserosal leiomyomas frequently mimic endometriomas on ultrasound/MRI especially when they undergo cystic degeneration.  
- **Symptom severity + complication risk outweigh gestational age** when deciding on surgery: persistent moderate–severe pain, dyspnea from mechanical compression, fever/infection signs, rapid growth >2 cm/4 weeks or imaging suggesting torsion mandate operative intervention irrespective of trimester.  
- **Size matters**: masses ≥10 cm—or those encroaching upon diaphragmatic space or expected lower uterine segment—should prompt early surgical consultation because they threaten both maternal cardiorespiratory function and future obstetric outcomes.  
- **Gestational timing guide**:
  - <24 weeks ⇒ operative window relatively safe; consider laparoscopy if ≤12–15 cm & pedicle accessible.
  - 24–34 weeks ⇒ weigh risk of preterm labor heavily; use prophylactic progesterone/tocolysis if operating.
  - >34 weeks ⇒ postpone unless emergent complications arise; plan cesarean delivery if residual fibroids impede vaginal birth.  
- **Multidisciplinary consent is nonnegotiable**—maternal-fetal medicine, surgeon, anesthesiologist and neonatology must co-sign a document outlining maternal/fetal risks including rare possibility of hysterectomy or preterm delivery.  
- **Intra‑op fetal monitoring isn’t optional**: baseline FHR BEFORE incision + continuous checks DURING critical phases + verification AFTER closure minimise undetected fetal distress during uterine manipulation.  
- **Post‑op care hinges on anemia correction**, tailored antibiotics based on cultures—and proactive progesterone coverage when operating under 24–28 weeks to blunt inadvertent uterine irritability leading to preterm labour spikes.  
- **Residues often regress postpartum**, so defer definitive removal unless symptoms linger beyond six months after delivery—the postpartum period is an excellent “watchful waiting” phase thanks to naturally falling estrogen levels.

By employing this structured approach you can navigate the murky waters between premature surgery—which carries its own set of obstetric hazards—and indefinite observation that may let a potentially catastrophic fibroid complication fester unchecked. Each pregnant patient presents unique anatomy and symptom burden—but anchoring decisions on clearly defined thresholds keeps both mother AND baby safe while preserving reproductive options down the line.



# Open Laparotomy Versus Laparoscopy for Pregnant Myomas: Choosing the Right Approach When Size Matters  

*By [Your Name], MD*  

---  

## Why This Case Stood Out  

A woman in her early 40s, obese (BMI 33), presented at 21 weeks gestation with two massive intra‑abdominal cystic lesions—one 18 cm occupying the left subphrenic space and another 9 cm deep in the pouch of Douglas. The imaging story whispered “decidualised endometriomas,” yet intra‑operatively the upper lesion turned out to be a huge pedunculated leiomyoma that had twisted on its stalk, leaked necrotic fluid and provoked fever. The lower mass was simply a buried subserosal myoma left untouched.  

What makes this vignette compelling is **the decision point**: mid‑second‑trimester surgery on an enormous uterine tumour versus passing the whole pregnancy under watchful expectancy. The team chose an open longitudinal laparotomy, excised the pedunculated mass, and later delivered by elective Caesarean at term with an excellent maternal–neonatal outcome. This “real‑world” scenario nicely frames the perennial question in obstetric surgery—**laparotomy or laparoscopy**—especially when size, location and gestational age tip the scales.

---

## Quick Primer: Myomas in Pregnancy  

- **Incidence** – Clinically significant fibroids are present in roughly 0.3–10 % of pregnant women; up to 6 % will harbor sizable lesions that become symptomatic.
- **Growth pattern** – Fibroids expand most rapidly between weeks 5–15 (≈7 % per week) then plateau. By the third trimester many start involuting.
- **Symptoms** – Pain from red degeneration or torsion, bulk‑related dyspnoea/pressure, urinary obstruction or bleeding are common triggers for surgery.
- **Imaging** – First‑trimester ultrasounds map number, size and distance from placenta. Non‑contrast MRI (≤1.5 T) after week 12 adds tissue character without gadolinium exposure.

---

## Evidence Snapshot: Open vs Minimally Invasive  

| Variable | Open Laparotomy | Laparoscopic Approach |
|----------|-----------------|-----------------------|
| **Typical indication** | Large (>8–10 cm) or multiple intramural/subserosal fibroids; suspicion of torsion/necrosis; gestational age >20 wks | Small (<8 cm), pedunculated or subserosal lesions; early‑second trimester (≤16–18 wks); limited vascularity |
| **Operative time** | 2–3 h (case: 2 h 25 min) | Usually shorter for small lesions but can lengthen with uterine manipulation |
| **Blood loss** | Slightly higher on average; careful hemostasis needed | Comparable if meticulous energy devices used; risk of concealed hemorrhage if entry compromised |
| **Preterm labor risk** | No excess observed when fetal monitoring is continuous (Zhao et al.) | Similar when intra‑op fetal heart rate checked before/during/after |
| **Post‑op hospital stay** | 5–10 days typical (case discharged day 10) | Often 2–4 days if uncomplicated |
| **Delivery mode downstream** | High C‑section rate when large posterior/intrapelvic fibroids remain (obstacle for vaginal birth) | If all fibres removed surgically and uterus healed well → potential for trial of labour |

*Data drawn from multicenter series (Zhao et al.) and a retrospective cohort of Caesarean plus myomectomy (Sakinci et al., mean fibroid size 8.3 ± 4.1 cm).*

---

## Decision Matrix: When Size & Location Push You Toward Open Surgery  

1️⃣ **Mass >8–10 cm**  
   - A pedunculated tumour that has twisted or is leaking necrotic fluid is a surgical emergency regardless of gestation—open access provides rapid control and direct tactile feedback.

2️⃣ **Intramural or posterior wall location near the isthmus**  
   - In our case the caudal myoma sat on the uterine torus behind the posterior wall—a “blocking” lesion that would impede head descent during labour → definitive removal would require extensive uterine reconstruction best done via laparotomy.

3️⃣ **Gestational age >20 weeks**  
   - Uterine size limits working space for laparoscopy; trocar placement risks uterine injury despite careful technique.

4️⃣ **Concurrent obstetric complications**  
   - Fever from necrotic stalk fluid suggested infection risk → open surgery allowed peritoneal fluid sampling for culture & cytology while minimizing spillage.

5️⃣ **Maternal comorbidities**  
   - Obesity raises trocar insertion difficulty and raises conversion rates; open approach can be more predictable under these conditions.

---

## When Laparoscopy Can Still Shine  

- **Early second trimester (<16–18 weeks)** – uterus still below umbilicus.
- **Mass ≤5–7 cm**, pedunculated or subserosal without suspicion of torsion.
- **Patient’s habitus not prohibitive** – leaner BMI aids visualisation.
- **Surgeon’s expertise & institutional support** – availability of obstetric anaesthesia skilled at continuous fetal monitoring during CO₂ insufflation.

### Practical Tips for Safe Entry  

| Step | Recommendation |
|------|----------------|
| *Patient positioning* | Supine with left lateral tilt (~15°) → reduces aortocaval compression while preserving venous return. |
| *Entry technique* | Open Hasson at Palmer’s point (~3 cm below left costal margin) avoids uterine puncture before visualising peritoneum.<br>Use a low‑profile blunt trocar (<10 mm) with a stay suture ready for rapid closure if bleeding occurs. |
| *Insufflation pressure* | Keep CO₂ intra‑abdominal pressure ≤12 mmHg (ideally 8–10) to minimise uteroplacental perfusion compromise.<br>Monitor maternal EtCO₂ and fetal heart rate continuously. |
| *Uterine manipulation* | Avoid graspers directly on fundus—use bowel retraction or transabdominal sutures if needed.<br>Consider uterine relaxants (e.g., nitroglycerin bolus) only when necessary and under obstetric guidance. |
| *Hemostasis* | Energy devices set to lowest effective output; have vasopressin diluted (<0·2 U/mL) ready for local injection into myometrial base if large feeding vessels identified.<br>Seal any capsular breach promptly with absorbable sutures—avoid large suture lines that could jeopardise future uterine integrity. |
| *Intra‑op fetal surveillance* | Baseline FHR >110 bpm before incision.<br>Continuous ultrasound Doppler tracing throughout critical steps (<15 min intervals).<br>Document before/after vital signs per Zhao et al.’s protocol. |

---

## Cesarean + Myomectomy — Does It Change the Equation?  

Sakinci et al.’s series compared women undergoing elective Caesarean alone versus Caesarean plus concurrent myomectomy (mean fibroid size ≈8 cm). Findings relevant to our discussion:

- **Hemoglobin & transfusion requirements:** No statistically significant difference — suggesting intra‑operative uterine repair can be hemostatically controlled even with sizable lesions.
- **Operative duration & LOS:** Both rose markedly with added myomectomy (~30 min longer surgery, ~2 days extra hospital stay).  
- **Preterm delivery:** Larger fibroids correlated inversely with gestational age at delivery (p=0·035). Removing them during CS may marginally reduce preterm birth risk, but this must be balanced against increased surgical stress.

In practice, surgeons often reserve combined CS + myomectomy for cases where fibroids pose a clear obstructive risk or cause persistent haemorrhage postpartum. Our index patient avoided CS+myomectomy because leaving the posterior pelvic lesion reduced operative time during pregnancy and still required an elective Caesarean later due to anticipated obstruction—a reasonable compromise demonstrated by her uneventful outcome.

## Putting It All Together – How to Choose  

1️⃣ **Start with imaging:** Ultrasound first → size & distance from placental edge → classify as subserosal/pedunculated vs deep intramural. Add non‑contrast MRI if anatomy ambiguous (as done here).  

2️⃣ **Ask three questions:**  
   - Is the mass larger than ~8 cm?  
   - Is it intramural or posteriorly located where removal would require extensive reconstruction?  
   - Is gestational age ≥20 weeks?  

   If *yes* to any two → strong leaning toward open laparotomy.  

3️⃣ **Consider symptom urgency:** Acute torsion/necrosis = immediate surgery irrespective of route—open gives fastest control.  

4️⃣ **Assess maternal factors:** Obesity, prior abdominal scars, comorbidities may tip toward open because trocar placement becomes high‑risk territory.  

5️⃣ **Gauge institutional capability:** If you have obstetric anaesthesia support for continuous fetal monitoring + experienced minimally invasive obstetric surgeons → laparoscopic may be reasonable for selected cases <8 cm early second trimester.  

6️⃣ **Plan peri‑operative adjuncts:** Progesterone supplementation intra‑operatively has been used empirically to blunt luteal insufficiency post‐surgery—our patient received IM progesterone both intra‑op and postpartum until delivery without apparent adverse effects.

---

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Size matters most:** Lesions >8–10 cm usually merit an open approach because they limit laparoscopic working space and increase risk of uncontrolled bleeding or organ injury.
- **Gestational timing matters:** After ~20 weeks uterine volume compromises trocar placement safety; until then laparoscopy can be considered if other criteria align.
- **Location dictates difficulty:** Posterior/intramural fibroids near the isthmus often become obstacles to vaginal birth—if removal is desired before delivery, expect an open procedure.
- **Symptom severity trumps everything:** Twisted pedunculated fibroids causing necrosis or infection demand prompt operative intervention—open access affords rapid control.
- **Multidisciplinary vigilance pays off:** Continuous fetal heart monitoring before/during/after surgery reduces anxiety about fetal compromise and meets guideline expectations.
- **Leave what you can:** In our case leaving the posterior pelvic myoma spared additional uterine trauma; staged management allowed elective Caesarean later without added morbidity.
- **Laparoscopy isn’t off limits**, but restrict it to early second trimester small (<7–8 cm), superficial lesions in patients without prohibitive body habitus—and always use an open Hasson entry plus low insufflation pressures.
- **Post‑op planning matters:** Iron replacement, blood transfusion if needed, progesterone support & close obstetric follow-up improve maternal recovery and fetal growth trajectories after surgical intervention.

---  

*When confronted with a pregnant patient harboring a giant fibroid—or several midsized ones—run through this matrix fast: size ➔ location ➔ gestational age ➔ symptom urgency ➔ maternal factors ➔ institutional skill set.* The answer will often point you toward an open longitudinal laparotomy when “mass matters,” but knowing exactly when you can safely go minimally invasive adds another tool to your obstetric surgical armamentarium—and keeps both mother *and* baby thriving through those critical weeks.*



# Safeguarding Mother and Baby: Anesthetic Strategies, Progesterone Prophylaxis, and Peri‑Operative Care in In‑Pregnancy Abdominal Surgery  

---

## Why This Case Stands Out  

A 42‑year‑old nulliparous patient in her second trimester presented with two **giant abdominal cystic masses** that masqueraded as endometriomas on ultrasound. The upper lesion was an 18 × 12 × 7 cm pedunculated leiomyoma perched atop the left hemidiaphragm; the lower lesion was a 9 cm cystic myoma tucked into the pouch of Douglas. She complained of severe left‑upper quadrant pain and “restrictive” dyspnoea caused by diaphragmatic compression—a rare presentation that forced us off the usual “wait‑and‑see” pathway for uterine fibroids in pregnancy.

What made the management truly knotty was the combination of **(1) an acute surgical indication (mass torsion/necrosis), (2) a concurrent Klebsiella urinary infection, (3) iron‑deficiency anaemia needing rapid correction, and (4) concerns about fetal well‑being** during a major laparotomy at 21 weeks gestation. The team ended up performing a targeted myomectomy under general anesthesia, gave peri‑operative intramuscular progesterone, used culture‑directed antibiotics, corrected anaemia with IV iron plus transfusion, and monitored fetal heart rate before, during and after surgery—all while preserving a healthy outcome for mother **and** baby.

This scenario forces us to confront three core questions that most obstetric surgeons will face sooner or later:

1. **How do we anesthetize a pregnant patient safely while maintaining uteroplacental blood flow?**  
2. **Is there any merit in giving progesterone around non‑obstetric surgery?**  
3. **What are the pragmatic steps for infection control and anemia management when time is limited?**  

Below is a “FOAM‑style” walk‑through of each component anchored on this case’s data and current best practices.

---

## Peri‑Operative Planning: The Multidisciplinary Checklist  

Before stepping into the OR you want a concrete checklist that brings obstetrics, surgery, anesthesia, infectious disease, and hematology onto the same page:

| Item | What We Did | Why It Matters |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| **Pre‑op fetal assessment** | Baseline bedside Doppler confirming normal fetal heart rate (FHR) and amniotic fluid index | Establishes baseline “normal” before any pharmacologic or hemodynamic perturbation |
| **Imaging review** | Ultrasound + non‑contrast MRI (1.5 T) confirmed mass origin & vascularity | Guides surgical approach (pedicle identified → myomectomy feasible) |
| **Laboratory workup** | CBC (Hb 9.9 g/dL), ferritin 25 µg/L, CRP 1.39 mg/dL, urine culture positive for Klebsiella | Identifies anemia severity, infection source, inflammatory status |
| **Medication reconciliation** | No teratogenic meds; added intra‑op progesterone & targeted antibiotics | Avoids drug–fetus interactions while treating maternal infection |
| **Consent dialogue** | Discussed risks of fetal loss vs benefit of relieving compression/torsion ↔ decision for laparotomy at 21 w+6 d | Transparency improves shared decision making & medico‑legal safety |

Having these items ticked off ahead of time reduces “surprise” intraoperatively—something we all know is associated with worse outcomes.

---

## Anesthetic Management of the Pregnant Surgical Patient  

### 1️⃣ Goals – Keep Uteroplacental Perfusion Above the Threshold  

* **Mean arterial pressure (MAP)** > 65 mmHg is commonly quoted as the lower bound to sustain utero‑placental flow.
* Avoid **maternal hypotension >20%** from induction or positioning.
* Maintain **normocapnia** (PaCO₂ ≈ 35–45 mmHg). Hyperventilation can cause uterine vasoconstriction via alkalosis.
* Prevent **maternal hypoxia** (SpO₂ ≥ 95%). Even brief dips matter because fetal O₂ extraction rises steeply after 20 weeks gestation.

### 2️⃣ Airway & Positioning  

* Pregnant abdomen at 21 weeks still lifts diaphragm but not enough to cause significant aortocaval compression—still place patient in left lateral tilt (~15°) after induction.
* Rapid sequence induction preferred because aspiration risk is increased throughout pregnancy.
* Use a cuffed endotracheal tube sized appropriately; confirm placement with capnography (maternal CO₂ equilibration reflects fetal CO₂ as well).

### 3️⃣ Choice of Induction & Maintenance Agents  

| Agent | Pregnancy Safety Profile | Practical Tips |
|-------|--------------------------|----------------|
| **Propofol** | Category B; crosses placenta but short half-life → minimal neonatal depression when used at induction doses ≤2 mg/kg | Ideal for smooth induction; combine with short‐acting opioid |
| **Thiopental/Etomidate** | Both Category B; Etomidate preserves hemodynamics better at cost of adrenal suppression—use sparingly | Etomidate useful if MAP expected to drop dramatically |
| **Fentanyl / Remifentanil** | Fentanyl modest placental transfer but generally safe; remifentanil ultra short acting → can be titrated intraoperatively without neonatal accumulation | Keep fentanyl ≤2–3 µg/kg total if possible |
| **Sevoflurane / Desflurane** | Low solubility volatile agents are category B; keep MAC ≤0.7 to avoid uterine relaxation & hypotension | Maintain stable depth using BIS or end‐tidal concentration monitors |
| **Nitrous Oxide** | Historically avoided due to concerns about DNA damage and decreased uterine blood flow – not recommended unless benefits outweigh risks | Usually omitted in obstetric cases |

In this case we opted for a standard balanced technique: *propofol* induction → *sevoflurane* maintenance <0.8 MAC + *fentanyl* bolus followed by an infusion titrated to analgesia while preserving MAP with phenylephrine boluses (prefer phenylephrine over ephedrine because it raises blood pressure without increasing fetal heart rate).

### 4️⃣ Intraoperative Fetal Monitoring  

Zhao et al.’s multicenter protocol recommends:

1. **Pre‐incision baseline FHR:** ≥2 minutes continuous doppler tracing showing baseline 110–160 bpm without decelerations.
2. **Intraoperative monitoring:** If gestational age ≥24 weeks many centres use continuous external doppler placed on maternal abdomen under sterile drape; under 24 weeks intermittent checks every 15–20 minutes are acceptable if continuous isn’t feasible.
3. **Post‐closure check:** Repeat baseline tracing before awakening from anaesthesia.

In our patient at 21 weeks we performed *intermittent* transabdominal doppler recordings: one before induction, one after incision closure, and one after emergence from anaesthesia while still supine left‐tilted. No decelerations were noted throughout—a reassuring sign that uteroplacental flow remained intact despite a two‐hour surgery.

### 5️⃣ Fluid Management  

Goal‐directed therapy using crystalloids plus judicious colloid boluses when MAP dips below target despite vasopressors—avoid excessive fluid overload which worsens pulmonary edema risk especially in obese patients (BMI 33). In our case total crystalloid volume was ~1500 ml over 2½ hours with two phenylephrine boluses (50 µg each). No significant hemodilution occurred beyond her baseline anemia.

---

## Hormonal Tweaks: Progesterone Prophylaxis  

The surgical team administered an IM dose of *100 mg micronized progesterone* intraoperatively then continued daily until discharge, followed by weekly *341 mg hydroxyprogesterone caproate* injections every three days until delivery.

### Rationale  

- **Progesterone maintains uterine quiescence**, reduces prostaglandin synthesis and theoretically lowers miscarriage risk after surgical stress.
- Small retrospective series have suggested that perioperative progesterone may blunt uterine irritability—particularly relevant after manipulation of myometrial tissue.
- No large RCTs exist specifically for non‐obstetric abdominal surgery in pregnancy; however data from threatened miscarriage management shows improvements in continuation rates with similar dosing regimens.

### Practical Takeaways  

- Consider a single dose intraoperatively if you’re operating on myometrial tissue or if there’s preexisting cervical insufficiency.
- Daily IM micronized progesterone (~100–200 mg) is generally well tolerated—minimal maternal side effects except mild somnolence.
- Hydroxyprogesterone caproate has an excellent safety record throughout pregnancy but requires deeper intramuscular injection; schedule may be adjusted based on patient preference and logistics.
- Document indication clearly because insurance coverage can be ambiguous outside obstetric protocols.

---

## Infection Control & Antibiotic Stewardship  

The patient arrived with *Klebsiella pneumoniae* bacteriuria proven by urine culture sensitive to cephalosporins and macrolides—a classic scenario where empirical broad spectrum coverage would be overkill if you have culture data preoperatively.

### Regimen Used  

1. **Surgical prophylaxis:** Cefazolin 2 g IV pre-incision → q8h post-op 1 g IV.
2. **Targeted therapy:** Added clarithromycin 500 mg PO BID based on sensitivity profile to cover potential atypical organisms found in pelvic fluid cultures—a prudent choice given her prior urinary infection history.
3. Duration: Cefazolin continued for 48 h post-op; clarithromycin completed after discharge per culture sensitivities (<7 days total).

### Safety Profile During Pregnancy  

- **Cefazolin**, first‐generation cephalosporin – FDA Category B – no teratogenic signal.
- **Clarithromycin**, Category B – acceptable when benefits outweigh risks; it penetrates well into genital tract secretions—it’s often used for atypical PID treatment during pregnancy.
  
Avoid fluoroquinolones unless absolutely necessary due to chondral toxicity concerns reported in animal studies and Category C status.

---

## Managing Pregnancy–Related Anemia Around Surgery  

Our patient's pre-op Hb was 9.9 g/dL with ferritin indicating true iron deficiency rather than physiological dilution alone—a common finding given her BMI obesity and chronic inflammation from the necrotic pedunculated leiomyoma (“low-grade fever”).

### Decision Pathway  

1. **IV Iron First Line:** Ferric carboxymaltose 1000 mg over ≤15 min delivers ~1000 mg elemental iron—ideal when oral iron would be insufficient before delivery (~16 weeks away). It raises ferritin rapidly without needing prolonged GI tolerance.
2. **Transfusion Threshold:** Guidelines suggest transfusing pregnant patients when Hb <7 g/dL or symptomatic anemia persists despite iron repletion—our threshold aligned with this as she dropped to Hb 7.5 g/dL post‐op due partly to intraoperative blood loss.
3. **Red Cell Transfusion:** Two units PRBC administered intra/post-op raised Hb back above 10 g/dL—adequate for oxygen delivery without excessive volume overload.

### Practical Tips  

- Check serum ferritin before deciding on IV iron—if >30 µg/L consider trialling oral ferrous sulfate unless time constraints dictate otherwise.
- Monitor hemoglobin daily post-op given ongoing physiological plasma expansion during pregnancy.
- Use small volume fluids concurrent with transfusions to avoid exacerbating pulmonary congestion—particularly important given underlying obesity and dyspnea from mass effect.
  
---

## Decision Tree for Surgical Approach in Pregnancy  

When faced with large pelvic masses you have three main options:

1. **Pure Expectant Management:** Most fibroids are observed unless symptomatic complications arise.
2. **Minimally Invasive Myomectomy/Laparoscopy:** Feasible for ≤8–10 cm subserosal lesions without pedicles when done early (<14 weeks). Requires experienced surgeons comfortable with obstetric laparoscopy under low insufflation pressures (<12 mmHg).
3. **Open Laparotomy Myomectomy:** Reserved for massive (>15 cm), pedunculated or torsed lesions where safe morcellation isn’t possible or where tactile assessment is essential—as was required here given suspected twisted stalk causing necrosis/fever.

Key determinants include:
- Gestational age >14 weeks → more space constraints + higher risk of uterine injury during trocar insertion.
- Mass size >10 cm → limited working space laparoscopically.
- Presence of acute complication (torsion/rupture/infection).
  
In this case MRI clarified that only one mass had a true stalk prone to torsion—so removal via laparotomy minimized operative time while preserving the secondary lesion that posed no imminent threat.

---

## Delivery Planning After Intra‑Pregnancy Myomectomy  

Even after successful myomectomy in pregnancy you must think ahead:

- A residual posterior intramural/subserosal leiomyoma ~7 cm could obstruct fetal head descent during labor → higher likelihood of C-section = advisable elective cesarean at term.
- Timing: Schedule cesarean at ≥37 weeks gestation once fetal lung maturity confirmed — minimizes prematurity risk while allowing any residual postoperative inflammation to resolve.
  
Our team chose an elective repeat low transverse incision through the prior laparotomy scar at 37 weeks → uncomplicated delivery with healthy neonate (birth weight appropriate for gestational age).

---

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Prioritize uteroplacental perfusion:** keep MAP >65 mmHg, avoid maternal hypoxia/hypocapnia; use phenylephrine over ephedrine when treating anesthesia‐induced hypotension.
- **Choose anesthetic agents wisely:** propofol induction + low‐dose sevoflurane maintenance + short‐acting opioids provide stable hemodynamics without compromising fetal oxygenation.
- **Fetal heart rate monitoring isn’t optional:** obtain baseline trace pre-incision, perform intermittent Doppler checks during surgery if continuous monitoring isn’t feasible (<24 weeks), repeat post closure per Zhao et al.’s protocol.
- **Progesterone can be considered perioperatively:** single IM dose intra-op plus daily micronized progesterone may help maintain uterine quiescence after myometrial manipulation—evidence limited but biologically plausible.
- **Let culture guide antibiotics:** cefazolin remains first line prophylaxis; add targeted agents such as clarithromycin when cultures identify susceptible organisms—even during pregnancy these drugs are Category B and safe.
- **Treat anemia aggressively yet rationally:** IV iron is first line when time is short or oral tolerance is poor; transfuse only when Hb <7 g/dL or symptomatic despite iron replacement—mindful of volume status especially in obese patients.
- **Surgical approach dictated by size/complication:** lymph huge (>15 cm) pedunculated lesions merit open myomectomy even mid‑trimester; smaller subserosal masses may be amenable to laparoscopy early in pregnancy.
- **Plan delivery early:** residual large myomas often mandate elective cesarean at term—to avoid obstructed labor and uterine rupture risk.

By marrying rigorous obstetric vigilance with tailored anesthetic/medical strategies—as illustrated by this case—we can safely navigate even the most intimidating abdominal surgeries during pregnancy while safeguarding both mother *and* baby. Stay curious, keep your multidisciplinary lines open, and never underestimate how far a well-coordinated peri-operative plan can go toward turning a potential catastrophe into an excellent outcome!



# From Myomectomy to Cesarean Delivery: Planning Birth After Intra‑Pregnancy Uterine Surgery  

---

## Why this case grabbed attention  

Most obstetricians have encountered a solitary subserosal or intramural fibroid that sits quietly through pregnancy. Rarely does a pregnant patient present with **two gigantic abdominal cystic masses** that mimic ovarian endometriomas and generate both severe left‑upper quadrant pain *and* dyspnoea from diaphragmatic compression. In the report by De Nola et al., an obese nulliparous woman in her second trimester required an **open myomectomy at 21 weeks** for a twisted pedunculated leiomyoma that was leaking into the peritoneal cavity. The operative decision set off a cascade of questions that most clinicians face only rarely:

* How do you decide whether a pregnant woman with a large fibroid needs surgery now versus waiting until after delivery?  
* Once you have cut a uterus in its most vulnerable state—mid‑pregnancy—what mode of delivery is safest?  
* Can you safely attempt a trial of labour after an intra‑pregnancy myomectomy (TOLAM), or does the presence of residual pelvic fibroids automatically dictate an elective Caesarean?  

The answers are not “one size fits all”; they sit at the intersection of obstetrics, surgical technique, maternal physiology, and neonatal timing. This blog walks through the decision tree illustrated by the case and extracts practical pearls for anyone who might face a similar dilemma in the labour ward or operating theatre.

---

## Leiomyoma dynamics during gestation  

Uterine leiomyomas are estrogen‑ and progesterone‑responsive smooth‑muscle tumours whose growth curve is decidedly non‑linear in pregnancy:

* **First‐trimester surge:** On average fibroids increase by **≈ 7 % per week**, driven by high circulating estradiol and progesterone.
* **Second‐trimester plateau:** Growth slows markedly (~3 % per week) but large masses can still outpace uterine expansion if they are pedunculated or located high on the fundus.
* **Third trimester/early puerperium:** Hormonal withdrawal usually leads to **rapid involution**, often shrinking by **30–50 %** within six weeks postpartum.  

In De Nola’s patient the upper mass enlarged enough to occupy the left subphrenic space while the lower lesion lay snugly against the posterior uterine wall. Both were highly vascular on colour Doppler—an expected feature because tumour vessels become engorged under progesterone’s vasodilatory effect—and therefore prone to torsion or necrosis, which ultimately prompted operative intervention.

---

## When do you operate during pregnancy?  

The classic teaching is “**avoid surgery**” unless life‑threatening complications arise. Modern practice refines this principle into three core indications:

1. **Acute complications** – torsion of a pedunculated subserosal leiomyoma, red degeneration causing severe pain or systemic signs (fever, leukocytosis), uncontrolled haemorrhage, or suspicion for rupture.
2. **Obstetric compromise** – massive enlargement that mechanically limits lung expansion (as happened here), threatens uterine rupture because it impinges on the lower segment, or creates obstructed labor forecasts.
3. **Oncologic suspicion** – rising tumour markers coupled with atypical imaging features merit definitive pathology before proceeding further into pregnancy.

All three were present in this patient: twisted vascular stalk (torsion), progressive dyspnoea from diaphragmatic compression, and an upward trend of CA‑125 above the expected second‑trimester median. The safe diagnostic workup—bimanual exam, high‑resolution ultrasound with colour Doppler followed by non‑contrast MRI (the latter being endorsed by ACOG for any gestational age beyond the first trimester when gadolinium is contraindicated)—provided enough certainty to move forward with an open approach rather than waiting until delivery.

Surgical goals must be laser‐focused:

* Obtain definitive diagnosis (histopathology).
* Remove tissue that poses imminent risk (the twisted pedicle).
* Preserve as much healthy myometrium as possible.
* Keep operative time short (<3 h) and avoid excessive blood loss using meticulous haemostasis (e.g., double clamps on pedicle) and appropriate prophylactic antibiotics/uterotonics (in this report progesterone was continued postoperatively).

The decision between laparoscopy versus laparotomy hinges on gestational age, size (>10 cm favours open access), number of lesions, and presumed adhesions from an enlarged uterus—all factors that steered De Nola’s team toward a midline xipho–pubic laparotomy.

## Delivery decisions after intra‑pregnancy myomectomy  

### 1️⃣ What does the uterine scar look like?  

A true hysterotomy—full‑thickness incision through the uterus—is rarely needed for excising a pedunculated subserosal lesion; instead surgeons close only the myometrial defect where the stalk attached (“pedicle repair”). Nevertheless any suture line represents a potential weak spot when subjected to late‑gestational stretch forces during labour. The risks traditionally quoted are:

* **Uterine dehiscence** or rupture during active labour.
* **Excessive intra‑partum haemorrhage** requiring transfusion.
* **Secondary infection** if closure was compromised by necrotic tissue exposure.

Published series (Zhao et al.; Sakinci et al.) suggest that when closure is performed with absorbable monofilament sutures under direct vision—as performed here—the incidence of clinically significant rupture remains low (<1 %). However robust prospective data are lacking because most cases are managed conservatively or delivered electively via Caesarean section out of caution.

### 2️⃣ Does residual pelvic fibrosis dictate mode of delivery?  

In our patient the lower mass measured roughly **9–10 cm**, occupied the pouch of Douglas and adhered firmly to an intramural‐subserosal fundal fibroid undergoing cystic degeneration. During labour such a structure behaves like a “rock” lodged behind the cervix:

* It obstructs descent of the fetal head.
* It may prevent adequate cervical effacement.
* It raises intra‑uterine pressure locally → risk of focal rupturing along suture line or tearing of overlying serosa when traction forces peak in second stage.

Consensus among obstetric societies states that any **fibroid larger than 5–8 cm located within ≤2 cm of the internal os—or projecting into the lower segment—requires an elective Caesarean** because successful vaginal birth rates fall dramatically (<30 %). For more proximal fundal or posterior lesions isolated from the lower segment it remains reasonable to consider trial of labour if cervical dilation proceeds normally and no other contraindication exists.

### 3️⃣ Timing – why ≥37 weeks?  

After any uterine manipulation surgeons aim for fetal lung maturity before placing additional stress on healing tissue. The envelope balancing act is:

| Goal | Rationale |
|------|-----------|
| **Avoid preterm birth** | Respiratory distress syndrome risks rise steeply before 34 weeks; even late preterm carries higher NICU admission rates compared with term deliveries |
| **Permit adequate scar remodeling** | Collagen turnover continues well into late third trimester; waiting until ≥37 weeks gives additional weeks for tensile strength restoration |
| **Minimise emergency Caesarean risk** | Elective scheduling avoids emergent scenarios where maternal haemodynamics are unstable ‑ the exact time point chosen by De Nola et al.’s team |

Thus their decision for an elective Caesarean at exactly 37 weeks aligns with both fetal maturity milestones and providing sufficient time for postoperative healing while maintaining tight obstetric surveillance every three weeks thereafter.

### 4️⃣ Choice of abdominal incision  

Reusing the original longitudinal xipho–pubic incision had two pragmatic benefits:

1️⃣ It avoids creating another fascial defect (“new Pfannenstiel”) that would sit close to an already compromised area beneath it.
2️⃣ It permits direct access through already exposed peritoneum without additional tissue devascularisation—a valuable consideration when placenta location is unknown or when future fertility preservation is desired.

While most modern Caesareans employ low transverse incisions (“bikini cut”) due its lower dehiscence rates compared with vertical scars, there are scenarios where reusing an existing vertical approach reduces total operative trauma—especially if it allows avoidance of extending incisions near bulky posterior fibroids that could otherwise be injured during entry through a low transverse entry point.

### 5️⃣ Prophylactic adjuncts – progesterone & antibiotics  

Progesterone supplementation postoperatively is increasingly common when there’s concern about maintaining uterine quiescence after mid‑gestation surgery—a strategy reflected in this case (IM hydroxyprogesterone caproate until delivery). Evidence from randomized trials suggests modest reductions in preterm birth rates among high‐risk women receiving weekly injections starting around 16–20 weeks gestation; however its routine use solely after myomectomy lacks solid data and should be individualized based on obstetrician judgment and patient preference.

Broad spectrum perioperative antibiotics tailored from culture results (Klebsiella sensitive Cephalosporin + Clarithromycin) ensured no lingering infection contributed toward preterm labour—a classic pitfall when operating amid urinary tract colonisation common in pregnant patients with BMI >30 kg/m² .

## Postpartum surveillance & when—or whether—to reoperate  

Leiomyomas seldom disappear entirely after delivery but undergo substantial involution owing to plummeting estrogen levels:

* Upward trends show *≈40–50% volume reduction* within six weeks postpartum.
* Symptom relief parallels shrinkage unless fibrosis has rendered lesions stiff (“hard nodules”).

Given these dynamics many clinicians adopt a watchful waiting policy unless one or more red flags emerge:

1️⃣ Persistent bulk symptoms impeding daily activity beyond six months postpartum.
2️⃣ Recurrent pain suggestive of secondary degeneration or torsion.
3️⃣ Growth on serial imaging—particularly if lesion exceeds 8–10 cm despite hormonal withdrawal.
4️⃣ Fertility concerns where location obstructs implantation or compromises future pregnancies.

For surveillance most centres rely on transabdominal ultrasound at six weeks postpartum combined with a physical exam focusing on pelvic floor tone and residual bulk sensation. If ultrasound shows stable or decreasing size (<5 cm) without solid components suspicious for sarcoma (rapid growth >1 cm/month beyond hormonal influence), routine follow-up may be spaced out annually until menopause—or indefinitely if patient remains asymptomatic—and definitive surgery deferred indefinitely.

MRI enters play only when sonographic findings are equivocal—for instance equivocal solid nodularity within cystic degeneration prompting suspicion for stromal overgrowth—or when mapping large posterior lesions before planning subsequent abdominal approaches becomes essential.

When operative intervention finally becomes necessary—in either delayed interval myomectomy or definitive hysterectomy—the prior adipose closure site may pose technical challenges mainly due to adhesions rather than altered uterine architecture because most sutures placed on stalks heal completely without leaving macro‐defects visible endometrially later in life.\(^\dagger\)

## Take‑home pearls  

- **Surgery mid‑pregnancy is reserved for acute complications (torsion/necrosis), mechanical compromise (dyspnoea/obstruction), or convincing oncologic suspicion**—otherwise expectant management remains safest.
- **Identify location relative to cervical canal:** Any fibroid ≥5–8 cm encroaching upon or bulging into the lower uterine segment mandates planned Caesarean delivery because it blocks fetal head descent and increases rupture risk.
- **Uterine scar integrity after pedicle repair is usually adequate**, yet lack robust prospective data means many teams opt for scheduled C‑section once fetal lung maturity (>37 weeks) is confirmed.
- **Reusing an existing abdominal incision can spare additional fascial trauma**, especially when large posterior masses limit safe entry via low transverse routes.
- **Progesterone support postoperatively may help maintain quiescence**, though its benefit specifically after intra‑pregnancy myomectomy remains anecdotal—not standard guideline therapy.
- **Postpartum follow‑up should start at six weeks with ultrasound**, focusing on size trend rather than static measurement; most lesions shrink dramatically first trimester after birth—reserve delayed definitive surgery for persistent bulk symptoms or atypical imaging features suggestive of sarcoma.
- **Counsel patients early:** Explain expected regression (>30% volume drop), reassure that reserve fertility generally preserved unless extensive hysterectomy is required later, and outline clear thresholds (>8 cm persistent bulk *or* symptomatic growth) that would trigger reconsideration of surgical removal after delivery.\(^\dagger\)

> *Bottom line:* An intra‑pregnancy myomectomy transforms your birth plan from “wait-and-see” into a coordinated choreography involving precise imaging, judicious timing of delivery ≥37 weeks, often an elective Caesarean via previously used incisionlines, followed by thoughtful postpartum monitoring where most residual tumors simply shrink away under natural hormonal withdrawal.*
