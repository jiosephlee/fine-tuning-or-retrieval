Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] After removal of an upper pedunculated leiomyoma but leaving a large posterior intramural/subserosal fibroid in situ, what obstetric considerations determine whether an elective cesarean delivery is recommended versus attempting a trial of labor?

**Clinical Pearl – Delivery planning after selective myomectomy**

In a pregnant woman in whom a pedunculated subserosal leiomyoma has been excised but a sizable posterior intramural/sub‑serosal fibroid remains, the choice between an elective cesarean section (CS) and a trial of labor hinges on how that residual tumor will affect the mechanics of birth and the integrity of the uterus. The case reported by De Nola et al. illustrates precisely how these considerations are weighed.

---

### 1.  Size matters – “> 5 cm” as a pragmatic threshold
*Large fibroids* (> 5–6 cm) are associated with a three‑to‑fourfold increase in cesarean delivery rates because they more often impede descent of the presenting part and diminish contractile efficiency. In the reported patient the posterior mass measured **9 cm** on ultrasound (later 7.5 cm at term). Even after modest involution it remained big enough to occupy the posterior isthmic region and was judged “a huge obstacle for fetal head progression” during the expulsive phase.

### 2.  Location is decisive
- **Lower‑segment or isthmic involvement** (within 2–3 cm of the internal cervical os) is an absolute indication for CS when the lesion occupies ≥ 50 % of the segment thickness.  
- **Posterior wall lesions** that abut the internal os or that sit along the uterine tube‑cervical junction can prevent adequate engagement and rotation of the fetal head, even if they are not directly in the lower segment. In this case the residual myoma lay “adjacent to the posterior uterine torus” and behaved like a fixed barrier in the pelvis.

### 3.  Effect on uterine contractility & scar integrity
After any myomectomy—especially when a vascular pedicle is ligated and the myometrial defect is closed—as in this patient (single absorbable suture of the upper pedicle), there is a **localized area of weakened myometrium**. While most surgeons consider isolated serosal‑muscular repairs safe for vaginal delivery, larger or deeper repairs raise concern for rupture when subjected to vigorous labor contractions. The team therefore factored in even this limited scar when planning delivery.

### 4.  Potential for obstructed labor / malpresentation
A bulky posterior mass can:
- *Distort the uterine cavity* → malpresentation (breech or transverse) or failure of engagement.
- *Obstruct rotation* → prolonged second stage → fetal distress or maternal exhaustion.
During serial ultrasounds this patient’s fetus remained cephalic but repeat imaging showed no space for safe vaginal passage given the size and fixed nature of the residual tumor.

### 5.  Placental relationship
When a fibroid overlies or lies within ≤ 2 cm of placental implantation, there is heightened risk for:
- Placental previa or accreta‑like phenomena,
- Intra‑operative hemorrhage,
- Post‑delivery atony.
The authors specifically noted that **placental location was distant**, so placental issues did not drive their decision; however, they emphasized that “the mode of delivery should depend on size, number and anatomical location” – reinforcing that placental proximity is another key variable when present.

### 6.  Maternal co‑factors
Obesity (BMI 33), prior laparotomy scar, anemia, and infection risk all tilt toward a controlled operative environment rather than emergent obstetric compromise during labor.

---

## Synthesis – Decision algorithm applied to this case

| Consideration | Threshold / Finding | Impact on Delivery Plan |
|---------------|---------------------|--------------------------|
| **Fibroid size** | ≥ 5–6 cm | Favors CS |
| **Location** | Posterior intramural/sub‑serosal near internal os / lower segment | Favors CS |
| **Myometrial integrity** | Prior myomectomy with suture repair | Caution—prefer CS if large scar or deep repair |
| **Mechanical obstruction** | Anticipated impediment to fetal head descent | Favors CS |
| **Placental proximity** | Not adjacent → neutral | Not determinant here |
| **Maternal comorbidity** | Obesity, anemia, prior abdominal surgery | Adds weight to operative planning |

Because **multiple independent criteria were met**, an elective cesarean was deemed safer than attempting vaginal birth. The operation was scheduled at 37 weeks using the same longitudinal xipho‑pubic incision that had been employed for myomectomy—minimizing additional trauma while ensuring adequate access for delivery.

---

### Take‑home message

When a large (>5 cm) intramural or sub‑serosal leiomyoma remains after selective myomectomy:

1. **Measure size** accurately by ultrasound/MRI; > 5–6 cm generally contraindicates vaginal delivery.
2. **Map location** relative to internal cervical os and lower uterine segment; any lesion encroaching on this zone mandates cesarean delivery.
3. **Assess myometrial integrity** post‑myomectomy; extensive repairs increase rupture risk.
4. **Predict mechanical obstruction** by simulating fetal head engagement on serial scans.
5. **Consider placental relationship** and maternal comorbidities as modifiers.

If two or more of these factors point toward impaired descent or compromised uterine wall strength—as illustrated by De Nola et al.’s patient—elective cesarean delivery should be recommended rather than a trial of labor. This proactive approach eliminates emergent scenarios such as obstructed labor or intra‑operative hemorrhage and optimizes outcomes for both mother and neonate.
