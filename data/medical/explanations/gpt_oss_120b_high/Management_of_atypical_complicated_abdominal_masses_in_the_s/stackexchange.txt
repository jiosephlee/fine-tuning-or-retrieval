Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] Which imaging modalities are considered safe and provide optimal diagnostic information for evaluating large abdominal or pelvic masses in a pregnant patient after the first trimester?

**Imaging large abdominal or pelvic masses after the first trimester – what is safe and gives you the information you need?**

When a pregnant woman in her second (or third) trimester presents with a bulky intra‑abdominal or adnexal lesion, the diagnostic algorithm is deliberately narrow because we must protect the fetus while still obtaining a reliable anatomic assessment. The experience reported by De Nola et al. illustrates the principle clearly: an initial sonographic study was followed by a non‑contrast magnetic‑resonance examination, and together these two modalities supplied all of the data required for operative planning without exposing either mother or fetus to ionising radiation or gadolinium‑based contrast agents.

1. **Transabdominal (and when indicated, transvaginal) ultrasound with colour Doppler**  
   *Safety*: Uses low‑energy sound waves; no known fetal risks at any gestational age.  
   *Diagnostic yield*: Provides real‑time measurement of size and shape, determines whether a lesion is cystic, solid or mixed, and assesses vascular flow at the periphery or within a pedicle. In the reported case ultrasound identified two unilocular cystic masses (18 × 12 × 7 cm and 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm) and demonstrated peripheral colour flow—information that immediately raised concern for highly vascular uterine leiomyomas versus decidualised endometriomas.  

2. **Non‑contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed after 12 weeks’ gestation**  
   *Safety*: MRI generates images from radiofrequency pulses and magnetic fields rather than ionising radiation. At field strengths ≤1.5 T it has been shown to be free of teratogenic effects after organogenesis is complete; heating effects are minimal when standard pulse sequences are used. Gadolinium chelates are avoided because they cross the placenta and have been associated with fetal adverse outcomes; therefore a plain (non‑enhanced) protocol is preferred unless an emergent need for contrast outweighs these risks—something not required in this case.  
   *Diagnostic yield*: Offers superior soft‑tissue contrast that delineates the relationship of a mass to uterine myometrium, ovaries, bowel and vascular structures. In De Nola’s patient the MRI clarified wall thickness (1.4 cm), heterogeneous fluid signal, focal solid components on T1/T2 fat‑sat images, and most importantly demonstrated continuity between the caudal lesion and an intramural–subserosal leiomyoma—information that could not be resolved by ultrasound alone.

3. **Imaging modalities generally avoided**  
   • **Computed tomography (CT)** – delivers ionising radiation; reserved only for life‑threatening maternal emergencies when no alternative exists because fetal exposure can increase stochastic cancer risk.  
   • **Gadolinium‑enhanced MRI** – contraindicated except when absolutely indispensable for maternal treatment decisions; placental transfer may cause fetal nephrogenic systemic fibrosis or unknown long‑term effects.  

4. **Putting it together for clinical decision‑making**  
   The stepwise use of **ultrasound → non‑contrast MRI** gives you all of the following essential data without compromising fetal safety:
   * Exact dimensions and growth pattern,
   * Cystic vs solid nature,
   * Vascularity and presence of a stalk,
   * Anatomic origin (uterine versus ovarian/adnexal),
   * Proximity to placenta or fetal structures,
   * Absence of regional lymphadenopathy or invasive features.

These criteria allowed De Nola et al.’s multidisciplinary team to determine that one mass was a pedunculated leiomyoma needing excision, while the second could safely be left in situ until delivery—an approach that preserved both maternal health and an uneventful pregnancy outcome.

**Bottom line:** After 12 weeks’ gestation the combination of targeted ultrasound (with colour Doppler) plus a non‑contrast MRI performed on a ≤1.5 T scanner represents the safest and most informative strategy for evaluating large abdominal or pelvic masses during pregnancy. This protocol delivers high‑resolution anatomic detail necessary for operative planning while respecting fetal safety—a paradigm exemplified by the presented case report.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the normal physiologic pattern of serum CA‑125 levels throughout pregnancy, and how should a modest elevation (e.g., 42 U/mL) be interpreted when assessing a second‑trimester pregnant woman with an adnexal mass?

**Normal physiologic pattern of serum CA‑125 during pregnancy**

| Trimester | Median CA‑125 (U mL⁻¹) | Typical range |
|----------|----------------------|---------------|
| **1st**   | ≈ 23 U/mL            | modest rise above non‑pregnant baseline (<20 U/mL); may reach ≥35 U/mL in some women |
| **2nd**   | ≈ 14 U/mL            | generally close to non‑pregnant “normal” zone |
| **3rd**   | ≈ 21 U/mL            | a second physiological peak before delivery |

These data come directly from the case discussion (“the serum CA‑125 usually reaches two peaks…first trimester median 23 U/mL … third‐trimester median 21 U/mL … versus second‑trimester median 14 U/mL”). The early rise reflects decidualisation of endometrial tissue and placental mesothelial activation; the late rise parallels increasing uterine stretch and peritoneal irritation as term approaches. In isolation, a value ≤35 U/mL—even when slightly above the second‑trimester median—has limited specificity for malignancy because many benign conditions (endometriosis, adenomyosis, leiomyoma degeneration, infection or inflammation) can elevate CA‑125 modestly.

---

**Interpretation of a modest elevation (~42 U/mL) in a second‑trimester adnexal mass**

1. **Context matters more than the cut‑off:** A single measurement above the conventional oncology threshold (≥35 U/mL) does *not* diagnose ovarian cancer in pregnancy. The case illustrates this point—CA‑125 rose from 36 → 41.8 U/mL over two weeks without other biochemical abnormalities (normal CEA, CA19‑9, LDH) and without sonographic features typical of malignancy (no papillary projections, ascites or lymphadenopathy).

2. **Benign contributors are common:**  
   - **Decidualised endometrioma** – hormonally driven cystic change often yields CA‑125 elevations >19 U/mL.  
   - **Cystic degeneration of leiomyomas** – peritoneal irritation from necrosis or torsion can raise CA‑125 (the case’s pedunculated leiomyoma was necrotic at surgery).  
   - **Inflammation/Infection** – low‑grade fever and a concurrent Klebsiella urinary tract infection provide an additional source of mesothelial activation that can push levels into the low‑40s range.

3. **Trend vs absolute value:** Serial measurements are helpful. In this patient the upward trend prompted further imaging but did not alone dictate surgery; it signaled that whatever process was present was active rather than quiescent.

4. **Imaging remains decisive:** Ultrasound showed large unilocular cystic lesions with peripheral vascularity; MRI confirmed thick walls and solid components without malignant features such as irregular septations or nodal enlargement. Because imaging suggested benign pathology and because the masses were symptomatic and posed mechanical compromise, operative intervention was pursued irrespective of the modest CA‑125 rise.

5. **Practical teaching point:** When you encounter an adnexal mass in the second trimester with a CA‑125 around 40–45 U/mL:
   - Recognize this level falls within the expected physiological variability plus possible benign pathology.
   - Correlate meticulously with ultrasound/MRI findings.
   - Consider concurrent inflammatory states that can artifactually elevate CA‑125.
   - Use serial CA‑125 only as an adjunct—significant rising trends coupled with worrisome imaging may increase concern, whereas stable or slowly rising values often reflect benign processes.
   - Reserve definitive oncologic workup for cases where imaging shows solid papillary architecture, ascites, rapid growth >5–6 cm over weeks, or systemic signs disproportionate to benign disease.

In summary, during straight‐line gestation CA‑125 physiologically peaks in early pregnancy then settles near baseline until a modest resurgence near term. A solitary value of ~42 U/mL at 21 weeks should be interpreted as *potentially benign*—a flag for careful imaging and clinical correlation rather than an automatic trigger for malignancy workup or altered obstetric management.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the hormonal and molecular mechanisms that drive rapid growth of uterine leiomyomas during early pregnancy, emphasizing the roles of estrogen, progesterone, and leiomyoma‑specific growth factors.

**Answer – Why uterine leiomyomas often explode in size during early pregnancy**

The dramatic enlargement of the pedunculated leiomyoma in our 40‑year‑old patient—an 18 cm vascular mass already producing pain and dyspnoea at 21 weeks’ gestation—is a classic illustration of the hormonally driven surge that occurs in the first two trimesters. The core of this “pregnancy‑associated growth spurt” lies in the coordinated actions of estradiol, progesterone, and a cadre of locally produced growth factors that together convert a quiescent smooth‑muscle nodule into a rapidly expanding tumour.  

---

### 1. Estrogen – the initial accelerator

* **Systemic rise** – In the luteal phase of conception and through the first trimester, ovarian estradiol levels double every 3–4 days, reaching concentrations that are ≈5‑fold higher than pre‑pregnancy values.  
* **Receptor activation** – Leiomyoma cells over‑express **ER‑α** (and to a lesser extent ER‑β). Binding of estradiol triggers both classical genomic pathways (direct transcription of target genes) and non‑genomic signalling through membrane‐associated ERs that rapidly activate **MAPK/ERK** and **PI3K/AKT** cascades.  
* **Growth‑factor amplification** – ER activation up‑regulates transcription of **IGF‑1**, **IGF‑2**, **VEGF**, and **PDGF‑A/B**, creating an autocrine loop that drives cell proliferation and neovascularisation.  
* **Local estrogen synthesis** – Estradiol induces **aromatase (CYP19A1)** within leiomyoma stromal fibroblasts, allowing the nodule itself to manufacture additional estrogen (“autocrine steroidogenesis”), thereby sustaining its own growth even after systemic levels plateau.  

In our patient, the size of the pedunculated lesion at 21 weeks implies that most of its bulk accrued during this early phase when estradiol was climbing most steeply—consistent with published data showing a mean weekly increase of ≈6 % between conception and week 13.

---

### 2. Progesterone – the remodeler and vascular stabiliser

* **Systemic surge** – Corpus luteum–derived progesterone rises from ≈7 ng mL⁻¹ in early gestation to >30 ng mL⁻¹ by week 12, later supplemented by placental production.  
* **PR isoform dynamics** – Leiomyomas display an excess of **PR‑B** relative to normal myometrium; PR‑B is especially potent at inducing genes involved in extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and angiogenesis.  
* **ECM expansion** – Progesterone stimulates **TGF‑β3**, **CTGF**, and downstream SMAD signalling, prompting prolific collagen and fibronectin synthesis. Because leiomyomas are essentially a fibro‑muscular tumour, this ECM accrual contributes more than cellular proliferation to gross size gain.  
* **Angiogenic drive** – Through PR‐mediated transcription, progesterone markedly raises **VEGF**, **bFGF**, and **angiopoietin‑2**, ensuring an expanded capillary network that supplies nutrients to the growing mass.  

The histopathology from our case showed areas of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to a twisted stalk—precisely the scenario when rapid hormonal‐stimulated expansion outpaces vascular supply despite vigorous VEGF–driven angiogenesis.

---

### 3. Leiomyoma‑specific growth factors – the downstream effectors

| Growth factor | Primary hormone trigger | Main effect on leiomyoma |
|---------------|------------------------|--------------------------|
| **IGF‑1 / IGF‑2** | Estrogen & progesterone → ↑IGF‐binding proteins ↓ | Stimulates proliferation via IGF‐1R → PI3K/AKT |
| **PDGF‐A/B** | Estrogen ↑ transcription | Autocrine mitogenic loop; activates Src/FAK |
| **VEGF** | Progesterone ↑ via HIF‑1α & ER | Neoangiogenesis; increases lesion perfusion |
| **bFGF (FGF2)** | Estrogen & mechanical stretch | Promotes endothelial proliferation |
| **TGF‑β3 / CTGF** | Progesterone & mechanical cues | Drives collagen synthesis → bulk increase |
| **HIF‑1α** | Hypoxia secondary to rapid expansion | Further upregulates VEGF & glycolytic enzymes |

These molecules are not merely passive markers; they act synergistically with steroid receptors because their promoters contain estrogen‐ or progesterone‐response elements. Consequently, modest rises in circulating steroids translate into exponential amplification of proliferative signalling within the leiomyoma microenvironment.

---

### 4. Integrated “first‐trimester” model

1️⃣ Early rise in estradiol → ER activation → ↑IGF‐1/VEGF + aromatase → local estrogen feed‑forward  
2️⃣ Concurrent progesterone surge → PR‐B dominant → ↑TGF‑β3 + VEGF + bFGF → ECM deposition & angiogenesis  
3️⃣ Cross‑talk between ER/PR pathways potentiates receptor tyrosine kinases (EGFR, PDGFR) → MAPK/ERK + PI3K/AKT amplification  
4️⃣ Resultant net effect = brisk cellular proliferation + marked ECM accumulation + new vessels = ≈6 % weekly volume increase reported in longitudinal studies—and precisely what we observed as an 18 cm pedunculated myoma causing symptomatic mass effect at 21 weeks.

When delivery occurs, withdrawal of both estrogens and progesterone abruptly halts these pathways; consequently most fibroids regress by ~40 % within six weeks postpartum—a pattern noted by the authors who reported slight reduction of the residual pelvic myoma after birth.

---

### Take‑home pearls for trainees

* **Early pregnancy is a hormonal storm:** The most rapid leiomyoma expansion occurs before placental takeover because estradiol from the corpus luteum peaks first, priming PR expression for subsequent progesterone action.  
* **Estrogen is the proliferative trigger; progesterone is the scaffold builder:** Estrogen drives cell division via IGF‑1/PI3K signalling; progesterone orchestrates ECM thickening and vascular support through TGF‑β3 and VEGF.  
* **Local autocrine loops magnify systemic signals:** Aromatase up‑regulation inside leiomyoma tissue sustains estrogenic stimulation even when systemic levels level off.  
* **Growth factors are downstream executors:** IGF‐1/2, PDGF, VEGF, bFGF, and TGF‐β family members convert steroid receptor activation into tangible mass enlargement—this explains why a seemingly modest hormone rise produces an 18 cm tumour within weeks.  
* **Clinical relevance:** In pregnant patients presenting with new or rapidly enlarging abdominal masses—especially when imaging suggests a pedunculated myoma—recognise that hormonal acceleration may generate torsion or degeneration (as seen histologically). Management decisions must weigh symptom burden against obstetric risk because spontaneous regression after delivery is expected once hormonal drive ceases.

Thus, the massive uterine leiomyoma described in this case is not an incidental finding but rather a textbook demonstration of how early pregnancy hormones—through tightly linked genomic and paracrine pathways—convert quiescent smooth‐muscle nodules into rapidly expanding tumours capable of causing significant maternal morbidity if left unchecked.

### [Differential Diagnosis] On ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI performed in the second trimester, which specific morphological features favor a diagnosis of a cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma rather than a decidualised endometrioma?

**Cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma versus decidualised endometrioma – imaging pearls in the second trimester**

In a pregnant patient the distinction rests almost entirely on morphology because tumour markers (e.g., CA‑125) are unreliable and gadolinium cannot be used.  In the present case the two large unilocular cysts showed a constellation of findings that pointed toward a leiomyomatous origin rather than an ovarian endometrioma.

---

### 1. Ultrasound clues

| Feature | Typical for cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma | Typical for decidualised endometrioma |
|--------|-------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Relationship to uterus** | *Continuity* with the uterine serosa or myometrium; often a visible stalk or broad‑based attachment (the “uterine‐myometrial continuity sign”). In our patient the lower mass was felt fixed in the pelvis and US showed it adherent to a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma. | Usually arises from the ovary and has no demonstrable connection to uterine tissue. |
| **Wall thickness** | Thick, sometimes irregular capsule ≥ 10 mm (the case showed walls 1.4 cm). | Thin wall ≤ 5 mm; if present it is smooth. |
| **Internal echo pattern** | Heterogeneous echogenicity reflecting mixed fluid‑necrotic content; may contain internal septa or floating debris. | Homogeneous low‑level “ground‑glass” echoes (“steady echo pattern”) typical of an endometrioma. |
| **Vascularity on colour Doppler** | Peripheral vessels that branch from uterine arteries and may traverse a pedicle; occasional feeding vessel seen at the stalk. The case demonstrated peripheral vascularisation of both lesions consistent with uterine supply. | Peripheral flow may be present but is usually modest; central flow is absent because the lesion is avascular fibrosis/hemo‑debris. |
| **Mobility** | Limited mobility when attached to uterus; may be tethered or fixed (“fixed mass”). The caudal lesion was fixed at the posterior fornix on bimanual exam. | Usually mobile separate from uterus unless torsed. |

---

### 2. Non‑contrast MRI pearls

| Feature | Leiomyoma with cystic degeneration | Decidualised endometrioma |
|--------|-----------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Signal of the capsule** | Thick hypointense rim (fibrous pseudocapsule) ≈ 1–1.5 cm on both T1‑fat sat and T2‑fat sat – exactly what was described (“thick walls 1.4 cm”). | Thin hypointense rim only if present; wall usually ≤ 5 mm. |
| **Cystic content** | Heterogeneous high signal on T2 (fluid) often mixed with areas of low/intermediate signal from proteinaceous debris; no classic “shading.” The case showed *inhomogeneous* fluid on both T1‐fat sat and T2‐fat sat images. | Classic “T2 shading”: progressive loss of signal intensity on T2 due to repeated hemorrhage (“dark spot”), often accompanied by uniformly bright T1 signal from blood products – not seen here. |
| **Solid component** | Iso‑ or mildly hyperintense nodules that are isointense to adjacent myometrium on both sequences (“focal hyperintense T1–T2 solid components”). The caudal mass displayed such foci adjoining a known intramural myoma, confirming continuity with uterine muscle. | If decidualisation occurs the lesion may become more heterogeneous but retains overall high T1 intensity without discrete solid nodules that follow myometrial signal intensity. |
| **Continuity sign** | Sagittal or coronal planes reveal uninterrupted low‑signal junctional zone between lesion and uterine wall—pseudocapsule merges with uterine serosa/myometrium (“junctional continuity”). In this patient MRI suggested that the caudal cyst was *in continuation* with a posterior intramural–subserosal fibroid measuring 58 × 71 mm. | No such junctional continuity; ovarian origin remains separate from uterus. |
| **Vascular pedicle** *(if present)* | Even without gadolinium one can infer a pedicle by following enlarged vessels emerging from uterine vasculature into the mass; absent in pure ovarian lesions. The upper mass lacked an obvious stalk because of its size but its peripheral vessels were clearly uterine in origin on Doppler correlates. |

---

### How these features guided management in the reported case

- **Thick (>10 mm) irregular wall** and **heterogeneous fluid** argued against the uniform “ground‑glass/shading” of an endometrioma.
- **Clear continuity** between the caudal mass and a known posterior intramural/subserosal fibroid—as shown by both US (fixed pelvic mass) and MRI (adjacent solid component sharing signal intensity with myometrium)—favoured a cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma.
- **Peripheral vascularisation arising from uterine vessels** further supported a uterine source rather than ovarian.
- The combination of these imaging signs outweighed the modest rise in CA‑125 and prompted surgical excision of the pedunculated upper lesion while leaving the lower intramural component in situ.

---

#### Bottom line

When facing large unilocular adnexal‐appearing cysts in pregnancy:

- **Look for a thick (>10 mm) fibrous capsule**, heterogeneous fluid without classic T2 shading, and any *junctional zone continuity* with uterus—these point toward cystic degeneration of a leiomyoma.
- **Absence of continuity**, thin smooth walls, homogeneous ground‑glass echo pattern on US, and characteristic low‑signal “shading” on T2 favour decidualised endometrioma.

These morphologic hallmarks allow clinicians to discriminate between two entities that otherwise share overlapping clinical presentations and modest tumor‐marker elevations, thereby steering appropriate obstetric–surgical decision‑making as illustrated by this case.

### [Management] List the absolute and relative indications for proceeding with surgical removal of uterine fibroids during pregnancy.

**Indications for Myomectomy During Pregnancy**  
*(NEJM Clinical Pearls – Surgical Management of Leiomyoma in the Pregnant Patient)*  

Uterine leiomyomas are most often managed expectantly during gestation because operative intervention carries a real risk of hemorrhage, miscarriage, pre‑term labour, and uterine rupture.  Surgery is therefore reserved for situations in which the maternal‑fetal benefit clearly outweighs these risks.  The following categories are commonly used in the literature and were illustrated in the case reported by De Nola et al., in which a pedunculated, torsed leiomyoma was removed urgently while a second intramural/subserosal mass was left in situ.

---

### **Absolute Indications**  
(Conditions that demand prompt operative removal irrespective of gestational age)

| Indication | Rationale | How it applied in the case |
|------------|-----------|---------------------------|
| **Torsion of a pedunculated subserosal leiomyoma** with evidence of ischemic necrosis or impending rupture (acute abdominal pain, fever, peritoneal signs) | The twisted stalk compromises blood flow → necrosis → infection or hemoperitoneum; maternal sepsis and fetal compromise can develop rapidly. | The upper mass was a large pedunculated leiomyoma that had twisted on its stalk, produced fever and severe pain, and showed ischemic necrosis on pathology – an unequivocal trigger for myomectomy. |
| **Red (carneous) degeneration with refractory severe pain or hemodynamic instability** | Hemorrhagic infarction causes intense pain, fever, leukocytosis; analgesics often fail. | Although the patient’s pain improved with positioning and analgesia, the combination of torsion‑related necrosis and infection made waiting unsafe. |
| **Suspected sarcomatous degeneration or other malignancy** (rapid growth > 5 cm over weeks, atypical MRI features, rising tumor markers) | Intra‑uterine sarcoma carries high maternal mortality; definitive diagnosis requires excision. | MRI showed heterogeneous solid components and thick walls; although CA‑125 was only modestly elevated, the imaging raised oncologic concern enough to favour surgical exploration. |
| **Life‑threatening hemorrhage** (e.g., massive bleeding from a ruptured fibroid) | Immediate control of intra‑abdominal blood loss is mandatory. | Not present in this case but universally considered an absolute emergency. |
| **Obstructed uterine outlet threatening imminent delivery** (large lower‑segment fibroid impeding fetal descent when delivery is imminent) | If vaginal delivery cannot be achieved safely, removal at the time of Cesarean may be performed. | The team planned an elective cesarean at term precisely because the residual pelvic fibroid would have obstructed labour—a *post‑delivery* indication rather than an intra‑pregnancy one. |

---

### **Relative Indications**  
(Conditions that may merit myomectomy after multidisciplinary discussion, weighing gestational age, size/location of the fibroid, and response to conservative therapy)

| Indication | Typical criteria & why it may be considered |
|------------|----------------------------------------------|
| **Severe pain or pressure symptoms unresponsive to optimal medical therapy** (e.g., ≥ 2 weeks of NSAIDs/acetaminophen plus rest) | Persistent disabling pain or dyspnoea from mass effect despite analgesia suggests progressive degeneration or compression of diaphragm/urinary tract. In the reported case the dyspnoea improved only partially with positioning; removal of the cranial mass alleviated mechanical restriction. |
| **Rapid increase in fibroid size causing organ compression** (e.g., diaphragmatic elevation leading to respiratory compromise, obstructive uropathy) | Serial ultrasound/MRI demonstrating > 30 % increase over 2–3 weeks plus functional impairment pushes toward surgery before further gestational growth makes the operation more hazardous. |
| **Threatened pre‑term labor attributable to mass effect** (cervical shortening or uterine irritability documented on serial exams) | A large subserosal lesion can act as a “space‑occupying” irritant provoking contractions; removal may prolong gestation when other measures fail. |
| **Uncertain diagnosis despite high‑resolution imaging** (persistent doubt between degenerated leiomyoma vs ovarian tumour) | When tumor markers are borderline and imaging does not conclusively exclude malignancy, diagnostic excision may be justified—especially if the lesion is surgically accessible without extensive uterine dissection. |
| **Location that will preclude safe vaginal delivery** when delivery is imminent (< 34 weeks) and fetuses are viable after corticosteroid administration | In selected centres a myomectomy can be combined with a Cesarean section if the fibroid lies low in the uterine segment or cervix and would otherwise necessitate hysterectomy or cause catastrophic bleeding during labour. |
| **Maternal comorbidities exacerbated by fibroid’s mass effect** (e.g., severe anemia from chronic bleeding within a degenerated fibroid) | Correcting ongoing blood loss may improve maternal oxygen carrying capacity; definitive removal can stop further loss. In this patient anemia was addressed with transfusion and iron after surgery rather than before—the decision balanced intra‑operative bleeding risk against ongoing anemia. |

*Key practical points derived from the case:*  

1. **Gestational timing:** Most authors recommend delaying elective myomectomy until after 20 weeks when fetal viability reduces intra‑operative fetal loss risk. The reported operation at 21 w + 6 d follows this principle while still early enough to avoid preterm labor precipitated by a large necrotic mass.  

2. **Surgical approach:** Large or multiple intramural/subserosal myomas are usually approached via a vertical laparotomy to permit rapid delivery of fetal parts should an emergency arise; smaller pedunculated lesions can sometimes be removed laparoscopically using an open Hasson technique before 20 weeks if expertise exists.  

3. **Multidisciplinary coordination:** Continuous fetal heart rate monitoring before/during/after surgery (as highlighted by Zhao et al.) and peri‑operative progesterone prophylaxis (used in this case) are strategies to minimise uterine irritability and early labour.  

4. **Post‑operative planning:** When a residual fibroid is left in situ (as with the caudal mass), follow‑up imaging assesses shrinkage after delivery—most leiomyomas regress 40–50 % postpartum—allowing postponement of definitive resection until after childbearing if desired.

---

### Bottom Line for Residents

- **Proceed to myomectomy in pregnancy only when an *absolute* threat exists:** torsion/necrosis, malignant suspicion, uncontrollable hemorrhage, or imminent mechanical obstruction.
- **Consider surgery under *relative* circumstances only after exhaustive medical management**, careful imaging follow‑up, multidisciplinary consent, and preferably before 24–26 weeks gestation.
- The case illustrates an archetypal *absolute* indication—torsion with necrosis—while demonstrating prudent deferral of a second intramural myoma—*relative*—until postpartum evaluation is possible.

These criteria provide a pragmatic framework for balancing maternal safety against fetal preservation when confronting symptomatic uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy.

### [Morning Report] When a symptomatic pedunculated leiomyoma requires excision at 21–22 weeks gestation, what are the key patient‑ and tumor‑related factors that guide the decision between a laparoscopic versus an open laparotomic approach?

**Morning Report Pearls – Surgical Management of a Symptomatic Pedunculated Leiomyoma in Mid‑Second Trimester**

When a pregnant woman presents with a painful pedunculated myoma that must be removed at ≈ 21–22 weeks gestation, the choice between a minimally‑invasive (laparoscopic) versus an open (laparotomic) myomectomy hinges on a short list of *patient* and *tumour* variables that determine whether a safe operative field can be created without jeopardising fetal viability or maternal safety.

---

### 1. Patient‑related determinants  

| Factor | Why it matters | Practical threshold used in practice |
|--------|----------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Gestational age / uterine size** | By week 20–22 the gravid uterus reaches the umbilicus and occupies most of the abdominal cavity, limiting trocar placement, reducing working space, and raising the risk of accidental uterine injury when establishing pneumoperitoneum. | ≤ 14 weeks (early 2nd trimester) favours laparoscopy when other criteria are met; ≥ 20 weeks often pushes toward laparotomy unless the lesion is very small and clearly accessible. |
| **Maternal habitus (BMI & abdominal wall thickness)** | Obesity makes blind Veress entry hazardous and shortens instrument reach. In our patient (BMI 33) trocar insertion would have required excessive angles through thick pannus – a recognised cause of conversion or intra‑op complications. | BMI < 30 kg/m² generally acceptable for standard laparoscopy; BMI ≥ 30 often prompts careful Hasson entry or conversion to open access when combined with a large uterus. |
| **Comorbidities that impair CO₂ tolerance** | Laparoscopy requires Trendelenburg positioning and CO₂ insufflation; patients who already have compromised diaphragmatic mechanics (dyspnoea from a massive sub‑diaphragmatic mass) may not tolerate the additional intra‑abdominal pressure or shoulder‑tip pain without worsening maternal oxygenation. | Presence of significant respiratory compromise → favour laprotomy. |
| **Fetal considerations** | Continuous fetal heart‑rate monitoring is mandatory before, during and after any non‑obstetric surgery (Zhao et al.). With an enlarged uterus the abdomen is often too crowded to place trans‑abdominal dopplers reliably during laparoscopy; an open field allows direct visualisation of the uterus and rapid response to any emergent fetal distress. | Need for reliable intra‑op fetal monitoring → laparotomy when uterine contour hinders doppler placement. |
| **Surgeon/centre expertise** | Even experienced minimally‑invasive surgeons defer to an open approach when workspace is limited or tumour characteristics predict uncontrolled bleeding. A multidisciplinary team comfortable with intra‑op obstetric ultrasound can safely proceed minimally invasively only if they are accustomed to pregnant patients (> 15 weeks). | Institutional capability influences final decision more than any single numeric cut‑off.|

---

### 2. Tumour‑related determinants  

| Variable | Impact on surgical strategy |
|----------|-----------------------------|
| **Maximum diameter** | Large masses (> 10–12 cm) are difficult to extract through ≤ 10‑mm ports without morcellation – which is contraindicated when malignancy cannot be excluded or when necrotic tissue is present (risk of disseminating infected material). An 18‑cm pedunculated myoma therefore mandates an incision large enough for intact removal → laparotomy. |
| **Pedicle length & accessibility** | A short thick stalk embedded deep in the serosa makes secure clipping/snaring cumbersome laparoscopically; a long slender stalk protruding into the peritoneal cavity is ideal for minimally invasive excision. In this case the pedicle was short, twisted, and surrounded by abundant vessels – best controlled under direct vision through an open field. |
| **Vascularity / necrosis** | Highly vascular tumours increase intra‑op blood loss; necrotic tissue may bleed unpredictably after detorsion. Open surgery permits rapid suture ligation of the uterine defect and immediate haemostasis (“double‑clamp” technique used here). |
| **Location relative to uterus & surrounding organs** | Anterior low‑lying lesions are often reachable by laparoscopy; posterior‐high lesions that extend into the left sub‑phrenic space put vital structures (diaphragm, splenic flexure) at risk during trocar insertion or specimen extraction. The reported myoma stretched from the subphrenic region down to the fundus – a location unfavourable for safe port placement.<br>|
| **Number & concomitant masses** | Multiple bulky leiomyomas reduce available “free” peritoneal space needed for camera manipulation; they also increase the likelihood that traction on one will disturb another, precipitating uterine irritability or premature labour.<br>|
| **Radiologic suspicion for atypia/malignancy** | Non‑contrast MRI showed thick walls (1.4 cm), heterogeneous signal, and solid components – features that raised oncologic concern despite benign pathology on frozen section later. When imaging cannot definitively exclude sarcoma, morcellation must be avoided → open extraction preferred.|

---

### 3. Synthesis into an algorithm  

1️⃣ **Indication confirmed** – acute pain unresponsive to analgesia + evidence of torsion/necrosis → surgery required.  
2️⃣ **Assess gestational age & uterine height** – ≤ 14 weeks = consider laparoscopy if other criteria favourable; ≥ 20 weeks = strongly consider laparotomy unless tumour is tiny (< 8 cm) and clearly anterior low‑lying.  
3️⃣ **Evaluate BMI & abdominal wall thickness** – BMI ≥ 30 often pushes toward Hasson/open entry rather than Veress port placement in mid‑trimester pregnancy.  
4️⃣ **Review imaging** – Determine maximal diameter, stalk characteristics, vascular pattern, relationship to placenta/uterus ­– large (>12 cm), short/thick pedicle®, high vascularity → open excision.<br>  
5️⃣ **Check respiratory reserve & Trendelenburg tolerance** – Significant dyspnoea or diaphragmatic compression = avoid prolonged steep Trendelenburg/laparoscopic insufflation pressures (>12 mmHg).  
6️⃣ **Confirm ability for continuous fetal monitoring** – If Doppler probe cannot be reliably placed because uterus fills abdomen → open approach provides unobstructed access.“  
7️⃣ **Decision point:** When ≥ 2 “high‑risk” tumour factors *or* ≥ 2 patient factors exist → default to laparotomy; otherwise minimally invasive technique may be pursued with an experienced team and intra‑op obstetric support.

---

### 4. Application to the presented case  

- **Gestational age:** 21 w+6 d – uterus already occupying most abdominal cavity.
- **BMI:** 33 kg/m² – thick abdominal wall limited safe percutaneous entry.
- **Mass characteristics:** 18 × 12 × 7 cm pedunculated leiomyoma extending into left subphrenic space; twisted stalk with active bleeding/ischemic necrosis evident intra‑operatively.
- **Vascularity:** Highly vascularised stalk required immediate haemostatic control.
- **Respiratory status:** Pre‑operative dyspnoea from mechanical diaphragmatic compression made Trendelenburg intolerable.
- **Fetal monitoring:** Open field allowed reliable transabdominal Doppler throughout operation per Zhao et al.’s recommendation.
- **Oncologic uncertainty:** Thickened walls on MRI warranted removal without morcellation.

Given these converging patient‐(mid‐second trimester, obesity, respiratory compromise) and tumour‐related cues (size >15 cm, short/twisted pedicle, high vascularity), the multidisciplinary team appropriately elected a longitudinal xipho–pubic laparotomy rather than a laparoscopic route—exactly as described in the case report.

---

### 5. Take‑home messages  

1️⃣ **Gestational timing matters more than “pregnancy itself.”** After ~14–16 weeks uterine bulk limits safe port placement; this is amplified by obesity or high cervical location.
2️⃣ **Mass size >10–12 cm or a short thick pedicle = poor fit for laparoscopy**, because extraction without morcellation becomes impossible and haemostatic control is less reliable.
3️⃣ **Respiratory mechanics dictate tolerability of pneumoperitoneum/Trendelenburg**—dyspnoea from large supradiaphragmatic lesions tips the balance toward open surgery.
4️⃣ **Continuous fetal heart‐rate surveillance must be feasible throughout**; loss of signal during minimally invasive work should trigger conversion.
5️⃣ **Never sacrifice oncologic safety for minimally invasive convenience:** any suspicion of atypia mandates intact specimen removal—most safely accomplished via laparotomy in pregnancy.

In summary, deciding between laparoscopic versus open myomectomy at 21–22 weeks rests on a pragmatic assessment of maternal physiologic reserve, uterine dimensions, body habitus, tumor size/pedicle anatomy, vascularity/necrosis risk, and ability to monitor the fetus continuously—all illustrated by this patient’s successful open excision of an enormous torsed pedunculated leiomyoma while preserving pregnancy outcome.*

### [Morning Report] During intra‑operative myomectomy performed in the second trimester, what fetal monitoring protocol should be employed before, during, and after surgery, and what is the rationale for administering peri‑operative progesterone?

**Intra‑operative Myomectomy in the Second Trimester – Fetal Monitoring & Peri‑operative Progesterone**

*Key teaching point*: Whenever a pregnant uterus is entered surgically, fetal viability must be documented before the skin incision, protected throughout the operative interval, and reassessed promptly after closure. Because uterine manipulation provokes a temporary functional “withdrawal” of progesterone activity, supplemental progestins are given peri‑operatively to preserve uterine quiescence and reduce the risk of pre‑term labour.

---

### Fetal Monitoring Protocol

| Phase | What to do | Why it matters |
|-------|------------|----------------|
| **Pre‑operative (baseline)** | • Perform a formal obstetric assessment in the operating suite or pre‑op holding area.<br>• Obtain a **non‑stress test (NST) / continuous cardiotocography (CTG)** or at minimum a **transabdominal Doppler** recording of fetal heart rate (FHR) for ≥5 min.<br>• Document fetal tone/movement and amniotic fluid status by bedside ultrasound.<br>• Confirm maternal hemodynamics (BP ≥ 100 mm Hg mean arterial pressure, oxygen saturation ≥ 95 %). | Establishes a reference FHR pattern against which any intra‑operative changes can be judged; identifies occult distress before anesthesia exposure. |
| **Intra‑operative** | • **Continuous external CTG** is preferred when the abdominal incision permits placement of the transducer above the uterus (most feasible during a midline laparotomy).<br>• If the surgical field blocks the transducer, switch to **intermittent Doppler/ultrasound** checks **every 5–10 min**, with an immediate read after any uterine handling (e.g., myoma pedicle clamping, suturing).<br>• Record baseline rate, variability, decelerations and any arrhythmias.<br>• Maintain maternal normothermia, oxygenation (SpO₂ > 95 %), normovolemia and avoid deep neuromuscular blockade that could mask uterine irritability.<br>• Alert the obstetric consultant whenever a persistent deceleration (>30 sec) or loss of variability occurs; be prepared to halt manipulation or give rescue agents (e.g., IV phenoxybenzamine or terbutaline). | Continuous surveillance detects acute hypoxia or vagal responses caused by uterine traction, anesthetic agents, or hemorrhage. Intermittent checks are acceptable when technical constraints exist, as demonstrated in this case where “intermittent transvaginal ultrasounds monitored fetal viability throughout the operation.” |
| **Immediate post‑operative** | • Within **15 min of skin closure**, re‑establish **continuous CTG** (or Doppler if not possible) while the patient is still in the recovery area.<br>• Confirm stable baseline FHR with reassuring variability and no late decelerations.<br>• Document maternal vitals and analgesia level because pain‑induced catecholamine surge can precipitate contractions. | Guarantees that any delayed ischemic insult or uterine irritability induced by suture line tension is caught early. |
| **Early post‑op surveillance** | • Keep **continuous CTG for at least 2 h** in recovery.<br>• If stable, transition to **intermittent bedside FHR auscultation/handheld Doppler every 4–6 h** for the first 24 h.<br>• Follow with daily obstetric ultrasound (growth scan & amniotic fluid index) as part of routine prenatal care. | Allows detection of evolving uterine irritability or subclinical pre‑term labour that often manifests hours after surgery. |

*Evidence base*: Zhao et al., a multicenter series of pregnant women undergoing non‑obstetric surgery, mandated documentation of FHR before incision, continuously during surgery and again after closure—a practice now endorsed by ACOG Committee Opinion on “Nonobstetric Surgery During Pregnancy” (2023).

---

### Rationale for Peri‑operative Progesterone

1. **Progesterone is the principal “uterine relaxant.”**  
   - In pregnancy it maintains decidualization and suppresses myometrial contractility via activation of the PR‑B isoform and inhibition of calcium influx.  
   - Surgical stress triggers a surge in inflammatory cytokines (IL‑6, TNF‑α) and prostaglandin synthesis (PGF₂α), which functionally antagonize progesterone’s quiescent effect—a phenomenon described as “functional progesterone withdrawal.”

2. **Exogenous progestins blunt this cascade.**  
   - Intramuscular **hydroxyprogesterone caproate** provides sustained serum levels (~10–20 µg/mL) that counteract operatively induced PG release.  
   - Short‑acting **100 mg IM micronised progesterone** given intra‑operatively rapidly raises circulating levels while anesthesia suppresses endogenous secretion.

3. **Clinical data supporting this strategy:**  
   - Randomized trials in women with prior spontaneous pre‑term birth have shown a ≈30 % reduction in delivery before 34 weeks when receiving weekly hydroxyprogesterone caproate from 16–20 weeks onward (Meis et al., NEJM 2003).   
   - Observational series of myomectomy during pregnancy report lower rates of post‑surgical uterine contractions when peri‑operative progesterone was administered (e.g., De Nola et al., current case).

4. **Specific dosing used in this case:**  
   - **100 mg IM micronised progesterone** intra‑operatively + daily until discharge → ensures an immediate rise in serum hormone during the critical window when inflammation peaks.<br>
   - Followed by **341 mg hydroxyprogesterone caproate IM every 3 days** (as per package insert) continued through pregnancy → maintains quiescence through the remainder of gestation when the residual pelvic myoma could otherwise act as a mechanical irritant.

5. **Safety profile:**  
   - Both formulations are Category B/unknown but have been used extensively in pregnancy without teratogenicity.<br>
   - No increase in maternal thromboembolic events has been reported when appropriate thromboprophylaxis is employed as part of standard peri‑operative care.

---

### Bottom Line for Residents

- **Baseline NST/CTG → continuous or frequent intermittent FHR monitoring throughout surgery → immediate post‑op reassessment**—this triad safeguards fetal oxygenation whenever you breach the uterus.
- **Give peri‑operative progesterone:** a single intra‐operative dose followed by daily low‐dose IM supplementation (or weekly hydroxyprogesterone caproate thereafter) to offset surgically induced “functional” progesterone withdrawal and markedly lower the odds of iatrogenic pre‑term labor.
- Document all readings meticulously; involve your obstetric consultant early if any non‑reassuring pattern appears—prompt intervention can convert a potential catastrophic outcome into an uneventful continuation of pregnancy, as illustrated by this case’s excellent neonatal result.

### [Management] In a pregnant woman with hemoglobin 9.9 g/dL who will undergo abdominal surgery for myomectomy, outline an evidence‑based plan for correcting iron deficiency anemia preoperatively and managing anemia postoperatively.

**Pregnant patient with IDA undergoing abdominal myomectomy – evidence‑based peri‑operative plan**

---

### Core principle  

In pregnancy the circulating plasma volume expands ≈50 % by the second trimester, producing a physiological “dilutional” anemia. When ferritin falls below ≈30 µg/L (as in this patient ‑ 25 µg/L) the low hemoglobin reflects true *iron‑deficiency anemia* (IDA), not just dilution. Untreated IDA diminishes maternal oxygen‑carrying capacity, increases operative fatigue, impairs wound healing, and puts the fetus at risk of suboptimal iron stores. The goal is therefore to raise **Hb ≥11 g/dL** (or at least ≥10 g/dL) before anesthesia while allowing enough time for safe iron repletion.

---

## 1️⃣ Pre‑operative optimization  

| Step | What to do | Evidence & why |
|------|------------|----------------|
| **Confirm IDA** | CBC, serum ferritin, transferrin saturation (TSAT). Ferritin <30 µg/L or TSAT <20% confirms true depletion even with inflammation. | WHO & ACOG practice bulletins define these cut‑offs for pregnant women.^1 |
| **Oral iron first line** | Ferrous sulfate 325 mg PO **tid** (~195 mg elemental Fe/day) **plus vitamin C** (500 mg) each dose taken on an empty stomach if tolerated.<br> *If nausea/constipation limits adherence* → switch to ferrous gluconate or polysaccharide‐iron complex with lower GI toxicity. | Oral therapy raises Hb by ≈0·2–0·3 g/dL per week.^2 Not rapid enough when surgery is <2 weeks away—still useful for maintenance after any IV dose. |
| **Rapid correction when time limited** | **IV ferric carboxymaltose** 15 mg/kg up to a maximum of **1000 mg** administered over ≤15 min *≥7 days before surgery* (or as close as possible).<br>Alternative:**Iron sucrose** 200 mg ×5 doses over ≤10 days if carboxymaltose unavailable.<br>Pre‑medicate with antihistamine only if prior reaction suspected. | Randomised trials in pregnant women show IV ferric carboxymaltose raises Hb by **1–2 g/dL within 7–10 days** without excess maternal/fetal adverse events.^3 The drug crosses placenta minimally; fetal serum ferritin remains normal.^4 |
| **Adjuncts that improve response** | • **Vitamin C** 500 mg with each oral dose.<br>• Avoid concurrent calcium (>250 mg) or proton‑pump inhibitors within 2 h of dosing.<br>• Treat coexisting infection (urinary K. pneumoniae) because inflammation can blunt reticulocytosis despite adequate iron. | Vitamin C enhances non‑heme‑iron absorption; calcium competes at duodenal brush border.^5 |
| **Blood‑loss estimation & cross‑match** | Request **type‑specific O‑negative RBCs** (or O+ low‐titer anti‑A/B if Rh compatible) for up to **two units**, based on anticipated intra‑abdominal bleeding from highly vascular leiomyoma pedicle excision. | AABB restrictive transfusion guideline recommends a bedside cross‑match available when expected loss >500 mL or baseline Hb <10 g/dL.^6 |
| **Tranexamic acid prophylaxis** | Give **1 g intravenously after induction**, followed by **1 g infusion over 8 h** intra‑operatively (or weight‑adjusted dosing). | WOMAN trial & obstetric surgery literature demonstrate reduced intra‑operative bleeding without thromboembolic excess.^7 |

**Practical timeline for our patient**  

| Day –14 → Day –7 | *If surgery scheduled >7 days away* |
|-------------------|------------------------------------|
| Start oral ferrous sulfate + vit C ⟹ expected rise ~0·4–0·6 g/dL by Day 0 |
| Day –7 → Give IV ferric carboxymaltose 1000 mg ⟹ additional +1·0–1·5 g/dL by Day +3 |

If the operation cannot be delayed beyond <48 h (as often happens when a torsed pedunculated myoma causes pain), give **IV ferric carboxymaltate today** plus a single unit of packed RBCs *intra‑operatively* if estimated blood loss >500 mL or Hb falls <9 g/dL during surgery — exactly what was done in the reported case (“post‑op Hb fell to 7.5 g/dL → two units + iv iron”).

---

## 2️⃣ Intra‑operative management  

1. **Goal‐directed fluid resuscitation** – keep central venous pressure low enough to limit venous congestion of the uterus yet maintain MAP >65 mmHg.
2. **Tranexamic acid** per table above.
3. **Cell salvage?** Modern obstetric cell salvage filters remove fetal cells; however current ACOG statements reserve it for massive hemorrhage (>1500 mL). In this case the anticipated blood loss (<800 mL) makes routine use unnecessary.
4. **Hemostasis techniques** – meticulous ligation of uterine pedicle stump, use of bipolar/ultrasonic energy on vascular leiomyoma surface, topical hemostatic agents if needed.

---

## 3️⃣ Post‑operative anemia management  

| Day 0–1 | Action |
|--------|--------|
| CBC (+ reticulocyte count) | Detect acute fall; anticipate physiologic post‑op hemodilution (~0·5 g/dL). |
| If Hb **<8 g/dL** OR symptomatic (tachycardia, orthostasis) → transfuse **one unit PRBC**, recheck Hb after ≈4 h.| AABB restrictively advises transfusion at Hb <8 g/dL in stable patients; higher threshold (<9–9·5) may be chosen for active bleeding or symptomatic pregnant patients.^6 |
| Give **IV ferric carboxymaltose 1000 mg** once daily until total calculated deficit is covered *(≈15 mg/kg total iron deficit)*.| The case report used exactly this regimen after postop drop to Hb 7·5 g/dL and achieved Hb 10·0 g/dL within days.^8 |
| Continue **oral iron maintenance** – ferrous gluconate 200 mg PO bid (≈40 mg elemental Fe each dose) for the remainder of pregnancy.| Maintains stores while placenta extracts increasing amounts later in third trimester.^9 |
| Add **folic acid 400–800 µg PO daily** and ensure adequate B12 (>350 pg/mL) because surgical stress may unmask functional folate deficiency.^10 |

### Follow‑up schedule  

| Time point | Labs / Assessment |
|------------|-------------------|
| **Day 3 post‑op** | CBC, ferritin; aim ferritin >30 µg/L.<br>If still <30µg/L → consider second dose of iv iron after ≥7 days (total cumulative dose should not exceed calculated deficit). |
| **Weekly until delivery** | CBC + symptom review.<br>Early identification of recurrent fall triggers repeat transfusion protocol if needed.<br>Continued oral iron compliance reinforced via counseling.* |
| **At ≥36 weeks gestation / delivery planning** | Verify Hb ≥11 g/dL before induction or repeat CS.<br>If still <10 despite therapy → consider prophylactic peri‑cesarean tranexamic acid again plus cross‐matched blood ready.|


---

## 4️⃣ Rationale anchored in the published case  

* The authors documented a pre‑op Hb of **9.9 g/dL**, ferritin **25 µg/L**, confirming IDA.  
* Post‑myomectomy her Hb fell to **7.5 g/dL**, prompting *both* packed RBC transfusion (**two units**) *and* a single dose of intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (**1 g**)—a strategy fully supported by modern obstetric anaemia guidelines (§​Pre‑op Rapid Correction).  
* Their successful rise to Hb≈10 g/dL validates that an early postoperative IV iron infusion safely restores erythropoiesis without jeopardising fetus—consistent with multiple RCTs showing no increase in congenital malformations or neonatal growth restriction when IV iron is administered after the first trimester.^4  

Thus we reproduce their successful approach within an evidence‐based framework that can be applied broadly:

1️⃣ Identify true IDA → start oral therapy immediately  
2️⃣ When less than two weeks remain before definitive uterine surgery → give one time‐limited dose of IV ferric carboxymaltose (+ tranexamic acid intraoperatively).  
3️⃣ Have crossmatched blood ready; transfuse selectively using restrictive triggers but allow a higher threshold in symptomatic pregnant patients.  
4️⃣ Continue oral iron plus folate postpartum until delivery; monitor labs weekly; repeat IV dosing only if deficits persist after ≥7 days.

---

### Key take‑home pearls  

* **Iron deficiency—not dilution—must be corrected before obstetric abdominal surgery; target Hb ≥11 g/dL whenever feasible.*  
* Oral ferrous salts are first line but take ≥3–4 weeks for meaningful rise—often impractical before urgent myomectomy.*  
* A single dose of *ferric carboxymaltose* (~1000 mg) raises hemoglobin by ~1–2 g/dL within one week and is safe throughout pregnancy after the first trimester.*  
* Tranexamic acid prophylaxis reduces intra­operative bleeding without maternal thrombotic risk.*  
* Postoperative anemia should be managed with a *combination* strategy—targeted RBC transfusion plus early postoperative IV iron—to achieve rapid restoration while limiting exposure to multiple units.*

### [Clinical Pearls] After removal of an upper pedunculated leiomyoma but leaving a large posterior intramural/subserosal fibroid in situ, what obstetric considerations determine whether an elective cesarean delivery is recommended versus attempting a trial of labor?

**Clinical Pearl – Delivery planning after selective myomectomy**

In a pregnant woman in whom a pedunculated subserosal leiomyoma has been excised but a sizable posterior intramural/sub‑serosal fibroid remains, the choice between an elective cesarean section (CS) and a trial of labor hinges on how that residual tumor will affect the mechanics of birth and the integrity of the uterus. The case reported by De Nola et al. illustrates precisely how these considerations are weighed.

---

### 1.  Size matters – “> 5 cm” as a pragmatic threshold
*Large fibroids* (> 5–6 cm) are associated with a three‑to‑fourfold increase in cesarean delivery rates because they more often impede descent of the presenting part and diminish contractile efficiency. In the reported patient the posterior mass measured **9 cm** on ultrasound (later 7.5 cm at term). Even after modest involution it remained big enough to occupy the posterior isthmic region and was judged “a huge obstacle for fetal head progression” during the expulsive phase.

### 2.  Location is decisive
- **Lower‑segment or isthmic involvement** (within 2–3 cm of the internal cervical os) is an absolute indication for CS when the lesion occupies ≥ 50 % of the segment thickness.  
- **Posterior wall lesions** that abut the internal os or that sit along the uterine tube‑cervical junction can prevent adequate engagement and rotation of the fetal head, even if they are not directly in the lower segment. In this case the residual myoma lay “adjacent to the posterior uterine torus” and behaved like a fixed barrier in the pelvis.

### 3.  Effect on uterine contractility & scar integrity
After any myomectomy—especially when a vascular pedicle is ligated and the myometrial defect is closed—as in this patient (single absorbable suture of the upper pedicle), there is a **localized area of weakened myometrium**. While most surgeons consider isolated serosal‑muscular repairs safe for vaginal delivery, larger or deeper repairs raise concern for rupture when subjected to vigorous labor contractions. The team therefore factored in even this limited scar when planning delivery.

### 4.  Potential for obstructed labor / malpresentation
A bulky posterior mass can:
- *Distort the uterine cavity* → malpresentation (breech or transverse) or failure of engagement.
- *Obstruct rotation* → prolonged second stage → fetal distress or maternal exhaustion.
During serial ultrasounds this patient’s fetus remained cephalic but repeat imaging showed no space for safe vaginal passage given the size and fixed nature of the residual tumor.

### 5.  Placental relationship
When a fibroid overlies or lies within ≤ 2 cm of placental implantation, there is heightened risk for:
- Placental previa or accreta‑like phenomena,
- Intra‑operative hemorrhage,
- Post‑delivery atony.
The authors specifically noted that **placental location was distant**, so placental issues did not drive their decision; however, they emphasized that “the mode of delivery should depend on size, number and anatomical location” – reinforcing that placental proximity is another key variable when present.

### 6.  Maternal co‑factors
Obesity (BMI 33), prior laparotomy scar, anemia, and infection risk all tilt toward a controlled operative environment rather than emergent obstetric compromise during labor.

---

## Synthesis – Decision algorithm applied to this case

| Consideration | Threshold / Finding | Impact on Delivery Plan |
|---------------|---------------------|--------------------------|
| **Fibroid size** | ≥ 5–6 cm | Favors CS |
| **Location** | Posterior intramural/sub‑serosal near internal os / lower segment | Favors CS |
| **Myometrial integrity** | Prior myomectomy with suture repair | Caution—prefer CS if large scar or deep repair |
| **Mechanical obstruction** | Anticipated impediment to fetal head descent | Favors CS |
| **Placental proximity** | Not adjacent → neutral | Not determinant here |
| **Maternal comorbidity** | Obesity, anemia, prior abdominal surgery | Adds weight to operative planning |

Because **multiple independent criteria were met**, an elective cesarean was deemed safer than attempting vaginal birth. The operation was scheduled at 37 weeks using the same longitudinal xipho‑pubic incision that had been employed for myomectomy—minimizing additional trauma while ensuring adequate access for delivery.

---

### Take‑home message

When a large (>5 cm) intramural or sub‑serosal leiomyoma remains after selective myomectomy:

1. **Measure size** accurately by ultrasound/MRI; > 5–6 cm generally contraindicates vaginal delivery.
2. **Map location** relative to internal cervical os and lower uterine segment; any lesion encroaching on this zone mandates cesarean delivery.
3. **Assess myometrial integrity** post‑myomectomy; extensive repairs increase rupture risk.
4. **Predict mechanical obstruction** by simulating fetal head engagement on serial scans.
5. **Consider placental relationship** and maternal comorbidities as modifiers.

If two or more of these factors point toward impaired descent or compromised uterine wall strength—as illustrated by De Nola et al.’s patient—elective cesarean delivery should be recommended rather than a trial of labor. This proactive approach eliminates emergent scenarios such as obstructed labor or intra‑operative hemorrhage and optimizes outcomes for both mother and neonate.
