Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Clinical Presentation and Early Post‑operative Course  

The story begins with a septuagenarian man who entered the operating theatre already predisposed to thrombo‑embolic events. He had lived with chronic atrial fibrillation for two years and was maintained on apixaban 5 mg twice daily. Aside from age‑related vascular changes, his only other cardiovascular abnormality was a newly discovered systolic murmur that prompted further investigation. Diagnostic coronary angiography performed through a transradial approach revealed three pertinent lesions – a moderate aortic stenosis, a dilated ascending aorta, and a 60 % stenosis of an obtuse marginal branch – all factors that eventually led the heart team to schedule an elective combined aortic valve replacement, ascending‑aorta grafting and single‑vessel coronary artery bypass one week later.

### Pre‑operative heparin exposure  
Because his apixaban was not held before the catheterisation, the interventionalist administered only a low “flushed” dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH) – 2 000 U – sufficient simply to prevent catheter clotting. Although such a minute exposure would rarely cause clinical sequelae on its own, it nevertheless introduced the patient’s immune system to the prototypical PF4/heparin complex that underlies heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). In patients who are subsequently re‑exposed to larger amounts of UFH (as during cardiopulmonary bypass), this prior priming can accelerate antibody‐mediated platelet activation and give rise to an earlier‐than‑expected presentation of HIT or its autoimmune variant (aHIT).

### Intra‑operative anticoagulation  
During cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), UFH remains the anticoagulant of choice because it rapidly achieves therapeutic activated clotting times, has predictable pharmacokinetics irrespective of renal or hepatic function, and can be titrated point‑by‑point using ACT monitoring. In this case the perfusionist administered a total of **45 000 U** UFH before initiating CPB – a standard bolus for adult cardiac operations – followed by additional dosing as required to keep ACT >480 seconds throughout the nine‑hour bypass run. No complications related directly to heparin administration were noted intraoperatively; the operation finished without excess bleeding or transfusion requirements and the patient was weaned off bypass uneventfully.

### Early postoperative platelet trajectory  
The immediate postoperative laboratory panel established a baseline platelet count of **210 × 10⁹/L**, well within normal limits. A fall in platelets is expected after CPB because circulating platelets become sequestered on artificial surfaces and are partially consumed during extracorporeal circulation. Consequently most patients display a **moderate decline (~30 %) by postoperative day 1 (POD1)** – exactly what occurred here: the count dropped from 210 × 10⁹/L preoperatively to **155 × 10⁹/L on POD1**. This initial dip is generally transient and platelets normally begin rebounding by POD3–4 as marrow production compensates for consumption.

In our patient this recovery never materialized. Instead, **platelet counts fell steadily each successive day** despite cessation of surgical bleeding sources:

| Day | Platelet count (×10⁹/L) |
|-----|------------------------|
| POD1 | 155 |
| POD2 | ~130 |
| POD3 | ~110 |
| POD4 | ~80 |
| POD5 | **50** |
| POD6 | 26 |
| POD7 | **13** |

By **POD5**, the count had plunged below **100 × 10⁹/L**, crossing the threshold that raises alarm for an abnormal postoperative process such as infection, consumptive coagulopathy or drug‐induced immune thrombocytopenia. The trend continued unabated through **POD7**, reaching an **exceedingly low nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L**, far lower than what is typically observed from isolated CPB‐related thrombocytopenia (which rarely falls below ≈75–100 × 10⁹/L). The rapidity and depth of this decline signaled an active platelet‐destructive mechanism rather than simple sequestration or dilutional effects.

### Thromboprophylaxis regimen and its timing  
Standard practice after cardiac surgery includes pharmacologic venous thromboembolism prophylaxis beginning on POD1 unless contraindicated by severe bleeding risk. This patient received **dalteparin**, a low‑molecular‑weight heparin (LMWH), at **5 000 U subcutaneously** on POD1, escalated modestly (**7 500 U** daily) through POD4—a regimen consistent with contemporary guidelines for postoperative DVT prevention.

When his platelet count fell below **50 × 10⁹/L on POD5**, dalteparin was withheld out of concern for possible HIT—LMWH shares immunogenic PF4 complexes with UFH and can perpetuate antibody‐mediated platelet activation. As an interim measure, **prophylactic fondaparinux** (**2·5 mg SC**) was administered later that evening (POD5). Fondaparinux is a synthetic pentasaccharide factor Xa inhibitor that does not bind PF4 and therefore does not trigger classic HIT antibodies; its use therefore became an empiric bridge while awaiting diagnostic confirmation.

### Neurological evolution prompting imaging  
Postoperative delirium is common after sternotomy and CPB owing both to neuroinflammation and metabolic disturbances. In this case delirium gradually worsened despite stable vital signs, raising suspicion for cerebral pathology beyond simple encephalopathy—especially given the concomitant profound thrombocytopenia suggestive of ongoing microvascular thrombosis. On **POD6**, when platelets fell further to **26 × 10⁹/L** and then reproduced at **13 × 10⁹/L** two days later, clinicians ordered an urgent non‑contrast CT head scan. The study disclosed an acute ischemic infarct measuring roughly **3·2 × 1·8 cm** within the left parietal lobe extending through cortical layers—a classic radiologic signature of arterial embolic occlusion rather than hemorrhage—even though hemorrhagic transformation is less common when platelets are profoundly low but can still occur under hypercoagulable conditions mediated by activated platelets trapped in fibrin clots.

The combination of severe thrombocytopenia *and* an arterial stroke strongly suggested an underlying prothrombotic immune process—fits neatly into the clinical phenotype described for autoimmune HIT, wherein antibodies trigger platelet activation independent of heparin concentration leading both to consumptive thrombocytopenia *and* vigorous arterial thrombosis.

### Temporal pattern versus typical post‑cardiac‑surgery HIT  
Typical “classic” HIT following cardiothoracic procedures emerges **between postoperative days 5 and 10**—the interval required for de novo IgG anti‐PF4/heparin antibodies generated during intraoperative UFH exposure to reach clinically relevant titres. Consequently most textbooks teach clinicians to suspect HIT only after five days when there is >30 % fall in platelets relative to baseline plus new thrombosis or skin necrosis.

In contrast, our patient exhibited a significant fall already by **POD3–4**, well before the conventional window—an occurrence reported in roughly **10 %** of post‑cardiac‐surgery patients who develop HIT. The key differentiator is recent preoperative heparin exposure (the diagnostic catheterisation performed one week before surgery); such “priming” shortens the latency period because memory B cells rapidly produce anti-PF4/heparin IgG upon re‑challenge with large intra‑operative UFH doses. Moreover, autoimmune variants can display even earlier activation because once anti-PF4 antibodies are present they may already be capable of binding PF4 released from activated platelets even *without* additional heparin—a fact reflected later by laboratory assays showing strong serotonin release at zero added heparin concentration.

Thus, recognizing that this patient's dramatic platelet decline began *prior* to day 5—and coincided temporally with new neurologic deficits—served as an essential clue steering clinicians toward testing for HIT antibodies promptly rather than attributing findings solely to routine postcardiac surgery cytopenias or sepsis-related disseminated intravascular coagulation.

---

In summary, this case illustrates how careful attention to **baseline comorbidities**, **preoperative low-dose UFH exposure**, standard intra‑operative anticoagulation practices, **postoperative platelet kinetics**, timely modification of anticoagulant agents, and evolving neurologic status collectively point toward an atypical yet life‑threatening entity—autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia—which demands rapid diagnostic confirmation and targeted therapy beyond routine postoperative care.

## Differential Diagnosis of Post‑Cardiac‑Surgery Thrombocytopenia and Stroke  

Severe thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery is a frequent laboratory abnormality, yet only a minority of cases are clinically important enough to herald life‑threatening complications such as arterial thrombosis or intracerebral infarction. In the presented patient the nadir platelet count fell to 13 × 10⁹/L and was accompanied by an acute left‑parietal cerebral infarct on postoperative day 6. A systematic appraisal of alternative etiologies—ranging from benign peri‑operative physiologic changes to immune‑mediated disorders—helps avoid premature anchoring on a single diagnosis and guides timely testing for heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). The discussion below follows the major categories outlined in the case report and emphasizes why classic HIT emerged as the most plausible explanation despite an apparently early onset.

### Common postoperative causes: dilutional effect and consumption coagulopathy  

The immediate postoperative period is characterized by fluid shifts, intra‑operative crystalloid/colloid administration, and peri‑cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) hemoconcentration changes that can produce a modest “dilutional” fall in platelets. Typically this decline peaks at 60–80 % of baseline by postoperative day 2–3 and then rebounds spontaneously without reaching counts below 100 × 10⁹/L. In contrast, this patient’s platelet trajectory was progressive from 155 × 10⁹/L on POD 1 to 50 × 10⁹/L on POD 5 and finally 13 × 10⁹/L on POD 7—a >90 % reduction far exceeding the expected dilutional range. 

Consumption coagulopathy such as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) may accompany major surgery or massive transfusion, producing both thrombocytopenia and a pro‑thrombotic state. DIC is usually signaled by prolonged PT/INR and aPTT, low fibrinogen levels early in the course, elevated D‑dimer, and peripheral blood smear evidence of microangiopathic hemolysis. The patient’s coagulation profile showed normal PT/INR, normal aPTT, an *initially* high fibrinogen (6.59 g/L)—consistent with an acute phase reaction—and only later did fibrinogen fall modestly while D‑dimer rose during argatroban therapy. The absence of early fibrinogen consumption or overt coagulopathic bleeding makes isolated DIC an unlikely primary driver of the platelet nadir or the cerebral infarct.

### Mechanical destruction related to CPB circuitry or prosthetic valves  

Circuitry shear forces during CPB can activate platelets and cause marginal platelet loss; similarly, prosthetic valve leaflets may generate turbulence that promotes platelet activation downstream. However, these mechanisms produce a mild-to-moderate fall in platelets that stabilizes once extracorporeal circulation ceases, rarely yielding counts below 50 × 10⁹/L nor provoking arterial emboli in isolation. Moreover, mechanical hemolysis from valve dysfunction would manifest as schistocytes on peripheral smear early after surgery—a finding absent on POD 5 blood film (only later did occasional fragments appear under argatroban). Therefore mechanical destruction alone cannot account for the severity of thrombocytopenia nor the pro‑thrombotic stroke seen in this case.

### Drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia  

β‑lactam antibiotics (e.g., cefazolin), vancomycin, tranexamic acid, furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole and midodrine are all agents known rarely to trigger immune-mediated platelet clearance through drug‑dependent antibodies. The hallmark timing is a latency period of **≥5–7 days** after first exposure unless prior sensitisation exists; rapid‐onset reactions (<48 h) require preceding treatment courses that are not documented here. In this patient cefazolin and vancomycin were administered only once intraoperatively—a single dose insufficient to prime an immune response within five days postoperatively. Other drugs were introduced postoperatively but their exposure timelines do not align with the observed precipitous drop beginning on POD 1–2; furthermore none are strongly implicated in severe thrombocytopenia without accompanying drug rash or eosinophilia. Consequently drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia was deemed improbable based on both chronology and lack of supporting clinical clues.

### Post‑transfusion purpura  

Immune-mediated platelet destruction following transfusion occurs when recipient antibodies target donor HPA‐1a antigens present on transfused platelets; it typically manifests **5–12 days** after transfusion with profound thrombocytopenia (<20 × 10⁹/L). A crucial prerequisite is receipt of platelets—or any blood component containing platelets—within the preceding weeks. This patient received **no blood products** intraoperatively nor postoperatively, removing the essential sensitisation event altogether; thus post‑transfusion purpura can be excluded definitively.

### Thrombotic microangiopathies (TTP/HUS)  

Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) presents with *pentad*: severe thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), neurologic change, renal dysfunction, fever—often accompanied by ADAMTS13 activity <10 %. Laboratory hallmarks include schistocytes on peripheral smear and markedly reduced haptoglobin reflecting ongoing intravascular hemolysis. At presentation this patient’s haptoglobin was **elevated** (3.19 g/L), reticulocyte count normal, no schistocytes were noted on POD 5 smear, renal function was preserved, and fever was absent—all arguing against active MAHA/TTP at the time of platelet nadir. Although sporadic schistocytes appeared later during argatroban therapy—likely reflecting consumptive coagulopathy secondary to ongoing HIT—the initial absence of these findings excludes primary TMA as an etiology for either thrombocytopenia or stroke in this scenario.

### Cardioembolic versus pro‑thrombotic mechanisms for stroke after valve replacement  

Ischemic stroke following valve replacement may arise from three broad pathways: embolization from atrial fibrillation (AF), mechanical emboli shed from prosthetic material during implantation or manipulation, and hypercoagulability secondary to perioperative inflammation or HIT. The patient had chronic AF managed preoperatively with apixaban but ceased anticoagulation per usual surgical protocol while receiving low molecular weight heparin prophylaxis thereafter. Nonetheless AF alone rarely produces large territorial infarcts immediately after surgery without additional provoking factors such as abrupt cessation of anticoagulation combined with endothelial injury—a scenario partially present here but insufficient to explain simultaneous profound platelet depletion and rising D‑dimer indicative of active clot formation elsewhere.

In contrast, HIT precipitates a **pro‑thrombotic** milieu through FcγRIIa‐mediated platelet activation leading to widespread microvascular thrombi that can involve cerebral vessels even in the absence of large emboli visible on imaging at presentation. The temporal association between worsening delirium/platelet count decline and detection of an acute parietal infarct aligns neatly with a HIT‐driven mechanism rather than an isolated cardioembolic event.

### Application of a modified 4Ts scoring system for post‑surgical patients  

The classic “4Ts” score—*Thrombocytopenia*, *Timing*, *Thrombosis*, *other causes*—has been adapted for surgical contexts by relaxing the timing window to include preoperative heparin exposures within one week before surgery (“early onset”). Applying this framework:

| Component | Observation | Points |
|-----------|-------------|--------|
| **Thrombocytosis** | Platelet count fell from 210 → 13 × 10⁹/L (>50 % decline); nadir <20 × 10⁹/L | **2** |
| **Timing** | First heparin exposure at diagnostic catheterisation (day ‑7); second exposure intraoperatively (>0 days); platelet fall began POD 1–2 → fits “early onset” category | **2** |
| **Thrombosis** | New cerebral infarct confirmed on CT | **2** |
| **Other causes** | No clear alternative explanation after systematic exclusion | **2** |

Total ≈8/8 points—strongly predictive (>85 % probability) for HIT despite atypical early onset relative to traditional cardiac surgery timelines (POD 5–10). The modified score underscores how integrating preoperative UFH exposure shifts the temporal expectations sufficiently to keep HIT atop the differential list even before confirmatory assays return.

### Integration of clinical clues that prioritize HIT  

Three overlapping data streams converged toward HIT as the leading diagnosis:

1️⃣ **Magnitude & kinetics of platelet fall** – A >90 % drop reaching extreme nadir (<15 × 10⁹/L) far exceeds what dilutional effects or CPB‐related consumption would produce.<br>
2️⃣ **Temporal relationship** – Though “classic” surgical HIT usually appears POD 5–10, recognition that UFH administered during preoperative catheterisation primed anti‐PF4 antibodies explains an earlier decline beginning POD 1.<br>
3️⃣ **Thrombotic manifestation concomitant with thrombocytopenia** – An acute ischemic stroke paired with high D‑dimer levels signals ongoing thrombin generation characteristic of HIT rather than isolated cardioembolic events.<br>

After exhausting more common explanations—dilutional changes, DIC without laboratory corroboration, drug‐dependent immune cytopenias lacking appropriate latency periods—and given the pretest probability furnished by the modified 4Ts score (>6 points being “high”), clinicians rightfully proceeded with PF4/heparin ELISA screening followed by functional testing (serotonin release assay). The eventual detection of heparin‐independent platelet activation confirmed autoimmune HIT—a rare variant but one whose identification was catalyzed by rigorous differential reasoning outlined above.

In practice this stepwise exclusion process safeguards against missing atypical presentations such as early‐onset autoimmune HIT while preventing unnecessary treatment for low‐probability alternatives. The subsequent sections will explore how laboratory confirmation guided therapeutic decisions in this patient’s complex postoperative course.

## Pathophysiology and Laboratory Diagnosis of Classic HIT versus Autoimmune HIT  

Heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is an immune‑mediated drug reaction in which antibodies directed against complexes of platelet factor 4 (PF4) and heparin trigger platelet activation, consumption, and a pro‑thrombotic state. Understanding how these antibodies form, how they behave at different heparin concentrations, and how they are detected in the laboratory is essential for interpreting the unusual laboratory findings in the present case of autoimmune HIT (aHIT).

### Complex formation and FcγRIIa‐mediated activation in classic HIT  

PF4 is a positively charged chemokine stored in α‑granules and released from activated platelets. When unfractionated heparin (UFH) or low‑molecular‑weight heparin (LMWH) is present, the negatively charged sulfate groups on the polysaccharide bind PF4 electrostatically, generating multimolecular PF4/heparin complexes that expose neo‑epitopes not present on either component alone. In a subset of patients, IgG antibodies recognise these neo‑epitopes. The immune complexes then bind platelet FcγRIIa (CD32a), an activating receptor that clusters when two or more IgG Fc regions are juxtaposed. Cross‑linking of FcγRIIa triggers intracellular signaling cascades leading to platelet degranulation, thromboxane A₂ production, and expression of pro‑coagulant phosphatidylserine – ultimately causing thrombocytopenia and thrombosis.

### Heparin concentration dependence  

The magnitude of platelet activation depends on heparin concentration because the stoichiometry of PF4/heparin complexes changes with dose. At low therapeutic concentrations (≈0.1–0.3 U/mL UFH), optimal PF4/heparin lattices form that maximize antibody binding and thus platelet activation. When the heparin concentration is excessively high (≥100 U/mL), excess heparin saturates PF4 binding sites, preventing lattice formation; the antibody can no longer bridge PF4 molecules and platelet activation falls dramatically (“high‑dose inhibition”). This biphasic response is the cornerstone of functional HIT testing.

### Autoimmune HIT (aHIT): Heparin‑independent antibodies  

A subset of patients develop anti‑PF4 antibodies that can activate platelets without any exogenous heparin – a phenomenon termed autoimmune or “spontaneous” HIT. In aHIT the antibody’s paratope has sufficient affinity for PF4 alone that it can form immune complexes on the platelet surface even when heparin is absent or minimal. Consequently, platelet activation occurs at “zero” heparin concentration (0 U/mL) and often persists despite high‑dose heparin because the antibody’s binding site does not require the polyanionic scaffold provided by heparin. Clinically, aHIT frequently presents with an unusually severe thrombocytopenia, rapid thrombotic complications such as arterial stroke, and can occur early after exposure to even modest amounts of UFH—as illustrated by our patient who had received only 2 000 U during pre‑operative cardiac catheterisation.

### Immunoglobulin subclass profile  

The pathogenic antibodies in both classic HIT and aHIT are overwhelmingly IgG (predominantly IgG1 and IgG3), reflecting their ability to engage FcγRIIa efficiently. Small contributions from IgM or IgA may be detected by some ELISA platforms but these subclasses lack the requisite Fc portion for robust FcγRIIa cross‑linking and are therefore less relevant to platelet activation. The dominance of IgG explains why assays that detect only IgG anti‑PF4/heparin antibodies have higher predictive value for clinically significant HIT.

### Screening immunoassay: PF4/polyanion ELISA  

The enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for PF4/polyanion antibodies is highly sensitive (>95 %) for detecting anti‑PF4 antibodies because it captures any immunoglobulin that binds immobilised PF4/heparin complexes regardless of functional activity. Results are reported as optical density (OD); laboratory‐specific cut‑offs vary but an OD ≥ 0.40 is usually considered positive, while OD ≥ 1.0–2.0 carries a greater positive predictive value for clinically relevant HIT. In our patient the ELISA yielded an OD = 2.2—a strong positive suggesting abundant antibody production.

Because ELISA cannot discriminate between non‑activating “benign” antibodies and pathogenic ones, a functional assay is required whenever clinical suspicion persists despite a positive ELISA.

### Functional assay fundamentals: Serotonine Release Assay (SRA)  

The gold standard functional test is the serotonin‑release assay. Donor platelets are washed and loaded with radiolabelled ^14C‑serotonine. Patient serum is added in parallel reactions containing three concentrations of UFH:

1. **No heparin** (0 U/mL)
2. **Low therapeutic dose** (~0.1–0.3 U/mL)
3

**High dose** (~100 U/mL)

If patient antibodies activate platelets, serotonin stored within granules is released into the supernatant; radioactivity is measured and expressed as percent release relative to maximal release induced by thrombin (set at 100 %). A result ≥20 % at low dose **and** ≤20 % at high dose fulfills the classic definition of a positive SRA (“low-dose enhancement + high-dose inhibition”). Importantly, no release at 0 U/mL differentiates classic HIT from aHIT.

In our case serologic testing showed:

* **0 U/mL:** 96 % release → clear evidence of heparindependent activation.
* **0.1–0.3 U/mL:** 97–99 % release → robust low‑dose enhancement.
* **100 U/mL:** 0 % release → preserved high‑dose inhibition characteristic of PF4/heparin complex formation.

This pattern satisfies criteria for aHIT rather than classic HIT.

### Role of FcγRIIa blockade with monoclonal antibody IV.​3  

To confirm that platelet activation is mediated through FcγRIIa, many laboratories add IV.​3—a monoclonal antibody that sterically blocks this receptor—to the SRA mixture. In classic HIT sera, IV.​3 almost completely abolishes serotonin release (<20 %). In our patient’s serum, however, IV.​3 reduced but did not eliminate activation at low UFH: ~76 % release remained despite receptor blockade. This incomplete inhibition can be interpreted in two ways:

1️⃣ The antibodies possess extremely high affinity/avidity such that transient residual crosslinking occurs even when one receptor site is occluded.
2️⃣ A proportion of activation proceeds through alternative pathways—e.g., direct PF4 clustering on the platelet surface independent of FcγRIIa—which is consistent with immune complexes capable of “auto” engagement as seen in aHIT.

Consequently the laboratory reported an “indeterminate” SRA because standard interpretation algorithms expect near‐total suppression by IV.​3 for HIT‐type antibodies. Recognising that partial inhibition may signify especially potent auto­immune antibodies prevents misclassification and prompts clinical management appropriate for severe aHIT—precisely what happened in this case.

### Other functional assays: P‑selectin expression  

When SRA results are equivocal or unavailable, flow cytometric detection of platelet P‑selectin (CD62P) upregulation after exposure to patient serum offers an alternative functional readout (the PF4‑dependent P‑selectin expression assay). Like SRA, it evaluates low versus high heparin conditions and can be performed with whole blood rather than radiolabelled platelets—enhancing safety and accessibility. However, its sensitivity is modestly lower than SRA’s (~85 %) though specificity remains high when combined with an ELISA‐positive result.

### Applying these concepts to the case  

The integration of pathophysiology with laboratory data allowed clinicians to identify an atypical manifestation of HIT:

* **Pathogenic mechanism:** Preoperative UFH primed anti‑PF4 IgG production; subsequent surgery supplied large quantities of PF4 from activated platelets, generating abundant PF4 alone sufficient to trigger auto­immune antibody binding without additional heparin.
* **Laboratory signature:** A strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA confirmed antibody presence; an SRA showing massive serotonin release at zero heparin plus only partial abrogation by IV.​3 cemented the diagnosis of autoimmune HIT.
* **Clinical implication:** Because standard protocols assume complete blockade by IV.​3 as proof of FcγRIIa dependence, recognizing partial inhibition as evidence for unusually potent auto­immune antibodies prevented dismissal as “indeterminate” and guided aggressive anticoagulation with argatroban followed by fondaparinux—a regimen suitable for severe aHIT where thrombotic risk outweighs bleeding concerns.

In summary, classic HIT relies on heparin-dependent immune complexes that activate platelets through FcγRIIa crosslinking at therapeutic heparin concentrations only; autoimmune HIT extends this paradigm by enabling platelet activation without any exogenous heparin—reflected by distinct patterns on both screening ELISA and functional assays such as the serotonin-release assay—and may exhibit incomplete suppression by receptor blocking agents due to heightened antibody potency or alternative activation mechanisms. Mastery of these concepts equips clinicians to detect atypical presentations like the one described herein and to institute timely therapy that averts catastrophic thrombosis while supporting platelet recovery.
