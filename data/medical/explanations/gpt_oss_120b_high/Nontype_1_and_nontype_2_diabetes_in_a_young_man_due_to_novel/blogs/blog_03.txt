Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# Rethinking First‑Line Therapy: GLP‑1 Receptor Agonists Versus Sulfonylureas in **HNF1A‑MODY**

---

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

When you think of the textbook treatment for HNF1A‑MODY you picture a low‑dose sulfonylurea—cheap, effective, and historically “the answer”. Yet a young adult with a **novel HNF1A missense mutation** (p.N270S) was taken off basal insulin **and** put on dulaglutide, a weekly GLP‑1 receptor agonist (GLRA). Within six months his HbA₁c plunged from **10 %** to **6 %** without any sulfonylurea exposure at all.  

That raises two provocative questions:

* Are sulfonylureas still the best first line for every HNF1A‑MODY patient?  
* Can we safely swing the therapeutic pendulum toward GLRAs—especially when they may spare β‑cell reserve and avoid hypoglycaemia?

The answer isn’t “yes” or “no” across the board; it’s nuanced by disease stage, comorbidities, patient preference, and now emerging comparative data.

---

## A rapid MODY refresher  

* **MODY** = monogenic β‑cell disease, autosomal dominant, usually diagnosed before age 25.  
* **HNF1A‑MODY** accounts for ~30–65 % of all MODY cases—making it the single most common subtype worldwide.  
* Classic clues: early‐onset hyperglycaemia, **glycosuria out of proportion to plasma glucose**, negative pancreatic autoantibids (GAD65, IA‑2), modestly elevated BMI (often <30 kg/m²), and an *autosomal dominant* family history of diabetes without obvious insulin resistance.  
* Pathophysiology hinges on reduced hepatic/glomerular expression of GLUT2‐regulated genes plus impaired transcriptional activation of the insulin gene cascade—culminating in progressive β‑cell dysfunction while renal SGLT2 expression falls, explaining the low renal glucose threshold.  

Because diagnosis hinges on suspicion rather than routine labs, tools such as the **Exeter MODY probability calculator** are invaluable—though they were derived primarily from White European cohorts and may over‐estimate probabilities in Asian patients (the case here scored **62 %**). A high‐index suspicion should prompt genetic testing early rather than waiting for years of “type 2” therapy failure.

---

## The historical dogma – low dose sulfonylureas  

Sulfonylureas (SUs) bind the K⁺_ATP channel on β‐cells → depolarisation → Ca²⁺ influx → insulin release independent of ambient glucose:

* **Pros**  
  * Potent glucose‐lowering even when endogenous insulin secretion is modestly preserved (stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L).  
  * Oral administration—no injection barrier.  
  * In several series they achieve HbA₁c reductions comparable to metformin + SU combos within weeks.
* **Cons**  
  * **Glucose‐independent** secretion = higher hypoglycaemia risk—particularly as disease progresses and residual β‐cell mass falls.  
  * Concerns (still debated) about accelerated β‑cell exhaustion when used chronically in MODY cohorts versus native secretory capacity.  
  * Weight gain (~2–3 kg) which may be undesirable even in modestly overweight patients like our 24‑year‐old with BMI 24.6 kg/m².

Guidelines have long placed low dose SUs as “first line” once the diagnosis is confirmed—often glimepiride 0.5–2 mg daily or gliclazide MR 30–60 mg daily.

---

## Emerging evidence – GLP‑1 receptor agonists  

GLRAs act **glucose‐dependently**, amplifying glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion while suppressing inappropriate glucagon release and slowing gastric emptying:

| Feature | Sulfonylurea | GLRA |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | K⁺_ATP closure → insulin release irrespective of glucose | cAMP ↑ → potentiated glucose‐dependent insulin release |
| Hypoglycaemia | Common if meals missed/overdose | Rare (<5 %) because effect wanes as glucose falls |
| Weight | Neutral/↑ | ↓ (average −3 kg) |
| Injection | No | Yes (weekly subQ for dulaglutide/semaglutide) |
| Renal clearance | Not dependent | Dose adjustment needed if eGFR <30 ml/min |

### Trial data in HNF1A‑MODY  

* A small double‐blind crossover trial directly compared **liraglutide** (0.9–1.8 mg daily) with **glimepiride** (up to 4 mg daily) in patients carrying pathogenic HNF1A variants. Both agents lowered fasting plasma glucose and postprandial excursions similarly—but liraglutide demonstrated a **significantly lower rate of mild hypoglycaemia** and produced modest weight loss compared with glimepiride’s slight weight gain.
* No major differences were noted in C‑peptide kinetics over the short crossover period (~8 weeks), suggesting comparable β‑cell stimulation when glucose levels are high.
* Importantly, because GLRAs require an intact incretin axis—a pathway preserved in HNF1A‑MODY—their efficacy appears robust even when basal β‑cell capacity is waning.

Thus while not yet powered trials, these data hint that GLRAs can match SUs on glycaemic control while offering a better safety/tolerability profile—particularly valuable when patients are already experiencing modest obesity or have a history of severe hypoglycaemia on SUs.

## The case dissected  

| Timeline | Intervention | Glycaemic outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Presentation (age 24) | Basal insulin degludec 8 U + metformin 1500 mg + saxagliptin 5 mg (empiric T2D regimen) | HbA₁c 10.2 % |
| Week 4 | Genetic panel ⇒ heterozygous pathogenic **HNF1A p.N270S** variant (+ negative GADA/IA‑2) | HbA₁c down to 9.1 % |
| Week 5–6 | Insulin stopped → dulaglutide 0·75 mg weekly added (titrated to 1·5 mg) while continuing metformin & saxagliptin | Steady decline |
| Month 6 | HbA₁c reached **6 %**, weight stable/no overt hypoglycaemia | Metformin + saxagliptin maintained |

Key observations:

* The patient’s **stimulated C‑peptide was only modestly preserved** (0·83 nmol/L), yet he achieved excellent control with a single weekly injection rather than increasing SU dose—suggesting adequate residual β‑cell response when driven by an incretin mimetic.
* Insulin discontinuation after just one month illustrates how decisive molecular diagnosis can be—it spared months-long exposure to basal insulin plus its associated monitoring burden.
* Transitioning back later to oral agents alone was possible after achieving target HbA₁c—a pragmatic real-world pathway many centres still miss because they assume lifelong injectables are required once started on GLRAs.

## Practical algorithm – From suspicion to tailored therapy  

Below is an approach distilled from current guidelines blended with insights from this case:

1. **Screen early:** Young adult (<30 y) with unexplained hyperglycaemia + glycosuria + negative autoantibodies → calculate MODY probability (>20–30 % warrants genetics).  
2. **Initiate provisional therapy:** Start basal insulin ***only*** if glucose >300–350 mg/dL or symptomatic hyperglycaemia/ketosis risk exists; add metformin ± DPP4i if BMI >25 kg/m² or metabolic syndrome features present—as we did here.
3. **Obtain confirmatory genetics:** NGS panel covering ≥15 MODY genes—including HNF1A exon coverage—and interpret per ACMG criteria.
4. **Post‑genetics decision tree:**  

   *If HNF1A pathogenic variant confirmed:*  

   - **Option A – Traditional SU route:** Initiate low dose glimepiride/gliclazide MR (≤2 mg daily). Titrate based on fasting SMBG goal <130 mg/dL; educate aggressively about hypoglycaemia avoidance especially if C-peptide <0·5 nmol/L.
   - **Option B – Incretin route:** Start weekly dulaglutide 0·75 mg → titrate to 1·5 mg after ≥4 weeks if tolerable; continue metformin if already established (<2500 mg/day). Reserve DPP4i for added modest benefit or cost considerations.
   - Assess response at 4–8 weeks: Target HbA₁c <7 %; evaluate weight trend (+/-5 %); review hypoglycaemia logs.
   - If control achieved ➜ Can de‐escalate insulins or shift entirely off injectables if Option B chosen.
   - Reassess ß-cell reserve periodically via stimulated C-peptide; consider SU if GLRA response wanes or cost becomes prohibitive.
5. **Special considerations:**  

   - Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors in confirmed HNF1A‐MODY—reduced renal GLUT expression predisposes to volume depletion & ketoacidosis despite normoglycaemia.
   - Counsel regarding family screening – cascade testing can uncover asymptomatic carriers who may benefit from early lifestyle measures or pharmacotherapy.
   - Document genotype for future precision medicine trials – each novel variant expands our therapeutic knowledge base.

## Clinical pearls & pitfalls  

* **Glycosuria disproportionate to plasma glucose (>180 mg/dL)** is a red flag for HNF1A/MOD​Y—not just uncontrolled diabetes.
* Negative pancreatic autoantibodies do NOT rule out type 1 diabetes early on—but together with preserved C-peptide they strongly suggest monogenic disease.
* Even modest BMI values can mask underlying MODY—in regions where obesity prevalence has risen dramatically these metrics lose discrimination power.
* Hypoglycaemia remains the main limiting factor for sulfonylureas—especially once beta cell reserve declines (<0·6 nmol/L stimulated C-peptide).
* GLRAs provide *glucose-dependent* augmentation—greatly reducing hypoglycaemia risk—but require injection training—and some patients have GI intolerance that may limit up-titration.
* When transitioning from insulin after genetic confirmation, consider “rapid de-escalation” if blood sugars stabilize under oral/incretin therapy—this avoids unnecessary long-term basal insulin exposure.
* Always screen for microvascular complications once control achieved—even though MODY patients often have fewer macrovascular comorbidities early on!

---

### Take‑home points  

- **Genetic confirmation changes everything:** In HNF1A-MODY it allows targeted therapy that can replace basal insulin within weeks.
- **Low-dose sulfonylureas remain effective**, especially early when C-peptide is robust—but carry notable hypoglycaemia risk as β-cell function wanes.
- **GLP‑1 receptor agonists are a viable first-line alternative:** Evidence shows comparable glycemic efficacy with lower hypoglycemia rates and weight loss benefits; dulaglutide enabled complete insulin discontinuation in the presented case.
- **Therapy choice should be individualized:** Consider patient BMI/comorbidities, tolerance for injections, cost/access issues, and residual β-cell reserve reflected by stimulated C-peptide.
- **Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors** in confirmed HNF1A-MODY due to impaired renal SGLT2 expression and heightened DKA/dehydration risks.
- **Implement a systematic algorithm** after genetic diagnosis—start low-dose SU *or* GLRA based on shared decision making, re-evaluate quarterly, and cascade test relatives promptly.

In an era where precision medicine promises “right drug for the right mutation,” this case reminds us that sometimes embracing newer pharmacologic tools like GLRAs can achieve outcomes previously reserved for injectable insulins—while preserving quality of life and minimizing adverse events. Keep your MODY suspicion high and your therapeutic toolbox diverse!
