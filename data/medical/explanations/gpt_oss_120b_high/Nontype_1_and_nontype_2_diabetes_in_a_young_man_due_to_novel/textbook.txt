Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Clinical Presentation & Initial Assessment  

The patient was a 22‑year‑old man who was referred to endocrinology after routine health screening revealed unexpected hyperglycaemia accompanied by glycosuria. Although his body‑mass index (BMI) was 24.6 kg/m²—borderline between normal weight and overweight—he did not display classic stigmata of insulin resistance such as acanthosis nigricans on physical examination. Vital signs were unremarkable (blood pressure 112/70 mmHg, heart rate 78 beats/min), suggesting that overt cardiovascular stress was absent at presentation. The chief complaints comprised classic osmotic symptoms of diabetes: increased thirst (polydipsia), excessive urination (polyuria), heightened appetite (polyphagia), and recent unintentional weight loss. These symptoms reflect the physiologic consequences of chronic hyperglycaemia—glucose spilling into the renal tubules creates an osmotic diuresis that drives fluid loss (polyuria) and triggers thirst, while cellular glucose deprivation stimulates hunger despite elevated plasma glucose levels.

A salient clue emerged from the three‑generation pedigree obtained during history taking. The patient’s maternal grandmother developed diabetes in her thirties, his mother was diagnosed in her late twenties, and his younger brother had become diabetic at age 20. Such an autosomal dominant pattern strongly raises suspicion for a monogenic form of diabetes rather than common type 1 or type 2 disease, where familial clustering typically follows different inheritance models or reflects polygenic risk factors combined with environmental contributors. In monogenic diabetes syndromes such as maturity‑onset diabetes of the young (MODY), the disease manifests before age 25 and often tracks through multiple successive generations with relatively early onset, as illustrated by this family tree.

Laboratory evaluation confirmed marked dysglycaemia. A fasting plasma glucose of 12.4 mmol/L exceeded the diagnostic threshold for diabetes (>7.0 mmol/L), while glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) was 10.2 % (88 mmol/mol), indicating chronic exposure of red blood cells to high glucose concentrations over approximately three months. To further characterize glucose handling, a standard 75‑g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was performed; two‑hour post‑challenge glucose rose to 19.8 mmol/L—well above the diagnostic cutoff of 11.1 mmol/L—demonstrating severe postprandial glucose intolerance typical of β‑cell dysfunction.

A concurrent lipid panel showed fasting triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L (≈382 mg/dL) together with a high‑density lipoprotein cholesterol level of 1.01 mmol/L (~39 mg/dL). These abnormalities suggest associated dyslipidaemia often seen in uncontrolled diabetes, yet they do not differentiate among diabetes subtypes because both insulin‑deficient and insulin‑resistant states can cause hypertriglyceridaemia through hepatic overproduction of very‑low‑density lipoprotein particles. Importantly, there were no clinical or laboratory signs of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA): arterial pH remained normal, serum ketones were undetectable, and anion gap was not elevated, arguing against fulminant β‑cell failure characteristic of classic autoimmune type 1 diabetes.

Autoimmune markers further helped narrow the differential diagnosis. Both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen‑2 antibodies (IA‑2A) were negative, decreasing the likelihood of an immune‐mediated β‑cell attack that underlies type 1 disease. To objectively assess residual β‑cell function, a glucagon stimulation test was performed; fasting C‑peptide measured 0–minute baseline at 0.39 nmol/L and rose modestly to 0.83 nmol/L at six minutes post‑glucagon injection. While any increase above ~0.60 nmol/L demonstrates some preserved endogenous insulin secretion, the absolute values remained low compared with healthy individuals (>1–2 nmol/L). This pattern—partial but insufficient C‑peptide response—is typical for HNF1A‐related MODY where progressive impairment rather than outright loss of β‑cell activity occurs.

Taken together—early adulthood onset, strong autosomal dominant family history, absence of obesity‐related skin changes, lack of pancreatic autoantibodies, preserved yet suboptimal C‑peptide response, and severe hyperglycaemia without ketosis—the clinical picture pointed toward an atypical form of diabetes that does not fit neatly into either classic type 1 or type 2 categories. These features justified proceeding to targeted molecular testing for MODY genes, particularly HNF1A given its prevalence among MODY subtypes and the biochemical phenotype observed in this patient. Early recognition at this stage is critical because it directly influences therapeutic choices and informs cascade screening for at‑risk relatives.

## Differential Diagnosis – Distinguishing MODY from Type 1 and Type 2 Diabetes  

The first step in evaluating any young adult with newly recognised hyperglycaemia is to determine whether the disorder follows an autoimmune pathway (type 1 diabetes mellitus, T1DM), an insulin‑resistance pathway (type 2 diabetes mellitus, T2DM), or a monogenic β‑cell defect such as maturity‑onset diabetes of the young (MODY). The distinction is not merely academic; it dictates therapy, informs prognosis, and guides family counselling. A systematic approach blends careful history‑taking with targeted laboratory testing and validated prediction tools. The present case—an otherwise healthy 23‑year‑old man with marked hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, a striking family history of early‑onset diabetes, and negative islet autoantibodies—illustrates how each element contributes to narrowing the differential diagnosis.

### Core Features Distinguishing Type 1 Diabetes  

T1DM results from immune‑mediated destruction of pancreatic β‑cells. Consequently three hallmarks dominate its presentation:

1. **Islet Autoantibodies** – At diagnosis the majority of patients have detectable antibodies against glutamic acid decarboxylase (GADA), insulinoma‑associated antigen‑2 (IA‑2A), zinc transporter‑8 (ZnT8) or insulin itself. Their presence carries a high positive predictive value for autoimmune diabetes and is incorporated into most diagnostic algorithms.  
2. **Rapid Loss of C‑Peptide** – Because β‑cell mass declines precipitously, fasting or stimulated C‑peptide falls below the threshold for clinically meaningful endogenous insulin within months. A stimulated C‑peptide ≤0.30 nmol/L after glucagon or mixed‑meal challenge virtually excludes significant residual secretion.  
3. **Lean Phenotype** – Most individuals are of normal weight or underweight at onset; overt signs of insulin resistance such as acanthosis nigricans are uncommon. Weight loss often accompanies hyperglycaemia due to catabolism driven by absolute insulin deficiency.

When these three elements co‐occur—early age at presentation (<30 years), positive autoantibody panel, and very low stimulated C‑peptide—a diagnosis of T1DM is strongly favoured.

### Core Features Distinguishing Type 2 Diabetes  

In contrast, T2DM stems primarily from peripheral insulin resistance superimposed on a relative defect in insulin secretion:

* **Evidence of Insulin Resistance** – Obesity (BMI >30 kg/m²) and cutaneous markers such as acanthosis nigricans point toward chronic hyperinsulinaemia driving glucose intolerance.
* **Metabolic‑Syndrome Lipid Pattern** – Elevated triglycerides (>1.7 mmol/L), reduced high‑density lipoprotein cholesterol (<1.0 mmol/L in men), and often hypertension together form a dyslipidaemic profile typical for T2DM.
* **Preserved C‑Peptide Early in Disease** – Because β‑cells remain viable for many years despite being overworked, fasting C‑peptide is usually >0·6 nmol/L at diagnosis; it may even be high because of compensatory hyperinsulinaemia.
* **Later Age at Onset** – Although childhood obesity has shifted the age spectrum downward, a median age >40 years remains characteristic for classic T2DM.

The presence of these findings supports an initial therapeutic strategy focussed on enhancing insulin sensitivity (e.g., metformin) rather than replacing insulin outright.

### Interpretation of Patient Data  

Applying these discriminators to our patient clarifies why neither classic T1DM nor classic T2DM fully fits:

* **Autoantibody Profile** – Both GADA and IA‑2A were absent. This finding essentially rules out an autoimmune etiology unless one invokes the rare “autoantibody‑negative” form of T1DM, which still would manifest rapid C‑peptide loss.
* **C‑Peptide Response** – The glucagon stimulation test yielded a basal level of 0·39 nmol/L rising modestly to 0·83 nmol/L after six minutes. While this value exceeds the ≥0·60 nmol/L cut‐off that denotes preserved secretion in many guidelines, it remains well below the normal range (>1·5 nmol/L). The pattern reflects *limited* but *present* β‑cell reserve—a typical picture in HNF1A‐MODY where secretory capacity is gradually eroded rather than abruptly destroyed.
* **Anthropometry and Skin Findings** – The BMI is 24·6 kg/m²—borderline overweight—but there is no acanthosis nigricans or other stigmata of marked insulin resistance. In pure T2DM at this BMI one would expect at least some degree of cutaneous hyperpigmentation.
* **Lipid Profile** – Triglycerides are elevated (4·31 mmol/L) while HDL is modestly reduced (1·01 mmol/L). This dyslipidaemia could reflect an emerging metabolic syndrome; however, it may also be secondary to chronic hyperglycaemia rather than primary insulin resistance.
* **Glycaemic Pattern** – Fasting glucose was already high (12·4 mmol/L) but the two‐hour OGTT value skyrocketed to 19·8 mmol/L, indicating profound postprandial excursion despite moderate fasting hyperglycaemia. In HNF1A‐MODY this disproportionate postprandial rise hints at a defect in glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion rather than impaired peripheral glucose uptake.
* **Glycosuria without Severe Hyperglycaemia** – The detection of urinary glucose at relatively modest plasma concentrations aligns with known HNF1A‐related downregulation of SGLT2 in renal proximal tubules—a mechanistic clue unavailable in conventional T1DM/T2DM.

Collectively these data create a pattern most compatible with a monogenic β‑cell defect rather than classic autoimmune or insulin‑resistant diabetes.

### Application of the Exeter MODY Probability Calculator  

To formalise clinical suspicion investigators have devised decision support tools such as the Exeter MODY probability calculator. This web‐based algorithm incorporates age at diagnosis, BMI, family history across three generations, HbA₁c level, treatment requirement at presentation, presence or absence of autoantibodies and oral hypoglycaemic agents used before testing. When entered with our patient’s values (diagnosis at <25 years, BMI 24·6 kg/m², strong autosomal dominant family history encompassing three affected relatives including his brother and mother, HbA₁c 10·2%, no autoantibodies), the calculator returns an estimated likelihood of ~62 % that his diabetes represents MODY—equating roughly to “one chance in two”.

#### Limitations in Non‑European Populations  
It is essential to recognise that this tool was derived from predominately White European cohorts where background prevalence rates for both MODY and conventional diabetes differ from Asian populations such as Taiwanese or Chinese groups. Ethnic variation influences baseline BMI distributions, frequency of metabolic syndrome components and even allele frequencies in MODY genes themselves. Consequently:

* The model may **overestimate** probability when applied to patients from populations with higher background rates of early‐onset T2DM.
* Conversely it might **underestimate** probability when familial aggregation reflects undisclosed monogenic inheritance amidst prevalent polygenic disease.
* The algorithm does not incorporate subtle biochemical clues unique to certain MODY subtypes—for example low renal glucose threshold in HNF1A‐MODY—so clinicians must still use independent reasoning.

Thus while a 62 % score strongly justifies genetic testing in any setting, it should supplement—not replace—clinical judgment anchored on patient‐specific findings.

### Rationale for Maintaining MODY Suspicion Despite Overlapping Features  

Even when individual data points resemble those seen in T2DM—such as mildly elevated BMI or dyslipidaemia—the convergence of several red flags sustains a high index of suspicion for MODY:

| Feature | Typical T2DM | Observed In Patient | Why It Points Toward MODY |
|--------|--------------|---------------------|--------------------------|
| Strong autosomal dominant family history | Variable due to polygenic inheritance | Affected grandmother, mother & brother | Consistent with monogenic transmission |
| Negative pancreatic autoantibodies | Usually positive in T1DM | Both GADA & IA‑2A negative | Excludes autoimmune process |
| Modest residual β‑cell function (stimulated C‑peptide ≈0·8 nmol/L) | Preserved (>1·5 nmol/L) early T2DM; absent (<0·3) early T1DM | Intermediate level | Fits progressive secretory defect seen in HNF1A |
| Absence of acanthosis nigricans | Common with marked insulin resistance | Not present | Reduces likelihood of severe IR |
| Disproportionate postprandial hyperglycaemia | Often blunted by higher basal insulin levels | Marked OGTT spike | Suggests impaired meal‐stimulated secretion |
| Glycosuria out‐of proportion to fasting glucose | Usually only when plasma glucose >13·9 mmol/L | Present at lower glucose values | Classic feature linked to SGLT2 downregulation by HNF1A mutation |

In addition, the clinical course—absence of ketoacidosis despite substantial hyperglycaemia—and the rapid drop in HbA₁c after initiating agents that act by augmenting endogenous insulin release (metformin + GLP‑1 receptor agonist) rather than by providing exogenous insulin further reinforce that residual β cell activity remains exploitable—a hallmark exploited therapeutically in HNF1A-MODY.

Finally—and perhaps most compellingly—the identification of a novel heterozygous pathogenic variant in exon 4 of HNF1A via next-generation sequencing seals the diagnosis while simultaneously confirming that all preceding phenotypic clues were correctly interpreted.

### Integrating Differential Diagnosis Into Clinical Practice  

In practice, clinicians should approach every young adult with unexplained hyperglycaemia through a stepwise algorithm:

1. **Document Detailed Family History** – Ascertain ages at diagnosis across at least three generations; note patterns suggestive of autosomal dominance.
2 **Screen for Autoimmunity** – Obtain GADA, IA‑2A (and preferably ZnT8) early; positivity redirects workup toward classic T1DM irrespective of age.
3 **Quantify Endogenous Insulin Reserve** – Measure fasting C‑peptide; if equivocal perform stimulated testing (glucagon or mixed meal). Interpret both absolute values and trends over time.
4 **Assess Insulin Resistance Markers** – Record BMI accurately; inspect for acanthosis nigricans; obtain lipid panel and blood pressure.
5 **Evaluate Glycaemic Dynamics** – Perform OGTT when feasible; note disproportionate postprandial spikes versus fasting elevations.
6 **Apply Predictive Tools Judiciously** – Use calculators like Exeter’s as adjuncts while remaining aware their performance varies by ethnicity.
7 **Proceed To Genetic Testing When Suspicion Remains High** – Targeted NGS panels covering known MODY genes can identify pathogenic variants even when databases are silent on novel changes—a scenario illustrated by this case’s p.N270S mutation.

By consciously layering these data points rather than relying on any single hallmark, clinicians can reliably differentiate MODY from both type 1 and type 2 diabetes—even when overlapping phenotypes exist—and deliver precision therapies that preserve β-cell function while avoiding unnecessary lifelong insulin exposure.

## Genetic Testing & Molecular Pathogenesis in HNF1A‑MODY  

When a young adult presents with persistent hyperglycaemia, a strong family history of early‑onset diabetes, absence of autoimmune markers and only modest insulin resistance, the clinician should move beyond the conventional type 1/type 2 dichotomy and consider monogenic diabetes. In the index case this “high clinical suspicion” arose from four converging clues: (1) diabetes diagnosed before age 30 in three successive generations, (2) normal‑weight phenotype without acanthosis nigricans, (3) negative GAD‑65 and IA‑2 antibodies, and (4) a modestly preserved C‑peptide response that nevertheless suggested β‑cell dysfunction rather than absolute insulin deficiency. International consensus statements recommend that any patient who meets these criteria be offered molecular diagnostics because a definitive genetic diagnosis changes therapeutic direction and informs cascade testing of relatives.

### Targeted next‑generation sequencing panel for MODY  

In contemporary practice the most efficient way to screen for monogenic diabetes is a targeted next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panel that interrogates all genes known to cause MODY. The panel used for this patient covered more than fifteen loci—including *HNF1A*, *HNF4A*, *GCK*, *HNF1B* and others—thereby providing a comprehensive “one‑stop” assay while keeping sequencing depth high enough (>200× median coverage) to detect heterozygous single‑nucleotide variants with confidence.  

The laboratory workflow begins with peripheral venous blood collected in an EDTA tube. Genomic DNA is extracted using silica‑membrane spin columns or magnetic beads and quantified fluorometrically. Library preparation employs either hybridisation capture or multiplex PCR amplicons that tile the exonic regions plus ±20 bp intronic splice junctions of each MODY gene. After adapter ligation and indexing, libraries are pooled and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq or equivalent platform producing paired‑end reads of 150 bp.  

Raw data are demultiplexed, trimmed for adapters and low‑quality bases, then aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome with a Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA). Variant calling is performed by GATK’s HaplotypeCaller or a comparable algorithm; resultant VCF files are filtered for minimum depth (>20×) and allele balance (>30%). Annotated variants are cross‑referenced against population databases (gnomAD), disease repositories (ClinVar/VarSome), and predictive tools (e.g., AlphaMissense). A secondary confirmation step—typically Sanger sequencing—is reserved for any variant deemed clinically actionable before reporting it to the ordering physician.  

Because therapeutic decisions may hinge on the result, most accredited clinical genetics laboratories aim for a turnaround time between two and three weeks from specimen receipt to final report. In the present case the test was ordered immediately after initial assessment; the provisional pharmacologic regimen (insulin plus metformin/dpp‑4 inhibitor) was therefore based on phenotypic inference while awaiting molecular confirmation.

### Application of ACMG criteria to the *HNF1A* c.809A>G (p.Asn270Ser) variant  

The identified alteration resides in exon 4 of *HNF1A* and is described using Human Genome Variation Society nomenclature as c.809A>G (p.Asn270Ser). It substitutes a polar asparagine with a similarly polar serine at position 270 within the protein’s DNA‑binding domain—a region densely populated by pathogenic missense changes. To translate this finding into a clinically meaningful classification, laboratories apply the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) framework. The following criteria were satisfied:

| ACMG Evidence | Rationale in this case |
|---------------|------------------------|
| **PM2** – absent from controls | The variant is not present in >140 000 individuals catalogued by gnomAD nor listed in ClinVar or VarSome entries, supporting rarity consistent with disease causality. |
| **PP3** – multiple lines of computational evidence support deleterious effect | AlphaMissense—a deep learning model trained on protein‐specific constraints—assigned a pathogenicity probability of 0.995 (>0.9 threshold). Additional tools (PolyPhen‑2, REVEL) similarly predicted damaging impact. |
| **PM1** – located in a mutational hotspot / critical functional domain | Position 270 lies within the highly conserved helix–turn–helix motif that mediates DNA binding; nearby residues N266K and R271W are established pathogenic variants documented across populations. |
| **PP1** – co‑segregation with disease | In the family pedigree three first‑degree relatives who carry the same allele manifested diabetes at ages comparable to the proband; no unaffected carrier was identified among tested relatives—a pattern consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. |
| **PP4** – patient’s phenotype highly specific for disease | The combination of early onset hyperglycaemia without autoimmunity or marked insulin resistance aligns closely with classic *HNF1A*‐MODY presentation. |

Collectively these observations satisfy one strong (*PM2*), one moderate (*PM1*) and three supporting (*PP3*, *PP1*, *PP4*) criteria—meeting ACMG’s threshold for “likely pathogenic.” Because no loss‐of‐function nonsense or frameshift change was observed (which would trigger PVS1), full “pathogenic” status cannot be assigned without functional assay data; however, given the concordance between genotype and phenotype it would be reasonable for clinicians to treat this variant equivalently to established pathogenic mutations when making management decisions.

### Molecular consequences of *HNF1A* loss‑of‑function  

*HNF1α* is a homeodomain-containing transcription factor expressed primarily in pancreatic β‑cells, hepatocytes and renal proximal tubule cells. It orchestrates a network of downstream genes essential for glucose homeostasis:

1. **β‑cell glucose sensing and insulin transcription** – *HNF1α* directly activates the insulin (**INS**) promoter and upregulates GLUT2 (**SLC2A2**) expression on β‐cell membranes. GLUT2 provides rapid equilibration between plasma glucose and intracellular concentrations, allowing β‑cells to detect postprandial rises accurately. Haploinsufficiency caused by heterozygous missense mutations dampens both GLUT2 surface density and INS transcriptional activity, blunting first‐phase insulin release after meals—a hallmark reflected by markedly elevated postload OGTT values (19.8 mmol/L at 2 h) despite relatively preserved fasting C‑peptide.

2. **Hepatic gluconeogenic regulation** – In hepatocytes *HNF1α* drives expression of key enzymes such as phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (**PEPCK**) and glucose‑6‑phosphatase (**G6PC**). Loss-of-function leads to modest reductions in hepatic glucose output; consequently fasting hyperglycaemia is often less pronounced than postprandial spikes in *HNF1A*‐MODY patients.

3. **Renal tubular glucose reabsorption** – SGLT2 (**SLC5A2**) transcription requires *HNF1α*. Diminished SGLT2 expression lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption (~8–10 mmol/L instead of ≥11 mmol/L). This mechanistic link explains why patients with *HNF1A* mutations can develop glycosuria even when plasma glucose is only moderately elevated—a feature observed early in our proband’s evaluation (“glycosuria without ketoacidosis”).

4. **Progressive β‑cell decline** – The mutation does not cause an outright loss of all β‑cell function; instead it produces a chronic state of partial impairment (“haploinsufficiency”). Over years this culminates in quantitative loss of β‐cell mass through apoptosis or de-differentiation pathways that remain incompletely understood but are accelerated by glucotoxicity when hyperglycaemia persists untreated. Clinically this translates into gradually rising HbA₁c levels unless therapy targets residual secretion rather than merely providing exogenous insulin.

Understanding these molecular derangements clarifies why agents that augment endogenous insulin release—such as low-dose sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 receptor agonists— are especially effective in *HNF1A*‐MODY: they bypass defective glucose sensing by amplifying residual secretory capacity while avoiding overt hypoglycaemia associated with higher sulfonylurea doses used for type 2 diabetes patients with intact β‐cells.

### Clinical relevance of confirming the genetic defect  

The definitive identification of a likely pathogenic *HNF1A* missense mutation permitted two decisive changes in management:

- **Discontinuation of unnecessary basal insulin** once glycaemic control was achieved with agents that stimulate remaining β‐cell activity.
- **Adoption of GLP‑1 receptor agonist therapy** as an alternative or adjunct to low-dose sulfonylurea—particularly appealing given its weight-neutral profile and lower hypoglycaemia risk —and later return to metformin plus DPP‑4 inhibition once HbA₁c approached target levels (<6 %).  

Moreover, confirmation allowed cascade testing: first-degree relatives carrying the same allele can be screened preemptively for dysglycaemia even before classical laboratory abnormalities appear, thereby enabling earlier lifestyle or pharmacologic interventions.

In summary, high clinical suspicion triggered timely molecular testing via an NGS MODY panel; rigorous application of ACMG standards classified the novel c.809A>G (*p.Asn270Ser*) alteration as likely pathogenic; finally, recognition that this missense change impairs *HNF1α*’s transcriptional control over insulin synthesis, GLUT2‐mediated glucosensing, hepatic gluconeogenesis and renal SGLT2 clarified both the patient’s phenotype—including glycosuria—and his favourable response to β‐cell–targeted therapy. This integrated diagnostic pathway exemplifies precision medicine where genotype directly shapes therapeutic choice and family counseling strategies in young adults with atypical diabetes presentations.

## Genotype‑Guided Therapeutic Decision‑Making  

The discovery of a pathogenic heterozygous **HNF1A** variant converted an initially nonspecific diabetes management plan into a precision‑medicine strategy that exploits the residual β‑cell reserve inherent in HNF1A‑MODY while avoiding drugs that carry an undue risk of hypoglycaemia or volume depletion in this genotype. The sequence of therapeutic choices illustrates how genotype information reshapes pharmacologic priorities when a monogenic form of diabetes is confirmed.

### Initial empiric regimen prior to genetic results  

When the young man first presented he had an HbA₁c of 10.2 % and fasting glucose above 12 mmol/L, together with symptoms suggestive of decompensated hyperglycaemia (polydipsia, polyphagia and weight loss). In the absence of a definitive genetic diagnosis he was managed under the working assumption that his disease behaved more like type 2 diabetes with possible early‐stage insulin resistance—a reasonable inference given his body‑mass index (~24 kg/m²) and elevated triglycerides.  

The empirical prescription therefore combined three agents whose mechanisms collectively address fasting hyperglycaemia and postprandial excursions while preserving endogenous insulin secretion:

* **Basal insulin degludec (8 U daily)** supplied a constant exogenous insulin supply aimed at promptly lowering fasting plasma glucose without causing peaks that could precipitate hypoglycaemia. Basal analogues such as degludec possess a flat pharmacokinetic profile and allow safe titration even when β‑cell function is uncertain.
* **Metformin (1500 mg/day)** was added because it suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis, improves peripheral insulin sensitivity and carries negligible risk of hypoglycaemia—a cornerstone approach in most adult‐onset diabetes phenotypes.
* **Saxagliptin (5 mg/day)**—a dipeptidyl peptidase‑4 (DPP‑4) inhibitor—was used for its ability to augment endogenous glucagon‑like peptide‑1 (GLP‑1) levels, thereby enhancing glucose‐dependent insulin release without increasing hypoglycaemia risk. In practice this combination provides rapid glycaemic lowering while awaiting definitive diagnostic information.

During the month before molecular testing returned, this triplet reduced HbA₁c from 10.2 % to roughly 9 %, indicating satisfactory initial control yet still leaving considerable hyperglycaemia requiring further therapeutic refinement.

### Transition after genetic confirmation  

The identification of a heterozygous **p.N270S** mutation in exon 4 of **HNF1A** confirmed HNF1A‑MODY—a disorder characterised by progressive β‑cell dysfunction yet usually preserving some secretory capacity for years after diagnosis. Two objective clues guided the decision to discontinue basal insulin:

* The patient’s stimulated C‑peptide rose from an undetectable baseline (<0.40 nmol/L) to **0.83 nmol/L** at six minutes after glucagon injection—exceeding the conventional ≥0·60 nmol/L threshold that denotes clinically meaningful endogenous insulin production.
* His HbA₁c had fallen below **9 %**, suggesting that fasting hyperglycaemia could now be managed without exogenous basal insulin.

Because HNF1A mutations impair transcriptional regulation of genes involved in pancreatic β‐cell glucose sensing (e.g., GLUT2) and renal sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (**SGLT2**) expression—but do not abolish all secretory capacity—a drug class that amplifies **glucose‐dependent** insulin release is preferred over agents that force secretion irrespective of ambient glucose levels. Consequently, basal insulin was tapered off over two weeks while a **once‑weekly dulaglutide** injection (**1·5 mg**) was introduced alongside ongoing metformin therapy.

### Benefits specific to HNF1A‑MODY  

**Glucagon‐like peptide‑1 receptor agonism** aligns precisely with the pathophysiology of HNF1A‐MODY:

* **Glucose‑dependent potentiation** – Dulaglutide acts through cAMP signalling only when plasma glucose rises above ~5 mmol/L, thereby minimizing iatrogenic hypoglycaemia even as β‐cell mass slowly declines.
* **Targeting postprandial spikes** – In HNF ​1α deficiency patients exhibit pronounced postprandial hyperglycaemia because impaired GLUT2–mediated uptake blunts early phase insulin release. The delayed gastric emptying caused by GLP‑​1RAs smooths nutrient absorption curves and dampens these excursions.
* **Weight modulation** – GLP‑​1RAs reduce appetite and caloric intake via central mechanisms; modest weight loss (~3–5 % body weight) counteracts the mild overweight state seen in this patient (BMI 24·6 kg/m²) and improves his dyslipidaemic profile.
* **Cardiovascular protection** – Large outcome trials have demonstrated reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events with GLP‑​1RAs across heterogeneous diabetic populations. Although specific data in MODY are lacking, extending this class benefit aligns with long‑term risk mitigation strategies for a young adult.
* **Preservation of remaining β‐cell reserve** – By enhancing physiologic secretory bursts rather than forcing constant depolarisation (as sulfonylureas do), dulaglutide may retard β‐cell exhaustion—a desirable feature given the progressive nature of HNF ¹α defects.

Collectively these attributes make dulaglutide an elegant choice once sufficient endogenous secretion has been documented genetically and biochemically.

### Alternative first–line option: low–dose sulfonylureas  

Before widespread use of incretin mimetics many clinicians opted for **low‐dose sulfonylureas** (e.g., glimepiride ≤ 2 mg/day) as first‐line therapy in HNF ​1α‐MODY because these drugs close K_ATP channels downstream from the transcriptional defect caused by HNF ​$^\,$ genes, thereby restoring stimulus–secretion coupling rapidly. Clinical series have shown swift reductions in HbA₁c (> 2 %) within weeks under such regimens.^[¹] Nonetheless two considerations temper their universal adoption:

* **Hypoglycaemia propensity** – Sulfonylureas stimulate release independent of ambient glucose levels; as β‐cell capacity wanes over time they become prone to overtreatment leading to severe lows, especially during periods of reduced intake or vigorous exercise.
* **Weight gain** – Sulfonylurea‐induced hyperinsulinaemia frequently promotes adipogenesis; this runs counter to the modest weight reduction goal desired in patients who already sit near the upper limit of normal BMI or who manifest components of the metabolic syndrome (elevated triglycerides).

These drawbacks influenced the treating team’s decision to favour dulaglutide once genotype evidence clarified continued reliance on a drug class whose safety profile better matches evolving β‐cell function.

### Rationale for avoiding SGLT₂ inhibitors  

HNF ​$^\,$ transcription directly regulates renal SGLT₂ expression; loss­of-function mutations diminish transporter density, lowering the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption (“renal glucosuria”). Consequently patients already exhibit consistent glucosuria at relatively normal plasma glucose concentrations. Adding an SGLT₂ inhibitor would exacerbate osmotic diuresis, heightening risks particular to this genotype:

* **Volume depletion** – Enhanced urinary water loss can precipitate orthostatic hypotension or pre­renal azotaemia especially during intercurrent illness.
* **Euglycaemic ketoacidosis** – Reduced carbohydrate reabsorption coupled with relative hypoinsulinaemia can provoke ketogenesis despite modest plasma glucose levels—a serious complication reported more frequently among patients receiving SGLT₂ blockade who possess inherent impairments in glucose handling.
* **Lack of efficacy data** – Randomised trials exploring cardiovascular or renal outcomes have excluded monogenic diabetes cohorts; thus any theoretical benefit remains speculative while safety concerns are concrete.^[²]

Given these mechanistic considerations—and recognizing his baseline glucosuria—the team elected not to introduce an SGLT₂ inhibitor at any stage.

### Continued role of DPP‑4 inhibition  

Saxagliptin remained part of his regimen after dulaglutide initiation because DPP‑4 inhibition provides a complementary augmentation of endogenous incretin tone without adding hypoglycaemic liability. Since dulaglutide supplies pharmacologic GLP‑​1 at supraphysiologic concentrations, saxagliptin’s incremental effect is modest but may be useful during periods when adherence lapses or dose adjustments are required. Moreover its favourable tolerability profile permits easy continuation until downstream simplification—ultimately achieved when metabolic targets were stably maintained using only metformin plus dulaglutide/SAX combination later substituted by metformin alone after six months’ remission stability was confirmed.

### Lifestyle measures integrated throughout treatment  

Pharmacotherapy was consistently paired with nonpharmacologic interventions understood to reinforce β‐cell health and mitigate cardiovascular risk:

* **Medical nutrition therapy** emphasized low glycaemic index carbohydrates, adequate protein distribution and avoidance of excessive simple sugars—all aimed at blunting postprandial surges that challenge compromised pancreatic secretion.
* **Regular aerobic activity** (≥150 min/week moderate intensity) enhances skeletal muscle GLUT4 translocation independently of insulin signalling,
supporting better utilisation previously impaired by GLUT2 deficiency in β cells.
* **Hydration counselling** addressed his propensity toward glucosuria-induced fluid losses—particularly relevant if future therapies affecting renal handling were ever considered.
* Periodic self‐monitoring-of-blood-glucose enabled detection early on whether any medication adjustment precipitated hypo/hyperglycaemic excursions whilst providing behavioural reinforcement during dietary modification phases.

### Summary  

In this case the identification of a novel pathogenic **HNF¹α** variant transformed a provisional type 2 diabetes protocol—basal insulin + metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor—into a genotype-tailored strategy centred on preserving residual β‐cell function through *glucose-dependent* stimulation (dulaglutide), while simultaneously withdrawing agents likely to provoke adverse outcomes specific to HNF¹α pathology (insulin overuse, sulfonylurea‐induced hypoglycaemia, SGLT₂ blockade). Over six months HbA₁c fell dramatically from >10 % down to ≈6 %, microvascular complications remained absent, and medication burden could later be streamlined further—all hallmarks demonstrating how molecular diagnosis directs optimal therapeutic selection beyond conventional phenotypic classifications.*
