Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how loss-of-function mutations within the DNA-binding domain of HNF1A cause both impaired insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells and glycosuria.

**Pathophysiology of HNF‑1α DNA‑binding‑domain loss‑of‑function mutations – why they produce both β‑cell failure and glycosuria**

The hepatocyte nuclear factor‑1α (HNF‑1α) protein is a homodimeric transcription factor that binds a highly conserved “P‑box” motif in the promoters of dozens of genes expressed in liver, pancreas and kidney.  Its three functional regions are  

1. an N‑terminal **DNA‑binding domain (DBD)** (exons 1–4),  
2. a dimerisation domain, and  
3. a C‑terminal **transactivation domain**.  

In most HNF1A‑MODY cases – including the novel p.N270S variant described in Liu et al. – the pathogenic change resides in the DBD (exon 4).  The mutation replaces an asparagine that directly contacts DNA (position 270) with a serine.  This subtle substitution weakens DNA binding without destroying protein stability; the mutant allele therefore behaves as a **loss‑of‑function (haploinsufficient)** allele that cannot fully drive transcription of HNF‑1α target genes.

---

### 1️⃣ Impaired insulin secretion

In pancreatic β‑cells HNF‑1α regulates three critical pathways:

| HNF‑1α target | Role in β‑cell physiology | Consequence of DBD loss |
|--------------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| **INS** (insulin gene) | Direct transcription of pre‑proinsulin | ↓ insulin mRNA → ↓ stores for release |
| **GLUT2** (SLC2A2) | High‑capacity glucose transporter that senses extracellular glucose | ↓ GLUT2 → blunted glucose entry → inadequate rise in ATP/ADP ratio |
| **PKLR** (pyruvate kinase L/R) | Glycolytic enzyme that helps generate ATP after glucose uptake | ↓ PK activity → further reduction in ATP generation |

Because glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion hinges on the rise in intracellular ATP that closes K_ATP channels, any defect that diminishes glucose uptake (GLUT2) or ATP production (PKLR) translates into a **lower secretory response** even when β‑cell mass is preserved.  The glucagon stimulation test in the case report showed a modest rise in C‑peptide from 0.39 to 0.83 nmol/L, confirming that residual β‐cell function is present but suboptimal—a classic laboratory hallmark of HNF1A‐MODY.

---

### 2️⃣ Glycosuria – a renal phenotype

HNF‑1α is also expressed in the **proximal tubule** where it drives transcription of **SLC5A2**, the gene encoding the sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2).  SGLT2 reabsorbs ~90 % of filtered glucose; its activity sets the renal “threshold” (~180 mg/dL) above which glucose appears in urine.

A DBD mutation reduces SGLT2 mRNA and protein levels, lowering the transport capacity:

* **Reduced tubular reabsorption** → urinary glucose spill at plasma concentrations that would be normal for individuals with intact SGLT2.
* Clinically manifests as **glycosuria out of proportion to hyperglycemia**, as was observed in the patient’s routine health exam (urine dipstick positive despite only modestly elevated fasting glucose).

The authors explicitly note this mechanism: “glycosuria might be detected… attributed to a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption resulting from compromised glucose transport in renal tubules.”  Their case exemplifies it—early‐onset hyperglycemia plus unexplained glycosuria tipped them toward MODY testing.

---

### Integrated view

A single heterozygous missense change within the DBD produces *parallel* defects:

* **Pancreas** – blunted insulin gene expression and impaired glucose sensing → progressive β‐cell dysfunction ➜ postprandial hyperglycemia.
* **Kidney** – decreased SGLT2 transcription → low renal glucose threshold ➜ glycosuria at relatively low plasma glucose.

Because both organ systems rely on the same transcriptional regulator, loss of DNA binding yields a combined “diabetes + glycosuria” phenotype that distinguishes HNF1A‐MODY from type 1 or classic type 2 diabetes.

---

### Teaching pearls

| Point | Clinical relevance |
|------|--------------------|
| **DNA‑binding domain mutations → haploinsufficiency** | Dominant inheritance; one mutant allele suffices to lower target gene expression by ~50 % |
| **Low renal threshold = early glycosuria** | A bedside clue: positive urine glucose with only mild hyperglycemia should raise suspicion for HNF1A-MODY |
| **β‑cell response is blunted but not absent** | Stimulated C‑peptide is often >0.6 nmol/L early on—helps differentiate from type 1 diabetes |
| **Therapeutic implications** | Sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 agonists are preferred; SGLT2 inhibitors are relatively contraindicated because baseline SGLT2 activity is already reduced and may precipitate volume depletion or ketoacidosis |
| **Genotype–phenotype link** | The p.N270S variant sits at a highly conserved residue within the DBD—its pathogenicity is corroborated by ACMG criteria, family segregation, and functional predictions (AlphaMissense 0.995). |

In summary, loss-of-function mutations in the DNA-binding domain of HNF1A impair transcription of insulin‐related genes in β‑cells *and* SGLT2 in proximal tubular cells.  The resulting dual defect explains why patients like the young man described by Liu et al. present simultaneously with impaired insulin secretion and glycosuria—a hallmark that should prompt early genetic testing and precision therapy.
