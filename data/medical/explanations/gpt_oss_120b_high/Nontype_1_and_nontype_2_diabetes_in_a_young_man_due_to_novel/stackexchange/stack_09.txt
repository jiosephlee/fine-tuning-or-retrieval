Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Clinical Pearls] Given an autosomal dominant pathogenic HNF1A mutation identified in an index case, outline an evidence-based cascade testing strategy for first-degree relatives and the appropriate follow-up monitoring if they test positive.

**Cascade Testing for a Pathogenic HNF1A Variant – A Practical “Morning‑Report” Pearl**

*Index case*: A young man was found to carry a heterozygous pathogenic HNF1‑A missense change (p.N270S).  His family history already included three affected relatives (maternal grandmother, mother and a younger brother).  The diagnosis enabled a rapid shift from basal insulin to a GLP‑1‑RA–based regimen with durable glycaemic control.  

**What we do next – systematic cascade testing of first‑degree relatives**

| Step | What we do | Why it matters |
|------|------------|----------------|
| **1️⃣ Pre‑test counseling** | A clinical geneticist or trained diabetes counselor meets each adult relative (and parents of minor children) to explain autosomal‑dominant inheritance (50 % transmission risk), variable age‑of‑onset (~70 % develop diabetes by age 25), possible treatment implications and psychosocial issues. | Informed consent is required under ACMG recommendations for cascade screening of dominantly inherited conditions[^1]. |
| **2️⃣ Identify the target population** | All **first‑degree** relatives: mother, father (if alive), siblings, and any children of these individuals. In this family the obvious candidates are (1) the mother – who already has early‑onset diabetes, (2) the father – who may be an obligate non‑carrier or silent carrier, and (3) the younger brother who was diagnosed at 20 y. | First‑degree contacts have the highest prior probability because each carries one allele from the proband’s parent[⁴]. |
| **3️⃣ Targeted genetic assay** | Instead of re‑running a full MODY panel, order a **single‑site PCR/ Sanger sequencing** (or a rapid targeted NGS assay) that interrogates the known c.809A>G (p.N270S) change. For distant relatives who cannot attend clinic in person, saliva kits can be mailed with identical assay design. | Cost‐effective (≈ US$50–100 per test) while preserving analytical sensitivity >99 % for a known variant[⁵]. |
| **4️⃣ Post‑test counseling & documentation** | • **Positive result** → confirm carrier status, discuss penetrance, outline personalized surveillance & treatment plan.<br>• **Negative result** → reassure no increased MODY risk but remind them that other forms of diabetes may still occur.<br>All results entered into the electronic health record with an alert flag for future clinicians. | Provides a clear “genetic flag” that drives downstream management decisions—as shown by the rapid de‑escalation from insulin in our proband once his genotype was known. |
| **5️⃣ Repeat cascade as family expands** | When a carrier has children or when additional siblings are identified, repeat step 3 automatically (“family‐wide” approach). | Early detection in younger generations prevents years of suboptimal therapy[⁶]. |

### Follow‑up Monitoring Once a Relative Tests Positive

Management splits into two pathways – **asymptomatic carriers** vs **carriers who already meet diabetes criteria**.

#### 1️⃣ Asymptomatic Carriers (no overt hyperglycaemia)

| Timing / Test | Rationale |
|---------------|-----------|
| **Baseline** – fasting plasma glucose (FPG), HbA₁c, 75 g OGTT ± C‑peptide<br>Renal panel & lipid profile | Establish current glycaemic status; OGTT most sensitive because HNF1A mutations often manifest as isolated postprandial spikes.[⁷] |
| **Every 12 months** – FPG + HbA₁c <br>If HbA₁c ≥ 5.7 % OR OGTT ≥ 11.1 mmol/L → proceed to treatment algorithm | Progressive β‑cell failure is expected; yearly labs catch conversion before complications develop.[⁸] |
| **Every 2–3 years** – repeat full OGTT </br>(or mixed meal tolerance test if OGTT not feasible) | Detect subtle deterioration in glucose tolerance that may not be reflected by HbA₁c alone owing to relatively low fasting glucose.[⁹] |
| **Annual review** – blood pressure, BMI, waist circumference, lipid panel | MODY patients have lower prevalence of metabolic syndrome than T2D but obesity may emerge over time; early lifestyle intervention reduces future cardiovascular risk.[¹⁰] |
| **Education & lifestyle** – advise low‐glycaemic diet, regular aerobic exercise and avoidance of excessive fructose intake which can unmask hyperglycaemia in HNF1A mutations.[¹¹] | Nonpharmacologic measures delay need for medication.^[12] |

#### 2️⃣ Carriers Who Meet Diabetes Criteria

| Intervention | Details & Evidence |
|--------------|-------------------|
| **First‑line pharmacotherapy** – low‑dose sulfonylurea (e.g., gliclazide 40–80 mg daily) OR GLP‑1 receptor agonist if hypoglycaemia risk high or β–cell reserve limited | Randomised crossover data show sulfonylureas and liraglutide produce comparable glucose lowering in HNF1A‑MODY—but sulfonylureas carry higher hypoglycaemia rates.[¹³] The index patient’s successful switch to dulaglutide illustrates how genotype‐directed therapy can spare insulin use and preserve β‐cell function.[¹⁴] |
| **If HbA₁c >9 % at diagnosis**, consider short course basal insulin until sulfonylurea/GLP‑1 titration stabilises glucose.| Mirrors initial approach taken in our case before genetic confirmation enabled de‑escalation.[¹⁵] |
| **Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors** unless compelling cardiovascular/kidney indication and careful hydration monitoring. | HNF1A loss reduces renal SGLT2 expression → baseline glycosuria; further inhibition risks marked dehydration and euglycaemic ketoacidosis – reported in MODY literature.[¹⁶] |
| **C‑peptide monitoring every 6–12 months** (fasting & stimulated) | Guides whether sulfonylurea dose remains effective or whether β‑cell reserve has waned enough to necessitate alternative agents.^[17] |
| **Standard ADA complication screening**, triggered after *5 years* of confirmed hyperglycaemia or at age ≥10 whichever comes first:<br>• Annual urine albumin/creatinine ratio<br>• Retinal exam every 2–5 years <br>• Foot exam if peripheral neuropathy present  | Although microvascular disease develops later than in type 1/2 diabetes, early onset means cumulative exposure can be significant.[¹⁸] The proband showed no microalbuminuria after six months—reassuring but still requires ongoing surveillance.|

### Putting It All Together – The Take‑Home Message

*Because an autosomal dominant HNF1A mutation confers a roughly 50 % chance per offspring and high penetrance by young adulthood,* systematic cascade testing of all first‑degree relatives is both evidence based and cost effective[^19]. Targeted genotyping eliminates unnecessary broad panels while delivering rapid results that directly inform management—just as it allowed our index patient’s therapy to be refined from insulin to a GLP‑1RA/metformin regimen within weeks.

For carriers without overt diabetes, annual fasting glucose/HbA₁c plus biennial OGTT provide an early warning system before β‑cell exhaustion becomes irreversible. Once diabetes manifests, low‐dose sulfonylureas—or increasingly preferred GLP‑1 receptor agonists—are the cornerstone therapies because they exploit residual β‐cell function without the hypoglycaemia risk associated with higher doses used in type 2 disease. Routine complication screening follows standard ADA timelines but should begin promptly given the often earlier age at onset characteristic of HNF1A‐MODY.

In short:

> **Identify every first-degree relative → offer targeted p.N270S testing → counsel both positive and negative results → monitor carriers annually with fasting glucose/HbA₁c + OGTT every 2–3 years → treat confirmed diabetes with genotype‐directed agents while avoiding SGLT2 inhibitors → institute complication surveillance on schedule.**

This algorithm converts an isolated genetic finding into a family‐wide precision health plan—a model that exemplifies modern “genomics in practice” discussed at morning report today.
