Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment  

The patient was a late‑childhood boy whose weight (44 kg) and height (112 cm) gave a body‑mass index of **35 kg/m²**, placing him in the obese range. Aside from a remote exposure to a grand‑father with pulmonary tuberculosis, his social history was otherwise unremarkable—no consanguinity, no known hereditary disease, and a dietary pattern dominated by processed high‑calorie foods. Although obesity is not directly linked to pancreatopleural fistula, excess adiposity can diminish ventilatory reserve and exaggerate the clinical impact of even modest pleural collections.  

### Presenting complaints  

The chief concerns that brought the child to medical attention were **progressive shortness of breath**, **right‑sided chest pain**, and an escalating sense of respiratory distress over the preceding week. The dyspnoea was described as steadily worsening rather than sudden onset, suggesting a gradually expanding intrathoracic process rather than an acute airway obstruction or pneumothorax. Chest pain was localized to the right hemithorax without radiation, fitting the typical distribution of pleuritic discomfort produced by accumulation of fluid beneath the parietal pleura.  

### Vital signs and initial physical examination  

On arrival his respiratory rate was **20 breaths/min**, just above the normal pediatric range for his age, indicating **mild tachypnoea**. Oxygen saturation on room air lingered at **88–89 %**, which promptly fell into the hypoxaemic range requiring supplemental oxygen. Tachycardia (**HR 110 /min**) accompanied these findings, reflecting both hypoxaemia‑driven sympathetic activation and the stress response associated with acute respiratory compromise.  

Inspection revealed **reduced chest expansion on the right** side compared with the left. Percussion over the right subscapular, infra‑axillary and axillary zones produced a **stony‑dull sound**, a classic sign of fluid replacing aerated lung tissue. Correspondingly, **vocal fremitus**—the palpable transmission of voice sounds—was markedly weaker on the right, consistent with an interposing fluid layer that dampens vibration. Auscultation demonstrated **markedly diminished breath sounds** over the same areas, confirming that most of the right hemithorax was occupied by an opaque medium rather than air‑filled lung parenchyma. No peripheral stigmata of chronic disease (pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing) or abdominal organomegaly were noted, narrowing the initial differential toward isolated thoracic pathology such as large pleural effusion or massive pneumonia with parapneumonic collection.  

### Bedside imaging – confirming a large pleural collection  

A standard postero‑anterior chest radiograph displayed **right costophrenic angle blunting** with an overall opacity obscuring lower lung fields—radiographic hallmarks of a sizeable pleural effusion. Because plain films cannot reliably quantify fluid volume in children or differentiate between simple transudates and haemorrhagic exudates, a bedside **lung ultrasound** was performed promptly. Sonography identified an anechoic-to‑heterogeneous space occupying approximately **one litre** of the right hemithorax; this confirmed both the presence and approximate volume of the collection while also ruling out complex septations that would have suggested empyema at this stage. Point‑of‑care ultrasound has become integral in pediatric emergency assessment because it is radiation‑free, rapidly repeatable, and provides real‑time guidance for thoracentesis.  

### First therapeutic thoracocentesis – immediate impact  

Given his hypoxaemia and discomfort, an urgent **ultrasound‑guided thoracocentesis** was performed under sterile conditions. Approximately **1 L** of dark red fluid was aspirated from the right pleural space via an appropriately sized needle inserted at the posterior axillary line under visual guidance. The dark hue indicated a **haemorrhagic component**, prompting immediate concern for either traumatic bleeding into the pleura or an inflammatory exudate rich in red cells. Following drainage:

* Respiratory rate decreased,
* Oxygen saturation rose markedly to **95–98 %** on ambient air,
* Chest pain subsided,
* The child no longer required supplemental oxygen after 24 hours.

These rapid changes confirmed that the fluid itself—not underlying pulmonary parenchymal disease—was responsible for his respiratory compromise. The aspirated fluid was sent for routine analysis (cell count, protein, glucose), microbiology (Gram stain/culture), biochemical markers (amylase/lipase), cytology, and adenosine deaminase (ADA) given the epidemiologic background of tuberculosis exposure.  

### Empirical supportive therapy  

While awaiting laboratory results, he was admitted to a pediatric intensive care unit where he received **humidified oxygen via mask at 5 L/min** until his saturation stabilized. Empiric broad‑spectrum antibiotics (**piperacillin–tazobactam**) were initiated after obtaining blood cultures to cover potential bacterial pneumonia or parapneumonic empyema—a prudent step because haemorrhagic effusions can be secondary to infected collections despite initially sterile appearances on microscopy.  

### Recurrence – pattern that raised diagnostic alarm  

The child's clinical course took an unexpected turn when he returned **six days later** with an identical presentation: right‑sided chest pain, tachypnoea and fresh large‑volume haemorrhagic effusion requiring repeat thoracocentesis (~1 L). The rapid reaccumulation within less than a week implied either ongoing leakage from an intra‑thoracic source or ineffective resolution of whatever had caused the first collection. A second thoracocentesis produced similar dark red fluid; this time laboratory testing revealed a mildly elevated ADA level in pleural fluid—an index often associated with tuberculous pleuritis—and because his family history included exposure to pulmonary TB he was empirically started on antitubercular therapy despite negative GeneXpert testing for *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*.  

Despite anti‑TB treatment, he experienced yet another episode **five days later**, again necessitating chest tube placement (intercostal drain) with drainage of another litre of dark red fluid before more advanced imaging (contrast‑enhanced CT followed by MRCP) finally disclosed a pancreatic source—the hallmark finding of a pancreatopleural fistula tracking through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity.

The pattern—recurrent massive haemorrhagic effusions occurring at roughly one‑week intervals despite antimicrobial therapy—served as an essential clue that traditional pulmonary causes were insufficient explanations and that an occult intra‑abdominal process needed investigation early in future similar presentations.

--- 

In summary, this child's initial assessment highlighted classic signs of a large unilateral pleural effusion—tachypnoea, hypoxaemia, decreased chest movement, dullness on percussion—and bedside imaging swiftly confirmed its presence and volume. Prompt thoracocentesis both alleviated his distress and generated diagnostic material (dark red fluid). However, rapid recurrence forced clinicians to reconsider their working diagnosis beyond common paediatric etiologies such as infection or tuberculosis—a pivotal learning point for trainees: when a pleural effusion repeatedly reaccumulates despite appropriate therapy directed at presumed causes, clinicians must broaden their differential diagnosis to include uncommon sources such as pancreatic fistulas or other intra‑thoracic communications.*
