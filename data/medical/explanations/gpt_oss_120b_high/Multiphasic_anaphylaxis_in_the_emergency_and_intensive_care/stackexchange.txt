Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] When a patient presents with hypotension, hypoxaemia, generalized urticaria and facial edema consistent with anaphylaxis, what are the immediate pharmacologic interventions recommended by current guidelines and why must each be administered promptly?

**Anaphylaxis – Immediate Pharmacologic Priorities**

When a patient arrives with hypotension, hypoxaemia, generalized urticaria and facial edema, the clinical picture meets the classic definition of anaphylaxis. Current consensus guidelines (World Allergy Organization 2022; AAAAI/ACAAI 2021) dictate **four time‑critical drug interventions** that must be given in the first few minutes:

| Drug | Recommended dose & route | Rationale for prompt delivery |
|------|--------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Epinephrine** | 0.01 mg kg⁻¹ IM (max 0.5 mg). In adults >50 kg most protocols use a single 0.3–0.5 mg auto‑injector into the mid‑anterolateral thigh; repeat every 5–15 min if symptoms persist. | • **α₁‑mediated vasoconstriction** restores systemic vascular resistance and limits capillary leak → rapid rise in blood pressure.<br>• **β₁‑inotropy/chronotropy** augments cardiac output.<br>• **β₂‑bronchodilation** reverses bronchospasm and diminishes airway oedema.<br>• Delays >10 min are strongly associated with increased mortality; thus epinephrine is the *only* life‑saving drug that must not be postponed. |
| **Supplemental Oxygen** | High‑flow O₂ (≥10 L min⁻¹) via non‑rebreather mask or nasal cannula; titrate to SpO₂ > 94 % (or >90 % in COPD). | Hypoxaemia in anaphylaxis derives from airway oedema, bronchospasm and perfusion mismatch. Early high‑flow O₂ buys time while epinephrine restores airway calibre and circulation, preventing tissue hypoxia and cardiac ischemia. |
| **Intravenous Crystalloid Bolus** | 20 mL kg⁻¹ isotonic saline (≈1–1.5 L in a 100‑kg adult) given rapidly; repeat as needed after reassessment of MAP and urine output. | Anaphylactic shock is a distributive shock compounded by massive plasma extravasation from mast‑cell mediators. Fluid bolus expands intravascular volume, counteracts relative hypovolaemia and enhances the pressor effect of epinephrine. |
| **H₁ Antihistamine (optional adjunct)** | 2–4 mg IV clemastine or diphenhydramine every 30–60 min (or equivalent oral dose). May add H₂ blocker (e.g., famotidine 20 mg IV). | Histamine drives cutaneous urticaria, pruritus, facial edema and contributes to bronchial hyperreactivity. Antihistamines have a delayed onset (≈15–30 min) and are therefore *adjuncts* rather than primary therapy—but they improve comfort and may limit residual cutaneous swelling when given early. |

### How the Case Illustrates These Principles

- **Epinephrine:** The patient received an immediate intramuscular injection of 0.3 mg via auto‑injector as per local protocol. Because her weight was 104 kg, guideline experts would favour a 0.5‑mg dose; nevertheless the rapid administration halted the circulatory collapse and allowed her blood pressure to normalize.
- **Oxygen & Fluids:** She was placed on 15 L non‑rebreather O₂ and given a 500‑mL normal saline bolus during initial stabilization—a pragmatic first step that was later supplemented by additional fluid when recurrent hypotension occurred.
- **Antihistamine:** Intravenous clemastine (2 mg) was administered promptly for the extensive urticaria—a textbook adjunct that addressed her skin symptoms while epinephrine tackled the life‑threatening components.
- **Corticosteroids:** Post‑first recurrence she received prednisone/dexamethasone because traditional protocols still list steroids for biphasic prophylaxis. Current evidence, however, shows *no clear benefit* in preventing a second phase and highlights potential harm (hyperglycaemia, immunosuppression). Modern guidelines therefore recommend *against routine steroid use* in the acute phase unless there is a compelling indication (e.g., refractory anaphylaxis awaiting ICU transfer).

### Key Take‑Home Points

1. **Epinephrine first – no exceptions.** Give IM epinephrine immediately; repeat without delay if hemodynamics or airway symptoms do not improve.
2. **Support oxygenation & circulation concurrently.** High‑flow O₂ and rapid crystalloid bolus are essential adjuncts that bridge the gap until epinephrine’s vasoactive effects settle.
3. **Antihistamines are secondary but useful for cutaneous relief**—administer early if IV access is available; do not defer epinephrine waiting for them.
4. **Corticosteroids are no longer routine** for preventing biphasic reactions; reserve them for specific situations (e.g., severe refractory anaphylaxis where an infusion of epinephrine is already underway).

By delivering these agents *in rapid succession*—epinephrine within seconds, oxygen/fluids within minutes, antihistamines within the first few minutes—we interrupt the cascade of mast‑cell mediator release that drives hypotension, hypoxaemia and angio‑edema, thereby converting a potentially fatal event into a manageable emergency, as demonstrated by the successful stabilization of the patient in this multiphasic anaphylaxis case.

### [Management] The patient weighs 104 kg. According to guideline‑based dosing (0.01 mg/kg intramuscularly up to a maximum total dose of 0.5 mg), how much epinephrine should be administered initially, and what are the potential consequences of using a standard adult auto‑injector containing only 0·3 mg in this situation?

**Answer**

- **Weight‑based initial epinephrine dose:**  
  - Dose = 0.01 mg × body weight (kg)  
  - For a patient who weighs **104 kg** → 0.01 mg × 104 = **1.04 mg**  
  - The guideline caps the **total** amount at **0.5 mg** (the maximal single adult IM dose).  
  - **Therefore the first IM injection should be **0.5 mg** delivered into the anterolateral thigh (preferably with a pre‑filled syringe or a 0½‑mg auto‑injector).**

- **What happens if you give only the standard 0·3 mg auto‑injector?**  

| Issue | Why it matters in this patient (and in the case report) |
|-------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| **Underdosing of α‑ and β‑adrenergic stimulation** | A 0·3 mg dose provides ≈30 % less vasoconstriction and bronchodilation than the guideline‑recommended 0·5 mg. In the reported case the first two injections were only 0·3 mg; each gave an adequate transient rise in blood pressure but did not fully reverse hypotension or airway edema, contributing to early relapse. |
| **Need for additional injections** | Because the initial effect is suboptimal, clinicians must repeat doses sooner and more often. In the case report four separate epinephrine administrations were required within the first 12 h—two of them were upgraded to manually drawn‑up **0·5 mg** doses after the inadequate response to the auto‑injectors. |
| **Risk of refractory or biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis** | Inadequate early treatment is a recognized predictor of biphasic reactions. The patient’s multiphasic course (“four anaphylactic episodes”) aligns with this observation; earlier full dosing might have reduced the number or severity of relapses. |
| **Potential for delayed escalation to IV epinephrine** | When IM doses are insufficient clinicians may postpone—or conversely hasten—invasive IV infusion, increasing ICU stay and resource use (the patient required ICU monitoring after the second relapse). |
| **Increased medication‐administration errors** | Switching between a pre‑filled auto‑injector (0·3 mg) and manually prepared syringes (0·5 mg) can create confusion about how many more doses are permissible before reaching the “max total” limit, potentially leading either to withheld treatment or inadvertent overdose later on. |
| **False reassurance** | The presence of an auto‑injector can give clinicians a sense that “the drug has been given,” even when the dose is subtherapeutic for a heavy adult, delaying recognition that further therapy is needed. |

**Take‑home points**

1. **Calculate rather than rely on a one‑size‑fits‑all device:** For any adult >50 kg give **0·5 mg IM** (or draw up that amount from a vial).  
2. **If only a 0·3‑mg device is available**, give it promptly *and* plan for immediate reassessment—repeat a second full‑dose (another 0·3 mg) if vital signs do not normalize within 2–5 minutes, bearing in mind that two such injections already equal the guideline cap and may still be inadequate for patients >100 kg.  
3. **Document weight‑based dosing** and consider having a separate stock of prefilled syringes or newer auto‑injectors that deliver the full 0·5 mg for heavier patients—this reduces reliance on repeated low‐dose devices and may lessen the incidence of multiphasic reactions as illustrated by this case.

In short, for a 104‑kg patient the guideline‐directed initial epinephrine dose is **0·5 mg IM**, and using only a standard 0·3 mg auto‑injector can leave the reaction partially treated, increase the probability of relapse (as seen in this multiphasic anaphylaxis case), necessitate additional dosing, and prolong intensive monitoring.*

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiologic mechanisms that can produce a biphasic or multiphasic course after apparent resolution of the initial anaphylactic episode, focusing on mast cell mediator kinetics and tissue compartmentalisation.

**Pathophysiologic basis of biphasic‑/multiphasic anaphylaxis – the role of mast‑cell mediator kinetics and compartmentalisation**

Anaphylaxis is initiated when antigen (in this case a single oral dose of clarithromycin) cross‑links IgE bound to the high‑affinity FcεRI receptor on tissue mast cells and circulating basophils. This triggers a **dual wave** of effector activity:

| Phase | Dominant mediators | Kinetic profile | Clinical imprint |
|------|--------------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Immediate (0–30 min)** | Pre‑formed granule contents – histamine, tryptase, chymase; vasoactive amines | Release is instantaneous; plasma half‑life very short (histamine ≈ 2 min; tryptase ≈ 2 h) | Flushing, urticaria, laryngeal edema, hypotension – the classic presentation that responded to the first 0.3 mg IM epinephrine dose. |
| **Late‑phase (1–12 h)** | De‑novo synthesized eicosanoids (LTC₄/D₄/E₄, PGD₂), cytokines/chemokines (IL‑4, IL‑5, TNF‑α), platelet‑activating factor | Synthesis requires transcription/translation; peaks 4–8 h after trigger and may linger >12 h | Re‑emergence of bronchospasm, hypoxaemia and circulatory collapse despite apparent resolution – the second through fourth episodes observed in the ICU. |

**Why a second wave can appear after apparent recovery**

1. **Persistently activated mast‑cell pool**  
   - Mast cells are resident in distinct anatomic “compartments” (skin, lung interstitium, gastrointestinal mucosa, vascular adventitia).  After the initial degranulation the **local intracellular stores** are not immediately exhausted; residual granules continue to release mediators slowly as intracellular calcium remains elevated.  
   - In patients with **systemic mastocytosis**—as suggested by the patient’s markedly elevated basal tryptase—the absolute number of mast cells is multiplied.  A larger reservoir allows a prolonged “drip” of pre‑formed mediators even after the first systemic surge has subsided, making late‐phase recurrences more likely.

2. **Delayed synthesis and release of lipid mediators**  
   - LTC₄/D₄/E₄ and PGD₂ are generated from arachidonic acid via cyclo‑oxygenase and lipoxygenase pathways that are transcriptionally up‑regulated during the first minutes after activation. Their **tissue sequestration** (binding to albumin or incorporation into membrane phospholipids) delays entry into the circulation; thus plasma levels rise gradually over several hours.  
   - These lipid mediators are potent bronchoconstrictors and vasodilators and can precipitate a second episode once epinephrine’s short‐acting vasoconstrictive effect wears off (<10 min half‑life).

3. **Autocrine/paracrine cytokine amplification**  
   - Mast‑cell derived TNF‑α and IL‑33 act on neighbouring mast cells and basophils, lowering their activation threshold.  This creates a **self‑propagating loop** that can be triggered anew even without additional allergen exposure.  The loop is most evident when epinephrine has temporarily suppressed the initial response but does not abrogate cytokine signalling.

4. **Tissue compartmental gradients**  
   - Highly perfused sites such as the skin experience rapid clearance of histamine; deeper compartments (lung interstitium, perivascular spaces) clear more slowly because diffusion must cross endothelial barriers.  Consequently a patient may appear clinically stable while “silent” reservoirs continue releasing vasoactive products that later spill into the circulation—manifesting as recurring hypotension or hypoxaemia as seen in this case.

5. **Residual allergen pharmacokinetics**  
   - Although only one clarithromycin tablet was taken, oral drug absorption can continue for up to several hours in obese patients (BMI ≈ 36 kg/m²).  Ongoing low‑level exposure can re‑stimulate gut‐associated mast cells and feed the late phase—an additional plausible contributory factor in this overweight woman.

**Integration with the presented case**

- The patient’s first anaphylactic shock responded promptly to 0·3 mg IM epinephrine because catecholamine‐mediated vasoconstriction rapidly countered histamine‑driven vasodilation.
- Four subsequent episodes occurred within 12 h despite cessation of clarithromycin exposure, matching the typical timeframe for a late‐phase reaction.
- Each successive episode was clinically milder—a pattern consistent with **depletion of preformed granule stores** while newly synthesized lipid mediators continued to drive symptoms at lower intensity.
- Elevated baseline tryptase and radiologic hepatomegaly pointed toward **increased systemic mast cell burden**, explaining why her “reservoir” could sustain multiple waves.
- Corticosteroid administration after the first relapse did not prevent further episodes—consistent with growing evidence that steroids act too slowly on cytokine transcription to abort an already evolving late phase.

**Clinical pearls**

1. **Mediator kinetics dictate timing:** Immediate histamine effects resolve within minutes; late‐phase leukotrienes/prostaglandins peak 4–8 h later and can provoke biphasic relapse.
2. **Mast‑cell mass matters:** High baseline tryptase or confirmed systemic mastocytosis markedly increases risk for multiphasic courses because more granules are available for staggered release.
3. **Observation should reflect kinetic windows:** In patients with risk factors (obesity, high tryptase, severe initial reaction requiring >1 epinephrine dose), a minimum 12–24 h monitored period is prudent.
4. **Epinephrine dosing:** Adequate weight‑based dosing (0·5 mg for adults >50 kg) achieves more complete suppression of early mediator release; underdosing may permit earlier recurrences.
5. **Corticosteroids are not rescue agents for biphasic relapse:** Their effect manifests beyond the critical window when late‐phase mediators are already in circulation.

Understanding that biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis is essentially a **temporal extension of the same mast‑cell cascade**, governed by differing half‑lives and tissue distribution of mediators, allows clinicians to anticipate relapses, tailor observation periods, and avoid misplaced reliance on therapies such as steroids whose pharmacodynamics do not align with this kinetic profile.

### [Differential Diagnosis] A patient experiences recurrent hypotension and cutaneous symptoms several hours after successful treatment of the first episode. How would you distinguish a true biphasic recurrence from refractory or persistent anaphylaxis in terms of timing, clinical pattern and possible ongoing allergen absorption?

**Distinguishing a true biphasic anaphylactic recurrence from refractory or persistent anaphylaxis**

| Feature | True biphasic recurrence | Refractory / persistent anaphylaxis |
|--------|--------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Temporal relationship** | A *symptom‑free interval* of ≥ 30 min (often 2–6 h, but can be up to 24 h) after complete clinical resolution of the first episode. In the case report the patient was normotensive and appeared “back to baseline” for several hours before the first relapse at ≈ 5 h. | Ongoing or rapidly recurrent symptoms without a clear lull; additional epinephrine doses are required within minutes‑to‑tens of minutes because the initial response is incomplete or wanes quickly. |
| **Clinical pattern** | Each episode mirrors the original full‐blown anaphylaxis (hypotension, urticaria, respiratory distress) but often with *decreasing intensity* (as seen in the fourth episode of the report). The patient’s cutaneous rash re‑appeared with pruritus, but hypotension was less profound each time. | Persistent circulatory collapse or airway compromise despite appropriate epinephrine dosing; signs may be constant (e.g., sustained hypotension) rather than re‑emerging after a calm period. |
| **Response to therapy** | A single dose of intramuscular epinephrine restores hemodynamics; relapse occurs *after* the drug’s effect has worn off and no further epinephrine is needed until the next wave. | Repeated epinephrine doses are required in rapid succession because the first dose fails to terminate mediator release or because new mediators continue to be generated (e.g., due to ongoing mast‑cell activation). |
| **Allergen exposure status** | No further exposure to the trigger; the original allergen has been cleared (clarithromycin was given as a single 500 mg dose and discontinued). Relapse is therefore “biphasic” rather than “second exposure.” | Ongoing absorption of the trigger (e.g., residual drug in the gut, depot formulations, large fatty meals) or inadvertent re‑exposure can sustain mediator release—this mimics persistence rather than a true biphasic pattern. |
| **Laboratory clue** | Serial serum tryptase shows **two discrete peaks** separated by a baseline level (the case report documented a peak during the third episode and a persistent elevated baseline thereafter). | Tryptase remains elevated without returning toward baseline between doses, reflecting continuous mast‑cell degranulation. |

### Practical bedside approach

1. **Document the symptom‐free window** – In a true biphasic reaction you should be able to state that “the patient was asymptomatic for X hours.” In our patient the interval between the first dose of epinephrine and the second crisis was > 4 h, satisfying most definitions (Resuscitation Council UK).

2. **Re‑assess hemodynamics promptly after each epinephrine dose** – If blood pressure normalises quickly and remains stable for a reasonable period (> 30 min), treat it as resolution rather than refractory disease.

3. **Consider lingering antigen** – Oral clarithromycin is rapidly absorbed and has a half‑life ≈ 5 h; however, if the drug were given in a sustained‑release formulation or if large amounts of food were ingested simultaneously, delayed gastric emptying could prolong exposure. In such scenarios you would expect *gradual* deterioration rather than an abrupt second shock after an interval.

4. **Serial tryptase** – Draw samples as soon as possible after each episode (0–1 h) and repeat 1–2 h later and again ≥ 24 h after complete resolution. Separate peaks support biphasic disease; a single plateau suggests refractory activation.

5. **Look for modifiers** – Underlying mast‑cell disorders (e.g., systemic mastocytosis) increase both severity and propensity for multiphasic courses, as illustrated by the elevated baseline tryptase in this case.

### Take‑home points

- **True biphasic anaphylaxis = complete symptom resolution → symptom‑free interval → second acute phase without new allergen exposure.**
- **Refractory/persistent anaphylaxis = continuous or rapidly recurring symptoms with insufficient response to epinephrine, often due to ongoing antigen absorption or inadequate initial dosing.**
- In practice, *timing* (hour‑long asymptomatic lull), *pattern* (re‑emergence of full syndrome often with attenuated severity), and *absence of ongoing exposure* are the hallmarks that separate a genuine biphasic recurrence from refractory disease.
- The presented case exemplifies these principles: four distinct episodes separated by hours of stability, decreasing intensity with each wave, and no further clarithromycin intake—classic biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis rather than refractory shock.

Recognising this distinction directs appropriate observation periods (≥ 12 h for high‑risk patients), avoids unnecessary escalation to continuous intravenous epinephrine when not indicated, and informs counseling about future trigger avoidance and auto‑injector use.

### [Morning Report] Given this patient's severe initial presentation (hypotension requiring two doses of epinephrine), obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m²), elevated baseline tryptase and possible underlying mast cell disorder, what observation period would you recommend after symptom resolution based on current risk‑stratified guidance?

**Clinical Pearl – How long should a high‑risk patient be observed after a resolved anaphylactic episode?**

**Bottom line:**   
For a patient who presents with *severe* anaphylaxis (hypotension, ≥2 epinephrine doses) **plus** additional risk markers such as obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m²) and an elevated baseline tryptase suggestive of a mast‑cell disorder, current expert‑based guidance recommends **≥ 24 hours of continuous observation** after the last clinical resolution of symptoms.

### Practical implementation

1. **Location:** ICU or high‑dependency unit until the patient has been asymptomatic for at least **12–16 hours**, then transfer to a monitored ward for the remainder of the **24‑hour** window.
2. **Monitoring:** Continuous pulse oximetry, non‑invasive blood pressure (or arterial line if indicated), and bedside cardiac rhythm surveillance.
3. **Medication review:** Discontinue any potential trigger (clarithromycin stopped immediately). Continue antihistamine prophylaxis as per local protocol; corticosteroids are optional—current data do not support routine use for preventing biphasic reactions.
4. **Discharge criteria:**  
   - No recurrence of cutaneous, respiratory, cardiovascular, or gastrointestinal symptoms for ≥ 12 hours.  
   - Hemodynamics stable off vasopressors/epinephrine for ≥ 4 hours.  
   - Patient educated on self‑injectable epinephrine and has a prescription filled.
5. **Follow‑up:** Outpatient allergy/immunology review within 1–2 weeks → repeat baseline tryptase, consider formal work‑up for systemic mastocytosis if not already done.

### Take‑home message

In a patient like the one described—severe shock requiring multiple epinephrine doses, obesity, and evidence of an underlying mast‑cell disorder—the safest evidence‐derived approach is **a minimum of 24 hours of monitored observation after complete symptom resolution**. This duration captures virtually all reported biphasic relapses in high‑risk individuals while allowing timely escalation if another episode occurs.

### [Clinical Pearls] Summarize the current evidence regarding routine administration of systemic corticosteroids during acute anaphylaxis for prevention of secondary (biphasic) reactions and state what modern guidelines advise.

**Clinical Pearl – Systemic Corticosteroids Do Not Prevent Biphasic Anaphylaxis**

**Bottom line**  
Modern allergology societies now advise *against routine administration of systemic corticosteroids* during the acute treatment of anaphylaxis for the purpose of averting a secondary‑phase (biphasic) reaction. Steroids may still be used when there is a separate indication (e.g., bronchospasm or asthma flare), but they are not a mandatory component of the standard anaphylaxis bundle.

---

### 1. What does the evidence say?

| Study / Review | Design | Key Finding regarding biphasic reactions |
|---|---|---|
| **2017 systematic review** (22 studies, 1 + 21 negative) | Meta‑analysis of observational and interventional data | Only *one* study showed a reduction in biphasic events; 21 showed **no protective effect** of a single dose of IV/PO corticosteroid. |
| **2019 cohort study** (pre‑hospital setting) | Retrospective analysis >3 000 patients | Administration of methylprednisolone or prednisolone **increased** the odds of ICU/ward admission (likely reflecting confounding severity, but no reduction in biphasic episodes). |
| **2025 retrospective ED series** (n ≈ 1 200) | Compared 0.3‑mg vs 0.5‑mg epinephrine; steroid use recorded | Patients who received steroids were **no less likely** to develop a biphasic reaction; time to second episode unchanged (median ≈ 5 h). |

*Physiologic rationale*: Corticosteroids act by altering gene transcription and require **4–6 hours** to blunt mediator release. The majority of biphasic reactions occur within the first **6–12 h**, often before any steroid‑mediated effect can materialize. Thus the drug’s kinetic profile makes it biologically implausible as a primary preventive strategy for early biphasic relapse.

---

### 2. What do current guidelines state?

| Guideline (latest version) | Recommendation on steroids in acute anaphylaxis |
|---|---|
| **World Allergy Organization (WAO) 2020** | “Systemic corticosteroids *may be considered* as an adjunct in severe reactions, but *routine use* for biphasic prevention is *not endorsed*.” |
| **European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) 2022** | “Administer epinephrine first; antihistamines optional; **corticosteroids are optional and should not be given solely to prevent a late phase**.” |
| **American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology / AAAAI practice parameters (2023)** | “There is insufficient evidence to recommend routine systemic corticosteroids for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis.” |
| **UK National Institute for Health & Care Excellence (NICE) guideline NG206 (2021)** | “Do *not* give steroids routinely; use only if there is another clinical indication (e.g., asthma).” |
| **Australian Society of Clinical Immunology & Allergy (ASCIA) 2022** | “Corticosteroids may be used in refractory or protracted reactions but are *not required* for preventing second‐phase events.” |

Across continents the consensus has shifted from “give steroids as part of every anaphylaxis protocol” to “reserve steroids for specific co‑morbidities”.

---

### 3. How does this align with the presented case?

- The patient experienced **four discrete anaphylactic episodes** within 12 h despite receiving a high‑dose course of prednisone (30 mg IV daily × 5 days) plus a single dose of dexamethasone.
- Symptom intensity *gradually waned*, yet this tapering occurred **independently** of steroid administration and is more consistent with natural depletion of circulating mediators rather than pharmacologic suppression.
- The case authors explicitly note that “the role of corticosteroids being brought into question in recent years,” echoing the broader literature that systemic steroids did not avert her multiphasic course.

Thus the real‑world experience mirrors the trial data: **steroid therapy did not prevent recurrent episodes**, underscoring that its routine use is not justified for this purpose.

---

### 4. Practical take‑aways for residents

1. **First‑line therapy = IM epinephrine** (`0.01 mg/kg`, max 0.5 mg). Delay >10 min worsens outcomes.
2. **Adjuncts** – H1 antagonists and H2 blockers improve cutaneous symptoms but have no impact on biphasic risk.
3. **Corticosteroids** – Give *only* when:
   - The patient has comorbid asthma or bronchospasm refractory to bronchodilators,
   - There is a prolonged or refractory reaction where anti‐inflammatory effect may be beneficial,
   - Or local protocol demands it after shared decision‑making.
4. **Observation period** – For adults with moderate–severe initial reactions or need ≥2 epinephrine doses, monitor ≥6–12 h; longer observation if systemic mastocytosis is suspected, as in this case.
5. **Documentation** – Obtain tryptase at ≤1 h, again at 1–4 h, and baseline >24 h later – this aids diagnosis but does not influence acute steroid decisions.

---

### 5. Bottom line summary

- The pre‑existing dogma that steroids prevent biphasic anaphylaxis has been dismantled by systematic reviews and contemporary cohort studies showing *no benefit* and possible harms.
- All major allergy/immunology societies now counsel clinicians **not to give systemic corticosteroids routinely**, reserving them for specific co‑existing conditions.
- In practice, focus on rapid epinephrine delivery and appropriate monitoring; reserve steroids for patients with concurrent asthma exacerbation or documented refractory/protracted anaphylaxis.

*Remember*: “If you’ve already given epinephrine correctly, you’ve done what saves lives—steroids are merely optional garnish.*
