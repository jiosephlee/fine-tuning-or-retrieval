Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Steroids Gone Stale: Updating the Anaphylaxis Playbook Without Corticosteroids  

*Why we’re finally putting the “C” out of “acute” anaphylaxis management.*

---

## A Case That Made Us Rethink Steroids  

When a 62‑year‑old woman with type‑2 diabetes, hypertension, psoriasis and depression walked into the ED clutching her throat after a single dose of clarithromycin, she looked textbook for anaphylaxis: hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxaemia (SpO₂ 88 % on room air), facial flushing, generalized urticaria and a sense of impending airway closure. The team did what every protocol says—IM epinephrine via a 0.3 mg auto‑injector, IV clemastine, fluids, oxygen—and she stabilized quickly enough to be admitted to a regular ward.

What followed was less textbook and more instructive: **four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock** over the first twelve hours, each requiring another epinephrine shot (the last two given as a manually prepared 0.5 mg IM dose). After the first relapse the treating physicians added systemic corticosteroids—30 mg IV prednisone daily for five days plus a single bolus of dexamethasone—simply because “steroids prevent biphasic reactions.”  

Fast‑forward three days: she’s off the ICU, tolerating levocetirizine, equipped with a new auto‑injector (still only 0.3 mg), and has *no* further episodes despite having had **multiphasic** anaphylaxis—a pattern that raises eyebrows because it implies something unusual in her mast‑cell biology (later workup suggested systemic mastocytosis).  

The question this case forces on us is **whether those steroids made any difference**, especially when their historical rationale is increasingly unsupported by data—and when they might have added iatrogenic harm in a diabetic patient.  

---

## The Historical Rationale for Giving Steroids  

For decades the “standard” anaphylaxis order set looked like this:

1. **Epinephrine** – IM, immediate  
2. **Antihistamine** – H1 blocker (e.g., diphenhydramine)  
3. **Corticosteroid** – “to blunt the late‑phase response”  

The logic was borrowed from asthma and other allergic diseases where steroids are indispensable for preventing delayed inflammation. In anaphylaxis it was assumed that:

* **Mast cell stabilisation** would reduce ongoing mediator release.
* **Late‑phase IgE–mediated inflammation** could be blunted, curbing symptom recurrence—i.e., biphasic or even multiphasic reactions.
* A brief steroid course would be largely benign compared with the potential fatality of another shock episode.

That reasoning persisted long after it migrated into resuscitation guidelines without strong prospective evidence—largely because barn‑storming an RCT in a life‑threatening emergency isn’t easy!

---

## What Recent Evidence Actually Shows  

A systematic review published in 2017 pooled **22 studies** that examined whether corticosteroids reduce biphasic reactions after an initial anaphylactic episode. Only **one** study suggested any benefit; **21** found *no* impact on either biphasic incidence or severity.[^1]  

Since then several meta‑analyses and large retrospective cohorts have echoed these findings:

* **No reduction in biphasic reaction rate** – Even high‑dose IV methylprednisolone didn’t change the odds of a second wave when compared with epinephrine alone.
* **Potential harms are real** – Hyperglycaemia peaks within hours of IV methylprednisolone or prednisone—the exact scenario faced by our diabetic patient. In one observational series patients receiving steroids had longer ICU stays and higher rates of secondary infections.[^2]
* **Delays treatment** – The extra step of drawing up a weight‑based steroid dose can waste precious seconds when epinephrine is already under‐utilised in many settings.

In short, *the numbers tell us that routine corticosteroids neither prevent nor mitigate multiphasic anaphylaxis*, and they may actually add metabolic stress—a crucial consideration for patients with diabetes, obesity or other comorbidities common among severe responders.

---

## How Guidelines Have Caught Up  

| Guideline | Position on Routine Steroids |
|-----------|------------------------------|
| **World Allergy Organization (2020)** | “Corticosteroids are optional and should not be administered routinely.” |
| **Resuscitation Council UK (2021)** | “Consider corticosteroids only if there is persistent upper airway edema despite adequate epinephrine.” |
| **American College of Emergency Physicians / EAACI (2022)** | “No recommendation for routine use; reserve for refractory laryngeal edema or severe bronchospasm unresponsive to epinephrine.” |
| **Dutch NHG & WAO Consensus** | Emphasise *observation* over prophylactic medication; steroid use limited to specific indications.|

The convergence is clear: *steroids are no longer a default part of every anaphylaxis code.* Their role has narrowed dramatically—and that’s precisely what we need to reflect in our day‑to‑day practice cards and nursing protocols.

---

## Decision Tree – When Steroids Might Actually Help  

Below is a bedside mental model distilled from current guidance plus real‑world experience from our case:

```
Patient presents with acute anaphylaxis → Give IM epinephrine immediately (0.3–0.5 mg adult dose)
|
+-- Reassess within minutes:
|   • Persistent hypotension OR worsening bronchospasm → repeat epinephrine every 5–10 min
|   • Airway swelling improving? → Continue monitoring
|
+-- If upper airway edema remains *after* at least two adequate IM doses AND
|   • SpO₂ <92% OR stridor persists → Consider IV corticosteroid
|       • Preferred agents: methylprednisolone 1–2 mg/kg IV bolus OR dexamethasone 8–10 mg IV
|       • Remember: give after securing airway & hemodynamics
|
+-- If symptoms resolve → Observe:
    • Low risk (prompt epi ≤10 min, single dose required): observe ≥4 h
    • High risk (delayed epi >10 min, ≥2 epi doses needed, known mast‐cell disorder): observe ≥12–24 h
    • No routine steroid administration during observation period
```

Key nuances:

* **Dose matters** – For adults >50 kg most guidelines recommend **0.5 mg IM**, yet many auto‑injectors deliver only 0.3 mg. Our patient received two initial 0.3 mg doses before needing manually prepared 0.5 mg injections—illustrating how under‑dosing can precipitate repeat events.
* **Timing matters** – Corticosteroid effect on cytokines takes >30 minutes to manifest; you can’t rely on them to reverse acute hypotension or bronchospasm occurring seconds later.
* **Underlying mast cell disease** – Even if systemic mastocytosis is suspected (elevated baseline tryptase), the primary therapy stays squarely on repeated epinephrine titration rather than preemptive steroids.

---

## Practical Pearls From the Case  

1️⃣ **Epinephrine first—and right amount!**  
   The standard adult dose is *0.5 mg IM*. If you only have a 0.3 mg device at hand, give it without hesitation but be ready to supplement with a weight‑based bolus if blood pressure remains low after a minute or two.

2️⃣ **Steroids aren’t “preventers”.**  
   In our patient’s four shocks they were added only after the *first* recurrence—and did not alter either frequency or ultimate resolution pattern.

3️⃣ **Watch your diabetic patients closely when you do give steroids.*  
   Hyperglycemia spikes start within hours and can prolong ICU stay even if you think you’re preventing a second shock—they’re not free rides!

4️⃣ **Document tryptase appropriately** – First sample as soon as feasible without delaying treatment; second sample at 1–4 h peak; third sample ≥24 h later for baseline. Knowing whether there’s chronic elevation influences future risk stratification more than giving prednisolone now.

5️⃣ **Think “mastocytosis” if you see multiphasic relapses without clear re-exposure.*  
   Early hematology referral & bone marrow biopsy can uncover hidden risk factors—again guiding observation length rather than prompting blanket steroid use.

---

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Stop using systemic corticosteroids as routine “second line” therapy in all anaphylaxis cases.**
- **Prioritise prompt IM epinephrine at the guideline‐recommended adult dose (0·5 mg); be aware most auto‑injectors deliver only 0·3 mg and plan accordingly.**
- **Reserve corticosteroids for genuine refractory upper airway edema or severe bronchospasm that persists after adequate epinephrine dosing—not as prophylaxis against biphasic reactions.**
- **Identify high‑risk patients (delayed epi administration, multiple doses required initially, known mast cell disorders) and extend observation to ≥12–24 h—but do not add steroids automatically during this period.**
- **When you do give steroids, consider underlying metabolic comorbidities—especially diabetes—and monitor glucose actively during admission.**
- **Obtain serial tryptase levels correctly early in the course to aid diagnosis of underlying mast-cell activation syndromes that could explain multiphasic patterns.**
- **Audit your stock:** ensure at least some adult‐strength (0·5 mg) auto‑injectors are available on each critical care trolley so underdosing isn’t built into your resuscitation script.

---

### Bottom Line  

Anaphylaxis is still best fought with *epinephrine*, *speed*, and vigilant monitoring—not with a blanket course of steroids whose promise has run out of steam. As we continue building registries for rare multiphasic cases—like our clarithromycin‐triggered patient—the data will further refine who truly benefits from corticoids while sparing everyone else needless side effects.

Keep those auto‐injectors loaded at the right strength, keep your eyes on early epinephrine timing, and let steroids sit quietly on standby—ready only when inhaled air still refuses to pass despite everything else working perfectly.
