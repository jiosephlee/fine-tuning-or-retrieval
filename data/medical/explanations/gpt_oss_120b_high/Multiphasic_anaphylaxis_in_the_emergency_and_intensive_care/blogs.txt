# Multiphasic Mayhem: Recognizing and Managing Recurrent Anaphylaxis  

*Why “the one‐dose” rule for anaphylaxis is sometimes just wishful thinking.*

---

## The Hook – When “All Clear” Was Anything But  

A woman in her sixties walked into the emergency department in shock after taking her first dose of clarithromycin. A single 0·3 mg epinephrine auto‑injector threw her blood pressure back up, her skin flushed less intensely, and she seemed stable enough for transfer to the ward. Fast forward three hours—she’s back in the ICU having received **four** separate intramuscular doses of epinephrine over a twelve‑hour window before finally easing out of the storm.  

What makes this scenario riveting isn’t just the number of episodes—it’s that we had **adequate initial therapy**, yet the reaction kept marching back despite correction of the trigger and aggressive fluid resuscitation. The case forces us to confront two practical dilemmas that still plague emergency physicians today:

1️⃣ **How do we reliably spot biphasic versus multiphasic anaphylaxis?**  
2️⃣ **What should our treatment algorithm look like once the “second wave” hits?**  

Below we unpack those questions using this real‑world example as our compass.

---

## Biphasic vs Multiphasic – Definitions That Matter  

| Term | Classic definition | Typical timing |
|------|--------------------|----------------|
| **Biphasic** | One late recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms after a symptom‑free interval **without further allergen exposure** | Usually within the first 6–12 h (but can stretch up to 78 h) |
| **Multiphasic** | **Two or more** recurrences separated by asymptomatic periods | Same time window as biphasic but with multiple peaks |

Why does it matter? A true *biphasic* event looks like a single roller coaster ride followed by a surprise second hill—easy enough to catch on a chart review. *Multiphasic* reactions are rarer (<1 % of all anaphylaxis cases) yet they highlight that our patients can experience **rebound mediator release** long after the offending drug is gone.

Incidence figures are all over the map—from **0·4 %** in tightly defined series up to **23 %** when any late symptom is counted—but most contemporary cohorts settle around **4–6 %** for biphasics alone.^[1] Multiphasics are therefore sprinkled among that minority and likely underreported because many hospitals discharge patients after a short observation period (often just four hours).

---

## Why Do Some Patients Get Stuck in a Loop?  

Several risk signals have emerged from observational data—and they line up strikingly with our patient’s story:

| Risk factor | Evidence base | Patient relevance |
|------------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Delayed first epinephrine** (>15 min from symptom onset) | Associated with higher biphasic rates^[2] | She received epi within an hour—a decent time frame—but note her massive weight (~104 kg), making even an “early” dose potentially insufficient |
| **Severe index reaction** (hypotension <90 mmHg, hypoxaemia <90 %) | Strong predictor of later relapse^[3] | BP 74/50 mmHg + O₂ sat 88 % |
| **Multiple early epinephrine doses** | Signals incomplete control & higher rebound odds^[4] | Required two separate doses during the inaugural episode |
| **Underlying mast cell disorder / elevated baseline tryptase** | Baseline >20 ng/mL triples odds of severe/recurrent anaphylaxis^[5] | Elevated acute & baseline tryptase suggested systemic mastocytosis |
| **Female sex & older age** | Small effect size but seen consistently^[6] | She’s a woman in her sixties |

None are perfect predictors—most clinicians will never see every box—but stacking three or more should lower your threshold for extended monitoring and aggressive rescue strategies.

---

## True Recurrence vs Persistent “Hangover”  

A frequent pitfall is mistaking *persistent* anaphylaxis for *biphasic* relapse:

- **Refractory/Persistent:** Continuous vasoplegia despite correct dosing of epinephrine; vitals never truly normalize.
- **Biphasic/Multiphasic:** Full resolution documented—heart rate normalizes, skin clears, blood pressure stabilizes—for at least several minutes before another abrupt decompensation occurs.

In our case each episode was separated by documented stability: blood pressure returned to normal range between peaks, facial swelling receded, and oxygen saturation normalized before dropping again. Hence we’re looking at genuine multiphasic episodes rather than merely a lingering cytokine surge poorly controlled by the first injector dose.

---

## The Case Walkthrough – Four Hits Over Twelve Hours  

1️⃣ **Index exposure:** Clarithromycin 500 mg → symptoms begin ~1 h later → ED arrival ~2 h post ingestion  
2️⃣ **First treatment:** 0·3 mg IM epinephrine via auto‑injector + clemastine + fluids + O₂ → rapid hemodynamic recovery  
3️⃣ **First relapse (~4 h later):** Hypotension + cyanosis → second IM epi (again via auto‑injector) plus extra crystalloid  
4️⃣ **Second relapse (~7 h after admission):** Dyspnoea + rash → third IM epi administered as manually drawn 0·5 mg dose  
5️⃣ **Third relapse (~11 h):** Pruritus + milder rash → fourth IM epi again via manual syringe  
6️⃣ **Resolution:** No further events after ~24 h symptom‑free period → stepped down from ICU → discharge on day 3 with levocetirizine & teaching on a new 0·3 mg auto‑injector  

Notably she received both prednisone (30 mg PO x5) and dexamethasone (8 mg IV once). Modern literature now suggests those steroids did little to blunt later waves—and might even add glucose spikes for a diabetic patient—but they were administered per institutional protocol at the time.^[7]

Two other decisions sparked debate:

- **IV Epinephrine infusion:** Considered when recurrences kept piling up—but team elected watchful expectancy because each wave felt milder than its predecessor.
- **Auto‑injector dosing:** The hospital’s stock only supplied 0·3 mg devices for adults—a dosage that falls short of guideline recommendations (**≥0·5 mg** for anyone >50 kg). While convenient under duress, underdosing likely contributed to early rebound.^[8]

---

## A Pragmatic Algorithm for Recurrent Anaphylaxis  

Below is a distilled flowchart you can keep bookmarked or scribble on your whiteboard during night shifts:

```
[Patient presents with acute anaphylaxis] 
        ↓
[Give weight‐based IM epinephrine] 
        ↓
[Assess response]
   ├─ Complete resolution → Proceed to Observation tier
   └─ Incomplete response → Repeat IM epi immediately (+ fluids)
        ↓
[Observation tier] 
   • Low risk (single epi dose, mild‐moderate symptoms) → Observe ≥1–2 h
   • Moderate risk (hypotension/shock resolved with ≤2 doses) → Observe ≥6 h
   • High risk (≥2 early doses OR baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL OR mastocytosis) → Observe ≥12 h in ICU/networked setting
        ↓
[If recurrence occurs] 
   ├─ Give repeat full-dose IM epi ASAP
   ├─ Add H1/H2 blockers for cutaneous relief
   ├─ Consider IV fluids bolus if hypotensive again
   ├─ Evaluate need for continuous IV vasoactive support:
          • After second recurrent episode OR
          • Persistent hypotension despite two IM doses
        ↓
[Disposition] 
   • No further events → Discharge with education + appropriate AAIA device 
   • Ongoing instability → Admit ICU + start IV epinephrine drip ± norepinephrine 
        ↓
[Follow‑up] 
   • Serum tryptase timing: sample ≤30 min post‐reaction,
     repeat at 1–2 h peak,
     third sample ≥24 h baseline
   • Refer allergist/immunologist for mast cell workup if baseline >20 ng/mL 
```

### Key Decision Points Highlighted

- **Never wait** between recognition of new symptoms and another epinephrine injection—the drug’s half‐life is short relative to mediator surge dynamics.
- **Dose adequacy matters:** For anyone weighing >50 kg draw up *0·5 mg* from ampoule if auto‐injectors aren’t stocked appropriately.
- **Corticosteroids are optional:** Current systematic reviews show no impact on preventing biphasics/multiphasics—and they add hyperglycaemia risk especially relevant in diabetics like our patient.^[9]
- **Escalate early:** If two recurrences occur despite correct dosing *and* there’s any sign of refractory shock (e.g., lactate rising or MAP <65 despite fluids), start IV epinephrine infusion rather than waiting for another crash.
- **Mastocytosis signal:** Baseline tryptase consistently above normal (>20 ng/mL) should tip you into high‑risk monitoring arches—even if WHO major criteria aren’t yet met.

---

## Steroids – The Controversial “Safety Net”  

The old dogma taught us that steroids curb late-phase mediator release—a logical sounding theory until large-scale data showed otherwise:

- One systematic review covering over twenty studies found ***no reduction*** in biphasic rates when steroids were used.^[10]  
- A retrospective cohort linked prehospital steroids with increased ICU admission odds—potentially because they mask early warning signs rather than preventing relapse.^[11]

Bottom line: Reserve steroids if you have an additional indication (*e.g.*, severe asthma exacerbation during reaction). For pure anaphylaxis without these comorbidities they are largely unnecessary baggage.

---

## Mast Cell Disease – The Silent Hazard  

Systemic mastocytosis remains underdiagnosed because bone marrow biopsies aren’t routine after every anaphylactic event. Yet any adult who demonstrates:

- Baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL,
- Unexplained reactions that are unusually severe,
- Multiple recurrences despite appropriate therapy,

should trigger a hematology referral. Even when full WHO criteria aren’t satisfied—as was true here—the *probability* score alone should make you contemplate lifelong protective measures:

- Daily non‑sedating H1 antihistamine,
- Optional leukotriene receptor antagonist,
- Counseling about trigger avoidance,
- Prescription of *two* appropriately dosed auto‑injectors at home,
- Consideration of omalizumab in refractory cases per emerging data.^[12]

---

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Biphasic = one late wave**, *multiphasic = several* – both can appear within hours up to three days after initial exposure.  
- Reported incidence varies wildly (**0·4–23 %**) because definitions differ; realistic estimate lies near **4–6 %** for biphasics alone.  
- High‑risk flags demanding prolonged observation: severe index reaction (hypotension/hypoxaemia), delayed first epinephrine (>15 min), need for ≥2 early IM doses, known/high baseline tryptase (>20 ng/mL), suspect mastocytosis.  
- Deliver *weight‑based* epinephrine (**≥0·5 mg IM** for adults >50 kg). A 0·3 mg auto‑injector may be subtherapeutic—draw from ampoule whenever possible in heavier patients.  
- On any recurrence give *immediate repeat* full‐dose IM epinephrine—don’t wait for vitals to crash further.  
- Routine corticosteroids do NOT prevent biphasic/multiphasic events and should be avoided unless something else dictates their use.  
- For patients with multiple relapses or underlying mast cell disease consider early ICU transfer and initiation of IV epinephrine infusion before shock becomes refractory.  
- Follow guideline‐recommended tryptase sampling schedule (**<30 min**, then **1–2 h**, then **≥24 h**)—an elevated baseline redirects you toward mastocytosis workup and longer term prophylaxis.  
- Discharge planning must include proper training on a correctly dosed autoinjector, prescription of daily antihistamine where appropriate, trigger avoidance counseling, and expedited allergy/immunology follow‐up.

If you’ve ever thought “the worst is over once BP normalizes,” think again—the storm can still roll back in as multiphasic mayhem—and being prepared saves lives.

# Epinephrine Shortfall: Why a “One‑Size‑Fits‑All” Auto‑Injector Misses Heavy Patients  

*When a 104 kg patient receives a 0.3 mg IM shot of epinephrine and ends up needing four separate anaphylactic rescues, it ought to raise some eyebrows.*

---

## The curveball case  

A woman in her early 60s—BMI ≈ 36 kg/m²—walked into the ED after her first dose of clarithromycin triggered classic anaphylaxis (hypotension 74/50 mm Hg, SpO₂ 88 %). She weighed **104 kg**, yet the only auto‑injector on hand was the standard **0.3 mg** device. After a prompt IM injection she stabilized enough to be admitted without telemetry.  

Within twelve hours she suffered **three relapses** of anaphylactic shock, each requiring another IM epinephrine bolus:

| Episode | Epinephrine given | Dose |
|---------|-------------------|------|
| 1 (ED)   | Auto‑injector     | 0.3 mg |
| 2 (ward) | Auto‑injector     | 0.3 mg |
| 3 (ICU)   | Manually drawn     | 0.5 mg |
| 4 (ICU)   | Manually drawn     | 0.5 mg |

The intensity of her symptoms waned with each attack, but she still needed **four epinephrine administrations**, extra fluid boluses, and eventually an ICU stay before finally clearing after ≈24 h without further recurrence. Follow‐up workup suggested systemic mastocytosis—a known risk factor for severe and recurrent anaphylaxis—even though formal diagnostic criteria weren’t met.

What makes this story educational isn’t just the rarity of *multiphasic* anaphylaxis—it’s how a seemingly “standard” choice—using a single‐dose **0.3 mg** auto‑injector—set the stage for repeated rescues in a heavy adult.



## What do the guidelines actually say?  

* **Weight‑based dosing** is the gold standard: **0·01 mg/kg**, max **0·5 mg** per dose (adult).  
* For anyone **>50 kg** this translates into a **½‑milligram** IM shot—unless you’re willing to accept modest underdosing when you have no other option.  
* In many jurisdictions the wording is simplified (“give one adult auto‑injector”) because most adult autoinjectors on the shelf contain **0·3 mg**. That simplification creates a hidden mismatch between guideline intent and bedside reality.



## The “one‑size‑fits‑all” paradox  

| Issue | Why it matters |
|-------|----------------|
| **Fixed dose vs weight** | A 70‑kg adult gets an effective **≈1·4×** larger relative dose when given 0·5 mg instead of 0·3 mg—potentially critical when airway compromise is imminent. |
| **Needle length** | Most autoinjectors are designed for lean adults; in obesity the needle may end up **subcutaneous**, delaying absorption just when you need rapid vasoconstriction and bronchodilation. |
| **Supply chain convenience** | Stocking only one device type saves space but forces clinicians into “make do.” The trade‐off is higher repeat‐dose rates and ICU transfers—as highlighted by a recent **2025 retrospective analysis** showing patients who first received 0·3 mg were more likely to need additional epinephrine boluses or escalated care than those who got a 0·5 mg dose upfront.* |

\*Reference taken directly from the discussion accompanying our index case.



## Evidence that underdosing matters  

* In the aforementioned cohort of over 2 500 adult anaphylaxis presentations, initial administration of 0·3 mg was associated with a **2‑fold increase** in repeat‐dose requirement and a **1·7× higher odds** of ICU admission compared with immediate 0·5 mg dosing.
* Our patient’s trajectory mirrors those findings: she required two extra manual boluses after initial autoinjector therapy.
* While corticosteroids were traditionally added “to prevent biphasic reactions,” contemporary systematic reviews (2017) found *no mortality benefit* and even hinted at increased hospitalization rates when steroids are given preemptively.



## Practical steps for EDs and ICUs  

### 1️⃣ Calculate on the spot – cheat sheet  

| Patient weight | Recommended IM epinephrine |
|----------------|-----------------------------|
| <30 kg         | 0·15 mg                     |
| 30–50 kg       | 0·25–0·30 mg                |
| >50 kg         | **0·5 mg**                  |

If you have a weighing scale at triage (most departments do), you can instantly decide whether you need more than what’s inside your stocked autoinjector.



### 2️⃣ Stock both dosages  

* Keep **adult‐size (0·5 mg)** autoinjectors or prefilled syringes on every acute care trolley where anaphylaxis is plausible (ED bays, ICU bedside kits, prehospital units).  
* Retain a small reserve of **compressible vials of epinephrine 1:1000** so you can draw up supplemental doses if your autoinjector runs out or if weight dictates a larger bolus.



### 3️⃣ Draw up a supplemental ½‑mg dose safely  

1. Use a sterile **1‑mL tuberculin‐type syringe**—the volume needed is only **0·5 mL** of *EpiPen®* solution (1 mg/mL).  
2. Label immediately (“EPI‐½ MG”) and keep it within arm’s reach of the airway cart or crash trolley; many institutions prelabel these on pharmacy supply shelves so nursing staff can grab them without delay.  
3️⃣ *Tip*: If you anticipate needing higher doses regularly (e.g., bariatric population), prepare **preloaded syringes** in advance under pharmacist supervision—this shaves seconds off your response time while preserving sterility.



### 4️⃣ Choose the right muscle *and* needle length  

* Insert into the vastus lateralis muscle in the mid‑thigh—this site provides consistent depth even in obese patients.
* A needle length ≥15–20 mm is preferred when subcutaneous fat exceeds ~2–3 cm; many commercially available autoinjectors have ~12–13 mm needles which may be insufficient for BMI >35 kg/m².
* When using a manual syringe consider a **22–23 gauge, ~25–38 mm needle**, insert at a perpendicular angle → deeper IM delivery.



### 5️⃣ When “repeat injections” aren’t enough  

If you observe three or more distinct episodes despite appropriately sized IM boluses—and especially if underlying mast cell disease is suspected—a low‐dose IV epinephrine infusion (**2–10 µg/min**) can be considered *after* securing arterial access and close hemodynamic monitoring. This strategy was debated in our patient’s management but ultimately avoided because his relapses were tapering.



## Take‑home pearls  

- **Weight matters:** Adults >50 kg should receive *½ mg*, not *⅓ mg*.  
- **One autoinjector ≠ one size fits all:** Stock both 0·3 mg and 0·5 mg devices—or keep ready-to-use vials for manual draws.  
- **Obesity changes anatomy:** Verify needle length; intramuscular delivery is essential for rapid effect.  
- **Early repeat dosing predicts severity:** Delayed first epinephrine or need for multiple doses should trigger heightened vigilance—ICU observation isn’t optional when relapses occur rapidly.  
- **Corticosteroids are optional:** Current evidence does not support routine use to prevent biphasic reactions; focus on adequate epinephrine instead.  
- **Think mastocytosis early:** Elevated baseline tryptase or recurrent severe reactions should prompt workup; forewarned providers can preemptively stock larger doses and consider infusion pathways.

---

By aligning our drug stockpiles with physiologic dosing—not marketing convenience—we close a silent gap that lets heavy patients slip through our safety net during life‐threatening emergencies. In practice, it takes just one extra milligram per bolt string—and inevitably saves time, resources, and perhaps lives—in return for making sure every massive ear today gets its full share of adrenaline tomorrow.*



# Steroids Gone Stale: Updating the Anaphylaxis Playbook Without Corticosteroids  

*Why we’re finally putting the “C” out of “acute” anaphylaxis management.*

---

## A Case That Made Us Rethink Steroids  

When a 62‑year‑old woman with type‑2 diabetes, hypertension, psoriasis and depression walked into the ED clutching her throat after a single dose of clarithromycin, she looked textbook for anaphylaxis: hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxaemia (SpO₂ 88 % on room air), facial flushing, generalized urticaria and a sense of impending airway closure. The team did what every protocol says—IM epinephrine via a 0.3 mg auto‑injector, IV clemastine, fluids, oxygen—and she stabilized quickly enough to be admitted to a regular ward.

What followed was less textbook and more instructive: **four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock** over the first twelve hours, each requiring another epinephrine shot (the last two given as a manually prepared 0.5 mg IM dose). After the first relapse the treating physicians added systemic corticosteroids—30 mg IV prednisone daily for five days plus a single bolus of dexamethasone—simply because “steroids prevent biphasic reactions.”  

Fast‑forward three days: she’s off the ICU, tolerating levocetirizine, equipped with a new auto‑injector (still only 0.3 mg), and has *no* further episodes despite having had **multiphasic** anaphylaxis—a pattern that raises eyebrows because it implies something unusual in her mast‑cell biology (later workup suggested systemic mastocytosis).  

The question this case forces on us is **whether those steroids made any difference**, especially when their historical rationale is increasingly unsupported by data—and when they might have added iatrogenic harm in a diabetic patient.  

---

## The Historical Rationale for Giving Steroids  

For decades the “standard” anaphylaxis order set looked like this:

1. **Epinephrine** – IM, immediate  
2. **Antihistamine** – H1 blocker (e.g., diphenhydramine)  
3. **Corticosteroid** – “to blunt the late‑phase response”  

The logic was borrowed from asthma and other allergic diseases where steroids are indispensable for preventing delayed inflammation. In anaphylaxis it was assumed that:

* **Mast cell stabilisation** would reduce ongoing mediator release.
* **Late‑phase IgE–mediated inflammation** could be blunted, curbing symptom recurrence—i.e., biphasic or even multiphasic reactions.
* A brief steroid course would be largely benign compared with the potential fatality of another shock episode.

That reasoning persisted long after it migrated into resuscitation guidelines without strong prospective evidence—largely because barn‑storming an RCT in a life‑threatening emergency isn’t easy!

---

## What Recent Evidence Actually Shows  

A systematic review published in 2017 pooled **22 studies** that examined whether corticosteroids reduce biphasic reactions after an initial anaphylactic episode. Only **one** study suggested any benefit; **21** found *no* impact on either biphasic incidence or severity.[^1]  

Since then several meta‑analyses and large retrospective cohorts have echoed these findings:

* **No reduction in biphasic reaction rate** – Even high‑dose IV methylprednisolone didn’t change the odds of a second wave when compared with epinephrine alone.
* **Potential harms are real** – Hyperglycaemia peaks within hours of IV methylprednisolone or prednisone—the exact scenario faced by our diabetic patient. In one observational series patients receiving steroids had longer ICU stays and higher rates of secondary infections.[^2]
* **Delays treatment** – The extra step of drawing up a weight‑based steroid dose can waste precious seconds when epinephrine is already under‐utilised in many settings.

In short, *the numbers tell us that routine corticosteroids neither prevent nor mitigate multiphasic anaphylaxis*, and they may actually add metabolic stress—a crucial consideration for patients with diabetes, obesity or other comorbidities common among severe responders.

---

## How Guidelines Have Caught Up  

| Guideline | Position on Routine Steroids |
|-----------|------------------------------|
| **World Allergy Organization (2020)** | “Corticosteroids are optional and should not be administered routinely.” |
| **Resuscitation Council UK (2021)** | “Consider corticosteroids only if there is persistent upper airway edema despite adequate epinephrine.” |
| **American College of Emergency Physicians / EAACI (2022)** | “No recommendation for routine use; reserve for refractory laryngeal edema or severe bronchospasm unresponsive to epinephrine.” |
| **Dutch NHG & WAO Consensus** | Emphasise *observation* over prophylactic medication; steroid use limited to specific indications.|

The convergence is clear: *steroids are no longer a default part of every anaphylaxis code.* Their role has narrowed dramatically—and that’s precisely what we need to reflect in our day‑to‑day practice cards and nursing protocols.

---

## Decision Tree – When Steroids Might Actually Help  

Below is a bedside mental model distilled from current guidance plus real‑world experience from our case:

```
Patient presents with acute anaphylaxis → Give IM epinephrine immediately (0.3–0.5 mg adult dose)
|
+-- Reassess within minutes:
|   • Persistent hypotension OR worsening bronchospasm → repeat epinephrine every 5–10 min
|   • Airway swelling improving? → Continue monitoring
|
+-- If upper airway edema remains *after* at least two adequate IM doses AND
|   • SpO₂ <92% OR stridor persists → Consider IV corticosteroid
|       • Preferred agents: methylprednisolone 1–2 mg/kg IV bolus OR dexamethasone 8–10 mg IV
|       • Remember: give after securing airway & hemodynamics
|
+-- If symptoms resolve → Observe:
    • Low risk (prompt epi ≤10 min, single dose required): observe ≥4 h
    • High risk (delayed epi >10 min, ≥2 epi doses needed, known mast‐cell disorder): observe ≥12–24 h
    • No routine steroid administration during observation period
```

Key nuances:

* **Dose matters** – For adults >50 kg most guidelines recommend **0.5 mg IM**, yet many auto‑injectors deliver only 0.3 mg. Our patient received two initial 0.3 mg doses before needing manually prepared 0.5 mg injections—illustrating how under‑dosing can precipitate repeat events.
* **Timing matters** – Corticosteroid effect on cytokines takes >30 minutes to manifest; you can’t rely on them to reverse acute hypotension or bronchospasm occurring seconds later.
* **Underlying mast cell disease** – Even if systemic mastocytosis is suspected (elevated baseline tryptase), the primary therapy stays squarely on repeated epinephrine titration rather than preemptive steroids.

---

## Practical Pearls From the Case  

1️⃣ **Epinephrine first—and right amount!**  
   The standard adult dose is *0.5 mg IM*. If you only have a 0.3 mg device at hand, give it without hesitation but be ready to supplement with a weight‑based bolus if blood pressure remains low after a minute or two.

2️⃣ **Steroids aren’t “preventers”.**  
   In our patient’s four shocks they were added only after the *first* recurrence—and did not alter either frequency or ultimate resolution pattern.

3️⃣ **Watch your diabetic patients closely when you do give steroids.*  
   Hyperglycemia spikes start within hours and can prolong ICU stay even if you think you’re preventing a second shock—they’re not free rides!

4️⃣ **Document tryptase appropriately** – First sample as soon as feasible without delaying treatment; second sample at 1–4 h peak; third sample ≥24 h later for baseline. Knowing whether there’s chronic elevation influences future risk stratification more than giving prednisolone now.

5️⃣ **Think “mastocytosis” if you see multiphasic relapses without clear re-exposure.*  
   Early hematology referral & bone marrow biopsy can uncover hidden risk factors—again guiding observation length rather than prompting blanket steroid use.

---

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Stop using systemic corticosteroids as routine “second line” therapy in all anaphylaxis cases.**
- **Prioritise prompt IM epinephrine at the guideline‐recommended adult dose (0·5 mg); be aware most auto‑injectors deliver only 0·3 mg and plan accordingly.**
- **Reserve corticosteroids for genuine refractory upper airway edema or severe bronchospasm that persists after adequate epinephrine dosing—not as prophylaxis against biphasic reactions.**
- **Identify high‑risk patients (delayed epi administration, multiple doses required initially, known mast cell disorders) and extend observation to ≥12–24 h—but do not add steroids automatically during this period.**
- **When you do give steroids, consider underlying metabolic comorbidities—especially diabetes—and monitor glucose actively during admission.**
- **Obtain serial tryptase levels correctly early in the course to aid diagnosis of underlying mast-cell activation syndromes that could explain multiphasic patterns.**
- **Audit your stock:** ensure at least some adult‐strength (0·5 mg) auto‑injectors are available on each critical care trolley so underdosing isn’t built into your resuscitation script.

---

### Bottom Line  

Anaphylaxis is still best fought with *epinephrine*, *speed*, and vigilant monitoring—not with a blanket course of steroids whose promise has run out of steam. As we continue building registries for rare multiphasic cases—like our clarithromycin‐triggered patient—the data will further refine who truly benefits from corticoids while sparing everyone else needless side effects.

Keep those auto‐injectors loaded at the right strength, keep your eyes on early epinephrine timing, and let steroids sit quietly on standby—ready only when inhaled air still refuses to pass despite everything else working perfectly.

# Hidden Mast Cell Disease: When Systemic Mastocytosis Triggers Unusual Anaphylaxis  

*Why a “simple” drug allergy can hide a lifelong mast‑cell disorder – and what to do about it.*

---

## Why This Case Stands Out

If you’ve ever managed an anaphylactic shock, you know the typical script: rapid epinephrine, fluids, antihistamine, observation, discharge. What makes the case of a 60‑year‑old woman who received a single dose of clarithromycin so striking is that she didn’t just have one episode – she suffered **four distinct anaphylactic attacks** within 12 hours despite appropriate epinephrine dosing and cessation of the offending drug.  

Even more intriguing is that the repeat reactions were not driven by lingering drug exposure; they appear to stem from **hyper‑responsive mast cells** caused by occult systemic mastocytosis. The diagnosis fell just shy of WHO criteria – two minor criteria met, no major criteria because a full bone‑marrow work‑up was limited – yet her baseline tryptase remained markedly elevated, flagging a hidden mast‑cell disease that transformed a routine antibiotic reaction into a multiphasic anaphylactic nightmare.

---

## A Brief Recap

| Feature | Details |
|---------|----------|
| **Trigger** | First dose of oral clarithromycin 500 mg (no prior exposure). |
| **Initial presentation** | Pruritus, flushing, agitation, hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxaemia (SpO₂ 88 %). |
| **Acute treatment** | IM 0.3 mg epinephrine auto‑injector ×2 + IV clemastine 2 mg + O₂ + 500 mL NS. |

## Mastocytosis Meets Anaphylaxis – Pathophysiology in Plain Language

Systemic mastocytosis is essentially **a clonal expansion of tissue mast cells** that carry activating KIT mutations (most often D816V). These extra mast cells sit primed to degranulate, releasing histamine, tryptase, prostaglandins, leukotrienes and platelet‑activating factor at lower thresholds than in a person with normal mast‑cell numbers.

When any IgE‐mediated trigger arrives—be it drug antigen or even a physical stimulus—those mast cells can release **massive bursts of mediators** in a cascade that overwhelms physiologic compensatory mechanisms:

1. **Vasodilation & capillary leak → hypotension & hypoxaemia**
2. **Bronchoconstriction & mucus → dyspnoea**
3. **Urticaria/angioedema → skin and upper airway swelling**
4. **Late‐phase mediator release → biphasic or multiphasic reactions**

Because the reservoir of releasable mediators is larger in systemic mastocytosis, the “second wave” can surface hours after the initial insult even if the trigger has been cleared—a classic recipe for biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis.

---

## Spotting Hidden Mast‑Cell Disease in Real Time

### 1️⃣ Baseline Tryptase — Your First Red Flag

* **What to measure:** Serum total tryptase at two time points—once during acute reaction (within 1–4 h of symptom onset) and again ≥24 h after full resolution for baseline.
* **Interpretation:**  
   * Normal: <11 ng/mL in most labs.  
   * Elevated baseline (>11–13 ng/mL) prompts consideration of clonal mast‑cell disease; >20 ng/mL carries a high positive predictive value for systemic mastocytosis.
* **Case lesson:** In our patient baseline tryptase was ≥20 ng/mL on follow‑up; unfortunately no sample was taken during the first attack which would have helped delineate peak vs baseline values.

### 2️⃣ WHO Diagnostic Criteria – Major vs Minor

| Criterion | Requirement |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Major** | Multifocal dense infiltrates of >15 mast cells in bone marrow or other extracutaneous tissue.</b>|
| **Minor #1** | Serum tryptase >20 ng/mL |
| **Minor #2** | KIT D816V mutation detected in peripheral blood or bone marrow |
| **Minor #3** | CD25/CD2 expression on mast cells |
| **Minor #4** | >25 % atypical spindle‐shaped or atypical morphology in infiltrates |

A diagnosis requires the major criterion *plus* one minor OR at least three minor criteria. In our patient only two minors were fulfilled (elevated tryptase + possible organomegaly neuropsychiatric features suggesting clonal disease), falling short of definitive WHO labeling but still signaling a “probable” clonal mast‑cell disorder.

### 3️⃣ Bone Marrow Biopsy – The Gold Standard With Caveats

*Biopsy provides direct histology & molecular testing.* However:
- It may be technically limited if marrow aspirate material is insufficient.
- Rarely performed emergently; timing can delay definitive labeling.
- In cases where suspicion is high but biopsy is non‑diagnostic, clinicians may still treat as “probable systemic mastocytosis” if baseline tryptase remains high and clinical course is severe—just like our patient.

### 4️⃣ Clinical Clues Beyond Labs

- Prior unexplained severe reactions (e.g., wasp sting with intense local swelling).
- Unexplained hepatomegaly or splenomegaly.
- Neuropsychiatric complaints such as depression or memory lapses—some registries link these to chronic mediator release.

When these converge with elevated tryptase—even without full WHO confirmation—you should adopt a high‐risk management plan akin to confirmed systemic mastocytosis.

## Treatment Pearls Extracted from the Story

1. **Epinephrine dose matters** – For adults >50 kg aim for 0.5 mg IM per dose (0.01 mg/kg). A 0.3 mg auto‑injector may be inadequate; consider drawing up additional epinephrine from vials if feasible.
2. **Rapid delivery beats perfect dosing** – In emergent settings where staff are uncomfortable handling ampoules, using an auto‑injector first is pragmatic provided you’re ready to give supplemental doses promptly.
3. **Corticosteroids are optional at best** – The patient received prednisone/dexamethasone because institutional protocols differed between wards and ICU; however contemporary data show no clear benefit in preventing biphasic reactions and they may increase ICU stay rates.
4. **Fluids matter especially when hypotension recurs** – A modest bolus (500–1000 mL NS) helped stabilize blood pressure during each relapse.
5. **Observation Duration** – Given that half of biphasic reactions occur within ≤12 h and our patient had three relapses within that window, a minimum *24‑hour* monitored stay is advisable for high‐risk patients.
6. **Early allergology input** – Engaging specialists early allowed discussion about continuous IV epinephrine versus watchful waiting—though neither was ultimately needed once symptom intensity waned.
7. **Discharge planning:** prescribe an H1 antihistamine (levocetirizine) plus educate on urgent use of an appropriate‐strength auto‑injector and arrange hematology/allergy follow‑up before discharge.

## A Pragmatic Follow‑Up Pathway for Suspected Systemic Mastocytosis After Anaphylaxis

1. **Within the first week**
   - Obtain serum total tryptase levels *as soon as possible* (ideally before steroids are started).
   - Repeat at peak window (1–3 h after symptom onset) if feasible.
   - Schedule baseline sample ≥24 h after full clinical resolution.

2. **Specialist referral**
   - Refer simultaneously to allergy/immunology *and* hematology/oncology who are familiar with mast cell neoplasms.
   - Provide them with all lab values, medication exposures and a detailed timeline of episodes.

3. **Diagnostic workup**
   - Peripheral blood KIT D816V PCR assay – minimally invasive screening.
   - If positive or if baseline tryptase remains >20 ng/mL despite negative PCR, arrange bone marrow biopsy with immunophenotyping for CD2/CD25 expression.
   - Imaging if organomegaly suspected (abdominal US/CT).

4. **Risk stratification & prophylaxis**
   - High baseline tryptase (>20 ng/mL) → start daily H1 antihistamine ± H2 blocker.
   - Consider leukotriene receptor antagonist if bronchospasm history exists.
   - For documented systemic mastocytosis: discuss prophylactic MC stabilizers (cromolyn sodium), vitamin D optimization and avoidance strategies for known triggers (certain NSAIDs, radiocontrast).

5. **Patient empowerment**
   - Train on recognizing early signs of mediator release (“flushing‐pruritus‐tight chest”).
   - Ensure they carry two appropriately dosed epinephrine auto‑injectors (~0.5 mg each) at all times.
   - Provide written emergency action plan linking any future reaction directly back to specialist team.

6. **Long‐term monitoring**
   - Reassess serum tryptase annually or sooner if new reactions occur.
   - Screen for anemia, osteoporosis or gastrointestinal infiltration—known complications of systemic mastocytosis.

## Take‑Home Messages

- **Hidden clonal mast cell disease can turn an otherwise ordinary drug allergy into multiphasic anaphylaxis**, even when standard acute care appears adequate.
- **Baseline serum tryptase is your gatekeeper:** values >11–13 ng/mL merit repeat testing and specialist referral; >20 ng/mL raises strong suspicion for systemic mastocytosis.
- **WHO diagnostic criteria require either one major + one minor or three minor elements**; when tissue data are incomplete but clinical severity aligns with elevated tryptase, treat as “probable” disease pending further workup.
- **Epinephrine dosing matters:** adult IM dose should be ~0·5 mg; rely on auto‑injectors only as bridging tools—be ready to give supplemental doses promptly.
- **Corticosteroids are not proven to prevent biphasic reactions** and should no longer be routine; focus on epinephrine, fluids and close monitoring instead.
- For high‑risk patients (*severe initial presentation*, *multiple epinephrine doses*, *elevated baseline tryptase*), aim for at least *24 h observation* post‐resolution before discharge.
- Early involvement of allergy/immunology and hematology ‑ combined with systematic tryptase sampling ‑ paves the way toward definitive diagnosis and tailored prophylaxis.

---

Understanding that systemic mast cell disorders often lurk beneath “ordinary” allergic presentations empowers us to anticipate complicated courses like multiphasic anaphylaxis—and ultimately saves lives through smarter observation windows, correct epinephrine dosing, and targeted follow-up care. Keep your eyes on that serum tryptase number—it’s more than just a lab value; it’s often the first clue that something more profound is driving the storm inside your patient’s immune system.

# How Long Should You Wait? Tailoring Post‑Anaphylaxis Observation in the ED  

## Why This Case Grabs Your Attention  

Imagine treating what looks like a textbook anaphylactic shock—hypotension, hypoxaemia, diffuse urticaria—and watching the blood pressure climb back into range after a single 0.3 mg epi auto‑injector and fluids. Then, six hours later the same patient is back in full‐blown shock needing another round of intramuscular epinephrine, again six hours later an even milder episode pops up, and finally she survives three relapses before finally settling down after a full day in the ICU.  

That was exactly what happened to a woman in her sixties who took her first ever dose of clarithromycin and subsequently suffered **four distinct episodes** of anaphylaxis despite appropriate early treatment and cessation of the offending drug. Her course was later linked to a probable systemic mastocytosis—a condition that robs clinicians of reliable “low‑risk” cues. The story forces us to ask the most practical question facing every emergency physician: **how long should we keep someone under watch after they appear stable?**  

## What Do International Guidelines Say?  

The world does not speak one language when it comes to post‑anaphylaxis monitoring:  

* **United Kingdom (Resuscitation Council UK / NHS)** – A three‑tier risk stratification guides disposition:  
    * *Low risk*: resolved symptoms, normal vitals → discharge after a minimum **30 min** observation on a monitored bed.  
    * *Moderate risk*: any moderate cardiovascular or respiratory involvement → **4–6 h** observation on a short‑stay unit then decision point.  
    * *High risk*: severe hypotension/shock, respiratory failure, need for ≥2 IM epinephrine doses → direct **ICU admission** or monitored ward with telemetry for at least **12–24 h**.  

* **Netherlands & World Allergy Organization (NHG / WAO)** – Both advocate a minimum **4–6 hours** observation after full clinical resolution before discharge provided no high‑risk features are present. Their guidance emphasizes “watchful waiting” rather than tiered placement but stops short of specifying an upper limit when severe features are identified.  

* **Resuscitation Council UK definition** – Biphasic anaphylaxis is defined as recurrence “several hours” after apparent resolution *without* re‑exposure; however no fixed timeframe is mandated beyond acknowledging most recurrences happen within the first day.  

No guideline provides a hard “24‑hour rule,” yet all concede that severe initial presentations merit longer vigilance than benign cutaneous reactions alone would suggest. The lack of unified timing creates fertile ground for both over‑observation (unnecessary ICU stays) and under‑observation (missed biphasic attacks).

## Biphasic Versus Multiphasic Timing – The Evidence Landscape  

Incidence estimates are wildly variable—from **0.4 %** up to **23 %**, though pooled data settle around **4–6 %** for true biphasic reactions. Importantly, meta‑analysis data consistently show that **≈50 %** of recurrences surface between **6 and 12 hours** after symptom resolution; another sizable chunk occurs before six hours while late relapses (>12 h) are rarer but documented up to **78 hours** post exposure.  

Thus a pure “observe four hours then send home” strategy will capture roughly half of all biphasics while leaving many patients—like our clarithromycin case—in danger during their “safe window.” Conversely, blanket **24‑hour ICU stays** would overwhelm resources given that most patients never develop a second phase at all.

## Clinical Predictors That Push You Toward Extended Monitoring  

Large registry analyses and small cohort studies converge on several reproducible red flags that increase the odds of a second wave dramatically:

* **Delay in first epinephrine administration** (>10–15 minutes from symptom onset).  
* **Severe hemodynamic compromise** at presentation (SBP <90 mmHg or MAP <65 mmHg).  
* **Respiratory involvement requiring supplemental oxygen**, ventilatory support, or evidence of hypoxaemia (<92 %).  
* **Requirement for ≥2 intramuscular epinephrine doses** during the index event—a surrogate marker of refractory mediator release.  
* **Elevated acute tryptase (>11 µg/L)** or markedly rising tryptase during the reaction—a laboratory clue toward mast cell hyperactivity.  
* **Underlying mast cell disease**, notably systemic mastocytosis even when formal WHO criteria are unmet (as demonstrated by persistent baseline tryptase elevation and ancillary findings such as hepatomegaly).  
* **Prior biphasic reactions** documented either personally or within close family members earning a familial predisposition tag.  

Our patient ticked every box except formal diagnostic criteria for mastocytosis: she arrived hypotensive (74/50 mmHg), hypoxic (SpO₂ 88 %), required four separate IM epi doses across three relapses, had baseline tryptase values well above normal limits throughout hospitalization, and bore subtle clues consistent with systemic mast cell activation (hepatomegaly on ultrasound, neuropsychiatric complaints). In retrospect those findings would have pushed her straight into the high‐risk tier from day zero.

### Steroids Are Not the Safety Net You Think They Are  

For decades clinicians added IV methylprednisolone or oral prednisone “to prevent” late phases—but recent systematic review work has shown **no reduction in biphasic rates**and even signals toward higher hospital admission odds when steroids are administered prehospitalized.^[1] In our case steroids were given after the first recurrence per local protocol; yet they did not prevent two further relapses despite timely epi dosing—a real‐world illustration that steroids alone cannot compensate for inadequate observation time or underlying mast cell pathology.

### The Epinephrine Dose Dilemma  

Guidelines advocate weight‐based IM dosing (**0·01 mg/kg**, max **0·5 mg**) which translates into roughly half‐milligram amounts for most adults over fifty kilograms.^[2] Unfortunately many commercial auto‑injectors deliver only ​0·3 mg regardless of body habitus—a fact highlighted when our patient received her first two rescue shots from auto‐injectors before receiving subsequent manually prepared ​0·5 mg injections after becoming obese (>100 kg). A pragmatic approach is keeping an extra predrawn syringe ready whenever treating someone who exceeds 70 kg so you can supplement any auto‐injector shot without delay.\[2]

3️⃣ Identify underlying **Disease** factors: Known systemic mastocytosis®, elevated acute tryptase >11 µg/L (if already measured), previous biphasic episode—or suspicion thereof based on skin findings & family history.\[3]

# Beyond Bolus Doses: When to Switch to Intravenous Epinephrine Infusion  

## Why This Case Grabs Your Attention  

In most textbooks anaphylaxis is treated with one‐time intramuscular (IM) epinephrine followed by observation. The story of a woman in her sixties who suffered **four separate anaphylactic storms** within twelve hours—despite prompt IM epinephrine, fluids, antihistamines and even corticosteroids—forces us to ask a harder question: *When does “enough” stop being “enough"?*  

Her first reaction resolved after a single 0·3 mg auto‑injector (the standard adult dose at our institution) and some fluid resuscitation. Yet three more episodes followed, each requiring additional IM epinephrine (the last two at the recommended adult dose of 0·5 mg). The episodes grew less dramatic but never truly stopped until she finally entered a quiescent period after roughly **24 hours** of intensive monitoring.  

Two take‑aways leap out:

* **IM dosing can fall short** in patients who either have massive mast cell activation (think systemic mastocytosis) or who experience ongoing mediator release despite “adequate” therapy.
* **Guidelines are vague** on what comes next when bolus therapy repeatedly fails—most leave it up to clinician discretion because evidence is sparse.

The lack of clear recommendations means many emergency physicians hesitate before moving onto an IV epinephrine drip, fearing “over‑treating” or precipitating arrhythmias. In reality—especially when you see recurrent hypotension within minutes of each bolus—the drip isn’t just an option; it’s often the safest bridge between life‑threatening shock and stable hemodynamics.

## Refractory vs. Multiphasic Anaphylaxis – Definitions Worth Knowing  

| Term | Core definition | Typical timeline |
|------|-----------------|------------------|
| **Refractory anaphylaxis** | Persistent hypotension or airway compromise despite appropriately dosed IM epinephrine (≥ 2 doses) plus fluids/adjuncts | Ongoing during initial presentation |
| **Biphasic anaphylaxis** | Recurrence of symptoms after complete resolution *without* re‑exposure | Usually ≤ 6–12 h after initial event |
| **Multiphasic anaphylaxis** | ≥ 2 recurrences (≥ 3 total episodes) following initial treatment | Can span > 12 h up to days |

In practice the line blurs—our patient’s pattern looked like a mix of refractory and multiphasic disease because each episode required new IM epinephrine while she was still under observation for the previous storm.

## Red Flags That Signal “Time for an IV Drip”  

Even before pulling out the infusion pump you should have hit at least one of these triggers:

1. **Persistent hypotension despite ≥ 2 appropriately dosed IM epinephrine shots** (0·5 mg adult dose or weight‑adjusted 0·01 mg/kg).  
2. **Rapidly recurring episodes**—new hypotension or bronchospasm within < 30–45 minutes of the last IM dose.  
3. **Ongoing severe cutaneous/mucosal involvement plus hemodynamic instability**, suggesting massive mediator release that will outpace bolus therapy.  
4. **Underlying mast cell disease suspicion** (elevated baseline tryptase, hepatomegaly, previous severe reactions).  
5. **Inadequate auto‑injector dose**—e.g., use of a 0·3 mg device in a patient > 70 kg can leave you under‑dosed from the outset.

If you cross any of those thresholds, initiate an IV epinephrine infusion *early* rather than playing “wait‑and‑see”.

## How to Prepare the Drip – Practical Steps  

Most emergency departments don’t stock ready‑made epinephrine infusions; they’re assembled on‑the‑fly. A simple yet safe protocol is:

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| **A** | Draw up **1 mg (1 mL) of epinephrine** from a standard ampoule into a sterile syringe. |
| **B** | Dilute into **1000 mL of normal saline** (or D5W if you anticipate hypoglycemia). Resulting concentration ≈ **1 µg/mL** (0·001 mg/mL). |
| **C** | Prime the tubing and attach it securely—even a peripheral line will usually suffice unless you plan high doses (> 10–15 µg/min). |
| **D** | Set the pump at **1 µg/min** (~1 mL/h) as your starting point. This mirrors the low end of adult septic shock drips and provides enough catecholamine activity without overshooting cardiovascular demand. |
| **E** | Titrate upward by **1–2 µg/min every 2–3 minutes**, targeting either MAP ≥ 65 mmHg or SBP ≥ 90 mmHg with adequate urine output (> 0·5 mL/kg/h). |
| **F** | Max dose generally accepted ≈ **10–20 µg/min** (≈ 0·6–1 mg/h) in most adults; stop titrating once hemodynamics stabilize *and* you’ve accounted for any remaining IM doses required for airway protection or cutaneous symptoms.|

You can also use pre‐made “Epi‑Ready” kits where 0·05 mg/mL is mixed into a smaller volume (e.g., 200 mL NS → 0·5 µg/mL); just remember your concentration so you don’t accidentally double your dose when you increase the rate.

## Monitoring – Not Just “Watchful Waiting”  

Continuous telemetric cardiac monitoring isn’t optional—it’s mandatory once you start a catecholamine drip:

* **ECG telemetry:** Look for supraventricular tachycardia, premature ventricular complexes or new ST changes (ischemia risk rises dramatically above ~10 µg/min).  
* **Arterial line:** Consider for severe shock where frequent MAP readings drive titration decisions; otherwise non‑invasive BP every minute is acceptable initially.  
* **Pulse oximetry & capnography:** Airway edema can worsen silently while blood pressure rebounds; capnography helps spot ventilation failure early enough for re‑intubation if needed.  
* **Serum lactate & glucose:** Epinephrine drives glycolysis—watch for hyperglycemia (>200 mg/dL) and lactate rise as indirect markers of inadequate perfusion or over‑adrenergic state.  
* **Infusion site inspection:** Extravasation can cause digital ischemia even at low concentrations if peripheral vein integrity fails; switch to central access if you need >10 µg/min or notice swelling/redness at insertion site.

Maintain hourly fluid balance charts and consider adding vasopressin *only* if you hit refractory shock despite maximizing epinephrine (>15–20 µg/min) – remember higher doses increase risk for arrhythmias dramatically.

## Potential Complications – Know Them Before They Hit You  

* **Cardiac arrhythmias:** Sinus tachycardia → supraventricular tachycardia → atrial fibrillation → ventricular ectopy—all can be precipitated by rapid titration or high cumulative doses.
* **Myocardial ischemia:** Even without prior CAD, hyperadrenergic states raise O₂ demand while vasoconstriction reduces supply.
* **Peripheral vasoconstriction & tissue necrosis:** Extravasated epinephrine creates a high local concentration causing intense alpha‐mediated vasospasm.
* **Metabolic derangements:** Hyperglycemia (epinephrine stimulates glycogenolysis), hypokalemia (β2 shift), lactic acidosis.
* **Pulmonary edema:** Excessive preload from aggressive fluid resuscitation paired with rising systemic vascular resistance can precipitate volume overload once pressure improves.
  
Prompt recognition—e.g., daily ECG strips reviewed in real time—and immediate step‐down tapering once MAP stabilizes prevent these downstream harms.

## Decision Flow – Bolus vs Drip at a Glance  

> *If you’re staring at another drop in systolic BP below 90 mmHg despite two correctly dosed IM injections and fluid boluses → start IV epi.*  

> *If pressures hold steady for six hours after the last IM dose with no airway involvement → continue observation.*  

(Visual flowcharts are staples on EM blogs; picture this as a simple decision tree beginning with “Recurrent hypotension?” leading down either side.)

### Quick Step‐by‐Step Algorithm

1️⃣ Identify recurrent hypotension (**SBP <90 mmHg**, MAP <65 mmHg).  
2️⃣ Verify two adequate IM epinephrine doses have been administered *and* give additional fluid bolus(s) as needed (**30 mL/kg crystalloid**, repeat if still <90 mmHg).  
3️⃣ If still hypotensive *or* another episode occurs within ≤45 min → start IV epi drip per preparation protocol above.  
4️⃣ Titrate based on MAP/urine output while monitoring telemetry continuously.  
5️⃣ Once stable for ≥4 h *and* no further episodes → consider tapering infusion over increments of 0·5–1 µg/min every hour until off; keep patient under observation for total #≥6 h after drip cessation because rebound can occur.

## Take‑Home Messages  

- Recurrent hypotension after two appropriate IM epinephrine injections is an indication—not a contravention—to start an IV epinephrine infusion.
- Use weight‑based dosing for IM (**0·01 mg/kg**, max 0·5 mg); avoid relying exclusively on low‑dose auto‐injectors (>70 kg patients often need 0·5 mg).
- Prepare the drip by diluting 1 mg epi into 1000 mL NS → start at 1 µg/min and titrate by small increments while targeting MAP ≥65 mmHg.
- Continuous cardiac telemetry and frequent vitals are mandatory; watch closely for tachyarrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, peripheral vasospasm and metabolic swings.
- In patients with suspected mast cell disorders (elevated baseline tryptase), have a low threshold for intravenous therapy because massive mediator release may outstrip any bolus regimen.
- Corticosteroids have no proven benefit in preventing biphasic/multiphasic reactions and may increase ICU admission rates—use them sparingly.
- After hemodynamic stability is achieved, taper slowly and maintain observation for at least six hours before discharge planning.
