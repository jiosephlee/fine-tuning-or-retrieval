Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology of Anaphylaxis and Biphasic/Multiphasic Reactions  

Anaphylaxis results from an abrupt surge of biologically active mediators released from tissue‑resident effector cells – principally mast cells and basophils – into the circulation. Understanding how these cells become activated, what they release, and why symptoms sometimes recur after an apparent resolution is essential for interpreting the clinical course described in the case of the 60‑year‑old woman who experienced four anaphylactic episodes after a single dose of clarithromycin.

### IgE‑dependent mast cell degranulation cascade  

In the classic pathway, a previously sensitised individual has produced allergen‑specific IgE that binds with high affinity to the FcεRI receptor complex on the surface of mast cells and circulating basophils. When the allergen (in this case a macrolide antibiotic) encounters these IgE‑coated receptors, it cross‑links adjacent FcεRI molecules. This cross‑linking activates the Src family kinase Lyn, which phosphorylates immunoreceptor tyrosine‑based activation motifs (ITAMs) on the FcεRI β and γ chains. Subsequent recruitment and activation of Syk kinase initiates a cascade leading to rapid intracellular calcium influx. Elevated calcium triggers exocytosis of preformed granules that contain histamine, tryptase, chymase, carboxypeptidase A3, heparin, and various cytokines.  

Simultaneously, activated mast cells turn on phospholipase A₂, liberating arachidonic acid from membrane phospholipids. This substrate is metabolised by both cyclo‑oxygenase (COX) enzymes into prostaglandins such as prostaglandin D₂ (PGD₂) and by 5‑lipoxygenase into cysteinyl leukotrienes (LTC₄ → LTD₄ → LTE₄). These newly synthesised lipid mediators appear more slowly than preformed granule contents—typically peaking 30–60 minutes after stimulation—and are responsible for sustained bronchoconstriction, increased vascular permeability, and smooth‑muscle effects that outlast the initial histamine surge.  

Mast cell activation also induces transcriptional programmes resulting in delayed release of cytokines (e.g., IL‑4, IL‑5, IL‑13), chemokines (e.g., CCL2), and growth factors that recruit eosinophils, neutrophils, and additional basophils into the tissue microenvironment. The influx of these secondary effector cells underlies the “late phase” reaction that can manifest several hours after the first wave—precisely the window during which biphasic anaphylaxis may emerge.

### Non‑IgE mechanisms – direct activation by macrolide antibiotics  

Not all anaphylactic reactions require allergen‑specific IgE. Certain drugs can trigger “pseudo‑allergic” degranulation by directly engaging receptors intrinsic to mast cells. The most clinically relevant receptor is Mas‑related G protein‑coupled receptor X2 (MRGPRX2). Unlike FcεRI signaling, MRGPRX2 activation does not depend on immunoglobulins; rather it responds to cationic amphiphilic compounds—including many quinolones, vancomycin, opioids, neuromuscular blocking agents—and several macrolides such as clarithromycin. Binding of clarithromycin—or its metabolites—to MRGPRX2 promotes Gαq/11 signaling that culminates in calcium influx and degranulation through pathways overlapping with those triggered by IgE but bypassing Syk phosphorylation steps.

In practice it is often impossible to distinguish a pure IgE reaction from an MRGPRX2‑mediated episode without specialized testing. In our patient no specific IgE testing was performed because repeat exposure could provoke further severe events—a reasonable decision given her clear clinical presentation after a single dose of a previously untried drug. The rapid onset (≈1 h post‑dose) conforms both with classic IgE kinetics and with direct‐activation kinetics reported for macrolides; therefore both mechanisms remain plausible contributors in this case.

### Major mediators: histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, tryptase  

| Mediator | Source | Peak timing | Primary clinical effects |
|----------|--------|-------------|--------------------------|
| **Histamine** | Preformed granule content | Minutes | Vasodilation → hypotension; ↑vascular permeability → urticaria/angioedema; bronchospasm → dyspnoea |
| **Tryptase** | Preformed granule protease | Minutes–1 h | Marker of mast cell activation; contributes to endothelial leakage & matrix degradation |
| **Cysteinyl leukotrienes** (LTC₄/D₄/E₄) | Synthesised de novo from arachidonic acid | 30–60 min | Potent bronchoconstriction & vascular leakage lasting several hours |
| **Prostaglandin D₂** (PGD₂) | De novo synthesis via COX‐1/COX‐2 | 30–90 min | Vasodilation + platelet‐aggregating activity; contributes to prolonged urticaria |
| **Cytokines/chemokines** (IL‑4/5/13) | Transcriptional up‐regulation | Hours → days | Recruitment & activation of eosinophils & other inflammatory cells → late‐phase tissue injury |

In acute management histamine antagonists (H1/H2 blockers) address only part of the cascade whereas epinephrine acts upstream—reversing vasodilation through α₁ agonism and bronchodilation via β₂ agonism—thereby counteracting all downstream mediators regardless of their source.

### ‘Biphasic’ versus ‘multiphasic’ anaphylaxis – definitions and temporal patterns  

The Resuscitation Council UK defines *biphasic anaphylaxis* as a recurrence of any anaphylactic symptom following complete or near‐complete resolution without further exposure to the inciting antigen. The recurrence typically occurs within 6–12 hours but may appear up to 78 hours later according to isolated reports. When two or more distinct recurrences are documented—each separated by an asymptomatic interval—the term *multiphasic* is applied. In our patient four discrete episodes occurred within a twelve‑hour window: one initial ED presentation followed by three subsequent relapses despite appropriate epinephrine dosing and fluid resuscitation.

The distinction has practical implications because observation periods recommended by guidelines generally target biphasic risk (often 4–6 hours post‐resolution), whereas multiphasic courses may demand longer monitoring or even admission to higher‑acuity settings such as ICU—precisely what was instituted after the second relapse in this case.

### Proposed pathophysiologic explanations for delayed phases  

Multiple complementary mechanisms have been postulated for why anaphylaxis may recur after apparent control:

1️⃣ **Late‑phase mediator synthesis** – After initial degranulation, activated mast cells continue producing leukotrienes and prostaglandins while recruited eosinophils release additional cationic proteins that perpetuate endothelial leakage.^[1] This second wave can manifest clinically once epinephrine levels wane or its β₂ bronchodilatory effect declines (~90 min half‐life).

2️⃣ **Incomplete epinephrine coverage** – Intramuscular epinephrine provides rapid reversal but its systemic concentration falls quickly without repeat dosing when severe hypotension persists or when body mass exceeds standard dosing thresholds.^[2] Suboptimal dosing—as observed when only 0·3 mg auto‑injectors were used in a >100 kg patient—may allow residual mediator activity sufficient for relapse once catecholaminergic tone diminishes.

3️⃣ **Mast cell ‘priming’** – An initial massive release leaves abundant intracellular stores empty while transcriptional programs replenish granules over minutes‑to‑hours.^[3] In patients with a high baseline burden (e.g., systemic mastocytosis), replenishment can be rapid enough that subsequent stimuli—such as circulating complement fragments generated during tissue injury—trigger additional degranulation without any external allergen reexposure.

4️⃣ **Persistent antigen absorption** – For oral agents or slow‐release formulations continued gastrointestinal uptake can sustain low levels of hapten capable of reactivating sensitised FcεRI pathways.^[4] In our scenario this mechanism was unlikely because only one clarithromycin dose had been ingested before presentation.

These concepts explain why even meticulous administration of epinephrine does not guarantee exemption from later episodes—a reality reflected in this patient’s need for multiple intramuscular doses over several hours.

### Incidence range of biphasic reactions in literature  

Published estimates vary dramatically because studies differ in inclusion criteria, definitions used for “biphasic,” observation windows employed, and whether only severe forms are counted:

* Very low estimates (~0·4 %) arise from large emergency department databases where modest symptom recurrence was not captured.^[5]  
* Upper bounds (~23 %) stem from prospective cohorts using intensive monitoring protocols over several days.^[6]  
* Most systematic reviews converge on an intermediate range between **4 % and 6 %**, reflecting clinically significant biphasic episodes requiring rescue medication or additional medical care.^[7]

Multiphasic reactions are even rarer—the literature contains only isolated case series aggregating <1 % of all anaphylaxis presentations—but they remain clinically important because each recurrence necessitates repeat epinephrine administration and extended observation time.

### Systemic mastocytosis – WHO minor criteria relevant to the case  

Systemic mastocytosis is defined by clonal proliferation of neoplastic mast cells infiltrating extracutaneous organs such as bone marrow or gastrointestinal tract.^[8] Diagnosis requires either one major criterion *or* at least **three minor criteria** per WHO classification:

1️⃣ **Major:** Multifocal dense infiltrates (>15 aggregated mast cells) detected histologically in bone marrow or another extracutaneous organ.<br>
2️⃣ **Minor:**  
   - Serum total tryptase persistently >20 ng/mL (§).  
   - Presence of KIT D816V point mutation detectable in peripheral blood or marrow aspirate.<br>
   - Mast cells expressing CD2 and/or CD25 aberrantly.<br>
   - ≥25 % spindle-shaped or atypical morphology among marrow aspirate MCs.<br>
   - Presence of another myeloid neoplasm associated with KIT mutation.<br>

In this patient only two minor criteria were documented: **elevated baseline serum tryptase** (>20 ng/mL measured weeks after recovery) and morphologically atypical MCs noted on limited bone marrow material (“only two WHO minor criteria”). Consequently she did not meet full diagnostic thresholds but exhibited a “probable systemic mastocytosis” phenotype—a state recognized as conferring heightened susceptibility to severe anaphylaxis despite lacking definitive disease confirmation.^[9]

### Impact of increased mast cell burden on severity/repetition of anaphylactic episodes  

A larger pool of tissue MCs translates into two measurable consequences:

* **Greater preformed mediator reserve** – More granules mean that any trigger releases larger absolute quantities of histamine, tryptase, chymase etc., precipitating more profound hypotension and airway compromise initially—as seen when our patient required repeated epinephrine boluses despite fluid resuscitation.
* **Accelerated replenishment** – Neoplastic MCs possess dysregulated KIT signaling which augments survival signaling pathways (PI3K/AKT). This hyperactive state promotes quicker synthesis/recycling of granule contents after depletion.^[10] Accordingly even after successful early treatment many MCs become “reloaded” within minutes-to-hours making them capable of secondary bursts without further antigen exposure—a plausible mechanistic substrate for multiphasic behavior observed here.

Clinically this translates into higher likelihood scores for biphasic/multiphasic courses among patients with systemic mastocytosis or suspected clonal MC disorders—justifying prolonged ICU observation irrespective of rapid symptom resolution after initial therapy.

#### Putting it together for our case

The woman’s reaction began one hour after ingesting clarithromycin—a drug capable both of classic IgE crosslinking (if prior sensitisation existed) *and* direct MRGPRX2-mediated degranulation. Immediate epinephrine reversed life‑threatening hypotension but did not prevent three subsequent recurrences—all managed successfully with additional intramuscular injections due largely to residual MC content still being released over time. Her elevated baseline tryptase together with atypical bone marrow findings suggest a heightened MC burden consistent with probable systemic mastocytosis. This predisposition likely amplified both the intensity of each episode (“deep hypoxaemia” at first peak) and facilitated repeated mediator discharge during later phases when catecholamine support waned—culminating in a classic multiphasic trajectory requiring ICU monitoring beyond standard biphasic observation windows.

**Key take‑aways for learners**

1️⃣ Anaphylaxis can arise from both *IgE-dependent* FcεRI crosslinking *and* *IgE‑independent* drug triggers such as macrolides acting through MRGPRX2.<br>
2️⃣ Immediate symptoms are driven mainly by preformed mediators (histamine/t​ryp ase); delayed phases involve newly synthesised leukotrienes/prostaglandins plus recruited inflammatory cells.<br>
3️⃣ Biphasic reactions involve *one* secondary flare usually within ≤12 h; *multiphasic* denotes ≥two recurrences spaced by asymptomatic intervals—as illustrated by four peaks over twelve hours.<br>
4️⃣ Reported biphasic incidence spans <1 % up to >20 %, most reliably quoted at **≈5 %**, whereas true multiphasic occurrences are far rarer.<br>
5️⃣ Systemic mastocytosis—even when only “probable”—provides a mechanistic substrate for unusually severe or recurrent anaphylaxis due to expanded MC reservoir and accelerated reloading.<br>
6️⃣ Recognising these pathophysiologic nuances guides practical decisions: using appropriately dosed epinephrine (>0·3 mg for adults >50 kg), extending observation beyond traditional 4–6 h windows when high MC burden is suspected, and considering specialist input early when drug-induced pseudoallergic mechanisms are plausible.
