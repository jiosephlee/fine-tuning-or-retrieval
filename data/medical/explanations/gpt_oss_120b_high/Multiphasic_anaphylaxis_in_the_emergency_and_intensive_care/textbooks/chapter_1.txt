Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 1: ## Initial Presentation and Immediate Differential Diagnosis  

The patient was a woman in her early sixties who presented to the emergency department after an acute deterioration that began approximately one hour after ingesting her first oral dose of clarithromycin 500 mg. Her recorded body weight was 104 kg; using a typical adult female height of ≈1·65 m this corresponds to a body‑mass index in the range of 38 kg/m²—a class II obesity. Obesity influences both the pharmacokinetics of vasoactive drugs and the recommended epinephrine dose (0·01 mg kg⁻¹ up to a maximum of 0·5 mg). Hence, even before other data were available the treating team recognized that dosing based solely on fixed‑dose auto‑injectors could result in relative under‑treatment for a patient of this size.

Her past medical history was notable for several chronic conditions that are relevant when interpreting an acute toxicological picture: type‑2 diabetes mellitus (treated with metformin and gliclazide), hypertension (hydrochlorothiazide taken >12 h before arrival), chronic plaque psoriasis, and major depressive disorder (sertraline). She had a documented β‑lactam allergy manifested previously as isolated facial swelling during childhood amoxicillin exposure and had experienced a pronounced local reaction to a wasp sting without systemic features. These observations suggested prior heightened mast‑cell reactivity although she had never before experienced fulminant anaphylaxis.

The medication timeline provided a crucial clue. Five days earlier her primary care physician had prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas; after five days she developed an abdominal rash attributed to clindamycin and the regimen was switched abruptly to clarithromycin—an antibiotic class she had never received before. The temporal relationship between clarithromycin ingestion and symptom onset (≈1 h) strongly suggested drug exposure as the precipitating event rather than delayed reactions such as DRESS, which typically evolve over weeks.

On arrival the patient was markedly ill appearing, agitated, sweating profusely, and complaining of throat “tightness,” pruritus, flushing, and generalized urticaria covering her entire body surface. Vital signs revealed profound circulatory compromise (blood pressure 74/50 mm Hg) together with hypoxaemia (SpO₂ 88 % on room air) and tachypnea (22 breaths·min⁻¹). Physical examination confirmed facial edema without obvious lingual or laryngeal swelling; cardiac auscultation was normal and breath sounds were clear bilaterally—findings consistent with distributive rather than obstructive or cardiogenic pathology at this stage. Point‑of‑care capillary glucose was elevated at 12·7 mmol·L⁻¹ (~229 mg·dL⁻¹), compatible with her known diabetes plus catecholamine‑mediated stress hyperglycaemia seen in severe allergic reactions. A rapid twelve‑lead ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm without ST‑segment deviation or arrhythmia, making acute coronary syndrome less likely at that moment.

The constellation—rapid onset (<1 h), involvement of cutaneous‐mucosal tissue (urticaria, facial edema), respiratory compromise (hypoxaemia), and cardiovascular collapse (hypotension)—met established clinical criteria for anaphylaxis such as those proposed by NIAID/FAAN or WAO: ≥2 organ systems involved OR ≥1 system with hypotension after exposure to a plausible allergen when alternative explanations are absent. The clarity of these bedside signs justified treating the episode as anaphylactic shock even before laboratory confirmation by serum tryptase could be obtained—a principle underscored repeatedly in emergency medicine curricula emphasizing “treat first, test later.”

Nonetheless, emergency physicians must entertain alternative diagnoses that could mimic this picture because therapeutic missteps carry significant risk:

* **Septic shock**—usually associated with a prodrome of infection, fever or leukocytosis, and a more gradual haemodynamic decline than seconds‑to‑minutes after drug exposure. No source of infection was identified on history or examination, body temperature was not reported as elevated, and the abrupt timing after clarithromycin argued against sepsis.
* **Acute coronary syndrome**—may present with hypotension or dyspnoea but classically includes chest discomfort or ischemic ECG changes (ST depressions/elevations) which were absent here.
* **Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms (DRESS)**—characterised by fever, facial edema, mucous‑membrane involvement, lymphadenopathy and marked eosinophilia developing over ≥2 weeks after offending drug exposure—features lacking in this presentation.
* **Anaphylactoid / non‑IgE mediated mast‑cell activation**—clinically indistinguishable from IgE‑mediated anaphylaxis yet still requires identical emergent therapy; thus it remains subsumed under the working diagnosis “anaphylaxis” until further immunologic testing can clarify mechanism.
* **Obstructive causes** such as massive pulmonary embolism could produce hypoxaemia & hypotension yet would be unlikely to generate diffuse urticaria or rapidly progressive facial swelling concurrently.

Given these considerations the most parsimonious explanation remained acute systemic hypersensitivity triggered by clarithromycin either through direct IgE binding or through non‑IgE mast cell degranulation—a scenario reinforced by her prior drug intolerance history despite lack of previous macrolide exposure.

**Immediate priority actions** were therefore aligned with current anaphylaxis protocols:

1. **Epinephrine** administered intramuscularly into the mid‑anterolateral thigh without delay—dose calculated as 0·3–0·5 mg based on her weight (>70 kg) despite the auto‑injector containing only 0·3 mg; acknowledgment of potential under‑dosing informed subsequent readiness for repeat dosing.
2. **Positioning** supine with legs elevated unless airway compromise dictated prone posture or assisted ventilation.
3. **High‑flow supplemental oxygen** initiated immediately via non‑rebreather mask at 15 L·min⁻¹ to correct hypoxaemia while monitoring SpO₂ trends.
4. **Large-bore intravenous access** followed by rapid isotonic crystalloid bolus (≥500 mL normal saline) aimed at counteracting distributive vasodilation.
5. **Adjunct pharmacotherapy** prepared—intravenous antihistamine (clemastine) offered symptomatic relief for cutaneous manifestations; bronchodilators reserved if wheeze developed.
6. **Continuous monitoring** of cardiovascular status—including frequent blood pressure checks—and observation for evolving airway obstruction despite its absence initially.
7. **Documentation** of exact times of symptom onset, epinephrine administration, and vital sign trajectories—to facilitate appropriate timing of serial serum tryptase measurements later per guideline recommendations without delaying lifesaving treatment.

In summary, careful synthesis of demographic data, medication exposures, rapid‐onset multisystem signs, basic investigations (glucose level reassuring against hypoglycaemic mimics; ECG lacking ischemia) allowed clinicians to swiftly converge on a provisional diagnosis of anaphylaxis while systematically excluding plausible alternatives such as septic or cardiogenic shock. This decisive reasoning directed immediate life‐saving interventions according to established emergency protocols—a critical learning point for trainees encountering similar ambiguous yet urgent presentations in real time.
