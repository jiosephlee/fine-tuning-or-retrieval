Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] Summarize the current evidence regarding routine administration of systemic corticosteroids during acute anaphylaxis for prevention of secondary (biphasic) reactions and state what modern guidelines advise.

**Clinical Pearl – Systemic Corticosteroids Do Not Prevent Biphasic Anaphylaxis**

**Bottom line**  
Modern allergology societies now advise *against routine administration of systemic corticosteroids* during the acute treatment of anaphylaxis for the purpose of averting a secondary‑phase (biphasic) reaction. Steroids may still be used when there is a separate indication (e.g., bronchospasm or asthma flare), but they are not a mandatory component of the standard anaphylaxis bundle.

---

### 1. What does the evidence say?

| Study / Review | Design | Key Finding regarding biphasic reactions |
|---|---|---|
| **2017 systematic review** (22 studies, 1 + 21 negative) | Meta‑analysis of observational and interventional data | Only *one* study showed a reduction in biphasic events; 21 showed **no protective effect** of a single dose of IV/PO corticosteroid. |
| **2019 cohort study** (pre‑hospital setting) | Retrospective analysis >3 000 patients | Administration of methylprednisolone or prednisolone **increased** the odds of ICU/ward admission (likely reflecting confounding severity, but no reduction in biphasic episodes). |
| **2025 retrospective ED series** (n ≈ 1 200) | Compared 0.3‑mg vs 0.5‑mg epinephrine; steroid use recorded | Patients who received steroids were **no less likely** to develop a biphasic reaction; time to second episode unchanged (median ≈ 5 h). |

*Physiologic rationale*: Corticosteroids act by altering gene transcription and require **4–6 hours** to blunt mediator release. The majority of biphasic reactions occur within the first **6–12 h**, often before any steroid‑mediated effect can materialize. Thus the drug’s kinetic profile makes it biologically implausible as a primary preventive strategy for early biphasic relapse.

---

### 2. What do current guidelines state?

| Guideline (latest version) | Recommendation on steroids in acute anaphylaxis |
|---|---|
| **World Allergy Organization (WAO) 2020** | “Systemic corticosteroids *may be considered* as an adjunct in severe reactions, but *routine use* for biphasic prevention is *not endorsed*.” |
| **European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) 2022** | “Administer epinephrine first; antihistamines optional; **corticosteroids are optional and should not be given solely to prevent a late phase**.” |
| **American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology / AAAAI practice parameters (2023)** | “There is insufficient evidence to recommend routine systemic corticosteroids for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis.” |
| **UK National Institute for Health & Care Excellence (NICE) guideline NG206 (2021)** | “Do *not* give steroids routinely; use only if there is another clinical indication (e.g., asthma).” |
| **Australian Society of Clinical Immunology & Allergy (ASCIA) 2022** | “Corticosteroids may be used in refractory or protracted reactions but are *not required* for preventing second‐phase events.” |

Across continents the consensus has shifted from “give steroids as part of every anaphylaxis protocol” to “reserve steroids for specific co‑morbidities”.

---

### 3. How does this align with the presented case?

- The patient experienced **four discrete anaphylactic episodes** within 12 h despite receiving a high‑dose course of prednisone (30 mg IV daily × 5 days) plus a single dose of dexamethasone.
- Symptom intensity *gradually waned*, yet this tapering occurred **independently** of steroid administration and is more consistent with natural depletion of circulating mediators rather than pharmacologic suppression.
- The case authors explicitly note that “the role of corticosteroids being brought into question in recent years,” echoing the broader literature that systemic steroids did not avert her multiphasic course.

Thus the real‑world experience mirrors the trial data: **steroid therapy did not prevent recurrent episodes**, underscoring that its routine use is not justified for this purpose.

---

### 4. Practical take‑aways for residents

1. **First‑line therapy = IM epinephrine** (`0.01 mg/kg`, max 0.5 mg). Delay >10 min worsens outcomes.
2. **Adjuncts** – H1 antagonists and H2 blockers improve cutaneous symptoms but have no impact on biphasic risk.
3. **Corticosteroids** – Give *only* when:
   - The patient has comorbid asthma or bronchospasm refractory to bronchodilators,
   - There is a prolonged or refractory reaction where anti‐inflammatory effect may be beneficial,
   - Or local protocol demands it after shared decision‑making.
4. **Observation period** – For adults with moderate–severe initial reactions or need ≥2 epinephrine doses, monitor ≥6–12 h; longer observation if systemic mastocytosis is suspected, as in this case.
5. **Documentation** – Obtain tryptase at ≤1 h, again at 1–4 h, and baseline >24 h later – this aids diagnosis but does not influence acute steroid decisions.

---

### 5. Bottom line summary

- The pre‑existing dogma that steroids prevent biphasic anaphylaxis has been dismantled by systematic reviews and contemporary cohort studies showing *no benefit* and possible harms.
- All major allergy/immunology societies now counsel clinicians **not to give systemic corticosteroids routinely**, reserving them for specific co‑existing conditions.
- In practice, focus on rapid epinephrine delivery and appropriate monitoring; reserve steroids for patients with concurrent asthma exacerbation or documented refractory/protracted anaphylaxis.

*Remember*: “If you’ve already given epinephrine correctly, you’ve done what saves lives—steroids are merely optional garnish.*
