Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] When a patient presents with hypotension, hypoxaemia, generalized urticaria and facial edema consistent with anaphylaxis, what are the immediate pharmacologic interventions recommended by current guidelines and why must each be administered promptly?

**Anaphylaxis – Immediate Pharmacologic Priorities**

When a patient arrives with hypotension, hypoxaemia, generalized urticaria and facial edema, the clinical picture meets the classic definition of anaphylaxis. Current consensus guidelines (World Allergy Organization 2022; AAAAI/ACAAI 2021) dictate **four time‑critical drug interventions** that must be given in the first few minutes:

| Drug | Recommended dose & route | Rationale for prompt delivery |
|------|--------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Epinephrine** | 0.01 mg kg⁻¹ IM (max 0.5 mg). In adults >50 kg most protocols use a single 0.3–0.5 mg auto‑injector into the mid‑anterolateral thigh; repeat every 5–15 min if symptoms persist. | • **α₁‑mediated vasoconstriction** restores systemic vascular resistance and limits capillary leak → rapid rise in blood pressure.<br>• **β₁‑inotropy/chronotropy** augments cardiac output.<br>• **β₂‑bronchodilation** reverses bronchospasm and diminishes airway oedema.<br>• Delays >10 min are strongly associated with increased mortality; thus epinephrine is the *only* life‑saving drug that must not be postponed. |
| **Supplemental Oxygen** | High‑flow O₂ (≥10 L min⁻¹) via non‑rebreather mask or nasal cannula; titrate to SpO₂ > 94 % (or >90 % in COPD). | Hypoxaemia in anaphylaxis derives from airway oedema, bronchospasm and perfusion mismatch. Early high‑flow O₂ buys time while epinephrine restores airway calibre and circulation, preventing tissue hypoxia and cardiac ischemia. |
| **Intravenous Crystalloid Bolus** | 20 mL kg⁻¹ isotonic saline (≈1–1.5 L in a 100‑kg adult) given rapidly; repeat as needed after reassessment of MAP and urine output. | Anaphylactic shock is a distributive shock compounded by massive plasma extravasation from mast‑cell mediators. Fluid bolus expands intravascular volume, counteracts relative hypovolaemia and enhances the pressor effect of epinephrine. |
| **H₁ Antihistamine (optional adjunct)** | 2–4 mg IV clemastine or diphenhydramine every 30–60 min (or equivalent oral dose). May add H₂ blocker (e.g., famotidine 20 mg IV). | Histamine drives cutaneous urticaria, pruritus, facial edema and contributes to bronchial hyperreactivity. Antihistamines have a delayed onset (≈15–30 min) and are therefore *adjuncts* rather than primary therapy—but they improve comfort and may limit residual cutaneous swelling when given early. |

### How the Case Illustrates These Principles

- **Epinephrine:** The patient received an immediate intramuscular injection of 0.3 mg via auto‑injector as per local protocol. Because her weight was 104 kg, guideline experts would favour a 0.5‑mg dose; nevertheless the rapid administration halted the circulatory collapse and allowed her blood pressure to normalize.
- **Oxygen & Fluids:** She was placed on 15 L non‑rebreather O₂ and given a 500‑mL normal saline bolus during initial stabilization—a pragmatic first step that was later supplemented by additional fluid when recurrent hypotension occurred.
- **Antihistamine:** Intravenous clemastine (2 mg) was administered promptly for the extensive urticaria—a textbook adjunct that addressed her skin symptoms while epinephrine tackled the life‑threatening components.
- **Corticosteroids:** Post‑first recurrence she received prednisone/dexamethasone because traditional protocols still list steroids for biphasic prophylaxis. Current evidence, however, shows *no clear benefit* in preventing a second phase and highlights potential harm (hyperglycaemia, immunosuppression). Modern guidelines therefore recommend *against routine steroid use* in the acute phase unless there is a compelling indication (e.g., refractory anaphylaxis awaiting ICU transfer).

### Key Take‑Home Points

1. **Epinephrine first – no exceptions.** Give IM epinephrine immediately; repeat without delay if hemodynamics or airway symptoms do not improve.
2. **Support oxygenation & circulation concurrently.** High‑flow O₂ and rapid crystalloid bolus are essential adjuncts that bridge the gap until epinephrine’s vasoactive effects settle.
3. **Antihistamines are secondary but useful for cutaneous relief**—administer early if IV access is available; do not defer epinephrine waiting for them.
4. **Corticosteroids are no longer routine** for preventing biphasic reactions; reserve them for specific situations (e.g., severe refractory anaphylaxis where an infusion of epinephrine is already underway).

By delivering these agents *in rapid succession*—epinephrine within seconds, oxygen/fluids within minutes, antihistamines within the first few minutes—we interrupt the cascade of mast‑cell mediator release that drives hypotension, hypoxaemia and angio‑edema, thereby converting a potentially fatal event into a manageable emergency, as demonstrated by the successful stabilization of the patient in this multiphasic anaphylaxis case.
