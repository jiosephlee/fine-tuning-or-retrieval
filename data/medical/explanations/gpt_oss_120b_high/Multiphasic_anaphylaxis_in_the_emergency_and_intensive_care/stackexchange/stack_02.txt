Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Management] The patient weighs 104 kg. According to guideline‑based dosing (0.01 mg/kg intramuscularly up to a maximum total dose of 0.5 mg), how much epinephrine should be administered initially, and what are the potential consequences of using a standard adult auto‑injector containing only 0·3 mg in this situation?

**Answer**

- **Weight‑based initial epinephrine dose:**  
  - Dose = 0.01 mg × body weight (kg)  
  - For a patient who weighs **104 kg** → 0.01 mg × 104 = **1.04 mg**  
  - The guideline caps the **total** amount at **0.5 mg** (the maximal single adult IM dose).  
  - **Therefore the first IM injection should be **0.5 mg** delivered into the anterolateral thigh (preferably with a pre‑filled syringe or a 0½‑mg auto‑injector).**

- **What happens if you give only the standard 0·3 mg auto‑injector?**  

| Issue | Why it matters in this patient (and in the case report) |
|-------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| **Underdosing of α‑ and β‑adrenergic stimulation** | A 0·3 mg dose provides ≈30 % less vasoconstriction and bronchodilation than the guideline‑recommended 0·5 mg. In the reported case the first two injections were only 0·3 mg; each gave an adequate transient rise in blood pressure but did not fully reverse hypotension or airway edema, contributing to early relapse. |
| **Need for additional injections** | Because the initial effect is suboptimal, clinicians must repeat doses sooner and more often. In the case report four separate epinephrine administrations were required within the first 12 h—two of them were upgraded to manually drawn‑up **0·5 mg** doses after the inadequate response to the auto‑injectors. |
| **Risk of refractory or biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis** | Inadequate early treatment is a recognized predictor of biphasic reactions. The patient’s multiphasic course (“four anaphylactic episodes”) aligns with this observation; earlier full dosing might have reduced the number or severity of relapses. |
| **Potential for delayed escalation to IV epinephrine** | When IM doses are insufficient clinicians may postpone—or conversely hasten—invasive IV infusion, increasing ICU stay and resource use (the patient required ICU monitoring after the second relapse). |
| **Increased medication‐administration errors** | Switching between a pre‑filled auto‑injector (0·3 mg) and manually prepared syringes (0·5 mg) can create confusion about how many more doses are permissible before reaching the “max total” limit, potentially leading either to withheld treatment or inadvertent overdose later on. |
| **False reassurance** | The presence of an auto‑injector can give clinicians a sense that “the drug has been given,” even when the dose is subtherapeutic for a heavy adult, delaying recognition that further therapy is needed. |

**Take‑home points**

1. **Calculate rather than rely on a one‑size‑fits‑all device:** For any adult >50 kg give **0·5 mg IM** (or draw up that amount from a vial).  
2. **If only a 0·3‑mg device is available**, give it promptly *and* plan for immediate reassessment—repeat a second full‑dose (another 0·3 mg) if vital signs do not normalize within 2–5 minutes, bearing in mind that two such injections already equal the guideline cap and may still be inadequate for patients >100 kg.  
3. **Document weight‑based dosing** and consider having a separate stock of prefilled syringes or newer auto‑injectors that deliver the full 0·5 mg for heavier patients—this reduces reliance on repeated low‐dose devices and may lessen the incidence of multiphasic reactions as illustrated by this case.

In short, for a 104‑kg patient the guideline‐directed initial epinephrine dose is **0·5 mg IM**, and using only a standard 0·3 mg auto‑injector can leave the reaction partially treated, increase the probability of relapse (as seen in this multiphasic anaphylaxis case), necessitate additional dosing, and prolong intensive monitoring.*
