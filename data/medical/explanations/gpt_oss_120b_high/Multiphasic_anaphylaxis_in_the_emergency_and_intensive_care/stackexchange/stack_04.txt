Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Differential Diagnosis] A patient experiences recurrent hypotension and cutaneous symptoms several hours after successful treatment of the first episode. How would you distinguish a true biphasic recurrence from refractory or persistent anaphylaxis in terms of timing, clinical pattern and possible ongoing allergen absorption?

**Distinguishing a true biphasic anaphylactic recurrence from refractory or persistent anaphylaxis**

| Feature | True biphasic recurrence | Refractory / persistent anaphylaxis |
|--------|--------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Temporal relationship** | A *symptom‑free interval* of ≥ 30 min (often 2–6 h, but can be up to 24 h) after complete clinical resolution of the first episode. In the case report the patient was normotensive and appeared “back to baseline” for several hours before the first relapse at ≈ 5 h. | Ongoing or rapidly recurrent symptoms without a clear lull; additional epinephrine doses are required within minutes‑to‑tens of minutes because the initial response is incomplete or wanes quickly. |
| **Clinical pattern** | Each episode mirrors the original full‐blown anaphylaxis (hypotension, urticaria, respiratory distress) but often with *decreasing intensity* (as seen in the fourth episode of the report). The patient’s cutaneous rash re‑appeared with pruritus, but hypotension was less profound each time. | Persistent circulatory collapse or airway compromise despite appropriate epinephrine dosing; signs may be constant (e.g., sustained hypotension) rather than re‑emerging after a calm period. |
| **Response to therapy** | A single dose of intramuscular epinephrine restores hemodynamics; relapse occurs *after* the drug’s effect has worn off and no further epinephrine is needed until the next wave. | Repeated epinephrine doses are required in rapid succession because the first dose fails to terminate mediator release or because new mediators continue to be generated (e.g., due to ongoing mast‑cell activation). |
| **Allergen exposure status** | No further exposure to the trigger; the original allergen has been cleared (clarithromycin was given as a single 500 mg dose and discontinued). Relapse is therefore “biphasic” rather than “second exposure.” | Ongoing absorption of the trigger (e.g., residual drug in the gut, depot formulations, large fatty meals) or inadvertent re‑exposure can sustain mediator release—this mimics persistence rather than a true biphasic pattern. |
| **Laboratory clue** | Serial serum tryptase shows **two discrete peaks** separated by a baseline level (the case report documented a peak during the third episode and a persistent elevated baseline thereafter). | Tryptase remains elevated without returning toward baseline between doses, reflecting continuous mast‑cell degranulation. |

### Practical bedside approach

1. **Document the symptom‐free window** – In a true biphasic reaction you should be able to state that “the patient was asymptomatic for X hours.” In our patient the interval between the first dose of epinephrine and the second crisis was > 4 h, satisfying most definitions (Resuscitation Council UK).

2. **Re‑assess hemodynamics promptly after each epinephrine dose** – If blood pressure normalises quickly and remains stable for a reasonable period (> 30 min), treat it as resolution rather than refractory disease.

3. **Consider lingering antigen** – Oral clarithromycin is rapidly absorbed and has a half‑life ≈ 5 h; however, if the drug were given in a sustained‑release formulation or if large amounts of food were ingested simultaneously, delayed gastric emptying could prolong exposure. In such scenarios you would expect *gradual* deterioration rather than an abrupt second shock after an interval.

4. **Serial tryptase** – Draw samples as soon as possible after each episode (0–1 h) and repeat 1–2 h later and again ≥ 24 h after complete resolution. Separate peaks support biphasic disease; a single plateau suggests refractory activation.

5. **Look for modifiers** – Underlying mast‑cell disorders (e.g., systemic mastocytosis) increase both severity and propensity for multiphasic courses, as illustrated by the elevated baseline tryptase in this case.

### Take‑home points

- **True biphasic anaphylaxis = complete symptom resolution → symptom‑free interval → second acute phase without new allergen exposure.**
- **Refractory/persistent anaphylaxis = continuous or rapidly recurring symptoms with insufficient response to epinephrine, often due to ongoing antigen absorption or inadequate initial dosing.**
- In practice, *timing* (hour‑long asymptomatic lull), *pattern* (re‑emergence of full syndrome often with attenuated severity), and *absence of ongoing exposure* are the hallmarks that separate a genuine biphasic recurrence from refractory disease.
- The presented case exemplifies these principles: four distinct episodes separated by hours of stability, decreasing intensity with each wave, and no further clarithromycin intake—classic biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis rather than refractory shock.

Recognising this distinction directs appropriate observation periods (≥ 12 h for high‑risk patients), avoids unnecessary escalation to continuous intravenous epinephrine when not indicated, and informs counseling about future trigger avoidance and auto‑injector use.
