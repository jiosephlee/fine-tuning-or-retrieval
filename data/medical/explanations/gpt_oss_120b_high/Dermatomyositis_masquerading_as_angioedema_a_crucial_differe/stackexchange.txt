Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Morning Report] A previously healthy man aged approximately 45 years presents after three weeks of progressively worsening non‑tender cervical edema involving the strap muscles demonstrated on CT scan. He reports mild proximal hip flexor weakness (strength 4/5), has a modestly elevated CRP (~55 mg/L), a normal white blood cell count, is afebrile and denies recent ACE‑inhibitor use or other medication exposure known to precipitate angioedema. Complement studies (C3/C4/C1‑esterase inhibitor activity) are normal. Which single initial laboratory test would best help distinguish an infectious deep‑neck space process from an inflammatory myopathy?

**Answer – Serum creatine kinase (CK) concentration**

In a patient with sub‑acute, non‑tender cervical edema the single most useful first laboratory test to separate an infectious deep‑neck space process from an inflammatory myopathy is a quantitative **serum CK level**.

**Why CK?**  

* **Pathophysiology** – Deep‑neck infections (e.g., Ludwig’s angina, retropharyngeal abscess) involve soft‑tissue cellulitis or pus formation but spare skeletal muscle fibers. Consequently, CK—an intracellular enzyme released when myocytes lyse—is usually normal or only marginally raised.  
* **Inflammatory myopathy** – Dermatomyositis and other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies cause immune‑mediated necrosis of striated muscle throughout the neck strap muscles and proximal limbs. This produces a marked release of CK into the bloodstream, often >10–20 × the upper limit of normal.  

In the presented case, after exclusion of infection and angioedema the team ordered a CK measurement; it returned **16 184 U/L**, clinching the diagnosis of dermatomyositis and prompting targeted immunotherapy. A normal or only mildly elevated CK would have kept infection higher on the differential and steered work‑up toward cultures or surgical drainage.

**Practical points for residents**

| Clinical scenario | Expected CK |
|-------------------|-------------|
| Deep neck space cellulitis/abscess | Normal–mildly elevated (<2× ULN) |
| Idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (e.g., dermatomyositis) | Markedly elevated (>5–10× ULN) |

A rapid CK result can be obtained within hours in most hospitals and does not require special handling, making it an ideal initial screen when the presentation could represent either pathology. If CK is markedly raised, proceed with myositis‐specific antibody panels, MRI of involved muscles, or muscle biopsy as illustrated in this case; if CK is low and infection remains plausible, obtain contrast‑enhanced CT/MRI of the neck and consider empirical antibiotics or surgical consultation.

**Bottom line:** In a patient with unexplained cervical “angioedema,” a **single serum CK measurement** provides rapid discriminative power between deep‑neck infection and an underlying inflammatory myopathy such as dermatomyositis.

### [Differential Diagnosis] A patient presents with cervical soft tissue swelling that clinically resembles angioedema but does not respond to antihistamines or C1‑esterase inhibitor replacement therapy; C4 levels are normal while serum creatine kinase is markedly elevated together with proximal muscle weakness. List three bedside clinical or laboratory findings that favor pseudo‑angioedema due to dermatomyositis over classic histamine‑mediated or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema.

**Pseudo‑angioedema from dermatomyositis – bedside clues that tip the balance away from classic histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema**

In the described patient the neck swelling persisted despite antihistamines and C1‑esterase inhibitor replacement, C4 was normal, and a striking muscular picture emerged. Three readily obtainable findings should raise suspicion for dermatomyositis‐related “angioedema” rather than true histaminergic or bradykinin‐driven edema:

1. **Symmetric proximal muscle weakness (often with dysphagia).**  
   - *What you see at the bedside*: reduced strength in hip flexors, shoulder girdle or neck flexors (the case had 4/5 hip flexor power) and difficulty swallowing despite an otherwise intact airway.  
   - *Why it matters*: Histamine‑mediated urticarial angioedema does not cause objective motor deficits; bradykinin‑driven hereditary/acquired angioedema spares skeletal muscle. Weakness points to an inflammatory myopathy as the source of tissue swelling.

2. **Marked elevation of serum creatine kinase (CK) – often >5 000 U/L – accompanied by parallel rises in AST/ALT/LDH.**  
   - *What you order at the bedside*: a rapid CK panel (results usually back within an hour in most hospitals). In this patient CK peaked at 16 184 U/L, far beyond levels seen in isolated angioedema.  
   - *Why it matters*: CK is a sensitive marker of active myofiber injury; it is essentially normal in both histamine‑mediated and bradykinin‑mediated angioedema where vascular leakage—not muscle necrosis—is the pathophysiology.

3. **Dermatologic / nailfold stigmata characteristic of dermatomyositis** (even when classic Gottron papules are absent).  
   - *What you look for*: peri‑ungual erythema or telangiectasia, nailfold capillary microhemorrhages on capillaroscopy, heliotrope hue over the eyelids or a “holster”/shawl sign on the trunk/shoulders. In the reported case subtle nailfold microhemorrhages and peri‑ungual erythema were present despite the absence of Gottron lesions.  
   - *Why it matters*: Angioedema does not produce these cutaneous microvascular changes; their presence signals an underlying inflammatory skin–muscle disease. When combined with an elevated CK and proximal weakness they form a diagnostic triad for pseudo‑angioedema caused by dermatomyositis.

*Putting it together*: In a patient with cervical soft‑tissue swelling that fails to improve after antihistamine or C1‑INH therapy, normal complement levels, **proximal muscle weakness**, **very high CK**, and **dermatomyositis‐type skin/nailfold changes** should prompt early consideration of inflammatory myopathy–related edema—often termed “pseudo‑angioedema”—and expedite appropriate serologic testing (e.g., myositis‐specific antibodies such as anti‑SAE1) and definitive management with immunosuppression rather than continued anti‑angioedema measures.

### [Pathophysiology] Anti–small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (anti‑SAE1) antibodies are strongly associated with a subset of dermatomyositis patients. Describe how these antibodies interfere with the SUMOylation pathway and how this disruption contributes mechanistically to the characteristic perifascicular microvascular injury seen in skin and muscle biopsies.

**Pathophysiology – How anti‑SAE 1 antibodies disrupt SUMOylation and provoke perifascicular microvascular injury**

The small‑ubiquitin‑like modifier–activating enzyme (SAE) is a heterodimer composed of SAE 1 (the regulatory subunit) and SAE 2/Uba2 (the catalytic subunit).  Together they constitute the E1 enzyme that initiates **SUMOylation**:

1. **Activation** – SAE (E1) adenylates the C‑terminus of a SUMO peptide and forms a thioester bond with its catalytic cysteine.
2. **Transfer** – The activated SUMO is transferred to the sole E2 enzyme, Ubc9.
3. **Conjugation** – Ubc9 together with one or more PIAS E3 ligases covalently attaches SUMO to lysine residues on target proteins, thereby altering their stability, subcellular localisation or transcriptional activity.

In normal skeletal‑muscle capillary endothelium SUMOylation has three critical immunoregulatory roles:

* **Repression of type I interferon (IFN) signalling** – SUMO‑modified IRF7/IκBα dampen IFN‐β transcription.
* **Maintenance of complement‐regulatory surface proteins** – e.g., CD55/CD59 are stabilised by SUMOylated membrane scaffolds.
* **Preservation of endothelial survival pathways** – Akt and eNOS activities are facilitated by SUMOylated adaptor proteins, protecting endothelial cells from complement‐mediated lysis.

---

### 1.  Anti‑SAE 1 antibodies blunt the E1 step

Autoantibodies directed at SAE 1 bind the regulatory subunit with high affinity and sterically hinder formation of the functional heterodimer.  In vitro studies using patient sera have shown:

* **Reduced thioester formation between SUMO and SAE 2**, indicating a block at the activation step.
* **Global hypSUMOylation** measurable by loss of high‑molecular‑weight SUMO conjugates in cultured myoblasts exposed to anti‑SAE 1 IgG.

Thus the presence of anti‑SAE 1 antibodies—demonstrated in our patient by a Euroline signal intensity 173—creates a functional **SUMOylation deficiency** within muscle fibers and their adjacent microvasculature.

---

### 2.  Consequences for endothelial cells

| Normal SUMOylation | Effect of anti‑SAE 1–mediated loss |
|--------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Repression of IFN pathways** (SUMO‐IRF7/IκBα) | Unchecked IRF7/NF‑κB activity → robust transcription of IFN‐stimulated genes (MXA, ISG15, CXCL10). These chemokines recruit plasmacytoid dendritic cells and CD68⁺ macrophages into perimysium—a feature noted as “scattered perimysial inflammation” on biopsy. |
| **Stabilisation of CD55/CD59** | Surface loss → heightened susceptibility of capillary endothelium to classical complement activation. |
| **Protection against apoptosis** (SUMO‐Akt/eNOS) | Endothelial cells become prone to calcium influx and mitochondrial injury following C5b‑9 insertion. |

Resultantly **capillary endothelium in dermatomyositis becomes a “perfect target” for the classic complement‐mediated microangiopathy** that defines the disease:

* Deposition of the terminal membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) on endomysial capillaries – exactly what was observed in our patient’s thigh biopsy (“C5b‑9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries”).  
* Endothelial swelling and necrosis → focal loss of perfusion (“perifascicular” because these capillaries sit at the most distal part of each fascicle’s vascular tree where oxygen tension is already lowest).  
* Ischemic stress triggers myofibre necrosis, vacuolation and regeneration in a characteristic **perifascicular distribution**, which was also described in the histology (“foci…in a perifascicular distribution”).

---

### 3.  Linking microvascular injury to clinical edema

Endothelial dysfunction not only allows MAC insertion but also increases vascular permeability via disorganisation of tight junctions—an effect amplified when CD55/CD59 are lost.  The net result is localized interstitial fluid accumulation (“pseudo‑angioedema”) that manifested as diffuse neck swelling in this case despite normal histamine/bradykinin pathways.

---

### Bottom line for the bedside clinician

*Anti‑SAE 1 antibodies act as functional inhibitors of the SUMO E 1 activating enzyme.*  
By curtailing global protein SUMOylation they unleash innate immune signalling (type I IFN/NF‑κB), diminish complement regulation on endothelial surfaces, and impair survival pathways—all converging on **perifascicular capillary injury** marked by C5b‑9 deposition.  The ensuing ischemia produces the classic perifascicular necrosis/regeneration seen on muscle biopsy and contributes clinically to tissue edema that can masquerade as angioedema, as illustrated by this patient’s cervical swelling.  

Understanding this mechanistic chain helps us recognise why patients with anti‑SAE 1 dermatomyositis often present with prominent cutaneous/muscular signs *and* unusual soft‐tissue oedema, prompting early laboratory screening for CK elevation and myositis‐specific autoantibodies when atypical “angioedema” is encountered.

### [Clinical Pearls] During your bedside examination you note periungual erythema with capillary loop hemorrhages, subtle facial erythema over the nasolabial folds (“holster sign”), and a faint erythematous rash over the lateral thigh while assessing a patient whose chief complaint is cervical swelling. How do these cutaneous findings assist in recognizing an underlying inflammatory myopathy such as dermatomyositus before serologic confirmation?

**Clinical Pearl – Skin clues can unmask hidden dermatomyositis**

When a patient presents with an atypical “angio‑edema”‑like neck swelling, the bedside skin exam often provides the first decisive clue that the process is immune‑mediated rather than histamine‑ or bradykinin‑driven. In the case reported by Chan et al., three subtle but characteristic findings tipped the clinicians toward inflammatory myopathy before any laboratory result was available:

1. **Periungual erythema with capillary loop hemorrhages** – A nailfold capillaroscopy performed at presentation showed peri‑ungual erythema and tiny avascular hemorrhagic areas. In dermatomyositis (DM), complement‑mediated injury of perifascicular capillaries produces *telangiectasia* and microhemorrhage that are visible at the nailfold long before overt muscle weakness appears. These changes are uncommon in isolated angio‑edema or infection and therefore serve as an early “vascular fingerprint” of DM.

2. **Facial erythema over the nasolabial folds (“holster sign”)** – The authors describe a faint violaceous discoloration across the nasolabial folds—a variant of the classic “V‑neck” or “shawl” distribution that follows sun‑exposed skin. This low‑grade dermatitis is often overlooked because it lacks the dramatic heliotrope hue of a true facial rash, yet its location (mid‑face) and violaceous tint are highly suggestive of a connective‑tissue disease rather than allergic edema.

3. **A discrete lateral‑thigh erythematous patch** – Even a solitary patch outside classic Gottron’s sites can be part of DM’s “mechanic’s hand” spectrum—an inflammatory eruption following a V‑shaped pattern on the trunk or proximal limbs (“shawl/holster” sign). In this patient it manifested as a faint violet patch on the right lateral thigh—again a clue that skin inflammation rather than simple swelling was present.

**Why these signs matter before serology**

- **Pattern recognition beats assumption:** Neck swelling without fever or infectious source is atypical for Ludwig’s angina; lack of medication exposure makes drug‑induced angioedema unlikely. The coexistence of microvascular nailfold changes and a non‑pruritic facial/limb rash argues for an autoimmune vasculopathy.
  
- **Prompt targeted testing:** Recognizing these dermatologic clues prompted an immediate creatine kinase measurement (which returned >16 000 U/L) and a myositis antibody panel that later identified anti‑SAE1 antibodies—a step that would have been delayed if the workup had remained anchored on idiopathic angioedema.
  
- **Early therapeutic window:** Dermatomyositis can progress rapidly to severe muscle involvement (dysphagia, respiratory compromise) as was seen in this patient’s neck‐muscle edema and aspiration pneumonitis. Initiating high‑dose steroids within days—guided by the skin findings—limited permanent functional loss.
  
- **Differential narrowing:** Nailfold telangiectasia is not seen in pure histaminergic or bradykinin‐mediated angioedema, nor in bacterial deep neck infections where erythema is typically warm, tender, and surrounded by cellulitic borders. Its presence narrows the differential toward connective‐tissue disorders such as DM or systemic sclerosis.

**Take‑home points for the ward round**

1. **Never skip the nailfold exam** – Simple inspection or bedside capillaroscopy can reveal periungual telangiectasias or microhemorrhages that are pathognomonic for DM.
2. **Look for subtle “V‑neck/holster” discoloration** – A faint violaceous hue over nasolabial folds or along anterior shoulder lines is more than cosmetic—it is an early dermatologic signature.
3. **Treat any odd rash on proximal limbs as a potential DM clue**, even if classic Gottron papules are absent.
4. **If these signs coexist with unexplained soft‑tissue swelling**, order CK immediately and refer for myositis serology rather than assuming idiopathic angioedema.
5. **Document and follow skin changes** – They often parallel disease activity and can guide tapering decisions once muscle enzymes normalize.

In summary, periungual microvascular injury, holster‐sign facial erythema, and even a single limb rash constitute a triad that should raise suspicion for dermatomyositis in patients with atypical neck edema. Recognizing this pattern allows clinicians to bypass a protracted infectious/allergic workup, obtain definitive laboratory confirmation promptly, and start immunosuppressive therapy before irreversible muscle damage occurs—exactly as illustrated by Chan et al.’s patient who achieved remission after early diagnosis guided by these subtle cutaneous clues.

### [Management] "High dose" intravenous methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone taper was coupled with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab in this patient, while methotrexate was deliberately avoided because of emerging interstitial lung changes. Explain the rationale for selecting this particular combination regimen over conventional methotrexate‑based steroid sparing therapy.

**Teaching Point – Why “pulse‑steroids + IVIG + mycophenolate + rituximab” was chosen instead of the more familiar methotrexate‑based steroid‑sparing regimen**

The patient had **moderate‑to‑severe anti‑SAE1–positive dermatomyositis** manifested by:

* a rapidly progressive cervical oedema that threatened the airway,
* marked proximal myopathy and dysphagia,
* early interstitial lung changes on pulmonary function testing (restrictive pattern) and chest CT,
* markedly elevated CK (>16 000 U/L).

These features dictate **aggressive early control** of inflammation while simultaneously protecting the lungs from further injury. The therapeutic choices therefore reflect three overarching goals:

1️⃣ **Immediate suppression of life‑threatening inflammation**  
2️⃣ **Rapid improvement of muscle strength/dysphagia**  
3️⃣ **Long‑term steroid sparing without aggravating underlying lung disease**

---

### 1. High‑dose methylprednisolone “pulse” → oral prednisone taper

* **Why pulse?** The neck swelling represented active inflammatory oedema of the strap muscles and posterior pharyngeal wall—essentially an “airway myositis.” Intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g × 3 days delivers plasma concentrations that outpace cytokine production (IL‑6, TNF‑α) and rapidly stabilise capillary leak. This approach is endorsed by IMACS for severe myositis with organ involvement.  
* **Why taper?** After the pulse, a moderate prednisone dose (60 mg daily) provides continuation of glucocorticoid suppression while allowing stepwise reduction to minimise long‑term adverse effects.

---

### 2. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)

* **Mechanistic fit:** IVIG blocks Fcγ receptors, neutralises pathogenic autoantibodies, dampens complement activation (C5b‑9 deposition noted on biopsy), and modulates cytokine networks—all relevant to a disease driven by anti‑SAE1 antibodies.  
* **Clinical advantage:** In severe dermatomyositis with dysphagia or rapid tissue swelling, IVIG yields functional improvement within days—faster than most conventional DMARDs. The case report documents a marked reduction in cervical oedema after the first five‑day course.

---

### 3. Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)

* **Safety profile in lung disease:** Methotrexate carries a well‑recognised risk of hypersensitivity pneumonitis and can exacerbate pre‑existing ILD—even when radiographic fibrosis is absent but pulmonary function is already abnormal, as was true here. MMF lacks pulmonary toxicity and has demonstrated efficacy in connective‑tissue‑disease–associated ILD (RCTs in systemic sclerosis‐ILD show stabilization/reversal).  
* **Efficacy in dermatomyositis:** Observational cohorts consistently show CK reduction and strength gains with MMF as a first‑line steroid‐sparing agent. The patient’s preference for a twice‑daily oral regimen also favored MMF over agents requiring intensive monitoring (e.g., azathioprine).  

---

### 4. Rituximab

* **Rationale for B‑cell depletion:** Anti‑SAE1 antibodies are produced by CD20⁺ B cells; depleting this compartment directly reduces autoantibody titers. Rituximab has become standard for refractory or severe idiopathic inflammatory myopathies after the RIM trial demonstrated modest but clinically meaningful improvements when added to background therapy.  
* **Synergy with other agents:** While steroids blunt acute inflammation and IVIG provides immediate immunomodulation, rituximab offers a longer‑acting “reset” of humoral immunity that sustains remission after steroids are tapered off—a key consideration given the need for eventual steroid discontinuation at 7 months.

---

### Why NOT Methotrexate?

| Feature | Methotrexate | Mycophenolate & Rituximab |
|--------|--------------|---------------------------|
| Pulmonary toxicity | ↑ risk of MTX pneumonitis & fibrosis → unacceptable in emerging ILD | No known pulmonary toxicity; actually used for CTD‑ILD |
| Speed of action | Takes ≥6–8 weeks for full effect → insufficient for airway compromise | IVIG gives rapid effect; rituximab adds durable B‑cell suppression |
| Liver safety | Already elevated transaminases from active myositis (+ potential MTX hepatotoxicity) | MMF has a more benign hepatic profile at therapeutic doses |
| Dosing convenience | Weekly oral/IM dosing → adherence issues when swallowing impaired | Twice daily oral MMF fits NG tube administration during dysphagia |

Because the patient already displayed interstitial changes on PFTs/CT, adding MTX risked precipitating an acute pneumonitis that could compound respiratory compromise—a scenario best avoided when alternative agents are available.

---

### Putting It Together – A “Hit‑Hard–Hit–Fast” Strategy

1. **Pulse steroids** → blunt overwhelming inflammation immediately.  
2. **IVIG** → provides near‑instant functional recovery of muscle and pharyngeal function while neutralising circulating autoantibodies.  
3. **MMF** → maintains immunosuppression without jeopardising lung health; serves as the backbone steroid‑sparing agent during tapering.  
4. **Rituximab** → ensures long‐term control by eradicating the source of pathogenic antibodies, allowing eventual withdrawal of glucocorticoids.

The resulting regimen achieved:
* Rapid resolution of neck swelling,
* Normalisation of CK within weeks,
* Full recovery of proximal strength,
* Safe discontinuation of prednisone by month 7,
* No progression of interstitial lung disease on follow‐up imaging/PFTs.

---

#### Take‑Home Messages

- In dermatomyositis with **severe organ involvement** (airway edema, dysphagia) and **early lung pathology**, avoid methotrexate because its pulmonary toxicity outweighs its convenience.
- Combine **high‐dose steroids + IVIG** for swift control of life‐threatening inflammation.
- Use **mycophenolate** as a frontline steroid‐sparing agent when lungs are involved—its safety profile aligns with connective‐tissue disease–associated ILD.
- Add **rituximab** early when autoantibody‐driven disease (e.g., anti‑SAE1) is present or when rapid steroid tapering is desired.
- The goal is not just “more drugs,” but a **targeted multi‑modal approach** that balances efficacy against organ‐specific risk—precisely what was accomplished in this case rather than relying on traditional methotrexate alone.

### ["Management"] "Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole" was initiated for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis but had to be stopped after the patient developed hemolytic anemia despite a negative G6PD screen. What alternative prophylactic regimens can be employed for PCP prevention in patients receiving high‑dose glucocorticoids together with additional immunosuppressants who cannot tolerate TMP–SMX?

**Alternative PCP Prophylaxis When TMP‑SMX Cannot Be Used**

In patients receiving high‑dose glucocorticoids (> 20 mg prednisone equivalent daily) together with another immunosuppressant (e.g., mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab), guideline‐directed Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP) prophylaxis is mandatory. Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP–SMX) remains the gold‑standard because it provides the best protection against both pulmonary and extrapulmonary disease and also covers *Toxoplasma* and *Nocardia*. However, as in our patient who developed hemolytic anemia despite a negative G6PD screen, intolerance or contraindication may force a change in regimen. The following agents are accepted second‑line alternatives:

| Agent | Typical Adult Dose | Advantages | Limitations / Monitoring |
|-------|-------------------|------------|---------------------------|
| **Aerosolised (nebulised) pentamidine** | 300 mg via jet nebuliser **once monthly** (or 150 mg weekly in some protocols) | • Good pulmonary concentrations <br>• No systemic hematologic toxicity <br>• Useful when oral agents are poorly tolerated | • Does **not** prevent extrapulmonary PCP <br>• No coverage for *Nocardia*, *Toxoplasma* <br>• Requires access to nebuliser; bronchospasm rare |
| **Oral dapsone** | 100 mg once daily (or 50 mg BID) | • Inexpensive <br>• Effective when G6PD activity is normal | • Must **screen** for G6PD deficiency → risk of severe hemolysis/methemoglobinemia <br>• Monitor CBC weekly for hemolysis & methemoglobin; avoid if prior sulfa‑related hemolysis |
| **Atovaquone** | 1500 mg PO **once daily** with a high‑fat meal (or 750 mg BID) | • Well tolerated gastrointestinally <br>• No hematologic toxicity | • Requires fatty meal for absorption → adherence issue <br>• More costly; slightly less efficacious than TMP‑SMX <br>• Monitor hepatic function |
| **Clindamycin + Primaquine** *(rarely used)* | Clindamycin 600 mg PO QID + Primaquine 15 mg PO daily | • Alternative when both sulfa drugs & dapsone are prohibited | • Primaquine → absolute contraindication in G6PD deficiency <br>• Frequent GI upset; drug interactions |

### How the Case Informs Our Choice
The described patient tolerated **nebulised pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks** without recurrence of anemia. This outcome underscores that inhaled pentamidine is a viable fallback when TMP‑SMX provokes hematologic toxicity—even in the absence of documented G6PD deficiency. It also reminds us that hemolysis can be an idiosyncratic reaction to sulfonamides and does not preclude use of other sulfa‑containing agents such as dapsone unless a true allergy is established.

### Practical Decision Algorithm

1. **First line:** TMP‑SMX DS (double strength) 1 tablet PO daily → or three times weekly if tolerability allows.
2. **If intolerance/allergy:**  
   - **Check G6PD:**  
     - Normal → try **dapsone** 100 mg daily, monitor CBC & methemoglobin.  
     - Deficient → avoid dapsone & primaquine.
3. **If oral agents unsuitable (e.g., GI malabsorption, poor adherence):**  
   - Initiate **aerosolised pentamidine** 300 mg monthly via nebuliser.  
   - Counsel that protection is limited to pulmonary disease only.
4. **If both oral agents are contraindicated and inhaled therapy impractical:**  
   - Consider **atovaquone** 1500 mg daily with fatty meals; ensure hepatic function monitoring.
5. **Rarely**, when all above are unavailable or ineffective, use **clindamycin + primaquine** after confirming G6PD sufficiency.

### Duration of Prophylaxis
Continue the chosen regimen while the patient remains on ≥ 20 mg prednisone equivalent daily *and* any additional immunosuppressant, typically until steroids have been tapered below this threshold for at least one month.

### Take‑Home Points
- **TMP‑SMX failure → move directly to nebulised pentamidine** when rapid pulmonary protection is needed and oral tolerance is doubtful – exactly what was done in our dermatomyositis case.
- When systemic coverage is desired (e.g., concern for extrapulmonary disease), prefer **dapsone** or **atovaquone**, after appropriate laboratory screening.
- Always verify G6PD status before dapsone or primaquine, monitor blood counts for dapsone‑related hemolysis/methemoglobinemia, and ensure adherence to dosing instructions for atovaquone.

These alternatives allow us to maintain robust PCP prophylaxis even in complex immunosuppressed patients who cannot tolerate TMP‑SMX.
