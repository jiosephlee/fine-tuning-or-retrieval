Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Neck Swelling Without Infection: Recognizing Pseudo‑Angioedema as a Manifestation of Dermatomyositis  

## Why this case caught our eye  
A healthy man in his 40s walked into the emergency department with a three‑week history of progressive neck swelling that looked exactly like Ludwig’s angina on CT. The ENT team cleared him of deep‑neck infection and the immunology service was consulted for “idiopathic angioedema.” What made the story twist was the discovery of a CK level >16 000 U/L and subtle nailfold changes—clues that pointed straight toward inflammatory myopathy rather than histamine or bradykinin‐mediated angioedema. In short, dermatomyositis was masquerading as an ENT emergency.  

## The vignette in brief  
The patient presented with diffuse non‑tender neck edema extending into the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space on contrast‐enhanced CT. He was afebrile, normoxic and lacked airway compromise, yet his CRP was modestly raised (≈56 mg/L). Physical exam revealed peri‑ungual microhaemorrhages on capillaroscopy, a faint erythematous rash over the right lateral thigh (“holster sign”), facial erythema over the nasolabial folds and mild hip flexor weakness (4/5). Laboratory workup showed CK = 16 184 U/L, elevated AST/ALT/LDH, ANA 1:1280 speckled pattern and a strongly positive anti‑SAE1 antibody (>170 IU). Complement levels and C1 inhibitor function were normal—making hereditary or acquired angioedema unlikely. MRI of the thighs demonstrated active myositis in multiple hip adductors and obturator muscles, while FDG‑PET highlighted diffuse hypermetabolism throughout head/neck musculature consistent with inflammatory edema rather than infection. Muscle biopsy confirmed perifascicular necrosis with C5b‑9 deposition—a hallmark of dermatomyositis.  

He received three days of intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g/day followed by an oral prednisone taper, IVIG (2 g/kg split over five days then monthly), mycophenolate mofetil 1 g BID and two doses of rituximab 1000 mg spaced two weeks apart. Dysphagia required nasogastric feeding for two months but resolved by three months post‑discharge after his cervical swelling fully subsided and CK normalized (<40 U/L).  

## Neck swelling: common versus uncommon culprits  
When faced with a “tight” neck we instinctively think infection (Ludwig’s angina), allergic angioedema or vascular obstruction (SVC syndrome). Less often considered are autoimmune processes that generate non‑histaminergic edema—dermatomyositis being a prime example despite its rarity (<10/million/year). In large series only ~6% of dermatomyositis patients develop overt facial or cervical swelling (“pseudo‑angioedema”), making it an easy pitfall if you’re only looking for pus or airway obstruction.  

## Red flags that tilt you away from infection or classic angioedema  

* **Insidious evolution** – swelling progressed over weeks rather than hours/days typical of infectious cellulitis or anaphylaxis.  
* **Absence of systemic inflammatory signs** – afebrile, normal white count despite CRP elevation modestly above baseline suggests sterile inflammation.  
* **Lack of airway compromise** – no stridor or hypoxia despite large soft tissue mass argues against life‑threatening infection needing emergent drainage.  
* **Concurrent muscle symptoms** – subtle proximal weakness (hip flexors) and dysphagia were present early on but might have been missed without targeted exam.  
* **Cutaneous clues** – peri‑ungual telangiectasias/microhaemorrhages on nailfold capillaroscopy and faint “holster” rash point toward an underlying connective tissue disease even when classic Gottron papules are absent.  

These cues should prompt you to look beyond ENT pathology before committing a patient to surgery or empiric antibiotics.  

## A focused bedside work‑up that can change the game  

The moment you suspect an inflammatory myopathy masquerading as angioedema, draw a basic metabolic panel plus CK—and if it’s markedly raised (as it was >16 000 U/L), you’ve essentially solved the mystery at once. While you’re waiting for serologies:

1️⃣ Perform a thorough skin exam—look for heliotrope discoloration, V‑neck/ shawl signs, holster rash or subtle erythema on trunk/extremities.^  
2️⃣ Inspect fingernail beds under magnification—peri‐ungual erythema or capillary loops with microhaemorrhages are highly suggestive of dermatomyositis.^  
3️⃣ Nailfold capillaroscopy (if available) quickly visualizes those microscopic changes without delay.^  
4️⃣ Order a limited myositis antibody panel—anti‑SAE1 positivity is strongly linked to cutaneous edema and dysphagia.^  
5️⃣ Consider MRI of involved muscle groups if clinical weakness is focal — it differentiates true inflammation from simple soft tissue congestion.^  

All these steps can be completed within hours in most tertiary EDs, sparing patients unnecessary invasive ENT procedures and allowing immunosuppression to start early.  

## How dermatomyositis produces “pseudo‑angioedema”  

Unlike histamine/bradykinin‐mediated angioedema that results from increased vascular permeability secondary to mast cell degranulation or C1 esterase inhibition failure, pseudo‐angioedema in DM stems from immune‐mediated inflammation within skeletal muscle fibers themselves:

* **Perifascicular capillary injury** – complement C5b‑9 deposits damage endothelium → leakage into interstitial space around neck muscles → soft tissue swelling visible on CT/MRI.^  
* **Cytokine storm** – infiltrating CD4⁺ T cells and macrophages release IL‐6, TNFα, interferon γ which promote local edematous response without cellulitic signs.^  
* **Sumoylation dysregulation** – anti‑SAE1 antibodies target the SUMO‐activating enzyme involved in post‐translational modification; experimental data suggest this pathway modulates inflammatory gene expression within muscle cells.^  

Thus what looks like “dark rind” neck edema on imaging is actually inflamed muscle compartments expanding into subcutaneous tissue—a crucial distinction because antibiotics won’t help while high‐dose steroids will rapidly reverse it.  

## When you suspect dermatomyositis—hit the right serologies  

A standard myositis panel includes Mi‑2α/β, Jo‑1 (anti‐histidyl tRNA synthetase), PL‐7/12 (anti–threonyl/tyrosyl tRNA synthetase), OJ (anti–isoleucyl tRNA synthetase), TIF1γ, MDA5 and SAE1 among others. In our patient:

* Anti‑SAE1 IgG index 173 (>35 cutoff) → strong association with cutaneous edema & dysphagia.^  
* ANA speckled pattern at high titer supports autoimmune background but isn’t specific.^  
* Absence of anti–MDA5 or anti–TIF1γ reduces—but does not eliminate—the risk profile for rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease or malignancy respectively.^  

Including SAE1 in your ordered panel is critical when facial/neck swelling appears out of proportion to any infectious trigger because traditional “DM” panels may miss this autoantibody if you rely only on older kits lacking it.  

## Imaging pearls & pitfalls  

Contrast‐enhanced neck CT is often the first study ordered when airway compromise is feared—it will show strap muscle enlargement and retropharyngeal thickening just as Ludwig’s angina does—but look carefully:

* **No focal fluid collection** – true abscesses usually have peripheral rim enhancement & occasionally gas formation.*  
* **Symmetric involvement** – infections tend to be unilateral or heavily lobulated whereas DM produces diffuse homogeneous edema across multiple muscle groups.*  
* **Absence of bony erosion** – helps rule out chronic osteomyelitis or neoplastic infiltration.*  

FDG-PET adds valuable context—global muscular hypermetabolism signals active inflammation rather than malignancy when coupled with negative tumor markers and clean PET elsewhere—as illustrated by our patient’s diffusely avid musculature without focal hypermetabolic masses outside head/neck/thorax/pelvis.^  

## Treatment principles – act fast before airway collapses  

I’m going back to basics here because time matters:

* **Pulse steroids** – methylprednisolone 1000 mg IV daily ×3 days curtails cytokine release instantly; most clinicians transition within 24–48 h to oral prednisone ≈0.8–1 mg/kg/day.^  
* **Steroid sparing agents** – mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) has excellent tolerability data in DM involving lung or esophagus; selected early here because methotrexate carries pulmonary fibrosis risk especially given underlying lung nodules.^  
* **IVIG** – provides immunomodulation via Fc blockade & neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies—particularly useful when rapid functional improvement is needed for swallowing muscles.^  
* **Rituximab** – B cell depletion can accelerate remission in refractory cases or when autoantibody titers are high (e.g., SAE1); used here synergistically after initial steroid burst.^  
* **Supportive care** – nasogastric feeds until dysphagia resolves prevents aspiration pneumonia; speech therapy accelerates safe oral intake return.^  
* **Prophylaxis** – PCP prophylaxis mandatory when high-dose steroids plus additional immunosuppressants are employed; our patient switched from TMP–SMX due to hemolytic anemia onto nebulised pentamidine successfully.^  

When faced with cervical swelling threatening airway patency—even if hemodynamically stable—early multidisciplinary coordination between ENT, rheumatology/immunology and critical care dramatically improves outcomes versus proceeding directly to surgical drainage which would have been fruitless here.  

## Follow up & malignancy vigilance  

Adult dermatomyositis carries an inherent malignancy risk (~15–20%). Heightened risk factors include male sex, dysphagia at presentation and certain autoantibodies such as anti‑SAE1 (intermediate risk relative to anti–TIF1γ which carries higher risk). According to IMACS cancer screening guidelines our patient warranted baseline whole-body imaging plus targeted tumor markers—which were all negative—and ongoing surveillance including periodic CT chest/abdomen/pelvis plus upper/lower GI endoscopy planned during follow up years later.^  

Serial CK measurement proved invaluable: falling from >16 000 U/L down <40 U/L correlated tightly with clinical resolution of both muscle strength deficits and cervical edema—reinforcing CK as both diagnostic anchor and therapeutic marker in this context.^  

## Take‑home messages  

- **Think inflammatory myopathy when neck swelling persists without fever, purulence or rapid progression despite standard therapy** → obtain CK promptly.  
- **Look beyond classic skin signs:** peri‑ungual telangiectasias/microhaemorrhages on nailfold capillaroscopy are often the first dermatologic clue in pseudo‑angioedema cases.  
- **Anti‑SAE1 positivity flags a propensity for cervical edema & dysphagia**, so request a full myositis antibody panel early if DM is suspected.  
- **CT may show “Ludwig’s”‐type edema**, but absence of fluid collection/rim enhancement plus symmetric muscular thickening should raise suspicion for DM rather than infection.  
- **High-dose steroids + IVIG ± rituximab can reverse life-threatening airway edema rapidly**; add MMF as steroid-sparing agent once acute phase settles.  
- **Malignancy screening is mandatory:** adult DM patients with dysphagia/right sex & SAE1 antibodies sit in a higher risk tier—baseline PET/CT plus age-appropriate endoscopic workup are essential.  
- **Early multidisciplinary communication (ENT ↔ Rheumatology ↔ Critical Care)** prevents unnecessary surgical interventions and accelerates initiation of disease-modifying therapy._
