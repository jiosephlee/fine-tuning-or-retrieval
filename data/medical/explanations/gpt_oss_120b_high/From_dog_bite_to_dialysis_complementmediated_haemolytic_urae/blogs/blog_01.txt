Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Biting Into Sepsis: Recognizing *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* After Dog Bites  

*When a seemingly harmless dog bite spirals into dialysis‑dependent kidney injury, you know you’re dealing with something rare—but not impossible.*

---

## Why this case grabs your attention  

Four days after a playful bite on her lip, a previously healthy 48‑year‑old woman was crashing into septic shock with profound leukopenia, DIC and an AKI that progressed to anuria. Blood cultures finally grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*—a fastidious gram‑negative rod most of us only see on an occasional microbiology board question. The twist? She was immunocompetent, yet the infection set off a cascade that culminated in complement‑mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS) requiring eculizumab and dialysis.  

The story is a perfect reminder that “ordinary” animal bites can hide an extraordinary pathogen—one that masquerades as routine sepsis until it hits you with microvascular thrombosis and kidney failure. If your emergency department or ICU isn’t primed to suspect *C. canimorsus*, you’ll miss the window for early targeted therapy and may hand your patient an avoidable future on dialysis.

---

## Epidemiology at a glance  

* *C. canimorsus* lives peacefully in the oral flora of dogs (≈ 80 % prevalence) and cats (≈ 30 %).  
* Human infection is rare—estimated incidence 0.5–0.7 cases per million person‑years—but its mortality approaches 30 % when it does occur.  
* Classic “high‑risk” hosts are asplenic patients, chronic alcoholics, cirrhotics or those on immunosuppressants. However, up to one‑third of reported cases occur in otherwise healthy adults—our patient being a textbook example.  

Key epidemiologic point: **any dog bite**—even from a well‑behaved family pet—has the potential to transmit *C. canimorsus*. The pathogen’s rarity makes it easy to overlook; its lethality makes it impossible to ignore once recognized.

---

## Clinical spectrum – from cellulitis to catastrophic multi‑organ failure  

The presentation is protean:

| Presentation | Frequency | Typical timeline |
|--------------|-----------|-----------------|
| Local wound infection (cellulitis/abscess) | ~60 % | Hours–days |
| Septic shock ± DIC | ~30 % | 2–5 days |
| Fulminant hemorrhagic pneumonia | Rare | ≤ 1 week |
| Hemolytic uremic syndrome / TMA | Very rare | 5–10 days post‑infection |

Our patient followed the dreaded trajectory: initial fever and malaise → rapid progression to refractory hypoglycaemia → DIC → AKI → delayed MAHA/TMA → CM‑HUS. The timing is instructive—the microangiopathic component manifested **after** her inflammatory markers were already receding, underscoring how secondary TMA can hide behind improving sepsis labs.

Clinical clues that tip you off early:

* **Severe leukopenia** (often < 1 × 10⁹/L) out of proportion to the apparent infection.
* **Disproportionate coagulopathy** (low fibrinogen, prolonged PT/aPTT) early in the course.
* **Non‑healing “cold” extremities** potentially reflecting microthrombi even before overt DIC.
* **Rapidly falling glucose** refractory to IV dextrose—an odd hallmark reported in several *C.* infections.

---

## Bite‑related risk factors – beyond “just a scratch”

1️⃣ **Depth matters** – puncture wounds through mucosa or near vascular structures provide direct access for organisms residing deep in animal saliva.

2️⃣ **Delayed antibiotics** – our patient never filled her prescription because she couldn’t collect it on time. Even a single dose of amoxicillin-clavulanate within 12 h dramatically cuts bacteraemia rates.

3️⃣ **Prophylactic gaps** – tetanus vaccination alone does nothing against bacterial flora; lack of appropriate antimicrobial coverage is the true missed opportunity.

4️⃣ **Host conditions** – while our patient lacked classic risk factors, subtle impairments (e.g., transient neutropenia from viral prodrome) can tip balance toward invasive disease.

Takeaway: **any bite that breaks skin integrity warrants prompt antimicrobial prophylaxis**, irrespective of perceived “low risk”.

---

## Diagnostics – culture versus molecular

### Traditional culture

* Grows best on chocolate agar under CO₂ enrichment.
* Time to positivity: 4–7 days (often longer because organism is notoriously slow).
* Sensitivity suffers when patients receive antibiotics before sampling—a common scenario in ED settings.
* Misidentification possible due to its Gram‐negative rod morphology resembling *Pasteurella* spp.

### Molecular tools

* **16S rRNA PCR** from blood or tissue dramatically shortens time‑to‑identification (hours). 
* **Real‑time nanopore sequencing** offers bedside pathogen detection within ~6 h and provides species‐level resolution even after antibiotics started.
* These platforms also detect resistant gene signatures—useful when selecting empiric agents for critically ill patients.

Practical tip: If you suspect *C.* spp., request **both conventional culture + molecular assay** early. Even if cultures stay negative after a week, a positive PCR will guide targeted therapy and prevent unnecessary broadening later on.

---

## Empiric antimicrobial regimens – covering zoonotic threats up front

The goal is simple: start an agent active against *Capnocytophaga*, *Pasteurella*, anaerobes and occasional MRSA if local prevalence dictates.

| Scenario | First line (no allergy) | Penicillin allergy |
|----------|------------------------|--------------------|
| Dog bite ≥ moderate severity | **Ampicillin‑sulbactam** 3 g q6h (or amoxicillin/clavulanate PO/IV) | **Doxycycline + metronidazole** OR **clindamycin + fluoroquinolone** |
| Severe sepsis / shock | Add **gentamicin** or use **piperacillin/tazobactam** if Gram‐negative rods suspected | Use **meropenem + vancomycin** followed by de‑escalation |
| Immunocompromised host | Consider adding **vancomycin** because *Staph aureus* could coexist | Same principle plus coverage for resistant organisms |

Key nuances:

- *C.* spp are generally susceptible to β‑lactams including penicillin G; resistance remains very low (<1 %). 
- In patients already receiving broad‐spectrum agents (e.g., piperacillin/tazobactam), keep an eye on renal dosing—especially crucial if AKI evolves quickly.
- When initiating eculizumab later for CM‑HUS, ensure meningococcal vaccination + prophylactic antibiotics (**penicillin G or ceftriaxone**) are administered at least two weeks before complement blockade—or give them concurrently if timing isn’t feasible—as done successfully in our case.

## When sepsis is just the tip of the iceberg – TMA & CM-HUS  

The cascade from *C.* infection → endothelial activation → uncontrolled alternative complement pathway gives rise to TMA manifestations:

1️⃣ Schistocytes appear once platelet consumption crosses threshold → MAHA.
2️⃣ Platelet count remains low despite transfusions.
3️⃣ Renal microthrombi lead quickly from AKI to anuria.
4️⃣ ADAMTS13 activity typically hangs around 40–50 % (vs <10 % in TTP).
5️⃣ Complement studies may look deceptively normal early on—normal C3/C4 does NOT exclude CM-HUS!

Therapeutic sequence observed:

1️⃣ Prompt plasma exchange while awaiting complement testing—provides temporary “reset”.
2️⃣ Early eculizumab when bacteremia cleared reduces terminal complement activation → rapid haemolysis resolution and diuresis return.
3️⃣ Continued renal support while monitoring for relapse after eculizumab taper/discontinuation.

Bottom line for clinicians: **If your septic patient develops late‐onset hemolysis + persistent thrombocytopenia + worsening kidney function**, think TMA—and urgently send ADAMTS13, Shiga toxin PCR, complement panels—and start plasma exchange while arranging eculizumab access if CM-HUS looks likely.

## Practical pearls & high‑yield takeaways  

### Clinical pearls  

- **Think *C.* early:** Any dog bite followed by fever within 72 h plus leukopenia/DIC = red flag.
- **Don’t wait for cultures:** Initiate appropriate β‑lactam coverage even before microbiology confirms *Capnocytophaga*.  
- **Molecular diagnostics are game changers:** Real‑time nanopore sequencing can shave days off identification—a crucial advantage when organ failure looms.
- **Normal C3/C4 ≠ rule out CM-HUS:** Complement activation may be compartmentalized; rely on functional assays and clinical picture rather than static levels.
- **Eculizumab timing matters:** Start after blood cultures sterilize but before irreversible renal injury occurs; vaccinate against meningococcus beforehand or use prophylactic antibiotics aggressively.
- **Watch for secondary TMA:** Even as inflammatory markers improve, new hemolysis signals an overlapping pathology—not treatment failure of sepsis alone.

### Take‐home messages  

- **Prompt antimicrobial prophylaxis after any dog bite saves lives**—a single dose of amoxicillin‐clavulanate within hours is worth more than an entire ICU stay later on.  
- **If severe sepsis follows a bite**, include *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* in your differential regardless of host immune status.  
- **Order both blood cultures and rapid PCR/sequencing concurrently** when suspicion is high; do not let negative early cultures lull you into complacency.  
- **When MAHA appears during recovery**, transition quickly from sepsis management to TMA workup—ADAMTS13 >10 % redirects you away from plasma exchange alone toward complement blockade.  
- **Eculizumab works even without identifiable complement gene mutations**, provided infection has triggered uncontrolled terminal pathway activation.  

---

### Bottom line

A dog bite isn’t just a nuisance—it can be the portal for a stealthy gram‐negative rod capable of hijacking the complement system and destroying kidneys within days. Early recognition hinges on pattern recognition (leukopenia + DIC after bite), rapid empiric β‐lactam therapy, employing modern molecular diagnostics when cultures lag, and being primed to pivot toward TMA treatment if hematologic derangements arise later in the course. In short: ***bite → cover → confirm → watch for TMA → treat aggressively***—and you’ll keep more patients out of dialysis units one paw at a time.
