Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Management] What are the current indications for initiating plasma exchange in a patient with suspected thrombotic microangiopathy, and why is plasma exchange only a temporizing measure in complement‑mediated HUS compared with its definitive role in TTP?

**Plasma‑exchange (PEX) in the initial work‑up of a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)**  
The bedside decision to start PEX hinges on *what we do not yet know* rather than on a definitive diagnosis. In an adult who presents with the classic triad of micro‑angiopathic haemolytic anaemia (schistocytes, low haptoglobin, ↑LDH), thrombocytopenia, and end‑organ injury (usually kidney or brain), the first priority is to ask – *could this be thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)?*  

Current guidelines (ASH 2023; ISTH 2022) recommend **immediate daily PEX** when any one of the following is true:

1. **High pre‑test probability of TTP** – a PLASMIC score ≥ 6 (or a score ≥ 5 with rapidly progressive neurologic or renal disease).  
2. **Severe MAHA + thrombocytopenia with organ dysfunction** when ADAMTS13 activity has not yet returned (the assay takes 24–48 h).  
3. **Overlap states** – DIC or sepsis‑associated TMA where the clinical picture is indistinguishable from TTP until the assay is back; in practice clinicians start PEX while awaiting results because withholding therapy can be fatal.  
4. **Rarely**, severe Shiga‑toxin–associated HUS with neurological involvement where early “plasma‑exchange‑plus‑supportive” strategies have modest benefit.

In all other defined TMAs—complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS), drug‑induced TMAs, malignant hypertension—the evidence does not support routine PEX as definitive therapy; instead it serves only as a *bridge* while the correct diagnosis is confirmed and disease‑specific treatment is arranged.

---

### How this applied to the presented case

- On day 8 the patient developed a new drop in haemoglobin, persistent platelet count < 50 × 10⁹/L, undetectable haptoglobin and 15–20 % schistocytes—classic MAHA with thrombocytopenia.
- Because **TTP could not be excluded** at that moment (ADAMTS13 results were pending), the team initiated three daily sessions of PEX with Octaplas®. This mirrors standard practice: treat presumptive TTP until laboratory data prove otherwise.
- The ADAMTS13 activity later returned at **43 %**, far above the <10 % threshold for TTP; consequently, attention shifted to other causes of TMA.
- Complement panels were essentially normal on routine testing; extended functional assays later revealed an alternative‑pathway block consistent with treatment effect rather than intrinsic deficiency.
- With confirmation that this was **complement‑mediated HUS**, eculizumab was administered after vaccination and negative blood cultures—​the disease‑specific therapy that ultimately produced sustained hematologic remission and partial renal recovery.

---

### Why plasma exchange is *definitive* in TTP but only *temporizing* in CM‑HUS

| Feature | Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura | Complement‑Mediated HUS |
|---|---|---|
| Pathogenesis | Severe **ADAMTS13 deficiency** (<10 %) → accumulation of ultra‑large vWF multimers → platelet‐rich microthrombi | Uncontrolled activation of the **alternative complement pathway** → C5b‑9 deposition on endothelium |
| What PEX removes | • Circulating anti‑ADAMTS13 autoantibodies<br>• Depleted ADAMTS13 enzyme | • Soluble complement split products (C3a, C5a)<br>• Inflammatory cytokines<br>• Any circulating autoantibodies (rare) |
| What PEX replaces | **Functional ADAMTS13** from donor plasma – restores proteolysis of vWF multimers → halts microthrombosis | No replacement for a dysregulated *genetic* or *acquired* complement control system; removal is fleeting |
| Clinical effect | Rapid normalization of platelet count and LDH within 2–4 days in > 80 % when started promptly → cure in most patients | Transient reduction in hemolysis/creatinine but relapse occurs as soon as complement generation resumes; renal recovery depends on sustained blockade |
| Definitive therapy | Plasma exchange alone (± adjunct steroids/rituximab) | **Eculizumab** or ravulizumab (C5 inhibition) – halts downstream MAC formation permanently while drug is given |

In our patient, after three exchanges her urine output improved—but platelets rose only modestly and hemolysis persisted until terminal complement inhibition was achieved. This illustrates that **PEX merely “dilutes” the pathogenic milieu** in CM‑HUS; removal of terminal complement fragments does not correct the upstream dysregulation driven by genetic variants or autoantibodies against factor H/I/B, nor does it prevent renewed activation triggered by infection.

---

### Practical take‑away for morning report

1. **Start PEX immediately whenever TTP cannot be ruled out**—use PLASMIC score to gauge urgency but never wait for ADAMTS13 if clinical suspicion is moderate/high.
2. **Interpret response:** A brisk rise in platelets + fall in LDH within 48 h strongly supports TTP; lack of such response should prompt vigorous pursuit of alternative diagnoses.
3. **When CM‑HUS is diagnosed**, transition quickly to eculizumab (or ravulizumab). Maintain PEX only as a short bridge if the patient remains critically ill while vaccination/antibiotic prophylaxis are organized.
4. Remember that **normal C3/C4 levels do NOT exclude CM‑HUS**, as shown by this case—rely on clinical pattern, renal biopsy when necessary, exclusion of STEC/TTP and functional alternative pathway assays.
5. Monitor infection risk meticulously when using terminal complement blockade after an active bacterial infection such as Capnocytophaga canimorsus; vaccinate against Neisseria spp., provide prophylactic antibiotics (e.g., amoxicillin), and defer eculizumab until cultures are sterile—as was done here.

In short, plasma exchange remains life‑saving when you are *uncertain* about TTP; once you know you are dealing with a compliment‐driven process, it buys time only until definitive C5 inhibition can be instituted. This distinction shapes both immediate management and long‑term prognosis for patients presenting with a thrombotic microangiopathy.
