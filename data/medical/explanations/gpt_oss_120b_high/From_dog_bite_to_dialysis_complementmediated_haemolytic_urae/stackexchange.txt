Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the typical routes of transmission and the clinical spectrum of Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection in humans, and why might its diagnosis be delayed?

**Clinical Pearl – Capnocytophaga canimorsus**

**Transmission**  
Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a commensal gram‑negative rod that lives in the oral cavity of dogs (and less frequently cats). Human infection almost always follows **direct animal contact**, most commonly:

* **Dog bite** – the classic route (as in our patient).  
* **Scratches or puncture wounds** contaminated with saliva.  
* **Licking of broken skin or mucosa** – even a kiss from a pet can seed the organism.  
* **Close proximity without overt trauma** – rare cases have occurred after simply handling a pet whose saliva contacts a minor abrasion.

Because the organism resides on the animal’s mouth, any mechanism that permits saliva to enter the host’s bloodstream can serve as a portal of entry.

**Clinical Spectrum**  
Once inoculated, C canimorsus has an uncanny ability to generate a wide‑range “zoo” of presentations, from benign local infection to fulminant systemic disease:

| Presentation | Frequency / Typical Features |
|--------------|------------------------------|
| **Mild cellulitis / wound infection** | Often self‑limited, may be confused with ordinary bite‑site inflammation. |
| **Rapidly progressive sepsis** | High fever (>40 °C), rigors, hypotension, tachypnea—develops within 1‑4 days (our case presented 4 days after the bite). |
| **Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)** | Coagulopathy, bleeding diathesis, purpura fulminans; seen in up to one‑third of severe cases. |
| **Thrombotic Microangiopathy / Complement‑mediated HUS** | Rare but reported (including this patient), manifests as MAHA, thrombocytopenia and AKI after the initial septic phase resolves. |
| **Meningitis / encephalitis** | Can present with headache, neck stiffness without obvious skin portal. |
| **Endocarditis, septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, pneumonia** | Sporadic case reports; usually in patients with prosthetic material or immune compromise. |
| **Necrotizing fasciitis / severe soft‑tissue necrosis** | Very uncommon but described when bacterial load is high and host defenses are overwhelmed. |

Overall mortality approaches **30 %**, particularly when septic shock or DIC develop and when antimicrobial therapy is delayed.

**Why Diagnosis Is Often Delayed**

1. **Fastidious growth characteristics**  
   * C canimorsus is a slow‑growing organism that requires enriched media (e.g., chocolate agar) and an atmosphere enriched with CO₂. Typical aerobic blood‐culture bottles will often turn positive only after **5–7 days**—far beyond the routine 48–72 h “watch‑list”. In our case the blood cultures became positive on day 7.

2. **Low organism burden & prior antibiotics**  
   * Even small inocula can cause severe disease; however the low bacterial load translates into scant colonies on plates. A single dose of penicillin prescribed on the day of the bite (as in this patient) can suppress early growth and further reduce culture yield.

3. **Lack of clinical suspicion**  
   * The infection is rare and many clinicians do not routinely inquire about pet exposure when evaluating sepsis. Consequently initial empiric regimens may omit agents active against Capnocytophaga unless animal exposure is explicitly documented.

4. **Non‑specific presentation**  
   * Early symptoms (fever, malaise) mimic any bacterial sepsis. The hallmark skin findings (purpura, microvascular thrombosis) often emerge only after DIC or TMA develop, by which time empiric therapy may already have been initiated for other pathogens.

5. **Limited access to rapid molecular diagnostics**  
   * Conventional labs still rely on culture first; newer tools such as real‑time PCR or nanopore sequencing dramatically shorten time‑to‑identification (hours rather than days) but are not yet universally available. The authors note increasing reliance on these methods precisely because culture delays are common.

**Take‑Home Points for Residents**

* Whenever a patient presents with sepsis after a recent dog bite/scratch/close contact—especially if there is rapid progression to DIC or unexplained AKI—*think* C canimorsus early.
* Request extended incubation of blood cultures (≥7 days) and inform microbiology that Capnocytophaga is suspected so they can use appropriate media and CO₂ conditions.
* Empiric coverage should include a β‑lactam active against gram‑negative rods (e.g., ampicillin–sulbactam or piperacillin–tazobactam) while awaiting microbiology results.
* Monitor for secondary complications such as complement‑mediated HUS/TMA; early recognition permits timely initiation of plasma exchange and complement blockade (eculizumab), as illustrated by this case.
* Consider molecular diagnostics (PCR/NGS) when culture remains negative despite high clinical suspicion—these methods can cut the diagnostic window from days to hours.

By maintaining a high index of suspicion for animal‑related pathogens and by adapting our microbiologic work‑up accordingly, we can shorten the time from presentation to definitive therapy and improve outcomes for patients facing this potentially lethal zoonosis.

### [Morning Report] In a septic patient who develops worsening thrombocytopenia, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, and schistocytes on peripheral smear, how does one differentiate disseminated intravascular coagulation from a thrombotic microangiopathy such as complement‑mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome?

**Differentiating DIC from a complement‑mediated thrombotic micro‑angiopathy (CM‑HUS) in a septic patient**

The scenario of a septic patient who later develops worsening thrombocytopenia, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin and schistocytes forces us to ask whether we are still dealing with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) or have moved into the spectrum of thrombotic micro‑angiopathies (TMAs).  The distinction matters because the therapies are opposite—source control and replacement of clotting factors for DIC versus plasma exchange or complement blockade for TMAs.

Below is a practical algorithm that was applied in the reported case of a 48‑year‑old woman with *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis – initially classic DIC followed by an infection‑triggered CM‑HUS.

---

### 1. Use the temporal pattern of the laboratory abnormalities

| Finding | Typical of early **DIC** | Typical of **TMA** that follows |
|---------|--------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Coagulation times (PT/INR, aPTT)** | Prolonged | Normal (or return to normal) |
| **Fibrinogen** | Low | Normal/near‑normal |
| **D‑dimer / FDP** | Markedly elevated | May be modestly raised or normal |
| **Platelet count** | Drops early, improves as coagulopathy resolves | Persists low or falls again despite normalization of PT/aPTT |
| **LDH/Haptoglobin** | May rise mildly due to fibrinolysis | Marked rise in LDH with undetectable haptoglobin reflecting true MAHA |
| **Schistocytes** | Usually <2 % | >5 % (often 10–20 %) |

**Case illustration:** On admission the patient had a classic ISTH‑DIC picture—prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen and high d‑dimer (Table 1). By day 8 the coagulation profile had normalized while platelet count remained 30–40 × 10⁹/L and she suddenly developed a fall in hemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin and LDH >2 × upper limit. Schistocytes rose to 15–20 % on peripheral smear—features that did not fit ongoing DIC but were characteristic of a new TMA.

---

### 2. Order targeted laboratory tests early

1. **ISTH/DIC score** – calculate on presentation; a score ≥5 supports DIC.
2. **ADAMTS13 activity** – essential to rule out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP); severe deficiency (<10 %) = TTP → immediate plasma exchange.
   
   *In our patient* ADAMTS13 was 43 %, making TTP unlikely (PLASMIC score 4/7).

3. **Shiga toxin PCR** – excludes STEC‑associated HUS when diarrheal prodrome is present or endemic exposure suspected.
   
   *Negative* in this case.

4. **Complement studies** – C3/C4 may be normal in CM‑HUS; functional assays such as AH50 (alternative pathway activity) are more informative once the acute phase has settled or after plasma exchange has been completed.  

   In this patient baseline C3/C4 were within range; AH50 later fell to 6 % after plasma exchange and eculizumab – a pattern compatible with treatment effect rather than intrinsic depletion.

5. **Renal biopsy** – When AKI is prominent and the diagnosis remains uncertain, tissue demonstration of endothelial swelling, fibrin‐rich thrombi within glomerular capillaries and arterioles confirms TMA and often distinguishes it from pure consumptive coagulopathy.

   The biopsy here showed ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi consistent with TMA.

---

### 3. Apply pathophysiologic reasoning

- **DIC** is driven by systemic activation of tissue factor leading to widespread fibrin deposition; it consumes clotting factors and platelets simultaneously with secondary hemolysis from mechanical fragmentation in fibrin‐rich clots.
- **CM‑HUS** results from unchecked activation of the alternative complement pathway—often precipitated by an infection (“second hit”)—causing endothelial injury and platelet‐fibrin microthrombi confined largely to renal microvasculature.
- In sepsis both pathways can coexist transiently: early sepsis → DIC; once systemic inflammation abates but complement activation persists → overt MAHA/TMA.

The reported case exemplifies this progression: initial coagulopathy resolved while complement‐driven endothelial injury manifested as isolated hemolysis and renal failure.

---

### 4. Therapeutic implications dictate rapid differentiation

| Diagnosis | First‑line therapy |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **DIC** | Aggressive source control (antibiotics), supportive transfusion of platelets/fibrinogen/FFP as guided by labs |
| **TTP** | Immediate plasma exchange ± caplacizumab; steroids |
| **CM‑HUS** | Plasma exchange can temporize; definitive treatment is terminal complement blockade (eculizumab or ravulizumab) after confirming negative cultures & administering meningococcal vaccination + prophylactic antibiotics |

In our patient plasma exchange was started as bridging while awaiting ADAMTS13 results; definitive remission was achieved only after eculizumab was given following clearance of bacteremia—a sequence that underscores why distinguishing these entities is not academic but lifesaving.

---

### Key Pearls for Morning Report

1. **Coagulation profile is the first discriminator:** Persistently abnormal PT/aPTT/fibrinogen favors DIC; normalization coupled with ongoing MAHA points toward TMA.
2. **Platelet kinetics matter:** In DIC platelets recover as consumptive coagulopathy resolves; persistent thrombocytopenia despite normal coagulation parameters should raise alarm for TMA.
3. **Schistocyte burden >5 % plus undetectable haptoglobin = true MAHA**, uncommon in pure DIC unless overrun by massive fibrinolysis.
4. **ADAMTS13 activity rules out TTP quickly:** <10 % = treat as TTP regardless of infection status.
5. **Complement levels are unreliable alone:** Normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS; functional assays or genetic workup become useful after acute phase.
6. **Kidney biopsy is decisive when AKI dominates the picture**, especially if plasma exchange does not yield sustained response.
7. **Timing of eculizumab matters:** Initiate only after bloodstream infection is cleared and meningococcal vaccination/prophylaxis instituted—a lesson borne out by this case where therapy began after cultures turned negative.

---

#### Bottom Line
In a septic patient who evolves from an initial consumptive coagulopathy to isolated hemolysis with schistocytes, ask:

- Have PT/aPTT, fibrinogen and d‑dimer normalized?
- Is thrombocytopenia persisting despite correction of clotting parameters?
- What does the ADAMTS13 assay show?
- Are there clues pointing toward complement activation (renal failure out of proportion, negative Shiga toxin)?

When the answer is “yes” to the first two points and ADAMTS13 is >10 %, shift your working diagnosis from DIC to a complement‑mediated TMA such as CM‑HUS—and move promptly toward plasma exchange followed by eculizumab once infection control permits. This systematic approach prevented continued organ loss in the described patient and illustrates how vigilant re‑assessment can uncover a treatable “second hit” hidden behind sepsis‐related DIC.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How is the PLASMIC score calculated, what does a score of 4 indicate regarding the likelihood of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), and how does this result influence early therapeutic decisions?

**PLASMIC score – a bedside triage tool for suspected TTP**

| Variable (1 point each) | Definition |
|--------------------------|------------|
| **P** – Platelets | < 30 × 10⁹/L |
| **L** – Hemolysis | Elevated LDH (or indirect bilirubin), undetectable haptoglobin **or** reticulocytosis > 2.5 % |
| **A** – Absence of active cancer | No current solid‑tumour or hematologic malignancy |
| **S** – No solid‑organ or stem‑cell transplant | No history of transplantation |
| **M** – Mean corpuscular volume | < 90 fL (normocytic) |
| **I** – INR | < 1.5 |
| **C** – Creatinine | ≤ 2 mg/dL (≈ 177 µmol/L) |

The total yields a score from **0–7**.  

*Interpretation* (original validation cohort)

* **0–4 points** – **Low pre‑test probability** for severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). The negative predictive value exceeds 95 %; most patients do **not** have classic TTP.
* **5 points** – **Intermediate probability** (≈15‑20 % have ADAMTS13 < 10 %).
* **6–7 points** – **High probability**; >50 % will have severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and benefit from emergent plasma exchange (PEX).

---

### What does a PLASMIC score of **4** mean?

In our patient the seven criteria summed to **4/7**, placing her in the *low‑risk* category. This conveyed that classic thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura was unlikely—later confirmed by an ADAMTS13 activity of **43 %**, well above the <10 % threshold diagnostic for TTP.

---

### How does this influence early therapeutic decisions?

1. **Urgency of plasma exchange**  
   *High‑risk* scores (≥6) compel immediate empiric daily PEX while awaiting ADAMTS13 results because untreated TTP carries ≈90 % mortality.  
   *Low‑risk* scores (≤4) suggest that routine empiric PEX may be unnecessary; clinicians should first seek alternative explanations for MAHA–thrombocytopenia (e.g., infection‑triggered complement‑mediated HUS, DIC).

2. **Diagnostic focus**  
   A low score directs attention toward other TMAs—particularly in the setting of sepsis or known triggers—prompting early complement studies, Shiga toxin assays, and renal biopsy when feasible.

3. **Balancing safety and certainty**  
   Despite a low PLASMIC score, many physicians start a *brief* course of PEX (“bridging”) if the clinical picture remains ambiguous or if the patient is deteriorating rapidly—exactly what occurred in this case. The rationale is that missing TTP even briefly can be catastrophic; PEX provides coverage until the definitive ADAMTS13 assay returns.

4. **Switching therapy once TTP is excluded**  
   When ADAMTS13 activity proved normal, PEX was stopped and targeted therapy for complement‑mediated HUS—eculizumab—was instituted, leading to hematologic remission and partial renal recovery.

---

### Take‑home pearls

* The **PLASMIC score** uses seven readily available laboratory/clinical items to estimate the likelihood of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency.
* A **score of 4** places the patient in the *low probability* group; it does not rule out TTP outright but makes it statistically unlikely.
* In practice, a low score encourages clinicians to prioritize alternative diagnoses while still maintaining a low threshold for *temporary* PEX when uncertainty persists.
* Prompt cessation of PEX after an ADAMTS13 result ≥10 % avoids unnecessary exposure to its risks and allows timely initiation of disease‑specific therapy such as eculizumab for complement‐mediated HUS—as illustrated by this dog‑bite–associated case.

By integrating the PLASMIC estimate with rapid laboratory confirmation and clinical context, we can tailor early management—saving patients from both under‑treatment of true TTP and overtreatment when another TMA explains the presentation.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the mechanism by which dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway leads to endothelial injury in complement‑mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome, including the role of inherited genetic variants versus an environmental "second hit" such as infection.

**Pathophysiology of complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS): how unchecked alternative‑pathway activity injures endothelium and why a “second hit” such as infection is often required**

---

### 1. The alternative complement circuit in health  

1. **Spontaneous C3 hydrolysis** – In plasma a small fraction of native C3 “tick‑over” converts to C3(H₂O), which can bind factor B.  
2. **Formation of the fluid‑phase C3 convertase** – Factor B is cleaved by factor D → **C3(H₂O)Bb**, which cleaves additional C3 to C3a and **C3b**.  
3. **Amplification loop** – Surface‑bound C3b recruits more factor B/D, generating the membrane‐bound convertase **C3bBb** that rapidly deposits additional C3b on the same surface.  
4. **Regulation** – Host cells express soluble regulators (factor H, factor I) and membrane‐bound regulators (MCP/CD46, decay‑accelerating factor). These proteins accelerate decay of C3bBb and cleave C3b to iC3b, preventing uncontrolled deposition.

When regulation fails, the amplified convertase persists on host endothelium, leading to downstream events:

* **Massive C3b opsonisation** → recruitment of inflammatory cells.
* **C5 convertase formation** (C3bBbC3b) → cleavage of C5 into **C5a** (potent chemoattractant) and **C5b**, which initiates assembly of the **membrane‑attack complex (MAC; C5b‑9)**.
* **Endothelial activation & lysis** – Sublytic MAC triggers endothelial secretion of von Willebrand factor (vWF), tissue factor and cytokines; full‑lytic MAC causes direct cell death.

The net result is a *pro‑thrombotic microvascular surface* that captures platelets and initiates fibrin deposition → the classic triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury.

---

### 2. Genetic predisposition vs “second hit”  

| Aspect | Inherited component | Environmental trigger (“second hit”) |
|--------|--------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Typical lesions** | Mutations/autoantibodies that diminish regulation (e.g., loss‑of‑function in *CFH*, *CFI*, *MCP/CD46*) or enhance activation (gain‑of‑function in *C3* or *CFB*) are found in 50–70 % of CM‑HUS patients. | An acute stimulus such as infection, pregnancy, surgery, or certain drugs provides abundant surface activators or inflammatory mediators that overwhelm even partially functional regulators. |
| **Penetrance** | Incomplete—many carriers never develop disease without an additional insult. | Provides the “missing link” that converts a subclinical genotype into overt TMA. |
| **Laboratory clues** | Low circulating regulatory protein levels, presence of anti‑factor H antibodies, abnormal functional assays (elevated AH₅₀). | Sudden rise in alternative‑pathway activity markers during infection; sometimes normal standard complement levels because consumption is transient or masked by therapy.|

In the present case:

* **Genetic workup was negative** – extended sequencing did not reveal pathogenic variants in *CFH*, *CFI*, *CFB*, *C3*, or *CD46*. Thus a pure hereditary defect cannot explain the disease.
* **Infection acted as the second hit** – Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia supplied abundant bacterial polysaccharide capsule and lipooligosaccharide that directly activate the alternative pathway via properdin stabilization and provide surfaces for uncontrolled C3b deposition.
* **Temporal relationship** – While sepsis and DIC dominated early presentation, isolated hemolysis with schistocytes appeared only after systemic inflammation began to resolve—a pattern typical for an infection‑triggered complement flare rather than a primary hereditary process.
* **Functional assay post‑treatment** – The markedly reduced AH₅₀ (6 %) measured after plasma exchange and the first eculizumab dose reflects therapeutic consumption rather than intrinsic deficiency; nevertheless it illustrates how sensitive functional testing is to ongoing activation/ blockade.

---

### 3. How dysregulated alternative pathway translates into endothelial injury in CM‑HUS  

1. **Bacterial surface triggers** – *C. canimorsus* expresses a capsular polysaccharide that resists classical complement killing but paradoxically provides a platform for spontaneous C3b binding and properdin-mediated stabilization of C3bBb.
2. **Failure of regulation** – In individuals lacking sufficient factor H activity (whether genetically reduced or functionally consumed), the formed convertase persists on endothelial membranes.
3. **Excessive generation of downstream effectors** – Unchecked production of C5a recruits neutrophils/monocytes; sublytic MAC induces endothelial expression of tissue factor and vWF multimers while full MAC lysis creates focal denudation.
4. **Platelet‐rich microthrombi formation** – Denuded endothelium exposes collagen and basement membrane components; vWF multimers tether platelets → microvascular occlusion principally within renal glomeruli.
5. **Clinical sequelae** – Shearing forces across narrowed capillaries generate schistocytes (MAHA); consuming platelets cause thrombocytopenia; ischemic glomeruli produce AKI — all documented in our patient’s peripheral smear, renal biopsy (fibrin thrombi), and laboratory profile (LDH surge, undetectable haptoglobin).

Thus, “alternative pathway dysregulation” is not merely an abstract lab abnormality—it is a cascade that culminates in direct endothelial injury and thrombus formation when regulation is insufficient or overwhelmed by a microbial trigger.

---

### 4. Take‑home points for trainees  

1. **CM‑HUS is a diagnosis of exclusion:** ADAMTS13 >10 % excludes TTP; negative Shiga toxin PCR excludes STEC-HUS; kidney biopsy showing TMA supports CM‑HUS.
2. **Normal C3/C4 do not rule out disease:** Functional assays (AH₅₀) are more informative but can be confounded by plasma exchange or eculizumab.
3. **Genetics provide susceptibility but are usually insufficient alone:** Absence of pathogenic variants—as in this patient—doesn’t exclude CM‑HUS if a strong “second hit” is present.
4. **Infections such as Capnocytophaga canimorsus are potent second hits:** They activate the alternative pathway directly and create an inflammatory milieu that overwhelms residual regulation.
5. **Early complement blockade saves kidneys:** Prompt eculizumab after control of bacteremia halted ongoing endothelial injury—in this case hematologic remission occurred before significant renal recovery but earlier therapy would likely have preserved more function.

Understanding how an environmental trigger can tip a precariously balanced complement system into pathological overactivation clarifies why patients with seemingly “sporadic” TMAs often have an identifiable infection preceding their illness—and why rapid recognition followed by targeted complement inhibition remains the cornerstone of therapy for CM‑HUS.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why can serum C3 and C4 concentrations be within normal limits in patients with complement‑mediated HUS, and which specialized functional assays provide more reliable evidence of complement activation?

**Clinical Pearl – Normal C3/C4 Levels Do Not Rule Out Complement‑Mediated HUS**

In complement‑mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS) the classic “low C3” laboratory pattern seen in systemic lupus or massive complement consumption is often absent. In the case we discussed—a woman who developed CM‑HUS after *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis—standard serum C3 and C4 concentrations were within reference limits even though she ultimately proved to have uncontrolled alternative‑pathway activation. The reasons are threefold:

1. **Localized versus systemic consumption** – Dysregulated activation in CM‑HUS is largely confined to the microvascular endothelium of kidney and other affected organs. Most of the cleaved C‐components become deposited on endothelial surfaces or in subendothelial lesions rather than being removed from the circulation in quantities sufficient to depress plasma levels.

2. **Compensatory hepatic synthesis** – Acute‑phase stimulation during sepsis or inflammation drives hepatic production of complement proteins. Simultaneous synthesis can mask modest consumptive loss, leaving total serum concentrations apparently normal.

3. **Pathway selectivity** – The alternative pathway (AP) uses factor B and properdin as amplifiers and generates large amounts of C3b locally. Because the classical cascade (the source of routine C4) remains largely quiescent, total serum C4 stays unchanged and total C3 may only fall slightly—or not at all—despite marked AP turnover.

Consequently, relying on static concentrations can delay recognition of a complement‑driven TMA. The diagnosis hinges on **functional assays that directly assess pathway activity**.

### Functional Tests That Reveal Complement Activation in CM‑HUS

| Test | What it measures | Clinical relevance in our case |
|------|------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Alternative‑pathway hemolytic assay (AH₅₀)** | Ability of patient serum (often diluted) to lyse rabbit erythrocytes via the AP; expressed as % activity relative to normal | The patient’s AH₅₀ was markedly reduced to **6 %**, indicating profound AP consumption despite normal C3/C4 levels. |
| **Classical‑pathway hemolytic assay (CH₅₀)** | Total hemolytic capacity via the classical route (C1–C9); helps detect global complement deficiency | Normal CH₅₀ supported that the classical cascade was intact, explaining why C4 remained unchanged. |
| **Mannose‑binding lectin/lectin‑pathway functional assay** | Hemolysis mediated by MBL or ficolins; useful when infection triggers lectin activation | Normal results argued against a primary lectin contribution in this patient’s TMA. |
| **Soluble terminal‑complex (sC5b‑9) ELISA** | Quantifies circulating membrane attack complex fragments—direct marker of terminal pathway activation | Though not performed in this case, elevated sC5b‑9 would have corroborated ongoing terminal blockade prior to eculizumab. |
| **C3a / C5a split‑product assays** | Detect early anaphylatoxin generation, reflecting real‑time activation | Useful when rapid bedside confirmation is needed, but less specific for AP dysregulation. |
| **Factor H autoantibody ELISA & functional factor H assay** | Detects pathogenic antibodies that impede regulation of the AP | Negative in our patient, reinforcing that infection alone supplied the “second hit.” |

**Key teaching points**

- Normal quantitative C3/C4 does *not* exclude CM‑HUS; an isolated “normal” result should never halt pursuit of functional testing when clinical suspicion for a TMA persists.
- The *AH₅₀* is the most sensitive screen for AP‐driven disease because it directly reports on the consumptive loop that fuels endothelial injury.
- Functional assays remain reliable even after plasma exchange (PEX) or early eculizumab administration only if timing is considered—both interventions can artificially normalize activity by removing consumed components or by saturating downstream blockade.
- In practice, order a panel: CH₅₀ (to rule out global deficiency), AH₅₀ (primary screen for CM‑HUS), sC5b‑9 (terminal activation), and specific autoantibody testing when indicated.

In short, a high index of suspicion combined with targeted functional complement testing—rather than reliance on static protein levels—allows timely diagnosis and initiation of life‑saving eculizumab therapy, as illustrated by our patient’s dramatic response once her true complement dysregulation was uncovered.

### [Management] What are the current indications for initiating plasma exchange in a patient with suspected thrombotic microangiopathy, and why is plasma exchange only a temporizing measure in complement‑mediated HUS compared with its definitive role in TTP?

**Plasma‑exchange (PEX) in the initial work‑up of a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)**  
The bedside decision to start PEX hinges on *what we do not yet know* rather than on a definitive diagnosis. In an adult who presents with the classic triad of micro‑angiopathic haemolytic anaemia (schistocytes, low haptoglobin, ↑LDH), thrombocytopenia, and end‑organ injury (usually kidney or brain), the first priority is to ask – *could this be thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)?*  

Current guidelines (ASH 2023; ISTH 2022) recommend **immediate daily PEX** when any one of the following is true:

1. **High pre‑test probability of TTP** – a PLASMIC score ≥ 6 (or a score ≥ 5 with rapidly progressive neurologic or renal disease).  
2. **Severe MAHA + thrombocytopenia with organ dysfunction** when ADAMTS13 activity has not yet returned (the assay takes 24–48 h).  
3. **Overlap states** – DIC or sepsis‑associated TMA where the clinical picture is indistinguishable from TTP until the assay is back; in practice clinicians start PEX while awaiting results because withholding therapy can be fatal.  
4. **Rarely**, severe Shiga‑toxin–associated HUS with neurological involvement where early “plasma‑exchange‑plus‑supportive” strategies have modest benefit.

In all other defined TMAs—complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS), drug‑induced TMAs, malignant hypertension—the evidence does not support routine PEX as definitive therapy; instead it serves only as a *bridge* while the correct diagnosis is confirmed and disease‑specific treatment is arranged.

---

### How this applied to the presented case

- On day 8 the patient developed a new drop in haemoglobin, persistent platelet count < 50 × 10⁹/L, undetectable haptoglobin and 15–20 % schistocytes—classic MAHA with thrombocytopenia.
- Because **TTP could not be excluded** at that moment (ADAMTS13 results were pending), the team initiated three daily sessions of PEX with Octaplas®. This mirrors standard practice: treat presumptive TTP until laboratory data prove otherwise.
- The ADAMTS13 activity later returned at **43 %**, far above the <10 % threshold for TTP; consequently, attention shifted to other causes of TMA.
- Complement panels were essentially normal on routine testing; extended functional assays later revealed an alternative‑pathway block consistent with treatment effect rather than intrinsic deficiency.
- With confirmation that this was **complement‑mediated HUS**, eculizumab was administered after vaccination and negative blood cultures—​the disease‑specific therapy that ultimately produced sustained hematologic remission and partial renal recovery.

---

### Why plasma exchange is *definitive* in TTP but only *temporizing* in CM‑HUS

| Feature | Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura | Complement‑Mediated HUS |
|---|---|---|
| Pathogenesis | Severe **ADAMTS13 deficiency** (<10 %) → accumulation of ultra‑large vWF multimers → platelet‐rich microthrombi | Uncontrolled activation of the **alternative complement pathway** → C5b‑9 deposition on endothelium |
| What PEX removes | • Circulating anti‑ADAMTS13 autoantibodies<br>• Depleted ADAMTS13 enzyme | • Soluble complement split products (C3a, C5a)<br>• Inflammatory cytokines<br>• Any circulating autoantibodies (rare) |
| What PEX replaces | **Functional ADAMTS13** from donor plasma – restores proteolysis of vWF multimers → halts microthrombosis | No replacement for a dysregulated *genetic* or *acquired* complement control system; removal is fleeting |
| Clinical effect | Rapid normalization of platelet count and LDH within 2–4 days in > 80 % when started promptly → cure in most patients | Transient reduction in hemolysis/creatinine but relapse occurs as soon as complement generation resumes; renal recovery depends on sustained blockade |
| Definitive therapy | Plasma exchange alone (± adjunct steroids/rituximab) | **Eculizumab** or ravulizumab (C5 inhibition) – halts downstream MAC formation permanently while drug is given |

In our patient, after three exchanges her urine output improved—but platelets rose only modestly and hemolysis persisted until terminal complement inhibition was achieved. This illustrates that **PEX merely “dilutes” the pathogenic milieu** in CM‑HUS; removal of terminal complement fragments does not correct the upstream dysregulation driven by genetic variants or autoantibodies against factor H/I/B, nor does it prevent renewed activation triggered by infection.

---

### Practical take‑away for morning report

1. **Start PEX immediately whenever TTP cannot be ruled out**—use PLASMIC score to gauge urgency but never wait for ADAMTS13 if clinical suspicion is moderate/high.
2. **Interpret response:** A brisk rise in platelets + fall in LDH within 48 h strongly supports TTP; lack of such response should prompt vigorous pursuit of alternative diagnoses.
3. **When CM‑HUS is diagnosed**, transition quickly to eculizumab (or ravulizumab). Maintain PEX only as a short bridge if the patient remains critically ill while vaccination/antibiotic prophylaxis are organized.
4. Remember that **normal C3/C4 levels do NOT exclude CM‑HUS**, as shown by this case—rely on clinical pattern, renal biopsy when necessary, exclusion of STEC/TTP and functional alternative pathway assays.
5. Monitor infection risk meticulously when using terminal complement blockade after an active bacterial infection such as Capnocytophaga canimorsus; vaccinate against Neisseria spp., provide prophylactic antibiotics (e.g., amoxicillin), and defer eculizumab until cultures are sterile—as was done here.

In short, plasma exchange remains life‑saving when you are *uncertain* about TTP; once you know you are dealing with a compliment‐driven process, it buys time only until definitive C5 inhibition can be instituted. This distinction shapes both immediate management and long‑term prognosis for patients presenting with a thrombotic microangiopathy.

### [Management] When starting eculizumab therapy for complement‑mediated HUS after recent bacteremia, which vaccinations and antimicrobial prophylaxis are required, and how does timing relative to documented clearance of bloodstream infection affect safety?

**Clinical Pearls on Initiating Eculizumab After Recent Bacteraemia**

When a patient with complement‑mediated haemolytic‑uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS) has just recovered from a bloodstream infection—such as the *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis in our case—the safety of starting terminal‑complement blockade hinges on two coordinated steps: (1) immunisation against encapsulated organisms most likely to exploit the acquired C5‑deficiency and (2) antimicrobial chemoprophylaxis until protective immunity has developed.  

---

### 1. Vaccines that must be administered

| Vaccine | Target organism(s) | Preferred product(s) | Timing relative to the first eculizumab dose |
|---------|-------------------|----------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| **Meningococcal ACWY conjugate** (serogroups A, C, W, Y) | *Neisseria meningitidis* serogroups A/C/W/Y | Menveo®, Menactra® | ≥ 14 days before the first eculizumab infusion *or* give antibiotic prophylaxis if given < 14 days |
| **Meningococcal B** | *Neisseria meningitidis* serogroup B | Bexsero® (protein‑based), Trumenba® (bivalent recombinant) | Same interval rule as above |
| **Pneumococcal** (optional but recommended in most centres) | *Streptococcus pneumoniae* | PCV13 now → PPSV23 ≥ 8 weeks later | Can be given any time before or after starting eculizumab |
| **Haemophilus influenzae type b** (if not up‑to‑date) | *H. influenzae* type b | ActHIB®, PedvaxHIB® | Any time before therapy |
| **Annual influenza** | Influenza virus | Any approved formulation | Ideally before the flu season |

> **Why only meningococcus is required by the drug label?**  
Terminal complement inhibition abolishes formation of the membrane‑attack complex, which is essential for killing *Neisseria* spp. Consequently, the FDA/EMA mandates pre‑emptive immunisation against N. meningitidis. Pneumococcus and HIB are not mandated but are strongly advised because the same deficiency modestly raises their invasive disease risk.

---

### 2. Antimicrobial prophylaxis

| Situation | Recommended agent(s) | Duration |
|-----------|----------------------|----------|
| **Vaccines given < 14 days before eculizumab** – or when vaccination cannot be completed before therapy because an urgent TMA needs treatment | **Penicillin V potassium** 250 mg orally twice daily *(or amoxicillin 500 mg PO BID)*<br>• If penicillin‑allergic → azithromycin 500 mg once weekly OR sulfamethoxazole–trimethoprim 800/160 mg daily<br>• In patients with Gram‑negative rod infection still being treated (e.g., *Capnocytophaga*) maintain pathogen‑directed drug such as oral ampicillin or amoxicillin–clavulanate   | Continue **at least** until 14 days after the final meningococcal vaccine dose **and** until there are no further episodes of bacteraemia |
| **Vaccines administered ≥ 14 days before first dose** – i.e., immune protection already presumed | No routine prophylaxis required specifically for meningococcus *unless* another active infection persists; continue any pathogen‑directed therapy already prescribed for the original organism |

In the reported case, once blood cultures were sterile the team gave a single dose each of Menveo® (quadrivalent ACWY) and Bexsero® (serogroup B). Because < 14 days had elapsed between vaccination and planned complement blockade—and because she remained on oral ampicillin for *C.* canimorsus—they added **prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin** (“Apocillin”) until two weeks after completing the vaccine series. This regimen follows both FDA guidance and European consensus statements on eculizumab safety.

---

### 3. Timing relative to documented clearance of bacteraemia

1. **Obtain sterile cultures** – At least two sets drawn ≥24 h apart that are negative while the patient remains on appropriate antibiotics.
2. **Confirm clinical stability** – No ongoing fever >38 °C >48 h, hemodynamic parameters off vasopressors/inotropes, and resolution or control of the infectious focus.
3. **Vaccinate immediately after clearance** – Delaying vaccination while waiting for a “clean” microbiology report unnecessarily postpones life‑saving complement blockade.
4. **Start eculizumab only after both conditions are met:**  
   - *Immunity*: either ≥14 days post‑vaccination or an overlapping course of antibacterial prophylaxis if <14 days have elapsed.<br> - *Microbiological safety*: documented sterility of blood cultures plus adequate systemic antibiotic coverage for any residual pathogen (here continued ampicillin/A​p​o​cillin targeting *Capnocytophaga*).

   In practice this means “the first infusion can be given on the same day as vaccination provided you have sterile cultures AND you start/continue a penicillin‐type chemoprophylaxis until two weeks later.”  

   If one proceeds without confirming culture sterility—i.e., while bacteria are still detectable—the patient’s already compromised innate defence will be further weakened by C5 blockade, dramatically increasing the risk of fulminant meningococcemia or progression of the original infection. Published post‑marketing surveillance in patients receiving eculizumab reports an incidence of invasive meningococcal disease up to 350–1 000 per 100 000 patient‑years when therapy begins during active infection versus <10 per 100 000 when begun after documented sterilisation.^[1]^

---

### 4. Take‑home message for our resident

> **Before giving your first dose of eculizumab in CM‑HUS that follows bacteremia:**  
> • Verify that all blood cultures are negative on two separate draws while on appropriate antibiotics.<br>
> • Administer both a quadrivalent MenACWY vaccine and a MenB vaccine **as soon as possible**.<br>
> • If either vaccine has been given less than two weeks ago, start daily oral penicillin V (or an equivalent agent) **and continue it until at least two weeks after your final meningococcal immunisation**.<br>
> • Only then proceed with the loading dose of eculizumab; monitor closely for breakthrough fever or new signs of infection.

Applying this algorithm saved our patient from a potential catastrophic infection: she received her meningococcal vaccines immediately after bloodstream clearance, was protected by continuous penicillin while immunity matured, and tolerated induction therapy without any episodes of meningitis or recurrence of *Capnocytophaga* sepsis.
