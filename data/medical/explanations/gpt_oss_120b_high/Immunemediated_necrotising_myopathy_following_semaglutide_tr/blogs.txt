Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# GLP‑1 Receptor Agonists Unmasked: First Report of Semaglutide‑Associated Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy  

## Why This Case Turns Heads  
When you think “GLP‑1 receptor agonist,” nausea, vomiting and mild weight loss are the first red flags that spring to mind—not a severe autoimmune myopathy that lands a patient in the ICU and forces you into high‑dose steroids plus IVIG. This report describes exactly that scenario—a single subcutaneous dose of semaglutide was followed weeks later by progressive dysphagia, dysphonia and crippling proximal weakness culminating in a creatine kinase (CK) surge close to 4 000 U/L. Muscle biopsy revealed classic necrotising changes with macrophage infiltrates—immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). The patient recovered after a brief course of immunosuppression and strict avoidance of all GLP‑1 agonists. It is the first documented link between semaglutide and IMNM and forces us to broaden our safety net when prescribing these agents.

## Semaglutide & Its Usual Side‑Effect Profile  
Semaglutide (commercially Wegovy®/Ozempic®) has become one of the most prescribed glucagon‑like peptide‐1 receptor agonists worldwide—for type 2 diabetes and obesity alike. Its efficacy is undeniable; real‑world data show prescriptions now numbering in the millions globally each year. The safety profile that most clinicians wrestle with includes:

* **Gastrointestinal upset** – nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea (often dose‐related).  
* **Pancreatic concerns** – rare reports of pancreatitis or elevated amylase/lipase.  
* **Gallbladder disease** – increased cholelithiasis risk especially during rapid weight loss.  
* **Thyroid tumours** – rodent‐specific medullary thyroid carcinoma signal leads to black‐box warnings in humans.  
* **Lean mass reduction** – modest loss of skeletal muscle has been observed secondary to calorie restriction, not a primary toxic effect.  

These events are well catalogued in product monographs and pharmacovigilance databases. What’s missing is any notion that these drugs could spark an autoimmune attack on muscle fibers—until now.

### Muscle Toxicity Goes Beyond Lean Mass Loss  
Two distinct entities have been reported in association with GLP‑1–based therapies:

| Entity | Typical Presentation | Temporal Pattern | Key Laboratory Feature |
|--------|----------------------|------------------|------------------------|
| **Drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis** | Acute muscle pain + dark urine | Hours–days after exposure | Explosive CK rise (often >10 000 U/L) that falls rapidly once the offending agent is stopped |
| **Immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy** | Progressive proximal weakness ± dysphagia/dysphonia | Days–months after exposure | Sustained CK elevation (often >1000 U/L) despite drug withdrawal; requires steroid/IVIG therapy |

A recent tirzepatide case highlighted classic rhabdomyolysis—CK spiked past 57 000 U/L and normalized within days after hydration and drug cessation. By contrast, our semaglutide patient displayed an insidious rise from a modest 337 U/L on admission up to nearly 4000 U/L over four months—a pattern more compatible with an immune process rather than simple toxicity.

## The Case In Detail  

A woman in her early sixties with obesity, non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), hypertension and hypothyroidism received her inaugural subcutaneous semaglutide injection (0.25 mg). Within hours she vomited repeatedly, developed metabolic acidosis and acute kidney injury requiring brief ICU stay for non‑invasive ventilation. Initial labs showed a modest CK elevation (337 U/L) without myoglobinuria or rash; troponin was normal despite an episode of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

After discharge she appeared deconditioned but stable—until four months later when she could no longer sit upright without assistance and complained of trouble swallowing liquids and solids. Admission labs now revealed a striking CK elevation (~3992 U/L). Neurology initiated pulse methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily ×5 days) followed by oral prednisone (~1 mg/kg/day). Rheumatology added intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at a total dose of 2 g/kg split over four days because her proximal weakness remained severe despite steroids alone.

A comprehensive workup showed:

* **Serology:** Negative ANA, anti‑SSA/A, anti‐DNA, ANCA, anti‐mitochondrial antibodies; full myositis panel negative for SRP, HMGCR and all other canonical autoantibodies; low‐titer isolated anti‐SSB positivity (<3× upper limit).
* **Inflammatory markers:** Mildly elevated ESR (22 mm/h) and CRP (22.8 mg/L).
* **Cardiac workup:** Slight troponin rise (62 pg/mL) and BNP elevation (~4350 pg/mL); cardiac MRI showed fleeting myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement—compatible with mild myocarditis versus stress cardiomyopathy.
* **Imaging:** Bilateral thigh MRI demonstrated symmetric edema involving proximal vastus lateralis/quadriceps muscles plus subcutaneous edema.
* **Biopsy:** Vastus lateralis specimen exhibited scattered necrotic/regenerative fibers punctuated by CD68⁺ macrophages with complement membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotic and intact fibers—a hallmark pattern consistent with IMNM rather than pure toxic myopathy.
* **Liver:** Repeat biopsy confirmed progression of steatosis but no new inflammatory component—suggesting persistently hepatic transaminase elevations were unrelated to muscle injury.

The patient’s CK fell dramatically over two weeks after steroids+IVIG initiation. She was discharged after almost four weeks inpatient care, completed a brief taper of prednisone over two months, avoided further IVIG due to cost considerations—and remained off all glucagon‑like peptide‐1 agents throughout recovery. Ten months later she returned to her pre‑illness functional baseline without any immunosuppressive therapy or CK rebound.

## Applying WHO–Uppsala Causality Criteria  

The World Health Organization–Uppsala Monitoring Centre framework classifies adverse drug reactions into six categories ranging from certain → unlikely → unassessable.^[WHO–UMC] For this patient:

| Criterion | Assessment |
|-----------|------------|
| **Temporal relationship** | Symptoms began hours after drug exposure; progressive myopathy manifested over subsequent months—a plausible latency for immune activation |
| **Dechallenge** | Discontinuation of semaglutide coincided with clinical improvement once immunosuppression was instituted |
| **Alternative explanations** | No statin exposure, no relevant supplements; exhaustive autoantibody panel negative; imaging ruled out structural causes |
| **Rechallenge** | Ethically prohibitive given severity |
| **Dose–response relationship** | Not applicable—the reaction occurred after a single low dose |
| **Previous reports** | Prior tirzepatide rhabdomyolysis exists; this is the inaugural semaglutide–IMNM description |

Collectively these elements satisfy WHO–UMC’s “probable” causality category—strong enough to alert prescribers while acknowledging absolute certainty remains elusive without rechallenge data.

## Management Strategy That Worked  

The therapeutic approach hinged on three pillars:

1️⃣ **Immediate cessation** of any GLP‑1 agonist—eliminates continued antigenic stimulation if one exists.  
2️⃣ **High‑dose glucocorticoids** – pulse methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone slowed necrosis while allowing regeneration on histologic level.^[EM]  
3️⃣ **Adjunctive IVIG** – administered early because sarcolemmal complement deposition suggested antibody‐mediated complement activation.^[JDM]  

Remarkably, once steroids were tapered off within two months—and IVIG limited to one course—the patient never required long‐term immunomodulators such as azathioprine or rituximab—a pattern more reminiscent of drug‐triggered forms of IMNM rather than idiopathic seronegative disease which often mandates sustained multidrug regimens.^[NEJM]

The brief septic episode secondary to pseudomonal UTI post-discharge did NOT provoke a CK flare—supporting remission rather than ongoing active disease at that point.

## Clinical Pearls & Bottom Line  

Below are actionable takeaways you can slot into your daily practice when prescribing GLP‑1 receptor agonists:

### Key Take‑Home Points
- **Think beyond GI upset** – New-onset proximal weakness or dysphagia weeks–months after starting any GLP‑1 agonist warrants prompt evaluation.
- **Check a baseline CK** before initiating therapy in patients who already have risk factors for myopathy (e.g., prior statin use, autoimmune predisposition).
- **Recheck CK** if unexplained muscle symptoms develop; persistent elevation (>1000 U/L) should trigger neurology/rheumatology referral.
- **Distinguish rhabdomyolysis vs IMNM** – Rapidly rising CK (>10 000) with pain/urine discoloration leans toward toxic rhabdo; gradual rise plus marked weakness suggests an immune process.
- **Muscle biopsy remains gold standard** when IMNM is suspected—look for necrosis plus macrophage infiltration with complement membrane attack complex deposition.
- **Stopping the offending agent can be curative**, often combined with a short steroid taper ± one course of IVIG.
- **Document & report** any suspected drug‐induced myopathy through FDA MedWatch or your national pharmacovigilance system—early signals guide future labeling updates.
- **Stay vigilant as usage explodes** – With millions receiving semaglutide annually worldwide, rare adverse events become clinically relevant sooner rather than later.

In short, while semaglutide continues reshaping diabetes care and weight management paradigms, it may also have a hidden capacity to incite autoimmune muscle injury in susceptible individuals. Early recognition saves patients from prolonged disability—and reminds us that even celebrated drugs can harbor surprising dark sides when viewed through an immunologic lens. Keep your differential broad, your labs handy, and your pharmacovigilance reflex sharp!



# Rhabdomyolysis or Autoimmune Necrotising Myopathy? A Practical Diagnostic Roadmap After GLP‑1 Therapy  

## Why This Case Grabs Your Attention  

GLP‑1 receptor agonists have exploded onto the obesity/diabetes scene—and most clinicians think of nausea, vomiting, and weight loss when something goes wrong. The first published case linking a GLP‑1 agonist (semaglutide) to **immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)** flips that script upside down. The distinction isn’t academic: toxic rhabdomyolysis dissolves with fluids, whereas IMNM requires high‑dose steroids ± IVIG and a prolonged immunosuppressive strategy. Missing the diagnosis can condemn a patient to months of debility or expose them unnecessarily to aggressive immunotherapy when simple hydration would have sufficed.  

The story also reminds us how easy it is to chalk a rising creatine kinase (CK) up to “drug‐induced” toxicity without looking deeper at pattern, exam findings, or pathology. Let’s walk through how you can dissect that knot when a patient presents after starting—or even taking a *single* dose of—a GLP‑1 agonist.

## The Index Patient in Brief  

* Female, mid‑60s with obesity, NAFLD, hypertension & hypothyroidism  
* Initiated **semaglutide 0·25 mg SC** for weight loss → hours later N/V → hospitalized 2 days later for metabolic acidosis, AKI & mild CK rise (**337 U/L**, ULN ≈ 190).  
* Discharged after five days once volume status corrected ­– GI symptoms resolved but she remained deconditioned.  
* Over **four months** she developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia and an alarming surge in CK (**peak ≈ 4 000 U/L**) coupled with marked elevations of AST/ALT that tracked her underlying steatosis rather than muscle breakdown alone.  
* Autoimmune serology was negative for HMGCR, SRP and all classic myositis antibodies; low‐titre SS‑B (+2·4) was an isolated finding.  
* MRI of thighs showed symmetric STIR hyperintensity in proximal quadriceps—a pattern more suggestive of inflammatory myopathy than pure toxin‐induced injury.  
* Quadriceps biopsy demonstrated necrotic/regenerating fibres with CD68⁺ macrophages, membrane attack complex deposition on sarcolemma and sparse CD3⁺ T cells — classic **IMNM** histology despite seronegativity.  
* Treated promptly with pulse methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone **(≈ 1 mg/kg)** plus one course of IVIG (**2 g/kg over four days**).  
* After ~4 weeks inpatient rehab she returned toward baseline strength; steroids were tapered over two months and she has remained off immunosuppression ≥ 8 months while avoiding all GLP‑1 agonists entirely.

## What We Know About GLP‑1 Agonists & Muscle Injury  

* **Toxic rhabdomyolysis** has been reported occasionally (e.g., tirzepatide–associated cases where CK rose into “the thousands” rapidly after continuous dosing). These patients typically improve within days once hydration restored volume status and the offending drug was stopped.  
* **Skeletal muscle mass loss** has been observed chronically on imaging studies among long‐term users; mechanisms may involve altered protein synthesis pathways rather than overt necrosis.  
* The current semaglutide‐IMNM report appears mechanistically distinct—it presents *delayed*, shows *progressive* weakness plus *bulbar involvement*, harbours *biopsy evidence of immune activation*, and improves only after targeted immunosuppression—not merely fluid resuscitation.

Understanding these divergent phenotypes guides our workup when faced with an elevated CK after GLP‑1 exposure.

## Toxic Rhabdomyolysis vs Immune‐Mediated Necrotising Myopathy  

| Feature | Toxic Rhabdomyolysis | Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy |
|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | Minutes–hours after exposure or intense exertion | Days–weeks after exposure; may be delayed even after a *single* dose |
| **CK trajectory** | Abrupt rise (>10× ULN) → peak within ≤48 h → rapid fall once the toxin removed + aggressive fluids | Steady upward trend over days–weeks (+12× ULN possible) despite drug cessation unless immunotherapy instituted |
| **Typical symptoms** | Muscle pain/tenderness, dark urine (“cola”), generalized fatigue | Proximal weakness predominates; dysphagia/dysphonia common when cricopharyngeal muscles involved |
| **Renal impact** | Acute tubular necrosis common secondary to myoglobinuria | May develop AKI secondary to volume depletion or comorbidities but not driven by massive myoglobin release |
| **Lab clues** | Elevated serum potassium/phosphate/additional metabolic derangements common | Mildly elevated ESR/CRP possible; transaminases may rise secondary to muscle breakdown—but patterns often mirror hepatic disease |
| **Biopsy** | Diffuse fibre necrosis without significant inflammatory infiltrate; minimal complement deposition | Necrotic/regenerating fibres + macrophage infiltration (CD68⁺), MAC positivity on sarcolemma; sparse T cells supportive |

These distinctions frame each decision node below.



## Step‑by‑Step Diagnostic Roadmap  

### Step 1 – Document Exposure & Initial Presentation  

Ask specifically about ***any*** GLP‑1 agonist taken within the past weeks—including “one‐off” trials under research protocols or insurance pre‑authorizations that result in asymptomatic dosing errors. Capture associated gastrointestinal upset or hypovolaemia events—as happened here—with nausea/vomiting leading directly to AKI.


### Step 2 – Chart Your Patient’s CK Kinetics  

Serial measurements are essential:

* **Acute spike >10× ULN within <48 h:** Lean toward classic toxic rhabdo → prioritize volume resuscitation.
* **Progressive rise over ≥72 h despite hydration:** Triggers suspicion for an immune process especially if accompanied by worsening strength.<br>
In our patient initial `337 U/L` rose stealthily over four months culminating near `4000 U/L`. That trend alone demanded further workup beyond fluids.



### Step 3 – Perform a Targeted Neuromuscular Exam  

Look beyond generalized aches:

* Rapidly evolving proximal weakness (`≥MRC grade ≤3` shoulders/hips) suggests inflammatory myopathy.
* Bulbar signs (`dysphagia`, `dysphonia`) are atypical for pure toxin injury yet hallmark features of many IMNMs—including HMGCR/SRP seropositive disease.
* Absence of overt tenderness may help differentiate—rhabdo often feels sore when pressed.



### Step 4 – Order Baseline Lab Adjuncts  

Even though seronegative IMNMs exist (~⅓), start broad screening:

* **Myositis panel:** Include HMGCR & SRP antibodies—it’s cheap enough now that ruling them out still leaves room for seronegative disease.
* **Inflammatory markers:** ESR/CRP modestly uptrending strengthen an immune hypothesis.
* **Thyroid profile:** Exclude hypothyroid myopathy—a low-hanging fruit.
* **Renal panel & electrolytes:** Needed regardless—for AKI monitoring.



### Step 5 – Image the Muscles Early  

A thigh MRI performed within days serves two purposes:

* STIR/T2 hyperintensity *symmetrically* involving proximal musculature aligns best with inflammatory pathology.
* Focal eccentric hyperintensity confined solely to one compartment would fit compartment syndrome/rhabdo sequelae better.<br>
Our patient’s MRI showed diffuse edema across vastus lateralis/quadriceps together with superficial subcutaneous changes—a pattern fitting necrotising inflammation.



### Step 6 – Decide Whether You Need an Immediate Muscle Biopsy  

Biopsy indications are clear when any *red flag* persists:

* Rising CK >6–8 weeks despite drug withdrawal & aggressive fluid resuscitation.
* Negative autoantibody panel combined with progressive functional decline.
* Concerning bulbar involvement suggesting systemic disease.<br>
A prompt open or needle biopsy will differentiate “pure” necrosis from immune cell infiltrates—the former steering away from prolonged steroids whilst confirming an autoimmune process when appropriate.



### Step 7 – Initiate Management Tailored to Etiology  

| Scenario | First Line |
|---|---|
| **Toxic Rhadomyolysis** | Aggressive isotonic fluid bolus ➜ goal urine output ≥200–300 mL/hr <br>Consider bicarbonate infusion if severe acidosis/high risk of calcium phosphate precipitation.<br>Discontinue offending drug permanently—or pause if benefits outweigh risks.* |
| **Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy** | Immediate cessation of GLP‑1 agent <br>High‐dose IV methylprednisolone (`1000 mg ×5 d`) followed by oral prednisone (~`1 mg/kg`) <br>IVIG (`2 g/kg` split over four days) especially when rapid functional recovery needed <br>If relapse/tapering challenges arise → add steroid-sparing agents (azathioprine/methotrexate/mycophenolate).<br>Supportive care includes physical therapy early since deconditioning worsens outcome.|

*(Rechallenge is unethical once an adverse immune reaction has occurred.)*

### Step 8 – Follow-Up & Taper Strategy  

Monitor serum CK *weekly* until normalization then space out testing every month during steroid tapering—a pragmatic cadence gleaned from our patient’s outpatient course where an early plateau allowed rapid prednisone withdrawal over two months without rebound elevation.


### Step 9 – Document Causality For Pharmacovigilance  

Applying WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre criteria can help institutions flag similar cases prospectively:

| Criterion | Our Case |
|---|---|
| Temporal relationship | Symptoms began within hours of first semaglutide dose + delayed progressive weakness thereafter |
| Alternative explanations excluded | No statins/resident supplements/relevant infections preceding onset |
| Dechallenge plausible response | Weakness improved markedly once drug stopped + immunotherapy instituted |
| Rechallenge avoided ethically appropriate |

Labeling this as “probable” raises awareness among prescribers who might otherwise dismiss future similar presentations as benign side effects.



## Key Take‑Home Messages  

- **Don’t stop at “drug‐induced.”** A rising CK over weeks plus proximal/bulbar weakness mandates assessment for *immune-mediated necrotising myopathy,* even when classic autoantibodies are negative.  
- **Serial CK trends beat single values.* A modest elevation soon after exposure may mask an evolving autoimmune process that peaks later.*  
- **MRI does more than rule out structural lesions.* Symmetric proximal STIR hyperintensity points toward inflammatory myopathy versus focal edema seen in trauma/rhabdo.*  
- **Muscle biopsy remains definitive.* When autoantibodies are absent and clinical suspicion persists, obtain tissue before committing patients to long‐term immunosuppression.*  
- **High-dose steroids ± IVIG can reverse disease quickly.* Our patient’s functional recovery occurred within weeks following pulse methylprednisolone/IVIG—a reminder that early immunotherapy changes outcomes.*  
- **Avoid re-exposure outright.* Once an adverse immune event has manifested you cannot ethically rechallenge—the risk outweighs any metabolic benefit.*  
- **Use causality frameworks.* Labeling reactions as “probable” helps collect safety data critical amid expanding GLP‑1 agonist use.*  
- **Plan follow-up rigorously.* Weekly CK checks during acute treatment followed by monthly surveillance during taper guard against relapse.*

---

When you see an elderly obese patient walk through your door clutching their throat while their labs scream “CK ∼4000,” remember that not every peak comes from dehydration alone—and occasionally a single injection enough triggers a full-blown autoimmune showdown backstage. Spotting it early leads straight into life-changing therapy rather than an endless loop of hydration cycles.\

# Seronegative Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy: When Muscle Biopsy Takes Center Stage  

---

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

We all know GLP‑1 receptor agonists like semaglutide as the “new darling” of obesity and type‑2 diabetes therapy. Nausea, vomiting and occasional gallstone formation are the textbook side‑effects you warn patients about—​muscle disease isn’t on most checklists. Yet here we have a 60‑year‑old woman who received **one single dose** of semaglutide and subsequently spiralled into a classic picture of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). Her autoantibody work‐up was completely negative—no HMGCR, no SRP, nothing—and yet her muscle **biopsy** lit up with the hallmarks of an autoimmune attack.  

The bottom line? When you’re faced with a seronegative myopathy that refuses to resolve on hydration alone, **muscle biopsy** isn’t just an option—it’s the decisive test that prevents misdiagnosis, mistreatment, and potentially permanent disability.  

---

## A quick refresher on IMNM  

Immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy sits at the intersection of classic inflammatory myopathies and pure toxic rhabdomyolysis:

* **Clinical phenotype** – Subacute–acute proximal weakness (often severe), dysphagia/dysphonia, CK often > 5 × ULN – occasionally reaching > 10 000 U/L.
* **Serology** – About two thirds of patients harbour either anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP antibodies. The remaining ~30 % are **seronegative**, making diagnosis tougher.
* **Pathology** – Patchy necrosis with regenerating fibres, brisk macrophage infiltrate (CD68⁺), complement membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition on intact and necrotic fibres, and only **sparse CD3⁺ T cells**.
* **Triggers** – Statins are the classic culprit in anti‑HMGCR disease; other drugs—including now GLP‑1R agonists—are emerging suspects.
* **Outcome** – Those who receive early immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids ± IVIG) generally recover better than those treated as “pure toxic” rhabdomyolysis where steroids are rarely used.

---

## The patient’s story in chronological snapshots  

| Timeline | Key events |
|----------|------------|
| **3 years pre‑presentation** | NAFLD diagnosed on liver biopsy → lifestyle counseling only |
| **2½ years later** | Initiated semaglutide 0.25 mg SC |
| **Hours after first dose** | Nausea/vomiting → dehydration → AKI & metabolic acidosis → ICU admission briefly |
| **Admission labs** | CK 337 U/L (mildly elevated), AST/ALT ~2×ULN |
| **Discharge** | GI symptoms resolved, deconditioned baseline |
| **Months 0–4 post‑dose** | Progressive proximal weakness → dysphagia/dysphonia → CK surges to 3 992 U/L |
| **Hospitalization 1** | Pulse methylprednisolone → oral prednisone 1 mg/kg → IVIG 2 g/kg over 4 days → MRI‐guided vastus lateralis biopsy confirms IMNM |
| **Post‐discharge course** | Rapid CK decline, functional recovery despite brief steroid taper; no further IVIG needed |
| **10 months later** | Baseline functional status restored; off immunosuppression for > 7 months |

No prior statin use or exposure to other known “myotoxic” supplements was identified—the temporal relationship with semaglutide remains strikingly tight.

---

## Lab workup & imaging: Helpful but not decisive  

* **CK trajectory:** modest rise during the initial AKI episode (337 U/L) followed by a dramatic increase months later (≈ 4000 U/L). This biphasic pattern argues against simple drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis where CK spikes acutely then falls rapidly after hydration.
* **Autoantibodies:** Negative comprehensive myositis panel—including HMGCR, SRP, Jo‐1 etc.—leaving us in the seronegative zone.
* **MRI thigh:** Symmetric edema in proximal vastus lateralis/quadriceps guided us toward a well‐vascularized biopsy site—critical because patchy involvement can lead to false‐negative pathology if sampling error occurs.
* **Cardiac workup:** Mild myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement suggested transient stress cardiomyopathy rather than overt myocarditis—a useful reminder that systemic inflammation can involve more than skeletal muscle.
* **Liver enzymes:** Persistently modest AST/ALT elevations were attributed to underlying steatohepatitis rather than ongoing muscle breakdown.

Overall these studies painted an incomplete picture until we turned to histology.

---

## Muscle biopsy – where the diagnosis materialised  

The right vastus lateralis sample showed several microscopic clues that together form the *textbook* pattern of IMNM:

1. **Necrotic & regenerating fibers** scattered throughout—typical of an active destructive process rather than chronic atrophy.
2. **CD68⁺ macrophages** coating necrotic fibers (“myophagocytosis”). This macrophage sea is one of the most reliable pillars distinguishing IMNM from polymyositis.
3. **Membrane attack complex (C5b–9) deposition** along sarcolemmal membranes both on necrotic and apparently healthy fibers—evidence of complement‐mediated injury which is absent in pure toxic rhabdomyolysis.
4. Only *rare CD3⁺ T cells* perivascularly—contrasting sharply with polymyositis where dense endomysial CD8⁺ infiltrates invade non‑necrotic fibers.
5. *Patchy inflammatory architecture*: focal perivascular cuffs but no endomysial lymphocytic “conspiracy”.

These findings clinch an autoimmune process despite negative serology—a perfect illustration that pathology can be your decisive ally when blood tests are silent.

---

## How does seronegative IMNM differ from its mimics?  

| Feature | Seronegative IMNM | Polymyositis | Drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis / Toxic myopathy |
|--------|-------------------|--------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| Onset | Subacute–acute over weeks–months | Insidious over months | Typically within days of exposure |
| CK peak | Often >5× ULN but may be moderate | Usually <10× ULN | Can exceed 50× ULN (>40 000 U/L) |
| Histology | Necrosis + macrophages + MAC • Sparse T cells | Endomysial CD8⁺ T cells invading non‑necrotic fibers • No MAC | Massive necrosis ± minimal inflammation • No MAC deposits |
| Response to hydration alone | Poor | Irrelevant (immune) | Excellent—CK normalises within days |
| Immunosuppression needed? | Yes – usually high‑dose steroids ± IVIG/other agents | Yes – steroids +/- steroid-sparing agents | Generally not indicated |

In other words:  
*If you see MAC on sarcolemma plus a sea of CD68⁺ macrophages while your T cells are huddled in corners—that’s IMNM waving its flag.*  

Conversely, a “pure toxic” picture will lack complement deposition and will resolve quickly once the offending drug is stopped—and muscle weakness usually recovers without steroids.  

---

## Treatment pearls from this case  

* **Induction:** Pulse methylprednisolone 1 g/day × 3–5 days followed by oral prednisone ~1 mg/kg/day is standard first line even when antibodies are absent.
* **IVIG:** Adding 2 g/kg split over 4–5 days can accelerate functional recovery especially when dysphagia or respiratory involvement is present—or when rapid steroid taper is desired due to infection risk.
* **Monitoring:** Serial CK every 1–2 weeks during induction helps gauge biochemical response; MRI can be repeated if clinical suspicion persists despite normalized CK.
* **Taper strategy:** Because this was likely a drug‐triggered phenomenon rather than idiopathic chronic IMNM, an aggressive taper (over 6–8 weeks) was feasible once CK normalized and strength returned.
* **Avoid rechallenge:** Ethical considerations preclude reexposing patients to presumptive culprits—in this scenario that meant permanently discontinuing all GLP‑1R agonists.
* **Adjunctive agents:** In seropositive cases refractory to steroids/IVIG one might bring in methotrexate or rituximab; however many seronegatives resolve with steroids/IVIG alone once the trigger is withdrawn.

---

## Practical takeaways for clinicians  

1️⃣ When a patient develops subacute proximal weakness *after initiating any new medication*, think beyond direct toxicity—especially if CK trends don’t follow the classic rapid rise/fall curve seen with rhabdomyolysis.  

2️⃣ Order an MRI early if you suspect an inflammatory myopathy—it will locate active edema and guide you toward an optimal biopsy site while sparing you from blind sampling errors.  

3️⃣ If autoantibody panels come back negative yet clinical suspicion remains high, proceed directly to muscle biopsy—don’t let a “seronegative” label lull you into diagnostic inertia.  

4️⃣ Remember that GLP‑1R agonists are now widely prescribed for obesity weight loss—not just diabetes—and emerging post‑marketing surveillance suggests they could act as novel triggers for immune-mediated muscle injury. Reporting such events through MedWatch or your national pharmacovigilance system helps build an evidence base faster than waiting for another case report three years down the line.  

5️⃣ Early escalation to steroids ± IVIG—even before definitive pathology returns—can prevent irreversible loss of swallowing function or respiratory compromise while you await tissue confirmation.  

6️⃣ Finally, if your patient responds dramatically after a short course of immunosuppression *and* withdrawal of the suspect drug, you’re likely dealing with drug-associated IMNM rather than classic idiopathic disease—which often means a shorter overall treatment duration and better prognosis.

---

## Key Take‑Home Points  

- Seronegative immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy can present after just one dose of a GLP‑1 receptor agonist such as semaglutide—high vigilance is required now that these drugs are ubiquitous.  
- A normal autoantibody panel does *not* rule out IMNM; muscle biopsy remains the gold standard when clinical suspicion persists.  
- Histopathologic hallmarks = necrotic/regenerating fibers + abundant CD68⁺ macrophages + membrane attack complex deposition + only sparse CD3⁺ T cells—a pattern distinct from polymyositis and pure toxic rhabdomyolysis.  
- MRI should be used early to identify edematous muscle for targeted biopsy and to assess disease extent (including cardiac involvement).  
- Prompt immunosuppression (high‐dose steroids ± IVIG) coupled with removal of the offending agent yields rapid biochemical and functional recovery in drug‐associated cases—and often allows a much shorter taper than idiopathic seropositive IMNM requires.  
- Always report suspected drug‐induced autoimmune myopathies → pharmacovigilance data will shape future prescribing safety nets for increasingly popular agents like semaglutide.  

Stay curious, keep your biopsies ready, and remember: when the labs stay quiet but the muscles scream louder than ever, it’s time to let pathology do the talking!



# Short‑Course Steroids plus IVIG for Drug‑Triggered IMNM: How Aggressive Early Therapy Leads to Rapid Recovery  

---

## Why This Case Grabs Your Attention  

A woman in her 60s walked into the clinic after a single subcutaneous dose of semaglutide—a GLP‑1 receptor agonist that most of us prescribe for obesity or type 2 diabetes. Within hours she vomited, became volume‑depleted and required a brief ICU stay. Four months later she was unable to stand without assistance and was choking on liquids. Her CK had skyrocketed from a modest 337 U/L to > 3 900 U/L, her MRI showed diffuse thigh edema and a muscle biopsy proved immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).  

What makes this story a FOAM-worthy lightning bolt is that **the offending drug was identified after just one dose**, and aggressive early immunotherapy (pulse methylprednisolone + a single 2 g/kg IVIG course) allowed her to wean off steroids within two months and return to baseline after ten months—without any maintenance immunosuppression. In an era where “statin‑induced” necrotising myopathy often drags patients into years of rituximab, azathioprine or MMF, this case suggests that **drug‑triggered IMNM may be a reversible phenomenon** if we intervene swiftly and stop the culprit.

---

## Quick Primer on Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy  

- **Pathology** – Patchy necrotic fibers with scant lymphocytes but prominent macrophage infiltration (CD68+), complement deposition on non‑necrotic fibers, occasionally perivascular inflammation.  
- **Serology** – Strongly linked to anti‑HMGCR (often statin related) or anti‑SRP antibodies. Up to one‑third are seronegative, as in our patient who tested negative for both panels (including Jo‑1, PL‑7/12, Mi‑2 etc.).  
- **Clinical picture** – Subacute proximal weakness ± dysphagia/dysphonia; CK usually > 1 000 U/L but can be modest early on.  
- **Differential** – Polymyositis, inclusion body myositis, toxic rhabdomyolysis, metabolic myopathies. Muscle biopsy remains the gold standard when serology is non‐diagnostic.  

Semaglutide isn’t on anybody’s “dangerous list” for necrotising myopathy (yet), but GLP‑1 agonists have been linked with loss of lean mass and isolated rhabdomyolysis (tirzepatide case). The timeline in this report—an acute GI reaction followed by insidious progressive weakness—fits a **drug‐triggered autoimmune mechanism** rather than pure toxic injury.

---

## The Treatment Protocol That Worked  

| Intervention | Dose & Duration | Rationale |
|--------------|----------------|-----------|
| Intravenous methylprednisolone | 1 g/day × 5 days | Pulse dosing quickly suppresses cytokine storm and halts necrosis; classic regimen used in severe polymyositis/dermatomyositis and necrotising myopathies |
| Oral prednisone | 1 mg/kg/day (≈70 mg) → taper over ~8 weeks | Transition maintains immunosuppression while steroids are tapered; low enough to minimize diabetogenic effects in patients at risk |
| Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG) | 2 g/kg split over 4 days | Neutralizes pathogenic autoantibodies (even if undetectable), interferes with complement activation seen on biopsy, provides Fc‐mediated immunomodulation |
| Discontinuation of semaglutide | Immediate | Removes the inciting antigenic stimulus—critical step per WHO-Uppsala causality criteria |

The patient’s CK fell from almost 4 000 U/L to the normal range within two weeks, functional strength improved markedly after the first week of steroids/IVIG, and she was off all immunosuppression by eight weeks post‑discharge.

---

## Why Pulse Steroids + One Course of IVIG Makes Sense  

1. **Speed matters** – Necrotic fibers release DAMPs that amplify inflammation. Pulse steroids blunt this “second hit” before irreversible fibrosis sets in.  
2. **Seronegative disease** – Without a clear autoantibody target you can’t tailor therapy (e.g., bortezomib against HMGCR). Broad immunomodulation via high-dose steroids + IVIG covers multiple pathways.  
3. **IVIG’s complement blockade** – The biopsy showed membrane attack complex deposition on intact fibers; IVIG competitively inhibits C5b–9 formation—a direct mechanistic hit that steroids alone don’t address.  
4. **Avoidance of long‑term agents** – Chronic agents such as azathioprine or rituximab carry infection risk and cost burdens; if withdrawal of the offending drug leads to remission, short-course therapy is both safe and cost-effective.

---

## Tapering Strategy – When Less Is More  

The patient’s CK normalized rapidly and remained low even after stopping steroids early—a sign that the immune cascade had been extinguished once the trigger was removed. An outpatient taper plan looked roughly like:

- Weeks 1–2: Prednisone 1 mg/kg/day  
- Weeks 3–4: Reduce by 10–20 mg every 5–7 days, targeting ≤ 30 mg/day by week 4  
- Weeks 5–8: Further reductions by 5–10 mg every week until off  

Key points:

- **Monitor CK weekly while tapering** – A rise would cue a slower taper or repeat pulse steroids.  
- **Watch glucose** – High-dose steroids can unmask hyperglycemia; consider using basal insulin temporarily – especially relevant in patients who were being considered for GLP‑1 agonists originally for metabolic disease.  
- **Functional assessments** – Daily MRC strength checks or timed‐up‐and‐go tests help ensure clinical correlation with lab trends.

By week 8 she was steroid‐free without relapse—a timeline that contrasts sharply with classic HMGCR‐positive IMNM where many patients remain on prednisone ≥0.5 mg/kg for >6 months plus steroid-sparing agents.

---

## Monitoring & Red Flags  

| Parameter | Frequency | What It Tells You |
|-----------|-----------|-------------------|
| CK (total) | Weekly while on steroids → every 2–4 weeks after taper | Biochemical response; rising levels suggest relapse |
| CBC / BMP / LFTs | Baseline & weekly initially | Steroid side effects & drug toxicity |
| HbA1c / fasting glucose | Baseline & weekly during high-dose steroids | Steroid-induced hyperglycemia |
| Infectious workup if febrile or new symptoms | Promptly when indicated | Early detection of opportunistic infections (patient had pseudomonal UTI at day 12 post-discharge) |
| Dysphagia assessment (speech therapist) | At presentation & before discharge | Guides nutrition route & monitors bulbar improvement |
| Repeat MRI or EMG (if plateau) | Consider if CK plateaus > 6 weeks or strength stalls | Helps differentiate persistent inflammation from fibrosis |

In our patient’s follow-up visits her CK stayed normal, dysphagia resolved, and repeat cardiac MRI showed resolution of prior edema—confirming that aggressive early therapy prevented collateral organ involvement.

---

## Pitfalls of Prolonged Immunosuppression  

- **Infection cascade** – The same steroid exposure that blunted muscle injury also predisposed her to pseudomonal UTI requiring hospital readmission. Longer courses would have amplified this risk further.  
- **Metabolic derangement** – Chronic glucocorticoids exacerbate insulin resistance—particularly concerning when GLP‑1 analogues were initially prescribed for weight management/diabetes control.  
- **Cost & logistics** – IVIG is expensive (~$30–40K per course); repeating it without clear indication could bankrupt patients or exhaust institutional resources. Single short course appears sufficient when trigger removed early.  
- **Masking underlying disease** – A hasty taper without confirming drug removal could leave subclinical autoimmune activity unchecked; always corroborate CK trend with clinical improvement before stopping therapy.

---

## Putting It All Together – A Practical Algorithm  

1️⃣ **Suspect IMNM** when proximal weakness + CK > 1 000 U/L + limited serologic clues → proceed to muscle biopsy early.  

2️⃣ **Identify/removal of possible trigger** – Review medication list meticulously; stop any plausible offender immediately (e.g., statin, GLP‑1 agonist). Use WHO-Uppsala causality categories to document “probable”.  

3️⃣ **Start pulse methylprednisolone** 1 g daily × 5 days once severe disease confirmed (dysphagia/dysphonia = high morbidity).  

4️⃣ **Add IVIG** 2 g/kg within first week if biopsy shows complement deposition or if serology negative/unclear — particularly when rapid functional recovery is desired.  

5️⃣ **Transition to oral prednisone ≤ 1 mg/kg**, monitor CK daily → aim for < 500 U/L within 10–14 days before beginning taper.

6️⃣ **Taper aggressively** once CK normalizes *and* strength improves (>80% baseline). Target complete withdrawal within 6–8 weeks unless relapse signs appear.

7️⃣ **Long-term follow-up** every 3–6 months for at least a year—monitor CK even after stopping therapy because late relapses can occur after drug re-exposure or other triggers.

---

## Take‑Home Messages  

- A single dose of a GLP‑1 receptor agonist can act as an inciting antigen for immune-mediated necrotising myopathy—even in seronegative patients.
- Early discontinuation of the offending medication combined with pulse methylprednisolone *plus* a one-time 2 g/kg IVIG course can induce rapid remission.
- Serial CK monitoring guides both initial treatment response and taper speed; normalization often permits steroid cessation within weeks rather than months.
- Prolonged immunosuppression adds infection risk and metabolic burden without proven benefit in drug-triggered cases.
- Muscle biopsy remains essential when serology is negative—its histologic clues (macrophage infiltrate, complement deposition) inform choice of adjunctive therapies like IVIG.
- In the era of expanding use of GLP‑1 agonists and other novel metabolic drugs, maintain high suspicion for atypical adverse immune reactions—and remember that *aggressive early therapy* may spare patients long-term immunosuppressive morbidity.

--- 

*Stay curious.* When your next obese diabetic patient asks about semaglutide’s safety profile, you now have a compelling story that underscores both vigilance and the power of prompt, targeted therapy when rare immune complications arise.*



# CK, AST/ALT, Troponin—and the Mystery Muscle Patient: Interpreting Multi‑Organ Lab Elevations in Myositis  

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

When a 60‑year‑old woman walked into the clinic with new‐onset dysphagia, dysphonia and steep proximal weakness, the first thing most of us imagined was “classic polymyositis.” What shocked the team (and me) was that her laboratory picture looked like a three‑way traffic jam: a CK climbing toward 4 000 U/L, modestly elevated AST/ALT (~200 U/L) and a high‑sensitivity troponin that nudged just above the reference range. Add an echo showing mildly raised right‑ventricular systolic pressure and a cardiac MRI with patchy edema—suddenly you’re juggling liver disease, renal insult, a possible drug reaction, and possible myocarditis all at once.

The kicker? The only culprit medication she’d ever taken was a **single subcutaneous dose of semaglutide** (a GLP‑1 receptor agonist). She’d never been on statins or red‑yeast rice and her myositis panel was negative for the usual suspects (HMGCR, SRP). This isn’t your textbook rhabdomyolysis story; it’s an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) that looks like it might have been *triggered* by a drug we rarely associate with autoimmunity.  

That combination—multifactorial lab abnormalities paired with an atypical drug exposure—makes this an ideal teaching case for anyone who’s ever stared at discordant labs and wondered whether they’re looking at one organ in distress or several.  

## The lab puzzle: CK versus AST/ALT versus troponin  

| Test | Typical organ source | What it tells us in isolation | What it can *mislead* you into thinking |
|------|----------------------|--------------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **CK** | Skeletal muscle (also cardiac & brain isoforms) | Quantifies muscle cell breakdown → severe necrotising myopathy or rhabdomyolysis when >10× ULN | May be “normal” in early IMNM if necrosis is focal |
| **AST** | Liver mitochondria *and* skeletal muscle cytosol | Rises with any cell death that contains mitochondria → can mirror CK spikes | Often taken as hepatic injury when ALT remains normal |
| **ALT** | Primarily hepatocyte cytosol | More liver‑specific; modest elevations can still come from severe muscle damage | Overlooks muscle contributions when ALT < AST |
| **High‑sensitivity troponin I/T** | Cardiac myocytes (cTnI highly specific) | Gold standard for myocardial necrosis/inflammation → guides coronary work‑up | Small elevations may appear after intense skeletal injury (especially cTnT) or via cross‑reactivity in regenerating muscle |

In our patient:

* **CK** started at ~340 U/L (just above reference) during an acute GI illness and spiked to ~4 000 U/L four months later—a pattern more compatible with *progressive* necrotising myopathy than with an isolated toxin‐driven rhabdomyolysis episode.
* **AST/ALT** rose together (~190/165 U/L) while alkaline phosphatase remained normal – consistent with *muscle* release rather than cholestatic liver disease.
* **hs‑troponin** was marginally high (62 pg/mL vs ≤53). Coupled with mild RV pressure elevation on echo and myocardial edema on cardiac MRI—but no late gadolinium enhancement—the picture suggested *subclinical myocarditis* or stress cardiomyopathy rather than frank infarction.
* **Pro‑BNP** was markedly elevated (>4 000 pg/mL). In this context it likely reflected transient volume overload from acute kidney injury plus catecholamine surge rather than chronic heart failure.

The key lesson? None of these numbers live in isolation. Their trajectories, ratios, and the clinical backdrop tell the real story.

## How to sort out the source – a stepwise approach  

### 1️⃣ Follow the CK trend  
* **Rhabdomyolysis:** CK skyrockets (>10–20× ULN) within hours–days of insult then drops rapidly once the offending agent is removed and hydration started.  
* **Immune–mediated necrotising myopathy:** CK may start modestly and *progressively climb* over weeks–months despite removal of any suspected drug. Persistent elevation (>5× ULN) >2 weeks after presumed trigger should raise suspicion for IMNM rather than pure toxicity.  

In our case CK surged months after semaglutide exposure while she was off the drug—a red flag for autoimmunity.



### 2️⃣ Interpret transaminases through a “muscle–liver” lens  

* **AST>ALT** can hint at muscular origin because both enzymes exist in mitochondria—but AST is more abundant in skeletal muscle.  
* Look at *other liver markers*: normal alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin make pure hepatic injury less likely.  
* The “CK‑to‑AST ratio” is a handy bedside clue: When CK≫AST (e.g., >10:1), think muscle. Here CK rose into thousands while AST hovered around 200 U/L → disproportionate => muscular source dominates.  

### 3️⃣ Decipher troponin spikes  

* **cTnI** is almost exclusively cardiac; even tiny rises usually merit cardiac work‐up unless there’s clear skeletal cause such as massive rhabdomyolysis or muscular regeneration with ectopic expression of cardiac isoforms.
* **cTnT** can be expressed by regenerating skeletal fibers; modest elevations may occur in inflammatory myopathies without true myocarditis.
* Correlate troponin trends with ECG changes, echocardiography, and—if uncertainty persists—cardiac MRI.



### 4️⃣ Leverage imaging  

| Modality | What you’re looking for | How it informs lab interpretation |
|----------|------------------------|------------------------------------|
| **Muscle MRI (STIR/T2)** | Edema pattern, focal vs diffuse inflammation | Confirms active myopathy when CK stays high; guides biopsy site |
| **Cardiac MRI** | Myocardial edema on T2 mapping + LGE patterns | Differentiates myocarditis (edema + LGE), stress cardiomyopathy (edema without LGE), or innocent troponin leak |
| **Echocardiography** | Wall motion abnormalities, pulmonary pressures | Quick screen for functional impact of any myocardial involvement |

Our patient’s thigh MRI showed symmetric proximal edema—the classic “muscle inflammation” picture that dovetailed nicely with her rising CK. Cardiac MRI revealed edema without LGE → suggestive of *transient inflammation*, explaining her borderline troponin rise without overt ischemia.



### 5️⃣ Put it all together – timing matters  

| Timeline | Labs & Imaging | Interpretation |
|----------|----------------|----------------|
| **Day 0–2 after semaglutide dose** – nausea/vomiting → AKI → CK ≈ 337 U/L; AST/ALT mildly ↑ ; Troponin barely ↑.| Acute volume depletion + possible direct drug effect but no massive muscle breakdown.| Toxic reaction + early systemic stress—CK still low enough that we could dismiss serious myopathy at this point.|
| **Weeks 3–16** – progressive weakness → CK up to ~4 000 U/L ; AST/ALT ≈200 U/L ; Troponin ≈62 pg/mL.| Ongoing necrotising process despite drug cessation.| Autoimmune necrotising myopathy triggered (or unmasked) by semaglutide; secondary low‐grade myocardial inflammation plausible given imaging findings.|

## Putting it together in our patient  

1️⃣ **Semaglutide exposure** appears plausible as the trigger because the symptom onset followed its first dose and there were no other new medications or supplements.  
2️⃣ **CK trajectory**—modest rise early then steep climb over months—fits IMNM rather than classic drug toxicity which would have resolved quickly after hydration.  
3️⃣ **AST/ALT levels** tracked closely with CK while alkaline phosphatase stayed flat—pointing toward skeletal‐muscle origin rather than worsening NAFLD.  
4️⃣ **Troponin elevation** was borderline and accompanied by non‑definitive cardiac MRI changes—addressed conservatively while monitoring for evolving myocarditis.  
5️⃣ **Therapeutic response:** High‑dose steroids + IVIG drove rapid decline in both CK and transaminases, mirroring improvement seen in other drug‑associated IMNM reports where brief immunosuppression suffices once the offending agent is withdrawn.  

The net result? By month 10 she was back to baseline strength, off all immunosuppression, with normalized labs—a textbook recovery when you correctly identify *the right organ(s)* responsible for each biomarker glitch.



## Clinical pearls & take‑home points  

- **Never assume elevated AST/ALT equals liver disease** when CK is also high; check the ratio and look for concurrent alkaline phosphatase/GGT data.
- **Serial CK matters more than a single value**—a rising trend over weeks strongly suggests immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy rather than an acute toxic rhabdomyolysis.
- **Small hs‑troponin rises in inflammatory myopathies may be “muscle‐derived.”** Correlate with ECG/Echo/MRI before invoking coronary work‑up.
- **Cardiac MRI is your gold standard when troponin is discordant:** edema without late gadolinium enhancement generally indicates reversible inflammation rather than infarction.
- **Drug‐triggered IMNM tends to improve after brief immunosuppression plus withdrawal**, whereas idiopathic IMNM usually needs prolonged multi‑agent therapy.
- When faced with multi‑organ lab derangements:
  1. Plot each biomarker over time.
  2. Cross‑reference non‑hepatic enzymes (CK) against hepatic enzymes (AST/ALT).
  3. Use imaging targeted to each organ suspected.
  4. Reassess after removing any potential trigger before escalating therapy.
- Finally, keep a high index of suspicion that newer metabolic agents—including GLP‑1 agonists—might have rare autoimmune sequelae even after just one dose.



--- 

In short, dissecting overlapping laboratory clues requires a systematic look at kinetics, ratios, and complementary imaging—not just treating each abnormal number as if it lived on its own island. When you do that—as illustrated by our “mystery muscle” patient—you’ll avoid misdiagnosing hepatic versus muscular injury, spare patients unnecessary invasive workup, and get treatment right on time. Happy scanning!



# Pharmacovigilance in the Era of Obesity Therapeutics: Building Safe Prescribing Practices for Semaglutide  

---

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

The diabetes/obesity armamentarium has exploded over the last few years thanks to glucagon‑like peptide‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs). Semaglutide sits at the top of that wave because it delivers impressive weight loss and glycaemic control while most prescribers expect mainly gastrointestinal side effects. The story below turned that expectation on its head—a single injection was followed several months later by an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). This isn’t simply another episode of rhabdomyolysis that resolves with fluids—it required steroids + IVIG and finally led the patient back to her baseline only after stopping semaglutide permanently. When a drug that millions are now taking shows up on an autoimmune muscle‐disease radar, every clinician who writes a prescription should pause and ask what we can do now to keep our patients safe while the signal matures.

---

## Clinical vignette recap  

A woman in her early sixties presented three years ago with non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and class II obesity. Her past medical history also included hypertension, hypothyroidism (TSH normal on assessment), remote deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism and no prior exposure to statins or other agents commonly linked to necrotising myopathies.  

Because her steatohepatitis persisted despite lifestyle measures, she was started on semaglutide 0.25 mg subcutaneously—the approved low‐dose initiation step. Within hours she developed severe nausea/vomiting leading to hypovolaemic acute kidney injury, metabolic acidosis and brief non‑invasive ventilation support two days later. At admission her creatine kinase (CK) was modestly raised at 337 U/L—far below levels seen in classic toxin‑driven rhabdo—and there were no pigmenturia or overt muscle pain reported at that time. She recovered renal function after aggressive hydration and was discharged five days later still feeling deconditioned but otherwise stable.  

Over the ensuing four months she noted insidious proximal limb weakness that progressed rapidly enough that she could no longer stand unaided and developed dysphagia/dysphonia affecting both liquids and solids. On re‑presentation her CK had peaked at 3 992 U/L accompanied by mildly elevated transaminases (AST 191 U/L, ALT 165 U/L) consistent with concurrent hepatic steatosis rather than pure muscle breakdown. A comprehensive autoimmune screen was essentially negative—including anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR, Mi‑2, Jo‑1 and other idiopathic inflammatory myopathy antibodies—though a low titre SS‑B antibody was present (2.4 × upper limit). 

MRI of both thighs showed symmetric oedema chiefly in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps regions without focal mass effect—a pattern compatible with an inflammatory process rather than isolated trauma or disuse atrophy. Definitive tissue diagnosis came from a right vastus lateralis biopsy revealing scattered necrotic fibers undergoing regeneration intermixed with abundant CD68⁺ macrophages phagocytosing debris (“myophagocytosis”), complement membrane attack complex deposition on sarcolemma and rare CD3⁺ T cells around vessels—all hallmarks of immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy despite negative canonical autoantibodies (seronegative IMNM).  

Therapy consisted initially of five days intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily followed by oral prednisone ≈ 1 mg/kg/day coupled with a total IVIG dose 2 g/kg spread over four days because her weakness remained strikingly profound despite steroids alone. Within two weeks her CK fell dramatically toward normal ranges and she began regaining functional strength enough to ambulate independently again. After an intercurrent pseudomonal urinary sepsis episode she was tapered off prednisone over roughly two months without relapse; she never required repeat IVIG owing both to clinical improvement and cost constraints surrounding continued therapy. Ten months after presentation she remained off all immunosuppressants, maintained normal CK values, regained pre‑illness functional status and avoided any further exposure to GLP‑1 RAs—including semaglutide—by choice.

---

## Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy—a quick primer  

IMNM occupies its own niche among inflammatory myopathies: rapid onset proximal weakness often accompanied by dysphagia/dysphonia, markedly elevated serum CK (typically > 1000 U/L), histology dominated by fiber necrosis/regeneration together with macrophage infiltrates rather than dense CD8⁺ T‐cell dominated endomysial inflammation seen in polymyositis. In roughly one third of patients an autoantibody target is identified—most commonly anti–HMGCR (often statin‐related) or anti–SRP—but seronegative disease still accounts for a substantial proportion where triggers remain cryptic until a medication exposure becomes temporally linked—as happened here with semaglutide.

---

## Differentiating immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy from classic rhabdomyolysis  

The familiar picture of drug­induced rhabdomyolysis features an abrupt surge in CK frequently exceeding ten thousand units per litre shortly after exposure followed within days by rapid decline once the offending agent is stopped and hydration administered; renal failure may ensue secondary to pigment nephropathy yet recovery usually occurs without immunosuppressive therapy. By contrast:

* Onset here was delayed—a single injection precipitated symptoms that intensified over weeks-to-months rather than hours.
* Peak CK rose gradually during out­patient follow up instead of crashing immediately.
* Muscle biopsy showed organized necrosis coupled with complement deposition—a signpost toward an antibody‐driven process rather than pure cytotoxic injury.
* Clinical trajectory responded robustly only after corticosteroids ± IVIG were added—not simply after aggressive fluids.
  
These distinctions matter because treating presumed rhabdo alone would miss an opportunity to modulate an evolving autoimmune attack amenable to early immunotherapy.

---

## Practical prescribing considerations  

When initiating any GLP‑1 RA today it remains reasonable simply to obtain baseline chemistry panels focusing on renal function + liver enzymes—as recommended in product labeling—but emerging anecdotal evidence argues that adding serum creatine kinase deserves serious consideration in selected individuals:

* Patients whose obesity coexist with hepatic steatosis/NASH already demonstrate chronic low‐grade inflammation—a milieu possibly priming aberrant immune responses.
* Those who harbor prior unexplained elevations in CK—even if modest—or who are taking concomitant agents known to affect skeletal muscle merit extra vigilance before adding another biologically active peptide.
* Thyroid dysfunction is another well recognized contributor to neuromuscular symptoms; confirming euthyroid status helps isolate the drug’s effect when weakness emerges unexpectedly soon after injection.

If baseline CK turns out markedly elevated (>5× upper limit), obtaining additional history directed toward cumulative exercise load, recent infections or latent inflammatory disease allows clinicians either deferred initiation pending clarification or close serial monitoring once treatment begins.

---

## Engaging patients – what they should watch out for  

Patients receiving semaglutide already expect gastrointestinal upset during titration week­ends—but they rarely anticipate progressive limb weakness or swallowing difficulty weeks down the road. A brief counselling script runs smoothly:

> “If you notice difficulty climbing stairs, rising from a chair without using your hands, trouble swallowing liquids versus solids—or even hoarseness that worsens—you should call us right away.”  
  
Encouraging adequate oral intake during titration helps prevent dehydration‐related renal insults that could confound subsequent laboratory interpretation (“was this just prerenal AKI versus evolving muscle injury?”). Offering concrete symptom checkpoints—in addition to standard glucose/BMI goals—creates a shared safety net where clinicians catch abnormal trajectories before irreversible damage accrues.

---

## How do we make sure these rare events get heard?  

Spontaneous adverse event reporting remains our primary conduit between bedside observation and regulatory action:

* In the United States practitioners file through **FDA MedWatch** via its web portal or downloadable Form 3500A; essential elements include product name/dose/date administered, succinct narrative describing the event chronology,
lab results—including peak CK,
diagnostic imaging,
biopsy findings,
and outcomes following drug discontinuation/therapy.
* European prescribers channel similar information through **EudraVigilance**, selecting “Suspected Adverse Reaction” followed by free‐text description mirroring MedWatch expectations.
* Institutional pharmacovigilance committees can streamline this workflow by flagging all outpatient lab results exceeding threefold ULN for CK among patients currently prescribed GLP‑1 RAs—and automatically prompting a brief electronic questionnaire sent backto the ordering provider regarding symptom context.
  
Documenting detailed investigations—even negative antibody panels—is crucial because aggregation tools such as WHO’s **VigiBase** rely on comprehensive data fields when performing disproportionality analyses that surface unexpected drug–event pairings across borders.

---

## Turning sporadic anecdotes into regulatory action  

When isolated instances accumulate enough statistical weight—in other words when disproportional reporting ratios climb above predefined thresholds—regulators launch formal signal assessments that may culminate in updated prescribing information handbooks (“monitor serum CK periodically when initiating therapy”) or black box warnings targeting specific high‐risk populations (“avoid use in known autoimmune inflammatory myopathies”). Historical precedent exists: repeated reports linking high‐intensity statins with anti–HMGCR antibodies propelled routine pre‑treatment antibody screens into cardiovascular practice guidelines decades ago. Likewise robust capture of GLP‑1 RA–associated IMNM could implant similar safeguards tomorrow—maybe even prompting trials evaluating whether alternative weight loss strategies bypass muscular autoimmunity altogether.

---

### Key Take‑Home Messages  

- **Baseline screening:** Think about obtaining serum creatine kinase before prescribing semaglutide—or any GLP‑1 receptor agonist—to individuals who carry obesity plus NAFLD/NASH, have had unexplained prior CK elevations or are concurrently on known myotoxic agents such as statins.
- **Patient empowerment:** Explicitly educate patients on early signs of proximal weakness, dysphagia or dysphonia and urge them to seek prompt evaluation rather than attributing these symptoms solely to deconditioning or weight loss fatigue.
- **Diagnostic vigilance:** When new muscular symptoms arise after starting a GLP‑1 RA obtain repeat CK promptly; consider MRI ± muscle biopsy early because seronegative IMNM will demand immunosuppression whereas simple toxin‐induced rhabdomyolysis generally resolves with hydration alone.
- **Therapeutic response:** Immediate cessation of the suspected agent combined with a short course of high-dose steroids +/- IVIG can lead back-to-baseline function when an immune mechanism is identified—in contrast to prolonged immunosuppressive regimens typically required for idiopathic IMNM.
- **Report relentlessly:** Use FDA MedWatch (or EudraVigilance abroad) to submit detailed adverse event reports—including biopsies—and encourage your health system’s pharmacovigilance team to build automated alerts around abnormal post‑initiation CK values.
- **Signal maturation:** Aggregated reports transform single anecdotes into actionable safety signals that ultimately shape labeling updates and guide future prescribing standards—your single contribution matters enormously toward protecting millions who will receive semaglutide worldwide.
