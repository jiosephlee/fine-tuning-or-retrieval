Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Pathophysiology] What histopathologic features on muscle biopsy distinguish immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from other inflammatory myopathies such as polymyositis and dermatomyositis?

**Clinical Pearls – How a Muscle Biopsy Distinguishes Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM) from Polymyositis (PM) and Dermatomyositis (DM)**  

| Feature | Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy | Polymyositis | Dermatomyositis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dominant lesion** | Widespread myofiber necrosis with regeneration → necrosis far out‑numbers inflammation | Endomysial CD8⁺ T‑cell–mediated invasion of otherwise intact fibers | Perifascicular / perimysial/vascular inflammation with **perifascicular atrophy** |
| **Inflammatory infiltrate** | **Paucity of lymphocytes**; predominance of **macrophages** (CD68⁺, acid‑phosphatase⁺) performing myophagocytosis | Dense **endomysial infiltrate** of CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells; often accompanied by up‑regulated MHC‑I on all fibers | Perivascular and interfascicular infiltrate rich in **CD4⁺ T cells**, B cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells |
| **Complement (C5b‑9/MAC) deposition** | **Sarcolemmal MAC** on both necrotic and non‑necrotic fibers → direct antibody‑mediated injury | Usually absent or focal; complement plays little role | MAC deposits primarily **on capillaries** and occasionally on endomysial vessels; not on sarcolemma |
| **MHC class I expression** | May be up‑regulated but not a defining feature | **Diffuse strong MHC‑I** expression on all fibers (helps confirm an immune process) | Often up‑regulated but less striking than PM |
| **Perifascicular changes** | None – fiber loss is random rather than perifascicular | None | **Perifascicular atrophy**, “puzzled” → key DM clue |
| **Helpful immunostains** | CD68⁺ macrophages, acid phosphatase+, sarcolemmal C5b‑9 → confirms IMNM; occasional sparse CD3⁺ T cells are permissible | CD8 immunostain highlighting invading cells; MHC‑I staining diffuse | C5b‑9 highlighting capillary walls, MXA or MxA staining for type I interferon signature |

### How These Findings Were Illustrated in the Semaglutide‑Associated Case

1. **Necrotic & regenerating fibers** – The biopsy showed “dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres,” the hallmark pattern in IMNM where active fiber death dominates the picture.
2. **Macrophage predominance** – Immunohistochemistry demonstrated strong CD68 positivity and acid phosphatase activity, indicating robust macrophage‐mediated phagocytosis (“myophagocytosis”). This is far more conspicuous than the scant lymphocytes seen in PM/DM.
3. **Sparse lymphocytes** – Only rare CD3⁺ T cells were identified, satisfying the “paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates” criterion that separates IMNM from polymyositis (dense endomysial CD8⁺ infiltrates).
4. **Sarcolemmal MAC deposition** – The membrane attack complex stained positively on both necrotising *and* non‑necrotising fibres—a pattern characteristic of antibody‑driven injury in IMNM. In DM the MAC is confined mainly to capillary endothelium; in PM it is typically absent.
5. **Lack of perifascicular atrophy or perimysial B‐cell zones** – No mention of perifascicular fiber loss or B‑cell–rich cuffs was made, arguing against dermatomyositis.

Together these morphologic clues—extensive fiber necrosis out of proportion to inflammation, macrophage‐rich phagocytosis, sarcolemmal MAC deposition, and only occasional T cells—fulfilled the ENMC/2021 consensus criteria for immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy and excluded polymyositis or dermatomyositis.

### Take‑Home Points for Residents

- **IMNM = “necrosis with few fighters.”** When you count fibers first, necrosis dominates; when you count immune cells first, they are few.
- **MAC matters:** Sarcolemmal C5b‑9 is a red flag for IMNM (or seronegative anti‑SRP/HMGCR disease); capillary C5b‑9 points toward DM.
- **Macrophages are the star:** Strong CD68/acid phosphatase staining signals myophagocytosis—central to IMNM pathology.
- **PM’s hallmark is an army:** Dense endomysial CD8⁺ T‐cell invasions into intact fibers, plus diffuse MHC‑I upregulation.
- **DM’s signature is geography:** Perifascicular atrophy with perivascular/perimysial inflammation and capillary MAC—often accompanied by a cutaneous rash.

In practice, when confronted with a patient who has an indeterminate CK elevation and proximal weakness, request:
1. Routine H&E staining to assess the ratio of necrosis → inflammation,
2. Immunostains for CD68 (macrophages), CD3/CD8 (lymphocytes), and C5b‑9 (MAC),
3. Optional MHC‑I stain if the diagnosis remains ambiguous.

These histopathologic fingerprints will reliably separate IMNM from its polymyalgia/myositis cousins and guide the clinician toward appropriate immunosuppressive therapy—as illustrated by this patient’s rapid response to steroids plus IVIG after semaglutide was withdrawn.

### [Morning Report] A patient presents with progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, CK > 3000 U/L, and normal ANA panel. Which sequence of laboratory and imaging studies is most appropriate to evaluate for IMNM?

**Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) – A step‑wise diagnostic pathway**  

*Clinical vignette*: A 60‑year‑old woman presents with progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, CK ≈ 4000 U/L and a negative ANA – the picture that prompted the work‑up described in Lam et al., *Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment*.

The goal is to move rapidly from “high CK” to a definitive diagnosis while excluding more common mimics (statin‑related toxic myopathy, endocrine myopathies, polymyositis). The most efficient sequence is:

---

### 1️⃣ Confirm the magnitude and persistence of muscle injury  
| Test | Why | Typical result in IMNM |
|------|-----|------------------------|
| **Serum CK** (repeat at least once) | Establish ≥5–10 × ULN and track trend | Markedly elevated (often >3000 U/L) and remains high despite rest |
| **Aldolase** (optional) | Additional muscle enzyme when CK interferes with monitoring | Often concordant with CK |
| **AST/ALT** | Distinguish hepatic from muscular transaminase rise | Mild‐to‑moderate elevation that mirrors CK change |

> *In the reported case the CK rose from 337 U/L at admission to a peak of 3992 U/L over 4 months.*

---

### 2️⃣ Exclude non‑immune/metabolic causes early  
| Test | Rationale |
|------|-----------|
| **CBC, CMP** (including electrolytes & renal function) | Look for hypophosphatemia, renal insufficiency that can aggravate rhabdomyolysis |
| **Thyroid panel** (TSH ± free T4) | Hyper‑ or hypothyroidism can mimic inflammatory myopathy |
| **Medication review** – especially statins, fibrates, red‑yeast rice or recent GLP‑1 RAs | Statin exposure raises suspicion for anti‑HMGCR disease; absence steers toward “seronegative” IMNM – exactly as in the case where no statins were used |
| **Vitamin D level** (optional) | Deficiency may exacerbate weakness but does not cause CK elevations this high |

---

### 3️⃣ Targeted auto‑immune serology – first‑tier tests  
1. **ANA** – screening test; often *negative* in pure IMNM (negative in the case).  
2. **Myositis‐specific antibody panel** – includes:
   - **Anti‑HMGCR**
   - **Anti‑SRP**
   - Other MSAs/MAAs (Jo‑1, PL‑7/12, Mi‑2, MDA5, TIF1‑γ, NXP‑2, cN1A)
   
> *Lam et al.* sent a comprehensive panel that returned negative for both HMGCR and SRP as well as all other MSAs—highlighting that ≈30 % of IMNM patients are seronegative.  

If any MSA is positive → strong support for antibody‑associated IMNM and often guides therapy (e.g., HMGCR positivity frequently follows statin exposure).

---

### 4️⃣ Imaging of symptomatic muscle groups – next logical step when serology is negative  
**MRI of affected proximal muscles (typically thighs or calves)**

- **Sequences:** STIR/T2 fat‑suppressed images are most sensitive for oedema.
- **Findings typical of IMNM:** Diffuse symmetric hyperintensity reflecting oedema without focal mass or fatty replacement; may show subcutaneous oedema as described in the case (“mild symmetric edema in proximal vastus lateralis”).  

Why imaging before EMG or biopsy?  

- Provides a “road map” for the most involved muscle → higher diagnostic yield on biopsy.
- Helps differentiate inflammatory from purely mechanical injury or compartment syndrome.
- In seronegative disease it bridges the gap left by a negative autoantibody screen—exactly what was done in the reported patient.

---

### 5️⃣ Electromyography (EMG) – optional adjunct  
- **When useful:** If MRI is unavailable or equivocal; to demonstrate myopathic motor unit potentials with fibrillation potentials/positive sharp waves.
- **Limitations:** Necrotising forms may have relatively bland EMG findings; therefore EMG is not mandatory if MRI already shows convincing oedema and clinical suspicion is high.  

In the indexed case EMG was not reported because MRI provided sufficient guidance toward biopsy.

---

### 6️⃣ Definitive tissue diagnosis – **muscle biopsy**  

**Procedure** – Open or needle core from the most edematous region identified on MRI.  

**Key histopathologic hallmarks of IMNM:**  
- Scattered necrotic + regenerating fibers  
- Sparse lymphocytic infiltrate (often CD68⁺ macrophages)  
- Deposition of complement membrane attack complex on sarcolemma  
- Minimal endomysial inflammation  

These criteria were fulfilled in the patient’s right vastus lateralis biopsy—confirming IMNM despite a negative serology panel.

---

### 7️⃣ Ancillary cardiac workup when clinically indicated  

Because IMNM can be associated with subclinical myocarditis:
- **High-sensitivity troponin / BNP**
- **Echocardiogram & cardiac MRI** if symptoms or biomarkers suggest cardiac involvement

The case demonstrated mild myocardial oedema on cardiac MRI that resolved after treatment—illustrating why an elevated troponin prompted further cardiac imaging despite primary skeletal muscle disease.

---

## Putting it together – Algorithm

1. **CK → repeat + aldolase + AST/ALT**  
2. **Basic labs & medication review** (statins, GLP‑1 RA) → exclude metabolic causes  
3. **ANA + comprehensive myositis antibody panel**  
   - *Positive*: treat as antibody-associated IMNM (often still obtain biopsy).  
   - *Negative*: proceed promptly to imaging.  
4. **MRI of proximal muscles** → locate active inflammation & plan biopsy site.  
5. *(If MRI unavailable)* Consider EMG to support myopathic process.  
6. **Targeted muscle biopsy** → confirm necrosis with scant infiltrate / MAC deposition.  
7. **Cardiac evaluation** when troponin/B‐type natriuretic peptide abnormal or symptoms present.

---

### Take‑home pearls

- **Seronegative IMNM is common** (~⅓ of cases); a normal ANA does not exclude disease.
- A *negative* HMGCR/SRP panel should prompt *immediate* muscle MRI rather than “watchful waiting.”
- Imaging guides *high-yield* biopsies—a critical step because the histologic picture distinguishes IMNM from polymyositis or drug‐induced rhabdomyolysis.
- Early recognition permits timely immunosuppression (corticosteroids ± IVIG), as shown by rapid CK fall and functional recovery in the semaglutide­–related case.

By adhering to this stepwise laboratory–imaging–biopsy sequence you can efficiently confirm—or exclude—IMNM even when autoantibodies are absent, enabling prompt therapy and preventing unnecessary delays.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which autoantibodies are most strongly associated with immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy, and how does their presence—or absence—affect diagnostic confidence and therapeutic decisions?

**Clinical Pearls – Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM)**  

**Key autoantibodies**

| Autoantibody | Frequency in IMNM | Typical clinical phenotype | Therapeutic implications |
|--------------|-------------------|----------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Anti‑HMG‑CoA reductase (anti‑HMGCR)** | ≈ 45 % of all IMNM cases | Often preceded by statin exposure (but can be “statin‑naïve”); sub‑acute proximal weakness, CK often >5 000 U/L; relatively good response to glucocorticoids ± IVIG | Steroids are usually sufficient; IVIG is added when CK remains high or weakness persists; relapses may be prevented with a steroid‑sparing agent (azathioprine, methotrexate). |
| **Anti‑signal‑recognition particle (anti‑SRP)** | ≈ 30 % of all IMNM cases | More severe and rapidly progressive weakness, frequent dysphagia & respiratory involvement; CK frequently >10 000 U/L; higher relapse rate | Early aggressive therapy is favoured – high‑dose steroids plus IVIG ± rituximab. Steroid‐sparing agents are added sooner because monotherapy often fails. |
| **Other myositis‑specific antibodies** (e.g., Jo‑1, PL‑7, PL‑12, Mi‑2, MDA5, TIF1‑γ) | Rare in “pure” necrotising myopathy | Usually accompany dermatomyositis or antisynthetase syndrome rather than isolated IMNM. | Their presence redirects evaluation toward those overlap syndromes and may influence choice of immunosuppressants (e.g., tacrolimus for antisynthetase disease). |

> **Bottom line:** Anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP are the two antibodies most tightly linked to IMNM. Detecting either strongly points toward this diagnosis and helps tailor therapy.

---

### How antibody status shapes our diagnostic confidence

1. **Positive anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP**  
   - Provides a *serological anchor* that the necrotising process is immune mediated rather than a toxic‐myopathy or metabolic myopathy.  
   - Allows us to skip extensive evaluation for secondary causes (e.g., endocrine disorders, infections) once CK elevation, proximal weakness and compatible MRI/EMG are documented.  
   - Confirms a *myopathy* distinct from classic polymyositis/dermatomyositis because those entities rarely harbour these antibodies.

2. **Seronegative IMNM** (~⅓ of cases)  
   - The lack of a defining antibody lowers pre‑test certainty and mandates a **muscle biopsy** for definitive diagnosis—as illustrated by the present case where an extensive myositis panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR and all other standard antibodies yet the biopsy showed necrotic fibers with macrophage predominance and scant CD3⁺ T cells—classic for IMNM.  
   - In seronegative patients we must also rule out *drug‐induced toxic rhabdomyolysis*, *metabolic myopathies*, or *inherited muscular dystrophies* by integrating CK trends, temporal relationship to drugs (here semaglutide), imaging findings, and clinical context.

3. **Low‐titer isolated SS‑B positivity**  
   - As seen in the case report, an isolated SS‑B without ANA or SS‑A does not currently influence IMNM classification; it should be regarded as an incidental finding unless Sjögren features emerge later.

---

### Impact on therapeutic decision‑making

| Antibody status | Initial therapy | When to intensify |
|-----------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Anti‑HMGCR positive** | Prednisone 1 mg/kg ± IVIG (2 g/kg) if CK >5 000 U/L or dysphagia present | Add methotrexate/azathioprine if CK plateau >2× ULN after 4–6 weeks; consider rituximab if refractory |
| **Anti‑SRP positive** | High‑dose steroids + IVIG upfront (often 2 g/kg) | Early rituximab or cyclophosphamide if weakness progresses despite 2 weeks of steroids/IVIG |
| **Seronegative** | Same regimen as anti‑HMGCR (steroids ± IVIG) guided by severity | Escalate based on functional response rather than serology—persistent CK elevation or failure to regain ambulation warrants IVIG re‑dose or addition of steroid sparers; monitor closely for relapse because seronegative disease can behave either way |

> In the report under discussion, despite being seronegative for both HMGCR and SRP, the patient responded dramatically to a short course of corticosteroids plus a single course of IVIG and remained relapse free after drug withdrawal. This illustrates that **treatment principles apply regardless of antibody status**, but seronegative disease obliges us to rely more heavily on the biopsy and clinical course for therapeutic judgment.

---

### Practical algorithm for the bedside

1. **Clinical suspicion** – proximal weakness ± dysphagia + CK >1 000 U/L → obtain MRI/EMG.
2.<ins>**Serology** – send extended myositis panel (incl. HMGCR & SRP).  
   • Positive → diagnose IMNM → start steroids ± IVIG per antibody type.  
   • Negative → proceed to muscle biopsy.
3.<ins>**Biopsy** – look for necrotic/regenerating fibers with macrophage infiltration, rare CD3⁺ cells, MAC deposition → confirms IMNM.
4.<ins>**Therapy** – initiate high‐dose prednisone; add IVIG early if CK >5 000 U/L or severe dysphagia; consider early rituximab for SRP or refractory disease.
5.<ins>**Follow‐up** – serial CK, functional assessment & taper steroids when CK normalizes (<2× ULN); monitor for relapse especially in seronegatives who may need longer immunosuppression.

---

### Take‑Home Message

- **Anti‑HMGCR and anti‐SRP antibodies are the hallmarks of immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy**, providing high diagnostic confidence and guiding intensity of immunotherapy.
- **Absence of these antibodies does not exclude IMNM**—about one third are seronegative as exemplified by the semaglutide case—and obliges confirmation by muscle pathology.
- **Therapeutic strategy is shaped more by clinical severity than by antibody status**, yet knowledge of which antibody is present helps anticipate disease aggressiveness (SRP→more aggressive) and informs choice of adjunctive agents (IVIG early in HMGCR vs rituximab early in SRP).

By integrating serology with histopathology and clinical phenotyping—as was necessary in the presented patient—we achieve both diagnostic clarity and rational treatment planning in this rare but treatable entity.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When a patient on a GLP‑1 receptor agonist develops elevated CK, how can clinicians differentiate acute drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis from a drug‑triggered immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy?

**Differential Diagnosis – Drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis versus drug‑triggered immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) in a patient receiving a GLP‑1 receptor agonist**

The case of a 60‑year‑old woman who received a single dose of semaglutide illustrates how the time course, clinical picture, laboratory dynamics, imaging, and histopathology separate an acute toxic myopathy from an immune‑mediated necrotising process.

---

### 1.  Temporal pattern and symptom evolution

| Feature | Acute drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis | Drug‑triggered IMNM |
|---------|-----------------------------------|----------------------|
| **Onset of CK rise** | Hours–days after exposure (often within 24 h) | Days–weeks after exposure; may continue to climb for weeks/months |
| **Peak CK** | Frequently >10 000 U/L; can exceed 50 000 U/L (e.g., tirzepatide case) | Usually <10 000 U/L but can be several thousand (patient’s peak ≈ 4 000 U/L) |
| **Course after drug stop** | Rapid decline once hydration and cessation occur (days) | Persistent or rising CK despite withdrawal; only falls after immunosuppression |
| **Associated symptoms** | Severe myalgias, tender swelling, dark (“cola‑coloured”) urine from myoglobinuria | Progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, minimal pain; no pigmenturia |

*In the present case* the first CK measurement (337 U/L) was taken during an acutely sick admission for nausea/vomiting and AKI – a level far below typical rhabdomyolysis thresholds and without myalgias or pigmenturia. Four months later the CK climbed to 3 992 U/L while the patient was off semaglutide and developed disabling proximal weakness—classic for IMNM rather than a transient toxic injury.

---

### 2.  Laboratory clues beyond raw CK

| Parameter | Rhadomyolysis | IMNM |
|-----------|----------------|------|
| **Serum myoglobin** – often detectable in rhabdo | Frequently elevated; falls quickly | May be normal or mildly elevated |
| **Renal function** – AKI usually due to myoglobin cast nephropathy | Common early on | May be normal or secondary to other causes |
| **Inflammatory markers** – CRP/ESR | Usually modestly raised or normal | Frequently elevated (ESR 22 mm/h, CRP 22.8 mg/L in patient) |
| **Autoantibodies** – anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP | Usually negative unless pre‑existing statin exposure | Positive in ~⅔ of cases; up to one third are seronegative as in this patient |

Thus a modest rise in inflammatory markers coupled with negative statin exposure points away from a pure toxic picture.

---

### 3.  Imaging

* **MRI of affected muscles**  
  - **Rhabdomyolysis:** diffuse edema limited to injured muscle groups, often symmetric but resolves rapidly after treatment.  
  - **IMNM:** symmetric proximal edema persisting beyond the acute phase; may show subcutaneous edema as seen on the patient’s femur MRI (mild symmetric edema of vastus lateralis/quadriceps).

MRI helps confirm ongoing inflammation when the clinical picture is indeterminate.

---

### 4.  Muscle biopsy – the definitive discriminant

| Histologic finding | Rhadomyolysis | IMNM |
|--------------------|---------------|------|
| Necrotic fibers with minimal inflammation | Yes ‑ necrosis dominates ‑ scant inflammatory cells | Yes ‑ necrosis + prominent macrophage infiltrates (CD68⁺) + complement membrane attack complex deposition on non‑necrotic fibers |
| Lymphocytic infiltrate density | Sparse or absent | Typically sparse lymphocytes but significant macrophages & perivascular inflammation (as in the case) |
| Regenerating fibers & sarcolemmal MAC positivity | May be present but limited | Characteristic MAC staining on both necrotic and intact fibers |

In this patient the biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic/regenerating fibers with CD68⁺ macrophages and MAC deposition – patterns that fulfill criteria for IMNM and are not seen in pure toxic rhabdomyolysis.

---

### 5.  Therapeutic response

- **Rhabdomyolysis:** resolution with aggressive IV fluids ± bicarbonate; CK falls within days without immunosuppression.
- **IMNM:** requires immunomodulation—high‑dose steroids ± IVIG or steroid‑sparing agents—often shows rapid CK decline only after such therapy (patient’s CK fell markedly after methylprednisolone pulse and IVIG).

A dramatic improvement after steroids/IVIG strongly supports an immune-mediated process.

---

### 6.  Practical algorithm for clinicians

1. **Identify timing** – if CK spikes >10 000 U/L within <48 h of GLP‑1RA dose → think rhabdo.
2. **Assess symptoms** – severe pain/myoglobinuria → rhabdo; progressive weakness without pain → consider IMNM.
3. **Check trends** – rapid normalization after drug stop → rhabdo; persistent elevation despite cessation → IMNM.
4. **Order labs** – CBC, CMP, ESR/CRP, urine dipstick for blood without RBCs, myositis autoantibody panel.
5. **Obtain MRI of symptomatic muscles** when CK >1500 U/L persisting >1 week.
6. **Proceed to muscle biopsy** if MRI or antibody workup is nondiagnostic and weakness is disabling.
7. **Initiate therapy**:
   - Rhabdo: vigorous hydration ± alkalinization.
   - IMNM: high-dose corticosteroids ± IVIG early; consider steroid-sparing agents if relapse occurs.

---

### Key teaching pearls from the semaglutide case

- A single dose of a GLP‑1RA can precipitate an immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy that evolves over months rather than minutes.
- Modest early CK elevation does *not* rule out later development of severe autoimmune myopathy.
- Absence of classic autoantibodies does not exclude IMNM—biopsy remains gold standard.
- Prompt immunosuppression can lead to full functional recovery even when the offending agent is stopped early.

By systematically evaluating onset, symptom type, biochemical trajectory, imaging findings, and histology, clinicians can reliably separate an acute toxic rhabdomyolysis from an emerging GLP‑1RA–triggered IMNM—and institute appropriate therapy before irreversible muscle loss occurs.

### [Clinical Pearls] According to WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality categories, what elements of this case satisfy the criteria for labeling semaglutide as a ‘probable’ cause of the patient’s IMNM?

**Answer – Why this case fulfills the WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre ‘probable’ causality criteria for semaglutide‑associated IMNM**

The WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) classifies a drug‑event relationship as **probable** when three core elements are present:

1. **A plausible temporal relationship** between drug exposure and onset of the adverse event.  
2. **The event is unlikely to be explained by the underlying disease or any other medication**.  
3. **A clinically relevant de‑challenge (withdrawal) response** is observed, and a rechallenge is either not performed or considered unethical.

Below is how each of these elements is satisfied in the present case:

| WHO‑UMC criterion | How the case meets it |
|-------------------|-----------------------|
| **1. Reasonable time‑relationship** | • The patient received **a single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose of semaglutide**.<br>• Within **hours she developed severe GI symptoms**, required hospitalization, and her CK was already modestly elevated (337 U/L).<br>• Four months later—while off the drug—she manifested **progressive proximal weakness**, dysphagia/dysphonia, and a **12‑fold rise in CK (peak ≈ 4 000 U/L)**, a pattern consistent with an immune‐mediated process that began after drug exposure rather than an acute toxic rhabdomyolysis which would have resolved rapidly. |
| **2. Alternative explanations are unlikely** | • **No exposure to known myotoxic agents**: she never used statins, red‑yeast rice, mushrooms or other supplements linked to IMNM.<br>• **Serologic work‑up was negative** for anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR and all other myositis‐specific antibodies, making an idiopathic seropositive IMNM less likely.<br>• **Autoimmune connective‐tissue disease was excluded** – ANA negative, only a low‑titer isolated SS‑B without clinical Sjögren’s features.<br>• **Her chronic liver disease cannot account for the marked CK surge**—transaminases were already modestly elevated before semaglutide and remained relatively stable while CK normalized.<br>• **Cardiac findings were mild, transient, and resolved**, arguing against myocarditis as the primary driver of muscle injury.<br>• Thus, *other diseases or drugs do not plausibly explain* the necrotising myopathy. |
| **3. Positive de‑challenge (withdrawal) response** | • After the single dose she discontinued semaglutide permanently.<br>• Following immunosuppressive therapy (IV methylprednisolone → oral prednisone + IVIG), her CK fell dramatically within two weeks and remained normal throughout follow‑up (≥ 10 months).<br>• No further worsening occurred despite continued exposure to all other medications, supporting that removal of semaglutide was a key factor in clinical improvement.<br>• A **rechallenge was deemed unethical** because re‑exposure would likely precipitate a severe recurrence of IMNM; therefore WHO‑UMC’s “rechallenge not performed or not ethical” condition is satisfied. |
| **4. Biological plausibility** *(supportive but not required for ‘probable’)* | • GLP‑1 receptor agonists have been reported to cause muscle injury (e.g., tirzepatide‐associated rhabdomyolysis) and are known to influence muscle metabolism.<br>• The histopathology showed necrotic fibres with macrophage infiltrates and complement deposition—findings observed in drug‑induced necrotising myopathies such as statin‐related HMGCR disease—providing a mechanistic bridge between drug exposure and immune activation. |

### Bottom line
The case fulfills all WHO‑UMC requirements for a **probable** adverse drug reaction:

- *Temporal proximity* between semaglutide initiation and symptom onset,
- *Exclusion of alternative etiologies* (no statins, seronegative IMNM, no other myotoxic exposures),
- *Improvement after drug withdrawal* (CK normalization with immunosuppression while remaining off semaglutide),
- *Inability/unethical nature of rechallenge*.

Consequently, semaglutide can be labelled as a *probable* trigger of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy in this patient—a classification that underscores vigilance when prescribing GLP‑1 receptor agonists but also respects the limits of definitive proof without re‑exposure.

### [Pathophysiology] Discuss plausible pathophysiologic mechanisms by which GLP‑1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide could precipitate an autoimmune muscle injury like IMNM.

**Pathophysiologic pathways by which a GLP‑1 receptor agonist such as sema­glutide could trigger an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**  

The presentation described by Lam et al. – a single dose of semaglutide followed weeks later by progressive proximal weakness, a >10‑fold rise in CK, macrophage‑rich necrotic fibers with membrane‑ attack complex (MAC) deposition, and recovery after drug withdrawal plus a short course of steroids/IVIG – is archetypal for a drug‑associated autoimmune myopathy.  Several non‑exclusive mechanisms are biologically plausible:

| Proposed Mechanism | Rationale & Supporting Data from the Case |
|-------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| **1. Hapten‑mediated neo‑antigen formation** | • Semaglutide (or a metabolite) may bind covalently to exposed muscle proteins after the initial hypovolaemic insult (the patient’s early acute kidney injury and modest CK rise). <br>• The resulting hapten–protein complex can be taken up by antigen‑presenting cells, breaking peripheral tolerance and generating **novel auto‑antibodies** that are not covered by routine panels (the biopsy showed MAC deposition despite negative anti‑SRP/HMGCR). <br>• Adaptive immunity requires weeks to months to mature—consistent with the 4‑month latency between exposure and overt myopathy. |
| **2. Amplification of innate immune danger signals** | • Acute GI toxicity → dehydration → renal hypoperfusion → necrotic myocytes release **damage‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)** such as mtDNA and HMGB‐1.<br>• DAMPs engage Toll‑like receptors on resident macrophages and circulating monocytes, driving IL‑6, TNF‑α, and IFN‑γ production.<br>• GLP‑1 receptors are expressed on monocytes/macrophages; in certain inflammatory milieus they can paradoxically enhance cytokine release rather than suppress it (Müller et al., *J Immunol* 2016). <br>• The biopsy’s CD68⁺ macrophage infiltrate and rare CD3⁺ T cells mirror this innate–adaptive bridge. |
| **3. Altered regulatory T–cell balance** | • Chronic metabolic disease (obesity, NAFLD) is associated with defective Treg function.<br>• GLP‑1RA therapy modulates the cAMP pathway in T cells; while generally anti‑inflammatory, it can also shift the Th17/Treg equilibrium toward **Th17 dominance** under stress conditions.<br>• A skewed Th17 response promotes autoantibody production and complement activation – exactly what is visualised as MAC positivity on non‑necrotic fibers in this patient. |
| **4. Unmasking of cryptic muscle antigens through rapid protein turnover** | • GLP‑1 agonists influence skeletal‐muscle metabolism—promoting modest catabolism initially before net anabolism develops.<br>• Sudden changes in protein synthesis/degradation may expose otherwise sequestered epitopes (e.g., α‐actinin, troponin I) that become immunogenic when presented alongside adjuvant signals from the preceding systemic stress. |
| **5. Parallel with other drug‐induced IMNMs** | • Statins provoke HMGCR antibodies through similar “first hit” muscle injury followed by loss of tolerance (as highlighted in the report).<br>• The current case mirrors that paradigm but without detectable HMGCR or SRP antibodies—suggesting either a yet unidentified target antigen or an antibody class not captured by commercial panels. |

### Integrating the mechanisms with the case

* Timing – A single dose provoked an immediate gastrointestinal insult; subsequent subclinical myocyte injury likely generated DAMPs that primed the immune system.
* Latency – The 4‑month interval allowed for hapten formation → antigen presentation → adaptive autoimmunity.
* Histology – Prominent macrophages (CD68⁺), sparse CD3⁺ T cells, and diffuse MAC deposition are hallmarks of an antibody/complement–driven process rather than pure toxic necrosis.
* Serology – Negative standard myositis panel does not exclude drug‐related autoimmunity; novel antibodies may have been generated against semaglutide–modified muscle proteins.
* Clinical course – Rapid CK fall after corticosteroids/IVIG and permanent drug avoidance mirrors other drug‐induced IMNMs where removal of the inciting antigen restores tolerance.

### Take‐home pearls

1. **Drug‐induced IMNM can follow a single exposure** when that exposure triggers acute metabolic stress plus immunologic priming.
2. **Absence of classic myositis antibodies does not rule out an autoimmune process**—muscle biopsy with MAC staining is diagnostic.
3. **GLP‑1R agonists act beyond glucose control**; their receptors on immune cells permit both anti- and pro-inflammatory signaling depending on context.
4. **Early drug withdrawal combined with brief immunosuppression often yields complete recovery**, as demonstrated by the patient’s return to baseline function within months.

In practice, when a patient receiving a GLP‑1RA develops unexplained CK elevation or proximal weakness—even weeks after the initial dose—consider IMNM in the differential, obtain a muscle biopsy if CK remains >10× ULN, discontinue the agent promptly, and initiate pulse steroids ± IVIG while awaiting definitive pathology.

### [Management] What is the recommended first‑line therapeutic regimen for seronegative IMNM, and why is intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) added to high‑dose corticosteroids in severe cases?

**First‑line therapy for seronegative immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**  
The initial regimen that most experts use for any adult patient with IMNM—including those who are seronegative—is:

1. **High‑dose glucocorticoid pulse:**  
   • Intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3–5 days (or 500–1000 mg daily if weight‑adjusted).  
   • Followed by oral prednisone ≈ 1 mg/kg day (usually tapered over 2–3 months once clinical improvement is evident).

2. **Early adjunctive immunomodulation** when the disease is moderate to severe—most commonly a steroid‑sparing agent such as methotrexate, azathioprine or mycophenolate mofetil started within the first few weeks. In practice many clinicians add these agents simultaneously with the steroid pulse if there are features that predict a poor steroid‐only response (bulbar or respiratory weakness, CK >5 000 U/L, rapid functional decline).

**Why add intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) in severe cases?**  

The decision to give IVIG on top of steroids (as was done in the case you reviewed) rests on three pillars:

| Reason | Pathophysiologic basis | Clinical cue from the case |
|--------|------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Rapid neutralisation of pathogenic humoral activity** | IMNM is driven by autoantibody‑mediated complement activation; even seronegative patients show membrane‑attack‑complex deposition on non‑necrotic fibers (the biopsy demonstrated C5b‑9 staining). IVIG contains pooled IgG that blocks Fcγ receptors on macrophages, competitively inhibits autoantibody binding, and supplies anti‑idiotypic antibodies that can neutralise unknown pathogenic antibodies. | The muscle biopsy showed MAC positivity on necrotic and non‑necrotic fibers—evidence of complement‐mediated injury that is amenable to IVIG’s complement‑inhibitory effects. |
| **Fast clinical improvement while waiting for slower agents** | Steroid‐sparing drugs take weeks to achieve therapeutic levels; IVIG produces biologic effects within days, leading to brisk declines in CK and measurable strength gains. | The patient remained profoundly weak after five days of methylprednisolone pulse and had bulbar signs (dysphagia/dysphonia). Adding IVIG (2 g/kg over 4 days) precipitated a marked CK fall over two weeks and functional recovery sufficient to wean steroids rapidly. |
| **Reduction of steroid exposure & prevention of irreversible damage** | Persistent necrosis leads to fibrosis and permanent deficit. Early aggressive control mitigates this risk and allows a shorter steroid taper—critical in older patients with co‑morbidities (obesity, hypertension). | By achieving remission after a single course of IVIG she could be tapered off prednisone within two months without relapse—a practical illustration of steroid sparing achieved through early IVIG use. |

**Practical algorithm derived from the case and consensus guidance**

1. **Diagnose IMNM (muscle biopsy with necrosis + MAC; negative SRP/HMGCR)** → start step 1 above.
2. **Assess severity**  
   – Bulbar/respiratory weakness, CK >4–5 × ULN, steep functional decline → label “severe”.  
   – Otherwise “moderate”.
3. **Severe disease** → give steroids **plus** either:
   - **IVIG** (2 g/kg split over 4–5 days) *or*  
   - Plasmapheresis if IVIG unavailable,
   followed by early initiation of a steroid‐sparing DMARD.
4. **Moderate disease** → steroids + early DMARD; reserve IVIG for refractory cases after 2–3 weeks.
5. **Follow up**: monitor CK weekly during acute phase; assess strength (MRC grade) and bulbar function biweekly; taper prednisone when CK stabilises <2×ULN and strength returns ≥4/5 in proximal muscles.

**Take‑home points**

*Seronegative IMNM behaves like its antibody‑positive counterparts.* The cornerstone is high‑dose glucocorticoids; however, when clinical features point toward aggressive disease—particularly bulbar involvement, markedly elevated CK, or lack of rapid response after pulse steroids—IVIG should be added promptly. In the presented patient, timely administration of a single course of IVIG was pivotal in halting necrosis, accelerating CK normalization, and permitting a short steroid course without relapse—a pattern that illustrates current best practice for severe seronegative IMNM.

### [Management] Given the patient’s mildly elevated troponin and cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement, what cardiac complications should be screened for in IMNM, and what follow‑up strategy is appropriate?

**Cardiac complications to look for in immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)  
and a pragmatic follow‑up plan – Lessons from the semaglutide‑associated case**

The patient described developed a mild rise in high‑sensitivity troponin (62 pg/mL) and a cardiac magnetic‑resonance (CMR) study that showed focal myocardial oedema with only modest hypokinesis and *no* late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). In the context of IMNM this pattern signals **early inflammatory myocardial involvement** – essentially a subclinical myocarditis – rather than irreversible scar. Although IMNM is principally a skeletal‑muscle disease, it shares the same spectrum of cardiac complications seen in other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs).

---

### 1. Cardiac complications that merit active screening

| Complication | Why it occurs in IMNM | How it may present / be detected |
|--------------|----------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Myocarditis** (active inflammation) | Autoimmune attack can involve the myocardium; CMR oedema is a sensitive marker | • Elevated troponin (>2–3× ULN) <br>• New ECG ST/T changes or low voltage <br>• New regional wall‑motion abnormality on echo <br>• CMR oedema ± LGE |
| **Myocardial fibrosis / scar** | Persistent inflammation may evolve into replacement fibrosis | • Presence of LGE on CMR <br>• Persistent elevation of troponin despite treatment |
| **Systolic or diastolic heart failure** | Myocardial injury reduces contractility & compliance | • Decline in EF on echo or CMR <br>• Rising NT‑proBNP/BNP <br>• Symptoms of dyspnoea, orthopnoea |
| **Arrhythmias** – atrial fibrillation/flutter, premature ventricular beats, non‑sustained VT | Inflammatory infiltration of conduction tissue & scar predispose to ectopy | • Palpitations, syncope <br>• ECG changes <br>• Ambulatory Holter/patch monitor |
| **Conduction disease** – AV block, bundle branch block | Same pathophysiology as arrhythmias | • New PR prolongation or QRS widening on ECG |
| **Pulmonary hypertension secondary to left‑heart disease** | Elevated RV systolic pressure noted on initial echo (46 mm Hg) | • Repeat Doppler echo <br>• Clinical signs (exertional dyspnoea) |

*Key point*: The absence of LGE in this patient’s CMR is reassuring that overt fibrosis has not yet developed, but it does not eliminate the risk of evolving myocarditis or later scar formation.

---

### 2. Immediate diagnostic work‑up (first 4–6 weeks)

1. **Baseline cardiac visit**  
   - Review symptoms (chest pain, dyspnoea, palpitations).  
   - Physical exam focused on volume status & heart sounds.
2. **12‑lead ECG** – look for rhythm disturbances or conduction delay.
3. **High‑sensitivity troponin & NT‑proBNP** – obtain at baseline and repeat in 1–2 weeks to determine trend.
4. **Transthoracic echocardiogram** – confirm left‑ventricular ejection fraction (≥60 % now), assess wall motion, estimate right‑ventricular systolic pressure again.
5. **24–48 h ambulatory rhythm monitor** (Holter or patch) because she already displayed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation during her first admission.
6. **Cardiac MRI re‑assessment** – not mandatory immediately unless symptoms worsen; however the initial study serves as a reference point.

---

### 3. Ongoing surveillance strategy

| Time point | Investigations | Rationale |
|------------|----------------|-----------|
| **1–2 months after discharge** | Repeat troponin & BNP; ECG ± short‑term Holter if prior arrhythmia | Detect lingering myocardial injury or early recurrence |
| **3 months** | Transthoracic echo + symptom review | Identify new systolic/diastolic dysfunction before it becomes symptomatic |
| **6 months** | Repeat CMR *if* initial scan showed oedema OR if troponin/B‐type natriuretic peptide remain mildly elevated >2× ULN | Determine whether oedema resolved, whether any LGE has appeared (scar), and guide duration of immunosuppression |
| **Every 6–12 months thereafter** | Symptom inquiry + ECG ± annual Holter (more frequently if prior arrhythmia). Repeat BNP/troponin only if clinically indicated.| Long‑term vigilance—late onset myocarditis or fibrosis can emerge even after skeletal‐muscle remission |

*Practical tip*: Use the same laboratory platform for serial troponins so that assay variability does not obscure true trends.

---

### 4. How this case informs the plan

- **Mild troponin rise** in an IMNM patient should be taken seriously despite its modest magnitude because high‑sensitivity assays are exquisitely sensitive to even focal inflammation.
- **CMR oedema without LGE** indicates early inflammatory change that often resolves with control of systemic disease; therefore a short‐interval repeat scan (~6 months) is appropriate rather than immediate reimaging.
- **Prior paroxysmal atrial fibrillation** justifies baseline rhythm monitoring and reinforces the need for vigilant symptom‐driven evaluation.
- The patient’s eventual normalization of CK, troponin, BNP, echocardiogram and repeat CMR underscores that *activity‑dependent* cardiac involvement can regress with prompt immunosuppression and removal of the trigger drug.

---

### 5. Take‑home pearls

1. **Screen all IMNM patients for cardiac involvement**, even when skeletal‐muscle disease appears isolated.  
2. **Troponin and BNP are useful triage tools**, but trends outweigh single values—persistent elevation warrants imaging and rhythm monitoring.  
3. **CMR is the gold standard** for detecting active myocarditis (edema) versus chronic scar (LGE); lack of LGE does not obviate follow‑up.  
4. **Arrange a baseline ECG/echo ± short Holter**, then tailor interval testing to biomarker dynamics and symptom evolution.  
5. **Collaborate closely with cardiology** from the outset—most centres now manage IIM‐related heart disease through shared rheumatology–cardiology clinics.

By adhering to this structured surveillance algorithm—clinical assessment plus serial biomarkers, imaging at key milestones, and rhythm monitoring—you can catch evolving cardiac pathology early while avoiding unnecessary testing in patients who remain stable after their myopathy has remitted.

### [Management] In seronegative IMNM patients who achieve remission after a short course of steroids and IVIG with withdrawal of the offending agent, how long should clinicians continue surveillance for disease relapse, and which biomarkers or clinical assessments are most useful during follow‑up?

**Answer – Follow‑up after drug‑induced seronegative IMNM**

In a patient such as the one described—seronegative immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) that remitted after a brief pulse of methyl‑prednisolone plus a single course of IVIG, together with prompt discontinuation of the offending GLP‑1‑RA—most experts would continue structured surveillance **for at least 12 months** after the last immunosuppressive dose, and many would extend it to **24 months** in the absence of relapse. The rationale is that relapses in IMNM tend to cluster in the first year after tapering therapy, even when the initial insult is drug‑related rather than idiopathic. A period of “observation only” beyond two years can be considered safe provided that both laboratory markers and clinical examinations remain unequivocally normal.

Below is a practical schedule derived from the published case and from the broader IMNM literature:

| Time from last immunosuppressive dose | Visit frequency | Key assessments |
|----------------------------------------|----------------|----------------|
| **0–4 weeks** | Every 2 weeks | Serum CK, aldolase (optional), ESR/CRP; focused MRC strength exam (proximal muscles); bedside dysphagia screen |
| **Weeks 5–12** | Monthly | Same laboratory panel; repeat manual muscle testing (MRC‐8 or MMT‑8); patient‑reported outcome (HAQ‑DI or PROMIS physical function); review of any new systemic symptoms |
| **Months 4–12** | Every 3 months | Serum CK; targeted strength exam; functional questionnaire; consider repeat MRI of thighs only if CK rises >2× ULN or symptoms recur |
| **Months 13–24** | Every 6 months (if previous visits all normal) | Serum CK; brief strength check; dysphagia query. If completely quiescent for ≥12 months you may space visits further or discharge from formal neuromuscular follow‑up, returning only for new symptoms. |

### Why this schedule works

1. **Serum creatine kinase is the most sensitive disease activity marker in seronegative IMNM.** In the reported case, CK fell from >3 900 U/L to within normal limits within two weeks of treatment and stayed normal even during a severe unrelated infection—a useful illustration that a rising CK often precedes clinical deterioration.

2. **Aldolase adds modest incremental sensitivity** when CK is borderline or when patients have concomitant hepatic enzyme elevation that can obscure interpretation of AST/ALT.

3. **Inflammatory indices (ESR/CRP) are not diagnostic but help flag concurrent infections or other inflammatory triggers** that might precipitate a flare; they are inexpensive and readily available at each visit.

4. **Manual muscle testing (MRC or MMT‑8) remains essential** because subclinical weakness may appear before biochemical changes—especially in seronegatives who lack an autoantibody titre to follow.

5. **Patient‑reported outcome measures** (HAQ‑DI, PROMIS PF) capture functional impact more sensitively than strength scores alone and are quick to obtain in clinic or via telemedicine—the same modality used for her initial neurology consult.

6. **Dysphagia assessment is mandatory early on** because bulbar involvement was prominent in this case and may recur independently of limb weakness.

7. **Imaging (muscle MRI) is reserved for equivocal laboratory results or recurrent symptoms**, as it reliably detects edema/active necrosis but adds cost and logistic burden when routine labs are normal.

### Special considerations for drug‑induced disease

- **Offending‑agent avoidance eliminates the ongoing antigenic stimulus**, which likely reduces long‑term relapse risk compared with idiopathic HMGCR/SRP‐positive IMNM where chronic autoimmunity persists.
- Nevertheless, published series report *late* relapses even after firm drug removal—hence an observation window extending beyond one year remains prudent.
  
### Practical take‑home points

1. **CK every visit is non‑negotiable** in seronegative IMNM; treat any confirmed rise >2× ULN as a signal to reinstitute low‑dose steroids or consider another IVIG cycle depending on clinical context.
2. **Clinical exam drives management decisions** when laboratory trends are flat but patients report new fatigue or difficulty swallowing.
3. **A minimum one‑year “intensive” surveillance period followed by quarterly then semi‑annual checks provides a safety net without excessive burden**, as demonstrated by the patient who remained relapse‑free at her 10‑month outpatient review while off all immunosuppression.
4. **Document medication exposure meticulously** – confirming permanent cessation of semaglutide (or any other suspect drug) simplifies counseling about future pharmacotherapy choices and informs pharmacovigilance reporting.

In summary, for seronegative IMNM triggered by an agent such as semaglutide—once remission is achieved with short‑course steroids plus IVIG—the safest approach is structured follow‑up for at least twelve months using serial CK measurements paired with regular strength and functional assessments, extending surveillance toward two years if any uncertainty remains regarding disease quiescence. This regimen balances the relatively favorable prognosis of drug‐induced disease against the known propensity of IMNM to flare early in its natural history.
