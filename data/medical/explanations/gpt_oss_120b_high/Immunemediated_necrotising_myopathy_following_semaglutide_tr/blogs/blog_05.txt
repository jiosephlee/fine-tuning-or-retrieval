Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# CK, AST/ALT, Troponin—and the Mystery Muscle Patient: Interpreting Multi‑Organ Lab Elevations in Myositis  

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

When a 60‑year‑old woman walked into the clinic with new‐onset dysphagia, dysphonia and steep proximal weakness, the first thing most of us imagined was “classic polymyositis.” What shocked the team (and me) was that her laboratory picture looked like a three‑way traffic jam: a CK climbing toward 4 000 U/L, modestly elevated AST/ALT (~200 U/L) and a high‑sensitivity troponin that nudged just above the reference range. Add an echo showing mildly raised right‑ventricular systolic pressure and a cardiac MRI with patchy edema—suddenly you’re juggling liver disease, renal insult, a possible drug reaction, and possible myocarditis all at once.

The kicker? The only culprit medication she’d ever taken was a **single subcutaneous dose of semaglutide** (a GLP‑1 receptor agonist). She’d never been on statins or red‑yeast rice and her myositis panel was negative for the usual suspects (HMGCR, SRP). This isn’t your textbook rhabdomyolysis story; it’s an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) that looks like it might have been *triggered* by a drug we rarely associate with autoimmunity.  

That combination—multifactorial lab abnormalities paired with an atypical drug exposure—makes this an ideal teaching case for anyone who’s ever stared at discordant labs and wondered whether they’re looking at one organ in distress or several.  

## The lab puzzle: CK versus AST/ALT versus troponin  

| Test | Typical organ source | What it tells us in isolation | What it can *mislead* you into thinking |
|------|----------------------|--------------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **CK** | Skeletal muscle (also cardiac & brain isoforms) | Quantifies muscle cell breakdown → severe necrotising myopathy or rhabdomyolysis when >10× ULN | May be “normal” in early IMNM if necrosis is focal |
| **AST** | Liver mitochondria *and* skeletal muscle cytosol | Rises with any cell death that contains mitochondria → can mirror CK spikes | Often taken as hepatic injury when ALT remains normal |
| **ALT** | Primarily hepatocyte cytosol | More liver‑specific; modest elevations can still come from severe muscle damage | Overlooks muscle contributions when ALT < AST |
| **High‑sensitivity troponin I/T** | Cardiac myocytes (cTnI highly specific) | Gold standard for myocardial necrosis/inflammation → guides coronary work‑up | Small elevations may appear after intense skeletal injury (especially cTnT) or via cross‑reactivity in regenerating muscle |

In our patient:

* **CK** started at ~340 U/L (just above reference) during an acute GI illness and spiked to ~4 000 U/L four months later—a pattern more compatible with *progressive* necrotising myopathy than with an isolated toxin‐driven rhabdomyolysis episode.
* **AST/ALT** rose together (~190/165 U/L) while alkaline phosphatase remained normal – consistent with *muscle* release rather than cholestatic liver disease.
* **hs‑troponin** was marginally high (62 pg/mL vs ≤53). Coupled with mild RV pressure elevation on echo and myocardial edema on cardiac MRI—but no late gadolinium enhancement—the picture suggested *subclinical myocarditis* or stress cardiomyopathy rather than frank infarction.
* **Pro‑BNP** was markedly elevated (>4 000 pg/mL). In this context it likely reflected transient volume overload from acute kidney injury plus catecholamine surge rather than chronic heart failure.

The key lesson? None of these numbers live in isolation. Their trajectories, ratios, and the clinical backdrop tell the real story.

## How to sort out the source – a stepwise approach  

### 1️⃣ Follow the CK trend  
* **Rhabdomyolysis:** CK skyrockets (>10–20× ULN) within hours–days of insult then drops rapidly once the offending agent is removed and hydration started.  
* **Immune–mediated necrotising myopathy:** CK may start modestly and *progressively climb* over weeks–months despite removal of any suspected drug. Persistent elevation (>5× ULN) >2 weeks after presumed trigger should raise suspicion for IMNM rather than pure toxicity.  

In our case CK surged months after semaglutide exposure while she was off the drug—a red flag for autoimmunity.



### 2️⃣ Interpret transaminases through a “muscle–liver” lens  

* **AST>ALT** can hint at muscular origin because both enzymes exist in mitochondria—but AST is more abundant in skeletal muscle.  
* Look at *other liver markers*: normal alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin make pure hepatic injury less likely.  
* The “CK‑to‑AST ratio” is a handy bedside clue: When CK≫AST (e.g., >10:1), think muscle. Here CK rose into thousands while AST hovered around 200 U/L → disproportionate => muscular source dominates.  

### 3️⃣ Decipher troponin spikes  

* **cTnI** is almost exclusively cardiac; even tiny rises usually merit cardiac work‐up unless there’s clear skeletal cause such as massive rhabdomyolysis or muscular regeneration with ectopic expression of cardiac isoforms.
* **cTnT** can be expressed by regenerating skeletal fibers; modest elevations may occur in inflammatory myopathies without true myocarditis.
* Correlate troponin trends with ECG changes, echocardiography, and—if uncertainty persists—cardiac MRI.



### 4️⃣ Leverage imaging  

| Modality | What you’re looking for | How it informs lab interpretation |
|----------|------------------------|------------------------------------|
| **Muscle MRI (STIR/T2)** | Edema pattern, focal vs diffuse inflammation | Confirms active myopathy when CK stays high; guides biopsy site |
| **Cardiac MRI** | Myocardial edema on T2 mapping + LGE patterns | Differentiates myocarditis (edema + LGE), stress cardiomyopathy (edema without LGE), or innocent troponin leak |
| **Echocardiography** | Wall motion abnormalities, pulmonary pressures | Quick screen for functional impact of any myocardial involvement |

Our patient’s thigh MRI showed symmetric proximal edema—the classic “muscle inflammation” picture that dovetailed nicely with her rising CK. Cardiac MRI revealed edema without LGE → suggestive of *transient inflammation*, explaining her borderline troponin rise without overt ischemia.



### 5️⃣ Put it all together – timing matters  

| Timeline | Labs & Imaging | Interpretation |
|----------|----------------|----------------|
| **Day 0–2 after semaglutide dose** – nausea/vomiting → AKI → CK ≈ 337 U/L; AST/ALT mildly ↑ ; Troponin barely ↑.| Acute volume depletion + possible direct drug effect but no massive muscle breakdown.| Toxic reaction + early systemic stress—CK still low enough that we could dismiss serious myopathy at this point.|
| **Weeks 3–16** – progressive weakness → CK up to ~4 000 U/L ; AST/ALT ≈200 U/L ; Troponin ≈62 pg/mL.| Ongoing necrotising process despite drug cessation.| Autoimmune necrotising myopathy triggered (or unmasked) by semaglutide; secondary low‐grade myocardial inflammation plausible given imaging findings.|

## Putting it together in our patient  

1️⃣ **Semaglutide exposure** appears plausible as the trigger because the symptom onset followed its first dose and there were no other new medications or supplements.  
2️⃣ **CK trajectory**—modest rise early then steep climb over months—fits IMNM rather than classic drug toxicity which would have resolved quickly after hydration.  
3️⃣ **AST/ALT levels** tracked closely with CK while alkaline phosphatase stayed flat—pointing toward skeletal‐muscle origin rather than worsening NAFLD.  
4️⃣ **Troponin elevation** was borderline and accompanied by non‑definitive cardiac MRI changes—addressed conservatively while monitoring for evolving myocarditis.  
5️⃣ **Therapeutic response:** High‑dose steroids + IVIG drove rapid decline in both CK and transaminases, mirroring improvement seen in other drug‑associated IMNM reports where brief immunosuppression suffices once the offending agent is withdrawn.  

The net result? By month 10 she was back to baseline strength, off all immunosuppression, with normalized labs—a textbook recovery when you correctly identify *the right organ(s)* responsible for each biomarker glitch.



## Clinical pearls & take‑home points  

- **Never assume elevated AST/ALT equals liver disease** when CK is also high; check the ratio and look for concurrent alkaline phosphatase/GGT data.
- **Serial CK matters more than a single value**—a rising trend over weeks strongly suggests immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy rather than an acute toxic rhabdomyolysis.
- **Small hs‑troponin rises in inflammatory myopathies may be “muscle‐derived.”** Correlate with ECG/Echo/MRI before invoking coronary work‑up.
- **Cardiac MRI is your gold standard when troponin is discordant:** edema without late gadolinium enhancement generally indicates reversible inflammation rather than infarction.
- **Drug‐triggered IMNM tends to improve after brief immunosuppression plus withdrawal**, whereas idiopathic IMNM usually needs prolonged multi‑agent therapy.
- When faced with multi‑organ lab derangements:
  1. Plot each biomarker over time.
  2. Cross‑reference non‑hepatic enzymes (CK) against hepatic enzymes (AST/ALT).
  3. Use imaging targeted to each organ suspected.
  4. Reassess after removing any potential trigger before escalating therapy.
- Finally, keep a high index of suspicion that newer metabolic agents—including GLP‑1 agonists—might have rare autoimmune sequelae even after just one dose.



--- 

In short, dissecting overlapping laboratory clues requires a systematic look at kinetics, ratios, and complementary imaging—not just treating each abnormal number as if it lived on its own island. When you do that—as illustrated by our “mystery muscle” patient—you’ll avoid misdiagnosing hepatic versus muscular injury, spare patients unnecessary invasive workup, and get treatment right on time.
