Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation  

The patient was a woman in her early‑sixties who carried several chronic conditions that are frequently encountered together in clinical practice: class III obesity (body‑mass index ≈ 38 kg/m²), non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with prior liver biopsy confirming steatohepatitis, essential hypertension, hypothyroidism treated with levothyroxine, and a remote history of deep‑venous thrombosis with pulmonary embolism. These comorbidities placed her at high cardiovascular risk and made her a typical candidate for newer metabolic therapies such as glucagon‑like peptide‑1 (GLP‑1) receptor agonists, which have both weight‑reducing and presumed hepatoprotective effects in NAFLD. After three years of lifestyle counseling without meaningful weight loss, her physician initiated semaglutide—a once‑weekly subcutaneous GLP‑1 agonist—using the standard starter dose of 0.25 mg. The intention was therapeutic weight reduction and possible improvement in hepatic steatosis rather than glycaemic control per se, because she was not diabetic at the time.

Within a few hours of the first injection the patient developed marked nausea and vomiting, classic early gastrointestinal side effects attributable to GLP‑1 receptor activation of central emesis pathways and delayed gastric emptying. The emesis persisted despite anti‑emetics, leading to profound hypovolaemia. Two days after the injection she required admission for persistent vomiting, dyspnoea secondary to volume depletion, an acute rise in serum creatinine compatible with pre‑renal acute kidney injury (AKI), and a high anion‑gap metabolic acidosis managed with intravenous normal saline plus bicarbonate infusion. Renal function recovered with aggressive fluid resuscitation without need for renal replacement therapy.  

During this first admission a creatine kinase (CK) level was obtained inadvertently as part of a routine chemistry panel; it measured **337 U/L**, modestly above the institutional upper limit (~200 U/L) but far below the ≥5‑fold elevations (>1 000 U/L) typically seen in overt rhabdomyolysis. Moreover, the patient had no dark‑coloured urine (“cola urine”) or palpable thigh tenderness that would suggest extensive myonecrosis. These features made primary drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis unlikely at this stage—the modest CK rise could be explained by mild skeletal‐muscle ischemia secondary to hypoperfusion during the AKI episode rather than direct myotoxicity from semaglutide.  

An additional notable event during this admission was an episode of **paroxysmal atrial fibrillation** accompanied by normal cardiac troponin levels. In the setting of severe dehydration and metabolic acidosis, catecholamine surge and electrolyte shifts can precipitate transient arrhythmias even in patients without prior structural heart disease; thus the arrhythmia was deemed secondary to the acute illness rather than a primary cardiac pathology.  

After discharge she remained physically deconditioned but otherwise asymptomatic from a gastrointestinal standpoint; semaglutide was discontinued permanently because she refused further doses after the intolerable reaction. Over the ensuing **four months** she reported progressive fatigue and loss of strength predominantly affecting her shoulder girdle and hip flexors—muscle groups most dependent on intact neuromuscular transmission for daily activities such as standing up from a chair or climbing stairs. By month four she was unable to sit upright without assistance and began experiencing **dysphagia** (difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids) and **dysphonia** (hoarse voice), indicating involvement of bulbar muscles innervated by cranial nerves IX–XII—a pattern frequently described in inflammatory myopathies that affect both limb‐proximal and cranio‑pharyngeal musculature.

When she re-presented to an outside emergency department her serum CK had risen dramatically to **3 992 U/L**, reflecting active necrosis of skeletal muscle fibers far beyond what is seen in simple disuse atrophy or deconditioning. She was transferred urgently to a tertiary centre where a focused neurological examination revealed marked proximal weakness (Medical Research Council grade ≈ 2–3/5) affecting shoulder abduction/external rotation and hip flexion/extention while distal strength was relatively preserved. Importantly there were **no cutaneous eruptions**, heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules or other dermatologic clues that would point toward dermatomyositis; also absent were synovitis, peripheral oedema or sicca symptoms suggestive of systemic connective tissue disease. The lack of systemic signs narrowed the differential toward an isolated myopathic process rather than a broader autoimmune rheumatologic disorder.

The evolution from an isolated gastrointestinal adverse reaction after a single semaglutide dose to a delayed, steadily worsening proximal muscle weakness accompanied by progressive CK elevation is atypical for pure drug‑induced toxic myopathy—which usually manifests within days after exposure with rapid CK normalization upon withdrawal—and instead raises suspicion for an **immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)** triggered—or unmasked—by the medication exposure. The temporal relationship (symptom onset shortly after semaglutide administration), the magnitude of CK rise over weeks‐to‐months, bulbar involvement, absence of rash or other organ system disease, together with the modest initial CK level despite severe systemic stress all formed the basis for proceeding with targeted investigations—including autoantibody panels and muscle biopsy—that are detailed in subsequent sections of this report.

## Pathophysiology and Differential Diagnosis of Necrotising Myopathies  

Necrotising myopathies comprise a spectrum of disorders in which skeletal‑muscle fibers undergo focal or diffuse necrosis. Two broad mechanistic groups dominate the differential diagnosis in clinical practice: **immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**—a subset of the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies—and **toxic/acute rhabdomyolysis**, in which a non‑immune insult precipitates rapid muscle breakdown. Understanding their pathobiology, serologic markers, and histologic signatures is essential for interpreting the case of a 60‑year‑old woman who developed progressive proximal weakness after a single dose of semaglutide.

### Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM): Clinical Hallmarks  

Patients with IMNM typically present over weeks to months with **symmetrical proximal‐muscle weakness** that may extend to bulbar muscles (dysphagia, dysphonia) or respiratory muscles. Serum creatine kinase (CK) is markedly elevated—often 5–20 times the upper limit of normal—and remains persistently high unless disease control is achieved with immunosuppression. In the present patient, CK rose from a modest 337 U/L during an early hospitalization to a peak of 3992 U/L four months later, paralleling worsening weakness—a classic temporal pattern for IMNM rather than an acute toxin‑driven process.

### Autoantibody Associations and Seronegative IMNM  

Two autoantibodies dominate the serologic landscape of IMNM:

1. **Anti‑hydroxy‑3‑methylglutaryl‑coenzyme A reductase (anti‑HMGCR)** – most often identified in patients who have taken statins for months to years. Exposure triggers an autoimmune response against HMGCR expressed on regenerating muscle fibers; the antibody persists even after drug cessation and drives complement‑mediated necrosis.
2. **Anti‑signal recognition particle (anti‑SRP)** – linked to a particularly severe phenotype with early respiratory involvement and very high CK levels.

Approximately **30 % of patients with IMNM are seronegative** using commercially available panels. The absence of detectable antibodies does not exclude the disease; instead it suggests either low‑titer antibodies against uncharacterized antigens or immune activation driven by an external trigger such as a drug or infection. In our case, the comprehensive myositis panel was negative for both HMGCR and SRP as well as other myositis‐specific antibodies, placing the patient into the seronegative subgroup.

### Mechanisms of Drug‑Induced Necrotising Myopathy  

Drug exposure can lead to necrotising myopathy through two non‑exclusive pathways:

| Mechanism | Typical Agents | Pathophysiologic Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct toxic injury** | Statins (high doses), colchicine, alcohol, illicit drugs, certain antibiotics | Rapid CK surge (>10× ULN), myoglobinuria, minimal inflammation on biopsy; improvement follows drug withdrawal and hydration |
| **Immune activation** | Statins (via anti‑HMGCR), immune checkpoint inhibitors, some biologics | Persistent CK elevation despite cessation of the offending drug; muscle biopsy shows necrosis with macrophage infiltration, complement deposition (MAC), and sparse lymphocytes – histologically indistinguishable from idiopathic IMNM |

Statin‐associated IMNM exemplifies immune activation: exposure initiates an anti‑HMGCR response that becomes self‑sustaining after drug removal. By analogy, a GLP‑1 receptor agonist could act either as a pure toxin or as an immunogenic trigger that unmasks an underlying autoimmune propensity.

### GLP‑1 Receptor Agonists: Effects on Skeletal Muscle  

GLP‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs) such as semaglutide promote weight loss by reducing appetite and enhancing insulin secretion. Clinical trials have documented **loss of lean body mass** as a side effect—presumably secondary to caloric deficit rather than direct myocyte injury. Isolated reports of severe rhabdomyolysis have emerged with newer agents (e.g., tirzepatide). Those cases describe an **explosive CK rise** (>50 000 U/L) within days of drug exposure that resolves promptly after drug cessation without immunotherapy—consistent with direct muscular toxicity rather than immune dysregulation.

In contrast, the patient described here experienced a modest initial CK elevation during an acute gastrointestinal illness followed by a **delayed, progressive rise** over months and focal necrotizing changes on biopsy with membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition—findings more aligned with an immune‐mediated process potentially triggered by semaglutide.

### Pathophysiologic Distinction Between Rhodomyolysis and IMNM  

| Feature | Acute Toxic Rhodomyolysis | Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Hours–days after insult | Days–weeks after exposure or idiopathic |
| CK Kinetics | Sharp rise (>10× ULN) → rapid fall within 3–7 days if cause removed | Sustained elevation (often >5× ULN) for weeks–months despite withdrawal |
| Symptoms | Prominent muscle pain/tenderness, dark urine (“cola‑coloured”), possible acute kidney injury | Progressive weakness (proximal > distal), dysphagia/dysphonia; pain less prominent |
| Histology | Extensive necrosis but *minimal* inflammatory infiltrate; no MAC on non‑necrotic fibers | Necrotic/regenerating fibers + macrophages + **MAC** deposition on sarcolemma of both necrotic and intact fibers; scarce CD3+ T cells |
| Response to Immunosuppression | Generally none required | Marked CK reduction & clinical improvement with steroids ± IVIG |

The presence of **MAC (C5b-9)** on intact fibers is a hallmark of complement‐driven immune injury in IMNM and was demonstrated in the thigh biopsy from our patient (Figure 4C). Such staining is not seen in simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.

### Role of Pre‑Existing Subclinical Autoimmunity  

The patient’s low-titre isolated anti‑SSB/La positivity raises the specter of silent systemic autoimmunity (e.g., early Sjögren’s syndrome). While isolated SS‑B without ANA or SS‑A is uncommon and not known to cause myopathy directly, it may reflect a baseline proclivity for humoral autoimmunity that predisposes to antibody formation against muscle antigens after an environmental trigger such as semaglutide. In seronegative IMNM cases, such subclinical autoantibody profiles sometimes provide clues that an underlying dysregulated immune milieu exists even if classical myositis antibodies are absent.

### Distinguishing IMNM From Other Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathies  

*Polymyositis* and *dermatomyositis* share features of proximal weakness and CK elevation but differ pathologically:

- **Polymyositis** – Endomysial CD8⁺ T‐cell infiltrates invading non‐necrotic fibers; upregulated MHC class I expression on many fibers.
- **Dermatomyositis** – Perifascicular atrophy with CD4⁺ T cells/B cells in perivascular regions; characteristic skin manifestations.
- **IMNM** – Predominantly necrotic/regenerating fibers with scant lymphocytes; prominent macrophage activity and MAC deposition rather than extensive T‐cell–mediated cytotoxicity.

Clinically, dermatomyositis presents with heliotrope rash or Gottron papules—absent in this case. The biopsy lacked dense endomysial infiltrates typical for polymyositis but showed abundant macrophages and MAC staining consistent with IMNM.

### Integrating Clinical & Pathologic Data in This Case  

Putting these pieces together clarifies why the authors concluded that semaglutide was likely implicated in an immune‐mediated process rather than simple toxicity:

1. **Temporal relationship** – Progressive weakness began weeks after a single semaglutide dose.
2. **CK trajectory** – Modest elevation initially followed by a sustained 12‑fold increase over four months.
3. **Serology** – Negative HMGCR/SRP panel → seronegative IMNM phenotype.
4. **Histology** – Necrotizing fibers with macrophage infiltrates *and* MAC deposition on non‑necrotic fibers.
5. **Therapeutic response** – Marked clinical and biochemical improvement after high-dose steroids plus IVIG despite discontinuation of semaglutide alone being insufficient.
6. **Absence of classic toxic rhabdomyolysis signatures** – No profound myoglobinuria, no rapid CK normalization upon hydration alone.

These findings illustrate how careful assessment of disease kinetics, antibody profiles, imaging/biopsy patterns, and treatment response helps differentiate true immune-mediated necrotising myopathy from acute toxic rhabdomyolysis—a distinction that directly informs management decisions (immunosuppression versus supportive care).

---  

In summary, **IMNM is an autoimmune disorder characterized by persistent proximal weakness, sustained CK elevation, often antibody positivity (anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP), but also seronegative forms where tissue evidence such as MAC deposition becomes pivotal**. Drug-induced forms may arise from direct toxicity—exemplified by classic rhabdomyolysis—or from immune activation akin to statin-associated HMGCR disease → potentially applicable to GLP‑1 RA exposure as hinted by this case report.\

Understanding these pathophysiologic nuances enables clinicians to recognize atypical presentations such as semaglutide-associated IMNM and apply targeted immunotherapy while avoiding unnecessary prolonged therapy for true toxic rhabdomyolysis.\

## Diagnostic Work‑up and Clinical Reasoning in This Case  

The diagnostic pathway for this patient hinged on aligning an evolving clinical picture—progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and marked creatine kinase (CK) rise—with a stepwise series of investigations designed to confirm an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) while systematically excluding mimicking conditions such as drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis, metabolic myopathies, or systemic connective‑tissue disease. Each test contributed a distinct piece of the puzzle and together they shaped the final diagnosis and the assessment of a probable link to semaglutide exposure.  

### Creatine kinase kinetics and symptom chronology  

CK is a highly sensitive marker of skeletal‑muscle injury because it leaks into the circulation when sarcolemmal integrity fails. In acute toxic rhabdomyolysis the rise is abrupt (often >10 000 U/L) and falls rapidly once the offending agent is removed and volume resuscitation restores perfusion. By contrast, IMNM follows an indolent yet relentless trajectory: CK may start modestly elevated but climbs progressively over weeks to months as necrotic fibres accumulate despite cessation of any obvious trigger. In this patient CK was 337 U/L during the initial hospitalisation for semaglutide‑related nausea/vomiting—a value only mildly above the laboratory upper limit yet disproportionate to her lack of muscular symptoms at that time. Over the next four months CK escalated to 3 992 U/L concomitant with new proximal weakness and dysphagia—a >12‑fold increase that mirrors the typical “slow burn” pattern seen in IMNM rather than the self‑limited spike expected from pure drug toxicity. Serial tracking therefore reinforced that muscle injury was ongoing rather than a one‑off insult.

### Interpreting transaminases in the setting of CK elevation  

Both aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) are present in skeletal muscle as well as hepatocytes; however their relative contribution differs—AST is more abundant in muscle whereas ALT is hepatocyte‑predominant. When muscle breakdown is severe, AST often rises out-of‑proportion to ALT, giving an AST > ALT ratio that suggests muscular origin. In this case AST was 191 U/L and ALT 165 U/L while CK was markedly elevated; the modest absolute increase coupled with a preserved AST/ALT ratio (≈1.2) made a purely muscular source unlikely because even severe necrotising myopathies rarely push liver enzymes past several hundred units unless there is concomitant hepatic involvement. Moreover, a liver ultrasound performed three years earlier had already documented non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), later confirmed by repeat biopsy showing macrovesicular steatosis involving ~70 % of parenchyma without significant inflammation. The chronic hepatic steatosis thus explained the baseline transaminitis independent of acute muscle injury. Recognising that steady‐state AST/ALT elevations can coexist with dynamic CK changes prevented misattribution of the whole picture to hepatic pathology alone.

### Autoimmune serology: negative ANA panel and absent HMGCR/SRP antibodies  

A comprehensive myositis serology panel was pursued early because autoantibodies help stratify idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) into distinct phenotypes with therapeutic implications. The panel excluded antinuclear antibodies (ANA), anti–double‐stranded DNA, extractable nuclear antigens (SSA/Ro, SSB/La), anti–Jo‑1 family members, anti–Mi‑2, anti–MDA‑5, anti–TIF1‑γ, anti–NXP‑2, anti–cN‑1A and—critically—the two antibodies most strongly associated with IMNM: anti–HMGCR (often statin‐related) and anti–SRP. All were absent. Seronegativity does not preclude IMNM; approximately one‑third of patients lack detectable autoantibodies (“seronegative IMNM”) yet demonstrate identical histopathologic features and comparable treatment responses.^[1] Hence, while negative serology lowered suspicion for overlapping connective‐tissue disease (e.g., antisynthetase syndrome) it reinforced reliance on tissue diagnosis rather than dismissing an autoimmune etiology outright.

### Low‑titer anti–SS‑B positivity without ANA or anti–SS‑A  

An isolated low‐titer anti–SS‑B (Ro 52) at 2.4 U/mL was observed despite negative ANA and absent anti–SS‑A antibodies. Anti–SS‑B can appear as an isolated finding in healthy individuals or in various non‑Sjogren’s conditions such as inflammatory myopathies.^[2] However its diagnostic weight hinges on accompanying clinical features—dry eyes/mouth, salivary gland enlargement—or corroborating serologies such as ANA positivity or high‑titer anti–SS‑A—all lacking here. Consequently the result was interpreted as an incidental antibody without pathogenic relevance to her myopathy and did not sway management decisions.

### Magnetic resonance imaging of the thighs  

MRI is increasingly employed as a noninvasive screen for inflammatory myopathies because oedematous signal changes mirror active fibre injury before overt atrophy appears on physical exam.^[3] The bilateral femur protocol showed symmetric hyperintensity on fluid‐sensitive sequences within the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles together with adjacent subcutaneous oedema—a distribution classic for proximal inflammatory myopathy rather than focal neurogenic atrophy or compartment syndrome which tend to be unilateral or asymmetric. The symmetry helped exclude mechanical injuries or disuse atrophy secondary to deconditioning after her acute renal episode while supporting an immune process affecting multiple proximal groups—consistent with IMNM’s typical predilection for hip girdle musculature.

### Cardiac assessment: echocardiography and cardiac MRI  

Myositis patients may harbor subclinical myocarditis or cardiomyopathic changes owing to shared autoimmunity across striated muscle.^[4] Transthoracic echocardiography revealed preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (65–70 %) but modestly elevated right ventricular systolic pressure (46 mm Hg), prompting further evaluation with cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR). CMR demonstrated mild mid-anteroseptal hypokinesis accompanied by myocardial oedema yet absent late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). LGE denotes irreversible fibrosis or necrosis characteristic of definitive myocarditis; its absence suggested either transient inflammation (“early myocarditis”) or secondary stress response rather than fulminant myocardial injury.^[5] Importantly, these cardiac abnormalities resolved on follow-up imaging after immunosuppression cessation—mirroring recovery seen in her skeletal muscles—thereby reinforcing that modest cardiac involvement was likely an epiphenomenon rather than an independent autoimmune process requiring separate therapeutic escalation.

### Thoracic CT excluding interstitial lung disease  

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) frequently accompanies antisynthetase syndrome or certain overlap IIMs.^[6] High-resolution computed tomography showed clear lung fields without ground-glass opacities, honeycombing or lymphadenopathy—effectively ruling out clinically significant ILD at this juncture. The absence mitigated concern for an antisynthetase phenotype despite seronegative status and placed greater diagnostic weight on isolated skeletal-muscle pathology rather than systemic connective tissue disease encompassing pulmonary fibrosis.

### Liver imaging & biopsy confirming chronic NAFLD  

Given persistent mild transaminitis it was essential to verify whether new hepatic injury contributed to her presentation—or alternatively signaled extrahepatic muscle breakdown masquerading as hepatic enzyme leakage. Serial abdominal ultrasonography confirmed fatty infiltration without biliary obstruction or focal lesions while percutaneous liver biopsy demonstrated macrovesicular steatosis with minimal portal inflammation—findings congruent with chronic NAFLD matched by prior histology three years earlier.^[7] Consequently hepatic disease was deemed chronic background noise unrelated to her acute neuromuscular decline and required no additional intervention beyond lifestyle counselling already underway before semaglutide initiation.

### Muscle biopsy—the diagnostic gold standard  

The definitive proof came from histopathology obtained from the right vastus lateralis—a targeted site showing MRI hyperintensity yet spared from extensive fibrosis that could confound interpretation.^[8] Light microscopy revealed scattered necrotic fibres interspersed with regenerated basophilic fibres—a hallmark pattern indicating active fibre turnover rather than chronic atrophy alone. Immunohistochemistry disclosed numerous CD68⁺ macrophages engaged in myophagocytosis surrounding necrotic cells—a feature distinctive for necrotising immune-mediated myopathies where complement activation recruits innate effector cells.^[9] Moreover, deposition of membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) along both necrotic and non-necrotic sarcolemma highlighted complement‐mediated injury central to IMNM pathogenesis.^[10] T-cell infiltrates were sparse (rare CD3⁺ cells), distinguishing this entity from polymyositis wherein dense endomysial CD8⁺ T‐cell infiltrates predominate.^[11] Together these microscopic hallmarks satisfied contemporary diagnostic criteria for IMNM as per European Neuromuscular Centre consensus statements.^[12]

### Applying WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality criteria  

To gauge whether semaglutide plausibly precipitated the IMNM episode we employed the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre’s algorithm which grades drug-event relationships into categories ranging from “certain” to “unlikely.” Three principal elements aligned with a “probable” designation:

1. **Temporal relationship** – The patient experienced immediate gastrointestinal intolerance within hours after her inaugural semaglutide dose followed by delayed progressive myopathy over four months—a latency compatible with immune sensitisation rather than direct toxicity.
2. **Exclusion of alternative causes** – Comprehensive laboratory workup ruled out statin exposure, other known offending agents (e.g., red yeast rice), endocrine disorders (thyroid normal), infectious triggers (negative viral panels), metabolic disturbances aside from pre-existing NAFLD, and systemic rheumatologic disease (negative ANA panel). Muscle biopsy confirmed an immune pathology independent of metabolic/myopathic mimicry.
3. **Positive dechallenge** – Cessation of semaglutide coincided temporally with initiation of immunosuppression during which CK dropped dramatically (< 500 U/L within two weeks) and functional recovery ensued despite brief corticosteroid exposure; relapse did not occur when immunosuppression was tapered off while semaglutide remained withheld.

Rechallenge would have been unethical given potential recurrence of severe necrosis; therefore collateral evidence could not be obtained—a common limitation when assessing rare serious adverse events linked to novel agents.[^13]

Collectively these criteria satisfy “probable” causality under WHO-Uppsala guidelines: reasonable time sequence plus lack of alternative explanations plus improvement after drug withdrawal—but without re-exposure proof required for “certain” classification.^[14]

---

In synthesis, methodical integration of serial CK trends, nuanced enzyme interpretation against a backdrop of chronic NAFLD, exhaustive autoimmune serology—including appreciation for seronegative disease—and targeted radiologic assessments converged on an inflammatory necrotising process confined primarily to skeletal muscle. Muscle biopsy provided irrefutable histologic confirmation while simultaneously excluding mimickers such as polymyositis or inclusion-body myositis through its distinctive complement‐mediated macrophage pattern. Finally applying formal pharmacovigilance criteria placed semaglutide as a probable trigger rather than an incidental finding—a conclusion that shapes both immediate patient management (drug avoidance) and future surveillance strategies for GLP‑1 receptor agonists.<sup>†</sup>

## Outcome, Clinical Significance, and Future Directions  

**Long‑term functional recovery and CK normalization**  
After an intensive inpatient course that included five days of pulse methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone (1 mg kg⁻¹ day⁻¹) and a single course of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG 2 g kg⁻¹), the patient’s creatine kinase (CK) fell from a peak of 3 992 U L⁻¹ to within the laboratory reference range (≤191 U L⁻¹) within two weeks (Fig. 1). Serial outpatient measurements remained normal throughout follow‑up. Clinically, proximal strength improved gradually: by the end of the first month she could sit unsupported and transfer from chair to bed; by month three she ambulated independently with a normal gait; at ten months she reported no residual weakness, dysphagia or dysphonia and was fully resuming her pre‑illness activities of daily living. This trajectory—rapid biochemical response followed by steady functional gain—mirrors the pattern described for drug‑induced immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), which often remits after brief immunosuppression and removal of the offending agent.

**Resolution of cardiac imaging abnormalities**  
Initial cardiac magnetic resonance imaging revealed focal myocardial oedema and mild hypokinesis in the mid‑anteroseptal region without late gadolinium enhancement, findings that were interpreted as probable inflammatory myocarditis versus stress‑related cardiomyopathy. Repeat cardiac MRI performed ten months after presentation showed complete normalization of ventricular wall motion and absence of myocardial oedema or fibrosis. The concurrent normalization of high‑sensitivity troponin (62 pg mL⁻¹ → <53 pg mL⁻¹) and pro‑BNP (4 358 pg mL⁻¹ → <125 pg mL⁻¹) supported true resolution rather than merely subclinical fluctuation. Moreover, the patient experienced no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation after discharge, suggesting that the transient arrhythmia was likely a consequence of the acute systemic illness rather than a persistent cardiac manifestation of IMNM.

**Sustained remission without ongoing immunosuppression**  
Because CK remained suppressed and functional status stayed intact, oral prednisone was tapered rapidly over eight weeks and discontinued after two months without rebound disease activity. No additional IVIG doses were administered after the initial inpatient course—a decision driven by the patient’s clinical stability and cost considerations. At her last clinic visit (10 months after onset), she remained off all immunosuppressive therapy for more than seven months, illustrating that drug‑related IMNM may achieve durable remission after short‑term treatment when the trigger is eliminated.

### Implications for Prescribing GLP‑1 Receptor Agonists  

The case underscores that clinicians should perform a focused risk assessment before initiating glucagon‑like peptide‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs) in patients who may harbor latent predisposition to autoimmune myopathy:

* **Baseline evaluation:** Obtain a thorough neuromuscular history (prior unexplained weakness or myalgias) and a baseline CK level in individuals with a history of autoimmune disease, prior statin exposure or unexplained transaminase elevation that may reflect occult myopathy.
* **Patient counseling:** Discuss the common gastrointestinal adverse profile **and** the rare possibility of severe muscle injury—including immune‑mediated necrosis—especially if patients develop persistent weakness or disproportionate CK rise after starting therapy.
* **Monitoring plan:** Reassess CK within 2–4 weeks if new muscle symptoms emerge; an isolated modest rise (<5× ULN) may represent exertional injury or mild drug toxicity, whereas progressive elevations (>10× ULN) warrant prompt neuromuscular workup (EMG/MRI/biopsy).

These steps do not preclude the benefits of GLP‑1 RAs in obesity or type 2 diabetes but help balance efficacy against an emerging safety signal.

### Pharmacovigilance: Reporting Rare Autoimmune Events  

Rare adverse reactions such as GLP‑1 RA–associated IMNM are unlikely to be captured in pre‑approval trials due to limited sample size and duration. Post‑marketing surveillance therefore relies heavily on clinician‑initiated case reporting:

* **Regulatory pathways:** Submit detailed reports to national pharmacovigilance systems (e.g., FDA’s FAERS in the United States or EudraVigilance in Europe), including temporal relationship, laboratory data, histopathology findings, management steps taken, and outcome.
* **Signal generation:** Aggregated individual case safety reports can trigger formal pharmacovigilance investigations leading to label updates or risk mitigation strategies.
* **Ethical duty:** Even single cases contribute valuable information about potential class effects—here extending concern from tirzepatide–related rhabdomyolysis to a possible semaglutide–induced autoimmune process.

Thus, systematic reporting is essential for early detection of previously unrecognized drug toxicities.

### Causality Considerations  

Applying the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre criteria yields a *probable* causal relationship between semaglutide exposure and IMNM in this patient:

* **Temporal association:** Onset of gastrointestinal toxicity within hours followed by progressive myopathic symptoms over four months.
* **Dechallenge response:** Discontinuation of semaglutide coincided with biochemical remission after immunosuppression.
* **Alternative explanations:** The absence of statin use, negative extended myositis panel—including anti‑SRP/HMGCR—and lack of other known myotoxic agents reduce competing etiologies.

However, limitations temper confidence:

* **Single‐case design** precludes statistical inference.
* **No rechallenge**, which would ethically be unacceptable given the severity.
* **Missing pre‑exposure CK** prevents definitive exclusion of subclinical preexisting IMNM.
* **Seronegative status**, while common (~⅓ cases), hampers mechanistic linking to known autoantibodies.

Consequently, clinicians should interpret this association as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive proof.

### Research Priorities  

1. **Mechanistic exploration** – In vitro studies suggest GLP‑1 receptors are expressed on immune cells (macrophages, dendritic cells); research should delineate whether GLP‑1 RA binding alters cytokine profiles or promotes antigen presentation that could trigger autoimmunity.
2. **Genetic susceptibility** – Genome‐wide association studies in patients who develop IMNM after GLP‑1 RA exposure could identify HLA alleles or other polymorphisms conferring risk.
3. **Serologic discovery** – Comprehensive proteomic screening may uncover novel autoantibodies specific to GLP‑1 RA–associated myopathy.
4. **Epidemiologic surveillance** – Prospective registries tracking CK trajectories in large GLP‑1 RA cohorts would quantify incidence and enable risk stratification.
5. **Therapeutic algorithms** – Controlled studies comparing short versus prolonged immunosuppression in drug‐triggered IMNM could clarify optimal treatment duration.

Addressing these gaps will inform safer prescribing practices and guide management when immune‐mediated muscle injury occurs.

### Broader Clinical Lesson  

The case illustrates that any unexplained elevation in CK occurring after initiation of a new medication—particularly when accompanied by progressive weakness—should prompt an early neuromuscular assessment rather than being dismissed as benign drug toxicity or simple rhabdomyolysis. Essential steps include:

* Detailed physical examination focusing on proximal strength and bulbar function.
* Prompt imaging (muscle MRI) to detect oedematous changes suggestive of inflammatory myopathy.
* Electromyography/electroneurography if indicated.
* Timely muscle biopsy when CK remains markedly elevated (>5–10× ULN) or when clinical features suggest an immune process.

Early identification enables targeted therapy (e.g., corticosteroids ± IVIG) before irreversible muscle loss ensues and minimizes unnecessary prolonged immunosuppression if removal of the inciting drug suffices for remission.

In sum, this patient’s trajectory—from acute semaglutide intolerance through severe IMNM to full recovery without ongoing immunosuppression—highlights both a potential new safety concern with GLP‑1 receptor agonists and an actionable framework for clinicians to monitor, report, and manage similar presentations in future practice.
