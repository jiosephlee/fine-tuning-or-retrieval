Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Differential Diagnosis] When a patient on a GLP‑1 receptor agonist develops elevated CK, how can clinicians differentiate acute drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis from a drug‑triggered immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy?

**Differential Diagnosis – Drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis versus drug‑triggered immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) in a patient receiving a GLP‑1 receptor agonist**

The case of a 60‑year‑old woman who received a single dose of semaglutide illustrates how the time course, clinical picture, laboratory dynamics, imaging, and histopathology separate an acute toxic myopathy from an immune‑mediated necrotising process.

---

### 1.  Temporal pattern and symptom evolution

| Feature | Acute drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis | Drug‑triggered IMNM |
|---------|-----------------------------------|----------------------|
| **Onset of CK rise** | Hours–days after exposure (often within 24 h) | Days–weeks after exposure; may continue to climb for weeks/months |
| **Peak CK** | Frequently >10 000 U/L; can exceed 50 000 U/L (e.g., tirzepatide case) | Usually <10 000 U/L but can be several thousand (patient’s peak ≈ 4 000 U/L) |
| **Course after drug stop** | Rapid decline once hydration and cessation occur (days) | Persistent or rising CK despite withdrawal; only falls after immunosuppression |
| **Associated symptoms** | Severe myalgias, tender swelling, dark (“cola‑coloured”) urine from myoglobinuria | Progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, minimal pain; no pigmenturia |

*In the present case* the first CK measurement (337 U/L) was taken during an acutely sick admission for nausea/vomiting and AKI – a level far below typical rhabdomyolysis thresholds and without myalgias or pigmenturia. Four months later the CK climbed to 3 992 U/L while the patient was off semaglutide and developed disabling proximal weakness—classic for IMNM rather than a transient toxic injury.

---

### 2.  Laboratory clues beyond raw CK

| Parameter | Rhadomyolysis | IMNM |
|-----------|----------------|------|
| **Serum myoglobin** – often detectable in rhabdo | Frequently elevated; falls quickly | May be normal or mildly elevated |
| **Renal function** – AKI usually due to myoglobin cast nephropathy | Common early on | May be normal or secondary to other causes |
| **Inflammatory markers** – CRP/ESR | Usually modestly raised or normal | Frequently elevated (ESR 22 mm/h, CRP 22.8 mg/L in patient) |
| **Autoantibodies** – anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP | Usually negative unless pre‑existing statin exposure | Positive in ~⅔ of cases; up to one third are seronegative as in this patient |

Thus a modest rise in inflammatory markers coupled with negative statin exposure points away from a pure toxic picture.

---

### 3.  Imaging

* **MRI of affected muscles**  
  - **Rhabdomyolysis:** diffuse edema limited to injured muscle groups, often symmetric but resolves rapidly after treatment.  
  - **IMNM:** symmetric proximal edema persisting beyond the acute phase; may show subcutaneous edema as seen on the patient’s femur MRI (mild symmetric edema of vastus lateralis/quadriceps).

MRI helps confirm ongoing inflammation when the clinical picture is indeterminate.

---

### 4.  Muscle biopsy – the definitive discriminant

| Histologic finding | Rhadomyolysis | IMNM |
|--------------------|---------------|------|
| Necrotic fibers with minimal inflammation | Yes ‑ necrosis dominates ‑ scant inflammatory cells | Yes ‑ necrosis + prominent macrophage infiltrates (CD68⁺) + complement membrane attack complex deposition on non‑necrotic fibers |
| Lymphocytic infiltrate density | Sparse or absent | Typically sparse lymphocytes but significant macrophages & perivascular inflammation (as in the case) |
| Regenerating fibers & sarcolemmal MAC positivity | May be present but limited | Characteristic MAC staining on both necrotic and intact fibers |

In this patient the biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic/regenerating fibers with CD68⁺ macrophages and MAC deposition – patterns that fulfill criteria for IMNM and are not seen in pure toxic rhabdomyolysis.

---

### 5.  Therapeutic response

- **Rhabdomyolysis:** resolution with aggressive IV fluids ± bicarbonate; CK falls within days without immunosuppression.
- **IMNM:** requires immunomodulation—high‑dose steroids ± IVIG or steroid‑sparing agents—often shows rapid CK decline only after such therapy (patient’s CK fell markedly after methylprednisolone pulse and IVIG).

A dramatic improvement after steroids/IVIG strongly supports an immune-mediated process.

---

### 6.  Practical algorithm for clinicians

1. **Identify timing** – if CK spikes >10 000 U/L within <48 h of GLP‑1RA dose → think rhabdo.
2. **Assess symptoms** – severe pain/myoglobinuria → rhabdo; progressive weakness without pain → consider IMNM.
3. **Check trends** – rapid normalization after drug stop → rhabdo; persistent elevation despite cessation → IMNM.
4. **Order labs** – CBC, CMP, ESR/CRP, urine dipstick for blood without RBCs, myositis autoantibody panel.
5. **Obtain MRI of symptomatic muscles** when CK >1500 U/L persisting >1 week.
6. **Proceed to muscle biopsy** if MRI or antibody workup is nondiagnostic and weakness is disabling.
7. **Initiate therapy**:
   - Rhabdo: vigorous hydration ± alkalinization.
   - IMNM: high-dose corticosteroids ± IVIG early; consider steroid-sparing agents if relapse occurs.

---

### Key teaching pearls from the semaglutide case

- A single dose of a GLP‑1RA can precipitate an immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy that evolves over months rather than minutes.
- Modest early CK elevation does *not* rule out later development of severe autoimmune myopathy.
- Absence of classic autoantibodies does not exclude IMNM—biopsy remains gold standard.
- Prompt immunosuppression can lead to full functional recovery even when the offending agent is stopped early.

By systematically evaluating onset, symptom type, biochemical trajectory, imaging findings, and histology, clinicians can reliably separate an acute toxic rhabdomyolysis from an emerging GLP‑1RA–triggered IMNM—and institute appropriate therapy before irreversible muscle loss occurs.
