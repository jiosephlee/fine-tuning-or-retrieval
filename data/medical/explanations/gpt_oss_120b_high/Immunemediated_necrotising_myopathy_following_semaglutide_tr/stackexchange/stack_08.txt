Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Management] Given the patient’s mildly elevated troponin and cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement, what cardiac complications should be screened for in IMNM, and what follow‑up strategy is appropriate?

**Cardiac complications to look for in immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)  
and a pragmatic follow‑up plan – Lessons from the semaglutide‑associated case**

The patient described developed a mild rise in high‑sensitivity troponin (62 pg/mL) and a cardiac magnetic‑resonance (CMR) study that showed focal myocardial oedema with only modest hypokinesis and *no* late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). In the context of IMNM this pattern signals **early inflammatory myocardial involvement** – essentially a subclinical myocarditis – rather than irreversible scar. Although IMNM is principally a skeletal‑muscle disease, it shares the same spectrum of cardiac complications seen in other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs).

---

### 1. Cardiac complications that merit active screening

| Complication | Why it occurs in IMNM | How it may present / be detected |
|--------------|----------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Myocarditis** (active inflammation) | Autoimmune attack can involve the myocardium; CMR oedema is a sensitive marker | • Elevated troponin (>2–3× ULN) <br>• New ECG ST/T changes or low voltage <br>• New regional wall‑motion abnormality on echo <br>• CMR oedema ± LGE |
| **Myocardial fibrosis / scar** | Persistent inflammation may evolve into replacement fibrosis | • Presence of LGE on CMR <br>• Persistent elevation of troponin despite treatment |
| **Systolic or diastolic heart failure** | Myocardial injury reduces contractility & compliance | • Decline in EF on echo or CMR <br>• Rising NT‑proBNP/BNP <br>• Symptoms of dyspnoea, orthopnoea |
| **Arrhythmias** – atrial fibrillation/flutter, premature ventricular beats, non‑sustained VT | Inflammatory infiltration of conduction tissue & scar predispose to ectopy | • Palpitations, syncope <br>• ECG changes <br>• Ambulatory Holter/patch monitor |
| **Conduction disease** – AV block, bundle branch block | Same pathophysiology as arrhythmias | • New PR prolongation or QRS widening on ECG |
| **Pulmonary hypertension secondary to left‑heart disease** | Elevated RV systolic pressure noted on initial echo (46 mm Hg) | • Repeat Doppler echo <br>• Clinical signs (exertional dyspnoea) |

*Key point*: The absence of LGE in this patient’s CMR is reassuring that overt fibrosis has not yet developed, but it does not eliminate the risk of evolving myocarditis or later scar formation.

---

### 2. Immediate diagnostic work‑up (first 4–6 weeks)

1. **Baseline cardiac visit**  
   - Review symptoms (chest pain, dyspnoea, palpitations).  
   - Physical exam focused on volume status & heart sounds.
2. **12‑lead ECG** – look for rhythm disturbances or conduction delay.
3. **High‑sensitivity troponin & NT‑proBNP** – obtain at baseline and repeat in 1–2 weeks to determine trend.
4. **Transthoracic echocardiogram** – confirm left‑ventricular ejection fraction (≥60 % now), assess wall motion, estimate right‑ventricular systolic pressure again.
5. **24–48 h ambulatory rhythm monitor** (Holter or patch) because she already displayed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation during her first admission.
6. **Cardiac MRI re‑assessment** – not mandatory immediately unless symptoms worsen; however the initial study serves as a reference point.

---

### 3. Ongoing surveillance strategy

| Time point | Investigations | Rationale |
|------------|----------------|-----------|
| **1–2 months after discharge** | Repeat troponin & BNP; ECG ± short‑term Holter if prior arrhythmia | Detect lingering myocardial injury or early recurrence |
| **3 months** | Transthoracic echo + symptom review | Identify new systolic/diastolic dysfunction before it becomes symptomatic |
| **6 months** | Repeat CMR *if* initial scan showed oedema OR if troponin/B‐type natriuretic peptide remain mildly elevated >2× ULN | Determine whether oedema resolved, whether any LGE has appeared (scar), and guide duration of immunosuppression |
| **Every 6–12 months thereafter** | Symptom inquiry + ECG ± annual Holter (more frequently if prior arrhythmia). Repeat BNP/troponin only if clinically indicated.| Long‑term vigilance—late onset myocarditis or fibrosis can emerge even after skeletal‐muscle remission |

*Practical tip*: Use the same laboratory platform for serial troponins so that assay variability does not obscure true trends.

---

### 4. How this case informs the plan

- **Mild troponin rise** in an IMNM patient should be taken seriously despite its modest magnitude because high‑sensitivity assays are exquisitely sensitive to even focal inflammation.
- **CMR oedema without LGE** indicates early inflammatory change that often resolves with control of systemic disease; therefore a short‐interval repeat scan (~6 months) is appropriate rather than immediate reimaging.
- **Prior paroxysmal atrial fibrillation** justifies baseline rhythm monitoring and reinforces the need for vigilant symptom‐driven evaluation.
- The patient’s eventual normalization of CK, troponin, BNP, echocardiogram and repeat CMR underscores that *activity‑dependent* cardiac involvement can regress with prompt immunosuppression and removal of the trigger drug.

---

### 5. Take‑home pearls

1. **Screen all IMNM patients for cardiac involvement**, even when skeletal‐muscle disease appears isolated.  
2. **Troponin and BNP are useful triage tools**, but trends outweigh single values—persistent elevation warrants imaging and rhythm monitoring.  
3. **CMR is the gold standard** for detecting active myocarditis (edema) versus chronic scar (LGE); lack of LGE does not obviate follow‑up.  
4. **Arrange a baseline ECG/echo ± short Holter**, then tailor interval testing to biomarker dynamics and symptom evolution.  
5. **Collaborate closely with cardiology** from the outset—most centres now manage IIM‐related heart disease through shared rheumatology–cardiology clinics.

By adhering to this structured surveillance algorithm—clinical assessment plus serial biomarkers, imaging at key milestones, and rhythm monitoring—you can catch evolving cardiac pathology early while avoiding unnecessary testing in patients who remain stable after their myopathy has remitted.
