Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Morning Report] A patient presents with progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, CK > 3000 U/L, and normal ANA panel. Which sequence of laboratory and imaging studies is most appropriate to evaluate for IMNM?

**Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) – A step‑wise diagnostic pathway**  

*Clinical vignette*: A 60‑year‑old woman presents with progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, CK ≈ 4000 U/L and a negative ANA – the picture that prompted the work‑up described in Lam et al., *Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment*.

The goal is to move rapidly from “high CK” to a definitive diagnosis while excluding more common mimics (statin‑related toxic myopathy, endocrine myopathies, polymyositis). The most efficient sequence is:

---

### 1️⃣ Confirm the magnitude and persistence of muscle injury  
| Test | Why | Typical result in IMNM |
|------|-----|------------------------|
| **Serum CK** (repeat at least once) | Establish ≥5–10 × ULN and track trend | Markedly elevated (often >3000 U/L) and remains high despite rest |
| **Aldolase** (optional) | Additional muscle enzyme when CK interferes with monitoring | Often concordant with CK |
| **AST/ALT** | Distinguish hepatic from muscular transaminase rise | Mild‐to‑moderate elevation that mirrors CK change |

> *In the reported case the CK rose from 337 U/L at admission to a peak of 3992 U/L over 4 months.*

---

### 2️⃣ Exclude non‑immune/metabolic causes early  
| Test | Rationale |
|------|-----------|
| **CBC, CMP** (including electrolytes & renal function) | Look for hypophosphatemia, renal insufficiency that can aggravate rhabdomyolysis |
| **Thyroid panel** (TSH ± free T4) | Hyper‑ or hypothyroidism can mimic inflammatory myopathy |
| **Medication review** – especially statins, fibrates, red‑yeast rice or recent GLP‑1 RAs | Statin exposure raises suspicion for anti‑HMGCR disease; absence steers toward “seronegative” IMNM – exactly as in the case where no statins were used |
| **Vitamin D level** (optional) | Deficiency may exacerbate weakness but does not cause CK elevations this high |

---

### 3️⃣ Targeted auto‑immune serology – first‑tier tests  
1. **ANA** – screening test; often *negative* in pure IMNM (negative in the case).  
2. **Myositis‐specific antibody panel** – includes:
   - **Anti‑HMGCR**
   - **Anti‑SRP**
   - Other MSAs/MAAs (Jo‑1, PL‑7/12, Mi‑2, MDA5, TIF1‑γ, NXP‑2, cN1A)
   
> *Lam et al.* sent a comprehensive panel that returned negative for both HMGCR and SRP as well as all other MSAs—highlighting that ≈30 % of IMNM patients are seronegative.  

If any MSA is positive → strong support for antibody‑associated IMNM and often guides therapy (e.g., HMGCR positivity frequently follows statin exposure).

---

### 4️⃣ Imaging of symptomatic muscle groups – next logical step when serology is negative  
**MRI of affected proximal muscles (typically thighs or calves)**

- **Sequences:** STIR/T2 fat‑suppressed images are most sensitive for oedema.
- **Findings typical of IMNM:** Diffuse symmetric hyperintensity reflecting oedema without focal mass or fatty replacement; may show subcutaneous oedema as described in the case (“mild symmetric edema in proximal vastus lateralis”).  

Why imaging before EMG or biopsy?  

- Provides a “road map” for the most involved muscle → higher diagnostic yield on biopsy.
- Helps differentiate inflammatory from purely mechanical injury or compartment syndrome.
- In seronegative disease it bridges the gap left by a negative autoantibody screen—exactly what was done in the reported patient.

---

### 5️⃣ Electromyography (EMG) – optional adjunct  
- **When useful:** If MRI is unavailable or equivocal; to demonstrate myopathic motor unit potentials with fibrillation potentials/positive sharp waves.
- **Limitations:** Necrotising forms may have relatively bland EMG findings; therefore EMG is not mandatory if MRI already shows convincing oedema and clinical suspicion is high.  

In the indexed case EMG was not reported because MRI provided sufficient guidance toward biopsy.

---

### 6️⃣ Definitive tissue diagnosis – **muscle biopsy**  

**Procedure** – Open or needle core from the most edematous region identified on MRI.  

**Key histopathologic hallmarks of IMNM:**  
- Scattered necrotic + regenerating fibers  
- Sparse lymphocytic infiltrate (often CD68⁺ macrophages)  
- Deposition of complement membrane attack complex on sarcolemma  
- Minimal endomysial inflammation  

These criteria were fulfilled in the patient’s right vastus lateralis biopsy—confirming IMNM despite a negative serology panel.

---

### 7️⃣ Ancillary cardiac workup when clinically indicated  

Because IMNM can be associated with subclinical myocarditis:
- **High-sensitivity troponin / BNP**
- **Echocardiogram & cardiac MRI** if symptoms or biomarkers suggest cardiac involvement

The case demonstrated mild myocardial oedema on cardiac MRI that resolved after treatment—illustrating why an elevated troponin prompted further cardiac imaging despite primary skeletal muscle disease.

---

## Putting it together – Algorithm

1. **CK → repeat + aldolase + AST/ALT**  
2. **Basic labs & medication review** (statins, GLP‑1 RA) → exclude metabolic causes  
3. **ANA + comprehensive myositis antibody panel**  
   - *Positive*: treat as antibody-associated IMNM (often still obtain biopsy).  
   - *Negative*: proceed promptly to imaging.  
4. **MRI of proximal muscles** → locate active inflammation & plan biopsy site.  
5. *(If MRI unavailable)* Consider EMG to support myopathic process.  
6. **Targeted muscle biopsy** → confirm necrosis with scant infiltrate / MAC deposition.  
7. **Cardiac evaluation** when troponin/B‐type natriuretic peptide abnormal or symptoms present.

---

### Take‑home pearls

- **Seronegative IMNM is common** (~⅓ of cases); a normal ANA does not exclude disease.
- A *negative* HMGCR/SRP panel should prompt *immediate* muscle MRI rather than “watchful waiting.”
- Imaging guides *high-yield* biopsies—a critical step because the histologic picture distinguishes IMNM from polymyositis or drug‐induced rhabdomyolysis.
- Early recognition permits timely immunosuppression (corticosteroids ± IVIG), as shown by rapid CK fall and functional recovery in the semaglutide­–related case.

By adhering to this stepwise laboratory–imaging–biopsy sequence you can efficiently confirm—or exclude—IMNM even when autoantibodies are absent, enabling prompt therapy and preventing unnecessary delays.
