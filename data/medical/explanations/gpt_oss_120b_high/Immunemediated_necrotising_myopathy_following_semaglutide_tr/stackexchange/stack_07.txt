Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Management] What is the recommended first‑line therapeutic regimen for seronegative IMNM, and why is intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) added to high‑dose corticosteroids in severe cases?

**First‑line therapy for seronegative immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**  
The initial regimen that most experts use for any adult patient with IMNM—including those who are seronegative—is:

1. **High‑dose glucocorticoid pulse:**  
   • Intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3–5 days (or 500–1000 mg daily if weight‑adjusted).  
   • Followed by oral prednisone ≈ 1 mg/kg day (usually tapered over 2–3 months once clinical improvement is evident).

2. **Early adjunctive immunomodulation** when the disease is moderate to severe—most commonly a steroid‑sparing agent such as methotrexate, azathioprine or mycophenolate mofetil started within the first few weeks. In practice many clinicians add these agents simultaneously with the steroid pulse if there are features that predict a poor steroid‐only response (bulbar or respiratory weakness, CK >5 000 U/L, rapid functional decline).

**Why add intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) in severe cases?**  

The decision to give IVIG on top of steroids (as was done in the case you reviewed) rests on three pillars:

| Reason | Pathophysiologic basis | Clinical cue from the case |
|--------|------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Rapid neutralisation of pathogenic humoral activity** | IMNM is driven by autoantibody‑mediated complement activation; even seronegative patients show membrane‑attack‑complex deposition on non‑necrotic fibers (the biopsy demonstrated C5b‑9 staining). IVIG contains pooled IgG that blocks Fcγ receptors on macrophages, competitively inhibits autoantibody binding, and supplies anti‑idiotypic antibodies that can neutralise unknown pathogenic antibodies. | The muscle biopsy showed MAC positivity on necrotic and non‑necrotic fibers—evidence of complement‐mediated injury that is amenable to IVIG’s complement‑inhibitory effects. |
| **Fast clinical improvement while waiting for slower agents** | Steroid‐sparing drugs take weeks to achieve therapeutic levels; IVIG produces biologic effects within days, leading to brisk declines in CK and measurable strength gains. | The patient remained profoundly weak after five days of methylprednisolone pulse and had bulbar signs (dysphagia/dysphonia). Adding IVIG (2 g/kg over 4 days) precipitated a marked CK fall over two weeks and functional recovery sufficient to wean steroids rapidly. |
| **Reduction of steroid exposure & prevention of irreversible damage** | Persistent necrosis leads to fibrosis and permanent deficit. Early aggressive control mitigates this risk and allows a shorter steroid taper—critical in older patients with co‑morbidities (obesity, hypertension). | By achieving remission after a single course of IVIG she could be tapered off prednisone within two months without relapse—a practical illustration of steroid sparing achieved through early IVIG use. |

**Practical algorithm derived from the case and consensus guidance**

1. **Diagnose IMNM (muscle biopsy with necrosis + MAC; negative SRP/HMGCR)** → start step 1 above.
2. **Assess severity**  
   – Bulbar/respiratory weakness, CK >4–5 × ULN, steep functional decline → label “severe”.  
   – Otherwise “moderate”.
3. **Severe disease** → give steroids **plus** either:
   - **IVIG** (2 g/kg split over 4–5 days) *or*  
   - Plasmapheresis if IVIG unavailable,
   followed by early initiation of a steroid‐sparing DMARD.
4. **Moderate disease** → steroids + early DMARD; reserve IVIG for refractory cases after 2–3 weeks.
5. **Follow up**: monitor CK weekly during acute phase; assess strength (MRC grade) and bulbar function biweekly; taper prednisone when CK stabilises <2×ULN and strength returns ≥4/5 in proximal muscles.

**Take‑home points**

*Seronegative IMNM behaves like its antibody‑positive counterparts.* The cornerstone is high‑dose glucocorticoids; however, when clinical features point toward aggressive disease—particularly bulbar involvement, markedly elevated CK, or lack of rapid response after pulse steroids—IVIG should be added promptly. In the presented patient, timely administration of a single course of IVIG was pivotal in halting necrosis, accelerating CK normalization, and permitting a short steroid course without relapse—a pattern that illustrates current best practice for severe seronegative IMNM.
