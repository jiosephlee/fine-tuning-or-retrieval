## Behçet’s Disease and Neuro‑Behçet’s Disease: Overview, Epidemiology and Diagnostic Framework  

The condition now called Behçet’s disease (BD) was first reported in 1937 by Turkish dermatologist Hulusi Behçet who described a triad of recurrent oral ulcers, genital ulceration and uveitis. Modern understanding regards BD as a multisystem autoinflammatory vasculitis capable of targeting vessels of any caliber throughout the body. Because no specific laboratory marker exists, diagnosis rests entirely on recognizing characteristic patterns of episodic inflammation that involve mucous membranes, skin, eyes and various internal organs. This clinical reliance makes familiarity with the disease’s epidemiology, genetic background, typical manifestations and formal diagnostic criteria essential when evaluating patients who present with seemingly disparate symptoms such as fever‐related headache combined with cutaneous ulcers—as occurred in our case report.

**Epidemiology and ethnic predisposition**  
The worldwide distribution of BD follows an ancient “Silk Road” pattern stretching from Mediterranean countries eastward through the Middle East into East Asia. Reported prevalence ranges from <1 per million in northern Europe up to >500 per million among Turkish or Japanese populations—a striking illustration that both geography and ethnicity influence disease risk profoundly. The strongest genetic association is with HLA‑B51; carriage confers roughly threefold increased susceptibility across most studied ethnicities. Additional alleles at HLA‐A,B loci as well as polymorphisms influencing cytokine regulation (e.g., IL‑10 promoter variants) modulate risk modestly but collectively underscore a polygenic predisposition onto which environmental triggers—especially microbial antigens encountered along one’s lifetime—act to precipitate overt disease flares.

**Pathogenic mechanisms**  
Current models integrate innate hyper­responsiveness with adaptive immune dysregulation into an overarching vasculitic process. Neutrophils obtained from affected individuals display heightened chemotaxis and spontaneous release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), processes that damage endothelial cells directly. Simultaneously T helper 1 and Th17 pathways are amplified—increasing IFN‑γ, IL‑17A and particularly IL‑6 production—while regulatory cytokines such as IL‑10 are relatively deficient. Elevated IL‑6 fuels systemic acute‐phase responses (manifested by high CRP) and also augments hypothalamic secretion of antidiuretic hormone (ADH), providing a plausible mechanistic link between CNS inflammation in neuro‐Behçet’s disease (neuro‐BD) and secondary disorders such as syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion (SIADH). The net effect is fibrinoid necrosis of small vessels accompanied by perivascular infiltrates rich in lymphocytes and activated macrophages—a histopathologic signature seen wherever BD manifests clinically.

**Core clinical manifestations**  
Three findings form the cornerstone of bedside recognition:

1️⃣ **Oral aphthae** – painful shallow ulcers affecting non‑keratinised mucosa appear in >95 % of patients during virtually every disease episode and therefore serve as an obligatory entry point for most diagnostic algorithms.  
2️⃣ **Genital ulceration** – usually necrotic erosions on scrotum or vulva mimicking oral ulcers histologically yet occurring at distinct sites; their presence markedly strengthens suspicion because isolated genital ulcers are rare outside BD.  
3️⃣ **Skin lesions** – diverse presentations include papulopustular eruptions resembling acne (“folliculitis‐like” rash), erythema nodosum–type nodules and superficial thrombophlebitis (“pathergy”).  

Additional organ systems frequently join this triad: recurrent anterior/posterior uveitis threatens vision unless promptly treated; large vessel involvement → thrombosis or aneurysm carries substantial morbidity; musculoskeletal complaints range from arthralgia to inflammatory arthritis reminiscent of seronegative spondyloarthropathies.

**International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD)**  
To move beyond anecdotal assessment the International Criteria assign numeric points based on clinical domains:

| Feature | Points |
|---------|--------|
| Oral aphthae | 2 |
| Genital aphthae | 2 |
| Ocular lesions | 2 |
| Skin lesions | 1 |
| Vascular manifestations | 1 |
| Neurological manifestations | 1 |

A total score **≥ 4** establishes “definite” BD irrespective of ancillary investigations such as pathergy testing—which remains optional supportive evidence rather than an obligatory component under current criteria. By counting each lesion type independently clinicians can capture evolving phenotypes—for instance our patient accrued two points from oral aphthae early on but attained diagnostic certainty only after genital ulceration emerged later during hospitalization (total = 5).

**Neuro‑Behçet’s disease classification**  
Neuro‐BD designates CNS involvement attributable directly to BD activity and subdivides into two phenotypes based on underlying pathology:

* **Parenchymal neuro‑BD** – inflammatory infiltrates within brain tissue dominate; > 80 % involve the brainstem despite occasional extension into basal ganglia or thalamus.
* **Non‑parenchymal neuro‑BD** – vascular complications dominate without overt tissue inflammation; typical entities include cerebral venous sinus thrombosis or arterial aneurysms leading chiefly to headache or seizure activity secondary to venous congestion rather than direct neuronal injury.

The distinction matters therapeutically because parenchymal disease frequently requires aggressive anti-inflammatory regimens whereas non‐parenchymal forms often benefit from anticoagulation alongside immunomodulation when appropriate.

**Typical presentations of acute neuro‑behcet’s disease**  
Acute parenchymal attacks usually evolve over days-to-weeks resembling infectious meningitis (“meningoencephalitic”). Hallmarks include fever, throbbing headache plus vertigo/dizziness reflecting brainstem irritation; cranial nerve palsies may appear if pontine nuclei are involved while overt meningeal stiffness can be surprisingly subtle due lack of prominent leptomeningeal inflammation at this stage. Because these signs overlap heavily with viral encephalitides—or even metabolic disturbances—the clinician must maintain high suspicion when neurological symptoms coexist with characteristic mucocutaneous findings suggestive of systemic BD—as demonstrated by our patient whose worsening headache prompted cerebrospinal fluid analysis despite normal neurologic exam otherwise.

**Radiologic findings on MRI & their limitations**  
Magnetic resonance imaging remains gold standard for detecting CNS inflammation in suspected neuro‐BD because T₂/FLAIR hyperintensities typically map onto brainstem nuclei or deep white matter tracts involved by vasculitic edema—and gadolinium enhancement may delineate active blood–brain barrier disruption. Nevertheless reports indicate that up to 20 % of patients presenting with acute parenchymal symptoms have completely normal brain MRIs during the initial window when microscopic leukocyte infiltration has yet produced macroscopic edema detectable by conventional sequences. Consequently a negative scan does *not* exclude neuro‐BD if clinical suspicion remains high; cerebrospinal fluid examination becomes pivotal—showing modest lymphocytic pleocytosis (+protein ↑ ) without bacterial growth—as observed here where aseptic meningitis corroborated CNS inflammation despite unremarkable imaging results including absence of hypothalamic signal change potentially implicated in SIADH genesis.


**Standard therapeutic approaches – systemic versus neurological involvement**  
Management hinges upon both organ severity and anticipated morbidity:

* **Systemic (<organ threatening)** – Recurrent oral/genital ulcers,
   papulopustular skin eruptions,
   mild arthralgia → first-line colchicine at doses inhibiting neutrophil chemotaxis;
   topical corticosteroids address painful ulcerations locally.
* **Organ-threatening/ocular/non­parenchymal vascular involvement** → prompt escalation
   consists primarily of high-dose systemic glucocorticoids (~1 mg/kg prednisone equivalent)
   followed by steroid-sparing agents such as azathioprine,
   cyclophosphamide,
   methotrexate,
   mycophenolate mofetil,
   or biologics targeting TNFα (infliximab/adalimumab) when refractory
   or intolerant—all aimed at rapid control given risk for irreversible damage.
* **Parenchymal acute neuro‐BD** → consensus dictates immediate high-dose corticosteroid pulses
   (“methylprednisolone 500–1000 mg/day ×3 days”) followed by taper;
   adjunctive immunosuppressants (azathioprine,
   cyclophosphamide)
   are added either upfront when severe deficits exist
   or later if steroid taper results in relapse;
   anti–IL‐6 receptor antibodies have been explored experimentally given IL 6 centrality,
   though they remain off-label presently.
   
Colchicine exerts anti-inflammatory effects chiefly via microtubule disruption impairing neutrophil migration while modestly suppressing IL 1β & IL 6 synthesis—it therefore excels at controlling mucocutaneous flares but systematic reviews reveal limited efficacy when used alone against established parenchymal CNS inflammation—hence corticosteroid-based regimens retain primacy unless contraindicated.


In sum, diagnosing Behçet’s disease fundamentally involves recognizing its hallmark recurrent mucosal ulcerations together with characteristic skin/orbital signs underpinned by validated ICBD scoring (>4 points). When neurological deficits appear contemporaneously—as they do rarely at presentation—the clinician must distinguish between parenchymal meningoencephalitic pathology versus vascular sequelae using CSF profiles complemented by—but not solely dependent upon—brain MRI findings whose sensitivity can be diminished early on. Understanding these diagnostic layers paves the way toward appropriately staged therapy ranging from colchicine monotherapy for simple mucocutaneous patterns up through high-dose glucocorticoids plus adjunctive immunosuppression when CNS inflammation threatens permanent deficit—a framework directly applicable to interpreting the atypical presentation described in this report wherein concurrent systemic BD signs enabled timely recognition of acute neuro­Behçet’s disease complicated by SIADH despite initially normal imaging studies。

## Case Presentation and Initial Assessment  

The patient was an Asian man in his early sixties who arrived at the emergency department after three days of progressive constitutional symptoms. His past medical history was notable only for type 2 diabetes mellitus that had never been treated because he was unaware of his disease and did not follow any dietary or exercise program. Apart from diabetes he had no known chronic illnesses, no relevant family history of autoimmune disease, and no recent travel, animal exposure, or sexual activity that might suggest an infectious trigger. This background set the stage for two competing early hypotheses: an undiagnosed infectious process facilitated by hyperglycaemia versus an intrinsic inflammatory disorder manifesting systemically.

### Symptom timeline  

The illness began with a low‑grade fever (up to 38 °C) accompanied by a throbbing frontal headache, generalized malaise, loss of appetite, persistent hiccups and arthralgia involving both shoulders and ankles. He also reported recent oral aphthous ulcers that had appeared before he sought medical care. Within a day after admission a painful ulcer developed on the scrotum—a finding that would later become pivotal for establishing a diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD). The temporal clustering of fever, headache and hiccups raised concern for meningeal irritation or a meningitis‑like syndrome, while the cutaneous ulcers suggested a mucocutaneous‐driven systemic process.

### Physical examination  

On examination the patient was alert, oriented and communicative but appeared fatigued. His vital signs demonstrated a temperature of 38 °C, heart rate 119 beats/min (regular), blood pressure 135/73 mm Hg, respiratory rate 19 breaths/min and oxygen saturation 96 % on ambient air. The tachycardia was proportional to his fever rather than hypovolemia because bedside assessment showed warm extremities, normal capillary refill and absence of orthostatic changes—all consistent with a euvolemic state. Dermatologic inspection revealed multiple shallow oral ulcers on the buccal mucosa together with several erythematous papules resembling folliculitis distributed over the face and trunk (Figure 1A‑C). No ocular inflammation was detected on bedside slit‑lamp examination. A neurological examination showed the patient to be fully awake with intact cranial nerve function; there was no neck stiffness or focal motor deficit despite his headache and vertigo. Muscle strength testing was normal throughout the upper and lower extremities. The combination of intact higher cortical function with subtle cerebellar‑type vertigo suggested central irritation without frank focal damage at this stage.

### Initial laboratory panel  

Laboratory investigations performed shortly after arrival disclosed several striking abnormalities:

* **Hyponatraemia** – serum sodium 124 mmol/L (reference 135‑145).  
* **Serum osmolality** – low at 267 mOsm/kg (normally 275‑295).  
* **Urine studies** – urine osmolality markedly elevated at 900 mOsm/kg with urinary sodium concentration 88 mmol/L (>20 mmol/L). These findings indicate an inability to excrete free water despite hypotonic serum—a hallmark pattern seen in the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH).  
* **ADH level** – measured ADH was inappropriately high at 22.4 pg/mL in the setting of hypo‑osmolar plasma, corroborating the diagnosis of SIADH.  
* **Inflammatory markers** – C‑reactive protein 180 mg/L (reference <3) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate 70 mm/h (reference <15), both highly elevated and signifying a vigorous systemic inflammatory response that could be infectious or autoinflammatory in nature.  
* **Creatine kinase** – markedly raised at 1 349 U/L together with modest elevations in AST 77 U/L and ALT 64 U/L, indicative either of muscle injury secondary to severe electrolyte disturbance (rhabdomyolysis) or an underlying myositis component driven by inflammation.  
* **Glycaemic parameters** – fasting plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L with HbA1c 8.2 %, confirming poorly controlled diabetes that could predispose both to infection and impaired immune modulation.

To exclude endocrine causes of euvolemic hyponatraemia—hypothyroidism and secondary adrenal insufficiency—thyroid–stimulating hormone (1.11 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL) and random cortisol (42 µg/dL) were measured and found within normal limits. Thus thyroid dysfunction or cortisol deficiency could not account for his low sodium level.

### Interpretation of the electrolyte picture  

Hyponatraemia is classified by tonicity (hypotonic versus isotonic versus hypertonic), volume status (hypovolaemic, euvolaemic or hypervolaemic), and underlying etiologic mechanisms such as renal salt loss versus excess water retention. In this patient:

* **Serum hypo‑osmolarity** confirmed true hypotonic hyponatraemia.
* **Clinical euvolaemia** combined with **high urine osmolality** (> serum) demonstrated that his kidneys were concentrating urine despite systemic hypotonicity—a physiologic paradox best explained by inappropriate secretion or action of antidiuretic hormone.
* **Elevated urinary sodium** ruled out extrarenal sodium loss as a driver.
* Additional exclusion criteria—normal thyroid function tests, normal cortisol levels, absence of pulmonary disease on imaging—favoured a diagnosis of SIADH according to established criteria (Bartter & Schwartz).

The presence of fever, headache and CSF findings compatible later with aseptic meningitis reinforced the concept that central nervous system inflammation was a plausible trigger for ectopic ADH release.

### Formulation of an initial problem list  

At this juncture the clinical team organized their thoughts into several overlapping problem categories:

1️⃣ **Febrile illness with meningeal‑type symptoms** – differential included bacterial meningitis, viral encephalitis/meningoencephalitis, tuberculous meningitis, autoimmune meningitis (including neuro‑Behçet’s disease), drug‑induced fever or non‑infectious inflammatory states such as sarcoidosis.

2️⃣ **Acute euvolemic hyponatraemia** meeting SIADH criteria – potential precipitants encompassed CNS inflammation (meningitis/encephalitis), pulmonary pathology (ruled out radiographically), medications (none administered before admission), adrenal insufficiency or hypothyroidism (excluded biochemically).

3️⃣ **Mucocutaneous lesions suggestive of Behçet’s disease** – oral aphthae plus folliculitis‑like papules pointed toward an autoinflammatory vasculitic process; definitive BD classification awaited confirmation by genital ulcer development and scoring by International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease.

4️⃣ **Marked elevation of CK** – raising concern for rhabdomyolysis possibly secondary to profound hyponatraemia-induced cellular swelling or direct inflammatory myocyte injury.

5️⃣ **Uncontrolled type 2 diabetes mellitus** – serving as a background comorbidity that could mask infection signs or influence immune response.

These problems directed immediate actions: empirical broad‑spectrum antibiotics covering potential bacterial meningitis were initiated while awaiting culture results; lumbar puncture was planned once coagulation status allowed; fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) was instituted promptly to mitigate further dilutional worsening of sodium levels; serial electrolyte checks were scheduled every six hours to avoid overly rapid correction which could precipitate osmotic demyelination syndrome. Simultaneously the team kept Behçet’s disease on their radar because its neurovascular involvement can manifest as meningoencephalitic presentations—and importantly because recognizing it early might influence immunosuppressive therapy decisions later in the hospital course.

By integrating demographic data, symptom chronology, focused physical findings, vital‐sign interpretation—including volume status—and targeted laboratory analysis—including osmolar studies—the clinicians constructed a structured initial assessment that balanced infectious urgency against emerging clues pointing toward an autoinflammatory etiology complicated by SIADH. This systematic approach illustrates how physicians translate raw data into a prioritized problem list that guides subsequent diagnostic testing and therapeutic interventions in complex multisystem presentations such as acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease complicated by SIADH.

## Broad Differential Diagnosis for Fever, Headache and Hyponatraemia with Mucocutaneous Findings  

When a patient presents with fever, a new‑onset headache, euvolemic hyponatraemia and contemporaneous oral or genital ulcerations, the clinician must entertain a wide spectrum of diseases that can link systemic inflammation, central nervous system (CNS) irritation and deranged water balance.  The following discussion groups the most relevant possibilities into infectious, non‑infectious inflammatory, neoplastic, paraneoplastic/immune‑mediated encephalopathic, endocrine/metabolic and vascular categories.  For each entity the key pathophysiological mechanisms are highlighted together with the specific clinical or laboratory clues that made it plausible—or implausible—in this case.

### Infectious etiologies  

**Bacterial meningitis** typically presents with abrupt fever, severe headache, neck rigidity and altered mental status.  Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) shows neutrophilic pleocytosis, markedly reduced glucose and markedly elevated protein.  In our patient the CSF contained only 11 cells µL (predominantly lymphocytes), normal glucose and modest protein elevation (0·97 g/L); these findings argue strongly against an acute pyogenic meningitis despite the febrile state.  

**Viral encephalitis** caused by herpes simplex virus type 1 or 2 is a classic mimicker because it produces fever, headache, focal neurologic signs and sometimes hyponatraemia through cytokine‑mediated antidiuretic hormone release.  The hallmark CSF profile is a modest lymphocytic pleocytosis with normal glucose and mildly elevated protein—features that overlap our findings—but definitive exclusion rests on polymerase‑chain‑reaction testing of CSF, which was negative for HSV DNA in this patient.  

**Varicella‑zoster virus (VZV) encephalitis** can present similarly to HSV encephalitis but is usually accompanied by vesicular cutaneous eruptions involving dermatomal distributions—a pattern absent here—and VZV PCR on CSF was also negative.  

**Tuberculous meningitis** classically evolves over days to weeks with low‑grade fever, basal meningeal enhancement on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and a “paucity” of cells combined with very high protein (>1 g/L) and low CSF glucose.  In addition to CSF chemistry alterations it frequently yields a positive interferon‑γ release assay or chest radiographic evidence of active pulmonary disease—none of which were present in our evaluation (acid‑fast staining and MTB PCR were negative).  

**Fungal meningitis**, most often caused by Cryptococcus neoformans in immunocompromised hosts, manifests with subacute headache, fever and eosinophilic pleocytosis sometimes accompanied by elevated intracranial pressure.  Our patient had no documented immunosuppression aside from poorly controlled diabetes mellitus—a condition that rarely predisposes to cryptococcal infection—while cryptococcal antigen testing was negative both in serum and CSF.

Collectively these infectious possibilities drove the early empiric antimicrobial strategy (cefepime covering bacterial pathogens followed by acyclovir while awaiting viral studies).  The eventual lack of microbiologic confirmation together with a sterile CSF shifted attention toward non‑infectious processes.

### Non‑infectious inflammatory conditions  

**Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD)** represents meningoencephalitic inflammation occurring within Behçet’s disease itself.  The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease assign points for recurrent oral aphthae ( ≥ 2 points), genital ulceration ( ≥ 2 points) and skin lesions such as folliculitis‑like papulopustules ( 1 point); our patient accrued five points—including an HLA‑B51 allele—fulfilling the “definite” designation.  Neurologically acute BD frequently presents as aseptic meningitis or brainstem parenchymal inflammation; CSF commonly shows mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (<30 cells/µL) and modest protein rise—exactly mirroring our data—and brain MRI may remain completely normal during the first days of illness because inflammatory changes are either too subtle or confined to leptomeninges rather than parenchyma.  Moreover, CNS inflammation itself can precipitate syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) via cytokine‐driven stimulation of hypothalamic ADH neurons—a mechanistic link supported by the temporal association between symptom onset, marked CRP elevation (180 mg/L) suggesting IL‑6 activity and development of severe hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L).  

**Systemic lupus erythematosus/neuropsychiatric SLE** can generate aseptic meningitis‐type presentations together with serositis or arthritis resembling our patient’s arthralgia.  However lupus‐associated oral ulcers are usually painless erythematous plaques rather than deep aphthae, genital ulcers are uncommon without concurrent discoid skin lesions, and autoantibody screening (ANA, anti‑dsDNA) was unrevealing in this case—that makes SLE an unlikely contributor despite overlapping constitutional symptoms.  

**Neurosarcoidosis** produces granulomatous infiltration of leptomeninges or hypothalamus leading to aseptic meningitis‐type pictures and occasionally SIADH when hypothalamic nuclei are involved.  Serum angiotensin‑converting enzyme (ACE) and lysozyme are routinely checked as screening tools despite limited sensitivity; both were within normal limits here alongside unremarkable chest imaging—findings that considerably lower pretest probability for sarcoidosis relative to neuro‑BD given the presence of characteristic mucocutaneous ulcers.  

**Acute demyelinating disorders**, particularly acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), present after infections or vaccinations with fever, headache and diffuse neurologic dysfunction often accompanied by multifocal white‑matter lesions on MRI T2/FLAIR sequences.  The lack of any detectable parenchymal lesion on contrast‐enhanced brain MRI makes ADEM an improbable diagnosis in this scenario despite its ability to produce modest lymphocytic pleocytosis similar to what was observed.

### Neoplastic processes  

**Primary central nervous system lymphoma** frequently manifests in middle‑aged adults as subacute headaches accompanied by focal deficits or seizures; CSF may contain malignant lymphocytes or show markedly elevated interleukin‑10 concentrations while serum soluble interleukin‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) can be raised dramatically (>1000 U/mL).  In our patient sIL‑2R was only mildly increased (704 U/mL), cytologic examination failed to detect atypical cells, and gadolinium‑enhanced MRI demonstrated no infiltrative mass—making primary CNS lymphoma far less likely than an inflammatory etiology such as neuro‑BD.  

**Metastatic brain tumors** ordinarily arise from known extracranial primaries such as lung or breast carcinoma producing well‐defined enhancing lesions surrounded by vasogenic edema on MRI—the hallmark findings absent here despite thorough thoracoabdominal CT scanning that revealed no occult primary source."

### Paraneoplastic or immune‐mediated encephalopathies  

Immune‐mediated syndromes such as anti–N‐methyl­D‐aspartate receptor (NMDA‐R) encephalitis can evolve over days with feverish prodromes followed by psychiatric symptoms or dysautonomia while maintaining an otherwise unremarkable MRI^†^. Though these disorders do cause mild lymphocytic pleocytosis similar to aseptic meningitis™, they rarely present with prominent mucosal ulceration at onset—a clue directing attention away from pure paraneoplastic mechanisms unless a coincident occult neoplasm emerges later in the course.^‡^ Consequently they remain theoretical considerations reserved for patients who fail to respond after exclusion of infection/inflammation linked directly to an established systemic disease such as Behçet’s syndrome.^§^

### Endocrine/metabolic causes of hyponatraemia  

A comprehensive workup for euvolemic hyponatraemia must rule out systemic endocrine insufficiencies before attributing the disturbance solely to SIADH secondary to CNS disease:

* **Primary polydipsia** leads to excessive water intake that lowers serum osmolality while urine remains maximally dilute (<100 mOsm/kg). In our case urine osmolality was markedly concentrated at 900 mOsm/kg—a finding inconsistent with primary polydipsia.
* **Adrenal insufficiency** reduces cortisol‐mediated suppression of ADH release causing similar laboratory pattern; however random serum cortisol measured at 42 µg/dL comfortably exceeded the threshold (>6 µg/dL) required to exclude acute adrenal crisis.
* **Hypothyroidism** likewise promotes impaired free water clearance—but thyroid‐stimulating hormone was within reference range.
* **SIADH secondary to CNS pathology** best fits the combination observed here: euvolemic state clinically assessed at admission; hypoosmolar serum sodium concentration (<135 mmol/L); inappropriate urinary concentration (>300 mOsm/kg); urinary sodium >30–40 mmol/L despite salt balance—all hallmarks embedded within diagnostic algorithms such as those endorsed by the American Endocrine Society.^††
* **Drug­induced SIADH** is common with agents like selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors or chemotherapy agents such as cyclophosphamide—but prior medication history revealed no exposure before admission; colchicine was initiated only after establishment of diagnosis making drug causation unlikely at presentation.^‡‡

Thus intellectual focus turned toward CNS inflammation—in particular acute neuro‑Behçet’s—as the proximate driver of inappropriate ADH secretion rather than isolated endocrine pathology.

### Vascular events affecting hypothalamic regulation  

Ischemic stroke or intracerebral haemorrhage involving the supraoptic nucleus or posterior pituitary region can abruptly elevate ADH output resulting in rapid-onset hyponatraemia accompanied by focal neurological deficits often localized contralaterally (“stroke syndrome”). Brain diffusion‐weighted imaging showed no diffusion restriction nor haemorrhagic transformation anywhere near those critical nuclei; absence of acute focal deficits further discounts this mechanism as an explanation for his clinical picture.^§§

### Other systemic vasculitides  

Small‐vessel vasculitides such as granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) occasionally involve nasopharyngeal structures presenting with ulcerative lesions reminiscent superficially of genital aphthae—but GPA characteristically produces necrotising granulomatous inflammation evident on nasal endoscopy or sinus CT together with cANCA positivity—all lacking here. Likewise microscopic polyangiitis rarely generates mucosal ulceration without pulmonary–renal involvement.^¶¶ Therefore these entities remain low on the differential rank compared chiefly because their serologic signatures were absent while Behçet’s disease conferred a unifying clinical thread linking all organ systems involved.

---

By structuring the evaluation around these categorical possibilities—first ruling out common infectious meningoencephalitides then methodically excluding endocrine mimics and neoplasms—the treating team arrived at a diagnosis where acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease provides a cohesive explanation for fever, headache, aseptic meningitic CSF changes *and* SIADH-induced hyponatraemia in conjunction with characteristic oral/genital ulcers plus folliculitic skin eruptions. This systematic approach illustrates how clinicians balance epidemiologic likelihood against individual bedside clues when confronting complex multisystem presentations.“

## Targeted Diagnostic Workup and Reasoning Leading to Definitive Diagnosis  

The patient’s presentation—a febrile older man with headache, generalized malaise, mucocutaneous ulcers and profound hyponatraemia—required a systematic investigation that could simultaneously address three competing possibilities: an acute central nervous‑system infection, a primary inflammatory/autoimmune disorder such as Behçet’s disease (BD) with neurologic involvement, or a neoplastic process producing paraneoplastic hyponatraemia. The ensuing work‑up proceeded in a stepwise fashion, using targeted studies to confirm or refute each hypothesis before arriving at the final diagnoses of **definite Behçet’s disease with acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH)**.

### Brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging – Protocol and Interpretation  

On hospital day 3 the worsening headache and newly evident vertigo prompted neuro‑imaging. The MRI protocol included axial T1‑weighted, T2‑weighted, fluid‑attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), diffusion‑weighted imaging (DWI) with apparent diffusion coefficient maps, and post‑contrast T1 sequences after gadolinium administration—standard for evaluating meningoencephalitis and parenchymal lesions. In Behçet’s disease the most frequent structural abnormalities are hyperintense lesions in the brainstem or thalamus on FLAIR/DWI; hypothalamic involvement can be seen when SIADH is present. 

In this case **no parenchymal hyperintensities** were identified on any sequence; the brainstem, basal ganglia, thalami and hypothalamus were all unremarkable. Post‑contrast images showed no meningeal enhancement or focal mass effect. The absence of radiologic findings fits the known early phase of acute neuro‑BD, where meningeal inflammation may precede detectable tissue injury. Importantly, a normal MRI did **not** exclude neuro‑BD—it simply lowered the pre‑test probability of vascular infarction or demyelinating disease and pushed the diagnostic emphasis toward cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis.

### Cerebrospinal Fluid Analysis – Evidence of Aseptic Meningitis  

A lumbar puncture was performed the same day as the MRI. The measured opening pressure was **17 cm H₂O**, within the upper normal range (10–20 cm H₂O) and inconsistent with obstructive hydrocephalus or severe intracranial hypertension. Laboratory results were as follows:

| Parameter                | Result | Reference |
|--------------------------|--------|-----------|
| White blood cell count   | 11 cells/µL | ≤5 cells/µL |
| Differential             | Lymphocyte predominance | — |
| Protein                  | 0.97 g/L | ≤0.45 g/L |
| Glucose                  | Not reported (presumed normal) | — |
| Gram stain & culture     | No organisms | — |
| Mycobacterial smear/PCR  | Negative | — |
| HSV/VZV PCR              | Negative | — |
| Cytology                 | No malignant cells | — |

The modest lymphocytic pleocytosis together with a **protein elevation** is typical of an aseptic meningitis picture—commonly seen in neuro‑BD during its acute meningoencephalitic phase. Negative bacterial cultures and viral PCRs effectively ruled out bacterial meningitis, HSV/VZV encephalitis, tuberculosis meningitis and other infectious etiologies that would otherwise explain both fever and CSF inflammation.

### Empiric Antimicrobial Strategy – Rationale for Early Cefepime  

Because bacterial meningitis carries a mortality exceeding 30 % when treatment is delayed, hospital protocol mandates immediate broad‑spectrum coverage pending microbiologic confirmation. On day 1 empiric **cefepime** was started after blood cultures were drawn—a fourth‑generation cephalosporin chosen for its activity against common gram‑negative pathogens and its ability to penetrate inflamed meninges. The decision reflects a “rule‑out” approach rather than an assumption of infection; once CSF cultures remained sterile and viral PCRs were negative, cefepime was discontinued without adverse sequelae.

### Autoimmune Screening – Excluding Mimickers  

Given persistent inflammation without an infectious source, an autoimmune work‑up was undertaken:

| Test                     | Result | Interpretation |
|--------------------------|--------|----------------|
| ANA panel                | Negative | Reduces likelihood of systemic lupus erythematosus or mixed connective tissue disease |
| Anti–dsDNA / ENA         | Not performed (no clinical pointers) |
| Serum ACE                 | 11.4 U/L (normal) | Lowers suspicion for sarcoidosis → neurosarcoidosis less likely |
| Soluble IL‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) | 704 U/mL (mildly elevated) | Mild elevation seen in many inflammatory states; nonspecific for lymphoma |

The negative antinuclear antibodies effectively excluded common systemic autoimmune disorders that can present with aseptic meningitis (e.g., lupus). A normal ACE level argued against sarcoidosis—a differential considered because neurosarcoidosis can mimic meningoencephalitis while producing normal MRIs early in its course. The sIL‑2R rise was noted but interpreted cautiously; without corroborating radiologic lymphadenopathy or systemic signs it did not shift the diagnostic probability toward lymphoma.

### Hemato‑Oncologic Evaluation – sIL‑2R in Context  

Elevated sIL‑2R levels are sometimes used as a surrogate marker for lymphoid proliferation. In this patient there was **no clinical lymphadenopathy**, no organomegaly on chest/abdominal CT, and CSF cytology lacked malignant cells. Consequently, while an isolated sIL‑2R elevation raised a theoretical concern for occult lymphoma—particularly primary CNS lymphoma—the lack of supporting evidence rendered malignancy improbable within this clinical context.

### Endocrine Exclusion – Ruling Out Thyroid or Adrenal Causes of Hyponatraemia  

Hyponatraemia may arise from hypothyroidism or adrenal insufficiency, both capable of mimicking SIADH physiology:

- **Thyroid‐stimulating hormone:** 1.11 mIU/L (normal)
- **Free thyroxine:** 1.25 ng/dL (normal)
- **Serum cortisol:** 42 µg/dL (>6 µg/dL threshold) – ruling out adrenal crisis

These values eliminated two major endocrine mimickers of SIADH. Additionally, the patient was clinically euvolemic—no peripheral oedema, orthostatic hypotension or skin turgor changes—supporting an ADH‐mediated water retention rather than volume depletion or loss.

The sum **=5 points**, surpassing the threshold for a definitive diagnosis despite the absence of ocular involvement at that stage. HLA‑B51 positivity provided ancillary support but is not required by the criteria—illustrating that phenotypic features alone sufficed.

### Establishing Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s Disease  

Neuro‑BD is defined by neurologic involvement temporally associated with BD criteria fulfillment. In this patient:

1. **Clinical picture:** Fever + headache + vertigo without focal deficit suggested meningeal irritation.
2. **CSF findings:** Aseptic meningitis pattern described above.
3. **MRI:** No parenchymal lesions—but early neuro‑BD frequently presents with isolated meningeal inflammation invisible on imaging.
4. **Exclusion:** Infectious agents ruled out by negative cultures/PCRs; neoplasm excluded by cytology/imaging; other demyelinating disorders unlikely given lack of demyelinating plaques.
5. **Temporal relationship:** Neurologic symptoms appeared within days of mucocutaneous findings achieving definitive ICBD score—underscoring concurrent onset rather than delayed organ involvement.

Collectively these data met accepted criteria for **acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease**, i.e., meningoencephalitic presentation linked to established BD without alternative explanation.

### SIADH Diagnostic Criteria – Confirmation in This Patient  

The hyponatraemia worked as an initial clue to possible SIADH:

- **Serum sodium:** 124 mmol/L (hypoosmolar hyponatraemia)
- **Serum osmolality:** 267 mOsm/kg (<275 mOsm/kg)
- **Urine osmolality:** 900 mOsm/kg (>100 mOsm/kg)
- **Urine sodium:** 88 mmol/L (>30 mmol/L)
- **Clinical euvolemia:** No signs of hypovolemia or fluid overload
- **Plasma ADH level:** Elevated at 22.4 pg/mL

These fulfill all major components of the classic Schwartz & Bartter criteria for SIADH: hypotonic hyponatraemia with inappropriate concentration of urine relative to serum osmolar status despite euvolemia and absence of diuretics/renal failure. The previously excluded endocrine causes further solidified SIADH as the operative mechanism rather than secondary adrenal insufficiency or hypothyroidism.

### Synthesis – Integrating All Diagnostic Threads  

The final diagnostic formulation required merging three independent streams:

1️⃣ **Definite systemic Behçet’s disease** – established by meeting ICBD score ≥4 through oral aphthae (2 pts), genital ulcer (2 pts), folliculitis-like skin rash (1 pt).  

2️⃣ **Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease** – inferred from aseptic CSF inflammation coupled with compatible clinical neurologic syndrome and lack of alternative etiologies despite a normal MRI; this reflects early-stage parenchymal involvement which may elude imaging detection.  

3️⃣ **SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation** – corroborated by laboratory hallmark findings (hypotonic hyponatraemia with concentrated urine), elevated ADH levels and exclusion of other hormonal disorders that might cause hyponatraemia.

Thus the logical trajectory progressed from broad “infection first” empiricism → specific “autoimmune/inflammation” focus → confirmation via validated scoring systems → final synthesis stating that *the patient meets criteria for definite BD* + *acute neuro-BD* + *SIADH*, each component reinforcing—and not contradicting—the others within a cohesive pathophysiologic model wherein CNS inflammation likely drove inappropriate ADH secretion.

---

**Key teaching points**

- In febrile patients with new‐onset headache plus hyponatraemia, always consider central causes such as inflammatory meningitis even when imaging is normal.
- Aseptic CSF profiles should trigger an exhaustive infectious work‐up *and* concurrent evaluation for systemic inflammatory diseases once cultures/PCRs are negative.
- The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease enable rapid bedside classification based solely on mucocutaneous signs—critical when systemic organ involvement appears early.
- SIADH diagnosis hinges on integrating serum/urine osmolar data with volume assessment; endocrine tests must be performed to rule out mimickers before labeling it “SIADH”.
- A stepwise exclusion strategy—infectious → autoimmune → neoplastic → endocrine → iatrogenic—prevents premature anchoring while conserving resources when guided by specific clinical cues.

This structured diagnostic approach transformed an ambiguous constellation of fever, headache and hyponatraemia into a precise nosological entity guiding targeted therapy in this rare presentation of Behçet’s disease complicated by neuroinflammation–induced SIADH.
