Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 4: ## Targeted Diagnostic Workup and Reasoning Leading to Definitive Diagnosis  

The patient’s presentation—a febrile older man with headache, generalized malaise, mucocutaneous ulcers and profound hyponatraemia—required a systematic investigation that could simultaneously address three competing possibilities: an acute central nervous‑system infection, a primary inflammatory/autoimmune disorder such as Behçet’s disease (BD) with neurologic involvement, or a neoplastic process producing paraneoplastic hyponatraemia. The ensuing work‑up proceeded in a stepwise fashion, using targeted studies to confirm or refute each hypothesis before arriving at the final diagnoses of **definite Behçet’s disease with acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) complicated by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH)**.

### Brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging – Protocol and Interpretation  

On hospital day 3 the worsening headache and newly evident vertigo prompted neuro‑imaging. The MRI protocol included axial T1‑weighted, T2‑weighted, fluid‑attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), diffusion‑weighted imaging (DWI) with apparent diffusion coefficient maps, and post‑contrast T1 sequences after gadolinium administration—standard for evaluating meningoencephalitis and parenchymal lesions. In Behçet’s disease the most frequent structural abnormalities are hyperintense lesions in the brainstem or thalamus on FLAIR/DWI; hypothalamic involvement can be seen when SIADH is present. 

In this case **no parenchymal hyperintensities** were identified on any sequence; the brainstem, basal ganglia, thalami and hypothalamus were all unremarkable. Post‑contrast images showed no meningeal enhancement or focal mass effect. The absence of radiologic findings fits the known early phase of acute neuro‑BD, where meningeal inflammation may precede detectable tissue injury. Importantly, a normal MRI did **not** exclude neuro‑BD—it simply lowered the pre‑test probability of vascular infarction or demyelinating disease and pushed the diagnostic emphasis toward cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis.

### Cerebrospinal Fluid Analysis – Evidence of Aseptic Meningitis  

A lumbar puncture was performed the same day as the MRI. The measured opening pressure was **17 cm H₂O**, within the upper normal range (10–20 cm H₂O) and inconsistent with obstructive hydrocephalus or severe intracranial hypertension. Laboratory results were as follows:

| Parameter                | Result | Reference |
|--------------------------|--------|-----------|
| White blood cell count   | 11 cells/µL | ≤5 cells/µL |
| Differential             | Lymphocyte predominance | — |
| Protein                  | 0.97 g/L | ≤0.45 g/L |
| Glucose                  | Not reported (presumed normal) | — |
| Gram stain & culture     | No organisms | — |
| Mycobacterial smear/PCR  | Negative | — |
| HSV/VZV PCR              | Negative | — |
| Cytology                 | No malignant cells | — |

The modest lymphocytic pleocytosis together with a **protein elevation** is typical of an aseptic meningitis picture—commonly seen in neuro‑BD during its acute meningoencephalitic phase. Negative bacterial cultures and viral PCRs effectively ruled out bacterial meningitis, HSV/VZV encephalitis, tuberculosis meningitis and other infectious etiologies that would otherwise explain both fever and CSF inflammation.

### Empiric Antimicrobial Strategy – Rationale for Early Cefepime  

Because bacterial meningitis carries a mortality exceeding 30 % when treatment is delayed, hospital protocol mandates immediate broad‑spectrum coverage pending microbiologic confirmation. On day 1 empiric **cefepime** was started after blood cultures were drawn—a fourth‑generation cephalosporin chosen for its activity against common gram‑negative pathogens and its ability to penetrate inflamed meninges. The decision reflects a “rule‑out” approach rather than an assumption of infection; once CSF cultures remained sterile and viral PCRs were negative, cefepime was discontinued without adverse sequelae.

### Autoimmune Screening – Excluding Mimickers  

Given persistent inflammation without an infectious source, an autoimmune work‑up was undertaken:

| Test                     | Result | Interpretation |
|--------------------------|--------|----------------|
| ANA panel                | Negative | Reduces likelihood of systemic lupus erythematosus or mixed connective tissue disease |
| Anti–dsDNA / ENA         | Not performed (no clinical pointers) |
| Serum ACE                 | 11.4 U/L (normal) | Lowers suspicion for sarcoidosis → neurosarcoidosis less likely |
| Soluble IL‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) | 704 U/mL (mildly elevated) | Mild elevation seen in many inflammatory states; nonspecific for lymphoma |

The negative antinuclear antibodies effectively excluded common systemic autoimmune disorders that can present with aseptic meningitis (e.g., lupus). A normal ACE level argued against sarcoidosis—a differential considered because neurosarcoidosis can mimic meningoencephalitis while producing normal MRIs early in its course. The sIL‑2R rise was noted but interpreted cautiously; without corroborating radiologic lymphadenopathy or systemic signs it did not shift the diagnostic probability toward lymphoma.

### Hemato‑Oncologic Evaluation – sIL‑2R in Context  

Elevated sIL‑2R levels are sometimes used as a surrogate marker for lymphoid proliferation. In this patient there was **no clinical lymphadenopathy**, no organomegaly on chest/abdominal CT, and CSF cytology lacked malignant cells. Consequently, while an isolated sIL‑2R elevation raised a theoretical concern for occult lymphoma—particularly primary CNS lymphoma—the lack of supporting evidence rendered malignancy improbable within this clinical context.

### Endocrine Exclusion – Ruling Out Thyroid or Adrenal Causes of Hyponatraemia  

Hyponatraemia may arise from hypothyroidism or adrenal insufficiency, both capable of mimicking SIADH physiology:

- **Thyroid‐stimulating hormone:** 1.11 mIU/L (normal)
- **Free thyroxine:** 1.25 ng/dL (normal)
- **Serum cortisol:** 42 µg/dL (>6 µg/dL threshold) – ruling out adrenal crisis

These values eliminated two major endocrine mimickers of SIADH. Additionally, the patient was clinically euvolemic—no peripheral oedema, orthostatic hypotension or skin turgor changes—supporting an ADH‐mediated water retention rather than volume depletion or loss.

### Application of International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease  

Having excluded infection,
autoimmune disease,
and malignancy,
the focus returned to the mucocutaneous manifestations already evident at presentation:

| Manifestation                | Points per ICBD* |
|------------------------------|-------------------|
| Oral aphthae                 | 2                 |
| Genital ulcer                | 2                 |
| Skin lesions (folliculitis-like rash) | 1           |

\*ICBD = International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease; ≥4 points = definite BD  

The sum **=5 points**, surpassing the threshold for a definitive diagnosis despite the absence of ocular involvement at that stage. HLA‑B51 positivity provided ancillary support but is not required by the criteria—illustrating that phenotypic features alone sufficed.

### Establishing Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s Disease  

Neuro‑BD is defined by neurologic involvement temporally associated with BD criteria fulfillment. In this patient:

1. **Clinical picture:** Fever + headache + vertigo without focal deficit suggested meningeal irritation.
2. **CSF findings:** Aseptic meningitis pattern described above.
3. **MRI:** No parenchymal lesions—but early neuro‑BD frequently presents with isolated meningeal inflammation invisible on imaging.
4. **Exclusion:** Infectious agents ruled out by negative cultures/PCRs; neoplasm excluded by cytology/imaging; other demyelinating disorders unlikely given lack of demyelinating plaques.
5. **Temporal relationship:** Neurologic symptoms appeared within days of mucocutaneous findings achieving definitive ICBD score—underscoring concurrent onset rather than delayed organ involvement.

Collectively these data met accepted criteria for **acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease**, i.e., meningoencephalitic presentation linked to established BD without alternative explanation.

### SIADH Diagnostic Criteria – Confirmation in This Patient  

The hyponatraemia worked as an initial clue to possible SIADH:

- **Serum sodium:** 124 mmol/L (hypoosmolar hyponatraemia)
- **Serum osmolality:** 267 mOsm/kg (<275 mOsm/kg)
- **Urine osmolality:** 900 mOsm/kg (>100 mOsm/kg)
- **Urine sodium:** 88 mmol/L (>30 mmol/L)
- **Clinical euvolemia:** No signs of hypovolemia or fluid overload
- **Plasma ADH level:** Elevated at 22.4 pg/mL

These fulfill all major components of the classic Schwartz & Bartter criteria for SIADH: hypotonic hyponatraemia with inappropriate concentration of urine relative to serum osmolar status despite euvolemia and absence of diuretics/renal failure. The previously excluded endocrine causes further solidified SIADH as the operative mechanism rather than secondary adrenal insufficiency or hypothyroidism.

### Synthesis – Integrating All Diagnostic Threads  

The final diagnostic formulation required merging three independent streams:

1️⃣ **Definite systemic Behçet’s disease** – established by meeting ICBD score ≥4 through oral aphthae (2 pts), genital ulcer (2 pts), folliculitis-like skin rash (1 pt).  

2️⃣ **Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease** – inferred from aseptic CSF inflammation coupled with compatible clinical neurologic syndrome and lack of alternative etiologies despite a normal MRI; this reflects early-stage parenchymal involvement which may elude imaging detection.  

3️⃣ **SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation** – corroborated by laboratory hallmark findings (hypotonic hyponatraemia with concentrated urine), elevated ADH levels and exclusion of other hormonal disorders that might cause hyponatraemia.

Thus the logical trajectory progressed from broad “infection first” empiricism → specific “autoimmune/inflammation” focus → confirmation via validated scoring systems → final synthesis stating that *the patient meets criteria for definite BD* + *acute neuro-BD* + *SIADH*, each component reinforcing—and not contradicting—the others within a cohesive pathophysiologic model wherein CNS inflammation likely drove inappropriate ADH secretion.

---

**Key teaching points**

- In febrile patients with new‐onset headache plus hyponatraemia, always consider central causes such as inflammatory meningitis even when imaging is normal.
- Aseptic CSF profiles should trigger an exhaustive infectious work‐up *and* concurrent evaluation for systemic inflammatory diseases once cultures/PCRs are negative.
- The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease enable rapid bedside classification based solely on mucocutaneous signs—critical when systemic organ involvement appears early.
- SIADH diagnosis hinges on integrating serum/urine osmolar data with volume assessment; endocrine tests must be performed to rule out mimickers before labeling it “SIADH”.
- A stepwise exclusion strategy—infectious → autoimmune → neoplastic → endocrine → iatrogenic—prevents premature anchoring while conserving resources when guided by specific clinical cues.

This structured diagnostic approach transformed an ambiguous constellation of fever, headache and hyponatraemia into a precise nosological entity guiding targeted therapy in this rare presentation of Behçet’s disease complicated by neuroinflammation–induced SIADH.
