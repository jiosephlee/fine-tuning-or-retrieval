Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 2: ## Case Presentation and Initial Assessment  

The patient was an Asian man in his early sixties who arrived at the emergency department after three days of progressive constitutional symptoms. His past medical history was notable only for type 2 diabetes mellitus that had never been treated because he was unaware of his disease and did not follow any dietary or exercise program. Apart from diabetes he had no known chronic illnesses, no relevant family history of autoimmune disease, and no recent travel, animal exposure, or sexual activity that might suggest an infectious trigger. This background set the stage for two competing early hypotheses: an undiagnosed infectious process facilitated by hyperglycaemia versus an intrinsic inflammatory disorder manifesting systemically.

### Symptom timeline  

The illness began with a low‑grade fever (up to 38 °C) accompanied by a throbbing frontal headache, generalized malaise, loss of appetite, persistent hiccups and arthralgia involving both shoulders and ankles. He also reported recent oral aphthous ulcers that had appeared before he sought medical care. Within a day after admission a painful ulcer developed on the scrotum—a finding that would later become pivotal for establishing a diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD). The temporal clustering of fever, headache and hiccups raised concern for meningeal irritation or a meningitis‑like syndrome, while the cutaneous ulcers suggested a mucocutaneous‐driven systemic process.

### Physical examination  

On examination the patient was alert, oriented and communicative but appeared fatigued. His vital signs demonstrated a temperature of 38 °C, heart rate 119 beats/min (regular), blood pressure 135/73 mm Hg, respiratory rate 19 breaths/min and oxygen saturation 96 % on ambient air. The tachycardia was proportional to his fever rather than hypovolemia because bedside assessment showed warm extremities, normal capillary refill and absence of orthostatic changes—all consistent with a euvolemic state. Dermatologic inspection revealed multiple shallow oral ulcers on the buccal mucosa together with several erythematous papules resembling folliculitis distributed over the face and trunk (Figure 1A‑C). No ocular inflammation was detected on bedside slit‑lamp examination. A neurological examination showed the patient to be fully awake with intact cranial nerve function; there was no neck stiffness or focal motor deficit despite his headache and vertigo. Muscle strength testing was normal throughout the upper and lower extremities. The combination of intact higher cortical function with subtle cerebellar‑type vertigo suggested central irritation without frank focal damage at this stage.

### Initial laboratory panel  

Laboratory investigations performed shortly after arrival disclosed several striking abnormalities:

* **Hyponatraemia** – serum sodium 124 mmol/L (reference 135‑145).  
* **Serum osmolality** – low at 267 mOsm/kg (normally 275‑295).  
* **Urine studies** – urine osmolality markedly elevated at 900 mOsm/kg with urinary sodium concentration 88 mmol/L (>20 mmol/L). These findings indicate an inability to excrete free water despite hypotonic serum—a hallmark pattern seen in the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH).  
* **ADH level** – measured ADH was inappropriately high at 22.4 pg/mL in the setting of hypo‑osmolar plasma, corroborating the diagnosis of SIADH.  
* **Inflammatory markers** – C‑reactive protein 180 mg/L (reference <3) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate 70 mm/h (reference <15), both highly elevated and signifying a vigorous systemic inflammatory response that could be infectious or autoinflammatory in nature.  
* **Creatine kinase** – markedly raised at 1 349 U/L together with modest elevations in AST 77 U/L and ALT 64 U/L, indicative either of muscle injury secondary to severe electrolyte disturbance (rhabdomyolysis) or an underlying myositis component driven by inflammation.  
* **Glycaemic parameters** – fasting plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L with HbA1c 8.2 %, confirming poorly controlled diabetes that could predispose both to infection and impaired immune modulation.

To exclude endocrine causes of euvolemic hyponatraemia—hypothyroidism and secondary adrenal insufficiency—thyroid–stimulating hormone (1.11 mIU/L), free T4 (1.25 ng/dL) and random cortisol (42 µg/dL) were measured and found within normal limits. Thus thyroid dysfunction or cortisol deficiency could not account for his low sodium level.

### Interpretation of the electrolyte picture  

Hyponatraemia is classified by tonicity (hypotonic versus isotonic versus hypertonic), volume status (hypovolaemic, euvolaemic or hypervolaemic), and underlying etiologic mechanisms such as renal salt loss versus excess water retention. In this patient:

* **Serum hypo‑osmolarity** confirmed true hypotonic hyponatraemia.
* **Clinical euvolaemia** combined with **high urine osmolality** (> serum) demonstrated that his kidneys were concentrating urine despite systemic hypotonicity—a physiologic paradox best explained by inappropriate secretion or action of antidiuretic hormone.
* **Elevated urinary sodium** ruled out extrarenal sodium loss as a driver.
* Additional exclusion criteria—normal thyroid function tests, normal cortisol levels, absence of pulmonary disease on imaging—favoured a diagnosis of SIADH according to established criteria (Bartter & Schwartz).

The presence of fever, headache and CSF findings compatible later with aseptic meningitis reinforced the concept that central nervous system inflammation was a plausible trigger for ectopic ADH release.

### Formulation of an initial problem list  

At this juncture the clinical team organized their thoughts into several overlapping problem categories:

1️⃣ **Febrile illness with meningeal‑type symptoms** – differential included bacterial meningitis, viral encephalitis/meningoencephalitis, tuberculous meningitis, autoimmune meningitis (including neuro‑Behçet’s disease), drug‑induced fever or non‑infectious inflammatory states such as sarcoidosis.

2️⃣ **Acute euvolemic hyponatraemia** meeting SIADH criteria – potential precipitants encompassed CNS inflammation (meningitis/encephalitis), pulmonary pathology (ruled out radiographically), medications (none administered before admission), adrenal insufficiency or hypothyroidism (excluded biochemically).

3️⃣ **Mucocutaneous lesions suggestive of Behçet’s disease** – oral aphthae plus folliculitis‑like papules pointed toward an autoinflammatory vasculitic process; definitive BD classification awaited confirmation by genital ulcer development and scoring by International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease.

4️⃣ **Marked elevation of CK** – raising concern for rhabdomyolysis possibly secondary to profound hyponatraemia-induced cellular swelling or direct inflammatory myocyte injury.

5️⃣ **Uncontrolled type 2 diabetes mellitus** – serving as a background comorbidity that could mask infection signs or influence immune response.

These problems directed immediate actions: empirical broad‑spectrum antibiotics covering potential bacterial meningitis were initiated while awaiting culture results; lumbar puncture was planned once coagulation status allowed; fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) was instituted promptly to mitigate further dilutional worsening of sodium levels; serial electrolyte checks were scheduled every six hours to avoid overly rapid correction which could precipitate osmotic demyelination syndrome. Simultaneously the team kept Behçet’s disease on their radar because its neurovascular involvement can manifest as meningoencephalitic presentations—and importantly because recognizing it early might influence immunosuppressive therapy decisions later in the hospital course.

By integrating demographic data, symptom chronology, focused physical findings, vital‐sign interpretation—including volume status—and targeted laboratory analysis—including osmolar studies—the clinicians constructed a structured initial assessment that balanced infectious urgency against emerging clues pointing toward an autoinflammatory etiology complicated by SIADH. This systematic approach illustrates how physicians translate raw data into a prioritized problem list that guides subsequent diagnostic testing and therapeutic interventions in complex multisystem presentations such as acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease complicated by SIADH.
