Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 3: ## Broad Differential Diagnosis for Fever, Headache and Hyponatraemia with Mucocutaneous Findings  

When a patient presents with fever, a new‑onset headache, euvolemic hyponatraemia and contemporaneous oral or genital ulcerations, the clinician must entertain a wide spectrum of diseases that can link systemic inflammation, central nervous system (CNS) irritation and deranged water balance.  The following discussion groups the most relevant possibilities into infectious, non‑infectious inflammatory, neoplastic, paraneoplastic/immune‑mediated encephalopathic, endocrine/metabolic and vascular categories.  For each entity the key pathophysiological mechanisms are highlighted together with the specific clinical or laboratory clues that made it plausible—or implausible—in this case.

### Infectious etiologies  

**Bacterial meningitis** typically presents with abrupt fever, severe headache, neck rigidity and altered mental status.  Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) shows neutrophilic pleocytosis, markedly reduced glucose and markedly elevated protein.  In our patient the CSF contained only 11 cells µL (predominantly lymphocytes), normal glucose and modest protein elevation (0·97 g/L); these findings argue strongly against an acute pyogenic meningitis despite the febrile state.  

**Viral encephalitis** caused by herpes simplex virus type 1 or 2 is a classic mimicker because it produces fever, headache, focal neurologic signs and sometimes hyponatraemia through cytokine‑mediated antidiuretic hormone release.  The hallmark CSF profile is a modest lymphocytic pleocytosis with normal glucose and mildly elevated protein—features that overlap our findings—but definitive exclusion rests on polymerase‑chain‑reaction testing of CSF, which was negative for HSV DNA in this patient.  

**Varicella‑zoster virus (VZV) encephalitis** can present similarly to HSV encephalitis but is usually accompanied by vesicular cutaneous eruptions involving dermatomal distributions—a pattern absent here—and VZV PCR on CSF was also negative.  

**Tuberculous meningitis** classically evolves over days to weeks with low‑grade fever, basal meningeal enhancement on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and a “paucity” of cells combined with very high protein (>1 g/L) and low CSF glucose.  In addition to CSF chemistry alterations it frequently yields a positive interferon‑γ release assay or chest radiographic evidence of active pulmonary disease—none of which were present in our evaluation (acid‑fast staining and MTB PCR were negative).  

**Fungal meningitis**, most often caused by Cryptococcus neoformans in immunocompromised hosts, manifests with subacute headache, fever and eosinophilic pleocytosis sometimes accompanied by elevated intracranial pressure.  Our patient had no documented immunosuppression aside from poorly controlled diabetes mellitus—a condition that rarely predisposes to cryptococcal infection—while cryptococcal antigen testing was negative both in serum and CSF.

Collectively these infectious possibilities drove the early empiric antimicrobial strategy (cefepime covering bacterial pathogens followed by acyclovir while awaiting viral studies).  The eventual lack of microbiologic confirmation together with a sterile CSF shifted attention toward non‑infectious processes.

### Non‑infectious inflammatory conditions  

**Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD)** represents meningoencephalitic inflammation occurring within Behçet’s disease itself.  The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease assign points for recurrent oral aphthae ( ≥ 2 points), genital ulceration ( ≥ 2 points) and skin lesions such as folliculitis‑like papulopustules ( 1 point); our patient accrued five points—including an HLA‑B51 allele—fulfilling the “definite” designation.  Neurologically acute BD frequently presents as aseptic meningitis or brainstem parenchymal inflammation; CSF commonly shows mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (<30 cells/µL) and modest protein rise—exactly mirroring our data—and brain MRI may remain completely normal during the first days of illness because inflammatory changes are either too subtle or confined to leptomeninges rather than parenchyma.  Moreover, CNS inflammation itself can precipitate syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) via cytokine‐driven stimulation of hypothalamic ADH neurons—a mechanistic link supported by the temporal association between symptom onset, marked CRP elevation (180 mg/L) suggesting IL‑6 activity and development of severe hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L).  

**Systemic lupus erythematosus/neuropsychiatric SLE** can generate aseptic meningitis‐type presentations together with serositis or arthritis resembling our patient’s arthralgia.  However lupus‐associated oral ulcers are usually painless erythematous plaques rather than deep aphthae, genital ulcers are uncommon without concurrent discoid skin lesions, and autoantibody screening (ANA, anti‑dsDNA) was unrevealing in this case—that makes SLE an unlikely contributor despite overlapping constitutional symptoms.  

**Neurosarcoidosis** produces granulomatous infiltration of leptomeninges or hypothalamus leading to aseptic meningitis‐type pictures and occasionally SIADH when hypothalamic nuclei are involved.  Serum angiotensin‑converting enzyme (ACE) and lysozyme are routinely checked as screening tools despite limited sensitivity; both were within normal limits here alongside unremarkable chest imaging—findings that considerably lower pretest probability for sarcoidosis relative to neuro‑BD given the presence of characteristic mucocutaneous ulcers.  

**Acute demyelinating disorders**, particularly acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), present after infections or vaccinations with fever, headache and diffuse neurologic dysfunction often accompanied by multifocal white‑matter lesions on MRI T2/FLAIR sequences.  The lack of any detectable parenchymal lesion on contrast‐enhanced brain MRI makes ADEM an improbable diagnosis in this scenario despite its ability to produce modest lymphocytic pleocytosis similar to what was observed.

### Neoplastic processes  

**Primary central nervous system lymphoma** frequently manifests in middle‑aged adults as subacute headaches accompanied by focal deficits or seizures; CSF may contain malignant lymphocytes or show markedly elevated interleukin‑10 concentrations while serum soluble interleukin‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) can be raised dramatically (>1000 U/mL).  In our patient sIL‑2R was only mildly increased (704 U/mL), cytologic examination failed to detect atypical cells, and gadolinium‑enhanced MRI demonstrated no infiltrative mass—making primary CNS lymphoma far less likely than an inflammatory etiology such as neuro‑BD.  

**Metastatic brain tumors** ordinarily arise from known extracranial primaries such as lung or breast carcinoma producing well‐defined enhancing lesions surrounded by vasogenic edema on MRI—the hallmark findings absent here despite thorough thoracoabdominal CT scanning that revealed no occult primary source."

### Paraneoplastic or immune‐mediated encephalopathies  

Immune‐mediated syndromes such as anti–N‐methyl­D‐aspartate receptor (NMDA‐R) encephalitis can evolve over days with feverish prodromes followed by psychiatric symptoms or dysautonomia while maintaining an otherwise unremarkable MRI^†^. Though these disorders do cause mild lymphocytic pleocytosis similar to aseptic meningitis™, they rarely present with prominent mucosal ulceration at onset—a clue directing attention away from pure paraneoplastic mechanisms unless a coincident occult neoplasm emerges later in the course.^‡^ Consequently they remain theoretical considerations reserved for patients who fail to respond after exclusion of infection/inflammation linked directly to an established systemic disease such as Behçet’s syndrome.^§^

### Endocrine/metabolic causes of hyponatraemia  

A comprehensive workup for euvolemic hyponatraemia must rule out systemic endocrine insufficiencies before attributing the disturbance solely to SIADH secondary to CNS disease:

* **Primary polydipsia** leads to excessive water intake that lowers serum osmolality while urine remains maximally dilute (<100 mOsm/kg). In our case urine osmolality was markedly concentrated at 900 mOsm/kg—a finding inconsistent with primary polydipsia.
* **Adrenal insufficiency** reduces cortisol‐mediated suppression of ADH release causing similar laboratory pattern; however random serum cortisol measured at 42 µg/dL comfortably exceeded the threshold (>6 µg/dL) required to exclude acute adrenal crisis.
* **Hypothyroidism** likewise promotes impaired free water clearance—but thyroid‐stimulating hormone was within reference range.
* **SIADH secondary to CNS pathology** best fits the combination observed here: euvolemic state clinically assessed at admission; hypoosmolar serum sodium concentration (<135 mmol/L); inappropriate urinary concentration (>300 mOsm/kg); urinary sodium >30–40 mmol/L despite salt balance—all hallmarks embedded within diagnostic algorithms such as those endorsed by the American Endocrine Society.^††
* **Drug­induced SIADH** is common with agents like selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors or chemotherapy agents such as cyclophosphamide—but prior medication history revealed no exposure before admission; colchicine was initiated only after establishment of diagnosis making drug causation unlikely at presentation.^‡‡

Thus intellectual focus turned toward CNS inflammation—in particular acute neuro‑Behçet’s—as the proximate driver of inappropriate ADH secretion rather than isolated endocrine pathology.

### Vascular events affecting hypothalamic regulation  

Ischemic stroke or intracerebral haemorrhage involving the supraoptic nucleus or posterior pituitary region can abruptly elevate ADH output resulting in rapid-onset hyponatraemia accompanied by focal neurological deficits often localized contralaterally (“stroke syndrome”). Brain diffusion‐weighted imaging showed no diffusion restriction nor haemorrhagic transformation anywhere near those critical nuclei; absence of acute focal deficits further discounts this mechanism as an explanation for his clinical picture.^§§

### Other systemic vasculitides  

Small‐vessel vasculitides such as granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) occasionally involve nasopharyngeal structures presenting with ulcerative lesions reminiscent superficially of genital aphthae—but GPA characteristically produces necrotising granulomatous inflammation evident on nasal endoscopy or sinus CT together with cANCA positivity—all lacking here. Likewise microscopic polyangiitis rarely generates mucosal ulceration without pulmonary–renal involvement.^¶¶ Therefore these entities remain low on the differential rank compared chiefly because their serologic signatures were absent while Behçet’s disease conferred a unifying clinical thread linking all organ systems involved.

---

By structuring the evaluation around these categorical possibilities—first ruling out common infectious meningoencephalitides then methodically excluding endocrine mimics and neoplasms—the treating team arrived at a diagnosis where acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease provides a cohesive explanation for fever, headache, aseptic meningitic CSF changes *and* SIADH-induced hyponatraemia in conjunction with characteristic oral/genital ulcers plus folliculitic skin eruptions. This systematic approach illustrates how clinicians balance epidemiologic likelihood against individual bedside clues when confronting complex multisystem presentations.“
