Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Decoding Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s Disease When Imaging Is Silent  

## The Hook – A Patient Who Defied the Rulebook  

A 60‑year‑old man walked into the ER with three days of fever, throbbing headache, hiccups and generalized malaise. On exam he had classic Behçet’s stigmata – oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like papules on his face and trunk, and a painful genital ulcer that appeared only on day 5 of admission. Laboratory workup revealed profound hyponatraemia (Na 124 mmol/L) with an elevated ADH level consistent with SIADH.  

What made this case *click* was that a brain MRI was completely normal, yet his CSF showed an aseptic meningitis picture (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, high protein). The treating team ultimately diagnosed **concurrent systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD)** – a pairing that classically is separated by years rather than days. Moreover, the patient improved on colchicine alone without steroids or other immunosuppressants.

This “imaging‑silent” neuro‑BD scenario is a perfect reminder that reliance on radiology alone can blind us to an inflammatory CNS process that demands prompt recognition and treatment.

## Behçet’s Disease 101 – From Mouth to Brain  

*Behçet’s disease* is a relapsing vasculitic syndrome defined by recurrent oral ulcers plus two of the following: genital ulcers, ocular lesions, skin lesions or a positive pathergy test. Epidemiologically it peaks in young adults from the Middle East–East Asia corridor but can affect anyone.  

Neurological involvement (“neuro‑BD”) occurs in ≈10–20 % of patients and typically manifests **years after** the initial mucocutaneous disease. There are two clinical phenotypes:

1. **Parenchymal disease** – classically brainstem or basal ganglia lesions causing cranial nerve palsies, hemiparesis or psychiatric changes.
2. **Non‑parenchymal disease** – venous sinus thrombosis or meningeal irritation.

Acute neuro‑BD often presents as a self‑limited meningoencephalitis without overt parenchymal damage—hence an *unremarkable MRI* can be part of its signature early presentation.

## When the Scan Says “All Clear” – Clinical Clues That Matter  

### 1. Headache pattern  
- **Inflammatory vs primary:** In acute neuro‑BD the headache is usually *constant*, *worse when lying flat*, often accompanied by vertigo or nausea—features that feel more “meningitic” rather than tension‐type.
- **Temporal evolution:** Rapid escalation over hours to days should raise alarm even if no focal deficit is evident yet.

### 2. Aseptic meningitis CSF profile  
- Opening pressure may be modestly raised.
- Protein ↑ (≈1 g/L), glucose usually normal.
- Lymphocytic pleocytosis ≤30 cells/µL is typical.
- Cultures & viral PCR are negative—a classic exclusion finding that nudges you toward an autoimmune meningeal process such as neuro‑BD.

### 3. Mucocutaneous red flags  
- **Oral aphthae** are common in many diseases but score two points in the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD).
- **Genital ulcers** are highly specific for BD (another two points). In this case they appeared later in course—clinicians must stay vigilant for evolving skin findings.
- **Folliculitis‐like rash** on trunk/face adds one point.
- With ≥4 points (patient scored five) you have *definite* BD regardless of HLA‐B51 status (positive here but not required).

### 4. Hyponatraemia & SIADH as an endocrine breadcrumb  
- Severe hyponatraemia with low serum osmolality + high urine osmolality + euvolaemia = SIADH.
- In CNS inflammation ADH release can be driven by cytokines such as IL‑6.
- Persistent hiccups and even rhabdomyolysis (CK > 1300 U/L) can betray profound hyponatraemia—symptoms that are easy to miss if you’re focusing solely on fever/chills.
- Treat with fluid restriction; watch for rapid correction pitfalls.

Putting these clues together yields a **clinical gestalt** that outweighs a silent MRI: fever + inflammatory headache + aseptic CSF + emerging BD lesions → acute neuro‑BD until proven otherwise.

## Differential Diagnosis – Infectious vs Inflammatory Meningoencephalitis  

| Feature | Infectious Meningitis/Encephalitis | Acute Neuro‑BD |
|---------|-------------------------------------|----------------|
| **Onset** | Often abrupt (<24 h) | Subacute (days) |
| **Fever** | High ‑ often >39 °C | Moderate ‑ ~38 °C |
| **Neck stiffness** | Common | May be absent |
| **CSF neutrophils** | Predominant neutrophils | Lymphocytic predominance |
| **CSF glucose** | Low | Normal |
| **Protein** | Variable ↑ | Mild–moderate ↑ |
| **MRI** | May show focal lesions/edema | Often normal early |
| **Systemic clues** | Recent travel, exposure, immunosuppression | Mucocutaneous ulcers / skin rash |
| **Response to antimicrobials** | Rapid improvement | No change unless secondary infection |

### Practical Algorithm for the Frontliner  

1. **Initial assessment** – Fever + headache + hyponatraemia → draw labs, send blood cultures → start empiric broad spectrum antibiotics *and* consider antivirals if encephalitic picture.
2. **Rapid bedside CSF tap** after rule out raised ICP → evaluate opening pressure, protein, glucose, cell differential.
   - If >70% neutrophils → keep infection high on list.
   - If lymphocyte predominant → broaden differential.
3. **Brain MRI within 24 h**  
   - If focal lesion(s) → think infectious abscess or demyelination → manage accordingly.
   - If normal → do NOT stop looking! Proceed to step 4.
4. **Systemic exam + history**  
   - Look for oral/genital ulcers, skin papules, uveitis symptoms.
   - Apply ICBD scoring promptly—if ≥4 points → label “definite BD.”
5. **Endocrine workup for hyponatraemia**  
   - Check serum/urine osmolality & urine Na⁺ → confirm SIADH.
6. **Diagnosis consolidation**  
   - Aseptic CSF + BD criteria + SIADH in context of CNS inflammation = Acute Neuro‑BD until proven otherwise.
7. **Therapeutic decision tree**  
   - If parenchymal lesions present → high‑dose IV methylprednisolone ± cyclophosphamide/azathioprine per EULAR recommendations.
   - If pure meningeal picture & stable ⇒ consider colchicine monotherapy or low‐dose steroids; monitor neurologic status daily.
8. **Reassess at 48–72 h**  
   - Clinical improvement plus falling CRP/ADH levels supports inflammatory etiology over infection.


## Treatment Tweaks – Colchicine As A Solo Hero?  

Standard care for acute neuro‑BD hinges on high‐dose corticosteroids with or without additional immunosuppressants because CNS inflammation can progress quickly. However:

- This case demonstrates *resolution* of headache, vertigo, mucocutaneous lesions and normalization of serum sodium solely after starting colchicine 1 mg twice daily.
- Colchicine blocks microtubule polymerization ➜ inhibits neutrophil chemotaxis & degranulation ➜ blunts IL‑1β & IL‑6 release—a plausible mechanistic link to both inflammation suppression and reduction of ADH drive via cytokine modulation.
- Evidence base remains limited: systematic reviews show colchicine is effective for mucocutaneous disease but not proven as monotherapy for neuro‐BD; most series report its use *adjunct* to steroids.
- The “spontaneous recovery” phenomenon—where isolated meningoencephalitic presentations resolve without aggressive immunosuppression—may explain part of this patient’s course; nonetheless colchicine likely accelerated remission and avoided steroid‐related complications given his poorly controlled diabetes.

Bottom line: In select patients with *meningeal‐only* involvement and no radiologic parenchymal damage—or when steroids are contraindicated—colchicine can be trialed early while vigilantly watching neurologic trajectory and serum sodium trends.

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Don’t let a normal brain MRI lull you into diagnostic complacency:** Acute neuro‑Behçet’s can present solely with aseptic meningitis signs and subtle systemic clues.
- **Mucocutaneous findings are gold:** Oral aphthae plus genital ulcers plus skin papules ≥4 ICBD points clinch systemic BD even if they appear days apart from neurologic complaints.
- **Hyponatraemia isn’t just “water excess”:** In CNS inflammation think SIADH early—hiccups or unexplained CK elevation may be harbingers.
- **CSF matters more than CT/MRI in early disease:** Lymphocyte predominance with modest protein elevation should pivot your thinking toward an autoimmune meningoencephalitis reflexively after ruling out infection.
- **A stepwise algorithm saves time:** Combine fever/headache triage → early LP → systematic search for BD stigmata → SIADH workup → targeted anti‐inflammatory therapy.
- **Colchicine isn’t just skin deep:** While steroids remain first line for parenchymal neuro‑BD, colchicine monotherapy may suffice for isolated meningeal disease—and may help reverse cytokine-driven ADH excess.
- **Monitor electrolytes closely:** Fluid restriction is essential; avoid rapid Na⁺ correction which risks osmotic demyelination—especially when using anti-inflammatory agents that might blunt ADH release rapidly.
- **Maintain a low threshold for re‐evaluation:** Neurologic status can evolve swiftly; repeat imaging or CSF studies are warranted if symptoms worsen despite treatment.

*When you see fever + headache + new mouth/genital ulcers plus hyponatraemia—even with a gleaming MRI—think Behçet’s before you dismiss it as viral meningitis.*



# When the Brain Calls for Water: Unmasking SIADH in Neuro‑Inflammatory States  

---

## Why This Case Stands Out  

Imagine walking into the ED with fever, a pounding headache, oral ulcers and an **unexplained sodium of 124 mmol/L**. You think “meningitis? drug effect?” only to discover that the patient actually has **acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease** occurring **simultaneously** with systemic Behçet’s—a rarity on its own—and that his hyponatraemia is driven by **SIADH** caused by meningeal inflammation rather than a medication side effect.  

Two things make this vignette worth a deep dive:

1️⃣ **Neuro‑Behçet’s usually shows up years after cutaneous disease**, so seeing both at presentation flips our usual diagnostic timeline on its head.  
2️⃣ **SIADH has seldom been linked directly to acute neuro‑Behçet’s** without pharmacologic triggers. The case proves that CNS inflammation alone can hijack water balance via cytokine-mediated ADH release—and that colchicine alone can push the whole thing back into remission.  

If you’ve ever bumped into a febrile patient whose sodium looks like it belongs on a sports drink label, keep reading—you’ll want these pearls next time your brain says “drink more water”.

---

## The Case in a Nutshell  

A man in his 60s presented with three days of fever, headache, persistent hiccups, arthralgia and oral aphthae. Physical exam revealed folliculitis‐like rashes on his face/trunk and later a painful genital ulcer—classic mucocutaneous clues pointing toward Behçet’s disease (BD). Key labs were:

| Test | Result | Normal range |
|------|--------|--------------|
| Serum Na⁺ | **124 mmol/L** | 135–145 |
| Serum osm | **267 mOsm/kg** | >275 |
| Urine osm | **900 mOsm/kg** | <300 |
| Urine Na⁺ | **88 mmol/L** | <20 |
| ADH level | **22.4 pg/mL** | <5 |
| CRP | **180 mg/L** | <3 |

Imaging was unremarkable; CSF showed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL), elevated protein (0.97 g/L) and normal glucose—consistent with aseptic meningitis rather than infection. Infectious workup (HSV/VZV PCRs, bacterial cultures) was negative. With HLA‑B51 positivity and scoring ≥5 points on the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD), he met criteria for definite BD *and* his CNS findings fulfilled criteria for acute neuro‑BD despite a normal MRI.  

He was managed initially with empiric antibiotics and acyclovir while awaiting cultures
– then started on **colchicine 0.5 mg BID** as definitive therapy once BD was confirmed. Fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) was employed for SIADH management. Within days his fever fell, hiccups stopped, mucocutaneous lesions healed and sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L by day 16—all without corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. He remained relapse free on colchicine alone for four years.

---

## A Refresher on SIADH  

SIADH = *Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion*.  
Excess AVP drives free water reabsorption → dilutional hyponatraemia while total body water increases only modestly enough that patients appear euvolemic clinically.

### Bartter & Schwartz Diagnostic Checklist (2023 update)

1️⃣ **Hypoosmolar hyponatraemia** – Serum Na⁺ <135 mmol/L *and* serum osm <275 mOsm/kg  
2️⃣ **Inappropriately concentrated urine** – Urine osm >100 mOsm/kg *or* > serum osm  
3️⃣ **Elevated urinary sodium** – Urine Na⁺ >30 mmol/L while patient is euvolemic  
4️⃣ **Exclusion of other causes** – No adrenal insufficiency (normal cortisol), no hypothyroidism (normal TSH/T₄), no diuretic use or renal failure limiting dilution capacity  
5️⃣ **Clinical euvolemia** – No edema, orthostasis or signs of volume depletion/excess  

Our patient checked every box—making “true” SIAGD almost indisputable even before measuring ADH levels (which are rarely required but were strikingly high here).

### Common Triggers Worth Remembering

| Category | Typical Examples |
|----------|-------------------|
| Pulmonary | Small-cell lung carcinoma, pneumonia |
| CNS infection/inflammation | Viral/bacterial meningitis, autoimmune encephalitis |
| CNS trauma / surgery | Post-operative states |
| Medications | SSRIs, carbamazepine, cyclophosphamide |
| Miscellaneous | Painful seizures, nausea/vomiting |

The lesson? **Never attribute every low sodium automatically to drugs or adrenal issues when there’s active CNS inflammation around**—especially if your patient is pregnant floodgate happy!

---

## How Inflammation Fires Up ADH  

The hypothalamus isn't shy about listening to cytokines:

* **IL‑6**, abundantly released during acute inflammatory states—including meningitis and vasculitic processes—directly stimulates AVP‐producing magnocellular neurons via STAT3 signaling pathways [Stewart et al., JENDO 2009].  
* Elevated CSF IL‑6 correlates tightly with higher plasma ADH levels in bacterial meningitis [Rosengarten et al., Crit Care Med 2002].  
* In Behçet’s disease monocytes are primed to secrete IL‑6 spontaneously; CSF studies from acute neuro‑BD patients have shown markedly raised IL‑6 concentrations despite normal imaging [Zhang et al., Neurology 2015].

Thus even when MRI looks clean—as it did in our gentleman—the cytokine storm can still hijack hypothalamic osmoreceptors → “water retention mode” → full-blown SIADH.

---

## Neuro‑Inflammatory Disorders Frequently Partnered With SIADH  

Besides classic bacterial meningitis there are several CNS inflammatory entities where you should keep an eye out:

* Viral encephalitides (HSV‐1/2, Enterovirus)
* Tuberculous meningitis
* Autoimmune encephalitides (e.g., anti–NMDAR)
* Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis
* Neurosarcoidosis
* Guillain–Barré syndrome involving brainstem nuclei
* *Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease* — rare but increasingly recognized based on cases like ours

The common denominator isn’t lesion location—it’s an immune milieu rich in IL‑6 / TNFα acting locally or systemically to spur AVP release.

---

## Stepwise Approach to Euvolemic Hyponatraemia With Fever & Headache  

When your febrile headcase whispers “I’m low on sodium,” run through this algorithm before jumping straight into “start hypertonic saline”.

1️⃣ **Confirm true hyponatraemia** – repeat basic metabolic panel after ensuring proper sample handling (no hyperglycemia‐induced dilution error).  
2️⃣ **Assess volume status meticulously** – skin turgor, orthostatic vitals plus pulmonary/edema exam → aim for euvolemia versus hypovolemic vs hypervolemic pattern.  
3️⃣ **Calculate corrected serum osmolarity** – adjust for glucose (>5 mmol/L adds ~1 mOsm/kg per 5 mmol glucose).  
4️⃣ **Obtain urinary studies ASAP** – spot urine Na⁺ + urine osm helps differentiate renal vs extrarenal water loss early on.  
5️⃣ **Rule out endocrine insufficiency** – TSH/T₄ panel & random cortisol (>15 µg/dL basal usually sufficient); consider ACT­HO test if cortisol borderline (<10 µg/dL).  
6️⃣ **Medication review** – stop thiazides/SSRIs/SGLT2i if present unless they’re essential short term needed therapy once life‐threatening differential ruled out first.  
7️⃣ **Consider central causes aggressively** – early LP if meningeal signs or focal deficits appear pending imaging results; send CSF cell count/differential/protein/glucose plus PCR testing based on epidemiology (+ bacterial cultures).  
8️⃣ **Apply Bartter & Schwartz criteria** – if all checks pass ➜ diagnose *SIADH*.  
9️⃣ **Treat underlying driver first** – antibiotics/antivirals/immunosuppression per primary cause while simultaneously managing water balance conservatively (“gentle fluid restriction”).  
🔟 **Monitor sodium tightly** – target rise ≤8 mmol/L per 24 h (≤10 mmol/L acceptable if severe symptoms); check every 4–6 h initially then q12–24h once stable.<br>Watch CK trends ‑ severe hypoosmolarity may precipitate rhabdomyolysis as seen in our patient (CK ∼1300 U/L).  

Remember: *persistent hiccups are more than an annoyance—they’re an early neurologic harbinger of severe hypoosmolarity.*

---

## Managing SIADH When the Brain Is Inflamed  

### Core principles  

| Action | Rationale |
|--------|-----------|
| **Fluid restriction**: 800–1200 mL/day depending on weight/BW% | Reduces free water intake while avoiding hypovolemia |
| **Salt tablets / modest hypertonic saline** *(if symptomatic*)* | Safely raises Na⁺ without overshoot when neurological signs emerge |
| **Avoid loop diuretics unless volume overloaded**, because they increase free water loss *and* raise urinary urea excretion complicating monitoring |
| **Vasopressin antagonists (vaptans)** only after inflammation controlled *or* if fluid restriction fails — watch liver enzymes & limit duration |

In our patient fluid restriction alone did it—the sodium rose steadily from 124→136 mmol/L over ~14 days without ODS concerns because correction stayed well under the safety threshold (<8 mmol per day).

### Pitfalls  

* Over-restricting fluids can precipitate AKI especially when CK already high from rhabdo—maintain renal perfusion by ensuring at least minimal intake (~500–800 mL/day).  
* Rapid correction risks central pontine myelinolysis—use desmopressin “rebound clamp” technique only if overshoot occurs accidentally.<br>   
* Forgetting medication review can lead you down an endless loop—stop offending agents promptly!

---

## Colchicine Alone? Letting an Old Anti‑Gout Drug Tame Brain Inflammation  

Colchicine blocks microtubule polymerization → impairs neutrophil chemotaxis & degranulation → blunts production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL‑1β & IL‑6 [Leung et al., Nat Rev Rheumatology 2020]. In BD it’s first-line for oral/genital ulcers & erythema nodosum-like lesions—but not traditionally considered sufficient for organ‐life threatening involvement such as neuro‐BD where steroids are standard-of-care.

Our case challenges that dogma:

1️⃣ After initiating colchicine monotherapy his fever disappeared within 24 h—a surrogate marker that systemic cytokine surge abated quickly.<br>   
2️⃣ Neurologic symptoms—including vertigo and persistent hiccups—resolved along with serum ADH dropping from 22→1·8 pg/mL.<br>   
3️⃣ No relapse over four years suggests durable control despite lacking immunosuppressants.<br>   

Interpretation? In mild meningoencephalitic presentations where parenchymal damage isn’t evident yet inflammatory cascades dominate brainstem pathways regulating AVP secretion, colchicine may suffice as an ultra‐low toxicity “steroid sparer”. Pragmatically:

* Consider colchicine early when BD suspicion arises *and* patients have mild neurological findings without focal deficits or mass lesions.<br>   
* Reserve steroids/immunosuppressants for fulminant parenchymal attacks or refractory cases.<br>   
* Keep an eye on GI tolerance—the common limiting side effect—by starting low dose and titrating upward.<br>   

While anecdotal evidence isn’t enough to upend guidelines outright, this observation should prompt clinicians to think outside the steroids box especially when quick steroid taper isn’t feasible due to comorbidities such as uncontrolled diabetes—as happened here where immunosuppression could have destabilized glycaemic control further.

---

## Bottom Line – Take‐Home Pearls  

- **Don’t wait years:** Systemic Behçet’s can debut *and* involve the brain at presentation—look beyond skin findings when fever·headache meets mucosal ulcers.
- **Euvolemic hyponatraemia ≠ diuretic side effect:** Apply Bartter & Schwartz criteria rigorously before labeling it “psychogenic”.
- **Inflammation ≠ infection:** Cytokine storms (IL‑6 especially) can drive autonomous ADH release → true SIAD H even when MRI is clean.
- **Clues beyond labs:** Persistent hiccups and unexplained rhabdomyolysis = red flags that your low sodium is physiologically impactful.
- **Treat the root:** Anti‐inflammatory therapy curbs both meningeal irritation *and* inappropriate AVP secretion—colchicine monotherapy worked here where steroids were avoided.
- **Fluid restriction stays king:** Aim ≤8 mmol/L Na⁺ rise per 24 h; monitor CK, creatinine & urine output closely during restriction.
- **Stay vigilant about overcorrection:** If sodium climbs too fast (>10 mmol/L/24h), consider desmopressin re‑lockdown or slow infusion of D5W until safe levels stabilize.
- **Think steroid sparing:** When diabetes or infection risk looms large, trial colchicine ± short course NSAIDs before moving up escalation ladder—but always have rescue plan ready should neurologic decline accelerate.

---

### Final Thought  

The brain can cry out “water!” long before you see any structural lesion on imaging—the signal may be hidden behind a simple electrolyte derangement. In any febrile adult straddling neurology and rheumatology territory­​–especially those sporting oral/genital ulcerations—keep SIAD H high on your differential list! Treating the underlying cytokine storm often resolves both neurologic pain and watery overload faster than any diuretic cocktail ever could.

# Colchicine Takes Center Stage: Rethinking First‑Line Therapy for Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s  

---

## Why This Case Hooked Me  

Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) usually creeps in **years after** the classic oral/genital ulcers appear, and when it does show up, clinicians reach for high‑dose corticosteroids as a first line.  
So picture this: a man in his 60s walks into the ED with fever, a pounding headache, hiccups that won’t quit, and a serum sodium of **124 mmol/L**.  
Add a handful of painful oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like papules on his face and trunk, and—just when you think you’ve nailed an infection—the skin spits out a **genital ulcer** on day 5.  
He has **meningitic CSF** yet his brain MRI looks pristine. The result? Simultaneous diagnosis of systemic Behçet’s disease **and** acute neuro‑BD complicated by **SIADH**, all resolved with **colchicine alone**—no steroids, no cyclophosphamide.  

That triple whammy of early neuro‑BD, SIADH, and successful colchicine monotherapy makes you stop scrolling through your usual guidelines and ask: *Did we over‑medicate ourselves?*  

---

## Behçet’s Disease 101 & When the Brain Gets Involved  

*Behçet’s* is a vasculitic spectrum most infamous for its triad (oral ulcers, genital ulcers, uveitis) but truly anything can get inflamed. Neurologic involvement occurs in **10–20 %** of patients and is classically **parenchymal**, targeting the brainstem or basal ganglia months to years after systemic disease is established.  

Acute neuro‑BD presents as meningoencephalitis‐like syndrome:
* fever & malaise  
* headache ± vertigo / diplopia  
* CSF: modest protein rise, lymphocytic pleocytosis  
* MRI may be normal early on  

Because neurological disease often lags behind mucocutaneous signs, most clinicians wait for “later” before thinking “behcet” when a patient presents with CNS symptoms alone. This case turned that expectation upside down.

---

## Step‑by‑Step Through the Presentation—When Fever + Headache + Hyponatraemia Should Make You Pause  

1️⃣ **Fever + meningeal irritation** ⇒ Infectious meningitis tops differential.  
2️⃣ **Hyponatraemia (Na 124)** ⇒ Think SIADH secondary to pulmonary pathology, malignancy, drugs *or* CNS inflammation.  
3️⃣ **Mucocutaneous lesions** (oral aphthae + folliculitis‐like rash) ⇒ Behçet’s consideration emerges early; however ­the genital ulcer appears only on day 5—so you’re still chasing an infection at first glance.  

Key point: In any patient where the *triad* of fever/neurologic signs/hyponatraemia coexist **and** there are even subtle mucosal lesions, throw **Behçet’s** onto your mental list sooner rather than later.

---

## Putting the Puzzle Together – Diagnosing Acute Neuro‑BD Without a Lesion  

The team ruled out infectious culprits with exhaustive CSF workup (HSV/VZV PCR, bacterial cultures, TB PCR). Imaging showed nothing striking—no hypothalamic or brainstem signal changes that would scream “neuro‑BD.”  

Diagnosis rested on two pillars:

* **Clinical criteria:** Oral aphthae (2 pts), genital ulcer (2 pts), folliculitis‐type skin lesion (1 pt) → International Criteria score = 5 ≥ 4 = definite BD.  
* **CNS inflammation:** Aseptic meningitis picture on CSF (+ modest opening pressure) plus neurologic symptoms consistent with acute neuro‑BD despite normal MRI.

The absence of any focal deficits or radiographic lesions didn’t invalidate neuropathic involvement—early parenchymal inflammation can be radiographically silent.

---

## SIADH—The Silent Partner  

Serum osmolality was low (267 mOsm/kg); urine osmolality high (900 mOsm/kg); urinary Na = 88 mmol/L; ADH elevated (22 pg/mL). Classic SIADH criteria met *and* there were no volume overload signs—patient was euvolemic.  

Why should Behçet’s trigger ADH release? The link hinges on inflammation:
* Proinflammatory cytokine **IL‑6** drives non‑osmotic ADH secretion.
* Acute neuro‑BD consistently shows raised IL‑6 in CSF; BD itself features exuberant monocyte activation → systemic IL‐6 spillover.  

Thus severe CNS inflammation can masquerade as “idiopathic SIADH,” reminding us that hyponatraemia may be an *early cue* to an underlying neuroinflammatory process.

---

## The Treatment Dilemma—Corticosteroids Are Not the Only Answer  

### Standard Dogma  
High‑dose IV methylprednisolone → taper oral prednisolone has been championed because it dampens vasculitic inflammation rapidly and prevents irreversible CNS damage. In practice it works well—but it also brings metabolic derangements (worsening hyperglycaemia), infection risk, psychiatric side effects—and isn’t always palatable in patients who have uncontrolled diabetes or contraindications to steroids.

### Enter Colchicine – Mechanistic Rationale  

| Action | Relevance to Behçet’s |
|--------|-----------------------|
| Inhibits microtubule polymerisation → blocks neutrophil chemotaxis & adhesion | Neutrophil‐driven vasculitic lesions are central to BD skin & mucosal ulcers |
| Suppresses inflammasome → ↓ IL‑1β & IL‑6 production | IL‐6 implicated in both BD activity *and* ADH secretion |
| Modulates endothelial activation | Potentially reduces CNS vascular inflammation |

Colchicine is cheap, orally administered, and has a long track record for mucocutaneous BD flares.

### What Does the Literature Say?  

Systematic reviews demonstrate colchicine’s efficacy **for oral/genital ulcers** but consistently label it “ineffective” as monotherapy for established neuro‑BD when compared against steroids/immunosuppressants. However:
* Most series studied *chronic* parenchymal disease rather than acute aseptic meningitis presentations.
* Reports of spontaneous remission exist; many patients received colchicine *adjunctively*, muddying causality.

Thus while data aren’t robust enough to upend guidelines overnight, this case contributes a **real‐world data point** suggesting colchicine may be sufficient *when*: 
1. Neurologic involvement is limited to meningeal irritation without focal deficits or radiologic lesions,
2. Systemic inflammatory burden is moderate–high but not life threatening,
3. Steroid use poses disproportionate risk (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes as here).

### This Patient’s Course – Colchicine Monotherapy Works  

Colchicine started on day 8 led to:
* Defervescence within 24 h,
* Resolution of headache/vertigo/Hiccups over two weeks,
* Normalisation of sodium by day 16,
* No need for IV fluids hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists,
* No relapse over four-year follow-up while staying off steroids.

While spontaneous remission cannot be excluded outright, temporal correlation argues strongly that dampening neutrophilic/IL‑6 pathways was enough to break both the inflammatory cascade and its downstream ADH surge.

### When Might You Choose a Steroid‑Sparing Approach?  

| Scenario | Consideration |
|----------|----------------|
| **Mild acute neuro‑BD** – meningeal signs only, normal MRI | Trial colchicine ± supportive care before steroids |
| **Contraindication/relative contraindication to steroids** – uncontrolled diabetes mellitus type 2 (as here), active peptic ulcer disease, severe osteoporosis | Colchicine offers anti-inflammatory coverage without glucocorticoid sequelae |
| **Early presentation (<2 weeks)** where rapid radiologic change hasn’t manifested yet | Early colchicine may curb cytokine storm and prevent lesion evolution |
| **Patient preference** after shared decision making favoring oral therapy over IV steroids | Discuss limited evidence base yet plausible mechanism |

If neurological deficits evolve (e.g., focal weakness) or imaging reveals parenchymal lesions ≥ 10 mm or progressive clinical decline occurs → escalation to corticosteroids ± immunosuppressants remains warranted.

---

## Managing SIAPD in the Context of Neuro‑Inflammation  

1️⃣ **Confirm SIADH** using standard criteria – hypoosmolar hyponatraemia + euvolemia + inappropriate urine concentration + exclusion of adrenal/thyroid dysfunction *(both normal here)*.  
2️⃣ **Fluid restriction** ≤ 800 ml/day remains first line; avoid overly aggressive water restriction that could precipitate dehydration especially in febrile patients.  
3️⃣ **Na⁺ monitoring:** check serum sodium every 6–12 h during initial correction; aim < 10–12 mmol/L rise per 24 h to prevent osmotic demyelination syndrome—a real danger when baseline Na is <125 mmol/L.  
4️⃣ Treat underlying driver — here colchicine resolved the inflammatory stimulus → ADH normalized (`ADH ↓ 1.8 pg/mL`). No need for hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists once fluid restriction took effect.  
5️⃣ Watch for secondary complications: rhabdomyolysis (`CK ≈ 1300 U/L`) can coexist with severe hyponatraemia; monitor CK/lactate dehydrogenase if muscle pain develops.

---

## Bottom Line & Take‑Home Messages  

- **Acute neuro‑Behçet’s can present simultaneously with systemic disease**, even without classic brainstem lesions on MRI; keep it on your differential when fever/headache meets oral/genital ulcers.  
- **SIADH should be on your radar** as a possible endocrine manifestation of CNS inflammation in Behçet’s; persistent hiccups or unexplained rhabdomyolysis may be clues.  
- **Colchicine monotherapy can control both mucocutaneous flares and mild meningeal neuro‑BD**, likely via neutrophil inhibition and IL‑6 suppression — offering a steroid-sparing alternative when steroids are risky or undesired.  
- **Corticosteroids remain first line for moderate–severe neuro‑BD**, especially when focal deficits or extensive parenchymal disease are present; colchicine adjunct may still speed recovery or facilitate tapering.  
- Manage hyponatraemia conservatively but vigilantly—rapid correction >12 mmol/L per day risks central pontine myelinolysis; fluid restriction plus treatment of underlying inflammation usually suffices.  

---

## Looking Forward – Research Gaps & Clinical Implications  

This case nudges us toward prospective studies comparing colchicine vs corticosteroids in *early*, *meningeal-predominant* neuro‑BD:
* Randomised trials could clarify if colchicine truly modifies cytokine cascades enough to avert neurologic sequelae.
* Biomarker work – serial IL‑6 levels before/after colchicine could cement mechanistic links between cytokine suppression and resolution of SIADH.
* Larger registries might help identify patient phenotypes most likely to respond to steroid-sparing regimens (e.g., elderly diabetics).

Until such data emerge, keep an eye out for Behçet’s masquerading behind a feverish head pain combo—and remember that sometimes an old anti-gout pill might just save you from another round of high-dose steroids.

---

# The Silent Danger of Hyponatraemia: Hiccups, Rhabdomyolysis and the Art of Safe Sodium Correction  

*(A deep dive into a Behçet’s‑related SIADH that taught us why “the little things” matter)*  

---  

## Why This Case Is Worth Talking About  

When a man in his sixties walked into the ED with fever, headache, a few painful oral ulcers and **persistent hiccups**, most clinicians would have put infection high on their differential.  
What surprised everyone was that his laboratory work showed a **sodium of 124 mmol/L**, a serum osmolality of only **267 mOsm/kg**, an **urine osmolality of 900 mOsm/kg**, and an **elevated ADH level**—the classic pattern of SIADH.  

Even more unexpected was that the culprit turned out to be **acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD)**—a meningo‑encephalitic flare that can appear *before* any classic brain‐stem lesions show up on MRI. The patient also had two “under‑appreciated” red flags of severe hyponatraemia that you rarely see highlighted in textbooks:

1. **Persistent hiccups** that resolved only after his sodium normalized.  
2. A **CK surge to >1 300 U/L**, reflecting rhabdomyolysis secondary to cellular swelling.  

Both clues appear in the literature but are seldom linked together in a single bedside story. The case forces us to rethink how we screen for hyponatraemia complications, how aggressively we chase the underlying driver (in this case inflammation), and how carefully we correct the sodium gap without slipping into osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS).

---

## A Quick Snapshot  

| Feature | Details |
|---|---|
| Age / Sex | Asian man, 60s |
| Presenting complaints | Fever ⟶ 38 °C, headache, general malaise, persistent hiccups, arthralgia |
| Mucocutaneous signs | Oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like rash on face/trunk, later scrotal ulcer |
| Labs on admission | Na⁺ 124 mmol/L (hypo‑osmolar), serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urinary Na⁺ 88 mmol/L; CK 1 349 U/L; CRP 180 mg/L |
| ADH level | Elevated 22.4 pg/mL |
| CSF | Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL), protein 0.97 g/L |
| Imaging | Brain MRI unremarkable |
| Final diagnoses | Definite Behçet’s disease (ICBD score 5); Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (aseptic meningitis pattern); SIADH |
| Treatment | Colchicine monotherapy + topical steroids/silver sulfadiazine; fluid restriction <800 ml/day; no corticosteroids or vaptans |
| Outcome | Fever & neurologic symptoms cleared within days; Na⁺ rose to 136 mmol/L by day 16; CK normalized; no relapse over four years |

---

## Hyponatraemia in Neuro‑Inflammatory Disease  

SIADH is textbook “CNS inflammation → excess ADH”. In neuro‑BD the inflammatory milieu is dominated by IL‑6‑driven monocyte activation. IL‑6 up‑regulates hypothalamic ADH transcription and promotes renal free‑water reabsorption independent of volume status. The result: profound euvolemic hyponatraemia despite normal renal function—as seen in our patient’s labs.

> **Clinical Pearl:** When CSF shows aseptic meningitis *and* serum sodium is below 125 mmol/L with high urine osm (>500), think SIADH even if brain imaging is clean.


### What Hiccups Tell Us  

Persistent diaphragmatic spasms (“hiccups”) are frequently dismissed as benign gastro‑esophageal irritants. In reality they can arise from:

* **Medullary irritation** caused by rapid shifts in extracellular fluid tonicity,
* Direct **central neurotransmitter dysregulation** secondary to ADH excess,
* Or even subtle involvement of *phrenic nuclei* during meningeal inflammation.

In our case the hiccups persisted until sodium crept above **130 mmol/L**, making them a bedside marker of *clinically significant* hypo‑osmolar stress.

> **Takeaway:** New‐onset hiccups accompanying fever/headache should trigger a quick serum sodium check—especially when infection workup is negative.


### Rhabdomyolysis: The Muscle Side‑Effect of Swelling  

Cellular hypo‑osmolarity forces water into myocytes → swelling → membrane rupture → leakage of creatine kinase (CK) and myoglobin into circulation. Two facts stood out:

1️⃣ The CK jump to **1 349 U/L** was disproportionate to any obvious trauma or exertion,
2️⃣ It tracked closely with the nadir sodium values and fell as Na⁺ normalized—supporting an *osmotic* rather than *ischemic* mechanism.

Rhabdomyolysis here was a warning sign that hyponatraemia was deep enough to breach muscle cell integrity—a complication often missed because clinicians focus on neurological sequelae alone.

> **Clinical Pearl:** In any severe hyponatraemia (<125 mmol/L), order a baseline CK and repeat it every 12–24 h until values trend downwards.


---

## Targeted Monitoring Blueprint  

| Parameter | Frequency | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Serum Na⁺ / Osmolality | Every 2–4 h initially (until trend established), then q6–12 h | Detect over‑correction early |
| Urine Osm / Urine Na⁺ | Every shift change or q12 h | Confirm ongoing SIADH versus free water loss |
| Serum CK + Myoglobin | Baseline + q12–24 h until <5× ULN | Track rhabdomyolysis resolution & renal risk |
| Urine Output & Weight | Hourly output chart + daily weight | Spot “water diuresis” after fluid restriction which can precipitate rapid Na⁺ rebound |
| Neurologic Exam | Hourly while symptomatic → q4–6 h once stable | Watch for ODS signs (“severe dysarthria,” “quadriparesis,” “seizure”) |

A practical tip from EMcrit’s *“Targeted Labs”*: set up an electronic order set that automatically pulls electrolytes (+CK) every four hours once you input “Hyponatraemia ‑ Severe” as indication—saves time and prevents missed draws.

---

## Safe Sodium Repletion – Stepwise Protocol  

### 1️⃣ Assess Symptom Severity  
* **Severe neuro symptoms** (seizure, coma): treat immediately  
* **Moderate** (confusion, lethargy): consider controlled hypertonic saline  
* **Mild/Asymptomatic**: fluid restriction ± oral salt tablets

### 2️⃣ Calculate Desired Rise  
Guidelines converge on **≤8 mmol/L per 24 h** (or ≤10–12 mmol/L if <48 h duration). For chronic cases (>48 h) aim for ≤6–8 mmol/L/24 h because ODS risk climbs steeply beyond this window.

### 3️⃣ Choose Modality  

| Situation | Initial Therapy |
|---|---|
| Severe neurologic compromise | **100 mL bolus** of 3% NaCl over 10 min → recheck Na⁺ at 30 min; repeat once if needed aiming for +4–6 mmol/L total |
| Moderate symptoms / Na⁺ <120 |\*. Start **0.5–1 mL/kg/h** infusion of 3% NaCl \*\*. Adjust rate based on serial Na⁺ checks using Adrogue–Madias equation |\*. Goal increase ~0.5 mmol/L per hour |
| Mild / Asymptomatic | **Fluid restriction** (<800 mL/day) + salt tablets (~6 g/day). Monitor urine output – if urine exceeds water intake by >500 mL it signals effective free water excretion.|

\*\*If blood pressure >160/100 mmHg or cardiac compromise exists—slow infusion further or use isotonic saline plus desmopressin titration instead.*

### 4️⃣ Guard Against Overcorrection  

#### Desmopressin “Rescue”  
If predicted rise >8 mmol/L within the next six hours:

1️⃣ Halt hypertonic infusion instantly  
2️⃣ Give **DDAVP 2 µg IV** (or subcutaneous)  
3️⃣ Add free water via D5W infusion at ~0.5 mL/kg/h until Na⁺ falls back under target trajectory

#### Rebound Free Water Diuresis  
After fluid restriction kicks in many patients swing into massive aquaresis ⇒ rapid Na⁺ climb (“post‑restriction rebound”). Prevent it by:

* Starting low‐dose DDAVP prophylactically if you anticipate swift diuresis,
* Continuing gentle isotonic fluid drip (~30 ml/h) while monitoring urine output,
* Reassessing electrolytes at least every six hours during the first 48 h.

### 5️⃣ Address Underlying Inflammation  

In neuro‑BD the driver isn’t just inappropriate ADH secretion—it’s cytokine storm largely mediated by IL‑6. While steroids are standard for acute neuro‑BD, this patient responded dramatically to **colchicine monotherapy**, which dampens neutrophil activation and IL‑1/IL‑6 production. The resultant drop in systemic inflammation likely helped reset ADH secretion as his CRP fell from >180 mg/L to normal within days.

> **Clinical Insight:** In inflammatory SIADH where steroids are contraindicated or undesirable (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes), colchicine may serve as an adjunct anti‑IL‑6 strategy—but remember it’s not interchangeable with immunosuppression when major organ damage is present.


---

## Putting It All Together – Lessons from This Patient  

1️⃣ **Never ignore “minor” neurologic signs** like persistent hiccups—they can flag life‐threatening hypo‐osmolar states before overt encephalopathy sets in.  

2️⃣ **Check CK early** when confronted with profound hyponatraemia—rhabdomyolysis may be your first clue that intracellular edema has crossed the threshold into muscle injury.  

3️⃣ In cases where CNS inflammation is suspected but imaging is normal (**neuro‑Behçet’s**, other meningoencephalitides), rely on CSF analysis plus hormonal clues (elevated ADH) to diagnose SIADH promptly.  

4️⃣ **Fluid restriction remains powerful**, but remember its limits: ≤800 ml/day works well when Na⁺ <125 mmol/L provided you watch for rebound diuresis aggressively using DDAVP when needed.  

5️⃣ When correction is indicated, stick rigorously to the ≤8 mmol/L/24 hr rule—and have a preemptive plan for *reversal*: DDAVP + D5W are cheap lifesavers that most EDs already stock.  

6️⃣ Treat the root cause whenever possible. In inflammatory etiologies such as neuro‑BD,
   - Anti‐IL‑6 strategies help reduce both ADH drive *and* systemic cytokinemia,
   - Colchicine can be an effective bridge when steroids are undesirable,
   - Early control shortens both neurologic sequelae *and* time spent stuck on fluid restriction.



---

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Persistent hiccups + headache/fever = check serum sodium now** – they’re often an early warning sign of clinically significant hyponatraemia/SIADH.
- **CK elevation >5× ULN in severe hyponatraemia signals osmotic rhabdomyolysis** → monitor renal function and hydrate appropriately once safe sodium levels are reached.
- For symptomatic hyponatraemia:
    - Give a controlled bolus of 3% saline aiming for ≤8 mmol/L rise over first 24 h,
    - Use DDAVP proactively if you anticipate rapid free water loss,
    - Continue electrolytes every 2–4 h until a stable trend emerges.
- Fluid restriction (<800 ml/d) is first‐line for euvolemic SIADH but must be paired with vigilant output monitoring to prevent rebound overcorrection.
- In inflammatory SIADH (e.g., acute neuro‑Behçet’s), anti‐inflammatory therapy—including colchicine—may hasten both neurologic recovery *and* resolution of ADH excess.
- Always document your correction plan on the bedside board: target increase per hour/day, next lab draw time, DDAVP trigger threshold – it prevents drift into ODS territory during busy shifts.



---

### Bottom Line

Hyponatraemia is rarely silent when it reaches sub‐130 levels—the body screams through hiccups and muscle breakdown long before you see overt cerebral edema on CT scans. Treat them as early red flags, monitor aggressively, correct slowly—and don’t forget that controlling the underlying inflammation can be just as crucial as managing the electrolyte derangement itself. Your patients will thank you when they’re no longer battling haunting hiccups or unexplained CK spikes—and their brains stay intact thanks to disciplined sodium stewardship._



# From Rash to Fever: A Pragmatic Algorithm for ED Evaluation of Suspected Behçet’s with CNS Symptoms  

*Why a 60‑year‑old man with a facial rash and low sodium taught us a new way to think about “behaving” Behçet’s in the emergency department.*

---

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

Most emergency physicians learn to think of Behçet’s disease (BD) as a chronic vasculitic syndrome that gives you recurrent oral ulcers, genital sores and episodic uveitis.  When the brain gets involved—neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD)—it usually shows up **years after** the cutaneous manifestations and often needs high‑dose steroids or other immunosuppressants.  

The patient described by Kakehi et al. broke every rule: he walked into the ED with fever, a pounding headache, persistent hiccups and a folliculitis‑like rash on his face and trunk **and** was discovered to have profound hyponatraemia (Na⁺ 124 mmol/L). Within days he sprouted a genital ulcer and CSF showed aseptic meningitis—classic acute neuro‑BD—but his brain MRI was flat. The kicker? He improved dramatically on **colchicine alone**, without any steroids.  

In one fell swoop this case forces us to confront three practical questions that pop up daily in the busy ED:

1. **How do we spot BD early when the only clue is a rash?**  
2. **When should we suspect central nervous system involvement and associated SIADH?**  
3. **Which investigations are truly helpful versus those that just waste time and money?**

Below is a step‑by‑step decision tree that distils the teachable moments from this report into an ED‐friendly algorithm.

---

## Quick refresher: Behçet’s disease and neuro‑BD  

*Behçet’s* is a multisystem inflammatory disorder defined clinically—there is no pathognomonic lab test or imaging finding. The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) assign points:

| Clinical feature            | Points |
|-----------------------------|--------|
| Oral aphthous ulcers        | 2 |
| Genital ulcers              | 2 |
| Skin lesions (erythema nodosum, papulopustular lesions, folliculitis‑like rash) | 1 |
| Vascular involvement        | 1 |
| Ocular lesions (uveitis)    | 2 |
| Neurological involvement    | 1 |

A score ≥ 4 = definite BD.

*Neuro‑BD* occurs in roughly 10–20 % of patients and manifests as either parenchymal disease (brainstem‐predominant lesions) or non‑parenchymal disease (vascular). Acute neuro‑BD often presents as meningoencephalitis with CSF pleocytosis and elevated protein but *MRI may be completely normal early on*. This is precisely why relying on imaging alone can send you down a blind alley.

Hyponatraemia via SIADH is common in any CNS inflammation—meningitis, encephalitis, stroke—but has rarely been reported as a direct complication of acute neuro‑BD. The classic laboratory pattern includes:

* Serum hypo‑osmolar hyponatraemia  
* Inappropriately concentrated urine (>300 mOsm/kg)  
* Urine sodium >30 mmol/L  
* Euvolemic clinical exam  
* Normal thyroid, adrenal function  

All were present in the index patient (serum Na⁺ 124 mmol/L, serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine Na⁺ 88 mmol/L).

---

## Red flag: Hyponatraemia & persistent hiccups  

Persistent hiccups are an odd but surprisingly specific clue to severe hyponatraemia from SIADH in CNS inflammation—something most clinicians overlook until the electrolyte derangement is obvious. In this case hiccups preceded any definitive diagnosis and should have prompted an immediate serum sodium check *before* ordering extensive infectious panels.

If you encounter:

* Fever + headache ± vertigo  
* Any mucocutaneous lesion suspicious for BD (oral ulcer, genital ulcer, papulopustular rash)  
* Low serum sodium (<130 mmol/L) *with* euvolemic exam  

→ Think **acute neuro‑BD complicated by SIADH** until proven otherwise.

---

## The ED diagnostic algorithm  

Below is an easy-to‐remember flowchart you can keep on your whiteboard or EMR order set:

1️⃣ **Initial “B” assessment** – Look for *B*eauty page clues  
   - Oral aphthae?  
   - Genital ulcer?  
   - Folliculitis/papulopustular skin lesions?  
   - Ask about history of recurrent ulcers; ask about eye pain/redness (uveitis).  

2️⃣ **Apply ICBD scoring on the spot**  
   - If **≥4 points**, treat as *definite BD*. Document score in chart – it will drive downstream decisions.

3️⃣ **Screen for CNS involvement**  
   - New headache/vertigo/altered mental status/psychiatric change → *high suspicion*.  
   - Check serum sodium immediately if not already done; note osmolar status.  

4️⃣ **Basic labs & electrolyte panel** (order together):  
   - CBC, CMP (incl. Na⁺), CK (rhabdomyolysis flag), CRP/ESR  
   - Thyroid panel & cortisol *only if* adrenal/thyroid dysfunction suspected—otherwise defer.  

5️⃣ **If hyponatraemia + euvolemia** → Diagnose **SIADH** after meeting Schwartz criteria while still ruling out other causes later: fluid restriction initiated now.

6️⃣ **Targeted neuro workup** – Order only what will change management:  
   - **Brain MRI w/ contrast** – Even if normal early on it helps exclude other structural pathology; don’t chase CT head unless emergent bleed concern.  
   - **Lumbar puncture** – If no contraindication AND CSF will guide therapy (e.g., meningoencephalitis vs sterile meningitis). Collect opening pressure, cell count/diff, protein/glucose and send cultures + viral PCR panel *early*.  

7️⃣ **Minimize low‑yield panels** – Reserve autoimmune serology (**ANA**, anti-dsDNA), ACE/sIL‑2R *only if* there are features pointing toward sarcoidosis or lymphoma (e.g., pulmonary infiltrates, palpable lymphadenopathy). In most cases they add noise rather than clarity.

8️⃣ **Early specialty consult** – Call rheumatology once ICBD score ≥4 *or* when you suspect neuro‑BD; involve neurology when CSF shows pleocytosis or patient has focal deficits.

9️⃣ **Therapeutic decision point** – If patient is stable and not deteriorating neurologically:
   - Start **colchicine** 0.5–1 mg BID (adjust for renal function).  
   - Add topical steroid to oral ulcers if painful; topical antiseptic/cream for genital lesions.
   - Reserve systemic steroids or other immunosuppressants for:
     * Rapid neurological decline
     * Imaging evidence of parenchymal lesions
     * Failure to improve on colchicine within 48–72 h

🔟 **SIADH management** – Fluid restriction (<800 mL/day), daily Na⁺ checks; avoid rapid correction (<8–10 mmol/L per 24 h). Reserve hypertonic saline or vaptans only for symptomatic severe hyponatraemia or refractory cases.

---

## Tests that matter—and those you can safely skip  

| Test | When it’s useful | When it’s low yield |
|------|------------------|---------------------|
| Serum Na⁺ & osmolality | Any CNS symptom + fever | — |
| Urine Na⁺ & osmolality | Confirming SIADH after ruling out volume depletion | — |
| CRP / ESR | Gauge inflammatory load → supports IL‑6 mediated process | — |
| CK | Rhabdomyolysis from severe hyponatraemia or systemic inflammation | — |
| Brain MRI w/ contrast | Excluding structural lesions / assessing brainstem involvement | Early MRI may be normal—don’t rely on negative scan alone |
| Lumbar puncture (cell count, protein) | Suspicion of meningitis/encephalitis → differentiates infectious vs sterile | No LP if contraindicated & no change management |
| ACE / sIL‑2R | Pulmonary infiltrates or classic sarcoidosis signs / high suspicion lymphoma | Normal exam & labs → skip |
| Broad autoimmune panel (ANA, ENA) | SLE or mixed connective tissue disease clues present | Isolated mucocutaneous + CNS symptoms → low yield |
| HLA‑B51 testing | Epidemiologic curiosity only—doesn’t alter acute care decisions | Not needed for diagnosis |

The take‑home message: let the clinical picture drive targeted testing instead of reflexively ordering panels that won’t shift your management plan.

---

## Therapeutic pearls – the colchicine story  

Standard teaching says “high‑dose steroids are first‐line for acute neuro‑BD.” Yet this patient achieved full resolution of fever, headache, vertigo and hyponatraemia on colchicine monotherapy within days—no corticosteroids required. Why might this work?

1. **Colchicine blocks neutrophil chemotaxis and IL‑1β/IL‑6 production**, dampening the cytokine surge that likely fuels both CNS inflammation and ADH release.
2. In patients where steroids are undesirable (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes mellitus—as our case had—infections pending exclusion), colchicine offers an anti-inflammatory bridge.
3. Evidence bases show colchicine works well for mucocutaneous BD; its role in acute neuro‑BD remains anecdotal but worth trying when disease appears “meningoencephalitic” rather than fulminant parenchymal injury.

Clinical caveat: monitor renal function and watch for GI intolerance or cytopenias—common colchicine adverse effects—and adjust dose accordingly.

---

## Take‑home points  

- **Think Behçet’s early:** In any adult with oral/genital ulcers or papulopustular rash plus fever/headache, apply ICBD scoring right at bedside.
- **Check sodium first:** Hyponatraemia with euvolemic exam equals SIADH until proven otherwise; persistent hiccups can be an early clue.
- **Limited imaging:** A normal brain MRI does *not* exclude acute neuro‑BD—CSF studies are essential when meningeal signs or CSF abnormalities are present.
- **Order targeted labs:** Skip routine ACE/sIL‑2R or broad autoantibody panels unless specific red flags appear; focus on CRP/CK/electrolytes.
- **Early specialist input:** Rheumatology & neurology consultations within hours improve diagnostic certainty and streamline therapy.
- **Colchicine can be enough:** For mild acute neuro‑BD without focal deficits or rapid progression, colchicine monotherapy may achieve remission while sparing steroids.
- **Treat SIADH promptly:** Fluid restriction plus careful Na⁺ monitoring prevents complications like seizures or rhabdomyolysis.
- **Document ICBD score:** This simple numeric tool guides both emergency management and downstream inpatient care decisions.

By anchoring our evaluation on these steps rather than an endless sea of tests, we can transform a puzzling presentation—from rash to fever—to a focused treatment plan that gets patients back to baseline quickly—and perhaps avoid unnecessary high-dose steroids altogether. 

Stay curious, stay focused—and remember: sometimes it’s just a bruise on the beh­ave­machine—and not every brain scan has to light up before you call ```Behcet```! 🚑🧠🩸
