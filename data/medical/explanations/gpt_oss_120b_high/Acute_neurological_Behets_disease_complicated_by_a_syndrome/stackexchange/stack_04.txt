Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Differential Diagnosis] A 62‑year‑old man presents with fever, worsening headache over three days, hyponatraemia due to SIADH and cerebrospinal fluid showing mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (~11 cells/µL) and elevated protein (~0.97 g/L). List the primary differential diagnoses that should be considered initially, grouping them into infectious, inflammatory/autoimmune, neoplastic and drug‑related categories.

**Acute fever + headache + SIADH + mildly inflammatory CSF**  
*Key bedside data*: 62 y/o man, 3 days of fever‑headache, serum Na⁺ 124 mmol/L with classic laboratory criteria for SIADH, CSF opening pressure 17 cm H₂O, protein ≈ 0·97 g/L, lymphocytes ≈ 11 µL⁽↑⁾; normal brain MRI; no obvious infectious focus on imaging or physical exam.

The first‐line differential must encompass all entities that can produce **(i)** a sub‑acute meningo‑encephalitic picture with a modest lymphocytic pleocytosis and protein rise and **(ii)** an associated ADH surge causing euvolemic hyponatraemia.  In the reported case the team pursued this broad list—initially treating empiric bacterial meningitis and HSV encephalitis before these possibilities were excluded by culture/PCR negativity—and later ruled out sarcoid‐type inflammation and lymphoma on the basis of normal imaging and negative cytology/specific labs.

Below is a concise “initial” differential grouped by pathophysiologic category:

---

### 1️⃣ Infectious etiologies  

| Disorder | Why it belongs in the early list | Typical clues that helped exclude it in this case |
|----------|----------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| **HSV‑1 / HSV‑2 meningoencephalitis** | Most common viral cause of fever–headache with lymphocyte‑predominant CSF; can trigger ADH release via cytokines | Negative HSV PCR on CSF → discontinued acyclovir |
| **Varicella‑zoster virus (VZV)** | Older adults frequently develop VZV meningoradiculopathy with modest pleocytosis | VZV PCR/antigen negative |
| **Enteroviruses / other non‑HSV viruses** (e.g., coxsackie, echovirus) | Typical aseptic meningitis picture; usually self‑limited | Viral panel negative; clinical course prolonged (>5 d) despite supportive care |
| **Listeria monocytogenes** | In patients >50 y/o can present with a *lymphocyte*‐rich CSF early on | No Gram stain organisms; cultures sterile; lack of typical exposure |
| **Tuberculous meningitis** | Subacute onset, high protein (>1 g/L), low glucose possible; often produces SIADH via hypothalamic involvement | Acid‐fast stain & MTB PCR negative; sIL‑2R only mildly ↑ (non‑specific) |
| **Cryptococcus neoformans** | HIV‑negative cryptococcal meningitis may show mild lymphocytes and elevated protein | Cryptococcal antigen negative |
| **Neurosyphilis** | Can give a chronic aseptic picture with modest pleocytosis | RPR/VDRL negative in serum & CSF |
| **Other zoonoses** (e.g., Lyme disease) | Possible when tick exposure present → CSF lymphocytes ↑ | No exposure history |

> *Teaching point*: In any febrile adult with new headache the first priority is to rule out treatable infections—bacterial (*including Listeria*), viral (*HSV/VZV*), mycobacterial (*TB*) and fungal—because delays increase morbidity/mortality.^1^

---

### 2️⃣ Inflammatory / Autoimmune disorders  

| Disorder | Rationale for inclusion | Features that eventually directed away from it |
|----------|------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| **Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease** | Meningoencephalitic presentation without early parenchymal MRI lesions is classic for “acute” neuro‑BD; cytokine surge (IL‑6) can precipitate SIADH ‑ exactly what occurred in the reported case once mucocutaneous ulcers appeared.^2^ | Initially absent genital ulcers → low suspicion until day 5 when scrotal ulcer emerged; diagnosis then confirmed by International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease |
| **Neurosarcoidosis** | Granulomatous CNS inflammation may mimic aseptic meningitis and cause hypothalamic involvement → SIADH | Normal serum ACE/lysozyme + lack of systemic sarcoid findings → deemed unlikely |
| **Systemic lupus erythematosus / Neuro‑SLE** | SLE can produce meningeal irritation (“lupus meningitis”) plus cytokine‐driven ADH release | ANA panel negative; no other SLE manifestations |
| **Primary CNS vasculitis** | Subacute headache/fever with modest CSF inflammation; can involve hypothalamus → SIADH | No angiographic abnormalities on CTA/MRA; absence of focal deficits |
| **Autoimmune encephalitides** (anti‑NMDA‑R, anti‑LGI1, anti‑CASPR2) | May start with fever/headache & lymphocytic CSF before overt neuropsychiatric signs appear | No seizures or psychiatric changes; antibody panel not ordered initially because clinical suspicion low |
| **Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM)** | Post‑infectious demyelinating process can have a sterile pleocytosis | MRI showed no white‑matter lesions at presentation |

> *Teaching point*: When infectious workup is negative but systemic inflammatory signs persist (high CRP/ESR), broaden the net to include Behçet’s disease—especially if any oral/genital ulceration surfaces—as demonstrated by this patient’s later evolution.^2^

---

### 3️⃣ Neoplastic causes  

| Disorder | Why consider early | Reason it fell lower on the list here |
|----------|-------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Primary CNS lymphoma** | Often presents in ≥60 y/o with focal symptoms or “pseudo‐meningitic” CSF picture (lymphocytes ↑, protein ↑); paraneoplastic ADH secretion can cause SIADH⁽³⁾ | Normal contrast MRI & absent atypical enhancement; CSF cytology bland |
| **Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis** (solid tumour metastasis) | Produces diffuse meningeal irritation → similar CSF profile + possible SIADH from hypothalamic infiltration | No known primary cancer; imaging unrevealing |
| **Paraneoplastic limbic encephalitis** | May have normal MRI early & inflammatory CSF changes while ectopic ADH secretion creates hyponatraemia⁽⁴⁾ | No lung/breast/ovarian tumour identified on CT chest/abdomen/pelvis |

> *Teaching point*: Even when brain MRI is normal, malignancy cannot be excluded outright in an older adult presenting with unexplained meningeal inflammation—CSF cytology and thorough cancer screening are essential steps.^3^

---

### 4️⃣ Drug–related mechanisms  

| Agent / syndrome | How it mimics the presentation |
|------------------|--------------------------------|
| **SIADH induced by medications** – SSRIs, carbamazepine, tramadol, cyclophosphamide *(not yet started in this patient)* – leads to euvolemic hyponatraemia that may coexist with an unrelated sterile meningitic process |
| **Drug–induced aseptic meningitis** – NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen), β‑lactam antibiotics (e.g., cefepime), sulfonamides or quinolones – typically produces low‐grade fever + headache ± mild lymphocytic pleocytosis = “meningism”; peripheral inflammation can secondarily stimulate ADH release |
| **Immunomodulators** – interferon‐α or immune checkpoint inhibitors – have been reported to trigger both central inflammation *and* SIADH |

In the index case there was no recent exposure to these agents at admission (patient was treatment naïve for BD), yet awareness of these entities is crucial because once colchicine had been started they deliberately avoided cyclophosphamide precisely because it has been associated with drug‑induced SIADH.^2^

---

#### Bottom line for residents
When you encounter a patient like this—feverish middle‐aged man with subacute headache, modestly inflammatory CSF and euvolemic hyponatraemia—think first of treatable infections (**HSV/VZV**, bacterial meningo­coccus including *Listeria*, TB/Fungal). If cultures/PCR are rapidly negative and MRI remains unremarkable, pivot toward **inflammatory mimics**, especially Behçet’s disease when any oral/genital ulceration appears. Keep an eye on **neoplastic masqueraders** such as primary CNS lymphoma or leptomeningeal metastasis despite a clean scan—they often reveal themselves only later on cytology or repeat imaging. Finally, always review current medications because several drugs provoke both SIADH and sterile meningeal irritation.
