Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Morning Report] The same patient has a normal brain MRI performed on day 3 despite ongoing symptoms. Given the CSF profile described above (low opening pressure ≈17 cm H₂O, mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, negative cultures and viral PCR), what diagnosis does this pattern most support and why does the normal MRI not exclude it?

**Answer – Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease presenting as aseptic meningitis**

The combination of a **sterile CSF**—opening pressure essentially normal (≈ 17 cm H₂O), **mild lymphocytic pleocytosis** (≈ 11 cells/µL), **elevated protein** (≈ 0.97 g/L) with **negative bacterial/fungal cultures and viral PCRs**—in a patient who already meets the International Criteria for Behçet’s disease (oral aphthae, genital ulcer, folliculitis‑like rash, HLA‑B51 positivity) most strongly points to **acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD)** rather than infection or malignancy.

| Differential | Typical CSF profile | Compatibility with this case |
|--------------|--------------------|-------------------------------|
| Bacterial meningitis | High opening pressure, neutrophilic pleocytosis >100 cells/µL, low glucose | Not compatible – pressure only mildly elevated, lymphocytes predominate |
| HSV/VZV encephalitis | Lymphocytes 50–200 cells/µL, often elevated RBCs or HSV DNA positive | Pleocytosis is far milder and PCR is negative |
| Tuberculous meningitis | Very high protein (>1 g/L), low glucose, lymphocytes often >50 cells/µL | Protein borderline high but glucose normal and cultures negative |
| CNS lymphoma / leptomeningeal metastasis | Variable cells, malignant cytology | Cytology negative |
| Acute neuro‑BD | Usually **aseptic meningitis** pattern—normal‑to‑low pressure, modest lymphocytes (≤30 cells/µL), moderately raised protein; cultures & PCR negative | **Exact match** |

Thus the CSF findings are classic for **“aseptic meningitis”** associated with neuro‑BD. The systemic Behçet features seal the diagnosis.

---

### Why a Normal Brain MRI Does NOT Exclude Acute Neuro‑BD

1. **Early disease may be limited to meningeal inflammation.**  
   In the first few days of neuro‑BD the inflammatory process often involves only the leptomeninges or perivascular spaces without frank parenchymal edema or necrosis. Conventional T1/T2 sequences are insensitive to pure meningeal irritation unless there is gadolinium‑enhancing leptomeningeal thickening—something that can be absent early on.

2. **Parenchymal lesions develop later and are often focal (brain‑stem, basal ganglia).**  
   The hallmark “brain‑stem hyperintensities” typical of chronic or subacute neuro‑BD appear weeks after symptom onset. A scan performed on day 3 therefore frequently looks completely normal.

3. **MRI sensitivity depends on technique.**  
   Standard sequences used in most emergency protocols miss subtle leptomeningeal enhancement. High‑resolution FLAIR or post‑contrast 3‑D T1‐weighted images improve detection but were not reported in this case.

4. **Clinical correlation outranks imaging in early inflammatory presentations.**  
   As demonstrated here, a *clinical* constellation of Behçet’s mucocutaneous lesions plus an aseptic CSF picture is sufficient to label the episode “acute neuro‑BD” even when imaging is unrevealing. The diagnosis is reinforced by rapid response to anti‑inflammatory therapy (colchicine) and concurrent resolution of hyponatraemia/SIADH—a downstream effect of CNS inflammation.

---

### Take‑Home Points for Residents

* **Aseptic meningitis (+ Behçet’s signs) → think acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease** even if the MRI is initially normal.
* **Normal opening pressure and modest lymphocytic pleocytosis** help differentiate from bacterial or tuberculous meningitis.
* **Absence of imaging abnormalities does not rule out CNS inflammation**, especially when the pathology is predominantly meningeal.
* Early recognition allows prompt anti‑inflammatory treatment (colchicine in this case) and prevents complications such as SIADH‐related hyponatraemia.
* Always correlate CSF data with systemic clinical clues—behavioural changes, oral/genital ulcers, skin lesions—rather than relying exclusively on radiology.

In short, this pattern most supports *acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease presenting as aseptic meningitis*, and a normal brain MRI on day 3 is expected rather than contradictory in the early phase of this disorder.
