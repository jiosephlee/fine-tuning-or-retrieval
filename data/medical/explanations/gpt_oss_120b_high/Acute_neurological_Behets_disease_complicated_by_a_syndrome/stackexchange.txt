Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] According to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD), which clinical manifestations earn points toward the diagnosis and what minimum total score is required to classify a patient as having definite Behçet’s disease?

**International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) – Point System and Diagnostic Threshold**

| Clinical manifestation | ICBD points* |
|------------------------|--------------|
| Oral aphthous ulcers   | 2 |
| Genital aphthous ulcers| 2 |
| Ocular lesions* (uveitis, retinal vasculitis, retinal infiltrates) | 2 |
| Skin lesions* (pseudofolliculitis, erythema nodosum‑like lesions, papulopustular eruptions) | 1 |
| Vascular involvement* (large‑vessel thrombosis, arterial aneurysm, superficial thrombophlebitis) | 1 |
| Neurologic involvement* (meningo‑encephalitis, parenchymal disease, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis) | 1 |
| Positive pathergy test† | 1 |

\*Included in the original 2014/2018 ICBD schema; each category is counted once regardless of the number of lesions.  
†The pathergy test is optional in many clinical settings; a positive result adds one point if performed.

**Diagnostic cut‑off:**  
A total **≥ 4 points** classifies a patient as having **definite Behçet’s disease**. Lower scores denote “possible BD” and require longitudinal follow‑up.

---

### How the case illustrates the scoring

The 60‑year‑old man reported in the case report had:

1. **Oral aphthae** – 2 points  
2. **Genital ulcer** – 2 points  
3. **Folliculitis‑like skin rash** – 1 point  

Total = **5 points**, which exceeds the ≥4‑point threshold; therefore he was appropriately classified as having *definite* Behçet’s disease. This scoring allowed the clinicians to anchor their diagnostic reasoning before invoking more specialized investigations for neuro‑Behçet’s disease and SIADH.

---

### Clinical pearls

- **Weighting reflects specificity:** Oral and genital ulcerations are the most characteristic mucocutaneous findings of BD; they are each worth 2 points because they dominate the clinical picture in >80 % of patients.
- **Ocular disease also receives 2 points** because uveitis can cause irreversible visual loss and often heralds systemic activity.
- **Skin, vascular, neurologic, and pathergy features are each given 1 point**, acknowledging their importance but also their relative lower specificity.
- **A ≥4‑point score is deliberately low enough to capture early or atypical presentations**—as in this patient, where neuro‑Behçet’s manifested simultaneously with mucocutaneous signs.
- **Use the ICBD early:** In a febrile patient with headache and hyponatraemia, recognizing oral/genital ulcers and skin lesions should prompt calculation of an ICBD score; reaching ≥4 points justifies treating as BD while still pursuing exclusion of infection or malignancy.
- **Neuro‑Behçet’s counts toward the score**, but can be diagnosed even when neuroimaging is normal (as seen here). Remember that acute neuro‑Behçet’s may present solely with aseptic meningitis findings on CSF.

---

**Bottom line:** The ICBD awards 2 points each for oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and ocular lesions; 1 point each for skin lesions, vascular involvement, neurologic involvement, and a positive pathergy test. A cumulative **≥ 4 points** confirms definite Behçet’s disease—exactly how the patient in this report was classified after accumulating five points from his mucocutaneous symptoms. This structured approach streamlines diagnosis and guides timely management of both systemic and neurologic manifestations.

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient with serum sodium 124 mmol/L, serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine sodium 88 mmol/L and a clinically euvolemic state, which components satisfy the diagnostic criteria for syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH)?

**Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH) – What the bedside data tell us**

In the classic work‑up for SIADH we apply a checklist that separates true “inappropriate” water retention from other causes of hyponatraemia. The patient described in the Behçet’s disease report fulfills every element of that checklist:

| Diagnostic element | Required finding | Patient’s value (case report) | Why it satisfies the criterion |
|--------------------|-----------------|------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **1. Hyponatraemia** | Serum Na⁺ < 135 mmol/L | **124 mmol/L** | Confirms hypotonic hyponatraemia. |
| **2. Low plasma osmolality** | Serum osmolality < 275 mOsm/kg | **267 mOsm/kg** | Demonstrates true hypotonic state rather than pseudohyponatraemia. |
| **3. Inappropriately concentrated urine** | Urine osm > 100 mOsm/kg *and* > plasma osmolality despite hyponatraemia | **900 mOsm/kg** (≫ plasma) | The kidneys are failing to dilute urine as they should when plasma is hypo‑osmolar. |
| **4. Elevated urinary sodium** | Urine Na⁺ > 30–40 mmol/L with normal salt intake | **88 mmol/L** | Indicates renal natriuresis rather than volume‑depletion‑driven avid sodium reabsorption. |
| **5. Clinically euvolaemic status** | No edema, orthostatic hypotension, or signs of hypovolaemia/ hypervolaemia | *“No peripheral oedema… volume status was clinically assessed as euvolemic.”* | Confirms that water retention is not compensatory to true volume loss or overload. |
| **6. Absence of other endocrine or renal causes** | Normal thyroid (TSH/T₄), adrenal (cortisol), renal function; no diuretics or nephrotoxic drugs | TSH 1.11 mIU/L, free T₄ 1.25 ng/dL, cortisol 42 µg/dL → all within reference ranges; creatinine normal; no diuretic exposure reported | Excludes hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, chronic kidney disease and drug‑induced SIADH—all “exclusion” criteria required for the diagnosis. |
| **7️⃣ Optional supportive data** | Elevated circulating ADH (or copeptin) in the setting of hyponatraemia | ADH **22.4 pg/mL** (high) → falls back on pathophysiology but is *not* needed for the formal definition |

**Putting it together**

The patient has *hypotonic hyponatraemia* (Na⁺ 124 mmol/L, serum osm 267 mOsm/kg) while—in stark contrast—excreting *hyper‑osmolar urine* (Uosm 900 mOsm/kg) rich in sodium (UNa⁺ 88 mmol/L). Clinically he is *euvolaemic*, and exhaustive testing has ruled out thyroid or adrenal failure, renal disease and offending medications. By the widely accepted Schwartz‑Bartter/European Society of Endocrinology algorithm, these findings satisfy all core criteria for SIADH; the markedly elevated ADH level merely corroborates the diagnosis.

**Take‑home pearls for the morning report**

1️⃣ Hyponatraemia + low serum osm + **urine osm ≥100 mOsm/kg (or >serum)** → “the kidney is not appropriately suppressing ADH.”  
2️⃣ Urine Na⁺ >30 mmol/L signals that the kidney’s sodium handling is intact → points away from hypovolaemia‑driven RAAS activation.  
3️⃣ Euvolaemia on exam plus exclusion of thyroid/adrenal disease = “pure” SIADH rather than a secondary hyponatraemic state.  
4️⃣ An elevated ADH measurement is supportive but not required—clinical/lab pattern clinches the diagnosis, as illustrated in this Behçet’s case.

In this patient with acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease the inflammatory stimulus to hypothalamic ADH secretion produced precisely these laboratory hallmarks, fulfilling every component of the SIADH diagnostic framework.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain how central nervous system inflammation in acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease can lead to inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone. Include the role(s) of cytokines such as interleukin‑6.

**Pathophysiology – Why acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease can precipitate SIADH**

The patient described by Kakehi et al. had fulminant meningo‑meningeal inflammation (CSF protein 0.97 g/L, lymphocytic pleocytosis) associated with a striking hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L) and an inappropriately high plasma ADH (22 pg/mL). No alternative trigger for the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) was identified—there was no pulmonary disease, no tumour, no offending medication—and the hyponatraemia resolved when the inflammatory process was dampened by colchicine. This temporal relationship illustrates the classic mechanistic cascade linking CNS inflammation to ectopic ADH release:

| Step | What happens | How it ties back to the case |
|------|--------------|------------------------------|
| 1️⃣  | **Aseptic meningeal inflammation** – “acute neuro‑Behçet’s” is characterised by cytokine‑rich meningo‑encephalitis that may be radiographically silent (MRI normal in our patient). | CSF showed mild pleocytosis and elevated protein despite a normal MRI. |
| 2️⃣  | **Cytokine surge** – activated monocytes/macrophages in Behçet’s overproduce pro‑inflammatory cytokines, especially interleukin‑6 (IL‑6); systemic CRP is a downstream surrogate for IL‑6 activity. | Serum CRP was markedly elevated (180 mg/L), implying an intense IL‑6–driven response even though IL‑6 itself was not measured. |
| 3️⃣  | **IL‑6–mediated stimulation of magnocellular neurons** – IL‑6 receptors are expressed on paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei. Experimental data show that systemic or central IL‑6 lowers the osmotic threshold for AVP release and can drive AVP secretion independent of plasma osmolality or baroreceptor input. | The patient’s high ADH level despite hypo‑osmolar plasma reflects this “reset” of the set point. |
| 4️⃣  | **Disruption of hypothalamic osmoreceptor signaling** – inflammatory mediators (IL‑1β, TNF‑α, prostaglandins) alter neuronal excitability and blunt the inhibitory feedback that normally suppresses ADH when plasma is dilute. | The persistent hyponatraemia despite euvolemia is typical for central SIADH rather than volume overload or renal loss. |
| 5️⃣  | **Water retention → hyponatraemia** – excess AVP promotes free water reabsorption in the collecting ducts via V2‑receptor–mediated insertion of aquaporin‐2 channels, diluting serum sodium. | Urine osmolality was inappropriately high (900 mOsm/kg) and urinary Na was elevated (88 mmol/L), confirming impaired free water excretion. |

### The specific contribution of IL‑6

1. **Source in Behçet’s disease** – Monocyte/macrophage activation is a hallmark of BD; circulating monocytes spontaneously secrete IL‑6 even without external stimuli. Elevated CSF IL‑6 has been documented in patients with acute neuro‑BD presenting with meningoencephalitis.

2. **Direct effect on AVP neurons** – In vitro studies demonstrate that IL‑6 binds gp130/IL‑6R on magnocellular neurons, activating STAT3 signaling and up‐regulating AVP gene transcription. In rodent models, intraventricular administration of recombinant IL‑6 raises plasma AVP within minutes.

3. **Correlation with clinical SIADH** – In viral meningitis and bacterial encephalitis, CSF IL‑6 concentrations correlate tightly with serum sodium nadir and urine concentrating ability—suggesting a dose–response relationship that likely applies to sterile inflammatory states such as neuro‑BD.

4. **Therapeutic implication illustrated by this case** – Colchicine impairs neutrophil chemotaxis and dampens inflammasome activation; it also reduces transcription of several cytokines including IL‑1β and IL‑6. The patient’s rapid clinical improvement, normalization of ADH (down to 1.8 pg/mL) and correction of sodium occurred after colchicine was introduced, supporting the hypothesis that decreasing IL‑6 production mitigated inappropriate ADH release.

### Take‐home points for trainees

* **SIADH should be suspected whenever hyponatraemia coexists with an acute neuroinflammatory picture—even when brain imaging is normal.**
* **In Behçet’s disease, meningeal inflammation can generate an IL‑6–dominant cytokine milieu that directly “reprograms” hypothalamic AVP secretion**, producing euvolemic hyponatraemia characteristic of SIADH.
* **A high serum CRP serves as a practical bedside clue that an upstream IL‑6 surge may be driving both systemic inflammation and ectopic ADH release.**
* **Control of the inflammatory driver—whether by corticosteroids, colchicine or other anti­cytokine agents—typically reverses SIADH without needing specific antidiuretic blockade**, provided rapid correction of sodium is avoided.

In this case, acute CNS inflammation from neuro‑Behçet’s created an environment rich in IL‑6 and related cytokines that bypassed normal osmotic regulation, prompting inappropriate ADH secretion and consequent hyponatraemia—a textbook illustration of how sterile neuroinflammation can masquerade as an endocrine disturbance.

### [Differential Diagnosis] A 62‑year‑old man presents with fever, worsening headache over three days, hyponatraemia due to SIADH and cerebrospinal fluid showing mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (~11 cells/µL) and elevated protein (~0.97 g/L). List the primary differential diagnoses that should be considered initially, grouping them into infectious, inflammatory/autoimmune, neoplastic and drug‑related categories.

**Acute fever + headache + SIADH + mildly inflammatory CSF**  
*Key bedside data*: 62 y/o man, 3 days of fever‑headache, serum Na⁺ 124 mmol/L with classic laboratory criteria for SIADH, CSF opening pressure 17 cm H₂O, protein ≈ 0·97 g/L, lymphocytes ≈ 11 µL⁽↑⁾; normal brain MRI; no obvious infectious focus on imaging or physical exam.

The first‐line differential must encompass all entities that can produce **(i)** a sub‑acute meningo‑encephalitic picture with a modest lymphocytic pleocytosis and protein rise and **(ii)** an associated ADH surge causing euvolemic hyponatraemia.  In the reported case the team pursued this broad list—initially treating empiric bacterial meningitis and HSV encephalitis before these possibilities were excluded by culture/PCR negativity—and later ruled out sarcoid‐type inflammation and lymphoma on the basis of normal imaging and negative cytology/specific labs.

Below is a concise “initial” differential grouped by pathophysiologic category:

---

### 1️⃣ Infectious etiologies  

| Disorder | Why it belongs in the early list | Typical clues that helped exclude it in this case |
|----------|----------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| **HSV‑1 / HSV‑2 meningoencephalitis** | Most common viral cause of fever–headache with lymphocyte‑predominant CSF; can trigger ADH release via cytokines | Negative HSV PCR on CSF → discontinued acyclovir |
| **Varicella‑zoster virus (VZV)** | Older adults frequently develop VZV meningoradiculopathy with modest pleocytosis | VZV PCR/antigen negative |
| **Enteroviruses / other non‑HSV viruses** (e.g., coxsackie, echovirus) | Typical aseptic meningitis picture; usually self‑limited | Viral panel negative; clinical course prolonged (>5 d) despite supportive care |
| **Listeria monocytogenes** | In patients >50 y/o can present with a *lymphocyte*‐rich CSF early on | No Gram stain organisms; cultures sterile; lack of typical exposure |
| **Tuberculous meningitis** | Subacute onset, high protein (>1 g/L), low glucose possible; often produces SIADH via hypothalamic involvement | Acid‐fast stain & MTB PCR negative; sIL‑2R only mildly ↑ (non‑specific) |
| **Cryptococcus neoformans** | HIV‑negative cryptococcal meningitis may show mild lymphocytes and elevated protein | Cryptococcal antigen negative |
| **Neurosyphilis** | Can give a chronic aseptic picture with modest pleocytosis | RPR/VDRL negative in serum & CSF |
| **Other zoonoses** (e.g., Lyme disease) | Possible when tick exposure present → CSF lymphocytes ↑ | No exposure history |

> *Teaching point*: In any febrile adult with new headache the first priority is to rule out treatable infections—bacterial (*including Listeria*), viral (*HSV/VZV*), mycobacterial (*TB*) and fungal—because delays increase morbidity/mortality.^1^

---

### 2️⃣ Inflammatory / Autoimmune disorders  

| Disorder | Rationale for inclusion | Features that eventually directed away from it |
|----------|------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| **Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease** | Meningoencephalitic presentation without early parenchymal MRI lesions is classic for “acute” neuro‑BD; cytokine surge (IL‑6) can precipitate SIADH ‑ exactly what occurred in the reported case once mucocutaneous ulcers appeared.^2^ | Initially absent genital ulcers → low suspicion until day 5 when scrotal ulcer emerged; diagnosis then confirmed by International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease |
| **Neurosarcoidosis** | Granulomatous CNS inflammation may mimic aseptic meningitis and cause hypothalamic involvement → SIADH | Normal serum ACE/lysozyme + lack of systemic sarcoid findings → deemed unlikely |
| **Systemic lupus erythematosus / Neuro‑SLE** | SLE can produce meningeal irritation (“lupus meningitis”) plus cytokine‐driven ADH release | ANA panel negative; no other SLE manifestations |
| **Primary CNS vasculitis** | Subacute headache/fever with modest CSF inflammation; can involve hypothalamus → SIADH | No angiographic abnormalities on CTA/MRA; absence of focal deficits |
| **Autoimmune encephalitides** (anti‑NMDA‑R, anti‑LGI1, anti‑CASPR2) | May start with fever/headache & lymphocytic CSF before overt neuropsychiatric signs appear | No seizures or psychiatric changes; antibody panel not ordered initially because clinical suspicion low |
| **Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM)** | Post‑infectious demyelinating process can have a sterile pleocytosis | MRI showed no white‑matter lesions at presentation |

> *Teaching point*: When infectious workup is negative but systemic inflammatory signs persist (high CRP/ESR), broaden the net to include Behçet’s disease—especially if any oral/genital ulceration surfaces—as demonstrated by this patient’s later evolution.^2^

---

### 3️⃣ Neoplastic causes  

| Disorder | Why consider early | Reason it fell lower on the list here |
|----------|-------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Primary CNS lymphoma** | Often presents in ≥60 y/o with focal symptoms or “pseudo‐meningitic” CSF picture (lymphocytes ↑, protein ↑); paraneoplastic ADH secretion can cause SIADH⁽³⁾ | Normal contrast MRI & absent atypical enhancement; CSF cytology bland |
| **Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis** (solid tumour metastasis) | Produces diffuse meningeal irritation → similar CSF profile + possible SIADH from hypothalamic infiltration | No known primary cancer; imaging unrevealing |
| **Paraneoplastic limbic encephalitis** | May have normal MRI early & inflammatory CSF changes while ectopic ADH secretion creates hyponatraemia⁽⁴⁾ | No lung/breast/ovarian tumour identified on CT chest/abdomen/pelvis |

> *Teaching point*: Even when brain MRI is normal, malignancy cannot be excluded outright in an older adult presenting with unexplained meningeal inflammation—CSF cytology and thorough cancer screening are essential steps.^3^

---

### 4️⃣ Drug–related mechanisms  

| Agent / syndrome | How it mimics the presentation |
|------------------|--------------------------------|
| **SIADH induced by medications** – SSRIs, carbamazepine, tramadol, cyclophosphamide *(not yet started in this patient)* – leads to euvolemic hyponatraemia that may coexist with an unrelated sterile meningitic process |
| **Drug–induced aseptic meningitis** – NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen), β‑lactam antibiotics (e.g., cefepime), sulfonamides or quinolones – typically produces low‐grade fever + headache ± mild lymphocytic pleocytosis = “meningism”; peripheral inflammation can secondarily stimulate ADH release |
| **Immunomodulators** – interferon‐α or immune checkpoint inhibitors – have been reported to trigger both central inflammation *and* SIADH |

In the index case there was no recent exposure to these agents at admission (patient was treatment naïve for BD), yet awareness of these entities is crucial because once colchicine had been started they deliberately avoided cyclophosphamide precisely because it has been associated with drug‑induced SIADH.^2^

---

#### Bottom line for residents
When you encounter a patient like this—feverish middle‐aged man with subacute headache, modestly inflammatory CSF and euvolemic hyponatraemia—think first of treatable infections (**HSV/VZV**, bacterial meningo­coccus including *Listeria*, TB/Fungal). If cultures/PCR are rapidly negative and MRI remains unremarkable, pivot toward **inflammatory mimics**, especially Behçet’s disease when any oral/genital ulceration appears. Keep an eye on **neoplastic masqueraders** such as primary CNS lymphoma or leptomeningeal metastasis despite a clean scan—they often reveal themselves only later on cytology or repeat imaging. Finally, always review current medications because several drugs provoke both SIADH and sterile meningeal irritation.

### [Morning Report] The same patient has a normal brain MRI performed on day 3 despite ongoing symptoms. Given the CSF profile described above (low opening pressure ≈17 cm H₂O, mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, negative cultures and viral PCR), what diagnosis does this pattern most support and why does the normal MRI not exclude it?

**Answer – Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease presenting as aseptic meningitis**

The combination of a **sterile CSF**—opening pressure essentially normal (≈ 17 cm H₂O), **mild lymphocytic pleocytosis** (≈ 11 cells/µL), **elevated protein** (≈ 0.97 g/L) with **negative bacterial/fungal cultures and viral PCRs**—in a patient who already meets the International Criteria for Behçet’s disease (oral aphthae, genital ulcer, folliculitis‑like rash, HLA‑B51 positivity) most strongly points to **acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD)** rather than infection or malignancy.

| Differential | Typical CSF profile | Compatibility with this case |
|--------------|--------------------|-------------------------------|
| Bacterial meningitis | High opening pressure, neutrophilic pleocytosis >100 cells/µL, low glucose | Not compatible – pressure only mildly elevated, lymphocytes predominate |
| HSV/VZV encephalitis | Lymphocytes 50–200 cells/µL, often elevated RBCs or HSV DNA positive | Pleocytosis is far milder and PCR is negative |
| Tuberculous meningitis | Very high protein (>1 g/L), low glucose, lymphocytes often >50 cells/µL | Protein borderline high but glucose normal and cultures negative |
| CNS lymphoma / leptomeningeal metastasis | Variable cells, malignant cytology | Cytology negative |
| Acute neuro‑BD | Usually **aseptic meningitis** pattern—normal‑to‑low pressure, modest lymphocytes (≤30 cells/µL), moderately raised protein; cultures & PCR negative | **Exact match** |

Thus the CSF findings are classic for **“aseptic meningitis”** associated with neuro‑BD. The systemic Behçet features seal the diagnosis.

---

### Why a Normal Brain MRI Does NOT Exclude Acute Neuro‑BD

1. **Early disease may be limited to meningeal inflammation.**  
   In the first few days of neuro‑BD the inflammatory process often involves only the leptomeninges or perivascular spaces without frank parenchymal edema or necrosis. Conventional T1/T2 sequences are insensitive to pure meningeal irritation unless there is gadolinium‑enhancing leptomeningeal thickening—something that can be absent early on.

2. **Parenchymal lesions develop later and are often focal (brain‑stem, basal ganglia).**  
   The hallmark “brain‑stem hyperintensities” typical of chronic or subacute neuro‑BD appear weeks after symptom onset. A scan performed on day 3 therefore frequently looks completely normal.

3. **MRI sensitivity depends on technique.**  
   Standard sequences used in most emergency protocols miss subtle leptomeningeal enhancement. High‑resolution FLAIR or post‑contrast 3‑D T1‐weighted images improve detection but were not reported in this case.

4. **Clinical correlation outranks imaging in early inflammatory presentations.**  
   As demonstrated here, a *clinical* constellation of Behçet’s mucocutaneous lesions plus an aseptic CSF picture is sufficient to label the episode “acute neuro‑BD” even when imaging is unrevealing. The diagnosis is reinforced by rapid response to anti‑inflammatory therapy (colchicine) and concurrent resolution of hyponatraemia/SIADH—a downstream effect of CNS inflammation.

---

### Take‑Home Points for Residents

* **Aseptic meningitis (+ Behçet’s signs) → think acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease** even if the MRI is initially normal.
* **Normal opening pressure and modest lymphocytic pleocytosis** help differentiate from bacterial or tuberculous meningitis.
* **Absence of imaging abnormalities does not rule out CNS inflammation**, especially when the pathology is predominantly meningeal.
* Early recognition allows prompt anti‑inflammatory treatment (colchicine in this case) and prevents complications such as SIADH‐related hyponatraemia.
* Always correlate CSF data with systemic clinical clues—behavioural changes, oral/genital ulcers, skin lesions—rather than relying exclusively on radiology.

In short, this pattern most supports *acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease presenting as aseptic meningitis*, and a normal brain MRI on day 3 is expected rather than contradictory in the early phase of this disorder.
