Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] Why was an elective cesarean delivery chosen over a trial of labor for this patient despite successful removal of one mass, and what anatomical considerations dictated this mode of delivery?

**Clinical Pearl:** In this case, the decision to proceed with an elective cesarean delivery rather than a trial of labor was dictated by the **size, location, and fixed nature of the residual pelvic leiomyoma**, which posed a mechanical obstruction to fetal descent.

While the upper mass was successfully resected via laparotomy because it was pedunculated and causing acute symptoms (torsion/necrosis), the lower mass was intentionally left *in situ* to minimize operative trauma and the risk of preterm labor. As noted in the case report, this residual mass was described as "fixed" and arising from the uterine torus. Its dimensions (approximately 9 cm) and posterior intramural-subserosal location created a significant "steric bulk" in the pelvic inlet. During a vaginal delivery, this fixed mass would act as an absolute barrier to the passage of the fetal head into the pelvis, leading to inevitable obstructed labor.

Furthermore, attempting a trial of labor in the setting of prior myomectomy involving deep uterine incisions carries a risk of uterine dehiscence or rupture, particularly when combined with a large residual fibroid distorting the lower uterine segment. The authors explicitly state that "the caudal myoma would have represented a huge obstacle for the fetal head progression during the expulsive phase." Consequently, scheduling an elective cesarean delivery at term allowed for controlled extraction while minimizing the risks associated with prolonged obstructed labor and potential uterine rupture at the site of previous surgical intervention. This aligns with general principles where mode of delivery is determined by tumor size, number, and anatomical relationship to the birth canal; when tumors occupy space critical for engagement or descent, cesarean delivery becomes mandatory regardless of prior successful tumor debulking elsewhere in the abdomen.
