Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Pathophysiology] How do physiological changes during pregnancy affect the interpretation of serum CA-125 levels, and why might this marker show an upward trend in a patient with benign uterine leiomyomas rather than malignancy?

**Clinical Pearl:** During pregnancy, serum CA-125 levels undergo distinct physiological fluctuations, rising in the first and third trimesters before normalizing in the second. Consequently, an isolated or mildly elevated CA-125 level in a pregnant patient—such as the 41.8 U/mL seen in this case—is frequently a false-positive indicator for malignancy when interpreted against non-pregnant reference ranges (<35 U/mL).

**Pathophysiological Reasoning:**
CA-125 is a glycoprotein expressed by coelomic epithelium, including the peritoneum, pleura, and pericardium. Its elevation is driven by mesothelial irritation or inflammation rather than tumor burden alone. In pregnancy, physiological changes such as uterine distension, increased vascularity, and hormonal shifts stimulate the peritoneal lining, leading to predictable peaks in CA-125 during early gestation (due to implantation and trophoblastic activity) and late gestation (due to mechanical stress). As noted in the discussion of this case report, the median CA-125 rises to approximately 23 U/mL in the first trimester and 21 U/mL in the third, compared to a baseline of ~14 U/mL in the second.

Furthermore, benign uterine pathology specifically drives these elevations. Leiomyomas (fibroids) can induce local inflammation, hemorrhage into the tumor (cystic degeneration), or ischemia via torsion of pedunculated masses. These processes cause serosal irritation and release of CA-125 from the adjacent peritoneum. In this specific patient, the "increasing trend" from 36 to 41.8 U/mL correlated with a large, pedunculated leiomyoma that was twisting and undergoing necrotic degeneration—a classic benign mimic of malignancy that explains the marker's rise without implying cancer.

**Management Implication:**
This case underscores that CA-125 lacks specificity in pregnancy; relying on it alone can lead to unnecessary anxiety or aggressive intervention. Diagnosis must instead rely on multimodal imaging (ultrasound with color Doppler and non-contrast MRI) to assess mass characteristics (e.g., cystic vs. solid components, attachment to uterine tissue) and clinical context (pain, rapid growth). While serial monitoring is useful for tracking trends, a mild elevation should be viewed through the lens of benign etiologies like fibroid degeneration or endometriosis until proven otherwise by histopathology or advanced imaging findings suggestive of malignancy.
