Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Multidisciplinary Assessment

The initial encounter with a pregnant patient presenting with atypical abdominal symptoms demands a high index of suspicion for pathology beyond the normal physiological changes of gestation. In this case, a nulliparous woman in her late forties presented in the second trimester (21 weeks) with severe, acute abdominal pain localized to the upper left quadrant and the pelvis, accompanied by mild dyspnea. While abdominal discomfort is common in pregnancy, the severity of the pain—quantified as a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score of 5—and the association with respiratory distress signal a deviation from standard antenatal care. These "atypical" symptoms necessitate immediate differentiation between uterine causes, such as placental abruption or labor, and extra-uterine etiologies like appendicitis or, as seen here, large compressive masses. The presence of dyspnea specifically raises concern for mechanical restriction of the diaphragm or underlying cardiopulmonary compromise, requiring prompt evaluation to rule out life-threatening emergencies before focusing on the obstetric implications.

Physical examination plays a pivotal role in distinguishing between expected uterine enlargement and pathological masses. Upon admission, the patient's abdomen appeared disproportionately enlarged relative to her gestational age. Palpation revealed a distinct taut-elastic mass measuring 18 cm occupying the entire upper left quadrant, separate from the gravid uterus. Concurrently, bimanual vaginal examination identified a fixed, hard mass in the lesser pelvis bulging into the posterior fornix. This dual finding—a large suprapubic/upper abdominal mass and a separate pelvic mass—is clinically significant because it suggests multifocal pathology rather than simple uterine fibroid degeneration alone. The ability to differentiate these findings relies on recognizing that while a gravid uterus expands uniformly upward and outward, discrete masses may remain fixed or exhibit irregular borders and tenderness distinct from the softer consistency of myometrium. In this scenario, the physical exam confirmed that the symptoms were driven by two distinct entities occupying different compartments of the abdomen and pelvis.

Despite the severity of her subjective complaints and physical findings suggesting significant pathology, the patient's systemic vitals remained remarkably stable upon arrival. Her oxygen saturation was maintained at 97%, heart rate at 74 bpm, and blood pressure at 100/60 mm Hg, with a normal electrocardiogram. This dissociation between severe local symptoms (pain VAS 5/10) and global hemodynamic stability is a crucial teaching point: it indicates that while there is likely local tissue stress or inflammation causing pain and mechanical compression leading to dyspnea, there is no immediate shock or sepsis requiring resuscitation prior to diagnostic workup. Stabilization efforts focused on analgesia using intravenous paracetamol and positioning strategies; placing the patient in a semi-orthostatic posture helped alleviate diaphragmatic compression caused by both the enlarging uterus and the massive abdominal tumors. This intervention successfully reduced her pain score to 1 without requiring supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support, demonstrating that conservative measures can effectively manage mechanical symptoms while diagnostics are pursued.

Given the complexity of diagnosing large abdominal masses during pregnancy—where imaging options are limited by radiation safety concerns—the involvement of a multidisciplinary team (MDT) is essential from the moment of triage. In this case, an MDT comprising obstetricians, gynecologic surgeons, radiologists, and hematologists coordinated immediately upon admission. Their collective expertise guided a tailored diagnostic pathway: avoiding ionizing radiation while maximizing soft-tissue resolution through ultrasound with color Doppler followed by non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The MDT also assessed concurrent complications that could confound management or mimic malignancy; notably, laboratory investigations revealed iron-deficiency anemia (hemoglobin 9.9 g/L) and an active urinary tract infection (*Klebsiella pneumoniae*) evidenced by low-grade fever and leukocytosis. Recognizing that white blood cell counts naturally rise during pregnancy makes interpreting leukocytosis challenging; therefore, isolating specific infectious sources like pyelonephritis becomes paramount before attributing fever solely to tumor necrosis or torsion. Addressing these comorbidities concurrently ensures that treatment decisions regarding potential surgical intervention are based on accurate data regarding infection control and hemodynamic reserve rather than conflated inflammatory markers.

## Diagnostic Imaging Strategies and Safety Protocols

The diagnostic evaluation of abdominal masses during pregnancy presents unique challenges due to the need to balance diagnostic accuracy with fetal safety. In the case of this 40-year-old primigravida presenting with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea in the second trimester, the initial imaging modality of choice was ultrasonography (US) with color Doppler. This approach serves as the primary screening tool because it is widely available, lacks ionizing radiation, and provides real-time visualization of fetal viability. In this specific scenario, US successfully identified two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and confirmed peripheral vascularization. Crucially, the use of color Doppler allowed clinicians to assess blood flow within the masses and their relationship to surrounding structures, helping to distinguish between avascular cystic lesions and highly vascularized tumors. The ultrasound also revealed that the lower mass appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma exhibiting signs of cystic degeneration, providing an initial morphological framework for the differential diagnosis.

When ultrasound findings were inconclusive regarding the precise anatomical boundaries or internal composition—particularly given the "steric bulk" created by the gravid uterus obscuring standard landmarks—an advanced imaging strategy involving non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was employed. MRI offers superior soft-tissue contrast resolution compared to US, making it invaluable for characterizing complex masses that mimic other pathologies. In this patient, MRI clarified that the cranial mass possessed thick walls and heterogeneous fluid content with focal solid components, while confirming that the caudal mass was continuous with uterine tissue rather than being a separate adnexal entity. This distinction was pivotal in differentiating between potential diagnoses such as decidualized endometriomas versus cystic degeneration of leiomyomas. While tumor markers like CA-125 provided supportive data showing a rising trend suggestive of benign pathology (given pregnancy physiology), they cannot definitively characterize tissue type; thus, MRI served as the critical adjunct to visualize mass boundaries, solid-cystic composition, and spatial relationships that were otherwise obscured by the enlarged uterus.

Safety protocols dictate strict limitations on imaging techniques during pregnancy to prevent teratogenicity or fetal harm. Specifically, gadolinium-based contrast agents are generally avoided in pregnant patients unless absolutely life-saving, as gadolinium crosses the placental barrier and has been associated with potential adverse outcomes including stillbirth or neonatal death in animal studies. Consequently, this patient underwent an MRI without contrast medium. Furthermore, while high-field magnets (such as 3 Tesla) are sometimes utilized for enhanced resolution in non-pregnant adults, guidelines recommend limiting MRI scans during pregnancy to 1.5 Tesla systems whenever feasible to minimize thermal effects and acoustic noise exposure to the fetus. These precautions ensure that diagnostic clarity is achieved without compromising fetal safety profiles established by major radiological societies.

Despite these advanced tools, interpreting imaging in late-second or third-trimester pregnancies carries inherent limitations due to anatomical distortion caused by uterine expansion. As seen in this case, the sheer size of both the fetus and the abdominal masses made it difficult to identify connective stalks or distinct feeding vessels for the upper mass via ultrasound alone ("steric bulk"). The proximity of organs compressed against each other can lead to ambiguous findings where a pedunculated myoma might mimic an ovarian neoplasm or vice versa. Clinicians must therefore integrate imaging data with clinical history and laboratory trends; for instance, distinguishing between a decidualized endometrioma (which may show mild CA-125 elevation) and a degenerating leiomyoma relies heavily on recognizing subtle textural differences on MRI sequences like T1/T2 fat-saturation weighting when anatomical separation is compromised by pregnancy-induced displacement. Ultimately, a multimodal approach combining targeted ultrasound and non-contrast MRI allows for a nuanced understanding of atypical masses while rigorously adhering to safety standards essential for managing high-risk obstetric cases.

## Biochemical Markers and Differential Diagnosis Formulation

The interpretation of serum tumor markers in pregnancy presents a unique diagnostic challenge due to profound physiological fluctuations that can mimic malignancy. In the case described, the patient's CA-125 level was elevated at 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the standard non-pregnant cutoff of 35 U/mL. While this might initially raise concern for epithelial ovarian carcinoma, understanding the natural history of CA-125 during gestation is paramount. Physiologically, CA-125 exhibits a biphasic pattern: it rises during the first trimester (peaking around 23 U/mL) and again in the third trimester (around 21 U/mL), typically dipping to a nadir of approximately 14 U/mL in the second trimester. The patient's value of 41.8 U/mL represents a significant deviation from this physiological baseline for her gestational age (second trimester), suggesting pathology beyond simple pregnancy-related changes. However, isolated elevations remain non-diagnostic because benign conditions such as uterine leiomyomas, endometriosis, and pelvic inflammation can also cause marked increases. Consequently, relying on a single static measurement is insufficient; instead, clinicians must analyze serial trends. In this case, the rising trajectory from 36 U/mL two weeks prior provided crucial context, indicating active biological processes—likely related to the ischemic necrosis and torsion of the pedunculated leiomyoma—rather than a stable neoplastic process alone.

Beyond CA-125, the utility of other tumor markers in this clinical scenario was assessed to rule out germ cell tumors or other malignancies. The patient's levels of CA 19-9, CEA, CA 15-3, β2-microglobulin, and LDH were all within normal limits. These markers serve as important "negative" predictors; their normalcy helps exclude high-risk entities such as mature teratomas (which may elevate LDH or AFP) or gastrointestinal metastases (elevated CEA). The combination of a mildly elevated CA-125 alongside otherwise normal markers narrows the differential diagnosis significantly towards benign gynecological pathologies with secondary inflammation or hemorrhage rather than primary ovarian malignancy. This highlights a fundamental principle in obstetric oncology: biochemical data must never be interpreted in isolation but rather integrated with the gestational timeline and clinical presentation to avoid false-positive alarms that could precipitate unnecessary termination of pregnancy or aggressive surgical intervention.

Constructing an accurate differential diagnosis requires synthesizing these biochemical findings with advanced imaging characteristics to distinguish between benign mimics like decidualized endometriomas and true malignancies or complex leiomyomas. Initially, ultrasound suggested decidualized endometriomas due to the cystic nature of the masses and the presence of peripheral vascularity. However, MRI provided critical architectural details that shifted the probability toward uterine leiomyomas with cystic degeneration. Specifically, the MRI revealed thickened walls (1.4 cm), heterogeneous fluid content on T1/T2 sequences with solid components, and crucially, a lack of clear separation between the lower mass and a known posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. This radiological continuity suggests that what appeared as separate cystic lesions were actually degenerating fibroids sharing tissue planes with the uterus. The slight elevation in CA-125 correlates perfectly with this finding; leiomyomas undergoing red degeneration or torsion release inflammatory cytokines that stimulate peritoneal mesothelial cells to produce CA-125, creating a "pseudo-tumor marker" profile indistinguishable from early-stage ovarian cancer without histological confirmation.

Ultimately, deciding whether to suspect malignancy relies heavily on distinguishing between steady-state elevation driven by physiology/benign pathology versus dynamic escalation indicative of neoplasia or acute catastrophic events like torsion or rupture. In this case, despite the elevated CA-125, several factors argued against malignancy: the absence of lymphadenopathy on MRI, normal levels of other specific markers (CEA, LDH), and clinical stability aside from acute pain caused by mechanical distortion rather than systemic spread. The decision-making process prioritized these converging lines of evidence—the specific timing relative to trimesters (ruling out first-trimester peak), the trend analysis (showing rapid change correlating with symptom onset), and imaging features—to formulate a working diagnosis of complicated benign leiomyomas requiring surgical decompression rather than radical oncologic resection. This approach underscores that in pregnant patients with large abdominal masses, biochemical markers act as supportive clues within a multimodal framework where imaging anatomy often supersedes serology for determining etiology and guiding management strategies aimed at preserving both maternal health and fetal viability.
