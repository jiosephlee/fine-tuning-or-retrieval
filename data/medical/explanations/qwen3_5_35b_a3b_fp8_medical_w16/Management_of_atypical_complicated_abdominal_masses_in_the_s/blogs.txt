Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# The Second-Trimester Trap: Navigating Diagnostic Ambiguity Between Endometriomas and Degenerating Leiomyomas

We've all seen the "scary" ultrasound in a pregnant patient. Two giant cystic masses, one pushing up against the diaphragm causing dyspnea, the other anchoring in the pelvis. Your differential diagnosis immediately screams **endometrioma** or **ovarian malignancy**, especially when you see that slight bump in CA-125. You start mentally drafting the referral letter to a gynecologic oncologist and planning for a complex resection that might jeopardize the pregnancy.

But wait. What if I told you that what looked like a "decidualized endometrioma" was actually a **pedunculated uterine leiomyoma**? And what if the "cystic" nature wasn't a primary ovarian cyst, but rather **red degeneration** (or cystic degeneration) of a fibroid?

This is exactly what happened in a fascinating case report recently published by De Nola et al., involving a 40-something G1P0 woman at 21 weeks gestation presenting with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. It's a masterclass in how anatomy can deceive us, why tumor markers need context, and why sometimes, leaving *some* stuff alone is the most aggressive thing you can do for fetal safety. Let's break down this diagnostic maze.

## When Anatomy Plays Tricks: The Great Mimicker

The patient arrived with classic signs of an acute abdomen: severe left upper quadrant pain, pelvic tenderness, and dyspnea. On physical exam, she had an abdominal circumference larger than expected for gestational age, with two distinct masses palpable—one massive 18 cm lump in the LUQ and a 9 cm fixed mass in the pelvis.

Initial ultrasound painted a picture that screamed "adnexal pathology." Two large, unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularity were visualized. The upper mass extended from the subphrenic space to the uterine fundus; the lower one sat in the pouch of Douglas, seemingly attached to a posterior intramural myoma with cystic degeneration.

Here lies the trap: **Pedunculated leiomyomas can look remarkably like ovarian tumors.** During pregnancy, hormonal surges cause rapid changes in myometrial tissue (decalcification/hyalinization/red degeneration). A pedunculated fibroid undergoing red degeneration becomes necrotic, hemorrhagic, and fluid-filled on imaging. To the radiologist's eye (and yours), it looks like a complex ovarian cyst or an endometrioma.

The case highlights how easily we fall into confirmation bias. The "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus makes it incredibly difficult to trace ligaments or identify feeding vessels that would clearly link these masses to the ovary vs. the uterus. Without seeing that connective stalk on ultrasound or MRI, we assume ovarian origin because they are separate entities visually. But once they twist (torsion) or undergo degeneration, their attachment becomes obscured by inflammation and edema.

## The Imaging Toolkit: Ultrasound vs. Non-Contrast MRI

So, how do we navigate this ambiguity without exposing mom or baby to unnecessary radiation or contrast agents?

**Ultrasound (US)** is your first line of defense, but as we saw here, it has limitations regarding tissue characterization when masses are huge or deep-seated behind bowel gas or bone. Color Doppler helps assess vascularity—these masses had peripheral flow—but differentiating between an inflamed fibroid and an endometrioma based on flow patterns alone is tricky.

**Non-contrast MRI** is where we gain clarity without crossing safety lines (remember: Gadolinium crosses the placenta and carries theoretical risks). In this case, MRI confirmed:
*   Thick walls (1.4 cm).
*   Inhomogeneous fluid content on T1/T2 fat-sat sequences (suggesting old hemorrhage/degeneration).
*   Focal hyperintense solid components (the remaining viable fibroid tissue).
*   Crucially, it showed continuity between the lower mass and a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma of 58x71 mm, despite being "stuck" to it visually on US alone.

The takeaway? If US is equivocal for large pelvic/abdominal masses in pregnancy, **non-contrast MRI** is superior for determining depth of invasion and relationship to the uterine cavity/placenta. It tells you if you are dealing with an ovarian mass or a uterine mass masquerading as one—a distinction that dictates surgical approach and delivery planning.

## The CA-125 Pitfall: Don't Panic Over Numbers

Let's talk about that CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL. Normal is <35 U/mL? Oh no! Ovarian cancer alert! 🚨

Hold your horses before calling Oncology Emergency Room consults for ovarian cancer staging procedures during pregnancy unless there is overwhelming evidence otherwise. As discussed extensively in literature cited by De Nola et al.:
*   CA-125 naturally rises during pregnancy due to peritoneal irritation from uterine expansion and hormonal shifts (peaks in 1st and 3rd trimesters).
*   Benign conditions like endometriosis, adenomyosis PID, menstruation (obviously not applicable here), and **uterine leiomyomas** elevate CA-125 significantly during gestation due to increased cell turnover and peritoneal friction from expanding myometrium/myomas themselves causing local inflammation/necrosis (as seen with torsion/degeneration here).
*   Isolated elevations are rarely diagnostic of malignancy in young women (<40s) even less so during pregnancy without suspicious morphological features on imaging (e.g., papillary projections, ascites with nodules).

In this case, while CA-125 was elevated (>35), it was only mildly so relative to known malignant thresholds for older populations ($\ge$35 U/mL has high PPV >50yo but poor PPV <40yo). Combined with normal CT/MRI findings of no lymphadenopathy or ascites with implants, we must interpret this as **"physiological/benign elevation"** rather than immediate malignancy until proven otherwise by histopathology post-delivery/surgery if indicated. Serial monitoring is far more useful than a single snapshot value here; note that her previous value was 36 U/mL two weeks prior—showing stability/rise consistent with growing inflammation rather than explosive metastatic spread often seen in Stage III/IV cancers requiring immediate hysterectomy/oophorectomy logic which isn't feasible mid-gestation yet anyway!

## Surgical Strategy: To Cut or Not To Cut?

This brings us to the crux of management: When do we operate? Conservative management is usually preferred for asymptomatic fibroids because surgery carries risks of preterm labor, hemorrhage, and rupture (especially if entering into decidua). However... **complications demand action**.

# When to Cut? Decision-Making Algorithms for Symptomatic Pelvic Masses in Pregnancy

Let's talk about the "wait and see" dogma in OB/Gyn. We've all heard it: *If it ain't broke, don't fix it.* Especially in pregnancy, where the goal is to keep the fetus alive and the uterus quiet. Most ovarian cysts resolve on their own, most fibroids sit back and watch TV, and we manage them conservatively until delivery.

But what happens when your "watchful waiting" patient wakes up gasping for air because a mass is crushing her diaphragm? Or when a simple cyst turns out to be a twisting, necrotic pedunculated tumor causing sepsis?

Today's case from *De Nola et al.* isn't just about managing a big lump; it's a masterclass in recognizing when the risk of **inaction** outweighs the risk of **intervention**. We're looking at a 40-year-old G1P0 woman at 21 weeks gestation with two massive abdominal masses causing severe pain and restrictive lung disease. It forces us to confront a hard truth: sometimes, doing nothing is actually the dangerous liability.

## The Clinical Dilemma: Compressive Physiology vs. Iatrogenic Risk

This patient presented with a classic "impossible" scenario: severe abdominal pain (VAS 5/10) and dyspnea in the second trimester. On paper, she looked stable—normal O2 sat, normal vitals—but physically, she was compromised. Her abdomen was taut with an 18 cm mass filling the left subphrenic space, pushing against her diaphragm and causing mechanical restriction.

Here is where the algorithm gets messy. The standard teaching is to delay surgery until after delivery unless there is acute torsion or rupture. Why? Because operating on a gravid uterus carries a terrifyingly high risk of preterm labor and hemorrhage. Yet, this patient wasn't just uncomfortable; she was developing a low-grade fever and had signs of inflammation (CRP elevation, leukocytosis). Was it just stress? Or was there ischemia brewing beneath that taut capsule?

The diagnostic workup here highlights a crucial FOEM pearl: **Do not rely solely on tumor markers.**
*   **CA-125:** She had an elevated level (41.8 U/mL). In a non-pregnant adult female >35 years old with an adnexal mass, this screams malignancy until proven otherwise. But in pregnancy? CA-125 rises naturally in the first and third trimesters due to decidualization of peritoneal surfaces. Relying on this alone could lead to unnecessary panic or false reassurance.
*   **Imaging Strategy:** Ultrasound gave us the basics but hit its ceiling with these massive structures. The real game-changer was the **non-contrast MRI**. As noted in the background literature, gadolinium should generally be avoided in pregnancy unless absolutely life-saving (due to potential fetal toxicity). Non-contrast MRI provided superior anatomical definition without crossing that safety line, revealing thickened walls and solid components that suggested cystic degeneration rather than simple functional cysts.

## The Turning Point: When Expectant Management Becomes Negligence

So, why did they cut? If you look strictly at size alone (two giant masses), you might argue for wait-and-see until term to avoid surgical trauma. But look closer at the physiology:
1.  **Mechanical Compromise:** The dyspnea wasn't just "big belly"; it was restrictive lung disease caused by steric bulk preventing diaphragmatic excursion. This is an immediate threat to maternal oxygenation and fetal perfusion if it worsens into respiratory failure.
2.  **Risk of Catastrophe:** The upper mass was found intraoperatively to be pedunculated, twisted, and leaking—likely explaining her fever prior to surgery (ischemic necrosis/inflammation). Waiting for spontaneous resolution of torsion leads to infarction and potential loss of fertility or septic shock once delivered vaginally/c-sectionally later down the road if it ruptures during labor contractions.
3.  **Diagnostic Uncertainty:** While imaging pointed toward leiomyomas with cystic degeneration or decidualized endometriomas, you cannot rule out rare neoplasms entirely without histology when markers are trending up and anatomy looks "atypical."

The decision tree here shifts from "Is it cancer?" to **"Is this going to kill her or hurt the baby if we leave it alone?"** In this case, yes—it was compressing vital organs and showing signs of impending necrosis/torsion evolution.

## Surgical Nuances: The Art of Partial Resection

What makes this case fascinating from an operative standpoint is how they handled the resection itself—a lesson in damage control obstetrics. They didn't remove everything they saw; they removed what threatened immediate harm while minimizing uterine manipulation elsewhere.
*   **Targeted Excision:** They excised only the upper pedunculated mass (the source of torsion/pain) via gentle clamping and suturing of the base.
*   **Strategic Retention:** They deliberately left the caudal pelvic mass (adherent to a posterior intramural myoma) *in situ*. Removing deep-seated pelvic fibroids carries a massive risk of uterine rupture or excessive bleeding during pregnancy leaving little margin for error later during delivery planning anyway (which ended up being an elective C-section due to obstruction risks).
*   **Prophylaxis Matters:** Notice they administered progesterone immediately intra-operatively (IM hydroxyprogesterone caproate). This isn't just protocol; it's targeted neuro-protection against preterm labor triggers caused by surgical irritation of the uterus/peritoneum.

They also managed anemia aggressively post-op with IV iron and transfusions because blood loss can spiral quickly when dissecting vascular fibroid beds in pregnant tissue where hemodynamics are already shifted toward hypercoagulability but hypovolemia tolerance is lower due to anemia baseline issues (her Hb dropped from 9.9 pre-op to 7.5 post-op!).

## Key Take-Home Points for Your Practice

When faced with complex abdominal masses in pregnancy, remember these principles derived from this case:

*   **Indications for Surgery:** Do not operate solely based on size or marker elevation alone unless malignant features are undeniable surgically confirmed or biopsy proves needed urgently (**rare**). Operate for: Acute torsion/necrosis suspected clinically or radiographically; Severe compressive symptoms affecting maternal organ function (e.g., respiratory compromise); Unremitting pain despite maximal medical management; High suspicion of malignancy requiring urgent debulking/immediate diagnosis that cannot wait until post-partum regression allows safer surgery later (*Note: This last point depends heavily on individual pathology*).
*   **Timing Matters:** Second trimester (ideally 14–20 weeks) remains the safest window for non-obstetric surgery if unavoidable—the uterus hasn't become too large yet, but morning sickness has passed and preterm labor risk is slightly lower than late second/third trimester windows compared to early first trimester miscarriage risks—but only if urgency dictates it now versus later!
    *   *Correction:* Actually, literature suggests delaying as long as possible unless emergent; however, this case illustrates that if symptoms are progressive/severe *now*, delaying past 20 weeks might mean worse outcomes due to larger size/mass effect making future dissection harder!
*   **Imaging Protocol:** Start with US + Color Doppler -> Move quickly to Non-Contrast MRI if US is indeterminate or anatomy obscured by gravidity/obesity/size Avoid Gadolinium unless absolutely necessary for life-or-death decisions regarding maternal survival vs fetal loss trade-offs which require extreme caution!
*   **Surgical Philosophy:** Be aggressive enough to solve the problem but conservative enough not to create new ones ("Partial Myomectomy"). Leave benign-feeling masses adherent to critical structures if removal poses undue risk; plan delivery method around remaining pathology (often necessitating C-section regardless). Always monitor FHR continuously intra-op!
*   **Post-Ops Care:** Aggressive anti-inflammatory/progesterone support + Iron/Blood product readiness + Multidisciplinary follow-up every few weeks ensuring fetal growth trajectory matches expectations despite surgical insult!

In conclusion, while expectant management remains the gold standard for asymptomatic masses in pregnancy, clinicians must remain vigilant for signs that biology has turned hostile—whether through mechanical compression turning into respiratory distress or subtle inflammatory cues suggesting impending torsion/ischemia like seen here where early intervention saved both mom's lungs AND allowed for successful delivery at term without catastrophic complications later!



# Surgical Strategy in Gravid Myomectomy: The Art of Partial Resection and Fetal Preservation

Let's talk about the ultimate surgical tightrope walk: operating on a pregnant uterus while trying to keep the fetus inside *and* keeping the mother alive. Usually, when we see a massive, symptomatic abdominal mass in a second-trimester pregnancy, our default setting screams "remove it now." But what happens when removing the whole problem puts the pregnancy at immediate risk of termination?

This case presents a fascinating deviation from standard dogma. We have a 40-year-old primigravida with two gigantic masses causing severe pain and dyspnea. One is high up, pedunculated, twisted, and actively leaking; the other is stuck deep in the pelvis, adherent to the cervix/isthmus. The surgeon didn't play "clean up everything" mode. They performed a **partial resection**. They took out the dangerous one (the cranial mass) and literally walked away from the pelvic one (the caudal mass), leaving it behind to ensure a successful term delivery. It's a masterclass in selective de-escalation.

### The Anatomy of Decision Making: To Cut or Not to Cut?

The crux of this case lies in the differential diagnosis and the specific anatomy of the masses. On paper, these looked like decidualized endometriomas or cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The CA-125 was mildly elevated (41.8 U/mL), which is tricky because pregnancy itself causes fluctuations, but combined with the imaging, it raised flags for potential malignancy or active inflammation. However, once they opened up (laparotomy), reality hit different: both were benign uterine leiomyomas.

The cranial mass was a **pedunculated** tumor that was already twisted and leaking. That is an absolute surgical indication regardless of pregnancy status—torsion leads to necrosis, sepsis, and catastrophic hemorrhage. Leaving it would have been malpractice.

But here is where it gets interesting: why leave the caudal mass? It was 9 cm, adherent to the posterior wall near the uterine torus/isthmus, and described as fixed with a smooth surface. If you try to dissect that out during pregnancy:
1.  You are cutting into a massively hypervascular organ that has grown significantly larger due to hormonal stimulation.
2.  You risk breaching the decidua basalis or creating a defect in the lower uterine segment that could lead to uterine rupture during labor or require an emergent hysterectomy if bleeding cannot be controlled.
3.  The manipulation required could trigger intense prostaglandin release and preterm labor contractions that are impossible to stop mid-stream without losing the baby.

The surgical team made a calculated bet: **Leave it.** They accepted that this residual mass might cause obstruction later (which led them to plan an elective C-section rather than Vaginal Birth After Cesarean or natural labor), but they prioritized preventing immediate catastrophe (hemorrhage/preterm labor) over complete anatomical correction at 21 weeks gestation.

### Managing the Hypervascular Gravid Uterus

Operating on a pregnant uterus is not just operating on muscle; you are dealing with a sponge soaked in blood flow that has increased by up to 50% compared to baseline non-pregnant states. This case highlights several critical technical pearls for managing hemostasis without sacrificing the fetus:

*   **Minimize Dissection Time:** Every minute on top of a gravid uterus increases fetal stress and maternal hemodynamic shifts. The goal isn't perfection; it's safety and speed. In this case, they excised only what was absolutely necessary (the twisted peduncle) rather than attempting enucleation of all fibroids simultaneously.
*   **Suture Technique Matters:** When closing myometrial defects in pregnancy, use absorbable sutures carefully placed to approximate tissue without strangling blood supply excessively but ensuring hemostasis immediately upon release of clamps or tourniquets if used (though tourniquets are controversial in some centers). Here, they used a single absorbable suture for the stump of the pedicle—a minimalist approach that reduced operative time significantly (total time ~2 hours).
*   **Prophylactic Progesterone:** Note that this patient received IM progesterone starting intraoperatively (100 mg daily) followed by Caproate Hydroxyprogesterone every 3 days until delivery. While data on its efficacy varies across studies regarding preterm prevention, many practitioners utilize this "brake" strategy during significant uterine manipulation to dampen contractility induced by surgical irritation or catecholamine surges from anesthesia/surgery stressors.
*   **Antibiotic Stewardship:** With any breach of sterile fields involving large masses and potential leakage (as seen with this patient's fever prior to surgery), broad coverage tailored to local flora (or culture results) is vital to prevent chorioamnionitis or wound infections which could force premature delivery anyway.

### Navigating Delivery Planning Based on Residual Masses

One of the most overlooked aspects of myomectomy during pregnancy is planning for delivery *before* you even make your incision for removal in step one? No wait—you plan for delivery based on what you *leave behind*.

In this scenario, leaving a 9 cm posterior pelvic mass meant that vaginal delivery was likely impossible due to cephalopelvic disproportion caused by the tumor obstructing the birth canal ("steric bulk"). Consequently, despite being able to carry the fetus safely through surgery at 21 weeks, they knew an elective C-section at term was inevitable regardless of how well she healed post-op. By acknowledging this upfront, they avoided false hope about spontaneous labor later on and scheduled their intervention strategically at 37 weeks via repeat C-section scar revision/removal if needed later post-partum—but actually deferred that entirely since she wanted to breastfeed first!

It's worth noting that while some literature suggests waiting until postpartum for myomectomy due to rapid involution (fibroids shrink ~44% within 6 weeks post-delivery), this case argues against blanket conservatism when acute complications arise *now*. However, aggressive debulking *during* C-section carries higher risks than doing so postpartum once vascularity normalizes unless there is torsion/necrosis as seen here with cranial mass #1 which forced hand-on-deck intervention earlier than planned for #2 (#2 stayed put).

# Beyond the Ultrasound: Interpreting Elevated CA-125 in a Pregnant Patient

Let's talk about the classic "don't panic" moment in OB/GYN oncology: the elevated tumor marker. We've all seen the labs come back with a red flag, and our brain immediately flashes red lights for malignancy. But pause. What happens when that patient is 21 weeks pregnant?

This case from *De Nola et al.* presents a fascinating diagnostic dilemma involving a 40-year-old primigravida presenting with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. On paper, she looked terrifying: two massive abdominal masses (one 18 cm!) and a **CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL**. For anyone outside of pregnancy, that number screams ovarian carcinoma or advanced endometriosis. It triggered enough concern to warrant an immediate laparotomy.

However, the twist here isn't just the size of the masses—it's how we interpret that CA-125 in the context of gestation. The final pathology revealed nothing malignant: two giant, degenerating uterine leiomyomas (fibroids), one of which had undergone torsion and ischemic necrosis. The "cancer scare" was actually a mechanical disaster caused by benign tissue reacting violently to pregnancy hormones and spatial constraints.

So, why did the CA-125 spike? And more importantly, how do we know when to operate vs. observe? Let's break down the physiology behind this case and why relying solely on static lab values in pregnancy is a trap.

## The Bimodal Nature of Pregnancy CA-125

We are taught that a CA-125 >35 U/mL is suspicious for epithelial ovarian cancer, particularly in older women. But pregnancy flips this script entirely due to peritoneal irritation and hormonal surges affecting mesothelial cells lining the uterus and peritoneum.

Research indicates that CA-125 follows a distinct **bimodal distribution** during gestation:
*   **First Trimester Peak:** Levels naturally rise, often reaching median values around 23 U/mL due to decidualization and increased peritoneal surface area exposure.
*   **Second Trimester Dip:** Levels typically normalize or drop to their lowest point (medians ~14 U/mL).
*   **Third Trimester Surge:** Levels climb again (medians ~21 U/mL) as the uterus expands and compresses surrounding structures.

In this case report, our patient was at 21 weeks—firmly in that "valley" where levels should be low. Yet, her baseline had already crept up to 36 U/mL two weeks prior, hitting 41.8 U/mL upon admission. While slightly above the normal cutoff for non-pregnant adults, it wasn't astronomically high (like hundreds or thousands). Crucially, the *trend* combined with her clinical picture (fever, acute pain) pointed toward inflammation/necrosis rather than neoplasia. If we had ignored the physiological context and treated her based on a single >35 cutoff alone, we might have missed the nuance of a degenerating fibroid versus an aggressive malignancy until it was too late—or worse, operated unnecessarily if she hadn't been symptomatic enough to justify it yet.

## When Imaging Lies: The Pitfalls of Mass Mimicry

This case serves as a masterclass in how anatomy can deceive us via imaging alone. Both Ultrasound (US) and Non-Contrast MRI initially suggested "decidualized endometriomas." Why? Because they are cystic masses with some solid components and vascularity—a classic appearance for endometriomas in pregnancy due to hormonal stimulation causing decidual change in ectopic endometrial tissue within cysts.

However, intraoperative findings corrected this assumption instantly:
*   The upper mass was a **pedunculated leiomyoma**, twisted and leaking fluid (explaining the fever).
*   The lower mass was an **intramural-subserosal myoma** with cystic degeneration adherent to another myoma.

The key takeaway here is that **degenerating fibroids can mimic complex adnexal masses.** During pregnancy, rapid fibroid growth leads to out-of-proportion blood supply issues, resulting in red degeneration (painful hemorrhagic infarction) or cystic changes that look like tumors on MRI/US sequences dominated by fluid signals. The lack of contrast on MRI (mandatory for safety) can sometimes blur the distinction between avascular necrotic centers of fibroids and true cystic neoplasms without histopathology or surgical exploration clues like vascular pedicles being visible only upon direct inspection or careful Doppler analysis for feeding vessels from the uterus itself rather than ovarian arteries alone.

## Surgical Decision Making: To Cut or Not to Cut?

The most critical part of this discussion is not just diagnosing the mass type, but deciding *when* to intervene surgically during pregnancy. Our patient underwent laparotomy because she had **complicated symptoms**: refractory pain (VAS 5->8 despite analgesia), dyspnea from steric bulk/compression of the diaphragm, fever suggesting infection/necrosis/torsion risk, and worsening anemia requiring transfusion support later on.

Isolated asymptomatic fibroids generally do not require removal during pregnancy due to high risks of bleeding and preterm labor induction. However, this case highlights specific indications where intervention becomes mandatory despite gestational age:
*   Acute torsion or suspected torsion (as seen with the twisted pedunculated upper mass).
*   Severe mechanical compromise leading to respiratory distress or inability to tolerate oral intake/fluids due to pain/crowding effects ("steric bulk").
*   High suspicion of malignancy that cannot be ruled out by serial monitoring (though rare).
*   Risk of rupture or secondary infection spreading into the uterine cavity/placental bed.

Notably, in this scenario, surgical conservatism was applied even within surgery itself—the team removed only one mass because it was clearly problematic (twisted/pedunculated) while leaving the other adherent mass intact to minimize trauma to the uterus and prevent preterm delivery triggers once stable hemodynamics were restored post-op with progesterone coverage. This strategy resulted in an excellent outcome at term via scheduled C-sections specifically timed because of pelvic obstruction concerns later on!

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

When evaluating abdominal masses in pregnant patients with elevated tumor markers:
*   **Contextualize CA-125:** Remember it rises physiologically; don't panic at >35 U/mL unless there is a dramatic upward trajectory (>double digits) accompanied by frank ascites or nodular implants suggestive of metastasis instead simple inflammation/degeneration trends observed over time (~slight increase noted here vs expected dip).
