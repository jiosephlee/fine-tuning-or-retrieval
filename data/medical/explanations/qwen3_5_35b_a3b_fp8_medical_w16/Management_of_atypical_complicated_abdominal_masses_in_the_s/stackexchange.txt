Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] Which specific imaging modality is recommended for evaluating complex pelvic masses in pregnancy when ultrasound findings are inconclusive, and what critical safety precaution regarding contrast agents must be observed?

When ultrasound findings are inconclusive for complex pelvic masses in pregnancy, **non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)** is the recommended second-line diagnostic modality. As illustrated in this case, where ultrasound suggested decidualized endometriomas or cystic degeneration of leiomyomas but failed to definitively characterize the extent of the masses or their relationship to the uterine cavity, non-contrast MRI provided superior soft-tissue resolution. It allowed for precise differentiation between benign entities—such as the pedunculated, twisted leiomyoma confirmed surgically—and malignancy, guiding the decision for conservative versus operative management without exposing the fetus to ionizing radiation.

The critical safety precaution regarding contrast agents is that **gadolinium-based contrast media should generally be avoided** during pregnancy unless absolutely necessary for life-threatening situations where the benefit clearly outweighs the risk. Gadolinium crosses the placental barrier and may accumulate in fetal tissues; animal studies have raised concerns regarding potential teratogenicity and risks of stillbirth or neonatal death. Consequently, as emphasized in this case's management, a high-quality non-contrast MRI protocol utilizing T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and fat-suppressed sequences is sufficient to evaluate tumor characteristics, vascularity via flow voids, and anatomical relationships while ensuring fetal safety. If contrast were deemed indispensable by a multidisciplinary team, it would typically be restricted to a 1.5-T scanner following extensive counseling and informed consent.

### [Pathophysiology] How do physiological changes during pregnancy affect the interpretation of serum CA-125 levels, and why might this marker show an upward trend in a patient with benign uterine leiomyomas rather than malignancy?

**Clinical Pearl:** During pregnancy, serum CA-125 levels undergo distinct physiological fluctuations, rising in the first and third trimesters before normalizing in the second. Consequently, an isolated or mildly elevated CA-125 level in a pregnant patient—such as the 41.8 U/mL seen in this case—is frequently a false-positive indicator for malignancy when interpreted against non-pregnant reference ranges (<35 U/mL).

**Pathophysiological Reasoning:**
CA-125 is a glycoprotein expressed by coelomic epithelium, including the peritoneum, pleura, and pericardium. Its elevation is driven by mesothelial irritation or inflammation rather than tumor burden alone. In pregnancy, physiological changes such as uterine distension, increased vascularity, and hormonal shifts stimulate the peritoneal lining, leading to predictable peaks in CA-125 during early gestation (due to implantation and trophoblastic activity) and late gestation (due to mechanical stress). As noted in the discussion of this case report, the median CA-125 rises to approximately 23 U/mL in the first trimester and 21 U/mL in the third, compared to a baseline of ~14 U/mL in the second.

Furthermore, benign uterine pathology specifically drives these elevations. Leiomyomas (fibroids) can induce local inflammation, hemorrhage into the tumor (cystic degeneration), or ischemia via torsion of pedunculated masses. These processes cause serosal irritation and release of CA-125 from the adjacent peritoneum. In this specific patient, the "increasing trend" from 36 to 41.8 U/mL correlated with a large, pedunculated leiomyoma that was twisting and undergoing necrotic degeneration—a classic benign mimic of malignancy that explains the marker's rise without implying cancer.

**Management Implication:**
This case underscores that CA-125 lacks specificity in pregnancy; relying on it alone can lead to unnecessary anxiety or aggressive intervention. Diagnosis must instead rely on multimodal imaging (ultrasound with color Doppler and non-contrast MRI) to assess mass characteristics (e.g., cystic vs. solid components, attachment to uterine tissue) and clinical context (pain, rapid growth). While serial monitoring is useful for tracking trends, a mild elevation should be viewed through the lens of benign etiologies like fibroid degeneration or endometriosis until proven otherwise by histopathology or advanced imaging findings suggestive of malignancy.

### [Management] Given the presence of two distinct masses—one pedunculated and one adherent to the uterine wall—what specific intraoperative factors determined the decision to resect only the upper mass while leaving the caudal mass in situ?

**Clinical Pearl:** In the management of complex abdominal masses during pregnancy, the decision to perform a limited resection versus a complete debulking hinges on the **anatomical attachment and vascularity of the mass**, balanced against the imperative to minimize uterine manipulation and prevent iatrogenic preterm labor.

In this case, the operative strategy was dictated by the distinct morphological characteristics of the two masses identified intraoperatively. The upper mass was identified as a **pedunculated** leiomyoma. Pedunculated fibroids carry a high intrinsic risk for acute complications such as torsion, ischemia, and rupture, which were already manifesting in this patient via pain and fever. Because the tumor was attached via a discrete stalk, it could be safely detached from the uterine serosa with minimal disruption to the underlying myometrium, allowing for rapid removal of the source of acute symptoms without compromising uterine integrity.

Conversely, the caudal mass was found to be **adherent** directly to the posterior uterine wall (intramural-subserosal) with no clear plane of separation ("cleavage") visible on preoperative MRI or intraoperative inspection. Attempting to dissect this mass would have required extensive dissection into the deep myometrium, significantly increasing the risk of catastrophic hemorrhage and creating a large defect that predisposes to uterine rupture during subsequent contractions or delivery. Given that removing an adherent mass offered no immediate life-saving benefit compared to the high risk of precipitating preterm labor or fetal loss, the surgical team elected to leave it *in situ*. This "damage control" approach prioritized fetal preservation over complete oncologic clearance when malignancy was unlikely (supported by normal tumor markers aside from physiologically elevated CA-125) and allowed for a planned cesarean delivery once term was reached, where both masses could potentially be addressed simultaneously if necessary.

**Take-home message:** When managing symptomatic masses in pregnancy, prioritize resection only for pedunculated lesions posing an imminent threat (torsion/rupture). Avoid aggressive dissection of broad-based or deeply adherent masses where surgical trauma outweighs immediate clinical benefit; instead, opt for expectant management with close surveillance and plan for delivery planning based on residual anatomy.

### [Morning Report] What are the primary indications for performing surgical intervention on uterine leiomyomas discovered during the second trimester of pregnancy, distinguishing them from cases managed conservatively?

**Clinical Pearl:** While the default management for uterine leiomyomas discovered during pregnancy is conservative, given the significant risks of hemorrhage, preterm labor, and uterine rupture associated with myomectomy, surgical intervention is reserved for specific high-risk scenarios. Based on the case of Rosalba De Nola and colleagues, the primary indications for operative intervention in the second trimester include **acute complications** (such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma, impending rupture, or infection/necrosis) and **severe symptomatology** refractory to medical management that compromises maternal physiology (e.g., restrictive lung disease or intractable pain).

In this case, the decision to proceed with laparotomy at 21 weeks was driven by the presence of a large, pedunculated mass exhibiting signs of ischemic necrosis (fever, rising inflammatory markers) and acute torsion ("twisted and leaking"), alongside severe dyspnea caused by mechanical compression. The surgical team prioritized removing only the symptomatic upper mass while leaving the asymptomatic pelvic mass *in situ* to minimize obstetric risk—a strategy underscoring that surgery should be limited to what is absolutely necessary to resolve immediate threats.

Conversely, conservative management remains appropriate when masses are stable, asymptomatic, or cause only mild discomfort manageable with analgesics and positional changes. As noted in the discussion of this case, myomas often undergo spontaneous involution postpartum; therefore, unless there is an oncological suspicion (which was ruled out here via non-contrast MRI and tumor markers) or a life-threatening complication requiring urgent decompression or source control, expectant management allows for fetal maturation and avoids unnecessary iatrogenic harm. Ultimately, the timing and extent of surgery must be guided by a multidisciplinary assessment balancing the severity of maternal symptoms against the potential for fetal loss.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the characteristic temporal pattern of uterine leiomyoma size changes throughout gestation and the immediate postpartum period, and explain how this knowledge influences timing of surgical planning.

**Clinical Pearl:** Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a distinct, non-linear trajectory of growth during pregnancy, characterized by rapid expansion in the first and early second trimesters followed by stabilization or modest growth in the late second and third trimesters. Crucially, these masses typically undergo significant involution immediately following delivery.

Pathophysiologically, this growth pattern is driven primarily by high circulating estrogen and progesterone levels, which stimulate cellular proliferation and edema within the fibroid stroma. As noted in the case report, studies indicate that myomas grow approximately **6.12% weekly between the first and second trimesters**, slowing to roughly **3.10% weekly between the second and third trimesters**. This acceleration correlates with the peak hormonal surge of early gestation. Conversely, the postpartum period marks a dramatic shift; due to the abrupt withdrawal of steroid hormones, myomas often regress significantly—averaging a **44% reduction in size by six weeks postpartum**.

This biological reality fundamentally dictates surgical strategy. Because spontaneous regression is so robust after delivery, immediate resection of asymptomatic or stable myomas discovered during pregnancy is generally discouraged due to the heightened risks of hemorrhage, uterine rupture, and preterm labor associated with operating on a highly vascularized organ. Instead, as illustrated by our patient's successful management where one mass was left *in situ*, a "wait-and-see" approach is preferred unless there are life-threatening complications (such as torsion or severe compression) that cannot be managed conservatively.

Therefore, when planning definitive surgical intervention for large or problematic leiomyomas found during pregnancy, clinicians should ideally defer myomectomy until the **postpartum period**. Waiting allows for natural involution to reduce tumor burden and vascularity, potentially converting a complex surgical scenario into a much safer one. If surgery becomes unavoidable during gestation—as it did in this case due to acute torsion and respiratory compromise—it should be performed with extreme caution, prioritizing fetal safety and minimizing uterine manipulation, recognizing that future operations may become unnecessary once the postpartum regression occurs.

### [Management] Why was an elective cesarean delivery chosen over a trial of labor for this patient despite successful removal of one mass, and what anatomical considerations dictated this mode of delivery?

**Clinical Pearl:** In this case, the decision to proceed with an elective cesarean delivery rather than a trial of labor was dictated by the **size, location, and fixed nature of the residual pelvic leiomyoma**, which posed a mechanical obstruction to fetal descent.

While the upper mass was successfully resected via laparotomy because it was pedunculated and causing acute symptoms (torsion/necrosis), the lower mass was intentionally left *in situ* to minimize operative trauma and the risk of preterm labor. As noted in the case report, this residual mass was described as "fixed" and arising from the uterine torus. Its dimensions (approximately 9 cm) and posterior intramural-subserosal location created a significant "steric bulk" in the pelvic inlet. During a vaginal delivery, this fixed mass would act as an absolute barrier to the passage of the fetal head into the pelvis, leading to inevitable obstructed labor.

Furthermore, attempting a trial of labor in the setting of prior myomectomy involving deep uterine incisions carries a risk of uterine dehiscence or rupture, particularly when combined with a large residual fibroid distorting the lower uterine segment. The authors explicitly state that "the caudal myoma would have represented a huge obstacle for the fetal head progression during the expulsive phase." Consequently, scheduling an elective cesarean delivery at term allowed for controlled extraction while minimizing the risks associated with prolonged obstructed labor and potential uterine rupture at the site of previous surgical intervention. This aligns with general principles where mode of delivery is determined by tumor size, number, and anatomical relationship to the birth canal; when tumors occupy space critical for engagement or descent, cesarean delivery becomes mandatory regardless of prior successful tumor debulking elsewhere in the abdomen.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a pregnant patient presenting with acute abdominal pain and fever alongside large pelvic masses, how does one differentiate between torsion/necrosis of a leiomyoma versus infectious etiologies like pyelonephritis or appendicitis based on clinical presentation and imaging?

**Clinical Pearl:** Differentiating between torsed/necrotic leiomyomas and infectious etiologies in pregnancy relies on integrating **imaging characteristics** regarding vascularity and structural integrity with **clinical context**, specifically the timing of symptom onset relative to mass size.

In the case presented, the differentiation hinged on the distinction between a systemic infectious process and localized ischemic necrosis. While the patient developed a *Klebsiella* urinary tract infection and a low-grade fever, these findings alone did not explain the severity of her acute abdominal pain or the specific nature of the masses. Crucially, imaging revealed that the upper mass was a **pedunculated, highly vascularized structure** that was found intraoperatively to be **twisted**. The fever in this scenario was driven by **myometrial necrosis** secondary to torsion rather than primary sepsis from an abscess or pyelonephritis.

To distinguish these entities clinically and radiographically:
1.  **Imaging Features:** Ultrasound and non-contrast MRI are pivotal. In this case, they identified "cystic degeneration" and heterogeneous signal intensity within a solid mass, suggestive of hemorrhage or infarction rather than simple pus collection. The identification of a **pedicle** (even if obscured by steric bulk) is highly suspicious for torsion. Conversely, appendicitis typically presents with a blind-ending tubular structure with surrounding fat stranding, while pyelonephritis manifests as renal enlargement with striated nephrogram patterns on CT (though avoided here) or hydronephrosis on ultrasound without discrete pelvic masses causing mechanical obstruction unless concurrent pathology exists.
2.  **Physical Exam Dynamics:** The patient's pain was disproportionate to vital sign instability initially (fever was low-grade despite severe pain), which aligns with ischemic pain from torsion more than early-stage infection where tachycardia and high fevers often precede maximal tenderness. The "taut-elastic" consistency noted on palpation supports a tense, ischemic fibroid rather than a fluctuant abscess.
3.  **Laboratory Trends:** While leukocytosis occurred, it must be interpreted cautiously in pregnancy as physiologic leukocytosis is common (WBC >10–12k). The key differentiator here was that the inflammatory markers (CRP) rose concurrently with the development of symptoms directly linked to the mass mechanics, and crucially, peritoneal fluid cultures were negative despite the fever, ruling out generalized peritonitis or ruptured infected cysts as the primary driver at that moment.

Therefore, when faced with large pelvic masses in pregnancy accompanied by fever and acute pain, one must prioritize ruling out **torsion-induced necrosis**. If imaging demonstrates a pedunculated mass with signs of compromised blood flow (heterogeneity, lack of flow on Doppler) and clinical deterioration persists despite treating concurrent infections (like UTI), surgical exploration is indicated to prevent uterine rupture or fetal compromise from prolonged maternal stress. As seen in this case, addressing the mechanical cause (excision of the twisted myoma) resolved the source of inflammation and allowed for successful continuation of pregnancy.
