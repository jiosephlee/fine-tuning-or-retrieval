Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Surgical Strategy in Gravid Myomectomy: The Art of Partial Resection and Fetal Preservation

Let's talk about the ultimate surgical tightrope walk: operating on a pregnant uterus while trying to keep the fetus inside *and* keeping the mother alive. Usually, when we see a massive, symptomatic abdominal mass in a second-trimester pregnancy, our default setting screams "remove it now." But what happens when removing the whole problem puts the pregnancy at immediate risk of termination?

This case presents a fascinating deviation from standard dogma. We have a 40-year-old primigravida with two gigantic masses causing severe pain and dyspnea. One is high up, pedunculated, twisted, and actively leaking; the other is stuck deep in the pelvis, adherent to the cervix/isthmus. The surgeon didn't play "clean up everything" mode. They performed a **partial resection**. They took out the dangerous one (the cranial mass) and literally walked away from the pelvic one (the caudal mass), leaving it behind to ensure a successful term delivery. It's a masterclass in selective de-escalation.

### The Anatomy of Decision Making: To Cut or Not to Cut?

The crux of this case lies in the differential diagnosis and the specific anatomy of the masses. On paper, these looked like decidualized endometriomas or cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The CA-125 was mildly elevated (41.8 U/mL), which is tricky because pregnancy itself causes fluctuations, but combined with the imaging, it raised flags for potential malignancy or active inflammation. However, once they opened up (laparotomy), reality hit different: both were benign uterine leiomyomas.

The cranial mass was a **pedunculated** tumor that was already twisted and leaking. That is an absolute surgical indication regardless of pregnancy status—torsion leads to necrosis, sepsis, and catastrophic hemorrhage. Leaving it would have been malpractice.

But here is where it gets interesting: why leave the caudal mass? It was 9 cm, adherent to the posterior wall near the uterine torus/isthmus, and described as fixed with a smooth surface. If you try to dissect that out during pregnancy:
1.  You are cutting into a massively hypervascular organ that has grown significantly larger due to hormonal stimulation.
2.  You risk breaching the decidua basalis or creating a defect in the lower uterine segment that could lead to uterine rupture during labor or require an emergent hysterectomy if bleeding cannot be controlled.
3.  The manipulation required could trigger intense prostaglandin release and preterm labor contractions that are impossible to stop mid-stream without losing the baby.

The surgical team made a calculated bet: **Leave it.** They accepted that this residual mass might cause obstruction later (which led them to plan an elective C-section rather than Vaginal Birth After Cesarean or natural labor), but they prioritized preventing immediate catastrophe (hemorrhage/preterm labor) over complete anatomical correction at 21 weeks gestation.

### Managing the Hypervascular Gravid Uterus

Operating on a pregnant uterus is not just operating on muscle; you are dealing with a sponge soaked in blood flow that has increased by up to 50% compared to baseline non-pregnant states. This case highlights several critical technical pearls for managing hemostasis without sacrificing the fetus:

*   **Minimize Dissection Time:** Every minute on top of a gravid uterus increases fetal stress and maternal hemodynamic shifts. The goal isn't perfection; it's safety and speed. In this case, they excised only what was absolutely necessary (the twisted peduncle) rather than attempting enucleation of all fibroids simultaneously.
*   **Suture Technique Matters:** When closing myometrial defects in pregnancy, use absorbable sutures carefully placed to approximate tissue without strangling blood supply excessively but ensuring hemostasis immediately upon release of clamps or tourniquets if used (though tourniquets are controversial in some centers). Here, they used a single absorbable suture for the stump of the pedicle—a minimalist approach that reduced operative time significantly (total time ~2 hours).
*   **Prophylactic Progesterone:** Note that this patient received IM progesterone starting intraoperatively (100 mg daily) followed by Caproate Hydroxyprogesterone every 3 days until delivery. While data on its efficacy varies across studies regarding preterm prevention, many practitioners utilize this "brake" strategy during significant uterine manipulation to dampen contractility induced by surgical irritation or catecholamine surges from anesthesia/surgery stressors.
*   **Antibiotic Stewardship:** With any breach of sterile fields involving large masses and potential leakage (as seen with this patient's fever prior to surgery), broad coverage tailored to local flora (or culture results) is vital to prevent chorioamnionitis or wound infections which could force premature delivery anyway.

### Navigating Delivery Planning Based on Residual Masses

One of the most overlooked aspects of myomectomy during pregnancy is planning for delivery *before* you even make your incision for removal in step one? No wait—you plan for delivery based on what you *leave behind*.

In this scenario, leaving a 9 cm posterior pelvic mass meant that vaginal delivery was likely impossible due to cephalopelvic disproportion caused by the tumor obstructing the birth canal ("steric bulk"). Consequently, despite being able to carry the fetus safely through surgery at 21 weeks, they knew an elective C-section at term was inevitable regardless of how well she healed post-op. By acknowledging this upfront, they avoided false hope about spontaneous labor later on and scheduled their intervention strategically at 37 weeks via repeat C-section scar revision/removal if needed later post-partum—but actually deferred that entirely since she wanted to breastfeed first!

It's worth noting that while some literature suggests waiting until postpartum for myomectomy due to rapid involution (fibroids shrink ~44% within 6 weeks post-delivery), this case argues against blanket conservatism when acute complications arise *now*. However, aggressive debulking *during* C-section carries higher risks than doing so postpartum once vascularity normalizes unless there is torsion/necrosis as seen here with cranial mass #1 which forced hand-on-deck intervention earlier than planned for #2 (#2 stayed put).

### Take-Home Points & Pearls

When facing complex abdominal/pelvic masses in pregnancy requiring surgical intervention:

*   **Prioritize Life-Saving Over Curative:** If one mass poses an imminent threat (twisting/bleeding/infection) while another does not yet threaten viability but complicates future logistics, consider staged management or partial resection only targeting the acute pathology first stage wise before considering full resolution laterally after birth depending on size reduction patterns observed clinically/imaging-wise thereafter...
