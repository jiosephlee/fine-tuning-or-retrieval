Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# The Art of Selective Resection: Leaving Masses Behind to Save a Fetus

We often operate on the principle that "if it's big, painful, and suspicious, we get it out." But in the high-stakes environment of pregnancy, particularly when dealing with massive abdominal pathology in the second trimester, the most aggressive intervention isn't always the safest. Today's case is a masterclass in surgical triage and restraint. It forces us to confront a difficult reality: sometimes, the best way to save a life—both maternal and fetal—is to leave a tumor alone.

This 40-year-old primigravida presented at 21 weeks with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. On paper, she looked like a textbook candidate for immediate exploration. She had two massive lesions: an 18 cm mass filling her left subphrenic space down to the uterine fundus, and a 9 cm mass in the pouch of Douglas causing pelvic pressure. The clinical picture was alarming: acute pain (VAS 5), restricted breathing due to mechanical compression, and a fever suggesting potential tissue compromise. Initial imaging painted a confusing picture of what might be decidualized endometriomas or cystic degenerating leiomyomas, with a mildly elevated CA-125 adding to the anxiety of malignancy versus benign pathology.

The real teaching moment here lies not in the diagnosis—which turned out to be benign pedunculated leiomyomas—but in the surgical strategy. Why did the team perform an upper myomectomy but leave the pelvic mass *in situ*? And why did they opt for a laparotomy rather than attempting laparoscopy despite their expertise?

### The Dilemma of "Complete" Excision

In non-obstetric surgery, complete resection is the gold standard. You remove everything involved to prevent recurrence or metastasis. In pregnancy, however, we are balancing oncologic principles against obstetric survival. Uterine manipulation carries inherent risks: hemorrhage leading to hysterectomy or shock, disruption of placental perfusion leading to fetal demise, and induction of preterm labor via prostaglandin release from myometrial irritation.

This patient had two distinct problems masquerading as one complex entity.
1.  **The Upper Mass:** An 18 cm pedunculated leiomyoma that was likely twisted (torsion) and leaking, explaining her fever and acute pain. This was an active threat requiring immediate decompression.
2.  **The Lower Mass:** A fixed, adherent mass connected to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. While large (9 cm), it was stable and posed no immediate risk of torsion or rupture at that moment.

If they had attempted to remove both masses simultaneously, they would have needed extensive dissection around the posterior uterine wall—the very site where future labor contractions generate forceful shear stress on any scar tissue created during surgery. By leaving the caudal mass alone, they avoided creating a second major incision into the uterine corpus near the cervix/isthmus junction. They accepted that leaving a 9 cm mass behind was safer than risking uterine rupture or catastrophic hemorrhage from trying to peel it off during pregnancy.

### Navigating Diagnostic Uncertainty Without Gadolinium

Before cutting, you must know what you are cutting (or choosing not to cut). This case highlights the utility of non-contrast MRI in pregnancy when ultrasound findings are equivocal regarding malignancy vs. benign degeneration. While CA-125 can be elevated in benign conditions like endometriosis or even normal pregnancy (peaking in T1/T3), this patient's rising trend combined with solid/cystic components warranted closer look without exposing the fetus to gadolinium contrast agents known to cross the placenta with unknown long-term effects on fetal development (specifically nephrogenic systemic fibrosis concerns).

The MRI confirmed thick walls and heterogeneous content but crucially failed to show lymphadenopathy or clear malignant invasion patterns. This allowed the team to pivot from "cancer workup" mode back to "obstetric safety" mode. They recognized that while CA-125 was >35 U/mL (the traditional cutoff for concern), it was only marginally so (41.8 U/mL) in a context where physiologic elevation is common enough that serial trends matter more than single values.

### The Decision Matrix: Laparotomy vs. Laparoscopy & Timing

Why laparotomy? Usually, we push for minimally invasive techniques whenever possible in pregnancy due to faster recovery and less adhesions formation (which reduces future C-section risks). However, this case presents a classic indication for conversion or primary laparotomy:
*   **Mass Size:** An 18 cm mass occupies almost half her abdominal cavity; visualization ports would be insufficient for safe dissection without blind injury risk.
*   **Adhesions/Location:** The lower mass was adherent to posterior structures; blindly dissecting this laparoscopically could damage bowel or ureters without tactile feedback from hands-on palpation found in open surgery ("hands-on" approach).
*   **Vascularity:** The upper mass was described as "highly vascularized." Controlling bleeding from such a large pedicle requires rapid clamping and suturing capabilities best achieved via direct vision in an open field before resorting to energy devices that might cause thermal spread near delicate fetal membranes if perforated inadvertently by instruments reaching too far back into retroperitoneal spaces (though rare).

Furthermore, note the timing: 21 weeks + 6 days is late enough that spontaneous abortion is less likely than earlier gestations but early enough that preterm labor remains a constant fear if uterine irritability occurs post-op. The choice of midline vertical incision allowed access without disturbing lateral ligaments excessively but required careful closure strategies later on for delivery planning (hence why she got an elective C-section anyway—the residual pelvic mass blocked descent).

### Outcomes Speak Louder Than Theory

The results speak volumes about selective resection being superior here:
*   **Maternal Outcome:** Despite losing significant blood (requiring transfusions) due to anemia exacerbation from surgery itself (iron deficiency baseline Hb ~9 g/dL -> post-op drop), she recovered well with progesterone prophylaxis preventing contractions until term-ish delivery plans were made clear (~37 weeks).
*   **Fetal Outcome:** Excellent growth trajectory maintained throughout follow-up; no signs of distress; successful C-section at term with minimal complications related solely because they didn't touch *everything*.
*   **Post-partum Reality Check:** Even now months later, she declined further surgery because her remaining myoma shrank naturally! Pregnancy hormones drive growth; postpartum involution drives shrinkage (approx 44% reduction observed in literature). Had they operated aggressively on *all* masses now instead of waiting? Likely unnecessary interventions later once physiology normalized them anyway!

### Key Takeaways for Your Practice

When facing large abdominal/pelvic masses during pregnancy:

*   **"Do No Harm" overrides "Get It All":** If removing one part causes unacceptable risk elsewhere (e.g., posterior wall scarring blocking future vaginal delivery), consider staged management or conservative observation for asymptomatic components—even if they look scary radiographically initially!
    *   *Pearl:* Resect only what poses immediate danger (torsion risk/bleeding source/severe symptoms); leave stable masses alone until postpartum regression allows safer excision if needed later!
*   **Imaging Matters More Than Markers Alone:** Rely heavily on US/MRI characteristics over isolated CA-125 spikes unless clearly malignant features exist; remember physiologic elevations occur regularly! Always prioritize non-gadolinium MRI sequences when uncertainty persists!
*   **Surgical Approach Must Be Tailored To Gestational Age & Anatomy:** Large/multiple/deep-seated lesions may necessitate laparotomy despite preference for MIS techniques—don't let ego override anatomy constraints! Ensure continuous fetal monitoring intraoperatively regardless of approach chosen!
*   **Expectant Management Can Win Wars Too:** Many myomas regress spontaneously after delivery; plan accordingly rather than rushing into definitive repairs prematurely unless absolutely indicated by acute catastrophe!
*   **Multidisciplinary Teams Are Non-Negotiable:** Obstetrics + Gyn Oncology + Radiology input ensures balanced decisions weighing maternal morbidity against fetal viability—not just treating numbers on lab reports but understanding whole-body dynamics unique unto each patient population involved herein today...and tomorrow too hopefully forevermore!! 🩺👶✨
