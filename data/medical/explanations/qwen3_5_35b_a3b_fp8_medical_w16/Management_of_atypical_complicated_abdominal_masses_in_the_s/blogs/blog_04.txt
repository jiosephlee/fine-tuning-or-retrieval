Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Imaging Without Gadolinium: Decoding Complex Abdominal Masses in Pregnant Patients

Imagine a 40-year-old, G1P0 woman at 21 weeks gestation walking into your ED. She's obese (BMI 33), complaining of severe abdominal pain in the LUQ and pelvis, accompanied by dyspnea. On palpation, you feel a taut, elastic mass in the upper quadrant and a fixed, hard mass in the pelvis. Your differential diagnosis is immediately broad: ovarian malignancy? Ruptured ectopic? Or perhaps something entirely different given the gestational context?

This case highlights a classic "perfect storm" in OB/GYN emergencies: advanced maternal age, obesity obscuring ultrasound windows, and complex pathology masquerading as simple cysts. The patient presents with what looks like two giant decidualized endometriomas based on initial ultrasound, yet the clinical picture—specifically the fever and worsening pain—suggests something more sinister or at least mechanically compromised. How do you distinguish between a benign degenerating fibroid and a malignant process when CT is off-limits and ultrasound hits its ceiling? The answer lies in the nuanced application of **non-contrast MRI**.

### The Radiologic Dilemma: When Ultrasound Isn't Enough

In pregnancy, our imaging toolkit is severely restricted. We cannot reach for CT due to ionizing radiation risks to the fetus, and while ultrasound is our first line, it often fails when faced with high BMI or deep-seated masses obscured by the gravid uterus. In this specific case, the patient's obesity and the sheer size of the masses (one filling the subphrenic space!) rendered ultrasound equivocal. It showed cystic masses with peripheral vascularity but couldn't definitively characterize the internal architecture or rule out malignancy versus degeneration.

Enter MRI. But here is where many clinicians hesitate: *Do we use gadolinium?* Absolutely not. Gadolinium crosses the placenta freely and has been associated with adverse fetal outcomes (including stillbirth and neonatal death) in animal models and observational data. Current guidelines strongly recommend against gadolinium enhancement unless absolutely life-saving for the mother (which was not the case here). Instead, we rely on **non-contrast sequences**, specifically T1-weighted fat-saturated images and T2-weighted sequences. These sequences are powerful enough to differentiate tissue types based on water content, fat content, and hemorrhage without exposing the fetus to potential toxins.

### Decoding the Signal: What Did We See?

In this case report, the non-contrast MRI provided the clarity needed to make a surgical decision without compromising fetal safety. Let's break down what those signals meant in plain English:

*   **T1 Fat-Sat & T2 Findings:** The MRI revealed thickened walls (1.4 cm) and heterogeneous fluid content. Crucially, there were focal hyperintense signals on both T1 and T2 fat-sat images representing solid components.
*   **The Differential:** Initially, these features mimicked decidualized endometriomas or even malignancy due to their size and complexity. However, the lack of clear invasion into surrounding structures (no adenopathy) and specific signal characteristics pointed away from carcinoma.
*   **The "Gotcha":** The most telling feature was the continuity between the caudal mass and a known posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma (fibroid). While ultrasound suggested they might be separate entities (adnexal vs. uterine), MRI clarified that they were connected, suggesting a degenerating fibroid rather than an independent ovarian tumor.

When we finally opened her abdomen during laparotomy, reality matched radiology perfectly: both masses were actually pedunculated uterine leiomyomas undergoing cystic degeneration and ischemic necrosis (torsion). The fever she developed wasn't sepsis from an ovarian abscess; it was systemic inflammation from necrotic tissue release! This underscores why distinguishing between an infected ovarian cyst/necrotic fibroid is vital for management strategy.

### Managing Masses >35 Years Old: A Practical Approach

Why did this patient get surgery at 21 weeks instead of waiting until postpartum? Because she had "complicated" masses causing mechanical compromise (restrictive lung disease/dyspnea) with signs of impending catastrophe (fever/pain). For asymptomatic fibroids found incidentally in pregnancy, observation is king ("wait-and-see"). But once you hit red flags—twisting pedicles (torsion), rapid expansion causing organ compression, or uncontrolled pain—the risk-benefit analysis shifts toward intervention.

However, surgery in pregnancy carries inherent risks: preterm labor, hemorrhage, and fetal loss. In this case study, despite leaving one mass behind to minimize trauma to the uterus (a strategic choice), they still required an elective Cesarean section later because that remaining pelvic mass was too large to allow for vaginal delivery mechanics at term. This reinforces that surgical decisions aren't just about saving mom today; they dictate how she delivers tomorrow.

### Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

To navigate similar cases in your practice without panicking about radiation or toxic contrast agents:

*   **Non-Contrast MRI is Safe & Superior:** Do not let fear of missing a diagnosis drive you toward CT or gadolinium-enhanced MRI in pregnancy when non-contrast protocols exist. They offer superior soft-tissue resolution for characterizing complex pelvic/abdominal masses without fetal risk after 1st trimester (>14 weeks).
*   **Interpret Signal Characteristics Carefully:** Look for T1 hyperintensity indicating hemorrhage/proteinaceous fluid within cysts (common in degenerating fibroids) versus homogeneous low signal typical of simple serous cysts. Continuity with uterine tissue on MRI helps reclassify "ovarian" masses as pedunculated fibroids early on.
*   **CA-125 Context Matters:** Remember that CA-125 rises physiologically during pregnancy peaks (especially late 3rd trimester), but can remain mildly elevated mid-pregnancy due to benign processes like endometriosis or myomas alone; do not panic solely on an elevated marker if imaging suggests benignity or if trends are slow-rising rather than explosive spikes typical of aggressive malignancy (<35 U/mL is normal cutoff but pregnancy alters baselines).
*   **Surgical Strategy Must Be Conservative Yet Decisive:** If surgery is unavoidable:
    *   Aim for minimal dissection time to reduce preterm labor triggers.
    *   Consider leaving inaccessible/deeply embedded tumors if removal threatens uterine integrity significantly more than leaving them alone temporarily (as done with her caudal mass).
    *   Always monitor fetal heart rate continuously before/during/post-op procedures per ACOG/SMFM guidelines regarding fetal monitoring during maternal surgery beyond viability thresholds (~24 weeks).
