Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Pediatric vs. Obstetric Dilemma: When to Operate on Symptomatic Leiomyomas in Pregnancy

We often talk about "watchful waiting" in OB/GYN emergencies, but let's be honest: sometimes the belly doesn't want to wait. We've all seen the classic textbook scenario where a pregnant patient presents with abdominal pain, we grab an ultrasound, see a mass, and immediately think "ovarian cyst." But what happens when that "cyst" turns out to be a massive, pedunculated leiomyoma that has twisted on itself? And worse, what happens when leaving it alone threatens both the mother's lungs and the pregnancy itself?

This case report from *De Nola et al.* serves as a stark reminder that while expectant management is the gold standard for asymptomatic fibroids, the line between "safe observation" and "immediate threat" can blur quickly when mechanical complications arise. This wasn't just a big fibroid; it was a ticking time bomb causing severe dyspnea and acute pain in a 40-year-old primigravida at 21 weeks gestation. The twist? The upper mass looked like an ovarian tumor on imaging, but turned out to be a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma that was actively twisting and leaking.

The clinical picture here is deceptively complex. A 40-year-old woman presents with severe left upper quadrant pain and dyspnea. On exam, she has two distinct masses: one massive (18 cm) filling the subphrenic space and another (9 cm) in the pelvis. The differential diagnosis is broad—decidualized endometrioma, cystic degeneration of a myoma, or even malignancy. The CA-125 was elevated (41.8 U/mL), which usually screams ovarian pathology or endometriosis in a non-pregnant patient. However, remember your physiology: CA-125 naturally fluctuates during pregnancy, peaking in the first and third trimesters. An isolated elevation isn't diagnostic of cancer here; it's likely driven by the sheer steric bulk of the masses irritating the peritoneum or underlying endometriosis/myomatous tissue.

The real pivot point in this case wasn't just the size of the mass—it was the **mechanical compromise**. The patient developed dyspnea due to diaphragmatic compression from an 18 cm mass pushing against her lungs. More critically, intraoperatively, they discovered the upper mass was actually a pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion and leakage, explaining her fever and localized inflammation (despite negative infectious workup for pneumonia). This confirms that "complicated" doesn't always mean malignant; it means mechanically unstable. If you leave a twisted pedunculated fibroid alone, you risk infarction, sepsis-like physiology from necrotic tissue release, or catastrophic hemorrhage upon rupture.

So, how do we decide? Do we cut into a pregnant uterus at 21 weeks with known risks of preterm labor and bleeding? Or do we wait? The literature suggests we generally wait unless there are specific red flags. In this case, those red flags were present: **acute worsening symptoms despite analgesia**, **restrictive lung disease** (dyspnea), and **radiologic/surgical suspicion of torsion/ischemia**. The team opted for laparotomy rather than laparoscopy because of the sheer size (>10 cm) and location (intramural/pedunculated), which would have made minimally invasive removal technically hazardous with high conversion rates or bleeding risks. They performed a targeted myomectomy on the dangerous upper mass but wisely left the pelvic mass alone to minimize uterine manipulation and preserve fetal viability—a crucial distinction in surgical planning during pregnancy.

Post-op management required aggressive support: IV iron for anemia (Hb dropped to 7.5 g/L), blood transfusions if needed, progesterone prophylaxis to prevent contractions triggered by uterine irritation/cervical stimulation from surgery, and close monitoring of fetal heart tones throughout. Interestingly, they waited until term (37 weeks) for an elective C-section because the remaining pelvic fibroid posed an obstruction risk for vaginal delivery. This highlights another pearl: **the mode of delivery is dictated by anatomy.** Even if you save parturition via C-section later because of residual scarring or obstruction from untreated masses is not uncommon practice when large posterior myomas persist near the cervix/isthmus.

The takeaway here isn't just "fibroids are scary"; it's about recognizing when conservative management fails due to mechanical constraints rather than biological ones. While most myomas regress post-partum (shrinking by ~44% within 6 weeks), waiting through pregnancy for a symptomatic torsed mass is rarely worth the gamble if maternal safety is compromised by respiratory distress or ischemic necrosis. The key is rapid multidisciplinary assessment involving OB-GYNs capable of handling high-risk pregnancies alongside general surgeons familiar with complex obstetric anatomy. Don't get distracted by tumor markers; look at the mechanics of pain and breathing.

*   **Diagnostic Caution:** Differentiate between ovarian tumors and pedunculated leiomyomas using MRI without contrast; CA-125 elevation is non-specific in pregnancy due to physiological peaks and peritoneal irritation from large masses/endometriosis/degeneration.
*   **Surgical Indications:** Operate during pregnancy only for clear complications: torsion/infarction (suspicion confirmed by acute worsening pain/fever), rupture risk requiring decompression due to organ compression (e.g., severe dyspnea), or inability to rule out malignancy despite benign imaging features when symptoms are refractory to conservative care.
*   **Surgical Strategy:** Prioritize minimizing uterine manipulation; consider staged procedures (remove only critical masses) rather than exhaustive debulking; use laparotomy for very large (>10cm) or deeply intramural masses where laparoscopy poses excessive bleeding risk or technical difficulty; administer perioperative progesterone prophylaxis if indicated by institutional protocols for threatened preterm labor post-myomectomy.
*   **Delivery Planning:** Anticipate Cesarean section if residual fibroids obstruct the birth canal or if significant uterine scarring exists from prior myomectomy; timing should balance fetal maturity against maternal comfort/respiratory mechanics caused by remaining masses.
