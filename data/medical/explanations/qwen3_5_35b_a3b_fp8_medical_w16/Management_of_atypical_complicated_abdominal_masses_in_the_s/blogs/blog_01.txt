Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# The Second-Trimester Trap: Navigating Diagnostic Ambiguity Between Endometriomas and Degenerating Leiomyomas

We've all seen the "scary" ultrasound in a pregnant patient. Two giant cystic masses, one pushing up against the diaphragm causing dyspnea, the other anchoring in the pelvis. Your differential diagnosis immediately screams **endometrioma** or **ovarian malignancy**, especially when you see that slight bump in CA-125. You start mentally drafting the referral letter to a gynecologic oncologist and planning for a complex resection that might jeopardize the pregnancy.

But wait. What if I told you that what looked like a "decidualized endometrioma" was actually a **pedunculated uterine leiomyoma**? And what if the "cystic" nature wasn't a primary ovarian cyst, but rather **red degeneration** (or cystic degeneration) of a fibroid?

This is exactly what happened in a fascinating case report recently published by De Nola et al., involving a 40-something G1P0 woman at 21 weeks gestation presenting with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. It's a masterclass in how anatomy can deceive us, why tumor markers need context, and why sometimes, leaving *some* stuff alone is the most aggressive thing you can do for fetal safety. Let's break down this diagnostic maze.

## When Anatomy Plays Tricks: The Great Mimicker

The patient arrived with classic signs of an acute abdomen: severe left upper quadrant pain, pelvic tenderness, and dyspnea. On physical exam, she had an abdominal circumference larger than expected for gestational age, with two distinct masses palpable—one massive 18 cm lump in the LUQ and a 9 cm fixed mass in the pelvis.

Initial ultrasound painted a picture that screamed "adnexal pathology." Two large, unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularity were visualized. The upper mass extended from the subphrenic space to the uterine fundus; the lower one sat in the pouch of Douglas, seemingly attached to a posterior intramural myoma with cystic degeneration.

Here lies the trap: **Pedunculated leiomyomas can look remarkably like ovarian tumors.** During pregnancy, hormonal surges cause rapid changes in myometrial tissue (decalcification/hyalinization/red degeneration). A pedunculated fibroid undergoing red degeneration becomes necrotic, hemorrhagic, and fluid-filled on imaging. To the radiologist's eye (and yours), it looks like a complex ovarian cyst or an endometrioma.

The case highlights how easily we fall into confirmation bias. The "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus makes it incredibly difficult to trace ligaments or identify feeding vessels that would clearly link these masses to the ovary vs. the uterus. Without seeing that connective stalk on ultrasound or MRI, we assume ovarian origin because they are separate entities visually. But once they twist (torsion) or undergo degeneration, their attachment becomes obscured by inflammation and edema.

## The Imaging Toolkit: Ultrasound vs. Non-Contrast MRI

So, how do we navigate this ambiguity without exposing mom or baby to unnecessary radiation or contrast agents?

**Ultrasound (US)** is your first line of defense, but as we saw here, it has limitations regarding tissue characterization when masses are huge or deep-seated behind bowel gas or bone. Color Doppler helps assess vascularity—these masses had peripheral flow—but differentiating between an inflamed fibroid and an endometrioma based on flow patterns alone is tricky.

**Non-contrast MRI** is where we gain clarity without crossing safety lines (remember: Gadolinium crosses the placenta and carries theoretical risks). In this case, MRI confirmed:
*   Thick walls (1.4 cm).
*   Inhomogeneous fluid content on T1/T2 fat-sat sequences (suggesting old hemorrhage/degeneration).
*   Focal hyperintense solid components (the remaining viable fibroid tissue).
*   Crucially, it showed continuity between the lower mass and a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma of 58x71 mm, despite being "stuck" to it visually on US alone.

The takeaway? If US is equivocal for large pelvic/abdominal masses in pregnancy, **non-contrast MRI** is superior for determining depth of invasion and relationship to the uterine cavity/placenta. It tells you if you are dealing with an ovarian mass or a uterine mass masquerading as one—a distinction that dictates surgical approach and delivery planning.

## The CA-125 Pitfall: Don't Panic Over Numbers

Let's talk about that CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL. Normal is <35 U/mL? Oh no! Ovarian cancer alert! 🚨

Hold your horses before calling Oncology Emergency Room consults for ovarian cancer staging procedures during pregnancy unless there is overwhelming evidence otherwise. As discussed extensively in literature cited by De Nola et al.:
*   CA-125 naturally rises during pregnancy due to peritoneal irritation from uterine expansion and hormonal shifts (peaks in 1st and 3rd trimesters).
*   Benign conditions like endometriosis, adenomyosis PID, menstruation (obviously not applicable here), and **uterine leiomyomas** elevate CA-125 significantly during gestation due to increased cell turnover and peritoneal friction from expanding myometrium/myomas themselves causing local inflammation/necrosis (as seen with torsion/degeneration here).
*   Isolated elevations are rarely diagnostic of malignancy in young women (<40s) even less so during pregnancy without suspicious morphological features on imaging (e.g., papillary projections, ascites with nodules).

In this case, while CA-125 was elevated (>35), it was only mildly so relative to known malignant thresholds for older populations ($\ge$35 U/mL has high PPV >50yo but poor PPV <40yo). Combined with normal CT/MRI findings of no lymphadenopathy or ascites with implants, we must interpret this as **"physiological/benign elevation"** rather than immediate malignancy until proven otherwise by histopathology post-delivery/surgery if indicated. Serial monitoring is far more useful than a single snapshot value here; note that her previous value was 36 U/mL two weeks prior—showing stability/rise consistent with growing inflammation rather than explosive metastatic spread often seen in Stage III/IV cancers requiring immediate hysterectomy/oophorectomy logic which isn't feasible mid-gestation yet anyway!

## Surgical Strategy: To Cut or Not To Cut?

This brings us to the crux of management: When do we operate? Conservative management is usually preferred for asymptomatic fibroids because surgery carries risks of preterm labor, hemorrhage, and rupture (especially if entering into decidua). However... **complications demand action**.

Okay seriously now – back to clinical pearls! The key takeaway from this article is that **when facing ambiguous abdominal/pelvic masses in pregnancy**:  
1) Trust your eyes/imaging but don't forget anatomy can lie – consider both ovarian AND uterine origins even if they appear separated;  
2) Remember CA-125 goes UP naturally during pregnancy due to benign causes including fibroids/endometriosis – don't jump straight to cancer alarm bells unless other red flags exist;  
3) Non-contrast MRI adds tremendous value when US fails to clarify relationships between masses & uterus;  
4) Surgery should be reserved ONLY when complications threaten life/function OR diagnosis cannot be confidently made without tissue biopsy AND risks outweigh benefits – otherwise wait till postpartum unless absolutely necessary;  
5) If operating leave behind anything removable quickly/safely vs trying complete removal risking excessive bleeding/preterm labor induction – prioritize fetal/maternal survival over perfect anatomical restoration;

## Key Takeaways for Your Practice  

When evaluating complex abdominal/pelvic masses in pregnant patients remember these essentials:  

*   **Imaging Interpretation:** Be cautious interpreting ultrasound findings alone; pedunculated leiomyomas can mimic ovarian pathology perfectly especially when undergoing red/cystic degeneration → Consider non-contrast MRI if diagnosis unclear → Look closely for continuity between masses & uterus even if not obvious initially →  
*   **Tumor Marker Context:** Elevated CA‑125 does NOT automatically equal ovarian cancer during pregnancy → Physiologic elevation common due to peritoneal stretching/inflammation/benign conditions → Interpret trends + clinical picture carefully → Avoid jumping straight toward oncologic intervention unless strongly indicated otherwise →  
*   **Surgical Decision Making:** Operate only when complications threaten maternal/fetal health OR definitive diagnosis impossible without tissue sampling AND risks manageable → Prioritize minimizing operative time/stress/anesthesia exposure → Leave behind anything removable quickly/safely vs attempting exhaustive removal risking hemorrhage/preterm labor induction → Multidisciplinary planning crucial → Always monitor fetal heart rate throughout procedure → Administer progesterone prophylaxis perioperatively if concerned about preterm contractions triggered by manipulation/inflammation/etc…
