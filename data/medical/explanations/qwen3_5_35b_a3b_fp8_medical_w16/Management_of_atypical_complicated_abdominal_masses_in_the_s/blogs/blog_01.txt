Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# The Second-Trimester CA-125 Trap: Why Elevated Markers Don't Always Mean Malignancy

We've all been there: a pregnant patient walks into the ED with abdominal pain, and your gut instinct screams "ovarian mass." You order the labs, and suddenly you're staring at an elevated CA-125. In a 40-year-old woman, that number feels like a red flag waving frantically for malignancy. But here's the trap: **pregnancy itself is a state of physiological inflammation and hormonal flux that can mimic cancer.**

This case report highlights a classic diagnostic dilemma involving a 40-something nulliparous woman at 21 weeks gestation presenting with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. On paper, she looked terrifying. Two massive abdominal masses, one filling the subphrenic space and another in the pouch of Douglas, combined with a CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL (above the standard cutoff of 35 U/mL). The differential diagnosis immediately swung toward ovarian carcinoma or complex endometriomas. Yet, the final pathology? Benign uterine leiomyomas.

The lesson isn't just about ruling out cancer; it's about understanding how pregnancy warps our reference ranges and why "normal" lab values are relative concepts when a fetus is involved.

## The Physiology of False Positives

Let's dissect the numbers first. The standard textbook cutoff for CA-125 is <35 U/mL for ovarian cancer screening in post-menopausal women. However, applying this static number to a pregnant woman in her second trimester is clinically dangerous. As discussed in the literature surrounding this case, CA-125 exhibits distinct physiological peaks during gestation: one in the first trimester (median ~23 U/mL) and another in the third (median ~21 U/mL). The second trimester typically sees lower levels (median ~14 U/mL).

In this patient, a level of 41.8 U/mL was indeed elevated *for* the second trimester baseline, triggering alarm bells. But why? Because CA-125 is not specific to epithelial ovarian cancer; it is an antigen found on mesothelial cells lining the peritoneum, pleura, and pericardium. Any irritation to these surfaces—whether from endometriosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, liver cirrhosis, or simply the mechanical stretching of ligaments and organs during rapid uterine growth—can cause spiking levels.

Furthermore, this patient had confirmed bacterial pyelonephritis (*Klebsiella pneumoniae*) concurrent with her presentation. While rare to see as a primary driver of such high markers alone, systemic inflammation adds fuel to the fire. Relying on an isolated snapshot of CA-125 without context regarding gestational age and concurrent infections leads directly to overtreatment anxiety and potentially unnecessary interventions.

## Imaging Over Biomarkers: Navigating the Fog

When faced with ambiguous markers and large masses in pregnancy, imaging becomes your most reliable ally—but only if chosen correctly. Ultrasound was the first line here, revealing two large cystic masses with peripheral vascularity. While helpful, US struggles to differentiate between decidualized endometriomas and cystically degenerated leiomyomas when they are massive (this patient had an 18 cm mass!).

Enter MRI without contrast. This is the gold standard for characterizing adnexal masses in pregnancy after the first trimester. Crucially, **gadolinium should generally be avoided** unless absolutely life-saving due to potential fetal risks (though recent data suggests safety at certain doses). In this case study, non-contrast MRI provided critical architectural detail: thickened walls (~1.4 cm), heterogeneous fluid content, and solid components suggestive of myomatous origin rather than pure neoplasm. It also clarified that the lower mass was continuous with a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma—a key differentiator from an independent ovarian tumor.

The combination of US findings (vascularity) and MRI characteristics (tissue continuity) allowed the team to pivot away from "cancer workup" toward "complicated fibroid management," even before surgery began. If you rely solely on CA-125 >35 in this setting, you might rush to resection; if you integrate imaging trends and clinical context (like her lack of ascites or lymphadenopathy), you buy yourself time for conservative management or targeted intervention.

## When Surgery Becomes Necessary: Balancing Risks

So, did she need surgery? Yes—but not because she likely had cancer; she needed it because she had **mechanical compromise**. The upper mass caused restrictive lung disease leading to dyspnea; both masses contributed to severe pain that wasn't responding to analgesics; and there was a genuine risk of torsion or rupture given their size and pedunculation noted intra-operatively (the upper mass was actually twisted).

This case reinforces that while expectant management is preferred for asymptomatic fibroids or simple cysts in pregnancy ("wait-and-see"), indications for intervention include:
*   Acute abdomen (torsion/rupture)
*   Severe pain refractory to conservative measures
*   Obstruction of labor or urinary tract
*   Suspicion of malignancy based on *structural* features on imaging (not just labs)
*   Rapid growth causing hemodynamic instability or respiratory distress

In this scenario, laparotomy was performed at 21 weeks + 6 days after multidisciplinary consensus removed only the symptomatic upper mass while leaving the pelvic mass intact to minimize uterine manipulation and preterm labor risk. Note that they used progesterone prophylaxis intraoperatively—a small but vital pearl for preventing contractions during such invasive procedures in mid-gestation pregnancies where fetal heart monitoring must be continuous before, during, and after surgery (as emphasized by Zhao et al.).

## Take-Home Pearls for Your Practice

Don't let an elevated CA-125 send you down a malignant rabbit hole when treating pregnant patients. Context is king:

*   **Know your baselines:** CA-125 fluctuates naturally during pregnancy peaks in T1/T3; levels >35 U/mL are common benign findings in T2 due to peritoneal irritation from uterine expansion or infection (e.g., pyelonephritis).
*   **Serial trends matter more than single values:** An isolated spike vs. a rising trajectory can change your risk stratification significantly; however, even rising trends can be benign if driven by mechanical factors like fibroid degeneration or inflammation.
*   **Imaging trumps biomarkers:** Non-contrast MRI is superior to CT/PET/Contrast-MRI for defining tissue architecture (myoma vs. ovary) without exposing fetus to unnecessary radiation or toxins like gadolinium unless absolutely critical for survival decisions.
*   **Surgery has strict indications:** Operate only for complications (torsion/rupture/severe obstruction/pain) or high suspicion of malignancy based on *imaging features*, not lab values alone; aim for minimal invasiveness (laparoscopy vs laparotomy depending on size/gestational age) and always monitor fetal heart rate continuously perioperatively with progesterone coverage if indicated by local protocols/history of preterm labor risk factors like prior C-sections or cervical insufficiency history may require additional precautions beyond just progesterone administration depending upon institutional guidelines regarding cerclage placement etcetera... wait no keep it simple - just mention progesterone prophylaxis as mentioned in case study since no other specific protocol mentioned here so stick strictly within text boundaries!
