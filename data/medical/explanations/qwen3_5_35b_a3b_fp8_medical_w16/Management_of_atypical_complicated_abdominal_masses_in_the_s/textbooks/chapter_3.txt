Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 3: ## Biochemical Markers and Differential Diagnosis Formulation

The interpretation of serum tumor markers in pregnancy presents a unique diagnostic challenge due to profound physiological fluctuations that can mimic malignancy. In the case described, the patient's CA-125 level was elevated at 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the standard non-pregnant cutoff of 35 U/mL. While this might initially raise concern for epithelial ovarian carcinoma, understanding the natural history of CA-125 during gestation is paramount. Physiologically, CA-125 exhibits a biphasic pattern: it rises during the first trimester (peaking around 23 U/mL) and again in the third trimester (around 21 U/mL), typically dipping to a nadir of approximately 14 U/mL in the second trimester. The patient's value of 41.8 U/mL represents a significant deviation from this physiological baseline for her gestational age (second trimester), suggesting pathology beyond simple pregnancy-related changes. However, isolated elevations remain non-diagnostic because benign conditions such as uterine leiomyomas, endometriosis, and pelvic inflammation can also cause marked increases. Consequently, relying on a single static measurement is insufficient; instead, clinicians must analyze serial trends. In this case, the rising trajectory from 36 U/mL two weeks prior provided crucial context, indicating active biological processes—likely related to the ischemic necrosis and torsion of the pedunculated leiomyoma—rather than a stable neoplastic process alone.

Beyond CA-125, the utility of other tumor markers in this clinical scenario was assessed to rule out germ cell tumors or other malignancies. The patient's levels of CA 19-9, CEA, CA 15-3, β2-microglobulin, and LDH were all within normal limits. These markers serve as important "negative" predictors; their normalcy helps exclude high-risk entities such as mature teratomas (which may elevate LDH or AFP) or gastrointestinal metastases (elevated CEA). The combination of a mildly elevated CA-125 alongside otherwise normal markers narrows the differential diagnosis significantly towards benign gynecological pathologies with secondary inflammation or hemorrhage rather than primary ovarian malignancy. This highlights a fundamental principle in obstetric oncology: biochemical data must never be interpreted in isolation but rather integrated with the gestational timeline and clinical presentation to avoid false-positive alarms that could precipitate unnecessary termination of pregnancy or aggressive surgical intervention.

Constructing an accurate differential diagnosis requires synthesizing these biochemical findings with advanced imaging characteristics to distinguish between benign mimics like decidualized endometriomas and true malignancies or complex leiomyomas. Initially, ultrasound suggested decidualized endometriomas due to the cystic nature of the masses and the presence of peripheral vascularity. However, MRI provided critical architectural details that shifted the probability toward uterine leiomyomas with cystic degeneration. Specifically, the MRI revealed thickened walls (1.4 cm), heterogeneous fluid content on T1/T2 sequences with solid components, and crucially, a lack of clear separation between the lower mass and a known posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. This radiological continuity suggests that what appeared as separate cystic lesions were actually degenerating fibroids sharing tissue planes with the uterus. The slight elevation in CA-125 correlates perfectly with this finding; leiomyomas undergoing red degeneration or torsion release inflammatory cytokines that stimulate peritoneal mesothelial cells to produce CA-125, creating a "pseudo-tumor marker" profile indistinguishable from early-stage ovarian cancer without histological confirmation.

Ultimately, deciding whether to suspect malignancy relies heavily on distinguishing between steady-state elevation driven by physiology/benign pathology versus dynamic escalation indicative of neoplasia or acute catastrophic events like torsion or rupture. In this case, despite the elevated CA-125, several factors argued against malignancy: the absence of lymphadenopathy on MRI, normal levels of other specific markers (CEA, LDH), and clinical stability aside from acute pain caused by mechanical distortion rather than systemic spread. The decision-making process prioritized these converging lines of evidence—the specific timing relative to trimesters (ruling out first-trimester peak), the trend analysis (showing rapid change correlating with symptom onset), and imaging features—to formulate a working diagnosis of complicated benign leiomyomas requiring surgical decompression rather than radical oncologic resection. This approach underscores that in pregnant patients with large abdominal masses, biochemical markers act as supportive clues within a multimodal framework where imaging anatomy often supersedes serology for determining etiology and guiding management strategies aimed at preserving both maternal health and fetal viability.
