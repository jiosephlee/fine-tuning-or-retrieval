# Airway Safety First: Managing Dysphagia and Respiratory Compromise in Acute Myositis

We've all seen the "neck swelling" patient. They walk into the ED, look panicked, and scream "Ludwig's Angina!" or "Angioedema!" until they get a CT scan. But what happens when the airway looks fine on pulse oximetry, yet the patient is silently drowning in their own secretions? This case of a middle-aged man presenting with progressive neck swelling serves as a brutal reminder that **muscle inflammation can mimic soft tissue edema**, and the airway threat in myositis isn't always about external compression—it's often about internal paralysis.

This patient didn't just have a swollen neck; he had *pseudoadge* caused by massive inflammation of the strap muscles and pharyngeal constrictors. The terrifying part? He was saturating 99% on room air. If you rely solely on SpO2, you might miss the fact that he is aspirating silently. During his nasendoscopy, he wasn't just having secretions; he was coughing violently because thin fluids were penetrating his vocal cords before he could protect them. This is not an allergy; it's not an infection. It is **myogenic respiratory failure** waiting to happen.

When you see neck swelling without fever or leukocytosis, your differential must expand beyond ENT emergencies. If the patient has proximal weakness—even subtle—and you suspect myositis, **do not wait**. The CK level here was astronomical (16,184 U/L), confirming that the "swelling" was actually inflamed muscle fibers leaking enzymes and fluid into the interstitium. The lesson? In undifferentiated neck swelling with dysphagia, check a Creatine Kinase immediately. If it's sky-high, you aren't dealing with angioedema; you're dealing with a ticking time bomb for aspiration pneumonia and respiratory arrest.

The management strategy here shifts from antihistamines and epinephrine to aggressive immunomodulation and airway protection. Because this patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia confirmed by videofluoroscopy showing pyriform fossae residue and penetration to the vocal cords, we cannot simply send him home on prednisone alone. We need to secure nutrition first—this patient required an NG tube for two months post-discharge because swallowing mechanics were too compromised to trust orally.

Furthermore, we must recognize that standard intubation criteria don't apply here until it's too late. This patient had significant muscle involvement in the head and neck region (confirmed by PET scan showing diffuse uptake). While his vitals were stable initially, the rapid progression of inflammation can lead to sudden loss of airway patency or inability to clear secretions once sedation wears off or disease progresses overnight. Early consultation with Speech Therapy for swallow studies is non-negotiable in these scenarios to quantify aspiration risk before deciding on oral intake versus NPO status with enteral access.

The treatment protocol for this specific presentation requires hitting hard and fast. High-dose IV methylprednisolone (1g/day) was started immediately to quell the acute inflammation causing the "pseudo-angioedema." However, steroids alone often take days to work; hence, the addition of IVIG (loading dose 2g/kg) provided rapid immunomodulation while Rituximab and Mycophenolate were layered in for long-term control. Note that Methotrexate was wisely avoided given his concurrent restrictive lung pattern—a common pitfall in DM patients where ILD risks outweigh benefits if not carefully weighed against toxicity profiles like hepatotoxicity or marrow suppression.

Finally, remember that this isn't just about clearing the throat today; it's about preventing long-term disability and ruling out malignancy. Anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis carries a distinct risk profile where dysphagia correlates strongly with poor outcomes if untreated early enough to prevent fibrosis or chronic aspiration pneumonia. This patient eventually normalized his CK and regained full swallowing function after months of rehab and maintenance therapy, but only because we recognized the red flags early: **dysphagia + neck swelling = Myositis until proven otherwise.** Don't let a normal oxygen saturation fool you into thinking the airway is safe when the muscles controlling it are failing silently beneath your eyes.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **"Normal" SpO2 does not rule out impending airway collapse:** In acute myositis with neck swelling, assess for silent aspiration via coughing during exams or videofluoroscopy before relying on pulse oximetry as a safety net.
*   **CK is your triage tool:** Any patient with unexplained neck swelling ("pseudo-angioedema") who lacks infectious signs should have an immediate Creatine Kinase drawn; values >10k confirm inflammatory myopathy over allergic/infectious etiologies.
*   **Secure nutrition early:** Severe dysphagia in myositis requires NPO status and enteral access (NG/G-tube) pending recovery of bulbar function; do not delay feeding decisions while waiting for steroid response which takes days to weeks to manifest clinically in severe cases like this one where IVIG/Rituximab are added early due to severity of bulbar involvement (airway muscle weakness).
*   **Avoid Methotrexate if ILD risk exists:** In patients with existing restrictive patterns or interstitial lung disease concerns (common in DM), prioritize alternatives like Mycophenolate Mofetil or IVIG/Methotrexate combinations cautiously based on individual risk stratification rather than default protocols used for pure cutaneous disease presentations where MTX might be preferred due to better tolerability profile compared against contraindications such as liver dysfunction or pregnancy considerations etc., though here specifically noted avoidance due to pulmonary fibrosis risk mentioned earlier in text regarding ILD association...
