Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# CK > 16,000: Why Creatine Kinase is Your Best Friend in Unexplained Edema

**Lead with the surprise:** Imagine a patient walking into your ED with massive neck swelling. You've ruled out Ludwig's angina, cleared them with ENT, and they're sitting there with "idiopathic" edema. Now, imagine the lab result that turns your entire diagnostic trajectory on its head: **Creatine Kinase (CK) of 16,184 U/L.**

This isn't just a number; it's a siren song screaming "myositis." In a recent case report, a middle-aged man presented with progressive neck swelling and hip weakness. Initially, he was funneled down the path of deep neck space infection and later idiopathic angioedema. It wasn't until the attending physician spotted subtle nailfold changes and ordered a CK—driven by the suspicion that this wasn't classic angioedema—that the diagnosis shifted to **Dermatomyositis**.

Today, we're dissecting why that single test order saved this patient from unnecessary antibiotics, invasive procedures, and potentially fatal airway compromise.

## The "Atypical" Presentation Trap

Let's be honest: Dermatomyositis (DM) loves to wear disguises. While textbooks describe Heliotrope rashes and Gottron's papules as hallmarks, up to 6% of patients present with pseudoangioedema—specifically subcutaneous tissue edema without the classic cutaneous stigmata. When this occurs in the neck or face, it mimics histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema almost perfectly.

Our patient had none of the textbook signs initially. No Heliotrope rash (initially), no Gottron's papules visible on exam. He had diffuse neck edema involving strap muscles extending to the retropharyngeal space. He was afebrile (making infection less likely) but had elevated CRPs (mimicking inflammation/infection).

The trap here is cognitive anchoring on the chief complaint ("swollen neck") rather than the underlying mechanism. If you treat this as pure angioedema or infection without looking deeper, you miss the muscle breakdown happening under the hood. The key differentiator? **The sheer magnitude of muscle injury.**

## The Power of One Test: Why Order CK?

In this case, the turning point was not a fancy MRI or a complex genetic panel—it was a basic metabolic/myositis screen triggered by clinical intuition regarding "atypicality."

Why did this matter so much?
*   **Magnitude Matters:** A CK elevation in DM can vary widely, but seeing levels exceeding 10x-20x normal limits is practically pathognomonic for active myositis in this context. This patient hit **16,184 U/L**.
*   **Ruling Out Mimics:** Normal CK helps rule out inflammatory myopathies but doesn't confirm them alone; however, *massively* elevated CK in an edematous patient essentially rules *out* simple allergic/vasogenic angioedema or localized bacterial abscesses as primary drivers of systemic illness.
*   **The "Silent" Signs:** The case report notes that despite lacking classic rashes initially, there were subtle clues: proximal hip flexor weakness (4/5 power), periungual erythema, and nailfold microhemorrhages on capillaroscopy. These should trigger a reflex action to check muscle enzymes before calling it "idiopathic."

If you see unexplained edema + dysphagia + proximal weakness + negative infectious workup = **Check CK immediately.** Do not wait for the rheumatology consult to happen first; get the data now.

## Beyond CK: The Diagnostic Cascade Once You Know It's Myositis

Once that red flag is raised by a sky-high CK, your differential narrows instantly from ENT emergencies to Autoimmune Inflammatory Myopathies (AIM). Here is how you proceed efficiently:

1.  **Myositis Antibody Panel:** Don't stop at ANA. This patient tested positive for **Anti-SAE1** antibodies (Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier Activating Enzyme). While Anti-MDA5 and Anti-TIF1-γ are often associated with skin-predominant disease or malignancy risk specifically linked to edema/skin ulceration, Anti-SAE1 carries its own unique risks: severe dysphagia and interstitial lung disease (ILD).
    *   *Pearl:* Always consider malignancy screening in new-onset DM patients (especially males >45yo), even if they lack classic cutaneous signs. This patient underwent extensive PET/CT/endoscopy because SAE1+ status places him in an intermediate-to-high risk category for occult cancer.
2.  **Imaging Confirmation:** MRI thighs confirmed active myositis in specific muscle groups (obturator internus/psoas). FDG-PET can show diffuse uptake across muscle groups but must be interpreted carefully to distinguish inflammation from tumor metabolism (though in this case, no malignancy was found).
3.  **Biopsy:** The gold standard remains muscle biopsy showing perifascicular atrophy and C5b-9 deposition—a hallmark of DM distinct from polymyositis or IBM.

## Treatment Implications: Aggressive Immunosuppression is Non-Negotiable

This case serves as a grim reminder that DM presenting with airway/cervical involvement is an emergency waiting to happen due to impending respiratory failure from swallowing dysfunction or laryngeal obstruction from edema.

Standard antihistamines or epinephrine won't touch this pathology because it's not mediated by histamine or bradykinin; it's mediated by complement deposition and T-cell infiltration causing muscle necrosis and fascial fluid shifts.

The treatment protocol for severe cases like this involves:
*   **High-dose Steroids:** IV Methylprednisolone pulses (1g/day) followed by aggressive oral tapering.
*   **Second-Line Agents:** Given the severity (dysphagia requiring NG tube), adding IVIG was crucial alongside Mycophenolate Mofetil and Rituximab. Note that Methotrexate was avoided here due to concurrent ILD concerns—a nuance often missed if treating blindly based on general guidelines rather than individual organ involvement.
*   **Airway Protection:** Dysphagia assessment via videofluoroscopy is mandatory early on to prevent aspiration pneumonia before intubation becomes necessary due to loss of airway protection mechanisms.

## Key Take-Home Points for EM & Primary Care Providers

*   **"Idiopathic Angioedema" is a Diagnosis of Exclusion:** Before labeling neck swelling as idiopathic angioedema in an adult male who hasn't failed ACE inhibitors or has no family history of C1-inhibitor deficiency, always ask about proximal weakness and check CPK/CK-LDH-AST/ALT patterns simultaneously with LFTs/CBC/ESR/CRP screens if available locally without delay!
*   **CK > 500 U/L warrants investigation; CK > 5000 U/L screams Myositis:** In any patient with unexplained edema/muscle pain/swelling where infection/allergy is unlikely, do not hesitate to order CK even if no overt rash is present yet ("Amyopathic" variants exist but true amyopathic DM rarely presents with massive CK spikes like this).
*   **Look Past the Rash:** Classic dermatologic signs may be absent early on ("clinically amyopathic"). Look for nailfold capillary changes (periungual erythema/hemorrhages), proximal weakness on exam (hip flexion resistance), and dysphagia complaints masked as "throat tightness."
*   **Antibody Specificity Drives Management:** Knowing it's Anti-SAE1 positive alerts you specifically to high risks for dysphagia and ILD compared to other myositides like Anti-Jo-1 (more common ILD but different phenotype) or Anti-MDA5 (rapidly progressive ILD/digital ulcers). This guides your monitoring frequency for pulmonary decline and malignancy screening urgency according to IMACS guidelines.
*   **Don't Miss Airway Compromise Early:** Subclinical pharyngeal dilation issues can lead to silent aspiration rapidly once steroids start working too slowly or if airway edema progresses during induction phases of therapy; ensure swallow studies are done early in these cases before discharge planning begins!
