Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# The Anti-SAE1 Phenotype: When Dysphagia Precedes the Rash

Let's play a game of "What am I?" You have a middle-aged man walking into the ED with a massive, progressive neck swelling. He looks like he has Ludwig's angina or perhaps a catastrophic allergic reaction. You've ruled out the infection, cleared him with ENT, and now you're staring at a picture that screams "idiopathic angioedema." But here is the twist: he isn't wheezing, he isn't hives, and his complement levels are normal. Instead, he has a CK of 16,000+ and profound dysphagia.

This case from Chan et al. serves as a masterclass in looking past the obvious. We often treat dermatomyositis (DM) as a skin-first disease where the rash leads us to the muscle weakness later. However, this patient highlights a distinct serological phenotype—**Anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis**—where the clinical narrative flips. Here, soft tissue edema and severe dysphagia are the heralds, often appearing before the classic Gottron's papules or heliotrope eruption make their debut. If you miss this window because you're fixated on ruling out anaphylaxis or deep neck space infections, you delay life-saving immunosuppression for a patient who is essentially drowning in his own inflamed tissues.

### The "Angioedema" Mimic

The most dangerous pitfall in this scenario is misdiagnosing the neck swelling as histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. As detailed in the case report, this patient was initially sent to ENT for suspected deep neck infection and then flagged for idiopathic angioedema workups. The reality? It was **myositis-driven pseudoangioedema**.

The mechanism here is fascinating but clinically straightforward: inflammation within the strap muscles and surrounding fascia leads to massive subcutaneous edema. This isn't vascular leakage mediated by mast cells; it's inflammatory infiltration mimicking fluid overload or allergic reactions. The key differentiator should always be your eyes and your ears (CK):
*   **The Look:** Generalized erythema of the face and neck, but *not* the classic urticaria of true angioedema.
*   **The Feel:** Non-tender swelling (often unlike cellulitis).
*   **The Sound:** Coughing during exam due to pharyngeal involvement and secretions, hinting at bulbar dysfunction before frank aspiration occurs.
*   **The Lab:** A CK that is sky-high (this patient hit ~16k U/L) immediately shifts the differential away from ENT emergencies toward inflammatory myopathies.

### Enter Anti-SAE1: The Edematous Profile

Why does this matter? Because not all dermatomyositis is created equal. While classic teaching emphasizes Anti-TIF1-γ for malignancy risk and Anti-Jo-1 for interstitial lung disease (ILD), **Anti-SAE1** carries its own unique signature profile that dictates our management strategy.

Literature suggests that Anti-SAE1 DM presents with a higher frequency of **dysphagia** (approx. 46%) and **soft tissue edema** compared to other myositis subsets. In fact, this specific antibody is increasingly recognized as a driver of severe esophageal involvement independent of ILD severity in some cohorts (though ILD remains common). In our case patient, despite having no significant pulmonary fibrosis on initial CT, he suffered from severe pharyngeal dysphagia leading to aspiration pneumonitis—a direct consequence of muscle weakness in the swallowing apparatus rather than parenchymal lung disease alone.

Crucially, unlike some other phenotypes where skin changes are overt at presentation, SAE1 cases can be "rashi-less" initially or present with only subtle clues like periungual erythema or microhemorrhages on nailfold capillaroscopy (which was positive in our patient). Relying solely on finding Gottron's papules might lead to diagnostic delay when those signs haven't erupted yet.

### Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

When evaluating a patient with unexplained neck swelling or dysphagia who fails standard workups for infection or allergy:

*   **Think Myositis Before Angioedema:** If a patient has "idiopathic" neck swelling without urticaria or bronchospasm, check Creatine Kinase immediately. High CK + Neck Swelling = Inflammation until proven otherwise.
*   **Look for Subtle Skin Clues:** Even if Gottron's papules aren't there yet, examine the nails meticulously for periungual erythema or telangiectasia ("Holster sign") and look for generalized facial erythema over nasolabial folds. These can be the first visible signs of SAE1-positive DM before full-blown rashes develop.
*   **Assess Swallowing Early:** Dysphagia in DM is not just about comfort; it predicts aspiration risk rapidly. Do not wait for barium swallows if clinical suspicion is high; consider early speech pathology consults if bulbar symptoms emerge alongside myalgia or weakness.
*   **Know Your Antibodies:** Recognizing Anti-SAE1 positivity alerts you to a phenotype prone to severe soft tissue edema and significant esophageal involvement. While malignancy risk exists (though perhaps slightly lower than TIF1-γ), prioritize aggressive control of inflammation to prevent permanent swallowing dysfunction and airway compromise from edema-induced compression or aspiration pneumonia.
*   **Don't Fear Steroids/Immunomodulators:** Once diagnosed, prompt initiation of high-dose steroids plus steroid-sparing agents (like IVIG or Rituximab as seen in this case) yields excellent outcomes even in severe presentations with massive tissue swelling that took weeks to resolve initially but responded dramatically once treated correctly.

This case reminds us that medicine rarely follows a textbook algorithm perfectly on day one. Sometimes, the "swollen neck" isn't an infection; sometimes it's an autoimmune storm hiding behind a mask of angioedema. By keeping anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis on your differential list for unexplained edematous presentations with elevated CKs, you can turn a confusing ENT referral into a definitive diagnosis before irreversible damage sets in.
