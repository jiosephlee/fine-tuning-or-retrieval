Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# When the Neck Swells Without Wheeze: Dermatomyositis Masquerading as Angioedema

Imagine an ED presentation where the patient looks like they are about to lose their airway. A middle-aged man walks in with three weeks of progressive, massive neck swelling. Your mental checklist immediately flashes *Ludwig's angina*, *retropharyngeal abscess*, or perhaps *idiopathic angioedema*. You order a CT neck, call ENT, and prepare for the intubation team. Then, you notice something odd: the swelling is diffuse, non-tender, and involves the strap muscles rather than a discrete fluid collection. The patient isn't febrile, he's not stridor-ing, but he does complain of "weakness" lifting his legs.

If you stop here and treat him as an allergic reaction or an infection, you've missed a life-threatening autoimmune storm. This case highlights a dangerous diagnostic pitfall: **non-histaminergic, non-bradykinin-mediated neck swelling** caused by severe inflammatory myositis masquerading as dermatomyositis.

### The Red Herrings: Why We Get Tricked
The classic presentation of dermatomyositis (DM) involves the "heliotrope" rash, Gottron's papules, and proximal weakness. However, up to 6% of DM patients present with subcutaneous edema ("pseudoangioedema"), often localized to the face or neck. In this specific case report, a male in his 40s presented with severe neck edema that led ENT surgeons to rule out deep space infection only to find him labeled with "suspected idiopathic angioedema."

The trap lies in the assumption that rapid-onset swelling must be vascular/histaminergic (allergy) or bradykinin-mediated (ACE-inhibitor use or hereditary angioedema). Here was a man who had never taken ACE inhibitors, had normal C1 esterase levels, and normal complement levels. The swelling wasn't fluid shifting into tissues due to permeability changes; it was **muscle inflammation**. The CT scan revealed diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space—essentially inflamed muscle acting like a mass effect.

Without recognizing this mimicry, the patient would have been subjected to unnecessary antihistamines, epinephrine trials, or even tracheostomies while his underlying pathology progressed unchecked.

### The Clues Hidden in Plain Sight
So how did this team break through the noise? They didn't rely on standard angioedema workups alone; they looked for the "zebras" hidden in the mundane physical exam. While the classic rashes (Gottron's papules) were absent—a phenomenon known as "amyopathic" or "hypomyopathic" variants which can still occur—the examiner spotted subtle clues that should trigger your internal alarm bell:

*   **Nailfold Capillaroscopy:** The patient had generalized erythema of the face but also specific nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas. These are hallmarks of connective tissue disease (CTD), specifically dermatomyositis and scleroderma spectrum disorders.
*   **Proximal Weakness:** Despite complaining mostly about neck swelling, he had hip flexor weakness (4/5). This is often overlooked until forced into history taking because patients attribute it to fatigue or aging until it becomes profound dysphagia or gait instability.
*   **Dysphagia:** Increased secretions and coughing during nasendoscopy hinted at bulbar involvement before it became obvious to the swallowing clinic later on video fluoroscopy.

When these subtle signs appeared alongside atypical neck edema in a non-allergic patient, the decision was made to check Creatine Kinase (CK). The result? A staggering **16,184 U/L**. That number alone screams myositis over allergy or infection. Further serology confirmed Anti-SAE1 positivity and MRI/PET scans showing diffuse muscle uptake confirming active myositis rather than just fluid retention.

### Management Implications
Treating pseudoangioedema from dermatomyositis requires aggressive immunosuppression, not steroids alone if severe airway compromise exists immediately. This patient received high-dose pulse IV methylprednisolone followed by IVIG (2g/kg loading dose), Rituximab, and Mycophenolate Mofetil. Notably, Methotrexate was avoided due to concurrent pulmonary restrictions and aspiration pneumonitis risks seen on his CT chest (which showed ground-glass opacities from aspiration).

The outcome underscores a vital teaching point: If you see unexplained neck swelling with elevated CK and no response to standard angioedema protocols, think inflammatory myopathy immediately. Delay leads to permanent muscle damage and potentially fatal airway collapse from laryngeal muscle infiltration or severe aspiration pneumonia.

### Key Take-Home Points
*   **Suspect Myositis in Unexplained Edema:** Any case of "idiopathic" or recurrent angioedema that lacks response to antihistamines/epinephrine/C1-INH replacement warrants a CK check and a full skin/nail exam for connective tissue disease signs.
*   **Look Beyond Classic Rashes:** Absence of Heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules does not rule out Dermatomyositis; look for periungual erythema, nailfold capillary dropout/microhemorrhage ("Holster sign"), and proximal weakness/dysphagia as primary clues.
*   **Check Complement & C1 Esterase Early:** Normal C3/C4 and functional C1 esterase help pivot away from Hereditary/Angioedema toward inflammatory etiologies when combined with muscular symptoms.
*   **Antibody Specificity Matters:** Anti-SAE1 positive DM carries a distinct phenotype; while often associated with cutaneous features (>80%), it can present with significant dysphagia and interstitial lung disease (ILD), necessitating careful pulmonary monitoring during treatment initiation.
*   **Airway Safety First:** Inflammatory swelling of strap muscles can mimic mass lesions; secure airway assessment early if bulbar dysfunction (dysphagia/coughing) is present alongside neck edema before discharging home on observation protocols for allergies.
