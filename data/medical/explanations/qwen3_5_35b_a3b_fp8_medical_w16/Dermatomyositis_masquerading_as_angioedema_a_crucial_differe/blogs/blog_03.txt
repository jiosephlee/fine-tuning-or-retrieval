Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# The Anti-SAE1 Signature: Recognizing the Silent Killer in Dermatomyositis

Let's talk about a diagnostic trap that could cost you a life if you miss it. We've all seen the "neck swelling" patient. They walk into the ED, looking panicked, maybe with stridor, and your brain immediately goes to Ludwig's angina, epiglottitis, or perhaps idiopathic angioedema. You call ENT, get a CT, clear them of deep neck space infection, and send them home with antihistamines or C1-inhibitor replacement. But what if the swelling isn't allergic? What if it's not infectious? And what if the real culprit is an autoimmune myositis screaming for attention through a very strange disguise?

This case report brings us a fascinating pivot point in clinical reasoning: **Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema.** Specifically, this involves the often-overlooked **Anti-SAE1** antibody subset. While we are taught to look for Gottron's papules and heliotrope rashes first, this patient presented with profound neck edema and proximal weakness *without* those classic cutaneous hallmarks initially visible to the naked eye. The swelling wasn't histamine-driven; it was inflammatory muscle edema mimicking a surgical emergency. If you don't check the Creatine Kinase (CK), you might miss the boat entirely.

### When "Angioedema" Isn't Angioedema

The patient in our case was a man in his 40s with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling and hip flexor weakness. He was initially flagged for Ludwig's angina. Even after ENT ruled out structural obstruction and infectious etiology, the team leaned toward idiopathic angioedema because he wasn't on ACE inhibitors and had no family history.

Here is where the "FOAM-style" pearl kicks in: **If you see unexplained neck swelling that doesn't fit the classic angioedema profile (normal C4/C1 esterase), think beyond histamine.** This patient had normal complement levels and negative C1 esterase function, yet he still had massive edema. Why? Because in dermatomyositis, particularly with certain antibody subsets, we aren't seeing vascular permeability issues; we are seeing **muscle inflammation**. The CK level here was astronomical—**16,184 U/L**. That is not just "high"; that is muscle breakdown territory comparable to severe rhabdomyolysis or trauma.

The physical exam eventually gave him away, but only upon close inspection:
*   Subtle nailfold changes (periungual erythema) suggesting capillary dropout.
*   Generalized facial erythema involving nasolabial folds.
*   Reduced hip flexor power (4/5).
*   A rash on the thigh (later confirmed as part of the "Holster sign").

Crucially, he lacked Gottron's papules and heliotrope eruptions at presentation. This highlights how deceptive dermatomyositis can be when it presents primarily as **pseudoangioedema**. Only 6-8% of DM patients present with this degree of edema, making it a rare but dangerous mimic of airway emergencies.

### The SAE1 Connection: More Than Just Skin Deep

So, what ties this together? The answer lies in the serology: **Anti-Small Ubiquitin-Like Modifier Activating Enzyme 1 (SAE1)**.

While classic textbooks often highlight Anti-Mi-2 or Anti-Jo-1 antibodies for Dermatomyositis (DM) and Polymyositis (PM), Anti-SAE1 represents a distinct phenotype with its own epidemiological fingerprint. Literature suggests that SAE1-positive DM is uniquely aggressive regarding specific organ systems:
*   **Dysphagia:** Reported in ~46% of SAE1+ cases versus lower rates in other subtypes. Our patient had severe dysphagia leading to aspiration pneumonitis before diagnosis even occurred—a direct result of pharyngeal muscle involvement rather than mechanical obstruction.
*   **Interstitial Lung Disease (ILD):** Occurs in roughly 43% of these patients. Our patient had an extrathoracic restrictive pattern and pulmonary nodules/consolidation on CT, which turned out to be aspiration-related initially but highlighted the lung vulnerability inherent to this subtype.
*   **Malignancy Risk:** This is the scary part. According to IMACS guidelines, SAE1 positivity places patients in a high-risk category for occult malignancy alongside DM status and dysphagia itself (~18% risk overall). While our patient didn't have cancer at presentation (and remains cancer-free at 12 months), the surveillance protocol must be rigorous because SAE1+ DM carries a significant paraneoplastic burden compared to other subtypes like Anti-MDA5 (which leans heavily toward rapidly progressive ILD).

### Why This Matters for Your Practice

The takeaway here isn't just about memorizing antibody names; it's about changing your algorithm for "idiopathic" swelling. When you encounter a patient with neck swelling who fails standard workups for infection or allergy:
1.  **Check CK immediately.** Do not wait for an immunology consult to order this basic metabolic panel extension if muscle pain or weakness is mentioned—even vaguely ("my legs feel heavy," "trouble climbing stairs").
2.  **Look closer at the nails.** Periungual erythema and telangiectasia are often missed unless you are actively hunting for them under magnification or good light during capillaroscopy prep (even without formal equipment).
3.  **Don't ignore dysphagia.** Inflammation of striated muscles can cause swallowing difficulties long before they become obvious choking events on video fluoroscopy studies like our patient's barium swallow did later on.

The treatment pathway for this case was aggressive but necessary: pulse steroids followed by IVIG, Rituximab, and Mycophenolate Mofetil (avoiding Methotrexate due to pre-existing lung concerns). The outcome was excellent once the correct diagnosis was made—the CK normalized within weeks, swallowing returned after two months off NG tubes, and the "swollen neck" vanished completely by month three without leaving permanent deformity or requiring tracheostomy/airway intervention that would have been inevitable if treated as simple angioedema or infection alone.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **"Angioedema" without allergens?** If C4/C1 esterase are normal and there is no response to antihistamines/epinephrine/corticosteroids typical for allergies, broaden your differential to include inflammatory myopathies causing pseudoangioedema via muscle inflammation/oedema rather than vascular leakiness alone.
*   **CK is King:** In any adult presenting with unexplained soft tissue swelling + proximal weakness/dysphagia/myalgia regardless of skin findings—order Creatine Kinase immediately values >5000 U/L should trigger an immediate rheumatology/myositis workup referral regardless of age or gender bias towards older females (this patient was male).
*   **Know your Antibodies:** Anti-SAE1 positivity signals a specific phenotype characterized by high rates of dysphagia (>40%), ILD (~40%), and significant malignancy risk (~20%). It requires aggressive induction therapy (often IVIG/Rituximab) rather than monotherapy steroids alone given its severity profile compared to Mi-2 positive disease which tends to respond better to steroids alone initially.
