Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# The Silent Airway: When "Angioedema" is Actually Bulbar Paralysis

Let's play a quick game of "What's the Diagnosis?" You walk into the ED with a patient in their 40s who presents with a massive, progressive neck swelling. They've got dysphagia, they're coughing up secretions, and their CT shows diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space. Your brain immediately flashes red flags for **Ludwig's Angina** or **Deep Neck Space Infection**. But wait—no fever, no leukocytosis, no pain on palpation. Now you pivot to **Acquired Angioedema**, maybe even idiopathic bradykinin-mediated swelling?

Here's where the trap snaps shut: What if neither of those is the culprit? What if this isn't an allergic reaction or a bacterial abscess, but rather **severe inflammatory myositis** mimicking angioedema?

This case report highlights a terrifyingly common diagnostic pitfall: mistaking the profound proximal muscle weakness and edema of dermatomyositis for an ENT emergency or primary angioedema. The stakes couldn't be higher because missing this diagnosis means missing the window to save the airway before it collapses from bulbar paralysis.

## The "Silent" Aspiration Trap

The most insidious aspect of this case wasn't just the neck swelling; it was the **silent aspiration** occurring in plain sight.

When we think of dysphagia in an acute setting, we often look for mechanical obstruction or obvious neurological deficits like stroke. However, in dermatomyositis, the pathology lies in the striated muscles responsible for swallowing—the pharyngeal constrictors and upper esophageal sphincter. These are proximal muscles that can fail catastrophically without much warning.

In our patient, a flexible nasendoscopy revealed *anatomically* normal structures (soft palate, vallecula, larynx looked fine), yet he was coughing violently during the exam and producing excessive secretions. This is classic for **bulbar dysfunction**. The subsequent video fluoroscopic swallow study confirmed the disaster waiting to happen: severe pyriform fossae residue and immediate post-swallow penetration of thin fluids into the vocal cords.

This is "silent" because the patient might not be choking loudly enough to trigger immediate panic until they develop hypoxic episodes or aspiration pneumonia—which he already had (patchy peribronchial consolidation). The lesson here is profound: **Anatomic integrity does not equal functional competence.** If a patient has unexplained neck swelling plus any hint of dysphagia or wet voice quality, assume airway muscle weakness until proven otherwise. Don't rely solely on visualizing the anatomy; test its function immediately.

## Why CK Matters More Than Complements

The turning point in this case was a single lab order that changed everything: **Creatine Kinase (CK)**.

While reviewing patients with suspected angioedema or deep neck infections, we often get bogged down checking C3/C4 levels and C1 esterase function (which were all normal here). We chase viral cultures and antibiotics. But when faced with "idiopathic" neck edema that doesn't fit the histaminergic or bradykinin mold, your gut should scream **"Check CK."**

Why? Because in this scenario, the "swelling" isn't fluid leaking from capillaries due to mast cells; it's inflammatory edema within inflamed muscle fibers themselves. This patient's CK skyrocketed to nearly 16,000 U/L—a level that screams rhabdomyolysis or severe myositis far more than angioedema ever could. The presence of periungual erythema and nailfold microhaemorrhages were subtle clues that should have triggered this workup earlier, but let's be honest: in a busy ED under pressure for an airway issue, those skin findings can easily be missed unless you know exactly what you're looking for (the Holster sign).

Once CK is elevated in this context, you don't wait days for a rheumatology consult to start thinking about immunosuppression; you call them *now*. Time is muscle—and time is airway patency.

## Aggressive Immunosuppression: It's Not Just Steroids Alone

So, once you diagnose anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis causing life-threatening bulbar weakness and airway compromise, how do you treat it? This isn't a case where low-dose prednisone will suffice while you watch and wait. We are dealing with potential respiratory failure due to diaphragm or phrenic nerve involvement alongside bulbar collapse.

The management strategy here needs to be aggressive and multimodal:
*   **High-Dose Pulse Steroids:** Immediate administration of IV methylprednisolone (1g/day) is standard to blunt the acute inflammatory storm rapidly reducing muscle edema and improving strength quickly enough to secure breathing patterns before intubation becomes necessary (if it hasn't happened already).
*   **IVIG Loading:** Intravenous Immunoglobulin acts fast as a bridge therapy while waiting for slower-acting agents to kick in. This patient received 2 g/kg over five days followed by maintenance cycles—a heavy lift that correlates with rapid clinical stabilization in refractory myositis cases involving airway compromise.
*   **Steroid-Sparing Agents:** Given the severity and specific antibody profile (SAE1), adding Rituximab and Mycophenolate Mofetil provides long-term control to prevent relapse once steroids are tapered off too quickly. Note specifically why Methotrexate might be avoided here: if there is any suspicion of interstitial lung disease (ILD) or significant pulmonary involvement (as seen by his restrictive pattern), MTX carries risks that might outweigh benefits compared to other agents like MMF or Rituximab.
*   **Nutritional Support:** Do not underestimate the role of nutrition here. With severe dysphagia leading to aspiration risk, placing an NG tube early is not just supportive care; it is an airway protection measure allowing safe caloric intake while preventing further pneumonitis from repeated micro-aspirations during feeding trials.

## Key Take-Home Points for Emergency Medicine & Acute Care Teams

*   **"Angioedema" without wheeze?** If neck swelling persists without response to epinephrine/antihistamines/corticosteroids AND lacks obvious infectious signs (fever/pain), consider inflammatory myopathy as a mimic.
*   **The CK Rule:** Any patient with unexplained soft tissue swelling involving skeletal muscle regions (neck/thighs/chest) requires a Creatine Kinase level immediately; it can distinguish between true angioedema and myositic edema instantly.
*   **Functional vs. Anatomical Dysphagia:** Normal nasendoscopy does not rule out dangerous aspiration if bulbar muscle strength is compromised; proceed directly to videofluoroscopy if clinical suspicion exists ("wet voice," coughing with eating/drinking).
*   **Don't Wait on Treatment:** Once SAE1+ dermatomyositis is suspected with airway/bulbar threat, initiate pulse steroids + IVIG ASAP while arranging MRI/PET/Muscle Biopsy confirmation later; delays lead to irreversible respiratory failure or permanent disability.
*   **Screen for Malignancy:** Anti-SAE1 positivity carries a significant malignancy risk (~18%); ensure comprehensive cancer screening (CT chest/abd/pelvis + endoscopies) occurs concurrently with treatment initiation per IMACS guidelines.
