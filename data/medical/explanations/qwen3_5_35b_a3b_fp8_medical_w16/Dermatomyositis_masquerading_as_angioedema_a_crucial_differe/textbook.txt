Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Clinical Presentation: The Masquerade of Neck Swelling

The initial presentation of this case underscores a classic diagnostic challenge in internal medicine: distinguishing between common, life-threatening emergencies and rare autoimmune presentations. A middle-aged male arrived at the emergency department with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness. The clinical picture immediately raised suspicion for Ludwig's angina, a deep neck space infection characterized by rapid spread of cellulitis that threatens the airway. Consequently, the patient underwent urgent imaging, which confirmed diffuse oedema involving the strap muscles and extension into the retropharyngeal space. However, several key features diverged from a typical infectious etiology. The patient was afebrile despite significant inflammatory markers (CRP 55.6 mg/L) and leukocytosis was absent (WBC 9.1×10⁹/L). Furthermore, the lack of response to conservative management and the persistence of swelling without signs of sepsis prompted a shift away from an infectious diagnosis toward an inflammatory or allergic process.

Following clearance by Ear, Nose, and Throat (ENT) specialists who ruled out structural obstruction or abscess formation via flexible nasendoscopy, the working diagnosis shifted to idiopathic angioedema. This classification is particularly dangerous because it assumes a histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated mechanism that does not respond to corticosteroids alone or requires specific antidotes like C1 esterase inhibitor replacement if hereditary angioedema is suspected. In this case, the "idiopathic" label was inappropriate given the absence of triggers and the failure of standard protocols to resolve symptoms. The pivotal moment in diagnostic reasoning occurred when clinicians recognized that true angioedema rarely presents with concurrent proximal muscle weakness or such extensive subcutaneous oedema confined to muscle planes without urticaria or pruritus. The atypical distribution—specifically the involvement of skeletal muscles in the neck rather than loose connective tissue—and the lack of response to antihistamines served as critical red flags suggesting an underlying myopathic process rather than an allergic reaction.

A meticulous physical examination ultimately provided the missing link between neck swelling and systemic inflammation. While classic cutaneous stigmata such as Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash were absent, closer inspection revealed subtle but pathognomonic signs often overlooked in acute settings: periungual erythema and nailfold microhaemorrhages visible on capillaroscopy. These vascular changes are hallmarks of dermatomyositis, reflecting microvascular damage characteristic of the disease's pathophysiology. Concurrently, the examiner identified reduced power in hip flexors (graded 4/5), indicating proximal myopathy that explained both the leg weakness complaints and potentially contributed to swallowing difficulties through bulbar muscle involvement. These findings transformed the clinical narrative from a localized head-and-neck issue to a systemic inflammatory myopathy affecting multiple organ systems simultaneously.

Finally, the presence of dysphagia must be recognized not merely as a symptom but as a cardinal early indicator of bulbar involvement in inflammatory myopathies. In this patient, dysphagia preceded significant motor deficits in some contexts and was later confirmed by barium swallow studies showing severe pharyngeal residue and aspiration risks. Bulbar dysfunction occurs due to direct infiltration and necrosis of striated muscles in the pharynx and esophagus, distinct from neurogenic causes like myasthenia gravis where fatigability is more prominent than fixed weakness or painless swelling. The coexistence of dysphagia with unexplained neck swelling should invariably trigger an immediate check for creatine kinase (CK) levels; in this case, CK was markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L, confirming massive muscle breakdown consistent with dermatomyositis rather than simple oedema or infection. This constellation of findings—neck swelling mimicking infection or allergy, subtle nailfold changes, proximal weakness, and dysphagia—highlights why dermatomyositis remains a crucial differential diagnosis for "unexplained" soft tissue swellings in adult patients.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: From CK Elevation to Autoantibody Specificity

The diagnostic journey for this patient began with a critical pivot triggered by the magnitude of the creatine kinase (CK) elevation. Initially, the clinical picture of progressive neck swelling and dysphagia strongly suggested an otorhinolaryngologic etiology, such as a deep neck space infection or idiopathic angioedema. However, the decision to measure CK was driven by the "atypical" nature of the edema—specifically its persistence without fever, lack of response to standard antibiotics, and inconsistency with classic histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated mechanisms. The resulting CK level of 16,184 U/L served as a definitive red flag that could not be explained by soft tissue infection or simple vascular leakage. Such profound elevations indicate massive skeletal muscle necrosis and membrane instability, effectively redirecting the differential diagnosis from an anatomical airway obstruction to a systemic inflammatory myopathy. This single data point forced a reconsideration of the initial working diagnosis, shifting the focus toward conditions causing severe muscle injury, thereby distinguishing true dermatomyositis from mimics like cellulitis or Ludwig's angina where CK would remain normal or only mildly elevated.

Once muscle injury was confirmed serologically, the diagnostic strategy narrowed to identifying the specific underlying autoimmune driver through myositis-specific antibody panels. While general screening tools like ANA provided supportive evidence with a high titer (1:1280), they lacked specificity for differentiating between various forms of inflammatory myopathy. The pivotal finding here was the strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme 1 (anti-SAE1) antibodies. Unlike anti-Jo-1 antibodies, which are classically associated with antisynthetase syndrome featuring prominent interstitial lung disease and arthritis, or anti-Mi-2 antibodies linked to classic cutaneous dermatomyositis with heliotrope rashes and Gottron's papules, anti-SAE1 represents a distinct subset often characterized by severe dysphagia and frequent cutaneous involvement that may be subtle or atypical. In this case, the absence of classic signs like Gottron's papules made the serological identification of SAE1 essential for confirming the diagnosis of dermatomyositis over immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy or inclusion body myositis. Furthermore, ruling out other antibodies helped clarify that this was not a paraneoplastic syndrome driven by anti-TIF1-γ, although malignancy screening remained prudent given the patient's demographic and severity.

Advanced imaging played a complementary yet distinct role in validating the extent of muscle involvement while simultaneously excluding life-threatening comorbidities such as malignancy or abscess formation. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thighs provided anatomical precision, revealing active inflammation specifically within proximal muscle groups like the obturator internus and iliopsoas in a perifascicular distribution. This pattern is highly characteristic of dermatomyositis and helps distinguish it from metabolic myopathies or neuropathic causes which typically spare these specific patterns. Concurrently, positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) scanning offered functional assessment across all muscle groups visible in Figure 3, demonstrating diffuse hypermetabolism consistent with widespread active myositis rather than focal infection. Crucially, this whole-body scan also served as a robust exclusion tool for occult malignancy; unlike some paraneoplastic syndromes where tumors drive cytokine release causing swelling here showed no metabolically active lesions outside the inflamed musculature. This distinction allowed clinicians to confidently attribute the neck swelling directly to inflammatory infiltration rather than tumor compression or metastatic spread.

Finally, rigorous exclusion criteria were applied to definitively rule out alternative causes of angioedema and systemic infection that could mimic this presentation. The normal C3 and C4 complement levels and preserved C1 esterase function immediately excluded hereditary angioedema and hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis syndromes, both of which rely on complement consumption for their pathogenesis. Similarly, extensive infectious workups—including negative cultures for common pathogens like *Staphylococcus* and *Streptococcus*, negative viral serologies for HIV and hepatitis viruses, and negative acid-fast bacilli smears—effectively dismissed deep neck space infections such as Ludwig's angina or tuberculous sialadenitis despite the elevated CRP which reflects sterile inflammation rather than sepsis in this context. By systematically eliminating these alternatives through targeted laboratory testing and ensuring no structural anomalies were found on nasendoscopy or CT neck imaging unrelated to muscle inflammation, the team established a confident diagnosis of anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis presenting as pseudoangioedema.

## Pathophysiology and Histopathological Correlation

The fundamental pathophysiology of dermatomyositis centers on a humoral immune response directed against the microvasculature of skeletal muscle and skin, distinct from the T-cell mediated processes seen in polymyositis or inclusion body myositis. In this specific case, the patient's dramatic neck swelling and proximal weakness stem from complement-mediated endothelial injury. Autoantibodies bind to vascular endothelium, triggering the activation of the classical complement pathway. This results in the formation of the terminal membrane attack complex (MAC), composed of components C5b-9, which deposits directly onto the capillary walls surrounding muscle fascicles. As evidenced by the patient's biopsy showing C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, this localized ischemic insult leads to selective necrosis and atrophy of muscle fibers located at the periphery of the fascicle—a hallmark known as perifascicular atrophy. The resulting disruption in muscle integrity manifests clinically as the profound proximal weakness and elevated creatine kinase levels observed in this patient.

The presence of Anti-Small Ubiquitin-Like Modifier Activating Enzyme 1 (SAE1) antibodies provides a specific molecular context for this patient's phenotype. These autoantibodies target a subunit of an enzyme responsible for sumoylation, a post-translational modification process regulating protein stability and transcriptional activity. While SAE1-positive dermatomyositis shares many features with other forms of inflammatory myopathy, it is increasingly recognized for a distinct clinical spectrum that includes severe dysphagia and interstitial lung disease, even in the absence of classic cutaneous signs like Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash. In this case, the absence of these "pathognomonic" skin lesions contributed to the initial misdiagnosis as angioedema or deep neck infection. However, the underlying mechanism remains consistent: widespread microvascular compromise driven by autoimmunity affects not only limb musculature but also critical structures involved in swallowing and respiration, explaining the patient's severe pharyngeal dysphagia and pulmonary findings despite minimal overt skin rash beyond subtle periungual erythema.

Histopathological analysis serves as the definitive bridge between these clinical observations and cellular pathology. The muscle biopsy obtained from this patient revealed foci of necrotic, vacuolated degenerate myofibers alongside regenerating fibers arranged in a perifascicular distribution. Crucially, the presence of scattered perimysial inflammation consisting of histiocytes confirms an active inflammatory process rather than simple dystrophic change. When viewed alongside immunohistochemical staining demonstrating MAC (C5b-9) deposition specifically within perifascicular capillaries, these findings provide irrefutable evidence supporting a diagnosis of dermatomyositis over other myopathies where such vascular injury is absent or differentially distributed. This microscopic picture validates that the massive soft tissue swelling was not merely fluid accumulation due to allergic or hormonal causes but represented actual inflammatory infiltration and edema secondary to active muscle fiber destruction and vascular leakage within the neck strap muscles.

Finally, understanding why this condition mimicked angioedema requires distinguishing between true histaminergic/bradykinin-mediated swelling and "pseudoangioedema." True angioedema involves rapid vasodilation and increased vascular permeability mediated by mast cells or kinins, typically resolving quickly with antihistamines or C1-inhibitor replacement. In contrast, this patient's neck swelling was caused by direct inflammation and edema within inflamed muscle bellies (the strap muscles) extending into retropharyngeal spaces due to cytokine release from activated macrophages and lymphocytes infiltrating damaged tissue. Because this swelling arises from structural muscle inflammation rather than transient plasma extravasation triggered by allergens or bradykinin pathways, it fails to respond to standard angioedema therapies like epinephrine or corticosteroids alone until potent immunosuppression targets the root cause: the ongoing complement-mediated microangiopathy. Recognizing this distinction is vital; treating pseudoangioedema as idiopathic angioedema delays life-saving intervention for a rapidly progressing autoimmune crisis affecting airway patency and swallowing function.
