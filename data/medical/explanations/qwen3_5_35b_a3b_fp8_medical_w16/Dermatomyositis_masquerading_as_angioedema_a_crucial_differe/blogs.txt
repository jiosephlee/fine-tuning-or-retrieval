Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# When the Neck Swells Without Wheeze: Dermatomyositis Masquerading as Angioedema

Imagine an ED presentation where the patient looks like they are about to lose their airway. A middle-aged man walks in with three weeks of progressive, massive neck swelling. Your mental checklist immediately flashes *Ludwig's angina*, *retropharyngeal abscess*, or perhaps *idiopathic angioedema*. You order a CT neck, call ENT, and prepare for the intubation team. Then, you notice something odd: the swelling is diffuse, non-tender, and involves the strap muscles rather than a discrete fluid collection. The patient isn't febrile, he's not stridor-ing, but he does complain of "weakness" lifting his legs.

If you stop here and treat him as an allergic reaction or an infection, you've missed a life-threatening autoimmune storm. This case highlights a dangerous diagnostic pitfall: **non-histaminergic, non-bradykinin-mediated neck swelling** caused by severe inflammatory myositis masquerading as dermatomyositis.

### The Red Herrings: Why We Get Tricked
The classic presentation of dermatomyositis (DM) involves the "heliotrope" rash, Gottron's papules, and proximal weakness. However, up to 6% of DM patients present with subcutaneous edema ("pseudoangioedema"), often localized to the face or neck. In this specific case report, a male in his 40s presented with severe neck edema that led ENT surgeons to rule out deep space infection only to find him labeled with "suspected idiopathic angioedema."

The trap lies in the assumption that rapid-onset swelling must be vascular/histaminergic (allergy) or bradykinin-mediated (ACE-inhibitor use or hereditary angioedema). Here was a man who had never taken ACE inhibitors, had normal C1 esterase levels, and normal complement levels. The swelling wasn't fluid shifting into tissues due to permeability changes; it was **muscle inflammation**. The CT scan revealed diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space—essentially inflamed muscle acting like a mass effect.

Without recognizing this mimicry, the patient would have been subjected to unnecessary antihistamines, epinephrine trials, or even tracheostomies while his underlying pathology progressed unchecked.

### The Clues Hidden in Plain Sight
So how did this team break through the noise? They didn't rely on standard angioedema workups alone; they looked for the "zebras" hidden in the mundane physical exam. While the classic rashes (Gottron's papules) were absent—a phenomenon known as "amyopathic" or "hypomyopathic" variants which can still occur—the examiner spotted subtle clues that should trigger your internal alarm bell:

*   **Nailfold Capillaroscopy:** The patient had generalized erythema of the face but also specific nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas. These are hallmarks of connective tissue disease (CTD), specifically dermatomyositis and scleroderma spectrum disorders.
*   **Proximal Weakness:** Despite complaining mostly about neck swelling, he had hip flexor weakness (4/5). This is often overlooked until forced into history taking because patients attribute it to fatigue or aging until it becomes profound dysphagia or gait instability.
*   **Dysphagia:** Increased secretions and coughing during nasendoscopy hinted at bulbar involvement before it became obvious to the swallowing clinic later on video fluoroscopy.

When these subtle signs appeared alongside atypical neck edema in a non-allergic patient, the decision was made to check Creatine Kinase (CK). The result? A staggering **16,184 U/L**. That number alone screams myositis over allergy or infection. Further serology confirmed Anti-SAE1 positivity and MRI/PET scans showing diffuse muscle uptake confirming active myositis rather than just fluid retention.

### Management Implications
Treating pseudoangioedema from dermatomyositis requires aggressive immunosuppression, not steroids alone if severe airway compromise exists immediately. This patient received high-dose pulse IV methylprednisolone followed by IVIG (2g/kg loading dose), Rituximab, and Mycophenolate Mofetil. Notably, Methotrexate was avoided due to concurrent pulmonary restrictions and aspiration pneumonitis risks seen on his CT chest (which showed ground-glass opacities from aspiration).

The outcome underscores a vital teaching point: If you see unexplained neck swelling with elevated CK and no response to standard angioedema protocols, think inflammatory myopathy immediately. Delay leads to permanent muscle damage and potentially fatal airway collapse from laryngeal muscle infiltration or severe aspiration pneumonia.

### Key Take-Home Points
*   **Suspect Myositis in Unexplained Edema:** Any case of "idiopathic" or recurrent angioedema that lacks response to antihistamines/epinephrine/C1-INH replacement warrants a CK check and a full skin/nail exam for connective tissue disease signs.
*   **Look Beyond Classic Rashes:** Absence of Heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules does not rule out Dermatomyositis; look for periungual erythema, nailfold capillary dropout/microhemorrhage ("Holster sign"), and proximal weakness/dysphagia as primary clues.
*   **Check Complement & C1 Esterase Early:** Normal C3/C4 and functional C1 esterase help pivot away from Hereditary/Angioedema toward inflammatory etiologies when combined with muscular symptoms.
*   **Antibody Specificity Matters:** Anti-SAE1 positive DM carries a distinct phenotype; while often associated with cutaneous features (>80%), it can present with significant dysphagia and interstitial lung disease (ILD), necessitating careful pulmonary monitoring during treatment initiation.
*   **Airway Safety First:** Inflammatory swelling of strap muscles can mimic mass lesions; secure airway assessment early if bulbar dysfunction (dysphagia/coughing) is present alongside neck edema before discharging home on observation protocols for allergies.



# The Anti-SAE1 Phenotype: When Dysphagia Precedes the Rash

Let's play a game of "What am I?" You have a middle-aged man walking into the ED with a massive, progressive neck swelling. He looks like he has Ludwig's angina or perhaps a catastrophic allergic reaction. You've ruled out the infection, cleared him with ENT, and now you're staring at a picture that screams "idiopathic angioedema." But here is the twist: he isn't wheezing, he isn't hives, and his complement levels are normal. Instead, he has a CK of 16,000+ and profound dysphagia.

This case from Chan et al. serves as a masterclass in looking past the obvious. We often treat dermatomyositis (DM) as a skin-first disease where the rash leads us to the muscle weakness later. However, this patient highlights a distinct serological phenotype—**Anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis**—where the clinical narrative flips. Here, soft tissue edema and severe dysphagia are the heralds, often appearing before the classic Gottron's papules or heliotrope eruption make their debut. If you miss this window because you're fixated on ruling out anaphylaxis or deep neck space infections, you delay life-saving immunosuppression for a patient who is essentially drowning in his own inflamed tissues.

### The "Angioedema" Mimic

The most dangerous pitfall in this scenario is misdiagnosing the neck swelling as histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. As detailed in the case report, this patient was initially sent to ENT for suspected deep neck infection and then flagged for idiopathic angioedema workups. The reality? It was **myositis-driven pseudoangioedema**.

The mechanism here is fascinating but clinically straightforward: inflammation within the strap muscles and surrounding fascia leads to massive subcutaneous edema. This isn't vascular leakage mediated by mast cells; it's inflammatory infiltration mimicking fluid overload or allergic reactions. The key differentiator should always be your eyes and your ears (CK):
*   **The Look:** Generalized erythema of the face and neck, but *not* the classic urticaria of true angioedema.
*   **The Feel:** Non-tender swelling (often unlike cellulitis).
*   **The Sound:** Coughing during exam due to pharyngeal involvement and secretions, hinting at bulbar dysfunction before frank aspiration occurs.
*   **The Lab:** A CK that is sky-high (this patient hit ~16k U/L) immediately shifts the differential away from ENT emergencies toward inflammatory myopathies.

### Enter Anti-SAE1: The Edematous Profile

Why does this matter? Because not all dermatomyositis is created equal. While classic teaching emphasizes Anti-TIF1-γ for malignancy risk and Anti-Jo-1 for interstitial lung disease (ILD), **Anti-SAE1** carries its own unique signature profile that dictates our management strategy.

Literature suggests that Anti-SAE1 DM presents with a higher frequency of **dysphagia** (approx. 46%) and **soft tissue edema** compared to other myositis subsets. In fact, this specific antibody is increasingly recognized as a driver of severe esophageal involvement independent of ILD severity in some cohorts (though ILD remains common). In our case patient, despite having no significant pulmonary fibrosis on initial CT, he suffered from severe pharyngeal dysphagia leading to aspiration pneumonitis—a direct consequence of muscle weakness in the swallowing apparatus rather than parenchymal lung disease alone.

Crucially, unlike some other phenotypes where skin changes are overt at presentation, SAE1 cases can be "rashi-less" initially or present with only subtle clues like periungual erythema or microhemorrhages on nailfold capillaroscopy (which was positive in our patient). Relying solely on finding Gottron's papules might lead to diagnostic delay when those signs haven't erupted yet.

### Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

When evaluating a patient with unexplained neck swelling or dysphagia who fails standard workups for infection or allergy:

*   **Think Myositis Before Angioedema:** If a patient has "idiopathic" neck swelling without urticaria or bronchospasm, check Creatine Kinase immediately. High CK + Neck Swelling = Inflammation until proven otherwise.
*   **Look for Subtle Skin Clues:** Even if Gottron's papules aren't there yet, examine the nails meticulously for periungual erythema or telangiectasia ("Holster sign") and look for generalized facial erythema over nasolabial folds. These can be the first visible signs of SAE1-positive DM before full-blown rashes develop.
*   **Assess Swallowing Early:** Dysphagia in DM is not just about comfort; it predicts aspiration risk rapidly. Do not wait for barium swallows if clinical suspicion is high; consider early speech pathology consults if bulbar symptoms emerge alongside myalgia or weakness.
*   **Know Your Antibodies:** Recognizing Anti-SAE1 positivity alerts you to a phenotype prone to severe soft tissue edema and significant esophageal involvement. While malignancy risk exists (though perhaps slightly lower than TIF1-γ), prioritize aggressive control of inflammation to prevent permanent swallowing dysfunction and airway compromise from edema-induced compression or aspiration pneumonia.
*   **Don't Fear Steroids/Immunomodulators:** Once diagnosed, prompt initiation of high-dose steroids plus steroid-sparing agents (like IVIG or Rituximab as seen in this case) yields excellent outcomes even in severe presentations with massive tissue swelling that took weeks to resolve initially but responded dramatically once treated correctly.

This case reminds us that medicine rarely follows a textbook algorithm perfectly on day one. Sometimes, the "swollen neck" isn't an infection; sometimes it's an autoimmune storm hiding behind a mask of angioedema. By keeping anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis on your differential list for unexplained edematous presentations with elevated CKs, you can turn a confusing ENT referral into a definitive diagnosis before irreversible damage sets in.



# Beyond the Skin: Decoding Subcutaneous Edema in Inflammatory Myopathies

Let's talk about a diagnostic trap that can send even seasoned clinicians chasing ghosts: the patient who presents with "angioedema" that just won't quit. You've got your classic workup—normal C1 esterase, normal complement levels, no ACE inhibitors on board—and yet, the swelling persists. We often default to idiopathic angioedema or hunt for obscure allergens, but sometimes the answer lies not in the mast cells, but in the muscle fibers themselves.

Consider a man in his 40s walking into the ED with three weeks of progressive neck swelling and hip weakness. To the uninitiated eye, this screams deep neck space infection or perhaps a bizarre case of idiopathic angioedema. He gets cleared by ENT; CT shows diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space, but no abscess. He's sent to immunology for presumed angioedema. But here is where the story pivots: **the CK is off the charts.**

This case of anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis serves as a stark reminder that "pseudo-angioedema" is a real entity in inflammatory myopathies. Unlike true histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, this swelling isn't driven by vascular permeability from mediator release; it is driven by profound inflammatory infiltration of skeletal muscle tissue. When the inflammation hits the cervical strap muscles, it mimics life-threatening airway compromise without ever touching the mucosa directly.

## Why Antihistamines Don't Work Here

The pathophysiological distinction is critical for management. In standard angioedema, whether allergic (histamine-driven) or hereditary/acquired (bradykinin-driven), we treat with epinephrine, antihistamines, corticosteroids (sometimes), and specific blockers like icatibant or C1-INH concentrates.

In this case, those interventions would have been futile because the mechanism is entirely different. The edema resulted from **myositis-induced inflammation** causing fluid shift and tissue expansion within the muscle bellies themselves. As seen in our patient, despite normal complement levels and negative C1 esterase testing (ruling out hereditary/acquired angioedema), the underlying driver was necrotizing inflammation confirmed later by muscle biopsy showing perifascicular atrophy and complement deposition (C5b-9).

When you see a patient with "idiopathic" neck swelling that fails to respond to standard allergy protocols, you must stop thinking about histamine receptors and start thinking about creatine kinase levels. The presence of subcutaneous edema in dermatomyositis is rare—reported in only ~6% of cohorts—but when it happens, it creates a clinical picture almost indistinguishable from an airway emergency until you check the labs.

## The Clues Hidden in Plain Sight

What tipped us off? It wasn't just the massive CK of 16,000 U/L; it was the subtle physical exam findings that are easily missed when you are fixated on a swollen neck:
*   **Nailfold Capillaroscopy:** Periungual erythema and microhemorrhages were present before any rash appeared on the face or trunk.
*   **Proximal Weakness:** While he complained of neck swelling first, he had reduced hip flexor power (4/5) and difficulty rising from a chair—a classic red flag for inflammatory myopathy masked by painless edema.
*   **"Gottron's Sign":** Even without full-blown Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash immediately visible, subtle erythematous changes on the lateral thigh and face were present upon closer inspection by immunology.
*   **Dysphagia:** Increased secretions and coughing during nasendoscopy pointed toward bulbar muscle involvement long before swallowing mechanics were formally assessed by barium swallow (which eventually confirmed severe pharyngeal dysphagia).

The MRI findings sealed it: active inflammation not just in peripheral limbs but prominently in head and neck muscles (deltoids, chest wall, abdomen). This wasn't fluid leaking from vessels; it was muscle screaming with inflammation.

## Treatment Implications: Immunosuppression Over Epinephrine

Once we recognized this as dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema, the treatment paradigm shifted instantly from supportive care to aggressive immunomodulation. This patient received high-dose IV methylprednisolone pulses followed by IVIG (2 g/kg total), rituximab, and mycophenolate mofetil—the exact protocol needed for severe inflammatory myopathy with organ involvement (specifically dysphagia and lung issues).

The outcome was dramatic: marked reduction in cervical swelling once inflammation subsided, normalization of CK within weeks, and eventual return of swallowing function after months of NG tube support. If we had treated him solely as an angioedema patient with steroids alone or tried to intubate based on false assumptions of upper airway obstruction without addressing the underlying myositis progression? The result could have been tragic given his aspiration pneumonitis risk.

It is worth noting that this patient tested positive for **anti-SAE1 antibodies**. While anti-MDA5 and anti-TIF1γ are more commonly associated with skin-limited disease or malignancy respectively, SAE1 positivity carries a specific phenotype often including severe dysphagia and interstitial lung disease risks (though this particular patient's lung findings were largely aspirational). Regardless of antibody subtype, if you suspect pseudo-angioedema due to myositis, you must screen for malignancy aggressively given his high-risk status per IMACS guidelines (male sex + dysphagia + DM diagnosis = high cancer risk).

## Take-Home Points for Emergency Clinicians & Intensivists

*   **Refractory Angioedema?** If a patient presents with neck/facial swelling that looks like angioedema but lacks urticaria/hives and fails to respond to antihistamines/epinephrine/C1-INH replacement within hours/days, order an immediate Creatine Kinase (CK) level before calling ENT back up again!
*   **Look Downstream:** Always examine hands/nails for periungual erythema/capillary loops and ask specifically about proximal weakness (stairs/rising from chairs) even if they complain primarily about "swelling."
*   **Mechanism Matters:** Remember that subcutaneous edema in dermatomyositis is caused by inflammatory muscle infiltration ("pseudoeurp"), not mast cell degranulation or bradykinin release; therefore, standard allergy treatments will fail.
*   **Airway Safety First:** Cervical myositis can mimic Ludwig's angina or tracheal compression on CT/MRI due to muscle bulk expansion; secure airways carefully as intubation may be difficult due to restricted movement rather than external mass effect alone.
*   **Antibody Panels Matter:** In confirmed cases of suspected inflammatory myopathy presenting atypically with edema/dysphagia/DNA-negative ANA patterns specific panels (including Anti-SAE1) should be ordered promptly to guide therapy and prognostication regarding ILD/malignancy risk.



# The Silent Airway: When "Angioedema" is Actually Bulbar Paralysis

Let's play a quick game of "What's the Diagnosis?" You walk into the ED with a patient in their 40s who presents with a massive, progressive neck swelling. They've got dysphagia, they're coughing up secretions, and their CT shows diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space. Your brain immediately flashes red flags for **Ludwig's Angina** or **Deep Neck Space Infection**. But wait—no fever, no leukocytosis, no pain on palpation. Now you pivot to **Acquired Angioedema**, maybe even idiopathic bradykinin-mediated swelling?

Here's where the trap snaps shut: What if neither of those is the culprit? What if this isn't an allergic reaction or a bacterial abscess, but rather **severe inflammatory myositis** mimicking angioedema?

This case report highlights a terrifyingly common diagnostic pitfall: mistaking the profound proximal muscle weakness and edema of dermatomyositis for an ENT emergency or primary angioedema. The stakes couldn't be higher because missing this diagnosis means missing the window to save the airway before it collapses from bulbar paralysis.

## The "Silent" Aspiration Trap

The most insidious aspect of this case wasn't just the neck swelling; it was the **silent aspiration** occurring in plain sight.

When we think of dysphagia in an acute setting, we often look for mechanical obstruction or obvious neurological deficits like stroke. However, in dermatomyositis, the pathology lies in the striated muscles responsible for swallowing—the pharyngeal constrictors and upper esophageal sphincter. These are proximal muscles that can fail catastrophically without much warning.

In our patient, a flexible nasendoscopy revealed *anatomically* normal structures (soft palate, vallecula, larynx looked fine), yet he was coughing violently during the exam and producing excessive secretions. This is classic for **bulbar dysfunction**. The subsequent video fluoroscopic swallow study confirmed the disaster waiting to happen: severe pyriform fossae residue and immediate post-swallow penetration of thin fluids into the vocal cords.

This is "silent" because the patient might not be choking loudly enough to trigger immediate panic until they develop hypoxic episodes or aspiration pneumonia—which he already had (patchy peribronchial consolidation). The lesson here is profound: **Anatomic integrity does not equal functional competence.** If a patient has unexplained neck swelling plus any hint of dysphagia or wet voice quality, assume airway muscle weakness until proven otherwise. Don't rely solely on visualizing the anatomy; test its function immediately.

## Why CK Matters More Than Complements

The turning point in this case was a single lab order that changed everything: **Creatine Kinase (CK)**.

While reviewing patients with suspected angioedema or deep neck infections, we often get bogged down checking C3/C4 levels and C1 esterase function (which were all normal here). We chase viral cultures and antibiotics. But when faced with "idiopathic" neck edema that doesn't fit the histaminergic or bradykinin mold, your gut should scream **"Check CK."**

Why? Because in this scenario, the "swelling" isn't fluid leaking from capillaries due to mast cells; it's inflammatory edema within inflamed muscle fibers themselves. This patient's CK skyrocketed to nearly 16,000 U/L—a level that screams rhabdomyolysis or severe myositis far more than angioedema ever could. The presence of periungual erythema and nailfold microhaemorrhages were subtle clues that should have triggered this workup earlier, but let's be honest: in a busy ED under pressure for an airway issue, those skin findings can easily be missed unless you know exactly what you're looking for (the Holster sign).

Once CK is elevated in this context, you don't wait days for a rheumatology consult to start thinking about immunosuppression; you call them *now*. Time is muscle—and time is airway patency.

## Aggressive Immunosuppression: It's Not Just Steroids Alone

So, once you diagnose anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis causing life-threatening bulbar weakness and airway compromise, how do you treat it? This isn't a case where low-dose prednisone will suffice while you watch and wait. We are dealing with potential respiratory failure due to diaphragm or phrenic nerve involvement alongside bulbar collapse.

The management strategy here needs to be aggressive and multimodal:
*   **High-Dose Pulse Steroids:** Immediate administration of IV methylprednisolone (1g/day) is standard to blunt the acute inflammatory storm rapidly reducing muscle edema and improving strength quickly enough to secure breathing patterns before intubation becomes necessary (if it hasn't happened already).
*   **IVIG Loading:** Intravenous Immunoglobulin acts fast as a bridge therapy while waiting for slower-acting agents to kick in. This patient received 2 g/kg over five days followed by maintenance cycles—a heavy lift that correlates with rapid clinical stabilization in refractory myositis cases involving airway compromise.
*   **Steroid-Sparing Agents:** Given the severity and specific antibody profile (SAE1), adding Rituximab and Mycophenolate Mofetil provides long-term control to prevent relapse once steroids are tapered off too quickly. Note specifically why Methotrexate might be avoided here: if there is any suspicion of interstitial lung disease (ILD) or significant pulmonary involvement (as seen by his restrictive pattern), MTX carries risks that might outweigh benefits compared to other agents like MMF or Rituximab.
*   **Nutritional Support:** Do not underestimate the role of nutrition here. With severe dysphagia leading to aspiration risk, placing an NG tube early is not just supportive care; it is an airway protection measure allowing safe caloric intake while preventing further pneumonitis from repeated micro-aspirations during feeding trials.

## Key Take-Home Points for Emergency Medicine & Acute Care Teams

*   **"Angioedema" without wheeze?** If neck swelling persists without response to epinephrine/antihistamines/corticosteroids AND lacks obvious infectious signs (fever/pain), consider inflammatory myopathy as a mimic.
*   **The CK Rule:** Any patient with unexplained soft tissue swelling involving skeletal muscle regions (neck/thighs/chest) requires a Creatine Kinase level immediately; it can distinguish between true angioedema and myositic edema instantly.
*   **Functional vs. Anatomical Dysphagia:** Normal nasendoscopy does not rule out dangerous aspiration if bulbar muscle strength is compromised; proceed directly to videofluoroscopy if clinical suspicion exists ("wet voice," coughing with eating/drinking).
*   **Don't Wait on Treatment:** Once SAE1+ dermatomyositis is suspected with airway/bulbar threat, initiate pulse steroids + IVIG ASAP while arranging MRI/PET/Muscle Biopsy confirmation later; delays lead to irreversible respiratory failure or permanent disability.
*   **Screen for Malignancy:** Anti-SAE1 positivity carries a significant malignancy risk (~18%); ensure comprehensive cancer screening (CT chest/abd/pelvis + endoscopies) occurs concurrently with treatment initiation per IMACS guidelines.
