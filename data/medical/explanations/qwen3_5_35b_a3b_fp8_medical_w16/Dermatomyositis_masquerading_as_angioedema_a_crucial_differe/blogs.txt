# When 'Angioedema' Isn't Angioedema: The Pitfalls of Neck Swelling in Autoimmune Disease

We've all been there: the patient walks into the ED with a rapidly expanding, terrifying neck swelling. Your brain immediately flashes "Ludwig's," "Deep Neck Space Infection," or perhaps "Hereditary/Acquired Angioedema." You call ENT, you grab the CT, you consider the airway. But what happens when the antibiotics don't work, the C1 esterase is normal, and the swelling doesn't respond to epinephrine?

This case report describes a man in his 40s who presented exactly like that. He had a three-week history of progressive neck swelling and proximal hip weakness. Initially, he was flagged for Ludwig's angina and even sent to ENT for a deep neck space workup. After being cleared of infection and structural anomalies, he was labeled as having "idiopathic angioedema" by immunology. The turning point? The attending physician noticed something off about the *nature* of the swelling—it didn't feel like true angioedema—and asked for a Creatine Kinase (CK).

That single lab value changed everything. With a CK of **16,184 U/L**, we weren't dealing with bradykinin; we were dealing with massive muscle destruction. This wasn't histamine-driven hives; it was **Dermatomyositis** masquerading as pseudo-angioedema.

## The Diagnostic Trap: Why Did We Miss It?

The allure of this case lies in how perfectly it hid behind common differentials. Dermatomyositis is rare (approx. 10 per million), but when it hits, it can mimic almost anything. In this specific instance, the patient lacked the classic "textbook" rashes we learn to spot in med school—no Gottron's papules on his knuckles, no obvious heliotrope rash on his eyelids initially. Instead, he presented with generalized erythema of the face and a swollen neck that looked suspiciously like an allergic reaction or an infection.

The key to unlocking this case wasn't just looking harder; it was questioning the mechanism of action. True angioedema involves histamine or bradykinin pathways leading to vascular permeability in loose connective tissue. However, this patient's swelling was driven by **myositis**—specifically inflammation within the strap muscles of the neck and retropharyngeal space extending into soft tissues. This phenomenon is termed "pseudo-angioedema." While rare (only ~6% of dermatomyositis patients exhibit this), it is a known pitfall that can lead to unnecessary intubation attempts or misdirected therapy like C1-inhibitor concentrates if one isn't thinking outside the box.

The clincher here was the combination of **atypical neck edema + proximal weakness**. Even though his hip flexor weakness was initially dismissed as mild myalgia or fatigue, coupled with dysphagia (which turned out to be severe enough to require NG feeding), these should have screamed "neuromuscular" long before they suggested "allergy."

## Beyond the Rashes: Reading Between the Lines

One might argue that without Gottron's papules or a shawl sign, diagnosing dermatomyositis seems impossible on physical exam alone. And technically, yes—you could miss it if you stop looking after clearing an ENT consult. However, closer inspection revealed subtle clues often overlooked in acute settings:
*   **Nailfold Capillaroscopy:** Revealed microhaemorrhages and avascular areas—a hallmark of connective tissue diseases like dermatomyositis and scleroderma.
*   **"Holster Sign":** Erythema on the lateral thigh (often missed unless specifically inspected).
*   **Periungual Erythema:** Subtle redness around the nails that hints at vasculopathy rather than simple allergy.
*   **Dysphagia:** Often underappreciated as a primary symptom until aspiration occurs (this patient had peribronchial consolidation from micro-aspiration).

Once suspicion rose, the labs told a dramatic story beyond just CK elevation (>16k!). The patient tested positive for **Anti-SAEl antibodies** (Small Ubiquitin-Like Modifier Activating Enzyme 1). While Anti-MDA5 and Anti-TIF1-γ are more famously associated with amyloidosis or skin-limited disease respectively, Anti-SAEl positivity is increasingly recognized as a distinct subset often featuring severe dysphagia and significant muscle inflammation without always presenting classic cutaneous signs immediately. The muscle biopsy confirmed perifascicular atrophy with C5b-9 deposition—the smoking gun for dermatomyositis pathology vs polymyositis or necrotizing myopathy.

## Treatment Implications: Don't Reach for Epinephrine First

If you treat this as true angioedema, you are treating a ghost ship with epinephrine and antihistamines while your patient's muscles disintegrate further due to untreated autoimmune activity. This patient required aggressive immunomodulation because his airway compromise was mechanical (muscle edema/inflammation) rather than functional (vasogenic leakage).

His management protocol underscores the severity required for SAE1-positive DM:
*   **High-dose Steroids:** IV Methylprednisolone 1g daily for 3 days ("pulse") followed by oral tapering prednisone up to 60mg/day initially due to airway threat from cervical swelling/dysphagia.
*   **Immunoglobulin (IVIG):** A heavy load totaling nearly 330g over two phases (loading dose + maintenance cycles). IVIG is particularly useful here given its rapid efficacy in refractory myositis cases where steroids alone aren't enough to halt progression quickly enough to protect airways/lungs.
*   **Steroid-Sparing Agents:** Mycophenolate mofetil was chosen over methotrexate due to pre-existing pulmonary concerns (restrictive pattern/nodules), highlighting how comorbidities dictate drug selection even within standard protocols Rituximab was also utilized early given severity markers like malignancy risk stratification by IMACS guidelines (high risk due to age/male/Dysphagia/SAE+ status).
*   **Prophylaxis:** Careful monitoring for PCP pneumonia despite switching TMP-SMX off due to hemolytic anemia complications shows real-world nuance in managing poly-immunosuppressed patients using nebulized pentamidine instead!

The outcome? Remarkable recovery once treated correctly: CK normalized within weeks; swallowing improved allowing decannulation; cervical swelling resolved completely by month 3 despite persisting slightly longer than muscle strength did—a reminder that soft tissue edema can lag behind biochemical resolution in inflammatory myopathies!

# CK > 16,000: Why Creatine Kinase is Your Best Friend in Unexplained Edema

**Lead with the surprise:** Imagine a patient walking into your ED with massive neck swelling. You've ruled out Ludwig's angina, cleared them with ENT, and they're sitting there with "idiopathic" edema. Now, imagine the lab result that turns your entire diagnostic trajectory on its head: **Creatine Kinase (CK) of 16,184 U/L.**

This isn't just a number; it's a siren song screaming "myositis." In a recent case report, a middle-aged man presented with progressive neck swelling and hip weakness. Initially, he was funneled down the path of deep neck space infection and later idiopathic angioedema. It wasn't until the attending physician spotted subtle nailfold changes and ordered a CK—driven by the suspicion that this wasn't classic angioedema—that the diagnosis shifted to **Dermatomyositis**.

Today, we're dissecting why that single test order saved this patient from unnecessary antibiotics, invasive procedures, and potentially fatal airway compromise.

## The "Atypical" Presentation Trap

Let's be honest: Dermatomyositis (DM) loves to wear disguises. While textbooks describe Heliotrope rashes and Gottron's papules as hallmarks, up to 6% of patients present with pseudoangioedema—specifically subcutaneous tissue edema without the classic cutaneous stigmata. When this occurs in the neck or face, it mimics histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema almost perfectly.

Our patient had none of the textbook signs initially. No Heliotrope rash (initially), no Gottron's papules visible on exam. He had diffuse neck edema involving strap muscles extending to the retropharyngeal space. He was afebrile (making infection less likely) but had elevated CRPs (mimicking inflammation/infection).

The trap here is cognitive anchoring on the chief complaint ("swollen neck") rather than the underlying mechanism. If you treat this as pure angioedema or infection without looking deeper, you miss the muscle breakdown happening under the hood. The key differentiator? **The sheer magnitude of muscle injury.**

## The Power of One Test: Why Order CK?

In this case, the turning point was not a fancy MRI or a complex genetic panel—it was a basic metabolic/myositis screen triggered by clinical intuition regarding "atypicality."

Why did this matter so much?
*   **Magnitude Matters:** A CK elevation in DM can vary widely, but seeing levels exceeding 10x-20x normal limits is practically pathognomonic for active myositis in this context. This patient hit **16,184 U/L**.
*   **Ruling Out Mimics:** Normal CK helps rule out inflammatory myopathies but doesn't confirm them alone; however, *massively* elevated CK in an edematous patient essentially rules *out* simple allergic/vasogenic angioedema or localized bacterial abscesses as primary drivers of systemic illness.
*   **The "Silent" Signs:** The case report notes that despite lacking classic rashes initially, there were subtle clues: proximal hip flexor weakness (4/5 power), periungual erythema, and nailfold microhemorrhages on capillaroscopy. These should trigger a reflex action to check muscle enzymes before calling it "idiopathic."

If you see unexplained edema + dysphagia + proximal weakness + negative infectious workup = **Check CK immediately.** Do not wait for the rheumatology consult to happen first; get the data now.

## Beyond CK: The Diagnostic Cascade Once You Know It's Myositis

Once that red flag is raised by a sky-high CK, your differential narrows instantly from ENT emergencies to Autoimmune Inflammatory Myopathies (AIM). Here is how you proceed efficiently:

1.  **Myositis Antibody Panel:** Don't stop at ANA. This patient tested positive for **Anti-SAE1** antibodies (Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier Activating Enzyme). While Anti-MDA5 and Anti-TIF1-γ are often associated with skin-predominant disease or malignancy risk specifically linked to edema/skin ulceration, Anti-SAE1 carries its own unique risks: severe dysphagia and interstitial lung disease (ILD).
    *   *Pearl:* Always consider malignancy screening in new-onset DM patients (especially males >45yo), even if they lack classic cutaneous signs. This patient underwent extensive PET/CT/endoscopy because SAE1+ status places him in an intermediate-to-high risk category for occult cancer.
2.  **Imaging Confirmation:** MRI thighs confirmed active myositis in specific muscle groups (obturator internus/psoas). FDG-PET can show diffuse uptake across muscle groups but must be interpreted carefully to distinguish inflammation from tumor metabolism (though in this case, no malignancy was found).
3.  **Biopsy:** The gold standard remains muscle biopsy showing perifascicular atrophy and C5b-9 deposition—a hallmark of DM distinct from polymyositis or IBM.

## Treatment Implications: Aggressive Immunosuppression is Non-Negotiable

This case serves as a grim reminder that DM presenting with airway/cervical involvement is an emergency waiting to happen due to impending respiratory failure from swallowing dysfunction or laryngeal obstruction from edema.

Standard antihistamines or epinephrine won't touch this pathology because it's not mediated by histamine or bradykinin; it's mediated by complement deposition and T-cell infiltration causing muscle necrosis and fascial fluid shifts.

The treatment protocol for severe cases like this involves:
*   **High-dose Steroids:** IV Methylprednisolone pulses (1g/day) followed by aggressive oral tapering.
*   **Second-Line Agents:** Given the severity (dysphagia requiring NG tube), adding IVIG was crucial alongside Mycophenolate Mofetil and Rituximab. Note that Methotrexate was avoided here due to concurrent ILD concerns—a nuance often missed if treating blindly based on general guidelines rather than individual organ involvement.
*   **Airway Protection:** Dysphagia assessment via videofluoroscopy is mandatory early on to prevent aspiration pneumonia before intubation becomes necessary due to loss of airway protection mechanisms.

## Key Take-Home Points for EM & Primary Care Providers

*   **"Idiopathic Angioedema" is a Diagnosis of Exclusion:** Before labeling neck swelling as idiopathic angioedema in an adult male who hasn't failed ACE inhibitors or has no family history of C1-inhibitor deficiency, always ask about proximal weakness and check CPK/CK-LDH-AST/ALT patterns simultaneously with LFTs/CBC/ESR/CRP screens if available locally without delay!
*   **CK > 500 U/L warrants investigation; CK > 5000 U/L screams Myositis:** In any patient with unexplained edema/muscle pain/swelling where infection/allergy is unlikely, do not hesitate to order CK even if no overt rash is present yet ("Amyopathic" variants exist but true amyopathic DM rarely presents with massive CK spikes like this).
*   **Look Past the Rash:** Classic dermatologic signs may be absent early on ("clinically amyopathic"). Look for nailfold capillary changes (periungual erythema/hemorrhages), proximal weakness on exam (hip flexion resistance), and dysphagia complaints masked as "throat tightness."
*   **Antibody Specificity Drives Management:** Knowing it's Anti-SAE1 positive alerts you specifically to high risks for dysphagia and ILD compared to other myositides like Anti-Jo-1 (more common ILD but different phenotype) or Anti-MDA5 (rapidly progressive ILD/digital ulcers). This guides your monitoring frequency for pulmonary decline and malignancy screening urgency according to IMACS guidelines.
*   **Don't Miss Airway Compromise Early:** Subclinical pharyngeal dilation issues can lead to silent aspiration rapidly once steroids start working too slowly or if airway edema progresses during induction phases of therapy; ensure swallow studies are done early in these cases before discharge planning begins!



# The Anti-SAE1 Signature: Recognizing the Silent Killer in Dermatomyositis

Let's talk about a diagnostic trap that could cost you a life if you miss it. We've all seen the "neck swelling" patient. They walk into the ED, looking panicked, maybe with stridor, and your brain immediately goes to Ludwig's angina, epiglottitis, or perhaps idiopathic angioedema. You call ENT, get a CT, clear them of deep neck space infection, and send them home with antihistamines or C1-inhibitor replacement. But what if the swelling isn't allergic? What if it's not infectious? And what if the real culprit is an autoimmune myositis screaming for attention through a very strange disguise?

This case report brings us a fascinating pivot point in clinical reasoning: **Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema.** Specifically, this involves the often-overlooked **Anti-SAE1** antibody subset. While we are taught to look for Gottron's papules and heliotrope rashes first, this patient presented with profound neck edema and proximal weakness *without* those classic cutaneous hallmarks initially visible to the naked eye. The swelling wasn't histamine-driven; it was inflammatory muscle edema mimicking a surgical emergency. If you don't check the Creatine Kinase (CK), you might miss the boat entirely.

### When "Angioedema" Isn't Angioedema

The patient in our case was a man in his 40s with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling and hip flexor weakness. He was initially flagged for Ludwig's angina. Even after ENT ruled out structural obstruction and infectious etiology, the team leaned toward idiopathic angioedema because he wasn't on ACE inhibitors and had no family history.

Here is where the "FOAM-style" pearl kicks in: **If you see unexplained neck swelling that doesn't fit the classic angioedema profile (normal C4/C1 esterase), think beyond histamine.** This patient had normal complement levels and negative C1 esterase function, yet he still had massive edema. Why? Because in dermatomyositis, particularly with certain antibody subsets, we aren't seeing vascular permeability issues; we are seeing **muscle inflammation**. The CK level here was astronomical—**16,184 U/L**. That is not just "high"; that is muscle breakdown territory comparable to severe rhabdomyolysis or trauma.

The physical exam eventually gave him away, but only upon close inspection:
*   Subtle nailfold changes (periungual erythema) suggesting capillary dropout.
*   Generalized facial erythema involving nasolabial folds.
*   Reduced hip flexor power (4/5).
*   A rash on the thigh (later confirmed as part of the "Holster sign").

Crucially, he lacked Gottron's papules and heliotrope eruptions at presentation. This highlights how deceptive dermatomyositis can be when it presents primarily as **pseudoangioedema**. Only 6-8% of DM patients present with this degree of edema, making it a rare but dangerous mimic of airway emergencies.

### The SAE1 Connection: More Than Just Skin Deep

So, what ties this together? The answer lies in the serology: **Anti-Small Ubiquitin-Like Modifier Activating Enzyme 1 (SAE1)**.

While classic textbooks often highlight Anti-Mi-2 or Anti-Jo-1 antibodies for Dermatomyositis (DM) and Polymyositis (PM), Anti-SAE1 represents a distinct phenotype with its own epidemiological fingerprint. Literature suggests that SAE1-positive DM is uniquely aggressive regarding specific organ systems:
*   **Dysphagia:** Reported in ~46% of SAE1+ cases versus lower rates in other subtypes. Our patient had severe dysphagia leading to aspiration pneumonitis before diagnosis even occurred—a direct result of pharyngeal muscle involvement rather than mechanical obstruction.
*   **Interstitial Lung Disease (ILD):** Occurs in roughly 43% of these patients. Our patient had an extrathoracic restrictive pattern and pulmonary nodules/consolidation on CT, which turned out to be aspiration-related initially but highlighted the lung vulnerability inherent to this subtype.
*   **Malignancy Risk:** This is the scary part. According to IMACS guidelines, SAE1 positivity places patients in a high-risk category for occult malignancy alongside DM status and dysphagia itself (~18% risk overall). While our patient didn't have cancer at presentation (and remains cancer-free at 12 months), the surveillance protocol must be rigorous because SAE1+ DM carries a significant paraneoplastic burden compared to other subtypes like Anti-MDA5 (which leans heavily toward rapidly progressive ILD).

### Why This Matters for Your Practice

The takeaway here isn't just about memorizing antibody names; it's about changing your algorithm for "idiopathic" swelling. When you encounter a patient with neck swelling who fails standard workups for infection or allergy:
1.  **Check CK immediately.** Do not wait for an immunology consult to order this basic metabolic panel extension if muscle pain or weakness is mentioned—even vaguely ("my legs feel heavy," "trouble climbing stairs").
2.  **Look closer at the nails.** Periungual erythema and telangiectasia are often missed unless you are actively hunting for them under magnification or good light during capillaroscopy prep (even without formal equipment).
3.  **Don't ignore dysphagia.** Inflammation of striated muscles can cause swallowing difficulties long before they become obvious choking events on video fluoroscopy studies like our patient's barium swallow did later on.

The treatment pathway for this case was aggressive but necessary: pulse steroids followed by IVIG, Rituximab, and Mycophenolate Mofetil (avoiding Methotrexate due to pre-existing lung concerns). The outcome was excellent once the correct diagnosis was made—the CK normalized within weeks, swallowing returned after two months off NG tubes, and the "swollen neck" vanished completely by month three without leaving permanent deformity or requiring tracheostomy/airway intervention that would have been inevitable if treated as simple angioedema or infection alone.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **"Angioedema" without allergens?** If C4/C1 esterase are normal and there is no response to antihistamines/epinephrine/corticosteroids typical for allergies, broaden your differential to include inflammatory myopathies causing pseudoangioedema via muscle inflammation/oedema rather than vascular leakiness alone.
*   **CK is King:** In any adult presenting with unexplained soft tissue swelling + proximal weakness/dysphagia/myalgia regardless of skin findings—order Creatine Kinase immediately values >5000 U/L should trigger an immediate rheumatology/myositis workup referral regardless of age or gender bias towards older females (this patient was male).
*   **Know your Antibodies:** Anti-SAE1 positivity signals a specific phenotype characterized by high rates of dysphagia (>40%), ILD (~40%), and significant malignancy risk (~20%). It requires aggressive induction therapy (often IVIG/Rituximab) rather than monotherapy steroids alone given its severity profile compared to Mi-2 positive disease which tends to respond better to steroids alone initially.

# Don't Miss the Perifascicular Clue: Biopsy Findings That Seal the Diagnosis

We've all seen the textbook photos: the heliotrope rash, the Gottron's papules, the "shawl" sign. But what happens when your patient presents with massive neck swelling that looks exactly like angioedema, yet they have zero classic skin findings? You're staring down a diagnostic abyss where the most obvious clues are missing.

This case of a middle-aged man presenting with profound neck edema and dysphagia serves as a stark reminder that dermatomyositis (DM) does not always play by the rules. Initially misdiagnosed as a deep neck space infection and later suspected idiopathic angioedema, this patient lacked the hallmark cutaneous signs entirely. Yet, the diagnosis wasn't missed because of a rash—it was sealed by a single slide under the microscope. When clinical intuition fails us, histopathology becomes our anchor. Today, let's dissect why **perifascicular atrophy** and **complement deposition** are the non-negotiable hallmarks that separate DM from its mimics.

## The Pathophysiology Behind the Microscope Slide

To understand why this biopsy was so definitive, we have to look at what distinguishes dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies like polymyositis (PM) or immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM). These conditions often share overlapping clinical features—proximal weakness, elevated CK—but their underlying mechanisms differ fundamentally.

Polymyositis is characterized by CD8+ T-cell mediated invasion of non-necrotic muscle fibers in a *endomysial* distribution. Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy involves direct antibody attack on muscle membranes leading to extensive necrosis with minimal inflammation. However, dermatomyositis is unique because it is primarily a **microvasculopathy**. It is driven by humoral immunity targeting the capillary endothelium of skeletal muscle.

In this case report, despite the absence of Gottron's papules or heliotrope eruption, the muscle biopsy revealed two critical findings that confirmed DM:
1.  **Perifascicular Atrophy:** The biopsy showed necrotic and regenerating fibers specifically located at the edges (periphery) of the muscle fascicles.
2.  **C5b-9 Deposition:** Immunohistochemistry highlighted complement component 5b-9 deposited in perifascicular endomysial capillaries.

Why does this matter? The perifascicular location corresponds to the area furthest from blood supply within a fascicle—the "watershed" zones prone to ischemia when capillaries are occluded by immune complexes and complement activation. This ischemic insult leads to selective atrophy of those peripheral fibers first. If you see endomysial inflammation without perifascicular atrophy, think PM or IMNM. If you see perifascicular atrophy with C5b-9 deposition on capillaries, you have Dermatomyositis, regardless of whether there is skin involvement or not.

## The "Silent" Presentation: Why We Miss It

This patient's case highlights a dangerous trap: relying solely on cutaneous phenotypes to rule out dermatomyositis in patients with myositis symptoms or unexplained edema. Up to 20% of adult-onset DM cases can be "clinically amyopathic," meaning they lack overt skin findings initially or present with subtle signs easily overlooked in an acute ED setting focused on airway compromise.

Our patient had "pseudoangioedema." Unlike true histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema which resolves quickly and lacks muscle pathology, this swelling was caused by severe inflammation and edema within the strap muscles themselves (confirmed by MRI showing diffuse infiltration). The lack of classic rashes led to an initial workup for infectious causes (Ludwig's angina) and immunologic causes (idiopathic angioedema). Even when C1 esterase levels were normal and CT ruled out abscesses, the team hesitated until they saw nailfold changes—a subtle clue—and ordered Creatine Kinase (CK), which skyrocketed to >16,000 U/L.

However, even with high CK and compatible antibodies (anti-SAE1), serology alone isn't enough for definitive classification in ambiguous cases. The biopsy provided the structural proof needed to differentiate this from Anti-SRP or Anti-HMGCR myopathies (which would show pure necrosis without perifascicular patterns) or PM (which lacks vascular injury). As noted in our case discussion, anti-SAE1 positivity correlates strongly with dysphagia and ILD but doesn't guarantee classic skin signs; hence, histology remains gold standard for confirming tissue-level pathology when clinical presentation is atypical.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Messages

When facing a patient with unexplained neck swelling ("angioedema"), proximal weakness, or dysphagia where common etiologies are excluded:

*   **Suspect Pseudoangioedema:** Neck swelling in DM results from inflammatory edema within strap muscles and subcutaneous tissue due to microvascular ischemia; it will not respond to antihistamines or epinephrine and often persists longer than true angioedema episodes.
*   **Look Beyond Classic Rashes:** Absence of Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash does not rule out dermatomyositis; up to 20% may present without cutaneous involvement initially ("amyopathic").
*   **The Biopsy is King:** If clinical suspicion remains high despite negative serology or equivocal exam:
    *   Look for **perifascicular atrophy**: Necrosis/regeneration restricted to fiber bundles' periphery indicates DM-specific ischemia.
    *   Look for **C5b-9 deposition**: Membrane attack complex on perifascicular capillaries confirms complement-mediated vasculopathy distinct from PM or IMNM.
*   **Check Nailfolds Early:** Periungual erythema and capillary dilation can be subtle but highly specific clues in patients lacking truncal rashes; don't skip hand inspection during airway assessments if neck swelling is present.
*   **Don't Forget Dysphagia:** In DM involving head/neck muscles (like our anti-SAE1 positive patient), dysphagia may precede limb weakness; consider barium swallows if tongue/base-of-tongue weakness is suspected during ENT exams.



# Airway Safety First: Managing Dysphagia and Respiratory Compromise in Acute Myositis

We've all seen the "neck swelling" patient. They walk into the ED, look panicked, and scream "Ludwig's Angina!" or "Angioedema!" until they get a CT scan. But what happens when the airway looks fine on pulse oximetry, yet the patient is silently drowning in their own secretions? This case of a middle-aged man presenting with progressive neck swelling serves as a brutal reminder that **muscle inflammation can mimic soft tissue edema**, and the airway threat in myositis isn't always about external compression—it's often about internal paralysis.

This patient didn't just have a swollen neck; he had *pseudoadge* caused by massive inflammation of the strap muscles and pharyngeal constrictors. The terrifying part? He was saturating 99% on room air. If you rely solely on SpO2, you might miss the fact that he is aspirating silently. During his nasendoscopy, he wasn't just having secretions; he was coughing violently because thin fluids were penetrating his vocal cords before he could protect them. This is not an allergy; it's not an infection. It is **myogenic respiratory failure** waiting to happen.

When you see neck swelling without fever or leukocytosis, your differential must expand beyond ENT emergencies. If the patient has proximal weakness—even subtle—and you suspect myositis, **do not wait**. The CK level here was astronomical (16,184 U/L), confirming that the "swelling" was actually inflamed muscle fibers leaking enzymes and fluid into the interstitium. The lesson? In undifferentiated neck swelling with dysphagia, check a Creatine Kinase immediately. If it's sky-high, you aren't dealing with angioedema; you're dealing with a ticking time bomb for aspiration pneumonia and respiratory arrest.

The management strategy here shifts from antihistamines and epinephrine to aggressive immunomodulation and airway protection. Because this patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia confirmed by videofluoroscopy showing pyriform fossae residue and penetration to the vocal cords, we cannot simply send him home on prednisone alone. We need to secure nutrition first—this patient required an NG tube for two months post-discharge because swallowing mechanics were too compromised to trust orally.

Furthermore, we must recognize that standard intubation criteria don't apply here until it's too late. This patient had significant muscle involvement in the head and neck region (confirmed by PET scan showing diffuse uptake). While his vitals were stable initially, the rapid progression of inflammation can lead to sudden loss of airway patency or inability to clear secretions once sedation wears off or disease progresses overnight. Early consultation with Speech Therapy for swallow studies is non-negotiable in these scenarios to quantify aspiration risk before deciding on oral intake versus NPO status with enteral access.

The treatment protocol for this specific presentation requires hitting hard and fast. High-dose IV methylprednisolone (1g/day) was started immediately to quell the acute inflammation causing the "pseudo-angioedema." However, steroids alone often take days to work; hence, the addition of IVIG (loading dose 2g/kg) provided rapid immunomodulation while Rituximab and Mycophenolate were layered in for long-term control. Note that Methotrexate was wisely avoided given his concurrent restrictive lung pattern—a common pitfall in DM patients where ILD risks outweigh benefits if not carefully weighed against toxicity profiles like hepatotoxicity or marrow suppression.

Finally, remember that this isn't just about clearing the throat today; it's about preventing long-term disability and ruling out malignancy. Anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis carries a distinct risk profile where dysphagia correlates strongly with poor outcomes if untreated early enough to prevent fibrosis or chronic aspiration pneumonia. This patient eventually normalized his CK and regained full swallowing function after months of rehab and maintenance therapy, but only because we recognized the red flags early: **dysphagia + neck swelling = Myositis until proven otherwise.** Don't let a normal oxygen saturation fool you into thinking the airway is safe when the muscles controlling it are failing silently beneath your eyes.
