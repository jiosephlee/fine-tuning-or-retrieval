Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation: The Masquerade of Neck Swelling

The initial presentation of this case underscores a classic diagnostic challenge in internal medicine: distinguishing between common, life-threatening emergencies and rare autoimmune presentations. A middle-aged male arrived at the emergency department with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness. The clinical picture immediately raised suspicion for Ludwig's angina, a deep neck space infection characterized by rapid spread of cellulitis that threatens the airway. Consequently, the patient underwent urgent imaging, which confirmed diffuse oedema involving the strap muscles and extension into the retropharyngeal space. However, several key features diverged from a typical infectious etiology. The patient was afebrile despite significant inflammatory markers (CRP 55.6 mg/L) and leukocytosis was absent (WBC 9.1×10⁹/L). Furthermore, the lack of response to conservative management and the persistence of swelling without signs of sepsis prompted a shift away from an infectious diagnosis toward an inflammatory or allergic process.

Following clearance by Ear, Nose, and Throat (ENT) specialists who ruled out structural obstruction or abscess formation via flexible nasendoscopy, the working diagnosis shifted to idiopathic angioedema. This classification is particularly dangerous because it assumes a histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated mechanism that does not respond to corticosteroids alone or requires specific antidotes like C1 esterase inhibitor replacement if hereditary angioedema is suspected. In this case, the "idiopathic" label was inappropriate given the absence of triggers and the failure of standard protocols to resolve symptoms. The pivotal moment in diagnostic reasoning occurred when clinicians recognized that true angioedema rarely presents with concurrent proximal muscle weakness or such extensive subcutaneous oedema confined to muscle planes without urticaria or pruritus. The atypical distribution—specifically the involvement of skeletal muscles in the neck rather than loose connective tissue—and the lack of response to antihistamines served as critical red flags suggesting an underlying myopathic process rather than an allergic reaction.

A meticulous physical examination ultimately provided the missing link between neck swelling and systemic inflammation. While classic cutaneous stigmata such as Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash were absent, closer inspection revealed subtle but pathognomonic signs often overlooked in acute settings: periungual erythema and nailfold microhaemorrhages visible on capillaroscopy. These vascular changes are hallmarks of dermatomyositis, reflecting microvascular damage characteristic of the disease's pathophysiology. Concurrently, the examiner identified reduced power in hip flexors (graded 4/5), indicating proximal myopathy that explained both the leg weakness complaints and potentially contributed to swallowing difficulties through bulbar muscle involvement. These findings transformed the clinical narrative from a localized head-and-neck issue to a systemic inflammatory myopathy affecting multiple organ systems simultaneously.

Finally, the presence of dysphagia must be recognized not merely as a symptom but as a cardinal early indicator of bulbar involvement in inflammatory myopathies. In this patient, dysphagia preceded significant motor deficits in some contexts and was later confirmed by barium swallow studies showing severe pharyngeal residue and aspiration risks. Bulbar dysfunction occurs due to direct infiltration and necrosis of striated muscles in the pharynx and esophagus, distinct from neurogenic causes like myasthenia gravis where fatigability is more prominent than fixed weakness or painless swelling. The coexistence of dysphagia with unexplained neck swelling should invariably trigger an immediate check for creatine kinase (CK) levels; in this case, CK was markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L, confirming massive muscle breakdown consistent with dermatomyositis rather than simple oedema or infection. This constellation of findings—neck swelling mimicking infection or allergy, subtle nailfold changes, proximal weakness, and dysphagia—highlights why dermatomyositis remains a crucial differential diagnosis for "unexplained" soft tissue swellings in adult patients.
