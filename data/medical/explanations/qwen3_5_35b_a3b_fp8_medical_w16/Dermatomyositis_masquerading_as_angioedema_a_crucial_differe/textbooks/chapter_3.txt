Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 3: ## Pathophysiology and Histopathological Correlation

The fundamental pathophysiology of dermatomyositis centers on a humoral immune response directed against the microvasculature of skeletal muscle and skin, distinct from the T-cell mediated processes seen in polymyositis or inclusion body myositis. In this specific case, the patient's dramatic neck swelling and proximal weakness stem from complement-mediated endothelial injury. Autoantibodies bind to vascular endothelium, triggering the activation of the classical complement pathway. This results in the formation of the terminal membrane attack complex (MAC), composed of components C5b-9, which deposits directly onto the capillary walls surrounding muscle fascicles. As evidenced by the patient's biopsy showing C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, this localized ischemic insult leads to selective necrosis and atrophy of muscle fibers located at the periphery of the fascicle—a hallmark known as perifascicular atrophy. The resulting disruption in muscle integrity manifests clinically as the profound proximal weakness and elevated creatine kinase levels observed in this patient.

The presence of Anti-Small Ubiquitin-Like Modifier Activating Enzyme 1 (SAE1) antibodies provides a specific molecular context for this patient's phenotype. These autoantibodies target a subunit of an enzyme responsible for sumoylation, a post-translational modification process regulating protein stability and transcriptional activity. While SAE1-positive dermatomyositis shares many features with other forms of inflammatory myopathy, it is increasingly recognized for a distinct clinical spectrum that includes severe dysphagia and interstitial lung disease, even in the absence of classic cutaneous signs like Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash. In this case, the absence of these "pathognomonic" skin lesions contributed to the initial misdiagnosis as angioedema or deep neck infection. However, the underlying mechanism remains consistent: widespread microvascular compromise driven by autoimmunity affects not only limb musculature but also critical structures involved in swallowing and respiration, explaining the patient's severe pharyngeal dysphagia and pulmonary findings despite minimal overt skin rash beyond subtle periungual erythema.

Histopathological analysis serves as the definitive bridge between these clinical observations and cellular pathology. The muscle biopsy obtained from this patient revealed foci of necrotic, vacuolated degenerate myofibers alongside regenerating fibers arranged in a perifascicular distribution. Crucially, the presence of scattered perimysial inflammation consisting of histiocytes confirms an active inflammatory process rather than simple dystrophic change. When viewed alongside immunohistochemical staining demonstrating MAC (C5b-9) deposition specifically within perifascicular capillaries, these findings provide irrefutable evidence supporting a diagnosis of dermatomyositis over other myopathies where such vascular injury is absent or differentially distributed. This microscopic picture validates that the massive soft tissue swelling was not merely fluid accumulation due to allergic or hormonal causes but represented actual inflammatory infiltration and edema secondary to active muscle fiber destruction and vascular leakage within the neck strap muscles.

Finally, understanding why this condition mimicked angioedema requires distinguishing between true histaminergic/bradykinin-mediated swelling and "pseudoangioedema." True angioedema involves rapid vasodilation and increased vascular permeability mediated by mast cells or kinins, typically resolving quickly with antihistamines or C1-inhibitor replacement. In contrast, this patient's neck swelling was caused by direct inflammation and edema within inflamed muscle bellies (the strap muscles) extending into retropharyngeal spaces due to cytokine release from activated macrophages and lymphocytes infiltrating damaged tissue. Because this swelling arises from structural muscle inflammation rather than transient plasma extravasation triggered by allergens or bradykinin pathways, it fails to respond to standard angioedema therapies like epinephrine or corticosteroids alone until potent immunosuppression targets the root cause: the ongoing complement-mediated microangiopathy. Recognizing this distinction is vital; treating pseudoangioedema as idiopathic angioedema delays life-saving intervention for a rapidly progressing autoimmune crisis affecting airway patency and swallowing function.
