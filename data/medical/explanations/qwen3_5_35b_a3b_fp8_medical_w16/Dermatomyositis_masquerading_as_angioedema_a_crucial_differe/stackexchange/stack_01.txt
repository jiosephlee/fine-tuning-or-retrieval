Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Morning Report] In a patient presenting with acute neck swelling where deep neck space infection and angioedema have been ruled out, what specific laboratory value and physical examination finding should immediately raise suspicion for dermatomyositis?

When evaluating a patient with acute neck swelling where life-threatening etiologies like deep neck space infection and classic angioedema have been excluded, the two critical indicators that should immediately trigger suspicion for dermatomyositis are **significantly elevated creatine kinase (CK)** levels and **subtle cutaneous or nailfold abnormalities**.

As illustrated in this case, the patient's neck swelling was actually "pseudoangioedema" resulting from inflammatory edema within the strap muscles rather than true histamine- or bradykinin-mediated vascular leakage. While the initial presentation mimicked an otolaryngologic emergency, the key to diagnosis lay in recognizing that the swelling was accompanied by proximal muscle weakness and specific skin changes. The definitive laboratory red flag was a massively elevated CK level of 16,184 U/L, reflecting severe muscle necrosis and inflammation. Concurrently, a meticulous physical examination revealed subtle but pathognomonic signs often missed in acute settings: **periungual erythema** and **nailfold microhemorrhages** (avascular areas seen on capillaroscopy). Although classic findings like Gottron's papules or a heliotrope rash were absent in this instance, the presence of generalized facial erythema and localized nail changes alongside unexplained neck swelling and dysphagia should prompt immediate measurement of muscle enzymes. Failure to recognize these clues leads to delays in initiating necessary immunosuppressive therapy, whereas early recognition allows for rapid intervention to prevent airway compromise from laryngeal muscle edema.
