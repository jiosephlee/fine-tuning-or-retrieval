Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Pathophysiology] How does the pathophysiological mechanism of 'pseudoangioedema' in dermatomyositis fundamentally differ from that of histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema?

The fundamental difference lies in the underlying mechanism driving the tissue expansion: **histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema results from plasma extravasation driven by vascular permeability**, whereas the "pseudoangioedema" seen in dermatomyositis represents **actual inflammatory infiltration and edema within skeletal muscle tissue**.

In classic angioedema, mast cell degranulation releases histamine, or complement activation generates bradykinin, both of which act directly on post-capillary venules to increase endothelial permeability. This allows fluid to leak into the *interstitium* without cellular infiltration. Consequently, this process is typically rapid-onset, transient (hours), and responds to antihistamines, corticosteroids, or specific antagonists like icatibant. As illustrated in our case, the patient's C3/C4 levels and C1 esterase inhibitor function were normal, effectively ruling out hereditary or acquired angioedema pathways.

Conversely, the swelling in this patient with anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is a manifestation of active myositis. The MRI confirmed diffuse inflammation specifically localized to the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space. The pathophysiology involves T-cell mediated cytotoxicity targeting muscle fibers and deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on perifascicular capillaries, leading to ischemia, necrosis, and a robust inflammatory infiltrate. This process creates true tissue mass from muscle fiber destruction and perimysial inflammation rather than simple fluid leakage. Therefore, unlike true angioedema which resolves quickly once the mediator is blocked, this "swelling" persists until the underlying autoimmune myositis is suppressed with intensive immunosuppression—such as the high-dose steroids and IVIG utilized in this patient—to halt muscle injury and resolve the inflammatory infiltrate. Recognizing this distinction prevents futile attempts at treating with epinephrine or antihistamines and directs immediate attention to checking creatine kinase levels and evaluating for cutaneous signs of dermatomyositis.
