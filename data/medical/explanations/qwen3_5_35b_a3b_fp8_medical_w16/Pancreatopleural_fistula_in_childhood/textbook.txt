Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Clinical Presentation and the 'Pulmonary Mask' Phenomenon

The clinical presentation of this pediatric case exemplifies the deceptive nature of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF), a condition where pancreatic pathology manifests almost exclusively through thoracic symptoms. The patient, a young boy, presented with progressive dyspnea and right-sided chest pain over one week. Physical examination revealed classic signs of significant pleural effusion, including reduced chest expansion on the right, stony dullness to percussion, diminished vocal fremitus, and decreased breath sounds in the right lower zones. His oxygen saturation was critically low at 88%–89% on room air. These findings prompted immediate respiratory intervention, including supplemental oxygen and serial thoracocentesis. Notably, approximately one liter of dark red, hemorrhagic fluid was aspirated during each of the first two admissions. While the temporary relief of symptoms following drainage suggested a mechanical resolution, the rapid recurrence of effusion and respiratory distress after six days signaled a continuous pathological process rather than a simple infectious or inflammatory event. This pattern of recurrent, massive, bloody pleural effusions necessitated a shift from symptomatic management to investigating an underlying etiology that could explain both the volume and the persistence of the fluid.

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates the "pulmonary mask" phenomenon characteristic of PPF. Because the fistulous tract allows pancreatic enzymes and fluid to track superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the pleural space, patients predominantly present with respiratory distress mimicking primary lung diseases such as pneumonia or empyema. In adult populations, alcohol-induced chronic pancreatitis is a common precursor; however, in pediatrics, trauma or genetic predispositions are more frequent triggers. The absence of overt abdominal symptoms—such as epigastric pain or vomiting—is typical in up to 80% of pediatric cases, as noted in broader literature reviews. Consequently, clinicians are often misled into focusing entirely on thoracic pathology while overlooking the abdominal source. In this specific instance, the initial clinical picture strongly suggested an infectious etiology given the patient's history of contact with a grandparent suffering from pulmonary tuberculosis (Koch's disease). Furthermore, biochemical analysis of the pleural fluid revealed slightly elevated Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) levels. Combined with the epidemiological risk factor and radiographic evidence of effusion on chest X-ray, this led to an empirical trial of anti-tubercular therapy (ATT). The failure of ATT to prevent re-accumulation highlighted the limitations of relying solely on surface-level correlations without considering alternative mechanisms for amylase-rich effusions.

The pivotal moment in establishing the correct diagnosis occurred upon obtaining a more granular history during the third admission. When questioned repeatedly about potential antecedents to his illness, the child disclosed a history of blunt abdominal trauma sustained one month prior from striking a bicycle handle against his abdomen—a detail previously unreported due to psychological factors such as fear or shame regarding bullying at school. This revelation reframed the entire clinical narrative: what appeared to be recurrent infectious pneumonia was actually post-traumatic pancreatitis complicated by a ruptured pancreatic duct and subsequent formation of a fistula connecting to the pleural cavity. The characteristics of the pleural fluid provided further confirmation; it was consistently dark red and blood-stained, distinguishing it from typical serous effusions associated with tuberculosis or heart failure. While hemoptysis is rare in PPF compared to pulmonary embolism or malignancy, traumatic disruption can lead to hemorrhagic content mixing with pancreatic secretions. However, it is crucial for learners to recognize that while blood staining raises suspicion for trauma or malignancy, it is not pathognomonic; definitive differentiation relies heavily on enzymatic profiling rather than gross appearance alone.

Ultimately, this case underscores why PPF is frequently misdiagnosed as pulmonary tuberculosis or malignancy in children presenting with recurrent effusions. The combination of TB exposure history and elevated ADA served as potent "red herrings," diverting diagnostic resources toward pulmonary investigations while masking the true pancreatic origin. The key teaching point here is that any child presenting with recurrent pleural effusion—even those without classic abdominal pain—must undergo evaluation for elevated amylase levels once infectious causes are unlikely to resolve spontaneously or respond to targeted therapy. The eventual identification of blunt abdominal trauma serves as a reminder that even subtle histories can hold critical clues when faced with complex presentations where organ systems overlap anatomically via natural diaphragmatic openings like the esophageal hiatus. Recognizing this "pulmonary mask" prevents unnecessary prolonged courses of antibiotics or chemotherapy and directs care toward appropriate imaging modalities like CT and MRCP that visualize both thoracic and retroperitoneal structures simultaneously.

## Pathophysiology of Pancreatopleural Fistula Formation

The development of a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) represents a distinct failure of containment wherein pancreatic secretions breach the anatomical barriers between the retroperitoneum and the thoracic cavity. In the pediatric patient described, the inciting event was blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle, which caused a rupture of the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst in the distal body and tail. Unlike adult populations where chronic alcohol-induced pancreatitis is the predominant driver, pediatric PPF arises primarily from traumatic injury or underlying genetic predispositions involving mutations in genes such as *PRSS1* (cationic trypsinogen) and *SPINK1* (serine peptidase inhibitor Kazal type 1). These genetic factors render the pancreatic ducts more susceptible to injury and inflammation, lowering the threshold for rupture even in the absence of significant trauma, though in this specific case, the mechanical force of trauma was the clear precipitant. Once the integrity of the pancreatic duct is compromised, high-pressure enzymatic fluid seeks the path of least resistance. Because fluid leaking from the anterior aspect of the pancreas typically accumulates in the peritoneal cavity causing ascites, PPF formation almost exclusively involves ruptures on the posterior surface of the gland. Here, fluid tracks posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and follows fascial planes upward toward mediastinal structures.

The critical anatomical pathway facilitating this migration involves passage through natural diaphragmatic hiatuses, most commonly the esophageal or aortic hiatus. As noted in this case's MRI findings, fluid traveled from a retroperitoneal collection near the pancreatic tail, extended superiorly through these hiatuses into the mediastinum, and eventually eroded through or tracked across to reach both pleural spaces, predominantly affecting the right side. This explains why PPF often presents with massive pleural effusions rather than localized intra-abdominal pathology; once inside the pleural space, there is no efficient mechanism to reabsorb large volumes of serous fluid rapidly enough to prevent accumulation. The sheer volume seen in this patient—over one liter aspirated repeatedly—is consistent with continuous secretion driven by active exocrine function. The "dark red" appearance of the pleural fluid observed upon aspiration indicates hemorrhage into the effusion, a hallmark result of proteolytic enzyme activity damaging local vasculature and tissues within both the retroperitoneum and pleura.

Biochemically, this process creates a self-perpetuating cycle of tissue destruction. Pancreatic enzymes released into an alkaline environment like urine or ascitic fluid are activated; however, within an acidic environment like blood or necrotic tissue, they remain latent until triggered by bile salts or low pH changes associated with inflammation. In PPF, these enzymes—specifically amylase and lipase—are highly concentrated in both serum and pleural fluid. The elevation of amylase to levels exceeding 6000 U/L and lipase to over 7000 U/L in this patient serves as biochemical proof that pancreatic juice is directly entering the pleural space. These enzymes exert potent proteolytic effects on surrounding tissues, leading to liquefactive necrosis and vascular erosion that manifests clinically as hemorrhagic effusion and severe chest pain. The persistence of these symptoms despite repeated thoracentesis underscores that simply removing fluid does not address the underlying leak; until fistulous closure is achieved either medically by suppressing exocrine secretion (via octreotide) or surgically/endoscopically repairing ductal defects, continued enzyme leakage ensures recurrent accumulation. Understanding this trajectory—from initial ductal disruption via trauma or genetics, through retroperitoneal-to-thoracic transit via diaphragmatic windows—to explain why PPF mimics primary pulmonary disease while originating entirely from abdominal pathology is essential for avoiding diagnostic delays like those encountered with antitubercular therapy in this case.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Imaging Strategy

The diagnostic journey in this pediatric case illustrates the critical challenge of "pancreatic masking" as a thoracic emergency. Initially, the clinical presentation of recurrent right-sided pleural effusion, chest pain, and respiratory distress led to a working diagnosis of infectious or neoplastic etiologies common in children. The initial thoracocentesis yielded approximately one liter of dark red fluid, a finding that raised immediate suspicion for malignancy or traumatic hemothorax. However, the rapid recurrence of large-volume effusions despite drainage necessitated a re-evaluation of the underlying pathology. The pivotal moment in this diagnostic algorithm occurred with the biochemical analysis of the pleural fluid. While adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels were mildly elevated—prompting an initial trial of anti-tubercular therapy—the defining feature emerged with the measurement of amylase and lipase. A pleural fluid amylase concentration exceeding 1000 IU/L is widely regarded as pathognomonic for a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF). In this specific case, the amylase level reached 6118 U/L, coupled with markedly elevated lipase (7211 U/L), effectively shifting the diagnostic focus from primary pulmonary disease to pancreatic pathology. This biochemical threshold serves as the key pivot point; while other conditions such as esophageal perforation or certain malignancies can elevate amylase, levels approaching those seen here are virtually exclusive to pancreatic leaks in the absence of concurrent salivary gland involvement.

Differentiating the source of this hyperamylasemia is essential to rule out mimics that may present with similarly high amylase levels but require vastly different management strategies. The primary differential diagnoses included tuberculosis, given the patient's contact history and mildly elevated ADA; malignancy, suggested by the hemorrhagic nature of the fluid; Boerhaave's syndrome (esophageal perforation); and hepatic hydrothorax. To distinguish between these, one must consider both clinical context and specific laboratory markers. For instance, TB was unlikely given the negative GeneXpert for *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* and lack of constitutional symptoms like fever or weight loss. Malignancy was excluded through normal blood counts, peripheral smears lacking leukemic features, and negative cytology for malignant cells in multiple aspirations. Crucially, distinguishing pancreatic from salivary amylase is vital because conditions like mumps or parotitis can raise serum and pleural amylase without involving the pancreas. Although not explicitly performed in every center, determining that the elevated enzyme activity was predominantly pancreatic-type (rather than salivary) further solidifies PPF as the diagnosis over systemic salivary disorders or simple mechanical irritation. Furthermore, Boerhaave's syndrome was ruled out by the absence of retching/vomiting prior to onset and normal gastrointestinal transit on history, while hepatic hydrothorax was excluded by normal liver function tests and imaging showing no cirrhosis or portal hypertension.

Imaging played a complementary yet distinct role in confirming anatomical disruption versus suggesting functional abnormalities. Initial cross-sectional imaging with contrast-enhanced CT revealed patchy consolidation and hydropneumothorax but failed to definitively visualize the fistula itself; instead, it showed a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the retroperitoneum toward the diaphragm. This limitation highlights a crucial teaching point: while CT is excellent for identifying complications like pseudocysts, calcifications, or ductal dilation associated with pancreatitis (present sensitivity ~33–47% for fistula visualization), it often misses small communications across tissue planes due to resolution limits or lack of dynamic flow data regarding bile/pancreatic juice leakage into adjacent spaces. Consequently, relying solely on CT could lead to persistent uncertainty regarding whether a thoracic abscess or an occult esophageal tear was responsible for the collection. The definitive confirmation arrived via Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), which demonstrated a direct tubular-cystic connection originating from the distal pancreatic duct and traversing superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. MRCP provides superior soft-tissue contrast compared to CT without ionizing radiation—a significant advantage in pediatrics—and successfully delineated complex fistulous tracts that were invisible on plain radiographs or conventional CT scans.

Ultimately, establishing this diagnosis required synthesizing three convergent lines of evidence: a high-yield clinical clue (history of blunt abdominal trauma concealed by social factors), pathognomonic biochemistry (pleural amylase >1000 IU/L with concomitant lipase elevation), and anatomical confirmation via MRCP tracing the fistula tract from pancreas to pleura. This stepwise reasoning underscores why PPF is frequently misdiagnosed initially; clinicians must maintain a low threshold for measuring pleural enzymes in any unexplained exudative effusion containing high protein content or appearing bloody/multiloculated on imaging. Once biochemical suspicion is raised by extreme elevations in amylase/lipase ratios relative to serum values (typically >2x serum upper limit), advanced imaging becomes mandatory to map the fistula before considering invasive interventions like ERCP or surgery. In this case, recognizing these elements prevented prolonged empiric treatment for tuberculosis and allowed for targeted conservative management aimed at reducing pancreatic secretion rather than treating an infectious pneumonia.

## Therapeutic Management Algorithm: From Conservative to Surgical

The management of a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) in the pediatric population requires a stepwise algorithmic approach, moving from conservative medical therapy to invasive endoscopic or surgical interventions. Given the extreme rarity of PPF in children—with fewer than 50 documented cases—standardized international guidelines are absent. Consequently, current practice relies on extrapolating data from adult cohorts and synthesizing outcomes from individual case reports, such as the one presented here. The primary goal of initial management is to minimize pancreatic exocrine stimulation, thereby reducing the volume of pancreatic fluid leaking through the fistulous tract and allowing spontaneous closure of the defect. This physiological rationale underpins the cornerstone of conservative therapy: strict bowel rest combined with nutritional support and pharmacologic suppression of enzyme secretion.

In the initial phase, immediate stabilization involves ensuring adequate oxygenation and draining large-volume effusions via thoracostomy tubes or serial thoracentesis to relieve respiratory compromise, as seen in our patient who required an intercostal drain for persistent reaccumulation of dark red fluid. Once stabilized, the definitive medical regimen begins with "nil per os" (NPO) status to eliminate cephalic and gastric phases of pancreatic stimulation. Concurrently, total parenteral nutrition (TPN) is initiated to maintain caloric needs while bypassing the gastrointestinal tract entirely. To further suppress acinar cell activity and reduce pancreatic juice production, somatostatin analogues like octreotide are administered continuously. In the case presented, a dose of 30 µg/kg/day was utilized effectively; this agent works by inhibiting the release of cholecystokinin and gastrin, directly dampening pancreatic exocrine function. Additionally, pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) may be employed in specific contexts to aid digestion if enteral feeding is attempted later, though its role during active fistula formation is nuanced compared to its use in chronic insufficiency. This conservative strategy typically encompasses a trial period of two to three weeks; success is defined by a cessation of fluid output from the chest tube and radiographic resolution of the collection.

If conservative management fails to achieve fistula closure within three to four weeks, or if there is evidence of worsening clinical status despite maximal medical therapy, escalation to endoscopic intervention becomes necessary. Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) serves both diagnostic and therapeutic roles in this setting. The procedure allows for direct visualization of the pancreatic ductal anatomy and precise identification of the leak site relative to major ductal branches. Therapeutically, transpapillary stenting is performed across the sphincter of Oddi or directly bridging the stricture/fistula opening. By increasing downstream flow through the ampulla, stents reduce intraductal pressure upstream from the leak, favorably altering flow dynamics so that secretions exit through their natural pathway rather than traversing the fistula into the pleural space. While success rates for endoscopic stenting vary widely depending on ductal anatomy and lesion location (ranging from approximately 48% in mixed populations), they offer a minimally invasive option with lower morbidity than open surgery. In pediatric series specifically reported in literature where anatomical constraints permitted stent placement, ERCP has demonstrated high efficacy in closing fistulas within weeks to months.

When endoscopic approaches are technically unfeasible—due to severe ductal obstruction preventing cannulation—or fail after multiple attempts at stenting over several months, surgical intervention represents the final line of defense. Surgical options include distal pancreatectomy if the lesion is localized to the tail or body (as suggested by our patient's CT findings tracking toward the tail), or diversion procedures such as pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow procedure) or cystojejunostomy for diffuse disease or large pseudocysts communicating with pleural spaces. Although surgery carries higher inherent risks including operative mortality below 5% and potential complications like diabetes or exocrine insufficiency due to tissue resection or bypass, it offers a definitive cure with reported success rates exceeding 90% in pediatric cohorts where other modalities have failed. Therefore, while surgery remains reserved for refractory cases due to its invasiveness, it provides a critical safety net when conservative and endoscopic strategies cannot halt life-threatening thoracic complications arising from persistent pancreatic leaks.
