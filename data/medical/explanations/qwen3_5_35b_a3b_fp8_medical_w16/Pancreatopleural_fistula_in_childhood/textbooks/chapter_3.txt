Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Reasoning and Imaging Strategy

The diagnostic journey in this pediatric case illustrates the critical challenge of "pancreatic masking" as a thoracic emergency. Initially, the clinical presentation of recurrent right-sided pleural effusion, chest pain, and respiratory distress led to a working diagnosis of infectious or neoplastic etiologies common in children. The initial thoracocentesis yielded approximately one liter of dark red fluid, a finding that raised immediate suspicion for malignancy or traumatic hemothorax. However, the rapid recurrence of large-volume effusions despite drainage necessitated a re-evaluation of the underlying pathology. The pivotal moment in this diagnostic algorithm occurred with the biochemical analysis of the pleural fluid. While adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels were mildly elevated—prompting an initial trial of anti-tubercular therapy—the defining feature emerged with the measurement of amylase and lipase. A pleural fluid amylase concentration exceeding 1000 IU/L is widely regarded as pathognomonic for a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF). In this specific case, the amylase level reached 6118 U/L, coupled with markedly elevated lipase (7211 U/L), effectively shifting the diagnostic focus from primary pulmonary disease to pancreatic pathology. This biochemical threshold serves as the key pivot point; while other conditions such as esophageal perforation or certain malignancies can elevate amylase, levels approaching those seen here are virtually exclusive to pancreatic leaks in the absence of concurrent salivary gland involvement.

Differentiating the source of this hyperamylasemia is essential to rule out mimics that may present with similarly high amylase levels but require vastly different management strategies. The primary differential diagnoses included tuberculosis, given the patient's contact history and mildly elevated ADA; malignancy, suggested by the hemorrhagic nature of the fluid; Boerhaave's syndrome (esophageal perforation); and hepatic hydrothorax. To distinguish between these, one must consider both clinical context and specific laboratory markers. For instance, TB was unlikely given the negative GeneXpert for *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* and lack of constitutional symptoms like fever or weight loss. Malignancy was excluded through normal blood counts, peripheral smears lacking leukemic features, and negative cytology for malignant cells in multiple aspirations. Crucially, distinguishing pancreatic from salivary amylase is vital because conditions like mumps or parotitis can raise serum and pleural amylase without involving the pancreas. Although not explicitly performed in every center, determining that the elevated enzyme activity was predominantly pancreatic-type (rather than salivary) further solidifies PPF as the diagnosis over systemic salivary disorders or simple mechanical irritation. Furthermore, Boerhaave's syndrome was ruled out by the absence of retching/vomiting prior to onset and normal gastrointestinal transit on history, while hepatic hydrothorax was excluded by normal liver function tests and imaging showing no cirrhosis or portal hypertension.

Imaging played a complementary yet distinct role in confirming anatomical disruption versus suggesting functional abnormalities. Initial cross-sectional imaging with contrast-enhanced CT revealed patchy consolidation and hydropneumothorax but failed to definitively visualize the fistula itself; instead, it showed a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the retroperitoneum toward the diaphragm. This limitation highlights a crucial teaching point: while CT is excellent for identifying complications like pseudocysts, calcifications, or ductal dilation associated with pancreatitis (present sensitivity ~33–47% for fistula visualization), it often misses small communications across tissue planes due to resolution limits or lack of dynamic flow data regarding bile/pancreatic juice leakage into adjacent spaces. Consequently, relying solely on CT could lead to persistent uncertainty regarding whether a thoracic abscess or an occult esophageal tear was responsible for the collection. The definitive confirmation arrived via Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), which demonstrated a direct tubular-cystic connection originating from the distal pancreatic duct and traversing superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. MRCP provides superior soft-tissue contrast compared to CT without ionizing radiation—a significant advantage in pediatrics—and successfully delineated complex fistulous tracts that were invisible on plain radiographs or conventional CT scans.

Ultimately, establishing this diagnosis required synthesizing three convergent lines of evidence: a high-yield clinical clue (history of blunt abdominal trauma concealed by social factors), pathognomonic biochemistry (pleural amylase >1000 IU/L with concomitant lipase elevation), and anatomical confirmation via MRCP tracing the fistula tract from pancreas to pleura. This stepwise reasoning underscores why PPF is frequently misdiagnosed initially; clinicians must maintain a low threshold for measuring pleural enzymes in any unexplained exudative effusion containing high protein content or appearing bloody/multiloculated on imaging. Once biochemical suspicion is raised by extreme elevations in amylase/lipase ratios relative to serum values (typically >2x serum upper limit), advanced imaging becomes mandatory to map the fistula before considering invasive interventions like ERCP or surgery. In this case, recognizing these elements prevented prolonged empiric treatment for tuberculosis and allowed for targeted conservative management aimed at reducing pancreatic secretion rather than treating an infectious pneumonia.
