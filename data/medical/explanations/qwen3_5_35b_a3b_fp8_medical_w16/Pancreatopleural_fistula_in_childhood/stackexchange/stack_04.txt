Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Morning Report] Why does the initial presentation of pancreatopleural fistula often lead to a delay in diagnosis compared to classic abdominal presentations of pancreatitis, and what historical detail in this specific case was critical for breaking that diagnostic impasse?

**Clinical Pearl:** Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) frequently mimics primary thoracic pathology because the underlying defect originates in the retroperitoneum, allowing pancreatic enzymes to track superiorly through the esophageal or aortic hiatus into the pleural space. This creates a "pulmonary mask" where respiratory distress and recurrent, often hemorrhagic, pleural effusions dominate the clinical picture, while abdominal symptoms—such as epigastric pain—are absent or subtle. Consequently, clinicians often pursue an exhaustive pulmonary workup for tuberculosis, malignancy, or empyema before suspecting a pancreatic etiology.

In this pediatric case, the diagnostic impasse was broken only upon revisiting the history to uncover **blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior**. While the child initially presented with classic signs of a massive right-sided pleural effusion requiring repeated thoracentesis, and even received empiric anti-tubercular therapy due to household exposure and mildly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA), the true etiology remained obscured until the traumatic event was recalled.

The critical pathophysiological link here is that pancreatic injury leads to disruption of the ductal system. When the rupture occurs on the posterior aspect of the pancreas, fluid leaks into the retroperitoneum and follows paths of least resistance upward through diaphragmatic hiatuses rather than presenting as intra-abdominal ascites. The definitive clue in this case was not just the history of trauma, but the laboratory confirmation of **pleural fluid amylase and lipase levels exceeding 6,000 U/L**, which is pathognomonic for PPF when combined with compatible imaging (CT/MRCP showing a tract from pancreas to pleura). Without identifying this specific historical detail and recognizing that high-amylase effusions can stem from extrapulmonary sources, further invasive procedures or prolonged inappropriate antimicrobial therapy would likely have occurred. This underscores that in any case of unexplained recurrent serous or hemorrhagic pleural effusion—especially in children—a detailed inquiry regarding abdominal trauma is essential before concluding on primary pulmonary disease.
