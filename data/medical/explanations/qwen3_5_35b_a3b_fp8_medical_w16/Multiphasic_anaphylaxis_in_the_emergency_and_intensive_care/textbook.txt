Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Stabilization of Severe Anaphylaxis

The case begins with a woman in her 60s presenting to the emergency department (ED) with a life-threatening systemic reaction, marking her first documented episode of anaphylaxis. Her background includes significant comorbidities, specifically type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression, alongside a notable history of drug allergies. While she experienced a mild localized reaction to amoxicillin in adolescence and a strong local reaction to a wasp sting without systemic involvement, her current presentation represents a dramatic escalation in severity. The precipitating event occurred five days prior to admission when she was treated for erysipelas with clindamycin, which induced a delayed cutaneous rash. Upon switching to clarithromycin—a macrolide antibiotic to which she had no prior exposure—the patient ingested the first 500 mg dose. Within one hour, she developed progressive symptoms characterized by intense pruritus, generalized flushing, diaphoresis, agitation, and a subjective sensation of throat swelling. These symptoms rapidly deteriorated from cutaneous manifestations to life-threatening cardiorespiratory compromise upon seeking medical attention.

Upon arrival at the ED, the patient exhibited profound hemodynamic instability consistent with anaphylactic shock. Vital sign assessments revealed severe hypotension with a blood pressure reading of 74/50 mm Hg and significant hypoxemia with peripheral oxygen saturation dropping to 88% on room air. Respiratory distress was evident with a tachypneic rate of 22 breaths per minute. Physical examination confirmed the multisystem nature of the reaction: facial edema was present alongside widespread erythema and urticaria covering the trunk and extremities. Although the patient reported throat swelling, direct inspection noted no visible obstruction of the tongue or pharynx at that moment; however, cardiac auscultation was unremarkable aside from sinus rhythm on ECG. The combination of acute hypotension, hypoxia, and diffuse cutaneous involvement satisfied the clinical criteria for severe anaphylaxis necessitating immediate intervention regardless of confirmatory laboratory values at that stage.

Immediate stabilization followed established emergency protocols prioritizing airway protection and circulatory support. The patient received supplemental oxygen via a non-rebreather mask at 15 L/min and underwent aggressive fluid resuscitation with 500 mL of isotonic sodium chloride solution to address vasodilation-induced volume depletion. Crucially, pharmacologic management focused on the administration of intramuscular epinephrine via auto-injector delivering 0.3 mg. While this dose is frequently utilized in pre-hospital settings due to device availability and ease of use in anxious providers or children weighing under 25 kg, it falls short of current guideline recommendations for adults weighing over 50 kg. Current consensus suggests an adult dose of approximately 0.01 mg/kg or a fixed dose of 0.5 mg for patients exceeding this weight threshold; thus, this patient likely received suboptimal initial dosing relative to her body mass (104 kg). Concurrently, she was administered intravenous clemastine (an antihistamine) for cutaneous relief and monitored closely for response. Following these interventions, her blood pressure normalized rapidly enough to allow temporary cessation of high-flow oxygen until recurrent episodes necessitated its reintroduction later in her course.

This initial encounter underscores several critical teaching points regarding the management of severe anaphylaxis in complex patients. First, it highlights the necessity of recognizing anaphylaxis based on clinical acumen alone; reliance on specific triggers or laboratory confirmation must never delay epinephrine administration when cardiorespiratory collapse is imminent. Second, the case illustrates the potential pitfalls in standardized emergency equipment availability versus physiological needs; while auto-injectors offer speed and familiarity in chaotic environments like an ED triage area or pre-hospital setting, their fixed low-dose formulation may result in therapeutic underdosing for heavier adults compared to manually drawn ampoules capable of delivering higher volumes (e.g., 0.5 mg). Finally, while antihistamines and fluids are essential adjunctive therapies for symptom control and volume expansion respectively, they do not substitute for epinephrine as the definitive treatment for reversing shock and preventing airway closure during severe reactions.

## Defining Biphasic and Multiphasic Anaphylaxis: Pathophysiology and Diagnostic Challenges

Understanding the evolution of anaphylaxis requires moving beyond the immediate recognition of the initial hypersensitivity event to appreciating its dynamic temporal profile. Clinicians must distinguish between a monophasic course, characterized by a single episode of symptoms resolving completely, and complex courses involving recurrence. **Biphasic anaphylaxis** is formally defined as the spontaneous return of anaphylactic symptoms hours after the initial reaction has resolved, occurring in the absence of further allergen exposure. When this pattern extends to multiple distinct episodes of relapse, as seen in our case patient who experienced four separate shock events over 12 hours, it is classified as **multiphasic anaphylaxis**. It is crucial to differentiate these phenomena from **refractory anaphylaxis**, which describes a state where symptoms persist or worsen despite appropriate administration of epinephrine and supportive care, rather than representing a discrete recurrence after resolution. Similarly, clinicians must rule out **sustained anaphylaxis**, where symptoms appear to recur but are actually driven by ongoing absorption of the allergen (such as residual food in the gut or slow-release medications) or inadequate initial treatment, rather than a secondary biological wave.

The pathophysiology underlying biphasic and multiphasic courses remains incompletely understood but is believed to involve a "second wave" of mediator release from mast cells and basophils without new antigen stimulation. Following the initial trigger, mast cells undergo degranulation, releasing histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins. While epinephrine effectively reverses hemodynamic collapse and bronchospasm by acting on adrenergic receptors, it does not necessarily halt the underlying cellular activation process immediately. Proposed mechanisms suggest that cytokines released during the initial phase may sustain mast cell activation or induce a delayed recruitment of inflammatory cells, creating a reservoir of mediators that precipitates a secondary reaction once drug levels wane or physiological stress shifts. In patients with underlying mast cell disorders, such as systemic mastocytosis—a condition strongly suspected in our patient based on elevated tryptase levels—the threshold for activation may be lowered significantly, facilitating these recurrent waves of severe reactions even after apparent clinical stabilization.

Epidemiologically, biphasic anaphylaxis represents a significant minority of cases but carries substantial variability in reported frequency. Literature estimates span a wide range from 0.4% to 23.3%, though contemporary meta-analyses generally converge on an incidence between 4% and 6%. This variation highlights challenges in study design and definition consistency across different cohorts. Risk factors identified for developing a biphasic course include delayed administration of epinephrine at onset, severity of the initial reaction requiring multiple doses of vasopressors or intubation, ingestion triggers compared to insect stings or drugs (though data varies), and elevated baseline serum tryptase levels suggesting occult mastocytosis. Our case illustrates several high-risk features: severe initial hypotension requiring aggressive resuscitation and repeated epinephrine dosing within minutes, alongside laboratory markers suggestive of systemic mast cell involvement.

Despite these recognized patterns, defining "safe" observation windows for discharge remains one of the most contentious issues in anaphylaxis management. Historically, observation periods ranged from 4 to 6 hours post-resolution; however, data indicates that approximately half of all biphasic reactions occur between 6 and 12 hours after the index event. Some reports document recurrences extending up to 78 hours post-exposure. Consequently, there is no universally agreed-upon duration for mandatory observation that balances resource utilization with patient safety for every individual case. The lack of robust predictive tools means clinicians often rely on heuristics derived from risk stratification rather than definitive algorithms. As demonstrated by our patient's course extending beyond typical short-stay protocols into intensive care monitoring until symptom-free stability was achieved over 24 hours alone underscores the limitations of fixed-time discharge criteria in managing patients with complex or multiphasic presentations where recurrence cannot be reliably predicted by initial severity alone.

## Diagnostic Reasoning in Complex Relapsing Courses and Underlying Etiologies

The clinical trajectory of this patient illustrates a profound diagnostic challenge: distinguishing between a standard biphasic anaphylactic reaction and a more complex, multiphasic course driven by an underlying mast cell disorder. Upon the patient's first relapse of anaphylactic shock while monitored on the acute internal ward, the decision to transfer to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) was driven by the failure of intermittent rescue therapy to provide sustained stability. Although the patient responded adequately to intramuscular epinephrine, the recurrence of life-threatening hemodynamic collapse indicated that the inflammatory cascade was self-perpetuating rather than resolving spontaneously. In an acute ward setting lacking continuous telemetry and immediate advanced resuscitation capabilities, this pattern necessitated escalation to ICU-level care. This move allowed for closer hemodynamic monitoring and facilitated the consideration of aggressive management strategies, such as continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion, which serves as a bridge therapy to suppress ongoing mediator release until spontaneous remission occurs. Conversely, an expectant approach was weighed but deemed risky given the unpredictable nature of multiphasic recurrences and the severity of previous episodes. Ultimately, the transition to higher-acuity care reflected a recognition that standard observation protocols were insufficient for managing a reaction characterized by multiple distinct relapses.

Once stabilized in the ICU and following the cessation of symptoms after approximately 24 hours, the clinical team initiated a search for secondary etiologies explaining the severity and multiplicity of the reactions. While typical triggers like foods or medications account for most anaphylaxis cases, unexplained recurrent events in adults warrant investigation into systemic mastocytosis (SM). SM involves a neoplastic proliferation of mast cells that lowers the threshold for degranulation and can lead to severe, refractory reactions upon minimal stimulation. The suspicion was heightened by the patient's unusually high number of relapses and her history of severe cutaneous reactions to insect stings. However, confirming SM presents significant diagnostic hurdles in acute settings due to strict adherence required by World Health Organization (WHO) criteria. Definitive diagnosis requires meeting one major criterion plus three minor criteria, or three minor criteria alone. In this case, tissue architecture assessment for bone marrow involvement—the major criterion—was compromised because limited material was obtained during biopsy, rendering it unassessable. Consequently, reliance shifted entirely to minor criteria; notably, serum tryptase levels exceeding 20 ng/mL were documented during both symptomatic phases and at baseline follow-up. While elevated baseline tryptase (>20 ng/mL) is a sensitive marker for SM, it lacks specificity without histological confirmation or genetic testing (c-KIT D816V mutation), leaving this patient with a "high probability" rather than a confirmed diagnosis based strictly on current guidelines.

A critical component of evaluating mast cell burden involves understanding "tryptase kinetics," particularly regarding sample timing relative to symptom onset and resolution. Tryptase is released rapidly upon mast cell activation but has a short half-life; levels typically peak within 1–2 hours post-symptom onset and return toward baseline within 24 hours. Standard guidelines recommend obtaining three samples: one immediately upon presentation (if feasible without delaying treatment), one at 1–2 hours post-onset to capture the peak release from degranulation during an attack, and a third sample taken at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution to establish a true baseline value indicative of underlying mast cell mass rather than acute stress release. In this case report, deviations occurred: no initial sample was drawn during the first two episodes due to prioritization of stabilization measures, which obscured whether early releases were disproportionately high compared to later ones. Furthermore, while follow-up samples were eventually collected at 27 hours post-last recurrence—a window sufficient for establishing baseline levels—the absence of earlier kinetic data limits the ability to correlate specific tryptase surges with individual relapse intensities. Despite these gaps, persistently elevated levels across different time points strongly suggested chronic mast cell activation consistent with SM rather than isolated allergic sensitization alone.

Beyond laboratory values, ancillary findings provided corroborative evidence supporting an underlying systemic process compatible with SM but falling short of formal diagnostic thresholds. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed hepatomegaly (enlarged liver), reflecting potential infiltration by mast cells within visceral organs—a feature frequently seen in systemic variants such as indolent systemic mastocytosis or smoldering forms that may precede overt malignancy. Additionally, neuropsychiatric symptoms including depression and memory deficits noted in her history aligned with known associations between chronic mast cell mediator release and central nervous system dysfunction mediated by cytokines like TNF-alpha or histamine crossing into neural pathways. These physical and historical clues collectively painted a picture where simple IgE-mediated allergy failed to explain the clinical phenotype entirely; instead, they pointed toward a dysregulated immune system primed for exaggerated responses regardless of external trigger intensity. Although definitive classification remained elusive due to incomplete biopsy results and lack of molecular profiling at presentation, recognizing these patterns is crucial for long-term management planning and counseling regarding future exposures or potential need for prophylactic therapies targeting mast cell stabilization rather than just episodic rescue interventions.
