Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# The Silent Trigger: Unmasking Systemic Mastocytosis in Complex Allergic Reactions

We've all seen the textbook case of anaphylaxis: a bee sting, a peanut butter sandwich, or a new prescription drug. You hit them with IM epi, they stabilize, you observe them for four hours, and they go home. It's a clean narrative. But sometimes, the story doesn't end when the vitals normalize. Sometimes, the "resolving" patient suddenly crashes again, and then again, creating a chaotic cycle of hypotension and bronchospasm that defies standard observation protocols.

This is exactly what happened in a fascinating case report recently published by Schneider et al., describing a woman in her 60s who experienced **multiphasic anaphylaxis** to clarithromycin. What makes this case particularly chilling isn't just the severity of the shock—it was that she required *four* separate doses of epinephrine within 24 hours. While biphasic reactions are well-documented (recurrence after an asymptomatic interval), multiphasic courses are rare beasts. More importantly, this patient's trajectory forced us to look past the immediate trigger (the antibiotic) and ask: *Why* did this happen? The answer lay hidden beneath the surface: **Systemic Mastocytosis**.

### The Clinical Picture: When "Resolved" Isn't Really Resolved

Let's walk through the chaos. This patient presented with classic signs of severe anaphylaxis—hypotension (74/50 mmHg), hypoxia (SpO2 88%), generalized urticaria, and throat tightness—one hour after taking her first dose of clarithromycin (which she had never taken before). She responded initially to IM epinephrine and IV fluids, allowing for discharge planning until... she didn't stay stable.

Within the first 12 hours of admission, she suffered three distinct relapses into anaphylactic shock. Each episode required rescue epinephrine. Interestingly, as noted by the authors, the severity seemed to wane slightly with each subsequent wave—from profound hypoxia and cyanosis to milder dyspnea and pruritus—but the threat remained real enough to warrant ICU transfer.

Here lies the teaching moment: **Do not assume stability based on a single successful bolus.** In patients with risk factors for severe mast cell activation or unexplained recurrent symptoms, a "good response" is merely a temporary reprieve. The team considered aggressive interventions like continuous IV epinephrine infusions versus an expectant watch-and-wait approach. Ultimately, they chose monitoring because symptoms began to taper off naturally after 24 hours without further triggers—a fortunate outcome given the lack of definitive guidelines for managing multiphasic events in this specific context.

### The Hidden Diagnosis: Why Suspect Mastocytosis?

So, why did this woman react so violently and repeatedly? The answer likely lies in **Systemic Mastocytosis (SM)**. While SM is rare, it should be top-of-mind whenever you encounter anaphylaxis that feels "out of proportion" or exhibits unusual patterns like multiphasic recurrence without re-exposure to the allergen.

In this case, several subtle clues pointed toward SM long before a bone marrow biopsy could confirm anything:
*   **Recurrent Severe Anaphylaxis:** The sheer frequency of relapses suggests a lowered threshold for mast cell degranulation across organ systems.
*   **Elevated Tryptase:** Crucially, her serum tryptase levels were persistently elevated during episodes and at baseline follow-up.
*   **"Red Herrings":** She had hepatomegaly on ultrasound and chronic neuropsychiatric symptoms (depression/memory issues), both known associations with mast cell infiltration in organs other than skin/bone marrow.
*   **The Diagnostic Gap:** Interestingly, she didn't meet *full* WHO diagnostic criteria because bone marrow material was insufficient to prove focal aggregates of mast cells (the major criterion). However, having two minor criteria plus elevated tryptase creates a very high clinical suspicion that warrants close follow-up even if formal classification isn't achieved immediately.

### The Tryptase Trap and Timing Matters

One of the most critical failures in this case wasn't clinical management—it was lab timing. To diagnose SM effectively after an anaphylactic event, guidelines recommend a strict triad of tryptase measurements:
1.  **Acute:** As soon as feasible (while treating).
2.  **Peak:** 1–4 hours post-symptom onset (when tryptase peaks).
3.  **Baseline:** >24 hours after full resolution to establish your personal floor level.

In this patient's case, they missed window #1 entirely due to prioritization errors ("first tryptase sample was taken 1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode"). They captured some data later (during episode #3 and at 27 hours), but missing that initial acute spike limits our ability to calculate delta-tryptase accurately ($\Delta$ = Acute - Baseline + 20%). If you suspect SM or want to rule out underlying mast cell disorders in complex cases, **you must get that baseline sample.** If your acute tryptase is >114% higher than baseline OR if your absolute value exceeds ~20 ng/mL consistently over time without another explanation (like renal failure), you have found your smoking gun for systemic disease rather than simple IgE-mediated allergy.

### Pearls for Your Next Shift

While we await better data on managing multiphasic anaphylaxis specifically related to mastocytosis, here are actionable takeaways from this complex case:

*   **High-Vigilance Observation:** Do not rush discharges for patients who require multiple epi doses or have hemodynamic instability during their initial presentation; consider extended observation (>24h) if relapse risk is high or underlying pathology is suspected.
*   **Tryptase Protocol Compliance:** Ensure you order acute AND baseline tryptase levels in every severe or recurrent anaphylaxis case; missing either renders the test useless for diagnosing SM or predicting recurrence risk.
*   **Look Beyond Skin Signs:** In patients with recurrent "idiopathic" allergies or unexplained hepatosplenomegaly/cognitive changes alongside allergic crises, keep Systemic Mastocytosis on your differential list even if formal criteria aren't met yet.
*   **Epinephrine Dosing Reality Check:** Be wary of relying solely on standard auto-injectors (often 0.3 mg) for larger adults (>50 kg); this patient received multiple sub-optimal doses before manual preparation allowed for proper weight-based dosing (0.5 mg). In refractory/multiphasic scenarios where volume status matters less but receptor occupancy does more precision dosing counts more than ever before—and don't hesitate to draw up your own vial if you need it now!
*   **Corticosteroids Are Not Magic Bullets:** Remember that while steroids were given here traditionally to prevent biphasic reactions today's evidence suggests they offer little protection against recurrence but carry significant metabolic risks; rely on vigilant monitoring rather than prophylactic steroids alone in these complex scenarios unless there is a compelling reason otherwise (e.g., asthma exacerbation component).
