# When 'Resolved' Means 'Wait for It': Defining Multiphasic vs. Refractory Anaphylaxis

We've all been there: You push epi, give fluids, toss in some Benadryl and steroids, and the patient's BP snaps back to 120/80. The wheezing clears, the hives fade, and you feel that sweet rush of "we got them." You discharge them home or transfer them to a step-down unit with a clean bill of health. Then, six hours later—or sometimes twelve—the patient crashes *again*.

This isn't just a bad day in the ED; it's **multiphasic anaphylaxis**.

In today's case report from Schneider et al., we dive into a terrifying scenario where a 60-something female experienced **four distinct episodes** of anaphylactic shock after a single dose of clarithromycin. She responded perfectly to initial resuscitation, only to relapse repeatedly over 12 hours despite standard care. What makes this case particularly gripping for us in Critical Care and Emergency Medicine is not just the sheer number of recurrences, but the diagnostic dilemma: Was this refractory anaphylaxis? Sustained absorption? Or true biphasic/multiphasic disease? And why did her tryptase levels suggest a hidden driver like systemic mastocytosis?

Let's break down the definitions, the pitfalls, and the management gray zones that this case illuminates.

## The Semantic Trap: Refractory vs. Biphasic vs. Multiphasic

One of the most dangerous errors in managing severe allergic reactions is misclassifying the *type* of reaction occurring. If you mistake one for another, your disposition strategy fails catastrophically.

**Refractory anaphylaxis** is defined as persistent symptoms with little to no response to correctly administered epinephrine and supportive care. In this state, the patient never truly "resolves"; they are stuck in a cycle of shock that requires escalating interventions like vasopressor drips or intubation immediately.

**Sustained anaphylaxis**, on the other hand, mimics biphasic reactions but is actually driven by ongoing allergen absorption (e.g., slow-release medications or food boluses still in the gut). The symptoms don't disappear because the trigger is still entering the system. Treating this as "biphasic" leads to premature discharge if you assume the allergen load has cleared when it hasn't.

Then we have **true biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis**. As seen in our case patient, this requires a complete return to baseline (an asymptomatic phase) followed by spontaneous recurrence *without* re-exposure to the allergen. This patient had four separate waves:
1.  Initial presentation (shock).
2.  Resolution with IM epi/IV fluids/benzos/steroids.
3.  Relapse #1 (requiring ICU transfer).
4.  Relapse #2 & #3 (less severe but still requiring repeated epi).

By hour 24, she was stable again with no further relapses. The distinction matters immensely for observation protocols. If you think it's refractory when it's actually biphasic, you might start a dopamine drip unnecessarily while ignoring that she needs extended observation rather than pressors alone. Conversely, assuming it's biphasic when it's sustained absorption could lead to sending her home too early while she absorbs more drug from her GI tract.

## The Systemic Mastocytosis Clue: Why Did She Crash Four Times?

The most fascinating aspect of this case isn't just the frequency of relapses; it's the search for an underlying mechanism that predisposed her to such volatility. While most biphasic cases are idiopathic or related to massive initial mast cell burden from foods/insects/anesthetics, this patient had features suggestive of **Systemic Mastocytosis**.

Although she didn't meet all formal WHO criteria (major criteria couldn't be confirmed due to limited biopsy material), several red flags appeared:
*   Persistently elevated baseline and peak serum tryptase levels (>11 ng/mL is often cited as suspicious; hers were significantly higher).
*   Hepatomegaly found on ultrasound.
*   Neuropsychiatric symptoms (depression/memory issues) which can be associated with mast cell mediator overload affecting cognition ("brain fog").
*   A history of strong local reactions to wasp stings without systemic spread until now—a classic warning sign that may evolve into systemic involvement later in life.

Patients with undiagnosed mastocytosis have an abnormally high density of mast cells throughout their bodies (skin, liver, bone marrow). When triggered by an antigen like clarithromycin (which can directly activate mast cells independent of IgE), they release massive quantities of mediators simultaneously across organ systems that healthy individuals wouldn't experience with similar severity or persistence. This explains why her attempts at stabilization failed repeatedly; every time epi wore off or symptoms dipped slightly, another wave of degranulation hit from a different reservoir of cells.

This highlights a critical pearl: **If a patient has recurrent anaphylaxis despite optimal treatment and appears unusually resistant or volatile compared to peers, consider checking tryptase levels aggressively.** Ideally, you want three samples: immediate (<2h), peak (~4h), and baseline (>24h post-resolution). Unfortunately, in this case report, they missed the initial window entirely because they were focused on saving lives! Always keep your finger on the pulse regarding timing—even if you're busy intubating someone else first thing tomorrow morning... wait for me... get those labs drawn!

## Management Gray Zones: To Pump Epi or Wait?

Here is where guidelines fall short and clinical judgment takes center stage regarding multiphasic courses. Once our patient stabilized enough for ICU admission after her second crash but before her third/fourth events faced us with two options discussed by allergists:
1.  **Continuous IV Epinephrine:** Pre-emptive coverage preventing any drop-off in hemodynamics by keeping receptors saturated constantly until risk passes (usually ~24 hours).
2.  **Expectant Management:** Watching closely and treating each recurrence individually based on clinical need ("treat as you go").

Both approaches carried significant risk without solid evidence backing either specifically for *multiphasic* cases (as opposed to biphasic).
