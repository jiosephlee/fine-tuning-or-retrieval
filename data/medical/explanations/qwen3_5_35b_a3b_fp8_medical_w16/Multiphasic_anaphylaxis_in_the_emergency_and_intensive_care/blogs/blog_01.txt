Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Beyond the Auto-Injector: Why 0.3 mg Might Be Underdosing Your Adult Patients

We've all seen the "textbook" anaphylaxis case: a young adult ingests a peanut, develops hives, gets hit with hypotension, receives an IM epi shot, and walks out stable. But what happens when your patient is a 104 kg woman in her 60s? What happens when she survives the initial crash, only to crash *again* six hours later? And again? And again?

This isn't just a story about biphasic anaphylaxis; it's a cautionary tale about how our reliance on convenient auto-injectors might be leaving heavy-set adults dangerously under-dosed.

In a fascinating new case report from Schneider et al., we meet a patient who suffered **multiphasic** anaphylaxis to clarithromycin. She didn't just have one relapse; she had four distinct episodes of shock over roughly 24 hours. While the underlying driver turned out to be a probable (though not formally diagnosed) systemic mastocytosis—a condition where mast cells run wild—the immediate lesson for us in the ED is far more practical: **the dose matters.**

### The Dose Gap: 0.3 mg vs. Reality

Let's look at the numbers. The international consensus guideline recommends epinephrine at **0.01 mg/kg**, capped at **0.5 mg**. For a patient weighing 104 kg, that math doesn't stop at 0.3 mg; it screams for closer to 1 mg if we strictly follow the weight-based formula, but certainly warrants the full 0.5 mg cap immediately rather than settling for half that amount.

Yet, this patient received two doses of **0.3 mg** via auto-injector during her stabilization phase because that is what was readily available and familiar to the team. It worked enough to buy time, but it wasn't enough to break the cycle of mast cell degranulation driven by her massive burden of mediators and her high body mass index (BMI). She required two subsequent doses of manually prepared **0.5 mg** IM epinephrine to finally stabilize after her second and third relapses.

Why does this distinction matter? Because relying on the default "adult" auto-injector (often stuck at 0.3 mg in many regions) creates a therapeutic ceiling for anyone over ~50 kg who needs aggressive vasopressor support. A recent retrospective study highlights exactly this friction: patients receiving the initial 0.3 mg dose required significantly more escalation of care compared to those who got 0.5 mg right out of the gate. In a multiphasic scenario like this case, that hesitation or under-dosing can mean the difference between managing symptoms on a regular ward and fighting for airways in the ICU repeatedly.

### The Mast Cell Connection

While we're debating micrograms of epi, we must acknowledge why this specific patient kept crashing even with treatment. Workup revealed elevated tryptase levels throughout her course and hepatomegaly, pointing strongly toward **systemic mastocytosis**. Even though she didn't meet every strict WHO criterion (likely due to sampling limitations), her physiology was primed for catastrophic mediator release upon stimulation by clarithromycin or stress alone.

Patients with underlying mast cell disorders are notorious for having more severe initial reactions and higher rates of biphasic/multiphasic courses because their "ammunition belt" is simply fuller than average. When you add obesity (which increases volume of distribution and alters clearance dynamics) to mastocytosis, you get a perfect storm where standard dosing feels insufficient until you push harder earlier on. This case underscores that while we don't need to screen everyone for mastocytosis immediately, we should suspect it in patients with repeated relapses despite adequate resuscitation efforts—and perhaps treat them more aggressively from hour zero.

### Corticosteroids: Do They Help Here?

It's worth noting another pillar of management discussed in this report: corticosteroids. You'll notice this patient received IV prednisone and dexamethasone after her first recurrence because "that's what we do." Historically, steroids were given prophylactically to prevent biphasic reactions based on theoretical grounds (slowing inflammatory cascades). However, modern evidence is increasingly skeptical. Systematic reviews suggest steroids offer no benefit in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis and may actually increase hospital admissions due to side effects like hyperglycemia (this patient had significant glucose spikes anyway).

While guidelines are shifting away from routine steroid use in pure anaphylaxis cases, some clinicians still reach for them in complex or multiphasic scenarios hoping for that extra layer of protection against relapse as seen here with prednisone/prednisolone protocols varying by institution (she got both IV prednisone daily *and* IV dexa). As we move forward, our focus should remain squarely on titrating epinephrine appropriately rather than betting on steroids that haven't proven their worth in trials yet found their way into our reflexive order sets.

### Take-Home Points for Tomorrow Morning Shift

This case forces us to rethink our approach to dosing in larger adults and those with unexplained relapsing courses:

*   **Don't let auto-injector convenience dictate your max dose:** If your patient weighs >50 kg (which includes almost all adult men and many women), ensure they receive **0.5 mg** IM epinephrine as the initial bolus if you have vials/syringes ready; do not limit yourself to 0.3 mg unless you are forced to wait for transport or equipment failure allows otherwise.
*   **Recognize multiphasic patterns early:** If a patient requires multiple doses of epinephrine within hours despite initial stabilization, think "refractory," consider underlying mast cell disease (check tryptase!), and prepare for prolonged observation or ICU transfer rather than discharging after one hour of silence post-treatment window closure rules change based on severity).
*   **Question the steroid reflex:** While not contraindicated per se in complex cases today, remember that corticosteroids likely won't stop the next wave of anaphylaxis; optimizing adrenergic support will do much more heavy lifting than adding methylprednisolone or dexamethasone alone will ever achieve regarding hemodynamics or symptom control during recurrence events themselves.)
*   **Know your limits:** In cases where standard intermittent dosing fails repeatedly (like this patient's four crashes), consider continuous IV infusion protocols if available, but recognize that evidence gaps exist here too—we often fly blind until we see patterns emerge across registries like this one provides inspiration for future data collection.)
