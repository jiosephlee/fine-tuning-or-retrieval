Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# The Corticosteroid Conundrum: Do They Prevent Biphasic Anaphylaxis?

We've all been there. You treat a severe anaphylactic reaction with IM epinephrine, fluids, and antihistamines. The patient stabilizes, looks great, and you feel confident sending them home—or admitting them to the ward for "observation." Then, 6 to 12 hours later, the alarm goes off. Hypotension returns. Urticaria flares. It's biphasic anaphylaxis.

In our latest case report from *Schneider et al.*, we witness a particularly dramatic version of this nightmare: **multiphasic** anaphylaxis. A woman in her 60s experienced four distinct episodes of shock after taking clarithromycin. Here is the kicker that should make us pause and rethink our reflexive prescribing habits: Upon her first relapse, the team didn't just give epinephrine; they aggressively loaded her with corticosteroids (IV dexamethasone and daily IV prednisone), adhering strictly to the old-school dogma that steroids prevent biphasic reactions.

And guess what happened? She went into shock again anyway. Twice more.

This case serves as a brutal reality check against historical teaching. Despite the patient receiving significant steroid coverage (30 mg prednisone IV daily plus 8 mg dexamethasone IV), she suffered four separate episodes of anaphylactic shock over roughly 24 hours. If steroids were truly protective against biphasic phenomena in this context, this patient should have remained stable after the initial stabilization. Instead, she required escalating doses of epinephrine and transfer to the ICU before finally settling down after 24 hours of observation without further relapse.

So, why do we still reach for the methylprednisolone drip? The short answer is: because we're afraid of missing something, but the long answer is that the evidence simply isn't there to support it—and there might actually be harm in doing so.

### The Evidence Gap

For decades, textbooks have told us that corticosteroids inhibit late-phase inflammatory responses and thus prevent biphasic reactions. It sounds logical on paper: stop the cytokines, stop the rebound. But clinical reality tells a different story. As noted in the discussion accompanying this case, a pivotal 2017 systematic review analyzed 22 studies on this exact topic and found only one study supporting steroid efficacy; 21 studies showed **no reduction** in biphasic rates with steroid administration.

Furthermore, recent data suggests that giving steroids might actually be detrimental in certain contexts. One study indicated an increased likelihood of ICU or ward admission among patients treated with pre-hospital steroids compared to those who weren't. Why? Perhaps because steroids create a false sense of security ("the patient is covered"), leading clinicians to discharge patients too early or fail to recognize subtle signs of impending collapse until it's too late because the anti-inflammatory effect masks early warning signs like rash or flushing while hemodynamic instability sets in unnoticed until profound hypotension occurs.

### The Diagnostic Mystery: Systemic Mastocytosis?

While this case highlights our failure to predict biphasic events using steroids, it also shines a light on a hidden variable: **Systemic Mastocytosis**.

Upon further investigation of this multiphasic case, the authors found elevated baseline and reactive tryptase levels (though drawn slightly outside ideal timing windows) alongside hepatomegaly and neuropsychiatric symptoms consistent with mast cell activation disorder syndrome (MCAS). While she didn't meet all formal WHO diagnostic criteria due to biopsy limitations, she had two minor criteria suggestive of systemic mastocytosis (SM).

Patients with SM are notorious for having severe, refractory, or multiphasic anaphylaxis due to their massive reservoirs of mast cells ready to degranulate repeatedly without needing fresh allergen exposure (or perhaps triggered by endogenous mediators). This explains why our patient bounced back from shock four times despite aggressive standard care including steroids and repeated epinephrine boluses (note: the team appropriately escalated from 0.3 mg auto-injectors to individualized 0.5 mg IM doses).

This raises a terrifying possibility for every clinician managing "simple" anaphylaxis: Could some fraction of our "idiopathic" biphasic cases actually be undiagnosed mastocytosis? If so, they require longer observation periods and perhaps different prophylactic strategies entirely (like leukotriene inhibitors or cromolyn sodium), none of which includes corticosteroids as a preventative measure for recurrence once degranulation begins.

### What About Dosing? A Side Note on Epinephrine

Before we close out on steroids, let's briefly touch on another lesson from this case regarding epinephrine dosing dynamics in obese patients or large adults (>50 kg). The patient weighed 104 kg yet received standard 0.3 mg auto-injector doses initially for two episodes before switching to manual preparation of 0.5 mg doses for subsequent relapses.

Current guidelines generally recommend **0.5 mg** for adults >50 kg (often calculated as 0.01 mg/kg up to a max). Yet, many hospitals stock only 0.3 mg devices due to legacy preferences or cost considerations in older generations of equipment design—a trend shifting now but still prevalent globally as seen here). Retrospective data suggests patients starting with 0.3 mg are far more likely to need rescue dosing than those getting the full therapeutic load upfront ($>5$ fold difference in some cohorts). In a multiphasic course where time-to-treatment is critical during subsequent relapses, relying on suboptimal auto-injector dosing could be contributing factor prolonging morbidity if not recognized quickly enough by staff comfortable preparing manual syringes immediately upon sign #1 of recurrence rather than waiting for an auto-injector run-out scenario or hesitation with larger vials/syringes available elsewhere in your facility.)

### Take-Home Points & Clinical Pearls

*   **Corticosteroids do NOT prevent biphasic anaphylaxis.** Relying on them creates a dangerous placebo effect; do not use them as a reason to shorten observation times or feel secure about discharge planning post-stabilization based solely on steroid administration history (*NEJM*, *JACI*, *Resuscitation*).
*   **Elevated Tryptase + Multiphase Course = Red Flag.** When a patient experiences multiple recurrences despite adequate epinephrine/fluids/steroids/pulse oximetry correction (<span style="color:red">even</span> if they respond transiently), suspect underlying mast cell disease (e.g., Systemic Mastocytosis) and consult Allergy/Immunology early for baseline tryptase workup (*Tryptase peaks within ~4h post-event; repeat at <span style="color:green">baseline</span> >24h later*).
*   **Dose Matters:** For adult patients weighing >50 kg (and certainly those approaching obesity thresholds like BMI >30), ensure your default IM dose is **0.5 mg**, not just whatever fits in your pocket-sized auto-injector drawer unless you can manually prepare higher volumes safely (*Avoid under-dosing delays*).
*   **Observation Duration Remains Uncertain:** Since we cannot reliably predict who will go biphasic/multiphase without biomarkers like tryptase trends or genetic markers currently unavailable at bedside, extended observation (often >6–8 hours minimum) remains mandatory regardless of steroid use (*Monitor closely until asymptomatic phase lasts >6–8h*).
*   **Steroid Harms Are Real:** Be aware that IV steroids can induce hyperglycemia (dangerous in diabetics like our patient!), fluid retention masking volume status changes ("pseudo-resuscitation"), and immunosuppression risks—all while offering zero protection against recurrence (*Watch glucose! Check BP carefully!*).

**Final Thought:**
Anaphylaxis management has evolved rapidly toward recognizing that epinephrine is king—but sometimes even kings need better advisors than "just give prednisone." In complex cases like ours where standard protocols fail repeatedly (multiphase shocks), look deeper into mast biology rather than stacking anti-inflammatories blindly until someone says "stop." Until we have robust data predicting who will recur naturally via biomarkers alone (**tryptase curves**), vigilance beats pharmacology every time when it comes to preventing death from missed second waves.*
