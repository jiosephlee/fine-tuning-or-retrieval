### [Differential Diagnosis] Based on the clinical definition provided in the case discussion, what distinguishes 'multiphasic' anaphylaxis from 'refractory' anaphylaxis, and why is this distinction critical for determining the timing of intervention?

**Clinical Pearl:**
The fundamental distinction between **multiphasic** and **refractory** anaphylaxis lies in the temporal pattern of symptom resolution and the response to epinephrine, a differentiation that dictates whether the clinician must prepare for *recurrence* or manage *persistent instability*.

**Multiphasic anaphylaxis**, as illustrated in our case report, is characterized by an initial reaction that resolves completely with appropriate therapy (an "asymptomatic phase"), followed by a spontaneous recurrence of symptoms hours later without re-exposure to the allergen. Our patient experienced four distinct episodes separated by periods of hemodynamic stability; notably, the intensity of symptoms actually decreased with each subsequent relapse. This pattern requires a strategy focused on extended observation and readiness to reinstitute treatment upon symptom return.

In contrast, **refractory anaphylaxis** is defined by persistent symptoms that fail to respond adequately to repeated, correctly administered doses of epinephrine. There is no true asymptomatic interval; rather, the patient remains in a state of shock or respiratory distress despite maximal standard therapy.

**Why this distinction matters for intervention timing:**
This distinction is critical because it determines the urgency and nature of therapeutic escalation. In **refractory anaphylaxis**, the clock starts immediately: failure to respond mandates immediate transition to advanced rescue therapies, such as intravenous epinephrine infusions or vasopressors, without waiting for further deterioration. Waiting for a "second opinion" or repeating IM doses indefinitely delays life-saving intervention.

Conversely, in **multiphasic anaphylaxis**, as seen in our patient's recovery trajectory, aggressive preemptive IV infusion is not automatically indicated unless the frequency or severity of recurrences suggests imminent collapse. Instead, the priority shifts to vigilant monitoring during the asymptomatic window. As noted in our case discussion, while continuous IV epinephrine was considered, it carries significant risks (e.g., arrhythmias) and was unnecessary once it became clear that recurrences were self-limiting and less intense over time. Misidentifying a multiphasic course as refractory could lead to unnecessary ICU admission with invasive lines; conversely, misclassifying refractory anaphylaxis as multiphasic could result in fatal delay if clinicians wait for a "resolution" that will never occur before escalating care.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why do current evidence-based guidelines generally advise against the routine administration of corticosteroids for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis, and what specific adverse outcomes have been associated with their pre-hospital use?

Current evidence-based guidelines advise against the routine administration of corticosteroids for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis because robust clinical data fails to demonstrate a reduction in recurrence rates. As highlighted in the discussion of this case report, a pivotal 2017 systematic review analyzed 22 studies and found that only one supported steroid efficacy, whereas 21 demonstrated no benefit in preventing biphasic reactions. The pathophysiological rationale for their historical use—that steroids would inhibit the late-phase inflammatory cascade—has not translated into improved clinical outcomes in randomized trials.

Furthermore, pre-hospital and early emergency administration of corticosteroids has been associated with specific adverse outcomes, including an increased likelihood of hospital admission or transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU). A 2019 study cited in the case report noted that patients receiving pre-hospital corticosteroids were more frequently admitted compared to those who did not receive them, suggesting that steroids may paradoxically prolong the need for higher-level monitoring without conferring prophylactic benefit.

In the case presented by Schneider et al., despite the patient's multiphasic course necessitating ICU admission and repeated epinephrine dosing, corticosteroids were administered only *after* the first recurrence of anaphylaxis based on traditional protocols rather than preventive necessity. Even with high-dose intravenous prednisone and dexamethasone, the patient experienced three additional relapses within 12 hours. This underscores that once a biphasic or multiphasic trajectory is established, steroids do not reliably abort subsequent episodes. Consequently, modern management prioritizes rigorous observation periods and immediate availability of epinephrine over pharmacologic prophylaxis with corticosteroids.

### [Morning Report] Describe the ideal temporal sequence for obtaining serum tryptase levels to confirm a diagnosis of anaphylaxis and evaluate for underlying mast cell disorders, highlighting the specific limitation encountered in this patient's workup.

To confirm a diagnosis of anaphylaxis and evaluate for underlying mast cell disorders like systemic mastocytosis, serum tryptase must be measured according to a strict temporal sequence that captures the acute surge and the return to baseline. The gold standard involves collecting three distinct samples: the first as soon as feasible without delaying resuscitative measures (typically within minutes of arrival), a second sample drawn 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset (and certainly within 4 hours) to capture the peak concentration, and a final "baseline" sample collected at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution.

In this case, the utility of this diagnostic algorithm was compromised because no tryptase level was obtained during the initial presentation or during the first two anaphylactic episodes. As noted in the discussion, tryptase has a short half-life; its elevation is transient, peaking rapidly before declining toward baseline within hours. By failing to draw blood during the initial crisis or shortly thereafter, the team missed the opportunity to document the characteristic acute spike associated with massive mast cell degranulation. Instead, sampling occurred only during the third episode (1 hour post-onset) and 27 hours later. While these later values were elevated and suggestive of systemic mastocytosis—supporting the hypothesis that this multiphasic course was driven by an underlying clonal disorder rather than simple biphasic allergy—the absence of concurrent baseline and peak measurements prevents definitive differentiation between a transient reaction in a susceptible individual versus established disease using standard WHO criteria alone. This highlights that in cases of recurrent or multiphasic anaphylaxis, obtaining that initial "acute" sample is critical; missing it renders the subsequent workup less robust for distinguishing reactive hypertryptasemia from chronic mastocytosis.

### [Pathophysiology] Although this patient did not meet all formal WHO criteria for Systemic Mastocytosis, what constellation of clinical findings (including laboratory values and organ involvement) supported a high suspicion of this underlying condition as a predisposing factor for multiphasic anaphylaxis?

This case illustrates how a constellation of "minor" clinical clues can raise high suspicion for systemic mastocytosis (SM) even when formal World Health Organization (WHO) diagnostic criteria are not fully met. While the patient lacked sufficient tissue for the major criterion (dense aggregates of mast cells in bone marrow), three key features strongly pointed toward SM as the underlying driver of her multiphasic anaphylaxis: **persistently elevated serum tryptase**, **hepatomegaly**, and **neuropsychiatric comorbidities**.

First, the biochemical hallmark was the markedly elevated baseline and reactive tryptase levels. Although the ideal protocol involves sampling at onset, 1–2 hours post-symptom peak, and >24 hours post-resolution to establish a true baseline, this patient's follow-up levels remained significantly elevated long after symptom resolution. In a patient with recurrent anaphylaxis, a baseline tryptase level consistently exceeding 20 ng/mL is a sensitive indicator of mastocytosis; higher thresholds (>11.4 ng/mL or >20% above baseline) are often cited as suggestive. The persistence of these elevations distinguishes SM from transient mediator release seen in simple IgE-mediated allergies.

Second, the presence of hepatomegaly on abdominal sonography represents direct organ infiltration by mast cells, fulfilling one of the WHO minor criteria for SM. While hepatomegaly can result from various etiologies (e.g., fatty liver disease common in obesity/diabetes), its discovery in conjunction with unexplained anaphylaxis warrants investigation for mast cell burden in visceral organs.

Finally, the patient's history included depression and memory problems ("possible neuropsychiatric symptoms"). Neurocognitive dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a manifestation of systemic mastocytosis, likely mediated by chronic cytokine release or direct CNS infiltration by mast cells. When combined with severe cutaneous reactions (flushing, generalized urticaria) and resistance to standard monophasic recovery patterns (multiphasic course), this triad—**elevated tryptase, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms**—creates a compelling clinical picture of occult systemic mastocytosis predisposing the patient to severe, recurrent anaphylaxis triggered by macrolides like clarithromycin.

### [Management] Given the uncertainty surrounding optimal observation durations for biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis, how do current guidelines attempt to balance resource utilization with patient safety regarding discharge planning after symptom resolution?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

Current guidelines acknowledge that while the optimal observation window for biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis remains undefined, the prevailing strategy attempts to balance resource utilization with patient safety by adopting a **risk-stratified approach** rather than a rigid, universal observation period. As illustrated in the case of this 60-year-old woman with clarithromycin-induced multiphasic anaphylaxis, reliance solely on a fixed "4-to-6-hour" rule is insufficient because biphasic reactions can recur up to 78 hours post-exposure, and nearly half of all biphasic events occur between 6 and 12 hours.

In this case, despite an initial robust response to epinephrine and fluid resuscitation, the patient experienced three distinct recurrences of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours. This underscores that symptom resolution does not guarantee immunity from relapse. To manage this uncertainty without overwhelming emergency departments with low-risk patients, modern frameworks—such as the UK's 2021 guideline referenced in the discussion—advocate for tiered monitoring based on severity markers. Key predictors for extended observation (>12–24 hours) identified in the literature and highlighted by this case include **delayed epinephrine administration**, **severe initial presentation** (e.g., hypotension/shock), **necessity for multiple epinephrine doses**, and **recurrence of symptoms**.

Conversely, patients with mild cutaneous-only reactions who respond rapidly to a single dose of epinephrine may safely undergo shorter observation periods (typically 4–6 hours). For complex cases like ours, where multiple relapses occurred despite standard therapy, guidelines suggest transitioning to higher-acuity settings (like the ICU) or extending observation until a stable period without symptoms has been established—in this instance, roughly 24 hours. It is crucial to note that while corticosteroids were administered in this case following historical paradigms to prevent biphasic reactions, current evidence suggests they offer no prophylactic benefit against recurrence; thus, management must rely on vigilant clinical monitoring and readiness to re-administer epinephrine rather than pharmacologic suppression alone. Ultimately, discharge planning should be driven by the stability of the patient over time and the presence of high-risk features rather than a blanket time limit.
