# Beyond the Auto-Injector: Why 0.3 mg Might Be Underdosing Your Adult Patients

We've all seen the "textbook" anaphylaxis case: a young adult ingests a peanut, develops hives, gets hit with hypotension, receives an IM epi shot, and walks out stable. But what happens when your patient is a 104 kg woman in her 60s? What happens when she survives the initial crash, only to crash *again* six hours later? And again? And again?

This isn't just a story about biphasic anaphylaxis; it's a cautionary tale about how our reliance on convenient auto-injectors might be leaving heavy-set adults dangerously under-dosed.

In a fascinating new case report from Schneider et al., we meet a patient who suffered **multiphasic** anaphylaxis to clarithromycin. She didn't just have one relapse; she had four distinct episodes of shock over roughly 24 hours. While the underlying driver turned out to be a probable (though not formally diagnosed) systemic mastocytosis—a condition where mast cells run wild—the immediate lesson for us in the ED is far more practical: **the dose matters.**

### The Dose Gap: 0.3 mg vs. Reality

Let's look at the numbers. The international consensus guideline recommends epinephrine at **0.01 mg/kg**, capped at **0.5 mg**. For a patient weighing 104 kg, that math doesn't stop at 0.3 mg; it screams for closer to 1 mg if we strictly follow the weight-based formula, but certainly warrants the full 0.5 mg cap immediately rather than settling for half that amount.

Yet, this patient received two doses of **0.3 mg** via auto-injector during her stabilization phase because that is what was readily available and familiar to the team. It worked enough to buy time, but it wasn't enough to break the cycle of mast cell degranulation driven by her massive burden of mediators and her high body mass index (BMI). She required two subsequent doses of manually prepared **0.5 mg** IM epinephrine to finally stabilize after her second and third relapses.

Why does this distinction matter? Because relying on the default "adult" auto-injector (often stuck at 0.3 mg in many regions) creates a therapeutic ceiling for anyone over ~50 kg who needs aggressive vasopressor support. A recent retrospective study highlights exactly this friction: patients receiving the initial 0.3 mg dose required significantly more escalation of care compared to those who got 0.5 mg right out of the gate. In a multiphasic scenario like this case, that hesitation or under-dosing can mean the difference between managing symptoms on a regular ward and fighting for airways in the ICU repeatedly.

### The Mast Cell Connection

While we're debating micrograms of epi, we must acknowledge why this specific patient kept crashing even with treatment. Workup revealed elevated tryptase levels throughout her course and hepatomegaly, pointing strongly toward **systemic mastocytosis**. Even though she didn't meet every strict WHO criterion (likely due to sampling limitations), her physiology was primed for catastrophic mediator release upon stimulation by clarithromycin or stress alone.

Patients with underlying mast cell disorders are notorious for having more severe initial reactions and higher rates of biphasic/multiphasic courses because their "ammunition belt" is simply fuller than average. When you add obesity (which increases volume of distribution and alters clearance dynamics) to mastocytosis, you get a perfect storm where standard dosing feels insufficient until you push harder earlier on. This case underscores that while we don't need to screen everyone for mastocytosis immediately, we should suspect it in patients with repeated relapses despite adequate resuscitation efforts—and perhaps treat them more aggressively from hour zero.

### Corticosteroids: Do They Help Here?

It's worth noting another pillar of management discussed in this report: corticosteroids. You'll notice this patient received IV prednisone and dexamethasone after her first recurrence because "that's what we do." Historically, steroids were given prophylactically to prevent biphasic reactions based on theoretical grounds (slowing inflammatory cascades). However, modern evidence is increasingly skeptical. Systematic reviews suggest steroids offer no benefit in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis and may actually increase hospital admissions due to side effects like hyperglycemia (this patient had significant glucose spikes anyway).

While guidelines are shifting away from routine steroid use in pure anaphylaxis cases, some clinicians still reach for them in complex or multiphasic scenarios hoping for that extra layer of protection against relapse as seen here with prednisone/prednisolone protocols varying by institution (she got both IV prednisone daily *and* IV dexa). As we move forward, our focus should remain squarely on titrating epinephrine appropriately rather than betting on steroids that haven't proven their worth in trials yet found their way into our reflexive order sets.

### Take-Home Points for Tomorrow Morning Shift

This case forces us to rethink our approach to dosing in larger adults and those with unexplained relapsing courses:

*   **Don't let auto-injector convenience dictate your max dose:** If your patient weighs >50 kg (which includes almost all adult men and many women), ensure they receive **0.5 mg** IM epinephrine as the initial bolus if you have vials/syringes ready; do not limit yourself to 0.3 mg unless you are forced to wait for transport or equipment failure allows otherwise.
*   **Recognize multiphasic patterns early:** If a patient requires multiple doses of epinephrine within hours despite initial stabilization, think "refractory," consider underlying mast cell disease (check tryptase!), and prepare for prolonged observation or ICU transfer rather than discharging after one hour of silence post-treatment window closure rules change based on severity).
*   **Question the steroid reflex:** While not contraindicated per se in complex cases today, remember that corticosteroids likely won't stop the next wave of anaphylaxis; optimizing adrenergic support will do much more heavy lifting than adding methylprednisolone or dexamethasone alone will ever achieve regarding hemodynamics or symptom control during recurrence events themselves.)
*   **Know your limits:** In cases where standard intermittent dosing fails repeatedly (like this patient's four crashes), consider continuous IV infusion protocols if available, but recognize that evidence gaps exist here too—we often fly blind until we see patterns emerge across registries like this one provides inspiration for future data collection.)



# The Corticosteroid Conundrum: Do They Prevent Biphasic Anaphylaxis?

We've all been there. You treat a severe anaphylactic reaction with IM epinephrine, fluids, and antihistamines. The patient stabilizes, looks great, and you feel confident sending them home—or admitting them to the ward for "observation." Then, 6 to 12 hours later, the alarm goes off. Hypotension returns. Urticaria flares. It's biphasic anaphylaxis.

In our latest case report from *Schneider et al.*, we witness a particularly dramatic version of this nightmare: **multiphasic** anaphylaxis. A woman in her 60s experienced four distinct episodes of shock after taking clarithromycin. Here is the kicker that should make us pause and rethink our reflexive prescribing habits: Upon her first relapse, the team didn't just give epinephrine; they aggressively loaded her with corticosteroids (IV dexamethasone and daily IV prednisone), adhering strictly to the old-school dogma that steroids prevent biphasic reactions.

And guess what happened? She went into shock again anyway. Twice more.

This case serves as a brutal reality check against historical teaching. Despite the patient receiving significant steroid coverage (30 mg prednisone IV daily plus 8 mg dexamethasone IV), she suffered four separate episodes of anaphylactic shock over roughly 24 hours. If steroids were truly protective against biphasic phenomena in this context, this patient should have remained stable after the initial stabilization. Instead, she required escalating doses of epinephrine and transfer to the ICU before finally settling down after 24 hours of observation without further relapse.

So, why do we still reach for the methylprednisolone drip? The short answer is: because we're afraid of missing something, but the long answer is that the evidence simply isn't there to support it—and there might actually be harm in doing so.

### The Evidence Gap

For decades, textbooks have told us that corticosteroids inhibit late-phase inflammatory responses and thus prevent biphasic reactions. It sounds logical on paper: stop the cytokines, stop the rebound. But clinical reality tells a different story. As noted in the discussion accompanying this case, a pivotal 2017 systematic review analyzed 22 studies on this exact topic and found only one study supporting steroid efficacy; 21 studies showed **no reduction** in biphasic rates with steroid administration.

Furthermore, recent data suggests that giving steroids might actually be detrimental in certain contexts. One study indicated an increased likelihood of ICU or ward admission among patients treated with pre-hospital steroids compared to those who weren't. Why? Perhaps because steroids create a false sense of security ("the patient is covered"), leading clinicians to discharge patients too early or fail to recognize subtle signs of impending collapse until it's too late because the anti-inflammatory effect masks early warning signs like rash or flushing while hemodynamic instability sets in unnoticed until profound hypotension occurs.

### The Diagnostic Mystery: Systemic Mastocytosis?

While this case highlights our failure to predict biphasic events using steroids, it also shines a light on a hidden variable: **Systemic Mastocytosis**.

Upon further investigation of this multiphasic case, the authors found elevated baseline and reactive tryptase levels (though drawn slightly outside ideal timing windows) alongside hepatomegaly and neuropsychiatric symptoms consistent with mast cell activation disorder syndrome (MCAS). While she didn't meet all formal WHO diagnostic criteria due to biopsy limitations, she had two minor criteria suggestive of systemic mastocytosis (SM).

Patients with SM are notorious for having severe, refractory, or multiphasic anaphylaxis due to their massive reservoirs of mast cells ready to degranulate repeatedly without needing fresh allergen exposure (or perhaps triggered by endogenous mediators). This explains why our patient bounced back from shock four times despite aggressive standard care including steroids and repeated epinephrine boluses (note: the team appropriately escalated from 0.3 mg auto-injectors to individualized 0.5 mg IM doses).

This raises a terrifying possibility for every clinician managing "simple" anaphylaxis: Could some fraction of our "idiopathic" biphasic cases actually be undiagnosed mastocytosis? If so, they require longer observation periods and perhaps different prophylactic strategies entirely (like leukotriene inhibitors or cromolyn sodium), none of which includes corticosteroids as a preventative measure for recurrence once degranulation begins.

### What About Dosing? A Side Note on Epinephrine

Before we close out on steroids, let's briefly touch on another lesson from this case regarding epinephrine dosing dynamics in obese patients or large adults (>50 kg). The patient weighed 104 kg yet received standard 0.3 mg auto-injector doses initially for two episodes before switching to manual preparation of 0.5 mg doses for subsequent relapses.

Current guidelines generally recommend **0.5 mg** for adults >50 kg (often calculated as 0.01 mg/kg up to a max). Yet, many hospitals stock only 0.3 mg devices due to legacy preferences or cost considerations in older generations of equipment design—a trend shifting now but still prevalent globally as seen here). Retrospective data suggests patients starting with 0.3 mg are far more likely to need rescue dosing than those getting the full therapeutic load upfront ($>5$ fold difference in some cohorts). In a multiphasic course where time-to-treatment is critical during subsequent relapses, relying on suboptimal auto-injector dosing could be contributing factor prolonging morbidity if not recognized quickly enough by staff comfortable preparing manual syringes immediately upon sign #1 of recurrence rather than waiting for an auto-injector run-out scenario or hesitation with larger vials/syringes available elsewhere in your facility.)

### Take-Home Points & Clinical Pearls

*   **Corticosteroids do NOT prevent biphasic anaphylaxis.** Relying on them creates a dangerous placebo effect; do not use them as a reason to shorten observation times or feel secure about discharge planning post-stabilization based solely on steroid administration history (*NEJM*, *JACI*, *Resuscitation*).
*   **Elevated Tryptase + Multiphase Course = Red Flag.** When a patient experiences multiple recurrences despite adequate epinephrine/fluids/steroids/pulse oximetry correction (<span style="color:red">even</span> if they respond transiently), suspect underlying mast cell disease (e.g., Systemic Mastocytosis) and consult Allergy/Immunology early for baseline tryptase workup (*Tryptase peaks within ~4h post-event; repeat at <span style="color:green">baseline</span> >24h later*).
*   **Dose Matters:** For adult patients weighing >50 kg (and certainly those approaching obesity thresholds like BMI >30), ensure your default IM dose is **0.5 mg**, not just whatever fits in your pocket-sized auto-injector drawer unless you can manually prepare higher volumes safely (*Avoid under-dosing delays*).
*   **Observation Duration Remains Uncertain:** Since we cannot reliably predict who will go biphasic/multiphase without biomarkers like tryptase trends or genetic markers currently unavailable at bedside, extended observation (often >6–8 hours minimum) remains mandatory regardless of steroid use (*Monitor closely until asymptomatic phase lasts >6–8h*).
*   **Steroid Harms Are Real:** Be aware that IV steroids can induce hyperglycemia (dangerous in diabetics like our patient!), fluid retention masking volume status changes ("pseudo-resuscitation"), and immunosuppression risks—all while offering zero protection against recurrence (*Watch glucose! Check BP carefully!*).

**Final Thought:**
Anaphylaxis management has evolved rapidly toward recognizing that epinephrine is king—but sometimes even kings need better advisors than "just give prednisone." In complex cases like ours where standard protocols fail repeatedly (multiphase shocks), look deeper into mast biology rather than stacking anti-inflammatories blindly until someone says "stop." Until we have robust data predicting who will recur naturally via biomarkers alone (**tryptase curves**), vigilance beats pharmacology every time when it comes to preventing death from missed second waves.*



# When 'Resolved' Means 'Wait for It': Defining Multiphasic vs. Refractory Anaphylaxis

We've all been there: You push epi, give fluids, toss in some Benadryl and steroids, and the patient's BP snaps back to 120/80. The wheezing clears, the hives fade, and you feel that sweet rush of "we got them." You discharge them home or transfer them to a step-down unit with a clean bill of health. Then, six hours later—or sometimes twelve—the patient crashes *again*.

This isn't just a bad day in the ED; it's **multiphasic anaphylaxis**.

In today's case report from Schneider et al., we dive into a terrifying scenario where a 60-something female experienced **four distinct episodes** of anaphylactic shock after a single dose of clarithromycin. She responded perfectly to initial resuscitation, only to relapse repeatedly over 12 hours despite standard care. What makes this case particularly gripping for us in Critical Care and Emergency Medicine is not just the sheer number of recurrences, but the diagnostic dilemma: Was this refractory anaphylaxis? Sustained absorption? Or true biphasic/multiphasic disease? And why did her tryptase levels suggest a hidden driver like systemic mastocytosis?

Let's break down the definitions, the pitfalls, and the management gray zones that this case illuminates.

## The Semantic Trap: Refractory vs. Biphasic vs. Multiphasic

One of the most dangerous errors in managing severe allergic reactions is misclassifying the *type* of reaction occurring. If you mistake one for another, your disposition strategy fails catastrophically.

**Refractory anaphylaxis** is defined as persistent symptoms with little to no response to correctly administered epinephrine and supportive care. In this state, the patient never truly "resolves"; they are stuck in a cycle of shock that requires escalating interventions like vasopressor drips or intubation immediately.

**Sustained anaphylaxis**, on the other hand, mimics biphasic reactions but is actually driven by ongoing allergen absorption (e.g., slow-release medications or food boluses still in the gut). The symptoms don't disappear because the trigger is still entering the system. Treating this as "biphasic" leads to premature discharge if you assume the allergen load has cleared when it hasn't.

Then we have **true biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis**. As seen in our case patient, this requires a complete return to baseline (an asymptomatic phase) followed by spontaneous recurrence *without* re-exposure to the allergen. This patient had four separate waves:
1.  Initial presentation (shock).
2.  Resolution with IM epi/IV fluids/benzos/steroids.
3.  Relapse #1 (requiring ICU transfer).
4.  Relapse #2 & #3 (less severe but still requiring repeated epi).

By hour 24, she was stable again with no further relapses. The distinction matters immensely for observation protocols. If you think it's refractory when it's actually biphasic, you might start a dopamine drip unnecessarily while ignoring that she needs extended observation rather than pressors alone. Conversely, assuming it's biphasic when it's sustained absorption could lead to sending her home too early while she absorbs more drug from her GI tract.

## The Systemic Mastocytosis Clue: Why Did She Crash Four Times?

The most fascinating aspect of this case isn't just the frequency of relapses; it's the search for an underlying mechanism that predisposed her to such volatility. While most biphasic cases are idiopathic or related to massive initial mast cell burden from foods/insects/anesthetics, this patient had features suggestive of **Systemic Mastocytosis**.

Although she didn't meet all formal WHO criteria (major criteria couldn't be confirmed due to limited biopsy material), several red flags appeared:
*   Persistently elevated baseline and peak serum tryptase levels (>11 ng/mL is often cited as suspicious; hers were significantly higher).
*   Hepatomegaly found on ultrasound.
*   Neuropsychiatric symptoms (depression/memory issues) which can be associated with mast cell mediator overload affecting cognition ("brain fog").
*   A history of strong local reactions to wasp stings without systemic spread until now—a classic warning sign that may evolve into systemic involvement later in life.

Patients with undiagnosed mastocytosis have an abnormally high density of mast cells throughout their bodies (skin, liver, bone marrow). When triggered by an antigen like clarithromycin (which can directly activate mast cells independent of IgE), they release massive quantities of mediators simultaneously across organ systems that healthy individuals wouldn't experience with similar severity or persistence. This explains why her attempts at stabilization failed repeatedly; every time epi wore off or symptoms dipped slightly, another wave of degranulation hit from a different reservoir of cells.

This highlights a critical pearl: **If a patient has recurrent anaphylaxis despite optimal treatment and appears unusually resistant or volatile compared to peers, consider checking tryptase levels aggressively.** Ideally, you want three samples: immediate (<2h), peak (~4h), and baseline (>24h post-resolution). Unfortunately, in this case report, they missed the initial window entirely because they were focused on saving lives! Always keep your finger on the pulse regarding timing—even if you're busy intubating someone else first thing tomorrow morning... wait for me... get those labs drawn!

2.  **Expectant Management:** Watching closely and treating each recurrence individually based on clinical need ("treat as you go").

Both approaches carried significant risk without solid evidence backing either specifically for *multiphasic* cases (as opposed to biphasic). # The Silent Trigger: Unmasking Systemic Mastocytosis in Complex Allergic Reactions

We've all seen the textbook case of anaphylaxis: a bee sting, a peanut butter sandwich, or a new prescription drug. You hit them with IM epi, they stabilize, you observe them for four hours, and they go home. It's a clean narrative. But sometimes, the story doesn't end when the vitals normalize. Sometimes, the "resolving" patient suddenly crashes again, and then again, creating a chaotic cycle of hypotension and bronchospasm that defies standard observation protocols.

This is exactly what happened in a fascinating case report recently published by Schneider et al., describing a woman in her 60s who experienced **multiphasic anaphylaxis** to clarithromycin. What makes this case particularly chilling isn't just the severity of the shock—it was that she required *four* separate doses of epinephrine within 24 hours. While biphasic reactions are well-documented (recurrence after an asymptomatic interval), multiphasic courses are rare beasts. More importantly, this patient's trajectory forced us to look past the immediate trigger (the antibiotic) and ask: *Why* did this happen? The answer lay hidden beneath the surface: **Systemic Mastocytosis**.

### The Clinical Picture: When "Resolved" Isn't Really Resolved

Let's walk through the chaos. This patient presented with classic signs of severe anaphylaxis—hypotension (74/50 mmHg), hypoxia (SpO2 88%), generalized urticaria, and throat tightness—one hour after taking her first dose of clarithromycin (which she had never taken before). She responded initially to IM epinephrine and IV fluids, allowing for discharge planning until... she didn't stay stable.

Within the first 12 hours of admission, she suffered three distinct relapses into anaphylactic shock. Each episode required rescue epinephrine. Interestingly, as noted by the authors, the severity seemed to wane slightly with each subsequent wave—from profound hypoxia and cyanosis to milder dyspnea and pruritus—but the threat remained real enough to warrant ICU transfer.

Here lies the teaching moment: **Do not assume stability based on a single successful bolus.** In patients with risk factors for severe mast cell activation or unexplained recurrent symptoms, a "good response" is merely a temporary reprieve. The team considered aggressive interventions like continuous IV epinephrine infusions versus an expectant watch-and-wait approach. Ultimately, they chose monitoring because symptoms began to taper off naturally after 24 hours without further triggers—a fortunate outcome given the lack of definitive guidelines for managing multiphasic events in this specific context.

### The Hidden Diagnosis: Why Suspect Mastocytosis?

So, why did this woman react so violently and repeatedly? The answer likely lies in **Systemic Mastocytosis (SM)**. While SM is rare, it should be top-of-mind whenever you encounter anaphylaxis that feels "out of proportion" or exhibits unusual patterns like multiphasic recurrence without re-exposure to the allergen.

In this case, several subtle clues pointed toward SM long before a bone marrow biopsy could confirm anything:
*   **Recurrent Severe Anaphylaxis:** The sheer frequency of relapses suggests a lowered threshold for mast cell degranulation across organ systems.
*   **Elevated Tryptase:** Crucially, her serum tryptase levels were persistently elevated during episodes and at baseline follow-up.
*   **"Red Herrings":** She had hepatomegaly on ultrasound and chronic neuropsychiatric symptoms (depression/memory issues), both known associations with mast cell infiltration in organs other than skin/bone marrow.
*   **The Diagnostic Gap:** Interestingly, she didn't meet *full* WHO diagnostic criteria because bone marrow material was insufficient to prove focal aggregates of mast cells (the major criterion). However, having two minor criteria plus elevated tryptase creates a very high clinical suspicion that warrants close follow-up even if formal classification isn't achieved immediately.

### The Tryptase Trap and Timing Matters

One of the most critical failures in this case wasn't clinical management—it was lab timing. To diagnose SM effectively after an anaphylactic event, guidelines recommend a strict triad of tryptase measurements:
1.  **Acute:** As soon as feasible (while treating).
2.  **Peak:** 1–4 hours post-symptom onset (when tryptase peaks).
3.  **Baseline:** >24 hours after full resolution to establish your personal floor level.

In this patient's case, they missed window #1 entirely due to prioritization errors ("first tryptase sample was taken 1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode"). They captured some data later (during episode #3 and at 27 hours), but missing that initial acute spike limits our ability to calculate delta-tryptase accurately ($\Delta$ = Acute - Baseline + 20%). If you suspect SM or want to rule out underlying mast cell disorders in complex cases, **you must get that baseline sample.** If your acute tryptase is >114% higher than baseline OR if your absolute value exceeds ~20 ng/mL consistently over time without another explanation (like renal failure), you have found your smoking gun for systemic disease rather than simple IgE-mediated allergy.

### Pearls for Your Next Shift

While we await better data on managing multiphasic anaphylaxis specifically related to mastocytosis, here are actionable takeaways from this complex case:

*   **High-Vigilance Observation:** Do not rush discharges for patients who require multiple epi doses or have hemodynamic instability during their initial presentation; consider extended observation (>24h) if relapse risk is high or underlying pathology is suspected.
*   **Tryptase Protocol Compliance:** Ensure you order acute AND baseline tryptase levels in every severe or recurrent anaphylaxis case; missing either renders the test useless for diagnosing SM or predicting recurrence risk.
*   **Look Beyond Skin Signs:** In patients with recurrent "idiopathic" allergies or unexplained hepatosplenomegaly/cognitive changes alongside allergic crises, keep Systemic Mastocytosis on your differential list even if formal criteria aren't met yet.
*   **Epinephrine Dosing Reality Check:** Be wary of relying solely on standard auto-injectors (often 0.3 mg) for larger adults (>50 kg); this patient received multiple sub-optimal doses before manual preparation allowed for proper weight-based dosing (0.5 mg). In refractory/multiphasic scenarios where volume status matters less but receptor occupancy does more precision dosing counts more than ever before—and don't hesitate to draw up your own vial if you need it now!
*   **Corticosteroids Are Not Magic Bullets:** Remember that while steroids were given here traditionally to prevent biphasic reactions today's evidence suggests they offer little protection against recurrence but carry significant metabolic risks; rely on vigilant monitoring rather than prophylactic steroids alone in these complex scenarios unless there is a compelling reason otherwise (e.g., asthma exacerbation component).
