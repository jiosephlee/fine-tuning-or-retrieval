Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] Why do current evidence-based guidelines generally advise against the routine administration of corticosteroids for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis, and what specific adverse outcomes have been associated with their pre-hospital use?

Current evidence-based guidelines advise against the routine administration of corticosteroids for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis because robust clinical data fails to demonstrate a reduction in recurrence rates. As highlighted in the discussion of this case report, a pivotal 2017 systematic review analyzed 22 studies and found that only one supported steroid efficacy, whereas 21 demonstrated no benefit in preventing biphasic reactions. The pathophysiological rationale for their historical use—that steroids would inhibit the late-phase inflammatory cascade—has not translated into improved clinical outcomes in randomized trials.

Furthermore, pre-hospital and early emergency administration of corticosteroids has been associated with specific adverse outcomes, including an increased likelihood of hospital admission or transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU). A 2019 study cited in the case report noted that patients receiving pre-hospital corticosteroids were more frequently admitted compared to those who did not receive them, suggesting that steroids may paradoxically prolong the need for higher-level monitoring without conferring prophylactic benefit.

In the case presented by Schneider et al., despite the patient's multiphasic course necessitating ICU admission and repeated epinephrine dosing, corticosteroids were administered only *after* the first recurrence of anaphylaxis based on traditional protocols rather than preventive necessity. Even with high-dose intravenous prednisone and dexamethasone, the patient experienced three additional relapses within 12 hours. This underscores that once a biphasic or multiphasic trajectory is established, steroids do not reliably abort subsequent episodes. Consequently, modern management prioritizes rigorous observation periods and immediate availability of epinephrine over pharmacologic prophylaxis with corticosteroids.
