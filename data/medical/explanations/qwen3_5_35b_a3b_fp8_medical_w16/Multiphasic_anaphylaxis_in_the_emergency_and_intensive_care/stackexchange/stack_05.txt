Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Pathophysiology] Although this patient did not meet all formal WHO criteria for Systemic Mastocytosis, what constellation of clinical findings (including laboratory values and organ involvement) supported a high suspicion of this underlying condition as a predisposing factor for multiphasic anaphylaxis?

This case illustrates how a constellation of "minor" clinical clues can raise high suspicion for systemic mastocytosis (SM) even when formal World Health Organization (WHO) diagnostic criteria are not fully met. While the patient lacked sufficient tissue for the major criterion (dense aggregates of mast cells in bone marrow), three key features strongly pointed toward SM as the underlying driver of her multiphasic anaphylaxis: **persistently elevated serum tryptase**, **hepatomegaly**, and **neuropsychiatric comorbidities**.

First, the biochemical hallmark was the markedly elevated baseline and reactive tryptase levels. Although the ideal protocol involves sampling at onset, 1–2 hours post-symptom peak, and >24 hours post-resolution to establish a true baseline, this patient's follow-up levels remained significantly elevated long after symptom resolution. In a patient with recurrent anaphylaxis, a baseline tryptase level consistently exceeding 20 ng/mL is a sensitive indicator of mastocytosis; higher thresholds (>11.4 ng/mL or >20% above baseline) are often cited as suggestive. The persistence of these elevations distinguishes SM from transient mediator release seen in simple IgE-mediated allergies.

Second, the presence of hepatomegaly on abdominal sonography represents direct organ infiltration by mast cells, fulfilling one of the WHO minor criteria for SM. While hepatomegaly can result from various etiologies (e.g., fatty liver disease common in obesity/diabetes), its discovery in conjunction with unexplained anaphylaxis warrants investigation for mast cell burden in visceral organs.

Finally, the patient's history included depression and memory problems ("possible neuropsychiatric symptoms"). Neurocognitive dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a manifestation of systemic mastocytosis, likely mediated by chronic cytokine release or direct CNS infiltration by mast cells. When combined with severe cutaneous reactions (flushing, generalized urticaria) and resistance to standard monophasic recovery patterns (multiphasic course), this triad—**elevated tryptase, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms**—creates a compelling clinical picture of occult systemic mastocytosis predisposing the patient to severe, recurrent anaphylaxis triggered by macrolides like clarithromycin.
