Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Based on the clinical definition provided in the case discussion, what distinguishes 'multiphasic' anaphylaxis from 'refractory' anaphylaxis, and why is this distinction critical for determining the timing of intervention?

**Clinical Pearl:**
The fundamental distinction between **multiphasic** and **refractory** anaphylaxis lies in the temporal pattern of symptom resolution and the response to epinephrine, a differentiation that dictates whether the clinician must prepare for *recurrence* or manage *persistent instability*.

**Multiphasic anaphylaxis**, as illustrated in our case report, is characterized by an initial reaction that resolves completely with appropriate therapy (an "asymptomatic phase"), followed by a spontaneous recurrence of symptoms hours later without re-exposure to the allergen. Our patient experienced four distinct episodes separated by periods of hemodynamic stability; notably, the intensity of symptoms actually decreased with each subsequent relapse. This pattern requires a strategy focused on extended observation and readiness to reinstitute treatment upon symptom return.

In contrast, **refractory anaphylaxis** is defined by persistent symptoms that fail to respond adequately to repeated, correctly administered doses of epinephrine. There is no true asymptomatic interval; rather, the patient remains in a state of shock or respiratory distress despite maximal standard therapy.

**Why this distinction matters for intervention timing:**
This distinction is critical because it determines the urgency and nature of therapeutic escalation. In **refractory anaphylaxis**, the clock starts immediately: failure to respond mandates immediate transition to advanced rescue therapies, such as intravenous epinephrine infusions or vasopressors, without waiting for further deterioration. Waiting for a "second opinion" or repeating IM doses indefinitely delays life-saving intervention.

Conversely, in **multiphasic anaphylaxis**, as seen in our patient's recovery trajectory, aggressive preemptive IV infusion is not automatically indicated unless the frequency or severity of recurrences suggests imminent collapse. Instead, the priority shifts to vigilant monitoring during the asymptomatic window. As noted in our case discussion, while continuous IV epinephrine was considered, it carries significant risks (e.g., arrhythmias) and was unnecessary once it became clear that recurrences were self-limiting and less intense over time. Misidentifying a multiphasic course as refractory could lead to unnecessary ICU admission with invasive lines; conversely, misclassifying refractory anaphylaxis as multiphasic could result in fatal delay if clinicians wait for a "resolution" that will never occur before escalating care.
