Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Despite normal serum C3 and C4 levels, why did this patient meet the criteria for CM-HUS rather than Secondary TMA due to Sepsis/DIC alone? What specific clinical timeline and histological features supported the diagnosis of CM-HUS over pure sepsis-induced coagulopathy?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

This patient meets the criteria for **complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS)** triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia rather than simple sepsis-induced disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) primarily due to the **temporal dissociation between the resolution of coagulopathy and the emergence of distinct thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)**.

While the patient initially presented with classic signs of sepsis-associated DIC—including pancytopenia, elevated D-dimer/fibrin degradation products, and consumptive coagulopathy—the diagnostic pivot point occurred during her clinical course. As her sepsis resolved and inflammatory markers normalized, her coagulation profile improved; however, she subsequently developed a **new, isolated episode of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA)** characterized by a sharp drop in hemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, and persistent thrombocytopenia that failed to improve with standard supportive care. This "split" presentation—where the consumptive coagulopathy resolves but the hemolysis and thrombocytopenia worsen—is highly characteristic of TMA evolving independently from DIC. In pure sepsis-induced DIC, hematologic parameters typically track together; a divergence suggests an alternative mechanism driving endothelial injury.

Furthermore, **histological confirmation** provided definitive evidence distinguishing this case from secondary TMA due to sepsis alone. The renal biopsy demonstrated severe acute tubular necrosis with **fibrin thrombi within glomerular capillaries and scattered crescent formation**, hallmarks of TMA. Crucially, immunofluorescence showed **no immune complex deposition**, ruling out immune-complex mediated glomerulonephritis or lupus nephritis. While DIC can cause ischemic changes, the presence of frank fibrin thrombi in conjunction with MAHA confirms a primary thrombotic microangiopathic process.

Finally, we must address why normal serum C3 and C4 levels did not exclude CM-HUS. In approximately 30% to 50% of CM-HUS cases (formerly known as atypical HUS), serum complement levels remain within normal ranges because they reflect steady-state production rather than dynamic consumption or local tissue activation. The diagnosis here relied on **clinical exclusion**:
1.  **ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%**, effectively ruling out Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP).
2.  **Stool PCR was negative** for Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli*, excluding typical HUS.
3.  The patient exhibited a dramatic response to **eculizumab** (terminal complement inhibition) after failing plasma exchange monotherapy, confirming the pathophysiology was driven by unregulated alternative pathway activation triggered by the bacterial infection.

In summary, this case illustrates that in adult patients with severe sepsis who develop persistent or delayed MAHA after initial stabilization—and where DIC has otherwise resolved—one must suspect infection-triggered CM-HUS regardless of normal baseline complement levels. Early recognition allows for targeted complement blockade, which is superior to plasma exchange alone in reversing the underlying pathology and preserving renal function.
