Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Management] What are the distinct roles of Plasma Exchange (PEX) versus Eculizumab in the treatment algorithm for CM-HUS, and why did sustained hematologic remission fail to occur until complement blockade was established in this case?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

In the management of complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), **plasma exchange (PEX)** and **eculizumab** serve fundamentally different mechanistic roles, a distinction clearly illustrated in this case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-associated TMA.

**Plasma Exchange (PEX)** acts primarily as a temporizing measure. Its mechanism involves the mechanical removal of circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, activated complement fragments, and potentially autoantibodies, thereby providing "bridging" support. In this patient, PEX was initiated empirically to stabilize the clinical picture while diagnostic workup excluded Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) and Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* HUS. We observed that while PEX facilitated a return of spontaneous diuresis and offered transient stabilization, it failed to arrest the relentless hemolysis or sustain platelet recovery once the procedure ceased. This underscores that PEX does not correct the underlying pathophysiology of uncontrolled alternative pathway activation; it merely dilutes or removes soluble effectives temporarily.

Conversely, **Eculizumab**, a monoclonal antibody against Complement Component 5 (C5), provides definitive therapy by blocking terminal complement activation. By preventing the cleavage of C5 into C5a and C5b-9 (the Membrane Attack Complex), eculizumab halts the endothelial injury driving the thrombotic microangiopathy. In this case, sustained hematologic remission—defined by the resolution of schistocytosis, normalization of platelets, and cessation of hemolysis—occurred only after eculizumab induction was completed. The persistence of hemolysis despite aggressive PEX confirms that the driver was an intrinsic dysregulation of the complement cascade rather than a reversible accumulation of circulating toxins alone.

The reason sustained remission failed until complement blockade was established lies in the pathophysiology of infection-triggered CM-HUS. Here, *C. canimorsus* acted as a potent "second hit," triggering excessive complement consumption and endothelial damage in a susceptible host (even without identified germline mutations). As long as C5 conversion proceeded unchecked by PEX alone, new microthrombi formed faster than they could be cleared or diluted. Only with specific inhibition of C5 was the feedback loop broken, allowing for true hematologic recovery and partial renal salvage.

Finally, regarding safety in this case: while eculizumab increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms like *Neisseria*, this patient's bacteremia had already cleared prior to initiation. The successful integration of vaccination (*Menveo*, *Bexsero*) and antibiotic prophylaxis allowed us to utilize life-saving complement blockade without precipitating recurrent sepsis. This case reinforces that in adult TMA where TTP is excluded, reliance on PEX alone is insufficient; timely transition to terminal complement inhibition is critical for durable remission.
