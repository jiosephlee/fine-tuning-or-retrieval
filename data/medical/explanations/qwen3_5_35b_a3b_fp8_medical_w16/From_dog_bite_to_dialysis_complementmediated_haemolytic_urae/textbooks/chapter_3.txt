Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 3: ## Pathophysiology of Complement-Mediated HUS and Infectious Triggers

Complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), historically referred to as atypical HUS, is fundamentally driven by the dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway. Under physiological conditions, this pathway serves as a critical line of defense against encapsulated bacteria through the generation of opsonins and membrane attack complexes. However, in CM-HUS, loss-of-function mutations or autoantibodies affecting regulatory proteins—such as Factor H, Factor I, Factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—prevent the timely termination of complement activation. This unchecked cascade leads to excessive consumption of terminal complement components and the deposition of inflammatory mediators on vascular endothelium. In the context of this case, *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* served as the inciting event for this pathological process. *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated gram-negative organism that expresses surface structures capable of directly activating the alternative pathway. While a robust immune response typically clears such pathogens, in a susceptible host, this interaction precipitates an overwhelming local inflammatory response that damages the glomerular endothelium, resulting in thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), hemolysis, and acute kidney injury.

The concept of the "second hit" is central to understanding why infection triggers manifest differently depending on individual susceptibility. While approximately 50% to 70% of patients with CM-HUS harbor identifiable germline mutations or acquired autoantibodies conferring genetic predisposition, clinical disease often remains latent until an environmental stressor occurs. Infection represents one of the most potent triggers among these stressors. For *C. canimorsus*, which exhibits variable serum susceptibility where some strains are fully susceptible while others display partial resistance due to their capsules, the bacterium creates a paradoxical scenario: it necessitates intense complement activation for clearance while simultaneously providing the substrate for uncontrolled endothelial injury in those with compromised regulation. This mechanism explains why a patient without overt chronic comorbidities could present with fulminant TMA solely following exposure to a specific pathogen. The infection acts as the "second hit" that overwhelms any marginal compensatory mechanisms inherent in the host's complement system.

Genetic testing plays a crucial role in stratifying these cases but does not always yield definitive answers regarding etiology. In hereditary forms of CM-HUS, identifying pathogenic variants guides long-term management and family counseling; however, as seen in this patient's case where no pathogenic variants were identified despite severe disease, a significant proportion of cases may be sporadic or triggered purely by infectious insults without detectable genetic defects. This distinction is vital because it suggests that not all CM-HUS requires lifelong genetic surveillance or implies an inherited disorder; rather, some represent extreme reactions to specific pathogens in individuals who may possess subtle or undiscovered regulatory weaknesses that are insufficient to cause disease spontaneously but fail under severe infectious pressure. Consequently, a negative genetic panel does not exclude a diagnosis of CM-HUS triggered by infection; instead, it reinforces the likelihood that the infection itself was sufficient to drive pathology in a potentially sub-clinically predisposed host or simply overwhelmed normal homeostatic controls acutely.

Diagnostic interpretation is further complicated by serological findings during acute phases. Standard screening tests measuring serum C3 and C4 levels often appear within normal limits in patients with active CM-HUS. This phenomenon occurs because C3 consumption is localized primarily within tissues and microvasculature rather than being reflected uniformly in systemic circulation during early stages, or because compensatory hepatic synthesis maintains baseline levels despite ongoing consumption at the site of injury. Furthermore, functional assays provide more insight than static concentration measurements; in this case, while C3/C4 were normal initially, subsequent functional testing revealed markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50), confirming dysregulation even when antigen levels were preserved. Therefore, reliance on normal C3/C4 values alone cannot rule out CM-HUS when clinical features strongly suggest TMA and other causes like Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) or Shiga toxin-producing *E.* coli HUS have been excluded.

Finally, distinguishing *C. canimorsus*-associated CM-HUS from other infectious triggers requires careful consideration of epidemiology and pathophysiology. Unlike Shiga toxin-producing *E.* coli (STEC)-HUS, which relies on bacterial toxins directly damaging endothelial cells independent of complement dysregulation (though complement amplification follows), *C. canimorsus* operates through direct pathway activation similar to meningococcal disease but distinct in its zoonotic vector and slower clinical evolution prior to crisis*. Other infections like parvovirus B19 or HIV can induce TMA via different mechanisms such as immune complex deposition or direct viral cytopathicity*. The uniqueness of this case lies in linking a rare canine zoonosis specifically to complement-driven TMA without STEC involvement or classic hereditary markers highlight that clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for infectious triggers across diverse microbial families when evaluating adult-onset TMA with renal failure and hemolysis.**
