Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 4: ## Therapeutic Strategy: Balancing Infection Control and Complement Blockade

The management of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia presents a distinct therapeutic challenge: the need to halt life-threatening thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) while simultaneously avoiding the exacerbation of an active bacterial infection. Terminal complement inhibition, specifically via eculizumab, is the definitive treatment for CM-HUS as it blocks the cleavage of C5, preventing the formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) responsible for endothelial damage. However, this mechanism also compromises host defense against encapsulated organisms, including *Neisseria* species and *Capnocytophaga*, which rely heavily on complement-mediated lysis for clearance. Consequently, clinicians must carefully weigh the timing of eculizumab initiation against the status of bacteremia. In this case, the decision to initiate eculizumab was deferred until blood cultures confirmed negativity and antibiotic therapy had been established. This strategy aimed to secure hematological control without precipitating uncontrolled sepsis, illustrating that while immediate blockade is ideal for renal preservation, it must be balanced against the risk of overwhelming infection in patients with active bacteremia.

Plasma exchange (PEX) serves as a crucial bridge therapy in this scenario, particularly when there is uncertainty regarding the specific subtype of thrombotic microangiopathy or when waiting for confirmatory genetic and enzymatic testing. Unlike in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), where PEX is curative by replacing deficient ADAMTS13 enzyme, its role in CM-HUS is primarily supportive. PEX functions by removing circulating complement fragments, inflammatory cytokines, and potentially autoantibodies that drive the alternative pathway activation. In this patient's course, PEX provided transient stabilization and facilitated the return of spontaneous diuresis; however, it did not sustain long-term remission once discontinued. The eventual resolution of hemolysis and platelet consumption occurred only after the initiation of eculizumab, confirming that PEX alone cannot correct the underlying dysregulation of the complement cascade. Therefore, PEX is appropriately utilized here as a temporizing measure to stabilize critically ill patients while preparing them for targeted biologic therapy.

Prophylactic measures are indispensable when administering terminal complement inhibitors due to their profound impact on immunity against encapsulated bacteria. Before commencing eculizumab induction in this case, rigorous preparation was undertaken involving both vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis. The patient received quadrivalent meningococcal conjugate vaccines covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W (Menveo) as well as serogroup B coverage (Bexsero). Ideally, vaccination should occur at least two weeks prior to therapy to ensure adequate antibody titers; however, given the urgency of her clinical deterioration requiring dialysis-dependent AKI and severe hemolysis, vaccination was expedited immediately before drug administration. To mitigate residual risk during this window and throughout treatment duration until booster doses take effect (typically eight weeks post-initial series), prophylactic antibiotics were mandated. In this instance, penicillin V (Apocillin) was continued based on her known sensitivity to *Capnocytophaga*, ensuring coverage against potential breakthrough infections while her humoral immunity was suppressed by complement blockade.

The long-term management plan hinges on balancing sustained renal protection with infection surveillance. While eculizumab induced a durable hematological remission and allowed for partial renal recovery in this patient, cessation carries a significant risk of relapse if underlying genetic predispositions exist or if infectious triggers recur without adequate immune containment. Since genetic testing did not reveal pathogenic variants in complement regulatory genes in this individual—a finding that distinguishes her from cases with familial atypical HUS—the duration of therapy remains flexible but cautious. Current protocols suggest continuing maintenance dosing until renal function stabilizes sufficiently to allow for safe tapering or discontinuation under strict observation. Ultimately, successful management relies on a multidisciplinary approach that integrates rapid diagnostic confirmation with a staged therapeutic intervention: securing source control through antibiotics first or concurrently with PEX stabilization before transitioning to definitive complement blockade supported by robust prophylactic strategies.
