Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Challenges

**Epidemiology and Transmission of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus***
*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a fastidious, Gram-negative, encapsulated rod that resides in the oropharyngeal flora of approximately 70% of dogs and cats. While human infection is relatively rare, transmission occurs readily through bites, scratches, or even close mucosal contact. The organism poses a particular threat to asplenic patients or those with compromised immune systems, yet it can cause fulminant sepsis in previously healthy adults. In this case, the index event was a dog bite to the upper lip four days prior to admission. Crucially, although the patient received a tetanus prophylaxis, she failed to initiate the prescribed antibiotic regimen (phenoxymethylpenicillin), leaving her defenseless against bacterial proliferation. This delay allowed the pathogen to invade the bloodstream, setting the stage for rapid systemic dissemination. Understanding this epidemiology is vital because *C. canimorsus* grows slowly in standard cultures and requires specific conditions; thus, clinical suspicion must precede laboratory confirmation to avoid fatal delays in treatment initiation.

**Clinical Timeline: From Bite to Septic Shock and Limb Ischemia**
The patient's clinical trajectory illustrates the aggressive nature of invasive *C. canimorsus* infection. Within four days of the bite, she progressed from local symptoms to high-grade fever exceeding 40°C and profound malaise. By the time she reached primary care, she exhibited signs of severe hemodynamic compromise, including refractory hypoglycemia (nadir 0.6 mmol/L) and mottling of the lower extremities indicative of poor perfusion. Upon transfer to the tertiary center, she presented with septic shock characterized by metabolic acidosis (lactate 5.7 mmol/L) and cold, mottled limbs without frank necrosis initially noted on physical exam but confirmed later by digital ischemia. The timeline underscores that what begins as a minor traumatic injury can evolve into life-threatening sepsis within 96 hours if untreated. The appearance of distal cyanosis and ischemia signaled early microvascular occlusion, foreshadowing the thrombotic complications that would define her subsequent course.

**Interpretation of Early Lab Values: Sepsis vs. HLH vs. Leukemia**
Upon admission, the laboratory profile revealed a complex picture dominated by pancytopenia (reduction in red cells, white cells, and platelets), acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated liver enzymes, and coagulopathy. This constellation initially raised concern for hematologic malignancies such as acute leukemia or hyperacute inflammatory states like hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). Consequently, hematology consultation was sought promptly to rule out these etiologies through flow cytometry and potential bone marrow biopsy planning. However, the presence of severe leukopenia combined with high inflammatory markers made HLH less likely than overwhelming bacteremia-driven consumption. Furthermore, flow cytometry returned negative for blast populations or abnormal phenotypes, effectively excluding leukemia at that moment. The key teaching point here is recognizing that severe sepsis itself can mimic hematologic crises through cytokine storm-mediated marrow suppression and peripheral destruction; relying solely on initial counts without correlating them with clinical context can lead to unnecessary invasive procedures if one fails to consider infectious causes first.

**Differentiating Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) from Early TMA**
A significant diagnostic challenge arose in distinguishing between classic disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to sepsis and evolving thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Initially, the patient's presentation fit perfectly with DIC: widespread microthrombi causing organ ischemia (limb discoloration), consumptive coagulopathy (low fibrinogen), and pancytopenia driven by systemic inflammation (*Candida*-like sepsis physiology). However, subtle distinctions existed: while DIC involves both procoagulant activation and anticoagulant depletion leading to bleeding risks alongside clotting, TMA is primarily an endothelial injury pattern characterized by mechanical fragmentation of erythrocytes (schistocytes) independent of fibrin deposition alone. In this case, early differentiation was difficult because both conditions present with thrombocytopenia and AKI simultaneously during severe sepsis. The pivotal clue pointing toward TMA rather than pure DIC emerged later when inflammatory markers began subsiding under antibiotic therapy yet hemolysis persisted independently—a phenomenon inconsistent with typical resolution patterns of sepsis-associated DIC where platelet counts usually recover once bacteremia is controlled.

**Initial Management Failures: Why Supportive Care Alone Was Insufficient**
Despite appropriate broad-spectrum antibiotic coverage with piperacillin/tazobactam aimed at covering dog-bite pathogens including *C. canimorsus*, and aggressive supportive measures including fluid resuscitation and blood product transfusions for coagulopathy, the patient deteriorated into complete anuria requiring continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). This lack of response highlighted a critical limitation: standard sepsis management targets bacterial clearance but does not address downstream complement-mediated endothelial damage characteristic of TMA-like syndromes triggered by infection alone until they manifest overtly as distinct entities like CM-HUS (complement-mediated HUS).
