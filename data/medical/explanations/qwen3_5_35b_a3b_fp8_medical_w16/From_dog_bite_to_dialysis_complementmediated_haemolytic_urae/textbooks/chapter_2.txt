Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Reasoning in Thrombotic Microangiopathy

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates the critical challenge of distinguishing secondary thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) from primary causes or other hematologic emergencies, particularly when sepsis obscures the clinical picture. Initially, the patient presented with signs of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and multiorgan failure driven by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia. During this acute septic phase, the development of hemolysis and thrombocytopenia was attributed to consumptive coagulopathy and organ ischemia. However, the pivotal moment for diagnosis occurred after the patient stabilized on antibiotics, yet exhibited a sudden, isolated deterioration characterized by a precipitous drop in hemoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia despite resolving coagulopathy. This "double-hit" pattern—initial sepsis followed by refractory hematologic collapse—is a hallmark clue that a superimposed TMA has emerged, necessitating a shift from treating infection alone to investigating endothelial injury.

Recognition of Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA) is the cornerstone of identifying TMA. In this patient, the transition from stable anemia to severe hemolysis was evidenced by specific laboratory trends: an undetectable haptoglobin level, a sharp rise in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and reticulocytosis that failed to compensate adequately. Crucially, the peripheral blood smear provided definitive morphological evidence with 15%–20% schistocytes (fragmented red blood cells). The presence of schistocytes confirms mechanical shearing of erythrocytes within damaged microvasculature, differentiating true TMA from other causes of anemia such as pure autoimmune destruction or hemorrhage. Without this morphological confirmation, the diagnosis might have been prematurely dismissed or misclassified solely as drug-induced hemolysis or worsening sepsis.

A fundamental step in evaluating adult TMA is the systematic exclusion of Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP), which carries an immediate mortality risk if untreated with plasma exchange alone. While clinical scoring systems like PLASMIC can assist in stratifying pre-test probability—our patient scored 4/7, suggesting low likelihood—the gold standard remains quantitative ADAMTS13 activity measurement. In classic TTP, ADAMTS13 activity is severely deficient (<10%) due to autoantibody inhibition or congenital defects. In this case, the assay returned 43.5%, effectively ruling out TTP and shifting the diagnostic focus toward other forms of TMA. This distinction is vital because while Plasma Exchange (PEX) is life-saving for TTP, it offers only transient benefit in Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS), where targeted complement inhibition is required for sustained remission.

Concurrently, Shiga toxin-producing *E.* coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) must be excluded through stool PCR analysis for Shiga toxins and serology for *E.* coli O157:H7 or non-O157 serogroups. The absence of prodromal bloody diarrhea and a negative stool PCR result made STEC-HUS highly unlikely in this patient, reinforcing the diagnosis of infection-triggered CM-HUS rather than typical post-infectious HUS seen in children. Additionally, clinicians must navigate potential confounders that mimic autoimmune processes; here, a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT/Coombs) initially raised suspicion for autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). However, penicillin exposure can induce drug-dependent antibodies causing a false-positive DAT without true immune-mediated destruction. The lack of response to corticosteroids and the persistence of schistocytes further invalidated AIHA as the primary driver, confirming that the positive DAT was an artifact rather than evidence of an independent autoimmune etiology.

When non-invasive testing yields inconclusive results regarding etiology or severity in adults with unexplained TMA and Acute Kidney Injury (AKI), renal biopsy serves as a definitive diagnostic tool. In this case, despite strong biochemical evidence pointing toward CM-HUS triggered by *C.* canimorsus, histological confirmation was pursued given the severity of renal failure requiring dialysis. The biopsy revealed characteristic features: fibrin thrombi within glomerular capillaries ("fibrinoid necrosis"), ischemic glomeruli with wrinkling of basement membranes, and cellular crescents indicative of severe acute tubular necrosis secondary to vascular occlusion. The absence of immune complex deposition on immunofluorescence helped distinguish this from immune-complex mediated diseases like lupus nephritis or post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. Ultimately, integrating these findings—the exclusion of TTP and STEC-HUS, the presence of MAHA despite resolving sepsis-coagulopathy dynamics led directly to a diagnosis of complement-mediated HUS triggered by bacterial infection rather than primary genetic dysregulation alone.
