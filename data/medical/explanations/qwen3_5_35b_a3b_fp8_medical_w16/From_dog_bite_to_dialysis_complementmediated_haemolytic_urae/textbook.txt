## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Challenges

**Epidemiology and Transmission of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus***
*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a fastidious, Gram-negative, encapsulated rod that resides in the oropharyngeal flora of approximately 70% of dogs and cats. While human infection is relatively rare, transmission occurs readily through bites, scratches, or even close mucosal contact. The organism poses a particular threat to asplenic patients or those with compromised immune systems, yet it can cause fulminant sepsis in previously healthy adults. In this case, the index event was a dog bite to the upper lip four days prior to admission. Crucially, although the patient received a tetanus prophylaxis, she failed to initiate the prescribed antibiotic regimen (phenoxymethylpenicillin), leaving her defenseless against bacterial proliferation. This delay allowed the pathogen to invade the bloodstream, setting the stage for rapid systemic dissemination. Understanding this epidemiology is vital because *C. canimorsus* grows slowly in standard cultures and requires specific conditions; thus, clinical suspicion must precede laboratory confirmation to avoid fatal delays in treatment initiation.

**Clinical Timeline: From Bite to Septic Shock and Limb Ischemia**
The patient's clinical trajectory illustrates the aggressive nature of invasive *C. canimorsus* infection. Within four days of the bite, she progressed from local symptoms to high-grade fever exceeding 40°C and profound malaise. By the time she reached primary care, she exhibited signs of severe hemodynamic compromise, including refractory hypoglycemia (nadir 0.6 mmol/L) and mottling of the lower extremities indicative of poor perfusion. Upon transfer to the tertiary center, she presented with septic shock characterized by metabolic acidosis (lactate 5.7 mmol/L) and cold, mottled limbs without frank necrosis initially noted on physical exam but confirmed later by digital ischemia. The timeline underscores that what begins as a minor traumatic injury can evolve into life-threatening sepsis within 96 hours if untreated. The appearance of distal cyanosis and ischemia signaled early microvascular occlusion, foreshadowing the thrombotic complications that would define her subsequent course.

**Interpretation of Early Lab Values: Sepsis vs. HLH vs. Leukemia**
Upon admission, the laboratory profile revealed a complex picture dominated by pancytopenia (reduction in red cells, white cells, and platelets), acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated liver enzymes, and coagulopathy. This constellation initially raised concern for hematologic malignancies such as acute leukemia or hyperacute inflammatory states like hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). Consequently, hematology consultation was sought promptly to rule out these etiologies through flow cytometry and potential bone marrow biopsy planning. However, the presence of severe leukopenia combined with high inflammatory markers made HLH less likely than overwhelming bacteremia-driven consumption. Furthermore, flow cytometry returned negative for blast populations or abnormal phenotypes, effectively excluding leukemia at that moment. The key teaching point here is recognizing that severe sepsis itself can mimic hematologic crises through cytokine storm-mediated marrow suppression and peripheral destruction; relying solely on initial counts without correlating them with clinical context can lead to unnecessary invasive procedures if one fails to consider infectious causes first.

**Differentiating Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) from Early TMA**
A significant diagnostic challenge arose in distinguishing between classic disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to sepsis and evolving thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Initially, the patient's presentation fit perfectly with DIC: widespread microthrombi causing organ ischemia (limb discoloration), consumptive coagulopathy (low fibrinogen), and pancytopenia driven by systemic inflammation (*Candida*-like sepsis physiology). However, subtle distinctions existed: while DIC involves both procoagulant activation and anticoagulant depletion leading to bleeding risks alongside clotting, TMA is primarily an endothelial injury pattern characterized by mechanical fragmentation of erythrocytes (schistocytes) independent of fibrin deposition alone. In this case, early differentiation was difficult because both conditions present with thrombocytopenia and AKI simultaneously during severe sepsis. The pivotal clue pointing toward TMA rather than pure DIC emerged later when inflammatory markers began subsiding under antibiotic therapy yet hemolysis persisted independently—a phenomenon inconsistent with typical resolution patterns of sepsis-associated DIC where platelet counts usually recover once bacteremia is controlled.

**Initial Management Failures: Why Supportive Care Alone Was Insufficient**
Despite appropriate broad-spectrum antibiotic coverage with piperacillin/tazobactam aimed at covering dog-bite pathogens including *C. canimorsus*, and aggressive supportive measures including fluid resuscitation and blood product transfusions for coagulopathy, the patient deteriorated into complete anuria requiring continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). This lack of response highlighted a critical limitation: standard sepsis management targets bacterial clearance but does not address downstream complement-mediated endothelial damage characteristic of TMA-like syndromes triggered by infection alone until they manifest overtly as distinct entities like CM-HUS (complement-mediated HUS). ## Diagnostic Reasoning in Thrombotic Microangiopathy

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates the critical challenge of distinguishing secondary thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) from primary causes or other hematologic emergencies, particularly when sepsis obscures the clinical picture. Initially, the patient presented with signs of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and multiorgan failure driven by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia. During this acute septic phase, the development of hemolysis and thrombocytopenia was attributed to consumptive coagulopathy and organ ischemia. However, the pivotal moment for diagnosis occurred after the patient stabilized on antibiotics, yet exhibited a sudden, isolated deterioration characterized by a precipitous drop in hemoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia despite resolving coagulopathy. This "double-hit" pattern—initial sepsis followed by refractory hematologic collapse—is a hallmark clue that a superimposed TMA has emerged, necessitating a shift from treating infection alone to investigating endothelial injury.

Recognition of Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA) is the cornerstone of identifying TMA. In this patient, the transition from stable anemia to severe hemolysis was evidenced by specific laboratory trends: an undetectable haptoglobin level, a sharp rise in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and reticulocytosis that failed to compensate adequately. Crucially, the peripheral blood smear provided definitive morphological evidence with 15%–20% schistocytes (fragmented red blood cells). The presence of schistocytes confirms mechanical shearing of erythrocytes within damaged microvasculature, differentiating true TMA from other causes of anemia such as pure autoimmune destruction or hemorrhage. Without this morphological confirmation, the diagnosis might have been prematurely dismissed or misclassified solely as drug-induced hemolysis or worsening sepsis.

A fundamental step in evaluating adult TMA is the systematic exclusion of Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP), which carries an immediate mortality risk if untreated with plasma exchange alone. While clinical scoring systems like PLASMIC can assist in stratifying pre-test probability—our patient scored 4/7, suggesting low likelihood—the gold standard remains quantitative ADAMTS13 activity measurement. In classic TTP, ADAMTS13 activity is severely deficient (<10%) due to autoantibody inhibition or congenital defects. In this case, the assay returned 43.5%, effectively ruling out TTP and shifting the diagnostic focus toward other forms of TMA. This distinction is vital because while Plasma Exchange (PEX) is life-saving for TTP, it offers only transient benefit in Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS), where targeted complement inhibition is required for sustained remission.

Concurrently, Shiga toxin-producing *E.* coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) must be excluded through stool PCR analysis for Shiga toxins and serology for *E.* coli O157:H7 or non-O157 serogroups. The absence of prodromal bloody diarrhea and a negative stool PCR result made STEC-HUS highly unlikely in this patient, reinforcing the diagnosis of infection-triggered CM-HUS rather than typical post-infectious HUS seen in children. Additionally, clinicians must navigate potential confounders that mimic autoimmune processes; here, a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT/Coombs) initially raised suspicion for autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). However, penicillin exposure can induce drug-dependent antibodies causing a false-positive DAT without true immune-mediated destruction. The lack of response to corticosteroids and the persistence of schistocytes further invalidated AIHA as the primary driver, confirming that the positive DAT was an artifact rather than evidence of an independent autoimmune etiology.

When non-invasive testing yields inconclusive results regarding etiology or severity in adults with unexplained TMA and Acute Kidney Injury (AKI), renal biopsy serves as a definitive diagnostic tool. In this case, despite strong biochemical evidence pointing toward CM-HUS triggered by *C.* canimorsus, histological confirmation was pursued given the severity of renal failure requiring dialysis. The biopsy revealed characteristic features: fibrin thrombi within glomerular capillaries ("fibrinoid necrosis"), ischemic glomeruli with wrinkling of basement membranes, and cellular crescents indicative of severe acute tubular necrosis secondary to vascular occlusion. The absence of immune complex deposition on immunofluorescence helped distinguish this from immune-complex mediated diseases like lupus nephritis or post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. Ultimately, integrating these findings—the exclusion of TTP and STEC-HUS, the presence of MAHA despite resolving sepsis-coagulopathy dynamics led directly to a diagnosis of complement-mediated HUS triggered by bacterial infection rather than primary genetic dysregulation alone.

## Pathophysiology of Complement-Mediated HUS and Infectious Triggers

Complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), historically referred to as atypical HUS, is fundamentally driven by the dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway. Under physiological conditions, this pathway serves as a critical line of defense against encapsulated bacteria through the generation of opsonins and membrane attack complexes. However, in CM-HUS, loss-of-function mutations or autoantibodies affecting regulatory proteins—such as Factor H, Factor I, Factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—prevent the timely termination of complement activation. This unchecked cascade leads to excessive consumption of terminal complement components and the deposition of inflammatory mediators on vascular endothelium. In the context of this case, *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* served as the inciting event for this pathological process. *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated gram-negative organism that expresses surface structures capable of directly activating the alternative pathway. While a robust immune response typically clears such pathogens, in a susceptible host, this interaction precipitates an overwhelming local inflammatory response that damages the glomerular endothelium, resulting in thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), hemolysis, and acute kidney injury.

The concept of the "second hit" is central to understanding why infection triggers manifest differently depending on individual susceptibility. While approximately 50% to 70% of patients with CM-HUS harbor identifiable germline mutations or acquired autoantibodies conferring genetic predisposition, clinical disease often remains latent until an environmental stressor occurs. Infection represents one of the most potent triggers among these stressors. For *C. canimorsus*, which exhibits variable serum susceptibility where some strains are fully susceptible while others display partial resistance due to their capsules, the bacterium creates a paradoxical scenario: it necessitates intense complement activation for clearance while simultaneously providing the substrate for uncontrolled endothelial injury in those with compromised regulation. This mechanism explains why a patient without overt chronic comorbidities could present with fulminant TMA solely following exposure to a specific pathogen. The infection acts as the "second hit" that overwhelms any marginal compensatory mechanisms inherent in the host's complement system.

Genetic testing plays a crucial role in stratifying these cases but does not always yield definitive answers regarding etiology. In hereditary forms of CM-HUS, identifying pathogenic variants guides long-term management and family counseling; however, as seen in this patient's case where no pathogenic variants were identified despite severe disease, a significant proportion of cases may be sporadic or triggered purely by infectious insults without detectable genetic defects. This distinction is vital because it suggests that not all CM-HUS requires lifelong genetic surveillance or implies an inherited disorder; rather, some represent extreme reactions to specific pathogens in individuals who may possess subtle or undiscovered regulatory weaknesses that are insufficient to cause disease spontaneously but fail under severe infectious pressure. Consequently, a negative genetic panel does not exclude a diagnosis of CM-HUS triggered by infection; instead, it reinforces the likelihood that the infection itself was sufficient to drive pathology in a potentially sub-clinically predisposed host or simply overwhelmed normal homeostatic controls acutely.

Diagnostic interpretation is further complicated by serological findings during acute phases. Standard screening tests measuring serum C3 and C4 levels often appear within normal limits in patients with active CM-HUS. This phenomenon occurs because C3 consumption is localized primarily within tissues and microvasculature rather than being reflected uniformly in systemic circulation during early stages, or because compensatory hepatic synthesis maintains baseline levels despite ongoing consumption at the site of injury. Furthermore, functional assays provide more insight than static concentration measurements; in this case, while C3/C4 were normal initially, subsequent functional testing revealed markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50), confirming dysregulation even when antigen levels were preserved. Therefore, reliance on normal C3/C4 values alone cannot rule out CM-HUS when clinical features strongly suggest TMA and other causes like Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) or Shiga toxin-producing *E.* coli HUS have been excluded.

Finally, distinguishing *C. canimorsus*-associated CM-HUS from other infectious triggers requires careful consideration of epidemiology and pathophysiology. Unlike Shiga toxin-producing *E.* coli (STEC)-HUS, which relies on bacterial toxins directly damaging endothelial cells independent of complement dysregulation (though complement amplification follows), *C. canimorsus* operates through direct pathway activation similar to meningococcal disease but distinct in its zoonotic vector and slower clinical evolution prior to crisis*. Other infections like parvovirus B19 or HIV can induce TMA via different mechanisms such as immune complex deposition or direct viral cytopathicity*. The uniqueness of this case lies in linking a rare canine zoonosis specifically to complement-driven TMA without STEC involvement or classic hereditary markers highlight that clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for infectious triggers across diverse microbial families when evaluating adult-onset TMA with renal failure and hemolysis.**

## Therapeutic Strategy: Balancing Infection Control and Complement Blockade

The management of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia presents a distinct therapeutic challenge: the need to halt life-threatening thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) while simultaneously avoiding the exacerbation of an active bacterial infection. Terminal complement inhibition, specifically via eculizumab, is the definitive treatment for CM-HUS as it blocks the cleavage of C5, preventing the formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) responsible for endothelial damage. However, this mechanism also compromises host defense against encapsulated organisms, including *Neisseria* species and *Capnocytophaga*, which rely heavily on complement-mediated lysis for clearance. Consequently, clinicians must carefully weigh the timing of eculizumab initiation against the status of bacteremia. In this case, the decision to initiate eculizumab was deferred until blood cultures confirmed negativity and antibiotic therapy had been established. This strategy aimed to secure hematological control without precipitating uncontrolled sepsis, illustrating that while immediate blockade is ideal for renal preservation, it must be balanced against the risk of overwhelming infection in patients with active bacteremia.

Plasma exchange (PEX) serves as a crucial bridge therapy in this scenario, particularly when there is uncertainty regarding the specific subtype of thrombotic microangiopathy or when waiting for confirmatory genetic and enzymatic testing. Unlike in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), where PEX is curative by replacing deficient ADAMTS13 enzyme, its role in CM-HUS is primarily supportive. PEX functions by removing circulating complement fragments, inflammatory cytokines, and potentially autoantibodies that drive the alternative pathway activation. In this patient's course, PEX provided transient stabilization and facilitated the return of spontaneous diuresis; however, it did not sustain long-term remission once discontinued. The eventual resolution of hemolysis and platelet consumption occurred only after the initiation of eculizumab, confirming that PEX alone cannot correct the underlying dysregulation of the complement cascade. Therefore, PEX is appropriately utilized here as a temporizing measure to stabilize critically ill patients while preparing them for targeted biologic therapy.

Prophylactic measures are indispensable when administering terminal complement inhibitors due to their profound impact on immunity against encapsulated bacteria. Before commencing eculizumab induction in this case, rigorous preparation was undertaken involving both vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis. The patient received quadrivalent meningococcal conjugate vaccines covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W (Menveo) as well as serogroup B coverage (Bexsero). Ideally, vaccination should occur at least two weeks prior to therapy to ensure adequate antibody titers; however, given the urgency of her clinical deterioration requiring dialysis-dependent AKI and severe hemolysis, vaccination was expedited immediately before drug administration. To mitigate residual risk during this window and throughout treatment duration until booster doses take effect (typically eight weeks post-initial series), prophylactic antibiotics were mandated. In this instance, penicillin V (Apocillin) was continued based on her known sensitivity to *Capnocytophaga*, ensuring coverage against potential breakthrough infections while her humoral immunity was suppressed by complement blockade.

The long-term management plan hinges on balancing sustained renal protection with infection surveillance. While eculizumab induced a durable hematological remission and allowed for partial renal recovery in this patient, cessation carries a significant risk of relapse if underlying genetic predispositions exist or if infectious triggers recur without adequate immune containment. Since genetic testing did not reveal pathogenic variants in complement regulatory genes in this individual—a finding that distinguishes her from cases with familial atypical HUS—the duration of therapy remains flexible but cautious. Current protocols suggest continuing maintenance dosing until renal function stabilizes sufficiently to allow for safe tapering or discontinuation under strict observation. Ultimately, successful management relies on a multidisciplinary approach that integrates rapid diagnostic confirmation with a staged therapeutic intervention: securing source control through antibiotics first or concurrently with PEX stabilization before transitioning to definitive complement blockade supported by robust prophylactic strategies.
