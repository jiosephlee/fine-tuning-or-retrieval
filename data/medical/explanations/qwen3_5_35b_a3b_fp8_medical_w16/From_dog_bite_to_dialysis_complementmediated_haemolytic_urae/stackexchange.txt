Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Diagnostic Reasoning] Which specific laboratory finding definitively distinguishes Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) from Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS) in this patient, and what is the threshold value for that marker?

The definitive laboratory finding that distinguishes Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) from Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS) in this patient is **ADAMTS13 activity**.

In TTP, the pathophysiology centers on a severe deficiency of the metalloprotease ADAMTS13, leading to the accumulation of ultra-large von Willebrand factor multimers and widespread platelet aggregation. Consequently, the diagnostic gold standard for TTP is an ADAMTS13 activity level **below 10%** (often <5%).

Conversely, in CM-HUS—and indeed in all forms of HUS and secondary TMAs—the ADAMTS13 activity is typically preserved. In this specific case, the patient's ADAMTS13 activity was measured at **43.5%**, which effectively rules out TTP and directs the diagnosis toward a complement-mediated mechanism. It is crucial to note that while clinical scores like PLASMIC can aid in triaging patients while waiting for these results, they cannot replace the definitive measurement of ADAMTS13 enzymatic activity. In this scenario, the preservation of ADAMTS13 activity confirmed that the thrombotic microangiopathy was driven by complement dysregulation triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia rather than acquired TTP.

### [Pathophysiology] How does the pathophysiological role of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* create a 'clinical paradox' regarding complement activation, and why might this organism trigger TMA in an otherwise immunocompetent adult?

**Clinical Pearl:** *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* creates a "clinical paradox" in the pathogenesis of Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) because the very mechanism intended to clear the infection—complement-mediated opsonization and lysis—is hijacked to cause host tissue destruction. As a Gram-negative encapsulated bacterium, *C. canimorsus* possesses surface structures that potently activate the alternative complement pathway. In a susceptible host, this leads to uncontrolled amplification of C3 convertase, resulting in massive consumption of complement regulators and generation of toxic membrane attack complexes (C5b-9).

While the classic teaching suggests that complement activation is protective, here it drives **endothelial injury**. The deposition of C5b-9 on vascular endothelium causes cell swelling, detachment, and exposure of subendothelial matrix, triggering platelet aggregation and microvascular thrombosis—the hallmark of TMA. This explains the paradox: the host's attempt to destroy the pathogen via complement inadvertently destroys its own vasculature.

Regarding your specific query on **immunocompetent adults**, this case illustrates that while genetic defects in complement regulation (e.g., Factor H mutations) are common precipitants of Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS), they are not strictly required. The "second hit" theory posits that intense inflammatory stimuli can overwhelm normal homeostatic controls in otherwise healthy individuals. In this patient, who lacked pathogenic genetic variants, the sheer magnitude of bacterial load and virulence factors from *C. canimorsus* acted as a sufficient trigger to tip the balance toward pathological complement activation.

Key teaching points from this case include:
1.  **Temporal Disconnect:** The TMA often manifests *after* initial sepsis control (as seen when hemolysis worsened despite improving inflammatory markers), which can delay diagnosis if clinicians rely solely on acute-phase reactants or assume resolution implies cure.
2.  **Diagnostic Pitfalls:** Normal serum C3/C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS; these proteins may be consumed locally at the site of endothelial injury without dropping significantly in circulation until late stages. Similarly, ADAMTS13 activity >10% rules out TTP but leaves open the diagnosis of infection-triggered CM-HUS.
3.  **Therapeutic Imperative:** Recognition is critical because Plasma Exchange alone offers only transient benefit in CM-HUS; definitive treatment requires terminal complement inhibition with agents like eculizumab to halt endothelial damage once active bacteremia is controlled.

### [Clinical Reasoning] Why was the initial diagnosis of autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) incorrect despite a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT), and what peripheral blood smear finding provided the critical pivot toward a diagnosis of Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA)?

**Clinical Pearl:** The initial diagnosis of autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) was incorrect because the positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) represented a **false-positive result**, likely induced by recent penicillin exposure or prior blood transfusions, rather than true alloimmune or autoantibody-mediated destruction. In this case, the DAT positivity misled the team into initiating corticosteroids, which failed to halt the hemolysis—a classic "therapeutic trial" clue that the mechanism was not antibody-driven.

The critical pivot to Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA) came from the peripheral blood smear revealing **schistocytes** (fragmented red blood cells) at a rate of 15% to 20%. While AIHA typically presents with spherocytes and a reticulocytosis without fragmentation, the presence of significant schistocytosis confirms mechanical shearing of erythrocytes within the microvasculature due to thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). In this specific case, the schistocytes signaled *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-triggered complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), distinguishing it from sepsis-associated Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) and confirming the need for complement blockade with eculizumab rather than immunosuppression alone.

### [Management] In the management of infection-triggered CM-HUS, what is the specific rationale for administering meningococcal vaccines and antibiotic prophylaxis prior to or concurrently with eculizumab initiation, and which bacterial genera are specifically implicated in this precaution?

When initiating eculizumab, a terminal complement inhibitor, for complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), the immediate priority is mitigating the loss of protective immunity against encapsulated organisms. The terminal complement cascade (specifically the membrane attack complex) is essential for lysing encapsulated bacteria; blocking this pathway leaves patients highly susceptible to overwhelming sepsis, particularly from *Neisseria* species and other encapsulated pathogens like *Capnocytophaga*.

In this case, the patient's CM-HUS was triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, an encapsulated gram-negative rod acquired from a dog bite. Because eculizumab inhibits C5 and prevents the formation of the C5b-9 complex, it compromises the host's ability to clear *C. canimorsus* and, critically, increases the risk of subsequent invasive meningococcal disease (*Neisseria meningitidis*). Therefore, standard guidelines mandate that patients receive vaccination against all serogroups of *N. meningitidis* (covering groups A, C, Y, W via Menveo, and group B via Bexsero) prior to drug initiation whenever feasible. As seen in this patient's management where vaccines were administered just prior to induction because there was insufficient time for a two-week pre-treatment window, antibiotic prophylaxis becomes mandatory until vaccine efficacy is established.

Consequently, we administer lifelong antibiotic prophylaxis—typically with penicillin or amoxicillin—to cover both *N. meningitidis* and *C. canimorsus* until at least two weeks after completing the vaccination series. This dual approach addresses the specific vulnerability introduced by complement blockade against the very bacterial genera implicated in triggering or complicating the syndrome: primarily *Neisseria* spp., but also *Capnocytophaga*, which shares similar virulence mechanisms involving capsular resistance to phagocytosis and reliance on complement-mediated lysis for clearance in susceptible hosts.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Despite normal serum C3 and C4 levels, why did this patient meet the criteria for CM-HUS rather than Secondary TMA due to Sepsis/DIC alone? What specific clinical timeline and histological features supported the diagnosis of CM-HUS over pure sepsis-induced coagulopathy?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

This patient meets the criteria for **complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS)** triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia rather than simple sepsis-induced disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) primarily due to the **temporal dissociation between the resolution of coagulopathy and the emergence of distinct thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)**.

While the patient initially presented with classic signs of sepsis-associated DIC—including pancytopenia, elevated D-dimer/fibrin degradation products, and consumptive coagulopathy—the diagnostic pivot point occurred during her clinical course. As her sepsis resolved and inflammatory markers normalized, her coagulation profile improved; however, she subsequently developed a **new, isolated episode of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA)** characterized by a sharp drop in hemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, and persistent thrombocytopenia that failed to improve with standard supportive care. This "split" presentation—where the consumptive coagulopathy resolves but the hemolysis and thrombocytopenia worsen—is highly characteristic of TMA evolving independently from DIC. In pure sepsis-induced DIC, hematologic parameters typically track together; a divergence suggests an alternative mechanism driving endothelial injury.

Furthermore, **histological confirmation** provided definitive evidence distinguishing this case from secondary TMA due to sepsis alone. The renal biopsy demonstrated severe acute tubular necrosis with **fibrin thrombi within glomerular capillaries and scattered crescent formation**, hallmarks of TMA. Crucially, immunofluorescence showed **no immune complex deposition**, ruling out immune-complex mediated glomerulonephritis or lupus nephritis. While DIC can cause ischemic changes, the presence of frank fibrin thrombi in conjunction with MAHA confirms a primary thrombotic microangiopathic process.

Finally, we must address why normal serum C3 and C4 levels did not exclude CM-HUS. In approximately 30% to 50% of CM-HUS cases (formerly known as atypical HUS), serum complement levels remain within normal ranges because they reflect steady-state production rather than dynamic consumption or local tissue activation. The diagnosis here relied on **clinical exclusion**:
1.  **ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%**, effectively ruling out Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP).
2.  **Stool PCR was negative** for Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli*, excluding typical HUS.
3.  The patient exhibited a dramatic response to **eculizumab** (terminal complement inhibition) after failing plasma exchange monotherapy, confirming the pathophysiology was driven by unregulated alternative pathway activation triggered by the bacterial infection.

In summary, this case illustrates that in adult patients with severe sepsis who develop persistent or delayed MAHA after initial stabilization—and where DIC has otherwise resolved—one must suspect infection-triggered CM-HUS regardless of normal baseline complement levels. Early recognition allows for targeted complement blockade, which is superior to plasma exchange alone in reversing the underlying pathology and preserving renal function.

### [Management] What are the distinct roles of Plasma Exchange (PEX) versus Eculizumab in the treatment algorithm for CM-HUS, and why did sustained hematologic remission fail to occur until complement blockade was established in this case?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

In the management of complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), **plasma exchange (PEX)** and **eculizumab** serve fundamentally different mechanistic roles, a distinction clearly illustrated in this case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-associated TMA.

**Plasma Exchange (PEX)** acts primarily as a temporizing measure. Its mechanism involves the mechanical removal of circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, activated complement fragments, and potentially autoantibodies, thereby providing "bridging" support. In this patient, PEX was initiated empirically to stabilize the clinical picture while diagnostic workup excluded Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) and Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* HUS. We observed that while PEX facilitated a return of spontaneous diuresis and offered transient stabilization, it failed to arrest the relentless hemolysis or sustain platelet recovery once the procedure ceased. This underscores that PEX does not correct the underlying pathophysiology of uncontrolled alternative pathway activation; it merely dilutes or removes soluble effectives temporarily.

Conversely, **Eculizumab**, a monoclonal antibody against Complement Component 5 (C5), provides definitive therapy by blocking terminal complement activation. By preventing the cleavage of C5 into C5a and C5b-9 (the Membrane Attack Complex), eculizumab halts the endothelial injury driving the thrombotic microangiopathy. In this case, sustained hematologic remission—defined by the resolution of schistocytosis, normalization of platelets, and cessation of hemolysis—occurred only after eculizumab induction was completed. The persistence of hemolysis despite aggressive PEX confirms that the driver was an intrinsic dysregulation of the complement cascade rather than a reversible accumulation of circulating toxins alone.

The reason sustained remission failed until complement blockade was established lies in the pathophysiology of infection-triggered CM-HUS. Here, *C. canimorsus* acted as a potent "second hit," triggering excessive complement consumption and endothelial damage in a susceptible host (even without identified germline mutations). As long as C5 conversion proceeded unchecked by PEX alone, new microthrombi formed faster than they could be cleared or diluted. Only with specific inhibition of C5 was the feedback loop broken, allowing for true hematologic recovery and partial renal salvage.

Finally, regarding safety in this case: while eculizumab increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms like *Neisseria*, this patient's bacteremia had already cleared prior to initiation. The successful integration of vaccination (*Menveo*, *Bexsero*) and antibiotic prophylaxis allowed us to utilize life-saving complement blockade without precipitating recurrent sepsis. This case reinforces that in adult TMA where TTP is excluded, reliance on PEX alone is insufficient; timely transition to terminal complement inhibition is critical for durable remission.
