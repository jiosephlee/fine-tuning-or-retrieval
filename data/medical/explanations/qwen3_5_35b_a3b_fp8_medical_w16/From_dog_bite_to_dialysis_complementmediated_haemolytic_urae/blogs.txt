# The Dog Bite That Didn't Stop: Unmasking Complement-Mediated HUS After Capnocytophaga Sepsis

We've all seen the "dog bite" vignette. It's a classic board question: a patient with a pet bite presents with fever, chills, and rapid deterioration. You think *Pasteurella*, you start empiric coverage, and you hope for the best. But what happens when the sepsis seems to resolve, the antibiotics are working, and then—bam—the patient suddenly develops frank hemolysis and renal failure?

That is exactly where we find ourselves in this fascinating case report from Fredriksen et al. A previously healthy woman in her late 40s presents with a dog bite on her lip four days prior. She skips her antibiotic prescription (the cardinal sin!), returns with septic shock, hypoglycemia, and DIC. Everything looks like classic *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia until the tide turns. As her sepsis clears and she stabilizes on antibiotics, she doesn't just get better; she gets worse in a very specific way: isolated hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, and anuria leading to dialysis dependence.

This isn't just sepsis-induced organ failure anymore. This is **complement-mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS)** triggered by an infection that should have been easily cleared by the same mechanism causing the damage. It forces us to confront a terrifying diagnostic reality: sometimes, the thing killing your patient isn't the bug itself, but their own immune system's response to it.

## The Diagnostic Trap: DIC vs. TMA

The initial presentation is a masterclass in "look-alikes." The patient arrived with pancytopenia, coagulopathy (low fibrinogen), AKI, and fever. Your differential list is long: Acute Leukemia? HLH? Classic HUS? Or simply overwhelming sepsis driving Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)?

In this case, the team correctly identified *C. canimorsus* early (thanks to blood cultures taking 7 days to grow—a reminder that molecular methods are our friends here), but they also faced a massive confounder: **normal C3/C4 levels**.

Here lies one of the most common pitfalls in diagnosing Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA). Many clinicians assume that if C3 and C4 are normal, it cannot be complement-mediated disease because "it's always low." **Wrong.** In CM-HUS (formerly called atypical HUS), baseline complement levels are often normal because the dysregulation is driven by genetic variants or autoantibodies that don't necessarily consume complement factors until a "second hit" occurs—or they may not consume them enough to fall outside reference ranges during acute phases. Relying solely on C3/C4 will miss up to half of these cases.

Furthermore, the timing of the hemolysis was deceptive. Initially, there was no schistocytosis; instead, there was leukopenia and thrombocytopenia typical of sepsis/DIC. The "smoking gun"—schistocytes appearing on smear—didn't emerge until day 8 or so, *after* the septic shock had resolved and inflammatory markers were dropping. If you stop looking for TMA once the blood pressure stabilizes and lactate drops, you miss this window entirely. The persistence of thrombocytopenia combined with new-onset hemolysis in a recovering patient is your alarm bell for TMA masquerading as post-septic recovery complications.

## Why Capnocytophaga? And Why Now?

*C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated Gram-negative rod found in canine oral flora. It has a notorious reputation for causing fulminant sepsis in asplenic patients or those with alcohol use disorder—but it strikes healthy people too if they skip prophylaxis (like our patient who didn't pick up her Amoxicillin).

What makes this case physiologically intriguing is the role of complement itself as both savior and executioner. Normally, complement helps clear encapsulated bacteria like *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*. However, in susceptible hosts (even those without known genetic mutations), an intense bacterial load can trigger uncontrolled alternative pathway activation via molecular mimicry or direct binding of bacterial surface proteins to complement regulators like Factor H or CD46 (Membrane Cofactor Protein).

In this specific case study, extended testing eventually showed markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50 ~6%), confirming complement consumption/dysfunction despite normal static C3/C4 levels earlier on. Interestingly, no pathogenic genetic variants were found upon deep sequencing of complement genes (Factor H/I/B). This suggests **infection-triggered CM-HUS** without a pre-existing heritable defect—a phenomenon where the sheer magnitude of the infection acted as the sole trigger for complement dysregulation in an otherwise genetically average individual. This reinforces that CM-HUS is often a clinicopathologic diagnosis of exclusion where infection acts as the necessary "second hit."

## Therapeutic Dilemma: To Block or Not To Block?

Once TMA is suspected after excluding Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) via ADAMTS13 activity (>10%) and Shiga toxin-negative status via stool PCR/plasticity testing (PLASMIC score low), we face a critical decision: Plasma Exchange (PLEX) vs. Eculizumab/Ravulizumab?

While PLEX has been historically used as a bridge for TTP-like syndromes including HUS data shows it offers only transient benefit in pure CM-HUS because it does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation—it merely washes away soluble factors temporarily. In this case diuresis returned briefly after PLEX but only sustained recovery happened after starting eculizumab blocking terminal complement C5 preventing further MAC formation on endothelial cells.)
The catch? **Eculizumab blocks terminal complement**, leaving patients vulnerable to encapsulated organisms like *N.* meningitidis—and now arguably *C.* canimorsus itself since we know they rely partly on complement clearance!
So how do we manage this paradoxical situation where treating one complication might exacerbate another?
The answer lies in strict protocolization:
1) Ensure blood cultures are negative before initiating blockade if possible (done here).
2) Administer meningococcal vaccines ideally 2 weeks prior; if emergent give MenACWY + MenB concurrently with aggressive antibiotic prophylaxis throughout treatment duration.)
Our patient received dual vaccination plus lifelong ciprofloxacin/amoxicillin prophylaxis while on therapy demonstrating safe integration despite active recent bacteremia.)
Delaying eculizumab increases risk of irreversible renal scarring; thus balancing infectious risk against organ loss favors early intervention once TTP/HUS distinction made.)
Key Take-Home Messages:
*   **Don't trust normal C3/C4:** Normal levels do not rule out CM-HUS; look at clinical trajectory and consider functional assays if suspicion remains high.)
*   **Timing matters:** New-onset MAHA/thrombocytopenia occurring during recovery from severe sepsis should trigger immediate TMA workup including peripheral smear review.)
*   **Exclude TTP first:** Always check ADAMTS13 activity before committing to eculizumab unless clinical picture strongly mandates empiric action while waiting.)
*   **Vaccinate & Protect:** If using anti-C5 agents post-infection ensure meningococcal coverage AND maintain prophylactic antibiotics against other encapsulated pathogens throughout therapy period.)
*   **Think beyond genetics:** Infection alone can trigger CM-HUS even without identifiable germline mutations especially when dealing with highly virulent organisms like Capnocytophaga.)



# Normal C3/C4 Does Not Rule Out Atypical HUS: Navigating the Diagnostic Pitfalls of Complement Testing

We've all been there. You're evaluating a patient with Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and Acute Kidney Injury (AKI). The differential is tight: Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP), Shiga-toxin HUS, or Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS, formerly "atypical" HUS). You send off the ADAMTS13 level and the Shiga toxin screen. Then, you glance at the basic complement panel: C3 and C4. Both are sitting comfortably within normal limits.

Your gut tells you "not CM-HUS." Your mind races toward TTP or drug-induced causes. But here's the hard truth from a recent case involving *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis: **Normal C3 and C4 levels do not rule out CM-HUS.**

Relying solely on static concentration values for C3 and C4 in the setting of acute thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) can lead to dangerous diagnostic delays. Let's walk through a fascinating case where a seemingly "normal" workup masked a life-threatening complement crisis, triggered by a dog bite.

## The Case: A Dog Bite That Became a Dialysis Nightmare

Our patient was a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who presented with rapidly progressive sepsis four days after a dog bite on her lip. Initially, she manifested classic signs of overwhelming *C. canimorsus* bacteremia: high fever (>40°C), hypoglycemia refractory to treatment, DIC, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury progressing to anuria requiring CRRT.

She stabilized somewhat with antibiotics and supportive care, but then came the curveball. As her inflammatory markers improved and coagulopathy resolved, she developed **isolated hemolysis**. Her hemoglobin plummeted from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days without bleeding. Platelets remained stubbornly low around 30–40 × 10⁹/L. Schistocytes were abundant on the peripheral smear (15–20%).

At this juncture, we faced a classic TMA puzzle. The initial suspicion leaned toward autoimmune hemolytic anemia because of a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT), likely a false positive induced by penicillin exposure or transfusion reactions (repeat testing was negative). With hemolysis persisting despite steroids and platelets failing to recover, we knew we needed to look deeper into the complement system.

## The Trap of "Normal" Complements

Here is where the diagnostic trap springs shut for many clinicians. Standard teaching often emphasizes that in complement-mediated diseases, you expect low C3 due to consumption via the alternative pathway. So, when this patient's labs came back showing **normal C3 and normal C4**, many would have safely dismissed CM-HUS from their differential list immediately. They might have chased malignancies or other autoimmune etiologies instead of treating for TMA caused by complement dysregulation.

However, this patient had CM-HUS triggered by infection. Why were her baseline levels normal?

First, remember that in acute settings, especially early in the disease course or during fluctuating phases of consumption/regeneration, serum concentrations may not yet reflect tissue-level consumption or functional depletion. Second—and crucially—**complement levels are dynamic**. In some cases of infection-triggered CM-HUS without underlying genetic defects (like Factor H mutations), the complement cascade is activated so acutely that homeostatic mechanisms maintain serum concentrations near baseline until catastrophic failure occurs later, or simply because the assay measures mass rather than function.

This patient's story highlights a critical distinction between **static levels** (concentration) and **functional capacity**. While her C3/C4 were normal locally available tests showed normal classical pathway activity too—but once we moved to functional assays measuring total alternative pathway activity (**AH50**), we saw the truth: her AH50 was markedly reduced at just **6%**. This indicated profound alternative pathway consumption that standard antigen-based tests missed entirely.

## Beyond Basic Screens: When to Order Advanced Testing

So, when should you stop trusting your reflexive "normal" result? If you see MAHA + Thrombocytopenia + AKI + Negative ADAMTS13 (>10%) + Negative Stool Toxin... you must suspect CM-HUS regardless of C3/C4 status unless proven otherwise by functional data or genetics later on.

The diagnostic algorithm needs an upgrade in high-stakes scenarios:
*   **Don't let normal C3/C4 close your differential:** These are necessary but insufficient exclusions for CM-HUS.
*   **Order Functional Assays Early:** If available, measure CH50/AH50 or specific factor activities rather than relying solely on immunoturbidimetry for antigen levels. A low AH50 with normal C3 suggests active alternative pathway consumption masking itself as "normal" mass levels due to compensatory synthesis or assay timing issues discussed above (though in this specific case report note regarding timing relative to PEX/Eculizumab is nuanced).
*   **ADAMTS13 is King for TTP Ruling Out:** Remember that <10% defines TTP; anything >25-30% essentially rules it out (PLASMIC score helps triage). This leaves TMA as either STEC-HUS or CM-HUS/Other TMAs if toxins are negative.
*   **Biopsy Matters:** Renal biopsy showing fibrin thrombi in glomeruli without immune complex deposition strongly supports TMA over other glomerulonephritides like ANCA vasculitis or Lupus Nephritis (which usually present with different serologies).

In our patient's case, despite normal local screening tests, the combination of persistent schistocytosis, lack of response to steroids/transfusion alone, and biopsy-proven TMA forced us down the path of plasma exchange and eventual eculizumab initiation after vaccination coverage was secured against encapsulated organisms (*Neisseria*, *Capnocytophaga*). The result? Hematologic remission and partial renal recovery only after blocking terminal complement activation with eculizumab (C5 inhibitor). Plasma exchange provided transient stabilization but could not sustain remission; only targeted blockade worked long-term once infection was controlled via blood culture clearance prior to drug administration.

## Take-Home Points for Your Next Shift

Diagnosing TMA is rarely straightforward; it requires peeling back layers of physiology that aren't always visible on standard chemistry panels. Here are your key takeaways from this case:

*   **Normal C3/C4 does NOT exclude Complement-Mediated HUS.** Static concentration measurements can be misleading during acute phases; rely on functional assays (AH50) if available when clinical suspicion is high despite "normal" screens.
*   **Watch for Secondary Triggers:** Infection (*C*. *canimorsus*, meningococcus), pregnancy, drugs, or surgery can precipitate CM-HUS even in patients without known genetic defects ("idiopathic"). Do not assume genetic absence just because family history is empty; up to half of cases have no identifiable mutation initially detected or require specialized deep sequencing later.
*   **Time is Kidney:** Early initiation of eculizumab/crislanib is critical for renal recovery once TTP and STEC-HUS are reasonably excluded; delaying therapy due to confusing lab values increases # ADAMTS13 is Your Gatekeeper: Distinguishing TTP from Infection-Triggered CM-HUS in Adults

We've all seen the "sepsis mimic" slide in the ED conference room. But few things are as terrifyingly deceptive as a patient who looks like they're dying of septic shock, only to reveal a hidden layer of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) once the antibiotics kick in.

Consider this recent case: a previously healthy woman in her late 40s presents four days after a dog bite with fever, hypotension, and rapid multiorgan failure. Initially, the picture screams *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis complicated by Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC). She gets aggressive resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and eventually lands on dialysis for acute kidney injury (AKI). She's doing slightly better until Day 8, when she suddenly develops profound hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia.

Here is where the clinical detective work becomes critical. Is this just lingering sepsis? Is it Evans syndrome? Or has she developed **Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS)** triggered by the infection?

The answer lies entirely in your gatekeeper test: **ADAMTS13**.

## The Diagnostic Pivot Point: Why ADAMTS13 Matters More Than You Think

When an adult presents with Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA)—schistocytes on smear, falling hemoglobin, rising LDH—and thrombocytopenia, the differential is narrow but deadly. It essentially boils down to Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) versus everything else (including CM-HUS and DIC).

In this specific case, the team initially considered TTP given the severe anemia and low platelets. However, applying the **PLASMIC score** provided crucial triage data before the lab results even came back. With a score of 4/7 (Low pre-test probability), TTP was less likely than high-risk categories suggest. But scores are just heuristics; we need definitive data.

The gold standard remains ADAMTS13 activity levels:
*   **<10%**: Diagnostic of TTP. This demands immediate Plasma Exchange (PLEX) regardless of other findings.
*   **>10%**: Rules out classic TTP and forces us to look elsewhere for causes of TMA, such as CM-HUS or drug-induced etiologies.

Our patient's ADAMTS13 activity came back at **43.5%**. This single number effectively removed TTP from the differential equation and shifted our entire therapeutic axis toward a diagnosis of infection-triggered CM-HUS. Without this result, we might have been stuck treating for TTP indefinitely or delaying life-saving complement blockade in favor of unnecessary PLEX alone.

## The Trap of Normal Complement Levels and False Positives

One of the most common pitfalls in diagnosing CM-HUS is relying solely on C3 and C4 levels. In this case, initial complement studies were normal. Does that rule out CM-HUS? Absolutely not. As we learned here, C3/C4 can remain within reference ranges during active disease because they are consumed faster than they are depleted in serum assays, or simply because the assay isn't sensitive enough to catch functional deficits early on.

Furthermore, consider the diagnostic noise introduced by acute infection itself:
*   **The Coombs Test:** Our patient had a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) for IgG early on. Was this autoimmune hemolytic anemia? No—it turned out to be a false positive likely driven by penicillin exposure or prior transfusions (which she received for her DIC). Relying on that DAT could have led down a rabbit hole of steroid trials that wouldn't address the underlying microvascular occlusion.
*   **Sepsis vs. TMA:** Early in her course, she had pancytopenia and coagulopathy typical of DIC. It wasn't until her inflammatory markers improved that the "isolated" hemolysis emerged—a classic "unmasking" phenomenon where resolving sepsis reveals an underlying complement-driven pathology that was previously obscured by overwhelming consumption coagulopathy.

## Therapeutic Strategy: Bridging vs. Blocking

So, you've ruled out TTP with an ADAMTS13 >10%. What now? Do you keep running PLEX loops hoping to clear toxins? Or do you jump straight to eculizumab?

This is where understanding the pathophysiology dictates management:
*   **Plasma Exchange (PLEX):** In TTP, PLEX replaces deficient enzyme and removes antibodies; it is curative if done quickly. In CM-HUS, PLEX offers only transient removal of inflammatory mediators but does not correct the fundamental dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway unless there are inhibitory antibodies present (like anti-factor H). In our case, diuresis returned after PEX sessions but renal function did not fully recover until eculizumab was initiated.
*   **Eculizumab/Ravulizumab:** For confirmed or strongly suspected CM-HUS (especially with AKI), blocking terminal complement C5 is superior to PLEX alone for sustained hematologic remission and renal salvage. Time-to-treatment is arguably more predictive of long-term renal recovery than anything else we do here in the ER or ICU setting for these patients who don't have classic STERCO-E.coli HUS features either!

However, we cannot ignore the elephant in the room: **Active Sepsis.** Administering terminal complement inhibitors leaves patients vulnerable against encapsulated organisms like *Neisseria*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, and yes... ***Capnocytophaga*** itself (*Ccanimorsus*). This case highlights a delicate balancing act:
1.  Ensure blood cultures are negative before starting eculizumab if possible (though sometimes you must weigh risk vs benefit if renal failure is imminent).
2.  Vaccinate aggressively against Meningococcus A,C,Y,W,B before induction (and give booster schedules).
3.  Maintain prophylactic antibiotics throughout treatment duration if feasible; this patient stayed on Amoxicillin/Apocillin coverage specifically because she had been infected with *C.canimorsus*.

## Take-Home Points for Emergency Physicians & Intensivists

Don't let "sepsis" become a diagnosis of exclusion when faced with MAHA and thrombocytopenia in an adult with recent trauma or animal exposure. Here is your checklist for navigating this minefield:

*   **ADAMTS13 is Non-Negotiable:** If you see schistocytes + low platelets + AKI/Hemolysis, order ADAMTS13 immediately while sending samples for stool PCR (for STEC) and complement studies (<- wait for functional assays if available). A value >10% rules out TTP; <10% mandates immediate PEX + steroids while awaiting confirmatory testing if clinically unstable.

# The Eculizumab Paradox: Balancing Complement Blockade Against Active Encapsulated Bacteremia

We've all seen the "classic" TMA algorithms. You see the schistocytes, the falling platelets, and the rising creatinine. Your brain immediately starts ticking through the differentials: TTP? Check ADAMTS13. STEC-HUS? Stool PCR. If those are negative, you're staring down the barrel of complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS). The solution seems straightforward: start eculizumab or ravulizumab immediately to block terminal complement activation and save the kidneys.

But what happens when your patient isn't just "positive for TMA," but they are actively septic with a known encapsulated organism in their blood?

That is exactly where we landed with a 40-something woman who came to us four days after a simple dog bite on her lip. What started as a routine prophylaxis oversight spiraled into *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis, DIC, and eventually, a classic case of infection-triggered CM-HUS. This case forces us to confront one of the most uncomfortable dilemmas in modern critical care: **How do you treat life-threatening thrombotic microangiopathy with an agent that cripples your host's defense against the very bug causing it?**

### The Clinical Conundrum

Our patient presented with a textbook storm: high fever, hypoglycemia, pancytopenia, and rapidly progressive renal failure requiring dialysis within days of admission. Initially, everything looked like overwhelming sepsis-driven DIC. But then came the twist. As her inflammatory markers improved and she stabilized on antibiotics, she suddenly developed isolated hemolysis with undetectable haptoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia. A peripheral smear screamed MAHA (schistocytes), but her ADAMTS13 was normal at 43%, ruling out TTP. Stool PCR was negative for Shiga toxin. We were left with a diagnosis of CM-HUS triggered by *C. canimorsus*.

Here lies the paradox. Eculizumab works beautifully here; it stopped the hemolysis and allowed her kidneys to recover enough to regain diuresis. However, *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated gram-negative rod that relies heavily on complement-mediated opsonization for clearance—much like *Neisseria meningitidis*. By blocking C5 with eculizumab, we essentially turn off the immune system's ability to kill these specific bacteria. In theory, this could unleash a fulminant recurrence of bacteremia or new metastatic infections (like meningitis) right under our noses while trying to fix the clotting cascade.

### Navigating the Therapeutic Window

So, do we hold back on eculizumab and let her die of renal failure? Or do we push ahead and hope she doesn't get meningitis? In this case report, the team opted for a calculated risk mitigation strategy that should be part of your mental playbook whenever facing this scenario:

1.  **Wait for Culture Negativity:** We did not rush to give eculizumab upon suspicion alone. We waited until blood cultures were definitively negative and clinical signs of active bacteremia had resolved (she was stable on antibiotics). This minimized the immediate risk of seeding uncontrolled infection during peak bacterial load.
2.  **Vaccinate First:** Before administering any dose of eculizumab, we ensured she received quadrivalent meningococcal conjugate vaccine (Menveo) and serogroup B vaccine (Bexsero). While immunity takes time to develop (~2 weeks), delaying treatment significantly increases morbidity/mortality from HUS; thus, vaccination is done concurrently with preparation for blockade if possible, but ideally well before initiation if clinical stability allows.
3.  **Prophylactic Antibiotics:** Because no vaccine offers 100% protection against all serogroups or other encapsulated organisms (including *C. canimorsus*), we placed her on lifelong prophylactic penicillin (Amoxicillin/Apocillin) once discharged from acute care coverage shifts to oral prophylaxis during complement inhibition therapy.
4.  **Bridge with Plasma Exchange:** While waiting for genetic testing or definitive diagnosis confirmation (and before eculizumab took full effect), we utilized plasma exchange (PEX). It's worth noting that PEX provided only transient stabilization in this case—it cleared some inflammatory mediators but didn't stop the hemolysis permanently until eculizumab was introduced once cultures turned sterile.

### Why This Matters Beyond One Case Report

While *C. canimorsus* is rare compared to other causes of sepsis-associated TMA, this case reinforces a critical teaching point: **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM-HUS.** Our patient had normal baseline complement levels because this was an acute infection-triggered event without underlying genetic predisposition (no pathogenic variants found). Relying solely on static C3/C4 values can lead to missed diagnoses in septic patients developing superimposed TMA later in their course—a phenomenon sometimes called "late-onset" or "secondary" CM-HUS masked by sepsis physiology initially resolving only to re-emerge as pure hemolysis later on day 8-9 of admission as seen here.

Furthermore, this case illustrates that "sepsis-induced TMA" is not always purely driven by cytokine storms or direct endothelial toxicity; sometimes it represents unmasking of subclinical complement dysregulation triggered by intense bacterial stimulation (*Cancan*). The timeline matters: when hemolysis appears *after* sepsis has seemingly controlled itself, think hard about shifting from "supportive care" to "complement blockade."

### Take-Home Points for Your Practice

*   **Don't miss the late switch:** In patients with severe sepsis/DIC who improve initially but then develop isolated MAHA/thrombocytopenia days later, consider infection-triggered CM-HUS even if initial C3/C4 are normal and ADAMTS13 is >10%.
*   **The Eculizumab Safety Protocol:** Never initiate terminal complement blockade in active bacteremia unless absolutely necessary as a last resort without backup plans if you must proceed earlier than ideal protocols allow:
    *   Confirm culture sterility first if clinically feasible without delaying life-saving intervention too long (though in TMA/HUS delays worsen renal outcomes).
    *   Administer MenACWY and MenB vaccines immediately prior to induction if time permits; otherwise vaccinate ASAP post-induction while starting prophylaxis simultaneously if vaccines aren't fully protective yet (ideally wait 2 weeks pre-vax if stable).
    *   Start lifelong antimicrobial prophylaxis covering encapsulated organisms (*N.* spp., *S.* pneumo*, C.* spp.) upon discharge/transition from IV antibiotics to oral agents during therapy duration + at least several months post-therapy depending on protocol/guidelines used locally).
*   **Plasma Exchange is Temporary:** Remember that PEX removes complement fragments temporarily but does not correct the underlying dysregulation; sustained remission usually requires eculizumab/ravulizumab once infection control is established.
