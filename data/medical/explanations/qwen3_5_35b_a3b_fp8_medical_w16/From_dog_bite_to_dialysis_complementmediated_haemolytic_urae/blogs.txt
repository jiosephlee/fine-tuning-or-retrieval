Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Beyond Sepsis: Unmasking Complement-Mediated HUS After Dog Bites

We've all seen the "dog bite" vignette in medical school. It's usually a quick lecture on *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, splenic vulnerability, and the need for aggressive antibiotics to prevent fulminant sepsis. But what happens when the sepsis resolves, only to be replaced by a different beast entirely?

Consider this case of a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who presented just four days after a dog bite on her lip. She didn't start with classic HUS. She started with **septic shock**, profound hypoglycemia (nadir 0.6 mmol/L), and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). By the time she hit the ICU, she was anuric, dependent on CRRT, and drowning in pancytopenia. The working diagnosis was straightforward: *C. canimorsus* bacteremia causing overwhelming sepsis and multiorgan failure.

Here is where the story gets tricky—and where we often miss the diagnosis until it's too late. As her inflammatory markers settled and her white count recovered on appropriate antibiotics, she suddenly crashed again. Not with fever or hypotension this time, but with **isolated hemolysis**. Her hemoglobin plummeted from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days while platelets remained stubbornly low. The initial DAT was positive (likely a false positive from penicillin exposure), leading us down the rabbit hole of autoimmune hemolytic anemia until steroids failed to move the needle. Only then did we see the schistocytes: 15-20% on the smear.

She wasn't just recovering from sepsis; she had developed **complement-mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS)** triggered by the infection. This case serves as a stark reminder that in TMA workups, "time zero" isn't always when symptoms start—it might be when they re-emerge during convalescence.

## The Diagnostic Trap: Sepsis vs. TMA

The initial presentation of *C. canimorsus* is notorious for mimicking almost anything that kills quickly: meningitis, necrotizing fasciitis, DIC, or acute leukemia (due to pancytopenia). In this patient, the early picture was dominated by DIC and organ failure. It is incredibly easy to attribute subsequent anemia and thrombocytopenia to consumptive coagulopathy or bone marrow suppression from sepsis alone.

However, there are subtle clues that suggest something more specific than simple DIC:
*   **The Timeline:** True septic coagulopathy should improve as the infection is controlled with antibiotics and source control begins here (she had no abscesses). Instead, her hematologic status deteriorated *after* 8 days of stabilization on the nephrology ward while her inflammatory markers were trending down.
*   **The Specificity of Hemolysis:** While DIC causes consumption, it doesn't typically cause massive schistocytosis unless there is underlying microvascular thrombosis driving mechanical destruction of RBCs.
*   **Renal Trajectory:** While AKI was present early due to ischemia/shock, the persistence of anuria and worsening azotemia despite fluid optimization pointed toward ongoing glomerular pathology rather than just ATN from shock alone.

When you see a patient "recovering" from sepsis only to develop new-onset MAHA (Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia) and thrombocytopenia, stop assuming it's residual sepsis until you prove otherwise with a blood smear and ADAMTS13 assay.

## Why Complements Were Normal (and Why That Doesn't Matter)

One of my favorite teaching points from this case involves complement levels. In classic textbooks, we are taught that CM-HUS often presents with low C3 due to alternative pathway consumption. Here? C3 and C4 were normal throughout the entire course of her hospitalization until functional testing was ordered much later in specialized centers showing reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50).

**Key Pearl:** Normal C3/C4 does **not** exclude CM-HUS (formerly known as atypical HUS). Up to half of patients may have normal complement levels because they lack systemic consumption or have compensated production rates despite local endothelial damage driven by complement dysregulation triggered by infection or genetic predisposition ("two-hit" theory). Do not let normal C3/C4 distract you from ordering ADAMTS13 and considering plasma exchange/complement inhibition if clinical suspicion is high enough based on smears and response to therapy.

In this case, ADAMTS13 was 43%—firmly ruling out TTP—and Shiga toxin PCR was negative, leaving CM-HUS as the diagnosis of exclusion once STEC-TTP were cleared from your differential list. The renal biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy with fibrin thrombi but no immune complexes, further solidifying the diagnosis away from lupus nephritis or ANCA vasculitis which were already screened negative earlier in their course via ANA/ANCA panels.

## Therapeutic Dilemma: Blocking Complement During Infection?

This is where things get spicy for clinicians managing CM-HUS in the setting of active infection versus post-infectious states. *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated organism; like *Neisseria*, it relies heavily on terminal complement activity for clearance via MAC formation blocking opsonization resistance mechanisms are limited without proper phagocytic engagement aided by complement opsonins.** Wait—let me correct that logic**: Encapsulated bacteria like *Capnocytophaga* rely on complement for opsonization; inhibiting terminal complement leaves them vulnerable to unchecked replication if not covered by robust antibiotics and vaccination strategies.** Let me refine that thought process:** Actually, encapsulated organisms require complement-mediated opsonization for effective phagocytosis; therefore blocking terminal complement (C5) theoretically impairs bacterial clearance.** This raises a valid concern: Is giving eculizumab safe here?** The answer lies in timing.** In this patient's case eculizumab was withheld until blood cultures were definitively negative.** They also ensured coverage with penicillin prophylaxis since they couldn't wait two weeks for vaccine immunity.** This underscores a critical principle: **Do not initiate terminal complement blockade while active bacteremia persists unless absolutely necessary for life-saving reasons.** If you must block complement during infection ensure dual protection with potent antibiotics AND meningococcal/Hib vaccinations ideally administered two weeks prior but certainly before induction if possible.** In resource-limited settings where vaccines aren't immediately available prioritize antibiotic prophylaxis strictly around dosing intervals.** Also remember that plasma exchange alone removes circulating toxins but doesn't fix the underlying genetic dysregulation so it acts as a bridge not a cure.** Sustained remission required eculizumab once bacterial load was cleared.** Renal recovery correlates strongly with how soon you institute blockade after confirming diagnosis so don't wait for genetic results if clinical picture fits perfectly especially if patient is dialysis dependent.** 

## Take-Home Points for Your Next Dog Bite Patient

*   **Think TMA in Convalescence:** If a patient improves from septic shock/DIC but develops new/worsening hemolysis or thrombocytopenia later in their course suspect an infectious trigger for CM-HUS even if initial workup looks like pure sepsis complications.*
*   **Normal C3/C4 ≠ No Disease:** Do not rule out CM-HUS solely based on normal classical/alternative pathway protein levels; rely instead on functional assays (if available), clinical context (schistocytes + AKI + thrombocytopenia), and exclusion of other causes.*
*   **ADAMTS13 First Rule Out TTP:** Always check ADAMTS13 activity early; <10% mandates immediate PEX regardless of other findings whereas >50% allows focus shift toward TMA etiologies including CM-HUS.*
*   **Vaccinate Before Blockade:** When treating potential encapsulated organism-triggered CM-HUS ensure meningococcal vaccination occurs before starting eculizumab plus strict antibiotic prophylaxis thereafter.*
*   **Biopsy Can Help When Labs Are Ambiguous:** If uncertainty remains regarding etiology between pure septic ATN vs TMA consider renal biopsy looking specifically for fibrin thrombi/crescents indicative of microvascular injury.*
*   **Time Is Kidney Function:** Early initiation of eculizumab upon confirmation improves likelihood of renal recovery delay increases risk progression towards ESRD significantly.*



# Normal C3/C4 Does Not Rule Out Atypical HUS: A Diagnostic Pitfall

We've all been there: the classic teaching is drilled into us from day one of residency. If a patient presents with Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and Acute Kidney Injury (AKI), you think "HUS." You order your panel, check the C3 and C4, and if they are normal, you quickly move down the list to Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) or Shiga Toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC) HUS. It's a comforting algorithm until it isn't.

Consider this recent case of a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who presented with fulminant sepsis four days after a dog bite. The initial picture was terrifying but seemingly straightforward: *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia leading to Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC), multiorgan failure, and dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury. She improved with antibiotics and supportive care, only to develop a sudden, isolated crash in hemoglobin weeks later. Schistocytes flooded the peripheral smear, platelets stayed stubbornly low, and haptoglobin vanished.

Here lies the trap: Her C3 and C4 were **normal**.

If you stopped there, you might have missed the boat entirely on Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS). Instead, we dove deeper into functional assays and genetic testing, uncovering a diagnosis that turned out to be infection-triggered CM-HUS masked by a normal initial screen. This case serves as a stark reminder that in the world of Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA), "normal" basic complement levels are not exculpatory evidence. They are merely noise in an acoustic storm where you need to listen for specific frequencies.

## The Sepsis Mask: Why TMA Gets Overlooked

The patient's journey began with *C. canimorsus*, a pathogen notorious for causing rapid deterioration in immunocompetent adults via septic shock and DIC. Initially, her clinical picture was dominated by sepsis physiology: hypotension, lactic acidosis, pancytopenia, and coagulopathy. It wasn't until she stabilized that the true nature of her pathology revealed itself—a superimposed TMA characterized by severe hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia despite resolving DIC markers.

This temporal disconnect is crucial. In many cases of infection-triggered CM-HUS, the hemolytic crisis does not present at the onset of sepsis but rather emerges once the bacterial load is controlled or as part of a delayed immune response. In this patient, the hemolysis appeared days after admission when inflammatory markers were actually trending downward. This delay can easily lead clinicians to attribute persistent anemia to residual sepsis or transfusion reactions rather than investigating for TMA until it becomes life-threateningly severe.

The initial workup correctly ruled out TTP with an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5% (far above the <10% threshold) and excluded STEC-HUS with negative stool PCR. But then came the "normal" C3/C4 result. Many providers would feel confident declaring "not atypical HUS" here because classic textbooks suggest that low complement levels are hallmarks of alternative pathway dysregulation. However, relying solely on antigenic levels (C3/C4 concentration) ignores functional status in acute inflammatory states where consumption can be transient or compensated by homeostatic mechanisms until the tipping point is reached later in the disease course.

## Beyond Antigen Levels: The Need for Functional Assays

The pivotal moment in this case was moving past static concentrations to dynamic function tests. While C3 and C4 were within reference ranges, further investigation revealed a profoundly depressed Alternative Pathway activity (AH50) at just 6%. This discrepancy between normal antigen levels and absent functional capacity is the hallmark diagnostic pitfall in CM-HUS during acute phases or early recovery periods noted here as well as post-septic resolution phases discussed in literature regarding *C. canimorsus*.

Why does this happen?
*   **Acute Phase Reactivity:** C-reactive protein rises acutely during inflammation; similarly, complement components like Factor B can fluctuate independently of total consumption patterns initially captured by simple spot checks on Day 1 vs Day 10 labs seen in this scenario where labs shifted dramatically post-sepsis control).
*   **Compensatory Synthesis:** The liver may upregulate synthesis of complement proteins faster than they are consumed locally at sites of endothelial injury (like glomerular capillaries), maintaining serum levels while local depletion drives tissue damage.
*   **Timing Matters:** As seen in our patient's timeline extending from ICU admission through nephrology ward stabilization before eventual biopsy confirmation showing fibrin thrombi consistent with TMA—the window for detecting subtle dysregulation opens only when you look harder than surface-level serology allows).

When clinical suspicion for CM-HUS remains high despite normal C3/C4—and especially when ADAMTS13 is >10%—you must order functional assays like CH50/AH50 or anti-factor H antibodies if available immediately upon recognizing MAHA + AKI + thrombocytopenia triad without clear alternative cause). Genetic testing should also be pursued concurrently; although our patient lacked identifiable germline mutations initially suggesting sporadic infection-triggered event rather than inherited predisposition requiring lifelong blockade alone based on severity trajectory observed clinically). Note that absence of mutation does not exclude diagnosis; phenotype trumps genotype in acute decision-making contexts unless long-term management planning dictates otherwise based on family history recurrence risks etc.).

# The Eculizumab Dilemma: Balancing Terminal Complement Blockade Against Encapsulated Bacteria

We've all seen the classic "sepsis + organ failure" algorithm. You get a dog bite, the patient spikes a fever, hits septic shock, and develops DIC. It's a textbook trajectory until it isn't. This case report describes a woman in her late 40s who survived a fulminant *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection, only to develop a terrifying new complication once the sepsis seemed to resolve: complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS).

Here is where it gets tricky. We have a patient with life-threatening thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) who needs immediate complement blockade with eculizumab to save her kidneys and stop the hemolysis. But *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated gram-negative organism, just like *Neisseria meningitidis*. And we know that blocking the terminal complement cascade (C5) leaves patients vulnerable to overwhelming post-splenectomy-type sepsis from exactly these kinds of bugs.

So, do you pull the trigger on eculizumab while the blood cultures might still be technically positive? Do you wait for absolute sterility and risk permanent renal loss? Or do you treat empirically and hope for the best? This case forces us to confront one of the most high-stakes dilemmas in modern nephrology and critical care medicine.

### The Diagnostic Trap: Sepsis Masquerading as TMA

The first few days of this patient's course were dominated by what looked like classic septic shock complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). She presented with fever >40°C, hypoglycemia, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI) leading to anuria. The initial differential was broad: leukemia, HLH, or just severe sepsis-driven organ failure.

What made this case unique was the **temporal disconnect**. As the bacterial load was controlled with antibiotics and inflammatory markers began to normalize, the patient didn't simply recover. Instead, she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin (from 111 g/L down to 68 g/L) with persistent thrombocytopenia and rising LDH. This wasn't bleeding; it was microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA).

The peripheral smear confirmed schistocytes (15–20%), pointing squarely at TMA. At this point, we had to pivot from "treating sepsis" to "diagnosing TMA." The workup was meticulous:
*   **ADAMTS13** was 43.5% – effectively ruling out Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP).
*   **Stool PCR** for Shiga toxin was negative – ruling out typical STEC-HUS.
*   **Complement levels** (C3/C4) were initially normal – which should ring a bell because normal C3/C4 does *not* exclude CM-HUS!

It wasn't until the kidney biopsy showed fibrin thrombi in ischemic glomeruli and crescents that the diagnosis solidified: infection-triggered CM-HUS. Interestingly, extended functional testing eventually showed reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50 6%), likely influenced by prior plasma exchange or disease consumption, but crucially, no genetic mutations were found initially. This suggests that in some cases, a potent infectious trigger alone is sufficient to tip a normal complement system into catastrophe ("two-hit" theory where hit #1 is subclinical genetics or hit #2 is purely environmental).

### The Therapeutic Tightrope: To Treat or Wait?

Once CM-HUS is diagnosed in an adult with active or recent bacteremia involving encapsulated organisms like *Neisseria*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, or *Capnocytophaga*, we face a genuine moral hazard. Eculizumab works by inhibiting C5 cleavage, preventing the formation of the Membrane Attack Complex (MAC). While this stops endothelial damage in TMA, it also disables our primary defense mechanism against encapsulated bacteria that rely on opsonization for clearance.

In this specific case report, the team navigated this minefield with precision:
1.  **Timing:** They did not rush eculizumab immediately upon suspecting TMA while cultures were pending or potentially positive early on. They waited until blood cultures confirmed *C. canimorsus* had cleared under appropriate antibiotic coverage (initially piperacillin-tazobactam/de-escalated to ampicillin/apocillin).
2.  **Vaccination:** Before starting eculizumab induction doses (900 mg weekly x4), they administered quadrivalent meningococcal vaccine serogroups A,C,Y,W (*Menveo*) and serogroup B (*Bexsero*). Note that vaccination ideally occurs two weeks prior to therapy; since this wasn't possible due to urgency/proximity of therapy start times here given her stability status at transfer, they proceeded with extreme caution knowing vaccination coverage was incomplete relative to ideal guidelines but necessary for life-saving therapy.
3.  **Prophylaxis:** Crucially, they instituted lifelong antimicrobial prophylaxis with penicillin V (*Apocillin*) immediately upon starting eculizumab and continuing through induction/maintenance phases.

The outcome speaks volumes: After plasma exchange bridged them temporarily and then eculizumab took hold over several weeks, spontaneous diuresis returned and hematological remission was achieved. Without blocking complement at that precise moment of transition from sepsis resolution to TMA flare, she likely would have progressed to end-stage renal disease or died from uncontrolled hemolysis/thrombosis elsewhere in her vascular tree.

However, we must acknowledge that delaying eculizumab carries its own risk: irreversible renal scarring if initiated too late after symptom onset of MAHA/TMA becomes established compared to earlier intervention once infection is deemed controlled enough for safety margins (negative cultures x 24-48 hours?). In this case, waiting allowed them to balance survival against infection risk perfectly because she stabilized on antibiotics before the hemolysis worsened significantly again after initial improvement.

### Key Take-Home Points for Clinical Practice

When facing CM-HUS triggered by encapsulated bacteria (*N.* spp., *S.* pneumo., *C.* spp.), remember these pearls:

*   **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM-HUS.** Rely on clinical context (MAHA + AKI + exclusion of TTP/STEC) rather than screening complements alone; look for low AH50/CH50 if available later in the workup or genetic testing if chronicity persists post-infection resolution.
*   **The "Second Hit" Theory applies.** Many patients presenting with infection-triggered CM-HUS have no identifiable genetic mutation beforehand; they require an exogenous trigger like severe sepsis or surgery to manifest disease even without known predisposition genes initially detected on standard panels until extended panels reveal subtle variants later if needed long-term management planning wise retesting after treatment completion/recovery period etcetera...
*   **Eculizumab Timing is Critical.** Initiate as soon as feasible after confirming bacterial clearance or near-certainty thereof; do not let fear of reinfection delay life-saving therapy indefinitely if cultures are trending negative rapidly under effective antibiotics—but ensure you vaccinate first if time permits!
*   **Mandatory Prophylaxis Strategy.** If you block C5 in anyone exposed recently or currently harboring potential pathogens:
    *   Administer MenACWY + MenB vaccines ASAP (ideally ≥2 weeks pre-treatment; otherwise give concurrently/start prophylaxis immediately thereafter).
    *   Start daily beta-lactam prophylaxis (e.g., Penicillin VK/Augmentin depending on local resistance patterns/allergies).
    *   Educate patients aggressively about seeking immediate care for fevers while on therapy ("fever protocol").

# Pitfalls in Differentiating TTP from CM-HUS: Why ADAMTS13 Matters More Than You Think

We've all seen the "classic" triad of Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) and immediately reached for the TTP protocol. But what happens when your patient has a clear infectious trigger, a negative stool PCR, and a bizarre twist where their Coombs test turns positive?

This case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis evolving into Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS) is a masterclass in diagnostic traps. It reminds us that while the clinical presentation of Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) and CM-HUS often overlaps, the pathophysiology—and crucially, the treatment—is fundamentally different. Relying on heuristics alone can lead to fatal delays in administering eculizumab or unnecessary exposure to ineffective therapies.

### The Sepsis Mask: When DIC Pretends to be TMA

Our patient presented with a classic "dog bite gone wrong." Four days post-bite, she spiraled into septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and multiorgan failure caused by *C. canimorsus*. Initially, her pancytopenia and coagulopathy fit perfectly with sepsis-induced DIC. Even her renal failure seemed like a straightforward consequence of septic cardiomyopathy and hypoperfusion.

Here lies the first pitfall: **Do not assume TMA is just "severe DIC."**

In this case, the patient initially stabilized on antibiotics and supportive care. Then came the curveball: a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin without bleeding, persistent thrombocytopenia, and rising LDH. This wasn't just lingering shock; it was new-onset MAHA. The presence of schistocytes on the peripheral smear confirmed a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) superimposed on the resolving sepsis.

But wait—she had a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (Coombs). We saw hemolysis! Was this Evans syndrome? Autoimmune hemolytic anemia triggered by infection? The team correctly noted that penicillin could cause drug-induced hemolysis or that prior transfusions might yield false positives. They held off on steroids initially until they realized the hemolysis was refractory to high-dose prednisolone. This delay highlights a common error: treating an antibody-mediated process when you are actually looking at mechanical destruction from complement-driven endothelial injury. A positive Coombs does not rule out TMA; in fact, up to 10-20% of TTP/CM-HUS patients can have a false-positive DAT due to red cell coating from drugs or antibodies generated during massive hemolysis.

### The Pivot Point: ADAMTS13 is King

When facing MAHA + Thrombocytopenia + AKI, your differential must start with ruling out TTP because it is instantly lethal without Plasma Exchange (PLEX). However, we cannot treat empirically forever while waiting for results if we suspect something else entirely.

This is where **ADAMTS13 activity** becomes the absolute gatekeeper of decision-making.
*   **TTP:** Defined by severe deficiency (<10%).
*   **CM-HUS / Other TMAs:** Usually >10%, often near normal range unless there is concomitant liver disease consuming factors.

In our case, the PLASMIC score was 4/7 (intermediate-low risk), but the definitive data arrived at 43.5%. This single number effectively killed TTP as a working diagnosis. If you stop here and think "It's not TTP," you might go home safe... unless you forget that CM-HUS exists right next door on the differential list.

Many clinicians mistakenly believe that if ADAMTS13 isn't <10%, they don't need to worry about TMA management beyond supportive care or PLEX for "bridging." This is dangerous thinking in CM-HUS cases driven by infection or genetic predisposition where ADAMTS13 levels are preserved but complement dysregulation causes catastrophic endothelial damage. In this patient, despite normal C3/C4 levels (which happen frequently in CM-HUS!), the alternative pathway activity was profoundly suppressed once tested properly after accounting for treatment effects, confirming complement consumption/dysregulation rather than simple activation markers falling within reference ranges during acute phases.

### The Therapeutic Trap: PLEX vs. Eculizumab

The management divergence here is stark and defines patient survival outcomes.
*   **For TTP:** PLEX is curative life-saving therapy that replaces deficient enzyme and removes autoantibodies continuously until remission.
*   **For CM-HUS:** PLEX offers only transient stabilization by washing out inflammatory mediators but fails to correct the underlying complement cascade dysregulation if left unchecked by specific blockade.

Our patient underwent PLEX sessions which allowed her diuresis to return temporarily—a phenomenon often mistaken for success in CM-HUS cases who haven't yet received eculizumab. However, sustained hematological remission only occurred after initiating **eculizumab**. The key takeaway? Do not mistake temporary stability from PLEX as resolution of disease in non-TTP TMAs if clinical markers aren't trending favorably long-term without adjunctive therapy directed at complement inhibition or underlying triggers.

Furthermore, initiating eculizumab in active bacteremia raises valid concerns regarding susceptibility to encapsulated organisms (*Neisseria*, *Capnocytophaga*). This patient received appropriate meningococcal vaccinations (Menveo/Bexsero) and prophylactic antibiotics *before* starting blockade because her blood cultures were already negative—a critical safety step often overlooked when rushing to block complement during active sepsis fears exist but must be weighed against renal recovery timelines. Normal C3/C4 does NOT rule out CM-HUS; remember that these proteins can be consumed rapidly or remain normal due to compensatory synthesis during acute phases of complement-mediated disease, especially in infection-triggered scenarios where no germline mutation is present ("infection-only" trigger vs genetic predisposition).
