# ADAMTS13 is Your Gatekeeper: Distinguishing TTP from Infection-Triggered CM-HUS in Adults

We've all seen the "sepsis mimic" slide in the ED conference room. But few things are as terrifyingly deceptive as a patient who looks like they're dying of septic shock, only to reveal a hidden layer of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) once the antibiotics kick in.

Consider this recent case: a previously healthy woman in her late 40s presents four days after a dog bite with fever, hypotension, and rapid multiorgan failure. Initially, the picture screams *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis complicated by Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC). She gets aggressive resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and eventually lands on dialysis for acute kidney injury (AKI). She's doing slightly better until Day 8, when she suddenly develops profound hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia.

Here is where the clinical detective work becomes critical. Is this just lingering sepsis? Is it Evans syndrome? Or has she developed **Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS)** triggered by the infection?

The answer lies entirely in your gatekeeper test: **ADAMTS13**.

## The Diagnostic Pivot Point: Why ADAMTS13 Matters More Than You Think

When an adult presents with Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA)—schistocytes on smear, falling hemoglobin, rising LDH—and thrombocytopenia, the differential is narrow but deadly. It essentially boils down to Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) versus everything else (including CM-HUS and DIC).

In this specific case, the team initially considered TTP given the severe anemia and low platelets. However, applying the **PLASMIC score** provided crucial triage data before the lab results even came back. With a score of 4/7 (Low pre-test probability), TTP was less likely than high-risk categories suggest. But scores are just heuristics; we need definitive data.

The gold standard remains ADAMTS13 activity levels:
*   **<10%**: Diagnostic of TTP. This demands immediate Plasma Exchange (PLEX) regardless of other findings.
*   **>10%**: Rules out classic TTP and forces us to look elsewhere for causes of TMA, such as CM-HUS or drug-induced etiologies.

Our patient's ADAMTS13 activity came back at **43.5%**. This single number effectively removed TTP from the differential equation and shifted our entire therapeutic axis toward a diagnosis of infection-triggered CM-HUS. Without this result, we might have been stuck treating for TTP indefinitely or delaying life-saving complement blockade in favor of unnecessary PLEX alone.

## The Trap of Normal Complement Levels and False Positives

One of the most common pitfalls in diagnosing CM-HUS is relying solely on C3 and C4 levels. In this case, initial complement studies were normal. Does that rule out CM-HUS? Absolutely not. As we learned here, C3/C4 can remain within reference ranges during active disease because they are consumed faster than they are depleted in serum assays, or simply because the assay isn't sensitive enough to catch functional deficits early on.

Furthermore, consider the diagnostic noise introduced by acute infection itself:
*   **The Coombs Test:** Our patient had a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) for IgG early on. Was this autoimmune hemolytic anemia? No—it turned out to be a false positive likely driven by penicillin exposure or prior transfusions (which she received for her DIC). Relying on that DAT could have led down a rabbit hole of steroid trials that wouldn't address the underlying microvascular occlusion.
*   **Sepsis vs. TMA:** Early in her course, she had pancytopenia and coagulopathy typical of DIC. It wasn't until her inflammatory markers improved that the "isolated" hemolysis emerged—a classic "unmasking" phenomenon where resolving sepsis reveals an underlying complement-driven pathology that was previously obscured by overwhelming consumption coagulopathy.

## Therapeutic Strategy: Bridging vs. Blocking

So, you've ruled out TTP with an ADAMTS13 >10%. What now? Do you keep running PLEX loops hoping to clear toxins? Or do you jump straight to eculizumab?

This is where understanding the pathophysiology dictates management:
*   **Plasma Exchange (PLEX):** In TTP, PLEX replaces deficient enzyme and removes antibodies; it is curative if done quickly. In CM-HUS, PLEX offers only transient removal of inflammatory mediators but does not correct the fundamental dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway unless there are inhibitory antibodies present (like anti-factor H). In our case, diuresis returned after PEX sessions but renal function did not fully recover until eculizumab was initiated.
*   **Eculizumab/Ravulizumab:** For confirmed or strongly suspected CM-HUS (especially with AKI), blocking terminal complement C5 is superior to PLEX alone for sustained hematologic remission and renal salvage. Time-to-treatment is arguably more predictive of long-term renal recovery than anything else we do here in the ER or ICU setting for these patients who don't have classic STERCO-E.coli HUS features either!

However, we cannot ignore the elephant in the room: **Active Sepsis.** Administering terminal complement inhibitors leaves patients vulnerable against encapsulated organisms like *Neisseria*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, and yes... ***Capnocytophaga*** itself (*Ccanimorsus*). This case highlights a delicate balancing act:
1.  Ensure blood cultures are negative before starting eculizumab if possible (though sometimes you must weigh risk vs benefit if renal failure is imminent).
2.  Vaccinate aggressively against Meningococcus A,C,Y,W,B before induction (and give booster schedules).
3.  Maintain prophylactic antibiotics throughout treatment duration if feasible; this patient stayed on Amoxicillin/Apocillin coverage specifically because she had been infected with *C.canimorsus*.
