Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Normal C3/C4 Does Not Rule Out Atypical HUS: Navigating the Diagnostic Pitfalls of Complement Testing

We've all been there. You're evaluating a patient with Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and Acute Kidney Injury (AKI). The differential is tight: Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP), Shiga-toxin HUS, or Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS, formerly "atypical" HUS). You send off the ADAMTS13 level and the Shiga toxin screen. Then, you glance at the basic complement panel: C3 and C4. Both are sitting comfortably within normal limits.

Your gut tells you "not CM-HUS." Your mind races toward TTP or drug-induced causes. But here's the hard truth from a recent case involving *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis: **Normal C3 and C4 levels do not rule out CM-HUS.**

Relying solely on static concentration values for C3 and C4 in the setting of acute thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) can lead to dangerous diagnostic delays. Let's walk through a fascinating case where a seemingly "normal" workup masked a life-threatening complement crisis, triggered by a dog bite.

## The Case: A Dog Bite That Became a Dialysis Nightmare

Our patient was a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who presented with rapidly progressive sepsis four days after a dog bite on her lip. Initially, she manifested classic signs of overwhelming *C. canimorsus* bacteremia: high fever (>40°C), hypoglycemia refractory to treatment, DIC, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury progressing to anuria requiring CRRT.

She stabilized somewhat with antibiotics and supportive care, but then came the curveball. As her inflammatory markers improved and coagulopathy resolved, she developed **isolated hemolysis**. Her hemoglobin plummeted from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days without bleeding. Platelets remained stubbornly low around 30–40 × 10⁹/L. Schistocytes were abundant on the peripheral smear (15–20%).

At this juncture, we faced a classic TMA puzzle. The initial suspicion leaned toward autoimmune hemolytic anemia because of a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT), likely a false positive induced by penicillin exposure or transfusion reactions (repeat testing was negative). With hemolysis persisting despite steroids and platelets failing to recover, we knew we needed to look deeper into the complement system.

## The Trap of "Normal" Complements

Here is where the diagnostic trap springs shut for many clinicians. Standard teaching often emphasizes that in complement-mediated diseases, you expect low C3 due to consumption via the alternative pathway. So, when this patient's labs came back showing **normal C3 and normal C4**, many would have safely dismissed CM-HUS from their differential list immediately. They might have chased malignancies or other autoimmune etiologies instead of treating for TMA caused by complement dysregulation.

However, this patient had CM-HUS triggered by infection. Why were her baseline levels normal?

First, remember that in acute settings, especially early in the disease course or during fluctuating phases of consumption/regeneration, serum concentrations may not yet reflect tissue-level consumption or functional depletion. Second—and crucially—**complement levels are dynamic**. In some cases of infection-triggered CM-HUS without underlying genetic defects (like Factor H mutations), the complement cascade is activated so acutely that homeostatic mechanisms maintain serum concentrations near baseline until catastrophic failure occurs later, or simply because the assay measures mass rather than function.

This patient's story highlights a critical distinction between **static levels** (concentration) and **functional capacity**. While her C3/C4 were normal locally available tests showed normal classical pathway activity too—but once we moved to functional assays measuring total alternative pathway activity (**AH50**), we saw the truth: her AH50 was markedly reduced at just **6%**. This indicated profound alternative pathway consumption that standard antigen-based tests missed entirely.

## Beyond Basic Screens: When to Order Advanced Testing

So, when should you stop trusting your reflexive "normal" result? If you see MAHA + Thrombocytopenia + AKI + Negative ADAMTS13 (>10%) + Negative Stool Toxin... you must suspect CM-HUS regardless of C3/C4 status unless proven otherwise by functional data or genetics later on.

The diagnostic algorithm needs an upgrade in high-stakes scenarios:
*   **Don't let normal C3/C4 close your differential:** These are necessary but insufficient exclusions for CM-HUS.
*   **Order Functional Assays Early:** If available, measure CH50/AH50 or specific factor activities rather than relying solely on immunoturbidimetry for antigen levels. A low AH50 with normal C3 suggests active alternative pathway consumption masking itself as "normal" mass levels due to compensatory synthesis or assay timing issues discussed above (though in this specific case report note regarding timing relative to PEX/Eculizumab is nuanced).
*   **ADAMTS13 is King for TTP Ruling Out:** Remember that <10% defines TTP; anything >25-30% essentially rules it out (PLASMIC score helps triage). This leaves TMA as either STEC-HUS or CM-HUS/Other TMAs if toxins are negative.
*   **Biopsy Matters:** Renal biopsy showing fibrin thrombi in glomeruli without immune complex deposition strongly supports TMA over other glomerulonephritides like ANCA vasculitis or Lupus Nephritis (which usually present with different serologies).

In our patient's case, despite normal local screening tests, the combination of persistent schistocytosis, lack of response to steroids/transfusion alone, and biopsy-proven TMA forced us down the path of plasma exchange and eventual eculizumab initiation after vaccination coverage was secured against encapsulated organisms (*Neisseria*, *Capnocytophaga*). The result? Hematologic remission and partial renal recovery only after blocking terminal complement activation with eculizumab (C5 inhibitor). Plasma exchange provided transient stabilization but could not sustain remission; only targeted blockade worked long-term once infection was controlled via blood culture clearance prior to drug administration.

## Take-Home Points for Your Next Shift

Diagnosing TMA is rarely straightforward; it requires peeling back layers of physiology that aren't always visible on standard chemistry panels. Here are your key takeaways from this case:

*   **Normal C3/C4 does NOT exclude Complement-Mediated HUS.** Static concentration measurements can be misleading during acute phases; rely on functional assays (AH50) if available when clinical suspicion is high despite "normal" screens.
*   **Watch for Secondary Triggers:** Infection (*C*. *canimorsus*, meningococcus), pregnancy, drugs, or surgery can precipitate CM-HUS even in patients without known genetic defects ("idiopathic"). Do not assume genetic absence just because family history is empty; up to half of cases have no identifiable mutation initially detected or require specialized deep sequencing later.
*   **Time is Kidney:** Early initiation of eculizumab/crislanib is critical for renal recovery once TTP and STEC-HUS are reasonably excluded; delaying therapy due to confusing lab values increases
