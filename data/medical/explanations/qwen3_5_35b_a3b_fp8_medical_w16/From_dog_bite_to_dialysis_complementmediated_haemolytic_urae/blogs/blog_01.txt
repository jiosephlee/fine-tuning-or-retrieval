Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# The Dog Bite That Didn't Stop: Unmasking Complement-Mediated HUS After Capnocytophaga Sepsis

We've all seen the "dog bite" vignette. It's a classic board question: a patient with a pet bite presents with fever, chills, and rapid deterioration. You think *Pasteurella*, you start empiric coverage, and you hope for the best. But what happens when the sepsis seems to resolve, the antibiotics are working, and then—bam—the patient suddenly develops frank hemolysis and renal failure?

That is exactly where we find ourselves in this fascinating case report from Fredriksen et al. A previously healthy woman in her late 40s presents with a dog bite on her lip four days prior. She skips her antibiotic prescription (the cardinal sin!), returns with septic shock, hypoglycemia, and DIC. Everything looks like classic *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia until the tide turns. As her sepsis clears and she stabilizes on antibiotics, she doesn't just get better; she gets worse in a very specific way: isolated hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, and anuria leading to dialysis dependence.

This isn't just sepsis-induced organ failure anymore. This is **complement-mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS)** triggered by an infection that should have been easily cleared by the same mechanism causing the damage. It forces us to confront a terrifying diagnostic reality: sometimes, the thing killing your patient isn't the bug itself, but their own immune system's response to it.

## The Diagnostic Trap: DIC vs. TMA

The initial presentation is a masterclass in "look-alikes." The patient arrived with pancytopenia, coagulopathy (low fibrinogen), AKI, and fever. Your differential list is long: Acute Leukemia? HLH? Classic HUS? Or simply overwhelming sepsis driving Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)?

In this case, the team correctly identified *C. canimorsus* early (thanks to blood cultures taking 7 days to grow—a reminder that molecular methods are our friends here), but they also faced a massive confounder: **normal C3/C4 levels**.

Here lies one of the most common pitfalls in diagnosing Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA). Many clinicians assume that if C3 and C4 are normal, it cannot be complement-mediated disease because "it's always low." **Wrong.** In CM-HUS (formerly called atypical HUS), baseline complement levels are often normal because the dysregulation is driven by genetic variants or autoantibodies that don't necessarily consume complement factors until a "second hit" occurs—or they may not consume them enough to fall outside reference ranges during acute phases. Relying solely on C3/C4 will miss up to half of these cases.

Furthermore, the timing of the hemolysis was deceptive. Initially, there was no schistocytosis; instead, there was leukopenia and thrombocytopenia typical of sepsis/DIC. The "smoking gun"—schistocytes appearing on smear—didn't emerge until day 8 or so, *after* the septic shock had resolved and inflammatory markers were dropping. If you stop looking for TMA once the blood pressure stabilizes and lactate drops, you miss this window entirely. The persistence of thrombocytopenia combined with new-onset hemolysis in a recovering patient is your alarm bell for TMA masquerading as post-septic recovery complications.

## Why Capnocytophaga? And Why Now?

*C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated Gram-negative rod found in canine oral flora. It has a notorious reputation for causing fulminant sepsis in asplenic patients or those with alcohol use disorder—but it strikes healthy people too if they skip prophylaxis (like our patient who didn't pick up her Amoxicillin).

What makes this case physiologically intriguing is the role of complement itself as both savior and executioner. Normally, complement helps clear encapsulated bacteria like *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*. However, in susceptible hosts (even those without known genetic mutations), an intense bacterial load can trigger uncontrolled alternative pathway activation via molecular mimicry or direct binding of bacterial surface proteins to complement regulators like Factor H or CD46 (Membrane Cofactor Protein).

In this specific case study, extended testing eventually showed markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50 ~6%), confirming complement consumption/dysfunction despite normal static C3/C4 levels earlier on. Interestingly, no pathogenic genetic variants were found upon deep sequencing of complement genes (Factor H/I/B). This suggests **infection-triggered CM-HUS** without a pre-existing heritable defect—a phenomenon where the sheer magnitude of the infection acted as the sole trigger for complement dysregulation in an otherwise genetically average individual. This reinforces that CM-HUS is often a clinicopathologic diagnosis of exclusion where infection acts as the necessary "second hit."

## Therapeutic Dilemma: To Block or Not To Block?

Once TMA is suspected after excluding Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) via ADAMTS13 activity (>10%) and Shiga toxin-negative status via stool PCR/plasticity testing (PLASMIC score low), we face a critical decision: Plasma Exchange (PLEX) vs. Eculizumab/Ravulizumab?

While PLEX has been historically used as a bridge for TTP-like syndromes including HUS data shows it offers only transient benefit in pure CM-HUS because it does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation—it merely washes away soluble factors temporarily. In this case diuresis returned briefly after PLEX but only sustained recovery happened after starting eculizumab blocking terminal complement C5 preventing further MAC formation on endothelial cells.)
The catch? **Eculizumab blocks terminal complement**, leaving patients vulnerable to encapsulated organisms like *N.* meningitidis—and now arguably *C.* canimorsus itself since we know they rely partly on complement clearance!
So how do we manage this paradoxical situation where treating one complication might exacerbate another?
The answer lies in strict protocolization:
1) Ensure blood cultures are negative before initiating blockade if possible (done here).
2) Administer meningococcal vaccines ideally 2 weeks prior; if emergent give MenACWY + MenB concurrently with aggressive antibiotic prophylaxis throughout treatment duration.)
Our patient received dual vaccination plus lifelong ciprofloxacin/amoxicillin prophylaxis while on therapy demonstrating safe integration despite active recent bacteremia.)
Delaying eculizumab increases risk of irreversible renal scarring; thus balancing infectious risk against organ loss favors early intervention once TTP/HUS distinction made.)
Key Take-Home Messages:
*   **Don't trust normal C3/C4:** Normal levels do not rule out CM-HUS; look at clinical trajectory and consider functional assays if suspicion remains high.)
*   **Timing matters:** New-onset MAHA/thrombocytopenia occurring during recovery from severe sepsis should trigger immediate TMA workup including peripheral smear review.)
*   **Exclude TTP first:** Always check ADAMTS13 activity before committing to eculizumab unless clinical picture strongly mandates empiric action while waiting.)
*   **Vaccinate & Protect:** If using anti-C5 agents post-infection ensure meningococcal coverage AND maintain prophylactic antibiotics against other encapsulated pathogens throughout therapy period.)
*   **Think beyond genetics:** Infection alone can trigger CM-HUS even without identifiable germline mutations especially when dealing with highly virulent organisms like Capnocytophaga.)
