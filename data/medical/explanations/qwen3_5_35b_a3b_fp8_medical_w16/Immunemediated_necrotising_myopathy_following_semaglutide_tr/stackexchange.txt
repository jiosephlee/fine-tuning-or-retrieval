Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Based on the muscle biopsy findings described in this case—specifically the presence of necrotizing fibers, macrophage infiltration (CD68+), and membrane attack complex deposition alongside a paucity of lymphocytes—which specific subtype of myopathy is confirmed, and how does its histological profile distinguish it from polymyositis?

Based on the muscle biopsy findings described in this case—necrotizing fibers, macrophage infiltration (CD68+), and membrane attack complex deposition with a **paucity of lymphocytes**—the confirmed diagnosis is **immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)**.

This histological profile distinguishes IMNM from polymyositis through the nature and location of the inflammatory infiltrate. In classic polymyositis, the pathology is characterized by endomysial inflammation dominated by **cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes** invading non-necrotic, infected muscle fibers. Conversely, as highlighted in this patient's biopsy, IMNM is defined by **myocyte necrosis** driven primarily by humoral immunity (evidenced here by membrane attack complex deposition) and phagocytic clearance. The key distinguishing feature is the relative absence of T-cells; while rare CD3+ cells were noted in this case, they are sparse compared to the abundant macrophages engaged in myophagocytosis. Essentially, polymyositis presents as a "T-cell mediated" invasion of viable fibers, whereas IMNM presents as a "macrophage-mediated" cleanup of necrotic fibers with minimal T-cell involvement. This distinction is critical because it explains why this patient required immunomodulation targeting B-cell pathways (such as IVIG) and corticosteroids rather than therapies specifically aimed at blocking T-cell cytotoxicity alone. Furthermore, unlike toxic myopathies which typically show necrosis without significant inflammation, the presence of membrane attack complexes confirms an active immune etiology consistent with IMNM.

### [Clinical Pearls] This patient presented with elevated transaminases (AST/ALT) and CK; explain the biochemical mechanism that allows clinicians to differentiate whether the enzyme elevation originates from hepatic pathology versus skeletal muscle injury in a patient with known NAFLD.

To distinguish whether elevated transaminases originate from hepatic pathology versus skeletal muscle injury, you must evaluate the **AST-to-ALT ratio** and correlate it with the **creatine kinase (CK)** level.

In this case, the patient's initial work-up three years prior revealed ALT levels higher than AST, a pattern classic for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Conversely, when she presented with severe myopathy, her AST (191 U/L) exceeded her ALT (165 U/L), yielding an AST/ALT ratio greater than 1. This reversal is a key discriminator: skeletal muscle contains abundant AST but negligible ALT. When muscle fibers undergo necrosis, they release both enzymes into the circulation; however, because ALT is absent in muscle tissue, its concentration does not rise proportionally to AST. Consequently, a rising AST relative to ALT in the presence of markedly elevated CK strongly points toward a musculoskeletal source rather than primary hepatocellular injury.

Furthermore, the magnitude of the CK elevation provides definitive context. In this patient, CK rose to nearly 4,000 U/L. While mild elevations in transaminases can occur in NAFLD alone, such massive CK elevation confirms extensive myocyte damage. As noted in the discussion of this case report, while "transaminaemia" is common in myopathies due to shared enzyme expression, the persistence of her specific AST/ALT pattern *after* her CK normalized confirmed that her baseline liver enzyme elevation was indeed of hepatic origin (biopsy-proven NAFLD), whereas the acute spike represented concurrent muscle breakdown. Thus, an AST > ALT combined with significant hyper-CKemia indicates that the transaminase elevation is largely artifactual and driven by muscle injury.

### [Management] Compare the clinical trajectory and management requirements of drug-induced immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) triggered by statins or GLP-1 agonists against classic toxic rhabdomyolysis. Why might reliance solely on drug cessation be insufficient for treating this patient's condition?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

This case illustrates a critical distinction between **classic toxic rhabdomyolysis** and **drug-induced immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)**, specifically highlighting why simple drug cessation is often insufficient for the latter.

Classic toxic rhabdomyolysis, such as that occasionally seen with statins or GLP-1 agonists like tirzepatide, represents a direct toxic injury to the myocyte. The pathophysiology involves mitochondrial dysfunction or direct membrane toxicity leading to cell death. Clinically, this presents with an acute spike in creatine kinase (CK) that typically resolves rapidly—often within days to weeks—once the offending agent is discontinued and volume resuscitation is provided. As noted in the discussion of this case, previous reports of GLP-1-associated rhabdomyolysis show a rapid normalization of CK without the need for immunosuppression.

In contrast, the patient in this case developed **immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy**. While triggered by semaglutide exposure, the mechanism shifted from direct toxicity to an autoimmune process characterized by humoral immunity attacking muscle antigens (similar to anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP pathways seen in statin-induced IMNM). The key differentiator here is the clinical trajectory: unlike toxic rhabdomyolysis, which improves immediately upon stopping the drug, this patient's CK levels rose progressively over four months *after* the single dose of semaglutide was stopped, and she developed severe proximal weakness and bulbar symptoms. Histologically, her muscle biopsy confirmed necrosis with macrophage infiltration and complement deposition (membrane attack complex), hallmarks of an active immune-mediated process rather than passive necrosis.

Therefore, relying solely on drug cessation was insufficient because it addresses only the trigger, not the established autoimmune cascade driving ongoing muscle destruction. Just as seen in statin-induced anti-HMGCR myopathy where patients continue to deteriorate despite stopping statins alone, this patient required active immunomodulation. She demonstrated significant clinical recovery and normalization of CK only after receiving high-dose corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). This underscores a vital management principle: when IMNM is suspected based on progressive weakness and rising CK despite drug withdrawal, early initiation of immunosuppressive therapy is mandatory to prevent permanent disability.

### [Pathophysiology] Despite the strong suspicion of drug-induced IMNM, why is testing for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies critical even when the clinical picture is compelling, considering that a significant proportion of IMNM patients may test negative for these markers?

While the clinical presentation—rapidly progressive proximal weakness, markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK), and a muscle biopsy showing necrosis with minimal inflammation—is highly suggestive of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), testing for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies remains mandatory. As highlighted in the background of this case report, approximately one-third of patients with definitive IMNM are seronegative; therefore, a negative result does not rule out the diagnosis, but a positive result provides critical prognostic and mechanistic clarity.

The distinction is vital because the presence of these specific autoantibodies defines the disease's trajectory and guides long-term management. Historically, many patients thought to have "statin-induced" or "toxic" myopathy were later identified as having true autoimmune IMNM driven by anti-HMGCR antibodies. Unlike pure drug toxicity, which typically resolves upon cessation of the offending agent, anti-HMGCR-positive disease often persists or progresses despite stopping the trigger, necessitating aggressive and prolonged immunosuppression. In contrast, as noted in this case discussion, some forms of drug-associated IMNM may resolve with a shorter course of steroids and IVIG once the drug is withdrawn. Identifying the specific antibody helps distinguish whether this patient's course represents a transient drug-triggered event or a persistent autoimmune process requiring maintenance therapy. Furthermore, since statins are common in metabolic populations like this patient (who has obesity and NAFLD), ruling out anti-HMGCR is essential to differentiate between classic statin-induced necrotizing myopathy and a novel GLP-1 receptor agonist association. Ultimately, serologic profiling anchors the diagnosis in objective evidence, ensuring that treatment intensity matches the underlying pathophysiology rather than relying solely on clinical suspicion.

### [Management] Given the severity of this patient's presentation—including bulbar symptoms (dysphagia/dysphonia) and rapid functional decline—what is the rationale for combining high-dose corticosteroids with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) rather than relying on steroids alone as monotherapy?

**Clinical Pearl:** In patients presenting with severe, rapidly progressive immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) featuring bulbar involvement and profound functional decline, the rationale for combining high-dose corticosteroids with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) lies in the need for **synergistic, rapid immunomodulation** to halt ongoing muscle necrosis when steroid monotherapy is insufficient or too slow to prevent irreversible disability.

While corticosteroids are the cornerstone of induction therapy for IMNM, they act primarily by suppressing cytokine production and inhibiting T-cell activation—a process that may take days to achieve maximal efficacy. In contrast, IVIG offers a mechanism of action that includes neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies via Fc-receptor blockade, inhibition of complement deposition on muscle fibers, and modulation of dendritic cell maturation. This allows for a more immediate dampening of the humoral component of the injury.

In the case presented by Lam et al., the patient exhibited classic "red flags" for severe disease: progressive dysphagia and dysphonia indicating bulbar muscle involvement, alongside an inability to stand or sit upright without assistance. Despite receiving pulse-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for 5 days), her weakness persisted. The decision to add IVIG (2 g/kg over 4 days) was driven specifically by these **severe features and persistent disability**. The literature supports this approach; guidelines and observational data suggest that adding IVIG early in cases with life-threatening or functionally devastating presentations improves the speed of recovery compared to steroids alone.

The outcome in this specific case validates this dual-threshold strategy: following the combined regimen, the patient demonstrated a marked reduction in creatine kinase levels within two weeks and achieved a return to baseline functional status off immunosuppression within months. Had we relied solely on steroids in a patient with such rapid decline and bulbar compromise, we risked prolonged immobilization, aspiration pneumonia from untreated dysphagia, and potentially permanent neuromuscular damage. Thus, in severe IMNM phenotypes characterized by bulbar signs and rapid deterioration, combination therapy is preferred to ensure timely control of the autoimmune cascade.

### [Morning Report] In evaluating the temporal relationship between semaglutide administration and symptom onset, what specific features in this case support a 'probable' causal link according to standard pharmacovigilance criteria, distinguishing it from a coincidental finding or pre-existing undiagnosed autoimmune disease?

Based on the principles of pharmacovigilance, specifically the World Health Organization-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO-UMC) causality assessment criteria applied in this case, the link between semaglutide and immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) is classified as **"probable"** based on three converging pillars of evidence that distinguish this from a coincidental event or pre-existing idiopathic disease.

First, the **temporal relationship** is both plausible and distinct. While the patient's initial GI symptoms occurred within hours, the hallmark of IMNM—the progressive proximal weakness and a dramatic 12-fold rise in creatine kinase (from 337 U/L to 3,992 U/L)—developed over four months *after* a single exposure. This delayed onset distinguishes it from direct toxic rhabdomyolysis (like that seen with tirzepatide), which typically resolves rapidly upon drug cessation. Instead, this timeline fits an autoimmune mechanism triggered by the drug but evolving independently once activated. Crucially, the patient had no prior history of muscle weakness or CK elevation; while she had stable transaminitis from known NAFLD, there was no evidence of "smoldering" myopathy prior to semaglutide initiation.

Second, the **exclusion of alternative etiologies** strengthens the causal inference. The patient lacked a history of statin use (the primary trigger for HMGCR-associated IMNM), denied other known myotoxins like red yeast rice or mushrooms, and possessed no clinical features suggestive of systemic connective tissue diseases such as SLE or dermatomyositis. Although she tested positive for low-titer anti-SSB antibodies in isolation—a finding not typically linked to IMNM—her broader autoimmune panel was negative. Furthermore, her muscle biopsy confirmed classic IMNM pathology: necrotic fibers with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex deposition but notably lacking significant lymphocytic infiltrates, ruling out polymyositis or infectious causes.

Finally, and perhaps most compellingly for establishing probability rather than just possibility, is the **clinical response to intervention**. The patient achieved complete resolution of weakness and normalization of CK levels following a relatively short course of immunosuppression (corticosteroids and IVIG) combined strictly with the permanent withdrawal of semaglutide. Idiopathic IMNM often requires prolonged maintenance immunotherapy to prevent relapse; conversely, drug-induced variants frequently resolve quickly once the antigenic stimulus is removed. The fact that she remained asymptomatic off all immunosuppressants for eight months post-recovery strongly supports a self-limiting process driven by a transient trigger rather than an intrinsic autoimmune diathesis requiring lifelong suppression.

In summary, while definitive proof would require re-challenge—which is ethically impermissible here—the convergence of a unique temporal sequence after single-dose exposure, the absence of competing diagnoses despite extensive workup, and the rapid reversal with drug cessation fulfills the criteria for a "probable" causal association under standard pharmacovigilance guidelines.

### [Pathophysiology] The patient exhibited cardiac biomarker elevations (troponin) and MRI evidence of myocardial edema. Discuss the differential diagnosis regarding cardiac involvement in IMNM versus stress-induced cardiomyopathy or primary myocarditis, and how these entities impact long-term prognosis.

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

This case presents a compelling diagnostic challenge where cardiac biomarker elevation and MRI findings initially raised concern for primary myocarditis or stress-induced cardiomyopathy, yet the ultimate diagnosis was immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) triggered by semaglutide. To navigate this, we must distinguish between true cardiac pathology and "spillover" phenomena from severe skeletal muscle injury.

First, consider **primary myocarditis**. Classic viral or autoimmune myocarditis typically presents with diffuse or patchy late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) on cardiac MRI corresponding to areas of fibrosis or necrosis. In this patient, the MRI showed myocardial edema and mild hypokinesis but *no* definite LGE, suggesting an acute inflammatory process without established scar formation. Furthermore, primary myocarditis rarely causes CK elevations exceeding 1,000 U/L unless there is massive multi-system involvement; here, the CK rose to nearly 4,000 U/L with frank muscle weakness, pointing toward a myopathic source.

Second, evaluate **stress-induced (Takotsubo) cardiomyopathy**. This entity can mimic ischemia or myocarditis with transient wall motion abnormalities and elevated troponin due to catecholamine surge. While the patient had paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and acute physiological stress (renal failure), Takotsubo usually follows a distinct apical ballooning pattern on echo/MRI. More importantly, Takotsubo does not explain the profound skeletal muscle weakness or the specific biopsy findings of necrotizing myopathy with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex deposition.

The critical teaching point here is recognizing **cardiac biomarker spillover from severe IMNM**. Troponin assays are generally specific for cardiac injury but can cross-react with skeletal muscle isoforms when there is extensive muscle necrosis, particularly in conditions like rhabdomyolysis or severe IMNM where cell membranes are disrupted globally. In this case, the "cardiac" MRI findings of edema likely represented concurrent inflammation or early reversible changes secondary to systemic cytokine storm or hemodynamic shifts from the acute illness rather than primary heart disease. Crucially, the patient's cardiac function normalized completely alongside her skeletal muscle recovery after immunosuppression and drug withdrawal.

Regarding **long-term prognosis**, this distinction is vital. Primary myocarditis carries risks of chronic dilated cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias depending on the extent of fibrosis (seen as LGE). Stress cardiomyopathy has excellent recovery rates but requires careful monitoring for recurrence during stressors. Conversely, drug-induced IMNM in this case portends an excellent prognosis provided the offending agent is avoided and immunosuppression is tapered appropriately once remission is achieved. As seen in this patient's 10-month follow-up, she regained baseline functional status with normal CK and normalized cardiac imaging after just a short course of steroids and IVIG. Unlike idiopathic anti-HMGCR IMNM which often requires prolonged maintenance therapy due to relapse risk upon tapering, this drug-associated phenotype appears self-limiting once the trigger is removed, highlighting that accurate differentiation prevents unnecessary long-term immunosuppression in patients presenting with "myocardial" symptoms driven by skeletal myopathy.
