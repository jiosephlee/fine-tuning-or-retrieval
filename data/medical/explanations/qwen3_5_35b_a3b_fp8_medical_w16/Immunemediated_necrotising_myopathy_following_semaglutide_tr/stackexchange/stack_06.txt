Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Morning Report] In evaluating the temporal relationship between semaglutide administration and symptom onset, what specific features in this case support a 'probable' causal link according to standard pharmacovigilance criteria, distinguishing it from a coincidental finding or pre-existing undiagnosed autoimmune disease?

Based on the principles of pharmacovigilance, specifically the World Health Organization-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO-UMC) causality assessment criteria applied in this case, the link between semaglutide and immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) is classified as **"probable"** based on three converging pillars of evidence that distinguish this from a coincidental event or pre-existing idiopathic disease.

First, the **temporal relationship** is both plausible and distinct. While the patient's initial GI symptoms occurred within hours, the hallmark of IMNM—the progressive proximal weakness and a dramatic 12-fold rise in creatine kinase (from 337 U/L to 3,992 U/L)—developed over four months *after* a single exposure. This delayed onset distinguishes it from direct toxic rhabdomyolysis (like that seen with tirzepatide), which typically resolves rapidly upon drug cessation. Instead, this timeline fits an autoimmune mechanism triggered by the drug but evolving independently once activated. Crucially, the patient had no prior history of muscle weakness or CK elevation; while she had stable transaminitis from known NAFLD, there was no evidence of "smoldering" myopathy prior to semaglutide initiation.

Second, the **exclusion of alternative etiologies** strengthens the causal inference. The patient lacked a history of statin use (the primary trigger for HMGCR-associated IMNM), denied other known myotoxins like red yeast rice or mushrooms, and possessed no clinical features suggestive of systemic connective tissue diseases such as SLE or dermatomyositis. Although she tested positive for low-titer anti-SSB antibodies in isolation—a finding not typically linked to IMNM—her broader autoimmune panel was negative. Furthermore, her muscle biopsy confirmed classic IMNM pathology: necrotic fibers with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex deposition but notably lacking significant lymphocytic infiltrates, ruling out polymyositis or infectious causes.

Finally, and perhaps most compellingly for establishing probability rather than just possibility, is the **clinical response to intervention**. The patient achieved complete resolution of weakness and normalization of CK levels following a relatively short course of immunosuppression (corticosteroids and IVIG) combined strictly with the permanent withdrawal of semaglutide. Idiopathic IMNM often requires prolonged maintenance immunotherapy to prevent relapse; conversely, drug-induced variants frequently resolve quickly once the antigenic stimulus is removed. The fact that she remained asymptomatic off all immunosuppressants for eight months post-recovery strongly supports a self-limiting process driven by a transient trigger rather than an intrinsic autoimmune diathesis requiring lifelong suppression.

In summary, while definitive proof would require re-challenge—which is ethically impermissible here—the convergence of a unique temporal sequence after single-dose exposure, the absence of competing diagnoses despite extensive workup, and the rapid reversal with drug cessation fulfills the criteria for a "probable" causal association under standard pharmacovigilance guidelines.
