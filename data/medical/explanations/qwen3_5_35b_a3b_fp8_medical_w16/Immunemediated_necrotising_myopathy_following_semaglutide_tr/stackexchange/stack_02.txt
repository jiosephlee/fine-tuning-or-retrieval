Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] This patient presented with elevated transaminases (AST/ALT) and CK; explain the biochemical mechanism that allows clinicians to differentiate whether the enzyme elevation originates from hepatic pathology versus skeletal muscle injury in a patient with known NAFLD.

To distinguish whether elevated transaminases originate from hepatic pathology versus skeletal muscle injury, you must evaluate the **AST-to-ALT ratio** and correlate it with the **creatine kinase (CK)** level.

In this case, the patient's initial work-up three years prior revealed ALT levels higher than AST, a pattern classic for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Conversely, when she presented with severe myopathy, her AST (191 U/L) exceeded her ALT (165 U/L), yielding an AST/ALT ratio greater than 1. This reversal is a key discriminator: skeletal muscle contains abundant AST but negligible ALT. When muscle fibers undergo necrosis, they release both enzymes into the circulation; however, because ALT is absent in muscle tissue, its concentration does not rise proportionally to AST. Consequently, a rising AST relative to ALT in the presence of markedly elevated CK strongly points toward a musculoskeletal source rather than primary hepatocellular injury.

Furthermore, the magnitude of the CK elevation provides definitive context. In this patient, CK rose to nearly 4,000 U/L. While mild elevations in transaminases can occur in NAFLD alone, such massive CK elevation confirms extensive myocyte damage. As noted in the discussion of this case report, while "transaminaemia" is common in myopathies due to shared enzyme expression, the persistence of her specific AST/ALT pattern *after* her CK normalized confirmed that her baseline liver enzyme elevation was indeed of hepatic origin (biopsy-proven NAFLD), whereas the acute spike represented concurrent muscle breakdown. Thus, an AST > ALT combined with significant hyper-CKemia indicates that the transaminase elevation is largely artifactual and driven by muscle injury.
