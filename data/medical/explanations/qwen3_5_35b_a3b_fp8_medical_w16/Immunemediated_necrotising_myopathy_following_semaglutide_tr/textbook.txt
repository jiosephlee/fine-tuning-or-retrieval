Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Introduction to GLP-1 Agonists and Emerging Myopathic Risks

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, including semaglutide and tirzepatide, have revolutionized the management of type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity by mimicking the endogenous incretin hormone. By binding to GLP-1 receptors in the pancreas, gut, and brain, these agents enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppress glucagon release, delay gastric emptying, and promote satiety. Their efficacy in lowering glycated hemoglobin and facilitating weight loss has led to their widespread adoption in clinical practice. However, as prescription volumes surge, clinicians must remain vigilant regarding a spectrum of musculoskeletal adverse effects that extend beyond the expected gastrointestinal disturbances. While weight loss itself can lead to a reduction in lean body mass—a phenomenon sometimes termed "sarcopenic obesity" or simply age-appropriate muscle loss—recent literature has highlighted more severe neuromuscular complications that require distinct diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

The musculoskeletal safety profile of GLP-1 receptor agonists ranges from benign reductions in muscle volume to acute, life-threatening conditions such as rhabdomyolysis. Historically, the primary concern has been the disproportionate loss of skeletal muscle relative to fat mass during rapid weight reduction. Yet, emerging case reports describe instances of direct myotoxicity manifesting as severe rhabdomyolysis. In these scenarios, patients present with massive elevations in creatine kinase (CK), often exceeding tens of thousands of units per liter, frequently accompanied by acute kidney injury due to pigment nephropathy. For instance, a previously documented case involving tirzepatide demonstrated a classic drug-induced rhabdolytic picture characterized by an abrupt spike in CK levels shortly after initiation, followed by a rapid normalization of enzymes upon drug discontinuation and supportive hydration alone. This pattern suggests a direct toxic mechanism where the agent induces acute muscle fiber necrosis without an intervening autoimmune process.

However, distinguishing between direct toxicity and immune-mediated etiologies is critical when evaluating patients with persistent weakness or atypical enzyme kinetics. Unlike direct toxicity, which typically resolves quickly once the offending agent is withdrawn, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) presents with progressive proximal muscle weakness that may worsen even after stopping the drug unless immunosuppression is administered. IMNM is characterized pathologically by extensive muscle fiber necrosis with minimal inflammatory infiltrate and is often associated with specific autoantibodies targeting hydroxymethylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR) or signal recognition particle (SRP). While statin use is a well-established trigger for HMGCR-associated IMNM—the so-called "statin-myopathy paradox"—the emergence of similar immune-mediated presentations following exposure to non-statin medications like GLP-1 agonists represents a novel clinical challenge. The distinction lies in the timeline and response to therapy: direct toxicity implies a reversible enzymatic leak that corrects with cessation of the drug, whereas IMNM involves an aberrant immune response against self-antigens triggered by molecular mimicry or epitope spreading necessitating corticosteroids or intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG).

The case presented herein illustrates this nuanced differentiation. While the patient's initial presentation included signs compatible with dehydration-induced rhabdomyolysis following her first dose of semaglutide, her subsequent clinical trajectory diverged sharply from typical drug toxicity. Over four months post-exposure, she developed profound proximal weakness and dysphagia with a twelve-fold increase in CK levels that failed to resolve spontaneously upon drug cessation alone until immunosuppressive therapy was introduced. Muscle biopsy confirmed necrotizing myopathy with membrane attack complex deposition but lacked the prominent lymphocytic infiltration seen in polymyositis or dermatomyositis. Although she tested negative for canonical myositis-specific antibodies (such as anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP), rendering her seronegative for known markers, the histological findings combined with her dramatic response to steroids and IVIG support a diagnosis of drug-associated IMNM rather than simple toxic myopathy or sarcopenia. This case underscores the necessity for clinicians to recognize that while GLP-1 agonists are generally safe regarding muscle integrity, they may rarely precipitate severe immune-mediated myopathies requiring early detection and aggressive immunomodulation to prevent permanent disability.

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Reasoning for IMNM

The clinical trajectory of this case illustrates a critical distinction between acute drug toxicity and a delayed immune-mediated process. The patient's presentation began with an immediate prodromal phase characterized by severe gastrointestinal intolerance—nausea, vomiting, and subsequent dehydration—following the administration of a single dose of semaglutide. This acute event precipitated metabolic derangements requiring hospitalization, including acute renal failure and metabolic acidosis. Crucially, during this initial admission, the patient exhibited a modest elevation in creatine kinase (CK) to 337 U/L. While this finding might initially suggest early muscle injury or rhabdomyolysis, the magnitude was insufficient to explain the severity of her systemic illness, and importantly, it lacked associated myalgia or dark urine. In the context of GLP-1 receptor agonists, such transient CK elevations can occur due to dehydration or minor myocyte stress; however, the absence of rapid normalization upon rehydration and fluid resuscitation raises suspicion for a more insidious underlying pathology rather than simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.

The diagnostic reasoning shifts significantly as the patient enters the subacute progression phase four months later. Following an initial period of deconditioning attributed to her acute illness, she developed a relentless decline in motor function. The emergence of progressive proximal muscle weakness, severe enough to prevent standing or sitting upright without assistance, coupled with new-onset dysphagia and dysphonia (bulbar signs), signals a specific pattern of neuromuscular involvement. This timeline is pivotal for differential diagnosis: true drug-induced rhabdomyolysis typically presents with acute onset and rapid resolution upon drug cessation within days to weeks. Conversely, the 12-fold increase in CK (peaking at 3992 U/L) over a four-month interval, combined with worsening strength despite discontinuation of semaglutide, strongly argues against direct toxicity and points toward an evolving autoimmune etiology. The presence of bulbar symptoms further narrows the differential to conditions affecting limb-girdle muscles and pharyngeal musculature, such as polymyositis or immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Interpreting the laboratory data requires careful differentiation between primary muscle pathology and concurrent comorbidities. The patient presented with elevated AST (191 U/L) and ALT (165 U/L); however, distinguishing hepatic origin from myogenic origin is essential given her history of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Since both AST and ALT are released from damaged muscle fibers alongside CK, their elevation could reflect severe myonecrosis rather than active hepatitis. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that these enzymes normalized only after successful treatment of the myopathy and stabilization of CK levels, whereas her underlying steatosis persisted independently on biopsy. Furthermore, while cardiac biomarkers showed mild troponin elevation and significantly elevated BNP suggesting myocardial strain or early myocarditis, cardiac MRI findings were nonspecific regarding scarring or infarction. In IMNM cases involving bulbar weakness and respiratory compromise (as evidenced here by prior intubation needs), cardiac involvement must be considered; however, the rapid reversal of these cardiac parameters alongside neurological recovery supports them as secondary manifestations of severe systemic inflammation rather than primary cardiomyopathy driving the clinical picture.

The final layer of diagnostic complexity lies in establishing IMNM in a seronegative patient with atypical autoantibody profiles. Classic IMNM is frequently associated with anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP antibodies; however, approximately one-third of cases remain seronegative for known myositis-specific antibodies. This patient tested negative for HMGCR, SRP, Jo-1, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-$\gamma$, NXP-2, cN-1A, and standard connective tissue disease markers like ANA and anti-Ro/SSA (though weakly positive for SS-B). In isolation, these results could obscure the diagnosis or lead to misclassification as "idiopathic" polymyositis or undifferentiated connective tissue disease-associated myopathy. Yet, the diagnostic certainty relies heavily on histopathological evidence rather than serology alone. The thigh muscle biopsy revealed scattered necrotic fibers with prominent macrophage infiltration (CD68+), minimal lymphocytic infiltrate (few CD3+ cells), and deposition of membrane attack complexes on sarcolemmas—a triad highly specific for necrotizing rather than inflammatory myopathies like polymyositis. Thus, despite the lack of canonical autoantibodies confirming an autoimmune trigger like statins or SLE in this instance, the convergence of temporal proximity to semaglutide exposure atypical progression patterns from simple toxicity versus chronic idiopathic disease makes a strong case for drug-induced IMNM pending further pharmacovigilance data.

## Pathophysiology and Histopathological Correlation

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a distinct pathophysiological entity within the spectrum of inflammatory myopathies, characterized fundamentally by primary muscle fiber necrosis driven by humoral immunity rather than T-cell mediated cytotoxicity. Unlike polymyositis or dermatomyositis, where lymphocytic invasion of non-necrotic muscle fibers is the dominant feature, IMNM is defined by a "paucity" of inflammatory cells relative to the extent of muscle damage. The core mechanism involves circulating autoantibodies targeting sarcolemmal proteins, such as hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR) or signal recognition particle (SRP), which trigger complement activation and subsequent membrane attack complex (MAC) formation. This cascade leads to direct lysis of the myofiber membrane and extensive necrosis. In the case presented, the patient's clinical trajectory—a rapid decline in strength and a twelve-fold rise in creatine kinase (CK) following semaglutide exposure—aligns with this aggressive necrotizing process, distinguishing it from milder toxic myopathies where inflammation typically dominates or from simple rhabdomyolysis where CK normalization occurs rapidly upon drug cessation without immunomodulation.

The histopathological analysis of this patient's thigh muscle biopsy provides critical evidence supporting the diagnosis of IMNM and elucidating its unique cellular environment. Microscopic examination revealed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers, a hallmark of active myocyte injury and attempted repair. Crucially, immunohistochemical staining highlighted the presence of CD68-positive macrophages actively engaging in myophagocytosis—the engulfment of dead muscle debris—which serves as a proxy for the clearance phase of necrosis. Furthermore, the demonstration of MAC deposition on both necrotic and viable-appearing myofibers confirms that complement-mediated cytotoxicity is driving the tissue destruction, even in areas lacking overt signs of cell death. This finding contrasts sharply with toxic myopathy induced directly by metabolic insults or drugs like statins (prior to autoantibody development), which typically shows diffuse degeneration without significant complement deposition or macrophage-rich phagocytosis unless an autoimmune component has emerged. Additionally, while rare T-cells (CD3+) were noted, their scarcity reinforces that this is not primarily a T-cell mediated disorder like classic polymyositis, but rather an antibody-complement driven process.

Serological profiling in this case underscores the complexity of diagnosing IMNM when traditional markers are absent. Approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative for known autoantibodies like anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. In populations frequently exposed to statins, anti-HMGCR positivity is common; however, this patient explicitly denied prior statin use, making an HMGCR-driven etiology less likely based on historical precedent alone. Despite testing negative for HMGCR, SRP, and other specific myositis panels (including Jo-1 and MDA-5), the patient exhibited low-titer SS-B positivity alongside negative ANA and SSA. While isolated SS-B positivity lacks established association with IMNM in current literature, it highlights a potential gap in our understanding of triggers in seronegative cases or suggests an unidentified autoantigen precipitated by semaglutide. The absence of these canonical antibodies does not rule out IMNM; rather, it necessitates reliance on histopathological confirmation—specifically the triad of necrosis without significant endomysial inflammation—and clinical response to immunotherapy to establish the diagnosis.

Finally, evaluating concurrent organ involvement offers insight into systemic immune dysregulation versus secondary physiological stressors observed in this patient. Imaging revealed myocardial edema on cardiac MRI with mild hypokinesis but no late gadolinium enhancement indicative of fibrosis or infarction. While this could represent early myocarditis paralleling the skeletal muscle pathology—a known complication in severe autoimmune myopathies—the transient nature of these findings and their complete resolution post-treatment suggest they may alternatively reflect stress-induced cardiomyopathy triggered by severe metabolic derangement (acidosis) and systemic inflammation during her initial hospitalization for renal failure and vomiting. The distinction is vital: true autoimmune myocarditis would imply a broader requirement for aggressive long-term immunosuppression across multiple organ systems, whereas stress cardiomyopathy resolves with supportive care once the systemic insult subsides. In this case, the normalization of cardiac parameters alongside skeletal muscle recovery supports a self-limiting inflammatory response rather than chronic multi-system autoimmune disease progression.

## Therapeutic Strategy: Immunomodulation in Drug-Induced Myopathy

The management of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) necessitates a prompt and robust approach to halt muscle destruction and prevent permanent functional impairment, particularly when the etiology is suspected to be drug-induced. In this case, the decision to initiate high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone was driven by the severity of the patient's presentation, characterized by profound proximal weakness preventing ambulation and bulbar symptoms causing dysphagia. Pulse steroid therapy serves as the cornerstone of acute induction, providing rapid suppression of the inflammatory cascade responsible for myofiber necrosis. Following the five-day pulse course, transitioning to a standard maintenance dose of 1 mg/kg/day allows for a controlled taper once clinical stabilization occurs. This strategy mirrors the standard of care for idiopathic IMNM; however, in drug-induced scenarios, the prognosis regarding treatment duration differs significantly. Because the inciting antigen (semaglutide) was identified and permanently withdrawn early in the course, the inflammatory drive is removed alongside pharmacologic intervention. Consequently, unlike idiopathic cases where lifelong or multi-year immunosuppression is often required to maintain remission against endogenous autoimmunity, drug-induced IMNM may resolve with a shorter course of therapy once the trigger is eliminated.

Given the patient's severe disability and persistent weakness despite initial steroid administration, adjunctive therapy with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was employed. While corticosteroids alone are effective for many patients with IMNM, IVIG acts through diverse mechanisms—including neutralization of circulating autoantibodies, modulation of Fc receptors on macrophages, and inhibition of complement activation—to provide rapid immunomodulation in refractory or life-threatening presentations. The combination of steroids and IVIG is particularly indicated here due to the aggressive nature of necrotizing myopathy and the risk of respiratory compromise from bulbar involvement. Although some literature suggests IVIG may be reserved for steroid-refractory cases or specific antibody subsets like anti-SRP or anti-HMGCR positivity (which were absent in this patient), its utility in severe phenotypes regardless of serostatus supports its inclusion when rapid functional recovery is critical. The decision to administer a total dose of 2 g/kg over four days reflects evidence-based protocols aimed at achieving swift clinical improvement without unnecessary prolonged exposure to high-cost therapies once a clear trajectory toward recovery is established.

Monitoring therapeutic response relies heavily on serial creatine kinase (CK) measurements coupled with rigorous functional assessment scales. In this case, a marked reduction in CK levels served as an objective biomarker confirming that immunosuppression was effectively arresting muscle breakdown; however, biochemical normalization often lags behind clinical improvement. Functional metrics—such as the ability to stand from a seated position independently or swallow without aspiration—are superior indicators of true recovery and guide dosing adjustments. For instance, this patient demonstrated significant strength gains within two weeks, correlating with falling CK levels but preceding complete biochemical normalization. Clinicians must distinguish between residual inflammation requiring escalation versus physiological recovery allowing for de-escalation. The absence of new symptoms or rising CK trends during outpatient follow-up provided confidence that the inflammatory process had subsided sufficiently to consider reducing immunosuppressive burden.

Weaning strategies in drug-associated IMNM differ fundamentally from idiopathic counterparts due to the removal of the exogenous trigger. Once CK levels normalize and functional status returns to baseline following cessation of the offending agent, immunosuppression can typically be tapered rapidly over weeks rather than months or years. In this case study, prednisone was successfully weaned over two months without relapse, highlighting that prolonged maintenance therapy may be unnecessary if strict avoidance of the culprit drug is maintained alongside adequate initial induction treatment. The patient remained off all immunosuppressants for eight months post-treatment without recurrence, reinforcing that drug withdrawal acts synergistically with short-course immunotherapy to induce durable remission. This contrasts sharply with idiopathic IMNM or statin-induced autoimmune myopathy where chronic low-dose maintenance is frequently mandated even after statin cessation due to persistent autoreactivity. Ultimately, recognizing "drug-induced" status allows clinicians to tailor a finite treatment plan focused on bridging recovery rather than managing chronic autoimmunity indefinitely.
