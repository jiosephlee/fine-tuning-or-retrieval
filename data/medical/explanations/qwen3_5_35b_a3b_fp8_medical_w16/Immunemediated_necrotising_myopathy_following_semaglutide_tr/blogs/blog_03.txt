Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# One Dose Away: Unraveling the Temporal Relationship Between Semaglutide and Autoimmunity

We are accustomed to thinking of drug-induced myopathies as a story of cumulative toxicity. Whether it's the slow burn of statin-associated HMGCR myopathy or the acute crash of rhabdomyolysis from a new prescription, there is usually a clear trajectory: start the drug, wait for exposure to accumulate, see the damage. But what happens when a single dose acts as the spark for an inferno that burns down muscle architecture months later?

This week, let's dissect a fascinating case report from *BMJ Case Reports* that challenges our temporal assumptions regarding GLP-1 receptor agonists. A woman in her 60s received **one** dose of semaglutide for weight management and subsequent liver disease management. Four months later, she presented with profound proximal weakness, dysphagia, and a CK level nearly 20 times the upper limit of normal. The result? A confirmed diagnosis of Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM).

While GLP-1s are increasingly linked to muscle loss and even rare cases of rhabdomyolysis (often requiring days to resolve upon cessation), this case stands out because the timeline doesn't fit "toxic" injury. Toxic rhabdomyolysis typically resolves quickly once the toxin is gone. This patient's CK didn't just spike; it climbed progressively over four months after she stopped taking the drug entirely. The question we must ask ourselves in the ER and clinic is: Can a single antigenic exposure trigger a delayed, autoimmune cascade in a susceptible host?

### The Clinical Picture: From GI Distress to Paralysis

The patient's journey began with classic semaglutide intolerance—nausea and vomiting immediately following that first 0.25 mg subcutaneous injection. She required hospitalization for dehydration and acute kidney injury, where her CK was mildly elevated at 337 U/L. At that moment, it looked like simple volume depletion or perhaps minor muscle strain from vomiting.

However, the narrative took a dark turn over the next four months. As she recovered from her initial illness, she began to deteriorate neurologically and musculoskeletally. She progressed from being fully independent to being unable to sit upright without assistance. By month four, she could not swallow liquids or solids due to bulbar involvement (dysphagia/dysphonia). When she finally reached tertiary care, her CK had skyrocketed to nearly 4,000 U/L.

What made this case diagnostically thrilling was the biopsy results. The muscle pathology showed necrotic fibers with macrophage infiltration (myophagocytosis) but notably lacked the dense lymphocytic infiltrate typical of polymyositis or dermatomyositis. Instead, it screamed **necrotizing**. Crucially, her extensive autoantibody panel was negative—notably lacking both anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies—which accounts for roughly one-third of IMNM cases ("seronegative IMNM"). Yet, she responded dramatically to immunosuppression (pulse steroids + IVIG) and returned to baseline function after stopping semaglutide indefinitely.

### Applying Causality: Is It Just Coincidence?

How do we link a drug taken four months ago to an autoimmune flare today? We rely on established frameworks like the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality assessment tools. In this case, three pillars support a "probable" link:

1.  **Temporal Relationship:** While long latency periods exist in autoimmunity (think SLE flares), there is no plausible alternative explanation for why her condition worsened specifically after this exposure while remaining stable otherwise for years prior.
2.  **De-challenge:** The patient improved significantly only after withdrawing semaglutide *and* starting immunotherapy. If this were idiopathic IMNM driven solely by genetics or environment unrelated to drugs, we might expect a slower or less complete response without strict avoidance of potential triggers.
3.  **Biological Plausibility:** We know GLP-1 receptors are expressed on various tissues, including skeletal muscle and potentially immune cells via cross-reactivity mechanisms like molecular mimicry or epitope spreading. While speculative here until proven otherwise in larger cohorts, it fits the emerging pattern where metabolic drugs act as environmental triggers for latent autoimmune processes in genetically primed individuals (similar to how statins trigger HMGCR autoimmunity).

It is worth noting that unlike tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis cases where CK normalizes within days of stopping the drug, this patient's pathology evolved slowly over months—a hallmark of an evolving immune response rather than direct cellular toxicity. Furthermore, her cardiac MRI showed transient myocardial edema which resolved completely alongside her muscle recovery, suggesting systemic inflammation rather than primary cardiac ischemia or stress cardiomyopathy alone.

### Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

This case serves as a critical reminder that not all drug-induced myopathies look alike on their timeline or pathophysiology spectrum. When evaluating patients on newer metabolic agents presenting with weakness and elevated enzymes:

*   **Don't ignore "delayed" presentations:** A negative CK at discharge does not rule out impending autoimmune myopathy if symptoms progress weeks later after drug cessation.
*   **Think Seronegative IMNM early:** Up to one-third of necrotizing myopathies lack standard autoantibodies (HMGCR/SRP). Do not delay empiric treatment based on negative panels if clinical suspicion is high; biopsy confirmation is key before committing to long-term immunosuppression blindly.
*   **Differentiate Toxic vs. Autoimmune:** Direct toxic myopathy (like rhabdo) resolves rapidly upon withdrawal; autoimmune myopathy often progresses independently or requires targeted immunotherapy even after stopping the trigger agent due to epitope spreading continuing unchecked by endogenous clearance mechanisms alone.
*   **Watch for Bulbar Signs:** Dysphagia and dysphonia in a patient with rising CK should immediately trigger concern for severe neuromuscular compromise regardless of etiology; airway protection takes precedence over diagnostic certainty in these scenarios.
*   **Pharmacovigilance matters:** With millions prescribed GLP-1s globally, recognizing patterns like this single-case signal allows us to refine safety profiles before they become widespread public health issues reported in major registries later on years down the line."
