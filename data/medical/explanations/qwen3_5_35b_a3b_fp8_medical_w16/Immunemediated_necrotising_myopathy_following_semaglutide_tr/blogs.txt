Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Beyond the GI Tract: When GLP-1 Agonists Trigger Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy

We've all seen the headlines: GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are revolutionizing weight management and diabetes care, but they come with a familiar script of nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. Recently, a new "script" has emerged regarding skeletal muscle loss—specifically rhabdomyolysis. We know that direct toxic injury to muscle fibers can occur, often presenting as an acute spike in creatine kinase (CK) that resolves rapidly once the offending agent is stopped. But what happens when the story doesn't end with drug cessation? What if the muscle damage continues to worsen *after* the drug is gone?

This is exactly what happened in a fascinating new case report of a woman in her 60s who developed **immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)** following a single dose of semaglutide. This isn't just another case of drug-induced rhabdo; it's a classic example of how a trigger can ignite a runaway autoimmune fire that requires immunosuppression to extinguish. If you think your patient's weakness is just "deconditioning" or simple toxin exposure after starting Ozempic or Wegovy, think again. The timeline here tells a very different story.

### The Case That Defied the Timeline

Our patient had a history of obesity and NAFLD but was otherwise healthy until she took her first dose of semaglutide 0.25 mg subcutaneously. The reaction was immediate: severe nausea, vomiting, and dehydration leading to acute kidney injury requiring ICU-level care for non-invasive ventilation and bicarbonate administration. Crucially, at this acute phase, her CK was only mildly elevated at 337 U/L—nowhere near the tens of thousands seen in frank rhabdomyolysis—and she had no overt muscle pain or dark urine. She recovered from the GI storm and was discharged deconditioned but stable.

Here is where the plot thickens: **four months later**, while completely off semaglutide, she began to deteriorate rapidly. She progressed from general fatigue to profound proximal weakness, unable to stand or sit upright without assistance. She developed dysphagia and dysphonia (difficulty speaking/swallowing). By the time she hit the emergency department four months post-exposure, her CK had skyrocketed to nearly 4,000 U/L—a twelve-fold increase from her baseline and far exceeding the initial value during the acute GI event.

If this were direct toxic rhabdomyolysis (like we see with some statins or other toxins), we would expect CK levels to normalize within days or weeks of stopping the drug. Instead, this patient's condition *progressively worsened* long after discontinuation. This delayed, relentless decline is the hallmark clue pointing away from direct toxicity and toward an immune-mediated process.

### Why This Isn't Just Rhabdomyolysis

The distinction between drug-induced rhabdomyolysis and IMNM is critical because their treatments could not be more different. In direct toxicity, removal of the agent + supportive care (hydration) usually leads to recovery. In IMNM, removing the trigger does nothing if you don't turn off the immune system attacking your own muscle fibers.

Biopsy results confirmed our suspicion: dispersed necrotic fibers with macrophage infiltration (myophagocytosis) and membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers. This histology screams **necrotizing myopathy**. Interestingly, despite this clear pathology, her autoimmune panel was largely negative for classic markers like anti-HMGCR (statin-associated) or anti-SRP (classic IMNM). Approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative initially, meaning we cannot rely solely on blood tests; we must trust the clinical picture and biopsy when faced with progressive weakness and rising CKs without an obvious alternative cause like polymyositis or dermatomyositis (which typically presents with skin changes).

Furthermore, while she had incidental mild elevations in liver enzymes consistent with her known fatty liver disease (notably AST/ALT can leak from muscle too), her liver biopsy confirmed NAFLD progression unrelated to muscle breakdown once CK normalized later on. Even cardiac involvement was noted on MRI as possible early myocarditis or stress cardiomyopathy (elevated troponin/proBNP), which fortunately resolved alongside her systemic recovery.

### Treatment Paradigm Shift: From Hydration to Immunotherapy

The management of this case highlights why recognizing IMNM matters so much in primary care and emergency settings involving GLP-1 users. Our patient required aggressive intervention once diagnosed: pulse-dose IV methylprednisolone followed by high-dose oral prednisone AND intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). 

Without IVIG and steroids alone relying on stopping semaglutide would likely have resulted in permanent disability given how aggressively her strength declined over those four months. Fortunately, she responded beautifully to immunosuppression. Over two months on prednisone and a single course of IVIG, her CK dropped significantly, her swallowing improved, and she regained full independence within ten months of symptom onset—all while remaining off immunosuppression for eight months prior to follow-up visit checks showing sustained remission.

This case serves as a stark reminder that "off-label" reactions aren't always immediate allergic responses; sometimes they are latent autoimmune phenomena triggered by molecular mimicry or epitope spreading initiated by a single exposure event weeks or months prior. The fact that she only received *one* dose makes it even more provocative—is there something about genetic susceptibility in this specific patient population that turns a standard metabolic injection into an autoimmune trigger? We don't know yet, but vigilance is key.

### Key Takeaways for Clinicians

While GLP-1 agonists remain safe for millions globally, this case underscores three vital lessons for managing patients presenting with unexplained weakness after starting these agents:

*   **Don't dismiss delayed weakness:** If a patient starts semaglutide/tirzepatide/other GLP-1s and develops progressive proximal weakness *weeks to months later*, especially if CK rises dramatically despite drug cessation, suspect IMNM rather than simple deconditioning or transient rhabdomyolysis.
*   **Timeline dictates mechanism:** Direct toxic rhabdomyolysis peaks acutely (hours/days) and resolves quickly upon withdrawal; Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy often has a lag period where symptoms worsen progressively even after stopping the drug due to ongoing autoimmunity.
*   **Early immunosuppression saves function:** Do not wait for seroconversion on autoantibody panels if clinical suspicion is high; proceed promptly with MRI confirmation if needed and initiate steroids/IVIG immediately upon biopsy confirmation to prevent irreversible disability in cases resembling steroid-responsive myopathies like IMNM where antibodies may be absent initially ("seronegative").



# The Seronegative Trap: Diagnosing IMNM Without Classic Autoantibodies

We've all seen the "classic" textbook presentation of Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM): a middle-aged patient presents with profound proximal weakness, sky-high CK levels in the tens of thousands, and a myositis panel screaming "Anti-HMGCR" or "Anti-SRP." But what happens when the lab results come back blank? What do you do when your clinical suspicion is at 100%, but the serology says zero?

This isn't just a theoretical puzzle; it's a real-world trap that recently caught a clinician in a fascinating new case report involving **semaglutide**. A woman in her 60s presented with progressive dysphagia, dysphonia, and an inability to stand. Her Creatine Kinase (CK) skyrocketed from a modest 337 U/L initially to nearly 4,000 U/L four months later. Yet, her extensive myositis panel was entirely negative for SRP, HMGCR, and every other known myositis-specific antibody. Even her ANA was negative.

If you stop here based on the labs alone, you miss the diagnosis. This case highlights a critical reality: **approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative.** When the antibodies aren't there to hold your hand, you must rely heavily on the muscle biopsy and the clinical narrative—especially when a new drug exposure enters the picture.

## The Biopsy Speaks Louder Than the Panel

In this specific case, the turning point wasn't another blood draw; it was the muscle biopsy of the vastus lateralis. While standard polymyositis shows heavy lymphocytic infiltration around endomysial vessels and fibers, IMNM tells a different story: **necrosis with relative sparing of inflammation.**

The pathology report described dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers. Crucially, immunohistochemistry revealed:
*   **Macrophage dominance:** Strong CD68 staining indicating active phagocytosis of necrotic debris (myophagocytosis).
*   **Complement activation:** Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) deposition on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers.
*   **Paucity of T-cells:** Only rare CD3+ cells were noted.

This histologic pattern—widespread necrosis driven by complement-mediated injury rather than cell-mediated cytotoxicity—is the hallmark of IMNM, even in seronegative patients. The presence of MAC is particularly telling; it suggests an antibody-independent mechanism or an unknown antibody targeting sarcolemmal components that weren't captured in standard panels. If you see this pattern in a patient with high CK and proximal weakness, treat it as IMNM until proven otherwise, regardless of the serology screen result.

## The Semaglutide Connection: Drug-Induced vs. Idiopathic

What makes this case truly unique—and why it matters for us right now—is the timing relative to semaglutide administration. The patient received a *single* dose three years ago (well, actually 2.5 years into her NAFLD workup timeline) and had an immediate GI reaction requiring ICU admission for dehydration and AKI. Four months later, she developed frank myopathy with CK >3000 U/L.

While GLP-1 agonists are known to cause weight loss via muscle wasting or even direct rhabdomyolysis (as seen with tirzepatide), this case diverges sharply from simple toxicity:
1.  **Time Course:** Toxic rhabdomyolysis typically resolves quickly upon drug cessation (days to weeks). This patient's CK rose progressively over four months *after* stopping the drug entirely.
2.  **Response to Therapy:** Simple toxin clearance doesn't usually require aggressive immunosuppression. This patient required pulse steroids and IVIG but responded dramatically within two weeks of starting them and normalized completely after avoiding semaglutide again for several months off meds.
3.  **Histology:** The biopsy confirmed an immune-mediated process (MAC deposition), not just ischemic or toxic necrosis from hypoperfusion during her initial dehydration event.

The authors classify this as "probable" drug-induced IMNM based on WHO-UMC criteria: reasonable time relationship, exclusion of other causes (she had no statin exposure), and improvement upon withdrawal plus treatment. It serves as a wake-up call that GLP-1s might trigger autoimmune phenomena in susceptible individuals, mimicking idiopathic autoimmune conditions like HMGCR-myopathy but lacking the classic autoantibody signature initially thought necessary for diagnosis.

## Navigating the Diagnostic Black Box

So, how do we manage this in practice? When faced with high CK + Proximal Weakness + Negative Myositis Panel:

**Don't rule out IMNM.** Assume it exists until you have definitive proof against it via biopsy or clinical evolution over time that contradicts it (e.g., complete spontaneous resolution without immunotherapy). A negative panel does not rule out disease; it simply means we haven't identified *which* autoantibody is driving it yet (or if there is none).

**Prioritize Muscle Imaging and Biopsy.** Look for edema on MRI in proximal muscles (vastus lateralis/quadriceps are common targets). If available and safe, proceed to biopsy early to look for necrosis without significant lymphocytic infiltrate and check for MAC deposition on frozen sections if possible (though IHC works too). Do not wait for serial titers if they are already negative; time is muscle function here.

**Consider Medication Triggers Aggressively.** In this era of widespread GLP-1 usage for obesity and diabetes, we must broaden our differential beyond statins and hydroxychloroquine or methotrexate when looking at drug-induced myopathies. Ask specifically about recent introductions of novel agents before dismissing symptoms as deconditioning or age-related sarcopenia—even if those agents were stopped long ago but triggered an autoimmune cascade that took months to manifest clinically once primed by initial exposure events like severe dehydration or metabolic shifts.

## Key Takeaways

*   **Seronegativity is Common:** Up to 30% of IMNM patients lack detectable anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP antibodies; do not let negative serology delay treatment if clinical suspicion is high.
*   **Biopsy is King:** Look for diffuse fiber necrosis with macrophage infiltration (CD68+) and Complement/MAC deposition on membranes; minimal lymphocytic infiltrate helps distinguish from polymyositis/dermatomyositis overlap syndromes in seronegative cases.
*   **Drug Induction Can Be Delayed:** Drug-induced IMNM may present months after initial exposure with progressive weakness rising over time rather than acute rhabdomyolysis resolving immediately upon cessation; consider GLP-1 RAs as potential triggers alongside statins/bisphosphonates/tirzepatide in appropriate contexts.
*   **Therapeutic Trial Matters:** Patients with suspected drug-induced IMNM often respond rapidly to short courses of IVIG/corticosteroids combined with strict avoidance of the offending agent, distinguishing them from chronic idiopathic forms requiring long-term maintenance therapy.



# One Dose Away: Unraveling the Temporal Relationship Between Semaglutide and Autoimmunity

We are accustomed to thinking of drug-induced myopathies as a story of cumulative toxicity. Whether it's the slow burn of statin-associated HMGCR myopathy or the acute crash of rhabdomyolysis from a new prescription, there is usually a clear trajectory: start the drug, wait for exposure to accumulate, see the damage. But what happens when a single dose acts as the spark for an inferno that burns down muscle architecture months later?

This week, let's dissect a fascinating case report from *BMJ Case Reports* that challenges our temporal assumptions regarding GLP-1 receptor agonists. A woman in her 60s received **one** dose of semaglutide for weight management and subsequent liver disease management. Four months later, she presented with profound proximal weakness, dysphagia, and a CK level nearly 20 times the upper limit of normal. The result? A confirmed diagnosis of Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM).

While GLP-1s are increasingly linked to muscle loss and even rare cases of rhabdomyolysis (often requiring days to resolve upon cessation), this case stands out because the timeline doesn't fit "toxic" injury. Toxic rhabdomyolysis typically resolves quickly once the toxin is gone. This patient's CK didn't just spike; it climbed progressively over four months after she stopped taking the drug entirely. The question we must ask ourselves in the ER and clinic is: Can a single antigenic exposure trigger a delayed, autoimmune cascade in a susceptible host?

### The Clinical Picture: From GI Distress to Paralysis

The patient's journey began with classic semaglutide intolerance—nausea and vomiting immediately following that first 0.25 mg subcutaneous injection. She required hospitalization for dehydration and acute kidney injury, where her CK was mildly elevated at 337 U/L. At that moment, it looked like simple volume depletion or perhaps minor muscle strain from vomiting.

However, the narrative took a dark turn over the next four months. As she recovered from her initial illness, she began to deteriorate neurologically and musculoskeletally. She progressed from being fully independent to being unable to sit upright without assistance. By month four, she could not swallow liquids or solids due to bulbar involvement (dysphagia/dysphonia). When she finally reached tertiary care, her CK had skyrocketed to nearly 4,000 U/L.

What made this case diagnostically thrilling was the biopsy results. The muscle pathology showed necrotic fibers with macrophage infiltration (myophagocytosis) but notably lacked the dense lymphocytic infiltrate typical of polymyositis or dermatomyositis. Instead, it screamed **necrotizing**. Crucially, her extensive autoantibody panel was negative—notably lacking both anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies—which accounts for roughly one-third of IMNM cases ("seronegative IMNM"). Yet, she responded dramatically to immunosuppression (pulse steroids + IVIG) and returned to baseline function after stopping semaglutide indefinitely.

### Applying Causality: Is It Just Coincidence?

How do we link a drug taken four months ago to an autoimmune flare today? We rely on established frameworks like the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality assessment tools. In this case, three pillars support a "probable" link:

1.  **Temporal Relationship:** While long latency periods exist in autoimmunity (think SLE flares), there is no plausible alternative explanation for why her condition worsened specifically after this exposure while remaining stable otherwise for years prior.
2.  **De-challenge:** The patient improved significantly only after withdrawing semaglutide *and* starting immunotherapy. If this were idiopathic IMNM driven solely by genetics or environment unrelated to drugs, we might expect a slower or less complete response without strict avoidance of potential triggers.
3.  **Biological Plausibility:** We know GLP-1 receptors are expressed on various tissues, including skeletal muscle and potentially immune cells via cross-reactivity mechanisms like molecular mimicry or epitope spreading. While speculative here until proven otherwise in larger cohorts, it fits the emerging pattern where metabolic drugs act as environmental triggers for latent autoimmune processes in genetically primed individuals (similar to how statins trigger HMGCR autoimmunity).

It is worth noting that unlike tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis cases where CK normalizes within days of stopping the drug, this patient's pathology evolved slowly over months—a hallmark of an evolving immune response rather than direct cellular toxicity. Furthermore, her cardiac MRI showed transient myocardial edema which resolved completely alongside her muscle recovery, suggesting systemic inflammation rather than primary cardiac ischemia or stress cardiomyopathy alone.

### Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

This case serves as a critical reminder that not all drug-induced myopathies look alike on their timeline or pathophysiology spectrum. When evaluating patients on newer metabolic agents presenting with weakness and elevated enzymes:

*   **Don't ignore "delayed" presentations:** A negative CK at discharge does not rule out impending autoimmune myopathy if symptoms progress weeks later after drug cessation.
*   **Think Seronegative IMNM early:** Up to one-third of necrotizing myopathies lack standard autoantibodies (HMGCR/SRP). Do not delay empiric treatment based on negative panels if clinical suspicion is high; biopsy confirmation is key before committing to long-term immunosuppression blindly.
*   **Differentiate Toxic vs. Autoimmune:** Direct toxic myopathy (like rhabdo) resolves rapidly upon withdrawal; autoimmune myopathy often progresses independently or requires targeted immunotherapy even after stopping the trigger agent due to epitope spreading continuing unchecked by endogenous clearance mechanisms alone.
*   **Watch for Bulbar Signs:** Dysphagia and dysphonia in a patient with rising CK should immediately trigger concern for severe neuromuscular compromise regardless of etiology; airway protection takes precedence over diagnostic certainty in these scenarios.
*   **Pharmacovigilance matters:** With millions prescribed GLP-1s globally, recognizing patterns like this single-case signal allows us to refine safety profiles before they become widespread public health issues reported in major registries later on years down the line."



# Decoding Elevated Liver Enzymes and Troponin: Don't Miss the Muscle!

We've all been there: a patient presents with profound proximal weakness, and your differential immediately narrows to neuromuscular junction disorders, critical illness polyneuropathy, or perhaps a stroke mimic. But then you look at the labs. You see AST and ALT in the hundreds, a troponin that's "elevated," and maybe even some ECG changes. Your brain instantly starts running through the hepatology and cardiology algorithms. You might order an echo for heart failure or start looking for viral hepatitis markers.

But what if the liver isn't inflamed, the heart isn't failing, and the real pathology is hiding in plain sight within the skeletal muscle?

This case of a woman in her 60s presenting with progressive dysphagia, dysphonia, and inability to stand serves as a stark reminder that **muscle enzymes are not organ-specific**. When you encounter elevated transaminases or cardiac biomarkers alongside severe weakness, you must consider immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) before you commit to a primary hepatic or cardiac diagnosis. Specifically, this case highlights a fascinating (and terrifying) link between GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide) and IMNM—a condition that mimics rhabdomyolysis but behaves like an autoimmune storm requiring immunosuppression, not just hydration.

### The "Liver" Problem Was Actually Muscle

Let's talk about those transaminases first. Our patient had a background of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), so seeing elevated AST and ALT initially felt familiar. However, during her acute crisis with CK peaking near 4000 U/L, both AST (191 U/L) and ALT (165 U/L) were significantly elevated relative to their baseline.

Here is the classic pitfall: **AST is found in skeletal muscle.** In fact, in pure muscle injury without concurrent liver disease, AST often exceeds ALT (an inverted AST:ALT ratio > 1). While this patient had confirmed NAFLD on prior biopsies, we must ask: *How much of this elevation was truly hepatic?*

In this case, despite the biopsy showing progression of steatosis to grade 5/8 stage 1, the persistence of transaminitis correlated perfectly with her CK trajectory. If you treat this purely as liver disease or assume the enzymes are solely from her chronic NAFLD flare, you miss the systemic myopathy driving her clinical decline. The key takeaway is that when CK is sky-high (>3000 U/L), **do not trust standard LFT panels** without checking LDH and specifically asking yourself if your "liver enzymes" are actually leaking from necrotic myocytes. Always calculate the ratio; an AST > ALT in a weak patient should trigger a myositis workup regardless of known liver history.

### The Cardiac Confounder: Myocarditis or Muscle Contamination?

Next up: the troponin. This patient had an hs-troponin elevation (62 pg/mL) and markedly elevated BNP/proBNP (4358 pg/mL). On paper, this looks like acute myocardial injury or stress cardiomyopathy (Takotsubo), especially given her recent paroxysmal atrial fibrillation during her initial GI presentation. The cardiac MRI showed mild hypokinesis and edema in the septal/anterior walls—findings that could easily be interpreted as early myocarditis or focal ischemia.

However, remember that **troponin assays are not perfect**. While modern high-sensitivity assays are specific to cardiac isoforms (cTnI/cTnT), they can sometimes cross-react with skeletal muscle isoforms released during massive necrosis, particularly in conditions like rhabdomyolysis or severe IMNM where sarcolemmal integrity is globally lost across all striated muscle types. Furthermore, severe systemic inflammation can cause "demand mismatch" or stress-induced changes without true coronary occlusion.

In this case, the cardiac findings normalized completely once her muscle inflammation was controlled with steroids and IVIG. The "myocarditis" was likely either false-positive assay interference from skeletal muscle debris or transient stress-induced stunning secondary to severe metabolic derangement from the myopathy itself—not primary cardiac disease requiring anticoagulation or invasive catheterization. If you miss the underlying myopathy here, you risk unnecessary cardiac interventions while ignoring the life-threatening autoimmune process eating away at her respiratory muscles (hence the dysphagia/dysphonia).

### The Semaglutide Connection: A New Red Flag?

What ties this together? The timing. This patient received a single dose of semaglutide three years into her obesity management journey—and while she tolerated it poorly initially (nausea/vomiting leading to AKI), she didn't develop full-blown weakness until four months later. By then, she had progressed from deconditioning to being unable to sit upright without assistance.

While GLP-1 agonists are notorious for causing weight loss via appetite suppression (which includes lean mass loss), direct immune-mediated toxicity is emerging as a concern similar to statin-associated autoimmune myopathy (SAAM). Unlike simple drug-induced rhabdomyolysis—which typically resolves quickly upon cessation—the IMNM phenotype here required aggressive immunosuppression (pulse steroids + IVIG) to reverse because it became self-perpetuating via autoimmunity against hydroxyl-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase-like pathways or unknown antigens triggered by the drug exposure.

Interestingly, our patient was seronegative for both anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies (~30% of IMNM cases fall into this category). This underscores why relying solely on antibody panels can lead to missed diagnoses; clinical suspicion based on biopsy findings (necrosis with minimal lymphocytic infiltration) remains king when antibodies are absent but clinical picture fits perfectly post-drug exposure.

### Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **AST/ALT Ratio Matters:** In patients with unexplained weakness and elevated CK >2000 U/L, an AST > ALT ratio strongly suggests skeletal muscle origin rather than primary hepatic pathology—even if they have known fatty liver disease.
*   **Troponin Isn't Always Heart:** Severe necrotizing myopathies can cause spurious elevations in cardiac biomarkers due to cross-reactivity or release of skeletal muscle isoforms; always correlate imaging findings with clinical context before diagnosing myocarditis in isolation.
