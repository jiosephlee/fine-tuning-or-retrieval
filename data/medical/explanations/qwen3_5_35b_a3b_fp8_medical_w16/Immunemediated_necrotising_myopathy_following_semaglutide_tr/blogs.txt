# Semaglutide and the Silent Muscle Killer: A New Face of Drug-Induced Myopathy?

We've all seen the headlines: GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are revolutionizing weight management and diabetes care. But they come with a familiar script of GI side effects—nausea, vomiting, diarrhea—and the occasional warning about gallbladder issues or pancreatitis. Recently, we even saw reports of direct toxic rhabdomyolysis associated with this class, characterized by massive CK spikes that resolve quickly once the drug is stopped.

But what happens when the story doesn't fit that mold? What happens when a patient receives **one single dose** of a medication, experiences an acute intolerance reaction, and then slowly descends into profound proximal weakness and bulbar palsy over four months, with CK levels climbing 12-fold long after the drug is gone?

This isn't just "drug-induced toxicity" in the traditional sense; this looks suspiciously like an autoimmune storm triggered by a molecular mimicry event or an idiosyncratic immune activation. Today, let's dissect a fascinating new case report describing Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM) temporally linked to semaglutide—a condition that demands we rethink how we monitor patients starting these popular agents.

## The Clinical Trajectory: Acute Intolerance vs. Delayed Autoimmunity

Let's look at the timeline of this 60-year-old woman with obesity and NAFLD. Three years prior to her crisis, she was diagnosed with fatty liver and started on semaglutide 0.25 mg SC. Within hours of that **single dose**, she hit the ground running—or rather, falling down—with nausea, vomiting, acute kidney injury requiring ICU-level care for dehydration/metabolic acidosis, and paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Crucially, at that moment of acute illness, her Creatine Kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated at 337 U/L. If this were classic statin-associated rhabdomyolysis or direct toxin-induced muscle breakdown (like we saw in the tirzepatide case mentioned in the literature), we'd expect CK to spike massively immediately and then plummet once hydration occurred. Instead, she walked away feeling deconditioned but stable enough to go home.

Here is where it gets scary: Over the next **four months**, while completely off semaglutide, her condition deteriorated relentlessly. She went from deconditioned to unable to sit upright without assistance. She developed dysphagia (difficulty swallowing both solids and liquids) and dysphonia (hoarseness). When she finally presented to an outside hospital four months later, her CK had skyrocketed to nearly **4000 U/L**.

This delay is the smoking gun. Direct toxic myopathies usually burn out fast upon cessation of the offending agent. An immune-mediated process, however, can take weeks or months to ramp up as autoreactive T-cells proliferate and B-cells produce pathogenic antibodies (even if they aren't detected in standard panels initially). The fact that her symptoms progressed *away* from the exposure window strongly argues for an autoimmune etiology rather than simple pharmacologic toxicity.

## Diagnostic Dilemma: The Seronegative Patient

When I see a patient with proximal weakness and CK >3000 U/L in the Emergency Department or clinic floor, my differential immediately narrows to critical illness myopathy vs. primary inflammatory myopathies like Polymyositis or Dermatomyositis vs. IMNM.

In this specific case report, the authors highlight a crucial diagnostic challenge: **Seronegativity**.
*   Standard IMNM markers like anti-HMGCR (statin-associated) and anti-SRP were negative.
*   A full myositis panel including Jo-1, PL-7/PL-12, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-gamma was negative.
*   Even ANA was negative (though she had a low-titer isolated SS-B).

Does seronegativity rule out IMNM? Absolutely not. Approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative at presentation. In fact, relying solely on antibody testing can lead to dangerous delays in diagnosis if you don't trust your clinical suspicion and muscle biopsy findings here were definitive:
*   **Histology:** Dispersed necrotic fibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltration (the hallmark of necrotizing myopathy).
*   **Immunohistochemistry:** MAC deposition on sarcolemma (Membrane Attack Complex), indicating complement-mediated damage—the mechanism shared by HMGCR-positive cases even if antibodies aren't circulating detectable levels yet.
*   **MRI:** Symmetric edema in proximal quadriceps/vastus lateralis muscles without significant fatty replacement early on suggests active inflammation rather than chronic degeneration alone.

The presence of isolated SS-B positivity is noted as an oddity since it rarely causes IMNM alone without SSA/ANA co-expression here; it serves as a reminder that "red herrings" exist in autoimmunity workups too!

## Treatment Response: Why Steroids + IVIG Worked So Well Here

If you suspect IMNM based on this clinical picture—rapidly progressive weakness + bulbar involvement + massive CK elevation—you must treat aggressively early before irreversible muscle loss occurs (fibrosis sets in quickly). This patient received pulse-dose methylprednisolone followed by high-dose oral prednisone plus IVIG (2 g/kg total).

The result? A dramatic turnaround within two weeks with normalization of CK trends and functional recovery over three months. By ten months post-diagnosis (eight months off meds), she was back to baseline without needing maintenance immunosuppression beyond that initial burst treatment period combined with strict avoidance of semaglutide.

Why does this matter for us clinically? Because unlike idiopathic antisynthetase syndrome or dermatomyositis which often require years of slow tapering steroids/immunomodulators due to relapse risk, **drug-induced** variants sometimes resolve faster once the trigger is removed and inflammation is suppressed acutely. This distinction changes our prognostication counseling significantly for families asking "Will she ever walk normally again?"

## Key Takeaways & Pearls for Practice

While one case report does not establish causality definitively (re-challenge would be unethical), this narrative fits perfectly into emerging pharmacovigilance data suggesting GLP-1s might trigger immune-mediated phenomena beyond simple GI upset or rhabdo in susceptible individuals via mechanisms yet undefined but resembling statin-induced HMGCR disease patterns without actual statin exposure history here either! Always consider drug-induced etiologies even when classic biomarkers are absent especially when timing correlates oddly! Remember these points moving forward!

*   **Differentiate Toxicity from Autoimmunity:** Do not assume all GLP-1 related muscle injuries are simple rhabdomyolysis resolving in days; watch for delayed onset weakness progressing over weeks/months after drug cessation—this screams autoimmune myopathy until proven otherwise!
*   **Biopsy Early:** In patients presenting with unexplained proximal weakness + high CK (>10x ULN) + bulbar signs—even if serology is negative—a muscle biopsy showing necrosis/macrophages without significant inflammation confirms IMNM phenotype allowing earlier targeted therapy!
*   **Treat Aggressively First:** Initiate pulse steroids + IVIG promptly if clinical suspicion is high; delaying treatment risks permanent contractures/fibrosis making rehabilitation much harder later regardless of final diagnosis confirmation!
*   **Medication Review:** Always review recent medication changes including weight loss injections like semaglutide/tirzepatide/liraglutide etc., especially when GI symptoms precede neuromuscular decline suggesting possible molecular mimicry triggering latent autoimmunity!
*   **"Silent" Progression Warning Signs:** Patients may appear fine post-discharge after treating acute GI side effects/dehydration only to develop insidious weakness weeks later; maintain high index suspicion if any residual fatigue persists beyond usual recovery timelines from initial illness episodes!



# The Seronegative Dilemma: Diagnosing IMNM When Antibodies Are Missing

We've all been there: a patient presents with crushing proximal weakness, CK in the thousands, and a clinical picture screaming "myositis." You order the myositis panel, hit send, and wait for the results. Then comes the email: *Negative.* No HMGCR, no SRP, no Jo-1. Suddenly, your differential diagnosis balloons from a clear-cut autoimmune etiology into a chaotic mess of hypothyroidism, polymyalgia rheumatica, or just plain old deconditioning.

This week's case report flips the script on that frustrating moment. A woman in her 60s developed devastating proximal weakness and dysphagia four months after a **single** dose of semaglutide. Despite having no prior statin exposure and a completely negative extensive myositis antibody panel—including the notorious anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP—she didn't just get better; she recovered fully. How? Because she had Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM), even though she was technically "seronegative."

This isn't just about finding a new drug side effect; it's a masterclass in why we must never let a negative antibody test rule out IMNM. If you rely solely on serology, you miss half the diagnosis. Let's walk through how to diagnose IMNM when the labs say "nothing," guided by morphology and clinical context.

## When Serology Lies: The Reality of Seronegative IMNM

The dogma in rheumatology and neurology is often simple: if it looks like an autoimmune myopathy but tests negative for HMGCR and SRP, it might not be one. But this patient reminds us that roughly one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative. In fact, relying exclusively on antibody testing creates a dangerous blind spot.

In this case, the patient had zero history of statin use (the classic trigger for anti-HMGCR), making pre-test probability lower for that specific antibody anyway. Yet, she had all the hallmarks of aggressive necrotizing myopathy: rapid decline to wheelchair dependence within months, dysphagia/dysphonia indicating bulbar involvement, and CK elevations skyrocketing from ~337 U/L initially to nearly 4000 U/L later.

When faced with a "negative" myositis panel in the face of such dramatic clinical progression, you must pivot immediately to tissue diagnosis. The absence of antibodies does not equal the absence of disease; it simply means we haven't identified the specific autoantigen yet—or perhaps this is driven by mechanisms not captured by current commercial assays. As we saw here, assuming seronegativity rules out IMNM could have delayed life-saving immunosuppression significantly.

## Morphology Over Markers: What Biopsy Tells Us That Labs Don't

So, if we can't trust the bloodwork alone, what do we look for? The answer lies in the muscle biopsy architecture. The defining feature of IMNM is distinct from Polymyositis or Dermatomyositis: **necrosis with minimal inflammation.**

While Polymyositis shows endomysial lymphocytic invasion (T-cells) attacking healthy fibers from within, IMNM is characterized by widespread fiber necrosis and regeneration with surprisingly few inflammatory cells initially present—a phenomenon known as "paucity of lymphocytes." However, don't mistake "paucity" for "absence." This patient's biopsy told a nuanced story that confirmed the diagnosis despite negative serology:
*   **Necrosis & Regeneration:** Dispersed necrotic fibers mixed with basophilic regenerating fibers were evident on H&E staining.
*   **Macrophage Infiltration:** While T-cells were rare (CD3+), there was prominent macrophage presence (CD68+) engaging in phagocytosis (myophagocytosis). This is crucial; macrophages clean up dead muscle faster than they recruit other immune cells early on in IMNM.
*   **Membrane Attack Complex (MAC):** Staining revealed MAC deposition on both necrotizing and non-necrotizing fibers via complement activation (C5b-9). This confirms an immune-mediated mechanism even without detectable circulating antibodies targeting specific nuclear antigens like HMGCR or SRP.

If you see massive necrosis with relatively sparse T-cell infiltrates and positive MAC staining on immunohistochemistry, treat it as IMNM regardless of what your ELISA panel says. The histology trumps the serology every time when clinical suspicion is high enough to warrant the procedure.

## The Drug Trigger Trap: Semaglutide vs. Statins vs. Idiopathic Pathways

What made this case particularly fascinating was the temporal relationship with semaglutide versus our usual suspect: statins. We know statins can cause HMGCR-associated myopathy where stopping the drug doesn't stop the autoimmune fire until long-term immunosuppression kicks in—a "toxic" event turning into an autoimmune one via epitope spreading or molecular mimicry triggered by cell damage from statins releasing self-antigens like HMGCR into circulation.

Here was different. This patient took **one dose** of semaglutide three years after starting weight management efforts (though note: she actually had only taken one dose total before symptoms began). She developed severe GI toxicity acutely (nausea/vomiting requiring ICU), but her neuromuscular decline started creeping forward over four months post-exposure without further dosing.

Why did this happen? Unlike typical drug-induced rhabdomyolysis (like seen with tirzepatide) where CK spikes acutely and resolves rapidly upon cessation without immunotherapy needed here required steroids + IVIG), this evolved into full-blown necrotizing myopathy requiring aggressive suppression to reverse disability permanently once off-drug withdrawal wasn't enough on its own to halt progression quickly enough to prevent permanent damage or require chronic maintenance therapy usually seen in idiopathic cases). # Beyond the GI Complaints: Recognizing Systemic Red Flags in GLP-1 Users

We've all seen the "Semaglutide Face" – the weight loss success stories flooding social media. But as the prescribing volume of GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) explodes, so does our responsibility to recognize their less heralded, life-threatening complications. We know they cause nausea, vomiting, and rarely, pancreatitis or gallbladder issues. But what happens when a patient presents not just with a stomach ache, but with **dysphagia** and **proximal muscle weakness**?

This case report forces us to expand our differential diagnosis for anyone starting these agents. It describes a woman in her 60s whose journey began with classic GI intolerance—a single dose of semaglutide triggered severe nausea, vomiting, and acute kidney injury requiring ICU-level support. On paper, that sounds like typical drug intolerance. But here is the twist: four months later, she could barely stand or swallow liquids. Her Creatine Kinase (CK) skyrocketed from a modest 337 U/L during her initial hospitalization to nearly 4,000 U/L.

The punchline? This wasn't simple rhabdomyolysis from dehydration or direct toxin damage that resolves upon stopping the drug. This was **immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**. She tested negative for the classic autoantibodies (HMGCR and SRP), making it even harder to diagnose initially. # The Cardiac Connection: Incidental Myocarditis or Stress Cardiomyopathy in Severe Myopathy?

We've all seen the classic "red flags" for immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM): the crushing proximal weakness, the sky-high CK, and the terrifying speed of decline. But in this fascinating new case report from *BMJ Case Reports*, a different set of alarms went off—one that might distract us from the primary diagnosis or lead us down a rabbit hole of unnecessary cardiology interventions.

This isn't just another statin-induced myopathy case; it's arguably the first reported instance of IMNM temporally linked to **semaglutide**. But here is where it gets clinically spicy for the critical care and rheumatology crowd: this patient didn't just have muscle weakness. She had **concurrent cardiac involvement** manifesting as elevated high-sensitivity troponin, a massive spike in proBNP (>4000 pg/mL), and a cardiac MRI showing hypokinesis and edema.

So, when you see a patient with severe IMNM presenting with heart failure physiology, do you treat them for autoimmune myocarditis? Or is this simply stress cardiomyopathy masquerading as myocarditis? Let's break down how to navigate this diagnostic tightrope.

## The Cardiac Conundrum: Autoimmune vs. Physiological Stress

When this 60-year-old woman presented with her worsening weakness, she wasn't just failing her limbs; her heart was sounding the alarm too. Her initial workup revealed a high-sensitivity troponin of 62 pg/mL and a proBNP of 4358 pg/mL. An echocardiogram showed preserved LVEF (65-70%) but elevated RV pressures, while the cardiac MRI painted a picture of "mild hypokinesis" in the septum/anterior wall with myocardial edema but *no* late gadolinium enhancement (LGE).

This creates a classic diagnostic dilemma common in inflammatory myopathies: **Is this true myocardial inflammation (myocarditis) part of the autoimmune spectrum, or is it "stress cardiomyopathy" driven by systemic physiological collapse?**

In idiopathic inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis or polymyositis, subclinical myocardial involvement is relatively common. If this were pure autoimmune myocarditis co-occurring with IMNM, we would typically expect to see patchy LGE on cardiac MRI indicating fibrosis or scarring—a hallmark of immune-mediated injury. The absence of LGE here is a crucial pivot point. Instead, we saw diffuse edema and regional wall motion abnormalities that resolved completely upon recovery from the systemic crisis.

The authors lean heavily toward **stress-induced cardiomyopathy** (or Takotsubo-like phenotype) triggered by the severe systemic insult of acute renal failure, dehydration, and metabolic derangement from her initial semaglutide reaction, compounded by the profound catabolic state of developing IMNM. The rapid normalization of both cardiac structure and function after treating the myopathy supports this view. The heart didn't heal itself via immunomodulation alone; it recovered once the systemic storm subsided.

## Why Distinguishing Matters for Management

Why does pedantic differentiation matter in a busy ED or ICU? Because your treatment trajectory hinges on it.

If you diagnose **autoimmune myocarditis**, your instinct might be to escalate immunosuppression aggressively—perhaps adding rituximab or cyclophosphamide earlier—and monitor for conduction defects or arrhythmias as signs of evolving myocardial destruction. You might worry about long-term fibrosis requiring chronic management.

However, if you recognize this as **physiological stress cardiomyopathy** secondary to severe myopathy/sepsis/dehydration, your approach shifts dramatically:
1.  **Supportive Care:** Focus on hemodynamic stability and avoiding catecholamine surges if possible (though she already had AFib).
2.  **Targeted Therapy:** Treat the root cause—the IMNM—with steroids and IVIG immediately, as they did here.
3.  **Prognosis:** Reassure yourself that unlike autoimmune myocarditis which can leave permanent scars, stress-induced dysfunction usually resolves fully once the precipitating event (the cytokine storm/muscle breakdown) is controlled. Indeed, this patient's cardiac parameters normalized entirely alongside her CK levels after just two months off immunosuppressants.

Furthermore, distinguishing these helps avoid misdiagnosing underlying ischemic events or coronary vasculitis when none exist. The negative LGE essentially rules out significant scar tissue formation at that time point, reinforcing that this was an acute reversible process rather than a destructive autoimmune attack on the myocardium specifically.

## Lessons from Semaglutide-Associated IMNM

While we debated hearts, let's not forget the elephant in the room: **Semaglutide.** This case highlights a potential new frontier in adverse event reporting for GLP-1 agonists. While muscle wasting is known with weight loss agents causing sarcopenia ("lean mass loss"), frank necrotizing myopathy requiring IVIG is rare and likely idiosyncratic immune-mediated rather than direct toxicity (which usually presents as rapid-onset rhabdo resolving quickly upon cessation).

Key distinctions from standard drug-induced rhabdomyolysis:
*   **Timeline:** True drug toxicity usually improves within days of stopping the agent (as seen in tirzepatide cases). This patient worsened over *four months*.
*   **Histology:** Biopsy showed necrotizing fibers with MAC deposition but minimal lymphocytes—classic IMNM architecture—not just simple degeneration seen in toxic rhabdo.
*   **Response:** Improvement required immunosuppression + drug cessation; stopping semaglutide alone wouldn't have fixed it given the delayed onset post-exposure window implied by some literature on trigger mechanisms (similar to statin-HMGCR associations where antibodies persist even after drug washout).
