Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Management] How does the natural history and progression of beta-cell dysfunction in HNF1A-MODY differ from that of Type 2 Diabetes, and how does this trajectory influence the decision-making process when transitioning a patient from insulin back to oral agents or incretin mimetics?

**Clinical Pearl:** The natural history of **HNF1A-MODY** is characterized by a progressive, irreversible decline in β-cell mass and function driven by a specific transcription factor defect, contrasting sharply with the variable trajectory of **Type 2 Diabetes (T2D)**, where insulin resistance and compensatory hyperinsulinemia often precede eventual failure. This distinction fundamentally alters management: unlike T2D, where escalating doses of oral agents or incretins may eventually fail due to absolute insulin deficiency, HNF1A-MODY patients possess a unique hypersensitivity to low-dose sulfonylureas and an intrinsic inability to sustain adequate insulin secretion regardless of the degree of insulin resistance.

In the case presented, the patient initially received a "Type 2" regimen including basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor. However, upon genetic confirmation of *HNF1A*-MODY and recognition of his preserved but insufficient C-peptide (0.83 nmol/L), the team successfully transitioned him away from insulin. This transition was guided by the understanding that HNF1A-MODY β-cells are highly sensitive to secretagogues. While GLP-1 receptor agonists (like dulaglutide) proved effective here as a bridge to maintain control and preserve function during the acute phase of decompensation, they are not always first-line for long-term maintenance in classic HNF1A-MODY unless sulfonylureas are contraindicated or unavailable; however, their use avoided hypoglycemia while allowing insulin discontinuation. Crucially, the case illustrates that once the correct genetic diagnosis is made, aggressive insulin therapy is often unnecessary and can be safely withdrawn in favor of agents that stimulate endogenous secretion (or potentiate it), provided β-cell reserve exists.

Furthermore, clinicians must avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY. Because HNF1A regulates renal glucose reabsorption (via SGLT2 expression), these patients already have a lowered renal threshold for glucose and prominent glycosuria; adding an SGLT-2 inhibitor exacerbates this mechanism, increasing the risk of euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis and volume depletion—a pitfall not inherent to typical T2D management. Thus, recognizing this distinct pathophysiology allows for de-escalation from complex multi-drug regimens (including insulin) to simpler, targeted therapies tailored to the specific molecular defect.
