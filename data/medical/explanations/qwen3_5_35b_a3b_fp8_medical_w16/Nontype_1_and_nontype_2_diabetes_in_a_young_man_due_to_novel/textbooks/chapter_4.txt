Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 4: ## Therapeutic Decision Making and Precision Medicine Implementation

The management trajectory of this case underscores the pivotal role of genetic diagnosis in refining therapeutic strategies for monogenic diabetes. Upon initial presentation, the patient exhibited classic signs of severe hyperglycemia with an HbA1c of 10.2%, accompanied by weight loss and glycosuria. Lacking immediate genetic confirmation, the clinical team adopted an empiric approach typical for newly diagnosed diabetes presenting with significant catabolic features: initiating basal-bolus physiology coverage with insulin degludec alongside agents targeting insulin resistance and incretin pathways, specifically metformin and saxagliptin. This decision was clinically prudent; regardless of the underlying etiology, rapid reduction of toxic glucose levels is essential to reverse glucotoxicity and stabilize the patient's acute metabolic state. Furthermore, the initial prescription reflected a differential diagnosis leaning toward Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) or advanced beta-cell failure, necessitating robust pharmacologic intervention until the specific pathophysiology could be elucidated.

Once next-generation sequencing confirmed a pathogenic *HNF1A* variant and clinical assessment verified moderately preserved endogenous insulin secretion (stimulated C-peptide 0.83 nmol/L), the therapeutic paradigm shifted decisively toward precision medicine. The discontinuation of exogenous insulin was justified by the understanding that HNF1A-MODY is characterized by defective glucose sensing rather than absolute insulin deficiency or autoimmune destruction. Unlike Type 1 diabetes, where insulin replacement is mandatory for survival, patients with HNF1A-MODY retain sufficient beta-cell mass to respond to secretagogues once glucose toxicity is resolved. Consequently, the regimen was streamlined to eliminate unnecessary injections and minimize hypoglycemia risk—a common concern in this population due to their heightened sensitivity to pro-insulin secretory drugs.

Traditionally, sulfonylureas represent the gold standard first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY because they act directly on the ATP-sensitive potassium channels in beta cells to stimulate insulin release, bypassing the upstream signaling defects caused by *HNF1A* mutations. Patients with this genotype are exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas, often requiring very low doses to maintain euglycemia. However, this case illustrates a strategic adaptation using Glucagon-like Peptide-1 Receptor Agonists (GLP-1 RAs) as an alternative or adjunctive therapy. While sulfonylureas remain highly effective, GLP-1 RAs offer distinct advantages in specific contexts: they provide potent glycemic control through glucose-dependent mechanisms that inherently limit hypoglycemia risk compared to sulfonylureas, promote modest weight loss (beneficial given the patient's borderline overweight status), and may exert direct protective effects on beta-cell function through anti-apoptotic signaling pathways. In this instance, transitioning from insulin and oral agents to dulaglutide allowed for superior glycemic stabilization (reducing HbA1c to 6.0%) without inducing hypoglycemic episodes. Although sulfonylureas would also have been appropriate here given the *HNF1A* diagnosis, the selection of a GLP-1 RA reflects a nuanced application of precision medicine tailored to optimize safety profiles and long-term outcomes while awaiting further longitudinal data on drug efficacy in diverse populations. This evolution from broad-spectrum empiric therapy to targeted genotypic management demonstrates how molecular diagnostics can prevent lifelong overtreatment and facilitate personalized care plans that align with the unique pathophysiology of monogenic diabetes syndromes.
