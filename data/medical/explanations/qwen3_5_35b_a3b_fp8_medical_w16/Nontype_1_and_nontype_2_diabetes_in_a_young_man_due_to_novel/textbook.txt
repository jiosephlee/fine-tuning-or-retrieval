Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Dilemma

The clinical narrative begins with a young male patient referred following incidental detection of hyperglycemia and glycosuria during an annual health examination. Despite lacking acute symptoms at the moment of discovery, he soon reported classic triad symptoms of uncontrolled diabetes: polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintended weight loss. His laboratory workup revealed significant metabolic derangement, with a glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) of 10.2%, a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, and a 2-hour post-load glucose of 19.8 mmol/L during an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Crucially, the absence of diabetic ketoacidosis distinguished him from typical presentations of insulin-deficient Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) requiring immediate emergency intervention. While the severity of his hyperglycemia suggested advanced beta-cell failure, the concurrent finding of glycosuria pointed toward a unique pathophysiological mechanism involving altered renal handling of glucose, specifically a lowered renal threshold for reabsorption—a hallmark feature often overlooked in standard diabetes classifications but characteristic of certain monogenic defects like *HNF1A*-MODY.

Physical examination provided further clues that complicated the initial differential diagnosis. The patient possessed a Body Mass Index (BMI) of 24.6 kg/m², placing him in the normal weight range rather than exhibiting the obesity typically associated with Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Notably, the absence of acanthosis nigricans argued against significant peripheral insulin resistance, another common feature in T2DM. Furthermore, vital signs were stable with no hypertension or tachycardia. These physical attributes created an immediate tension in clinical reasoning: the patient displayed catabolic symptoms reminiscent of T1DM yet lacked autoimmune markers and ketotic instability; conversely, while his glucose levels were severe enough to mimic poorly controlled T2DM, he lacked the adiposity and clear signs of metabolic syndrome usually required to justify that diagnosis clinically.

The strongest indicator pointing away from sporadic autoimmune or lifestyle-driven etiologies lay in the detailed family history. The pedigree exhibited a striking pattern consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance across three generations: a maternal grandmother diagnosed in her thirties, a mother diagnosed in her late twenties, and a younger brother diagnosed at age twenty. This multi-generational clustering of early-onset diabetes strongly suggested a Mendelian disorder rather than complex multifactorial diseases like T1DM or T2DM. In clinical practice, such a history warrants high suspicion for Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY), yet without confirmatory genetic testing or knowledge of rare phenotypes, clinicians must first navigate through more common diagnoses. The overlap in symptomatology meant that until serological testing ruled out autoimmunity and genetic profiling excluded common variants, this case remained indistinguishable from "atypical" T1DM or lean-T2DM based solely on clinical grounds.

Laboratory investigations initially reinforced this diagnostic ambiguity by presenting conflicting signals relative to traditional paradigms. Although the patient tested positive for glycosuria—a finding driven here by defective renal tubular transport rather than merely overwhelming systemic hyperfiltration—he also demonstrated preserved but insufficient endogenous insulin secretion upon glucagon stimulation testing (C-peptide rose to 0.83 nmol/L). This level exceeded the cutoff for absolute insulin deficiency seen in long-standing T1DM but fell short of robust reserve expected in uncomplicated early-stage T2DM or healthy controls. Combined with negative autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A), these results defied simple categorization into either major diabetes subtype classically taught as mutually exclusive entities based on etiology (autoimmune vs. secretory/resistance). Consequently, this case underscores why early-onset hyperglycemia requires careful scrutiny beyond surface-level metrics; it serves as a reminder that "non-Type 1" status necessitates deeper investigation into monogenic causes when standard risk factors are absent or discordant with clinical presentation.

## Pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY and Renal Glucose Handling

To fully appreciate the clinical presentation of this patient, one must first understand the dual-pathway pathophysiology inherent to Hepatocyte Nuclear Factor 1-alpha (HNF1A)-MODY. Unlike Type 1 diabetes, which is driven by autoimmune destruction, or typical Type 2 diabetes, which is characterized primarily by peripheral insulin resistance, HNF1A-MODY stems from a specific genetic defect affecting the development and functional capacity of the pancreas and kidneys simultaneously. The *HNF1A* gene encodes a transcription factor that is ubiquitously expressed but plays a pivotal regulatory role in three key organs: the liver, the kidney, and the pancreatic $\beta$-cells. In this case report, the discovery of a novel heterozygous mutation (p.N270S) disrupts the protein's ability to bind DNA effectively, initiating a cascade of cellular failures that define the disease phenotype.

In the pancreas, HNF1A acts as a master regulator required for proper $\beta$-cell differentiation and survival. Its primary physiological function here includes maintaining the expression of genes essential for glucose sensing and insulin synthesis, notably *INS* (insulin) itself and *SLC2A2*, which encodes GLUT2—the major glucose transporter responsible for allowing glucose entry into $\beta$-cells to trigger insulin release. Furthermore, HNF1A regulates enzymes involved in glycolysis, such as pyruvate kinase. Consequently, mutations in *HNF1A* result in a progressive decline in $\beta$-cell mass and function. As illustrated in this patient's glucagon stimulation test showing insufficient C-peptide response relative to his hyperglycemia ($0.83\text{ nmol/L}$ post-stimulation), the underlying issue is not an absolute deficiency at onset but a gradual inability of remaining cells to secrete adequate insulin in response to rising glucose loads. This explains why patients often remain asymptomatic until stressors like puberty or pregnancy exacerbate their already compromised secretory reserve.

Concurrently, HNF1A governs renal physiology through its influence on sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) expression in the proximal convoluted tubule of the nephron. Under normal circumstances, SGLT2 reabsorbs approximately 90% of filtered glucose back into the bloodstream up to a renal threshold of roughly $180\text{ mg/dL}$. However, because *HNF1A* controls the transcriptional machinery driving SGLT2 production, mutant alleles lead to markedly reduced SGLT2 density on the apical membrane of renal tubular cells. This creates a "renal leak" phenomenon where glucose cannot be efficiently reclaimed even at modest elevations in blood concentration. In this case report, the patient exhibited profound glycosuria alongside hyperglycemia without frank ketosis or severe volume depletion initially—a classic signature where urinary glucose losses occur disproportionately early compared to Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes. While standard diabetes mellitus requires sustained blood glucose levels exceeding $>180\text{ mg/dL}$ before glycosuria becomes prominent due to saturation of transporters above their maximal capacity ($T_m$), HNF1A-MODY lowers this threshold significantly; thus, glycosuria appears much earlier and at lower plasma glucose concentrations simply because fewer transporters are available to reclaim what would normally be absorbed.

The interplay between these mechanisms dictates both the diagnostic challenge and natural history observed in this young man. Initially presenting with unexpected hyperglycemia during routine screening and later progressing to classic triad symptoms (polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia) with weight loss reflects the temporal progression from compensated dysregulation to overt decompensation as $\beta$-cell function wanes further over time. The renal manifestation serves as an early clue: if a clinician encounters glycosuria out of proportion to moderate hyperglycemia in a lean or normal-BMI individual with a strong multigenerational family history—as seen here with diagnoses across three generations—they should suspect HNF1A-MODY rather than attributing findings solely to poor adherence or advanced Type 2 diabetes. Understanding that this condition represents a quantitative reduction in both insulin secretion efficiency and renal reabsorption capacity underscores why therapies targeting incretin pathways (like GLP-1 RAs) or reducing hepatic gluconeogenesis may be effective alternatives once genetic confirmation is achieved, bypassing reliance on high-dose insulin that fails to address the root molecular deficit.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: From Biochemistry to Molecular Confirmation

The diagnostic journey for this young male patient began with the recognition of a clinical paradox: he presented with significant hyperglycemia and classic catabolic symptoms like weight loss and polydipsia, yet lacked the hallmarks of typical Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM) or overt Type 2 Diabetes (T2D). His body mass index was 24.6 kg/m², placing him in the normal range, and physical examination revealed no acanthosis nigricans, making severe insulin resistance unlikely. Crucially, the initial workup included a comprehensive autoantibody panel targeting glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibodies (IA-2A). The negative results for these markers were pivotal; they effectively ruled out autoimmune-mediated beta-cell destruction, shifting the differential diagnosis away from T1DM toward monogenic causes or atypical presentations of T2D. While autoantibodies are highly specific for T1DM, their absence does not definitively exclude it in rare seronegative cases but strongly supports looking elsewhere when combined with a robust family history of early-onset diabetes across three generations.

To further characterize the underlying pathophysiology, specifically regarding endogenous insulin production, a glucagon stimulation test was employed. Unlike resting C-peptide measurements which can fluctuate based on acute glycemic status, this dynamic test challenges the remaining beta cells to secrete insulin upon exposure to glucagon. In this patient, the stimulated C-peptide rose from 0.39 nmol/L to 0.83 nmol/L. Although this increment falls short of the optimal threshold (>0.60 nmol/L often cited as indicative of robust preservation), it confirms the presence of functional beta-cell mass sufficient to respond to secretagogues, thereby excluding absolute insulin deficiency characteristic of advanced T1DM. This finding suggested a state of relative insufficiency driven by intrinsic cellular defects rather than total immune-mediated ablation. Interestingly, concurrent sudomotor testing revealed severely reduced conductance in the hands, indicating autonomic neuropathy—a finding unexpected in such a young patient without long-standing disease duration. While autonomic dysfunction is traditionally associated with chronic hyperglycemia in T2D or long-duration T1DM, its early appearance here underscores the aggressive nature of untreated HNF1A-MODY or perhaps points to unique phenotypic expressions of this genotype that warrant closer scrutiny during future monitoring.

With clinical suspicion firmly anchored on Monogenic Diabetes due to the triad of young onset (<25 years), strong autosomal dominant family history, negative autoimmunity, and preserved but declining beta-cell function, the next logical step was molecular confirmation via Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS). Standard clinical algorithms recommend NGS panels over single-gene testing for young-onset diabetes precisely because phenotypes overlap heavily between MODY subtypes and other forms; relying solely on clinical features often leads to misclassification as "atypical" T1DM or T2D. The NGS panel successfully identified a heterozygous substitution in *HNF1A* (exon 4: c.809A>G; p.N270S). Because this specific variant was absent from major public repositories like ClinVar and gnomAD at the time of discovery, rigorous application of American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines became essential for classification. The variant was deemed Likely Pathogenic/Pathogenic based on several converging lines of evidence: it occurred in a critical DNA-binding domain where most *HNF1A* mutations cluster; it segregated perfectly with diabetes in the affected family lineage; computational prediction tools yielded an exceptionally high AlphaMissense score (0.995); and it affected an amino acid residue immediately adjacent to other known pathogenic sites. This multi-faceted validation allowed clinicians to confidently diagnose HNF1A-MODY despite the variant's novelty, demonstrating that modern genomic diagnostics rely not just on database frequency but also on structural biology predictions and familial segregation patterns to interpret rare discoveries accurately.

## Therapeutic Decision Making and Precision Medicine Implementation

The management trajectory of this case underscores the pivotal role of genetic diagnosis in refining therapeutic strategies for monogenic diabetes. Upon initial presentation, the patient exhibited classic signs of severe hyperglycemia with an HbA1c of 10.2%, accompanied by weight loss and glycosuria. Lacking immediate genetic confirmation, the clinical team adopted an empiric approach typical for newly diagnosed diabetes presenting with significant catabolic features: initiating basal-bolus physiology coverage with insulin degludec alongside agents targeting insulin resistance and incretin pathways, specifically metformin and saxagliptin. This decision was clinically prudent; regardless of the underlying etiology, rapid reduction of toxic glucose levels is essential to reverse glucotoxicity and stabilize the patient's acute metabolic state. Furthermore, the initial prescription reflected a differential diagnosis leaning toward Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) or advanced beta-cell failure, necessitating robust pharmacologic intervention until the specific pathophysiology could be elucidated.

Once next-generation sequencing confirmed a pathogenic *HNF1A* variant and clinical assessment verified moderately preserved endogenous insulin secretion (stimulated C-peptide 0.83 nmol/L), the therapeutic paradigm shifted decisively toward precision medicine. The discontinuation of exogenous insulin was justified by the understanding that HNF1A-MODY is characterized by defective glucose sensing rather than absolute insulin deficiency or autoimmune destruction. Unlike Type 1 diabetes, where insulin replacement is mandatory for survival, patients with HNF1A-MODY retain sufficient beta-cell mass to respond to secretagogues once glucose toxicity is resolved. Consequently, the regimen was streamlined to eliminate unnecessary injections and minimize hypoglycemia risk—a common concern in this population due to their heightened sensitivity to pro-insulin secretory drugs.

Traditionally, sulfonylureas represent the gold standard first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY because they act directly on the ATP-sensitive potassium channels in beta cells to stimulate insulin release, bypassing the upstream signaling defects caused by *HNF1A* mutations. Patients with this genotype are exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas, often requiring very low doses to maintain euglycemia. However, this case illustrates a strategic adaptation using Glucagon-like Peptide-1 Receptor Agonists (GLP-1 RAs) as an alternative or adjunctive therapy. While sulfonylureas remain highly effective, GLP-1 RAs offer distinct advantages in specific contexts: they provide potent glycemic control through glucose-dependent mechanisms that inherently limit hypoglycemia risk compared to sulfonylureas, promote modest weight loss (beneficial given the patient's borderline overweight status), and may exert direct protective effects on beta-cell function through anti-apoptotic signaling pathways. In this instance, transitioning from insulin and oral agents to dulaglutide allowed for superior glycemic stabilization (reducing HbA1c to 6.0%) without inducing hypoglycemic episodes. Although sulfonylureas would also have been appropriate here given the *HNF1A* diagnosis, the selection of a GLP-1 RA reflects a nuanced application of precision medicine tailored to optimize safety profiles and long-term outcomes while awaiting further longitudinal data on drug efficacy in diverse populations. This evolution from broad-spectrum empiric therapy to targeted genotypic management demonstrates how molecular diagnostics can prevent lifelong overtreatment and facilitate personalized care plans that align with the unique pathophysiology of monogenic diabetes syndromes.
