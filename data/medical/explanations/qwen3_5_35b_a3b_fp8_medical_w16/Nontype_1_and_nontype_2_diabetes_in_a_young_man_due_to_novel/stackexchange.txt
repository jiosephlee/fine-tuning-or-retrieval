Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Pathophysiology] Based on the clinical presentation of this young adult with hyperglycemia, glycosuria, and a strong multigenerational family history of early-onset diabetes, what specific pathophysiological mechanism explains the prominent glycosuria despite the absence of severe hyperglycemia relative to typical Type 2 Diabetes thresholds?

The prominent glycosuria observed in this patient with *HNF1A*-MODY, occurring despite glucose levels that may not reach the extreme highs typical of untreated Type 2 diabetes, is caused by a **reduced renal threshold for glucose**.

Pathophysiologically, the *HNF1A* gene encodes a transcription factor that regulates the expression of several downstream targets, including **Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter 2 (SGLT2)** in the proximal renal tubules. In *HNF1A*-MODY, the mutant allele leads to diminished transcriptional activation of SGLT2. Consequently, the kidney's capacity to reabsorb filtered glucose is impaired. While systemic hyperglycemia drives filtration load upward, the primary driver of glycosuria here is the intrinsic defect in renal handling; even modest elevations in plasma glucose exceed this lowered reabsorptive threshold, causing significant glucose spillage into the urine.

This mechanism distinguishes *HNF1A*-MODY from other forms of diabetes where glycosuria strictly correlates with severe hyperglycemia exceeding ~180 mg/dL (the normal renal threshold). In this case report, the authors explicitly attribute the "significant" glycosuria noted on presentation to this "compromised glucose transport in renal tubules." Clinically, this finding serves as a crucial diagnostic clue: when a young patient presents with marked glycosuria disproportionate to their degree of hyperglycemia—or simply with glycosuria itself—it should raise immediate suspicion for MODY and prompt investigation for *HNF1A* mutations, as emphasized in this case where it contributed to the eventual correct diagnosis alongside the strong multigenerational family history.

### [Diagnostic Reasoning] Which three autoantibodies must be tested and found negative to strongly support a diagnosis of MODY over Type 1 Diabetes in a patient presenting with early-onset hyperglycemia and preserved C-peptide levels?

To distinguish Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) from Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM) in a patient with early-onset hyperglycemia and preserved beta-cell function, one must first exclude the autoimmune destruction characteristic of T1DM. While the presence of positive autoantibodies confirms T1DM, their absence does not definitively prove MODY but is a mandatory prerequisite for pursuing genetic testing.

According to the case report provided, the diagnostic algorithm relies heavily on demonstrating a lack of autoimmune markers alongside clinical features like a strong family history and preserved C-peptide levels. Specifically, the text notes that "negative autoantibodies associated with type 1 diabetes" are required before molecular genetic testing becomes the next logical step. The three specific autoantibodies that must be tested and found negative to strongly support a diagnosis of MODY over Type 1 Diabetes are:

1.  **Glutamic Acid Decarboxylase Antibodies (GADA)**
2.  **Insulinoma Antigen-2 Autoantibodies (IA-2A)**
3.  **Zinc Transporter 8 Antibodies (ZnT8A)**

In the patient described in this case, GADA and IA-2A were explicitly noted as negative. Although ZnT8 was not individually listed in the *results* section of this specific abstract, it is cited in the background discussion as one of the standard triad of antibodies included in modern diagnostic algorithms ("including glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies... insulinoma antigen-2... and zinc transporter 8"). Furthermore, the Discussion references a Russian study that utilized exactly this trio (IAA, GADA, and ZnT8) to screen patients suspected of hereditary diabetes without autoimmune features. Therefore, finding all three negative is essential to rule out T1DM sufficiently to justify proceeding with *HNF1A* genetic sequencing for confirmation.

### [Management] Given the confirmed diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY in this patient, why is the use of Sulfonylureas considered the classic first-line pharmacologic therapy, and specifically which cellular channel do these agents target to restore insulin secretion?

**Clinical Pearl:** In patients with *HNF1A*-MODY, sulfonylureas are the classic first-line pharmacologic therapy because these agents bypass the specific transcriptional defects caused by the mutation. Unlike Type 2 diabetes, where insulin resistance is prominent, *HNF1A*-MODY is characterized by a selective defect in pancreatic $\beta$-cell maturation and function. The mutated HNF1$\alpha$ protein fails to adequately upregulate key genes required for glucose sensing and insulin release, notably **glucokinase**, **GLUT2**, and the **potassium-sensitive ATP ($K_{ATP}$) channel subunits**. Consequently, these cells have a markedly lowered threshold for glucose-stimulated insulin secretion.

Sulfonylureas act by binding to the **sulfonylurea receptor 1 (SUR1)** subunit of the $K_{ATP}$ channel on the $\beta$-cell membrane. By closing this channel, they depolarize the cell membrane regardless of ambient glucose levels or the underlying transcriptional deficit in glucose sensing machinery. This triggers voltage-gated calcium influx and subsequent insulin granule exocytosis. Because the downstream secretory machinery remains intact, even low doses of sulfonylureas can potently stimulate insulin release in *HNF1A*-MODY patients, making them exquisitely sensitive to this drug class compared to patients with Type 2 diabetes or other forms of MODY. While this patient's management utilized a GLP-1 receptor agonist following genetic confirmation—a valid strategy that offers comparable efficacy with a lower risk of hypoglycemia—the mechanism of action described above explains why sulfonylureas remain the historical "gold standard" for restoring glycemic control in this specific genotype.

### [Pathophysiology] This case highlights a specific contraindication or caution regarding the use of SGLT-2 inhibitors in patients with HNF1A-MODY. What is the underlying mechanism linking HNF1A deficiency to renal glucose handling that creates this risk?

**Clinical Pearl:**
In patients with *HNF1A*-MODY, SGLT-2 inhibitors are relatively contraindicated or require extreme caution because the pathophysiology of the disease itself mimics the drug's intended mechanism of action.

**Explanation:**
The *HNF1A* gene encodes a transcription factor essential for regulating glucose homeostasis in the kidney, specifically controlling the expression of **SGLT2** (the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2) in the proximal renal tubule. In *HNF1A*-MODY, the mutant allele leads to reduced transcriptional activity, causing an intrinsic downregulation of SGLT2 expression. Consequently, these patients already possess a significantly lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, leading to spontaneous glycosuria and "renal" diabetes even when plasma glucose levels are only modestly elevated.

Administering an exogenous SGLT-2 inhibitor to these patients adds a second layer of blockade on top of their genetically deficient transporter system. This synergistic effect precipitates profound glycosuria and osmotic diuresis far exceeding what would occur in typical type 2 diabetes. As noted in this case report's discussion, this excessive fluid loss places these patients at a uniquely high risk for **severe dehydration**, hemodynamic instability (vascular collapse), and euglycemic **diabetic ketoacidosis**. Because the baseline renal handling of glucose is already compromised by the genetic defect, adding pharmacologic inhibition offers little additional glycemic benefit while drastically amplifying safety risks. Therefore, first-line therapy for *HNF1A*-MODY relies on agents that stimulate insulin secretion (low-dose sulfonylureas) or augment incretin effects (GLP-1 receptor agonists/DPP-4 inhibitors), avoiding drugs that target the already defective renal glucose pathway.

### [Clinical Pearls] In the context of genetic variant interpretation for MODY, if a novel HNF1A variant is not found in public databases like ClinVar but shows segregation with disease in the family, high AlphaMissense scores, and affects an amino acid adjacent to known pathogenic residues, which ACMG classification criteria are satisfied to designate it as 'Likely Pathogenic'?

To classify the novel *HNF1A* variant **p.N270S** as **"Likely Pathogenic"** in this case, we rely on specific ACMG/AMP criteria that collectively bridge the gap left by the variant's absence in public databases like ClinVar.

First, the criterion **PP3** is satisfied. This computational evidence supports a damaging effect when multiple lines of algorithmic prediction agree. In this patient, the variant achieved a very high **AlphaMissense score of 0.995**, providing strong computational evidence that the amino acid change is deleterious to protein function.

Second, the criterion **PM1** is met. This applies when a missense variant affects an amino acid residue located within a known functional domain or at a site critical for function where other pathogenic variants have been documented. Here, p.N270S resides in the **DNA-binding domain** of *HNF1A*, specifically affecting an amino acid residue **adjacent to known pathogenic residues**. Given that *HNF1A*-MODY mutations predominantly cluster in this DNA-binding domain to disrupt transcriptional regulation of insulin and GLUT2 genes, proximity to established hotspots strongly implies pathogenicity.

Third, the criterion **PS4** (or potentially PM2_Supporting combined with PP1) is fulfilled through clinical observation. While PS4 typically requires statistical enrichment in cases versus controls, in single-case reporting contexts, robust **segregation with disease** serves as powerful corroborating evidence (often mapped to **PP1_Strong**). The case report confirms an autosomal dominant pattern where the variant co-segregates with early-onset diabetes across three generations (maternal grandmother, mother, and brother), aligning perfectly with the clinical phenotype of HNF1A-MODY.

Finally, while the absence from databases satisfies **PM2**, it does not prove pathogenicity on its own; however, when combined with PP3 (high predictive score), PM1 (critical domain location), and PP1 (family segregation), these lines of evidence satisfy the threshold for **"Likely Pathogenic."** This classification justifies treating this novel finding as clinically actionable for precision medicine decisions, such as transitioning from insulin to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists as seen in this patient's successful management.

### [Management] How does the natural history and progression of beta-cell dysfunction in HNF1A-MODY differ from that of Type 2 Diabetes, and how does this trajectory influence the decision-making process when transitioning a patient from insulin back to oral agents or incretin mimetics?

**Clinical Pearl:** The natural history of **HNF1A-MODY** is characterized by a progressive, irreversible decline in β-cell mass and function driven by a specific transcription factor defect, contrasting sharply with the variable trajectory of **Type 2 Diabetes (T2D)**, where insulin resistance and compensatory hyperinsulinemia often precede eventual failure. This distinction fundamentally alters management: unlike T2D, where escalating doses of oral agents or incretins may eventually fail due to absolute insulin deficiency, HNF1A-MODY patients possess a unique hypersensitivity to low-dose sulfonylureas and an intrinsic inability to sustain adequate insulin secretion regardless of the degree of insulin resistance.

In the case presented, the patient initially received a "Type 2" regimen including basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor. However, upon genetic confirmation of *HNF1A*-MODY and recognition of his preserved but insufficient C-peptide (0.83 nmol/L), the team successfully transitioned him away from insulin. This transition was guided by the understanding that HNF1A-MODY β-cells are highly sensitive to secretagogues. While GLP-1 receptor agonists (like dulaglutide) proved effective here as a bridge to maintain control and preserve function during the acute phase of decompensation, they are not always first-line for long-term maintenance in classic HNF1A-MODY unless sulfonylureas are contraindicated or unavailable; however, their use avoided hypoglycemia while allowing insulin discontinuation. Crucially, the case illustrates that once the correct genetic diagnosis is made, aggressive insulin therapy is often unnecessary and can be safely withdrawn in favor of agents that stimulate endogenous secretion (or potentiate it), provided β-cell reserve exists.

Furthermore, clinicians must avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY. Because HNF1A regulates renal glucose reabsorption (via SGLT2 expression), these patients already have a lowered renal threshold for glucose and prominent glycosuria; adding an SGLT-2 inhibitor exacerbates this mechanism, increasing the risk of euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis and volume depletion—a pitfall not inherent to typical T2D management. Thus, recognizing this distinct pathophysiology allows for de-escalation from complex multi-drug regimens (including insulin) to simpler, targeted therapies tailored to the specific molecular defect.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Considering the limitations of traditional diagnostic tools like BMI and metabolic syndrome criteria in distinguishing MODY from Type 2 Diabetes, what specific clinical feature mentioned in this case (related to autonomic function) serves as an unusual but documented complication that may prompt further investigation for monogenic diabetes?

While Body Mass Index (BMI) and traditional metabolic syndrome criteria are increasingly unreliable for distinguishing Monogenic Diabetes of the Young (MODY) from Type 2 Diabetes due to rising obesity rates in the general population, this case highlights **severe sudomotor dysfunction** as a critical, albeit unusual, clinical clue pointing toward HNF1A-MODY.

Typically, autonomic neuropathy is viewed as a late-stage complication of chronic hyperglycemia in longstanding Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes. However, in this young patient presenting with newly diagnosed hyperglycaemia and an HbA1c of 10.2%, electrochemical skin conductance testing revealed **severely reduced sudomotor function** in the hands. This finding is highly atypical for such a short duration of undiagnosed diabetes and suggests that the underlying pathophysiology involves more than just glucose toxicity.

The mechanism here lies in the specific biology of *HNF1A*. The *HNF1A* gene encodes a transcription factor expressed not only in the pancreas but also in the kidneys and peripheral nerves. Consequently, *HNF1A* mutations can lead to direct developmental or functional defects in small-fiber autonomic nerves independent of prolonged exposure to high glucose levels. While glycosuria is a classic hallmark of HNF1A-MODY (due to a lowered renal threshold), significant early-onset autonomic dysfunction should raise immediate suspicion for monogenic etiologies when standard risk factors like obesity or insulin resistance markers (e.g., acanthosis nigricans) are absent or equivocal. Recognizing this feature prompts timely genetic confirmation, allowing for a shift away from ineffective insulin regimens toward targeted therapies like GLP-1 receptor agonists or sulfonylureas that specifically address the *HNF1A*-deficient beta-cell physiology.
