# The 'Metabolic Mimic': Why Young-Onset Diabetes Isn't Always Type 1 or Type 2

We've all been there: a young adult walks into the ER or clinic presenting with classic "red flag" symptoms of diabetes. Polydipsia, polyuria, unintentional weight loss, and a staggering HbA1c of 10.2%. Your differential screams Type 1 DM until you check the autoantibodies and find them negative. Then it whispers Type 2 because of the metabolic syndrome markers (high triglycerides, low HDL) and a BMI that isn't exactly emaciated. You start the patient on basal-bolus insulin and metformin, feeling reasonably confident you're treating the most common etiologies.

But what if neither is true?

This case of a young man with **HNF1A-MODY** illustrates one of the most frustrating diagnostic dilemmas in endocrinology: the "metabolic mimic." He presented with weight loss and severe hyperglycemia, features we usually reserve for insulin-deficient states like T1DM. Yet, he also carried markers of insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome often seen in T2DM. His family history was a screaming clue—grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, brother at age 20—but without specific genetic testing, this pattern can easily be dismissed as "familial T2DM."

The beauty—and danger—of this case lies in the overlap. This patient had **negative autoantibodies** (ruling out classic autoimmune T1DM) but also **preserved C-peptide** (initially misleading us away from absolute deficiency). He had glycosuria not just from hyperglycemia exceeding the renal threshold, but specifically due to HNF1A-related defects in renal glucose reabsorption (SGLT2 downregulation). Initially treated with insulin degludec and metformin based on a presumed mixed picture, his trajectory changed dramatically once the genetic puzzle piece fell into place: a novel heterozygous mutation (**p.N270S**) in *HNF1A*.

Once confirmed as HNF1A-MODY, we saw that insulin was unnecessary for long-term control. The patient was successfully de-escalated from basal insulin to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) combined with metformin, dropping his A1c from 9.1% to a stable 6.0% within six months. This case serves as a stark reminder that when clinical heuristics fail—when BMI doesn't fit neatly into "thin vs. obese," or when antibodies are negative but C-peptide seems "low-normal"—you must look deeper. The spectrum of MODY is expanding rapidly with Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS), uncovering novel variants like this one that don't appear in standard databases like ClinVar but still drive profound clinical pathology.

## The Diagnostic Grey Zone: When Rules Don't Apply

Differentiating MODY from T1DM and T2DM is notoriously difficult because they share overlapping phenotypes. Historically, we relied on simple rules: thin + antibodies = T1; fat + resistant = T2; thin + no antibodies = MODY? Not so fast.

In this case, our patient had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m²—technically normal weight—which argues against typical obesity-driven T2DM. However, his lipid profile showed high triglycerides (4.31 mmol/L) and low HDL (1.01 mmol/L), mimicking metabolic syndrome often seen in T2D. Furthermore, he exhibited significant autonomic dysfunction (severe reduction in sudomotor function), which is more commonly associated with chronic hyperglycemia in longer-standing disease but appeared here relatively early due to the severity of his untreated hyperglycemia (A1c 10.2%).

The key differentiator here wasn't just one lab value but the constellation of findings:
*   **Negative Autoimmunity:** GADA and IA-2A were negative, making autoimmune destruction unlikely despite the weight loss and ketone-negative DKA presentation (though he never actually reached DKA).
*   **C-Peptide Nuance:** While his stimulated C-peptide was <0.6 nmol/L at baseline (indicating insufficiency), it rose to 0.83 nmol/L after glucagon stimulation. In pure LADA/T1DM progression to frank failure, we expect near-zero responses; here, there was residual function that responded to secretagogues later on.
*   **Family History:** The vertical transmission across three generations is the hallmark of autosomal dominant inheritance characteristic of MODY (specifically HNF1A-MODY accounts for ~30-65% of cases). Even though he didn't meet every textbook criterion for MODY initially due to his metabolic markers, the pedigree was undeniable upon review.

It is worth noting that while calculators like the Exeter Group's MODY probability calculator suggested a 62% likelihood for this patient, these tools are largely derived from White European cohorts and may have variable sensitivity in other ethnicities or complex presentations involving metabolic syndrome components as seen here with rising global obesity rates masking monogenic traits.

## Precision Medicine Changes Management: From Insulin to Secretagogues

The most impactful lesson from this case is how genetics dictates therapy in HNF1A-MODY versus other forms of diabetes. Patients with HNF1A-MODY are exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas because their primary defect is reduced insulin secretion driven by impaired beta-cell glucose sensing, not immune destruction or peripheral resistance alone (*HNF-α* regulates *GLUT2*, *Insulin*, and *Pyruvate Kinase*). Consequently, sulfonylureas are historically first-line therapy due to their efficacy at very low doses compared to standard diabetic regimens used for T2DM or TIDM patients requiring massive insulin loads daily (*sulfonylurea sensitivity*).

However, our patient's journey highlights modern nuances:
*   **Initial Misstep:** He started on basal insulin degludec plus metformin and saxagliptin because clinicians were playing it safe given his high A1c and weight loss fears of catabolic state ("treat until proven wrong").
*   **The Pivot:** Once genetic confirmation arrived (novel variant p.N270S), insulin was stopped immediately after achieving modest control via other means ($HbA_1c$ dropped to 9.1%).
*   **Optimization:** He was switched to Dulaglutide (GLP-RA). While sulfonylureas remain gold standard for many HNF-A patients due to cost and ease of use, GLP-RAs offer excellent glycemic control with lower hypoglycemia risk—a crucial consideration as beta-cell function naturally declines over time in MODY patients too (*progressive nature*).
*   **Long-term Maintenance:** Eventually stabilized on Metformin + Saxagliptin (DPP-4i), maintaining an A1c <6.5% without hypoglycemia events or need for injections beyond occasional dosing adjustments required by GLP-RAs earlier on (*note: DPP-4is are generally considered second/third line agents but effective here*).

Crucially, we must avoid certain drugs in HNF-A-MODY unless absolutely necessary due to safety concerns regarding dehydration and euglycemic DKA risk associated with SGLT-2 inhibitors (*which target SGLT-2 proteins already downregulated by HNF-A mutation*). Using SGLT-Is could precipitate volume depletion or ketoacidosis by further inhibiting renal glucose reabsorption mechanisms already compromised by the mutation itself (*theoretically worsening intravascular volume status*).

## Key Takeaways for the Emergency Physician & Endocrinologist

Don't let "Type 2" or "Type 1" labels blind you when your young patient doesn't quite fit either box perfectly.* Early-onset diabetes requires a high index of suspicion for monogenic causes whenever:*

*   **Autoantibodies are negative** despite rapid onset or weight loss symptoms suggesting beta-cell failure.*
*   **There is a strong multi-generational family history** of early-onset diabetes (<35 years).*
*   **Patients present with glycosuria disproportionate** to their actual blood glucose levels due to renal tubular defects.*
*   **"Mixed" features exist**: Normal/low BMI but clear metabolic syndrome signs; or high BMI but lack of typical insulin resistance patterns.*
*   **Standard therapies fail** or require unusually low/high doses compared to expectations.*
    *If you suspect MODY,* order comprehensive genetic panels (*Next Generation Sequencing*) rather than relying solely on phenotypic predictions.* Confirming HNF-A-MODY allows for de-intensification from aggressive insulin protocols toward highly effective oral agents (*sulfonylureas/GLP-RAs*), reducing hypoglycemia risk and healthcare costs while preventing unnecessary exposure to potentially harmful drug classes like SGLT-Inhibitors.* Finally,* remember that novel variants like p.N270S may not be listed in public databases yet; rely on functional assays (*AlphaMissense scores*), segregation analysis within families,*and ACMG guidelines rather than database absence alone.*



# Beyond Autoantibodies: Leveraging Glucagon Stimulation Tests and Family History for MODY Suspicion

We've all been there: the young patient walks in with a BMI of 24.6 kg/m², no acanthosis nigricans, and an HbA1c of 10.2%. They have glycosuria, they're losing weight, and their parents are terrified it's Type 1 Diabetes (T1D). You run the autoimmune panel—negative. You check the C-peptide—it's "okay," but maybe not *perfect*. Do you start them on lifelong insulin? Or do you suspect something else entirely?

This case report of a young man with a **novel HNF1A mutation** serves as a masterclass in why we must look past the binary labels of T1D vs. T2D. The patient presented with classic "Type 1-like" symptoms (weight loss, polydipsia) but lacked the autoimmune signature. He also didn't fit the typical "obese metabolic syndrome" profile of T2D, despite having dyslipidemia. The turning point wasn't just the genetics; it was the clinical reasoning that connected a specific family history pattern with subtle physiological clues that standard screening misses.

### The Diagnostic Dilemma: When the Standard Algorithm Fails

The biggest pitfall in diagnosing Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) is assuming that negative autoantibodies automatically rule out T1D or that preserved C-peptide rules out beta-cell failure. In this case, the patient had negative GADA and IA-2A antibodies, yet he required insulin initially because his HbA1c was dangerously high (10.2%).

What made us pause? Two things stood out immediately upon reviewing the history and labs:
1.  **The Pedigree:** This wasn't sporadic diabetes. His mother was diagnosed in her late 20s, his grandmother in her 30s, and his younger brother at age 20. That is a textbook vertical transmission pattern suggestive of an autosomal dominant trait.
2.  **The Physiology:** While his fasting C-peptide looked decent at baseline, the dynamic testing told a different story.

It is easy to get distracted by metabolic syndrome features here—the patient had high triglycerides (4.31 mmol/L) and low HDL (1.01 mmol/L). Historically, we taught ourselves that MODY patients are lean and metabolically pristine compared to T2D patients. But as this case illustrates, rising obesity rates are blurring these lines. Relying solely on BMI or lipid profiles to exclude MODY is dangerous; this patient had features mimicking metabolic syndrome but turned out to be monogenic diabetes driven by an HNF1A defect affecting renal glucose handling (hence the prominent glycosuria) and beta-cell function.

### The Power of Dynamic Testing: Why We Need More Than Fasting C-Peptide

## Key Takeaways for Clinical Practice

To wrap up this discussion on leveraging clinical clues before ordering expensive genetic panels:

*   **Suspect MODY Early:** If you see early-onset diabetes (<35 yrs) with strong autosomal dominant family history AND negative autoantibodies—even if BMI is normal/high or lipids are abnormal—think MONOGENIC DIABETES first before settling into generic TMD protocols permanently without investigation.**
*   **Don’t Ignore Negative Antibodies Alone:** Negative GADA/IA-2/ZnT8 does NOT guarantee Type II Diabetes until proven otherwise by genetic testing when suspicion arises from pedigree analysis!**
*   **Use Dynamic Testing Wisely:** Glucose-stimulated C-peptide curves provide superior insight into residual β-cell capacity compared static measurements alone helping guide therapeutic choices away from premature permanent insulin initiation.**
*   **Avoid Harmful Drugs:** Remember that patients harboring *HNFIA* mutations possess inherently altered renal glucose handling mechanisms rendering them highly susceptible adverse events related usage Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter Protein Type-II Inhibitors(SGLTI); therefore avoid prescribing these agents unless absolutely necessary under strict specialist supervision following careful risk-benefit assessments prioritizing safety over theoretical efficacy gains!**
*   **Precision Saves Lives & Money:** Identifying monogenic causes enables tailored treatments reducing unnecessary medication burdens preventing costly hospitalizations due preventable complications # Genetic Nuances: Interpreting Novel Variants and the Role of AlphaMissense in Precision Medicine

Let's talk about the "gray zone" of genetics. You know the scenario: a young patient presents with a classic MODY phenotype—early onset, strong family history, negative autoantibodies—but your genetic report comes back saying you've found a "novel" variant. It's not in ClinVar. It's not in gnomAD. The automated pipeline flags it as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). Do you ignore it? Or do you treat based on the clinical picture?

This case from Liu et al. offers a masterclass in why we shouldn't wait for the database to catch up before making life-changing treatment decisions. They describe a young man with HNF1A-MODY caused by a completely unreported heterozygous substitution: **p.N270S** (*HNF1A*:c.809A>G). He was initially treated aggressively with insulin, metformin, and saxagliptin because he looked like Type 2 Diabetes (obese enough for metabolic syndrome markers, though BMI 24.6 kg/m² isn't morbidly so). But once the genetic truth came out—and we learned *how* they proved it—the team pivoted instantly. Insulin was stopped, and he was switched to a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin, dropping his HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% in six months without hypoglycemia.

The real takeaway here isn't just that GLP-1s work; it's how we justified treating him as if we knew the mutation was pathogenic when the public databases said otherwise.

## Beyond the Database: Applying ACMG Guidelines to "Unknowns"

In modern EM and Endo practice, relying solely on ClinVar is dangerous because it is inherently retrospective. If a variant hasn't been published or submitted yet, it won't be there. So, how does one move from "we don't know" to "this causes disease"? The authors walked us through a rigorous application of ACMG guidelines using three pillars of evidence that go far beyond simple frequency checks:

First, **segregation**. In this family, diabetes wasn't sporadic; it ran vertically down generations (grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, brother at 20). The variant co-segregated perfectly with the disease phenotype across three generations in an autosomal dominant pattern. That is powerful evidence for pathogenicity on its own in many contexts.

Second, **structural biology and domain mapping**. Not all parts of the *HNF1A* gene are created equal. The gene encodes a transcription factor with distinct domains: the DNA-binding domain and the transactivation domain. Historically, mutations cluster heavily in the DNA-binding domain because disrupting DNA interaction cripples the protein's ability to regulate downstream targets like GLUT2 and insulin genes entirely. The p.N270S mutation sits squarely in this critical DNA-binding domain (exon 4), affecting an amino acid residue adjacent to known pathogenic sites. A mutation here is biologically plausible for causing MODY; one floating randomly in a non-functional loop would raise red flags differently.

Third, **bioinformatic prediction scores**, specifically **AlphaMissense**. This is where things get cutting-edge for clinicians who aren't geneticists but need to understand their reports quickly. AlphaMissense uses AI models trained on human protein structures to predict whether an amino acid change disrupts protein function. For p.N270S, this tool gave an incredible score of **0.995** (where 1.0 is maximum confidence of disruption). While not perfect proof of causality alone, combined with segregation and location data, it pushes the classification firmly toward Likely Pathogenic (LP) or Pathogenic (P), bypassing the VUS trap that often delays care or leads to unnecessary anxiety for families waiting for re-analysis years later.

## The Therapeutic Pivot: Why Diagnosis Changes Everything

Once you accept p.N270S as pathogenic despite its absence from ClinVar, the treatment algorithm shifts dramatically away from standard Type 2 protocols toward precision medicine tailored for HNF1A-MODY.

Initially, this patient was on basal insulin degludec plus metformin and saxagliptin—a heavy-handed approach typical for someone presenting with an HbA1c of 10% and weight loss symptoms mimicking T1D/T2D overlap. However, knowing this is HNF1A-MODY changes everything regarding drug choice:
*   **Insulin:** Often unnecessary unless there is acute catabolism or extreme hyperglycemia that needs rapid stabilization (which happened briefly here). Once controlled, insulin can be withdrawn safely because these patients retain beta-cell mass but have defective secretion regulation that responds better to secretagogues or incretins than constant exogenous infusion might suggest long-term without side effects like hypoglycemia or weight gain concerns relative to sulfonylureas (though sulfonylureas remain first-line historically).
*   **Sulfonylureas vs. GLP-1 RAs:** While low-dose sulfonylureas are traditionally considered first-line for HNF1A-MODY due to their mechanism of closing KATP channels to stimulate insulin release directly from beta cells that are still present but sensitive to glucose sensing defects, this patient was successfully managed with Dulaglutide (GLP-1 RA). Why? Because as beta-cell function declines over time in HNF1A-MODY (evidenced by his stimulated C-peptide of only 0.83 nmol/L), GLP-1 RAs offer glucose-dependent insulin secretion with a lower risk of hypoglycemia compared to sulfonylureas and provide weight neutrality/benefit—which matters even if BMI isn't sky-high yet given his metabolic syndrome markers (high triglycerides).

# From Insulin to GLP-1: Re-evaluating Pharmacotherapy Algorithms in HNF1A-MODY

Let's talk about the "diagnostic trap" of early-onset diabetes. We've all seen it: a young patient walks into the ER or clinic with an HbA1c of 10.2%, weight loss, polydipsia, and glycosuria. The instinctive reflex? Slap them on basal insulin and call it Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM) until proven otherwise. It's a safety-first approach, but in the realm of Monogenic Diabetes, specifically **HNF1A-MODY**, this reflex can delay precision care and expose patients to unnecessary injections and hypoglycemia risks.

This week's case is a masterclass in why we need to pause before starting the needle. A young man presented with classic "Type 2-like" symptoms (weight loss, polyuria) but a lean BMI (24.6 kg/m²) and a striking three-generation family history of early-onset diabetes (grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s). Despite the dramatic presentation, he had no DKA and negative islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A). He was initially managed aggressively with insulin degludec, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor based on the assumption of T2D/T1DM overlap.

**Here is the twist:** Once genetic testing confirmed a novel *HNF1A* mutation (p.N270S), we didn't just tweak the dose; we fundamentally changed the trajectory. Within months of switching from insulin to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) alongside metformin, his HbA1c plummeted from 9.1% to 6.0%. This isn't just about lowering numbers; it's about recognizing that HNF1A-MODY is a disease of *quantitative* insulin deficiency driven by renal glucosuria and progressive beta-cell failure, not absolute insulin dependence or peripheral resistance alone.

### The Pathophysiology: Why Sulfonylureas Are King (But Not Always Enough)

To understand why we moved away from insulin here, we have to look at the biology of *HNF1A*. This transcription factor regulates glucose sensing in both the pancreas and the kidney. In HNF1A-MODY, two things happen simultaneously:
1.  **Pancreatic:** Impaired expression of GLUT2 and pyruvate kinase leads to reduced insulin secretion capacity relative to glucose load.
2.  **Renal:** Reduced expression of SGLT2 lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, causing massive glycosuria even at modest blood glucose levels.

Historically, low-dose sulfonylureas have been the gold standard because they stimulate insulin release via ATP-sensitive potassium channels, bypassing some of the defective signaling pathways caused by the mutation. Patients with HNF1A-MODY are notoriously sensitive to sulfonylureas; doses required are often tiny compared to typical T2D regimens.

However, our case highlights a nuance often missed in textbooks: **beta-cell reserve.** Our patient had a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L post-glucagon challenge—a value that indicates preserved but insufficient function (>0.60 nmol/L is generally considered preserved). While sulfonylureas work well for stimulating remaining cells, they come with a steep price tag: hypoglycemia risk as beta-cell function inevitably declines over time. Furthermore, this specific patient had significant dyslipidemia (Triglycerides 4.31 mmol/L), making us wary of agents that might worsen metabolic parameters or simply fail if beta-cell exhaustion accelerates faster than anticipated.

### The GLP-1 Alternative: More Than Just Weight Loss

Why did we pivot to Dulaglutide? While sulfonylureas are first-line for many guidelines when beta-cell function is intact enough to respond, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a distinct advantage in HNF1A-MODY: **glucose-dependent action**. They stimulate insulin secretion only when glucose is high and suppress glucagon appropriately without driving hypoglycemia when levels drop—a crucial safety feature for patients whose renal threshold for glucose fluctuates wildly due to glycosuria losses.

Recent evidence suggests that GLP-1 RAs can be superior or equivalent to sulfonylureas in reducing fasting and postprandial excursions while offering cardiometabolic protection (improving lipids and weight). In this case study, replacing basal insulin with dulaglutide resulted in tighter control without the fear of nocturnal lows associated with high-sensitivity sulfonylurea therapy or fixed-dose insulin regimens designed for absolute deficiency states like T1DM.

It is also worth noting what *not* to do here: **Avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors.** Since HNF1A-MODY already causes profound downregulation of native SGLT2 activity leading to glycosuria and dehydration risk, adding an SGLT-2 inhibitor can precipitate severe volume depletion or euglycemic DKA in these fragile patients. The mechanism works against us here!

### De-Escalation Strategy: When You Have Insulin on Board

This case forces us to rethink our algorithmic thinking for young diabetics who don't fit neatly into boxes A or B. If you start on insulin because you suspect T2D or T1DM in a young patient with negative antibodies but strong family history:

*   **Do not wait** for genetic results before starting therapy if acute stabilization is needed (which we did here).
*   **Once genetics confirm MODY**, immediately reassess the necessity of exogenous insulin unless there is frank ketosis or extremely low C-peptide (<0.6 nmol/L).
*   **Transition logic:** If C-peptide shows preserved reserve (>0.6 nmol/L), consider stepping off basal insulin entirely once stable on oral/GLP-1 therapy rather than titrating down slowly over weeks while maintaining unnecessary coverage for non-existent absolute deficiency.

Our patient went from "basal + metformin + DPP-4" to "metformin + GLP-RA," then eventually stabilized on "metformin + DPP-4." The key takeaway wasn't just that he responded; it was that he never needed chronic insulin again once the correct pathophysiology was understood.

# The SGLT2 Trap: Why Renal Threshold Changes in HNF1A-MODY Contraindicate Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter Inhibitors

We've all seen the classic vignette: a lean young adult with "Type 2" diabetes who just doesn't fit the mold. They're thin, they have a strong family history, and their autoantibodies are negative. You think MODY. But then you see the lab work: **significant glycosuria** out of proportion to their blood glucose levels.

If your first instinct is to reach for an SGLT2 inhibitor to tackle that glycosuria and drop the A1C, stop right there. You might just be walking straight into a trap.

This case report from Liu et al. brings home a terrifyingly specific contraindication hidden in plain sight within the pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY. While SGLT2 inhibitors are the gold standard for modern Type 2 Diabetes management, in patients with Hepatocyte Nuclear Factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) mutations, these drugs can precipitate severe dehydration, euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), and even vascular collapse. Here's why understanding the renal physiology of MODY changes everything about how we treat it.

## The Pathophysiology of the "Leaky Kidney"

To understand why SGLT2 inhibitors are dangerous here, we have to look at what goes wrong in HNF1A-MODY at the cellular level. The *HNF1A* gene encodes a transcription factor expressed not only in the liver and pancreas but also in the proximal renal tubules. Its job? Regulating glucose transporters, specifically **SGLT2**.

In a healthy kidney, SGLT2 reabsorbs ~90% of filtered glucose back into the bloodstream until a threshold is reached (usually around 180 mg/dL or 10 mmol/L). Once that threshold is crossed, glucose spills into urine.

In HNF1A-MODY, however, the mutation leads to reduced expression of SGLT2 proteins on the brush border membrane of the proximal tubule. This means the renal threshold for glucose is drastically lowered—often well below normal physiologic limits—even when blood glucose levels are only mildly elevated or normal. This explains why our patient presented with massive glycosuria despite not being in frank DKA initially; his kidneys were dumping sugar simply because they lacked the machinery to hold onto it.

When you add an exogenous SGLT2 inhibitor to this mix—a drug designed to block whatever functional SGLT2 receptors remain—you are essentially turning off the last remaining brake on renal glucose reabsorption. The result isn't just "more sugar in urine"; it's a runaway osmotic diuresis that depletes intravascular volume rapidly.

## The Clinical Danger Zone: Dehydration and Euglycemic DKA

The case report details exactly how precarious this situation can get. Because HNF1A-MODY patients already have a low baseline renal threshold for glucose, adding an SGLT2 inhibitor creates a perfect storm for **severe dehydration**. The osmotic diuresis drives fluid loss faster than many patients can compensate, leading to pre-renal azotemia and potential vascular collapse.

Even more insidious is the risk of **euglycemic DKA**. In typical T2D or T1D treated with SGLT2 inhibitors, DKA usually presents with hyperglycemia (>250 mg/dL). But in HNF1A-MODY, because the kidneys cannot retain glucose regardless of insulin status (due to low SGLT2), serum glucose may remain relatively low while ketogenesis runs wild due to relative insulin deficiency and stress hormones triggered by volume depletion and catabolic state. Patients can present with pH <7.35 and bicarbonate <18 mEq/L with blood sugars often under 250 mg/dL—a scenario where clinicians frequently miss DKA because they aren't looking for it at "normal" glucose levels.

The authors explicitly warn that while these drugs offer benefits in metabolic syndrome via weight loss and glycemic control, **"the risks associated with using SGLT-2 inhibitors... may outweigh the potential benefits."** For a patient whose primary defect is already a failure of renal retention mechanisms, inhibiting them further is counter-intuitive and dangerous.
