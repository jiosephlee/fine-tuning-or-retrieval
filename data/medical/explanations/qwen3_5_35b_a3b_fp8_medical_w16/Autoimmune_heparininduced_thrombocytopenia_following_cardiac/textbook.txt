Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Dilemma: Early-Onset Thrombocytopenia Post-Cardiac Surgery

The clinical narrative begins with a man in his seventies presenting with exertional dyspnea and a long-standing history of atrial fibrillation managed with oral apixaban. Prior to his definitive cardiac intervention, he underwent a diagnostic heart catheterization via a transradial approach. Notably, because the patient had not withheld his antiplatelet therapy, the procedural team utilized a conservative anticoagulation strategy, administering only 2,000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH). One week later, the patient proceeded to complex open-heart surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), requiring a massive systemic dose of 45,000 units of UFH for anticoagulation during the procedure. The operation itself—an aortic valve replacement, ascending aorta grafting, coronary artery bypass, and left atrial appendage excision—proceeded without complication, and the patient demonstrated excellent hemodynamics upon separation from bypass. Initially, the postoperative course appeared reassuring; while the platelet count dropped from a baseline of 210×10⁹/L to 155×10⁹/L on postoperative day (POD) 1—a decline consistent with the physiological effects of CPB—the trajectory soon deviated from expectations.

Despite initiating standard thromboprophylaxis with dalteparin on POD 1 and escalating doses thereafter, the patient experienced a progressive and unexpected decline in platelet counts. By POD 5, the count had fallen to 50×10⁹/L. This "early-onset" thrombocytopenia presented a significant diagnostic dilemma because classic heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) typically manifests between POD 5 and POD 10 in patients receiving intraoperative heparin alone. A drop occurring prior to POD 5 raises suspicion for alternative etiologies such as sepsis, cardiogenic shock, or drug reactions to antibiotics like vancomycin or cephalosporins administered perioperatively. Furthermore, routine surgical stressors often cause transient thrombocytopenia that stabilizes quickly; however, this patient's counts continued to plummet despite clinical stability and negative infectious workups. The absence of fever, stable blood pressure rules out septic shock or cardiogenic collapse as primary drivers. Additionally, drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was deemed unlikely given the short latency period required for antibody formation against newly introduced agents like furosemide or metoprolol.

The critical clue resolving this diagnostic puzzle lay in tracing the timeline of heparin exposure back beyond the index surgery. The rapid onset of thrombocytopenia before POD 5 suggested that the immune system had already been primed by prior antigen exposure rather than reacting de novo to the intraoperative UFH load. Inquiry into the patient's history revealed that he had received UFH during the diagnostic catheterization one week earlier. In cases where patients receive heparin shortly before surgery (within days), they may possess circulating IgG antibodies capable of activating platelets immediately upon re-exposure during CPB or even during subsequent prophylactic dosing. This scenario explains why the platelet nadir occurred so rapidly; unlike classic HIT which requires several days for *de novo* antibody production (the "latent phase"), this patient suffered from accelerated immunity driven by pre-existing memory B-cell responses triggered by the preoperative catheterization. Consequently, what initially appeared as unexplained surgical complications evolved into a life-threatening hypercoagulable state culminating in an acute parietal stroke by POD 6 as platelet counts crashed further to below 25×10⁹/L. Recognizing this early onset pattern is essential for clinicians managing post-cardiac surgery patients; it shifts the differential diagnosis away from generic postoperative complications toward immune-mediated mechanisms triggered by antecedent heparin exposure.

## Pathophysiological Spectrum: Classic HIT vs. Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)

To fully appreciate the complexity of the presented case, one must distinguish between the conventional pathophysiology of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and the distinct mechanisms driving autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Classic HIT is fundamentally an immune reaction dependent on the formation of a specific antigenic complex. When unfractionated heparin (UFH) binds to platelet factor 4 (PF4), a cationic chemokine released from platelet alpha-granules, it creates large, positively charged complexes. In susceptible individuals, these complexes induce the production of pathogenic IgG antibodies. Upon binding to these PF4/heparin complexes, the Fc portion of the IgG antibody engages with the activating Fc gamma receptor IIa (FcγRIIa) on the platelet surface. This cross-linking triggers robust platelet activation, leading to aggregation, consumption of circulating platelets (thrombocytopenia), and a paradoxical hypercoagulable state that predisposes patients to arterial and venous thrombosis. Crucially, this activation is strictly heparin-dependent; at very high concentrations of heparin, the stoichiometry shifts so that each PF4 molecule is saturated with multiple heparin chains, preventing the formation of the multivalent lattice required for antibody bridging and subsequent Fc receptor clustering. Consequently, classic HIT assays typically show no platelet activation at supratherapeutic heparin doses (>100 U/mL).

In contrast, auto-immune HIT represents a more aggressive and mechanistically divergent subset where antibodies possess intrinsic capacity to activate platelets independent of exogenous heparin. While these antibodies still recognize PF4-heparin complexes under physiological conditions, they acquire or inherently possess structural features allowing them to bind directly to platelets or other cellular components even in the absence of significant heparin concentration. This "heparin-independent" activity means that in laboratory settings like the serotonin-release assay (SRA), serum from a patient with aHIT induces potent serotonin release even at zero added heparin. Furthermore, unlike classic HIT where antibody binding relies heavily on Fc receptor engagement that can be competitively blocked by specific monoclonal antibodies targeting the Fc region (such as IV.3), aHIT antibodies often demonstrate partial resistance to such blockade. In our patient's case, this manifested as persistent serotonin release despite the addition of an Fc-receptor blocking agent, suggesting that while Fc receptors contribute to activation, alternative pathways or higher avidity interactions allow for continued platelet stimulation. This hyper-reactivity explains why standard diagnostic algorithms might label such results "indeterminate," yet clinically they represent a profound deviation from classic HIT behavior.

The clinical implications of this pathophysiological shift are profound regarding severity and treatment response. Because aHIT antibodies drive continuous platelet activation regardless of circulating heparin levels or therapeutic anticoagulation strategies aimed solely at neutralizing free heparin-PF4 complexes, patients frequently present with more profound thrombocytopenia than seen in classic HIT cases. Our patient exemplifies this with a nadir platelet count dropping to 13×10⁹/L and signs of consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimers) despite appropriate anticoagulation with argatroban. The persistence of thrombocytopenia suggests that simply stopping heparin or switching to a different anticoagulant may be insufficient if the underlying driver is an antibody capable of autonomous activation; thus, adjunctive therapies like intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) are often employed to dampen this intense immune response by saturating macrophage Fc receptors or inhibiting complement fixation. Additionally, because classic HIT requires roughly 5 days post-exposure for antibody titers to reach pathological levels due to primary sensitization kinetics, early-onset thrombocytopenia (<POD5) usually implies prior exposure within weeks rather than new sensitization from current hospital care alone. However, in aHIT scenarios involving pre-existing immunity combined with hyper-reactive clones triggered by massive antigen load (as seen here after preoperative catheterization), clinicians must maintain suspicion even when timelines appear compressed or atypical for traditional HIT presentations. Understanding this spectrum allows clinicians to interpret "atypical" serological profiles not as errors in testing but as hallmarks of a distinct disease entity requiring heightened vigilance for severe complications like stroke and refractory thrombocytopenia.

## Advanced Serological Interpretation: Decoding Atypical SRA Profiles

In the diagnostic algorithm for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), the transition from a presumptive screen to a definitive confirmation often hinges on the interpretation of complex serological data. For this patient, the journey begins with the screening immunoassay, specifically the PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). While this test possesses high sensitivity, it lacks specificity, frequently yielding false positives in the postoperative setting due to non-immune binding of PF4 to inflammatory proteins or heparin released from tissue damage. However, in this case, the optical density was 2.2 units, far exceeding the typical cutoff of 0.4 units. Such a "strong-positive" titer significantly raises the pre-test probability of true HIT, particularly when correlated with a clinical scenario involving thrombosis and profound thrombocytopenia. Yet, relying solely on ELISA is insufficient; one must proceed to functional testing to distinguish between benign anti-PF4/heparin antibodies and those capable of activating platelets *in vivo*.

The gold standard for confirming HIT is the Serotonin Release Assay (SRA), which measures actual platelet activation induced by patient serum in the presence of varying heparin concentrations. Classic HIT presents with a distinct kinetic profile: minimal serotonin release at zero heparin (baseline), robust release at low therapeutic doses (e.g., 0.1 or 0.3 U/mL), and an abrupt cessation of release at supratherapeutic doses (e.g., 100 U/mL) where excess heparin prevents immune complex formation. This patient's SRA profile deviated sharply from this paradigm, leading to an initial classification as "indeterminate." Crucially, however, this deviation provided the key diagnostic clue for autoimmune HIT (aHIT). The patient's serum induced nearly maximal serotonin release (96%) even in the absolute absence of added exogenous heparin (0 U/mL). This finding indicates that the circulating antibodies possess intrinsic platelet-activating capabilities independent of heparin cofactors—a hallmark of aHIT where antibodies bind directly to native PF4 or other targets on the platelet surface without requiring polyanionic bridging.

To further characterize these atypical antibodies, laboratories employ an Fc receptor-blocking challenge using monoclonal antibody IV.3, which normally inhibits platelet activation mediated by IgG-FcγRIIa cross-linking in classic HIT. In typical cases, adding IV.3 suppresses serotonin release to background levels (<20%). In this case study, however, IV.3 demonstrated only partial resistance; while some inhibition occurred compared to baseline activation at low heparin concentrations, significant serotonin release persisted (76% release remained even with blocking agents present). This partial failure of blockade suggests that the pathogenic mechanism involves either a different subclass of IgG not fully blocked by IV.3 or alternative signaling pathways activated by these potent autoantibodies. Consequently, what appeared initially as an indeterminate result was re-evaluated not as a negative test for HIT, but as a specific signature of severe aHIT characterized by heparin-independent activation and resistance to standard Fc-receptor inhibition strategies.

Ultimately, recognizing these atypical kinetics transforms an ambiguous laboratory finding into a precise diagnosis. The combination of strong ELISA positivity paired with an SRA showing activation at zero heparin and incomplete response to Fc-blockade defines a unique subset of HIT with aggressive clinical behavior. Learners must appreciate that "indeterminate" results in functional assays should trigger deeper scrutiny rather than dismissal; in this context, they serve as critical indicators of severe autoimmune pathology requiring immediate therapeutic escalation beyond standard anticoagulation protocols alone.

## Therapeutic Decision-Making: Navigating Refractory Hypercoagulability

The cornerstone of managing heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is the immediate and absolute cessation of all heparin exposure, including unfractionated heparin (UFH), low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), and even small amounts found in flushes or catheter locks. In this case, the prompt discontinuation of dalteparin and the initiation of fondaparinux were critical first steps upon recognizing the precipitous drop in platelets and the development of a new stroke. However, the clinical picture evolved rapidly into a life-threatening scenario characterized by refractory hypercoagulability. Despite stopping the offending agent, the patient's condition deteriorated with a platelet nadir of 13×10⁹/L, falling fibrinogen levels, rising D-dimers, and the emergence of schistocytes on peripheral smear. These findings indicated that standard anticoagulation alone was failing to halt the massive consumption of clotting factors driven by potent autoantibodies—a hallmark of severe autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Consequently, therapeutic decision-making shifted toward aggressive intervention combining direct thrombin inhibition with immunomodulation to arrest the inflammatory cascade driving platelet activation.

Given the patient's concurrent acute ischemic stroke and profound thrombocytopenia, selecting an appropriate alternative anticoagulant required balancing efficacy against bleeding risk. While fondaparinux is often used off-label for HIT due to its predictable pharmacokinetics and lack of cross-reactivity with HIT antibodies, it relies on functional antithrombin (AT) activity to inhibit Factor Xa. Initial therapy involved switching from prophylactic to therapeutic doses of fondaparinux; however, concerns persisted regarding its ability to provide sufficient "brakes" on a hypercoagulable state fueled by Fc-receptor-independent mechanisms. Furthermore, because AT levels can be consumed in severe HIT or reduced post-operatively, reliance on indirect inhibitors like fondaparinux carries a risk of therapeutic failure if AT is depleted. Therefore, the team elected to transition to argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor (DTI) that does not require antithrombin as a cofactor. Argatroban was chosen specifically for its short half-life and titratability via activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), allowing rapid adjustment should bleeding complications arise in the setting of recent cerebral infarction.

Despite initiating therapeutic argatroban alongside high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), the patient demonstrated a paradoxical lack of response typical of classic HIT cases where IVIG induces a brisk platelet count recovery within days. Instead, this patient experienced only a modest rise in platelets despite maximal immunosuppression attempts with IVIG (totaling 300 g over three days). The persistence of falling fibrinogen and rising D-dimers suggested that argatroban was insufficient to fully suppress the underlying thrombotic drive associated with this specific serological phenotype—antibodies exhibiting partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade. This clinical stagnation necessitated another strategic pivot: reverting from argatroban back to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux. This decision was counterintuitive yet rationalized by addressing two key variables: first, ensuring adequate substrate availability by administering exogenous antithrombin concentrate prior to restarting fondaparinux; second, leveraging fondaparinux's mechanism as an indirect Factor Xa inhibitor combined with optimized AT levels to potentially achieve better control over the specific hypercoagulable pathway than DTIs provided in this context. Additionally, switching away from continuous IV infusion allowed for easier monitoring and administration while maintaining potent anticoagulation as platelet counts slowly began their eventual recovery trajectory toward normalization by postoperative day 62.
