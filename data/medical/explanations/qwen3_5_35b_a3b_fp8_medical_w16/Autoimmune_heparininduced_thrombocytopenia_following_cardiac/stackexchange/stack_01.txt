Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Morning Report] In a patient undergoing cardiac surgery who develops thrombocytopenia within the first 5 postoperative days, what specific historical detail distinguishes 'early-onset' heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) from other causes of postoperative platelet drop, and why does this timing challenge standard diagnostic assumptions?

The critical historical detail distinguishing early-onset heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) in this case from other causes of postoperative thrombocytopenia is **preoperative exposure to unfractionated heparin**, specifically the intraprocedural administration of 2,000 U of heparin during a diagnostic heart catheterization one week prior to surgery. 

This finding challenges the standard diagnostic assumption that "early" postoperative thrombocytopenia (within the first 5 days) cannot be immune-mediated because it requires a primary sensitization period. Classically, HIT antibodies require 5 to 10 days to develop de novo after initial heparin exposure; therefore, a platelet drop occurring immediately after surgery is often dismissed as mechanical destruction from cardiopulmonary bypass or sepsis. However, in patients with recent preoperative heparin contact, memory B-cells can rapidly produce high-titer antibodies upon re-exposure during the massive systemic anticoagulation required for cardiac surgery. As demonstrated in this case, the patient's "early" onset before postoperative day 5 was driven by this pre-existing sensitization rather than new antibody formation. Recognizing this history is vital because missing the diagnosis leads to continued heparin exposure (even if only low-molecular-weight heparin), which can precipitate catastrophic arterial or venous thrombosis, such as the parietal stroke seen here, whereas standard post-bypass thrombocytopenia typically resolves spontaneously without such severe complications.
