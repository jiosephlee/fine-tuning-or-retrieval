Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Pathophysiology] How do the serological findings in autoimmune HIT (aHIT) differ from classic HIT regarding the Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) profile at zero heparin concentration and the response to Fc receptor blockade, and what underlying mechanism explains these differences?

In classic heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), the Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) is strictly heparin-dependent; platelet activation requires the formation of immune complexes where anti-PF4/heparin antibodies cross-link platelet FcγRIIa receptors. Consequently, at **zero heparin concentration**, classic HIT sera induce negligible serotonin release (<20%), and the addition of an Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (such as IV.3) almost completely abolishes activation.

By contrast, this case illustrates **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)**, characterized by a distinct serological divergence. As seen in our patient's SRA profile, aHIT demonstrates **robust serotonin release even at zero heparin concentration** (96% release in this case). This indicates that the pathogenic antibodies possess intrinsic, heparin-independent platelet-activating capabilities, often recognizing epitopes on PF4 or other targets independent of polyanion binding. Furthermore, unlike classic HIT, aHIT antibodies can display **partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade**. While IV.3 typically inhibits >80% of activation in classic cases, our patient's serum retained significant activity (76% release) despite the blocker.

The underlying mechanism explaining these differences lies in the **avidity and structural nature of the autoantibodies**. In aHIT, the antibodies appear to have extremely high affinity or multivalent binding capacity that allows them to cluster Fc receptors directly or overcome the steric hindrance provided by blocking agents like IV.3. This "hyper-reactive" phenotype drives profound thrombocytopenia and severe thrombotic complications independent of exogenous heparin levels, distinguishing it mechanistically from the transient, complex-dependent activation of classic HIT.
