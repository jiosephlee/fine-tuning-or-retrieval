Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Differential Diagnosis] When evaluating a post-cardiac surgery patient with suspected HIT but an 'indeterminate' SRA result characterized by strong platelet activation at zero heparin and incomplete inhibition by anti-Fc receptor antibodies, how should clinicians interpret these results to differentiate autoimmune HIT from false positives or other drug reactions?

In this case, the "indeterminate" serotonin-release assay (SRA) result—characterized by robust platelet activation at zero heparin and only partial inhibition by the Fc-receptor blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3)—should be interpreted not as a failure of the assay or a false positive, but as a specific serological signature of **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)**.

Classically, HIT antibodies require the formation of large immune complexes involving platelet factor 4 (PF4), heparin, and IgG to cross-link platelet FcγRIIa receptors and trigger activation. Consequently, classic HIT SRAs show no activation in the absence of heparin and are almost completely inhibited (>80%) by agents like IV.3 that block Fc receptor engagement. In contrast, this patient's serum demonstrated strong serotonin release even at **0 U/mL heparin**, indicating that the circulating antibodies possess intrinsic, heparin-independent platelet-activating capacity. Furthermore, the **partial resistance** to IV.3 suggests that these autoantibodies may utilize alternative mechanisms for platelet activation or bind with such high avidity that they overcome competitive blockade, distinguishing them from typical drug-dependent antibodies.

This interpretation is critical because relying solely on strict SRA criteria can lead to misdiagnosis in complex post-cardiac surgery cases where preoperative heparin exposure creates a window for early-onset disease. As seen here, despite the atypical laboratory profile, the combination of profound thrombocytopenia (<50×10⁹/L), thrombotic complications (stroke), a strongly positive PF4 ELISA, and exclusion of other causes confirms aHIT. Clinicians must recognize that an "indeterminate" SRA in this context often represents a severe variant of HIT with distinct pathophysiology requiring aggressive management beyond standard anticoagulation alone.
