Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Dilemma: Early-Onset Thrombocytopenia Post-Cardiac Surgery

The clinical narrative begins with a man in his seventies presenting with exertional dyspnea and a long-standing history of atrial fibrillation managed with oral apixaban. Prior to his definitive cardiac intervention, he underwent a diagnostic heart catheterization via a transradial approach. Notably, because the patient had not withheld his antiplatelet therapy, the procedural team utilized a conservative anticoagulation strategy, administering only 2,000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH). One week later, the patient proceeded to complex open-heart surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), requiring a massive systemic dose of 45,000 units of UFH for anticoagulation during the procedure. The operation itself—an aortic valve replacement, ascending aorta grafting, coronary artery bypass, and left atrial appendage excision—proceeded without complication, and the patient demonstrated excellent hemodynamics upon separation from bypass. Initially, the postoperative course appeared reassuring; while the platelet count dropped from a baseline of 210×10⁹/L to 155×10⁹/L on postoperative day (POD) 1—a decline consistent with the physiological effects of CPB—the trajectory soon deviated from expectations.

Despite initiating standard thromboprophylaxis with dalteparin on POD 1 and escalating doses thereafter, the patient experienced a progressive and unexpected decline in platelet counts. By POD 5, the count had fallen to 50×10⁹/L. This "early-onset" thrombocytopenia presented a significant diagnostic dilemma because classic heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) typically manifests between POD 5 and POD 10 in patients receiving intraoperative heparin alone. A drop occurring prior to POD 5 raises suspicion for alternative etiologies such as sepsis, cardiogenic shock, or drug reactions to antibiotics like vancomycin or cephalosporins administered perioperatively. Furthermore, routine surgical stressors often cause transient thrombocytopenia that stabilizes quickly; however, this patient's counts continued to plummet despite clinical stability and negative infectious workups. The absence of fever, stable blood pressure rules out septic shock or cardiogenic collapse as primary drivers. Additionally, drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was deemed unlikely given the short latency period required for antibody formation against newly introduced agents like furosemide or metoprolol.

The critical clue resolving this diagnostic puzzle lay in tracing the timeline of heparin exposure back beyond the index surgery. The rapid onset of thrombocytopenia before POD 5 suggested that the immune system had already been primed by prior antigen exposure rather than reacting de novo to the intraoperative UFH load. Inquiry into the patient's history revealed that he had received UFH during the diagnostic catheterization one week earlier. In cases where patients receive heparin shortly before surgery (within days), they may possess circulating IgG antibodies capable of activating platelets immediately upon re-exposure during CPB or even during subsequent prophylactic dosing. This scenario explains why the platelet nadir occurred so rapidly; unlike classic HIT which requires several days for *de novo* antibody production (the "latent phase"), this patient suffered from accelerated immunity driven by pre-existing memory B-cell responses triggered by the preoperative catheterization. Consequently, what initially appeared as unexplained surgical complications evolved into a life-threatening hypercoagulable state culminating in an acute parietal stroke by POD 6 as platelet counts crashed further to below 25×10⁹/L. Recognizing this early onset pattern is essential for clinicians managing post-cardiac surgery patients; it shifts the differential diagnosis away from generic postoperative complications toward immune-mediated mechanisms triggered by antecedent heparin exposure.
