Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 4: ## Therapeutic Decision-Making: Navigating Refractory Hypercoagulability

The cornerstone of managing heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is the immediate and absolute cessation of all heparin exposure, including unfractionated heparin (UFH), low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), and even small amounts found in flushes or catheter locks. In this case, the prompt discontinuation of dalteparin and the initiation of fondaparinux were critical first steps upon recognizing the precipitous drop in platelets and the development of a new stroke. However, the clinical picture evolved rapidly into a life-threatening scenario characterized by refractory hypercoagulability. Despite stopping the offending agent, the patient's condition deteriorated with a platelet nadir of 13×10⁹/L, falling fibrinogen levels, rising D-dimers, and the emergence of schistocytes on peripheral smear. These findings indicated that standard anticoagulation alone was failing to halt the massive consumption of clotting factors driven by potent autoantibodies—a hallmark of severe autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Consequently, therapeutic decision-making shifted toward aggressive intervention combining direct thrombin inhibition with immunomodulation to arrest the inflammatory cascade driving platelet activation.

Given the patient's concurrent acute ischemic stroke and profound thrombocytopenia, selecting an appropriate alternative anticoagulant required balancing efficacy against bleeding risk. While fondaparinux is often used off-label for HIT due to its predictable pharmacokinetics and lack of cross-reactivity with HIT antibodies, it relies on functional antithrombin (AT) activity to inhibit Factor Xa. Initial therapy involved switching from prophylactic to therapeutic doses of fondaparinux; however, concerns persisted regarding its ability to provide sufficient "brakes" on a hypercoagulable state fueled by Fc-receptor-independent mechanisms. Furthermore, because AT levels can be consumed in severe HIT or reduced post-operatively, reliance on indirect inhibitors like fondaparinux carries a risk of therapeutic failure if AT is depleted. Therefore, the team elected to transition to argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor (DTI) that does not require antithrombin as a cofactor. Argatroban was chosen specifically for its short half-life and titratability via activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), allowing rapid adjustment should bleeding complications arise in the setting of recent cerebral infarction.

Despite initiating therapeutic argatroban alongside high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), the patient demonstrated a paradoxical lack of response typical of classic HIT cases where IVIG induces a brisk platelet count recovery within days. Instead, this patient experienced only a modest rise in platelets despite maximal immunosuppression attempts with IVIG (totaling 300 g over three days). The persistence of falling fibrinogen and rising D-dimers suggested that argatroban was insufficient to fully suppress the underlying thrombotic drive associated with this specific serological phenotype—antibodies exhibiting partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade. This clinical stagnation necessitated another strategic pivot: reverting from argatroban back to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux. This decision was counterintuitive yet rationalized by addressing two key variables: first, ensuring adequate substrate availability by administering exogenous antithrombin concentrate prior to restarting fondaparinux; second, leveraging fondaparinux's mechanism as an indirect Factor Xa inhibitor combined with optimized AT levels to potentially achieve better control over the specific hypercoagulable pathway than DTIs provided in this context. Additionally, switching away from continuous IV infusion allowed for easier monitoring and administration while maintaining potent anticoagulation as platelet counts slowly began their eventual recovery trajectory toward normalization by postoperative day 62.
