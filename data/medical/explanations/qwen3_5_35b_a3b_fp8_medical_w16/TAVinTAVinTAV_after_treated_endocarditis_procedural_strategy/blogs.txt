# Triple Trouble: Navigating the Anatomy of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV Procedure

Let's talk about "lifetime management" in structural heart disease. For years, we've accepted that if a surgical valve fails, you go back to the OR. If a TAVI fails, we now have the robust toolkit of TAV-in-TAV. But what happens when the math gets weird? What happens when you run out of room in the box?

Today's case isn't just a "redo." It is a **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV** performed on a septuagenarian with a history of treated prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). We aren't just stacking metal; we are navigating a crowded annulus where every millimeter counts, coronary ostia heights are measured in fractions of centimeters, and the effective orifice area (EOA) is fighting for its life against three overlapping stent frames.

This case from Merkulov et al. is a masterclass in "plan before you puncture." It challenges our assumptions about what is technically feasible and reminds us that in these complex triple-stent scenarios, your pre-procedural CT isn't just a nice-to-have—it's the difference between a successful intervention and catastrophic coronary occlusion.

## The Geometry of Desperation: Why Three Valves is Harder Than You Think

Imagine trying to fit three nested Russian dolls into a space designed for one. That is essentially what happens during a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV. Every time you deploy a new valve inside an old one, you lose radial freedom. The inner diameter shrinks, creating a funnel effect that drastically increases the risk of Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM).

In this specific case, the patient had undergone two prior interventions:
1.  **2014:** Initial Lotus valve (Boston Scientific).
2.  **2022:** TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut Pro (Medtronic) due to severe stenosis (gradient up to 83 mmHg).
3.  **The Complication:** In September 2022, he developed *Enterococcus faecalis* prosthetic valve endocarditis. After completing antibiotics and confirming eradication via TEE and CT, he returned in 2024 with severe restenosis again (mean gradient 49 mmHg).

The decision to proceed with a third valve wasn't taken lightly. The surgical risk was prohibitive (EuroSCORE II ~27%), and the anatomy was hostile due to deep fixation of previous valves into the aortic wall. This left us with one option: another transcatheter solution. But here is where it gets tricky: **frame interaction.**

When you stack frames, you create "neo-skirts"—the protruding material below the main stent struts—which can easily obstruct coronary flow if not meticulously planned. In this patient, the minimum lumen diameter within the Evolut frame was already tight at 13.7 mm before the third valve went in. Adding an Acurate Neo2 meant we were dealing with extreme geometric constraints where even minor misalignment could lead to immediate hemodynamic collapse or permanent loss of coronary access.

## Strategy Over Brute Force: The Art of High Implantation and Commissural Alignment

So, how did they pull this off without blowing up coronaries or leaving the patient with severe PPM? They didn't rely on brute force; they relied on **anatomical precision**.

The team utilized advanced Multislice CT (MSCT) modeling to map out exactly where every strut sat relative to the coronary ostia. The goal was twofold: preserve coronary flow and maximize EOA. To achieve this, they employed two critical technical maneuvers:

1.  **High Implantation Strategy:** Instead of sinking deep into the native annulus or relying on standard anchoring zones, they deliberately deployed the third valve at the level of the *previous* Evolut leaflets ("high"). This served two purposes: it fixed any remaining flail leaflets against the stent frame to prevent inward folding (which eats up EOA), and crucially, it kept the new valve frame away from the coronary ostia by minimizing neo-skirt formation below them.
2.  **Commissural Alignment:** Using pre-procedural CT data combined with fluoroscopic landmarks, they aligned the commissures of the Acurate Neo2 specifically to match the Evolut frame orientation. Why does this matter? Because in triple-valve scenarios, future access is everything. Misaligned commissures can make accessing either coronary artery impossible down the line without complex wire manipulation or even surgical bypass later on.

The procedure itself was elegant in its execution but risky in its setup:
*   **Access:** Radial + Femoral hybrid approach for stability and maneuverability.
*   **Pre-dilation:** Aggressive ballooning with rapid pacing to ensure smooth delivery without dislodging layers prematurely.
*   **Deployment:** Careful tracking through three distinct stent meshes into position overleaflets rather than deep annulus anchoring initially suggested by standard protocols for single valves.
*   **Post-Dilation:** High-pressure balloon inflation was used not just for seating, but likely intended to fracture or expand underlying rigid frames slightly to improve flow dynamics—a subtle but vital step often overlooked in simple TAVIs but essential here to overcome resistance from stacked metalwork.

Interestingly, despite excellent deployment mechanics and zero regurgitation immediately post-op, there was an alarming finding: **Severe PPM** right out of the gate (Mean Gradient 67 mmHg! AVA 0.9 cm²!). This highlights that sometimes your best plan yields suboptimal numbers immediately because you simply cannot fit enough tissue area into that tiny hole without compromising structure or coronaries entirely! However—and this is key—the gradient dropped significantly by day 30 (to 27 mmHg) as remodeling occurred and frames settled perfectly together without causing obstruction or thrombosis at follow-up intervals up to one year later (stable mean gradient ~26 mmHg).

## Key Takeaways for Your Next Complex Re-do Case

We are entering an era where patients live longer after their first TAVI than ever before inevitably leading to these "stacked" scenarios more frequently than anyone predicted five years ago here are some non-negotiable pearls from this case report:

*   **"No Active Endocarditis" is Mandatory:** Do not attempt serial TAVIs until infection is fully cleared with negative cultures and imaging confirmation via TEE/CT showing no vegetations or paravalvular abscesses; operating through active inflammation leads to disaster regardless of technical skill!
*   **CT Modeling is Non-Negotiable:** You cannot eyeball triple-stent interactions; precise measurement of valve-to-coronary distances and prediction of neo-skirt geometry requires dedicated software analysis prior to cutting skin!
*   **Embrace High Implantation When Necessary:** In crowded annuli forcing deep placement risks crushing coronaries; consider deploying higher up along existing leaflet planes if it preserves EOA and keeps metal away from ostia!
*   **Expect Immediate Hemodynamic Stress:** Even perfect tech may yield scary gradients initially due to restricted flow paths; monitor closely as frames settle over weeks/months rather than declaring failure based solely on Day-1 echo data!
*   **Future Access Planning Trumps Current Optimization:** Always prioritize commissural alignment that allows potential future PCI even if it means accepting slightly less-than-perfect current hemodynamics; remember tomorrow's surgeon needs tomorrow's wires too!

## Final Thoughts on Lifetime Management Strategies

This case serves as both inspiration and cautionary tale regarding our evolving role as operators managing structural heart disease throughout entire lifetimes rather than single events anymore! While ESC guidelines still favor surgery for PVE whenever possible we recognize real-world constraints often leave us choosing between bad options versus worse ones making careful selection paramount especially considering individualized risk factors like COPD diabetes prior GI bleeds etcetera... Ultimately though success hinges upon meticulous planning multidisciplinary heart teams working together seamlessly utilizing cutting-edge imaging technologies alongside traditional wisdom gained decades ago... So next time someone asks whether doing yet another TAVI makes sense ask yourself first Can I see clearly enough second Will my hands hold steady enough third Does my brain understand exactly what lies beneath those shiny metallic surfaces... If yes then maybe just maybe we can keep pushing boundaries safely forward toward better outcomes overall!



# To Re-Treat or Not? Managing Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis After Multiple TAVIs

Let's talk about the "Triple Crown" of transcatheter complexity: **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV**.

We've all seen the horror stories of coronary occlusion in a standard valve-in-valve, and we've grappled with the anatomy of a failed surgical bioprosthesis. But imagine a patient who has already survived two prior TAVI procedures, developed prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), survived aggressive antibiotic therapy, and now presents with a failing third valve. Is this a surgical nightmare waiting to happen, or is there a place for another catheter intervention?

This case report from Merkulov et al. isn't just a procedural feat; it's a masterclass in risk stratification and the evolving definition of "inoperability." It forces us to confront a difficult question: **When does the history of infection become an absolute contraindication for TAVI?**

### The Dilemma: Surgery vs. Catheter in the Setting of Prior Endocarditis

The 2025 ESC guidelines are clear: Surgical valve replacement is generally preferred for Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE), especially when heart failure is present or infection is uncontrolled. The logic is sound—debridement removes infected tissue that antibiotics cannot reach. However, this patient was not "uncontrolled." He had completed 6 weeks of IV ampicillin for *Enterococcus faecalis*, followed by oral suppression, with sterile cultures and no vegetations on Transesophageal Echocardiogram (TEE).

Here lies the crux of the case. If you treat every PVE patient as a mandatory surgical candidate regardless of their frailty, you deny life-saving therapy to those who simply cannot survive open heart surgery. This gentleman had a EuroSCORE II of **27.36%**, COPD, diabetes, recurrent GI bleeds, and two prior TAVIs deeply integrated into his aortic wall. His surgical risk wasn't just "high"; it was prohibitive. A redo sternotomy would likely require total root replacement—a mortality risk so astronomical that the Heart Team rightly pivoted back to catheterization.

**The Takeaway:** A history of treated PVE is not an automatic exclusion for TAVI if strict criteria are met: eradicated infection (negative cultures), resolution of vegetation on imaging, and prohibitive surgical risk.

### The Anatomy Nightmare: Layering Three Frames

Once we agreed on proceeding with TAVI, the real challenge began: how do you fit *three* valves inside one annulus without crushing the coronaries?

The patient had a 27mm Lotus valve (2014) → 23mm Evolut (2022) → and now needed an Acurate Neo2 (2024).
The risks here are twofold:
1.  **Coronary Obstruction:** Every new stent frame reduces the space available for coronary ostia access.
2.  **Severe Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM):** Stacking metal layers eats up your effective orifice area (EOA).

In this case, they achieved success through meticulous CT-guided planning. They didn't just drop a valve in; they utilized **high implantation** techniques. By anchoring the Acurate Neo2 at the level of the previous Evolut leaflets rather than deep in the annulus, they prevented inward folding of old leaflets and minimized "neo-skirt" formation near the coronaries. This strategic depth placement preserved coronary flow without needing protective wires or balloons initially—a bold move that paid off handsomely.

It's worth noting that despite immediate post-op gradients looking scary (Mean gradient ~67 mmHg!), these normalized over time as the frames expanded and leaflets mobilized. This highlights why we shouldn't panic over immediate hemodynamics in complex re-do cases; tissue remodeling takes time.

### Why This Matters for Your Practice

This case pushes our comfort zone regarding "lifetime management" of structural heart disease. As TAVI populations age less rapidly and live longer due to better devices, we will see more patients cycling through multiple interventions before hitting biological limits or anatomical impossibility.

Key lessons from this triple-intervention saga include:
*   **Imaging is non-negotiable:** You cannot navigate triple-stented anatomy with echo alone. Multislice CT must map every millimeter of stent overlap relative to coronary ostia height and sinus dimensions *before* you touch a wire.
*   **"Cured" PVE is manageable:** Once infection is cleared and confirmed by multimodal imaging (blood + TEE + CT), the pathophysiology shifts back to structural degeneration rather than active sepsis, making catheter salvage viable even after prior endocarditis events.
*   **Device Selection Strategy:** In tight spaces with multiple frames, choosing devices with low profile heights (like Acurate Neo) and supra-annular designs helps maximize EOA where possible, though some degree of PPM may be inevitable in these extreme scenarios.
*   **Future Proofing:** Commissural alignment becomes exponentially harder with each layer but remains critical for future coronary access or potential leaflet laceration/BASILICA procedures down the line.

### Bottom Line

This patient walked away from his third TAVI asymptomatic at one year with preserved LV function—a result that would have been impossible if he had waited for open surgery he couldn't tolerate or died from untreated heart failure while awaiting clearance for high-risk operations. It serves as a powerful reminder that while guidelines provide frameworks, individualized Heart Team decisions based on rigorous imaging and infection status can unlock doors we thought were permanently sealed shut.

# The Coronary Minefield: Preventing Obstruction in Sequential Transcatheter Reinterventions

Let's be honest: the "Valve-in-Valve" (ViV) concept revolutionized structural heart care. But what happens when the valve fails *again*? Or *three times*?

We are entering an era where patients survive longer after their first TAVI, meaning we are inevitably seeing more structural valve deterioration. This has birthed the "Valve-in-Valve-in-Valve" (ViV-ViV) scenario. It sounds like a procedural nightmare, and frankly, it often is. But today, we aren't just talking about getting a third valve in; we are talking about navigating a **coronary minefield** where every additional stent frame increases the risk of catastrophic coronary obstruction.

Consider this recent case report of a septuagenarian male who survived three distinct TAVI procedures over a decade, including a stint with infective endocarditis. His journey isn't just a testament to endurance; it's a masterclass in why pre-procedural CT planning is now the single most critical step in serial interventions. When you stack three metal cages inside your aortic root, the geometry changes so drastically that standard assumptions no longer apply. If you don't measure twice and cut once, you're playing Russian Roulette with the left main coronary artery.

## The Anatomy of Chaos: Why Layering Matters

In our first ViV procedure, we usually worry about the existing bioprosthesis frame blocking the coronaries during deployment or post-dilation. That's hard enough. But add a second layer (the first ViV), and suddenly you have two nested frames interacting with your own native anatomy. Add a third layer—the subject of this case—and you are dealing with complex interactions between three different stent designs (Lotus, Evolut, and Acurate neo2).

The primary fear here is **coronary obstruction**. As layers accumulate, they effectively shorten the space available for blood flow from the sinuses to the ostia. In this patient's case, the team didn't guess; they mapped it out using Multislice Computed Tomography (MSCT). They specifically measured:
*   **Coronary Ostial Height:** How close are your arteries sitting to the top of these stacked frames?
*   **Sinus of Valsalva Dimensions:** Is there room for maneuvering?
*   **Valve-to-Coronary Distance:** The critical metric that determines if your new valve skirt will physically plug up your left main stem.

Without this granular data, proceeding to implant an Acurate neo2 in a triple-stented environment would have been reckless. The CT didn't just show us *where* to put the valve; it told us *how high* to place it to avoid creating a "neo-skirt" that seals off coronary inflow.

## Strategic Deployment: Beyond Just "In and Out"

So, how do you safely navigate this maze? In this specific case, the Heart Team employed several high-level strategies that go beyond simple balloon inflation and release.

First came **commissural alignment**. You might think this is mostly for aesthetics or reducing future PPM issues, but in serial ViVs, aligning the new valve's commissures relative to the previous ones is crucial for maintaining clear pathways for future coronary access. If you deploy haphazardly, you might accidentally trap a coronary ostium behind a rigid strut of an older frame that wasn't fully expanded until now.

Second was the decision on **implantation depth**. The team opted for high implantation—specifically aligning the new Acurate neo2 leaflets with the leaflets of the underlying Evolut frame rather than sinking deep into the annulus. Why? Because dropping too low could cause inward folding of leaflets or compress existing struts against the coronary ostia. By keeping it high, they ensured fixation against existing leaflets while maximizing effective orifice area (EOA) and preserving that vital gap above coronaries for future access.

Finally, they utilized **balloon post-dilation** strategically. While intentional frame fracture wasn't performed here (which carries its own risks), high-pressure ballooning helped expand the prior Evolut frame radially without crushing it against the coronary arteries, optimizing flow dynamics immediately post-op despite initial concerns about severe Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM).

## The Endocarditis Variable: Timing is Everything

One detail that makes this case particularly instructive is the history of Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE). Historically, operating on infected tissue was taboo unless absolutely necessary due to sepsis risk or abscess formation. However, guidelines emphasize individualization based on infection status and surgical risk.

In this patient, by 2024 (five years after his last TAVI), he had completed antibiotics for *Enterococcus faecalis*, had negative blood cultures repeatedly, and showed no vegetations on TEE or MSCT before his third intervention. Crucially, he did not require long-term suppressive therapy at that moment because infection was deemed resolved. This cleared him for intervention based on hemodynamics rather than infection status alone—but it underscored that even "clean" fields after endocarditis can lead to rapid degeneration due to mechanical stress and scarring from prior inflammation. It reminds us that treating PVE doesn't always solve long-term durability issues if structural degeneration is already underway or accelerated by prior damage.

## Key Takeaways for Your Practice

Serial transcatheter interventions are becoming reality as our population ages and survives longer post-TAVI. Here is what we learned from this triple-intervention survivor:

*   **CT Planning is Non-Negotiable:** In any ViV-ViV scenario, standard echo assessments are insufficient你必须 rely on detailed MSCT analysis of coronary heights and valve-to-coronary distances before cutting skin or inflating balloons.
*   **High Implantation Strategy:** Consider deploying new valves higher within previous frames to maximize EOA and prevent compression of coronary ostia by lower stent skirts or folded leaflets.
*   **Commissural Alignment Matters More Than Ever:** Aligning newer valves with older frames preserves future coronary access routes and reduces procedural complexity down the line when things eventually fail again (if they do).
*   **Infection Status Dictates Timing:** Ensure absolute resolution of endocarditis (negative cultures + imaging clearance) before attempting another catheter-based repair; do not rush intervention solely based on gradients if active infection persists or immunocompromise exists from prior treatments without suppression plans being ready (though suppression isn't needed if cured).
*   **Manage Expectations Regarding PPM:** Even with perfect technique, stacking valves inherently reduces EOA leading to potential PPM initially; monitor closely as remodeling occurs over weeks/months as seen in this patient's trajectory from severe PPM at day 1 to stability at 1 year.
*   **Future Access Planning:** Every time you add metal layers inside an artery/root system make sure there remains some possibility ever accessing those vessels again via PCI should ischemia develop later on—sometimes sacrificing immediate optimization slightly helps preserve future options!

This case proves that while "lifetime management" sounds daunting when looking at three stacked valves in one person today's technology allows us to push boundaries safely—if we respect anatomy above all else next time someone asks whether another TAVI is possible remember: check your CTs measure everything twice plan meticulously then maybe... just maybe—you can save them yet another life without opening their chest again tomorrow morning!



# Beyond Survival: The Imperative for Lifetime Planning in Structural Heart Disease

Let's talk about the "endless hallway" of modern structural heart disease. For years, our goal was simple: get the valve in, fix the stenosis, and send the patient home. But as the TAVI population ages and survives longer, we are encountering a new reality: the patient who needs *three* valves.

We recently reviewed a staggering case report from Moscow describing a septuagenarian male who underwent **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV**. To put that in perspective, this wasn't just a routine redo; it was a third transcatheter intervention in a patient with a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), severe coronary disease, and prohibitive surgical risk. He started with a Lotus valve in 2014, moved to an Evolut Pro in 2022 (after which he unfortunately developed *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis), and finally received an Acurate Neo2 in 2024.

What makes this case so chilling—and yet so instructive—isn't just that it worked (he is doing well at one year); it's that every single step required navigating a minefield of anatomical constraints created by previous interventions. This isn't just about fixing a broken valve today; it's about realizing that the decision we make on Day 1 dictates whether our patients have any options left on Day 10.

### The Anatomy of Complexity

Imagine standing over an angiogram table looking at three concentric rings of metal stents crisscrossing your field of view. That was the landscape for this patient. When you stack valves, you aren't just adding layers; you are creating "neo-skirts," narrowing effective orifice areas (EOA), and threatening coronary ostia with unprecedented frequency.

In this specific case, the team faced a classic dilemma: how to fit a third valve inside two existing ones without occluding the coronaries? The answer lay in **high-implantation strategy** combined with meticulous CT planning. By anchoring the new Acurate Neo2 at the level of the middle Evolut leaflets rather than lower down, they prevented inward folding of leaflets (which causes severe PPM) and kept the new frame away from the coronary ostia.

If they had chosen differently—say, if they had opted for a different platform earlier or ignored commissural alignment—the result could have been catastrophic coronary obstruction or severe patient-prosthesis mismatch right out of the gate. The fact that they achieved this without needing BASILICA or other extreme rescue maneuvers is remarkable, but it underscores how fragile these margins are becoming with each successive intervention.

### The Endocarditis Elephant in the Room

Another layer of complexity here is the history of infection. The patient developed PVE shortly after his second TAVI (*E. faecalis*). Historically, once you've had infected hardware removed surgically, you're often told "no more catheters." However, this case challenges that dogma when surgery is truly impossible.

The key takeaway isn't that TAVI is safe for active endocarditis—it absolutely isn't—but rather that **once infection is eradicated**, the pathway opens up again if anatomy permits. This patient completed six weeks of IV antibiotics and had negative workups before proceeding to his third intervention. It forces us to ask: Are we too quick to close doors on catheter-based salvage for patients who simply cannot survive another sternotomy? In this case, refusing TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV would have meant certain death via surgery; choosing it offered hope for continued life.

### Why Your First Decision Matters Most

This brings us back to our core message: **Lifetime Management.**

When we select a valve today—whether it's a self-expanding Evolut or an older generation Lotus—we aren't just picking a device for five years; we are picking a trajectory for twenty years.
*   Did we choose a valve profile that allows future coaxial delivery?
*   Is there enough room above for another frame?
*   Will commissural alignment facilitate future access?
*   Have we documented the exact implantation depth relative to coronaries?

In this patient's journey, each step narrowed his options until only one path remained: another catheter-based solution guided by advanced imaging. If he had been able to tolerate surgery after his first failure (the Lotus), perhaps he wouldn't be stuck in this triple-stent nightmare today. Or conversely, if his first TAVI had been done with less regard for future geometry, he might never have survived to need a third one without immediate mortality risk from coronary occlusion.

### Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

As we move forward into an era where "one-and-done" is no longer guaranteed for many patients, keep these principles front-of-mind during your heart team meetings:

*   **Plan Backwards:** When selecting a primary TAVI device and sizing strategy, explicitly consider "what happens if this fails?" Does this frame allow for future oversizing or re-access without compromising coronary flow?
*   **Imaging is Non-Negotiable:** You cannot navigate TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV blindfolded. Multislice CT must be used not just for sizing now, but specifically to model frame interactions and predict coronary clearance zones for *future* implants.
*   **Commissural Alignment = Future Access:** Aligning commissures correctly during every stage preserves rotational freedom and ensures future operators can find their way back through the meshwork if re-do becomes necessary years later.
*   **Endocarditis Doesn't Always Mean Surgery:** Once infection is cleared and surgical risk remains prohibitive, catheter-based salvage may be viable even after multiple prior interventions—provided anatomy allows it.
*   **Document Everything:** Every millimeter matters when stacking valves detailed records of implant depth relative to annulus height are crucial data points for anyone attempting intervention #3 or #4 down the road.

This case serves as both an inspiration and a warning shot across our bows. We have extended lives significantly through TAVI technology; now we must extend our thinking beyond acute success to ensure those lives don't hit dead ends because we didn't plan ahead today's procedure affects tomorrow's possibilities more than ever before.
