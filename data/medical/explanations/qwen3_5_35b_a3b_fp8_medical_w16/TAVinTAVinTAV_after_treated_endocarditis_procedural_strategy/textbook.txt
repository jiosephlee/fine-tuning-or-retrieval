Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Clinical Presentation and Complex Historical Context

The patient presenting for this case report is a male in his late 70s whose clinical trajectory exemplifies the escalating complexity of "lifetime" management in structural heart disease. Upon admission to the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center, he presented with classic symptoms of advanced aortic stenosis and heart failure, specifically exertional dyspnea, dizziness, and fatigue. Notably, he described a distinct spastic sensation in the left side of the chest during physical exertion, limiting his walking distance to approximately 200–300 meters. These symptoms signaled a critical hemodynamic compromise that necessitated immediate reassessment of his valvular status. Understanding this presentation requires recognizing that these are not isolated complaints but the culmination of decades of cardiovascular morbidity and multiple prior interventions.

The foundation of this patient's high-risk profile lies in a dense constellation of comorbidities that have progressively narrowed therapeutic options over time. His medical history includes severe three-vessel coronary artery disease, which required multiple percutaneous coronary interventions between 2009 and 2014 involving the right coronary artery, left anterior descending (LAD) artery, and circumflex artery. Furthermore, he suffers from third-degree atrioventricular block requiring permanent pacemaker implantation in 2014, alongside type 2 diabetes mellitus and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Complicating matters further is a history of recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022. This multimorbidity creates a physiological landscape where any invasive procedure carries substantial risk, effectively rendering him ineligible for standard surgical approaches due to prohibitive operative mortality predicted by scoring systems like EuroSCORE II.

To comprehend why this patient required a third transcatheter intervention one must trace the chronology of his valvular treatments. In 2014, facing severe degenerative aortic stenosis and elevated surgical risk, he underwent his first Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation (TAVI) utilizing a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). Over the ensuing years, structural valve deterioration occurred; by late 2021, echocardiography confirmed severe bioprosthetic stenosis with a mean gradient reaching 83 mm Hg and a concomitant decline in left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) to 42%. Consequently, in May 2022, he underwent a second intervention: a TAV-in-TAV procedure with the implantation of a 23 mm Evolut prosthesis (Medtronic). While this successfully restored forward flow—evidenced by an EF recovery to 54%—the procedure itself was complicated by significant vascular injury requiring open surgical repair for a femoral pseudoaneurysm.

However, the clinical narrative took an acute turn just three months later in September 2022 when the patient returned with persistent fever and worsening heart failure. This episode marked the onset of Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE), diagnosed through positive blood cultures for *Enterococcus faecalis* and echocardiographic findings of severe restenosis (mean gradient 35 mm Hg). The management strategy shifted acutely toward infection control; the patient received a rigorous six-week course of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy followed by oral antibiotics per infectious disease guidelines. Crucially, subsequent evaluations using transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice computed tomography (MSCT) confirmed the eradication of infection with no evidence of active vegetation or paravalvular leak at the time planning resumed for definitive valve correction.

Despite successful antimicrobial treatment and temporary symptom relief, the underlying mechanical problem persisted. By January 2024, follow-up assessments revealed that while infection was cleared, prosthetic dysfunction had recurred with severe stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean gradient stood at 49 mm Hg with an calculated Aortic Valve Area (AVA) of merely 0.5 cm² derived from Doppler velocity-time integrals via the continuity equation. Echocardiography visualized two overlapping bioprosthetic frames—the original Lotus and the secondary Evolut—creating a highly constrained flow channel. This final pre-reintervention assessment underscored that despite two prior successful transcatheter attempts and resolved sepsis, the patient faced life-threatening hemodynamic collapse due to cumulative structural failure in an environment where surgical revision was deemed impossible due to deep stent integration into the aortic wall and extreme frailty.

## Pathophysiology of Sequential Structural Valve Deterioration

The failure of a transcatheter aortic valve in a "TAV-in-TAV" or "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV" scenario represents a convergence of distinct pathological processes that differ significantly from the natural history of native valve disease or isolated surgical valve replacement. In the case presented, the patient's final intervention addressed a complex interplay of standard structural valve deterioration (SVD), the mechanical constraints imposed by multiple overlapping stent frames, and sequelae of prior infective endocarditis. Understanding these mechanisms is critical for appreciating why each successive reintervention presents escalating technical hurdles and why the physiological reserve required to tolerate them diminishes rapidly.

Standard structural valve deterioration in bioprosthetic transcatheter valves typically manifests through three primary pathways: accelerated calcification, leaflet fibrosis, and thrombotic occlusion. Over time, the xenograft tissue undergoes degenerative changes characterized by calcium deposition on the leaflets, leading to restricted motion and stenosis. Concurrently, chronic inflammation can induce fibrotic thickening and retraction of the leaflets, reducing their coaptation surface area and predisposing to regurgitation. Thrombosis, often subclinical initially, can accumulate on the non-endothelialized surfaces of the stent frame or within the interstitial spaces between leaflets, acting as a nidus for further stiffening or embolization. In this patient's timeline, these age-dependent processes were likely exacerbated by hemodynamic stressors inherent to the TAVI procedure itself, such as high radial forces applied during deployment which can cause micro-trauma to the tissue over years. The progression from mild stenosis to severe obstruction (mean gradient rising from 83 mm Hg in 2022 to 49 mm Hg in 2024) reflects this cumulative biological degradation of the bioprosthesis material.

However, the pathophysiology becomes uniquely complex when multiple devices are stacked within a single annulus. Each additional transcatheter valve introduces a new metallic scaffold that occupies space previously available for blood flow or native tissue expansion. This creates a "stacking effect," where the innermost stent frames constrain one another and limit the ability of newer leaflets to fully expand. The effective orifice area (EOA) is determined not just by the nominal size of the new valve but by the internal diameter remaining after accounting for all previous metal layers. In this case, despite implanting a third-generation Acurate neo2 valve intended to maximize flow, immediate post-procedural echocardiography revealed severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM) with an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² and a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. This discrepancy arises because the cumulative thickness of three stent frames effectively reduces the landing zone diameter below what would be predicted by single-valve geometry alone. Furthermore, if any residual vegetation or calcified debris from prior infections was not fully cleared before reimplantation, these particles could act as physical obstructions within the narrowed channel created by stacked frames, precipitating acute hemodynamic collapse or embolic events upon deployment.

The specific contribution of prior infective endocarditis (IE) adds another layer of pathophysiological complexity distinct from pure degenerative failure. Infection does not merely cause temporary inflammation; it induces permanent architectural damage to both native tissue and prosthetic materials. Bacterial adherence leads to localized destruction of collagen fibers in bioprosthetic leaflets and erosion of suture lines or anchoring points on stents. Even after successful antimicrobial eradication—as confirmed in this patient by negative cultures and resolution on CT—the damaged tissue retains altered biomechanical properties; it becomes friable prone to tearing under pressure or prone to irregular scarring that restricts motion asymmetrically. Additionally, infection can trigger intense periprosthetic inflammation that accelerates fibrosis around the stent struts, potentially fusing adjacent layers together or altering compliance at the interface between old and new valves. This history complicates future interventions because infected sites may harbor microscopic biofilms resistant to sterilization during surgery or catheter manipulation, raising concerns about recurrence if prophylaxis is inadequate during subsequent procedures like angioplasty or re-cannulation attempts described in future scenarios involving BASILICA techniques.

Ultimately, the clinical challenge lies in balancing these competing factors: restoring sufficient flow across an increasingly crowded annulus while mitigating risks posed by fragile post-infectious tissues and rigid metal-on-metal interactions. The decision-making process must therefore transcend simple anatomical sizing; it requires anticipating how each added layer alters fluid dynamics within a confined space compromised by biological scarring and mechanical constraint. As evidenced by this case's recovery trajectory—from initial severe gradients resolving only after optimized commissural alignment and high-pressure post-dilation—the pathophysiology demands precise engineering solutions tailored to individualized anatomical realities rather than generic algorithmic approaches used for first-time implants or uncomplicated redo cases.
