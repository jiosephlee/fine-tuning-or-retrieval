Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Pathophysiology] In the context of a 'TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV' procedure, explain the specific anatomical rationale for utilizing a 'high implantation' strategy (aligning with previous leaflets) versus a standard annular position, and how does this strategy specifically mitigate the risks of Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM) and coronary obstruction?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

In the context of a "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV" procedure, the strategic decision to utilize **high implantation**—specifically aligning the new valve frame with the leaflets of the second-generation prosthesis (the Evolut valve in this case)—is a nuanced maneuver designed to navigate the competing threats of Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM) and coronary obstruction.

Anatomically, standard annular implantation in a multi-layered scenario often results in the new valve frame sitting lower, potentially compressing the innermost layers of the existing prosthetic frames against the aortic wall or creating a "neo-skirt" that extends inferiorly toward the coronary ostia. In this patient's triple-stented anatomy, such a position would effectively reduce the available cross-sectional area for blood flow, exacerbating PPM. More critically, it increases the risk of coronary obstruction if any portion of the new stent or compressed leaflets occludes the ostium.

By intentionally deploying the third valve (Acurate neo2) at a higher level, directly engaging and pinning down the leaflets of the intermediate Evolut valve against its own stent frame, we achieve two distinct hemodynamic advantages. First, this prevents the inward folding or "tenting" of these intermediate leaflets into the lumen, which would otherwise create a secondary stenotic barrier and drastically reduce the effective orifice area (EOA). Second, and perhaps most vital in this case, high implantation positions the new stent frame superior to the lowest points of previous frames and away from the immediate vicinity of the coronary ostia. This preserves a clear channel for coronary flow and maintains sufficient distance between any potential "neo-skirt" material and the coronary arteries.

As illustrated in this case report, despite an initial post-procedure gradient suggesting severe PPM (mean gradient 67 mm Hg), this strategy facilitated necessary mechanical expansion of prior frames through high-pressure ballooning and optimized commissural alignment. Over time, this resulted in significant hemodynamic recovery (gradient dropping to 26 mm Hg at one year) while successfully avoiding catastrophic coronary compromise. Thus, high implantation serves as a deliberate trade-off: sacrificing some annular anchoring depth to maximize flow dynamics and ensure coronary patency in an increasingly crowded anatomical space.
