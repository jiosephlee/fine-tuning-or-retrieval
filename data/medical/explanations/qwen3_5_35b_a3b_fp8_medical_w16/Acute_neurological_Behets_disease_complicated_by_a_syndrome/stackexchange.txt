Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] Based on the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD), what specific clinical findings in this patient contributed to his definitive diagnosis, and why is the presence of genital aphthosis considered particularly weighty in this scoring system?

Based on the **International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD)**, this patient met the threshold for a definitive diagnosis with a total score of **five points**. The specific clinical findings contributing to this score were:

*   **Oral aphthosis** (2 points): Documented at presentation.
*   **Genital aphthosis** (2 points): Appeared on day 5 of admission as a painful scrotal ulcer.
*   **Skin lesions** (1 point): Specifically, the "folliculitis-like" rashes noted on the face and trunk.

While the patient also exhibited fever, fatigue, and neurological symptoms, these are not assigned specific numerical weights in the ICBD scoring system itself; rather, they support the clinical picture of systemic involvement once the mucocutaneous criteria are met.

The presence of **genital aphthosis** is considered particularly weighty in this scoring system because it carries high specificity for Behçet's disease compared to oral ulcers alone. Oral aphthae are extremely common in the general population and can be seen in many other conditions, whereas genital ulcers—particularly those resembling herpetic lesions but lacking viral etiology—are much more distinctive of BD. Consequently, the ICBD assigns genital aphthosis double the weight (2 points) of oral aphthosis (2 points vs. 1 point for some older criteria, though both are weighted heavily here relative to skin lesions). In this case, the development of genital ulcers transformed a potentially ambiguous presentation into a definitive diagnosis, allowing clinicians to rule out infectious etiologies like HSV/VZV and proceed with targeted management for neuro-Behçet's disease.

### [Pathophysiology] This patient presented with hyponatremia, persistent hiccups, and elevated creatine kinase. Explain the pathophysiological link between severe hyponatremia and both hiccups and rhabdomyolysis, and how these 'non-neurological' symptoms served as critical clues to the severity of the SIADH.

**Clinical Pearl:** Severe hyponatremia in the context of central nervous system (CNS) inflammation—such as acute neuro-Behçet's disease—can manifest with "non-neurologic" symptoms like persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis. These findings are not merely coincidental but represent direct physiological consequences of profound sodium depletion and should serve as red flags for the severity of the underlying Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH).

The pathophysiological link begins with **persistent hiccups**. Hyponatremia alters neuronal membrane potentials, lowering the threshold for excitation in the medullary centers responsible for the hiccup reflex arc. In this patient, whose serum sodium dropped to 124 mmol/L, this excitability likely resulted from cerebral edema or direct irritation of the hypothalamic-brainstem axis. As noted in the discussion of this case, persistent hiccups are a recognized, though often overlooked, clinical clue to severe hyponatremia secondary to CNS pathology. They signal that the electrolyte disturbance has reached a threshold where even subtle brainstem dysfunction manifests as motor spasms.

Regarding **rhabdomyolysis**, evidenced here by an elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 1349 U/L, the mechanism is multifactorial. First, severe hyponatremia induces cellular swelling and disrupts intracellular ion homeostasis, leading to direct myocyte injury and membrane instability. Second, in states of CNS inflammation and SIADH, patients often experience generalized malaise and fatigue; if they become immobilized or develop subclinical seizures (which can occur with significant hyponatremia), muscle breakdown ensues. The case report explicitly identifies rhabdomyolysis as a "recognised but often overlooked complication" of severe hyponatremia that resolves alongside the correction of sodium levels once the inflammatory driver is treated with colchicine.

These symptoms served as critical clues because they highlighted a discrepancy between the patient's mental status ("alert") and the severity of his biochemical derangement. While classic severe hyponatremia presents with confusion or coma, this patient's prominent somatic complaints—hiccups and muscle pain/failure—directed attention toward the magnitude of the osmotic shift rather than just looking for structural brain lesions on MRI (which were normal). Recognizing these extra-neurologic signs prevented a delay in diagnosing severe SIADH driven by acute neuro-Behçet's disease and underscored that even without frank altered consciousness, profound hyponatremia carries immediate risks of neuromuscular compromise requiring urgent intervention.

### [Diagnostic Reasoning] Given that the patient's brain MRI was completely normal despite clinical evidence of CNS inflammation (headache, vertigo, aseptic meningitis), how does this imaging finding influence your diagnostic confidence regarding acute neuro-Behçet's disease compared to other causes like tumoral meningitis or abscess?

**Clinical Pearl:** A normal brain MRI does **not** exclude the diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD); instead, it helps distinguish aseptic meningitis from space-occupying lesions like tumors or abscesses.

In this case, the patient presented with classic signs of CNS inflammation—fever, headache, vertigo, and aseptic meningitis on lumbar puncture—but lacked parenchymal signal abnormalities on MRI. This imaging finding significantly increases diagnostic confidence for **acute neuro-BD presenting as isolated meningeal inflammation** while simultaneously lowering the probability of structural etiologies such as tumoral meningitis or brain abscess.

Here is the clinical reasoning based on the case:

1.  **Exclusion of Structural Pathology:** Tumors (e.g., lymphoma) and abscesses typically manifest as mass lesions, ring-enhancing structures, or distinct areas of edema on MRI. In this patient, the "completely normal" MRI, combined with negative CSF cytology and lack of focal deficits on exam, effectively rules out these compressive or infiltrative processes. As noted in the discussion, while *Neurosarcoidosis* can sometimes present with normal early MRIs, the specific constellation of mucocutaneous findings (oral/genital ulcers) in this patient makes BD far more likely than sarcoidosis once infectious causes are excluded.
2.  **Pathophysiology of Normal Imaging in Acute Neuro-BD:** Acute neuro-BD encompasses two phenotypes: parenchymal (brainstem/basal ganglia involvement) and meningeal. While parenchymal lesions are common in chronic or severe cases requiring aggressive immunosuppression, this patient exemplifies the **meningeal variant**. In acute meningoencephalitis without parenchymal destruction or significant edema, MRI sensitivity is limited; the pathology resides in the leptomeninges and CSF chemistry rather than visible tissue change. Therefore, a normal scan reflects a less destructive inflammatory process confined to the meninges rather than an absence of disease.
3.  **Diagnostic Integration:** The diagnosis rests on integrating three pillars seen here:
    *   **Systemic Clues:** Definite Behçet's disease based on oral aphthae + genital ulcers + skin lesions (International Criteria for BD).
    *   **CSF Evidence:** Aseptic meningitis (elevated protein/pleocytosis) without bacteria/virus/fungi/malignancy.
    *   **Imaging Context:** The absence of tumor/abscess allows us to attribute the aseptic meningitis directly to the systemic inflammatory driver (BD).

**Takeaway for Rounds:** When evaluating a patient with suspected neuro-inflammation where infectious workups are negative and clinical suspicion for autoimmune disease is high (especially with mucocutaneous stigmata), do not let a normal MRI dissuade you from a diagnosis of acute neuro-BD if CSF supports aseptic meningitis. Conversely, use that same normal MRI to confidently pivot away from surgical emergencies like abscesses or neoplasms that would mandate biopsy or resection.

### [Morning Report] Differentiate the expected cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) profile in acute neuro-Behçet's disease presenting as aseptic meningitis versus tuberculous meningitis and viral encephalitis (e.g., HSV/VZV), specifically focusing on cell count differentials, protein levels, and culture results.

**Clinical Pearl: Distinguishing Acute Neuro-Behçet's from Infectious Meningoencephalitis via CSF Analysis**

In the evaluation of a patient presenting with fever, headache, and neurological deficits, distinguishing **acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD)** presenting as aseptic meningitis from infectious etiologies like **tuberculous meningitis (TBM)** or **viral encephalitis (HSV/VZV)** relies heavily on the specific pattern of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) findings combined with clinical context.

Based on the case presented, **acute neuro-BD** manifests as a sterile, inflammatory process characterized by **mild lymphocytic pleocytosis** and moderately elevated protein. In this specific patient, the CSF showed 11 cells/µL (predominantly lymphocytes) and a protein level of 0.97 g/L. Crucially, all microbiological workups—including bacterial smears/cultures, mycobacterial stains/DNA testing for TB, and PCR/antigen assays for HSV and VZV—were negative. This "aseptic" profile in the presence of classic mucocutaneous Behçet's stigmata (oral/genital aphthae) supports a diagnosis of neuro-inflammation rather than infection.

To differentiate this from **tuberculous meningitis**, one must look beyond the cell count. While TBM also presents with lymphocytic pleocytosis, it typically exhibits a much higher white blood cell count (often >50–100 cells/µL), significantly higher protein levels (>1.0 g/L), and critically, low glucose (hypoglycorrhachia). Furthermore, TBM would yield positive acid-fast bacilli smears or nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT), whereas the case report explicitly notes these were negative in neuro-BD. Similarly, while **viral encephalitis** shares the feature of lymphocytic predominance, it is distinguished by positive PCR or serology for specific viruses (e.g., HSV-1 IgM/IgG or VZV DNA). The case report confirms that the absence of viral antigens and antibodies effectively ruled out HSV/VZV etiology despite the initial clinical suspicion driven by fever and headache.

Therefore, the key differentiator is the combination of a mild-to-moderate inflammatory profile with strictly negative cultures and molecular testing in a patient exhibiting pathognomonic systemic features of Behçet's disease. As highlighted in this case, a normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD if CSF shows aseptic meningitis; conversely, the absence of organisms in CSF makes primary infectious causes highly unlikely once clinical suspicion for BD is raised by mucocutaneous findings.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why is the timing of neurological involvement in this case considered atypical for Behçet's disease, and what are the classic demographic or clinical predictors that usually distinguish isolated mucocutaneous BD from those who will develop major organ involvement?

The timing of neurological involvement in this case is atypical because **acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD)** classically manifests years after the initial diagnosis of systemic mucocutaneous disease. As noted in the background of this report, major organ involvement, including CNS pathology, typically develops approximately four years after the onset of Behçet's disease. Furthermore, neurological symptoms at the very presentation of BD are rare, occurring in fewer than 5% of patients. In contrast, this patient presented with **concurrent** diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD; the neurological symptoms (fever, headache, aseptic meningitis) and mucocutaneous lesions (oral aphthae, genital ulcers) emerged simultaneously within days of each other. While acute neuro-BD can present as meningoencephalitis with brainstem lesions or parenchymal abnormalities on MRI, this patient's "pure" aseptic meningitis without radiographic evidence further complicates the typical clinical picture where structural brainstem involvement is expected.

Regarding predictors distinguishing isolated mucocutaneous disease from those progressing to major organ involvement:
*   **Demographics:** While this case involves a male patient (men are statistically more prone to severe vascular and neurological forms than women), age alone is not a definitive predictor here as he was in his 60s at onset. The critical distinction lies in the **phenotype**. Patients who progress to major organ involvement often have a younger age at onset (typically second or third decade), though late-onset cases like this one exist.
*   **Clinical Predictors:** The presence of **genital ulcers** is highly specific for BD and carries a higher risk for severe extracutaneous manifestations compared to oral aphthae alone. Additionally, the development of **uveitis** is a strong predictor for systemic severity and neurologic progression. In this case, the patient lacked ocular involvement initially but developed genital ulcers shortly after admission alongside neurological decline.
*   **Pathophysiology:** The transition from mucocutaneous to major organ disease is driven by widespread vasculitis and intense immune activation. The concurrent appearance of symptoms suggests an aggressive initial inflammatory phenotype rather than a gradual evolution over years.

Finally, it is crucial to recognize that while colchicine is standard for mucocutaneous BD, it is generally considered ineffective for controlling acute neuro-BD monotherapy according to systematic reviews; however, this unique case demonstrated remission with colchicine alone, likely due to its ability to suppress IL-6 mediated inflammation driving both the meningeal irritation and the resultant SIADH.

### [Management] While high-dose corticosteroids are traditionally the first-line therapy for acute neuro-Behçet's disease, discuss the unique factors in this case that justified a trial of colchicine monotherapy and what mechanistic properties of colchicine might explain its efficacy in resolving both mucocutaneous lesions and SIADH-associated CNS inflammation.

**Clinical Pearl:** While high-dose corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of therapy for acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD), this case illustrates that **colchicine monotherapy** can be a viable, effective alternative in select presentations, specifically when the disease manifests primarily as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal brainstem lesions.

In this patient, the decision to utilize colchicine instead of immediate corticosteroids was driven by three distinct factors:
1.  **Radiographic Absence of Parenchymal Disease:** The patient presented with "acute" neuro-BD characterized by aseptic meningitis (elevated CSF protein and lymphocytes) but lacked the hallmark brainstem lesions or parenchymal edema seen on MRI. Spontaneous resolution has been documented in similar meningoencephalitic variants, suggesting a potentially less aggressive course amenable to targeted anti-inflammatory therapy rather than broad immunosuppression.
2.  **Contraindications and Comorbidities:** The patient had poorly controlled type 2 diabetes (HbA1c 8.2%) and rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK). Initiating high-dose corticosteroids would have posed a significant risk of exacerbating hyperglycemia and worsening muscle injury, making colchicine a safer initial choice.
3.  **Systemic Phenotype:** As the patient fulfilled criteria for *definite* systemic BD with active mucocutaneous involvement (oral/genital ulcers, folliculitis), colchicine—already the gold standard for these specific manifestations—offered a dual-target strategy to treat both the skin/mucosa and the CNS simultaneously.

Regarding mechanism, colchicine likely addressed both the neurological inflammation and SIADH through its inhibition of microtubule polymerization, which disrupts **neutrophil chemotaxis and degranulation**. Crucially, colchicine suppresses the production of proinflammatory cytokines, particularly **interleukin-6 (IL-6)** and IL-1β. Since IL-6 is a potent stimulator of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) release from the hypothalamus-pituitary axis, reducing IL-6 levels via colchicine provides a plausible biological explanation for the rapid resolution of SIADH alongside neurological symptoms in this case. Thus, in patients with acute neuro-BD presenting as aseptic meningitis where steroid toxicity is a concern, colchicine represents a mechanistically sound therapeutic trial that targets the upstream cytokine drive linking CNS inflammation to endocrine dysfunction.

### [Differential Diagnosis] List three distinct etiologies outside of primary CNS pathology that must be rigorously excluded before attributing SIADH to Behçet's disease-related inflammation, citing specific diagnostic modalities mentioned in this case report that helped rule them out.

To attribute SIADH to Behçet's disease-related inflammation, one must first rigorously exclude three distinct categories of non-primary CNS pathology: **infectious etiologies**, **primary CNS malignancy**, and **other systemic autoimmune/inflammatory disorders**.

First, **infectious causes** such as viral meningoencephalitis (specifically HSV or VZV) and bacterial or tuberculous meningitis must be ruled out. In this case, these were definitively excluded through cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis demonstrating aseptic meningitis (lymphocytic pleocytosis without neutrophils), alongside negative CSF cultures, acid-fast stains, tuberculosis DNA testing, and specific polymerase chain reaction assays for HSV and VZV.

Second, **primary CNS malignancy**, particularly lymphoma or metastatic disease, requires exclusion given the potential for paraneoplastic SIADH. The case report confirms this was addressed via a normal brain MRI showing no parenchymal lesions or mass effect and negative CSF cytology which revealed no malignant cells. While the soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) was mildly elevated—a marker sometimes associated with lymphoma—the authors noted this finding was non-specific in the absence of radiographic evidence or other hematologic signs of malignancy.

Third, other **systemic granulomatous diseases**, specifically neurosarcoidosis, must be distinguished from Behçet's. Although neurosarcoidosis can mimic neuro-BD with normal MRI findings in early stages, this etiology was excluded by normal serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) levels and normal lysozyme concentrations. Furthermore, the absence of characteristic systemic symptoms like uveitis or hilar adenopathy on imaging supported the rejection of sarcoidosis in favor of Behçet's disease.

Only after systematically excluding infections via CSF microbiology/virology, ruling out neoplasm via MRI/cytology/sIL-2R interpretation, and dismissing sarcoidosis via ACE/lysozyme levels could the diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet's disease complicated by SIADH be confidently established.
