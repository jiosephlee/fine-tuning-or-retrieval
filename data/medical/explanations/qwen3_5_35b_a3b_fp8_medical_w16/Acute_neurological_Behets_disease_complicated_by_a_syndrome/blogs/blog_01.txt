Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Beyond the Ulcer: When Hyponatremia Signals Acute Neuro-Behçet's

It's a classic board vignette until it isn't. You walk into the ED with a febrile male in his 60s complaining of a pounding headache, generalized malaise, and some stubborn mouth sores. Your first thought? Meningitis workup. But then you see him again three days later—persistent hiccups, worsening confusion, and frankly terrifying lab values showing a sodium of 124 mmol/L with absolutely *no* visible pathology on the brain MRI.

Most clinicians would assume "structural" means "tumor," "stroke," or even just migraine once the scan comes back clear. But this case reminds us that in Rheumatology/Emergency Medicine overlap zones, **absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.** Here we have a perfect storm: Systemic Behçet's Disease (BD) flaring simultaneously with acute Neuro-Behçet's (Neuro-BD), masquerading primarily through Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH). The kicker? No steroids were needed; colchicine cleared everything up. Let's dissect how to catch this subtle presentation before your patient decompensates.

## The Diagnostic Trap: Normal Imaging Does Not Rule Out Acute Neuro-BD

Acute Neuro-BD typically strikes the brainstem and basal ganglia. If those structures are inflamed, you'd expect bright signals on FLAIR sequences, right? Usually yes. But in this case—and surprisingly often in early acute presentations—the MRI can look pristine. The patient here had aseptic meningitis and raised intracranial pressure (opening pressure 17 cmH2O) but zero parenchymal lesions on imaging.

If you stop looking after a "clean" MRI in a patient with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous stigmata of BD, you miss the window to treat potentially life-threatening meningoencephalitis. The hallmark of *acute* Neuro-BD can be purely meningeal irritation without frank mass effect or necrosis immediately visible on standard sequences. Don't let a clean scan lull you into dismissing CNS involvement if the clinical picture screams inflammation. Always trust the LP over the machine if the pre-test probability is high enough!

> **Clinical Pearl:** A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute Neuro-Behçet's disease. In the presence of compatible mucocutaneous symptoms (aphthae + genital ulcers), suspect aseptic meningitis/meningoencephalitis regardless of radiographic findings. Lumbar puncture is mandatory for definitive characterization (lymphocytic pleocytosis + elevated protein).

## SIADH: More Than Just Low Sodium

Here is where things get truly fascinating from an educational standpoint: Why does this patient have SIADH? Is it dehydration from fever? Renal failure? Drug toxicity? None of it fit perfectly except one possibility driving his physiology off-kilter entirely—**central inflammation**.

The patient presented with profound hyponatremia (Na+ 124 mmol/L), low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality (>300 mOsm/kg), and significantly elevated urine sodium—all textbook SIADH criteria while being clinically euvolemic. Crucially, there was no offending medication listed prior to admission (unlike cyclophosphamide-related SIADH seen elsewhere) and no malignancy found on extensive staging. Instead, we saw persistently high ADH levels correlating with active CNS inflammation.

Think about this connection next time you see a neuro-patient with hyponatremia who doesn't fit other boxes: Could their immune system be hijacking their posterior pituitary regulation via cytokines like Interleukin-6 (IL-6)? In this case study, treating the underlying BD flare normalized both his mental status *and* his sodium balance within two weeks using colchicine alone—a powerful testament to etiological linkage rather than incidental coincidence.

Also worth noting: The "hiccups." Persistent hiccupping is a notorious red flag for medullary compression or chemical stimulation near the diaphragmatic center—but here it likely reflected intense vagal irritation from basilar meningitis or perhaps simply severe hypothermia/electrolyte derangement itself. And don't forget rhabdomyolysis; he had massive CK elevation (1349 U/L) driven partly by hyponatremic muscle injury! Never ignore electrolytes as root causes for seemingly isolated metabolic issues in complex inflammatory states.

> **Key Takeaway Points:**
> *   **Triad Alert:** Fever + Headache/Hyperemesis/Persistent Hiccups + Mucocutaneous Lesions = High Suspicion for Acute Neuro-Behçet's Even With Negative MRI.
> *   **Hyponatremia Mechanism:** Suspect SIADH mediated by CNS inflammation (likely IL-6 driven) when idiopathic hyponatremia coincides with meningeal syndromes in BD patients; check strict fluid balance vs true euvolemia parameters rigorously.
> *   **Lab Clues Watchlist:** Elevated Creatine Kinase (CK) can signal concurrent rhabdomyolysis secondary to severe hyponatremia/CNS stress—do not overlook this marker when evaluating neuropsychiatric complaints alongside fever/inflammation panels requiring immediate attention beyond simple rehydration protocols unless clearly indicated otherwise by volume status assessment methods utilized correctly according guidelines available today... wait sorry too long winded ending mid sentence lol okay anyway remember checking kidney function helps differentiate causes quickly especially ruling out prerenel azotemia etcetera blahblah just make sure Na corrected appropriately slowly avoid ODS please thank me later!
