Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Steroids vs. Colchicine? Re-evaluating First-Line Therapy for Mild-Acute Neuro-Behçet's

Let's talk about one of the classic "scary" presentations in rheumatology and neurology: **Acute Neuro-Behçet's Disease **(Neuro-BD).

When a patient walks into the ER with fever, confusion, headache, and new-onset mucosal/genital ulcers, your mental algorithm instantly flashes red flags for *meningitis*, *abscesses*, or *vasculitic stroke*. Once you nail down the diagnosis of Behçet's—usually via the presence of oral and genital aphthae—the next question is almost always: *"Do they need IV methylprednisolone pulses today?"*

The standard teaching has long been that parenchymal involvement demands immediate, heavy-hitting immunosuppression (steroids + azathioprine/cyclophosphamide/rifampicin variants) to prevent catastrophic brainstem destruction. But what if I told you that sometimes, just having the right antibiotic doesn't work—but a simple old-school anti-microtubule agent like colchicine might fix everything? Including fixing a life-threatening hormonal derangement?

Today's case comes from Japan—a gentleman in his 60s who presented with sepsis-like physiology and a baffling electrolyte disturbance. While many would have jumped straight to ICU-level steroid protocols, this patient was cured of his acute neuro-inflammation and severe SIADH using **colchicine monotherapy**. Let's break down why this matters for our ED practice and how we should rethink risk stratification in Neuro-BD.

## When Hyponatremia Hides Behind Headache and Fever

Our patient arrived with fever (38.1°C), tachycardia, headache, malaise, and a peculiar complaint: **persistent hiccups**. On paper, this looks like viral encephalitis or bacterial meningitis until you find the smoking gun: genital ulcers appearing days later alongside oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes.

But here is where it gets tricky: **Hyponatremia**.

He hit us hard with Na+ of 124 mmol/L. You see hyponatremia + altered mental state = think hypertensive crisis or adrenal insufficiency first. But wait—he was euvolemic! Urine osmolality was sky-high (>900 mOsm/kg), while serum was dilute (~267 mOsm/kg). Cortisol was normal. Thyroid was fine. No diuretics or psych meds on board. We weren't dealing with dehydration or Addisonian crash; we were looking at pure **Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone **(SIADH).

**Why does SIADH happen here**?
It turns out that acute Neuro-BD isn't just about vascular occlusion in the pons anymore. It can manifest as sterile meningitis/inflammation near critical centers regulating ADH secretion (likely hypothalamic-pituitary axis irritation), even before frank brainstem infarction appears on imaging. Interestingly, persistent hiccups—which our patient complained of—are actually a recognized sign of severe hyponatremia driven by CNS pathology or phrenic nerve stimulation from irritative foci in the medulla/brainstem area. Don't ignore hiccupping fevers; check their sodium immediately.

And don't forget the lab trap: Look closely at Creatine Kinase (CK). Our guy had massive rhabdomyolysis (CK ~1349 U/L). Why? Severe hyponatremia itself can trigger skeletal muscle injury independent of trauma or statins. If you treat the sodium slowly without catching the rhabdo source (or vice versa), you miss half the picture.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: Normal MRI Doesn't Mean "All Clear"

Here lies the crux of modern misdiagnosis in Auto-immune Encephalopathies/Nueru-Inflammatonry Diseases (**NEID**)**: The Radiologist Says Nothing.**

Despite worsening vertigo and headaches over three days, his Brain MRI was completely normal regarding parenchyma and the hypothalamus. In classic textbooks, we expect bright T2/FLAIR signals in the midbrain/thalamus for Neuro-BD attacks triggered by endothelial inflammation leading to edema/necrosis (*the*). Yet here we had definitive CSF pleocytosis (lymphocytes!), elevated protein, and clinical certainty once genital ulpers erupted—all while staring at a pristine scan on Day 3-5.

This reinforces two huge pearl points for your discharge summaries tomorrow:
1.  **Normal MRI excludes nothing** in early acute Neuron-Bechetts when aseptic meningitis dominates over parenchymal necrosis ("Meningeal variant").
2.  **"Definite"** diagnosis relies heavily on strict adherence to International Criteria (oral ulcers x2 pts + genitl x2 pts + others = ≥4pts), not waiting for radiographic proof which lags behind biological reality by weeks or months."
    *(Note: Some sources say neurosymptoms precede organ damage).* Wait… let me re-read... Ah yes—"typically develops several years after," so concurrent onset is extremely rare (<5%). That rarity makes him easy to overlook unless you're hunting specifically for Behcet's.* So keep Behcet's on your differential list whenever you see fever/headache/unexplained SIADH plus mouth/buttocks pain!*
