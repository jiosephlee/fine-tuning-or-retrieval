Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Steroid-Sparing Success? Re-evaluating Colchicine Monotherapy in Acute Neuro-Behçet's

Let's talk about a diagnostic puzzle that defies the textbook timeline. We've got a man in his 60s walking into the ED with fever, a headache that won't quit, and persistent hiccups—a classic "red flag" for intracranial pathology. But here's the twist: he has active mouth and genital ulcers, yet his brain MRI is completely normal. And instead of landing him on a heavy regimen of IV steroids and cyclophosphamide, he gets better on **colchicine** alone.

This case from Kakehi et al. challenges one of the most entrenched dogmas in neuro-rheumatology: *Acute Neuro-Behçet's Disease (Neuro-BD) requires aggressive immunosuppression.* While standard guidelines scream for high-dose corticosteroids plus pulse cyclophosphamide or TNF-alpha inhibitors for meningoencephalitic presentations, this patient responded spectacularly to colchicine monotherapy. Even more fascinating? He had SIADH driving his hyponatremia, likely driven by the same CNS inflammation causing his headache, and everything resolved together once the inflammation was tamed.

### The Clinical Picture: When "Normal" Isn't Normal

The presentation was deceptively subtle until you connected the dots. Our patient had the full triad of mucocutaneous Behçet's: oral aphthae, genital ulcers (which appeared later in the admission), and folliculitis-like rashes. But the neurological symptoms—fever, headache, vertigo, and those relentless hiccups—were happening *concurrently* with the skin findings. This is rare; typically, Neuro-BD strikes years after the initial diagnosis of systemic disease.

What makes this case particularly tricky for the ER physician is the imaging. You expect to see brainstem enhancement or parenchymal lesions in acute Neuro-BD. Here? Nothing on MRI. Yet, the CSF told a different story: aseptic meningitis with lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein. The diagnosis hinges entirely on clinical synthesis: **Mucocutaneous findings + Meningeal signs = Acute Neuro-BD**, even if your MRI looks clean. Remember, early meningeal-predominant disease often lacks visible parenchymal lesions on radiology.

Then came the electrolyte chaos: Hyponatremia (Na+ 124 mmol/L) with low serum osmolality and high urine osmolality/sodium in a euvolemic patient. The workup ruled out adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism, confirming **SIADH**. While SIADH is classically taught as a side effect of drugs like carbamazepine or chemotherapy (like cyclophosphamide), this patient developed it purely from CNS inflammation before any immunosuppressants were given. The persistent hiccups were likely an early marker of this hypothalamic irritation or severe hyponatremia itself—a clue often missed until it becomes obvious why they are hiccupping constantly!

### The Treatment Controversy: Colchicine vs. Steroids

Here is where we have to pause and rethink our algorithms. The consensus in almost every major review article is clear: For acute Neuro-BD with parenchymal involvement or severe meningoencephalitis, you must hit hard with steroids and cytotoxic agents immediately to prevent permanent disability or death. Colchicine is widely cited as effective for mucocutaneous disease but largely ineffective for neurological involvement unless used as an adjunct to stronger agents.

So, how did this guy get better on colchicine alone?
1.  **Phenotype Matters:** Perhaps this wasn't "severe" parenchymal encephalitis requiring massive suppression; maybe it was a milder meningeal variant that could be controlled by dampening neutrophil migration (the primary mechanism of colchicine).
2.  **Spontaneous Remission?** Some forms of acute Neuro-BD are self-limiting over weeks to months without intervention, though relying on chance is risky medicine.
3.  **The SIADH Connection:** If the SIADH was driven by IL-6 mediated inflammation (a plausible theory given the high CRP), then reducing that systemic cytokine storm via colchicine would naturally resolve both the neurological symptoms and the hormonal dysregulation simultaneously without needing massive steroid load—which carries its own risks in a diabetic patient with new-onset hyperglycemia (this patient had undiagnosed T2DM!).

It is crucial not to interpret this single case as a mandate to stop giving steroids to everyone with Neuro-BD tomorrow. However, it forces us to ask: Are we over-treating select phenotypes? Is there a subset of patients with predominant meningeal symptoms (no parenchymal lesions) where colchicine offers a viable steroid-sparing strategy?

### Key Takeaways & Pearls

*   **Think Behçet's First:** In any patient presenting with fever + headache + mucocutaneous ulcers (even if genital ulcers haven't appeared yet), keep Neuro-Behçet's on your differential regardless of MRI findings early in the course. A normal MRI does not rule out aseptic meningitis due to BD.
*   **Hiccups + Hyponatremia = Check Your Sodium:** Persistent hiccups can be an ominous sign of severe hyponatremia or CNS irritation secondary to SIADH caused by neuro-inflammation (meningitis/meningoencephalitis). Always check sodium in febrile patients with non-specific CNS complaints before attributing symptoms solely to infection or migraine equivalents.
*   **The Colchiche Question:** While steroids remain first-line for severe/parenchymal Neuro-BD, this case suggests colchicine monotherapy *might* suffice for mild-to-moderate meningeal-predominant cases or those where steroid toxicity (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes) is prohibitive—but this remains an outlier that requires replication before changing guidelines broadly.
*   **Don't Miss the Endocrine Link:** SIADH can be a direct paraneoplastic-like manifestation of CNS inflammation itself (as seen here) independent of medication side effects or malignancy when working up hyponatremia in inflammatory contexts like SLE or Vasculitides/Neuro-BD/Sarcoidosis/TB meningitis spectrum).

This case serves as a reminder that while we love our protocols and algorithms, biology loves outliers just enough to teach us something new about disease mechanisms—and perhaps therapeutic flexibility—in rare inflammatory syndromes like Behçet's disease.**
