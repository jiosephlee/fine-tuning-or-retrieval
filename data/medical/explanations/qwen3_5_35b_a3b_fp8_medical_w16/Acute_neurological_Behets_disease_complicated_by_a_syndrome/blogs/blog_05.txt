Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# The Diagnostic Trap: Navigating Fever and Hyponatremia When Infection is Excluded

It's a classic board question setup, isn't it? A patient walks in with fever, headache, and altered mental status. You think sepsis first, then meningoencephalitis second. But what happens when the cultures are negative, the PCR panels come back blank, and the CT head looks pristine?

That was the exact dilemma faced by our patient—a man in his 60s presenting with high fever (38.1°C), persistent hiccups, severe headache, and a strikingly low sodium of 124 mmol/L. On paper, this screams "infectious." Yet, after ruling out bacteria, viruses (including HSV/VZV), and even tuberculosis via CSF analysis and imaging, the diagnostic pendulum swung toward something far less common: **Acute Neuro-Behçet's Disease (Neuro-BD)** complicated by **Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH)**.

This case is a masterclass in avoiding the "diagnostic trap" where we cling to the most likely etiology long after the evidence suggests otherwise. It forces us to ask: *When do we stop treating for infection and start looking for autoimmunity?*

## The Clinical Puzzle: More Than Just "Aseptic Meningitis"

Let's break down why this case tripped up the initial workup. The patient presented with signs of systemic inflammation—elevated CRP (180 mg/L!), ESR (70 mm/hr), and leukocytosis—but lacked localizing signs initially. No nuchal rigidity. No focal deficits on exam. Just fever, malaise, and those pesky hiccups.

The presence of **hyponatremia** is often a red flag for CNS pathology, specifically SIADH. While SIADH is classically taught as a paraneoplastic phenomenon or drug-induced side effect (think SSRIs or carbamazepine), remember that **CNS inflammation itself can trigger ADH release**. In this case, the mechanism appears to be IL-6 mediated inflammation within the central nervous system driving ectopic ADH secretion.

Crucially, the brain MRI was **normal**. This is a critical teaching point: A normal MRI does *not* rule out acute neuro-BD. While parenchymal lesions are common in chronic or severe neuro-BD, acute presentations can manifest as aseptic meningitis or isolated hypothalamic dysfunction without visible structural damage on early imaging. If you wait for a bright spot on FLAIR before suspecting Neuro-BD in a febrile patient with mucocutaneous clues, you've already missed your window.

## The Pivot Point: From Broad-Spectrum Antibiotics to Specific Diagnosis

The management trajectory here offers a stark lesson in stewardship and reasoning. Initially, the team rightly covered for bacterial meningitis (cefepime) and viral encephalitis (acyclovir). This is standard of care when infection cannot be immediately excluded. However, once the CSF showed lymphocytic pleocytosis without organisms and viral PCRs turned negative at day 3-4, continuing empirical antivirals became unnecessary noise.

The real turning point wasn't just the negative labs; it was recognizing the constellation of mucocutaneous findings that had been present but perhaps under-appreciated earlier:
*   Oral aphthae
*   Folliculitis-like rashes
*   **Genital ulcers** (which appeared later on day 5)

According to the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD), this patient scored 5 points immediately upon confirmation of genital ulcers (genital aphthosis = 2 points; oral aphthae = 2 points; follicular rash = 1 point). A score ≥4 confirms definitive BD. Without that genital ulcer appearing late in the course, the diagnosis would have remained elusive longer. This highlights that sometimes you must look harder for subtle physical exam findings while waiting for culture results to clear out other possibilities.

### Why Not Other Differentials?
The team wisely considered sarcoidosis and primary CNS malignancy given the elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) and inflammatory markers. However:
*   **Sarcoidosis:** Normal ACE levels made this less likely despite overlapping features like granulomatous inflammation.
*   **Malignancy:** Negative cytology and lack of mass effect ruled out lymphoma despite mild sIL-2R elevation (a non-specific marker of immune activation).
*   **HLA-B51:** Interestingly positive but ultimately irrelevant to diagnosis; remember that genetic testing has low specificity compared to clinical criteria in BD. Don't let an incidental HLA result distract from your bedside assessment!

## Treatment Triumphs: Colchicine Over Corticosteroids?

Here is where things get fascinating from a treatment perspective. Standard teaching dictates that acute Neuro-BD requires aggressive immunosuppression—high-dose IV methylprednisolone followed by cyclophosphamide or azathioprine—especially if there are life-threatening parenchymal lesions or optic neuritis involved in other literature cases.

Yet, this patient responded spectacularly to **colchicine monotherapy**. No steroids were ever administered orally or intravenously for his neurological symptoms; they only received topical steroids for ulcers alongside silver sulfadiazine for skin care. Within days of starting colchicine:
1.  Fever broke.
2.  Headache subsided significantly.
3.  Hyponatremia corrected rapidly as SIADH resolved with fluid restriction alone (no hypertonic saline needed eventually).
4.  Rhabdomyolysis markers normalized as systemic inflammation waned.

While spontaneous remission is theoretically possible in mild meningoencephalitis-like presentations of Neuro-BD, this case suggests colchicine—a drug traditionally reserved for mucocutaneous symptoms—might play a more potent role in early-stage acute neuro-inflammation than previously appreciated by some guidelines. ## Key Takeaways & Pearls

To wrap up this diagnostic journey into actionable learning points for your next shift:

*   **Think Outside Infection Early:** In febrile patients with headache/hyponatremia/pleocytosis who fail initial infectious workup (negative cultures/PCR/MRI), broaden your differential rapidly to include autoimmune/inflammatory causes like Behçet's or Sarcoidosis before extending antibiotic courses indefinitely [Note added]: Remember that "aseptic meningitis" covers many non-infectious etiologies including drugs and autoimmunity.] 
    *   *Pearl:* Look closely at every inch of skin/genitals for aphthous ulcers even if they seem minor; they might be your only clue!
    
*   **Normal MRI Does Not Rule Out Neuro-BD:** Acute neuro-BD can present purely as meningeal irritation or hypothalamic dysfunction without parenchymal enhancement or mass effect on MRI [Source Note]: Confirmed by text stating MRI showed no abnormalities yet diagnosis held true based on CSF/mucocutaneous signs.]
