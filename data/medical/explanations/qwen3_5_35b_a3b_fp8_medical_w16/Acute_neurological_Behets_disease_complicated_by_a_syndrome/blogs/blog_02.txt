# The Silent Culprit: When Hyponatremia Points to Behçet's

**By [Your Name/AI Assistant]** | **EMCrit / Foamy Style**

Let's play a game of "Don't Miss." You walk onto your shift, and the patient is a guy in his 60s. He's got a fever, a headache so bad he hasn't slept, some hiccups that won't quit, and frankly, he looks tired. His labs scream **hyponatremia** (Na+ 124 mmol/L). Your immediate mental checklist? Sepsis. Meningitis. Malignancy. Maybe he ate too many crackers last night? You're looking for a bug or a tumor hiding behind those numbers.

But here's the twist: What if the culprit isn't an infection or a metastasis? What if it's an autoimmune storm where the brain itself becomes the source of an endocrine glitch? That is exactly what happened in this fascinating case of concurrent Behçet's disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet's disease (Neuro-BD) complicated by SIADH.

Usually, when we think of Neuro-BD, we picture dramatic brainstem strokes or meningeal enhancement on MRI. But this patient threw us off balance first with **persistent hiccups**. While we were hunting for HSV encephalitis (and running acyclovir just in case) and ruling out tubercular meningitis via lumbar puncture, the real story unfolded slowly over days as oral aphthae evolved into genital ulcers and folliculitic rashes emerged. The MRI was eerily *normal*. No hypothalamic mass. No stroke. Just pure CNS inflammation manifesting as aseptic meningitis and a bizarre failure of water homeostasis: SIADH.

### Why This Matters Beyond the Basics

Most clinicians associate SIADH with small cell lung cancer, SSRIs, or subarachnoid hemorrhage. Yes, central nervous system inflammation is a recognized trigger—think meningitis—but recognizing **primary autoimmune etiology** like Neuro-BD requires stepping back from the "infection-first" reflex arc.

What makes this case truly distinct—and worthy of our attention—is not just the rarity of BD presenting simultaneously with neurological disease (usually they lag years apart), but the specific constellation of **headache + hiccups + hyponatremia** acting as a triad for non-infectious meningoencephalitis driven by immune dysregulation. The hiccups aren't random; they likely represent diaphragmatic irritation from altered osmolarity or direct hypothalamic-brainstem interface dysfunction often seen in SIADH-related states. And don't forget the rhabdomyolysis! With CPKs hitting ~1350 U/L, one could easily miss how deep-seated electrolyte shifts drive systemic toxicity until it hits hard enough to warrant investigation for muscle breakdown rather than sepsis-associated myopathy.

### The Pathophysiology Connection: IL-6 and Water Retention

So, why would Behçet's make him retain water? This gets into that beautiful intersection of immunology and physiology that keeps us awake at night reading journals late at night.

Acute Neuro-BD is characterized by intense CNS inflammation involving meninges and potentially subtle parenchymal edema even before it lights up on MRI. The proposed mechanism involves cytokines flooding the scene—specifically **Interleukin-6 **(IL-6). IL-6 is notorious in BD pathology; it drives neutrophil chemotaxis but also acts as a potent stimulator of Arginine Vasopressin (AVP/ADH) release from posterior pituitary neurons or ectopic expression within inflamed tissue itself. In short: High inflammation = High IL-6 = Suppressed thirst regulation + Increased ADH output = Dilutional hyponatremia without hypovolemia (classic SIADH profile: high urine Na+, high urine OsMolality, low serum OsMolality).

Crucially, this wasn't iatrogenic (he hadn't received Cyclophosphamide yet!) nor paraneoplastic (no mass!). It was purely inflammatory-driven autonomic chaos centered on the hypothalamus/pituitary axis while leaving structural imaging largely blind to it in early phases. As noted in similar literature, normalizing sodium doesn't always resolve immediately upon treating sodium; resolving the upstream *cause* clears the signal transduction error holding open that AVP door shut tightest against dilution issues naturally once calm returns to CNS watersheds through targeted suppression of said inflammatory drivers—not necessarily steroids right away either!
