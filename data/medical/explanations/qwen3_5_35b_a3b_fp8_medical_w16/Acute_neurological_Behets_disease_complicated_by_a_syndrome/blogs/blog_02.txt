Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Beyond the Brain: When Hyponatremia Signals Neuro-Behçet's

We've all seen the classic "fever, headache, and confusion" triad in the ED. Usually, our brains immediately sprint toward meningitis, encephalitis, or sepsis. But what happens when the fever persists, the headache is relentless, and the patient is profoundly hyponatremic? If you're thinking about SIADH as your primary suspect, you might be missing a crucial piece of the puzzle if the patient also has mouth sores or genital ulcers.

Today's case comes from *BMJ Case Reports* and flips the script on how we think about neuro-inflammation. We aren't just talking about a standard case of Behçet's disease (BD); we're looking at a rare instance where **acute neuro-Behçet's disease** presented concurrently with systemic findings and was complicated by **Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH)**. What makes this even more fascinating is that this patient didn't get hit with steroids or cyclophosphamide—the usual culprits for drug-induced SIADH—and still developed profound hyponatremia driven purely by CNS inflammation.

### The Presentation: More Than Just a Headache

Our patient was a man in his 60s walking into the ER with three days of fever (38.1°C), severe headache, generalized malaise, and persistent hiccups. He also complained of arthralgia and noted oral aphthae. On paper, this looks like viral encephalitis or perhaps early bacterial meningitis. However, a closer look at his physical exam revealed the tell-tale signs of Behçet's: oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes on his trunk, and later, a painful scrotal ulcer appeared on day 5.

The lab work painted a confusing picture initially:
*   **Severe Hyponatremia:** Sodium dropped to 124 mmol/L.
*   **Hyperthermia & Elevated Inflammatory Markers:** CRP was sky-high at 180 mg/L; ESR was 70 mm/hr.
*   **"Red Herrings":** He had elevated CK (rhabdomyolysis likely from severe hyponatremia/stress) and hyperglycemia (undiagnosed Type 2 DM).

Crucially, his volume status was clinically euvolemic. No edema, no dehydration signs—just pure water retention issues masked as fatigue and hiccups.

### The Diagnostic Dilemma: Ruling Out the Usual Suspects

When you see hyponatremia + CNS symptoms + fever, your differential is long. Did he have HSV/VZV encephalitis? Bacterial meningitis? Tuberculosis? Or maybe a paraneoplastic syndrome like small cell lung cancer?

In this case, we had to systematically dismantle every alternative diagnosis:
*   **Infection:** CSF showed aseptic meningitis (lymphocytic pleocytosis) but cultures were negative for bacteria, TB, fungi (Cryptococcus), and viruses (HSV/VZV).
*   **Malignancy:** Chest/Abdomen CTs were clear. CSF cytology was negative for malignant cells. While sIL-2R was slightly elevated (often seen in lymphoma), it wasn't specific enough to override clinical findings without radiographic evidence of masses or adenopathy.
*   **Autoimmune Mimics:** Sarcoidosis was considered but ruled out by normal ACE levels and lack of granulomas on biopsy (not done here but implied by workup). ANA/ANCA profiles were negative for other connective tissue diseases.

Then came the MRI brain scan... which was essentially normal regarding parenchymal lesions in the brainstem or hypothalamus—a classic location for neuro-BD lesions! This is a critical teaching point: **Acute neuro-Behçet's can present as aseptic meningitis without visible parenchymal brain lesions on MRI.** Do not let a "normal" MRI dissuade you from pursuing neuro-BD if the clinical picture fits perfectly with mucocutaneous findings.

### The Mechanism: Why SIADH Happens Here

So, why did this patient develop SIADH? Is it just random chance? Probably not. The literature suggests that SIADH in BD isn't always drug-induced (like with cyclophosphamide); it can be a direct consequence of CNS inflammation itself.

The proposed mechanism involves **Interleukin-6 (IL-6)**. In acute neuro-BD, aberrant monocyte activation leads to massive IL-6 production in both serum and CSF. IL-6 acts as a potent stimulator of ADH release from the posterior pituitary or hypothalamus. Even though we didn't measure IL-6 directly in this specific case report (a missed opportunity!), his CRP was astronomically high—a surrogate marker for intense IL-6-driven inflammation—supporting this theory. The hiccups mentioned earlier are another subtle clue; persistent hiccups are increasingly recognized as a sign of severe hyponatremia or direct irritation to the medullary centers caused by this inflammatory storm.

### Treatment Triumphs Without Steroids?

Here is where things get really interesting for us clinicians managing difficult inflammatory cases. Standard care for acute neuro-BD usually screams "High-dose IV Methylprednisolone." Sometimes we add Cyclosporine or Anti-TNF agents if refractory. Yet, this patient received **Colchicine monotherapy**.

Wait—colchicine for *neuro*-Behçet's? Most guidelines say colchicine works great for mucocutaneous symptoms but fails against serious organ involvement like neurology or vascular disease requiring immunosuppression due to its inability to penetrate deep tissues effectively compared to steroids/cyclophosphamide/anti-TNFs. And yet:
*   Fever broke within hours of starting colchicine.
*   Neurological symptoms improved over two weeks.
*   Sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L by Day 16 alongside fluid restriction alone (no hypertonic saline needed!).
*   No recurrence after 4 years on maintenance colchicine alone!

While spontaneous remission is possible in some forms of meningeal Behçet's, this outcome challenges our dogma that severe neuro-involvement *always* mandates heavy immunosuppression immediately upon diagnosis in every single scenario. It suggests that in select cases with predominant meningeal inflammation (aseptic meningitis phenotype) rather than destructive parenchymal encephalopathy ("brainstem syndrome"), less aggressive anti-inflammatory strategies might suffice—or at least warrant consideration before jumping straight to steroids if comorbidities exist (like his undiagnosed diabetes).

### Key Takeaways for Your Practice

This case serves as a powerful reminder that Behçet's can wear many masks and strike unexpectedly early in its course (concurrent diagnosis vs typical delay). It forces us to broaden our lens when evaluating hyponatremic patients with neurological complaints who don't fit standard infectious molds.

**Clinical Pearls & Take-Homes:**
*   **Think Behçet's Early:** In any patient presenting with fever + headache + hyponatremia + mucocutaneous lesions (oral/genital ulcers), consider acute neuro-Behçet's even before imaging confirms parenchymal lesions; aseptic meningitis can be the sole neurological presentation initially.
*   **SIADH as an Inflammatory Marker:** Don't automatically attribute SIADH to drugs or tumors in inflammatory contexts; CNS inflammation itself can drive ADH release via IL-6 pathways, especially when presenting with non-specific symptoms like persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis secondary to severe electrolyte shifts.
*   **Normal MRI Doesn't Rule It Out:** Acute neuro-BD can manifest as isolated aseptic meningitis without detectable brainstem or parenchymal lesions on initial MRI; trust your clinical gestalt over radiology if suspicion remains high based on mucocutaneous findings plus CSF evidence of inflammation (elevated protein/pleocytosis).
*   **Re-evaluate Treatment Intensity:** While steroids remain first-line for most severe neuro-BD phenotypes, monitor closely whether milder presentations responding robustly to colchicine alone represent a distinct subset requiring less toxic intervention than traditionally assumed; avoid premature escalation if clinical trajectory improves rapidly with conservative measures while maintaining strict safety monitoring protocols for sodium correction rates (>8 mEq/L/24h limit applies regardless).
