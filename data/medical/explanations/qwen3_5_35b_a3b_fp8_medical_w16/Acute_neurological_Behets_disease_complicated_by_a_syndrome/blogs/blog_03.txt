# More Than Just Headache: Hidden Clues Like Rhabdo and Hiccups in Severe Hyponatremia

Let's talk about **hyponatremia**. Specifically, let's stop treating every "confused old guy" or "feverish headache sufferer" purely through a standard algorithm until they die. Today's case brings home a terrifying lesson from the world of rheumatology disguised as an ID mystery: **severe hyponatremia can manifest with bizarre, seemingly unrelated systemic symptoms** if you aren't looking closely enough.

Meet a gentleman in his 60s walking into your ED with fever, headache, and… *persistent hiccups*. On paper, he looks septic. You order cultures, start cefepime, maybe run a CT head just to rule out a bleed. But wait—his sodium? 124 mmol/L. That's not "I'm thirsty"; that's dangerous territory. And while everyone focuses on the Na+ number, don't miss the other two silent killers in this triad: **rhabdomyolysis** (CK nearly 1350 U/L) and the actual etiology hiding behind it all.

Here is why this matters for us in Emergency Medicine. When a patient presents with non-specific CNS symptoms (headache, altered mental status, lethargy) plus hyponatremia, think beyond the simple "SIADH vs Dehydration" binary. Think about **what caused the SIADH?** Is it pneumonia? Sepsis? Or something far less obvious like acute Neurenteric inflammation driving up Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH)?

### The Diagnostic Trap: Why MRI Was Normal
Our patient didn't fit the classic textbook mold immediately. He had fever and confusion. Your first thought? Meningoencephalitis. So you lumbar puncture him. The opening pressure was slightly high (17 cmH2O), protein was elevated, and there was mild lymphocytic pleocytosis—a sterile picture screaming "viral," but PCR for HSV/VZV/TB came back negative again and again.

Then comes the twist: The brain MRI was perfectly normal. No brainstem edema! No enhancement! If you saw this case today on rounds without knowing the answer beforehand, would you suspect Behçet's Disease (BD)? Probably not unless you noticed those mouth sores earlier. Most of us associate Neuro-Behçet's with dramatic stroke-like syndromes or distinct midbrain lesions on imaging. This patient had **"Acute Menigoencephalitis"** driven by systemic inflammation without visible structural damage on MRI yet—and crucially, he had SIADH complicating everything else.

But here is where many clinicians trip over their own feet: They treat the hyponatremia as a standalone lab value issue or attribute it entirely to pain meds/sepsis protocols without hunting down the root driver of ADH release—the neuro-inflammation itself.

### Red Flags Beyond Confusion
Why did I highlight **hiccups**? Because this isn't just folklore anymore; it's physiology meeting pathology in real-time. Persistent hiccups (singultus) are notoriously difficult to manage medically once established in adults unless you fix the underlying trigger (acid reflux is common, but CNS irritation is scary). In this case report from Japan, hiccups persisted alongside vertigo and were noted before discharge planning began as resolving with treatment of both hydro-electrolytes and inflammation. Why? Likely irritative stimulation of the phrenic nucleus or vagus pathways within the medulla oblongata—a consequence of either direct inflammation or severe osmotic shifts disrupting ion channels locally.**Don't ignore hiccups**; especially if paired with hyponatremia and headache—it could signal medullary involvement long before a hemiparesis appears.*

And then there's **the CK spike**. Did someone beat him up? Is he having seizures? Nope—heckling rhabdomyolysis turned out to be another subtle side effect of profound hypokalemia-hypophosphate states induced by dilutional forces or perhaps simply severe dehydration masked by water retention dynamics inherent to SIADH management errors prior to arrival.**When CK shoots up in febrile delirium + hyponatremia**, ask yourself: Are we missing an electrolyte catastrophe masquerading as sepsis?* While some argue CK elevation can stem from agitation/rubbing against sheets ("self-inflicted"), in this case, the normalization paralleled sodium correction exactly.*It serves as a visceral marker that cellular stress exists somewhere outside your usual ICU monitor alarms*.*

### Treatment Takeaways & Pearls
So what happens next? Do we throw massive steroids at everything assuming neuro-Behçet's needs aggressive suppression right away? Maybe not always—or at least not exclusively depending on severity and availability risks... Wait—this patient got better fast on *Colchicine Alone*!**That sounds counter-intuitive given current guidelines saying Corticosteroids = Gold Standard***for active Neuro-BD,*but remember:*Spontaneous remission IS possible*, AND sometimes mild-to-moderate presentations responding quickly to anti-neutrophil drugs avoid toxic iatrogenics later!* Regardless of whether Colchi helped directly vs natural course,**fluid restriction became critical**: restricting intake below 800ml/day allowed kidneys to finally dump excess free water without needing hypertonic saline rescue doses (*which carry CPME risk*).
