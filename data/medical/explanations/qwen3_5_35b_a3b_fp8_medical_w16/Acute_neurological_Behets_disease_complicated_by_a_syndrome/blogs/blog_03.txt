Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Silent Clues: Hiccups and Rhabdomyolysis as Markers of Severe Hyponatremic Encephalopathy

We've all seen the "hyponatremia checklist" a dozen times: confusion, nausea, seizures, coma. But sometimes, the presentation is far more subtle, hiding behind symptoms we might dismiss as minor or unrelated to electrolyte derangement. Consider this striking case where a man in his 60s arrived with fever and headache, only to reveal a triad of **persistent hiccups**, **rhabdomyolysis** (evidenced by a massive CK elevation), and severe hyponatremia—all driven by an undiagnosed case of acute neuro-Behçet's disease complicated by SIADH.

What makes this case so compelling isn't just the rarity of neuro-Behçet's presenting concurrently with systemic symptoms (which is itself unusual); it's the specific constellation of "silent clues." While the patient's oral aphthae and genital ulcers eventually pointed toward Behçet's, the immediate red flags for severe CNS dysfunction were hidden in plain sight as persistent hiccups and muscle breakdown. In the era of "test everything," it is easy to miss the forest for the trees if we don't recognize that persistent hiccups can be a sign of diaphragmatic irritation from hyponatremic encephalopathy, and that rhabdomyolysis can be a direct metabolic consequence of severe sodium depletion. This case serves as a potent reminder that in patients with suspected CNS inflammation or SIADH, asking about hiccups and ordering a Creatine Kinase (CK) might be the difference between a delayed diagnosis and rapid stabilization.

The pathophysiology here offers a fascinating window into how severe hyponatremia wreaks havoc beyond simple cerebral edema. When serum sodium drops precipitously (this patient was at 124 mmol/L), water shifts into cells across all tissues, not just neurons. In the brainstem nuclei controlling respiration and swallowing, even mild swelling can trigger diaphragmatic spasms manifesting as intractable hiccups. The literature notes that while hiccups are often benign or transient, their persistence in the context of CNS pathology should raise immediate suspicion for intracranial pressure changes or direct brainstem involvement due to osmotic stress. Similarly, skeletal muscle cells are not immune to osmotic shock. The influx of water disrupts cellular membranes and mitochondrial function, leading to myonecrosis—a phenomenon clearly demonstrated in this patient where CK soared to 1349 U/L alongside hyponatremia. This wasn't exertional rhabdo; it was metabolic rhabdo masquerading as general malaise or arthralgia until lab work caught up with clinical suspicion.

Diagnostically, this case forces us to confront the difficulty of identifying acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) when classic parenchymal lesions are absent on MRI. Typically, neuro-BD presents with brainstem lesions visible on imaging months or years after initial mucocutaneous diagnosis. Here, we saw aseptic meningitis with a normal MRI but elevated CSF protein and lymphocytes—a pattern easily mistaken for viral meningoencephalitis or tuberculous meningitis. The presence of SIADH added another layer of complexity; while drug-induced SIADH is common in autoimmune management (e.g., cyclophosphamide), primary SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation is less recognized but plausible via IL-6 mediated pathways driving ADH release from inflamed hypothalamic nuclei. Crucially, ruling out infections like HSV/VZV through PCR was essential before committing to an autoimmune etiology, especially since the patient lacked initial genital ulcers—the hallmark sign that later emerged on day 5.

The treatment trajectory challenges some conventional wisdom regarding acute neuro-BD management. Standard teaching often dictates high-dose corticosteroids or aggressive immunosuppression for neurological involvement in Behçet's due to its life-threatening potential for parenchymal destruction (meningoencephalitis). However, this patient responded remarkably well to **colchicine monotherapy**. The resolution of his neurological symptoms, mucocutaneous lesions, and hyponatremia within days suggests that colchicine—perhaps via inhibition of neutrophil chemotaxis and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6—was sufficient to dampen this specific inflammatory cascade without needing steroids or other heavy hitters like cyclophosphamide or azathioprine. Of course, one must remain cautious; spontaneous remission is possible in meningoencephalitic presentations alone—but relying on chance is risky when treating inflammatory CNS disease. The success here highlights colchicine as a viable first-line consideration for early-stage neuro-inflammation where steroid side effects (particularly concerning given his undiagnosed Type 2 Diabetes) would have been detrimental.

For those managing complex cases involving fever and altered mental status (or subacute neurological complaints), remember that "nonspecific" doesn't mean "unimportant." If you encounter a patient with suspected CNS inflammation or SIADH who complains of stubborn hiccups or appears lethargic with vague muscle pain, look deeper than your basic metabolic panel order set includes CK levels explicitly? And ask specifically about hiccups—they could be your earliest warning sign of severe osmotic insult to the brainstem before seizures ever occur. Don't let the silence of early symptoms fool you; sometimes the loudest clues are hidden in plain sight among seemingly trivial complaints.

*   **Persistent Hiccups:** In patients with suspected CNS inflammation or SIADH, treat persistent hiccups as a potential marker of brainstem irritation from severe hyponatremia rather than dismissing them as gastrointestinal annoyance.
*   **Order CK Early:** Suspect metabolic rhabdomyolysis secondary to hyponatremia when patients present with generalized fatigue, malaise, or arthralgia alongside low sodium; check Creatine Kinase immediately rather than attributing it solely to sepsis or myalgias from infection.
*   **Normal MRI Doesn't Rule Out Neuro-BD:** Acute neuro-Behçet's can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal enhancement on MRI; do not exclude neuro-BD based on negative imaging if clinical suspicion remains high based on mucocutaneous findings + CSF evidence + exclusionary workup for infection/malignancy.
*   **Colchicine Potential:** While steroids are standard for acute neuro-BD colchicine may suffice in select cases presenting primarily with meningoencephalitis-like syndromes combined with mucocutaneous disease; monitor closely but avoid unnecessary immunosuppression if feasible (especially in diabetic patients).
*   **SIADH Mechanism Awareness:** Be aware that SIADH can arise de novo from CNS inflammation (via IL-6/brainstem pathways) independent of medication triggers; fluid restriction alone may resolve both electrolytes and neurological symptoms once the underlying inflammation subsides
