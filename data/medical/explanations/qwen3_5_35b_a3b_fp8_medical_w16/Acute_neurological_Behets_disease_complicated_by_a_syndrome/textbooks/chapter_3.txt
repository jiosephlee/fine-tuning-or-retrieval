Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Reasoning: Confirming SIADH and Excluding Mimics

The confirmation of Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH) in this case relied on the rigorous application of biochemical criteria that distinguish true SIADH from other causes of hyponatremia. The patient presented with profound hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) and hypo-osmolar serum (267 mOsm/kg), yet his urine was inappropriately concentrated (900 mOsm/kg) with elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L). Crucially, the patient was clinically euvolemic, lacking signs of dehydration or edema, which rules out hypovolemic or hypervolemic etiologies. The coexistence of low serum osmolality with high urine osmolality and sodium confirms a defect in water excretion driven by excess antidiuretic hormone (ADH), evidenced here by an elevated ADH level of 22.4 pg/mL. To validate this diagnosis, one must first exclude primary hormonal deficiencies that mimic SIADH physiology; consequently, the normal thyroid-stimulating hormone, free T4, and serum cortisol levels definitively ruled out hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency as contributors to the electrolyte disturbance.

With the endocrine causes excluded, the diagnostic focus shifted to identifying the trigger for SIADH within the context of acute neurological symptoms. The differential diagnosis prioritized infectious etiologies given the fever and headache profile. Extensive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was critical in this regard; while it revealed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein indicative of aseptic meningitis, all specific microbiological workups—including bacterial cultures, acid-fast bacilli stains for tuberculosis, polymerase chain reaction for Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) and Varicella Zoster Virus (VZV)—were negative. This effectively eliminated common viral meningoencephalitis and tuberculous meningitis as primary drivers of the clinical picture. Similarly, while primary central nervous system malignancy was considered due to the neurological decline, brain MRI showed no mass effect or hypothalamic lesions, and CSF cytology was devoid of malignant cells.

A nuanced aspect of this case involved interpreting the mildly elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level (704 U/mL). While significantly raised sIL-2R can be a marker for hematologic malignancies like CNS lymphoma or neurosarcoidosis—both conditions capable of causing CNS inflammation—the absence of corresponding radiographic findings on MRI and normal angiotensin-converting enzyme levels rendered these diagnoses unlikely. Instead, the elevation was interpreted as a non-specific reflection of systemic immune activation associated with Behçet's disease itself rather than a distinct neoplastic process. Finally, establishing the underlying etiology required confirming systemic Behçet's disease (BD). Using the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease, the patient scored five points: two for oral aphthae, two for genital ulcers (which developed shortly after admission), and one for folliculitis-like rashes. A score ≥4 defines definite BD. With infectious, neoplastic, and other autoimmune mimics rigorously excluded via targeted testing and clinical observation, the convergence of mucocutaneous findings consistent with BD and aseptic meningitis confirmed a diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet's disease complicated by SIADH.
