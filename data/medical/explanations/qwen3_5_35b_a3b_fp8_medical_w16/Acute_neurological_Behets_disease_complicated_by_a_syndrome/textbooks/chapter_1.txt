Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Dilemma

The clinical narrative begins with a man in his 60s presenting to the emergency department with an acute constellation of symptoms that initially obscured the underlying etiology. His chief complaints included a three-day history of high-grade fever, severe headache, general malaise, and persistent hiccups, accompanied by arthralgia affecting the shoulders and ankles. While the fever and headache are non-specific indicators of infection or inflammation, the persistence of hiccups served as a crucial clinical clue pointing toward central nervous system irritation or metabolic derangement. Concurrently, the patient reported oral aphthae, though the classic mucocutaneous stigmata of Behçet's disease (BD)—specifically genital ulcers—had not yet manifested at this initial visit. This triad of fever, neurological symptoms (headache and hiccups), and early mucosal involvement created a diagnostic challenge where the primary pathology could easily be misattributed to common infectious etiologies like viral meningoencephalitis or bacterial meningitis before the systemic nature of the disease became apparent.

Physical examination revealed a febrile patient with tachycardia but stable hemodynamics. Crucially, while he exhibited significant mucocutaneous findings including oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk, manual muscle testing was unremarkable, showing no focal neurological deficits. Perhaps most importantly for interpreting his laboratory results, the patient was clinically assessed as euvolemic; there was no evidence of peripheral edema or dehydration. This distinction is pivotal when evaluating hyponatremia. In a hypovolemic state, hyponatremia typically reflects volume depletion with appropriate antidiuretic hormone (ADH) release driven by hemodynamic stress. Conversely, in this euvolemic patient with marked hyponatremia (serum sodium 124 mmol/L), the differential narrows significantly toward conditions causing inappropriate water retention relative to solute load, such as Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH), rather than simple volume loss or adrenal insufficiency.

The significance of persistent hiccups in this context cannot be overstated. While often dismissed as benign gastrointestinal distress or post-anesthetic sequelae, persistent hiccups in a febrile patient with altered mental status or headache can indicate stimulation of the hiccup reflex arc within the medulla oblongata. In this case, they likely represented early signs of raised intracranial pressure or direct chemical irritation from severe hyponatremia itself. Hyponatremia disrupts neuronal cell volume regulation; even moderate reductions in serum sodium can lead to cerebral edema if uncompensated by cellular mechanisms. The presence of such specific neurological symptoms alongside profound hyponatremia prompted immediate investigation into whether an endocrine disturbance was driving central nervous system dysfunction or if both were secondary to a shared inflammatory process.

Formulating an initial differential diagnosis required navigating between infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune pathways given the overlapping features of fever, CNS inflammation (evidenced later by CSF pleocytosis), and electrolyte imbalance. Infectious causes were paramount concerns; viral encephalitis (particularly Herpes Simplex Virus) and bacterial meningitis were top considerations given the acute onset and meningeal signs like headache and fever. Tuberculous meningitis was also on the list due to its ability to cause subacute CNS inflammation and SIADH-like physiology. Simultaneously, primary CNS malignancies such as lymphoma were considered due to elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptors (sIL-2R) found on screening and constitutional symptoms like weight loss and fatigue. Finally, autoimmune entities like neurosarcoidosis entered the differential due to their capacity to mimic inflammatory CNS syndromes without distinct radiological findings in early stages.

However, as investigations progressed over days 3 through 5, several key developments shifted the diagnostic trajectory away from these initial broad categories toward Behçet's disease. The emergence of painful genital ulcers on day 5 provided the definitive "anchor" sign for BD according to international classification criteria. When combined with pre-existing oral aphthae and folliculitic rashes—the latter being highly specific for BD—the cumulative score reached five points (with genital aphthosis carrying two points), satisfying the threshold for definite disease (>4 points). At this juncture, it became clear that what appeared as a complex interplay of sepsis-like illness or occult cancer was actually an acute flare of systemic vasculitis manifesting simultaneously with neurological involvement (neuro-BD). The initial diagnostic dilemma thus resolved into recognizing that acute neuro-BD could present concurrently with systemic features rather than years later—a rare presentation—and that SIADH was likely a paraneoplastic-like consequence of this intense CNS inflammation rather than a separate entity or medication side effect pending further confirmation via treatment response.
