Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 4: ## Therapeutic Strategy: Colchicine Monotherapy in Acute Neuro-BD

The management of acute neurological Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) traditionally relies on high-dose intravenous corticosteroids, often followed by aggressive immunosuppressive agents such as cyclophosphamide or azathioprine, particularly when parenchymal brainstem lesions are present. In this case, the patient presented with a complex clinical picture involving fever, headache, aseptic meningitis, and syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). While the severity of neurological symptoms typically mandates immediate steroid intervention to prevent irreversible parenchymal damage, the treating team opted for colchicine monotherapy. This decision was driven by the specific phenotype of the patient's disease—primarily meningeal involvement without distinct parenchymal lesions on MRI—and the presence of concurrent systemic inflammation manifesting as mucocutaneous ulcers. The choice to avoid corticosteroids initially reflects a nuanced application of therapeutic principles where the risks of long-term immunosuppression must be weighed against the potential for spontaneous remission in less destructive forms of neuro-BD.

Colchicine functions through mechanisms distinct from corticosteroids, primarily by binding to tubulin and inhibiting microtubule polymerization. This action disrupts neutrophil chemotaxis, adhesion molecule expression, and degranulation, thereby dampening the innate immune response that drives Behçet's vasculitis. Recent evidence suggests that colchicine may also suppress proinflammatory cytokines relevant to neuro-inflammation, including Interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-6. Since elevated IL-6 is implicated in the pathogenesis of SIADH via stimulation of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) release from the posterior pituitary, colchicine offers a dual therapeutic benefit in this scenario: controlling the underlying mucocutaneous and neurological inflammation while potentially mitigating the cytokine storm driving water retention. The successful resolution of both neurological symptoms and hyponatremia with colchicine alone supports the hypothesis that modulating this specific inflammatory pathway can reverse SIADH associated with acute neuro-BD without requiring potent glucocorticoid suppression.

Concurrently, the management of SIADH required a conservative strategy focused on fluid restriction rather than pharmacological blockade or hypertonic saline administration. The patient exhibited euvolemic hyponatremia with serum sodium dropping to 124 mmol/L but lacked severe neurological sequelae such as seizures or coma that would necessitate urgent correction with hypertonic saline. Fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) allowed for gradual normalization of serum osmolality and sodium levels by creating a negative water balance while preventing rapid shifts that could lead to osmotic demyelination syndrome. The absence of edema and preserved renal function facilitated this approach, allowing natural diuresis to correct the electrolyte disturbance once the underlying inflammatory drive was suppressed by colchicine. This combined approach highlights that in select cases of acute neuro-BD presenting with aseptic meningitis and SIADH, targeted anti-inflammatory therapy paired with supportive fluid management can achieve complete remission without resorting to standard high-risk immunosuppressive protocols.
