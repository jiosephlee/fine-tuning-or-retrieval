Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] Why is the timing of neurological involvement in this case considered atypical for Behçet's disease, and what are the classic demographic or clinical predictors that usually distinguish isolated mucocutaneous BD from those who will develop major organ involvement?

The timing of neurological involvement in this case is atypical because **acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD)** classically manifests years after the initial diagnosis of systemic mucocutaneous disease. As noted in the background of this report, major organ involvement, including CNS pathology, typically develops approximately four years after the onset of Behçet's disease. Furthermore, neurological symptoms at the very presentation of BD are rare, occurring in fewer than 5% of patients. In contrast, this patient presented with **concurrent** diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD; the neurological symptoms (fever, headache, aseptic meningitis) and mucocutaneous lesions (oral aphthae, genital ulcers) emerged simultaneously within days of each other. While acute neuro-BD can present as meningoencephalitis with brainstem lesions or parenchymal abnormalities on MRI, this patient's "pure" aseptic meningitis without radiographic evidence further complicates the typical clinical picture where structural brainstem involvement is expected.

Regarding predictors distinguishing isolated mucocutaneous disease from those progressing to major organ involvement:
*   **Demographics:** While this case involves a male patient (men are statistically more prone to severe vascular and neurological forms than women), age alone is not a definitive predictor here as he was in his 60s at onset. The critical distinction lies in the **phenotype**. Patients who progress to major organ involvement often have a younger age at onset (typically second or third decade), though late-onset cases like this one exist.
*   **Clinical Predictors:** The presence of **genital ulcers** is highly specific for BD and carries a higher risk for severe extracutaneous manifestations compared to oral aphthae alone. Additionally, the development of **uveitis** is a strong predictor for systemic severity and neurologic progression. In this case, the patient lacked ocular involvement initially but developed genital ulcers shortly after admission alongside neurological decline.
*   **Pathophysiology:** The transition from mucocutaneous to major organ disease is driven by widespread vasculitis and intense immune activation. The concurrent appearance of symptoms suggests an aggressive initial inflammatory phenotype rather than a gradual evolution over years.

Finally, it is crucial to recognize that while colchicine is standard for mucocutaneous BD, it is generally considered ineffective for controlling acute neuro-BD monotherapy according to systematic reviews; however, this unique case demonstrated remission with colchicine alone, likely due to its ability to suppress IL-6 mediated inflammation driving both the meningeal irritation and the resultant SIADH.
