Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Management] While high-dose corticosteroids are traditionally the first-line therapy for acute neuro-Behçet's disease, discuss the unique factors in this case that justified a trial of colchicine monotherapy and what mechanistic properties of colchicine might explain its efficacy in resolving both mucocutaneous lesions and SIADH-associated CNS inflammation.

**Clinical Pearl:** While high-dose corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of therapy for acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD), this case illustrates that **colchicine monotherapy** can be a viable, effective alternative in select presentations, specifically when the disease manifests primarily as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal brainstem lesions.

In this patient, the decision to utilize colchicine instead of immediate corticosteroids was driven by three distinct factors:
1.  **Radiographic Absence of Parenchymal Disease:** The patient presented with "acute" neuro-BD characterized by aseptic meningitis (elevated CSF protein and lymphocytes) but lacked the hallmark brainstem lesions or parenchymal edema seen on MRI. Spontaneous resolution has been documented in similar meningoencephalitic variants, suggesting a potentially less aggressive course amenable to targeted anti-inflammatory therapy rather than broad immunosuppression.
2.  **Contraindications and Comorbidities:** The patient had poorly controlled type 2 diabetes (HbA1c 8.2%) and rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK). Initiating high-dose corticosteroids would have posed a significant risk of exacerbating hyperglycemia and worsening muscle injury, making colchicine a safer initial choice.
3.  **Systemic Phenotype:** As the patient fulfilled criteria for *definite* systemic BD with active mucocutaneous involvement (oral/genital ulcers, folliculitis), colchicine—already the gold standard for these specific manifestations—offered a dual-target strategy to treat both the skin/mucosa and the CNS simultaneously.

Regarding mechanism, colchicine likely addressed both the neurological inflammation and SIADH through its inhibition of microtubule polymerization, which disrupts **neutrophil chemotaxis and degranulation**. Crucially, colchicine suppresses the production of proinflammatory cytokines, particularly **interleukin-6 (IL-6)** and IL-1β. Since IL-6 is a potent stimulator of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) release from the hypothalamus-pituitary axis, reducing IL-6 levels via colchicine provides a plausible biological explanation for the rapid resolution of SIADH alongside neurological symptoms in this case. Thus, in patients with acute neuro-BD presenting as aseptic meningitis where steroid toxicity is a concern, colchicine represents a mechanistically sound therapeutic trial that targets the upstream cytokine drive linking CNS inflammation to endocrine dysfunction.
