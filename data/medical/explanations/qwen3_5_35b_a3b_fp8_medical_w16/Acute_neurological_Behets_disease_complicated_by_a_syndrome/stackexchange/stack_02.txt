Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Pathophysiology] This patient presented with hyponatremia, persistent hiccups, and elevated creatine kinase. Explain the pathophysiological link between severe hyponatremia and both hiccups and rhabdomyolysis, and how these 'non-neurological' symptoms served as critical clues to the severity of the SIADH.

**Clinical Pearl:** Severe hyponatremia in the context of central nervous system (CNS) inflammation—such as acute neuro-Behçet's disease—can manifest with "non-neurologic" symptoms like persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis. These findings are not merely coincidental but represent direct physiological consequences of profound sodium depletion and should serve as red flags for the severity of the underlying Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH).

The pathophysiological link begins with **persistent hiccups**. Hyponatremia alters neuronal membrane potentials, lowering the threshold for excitation in the medullary centers responsible for the hiccup reflex arc. In this patient, whose serum sodium dropped to 124 mmol/L, this excitability likely resulted from cerebral edema or direct irritation of the hypothalamic-brainstem axis. As noted in the discussion of this case, persistent hiccups are a recognized, though often overlooked, clinical clue to severe hyponatremia secondary to CNS pathology. They signal that the electrolyte disturbance has reached a threshold where even subtle brainstem dysfunction manifests as motor spasms.

Regarding **rhabdomyolysis**, evidenced here by an elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 1349 U/L, the mechanism is multifactorial. First, severe hyponatremia induces cellular swelling and disrupts intracellular ion homeostasis, leading to direct myocyte injury and membrane instability. Second, in states of CNS inflammation and SIADH, patients often experience generalized malaise and fatigue; if they become immobilized or develop subclinical seizures (which can occur with significant hyponatremia), muscle breakdown ensues. The case report explicitly identifies rhabdomyolysis as a "recognised but often overlooked complication" of severe hyponatremia that resolves alongside the correction of sodium levels once the inflammatory driver is treated with colchicine.

These symptoms served as critical clues because they highlighted a discrepancy between the patient's mental status ("alert") and the severity of his biochemical derangement. While classic severe hyponatremia presents with confusion or coma, this patient's prominent somatic complaints—hiccups and muscle pain/failure—directed attention toward the magnitude of the osmotic shift rather than just looking for structural brain lesions on MRI (which were normal). Recognizing these extra-neurologic signs prevented a delay in diagnosing severe SIADH driven by acute neuro-Behçet's disease and underscored that even without frank altered consciousness, profound hyponatremia carries immediate risks of neuromuscular compromise requiring urgent intervention.
