Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Diagnostic Reasoning] Given that the patient's brain MRI was completely normal despite clinical evidence of CNS inflammation (headache, vertigo, aseptic meningitis), how does this imaging finding influence your diagnostic confidence regarding acute neuro-Behçet's disease compared to other causes like tumoral meningitis or abscess?

**Clinical Pearl:** A normal brain MRI does **not** exclude the diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD); instead, it helps distinguish aseptic meningitis from space-occupying lesions like tumors or abscesses.

In this case, the patient presented with classic signs of CNS inflammation—fever, headache, vertigo, and aseptic meningitis on lumbar puncture—but lacked parenchymal signal abnormalities on MRI. This imaging finding significantly increases diagnostic confidence for **acute neuro-BD presenting as isolated meningeal inflammation** while simultaneously lowering the probability of structural etiologies such as tumoral meningitis or brain abscess.

Here is the clinical reasoning based on the case:

1.  **Exclusion of Structural Pathology:** Tumors (e.g., lymphoma) and abscesses typically manifest as mass lesions, ring-enhancing structures, or distinct areas of edema on MRI. In this patient, the "completely normal" MRI, combined with negative CSF cytology and lack of focal deficits on exam, effectively rules out these compressive or infiltrative processes. As noted in the discussion, while *Neurosarcoidosis* can sometimes present with normal early MRIs, the specific constellation of mucocutaneous findings (oral/genital ulcers) in this patient makes BD far more likely than sarcoidosis once infectious causes are excluded.
2.  **Pathophysiology of Normal Imaging in Acute Neuro-BD:** Acute neuro-BD encompasses two phenotypes: parenchymal (brainstem/basal ganglia involvement) and meningeal. While parenchymal lesions are common in chronic or severe cases requiring aggressive immunosuppression, this patient exemplifies the **meningeal variant**. In acute meningoencephalitis without parenchymal destruction or significant edema, MRI sensitivity is limited; the pathology resides in the leptomeninges and CSF chemistry rather than visible tissue change. Therefore, a normal scan reflects a less destructive inflammatory process confined to the meninges rather than an absence of disease.
3.  **Diagnostic Integration:** The diagnosis rests on integrating three pillars seen here:
    *   **Systemic Clues:** Definite Behçet's disease based on oral aphthae + genital ulcers + skin lesions (International Criteria for BD).
    *   **CSF Evidence:** Aseptic meningitis (elevated protein/pleocytosis) without bacteria/virus/fungi/malignancy.
    *   **Imaging Context:** The absence of tumor/abscess allows us to attribute the aseptic meningitis directly to the systemic inflammatory driver (BD).

**Takeaway for Rounds:** When evaluating a patient with suspected neuro-inflammation where infectious workups are negative and clinical suspicion for autoimmune disease is high (especially with mucocutaneous stigmata), do not let a normal MRI dissuade you from a diagnosis of acute neuro-BD if CSF supports aseptic meningitis. Conversely, use that same normal MRI to confidently pivot away from surgical emergencies like abscesses or neoplasms that would mandate biopsy or resection.
