Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Dilemma

The clinical narrative begins with a man in his 60s presenting to the emergency department with an acute constellation of symptoms that initially obscured the underlying etiology. His chief complaints included a three-day history of high-grade fever, severe headache, general malaise, and persistent hiccups, accompanied by arthralgia affecting the shoulders and ankles. While the fever and headache are non-specific indicators of infection or inflammation, the persistence of hiccups served as a crucial clinical clue pointing toward central nervous system irritation or metabolic derangement. Concurrently, the patient reported oral aphthae, though the classic mucocutaneous stigmata of Behçet's disease (BD)—specifically genital ulcers—had not yet manifested at this initial visit. This triad of fever, neurological symptoms (headache and hiccups), and early mucosal involvement created a diagnostic challenge where the primary pathology could easily be misattributed to common infectious etiologies like viral meningoencephalitis or bacterial meningitis before the systemic nature of the disease became apparent.

Physical examination revealed a febrile patient with tachycardia but stable hemodynamics. Crucially, while he exhibited significant mucocutaneous findings including oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk, manual muscle testing was unremarkable, showing no focal neurological deficits. Perhaps most importantly for interpreting his laboratory results, the patient was clinically assessed as euvolemic; there was no evidence of peripheral edema or dehydration. This distinction is pivotal when evaluating hyponatremia. In a hypovolemic state, hyponatremia typically reflects volume depletion with appropriate antidiuretic hormone (ADH) release driven by hemodynamic stress. Conversely, in this euvolemic patient with marked hyponatremia (serum sodium 124 mmol/L), the differential narrows significantly toward conditions causing inappropriate water retention relative to solute load, such as Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH), rather than simple volume loss or adrenal insufficiency.

The significance of persistent hiccups in this context cannot be overstated. While often dismissed as benign gastrointestinal distress or post-anesthetic sequelae, persistent hiccups in a febrile patient with altered mental status or headache can indicate stimulation of the hiccup reflex arc within the medulla oblongata. In this case, they likely represented early signs of raised intracranial pressure or direct chemical irritation from severe hyponatremia itself. Hyponatremia disrupts neuronal cell volume regulation; even moderate reductions in serum sodium can lead to cerebral edema if uncompensated by cellular mechanisms. The presence of such specific neurological symptoms alongside profound hyponatremia prompted immediate investigation into whether an endocrine disturbance was driving central nervous system dysfunction or if both were secondary to a shared inflammatory process.

Formulating an initial differential diagnosis required navigating between infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune pathways given the overlapping features of fever, CNS inflammation (evidenced later by CSF pleocytosis), and electrolyte imbalance. Infectious causes were paramount concerns; viral encephalitis (particularly Herpes Simplex Virus) and bacterial meningitis were top considerations given the acute onset and meningeal signs like headache and fever. Tuberculous meningitis was also on the list due to its ability to cause subacute CNS inflammation and SIADH-like physiology. Simultaneously, primary CNS malignancies such as lymphoma were considered due to elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptors (sIL-2R) found on screening and constitutional symptoms like weight loss and fatigue. Finally, autoimmune entities like neurosarcoidosis entered the differential due to their capacity to mimic inflammatory CNS syndromes without distinct radiological findings in early stages.

However, as investigations progressed over days 3 through 5, several key developments shifted the diagnostic trajectory away from these initial broad categories toward Behçet's disease. The emergence of painful genital ulcers on day 5 provided the definitive "anchor" sign for BD according to international classification criteria. When combined with pre-existing oral aphthae and folliculitic rashes—the latter being highly specific for BD—the cumulative score reached five points (with genital aphthosis carrying two points), satisfying the threshold for definite disease (>4 points). At this juncture, it became clear that what appeared as a complex interplay of sepsis-like illness or occult cancer was actually an acute flare of systemic vasculitis manifesting simultaneously with neurological involvement (neuro-BD). The initial diagnostic dilemma thus resolved into recognizing that acute neuro-BD could present concurrently with systemic features rather than years later—a rare presentation—and that SIADH was likely a paraneoplastic-like consequence of this intense CNS inflammation rather than a separate entity or medication side effect pending further confirmation via treatment response.

## Pathophysiological Basis: Behçet's Disease and Neuro-Inflammation

Behçet's disease (BD) is a chronic, relapsing, multisystem vasculitis characterized primarily by recurrent oral aphthous ulcers, genital ulcers, and uveitis, often accompanied by distinct skin lesions such as erythema nodosum or folliculitis-like rashes. While the disease affects multiple organ systems, neurological involvement—termed neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD)—occurs in approximately 10% to 20% of patients. In the vast majority of cases, neuro-BD manifests as a delayed complication, appearing several years after the initial diagnosis of systemic BD. Consequently, the concurrent presentation of active systemic mucocutaneous symptoms and acute neurological deficits, as seen in this patient, represents a rare clinical phenotype where the central nervous system is involved at the very inception of the disease. Understanding this deviation from the typical timeline is crucial for early recognition; while classic presentations evolve slowly over years, acute neuro-BD can present abruptly with meningoencephalitic features that mimic infectious etiologies.

Acute neuro-BD typically involves two distinct pathological patterns: parenchymal brain lesions and non-parenchymal inflammation. Parenchymal lesions predominantly affect the brainstem and basal ganglia due to their rich vascular supply and susceptibility to small-vessel vasculitis; these patients often present with focal neurological deficits such as hemiparesis or cranial nerve palsies. Conversely, non-parenchymal inflammation presents as aseptic meningitis or meningoencephalitis without discrete structural damage visible on standard imaging. The case presented highlights this latter pattern. The patient exhibited severe headache, fever, and vertigo indicative of meningeal irritation and diffuse cerebral inflammation, yet his brain MRI was entirely normal regarding parenchymal signal abnormalities or mass effect. This underscores a critical diagnostic principle: in the early phases of acute neuro-BD, particularly when the primary pathology is confined to the meninges or perivascular spaces rather than causing frank tissue necrosis or edema detectable by conventional MRI sequences, radiological findings may be absent. Therefore, a normal MRI does not exclude the diagnosis if clinical suspicion remains high based on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) evidence of aseptic meningitis (lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein) alongside characteristic mucocutaneous findings.

The development of Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH) in this context provides a compelling example of how localized CNS inflammation can disrupt systemic homeostasis through cytokine-mediated pathways. SIADH is defined by euvolemic hyponatremia resulting from excessive release of arginine vasopressin (AVP), leading to impaired free water excretion. While SIADH is classically associated with malignancy or drugs in BD patients (such as cyclophosphamide), this case supports a direct mechanistic link between untreated neuro-inflammation and AVP dysregulation. The proposed pathophysiology centers on monocyte activation within the inflamed CNS environment. Activated monocytes produce proinflammatory cytokines, most notably Interleukin-6 (IL-6). Elevated levels of IL-6 have been documented in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-BD and are known to stimulate hypothalamic neurons to secrete AVP independently of plasma osmolality signals. Although IL-6 levels were not directly measured in this specific case report, the markedly elevated serum C-reactive protein serves as a surrogate marker for this intense systemic inflammatory state driven by IL-6 production. Thus, the patient's hyponatremia was likely not merely coincidental but a downstream consequence of cytokine-driven hypothalamic stimulation triggered by the underlying vasculitic process affecting the central nervous system.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: Confirming SIADH and Excluding Mimics

The confirmation of Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH) in this case relied on the rigorous application of biochemical criteria that distinguish true SIADH from other causes of hyponatremia. The patient presented with profound hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) and hypo-osmolar serum (267 mOsm/kg), yet his urine was inappropriately concentrated (900 mOsm/kg) with elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L). Crucially, the patient was clinically euvolemic, lacking signs of dehydration or edema, which rules out hypovolemic or hypervolemic etiologies. The coexistence of low serum osmolality with high urine osmolality and sodium confirms a defect in water excretion driven by excess antidiuretic hormone (ADH), evidenced here by an elevated ADH level of 22.4 pg/mL. To validate this diagnosis, one must first exclude primary hormonal deficiencies that mimic SIADH physiology; consequently, the normal thyroid-stimulating hormone, free T4, and serum cortisol levels definitively ruled out hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency as contributors to the electrolyte disturbance.

With the endocrine causes excluded, the diagnostic focus shifted to identifying the trigger for SIADH within the context of acute neurological symptoms. The differential diagnosis prioritized infectious etiologies given the fever and headache profile. Extensive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was critical in this regard; while it revealed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein indicative of aseptic meningitis, all specific microbiological workups—including bacterial cultures, acid-fast bacilli stains for tuberculosis, polymerase chain reaction for Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) and Varicella Zoster Virus (VZV)—were negative. This effectively eliminated common viral meningoencephalitis and tuberculous meningitis as primary drivers of the clinical picture. Similarly, while primary central nervous system malignancy was considered due to the neurological decline, brain MRI showed no mass effect or hypothalamic lesions, and CSF cytology was devoid of malignant cells.

A nuanced aspect of this case involved interpreting the mildly elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level (704 U/mL). While significantly raised sIL-2R can be a marker for hematologic malignancies like CNS lymphoma or neurosarcoidosis—both conditions capable of causing CNS inflammation—the absence of corresponding radiographic findings on MRI and normal angiotensin-converting enzyme levels rendered these diagnoses unlikely. Instead, the elevation was interpreted as a non-specific reflection of systemic immune activation associated with Behçet's disease itself rather than a distinct neoplastic process. Finally, establishing the underlying etiology required confirming systemic Behçet's disease (BD). Using the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease, the patient scored five points: two for oral aphthae, two for genital ulcers (which developed shortly after admission), and one for folliculitis-like rashes. A score ≥4 defines definite BD. With infectious, neoplastic, and other autoimmune mimics rigorously excluded via targeted testing and clinical observation, the convergence of mucocutaneous findings consistent with BD and aseptic meningitis confirmed a diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet's disease complicated by SIADH.

## Therapeutic Strategy: Colchicine Monotherapy in Acute Neuro-BD

The management of acute neurological Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) traditionally relies on high-dose intravenous corticosteroids, often followed by aggressive immunosuppressive agents such as cyclophosphamide or azathioprine, particularly when parenchymal brainstem lesions are present. In this case, the patient presented with a complex clinical picture involving fever, headache, aseptic meningitis, and syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). While the severity of neurological symptoms typically mandates immediate steroid intervention to prevent irreversible parenchymal damage, the treating team opted for colchicine monotherapy. This decision was driven by the specific phenotype of the patient's disease—primarily meningeal involvement without distinct parenchymal lesions on MRI—and the presence of concurrent systemic inflammation manifesting as mucocutaneous ulcers. The choice to avoid corticosteroids initially reflects a nuanced application of therapeutic principles where the risks of long-term immunosuppression must be weighed against the potential for spontaneous remission in less destructive forms of neuro-BD.

Colchicine functions through mechanisms distinct from corticosteroids, primarily by binding to tubulin and inhibiting microtubule polymerization. This action disrupts neutrophil chemotaxis, adhesion molecule expression, and degranulation, thereby dampening the innate immune response that drives Behçet's vasculitis. Recent evidence suggests that colchicine may also suppress proinflammatory cytokines relevant to neuro-inflammation, including Interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-6. Since elevated IL-6 is implicated in the pathogenesis of SIADH via stimulation of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) release from the posterior pituitary, colchicine offers a dual therapeutic benefit in this scenario: controlling the underlying mucocutaneous and neurological inflammation while potentially mitigating the cytokine storm driving water retention. The successful resolution of both neurological symptoms and hyponatremia with colchicine alone supports the hypothesis that modulating this specific inflammatory pathway can reverse SIADH associated with acute neuro-BD without requiring potent glucocorticoid suppression.

Concurrently, the management of SIADH required a conservative strategy focused on fluid restriction rather than pharmacological blockade or hypertonic saline administration. The patient exhibited euvolemic hyponatremia with serum sodium dropping to 124 mmol/L but lacked severe neurological sequelae such as seizures or coma that would necessitate urgent correction with hypertonic saline. Fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) allowed for gradual normalization of serum osmolality and sodium levels by creating a negative water balance while preventing rapid shifts that could lead to osmotic demyelination syndrome. The absence of edema and preserved renal function facilitated this approach, allowing natural diuresis to correct the electrolyte disturbance once the underlying inflammatory drive was suppressed by colchicine. This combined approach highlights that in select cases of acute neuro-BD presenting with aseptic meningitis and SIADH, targeted anti-inflammatory therapy paired with supportive fluid management can achieve complete remission without resorting to standard high-risk immunosuppressive protocols.
