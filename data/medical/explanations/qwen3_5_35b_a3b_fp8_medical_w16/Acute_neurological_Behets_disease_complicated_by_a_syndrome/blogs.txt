Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# When the Headache is the First Sign: Concurrent Diagnosis of Systemic Behçet's and Acute Neuro-Behçet's

Let's talk about a diagnostic trap that could easily turn into a nightmare if you miss the subtle clues. Usually, when we think of **Neuro-Behçet's Disease (Neuro-BD)**, we picture a patient who has lived with "classic" Behçet's—recurrent oral ulcers, genital sores, uveitis—for years before suddenly crashing with brainstem encephalitis. The literature tells us neurological involvement typically strikes 4 to 5 years *after* the initial diagnosis.

But what happens when the neurology hits first? Or worse, when it hits *concurrently* with the systemic disease?

This is exactly what happened in a fascinating case report where a man in his 60s walked into the ED with fever, headache, and hyponatremia. He didn't have genital ulcers yet. His brain MRI was pristine. And yet, he had acute Neuro-BD complicated by SIADH (Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion). This case challenges our default algorithms: just because the MRI doesn't show a lesion doesn't mean there isn't inflammation in the CNS, and just because you don't see genital ulcers on Day 1 doesn't rule out Behçet's until they appear.

## The "Normal" MRI Trap in Early Neuro-Inflammation

One of the most striking aspects of this case is the brain MRI. In many forms of meningoencephalitis or parenchymal Neuro-BD, we expect to see hyperintensities in the basal ganglia or brainstem on FLAIR sequences. Here? Nothing. No lesions. No edema. Just a man complaining of worsening headaches and vertigo three days after admission.

This is a critical teaching moment for anyone managing febrile patients with altered mental status or headaches: **A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute Neuro-Behçet's.**

Acute Neuro-BD often presents as aseptic meningitis or meningoencephalitis before parenchymal destruction occurs. In this patient, while his CSF showed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein (signaling inflammation), the radiologist saw nothing suspicious for typical Behçet's lesions or even mass effect from edema. If you rely solely on imaging to rule out CNS pathology in a patient with compatible clinical signs (fever + headache + mucocutaneous hints), you risk missing the window for early intervention. The diagnosis here rested entirely on clinical synthesis: fever + oral aphthae + eventual genital ulcer + aseptic meningitis = Definite Behçet's + Acute Neuro-BD.

## Hyponatremia as a Hidden Clue: The SIADH Connection

The patient arrived with severe hyponatremia (Na+ 124 mmol/L) and persistent hiccups. While we instinctively look for infections like HSV/VZV or malignancies when seeing hyponatremia plus CNS symptoms, this case shines a light on a less discussed phenomenon: **SIADH driven purely by CNS inflammation.**

The patient met strict criteria for SIADH: hypotonic hyponatremia, elevated urine osmolality (>300), elevated urine sodium (>40), euvolemia, and suppressed cortisol/thyroid function—all without offending drugs like carbamazepine or cyclophosphamide (which are known culprits in BD patients). But what triggered it? Likely an IL-6 mediated storm from the inflamed meninges stimulating ADH release centrally.

**Clinical Pearl:** Don't ignore "non-neurologic" symptoms like persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis (this patient had massive CK elevation) in the setting of fever and headache. They can be downstream effects of severe hyponatremia caused by central inflammation, pointing you toward an etiology like Neuro-BD rather than primary metabolic issues.

## Treating Without Steroids: The Colchicine Surprise

Here comes the kicker that will make your eyes pop off your screen regarding management guidelines. Standard textbooks scream "High-dose IV steroids!" for acute Neuro-BD because it prevents permanent parenchymal damage and reduces mortality in large vessel or deep gray matter involvement. Yet, this patient was treated successfully with **colchicine monotherapy**.

No pulse steroids. No cyclophosphamide. No azathioprine just colchicine plus fluid restriction for SIADH and topical care for ulcers. The result? Fever broke within 24 hours of starting colchicine; sodium normalized; neurological symptoms vanished over two weeks; no relapse after four years of maintenance therapy.

While spontaneous remission is always a confounder in single-case reports, this outcome suggests that in selected cases of acute Neuro-BD presenting primarily as aseptic meningitis (without frank parenchymal infarction or hemorrhage), colchicine might be sufficient to dampen the inflammatory cascade via neutrophil inhibition and cytokine suppression (specifically IL-1β and IL-6). It forces us to reconsider whether we must always jump straight to heavy immunosuppression for every new-onset neuro-case if the phenotype fits meningeal inflammation better than parenchymal necrosis—but obviously, this requires extreme vigilance given the stakes involved in untreated neuro-behçet's!

## Key Take-Home Points for the Emergency Physician & Hospitalist

*   **Concurrent Presentation is Possible:** Do not wait years to suspect Neuro-Behçet's if mucocutaneous signs are emerging simultaneously with neurological complaints; up to 5% present concurrently at onset rather than years later.
*   **MRI Can Be Normal:** A negative brain MRI does not rule out acute meningoencephalitic Neuro-BD; rely heavily on CSF analysis (lymphocytic pleocytosis) combined with clinical findings when suspicion is high enough based on mucosal lesions appearing during hospitalization (e.g., scrotal ulcer appearing days later).
*   **Hyponatremia = Red Flag:** In patients with fever/headache/meningismus who develop SIADH without obvious medication triggers or lung cancer/malignancy immediately evident on CT chest/abdomen, consider CNS inflammation as an intrinsic driver (potentially IL-6 mediated).
*   **Look for Genital Ulcers:** If oral aphthae are present but BD criteria aren't fully met initially, keep searching daily; genital ulcers can appear late in their course and provide definitive scoring points (+2) to push diagnosis over threshold (+4 needed).
*   **Colchicine May Suffice:** While steroids remain standard for severe parenchymal disease, consider that colchicine monotherapy *might* resolve acute meningeal-predominant presentations quickly without immediate need for aggressive immunosuppression—though close monitoring is mandatory given individual variability in response rates compared to steroid-responsive cohorts.



# Beyond the Brain: When Hyponatremia Signals Neuro-Behçet's

We've all seen the classic "fever, headache, and confusion" triad in the ED. Usually, our brains immediately sprint toward meningitis, encephalitis, or sepsis. But what happens when the fever persists, the headache is relentless, and the patient is profoundly hyponatremic? If you're thinking about SIADH as your primary suspect, you might be missing a crucial piece of the puzzle if the patient also has mouth sores or genital ulcers.

Today's case comes from *BMJ Case Reports* and flips the script on how we think about neuro-inflammation. We aren't just talking about a standard case of Behçet's disease (BD); we're looking at a rare instance where **acute neuro-Behçet's disease** presented concurrently with systemic findings and was complicated by **Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH)**. What makes this even more fascinating is that this patient didn't get hit with steroids or cyclophosphamide—the usual culprits for drug-induced SIADH—and still developed profound hyponatremia driven purely by CNS inflammation.

### The Presentation: More Than Just a Headache

Our patient was a man in his 60s walking into the ER with three days of fever (38.1°C), severe headache, generalized malaise, and persistent hiccups. He also complained of arthralgia and noted oral aphthae. On paper, this looks like viral encephalitis or perhaps early bacterial meningitis. However, a closer look at his physical exam revealed the tell-tale signs of Behçet's: oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes on his trunk, and later, a painful scrotal ulcer appeared on day 5.

The lab work painted a confusing picture initially:
*   **Severe Hyponatremia:** Sodium dropped to 124 mmol/L.
*   **Hyperthermia & Elevated Inflammatory Markers:** CRP was sky-high at 180 mg/L; ESR was 70 mm/hr.
*   **"Red Herrings":** He had elevated CK (rhabdomyolysis likely from severe hyponatremia/stress) and hyperglycemia (undiagnosed Type 2 DM).

Crucially, his volume status was clinically euvolemic. No edema, no dehydration signs—just pure water retention issues masked as fatigue and hiccups.

### The Diagnostic Dilemma: Ruling Out the Usual Suspects

When you see hyponatremia + CNS symptoms + fever, your differential is long. Did he have HSV/VZV encephalitis? Bacterial meningitis? Tuberculosis? Or maybe a paraneoplastic syndrome like small cell lung cancer?

In this case, we had to systematically dismantle every alternative diagnosis:
*   **Infection:** CSF showed aseptic meningitis (lymphocytic pleocytosis) but cultures were negative for bacteria, TB, fungi (Cryptococcus), and viruses (HSV/VZV).
*   **Malignancy:** Chest/Abdomen CTs were clear. CSF cytology was negative for malignant cells. While sIL-2R was slightly elevated (often seen in lymphoma), it wasn't specific enough to override clinical findings without radiographic evidence of masses or adenopathy.
*   **Autoimmune Mimics:** Sarcoidosis was considered but ruled out by normal ACE levels and lack of granulomas on biopsy (not done here but implied by workup). ANA/ANCA profiles were negative for other connective tissue diseases.

Then came the MRI brain scan... which was essentially normal regarding parenchymal lesions in the brainstem or hypothalamus—a classic location for neuro-BD lesions! This is a critical teaching point: **Acute neuro-Behçet's can present as aseptic meningitis without visible parenchymal brain lesions on MRI.** Do not let a "normal" MRI dissuade you from pursuing neuro-BD if the clinical picture fits perfectly with mucocutaneous findings.

### The Mechanism: Why SIADH Happens Here

So, why did this patient develop SIADH? Is it just random chance? Probably not. The literature suggests that SIADH in BD isn't always drug-induced (like with cyclophosphamide); it can be a direct consequence of CNS inflammation itself.

The proposed mechanism involves **Interleukin-6 (IL-6)**. In acute neuro-BD, aberrant monocyte activation leads to massive IL-6 production in both serum and CSF. IL-6 acts as a potent stimulator of ADH release from the posterior pituitary or hypothalamus. Even though we didn't measure IL-6 directly in this specific case report (a missed opportunity!), his CRP was astronomically high—a surrogate marker for intense IL-6-driven inflammation—supporting this theory. The hiccups mentioned earlier are another subtle clue; persistent hiccups are increasingly recognized as a sign of severe hyponatremia or direct irritation to the medullary centers caused by this inflammatory storm.

### Treatment Triumphs Without Steroids?

Here is where things get really interesting for us clinicians managing difficult inflammatory cases. Standard care for acute neuro-BD usually screams "High-dose IV Methylprednisolone." Sometimes we add Cyclosporine or Anti-TNF agents if refractory. Yet, this patient received **Colchicine monotherapy**.

Wait—colchicine for *neuro*-Behçet's? Most guidelines say colchicine works great for mucocutaneous symptoms but fails against serious organ involvement like neurology or vascular disease requiring immunosuppression due to its inability to penetrate deep tissues effectively compared to steroids/cyclophosphamide/anti-TNFs. And yet:
*   Fever broke within hours of starting colchicine.
*   Neurological symptoms improved over two weeks.
*   Sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L by Day 16 alongside fluid restriction alone (no hypertonic saline needed!).
*   No recurrence after 4 years on maintenance colchicine alone!

While spontaneous remission is possible in some forms of meningeal Behçet's, this outcome challenges our dogma that severe neuro-involvement *always* mandates heavy immunosuppression immediately upon diagnosis in every single scenario. It suggests that in select cases with predominant meningeal inflammation (aseptic meningitis phenotype) rather than destructive parenchymal encephalopathy ("brainstem syndrome"), less aggressive anti-inflammatory strategies might suffice—or at least warrant consideration before jumping straight to steroids if comorbidities exist (like his undiagnosed diabetes).

### Key Takeaways for Your Practice

This case serves as a powerful reminder that Behçet's can wear many masks and strike unexpectedly early in its course (concurrent diagnosis vs typical delay). It forces us to broaden our lens when evaluating hyponatremic patients with neurological complaints who don't fit standard infectious molds.

**Clinical Pearls & Take-Homes:**
*   **Think Behçet's Early:** In any patient presenting with fever + headache + hyponatremia + mucocutaneous lesions (oral/genital ulcers), consider acute neuro-Behçet's even before imaging confirms parenchymal lesions; aseptic meningitis can be the sole neurological presentation initially.
*   **SIADH as an Inflammatory Marker:** Don't automatically attribute SIADH to drugs or tumors in inflammatory contexts; CNS inflammation itself can drive ADH release via IL-6 pathways, especially when presenting with non-specific symptoms like persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis secondary to severe electrolyte shifts.
*   **Normal MRI Doesn't Rule It Out:** Acute neuro-BD can manifest as isolated aseptic meningitis without detectable brainstem or parenchymal lesions on initial MRI; trust your clinical gestalt over radiology if suspicion remains high based on mucocutaneous findings plus CSF evidence of inflammation (elevated protein/pleocytosis).
*   **Re-evaluate Treatment Intensity:** While steroids remain first-line for most severe neuro-BD phenotypes, monitor closely whether milder presentations responding robustly to colchicine alone represent a distinct subset requiring less toxic intervention than traditionally assumed; avoid premature escalation if clinical trajectory improves rapidly with conservative measures while maintaining strict safety monitoring protocols for sodium correction rates (>8 mEq/L/24h limit applies regardless).



# Silent Clues: Hiccups and Rhabdomyolysis as Markers of Severe Hyponatremic Encephalopathy

We've all seen the "hyponatremia checklist" a dozen times: confusion, nausea, seizures, coma. But sometimes, the presentation is far more subtle, hiding behind symptoms we might dismiss as minor or unrelated to electrolyte derangement. Consider this striking case where a man in his 60s arrived with fever and headache, only to reveal a triad of **persistent hiccups**, **rhabdomyolysis** (evidenced by a massive CK elevation), and severe hyponatremia—all driven by an undiagnosed case of acute neuro-Behçet's disease complicated by SIADH.

What makes this case so compelling isn't just the rarity of neuro-Behçet's presenting concurrently with systemic symptoms (which is itself unusual); it's the specific constellation of "silent clues." While the patient's oral aphthae and genital ulcers eventually pointed toward Behçet's, the immediate red flags for severe CNS dysfunction were hidden in plain sight as persistent hiccups and muscle breakdown. In the era of "test everything," it is easy to miss the forest for the trees if we don't recognize that persistent hiccups can be a sign of diaphragmatic irritation from hyponatremic encephalopathy, and that rhabdomyolysis can be a direct metabolic consequence of severe sodium depletion. This case serves as a potent reminder that in patients with suspected CNS inflammation or SIADH, asking about hiccups and ordering a Creatine Kinase (CK) might be the difference between a delayed diagnosis and rapid stabilization.

The pathophysiology here offers a fascinating window into how severe hyponatremia wreaks havoc beyond simple cerebral edema. When serum sodium drops precipitously (this patient was at 124 mmol/L), water shifts into cells across all tissues, not just neurons. In the brainstem nuclei controlling respiration and swallowing, even mild swelling can trigger diaphragmatic spasms manifesting as intractable hiccups. The literature notes that while hiccups are often benign or transient, their persistence in the context of CNS pathology should raise immediate suspicion for intracranial pressure changes or direct brainstem involvement due to osmotic stress. Similarly, skeletal muscle cells are not immune to osmotic shock. The influx of water disrupts cellular membranes and mitochondrial function, leading to myonecrosis—a phenomenon clearly demonstrated in this patient where CK soared to 1349 U/L alongside hyponatremia. This wasn't exertional rhabdo; it was metabolic rhabdo masquerading as general malaise or arthralgia until lab work caught up with clinical suspicion.

Diagnostically, this case forces us to confront the difficulty of identifying acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) when classic parenchymal lesions are absent on MRI. Typically, neuro-BD presents with brainstem lesions visible on imaging months or years after initial mucocutaneous diagnosis. Here, we saw aseptic meningitis with a normal MRI but elevated CSF protein and lymphocytes—a pattern easily mistaken for viral meningoencephalitis or tuberculous meningitis. The presence of SIADH added another layer of complexity; while drug-induced SIADH is common in autoimmune management (e.g., cyclophosphamide), primary SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation is less recognized but plausible via IL-6 mediated pathways driving ADH release from inflamed hypothalamic nuclei. Crucially, ruling out infections like HSV/VZV through PCR was essential before committing to an autoimmune etiology, especially since the patient lacked initial genital ulcers—the hallmark sign that later emerged on day 5.

The treatment trajectory challenges some conventional wisdom regarding acute neuro-BD management. Standard teaching often dictates high-dose corticosteroids or aggressive immunosuppression for neurological involvement in Behçet's due to its life-threatening potential for parenchymal destruction (meningoencephalitis). However, this patient responded remarkably well to **colchicine monotherapy**. The resolution of his neurological symptoms, mucocutaneous lesions, and hyponatremia within days suggests that colchicine—perhaps via inhibition of neutrophil chemotaxis and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6—was sufficient to dampen this specific inflammatory cascade without needing steroids or other heavy hitters like cyclophosphamide or azathioprine. Of course, one must remain cautious; spontaneous remission is possible in meningoencephalitic presentations alone—but relying on chance is risky when treating inflammatory CNS disease. The success here highlights colchicine as a viable first-line consideration for early-stage neuro-inflammation where steroid side effects (particularly concerning given his undiagnosed Type 2 Diabetes) would have been detrimental.

For those managing complex cases involving fever and altered mental status (or subacute neurological complaints), remember that "nonspecific" doesn't mean "unimportant." If you encounter a patient with suspected CNS inflammation or SIADH who complains of stubborn hiccups or appears lethargic with vague muscle pain, look deeper than your basic metabolic panel order set includes CK levels explicitly? And ask specifically about hiccups—they could be your earliest warning sign of severe osmotic insult to the brainstem before seizures ever occur. Don't let the silence of early symptoms fool you; sometimes the loudest clues are hidden in plain sight among seemingly trivial complaints.

*   **Persistent Hiccups:** In patients with suspected CNS inflammation or SIADH, treat persistent hiccups as a potential marker of brainstem irritation from severe hyponatremia rather than dismissing them as gastrointestinal annoyance.
*   **Order CK Early:** Suspect metabolic rhabdomyolysis secondary to hyponatremia when patients present with generalized fatigue, malaise, or arthralgia alongside low sodium; check Creatine Kinase immediately rather than attributing it solely to sepsis or myalgias from infection.
*   **Normal MRI Doesn't Rule Out Neuro-BD:** Acute neuro-Behçet's can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal enhancement on MRI; do not exclude neuro-BD based on negative imaging if clinical suspicion remains high based on mucocutaneous findings + CSF evidence + exclusionary workup for infection/malignancy.
*   **Colchicine Potential:** While steroids are standard for acute neuro-BD colchicine may suffice in select cases presenting primarily with meningoencephalitis-like syndromes combined with mucocutaneous disease; monitor closely but avoid unnecessary immunosuppression if feasible (especially in diabetic patients).
*   **SIADH Mechanism Awareness:** Be aware that SIADH can arise de novo from CNS inflammation (via IL-6/brainstem pathways) independent of medication triggers; fluid restriction alone may resolve both electrolytes and neurological symptoms once the underlying inflammation subsides



# Steroid-Sparing Success? Re-evaluating Colchicine Monotherapy in Acute Neuro-Behçet's

Let's talk about a diagnostic puzzle that defies the textbook timeline. We've got a man in his 60s walking into the ED with fever, a headache that won't quit, and persistent hiccups—a classic "red flag" for intracranial pathology. But here's the twist: he has active mouth and genital ulcers, yet his brain MRI is completely normal. And instead of landing him on a heavy regimen of IV steroids and cyclophosphamide, he gets better on **colchicine** alone.

This case from Kakehi et al. challenges one of the most entrenched dogmas in neuro-rheumatology: *Acute Neuro-Behçet's Disease (Neuro-BD) requires aggressive immunosuppression.* While standard guidelines scream for high-dose corticosteroids plus pulse cyclophosphamide or TNF-alpha inhibitors for meningoencephalitic presentations, this patient responded spectacularly to colchicine monotherapy. Even more fascinating? He had SIADH driving his hyponatremia, likely driven by the same CNS inflammation causing his headache, and everything resolved together once the inflammation was tamed.

### The Clinical Picture: When "Normal" Isn't Normal

The presentation was deceptively subtle until you connected the dots. Our patient had the full triad of mucocutaneous Behçet's: oral aphthae, genital ulcers (which appeared later in the admission), and folliculitis-like rashes. But the neurological symptoms—fever, headache, vertigo, and those relentless hiccups—were happening *concurrently* with the skin findings. This is rare; typically, Neuro-BD strikes years after the initial diagnosis of systemic disease.

What makes this case particularly tricky for the ER physician is the imaging. You expect to see brainstem enhancement or parenchymal lesions in acute Neuro-BD. Here? Nothing on MRI. Yet, the CSF told a different story: aseptic meningitis with lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein. The diagnosis hinges entirely on clinical synthesis: **Mucocutaneous findings + Meningeal signs = Acute Neuro-BD**, even if your MRI looks clean. Remember, early meningeal-predominant disease often lacks visible parenchymal lesions on radiology.

Then came the electrolyte chaos: Hyponatremia (Na+ 124 mmol/L) with low serum osmolality and high urine osmolality/sodium in a euvolemic patient. The workup ruled out adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism, confirming **SIADH**. While SIADH is classically taught as a side effect of drugs like carbamazepine or chemotherapy (like cyclophosphamide), this patient developed it purely from CNS inflammation before any immunosuppressants were given. The persistent hiccups were likely an early marker of this hypothalamic irritation or severe hyponatremia itself—a clue often missed until it becomes obvious why they are hiccupping constantly!

### The Treatment Controversy: Colchicine vs. Steroids

Here is where we have to pause and rethink our algorithms. The consensus in almost every major review article is clear: For acute Neuro-BD with parenchymal involvement or severe meningoencephalitis, you must hit hard with steroids and cytotoxic agents immediately to prevent permanent disability or death. Colchicine is widely cited as effective for mucocutaneous disease but largely ineffective for neurological involvement unless used as an adjunct to stronger agents.

So, how did this guy get better on colchicine alone?
1.  **Phenotype Matters:** Perhaps this wasn't "severe" parenchymal encephalitis requiring massive suppression; maybe it was a milder meningeal variant that could be controlled by dampening neutrophil migration (the primary mechanism of colchicine).
2.  **Spontaneous Remission?** Some forms of acute Neuro-BD are self-limiting over weeks to months without intervention, though relying on chance is risky medicine.
3.  **The SIADH Connection:** If the SIADH was driven by IL-6 mediated inflammation (a plausible theory given the high CRP), then reducing that systemic cytokine storm via colchicine would naturally resolve both the neurological symptoms and the hormonal dysregulation simultaneously without needing massive steroid load—which carries its own risks in a diabetic patient with new-onset hyperglycemia (this patient had undiagnosed T2DM!).

It is crucial not to interpret this single case as a mandate to stop giving steroids to everyone with Neuro-BD tomorrow. However, it forces us to ask: Are we over-treating select phenotypes? Is there a subset of patients with predominant meningeal symptoms (no parenchymal lesions) where colchicine offers a viable steroid-sparing strategy?

### Key Takeaways & Pearls

*   **Think Behçet's First:** In any patient presenting with fever + headache + mucocutaneous ulcers (even if genital ulcers haven't appeared yet), keep Neuro-Behçet's on your differential regardless of MRI findings early in the course. A normal MRI does not rule out aseptic meningitis due to BD.
*   **Hiccups + Hyponatremia = Check Your Sodium:** Persistent hiccups can be an ominous sign of severe hyponatremia or CNS irritation secondary to SIADH caused by neuro-inflammation (meningitis/meningoencephalitis). Always check sodium in febrile patients with non-specific CNS complaints before attributing symptoms solely to infection or migraine equivalents.
*   **The Colchiche Question:** While steroids remain first-line for severe/parenchymal Neuro-BD, this case suggests colchicine monotherapy *might* suffice for mild-to-moderate meningeal-predominant cases or those where steroid toxicity (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes) is prohibitive—but this remains an outlier that requires replication before changing guidelines broadly.
*   **Don't Miss the Endocrine Link:** SIADH can be a direct paraneoplastic-like manifestation of CNS inflammation itself (as seen here) independent of medication side effects or malignancy when working up hyponatremia in inflammatory contexts like SLE or Vasculitides/Neuro-BD/Sarcoidosis/TB meningitis spectrum).

This case serves as a reminder that while we love our protocols and algorithms, biology loves outliers just enough to teach us something new about disease mechanisms—and perhaps therapeutic flexibility—in rare inflammatory syndromes like Behçet's disease.**



# The Diagnostic Trap: Navigating Fever and Hyponatremia When Infection is Excluded

It's a classic board question setup, isn't it? A patient walks in with fever, headache, and altered mental status. You think sepsis first, then meningoencephalitis second. But what happens when the cultures are negative, the PCR panels come back blank, and the CT head looks pristine?

That was the exact dilemma faced by our patient—a man in his 60s presenting with high fever (38.1°C), persistent hiccups, severe headache, and a strikingly low sodium of 124 mmol/L. On paper, this screams "infectious." Yet, after ruling out bacteria, viruses (including HSV/VZV), and even tuberculosis via CSF analysis and imaging, the diagnostic pendulum swung toward something far less common: **Acute Neuro-Behçet's Disease (Neuro-BD)** complicated by **Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH)**.

This case is a masterclass in avoiding the "diagnostic trap" where we cling to the most likely etiology long after the evidence suggests otherwise. It forces us to ask: *When do we stop treating for infection and start looking for autoimmunity?*

## The Clinical Puzzle: More Than Just "Aseptic Meningitis"

Let's break down why this case tripped up the initial workup. The patient presented with signs of systemic inflammation—elevated CRP (180 mg/L!), ESR (70 mm/hr), and leukocytosis—but lacked localizing signs initially. No nuchal rigidity. No focal deficits on exam. Just fever, malaise, and those pesky hiccups.

The presence of **hyponatremia** is often a red flag for CNS pathology, specifically SIADH. While SIADH is classically taught as a paraneoplastic phenomenon or drug-induced side effect (think SSRIs or carbamazepine), remember that **CNS inflammation itself can trigger ADH release**. In this case, the mechanism appears to be IL-6 mediated inflammation within the central nervous system driving ectopic ADH secretion.

Crucially, the brain MRI was **normal**. This is a critical teaching point: A normal MRI does *not* rule out acute neuro-BD. While parenchymal lesions are common in chronic or severe neuro-BD, acute presentations can manifest as aseptic meningitis or isolated hypothalamic dysfunction without visible structural damage on early imaging. If you wait for a bright spot on FLAIR before suspecting Neuro-BD in a febrile patient with mucocutaneous clues, you've already missed your window.

## The Pivot Point: From Broad-Spectrum Antibiotics to Specific Diagnosis

The management trajectory here offers a stark lesson in stewardship and reasoning. Initially, the team rightly covered for bacterial meningitis (cefepime) and viral encephalitis (acyclovir). This is standard of care when infection cannot be immediately excluded. However, once the CSF showed lymphocytic pleocytosis without organisms and viral PCRs turned negative at day 3-4, continuing empirical antivirals became unnecessary noise.

The real turning point wasn't just the negative labs; it was recognizing the constellation of mucocutaneous findings that had been present but perhaps under-appreciated earlier:
*   Oral aphthae
*   Folliculitis-like rashes
*   **Genital ulcers** (which appeared later on day 5)

According to the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD), this patient scored 5 points immediately upon confirmation of genital ulcers (genital aphthosis = 2 points; oral aphthae = 2 points; follicular rash = 1 point). A score ≥4 confirms definitive BD. Without that genital ulcer appearing late in the course, the diagnosis would have remained elusive longer. This highlights that sometimes you must look harder for subtle physical exam findings while waiting for culture results to clear out other possibilities.

### Why Not Other Differentials?
The team wisely considered sarcoidosis and primary CNS malignancy given the elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) and inflammatory markers. However:
*   **Sarcoidosis:** Normal ACE levels made this less likely despite overlapping features like granulomatous inflammation.
*   **Malignancy:** Negative cytology and lack of mass effect ruled out lymphoma despite mild sIL-2R elevation (a non-specific marker of immune activation).
*   **HLA-B51:** Interestingly positive but ultimately irrelevant to diagnosis; remember that genetic testing has low specificity compared to clinical criteria in BD. Don't let an incidental HLA result distract from your bedside assessment!

## Treatment Triumphs: Colchicine Over Corticosteroids?

Here is where things get fascinating from a treatment perspective. Standard teaching dictates that acute Neuro-BD requires aggressive immunosuppression—high-dose IV methylprednisolone followed by cyclophosphamide or azathioprine—especially if there are life-threatening parenchymal lesions or optic neuritis involved in other literature cases.

Yet, this patient responded spectacularly to **colchicine monotherapy**. No steroids were ever administered orally or intravenously for his neurological symptoms; they only received topical steroids for ulcers alongside silver sulfadiazine for skin care. Within days of starting colchicine:
1.  Fever broke.
2.  Headache subsided significantly.
3.  Hyponatremia corrected rapidly as SIADH resolved with fluid restriction alone (no hypertonic saline needed eventually).
4.  Rhabdomyolysis markers normalized as systemic inflammation waned.

While spontaneous remission is theoretically possible in mild meningoencephalitis-like presentations of Neuro-BD, this case suggests colchicine—a drug traditionally reserved for mucocutaneous symptoms—might play a more potent role in early-stage acute neuro-inflammation than previously appreciated by some guidelines.
