Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Clinical Pearls] Which three classic mucocutaneous features define Dyskeratosis Congenita, and why might their absence in this patient have delayed the diagnosis of a Telomere Biology Disorder?

**Answer:**

The three classic mucocutaneous features defining Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC) are **oral leukoplakia**, **nail dystrophy**, and **reticular skin pigmentation**. According to the case report, the absence of these specific triad elements in this patient contributed significantly to the delay in diagnosis. Instead of presenting with the hallmark skin and nail changes typical of childhood-onset DC, this young woman presented with a "silent" phenotype characterized by multisystem organ failure: pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.

The clinical reasoning here hinges on recognizing that Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs) represent a heterogeneous spectrum where the traditional DC triad is not universally present, particularly in adult presentations or those driven by monoallelic mutations. As highlighted in the discussion of this case, monoallelic *PARN* variants are increasingly recognized to cause telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure without the full mucocutaneous syndrome. Consequently, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for TBDs when encountering young adults with unexplained combinations of bone marrow failure (ineffective erythropoiesis), organ fibrosis (hepatic or pulmonary), and features of premature aging—such as the premature greying and osteoporosis noted in this patient—even when the classic dermatologic signs are missing. The definitive diagnosis relied on identifying the *PARN* variant and correlating it with the clinical picture, underscoring that in TBDs, the absence of the classic triad should never rule out the diagnosis if other systemic clues are present.

### [Pathophysiology] How does the genotype-phenotype correlation differ for *PARN* mutations regarding inheritance patterns, specifically comparing biallelic versus monoallelic variants?

In the context of *PARN*-associated telomere biology disorders, the genotype-phenotype correlation is strictly dictated by zygosity, reflecting a shift from a classic dyskeratosis congenita phenotype to a more variable, organ-specific presentation. **Biallelic** pathogenic variants in *PARN* follow an **autosomal recessive** inheritance pattern and are classically associated with **dyskeratosis congenita type 6 (DKCB6)** and severe pediatric syndromes such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. These patients typically present with profound bone marrow failure and the full mucocutaneous triad of nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and reticular skin pigmentation, often manifesting in childhood.

Conversely, **monoallelic** *PARN* variants generally exhibit an **autosomal dominant** pattern of inheritance (often with incomplete penetrance) and are predominantly linked to **telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4)**. As illustrated in this case report, the patient harbored a single monoallelic missense variant yet presented with significant multisystem involvement, including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, pancytopenia, and features of premature aging like premature graying. This underscores that while biallelic loss leads to severe developmental failure characteristic of DKCB6, heterozygous mutations can still drive significant telomere attrition sufficient to cause adult-onset organ fibrosis and hematologic compromise. Therefore, clinicians must recognize that the absence of a second allele does not rule out a *PARN*-mediated telomere biology disorder; indeed, monoallelic variants expand the phenotypic spectrum beyond classical dyskeratosis congenita into isolated or combined pulmonary-hepatic-hematologic presentations in young adults.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In this case, the patient exhibited pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count and bone marrow showing erythroid hyperplasia. How do you distinguish the mechanism of 'ineffective erythropoiesis' seen here from true hemolytic anemia, particularly given the positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT)?

**Clinical Pearl:**
In this case, distinguishing ineffective erythropoiesis from true hemolytic anemia hinges on the **reticulocyte response** and the interpretation of a discordant **Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT)** in the setting of a telomere biology disorder.

While hemolytic anemia is characterized by peripheral red blood cell destruction, the hallmark of ineffective erythropoiesis is profound **marrow failure to release mature cells**. In this patient, the critically low reticulocyte count (0.39%) and bone marrow findings of **erythroid hyperplasia with increased iron stores** confirm that the marrow is attempting to compensate but failing due to intramedullary death of precursors, rather than peripheral destruction. This contrasts sharply with hemolysis, where one would expect a robust reticulocytosis (unless there is concurrent pure red cell aplasia or severe suppression) and depleted marrow iron stores due to rapid turnover.

The positive DAT (2+ IgG) in this case represents a "red herring" often seen in severe ineffective erythropoiesis. The pathophysiology involves the coating of dying erythroid precursors within the marrow with immunoglobulins or complement fragments prior to their lysis; these coated cells are then detected by flow cytometry or agglutination assays, yielding a false-positive signal for autoimmune hemolysis. As highlighted in this case's differential diagnosis, the combination of a positive DAT *without* evidence of peripheral hemolysis (e.g., sustained high LDH relative to retic output, splenic sequestration patterns) and a blunted reticulocyte response strongly favors ineffective erythropoiesis driven by the underlying telomere biology disorder and severe B12 deficiency.

Therefore, when evaluating pancytopenia with marrow hypercellularity, prioritize the **reticulocyte index**: if it is low despite marked anemia and hyperplasia, suspect ineffective erythropoiesis (as seen here with PARN-related TBD), regardless of a positive DAT.

### [Clinical Pearls] Beyond hematologic failure and pulmonary fibrosis, what is the significance of identifying hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis in a young adult with a suspected Telomere Biology Disorder, and how does this expand the traditional phenotypic spectrum of *PARN*-associated disease?

Identifying hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis in a young adult with a suspected Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD) is a critical diagnostic pivot that expands the traditional phenotypic spectrum of *PARN*-associated disease beyond its classic association with bone marrow failure and pulmonary fibrosis. Historically, monoallelic *PARN* variants were primarily linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and bone marrow failure, while biallelic variants were reserved for severe autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6). However, this case illustrates that hepatic involvement represents a significant, previously under-recognized manifestation of monoallelic *PARN* disease.

The clinical reasoning here rests on the pathophysiology of telomere attrition acting as a "second hit" in tissues with high regenerative demand. As detailed in the discussion, actively dividing cells—including hepatocytes—are particularly vulnerable to shortened telomeres. The presence of cirrhosis in this patient, alongside pulmonary fibrosis and pancytopenia, provides a unifying diagnosis that explains why the patient's symptoms persisted despite correction of severe vitamin B12 deficiency. While nutritional deficits contributed to the hematologic picture, they did not account for the multisystem fibrotic burden or premature aging features like osteoporosis and neuropathy.

This case challenges the dogma that hepatic fibrosis is exclusive to biallelic mutations or other TBD genes like *TERT*. Instead, it supports emerging registry data suggesting that hepatic dysfunction is more prevalent in TBDs than historically appreciated. By recognizing hepatic fibrosis as part of the *PARN* phenotype, clinicians can broaden their differential diagnosis for young adults presenting with unexplained liver disease or concurrent organ failure, facilitating earlier genetic confirmation and appropriate surveillance for malignancy and transplant eligibility before irreversible end-stage organ damage occurs.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the specific molecular function of the PARN enzyme in relation to telomerase RNA (TERC), and how does a defect in this exoribonuclease lead to telomere shortening?

The **PARN** gene encodes a cytoplasmic exoribonuclease responsible for the regulated deadenylation of messenger RNA. In the specific context of telomere biology, PARN performs a critical maturation step for **telomerase RNA component (TERC)**. After transcription, TERC acquires non-template-encoded oligo(A) tails at its 3′ terminus. PARN functions as a 3′-to-5′ exoribonuclease to remove these aberrant adenylate residues, thereby generating the mature, stable form of TERC necessary for the assembly of a functional telomerase holoenzyme.

When PARN function is compromised by pathogenic variants—as seen in this patient's monoallelic *PARN* mutation—the enzyme fails to efficiently degrade these post-transcriptional poly(A) tails. Consequently, immature TERC molecules accumulate and are targeted for rapid nuclear degradation rather than incorporation into the active telomerase complex. This results in a reduction in available functional telomerase activity within hematopoietic stem cells and epithelial tissues. Without adequate telomere elongation by telomerase, telomeres shorten prematurely with each cell division, triggering cellular senescence or apoptosis in high-turnover tissues. This mechanistic deficit explains the patient's clinical phenotype of bone marrow failure (pancytopenia), hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis, all hallmarks of a Telomere Biology Disorder driven by defective TERC processing.

### [Management] Given that this patient's pancytopenia was exacerbated by severe Vitamin B12 deficiency but driven by an underlying genetic susceptibility, what is the recommended long-term management strategy regarding surveillance for bone marrow failure progression once nutritional deficiencies are corrected?

**Clinical Pearl:** In patients with a Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD) such as *PARN*-associated disease, where pancytopenia is initially unmasked by a reversible nutritional insult (e.g., severe Vitamin B12 deficiency), the definitive long-term management strategy involves **lifelong, multidisciplinary surveillance for progressive bone marrow failure**, even after hematologic parameters normalize.

While the acute cytopenias in this case were driven by B12 deficiency and resolved with replacement therapy, the underlying monoallelic *PARN* mutation indicates a fundamental defect in telomere maintenance that compromises the replicative reserve of hematopoietic stem cells. As highlighted in the discussion of this case, telomere shortening renders the bone marrow uniquely susceptible to "second hits" or physiological stressors. Consequently, normalization of blood counts does not equate to cure; rather, it represents a period of stability in a patient with an inherent risk of transitioning to frank Bone Marrow Failure Syndrome (BMFS).

Surveillance must be rigorous and continuous. Guidelines for TBDs recommend frequent complete blood counts (CBCs) with differential and reticulocyte indices to detect early signs of marrow decline before they become clinically apparent. Furthermore, given the pleiotropic nature of TBDs illustrated by this patient's concurrent hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, surveillance cannot be limited to hematology alone. Patients require periodic assessment of liver function and imaging for hepatic fibrosis progression, alongside respiratory evaluation for interstitial lung disease. Crucially, because TBDs carry an elevated risk for malignant transformation (particularly myelodysplastic syndrome and squamous cell carcinomas), vigilance for clonal evolution is mandatory. If progressive cytopenias develop despite optimal supportive care, early referral for **hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)** evaluation is indicated, as HSCT remains the only potentially curative option for advanced marrow failure in this population.

### [Morning Report] If a patient presents with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and unexplained cytopenias but lacks the classic Dyskeratosis Congenita triad, what diagnostic modality serves as the gold standard alongside genetic sequencing to confirm shortened telomeres?

When evaluating a patient with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and unexplained cytopenias where the classic Dyskeratosis Congenita triad is absent, the gold standard for confirming shortened telomeres—alongside genetic sequencing—is **flow cytometry-based fluorescence *in situ* hybridization (Flow-FISH)**.

While the clinical case you reviewed describes a patient with a monoallelic *PARN* variant and features suggestive of a Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD), it is important to recognize that genetic testing alone can sometimes yield inconclusive results. As noted in the discussion of this case, the patient's specific *PARN* variant was classified as a "Variant of Uncertain Significance" based on current databases, despite strong bioinformatic predictions of pathogenicity. Therefore, demonstrating the functional consequence of the genetic defect—specifically, abnormally short telomeres—is essential for definitive diagnosis.

Pathophysiologically, TBDs arise from defects in telomere maintenance machinery, leading to premature telomere attrition. This manifests clinically as bone marrow failure (pancytopenia) and organ fibrosis (pulmonary or hepatic) because tissues with high turnover rates cannot sustain replication. Flow-FISH allows for the precise quantification of telomere length in lymphocytes relative to age-matched controls. In this context, it provides objective confirmation of the diagnosis when genotype-phenotype correlations are ambiguous or when only monoallelic variants are found, which is common in adult presentations of TBDs involving genes like *PARN*, *TERT*, or *TERC*. Thus, combining genetic sequencing with Flow-FISH ensures accurate identification of the disorder to guide appropriate surveillance and management strategies.
