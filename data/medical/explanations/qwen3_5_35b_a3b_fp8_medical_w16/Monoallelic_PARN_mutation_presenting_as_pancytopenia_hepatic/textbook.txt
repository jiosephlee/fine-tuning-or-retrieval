Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Clinical Presentation and Phenotypic Clustering

The clinical narrative begins with a woman in her late twenties presenting with a five-year history of insidious, progressive fatigue and generalized weakness. Unlike typical acute presentations, her symptoms evolved slowly, gradually eroding her functional capacity until she required assistance with activities of daily living. This protracted course, accompanied by exertional dyspnea, weight loss, and anorexia, initially suggested common acquired etiologies such as chronic infection, malignancy, or metabolic derangement. However, the sheer duration and multisystem nature of the decline served as the first "red flag," prompting a shift from a single-system focus to a syndromic evaluation. The key to unlocking this case lies in recognizing that while individual symptoms might be attributed to common conditions (e.g., anemia causing fatigue), their simultaneous clustering in a young adult signals an underlying genetic or systemic disorder rather than independent comorbidities.

Physical examination revealed a striking constellation of findings that defied simple categorization. Most notably, the patient exhibited signs of premature aging, specifically premature greying of hair and knuckle hyperpigmentation. These mucocutaneous changes are hallmark features of telomere biology disorders (TBDs), reflecting the high turnover rate of rapidly dividing epithelial tissues which fail to maintain telomere integrity. Concurrently, the examination identified hepatosplenomegaly and skeletal abnormalities including thoracic scoliosis and thenar atrophy. The presence of a bald tongue with atrophic lingual papillae further underscored mucosal vulnerability. These physical signs collectively painted a picture of accelerated biological aging occurring far earlier than expected for chronological age, suggesting a fundamental defect in cellular replication and maintenance mechanisms.

Systemic involvement was profound and integrated across multiple organ systems, creating a complex clinical puzzle. Hematologically, the patient suffered from pancytopenia driven by ineffective erythropoiesis rather than peripheral destruction or marrow infiltration. Respiratorily, she presented with dyspnea indicative of restrictive lung disease; imaging later confirmed diffuse pulmonary fibrosis. Hepatically, non-invasive elastography revealed significant fibrosis approaching cirrhosis despite negative viral and autoimmune serologies. Furthermore, musculoskeletal evaluation uncovered osteoporosis with recurrent fractures and an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. This integration of bone marrow failure (pancytopenia), organ fibrosis (lung/liver), and features of premature aging (greying, osteoporosis) formed a cohesive phenotypic cluster characteristic of TBDs. The coexistence of these seemingly disparate issues is not coincidental but stems from the shared pathophysiology: critically short telomeres leading to cellular senescence in tissues with high regenerative demands.

The diagnostic strategy employed here emphasizes the critical need to distinguish between nutritional deficiencies and genetic syndromes when faced with overlapping features. Initially, the severe vitamin B12 deficiency explained many aspects of the patient's hematologic profile and neurological symptoms (pernicious anemia). However, relying solely on this diagnosis would leave significant questions unanswered regarding the extent of hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary involvement, skeletal deformities, and the disproportionate nature of her aging phenotype relative to her nutritional status alone. The differential diagnosis must therefore expand beyond acquired causes like aplastic anemia or autoimmune hepatitis to include inherited disorders where nutritional deficiencies act as exacerbating factors rather than root causes. In this case, recognizing that correcting B12 levels did not resolve the systemic dysfunction was pivotal; it forced clinicians to look deeper for a unifying genetic etiology that rendered these organ systems uniquely vulnerable to stressors like malabsorption or oxidative damage.

## Diagnostic Workup and Exclusion of Acquired Etiologies

The diagnostic journey for this patient began with a comprehensive hematologic evaluation necessitated by a five-year history of progressive fatigue and generalized weakness. Initial laboratory data revealed pancytopenia characterized by a critically low reticulocyte count of 0.39%, indicating a failure of the bone marrow to compensate for peripheral blood loss or consumption. The peripheral smear provided crucial mechanistic insight, displaying dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils. These morphological features are hallmarks of ineffective erythropoiesis, where red blood cell precursors undergo apoptosis within the marrow before entering circulation. To differentiate between hemolytic destruction and production failure, markers of hemolysis such as lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin were assessed; while mildly elevated, they were accompanied by a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG. However, the discordance between the positive DAT and the persistently low reticulocyte response suggested that immune-mediated destruction was not the primary driver. Instead, the bone marrow examination clarified the etiology, demonstrating trilineage hyperplasia with erythroid predominance and increased iron stores but no evidence of malignancy or dysplasia. This finding effectively ruled out aplastic anemia or leukemia as primary causes and confirmed a state of ineffective hematopoiesis driven by profound nutritional deficits rather than intrinsic marrow failure at that stage.

Concurrent with the hematologic workup, a rigorous nutritional assessment was undertaken to address the suspected cause of ineffective erythropoiesis. The patient tested positive for severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, alongside iron studies showing elevated serum iron and ferritin consistent with intramedullary hemolysis (ineffective erythropoiesis). An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed mild chronic gastritis with parietal cell reduction, suggesting malabsorption due to impaired intrinsic factor production as a precipitating factor for B12 deficiency. Consequently, aggressive replacement therapy with intravenous cyanocobalamin and oral folic acid was initiated. While this intervention successfully corrected the biochemical deficiencies and stabilized blood counts temporarily, it failed to resolve the patient's systemic phenotype. The persistence of debilitating fatigue, weight loss, hepatosplenomegaly, premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and osteoporosis despite adequate nutritional repletion signaled that these deficiencies were merely comorbidities or exacerbating factors rather than the root cause of the multisystem disease. This therapeutic disconnect forced clinicians to reconsider a syndromic or genetic etiology capable of mimicking acquired conditions while driving multiorgan pathology independent of simple nutrient availability.

With acquired metabolic causes addressed but clinical suspicion remaining high for an underlying structural or genetic disorder directed at rapidly dividing tissues (bone marrow, liver epithelium), advanced imaging was employed to characterize hepatic and pulmonary involvement. Transient elastography provided quantitative evidence of significant liver pathology; a controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) indicated steatosis while liver stiffness measurements reached 18.1 kPa, confirming advanced fibrosis approaching cirrhosis in a young adult without traditional risk factors such as alcohol use or metabolic syndrome. Similarly, positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) delineated diffuse ground-glass opacities with subpleural fibrotic changes in both lung fields, consistent with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). Crucially, PET-CT also demonstrated intense fluorodeoxyglucose uptake in the axial skeleton without focal lytic lesions or lymphadenopathy that would suggest overt malignancy or granulomatous disease like sarcoidosis. The combination of non-malignant hepatic fibrosis and interstitial lung disease in a young patient without autoimmune markers strongly pointed toward a telomere biology disorder (TBD), where shortened telomeres compromise tissue regeneration across multiple organ systems simultaneously.

The final step in narrowing the differential diagnosis involved strict exclusion criteria based on immunological profiling and physical examination patterns typical of Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC). Extensive autoimmune serologies were negative for antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis markers, ruling out lupus or autoimmune hepatitis as drivers of cytopenia and liver injury. Viral panels for hepatitis B/C, HIV, CMV, EBV (where only past exposure was noted), and scrub typhus were largely unremarkable regarding acute infection causing current symptoms. Furthermore, while the patient exhibited cutaneous findings such as knuckle hyperpigmentation and premature greying—features suggestive of premature aging—she lacked the classic mucocutaneous triad defining DC: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation involving all three sites simultaneously. The absence of this triad initially obscured the diagnosis but is increasingly recognized in adult-onset TBDs where penetrance varies or presents atypically. By systematically excluding common acquired etiologies—including nutritional deficiencies alone (due to treatment resistance), autoimmune disorders (negative serology), infectious causes (negative workup), and malignancy (normal imaging/biopsy)—the clinical team identified a "syndromic gap" best explained by a monogenic defect in telomere maintenance mechanisms. This logical deduction paved the way for clinical exome sequencing to identify the monoallelic *PARN* variant responsible for her unique constellation of bone marrow failure susceptibility, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and accelerated aging phenotypes.

## Pathophysiology of Telomere Biology Disorders

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a heterogeneous group of inherited conditions driven by defects in the machinery responsible for maintaining telomere integrity. To understand the pathogenesis of the case presented, one must first appreciate the fundamental structure and function of telomeres. These are repetitive nucleotide sequences (TTAGGG in humans) located at the termini of linear chromosomes, acting as protective caps that shield the genome from degradation and prevent chromosomal fusion. Because DNA polymerase cannot fully replicate the extreme 3' ends of linear DNA molecules during cell division—a phenomenon known as the "end-replication problem"—telomeres undergo progressive shortening with every mitotic event. Under physiological conditions, this attrition serves as a molecular clock limiting cellular lifespan. However, in healthy somatic cells, particularly those with high turnover rates such as hematopoietic stem cells, hepatocytes, and lung epithelial cells, this shortening is counteracted by the enzyme telomerase.

The telomerase complex is a ribonucleoprotein composed primarily of two core components: telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), which provides the catalytic activity to synthesize new DNA sequences, and telomerase RNA component (TERC), which serves as the template for elongation. The proper function of this complex relies on additional accessory proteins involved in recruitment and regulation. In TBDs, germline mutations in genes encoding these components—or enzymes that regulate them—disrupt telomere homeostasis. For instance, in the patient described, a monoallelic variant in *PARN* was identified. The *PARN* gene encodes an exoribonuclease essential for processing TERC; specifically, it removes non-templated adenylate residues added to the 3' end of TERC during transcription to facilitate its maturation and nuclear retention. Without functional PARN protein, TERC levels drop or become unstable, rendering telomerase unable to maintain adequate telomere lengths despite normal TERT activity. Consequently, cells enter a state of replicative crisis earlier than expected.

When telomeres reach a critically short length, they lose their protective cap structure and are recognized by the cell's DNA damage response machinery as double-strand breaks. This triggers either permanent growth arrest (cellular senescence) or programmed cell death (apoptosis). The clinical manifestation of TBDs is dictated by tissue tropism for actively dividing cells; organs reliant on rapid regeneration suffer most acutely. In hematopoietic tissues, insufficient renewal leads to bone marrow failure and pancytopenia, mirroring the patient's presentation of anemia with ineffective erythropoiesis and low reticulocyte counts. Similarly, in the liver and lungs where epithelial turnover is continuous but less frequent than in blood precursors, cumulative cellular exhaustion manifests as fibrosis rather than immediate organ failure. The patient's hepatic cirrhosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis are direct consequences of this accelerated aging phenotype within these specific parenchymal tissues.

The clinical spectrum of TBDs ranges from severe pediatric syndromes to subtle adult-onset presentations depending on the specific gene involved, the nature of the mutation (e.g., dominant vs. recessive), and residual enzyme activity. Classic Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC) is characterized by a mucocutaneous triad: abnormal skin pigmentation (reticular hyperpigmentation), nail dystrophy, and oral leukoplakia, often accompanied by bone marrow failure. More severe forms involving multiorgan failure early in life include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome and Revesz syndrome. Conversely, adult presentations often lack these classic dermatological features entirely or present them mildly later in life. Instead, they may manifest exclusively as isolated pulmonary fibrosis or unexplained cytopenias/pancreatitis/fibrosis combinations later in adulthood due to lower penetrance or milder genetic defects like monoallelic variants seen here with *PARN*. Understanding this continuum is vital; clinicians must recognize that "idiopathic" organ failure or cytopenias in young adults may actually be manifestations of an underlying telomere biology defect requiring genetic confirmation rather than empirical management alone.

## *PARN* Gene Variants and Genotype-Phenotype Correlation

The *PARN* gene encodes a highly conserved exoribonuclease belonging to the Deadenylase family, playing a critical role in telomere biology beyond simple telomerase activity. Its primary biochemical function involves the regulation of gene expression through the deadenylation of messenger RNA poly(A) tails. Crucially, *PARN* is essential for the post-transcriptional maturation of the Telomerase RNA Component (TERC). Specifically, it removes oligo(A) tails added to nuclear RNAs during transcription, facilitating their proper 3'-end processing. Without functional *PARN*, TERC fails to mature correctly, leading to impaired telomerase holoenzyme assembly and subsequent acceleration of telomere attrition. This mechanistic deficit places actively dividing tissues—such as hematopoietic stem cells, hepatocytes, and alveolar epithelial cells—at high risk for premature senescence and organ failure, forming the basis of Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs).

The clinical manifestation of *PARN*-related TBDs follows a distinct genotype-phenotype correlation based on zygosity. Biallelic pathogenic variants in *PARN* cause Dyskeratosis Congenita Autosomal Recessive 6 (DKCB6), a severe form characterized by early-onset bone marrow failure and classic mucocutaneous features similar to X-linked DC. In contrast, monoallelic variants are generally associated with a dominant-like pattern of susceptibility known as Telomere-related Pulmonary Fibrosis and/or Bone Marrow Failure type 4 (PFBMFT4). Patients with monoallelic mutations often present later in life with isolated organ involvement, particularly idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or unexplained cytopenias, lacking the severe congenital anomalies seen in recessive forms. This distinction is vital for prognostication; while biallelic defects imply profound haploinsufficiency affecting all lineages from birth, monoallelic defects create a "second-hit" scenario where the remaining wild-type allele provides partial protection until cumulative stressors or aging precipitate clinical disease.

In this case, clinical exome sequencing identified a heterozygous missense variant in *PARN* (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), located within the R3H domain (residues 178–245), which was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). Classifying such variants requires rigorous application of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG/AMP) guidelines. Although definitive pathogenicity was not established solely by segregation data due to the lack of family history or additional affected relatives, several lines of evidence support its potential deleterious nature. The variant satisfies criterion **PM1** because it affects a critical functional domain where no benign variations are known; **PM2**, as it is absent from population frequency databases; **PP3**, supported by multiple computational algorithms predicting damage; and **PP2**, reflecting that *PARN* is intolerant to missense variation. The convergence of these criteria suggests that while statistical certainty is pending larger cohorts, the biological plausibility aligns with known pathogenic mechanisms in TBDs.

The patient's presentation challenges the traditional view that monoallelic *PARN* mutations primarily manifest as isolated pulmonary fibrosis. Instead, she exhibited a multisystem phenotype involving hepatic fibrosis, bone marrow suppression (pancytopenia), osteoporosis, and neuropathy alongside pulmonary involvement. This expanded phenotype likely reflects the concept of incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity inherent to TBDs. While monoallelic mutations may suffice to drive lung fibrosis due to high turnover rates in alveolar tissue, they may require additional "hits"—such as severe nutritional deficits or environmental stress—to overwhelm hematopoietic reserve sufficiently to cause frank bone marrow failure or significant hepatic injury. Furthermore, somatic mosaicism or unidentified second alleles could contribute to this severity. Ultimately, this case illustrates that even single-pathway defects in telomere maintenance can produce broad systemic consequences when combined with metabolic vulnerabilities like Vitamin B12 deficiency, necessitating a low threshold for genetic investigation in young adults with unexplained multisystem fibrosis and cytopenia.
