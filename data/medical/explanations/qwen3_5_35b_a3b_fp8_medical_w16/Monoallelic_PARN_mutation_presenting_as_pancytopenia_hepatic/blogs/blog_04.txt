Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Silent Fibrosis: Hepatic and Pulmonary Involvement as Hallmarks of Undiagnosed TBDs

We often teach medical students to look for the "classic triad" when evaluating a patient with bone marrow failure or interstitial lung disease: nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and reticular skin pigmentation. But what happens when those mucocutaneous signs are completely absent? What if your patient is a woman in her late 20s presenting with nothing but crushing fatigue, pancytopenia, and a liver that feels like stone?

In a recent case report by Soni et al., we see exactly this scenario. A young woman presented with a five-year history of progressive weakness, premature greying, and pancytopenia. Despite severe vitamin B12 deficiency, the clinical picture didn't add up to simple malnutrition. The real kicker? She had **hepatic fibrosis** (cirrhosis on elastography) and **pulmonary fibrosis** alongside her hematologic issues. Genetic testing revealed a monoallelic *PARN* variant. This case serves as a stark reminder that Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs) are masters of disguise, often masquerading as isolated organ failures in young adults where the classic "Dyskeratosis Congenita" features never appear.

## The Hidden Cost of Short Telomeres

When we think about telomere biology disorders, our minds usually jump straight to the bone marrow or the lungs. However, the liver is an unsuspecting victim in this game of biological attrition. As discussed in the source material, actively dividing cells—including hepatocytes—are particularly vulnerable to telomere shortening.

While Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) has become a well-known red flag for TBDs in pulmonology circles, hepatic involvement is frequently missed until it presents as advanced cirrhosis or unexplained transaminitis. In this specific case, the patient had significant hepatic dysfunction with no history of alcohol abuse, viral hepatitis (despite EBV past infection), or metabolic syndrome. Transient elastography revealed a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa—firmly cirrhotic territory—in a patient in her twenties.

Registry data supports this hidden connection. Studies from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry indicate that up to one-third of adult patients with pathogenic *TERT* or *TERC* mutations develop cirrhosis. More strikingly, nearly 90% show at least moderate hepatic fibrosis on elastography. This suggests that hepatic fibrosis might be far more common in TBDs than we realize, simply because clinicians aren't looking for it unless they suspect a genetic syndrome immediately upon seeing lung disease or cytopenias.

## The *PARN* Puzzle: Why One Allele Matters

The genetics here are fascinating and clinically nuanced. *PARN* encodes an exoribonuclease essential for processing TERC RNA (the template for telomerase). Unlike many TBD genes where biallelic mutations cause severe childhood onset (like Dyskeratosis Congenita type 6), monoallelic *PARN* variants are increasingly linked to adult-onset phenotypes involving pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure (specifically PFBMFT4).

This patient carried a monoallelic missense variant (*c.613T>C*; p.Cys205Arg). While classified technically as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), its location in the highly conserved R3H domain and its predicted damaging effect align it perfectly with known pathogenic mechanisms. The takeaway isn't just about the specific variant; it's about the concept of **haploinsufficiency**. Even having one defective copy can compromise telomere maintenance enough to cause multi-system organ failure when compounded by other stresses—like severe B12 deficiency in this case—which likely accelerated the demise of already fragile hematopoietic stem cells.

It is crucial to recognize that "idiopathic" labels should be applied sparingly in young patients with multisystem involvement. If you have a patient under 40 with unexplained pulmonary fibrosis AND liver stiffness OR cytopenias, you must consider TBD before settling on "sporadic" diagnoses.

## Screening Young Patients: Beyond the Skin Exam

So, how do we catch these silent killers before they reach end-stage organ failure? The answer lies in lowering our threshold for investigation when clinical clues don't fit standard paradigms.

If you encounter a young adult with:
*   Unexplained pancytopenia or reticulocytopenia
*   Interstitial lung disease without smoking history
*   Liver disease without traditional risk factors (viral/metabolic/alcohol)
*   Features of premature aging (premature greying, osteoporosis)

...you need to move beyond routine labs immediately. The diagnostic algorithm should include:
1.  **Flow Cytometry:** To measure lymphocyte telomere length (though results can vary based on age-matched controls).
2.  **Genetic Testing:** Whole-exome sequencing panels specifically targeting telomere biology genes (*TERT*, *TERC*, *PARN*, *RTEL1*, etc.). Do not wait for family history; de novo mutations occur frequently enough that their absence does not rule out the diagnosis.
3.  **Transient Elastography:** Given the high prevalence of subclinical hepatic fibrosis in TBD registries, utilizing non-invasive liver stiffness measurement in these high-risk cohorts could detect cirrhosis earlier than standard LFTs ever would have flagged it in this case study's patient who was otherwise asymptomatic regarding abdominal pain until late stages were reached by imaging findings alone!
