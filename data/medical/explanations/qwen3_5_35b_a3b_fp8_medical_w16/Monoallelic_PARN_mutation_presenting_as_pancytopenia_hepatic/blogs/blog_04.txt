Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Silent Fibrosis: When Telomere Shortening Hits the Liver and Lungs

Let's talk about a diagnostic trap that catches even seasoned clinicians off guard. We've all seen the "classic" Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC) triad: nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and reticular skin pigmentation. But what happens when a young adult walks into your clinic with *none* of those classic cutaneous signs? What if they just look tired, pale, and perhaps a bit gray before their time?

Consider the case of a woman in her late 20s presenting with a five-year history of debilitating fatigue. She wasn't showing up with brittle nails or white patches in her mouth. Instead, she had pancytopenia, profound weakness, hepatosplenomegaly, and signs of pulmonary distress. The initial workup screamed nutritional deficiency—specifically severe Vitamin B12 malabsorption. You treat the B12, you give the folate, you transfuse the blood... but does everything resolve? In many cases of Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs), the answer is **no**.

This case highlights a terrifying reality: **Silent Fibrosis**. While we are becoming increasingly aware that telomere shortening drives Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF), we often completely overlook the liver until it's too late. Our patient didn't just have "B12 deficiency"; she had a monoallelic *PARN* mutation driving a full-blown Telomere Biology Disorder that manifested as hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis alongside bone marrow failure.

### The "Hidden" Organ Damage in TBDs

When we think about TBDs, our mental checklist usually defaults to hematologic failure (aplastic anemia) and lung disease (IPF). Why? Because those are life-threatening and relatively easier to spot in specialized centers. However, recent registry data suggests that **hepatic fibrosis** is far more prevalent in TBDs than historically thought—and much more insidious because it lacks specific symptoms until it reaches cirrhosis.

In this case report, our patient presented with hepatomegaly and elevated inflammatory markers. Initial thoughts might swing toward autoimmune hepatitis or viral reactivation (she did have past EBV exposure). But once you rule out the obvious infectious and autoimmune culprits, you have to ask: Is this telomere-related?

The utility of transient elastography cannot be overstated here. In this patient, it revealed Grade II hepatic steatosis but, more critically, a liver stiffness measurement of **18.1 kPa**, indicative of significant fibrosis or early cirrhosis. This finding was pivotal. It shifted the differential from simple metabolic issues to a syndromic genetic etiology involving tissue regeneration failure. As noted in large registries like the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, up to 33% of adult patients with *TERC* or *TERT* mutations develop cirrhosis. While our patient had only a monoallelic *PARN* variant—a genotype often linked primarily to lung disease—the outcome was similar: multi-organ fibrotic collapse.

### Why Does This Happen? The Mechanism Matters

It helps to understand *why* this happens to better appreciate the clinical urgency. Telomeres act as caps on our chromosomes; every time a cell divides, they get shorter. Once they hit critical length, the cell stops dividing (senescence) or dies (apoptosis).

Cells with high turnover rates—bone marrow epithelium of the gut/skin/hair follicles—are hit hardest first. That explains why we see cytopenias and mucocutaneous changes so readily in children with severe forms like Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. But actively dividing cells also exist in the liver (hepatocytes regenerate constantly during injury) and lungs (repairing alveolar damage).

The *PARN* gene encodes an exoribonuclease responsible for processing TERC RNA (the template for telomerase). When *PARN* is defective—even as a single allele—it impairs telomerase function indirectly through haploinsufficiency or dominant-negative effects. The result? A bone marrow that fails under stress (like severe B12 deficiency) and organs that fail to repair micro-injuries effectively over decades, leading to silent scarring in both lungs and livers before the patient hits middle age.

### Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Messages

This case serves as a powerful reminder that "nutritional deficiency" can sometimes be merely the precipitating event exposing an underlying genetic fragility in tissues already struggling with replication limits. If you see pancytopenia + unexplained organ fibrosis + premature aging features (gray hair early on!), stop thinking purely about vitamins and start thinking about telomeres.

Here are your key takeaways for managing these elusive cases:

*   **Look Beyond the Triad:** Do not wait for nail dystrophy or leukoplakia to suspect Dyskeratosis Congenita or related TBDs; adult presentations often lack these classic features entirely ("silent" phenotype).
*   **Screen for Liver Stiffness:** In any patient with unexplained cytopenias or interstitial lung disease suggestive of IPF-like patterns but lacking smoking/radiation history order transient elastography (FibroScan); liver stiffness >7-8 kPa should trigger urgent genetic workup regardless of transaminitis status.
*   **The B12 Trap:** Severe nutritional deficiencies can unmask latent TBDs by overwhelming already compromised hematopoietic stem cells; always consider genetic testing if clinical features persist despite aggressive replacement therapy or if extra-hematologic signs appear simultaneously.
*   **Monoallelic Matters:** Remember that monoallelic variants in genes like *PARN*, *TERT*, or *TERC* can cause significant morbidity later in life without family history; de novo mutations are common enough that "negative family history" is not exclusionary evidence against TBDs.
