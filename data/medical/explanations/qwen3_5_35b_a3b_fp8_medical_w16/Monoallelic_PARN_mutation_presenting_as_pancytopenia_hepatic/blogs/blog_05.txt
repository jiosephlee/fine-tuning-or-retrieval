Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Dimorphic Anemia + Positive DAT = Hemolysis? Think Telomeres First.

It is a classic diagnostic trap in Emergency Medicine and Hematology: You see a patient with fatigue, pancytopenia, jaundice, and an elevated LDH. Your brain immediately fires up the "hemolysis" algorithm. Then, the Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT/Coombs) comes back positive (2+), and the puzzle seems solved: Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia (AIHA). Right?

Wrong.

In a recent case report, we encountered a woman in her late 20s presenting with profound weakness, hepatosplenomegaly, and a lab profile that screamed "autoimmune disaster." She had dimorphic anemia, severe B12 deficiency, elevated LDH, indirect hyperbilirubinemia, *and* a positive DAT for IgG. By all textbook heuristics for acute hemolysis, AIHA should have been the working diagnosis. Instead, the final verdict was **ineffective erythropoiesis** driven by a convergence of severe nutritional deficiency and an underlying **Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD)** caused by a monoallelic *PARN* mutation.

This case serves as a masterclass in not letting one abnormal value blind you to the entire picture. When faced with a "positive" hemolysis panel that doesn't fit the clinical trajectory, look closer at the morphology. Sometimes, what looks like destruction is actually failed production.

## The Great Mimicker: Why This Smear Wasn't Hemolysis

The confusion here stemmed from the overlap between peripheral destruction and marrow failure. Both processes release intracellular enzymes like LDH and bilirubin into circulation because red blood cells are dying before they can mature properly (intramedullary death). In this patient, the combination of high LDH and indirect bilirubin made hemolysis seem inevitable. Even worse, the DAT was positive for IgG. While we associate DAT positivity exclusively with antibody-mediated lysis (like Warm AIHA), false positives or non-specific binding can occur in states of massive cellular turnover or dyserythropoiesis.

However, if you zoom in on the peripheral smear—which is often skipped when we rely too heavily on automated indices—you find the truth hiding in plain sight: **Basophilic stippling**, **Cabot rings**, and **hypersegmented neutrophils**.

These aren't signs of immune attack; they are hallmarks of disrupted DNA synthesis and nuclear-cytoplasmic asynchrony.
*   **Basophilic stippling:** Aggregates of ribosomal RNA remaining because mRNA processing is defective during rapid but flawed erythropoiesis.
*   **Cabot rings:** Remnants of microtubule structures from spindle fibers that fail to disassemble correctly during mitosis errors in dysplastic precursors.
*   **Hypersegmented neutrophils:** The tell-tale sign of megaloblastic change due to B12/folate deficiency preventing thymidine synthesis.

In true hemolytic anemia (especially extravascular), you expect spherocytes or schistocytes depending on the cause. You rarely see Cabot rings or significant basophilic stippling unless there is concurrent severe dyserythropoiesis or lead poisoning. Furthermore, despite the "positive" DAT and high inflammatory markers suggesting autoimmunity, there was no evidence of splenic sequestration typical of warm AIHA beyond what was explained by hepatosplenomegaly from extramedullary hematopoiesis (a feature of myelofibrosis/TBD). The bone marrow wasn't empty; it was hypercellular with trilineage activity but ineffective output—a classic picture of marrow trying desperately to compensate for short telomeres but failing to produce viable cells due to genomic instability exacerbated by B12 deficiency.

## The Hidden Driver: Telomere Crisis

Why did this happen in her late 20s? While severe B12 deficiency explains the immediate cytopenia and neural symptoms (neuropathy), it does not fully explain the hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, or premature greying seen in this patient until she hit her "crisis point."

This constellation points to an underlying Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD). The patient carried a monoallelic *PARN* variant (*c*.613T>C). Normally associated with autosomal dominant pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure when heterozygous (*PARN*-related PFBMFT4), this single allele rendered her hematopoietic stem cells fragile ("haploinsufficiency"). Her telomeres were already critically short before she even knew she had them issues shortening faster than normal due to replication stress.

Think of it this way: The B12 deficiency acted as a metabolic stressor on top of genetically compromised stem cells. Healthy stems might handle mild nutritional deficits fine; these stems couldn't replicate fast enough to maintain homeostasis once challenged by low folate/B12 levels needed for DNA repair during division. The result was a catastrophic drop in effective output (pancytopenia) masquerading as immune destruction because so many precursor cells were dying inside the marrow before reaching circulation (ineffective erythropoiesis).

The presence of hepatic fibrosis (cirrhosis) and pulmonary fibrosis in such a young adult should always trigger a search for TBDs, even without the classic mucocutaneous triad (oral leukoplakia/nail dystrophy/skin pigmentation) seen in Dyskeratosis Congenita type 6 cases caused by biallelic mutations here we saw premature greying and neuropathy instead.) The absence of family history shouldn't deter you either; de novo mutations are possible, though less likely than inherited ones with variable penetrance.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Messages

When evaluating pancytopenia with signs suggestive of hemolysis but lacking clear etiology:
*   **Don't trust labs blindly:** A positive DAT can be misleading in states of intense marrow turnover or dyserythropoiesis; correlate strictly with reticulocyte count (low here indicates production failure) and smear morphology.
*   **Look for "dysplasia" markers:** Basophilic stippling and Cabot rings are your red flags for ineffective erythropoiesis rather than peripheral destruction; they signal DNA/RNA processing defects often linked to B12/Folate status or genetic syndromes like TBDs/DKCsylated variants are rare but critical clues for telomere biology disorders which require long-term surveillance beyond just treating deficiencies.**
    *   Consider TBDs early: If you see unexplained pulmonary/hepatic fibrosis combined with cytopenias in <40 year olds—even without classic mucocutaneous features—order genetic testing for telomere genes (*TERT*, *TERC*, *PARN*).
    *   Treat aggressively but holistically: Correcting nutritional deficiencies is step one to stabilize the patient; however, recognizing the genetic underpinning dictates future management regarding transplant eligibility and malignancy surveillance risks inherent to TBDs.**
