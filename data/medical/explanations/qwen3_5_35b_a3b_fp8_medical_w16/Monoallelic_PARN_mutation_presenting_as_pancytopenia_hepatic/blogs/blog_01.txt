Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Beyond the Mucocutaneous Triad: Recognizing Telomere Biology Disorders in Young Adults

We've all heard the mantra: "If you hear hoofbeats, think horses, not zebras." But in Emergency Medicine and Internal Medicine, sometimes we're so focused on ruling out the common zebra (like Vitamin B12 deficiency or autoimmune hepatitis) that we miss the actual horse hiding behind a mask. Or worse, we treat the symptoms of a zebra and ignore the fact that it's actually a unicorn—a rare genetic entity that requires a completely different management strategy.

Consider a woman in her late 20s presenting with a crushing 5-year history of progressive fatigue, weakness, and declining functional capacity. On paper, she looks like a textbook case of severe nutritional deficiency. She has pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, and frank neurological deficits. Her labs scream "Vitamin B12 deficiency," and her bone marrow shows ineffective erythropoiesis. You start the shots. You give the folate. You correct the electrolytes.

But here's where the story gets interesting: **the patient doesn't just improve; she improves *too* much.** The systemic dysfunction persists disproportionately to the degree of malabsorption found on endoscopy. And if you look closer at her physical exam? She has premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and hepatosplenomegaly. She has pulmonary fibrosis in her 20s. She has hepatic cirrhosis without alcohol or viral hepatitis.

This isn't just bad luck or simple malnutrition. This is a Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD). Specifically, this case highlights a monoallelic *PARN* mutation masquerading as a metabolic crisis in a young adult who lacks the classic mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC).

## The Trap of Classical Definitions

For decades, our mental model for DC—and by extension, TBDs—has been anchored to the "classic triad": oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. If those aren't present, many clinicians rightly hesitate to order expensive genetic panels for telomere biology. They assume DC is primarily a pediatric disease characterized by these visible stigmata or severe immunodeficiency syndromes like Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome.

However, this patient challenges that paradigm head-on. She presented with **pancytopenia**, **premature aging** (greying hair), **osteoporosis**, **hepatic fibrosis**, and **pulmonary fibrosis**. There was no mention of leukoplakia or nail dystrophy at presentation. If we had relied strictly on the presence of the mucocutaneous triad to trigger our suspicion for telomere biology disorders, this patient would likely have continued down a rabbit hole of searching for obscure autoimmune etiologies or malignancy until significant organ damage occurred.

The reality is that TBDs exist on a vast phenotypic spectrum. Adult-onset presentations often lack the dramatic cutaneous findings seen in childhood forms like DC or HH syndrome. Instead, they present as "idiopathic" pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) in young smokers/non-smokers alike or as unexplained bone marrow failure (BMFS) with subtle clues scattered across other organ systems. The "silence" of their physical appearance does not mean silence in their molecular machinery; it means we need to be listening harder to the lab values and cross-system connections rather than just looking at fingernails and tongues.

## Decoding the Clues: Why This Case Matters

What should have tipped us off earlier than genetic sequencing? It wasn't one single finding but rather the **constellation** of findings that defied a single organic etiology:

*   **Ineffective Erythropoiesis:** The combination of pancytopenia with high reticulocyte consumption markers (low retic count), elevated LDH/hyperbilirubinemia (suggesting hemolysis-like picture), yet negative direct antiglobulin tests (DAT) for true auto-immune hemolysis points toward marrow failure driven by cellular senescence rather than immune destruction or simple lack of substrate—even though B12 was low simultaneously (a classic trap!).
*   **"Idiopathic" Fibrosis:** Finding hepatic cirrhosis (confirmed by elastography showing >18 kPa stiffness) and pulmonary fibrosis in a young woman without exposure risks is red flag territory for TBDs up to 30% of cases involve liver involvement before hematologic issues dominate.
*   **Premature Aging Phenotype:** Premature greying combined with osteoporosis and neuropathy suggests accelerated cellular turnover limits across multiple tissues—hair follicles, bone marrow neurons—all sharing high regenerative demands dependent on telomere length maintenance.
*   **The Genetic Hit:** Exome sequencing revealed a monoallelic *PARN* variant (*c.*613*T*>C; *p*.Cys205Arg). While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) due to rarity, its location in the highly conserved R3H domain and predictions from bioinformatics tools strongly suggested pathogenicity within the context of her clinical phenotype (*PARN* is known for both recessive DCB6 and dominant/telomeric PFBMFT4 phenotypes depending on zygosity).

It is crucial to note that while this patient had severe B12 deficiency requiring aggressive replacement, correcting it did not resolve the underlying susceptibility to marrow failure or organ fibrosis caused by shortened telomeres triggered by oxidative stress from B12 metabolism errors acting on an already compromised background genotype. The B12 deficiency was likely an "accelerant" hitting an engine already running hot because it lacked proper lubrication (telomeres).

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

When evaluating young adults with unexplained multi-system decline—specifically involving hematopoietic lines and parenchymal organs—we must expand our differential beyond common acquired diseases:

*   **Think TBD First:** Do not wait for oral leukoplakia or nail dystrophy to suspect Dyskeratosis Congenita/TBDs in adults presenting with Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF), Bone Marrow Failure Syndrome (BMFS), or cryptic Hepatic Fibrosis/Cirrhosis (<40 years old).
*   **Look for Systemic Synergy:** A cluster of cytopenias + premature greying + osteoporosis + neuropathy + organ fibrosis should trigger immediate reflex testing for telomere biology disorders via Next Generation Sequencing panels targeting *TERT*, *TERC*, *RTEL1*, *PARN*, etc., regardless of family history (de novo mutations occur).
*   **The VUS Paradox:** Be cautious about dismissing variants labeled "Uncertain Significance" if they hit critical domains (like *PARN*'s R3H domain) in patients with strong clinical phenotypes consistent with known pathways; clinical-genetic correlation often supersedes initial classification algorithms in rare diseases.
*   **Management Implications:** Once diagnosed as TBD:
    *   Avoid myelosuppressive drugs if possible during acute crises unless life-saving measures are needed carefully monitored via transplant teams later if indicated for BMFS progression? Wait... actually avoid unnecessary chemotherapy/radiation which accelerates telomere loss further!
    *   Monitor closely for progression to overt malignancy (especially squamous cell carcinoma and leukemia).
    *   Consider Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation (HSCT) early if marrow failure becomes refractory; however, conditioning protocols require modification due to hypersensitivity to alkylating agents/radiation inherent in TBDs!
    *   Provide genetic counseling immediately; even monoallelic mutations carry transmission risks ranging from 50% chance passing allele vs full blown recessive disease depending on partner status/future offspring considerations!
    *   Screen family members asymptomatic relatives too since penetrance varies widely based upon age/environment/lifestyle factors influencing replicative capacity differences among individuals sharing same genotype!

This case serves as a stark reminder that medicine is rarely linear; sometimes treating one problem reveals another entirely different universe underneath it all.
