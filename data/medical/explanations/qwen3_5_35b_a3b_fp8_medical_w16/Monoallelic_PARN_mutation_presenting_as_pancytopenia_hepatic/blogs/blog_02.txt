Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# The PARN Gene Paradox: Why Monoallelic Variants Can Cause Multi-Organ Failure

Let's talk about the "great mimickers" in internal medicine. You know the type: young patients presenting with unexplained pancytopenia, liver scarring, or "idiopathic" lung fibrosis who don't fit neatly into any single diagnostic box. Usually, we chase down infections, autoimmune panels, and nutritional deficits until the trail goes cold. But what if the answer isn't a missing vitamin or an autoantibody, but a subtle genetic glitch that turns a standard deficiency into a catastrophe?

This week's case is a masterclass in recognizing Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs) outside their classic textbook presentation. We are looking at a woman in her late 20s with a five-year history of crushing fatigue, premature greying, and multi-system organ failure. She had pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis (cirrhosis), pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, and neuropathy. On paper, she looked like a walking list of disparate pathologies. But the thread tying them all together wasn't an infection or a toxin—it was a **monoallelic** mutation in the *PARN* gene.

What makes this case particularly fascinating for us clinicians is the "variant paradox." Her genetic sequencing returned a "Variant of Uncertain Significance" (VUS). By strict classification standards, we might hesitate to label this definitive. Yet, the clinical phenotype was so stereotypically TBD—combining bone marrow failure with organ fibrosis and signs of accelerated aging—that ignoring the genetic finding would have been malpractice. This case forces us to confront a hard truth: sometimes, the genotype doesn't need to be "proven pathogenic" by every database criterion if the phenotype screams it loud enough.

## Beyond the Classic Triad: When Dyskeratosis Congenita Hides

For many of us trained in hematology or pulmonology, Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC) brings to mind a very specific set of physical exam findings: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. If your patient lacks this "mucocutaneous triad," DC often gets tossed off the differential entirely.

However, as this case illustrates and recent literature confirms, TBDs are notorious for their phenotypic variability. The classic triad appears in only a subset of patients; many present with isolated hematologic failure ("aplastic anemia"), isolated pulmonary fibrosis ("IPF"), or liver disease before ever showing skin changes. In fact, up to half of adults with TBDs present without mucocutaneous features at all.

In our patient's case, we saw hints that should have triggered a higher index of suspicion much earlier:
*   **Premature greying:** A hallmark sign of accelerated biological aging often overlooked until telomeres are measured.
*   **Neurological decline:** Sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy is increasingly recognized as part of the TBD spectrum (particularly in Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson variants), though less common in adult presentations.
*   **Disproportionate organ damage:** The combination of severe B12 deficiency-induced marrow issues *plus* liver cirrhosis in a young person is statistically improbable unless there is an underlying repair defect driving cellular senescence across multiple tissues simultaneously.

The key takeaway here is that when you see **bone marrow failure + pulmonary/liver fibrosis + signs of premature aging**, think Telomere Biology Disorder immediately—even if the nails look fine and there's no leukoplakia in sight.

## Decoding the *PARN* Enigma: Biallelic vs. Monoallelic

To understand why this patient got sick despite having only one bad copy of *PARN*, we have to look at gene dosage and inheritance patterns within telomere biology genes (*TERT*, *TERC*, *PARN*). It is generally taught as follows:
1.  **Biallelic Pathogenic Variants:** These cause Autosomal Recessive forms like DKCB6 or severe pediatric syndromes like Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH). These patients usually present very early with profound immunodeficiency and cerebellar hypoplasia (in HH).
2.  **Monoallelic Pathogenic Variants:** These are typically associated with autosomal dominant susceptibility to Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) or Bone Marrow Failure Syndromes later in life (often termed PFBMFT4). They act via haploinsufficiency—essentially, 50% function isn't enough to maintain telomere length under stress over decades.

Here lies the complexity of our case report: Our patient had a **monoallelic** missense variant (*c.613T>C*; *p.Cys205Arg*) labeled as VUS by current databases because few similar cases exist specifically linking this exact amino acid change to disease severity on its own. However, bioinformatic tools predicted it damaging (PM1/P3 criteria), and it sits in a highly conserved domain critical for TERC maturation (the R3H domain).

Why did she get such severe multi-organ disease from just one allele? Two theories emerge from the discussion:
*   **The "Two-Hit" Hypothesis:** Perhaps environmental stressors (like severe B12 deficiency) acted as an accelerant on already compromised stem cells due to partial telomerase dysfunction. The B12 deficiency didn't cause the fibrosis directly; rather, it tipped an unstable system into irreversible collapse because her cells couldn't divide fast enough to repair themselves once nutrients were restored but telomeres were critically short.
*   **Incomplete Penetrance & Modifier Genes:** Monoallelic variants often show variable expressivity depending on other genetic modifiers or epigenetic factors not captured by standard exome sequencing alone.

Regardless of whether this specific variant is technically "pathogenic" or just "highly suspicious," treating it as such guided management toward transplant evaluation and surveillance for malignancy—which is exactly where we want to be heading when dealing with TBDs.

## Clinical Pearls for Managing Genetic Ambiguity

So how do we handle these grey areas when guidelines are based on black-and-white classifications? Here are my take-homes from this challenging case:

*   **Don't let VUS stop you:** A Variant of Uncertain Significance does not mean "ignore me." If your clinical picture fits perfectly—pancytopenia + fibrosis + premature aging—and you find a monoallelic hit in a known TBD gene (*TERT*, *TERC*, *PARN*), treat it as real disease pending further family segregation studies or functional assays. The genotype-phenotype correlation often outweighs current database limitations for rare variants.
*   **Look beyond B12/Folate:** Yes, our patient had severe B12 deficiency causing ineffective erythropoiesis and neurological symptoms (subacute combined degeneration-like picture). But correcting those levels did not resolve the liver stiffness or lung findings completely; they merely stabilized her acute crisis while exposing the underlying fragility of her marrow reserve over time. Always ask: Is this nutritional deficiency masking an inherent inability to regenerate?
*   **Screening Tools Matter:** If you suspect TBD but aren't ready for full exome sequencing tomorrow, start measuring relative leukocyte telomere length (via flow-FISH) immediately while waiting for results? While not perfect alone due to age-dependent norms and assay variability, it provides rapid mechanistic insight into whether your patient truly has short telomeres regardless of what their genetic report says today's night shift reads as "uncertain."
*   **Surveillance > Cure:** Currently, there is no FDA-approved therapy that reverses established organ fibrosis in adult TBDs aside from transplant considerations for marrow failure/hypersensitivity reactions to chemotherapy agents used during conditioning regimens for HSCT must be avoided unless absolutely necessary due to toxicity risks related to shortened telomeres! Danazol/nandrolone may be tried for hematologic stabilization but require careful monitoring for hepatic side effects—ironically relevant since our patient already has cirrhosis!
*   **Family Counseling is Critical:** Even if this looks like a de novo mutation (as suggested by lack of family history), parents should ideally undergo screening because germline mosaicism exists—or siblings may carry lower-risk alleles that could manifest differently later in life given incomplete penetrance patterns typical of these disorders!
