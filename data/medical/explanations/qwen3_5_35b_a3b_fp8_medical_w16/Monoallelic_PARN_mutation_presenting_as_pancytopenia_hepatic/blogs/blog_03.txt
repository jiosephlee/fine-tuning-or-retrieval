Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# When Nutritional Deficiencies Mask Genetic Syndromes: The B12-Telomere Interaction

Let's talk about "red herrings" in medicine. Sometimes they are the glittering promise of a quick fix that turns out to be just that—a distractor.

Consider a woman in her late 20s presenting with crushing fatigue, generalized weakness, and pancytopenia. On paper, the story seems straightforward: she has severe Vitamin B12 deficiency, likely from pernicious anemia or malabsorption. You find the low levels, you start the shots, and you wait for the recovery. That is exactly where many clinicians would stop. But in this fascinating case report by Soni et al., stopping at "B12 deficiency" would have missed the boat entirely on a much more sinister diagnosis lurking beneath the surface.

This patient didn't just *have* B12 deficiency; she had a **telomere biology disorder (TBD)** that was unmasked by a nutritional "second hit." The B12 deficiency was real—it was severe enough to cause ineffective erythropoiesis—but it was acting as an accelerant on an engine already running on empty because of shortened telomeres. Without recognizing the underlying genetic substrate, we risk treating only the symptom while missing a condition that requires lifelong surveillance for pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic cirrhosis, and impending bone marrow failure.

## The "Second Hit" Phenomenon

What makes this case so compelling is the interplay between a classic metabolic crisis and a rare genetic syndrome. The patient presented with all the hallmarks of severe megaloblastic anemia: dimorphic red cells, hypersegmented neutrophils, high LDH, and elevated bilirubin. Her bone marrow showed erythroid hyperplasia with massive iron stores but poor reticulocyte output—a textbook picture of ineffective erythropoiesis driven by B12/folate lack.

However, look closer at the constellation of findings that shouldn't fit into a simple nutritional hole:
*   **Premature aging:** Grey hair in her late 20s, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and bald tongue with atrophy.
*   **Multisystem Fibrosis:** Hepatic fibrosis (cirrhosis) and Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) in a non-smoker/non-alcoholic young adult.
*   **Neurologic & Skeletal issues:** Sensorimotor neuropathy and osteoporosis with fractures unrelated to trauma.
*   **Hepatomegaly/Splenomegaly** disproportionate to simple liver injury.

Even after aggressive replacement therapy (IV cyanocobalamin), her systemic dysfunction persisted beyond what one would expect from simple repletion alone. The clinical team correctly identified that while B12 deficiency explained the *acute* cytopenia, it failed to explain the *syndromic* nature of her illness—specifically the combination of marrow failure signals + premature aging + organ fibrosis in a young person.

The pathophysiology here is elegant in its tragedy: Telomeres cap our chromosomes and dictate cellular lifespan. In TBDs like Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC), telomeres shorten prematurely due to mutations in repair genes (like *PARN*, *TERT*, or *TERC*). Cells that divide rapidly—hematopoietic stem cells—are hit hardest when their telomeres reach critical length; they enter senescence or die rather than replicate.

In this patient, she carried a monoallelic missense variant in **_PARN_**. While biallelic mutations usually cause classic recessive DC (often lethal in childhood), monoallelic variants are increasingly recognized as causes of Adult-Onset Pulmonary Fibrosis/Bone Marrow Failure Syndrome (PFBMFT4). This variant acts as a baseline vulnerability ("first hit"). Then comes the severe B12 deficiency ("second hit"). B12 is essential for DNA synthesis; without it, rapidly dividing cells stall immediately. For someone with healthy telomeres, this is manageable with supplements. For someone whose stem cells are already teetering on senescence due to _PARN_ haploinsufficiency? It pushes them over the edge into frank pancytopenia and rapid clinical decline.

## Decoding the PARN Connection

The diagnostic breakthrough came via clinical exome sequencing identifying a monoallelic missense variant (**c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg**) in _PARN_. While technically classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) initially due to limited prior data on this specific allele, its location matters immensely. It sits within the highly conserved R3H domain of the protein—a region critical for binding RNA substrates necessary for telomerase maturation (_TERC_ processing).

_PARN_ encodes an exoribonuclease responsible for trimming poly-A tails from RNAs to regulate stability and turnover of _TERC_, the RNA component of telomerase without which telomerase cannot function effectively. Therefore, even heterozygous loss-of-function can reduce telomere maintenance capacity below optimal thresholds over time.

Why did this matter? Because monoallelic _PARN_ variants are now firmly linked to:
1.  **Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF):** Often appearing before other symptoms.
2.  **Bone Marrow Failure:** Which can be subtle until triggered by stressors like infection or nutritional deficiency.
3.  **Hepatic Fibrosis:** Emerging evidence suggests liver involvement is more common than historically thought in TBDs involving telomerase pathway genes (upwards of 33% cirrhosis rates in registry data).

The absence of classic mucocutaneous triads (nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia) should never rule out Dyskeratosis Congenita or related TBDs in adults up to 50% of cases! As highlighted here, "classic" presentations are disappearing; modern phenotypes often present as isolated organ failure or unexplained fatigue in young adults lacking family history (de novo mutations occur).

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Messages

This case serves as a stark reminder that "reversible" does not always mean "benign," especially when multisystem involvement is present early on if you see pancytopenia + premature greying + fibrosis = think Telomere Biology Disorder regardless of whether you found low B12 first.**Here are your actionable take-homes:**

*   **The "Two-Hit" Hypothesis:** In young adults presenting with unexplained bone marrow failure or pulmonary fibrosis aggressively screen for nutritional deficiencies (B12/Folate/Iron), but remember: if symptoms persist disproportionately after correction OR if there are extra-hematologic signs (fibrosis, graying), investigate for an underlying genetic predisposition immediately.**Never assume nutritional deficiency explains everything.** Even if you treat it successfully, monitor closely for recurrence or progression.**If you see premature greying + hepatosplenomegaly + pancytopenia**, order telomere length testing or genetic panels even without family history.*Genetic counseling is mandatory once TBD is suspected; siblings/offspring need screening.*Early recognition allows for transplant planning if marrow fails again.*Surveillance protocols must include annual lung function tests (spirometry/DLCO) and liver imaging/stiffness measurement.*Be aware that monosomy-like states can arise from haploinsufficiency; don't dismiss single-allele findings if they land in critical domains like _PARN_'s R3H loop.*Don't let negative autoimmunity screens close your differential too quickly—this wasn't AIHA despite positive DAT!*When faced with "simple" malabsorption causing profound cytopenias,**ask yourself:* Is my patient old enough for age-related wear-and-tear?*No?* Think genetic susceptibility.*Is there extra-hematologic stuff going on?*Yes?* Look harder.*Did I check for premature aging signs?*Greying skin/hair/nails?* Yes?* Genotype!**In summary**: Treat the acute deficit yes but keep your eye on the horizon for the hidden syndromic driver.**Thank you reading!
