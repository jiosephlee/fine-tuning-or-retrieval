Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Revisiting Old Scans: The Diagnostic Power of Retrospective Imaging Review in Delayed Diagnoses

**Lead:** What happens when a patient spends 14 years chasing "anxiety" and "reflux" for choking spells and midnight hypoxia, only to find the answer sitting in a drawer labeled "Normal"? In this case, a woman in her early 40s suffered from recurrent choking, chronic occipital headaches, and terrifying drops in oxygen saturation down to 70%. She was a walking paradox: severe respiratory compromise without dyspnea, normal lungs, normal GI workups, and a history of being told everything was fine. The missing piece wasn't new technology; it was looking back at an old CT scan that had been dismissed as normal fourteen years prior.

This case serves as a stark reminder that in complex clinical puzzles, the answer is often hiding in plain sight within historical data. We are discussing **Chiari Malformation Type I (CM1)** masquerading as acquired central hypoventilation syndrome ("Ondine's Curse"). The key takeaway? When faced with unexplained bulbar dysfunction or central sleep apnea, your eyes need to travel backward—to the archives—and look closer at the foramen magnum.

### The Case That Was Almost Missed Again

Our patient presented with a classic "red herring" profile. For seven years, she endured unpredictable choking on both solids and liquids, waking up gasping three to four nights a week. Laryngoscopies and endoscopies were repeatedly normal. The GI team ruled out reflux; the ENT team ruled out structural obstruction; the pulmonologists cleared her lungs. Yet, she would wake up with oxygen saturations plummeting to 70–80%, often unaware of the event until she checked her home pulse oximeter. At one point, she was denied boarding at an airport because her O2 sat dropped to 78% despite having no subjective shortness of breath—a hallmark sign of autonomic respiratory failure.

She also carried a 14-year history of constant vertex and occipital headaches, allodynia so severe that cutting her hair provided relief (a rare but documented phenomenon involving scalp hypersensitivity). After a minor car accident 14 years ago where she briefly lost consciousness, she developed these headaches. An urgent CT scan was done then but reported as normal.

The turning point came when we decided to re-examine that 14-year-old non-contrast head CT alongside her recent MRI. While the original radiologist saw nothing abnormal, our second look revealed a **crowded foramen magnum** with cerebellar tonsils extending to the level of the opisthion and obliteration of CSF spaces at the craniocervical junction. Her modern MRI confirmed **Chiari I** with 11 mm of tonsillar descent (diagnostic threshold is >5 mm). A sleep study later showed predominantly central apneas (72%), confirming that her brainstem compression was disrupting automatic respiratory drive while leaving voluntary breathing intact.

### Why Did It Take So Long? The Concept of "Overlooked Anatomy"

It is tempting to blame human error when a diagnosis takes 14 years to emerge, but we must recognize that CM1 can be deceptively subtle on older imaging modalities like non-contrast CT. Unlike modern high-resolution MRI which clearly delineates soft tissue boundaries against CSF flow dynamics, CT relies on bony landmarks and density differences. A crowded posterior fossa might be interpreted as "within normal limits" if the reader isn't specifically looking for tonsillar descent or loss of subarachnoid space at the foramen magnum.

Furthermore, there is a growing body of evidence suggesting that CM1 exists on a spectrum from benign anatomical variants to pathological entities requiring intervention. With an estimated prevalence of 1% in adults (though symptomatic cases are far rarer), incidental findings are common. If you see tonsillar descent without symptoms like headache or hydrocephalus, you don't operate. But if you see *symptoms* matching the anatomy—like our patient's central hypoventilation—you have your diagnosis regardless of how many times it was missed previously.

The pathophysiology here is fascinating: compression of the medulla oblongata disrupts the autonomic centers controlling respiration during sleep (automatic breathing) while sparing cortical pathways needed for voluntary breathing during wakefulness (unless distracted). This explains why she could hold her breath voluntarily but would stop breathing automatically during sleep or moments of distraction (like waiting in line at an airport), leading to silent hypoxia without the urge to breathe (dyspnea).

### Clinical Pearls & Framework for Action

When evaluating patients with refractory respiratory issues or unexplained neurological deficits, consider this approach:

*   **Suspect Brainstem Compression in "Unexplained" Central Apnea:** If you encounter central sleep apnea or hypoventilation where pulmonary causes are ruled out, think about structural brainstem pathology first. CM1 is a known cause of acquired central hypoventilation syndrome mimicking Ondine's Curse.
*   **Re-review Historical Imaging:** Never assume an old scan is truly "normal." Specifically ask radiology colleagues to re-read old CT scans or MRIs focusing on the posterior fossa dimensions and tonsillar position relative to McGregor's line or Chamberlain's line when clinical suspicion is high but current imaging hasn't yielded answers yet (or when no current imaging exists).
*   **Differentiate Bulbar Symptoms:** Choking on *both* solids and liquids equally suggests a neurogenic etiology rather than mechanical obstruction (which usually affects solids first). If endoscopy/laryngoscopy is negative in these cases, expand your differential to include skull base anomalies like CM1 or syringobulbia.
*   **Look Beyond Standard Headache Descriptions:** Chronic occipital headaches are common in CM1 (>80% of cases), often precipitated by Valsalva maneuvers (coughing/straining). However, unique presentations like vertex allodynia relieved by hair removal should raise suspicion for cranial nerve irritation associated with posterior fossa crowding.
*   **Understand Sleep Study Limitations:** As seen in this case, Type III portable sleep studies can underestimate severity if EEG data isn't available (since Total Recording Time includes wakefulness). A low AHI does not rule out significant pathology if there is strong clinical evidence of central events and desaturation; always correlate physiologic data with anatomical findings and clinical context.
*   **Trauma Can Unmask Congenital Defects:** Be cautious about attributing late-onset symptoms solely to trauma without considering that trauma may have altered CSF dynamics enough to exacerbate a previously asymptomatic congenital variant like CM1 into symptomatic Chiari Syndrome.

### Final Thoughts

This patient's journey underscores a critical lesson for clinicians: **the past matters.** In an era obsessed with ordering new tests—the latest MRI sequence or genetic panel—we sometimes forget that the most valuable diagnostic tool might already exist in our institution's archive system. A simple retrospective review changed this woman's life trajectory from decades of anxiety and misdiagnosis toward potential surgical cure via posterior fossa decompression.

Next time you see a patient with mysterious hypoxia who feels fine while drowning silently on paper charts—or someone whose "normal" swallow study doesn't explain their choking—take ten minutes to pull up those old scans from five years ago (or fifteen!). You might just find that the crowdedspace at the bottom of their skull holds the key they've been missing all along.
