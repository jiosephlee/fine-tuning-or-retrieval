Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# The Choking Mystery: Why Normal Endoscopy Should Trigger a Neurological Workup for Dysphagia

Let's talk about the "diagnostic odyssey" of the undiagnosed choker. You know the patient: they come in terrified, recounting stories of waking up gasping in the middle of the night or choking on a simple bite of toast. They've seen the ENT, they've seen GI, they've had three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper endoscopies. And every single time? The scope comes back clean. The anatomy looks perfect. The mucosa is pristine.

So, what do you tell them? "It's probably anxiety," or "Maybe it's just stress."

Wrong answer. Or at least, incomplete.

Consider this case of a woman in her early 40s who suffered for **seven years** of recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids equally. She had nocturnal choking 3–4 times a week, chronic occipital headaches for 14 years, and terrifying episodes of spontaneous oxygen desaturation down to 70–80% without ever feeling short of breath. She was denied boarding flights because she couldn't catch her breath while sitting still at an airport gate. Despite being sent to pulmonary specialists and having normal PFTs and chest X-rays, she was labeled anxious until someone finally looked *up*—specifically, at the base of her skull.

The culprit wasn't a tumor, a stricture, or myasthenia gravis. It was Chiari Malformation Type I (CM1) causing acquired central hypoventilation syndrome—a modern-day version of Ondine's Curse driven by brainstem compression.

### When "Normal" Means "Neurological"

The classic teaching on dysphagia is straightforward: if it's mechanical (like a ring or stricture), you see it on the scope or feel it as resistance to solids first. If it's neurogenic (like stroke or ALS), you might get aspiration later in the course. But here lies the trap: **neurogenic dysphagia caused by brainstem compression often presents with perfectly normal endoscopic findings.**

In this patient's case, she choked on everything equally—solids and liquids alike—and had no structural lesions visible on repeated scopes. This is a massive red flag for a central nervous system etiology rather than an esophageal one. As highlighted in recent literature regarding CM1, lower cranial nerve involvement occurs in roughly 15–26% of symptomatic patients, manifesting specifically as palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia that mimics mechanical obstruction but isn't there structurally.

When your GI workup hits a dead end with normal findings but symptoms persisting for years, stop looking down and start looking up at the foramen magnum.

### The Respiratory Red Flag: Forgetting to Breathe

What truly sets this case apart from typical swallowing disorders is the respiratory component. This patient didn't just choke; she stopped breathing automatically during sleep and distraction while maintaining voluntary control awake (until she got tired). This is **central hypoventilation**.

During her airport episode, her O2 dropped to 78%. She felt dizzy but not "air hungry." Why? Because the brainstem centers responsible for automatic respiratory drive were compressed by herniating cerebellar tonsils (measuring 11 mm descent). Meanwhile, her cortical ability to voluntarily breathe remained intact until fatigue set in or distraction took over her focus ("I forgot to breathe").

This dissociation between voluntary and automatic breathing is rare outside of congenital central hypoventilation syndromes (PHOX2B mutations) but can be acquired via structural brainstem pathology like CM1. If a patient has unexplained hypoxia without dyspnea, especially with nocturnal events that don't match standard obstructive sleep apnea patterns (remember, this patient had 72% *central* events), think brainstem compression before you reach for CPAP machines alone.

### Don't Miss the Old Scan

One of the most chilling aspects of this case was that the diagnosis was staring them in the face 14 years ago—but nobody saw it until now. A non-contrast CT done after a minor car accident showed a "crowded foramen magnum" that was dismissed as incidental or normal at the time. Only upon re-review did we see cerebellar tissue extending to the opisthion with loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction.

This underscores a critical lesson: **Re-review old imaging.** Especially when you have new-onset neurological symptoms that seem disconnected from previous trauma or complaints that span decades but were never fully connected to a structural deficit. The "normal" scan from 2010 held the key to unlocking her entire clinical picture today.

### Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

While posterior fossa decompression can reverse these devastating symptoms, recognition is half the battle here are your actionable take-homes:

*   **The Equal Consistency Rule:** Dysphagia affecting solids AND liquids equally with normal endoscopic visualization should trigger an immediate search for neurological causes (brainstem/cranial nerves) rather than further GI workups like pH probes or manometry unless indicated otherwise.
*   **Hypoxia Without Hypoxemia Sensation:** If a patient presents with severe oxygen desaturation (<80%) without subjective dyspnea ("air hunger"), suspect central hypoventilation due to brainstem compression (e.g., Chiari malformation) rather than primary pulmonary disease or anxiety alone.
*   **Re-evaluate Old Imaging:** Always pull old head CTs/MRIs if there is any history of trauma or long-standing headaches; subtle crowding at the foramen magnum can be easily missed on initial reads but becomes obvious once you know what pattern to look for (tonsillar descent >5mm + loss of CSF cisterns).
*   **Polysomnography Nuance:** Be wary of Type III sleep studies in these patients; they may underestimate central events if EEG monitoring isn't present to calculate true sleep time versus total recording time. A high percentage of central apneas (>50%) even with a low AHI should raise suspicion regardless of technical limitations.
*   **The Headache Connection:** Do not ignore chronic occipital headaches or unique scalp phenomena like allodynia/burning vertex syndrome; these can be part of the same craniovertebral junction pathology driving bulbar dysfunction later in life.
