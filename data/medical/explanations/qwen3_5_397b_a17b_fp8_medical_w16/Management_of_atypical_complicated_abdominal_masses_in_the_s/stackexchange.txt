Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a pregnant patient presenting with an elevated CA-125 level (e.g., 41.8 U/mL) during the second trimester, why is this finding insufficient to diagnose ovarian malignancy, and what benign physiological or pathological conditions commonly cause such elevations?

An isolated elevation of CA-125 in the second trimester, such as the 41.8 U/mL observed in this case, is insufficient to diagnose ovarian malignancy because CA-125 lacks specificity during pregnancy. While typically used as an oncologic marker with a cutoff of ≥35 U/mL in non-pregnant populations, serum CA-125 levels physiologically fluctuate during gestation, often peaking in the first and third trimesters. More importantly, this antigen is expressed by coelomic epithelium and can be elevated by various benign physiological states and pathological conditions unrelated to cancer.

As illustrated in the reported case of a woman with massive abdominal masses ultimately proven to be benign uterine leiomyomas, several non-malignant etiologies can drive CA-125 elevation. These include:
*   **Benign Uterine Pathology:** Large or degenerating leiomyomas (fibroids), particularly those undergoing cystic degeneration, necrosis, or torsion as seen in this patient's pedunculated mass, can cause significant release of CA-125.
*   **Endometriosis and Adenomyosis:** Decidualized endometriomas were a primary differential diagnosis in this case specifically due to the marker's association with ectopic endometrial tissue.
*   **Physiological Pregnancy Changes:** Normal placental development and peritoneal irritation from the expanding uterus can elevate levels.
*   **Inflammation and Infection:** Concurrent conditions such as pelvic inflammatory disease or, as noted in this patient's course, systemic inflammation (evidenced here by leukocytosis and a urinary *Klebsiella* infection) can contribute to rising titers.

Consequently, relying on a single CA-125 measurement risks significant overtreatment. The clinical reasoning must prioritize multimodal assessment—specifically high-resolution ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—to characterize mass morphology (e.g., distinguishing solid vs. cystic components, vascularity, and continuity with the uterus) rather than depending solely on serology. In this case, despite the "slight positivity" of the tumor marker suggesting potential malignancy or endometrioma, the definitive diagnosis relied on surgical pathology confirming benign leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis. Therefore, an elevated CA-125 in pregnancy should be interpreted as a nonspecific indicator of peritoneal or mesothelial irritation rather than a standalone diagnostic criterion for ovarian cancer.

### [Management] Given the limitations of ionizing radiation and contrast agents in pregnancy, what is the recommended imaging algorithm for characterizing complex abdominal masses when ultrasound findings are inconclusive, and what specific safety constraints apply to MRI in this population?

When ultrasound findings are inconclusive for complex abdominal masses in pregnancy, **non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)** is the recommended second-line modality to characterize tissue composition, vascularity, and anatomical relationships without exposing the fetus to ionizing radiation. As illustrated in the case of the pregnant patient with symptomatic subphrenic and pelvic masses, MRI provided superior diagnostic accuracy over ultrasound by distinguishing between decidualized endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas, specifically identifying the continuity of the caudal mass with the uterine wall and the pedunculated nature of the cranial mass.

Regarding safety constraints, two critical principles must guide MRI utilization in this population. First, **gadolinium-based contrast agents are contraindicated** unless absolutely vital for immediate maternal survival, as gadolinium crosses the placenta, enters fetal circulation, and is excreted into the amniotic fluid where it may remain for prolonged periods; consequently, the case report explicitly notes that the multidisciplinary team performed a non-enhanced study to avoid these risks. Second, while MRI is generally safe after the first trimester, current recommendations suggest avoiding scanners with field strengths **greater than 1.5 Tesla** when possible, although the primary restriction remains the avoidance of contrast rather than the magnetic field strength itself. This imaging algorithm allows clinicians to differentiate benign entities like twisted or degenerating fibroids from malignancies based on signal characteristics (e.g., T1/T2 weighting) and morphological features, thereby informing the decision between expectant management and surgical intervention while prioritizing fetal safety.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do the sonographic and MRI features of a 'decidualized endometrioma' mimic those of a cystic degenerating leiomyoma, and what specific anatomical clue on imaging helps distinguish a pedunculated uterine myoma from an primary adnexal mass?

Both decidualized endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas present a diagnostic challenge in pregnancy because they share overlapping sonographic and MRI characteristics: both appear as large, complex, predominantly cystic masses with thickened walls, internal hemorrhagic or necrotic debris, and peripheral vascularity. In the setting of pregnancy, the hormonal milieu induces decidualization of endometrial tissue within an endometrioma, creating solid-appearing nodules that mimic the hyperechoic or hyperintense solid components seen in a myoma undergoing red or cystic degeneration. As illustrated in this case, the initial imaging erroneously suggested "huge decidualised endometriomas" due to these shared features of unilocular cystic structures with inhomogeneous fluid content and solid-appearing elements.

The specific anatomical clue that definitively distinguishes a pedunculated uterine myoma from a primary adnexal mass is the identification of a **connective tissue stalk (pedicle) establishing continuity between the mass and the uterine serosa**. While advanced imaging (such as non-contrast MRI) can suggest this relationship by demonstrating the lack of a cleavage plane between the mass and the uterus—as noted in the report where the caudal mass appeared "in continuity with the posterior leiomyoma"—definitive confirmation often requires surgical visualization. In this case, despite the inability of ultrasound and MRI to visualize a feeding vessel or stalk for the upper mass due to the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus, laparotomy revealed a distinct pedicle connecting the cranial mass to the uterine fundus. This finding confirmed the diagnosis of a twisted, pedunculated leiomyoma rather than an ovarian neoplasm, highlighting that while imaging raises suspicion, the physical demonstration of uterine continuity remains the gold standard for differentiation.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the typical non-linear trajectory of uterine leiomyoma size changes throughout the three trimesters of pregnancy and the immediate postpartum period, and how does this influence the timing of potential surgical intervention?

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a distinct, non-linear trajectory in size throughout gestation and the puerperium, driven primarily by hormonal fluctuations and hemodynamic changes. As highlighted in the discussion of this case, myoma growth is most rapid during the first trimester, increasing by approximately 6.12% per week, likely due to surging estrogen and progesterone levels which stimulate cellular proliferation and extracellular matrix deposition. This growth rate decelerates significantly in the second and third trimesters (approximately 3.10% per week), despite continued absolute uterine expansion.

Crucially, this trend reverses sharply in the late third trimester and immediate postpartum period. Following delivery, the withdrawal of ovarian steroids and the involution of the uterus lead to a substantial reduction in myoma volume, with studies cited in the case reporting a mean decrease of roughly 44% by six weeks postpartum. This physiological regression profoundly influences surgical timing and strategy. In the presented case, although the patient required acute intervention in the second trimester for a twisted, symptomatic pedunculated mass causing respiratory compromise, the surgical team elected to leave a large, asymptomatic caudal myoma *in situ*. This decision was grounded in the expectation of spontaneous postpartum shrinkage. Indeed, follow-up confirmed the residual mass had reduced in size, rendering immediate further surgery unnecessary and allowing the patient to defer definitive management while breastfeeding. Consequently, unless life-threatening complications such as torsion, uncontrollable pain, or malignancy suspicion arise, surgical intervention for incidental or stable myomas is generally deferred until after delivery to leverage this natural involution and avoid the heightened risks of hemorrhage and pregnancy loss associated with antepartum myomectomy.

### [Management] In a case of a symptomatic, large pedunculated leiomyoma causing acute pain and respiratory compromise at 21 weeks gestation, what are the primary indications for proceeding with surgical myomectomy during pregnancy rather than adopting an expectant management approach?

Surgical myomectomy during pregnancy is reserved for rare, compelling indications where the immediate threat to maternal physiology or pregnancy viability outweighs the inherent risks of operative intervention, such as hemorrhage, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. Based on the principles illustrated in this case, the primary indications for proceeding with surgery rather than expectant management include **acute mechanical compromise**, **failure of conservative therapy**, and **suspected catastrophic complications** such as torsion or necrosis.

In the presented case, the decision to operate at 21 weeks was driven by the patient's development of **restrictive lung disease (dyspnea)** and **severe, persistent abdominal pain** caused by the "steric bulk" of an 18-cm pedunculated leiomyoma. Crucially, these symptoms did not resolve with standard conservative measures, including analgesia and positional changes; while pain scores improved slightly, the underlying respiratory compromise and mass effect persisted. The clinical picture was further complicated by signs suggestive of **torsion and ischemic necrosis**—specifically acute pain localization, low-grade fever, and leukocytosis—which were confirmed intraoperatively by the finding of a twisted, leaking pedicle with areas of infarction.

Furthermore, surgical intervention becomes necessary when there is a realistic concern that continued fetal growth will exacerbate mass-related hypoxia or precipitate rupture before fetal viability is assured. In this scenario, the multidisciplinary team determined that the risk of leaving a twisting, necrotic, and space-occupying lesion in situ exceeded the surgical risks. Therefore, the hallmark indications for antepartum myomectomy are not merely the presence of a fibroid, but rather the triad of **intractable symptoms**, **evidence of acute degeneration or torsion**, and **threatened maternal organ function** (such as respiratory compromise) that cannot be managed medically. Expectant management remains the standard for asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic cases, but it is contraindicated when the mass poses an imminent threat to maternal stability or pregnancy continuation.

### [Clinical Pearls] During an intraoperative myomectomy in the second trimester, what specific pharmacologic prophylaxis is indicated to mitigate the risk of preterm labor, and what monitoring standards must be maintained regarding fetal viability throughout the procedure?

In the setting of an intraoperative myomectomy during the second trimester, **intramuscular progesterone prophylaxis** is indicated to mitigate the risk of uterine irritability and preterm labor. As illustrated in the reported case of a 21-week gestation undergoing laparotomy for a twisted, symptomatic leiomyoma, the surgical team initiated therapy with **100 mg of intramuscular progesterone intraoperatively**, continued daily until discharge, and transitioned to **hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg)** administered every three days until elective delivery. This regimen addresses the heightened risk of myometrial contraction triggered by surgical manipulation of the gravid uterus.

Regarding fetal surveillance, the standard of care mandates **continuous verification of fetal viability** throughout the procedure. In this case, fetal cardiac activity was confirmed via exploration prior to incision and maintained through **serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasound monitoring** during the operation. This approach aligns with established recommendations that fetal heart rate must be documented before, during, and after surgery to ensure immediate detection of fetal distress or compromise resulting from hemodynamic shifts or uterine manipulation. The successful outcome in this case—resulting in an elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks with a healthy neonate—underscores the efficacy of combining pharmacologic uterine quiescence with rigorous intraoperative fetal monitoring when surgical intervention for complicated myomas cannot be deferred.

### [Management] When encountering multiple large uterine masses intraoperatively where one is pedunculated and twisting while another is broad-based and intramural, what strategic decision-making process guides the surgeon to excise one mass while leaving the other in situ?

The decision to excise the pedunculated mass while leaving the broad-based intramural lesion in situ is guided by a risk-benefit analysis prioritizing immediate maternal stabilization against the profound obstetric risks of extensive uterine manipulation. In this case, the cranial mass was identified intraoperatively as a pedunculated leiomyoma that was actively twisted, ischemic, and leaking. This configuration represents an acute surgical emergency; the torsion compromises vascular supply, leading to necrosis and potential infection or rupture, which directly threatens both maternal stability and fetal viability. Consequently, removal of this specific mass via clamping and suturing the stalk was mandatory to resolve the source of acute pain, fever, and hemodynamic stress.

Conversely, the caudal mass was a broad-based, intramural-subserosal leiomyoma firmly integrated into the uterine torus. Attempting to enucleate such a deeply embedded mass during pregnancy carries a prohibitive risk of catastrophic hemorrhage due to the heightened vascularity of the gravid uterus and the difficulty in achieving hemostasis within the myometrial bed. Furthermore, extensive dissection increases the likelihood of triggering preterm labor or compromising uterine integrity, potentially leading to rupture in future pregnancies. As noted in the case report, leaving this asymptomatic (regarding acute torsion) but bulky mass in situ allowed for the preservation of the pregnancy with close monitoring. The strategic compromise accepted the known future need for an elective cesarean delivery—required because the residual pelvic mass would obstruct labor—in exchange for avoiding immediate life-threatening surgical complications and ensuring fetal survival to term. This approach underscores the principle that during pregnancy, myomectomy should be restricted to treating acute complications like torsion, while stable, albeit large, intramural fibroids are managed conservatively until delivery.

### [Management] How does the presence and location of a large residual cervical or lower uterine segment leiomyoma influence the determination of the mode of delivery, and what are the risks associated with attempting vaginal birth in this scenario?

The presence of a large residual leiomyoma in the cervix or lower uterine segment fundamentally alters the mechanics of labor, typically necessitating an elective cesarean delivery rather than a trial of labor. As illustrated in the reported case, when a substantial myoma (in this instance, a 7.5 cm residual mass following partial myomectomy) occupies the pelvic inlet or lower uterine segment, it creates a mechanical obstruction known as dystocia. During the expulsive phase of labor, the fetal head requires adequate space to descend and engage; a fixed, bulky mass in this location physically blocks this progression, rendering vaginal delivery impossible and posing a high risk of obstructed labor.

Attempting vaginal birth in the setting of such an obstruction carries severe risks for both mother and fetus. For the mother, prolonged obstruction can lead to uterine inertia, excessive thinning of the lower uterine segment, and ultimately, catastrophic uterine rupture. Furthermore, the pressure exerted by the trapped fetal head against the soft tissues and the myoma itself increases the risk of extensive soft tissue trauma, fistula formation, and postpartum hemorrhage. For the fetus, the inability to descend results in prolonged compression, leading to fetal distress, hypoxia, and potential stillbirth.

In the specific case presented by De Nola et al., despite successful resection of a symptomatic pedunculated fundal myoma during the second trimester, the surgical team deliberately left a large intramural-subserosal myoma in situ within the lesser pelvis to minimize intraoperative obstetric risks. The authors explicitly noted that this residual "caudal myoma would have represented a huge obstacle for the fetal head progression." Consequently, the determination for an elective cesarean section at 37 weeks was driven directly by this anatomical constraint. This approach prioritized safety by avoiding the unpredictability of labor against a known mechanical barrier, resulting in an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome without the complications associated with emergency intervention for obstructed labor. Therefore, when imaging confirms a large leiomyoma impinging on the birth canal or lower uterine segment, cesarean delivery is the indicated management strategy.
