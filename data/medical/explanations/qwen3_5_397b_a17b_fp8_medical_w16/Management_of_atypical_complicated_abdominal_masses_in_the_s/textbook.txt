Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment of Abdominal Masses in Pregnancy

The clinical presentation of large abdominal masses during the second trimester often mimics acute obstetric emergencies, necessitating a high index of suspicion to differentiate between uterine pathology, adnexal disease, and standard pregnancy complications. In this case, a nulliparous woman in her 40s presented at 21 weeks gestation with severe, acute abdominal pain localized to the left upper quadrant and pelvis, accompanied by mild dyspnea. While abdominal pain is a common complaint in pregnancy, the severity and specific localization described here—distinct from the diffuse discomfort of round ligament strain or the rhythmic cramping of preterm labor—should prompt an evaluation for space-occupying lesions. The accompanying dyspnea is a critical clue; in the absence of primary cardiopulmonary disease, as evidenced by the patient's normal oxygen saturation (97%), heart rate, and electrocardiogram, respiratory compromise is likely mechanical. Massive abdominal distension can elevate the diaphragm, reducing functional residual capacity and causing restrictive lung symptoms. Recognizing this "mass effect" is vital, as it distinguishes the pathology from conditions like pulmonary embolism or pneumonia, directing the clinician toward abdominal imaging rather than solely chest-focused diagnostics.

Physical examination in a gravid patient presents unique challenges due to the enlarging uterus obscuring intra-abdominal structures. In this scenario, inspection revealed an abdomen disproportionately enlarged for the gestational age, with a palpable, taut-elastic mass occupying the entire left upper quadrant. While superficial palpation identifies large suprapubic or subphrenic masses, assessing pelvic pathology requires a bimanual vaginal examination. Despite the limitations imposed by the gravid uterus, this maneuver successfully identified a fixed, hard, rounded mass approximately 9 cm in diameter within the lesser pelvis, bulging into the posterior fornix. This finding underscores the necessity of combining abdominal and pelvic examinations; relying on abdominal palpation alone might have missed the distinct caudal component or misidentified it as part of the uterine fundus. The ability to characterize the consistency (hard vs. cystic) and mobility (fixed vs. mobile) of these masses provides immediate diagnostic data regarding their potential origin—whether myomatous, ovarian, or gastrointestinal.

Initial stabilization focuses on symptom management and addressing concurrent physiological stressors while avoiding interventions that could harm the fetus. In this case, conservative measures proved partially effective: placing the patient in a semi-orthostatic position helped alleviate diaphragmatic compression, reducing pain scores and improving respiratory comfort without requiring supplemental oxygen. Analgesia was achieved safely with intravenous paracetamol. However, the clinical picture was complicated by a low-grade fever and laboratory evidence of inflammation. It is crucial to distinguish between fever caused by tumor necrosis (common in rapidly growing or twisting leiomyomas) and infectious etiologies. Here, workup excluded viral respiratory pathogens but confirmed a *Klebsiella pneumoniae* urinary tract infection (UTI). The presence of a UTI in a patient with large pelvic masses requires aggressive management, as infection can precipitate uterine irritability and preterm contractions. Consequently, appropriate antibiotic therapy targeting the identified pathogen became an integral part of the initial stabilization protocol alongside pain control.

Interpreting baseline laboratory results in pregnancy requires adjusting for physiological norms to avoid misdiagnosis. This patient presented with iron deficiency anemia (hemoglobin 9.9 g/L) and leukocytosis (11.29 × 10³/µL) with neutrophilia. While hemodilution naturally lowers hemoglobin levels during pregnancy, a value below 11 g/L warrants investigation for underlying deficits or chronic blood loss, which may be exacerbated by large vascularized tumors. Similarly，妊娠-related leukocytosis typically ranges up to 15 × 10³/µL; therefore, this patient's white blood cell count fell within expected limits despite her fever and pain. However, the elevation of C-reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL) above the normal non-pregnant range suggests an active inflammatory process beyond normal gestational changes. When combined with the slightly elevated CA-125 (41.8 U/mL), these labs create a complex diagnostic puzzle: while CA-125 can rise physiologically in pregnancy or due to benign conditions like endometriosis and leiomyomas, its upward trend alongside inflammatory markers necessitates ruling out malignancy or acute complications such as torsion or infarction. This nuanced interpretation guides the decision to proceed with advanced imaging rather than adopting a purely expectant approach.

## Diagnostic Imaging Strategies and Interpretation of Biomarkers

The evaluation of complex abdominal masses during pregnancy requires a strategic, stepwise diagnostic approach that balances diagnostic accuracy with fetal safety. In this case, the initial assessment relied heavily on ultrasonography (US), which served as the primary screening modality to characterize the two distinct lesions. The ultrasound revealed large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularization. A critical challenge in this scenario was distinguishing between decidualized endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas, as both can present with similar sonographic features during pregnancy. Decidualization, a physiological transformation of endometrial stroma induced by high progesterone levels, can cause endometriomas to appear solid or mixed, mimicking malignancy or other neoplasms. Similarly, leiomyomas undergoing red or cystic degeneration lose their classic homogeneous hypoechoic appearance, becoming heterogeneous and cystic. In this patient, the upper mass lacked a visible connective stalk due to the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus, complicating the determination of its origin, while the caudal mass appeared adherent to the posterior uterine wall, suggesting a myomatous origin. Despite these detailed findings, the sheer size of the masses and their overlapping characteristics with the uterus rendered ultrasound insufficient for a definitive preoperative diagnosis.

When ultrasound findings are equivocal or when precise anatomical mapping is required for surgical planning, non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) becomes the gold standard adjunctive tool. Unlike CT scans, MRI avoids ionizing radiation, making it safe for fetal evaluation provided specific protocols are followed. Crucially, gadolinium-based contrast agents are generally contraindicated during pregnancy due to potential risks of stillbirth, neonatal death, and rheumatological conditions in the offspring; therefore, diagnostic reliance must rest entirely on native tissue signal characteristics. In this case, the non-contrast MRI utilized T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences to differentiate tissue composition. The imaging demonstrated thick-walled masses with inhomogeneous fluid content and focal hyperintense solid components on both T1 and T2 weighting. These signal intensities helped characterize the internal architecture, confirming the presence of mixed solid-cystic elements consistent with degenerating leiomyomas rather than simple cysts. Furthermore, MRI provided superior spatial resolution regarding the relationship between the caudal mass and the uterus, revealing continuity with a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma—a detail obscured on ultrasound. This clarification was pivotal in shifting the differential diagnosis away from primary ovarian malignancy toward complicated uterine leiomyomas.

Parallel to imaging interpretation, the analysis of serum tumor markers requires extreme caution due to significant physiological alterations during gestation. CA-125, commonly used as a marker for epithelial ovarian cancer in non-pregnant populations with a standard cutoff of 35 U/mL, exhibits dynamic fluctuations throughout pregnancy. Levels typically peak in the first trimester (median ~23 U/mL) and again in the third trimester, while reaching a nadir in the second trimester (median ~14 U/mL). Consequently, an absolute value of 41.8 U/mL in a second-trimester patient represents a notable deviation from the expected physiological baseline, yet it remains within a range frequently observed in benign conditions. Elevated CA-125 in pregnancy is not specific to malignancy; it can be driven by peritoneal irritation from decidualized endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, or even large leiomyomas undergoing degeneration or torsion. In this case report, the slight elevation and increasing trend of CA-125 initially favored a diagnosis of endometrioma or raised concern for oncological potential. However., when viewed in isolation, this biomarker lacked specificity. Its utility lay not in confirming cancer but in contributing to a cumulative risk profile when combined with symptomatic worsening and ambiguous imaging.

Synthesizing multimodal data is essential to navigate these diagnostic ambiguities and guide management decisions. The clinical picture here involved massive abdominal distension causing restrictive lung disease and acute pain alongside imaging suggestive of either giant decidualized endometriomas or degenerating pedunculated leiomyomas. While the CA-125 level was mildly elevated, it did not reach thresholds typically associated with advanced ovarian carcinoma, nor were other markers like CA 19-9 or CEA abnormal. The MRI findings of continuity between the pelvic mass and the uterine wall strongly argued against an independent ovarian primary for at least one lesion. Ultimately, the convergence of imaging morphology (cystic degeneration within a uterine context), biomarker trends (mild elevation explainable by benign degeneration), and clinical severity (pain and dyspnea indicative of torsion or necrosis) pointed toward complicated leiomyomas rather than malignancy. This integrated interpretation justified proceeding with conservative surgical intervention aimed at removing only the symptomatic, twisted pedunculated component while preserving the pregnancy and leaving asymptomatic intramural masses in situ.

## Decision-Making Framework: Conservative Management vs. Surgical Intervention

The management of large abdominal masses during pregnancy requires a nuanced decision-making framework that balances immediate maternal morbidity against significant obstetric risks. In the majority of cases involving uterine leiomyomas or adnexal masses, conservative expectant management is the standard of care. This approach relies on analgesia, rest, and close monitoring, as most masses remain stable or regress postpartum without intervention. However, the threshold for surgical intervention lowers considerably when a mass becomes "complicated." As illustrated in this case, indications for surgery include acute complications such as torsion, rupture, or infection, as well as intractable symptoms that fail to respond to medical therapy. The patient's presentation of severe, localized pain accompanied by restrictive lung disease (dyspnea) due to mechanical compression represented a failure of conservative measures. Furthermore, the imaging findings suggestive of a twisted pedicle and the potential for rapid growth leading to necrosis created an urgent clinical scenario where delaying surgery posed a greater threat to both mother and fetus than the operative procedure itself.

Central to this decision is a rigorous risk-benefit analysis weighing maternal physiological compromise against the dangers of pregnancy loss or preterm delivery. While surgery during pregnancy carries inherent risks—including hemorrhage, spontaneous abortion, preterm labor, and uterine rupture—the consequences of leaving a compromised mass untreated can be catastrophic. In this specific case, the massive size of the lesions threatened further uterine expansion, potentially compromising fetal growth and causing progressive maternal respiratory distress. The slight elevation of CA-125, while nonspecific in pregnancy due to physiological variations and benign conditions like leiomyomas or endometriosis, contributed to the diagnostic uncertainty regarding malignancy. Although tumor markers alone rarely dictate immediate resection in pregnant patients, their trend combined with suspicious imaging features (solid components, thick walls) and clinical deterioration necessitated a definitive histological diagnosis to rule out oncological pathology. The decision to operate was ultimately driven by the convergence of worsening clinical status, mechanical obstruction, and the high probability of acute surgical emergencies like frank necrosis or sepsis if the twisted pedunculated myoma were left in situ.

Executing such complex interventions demands a coordinated multidisciplinary approach involving obstetricians, gynecologic surgeons, anesthesiologists, and neonatologists. This collaboration ensures that every aspect of care addresses both oncological/gynecological safety and fetal preservation. In this case report, the team utilized intraoperative strategies specifically designed to mitigate obstetric risk, including continuous fetal heart rate monitoring via serial ultrasound throughout the procedure and the administration of prophylactic progesterone to suppress uterine contractility. The involvement of infectious disease specialists was also critical given the concurrent *Klebsiella pneumoniae* urinary tract infection, requiring tailored antibiotic prophylaxis that was safe for the fetus yet effective against the identified pathogen. This holistic planning allowed the surgical team to perform a targeted myomectomy of the symptomatic upper mass while deliberately leaving the caudal pelvic mass untouched to minimize uterine trauma and reduce the risk of precipitating labor.

The timing of surgical intervention is another pivotal element of the management algorithm. When surgery is deemed unavoidable during pregnancy, the second trimester (typically between 14 and 24 weeks) is widely regarded as the optimal window for non-emergent procedures. By this stage, organogenesis is complete, reducing the teratogenic risks associated with anesthesia and surgical stress seen in the first trimester. Additionally, the uterus has not yet grown large enough to significantly obscure the surgical field or mechanically interfere with access to abdominal organs, a challenge that becomes pronounced in the third trimester. At 21 weeks gestation, this patient fell squarely within this preferred timeframe. Operating at this juncture allowed for adequate visualization of the pedunculated mass and safe dissection while avoiding the heightened risk of preterm labor associated with late-gestation surgeries. Had the intervention been delayed until term, the enlarging uterus might have exacerbated the compressive symptoms or led to spontaneous torsion during labor; conversely., earlier intervention would have carried higher risks regarding fetal viability and developmental integrity.

## Surgical Technique, Intraoperative Management, and Perioperative Care

The decision to proceed with surgical intervention in a pregnant patient with large abdominal masses requires a careful balance between resolving maternal complications and preserving fetal viability. In this case, the selection of a longitudinal xiphos-pubic laparotomy over a laparoscopic approach was driven by the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus combined with two massive lesions, one extending to the subphrenic space and the other fixed in the pelvis. While laparoscopy is increasingly utilized for myomectomy in early pregnancy or for smaller, accessible masses, it presents significant technical limitations when dealing with giant, vascularized tumors that obscure anatomical landmarks and restrict working space. The sheer size of the upper mass (18 cm) and the deep pelvic location of the lower mass necessitated the broad exposure provided by an open approach to ensure safe dissection, adequate visualization of potential feeding vessels, and rapid control of hemorrhage should it occur. Furthermore, the uncertainty regarding the origin of the masses and the high vascularity noted on preoperative imaging favored an approach that allowed for immediate conversion to more extensive procedures if malignancy were suspected, although the primary goal remained organ preservation.

The operative strategy employed a selective myomectomy tailored to the specific characteristics of each lesion to minimize uterine trauma. Upon exploration, the cranial mass was identified as a pedunculated leiomyoma exhibiting signs of torsion and leakage, which correlated with the patient's acute pain and low-grade fever. Given the risk of necrosis, infection, and impending rupture, excision was mandatory. The surgical team utilized a gentle double-clamp technique on the pedicle before transection, followed by precise closure of the uterine defect with a single layer of absorbable suture to achieve hemostasis while limiting thermal or mechanical injury to the myometrium. Conversely, the caudal mass, which appeared as a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma firmly adherent to the posterior uterine torus, was deliberately left *in situ*. Attempting to enucleate a deeply embedded or broadly attached myoma during pregnancy carries a prohibitive risk of catastrophic hemorrhage and uterine irritability that could precipitate abortion or preterm labor. By removing only the symptomatic, compromised pedunculated mass and leaving the stable intramural component untouched, the surgeons effectively mitigated immediate life-threatening risks while preserving the structural integrity of the uterus necessary for continued gestation.

Protecting the fetus throughout the perioperative period involved a multimodal protocol focusing on continuous monitoring and pharmacological prophylaxis. Recognizing that surgical manipulation of the gravid uterus can trigger contractions or compromise placental perfusion, fetal cardiac activity was verified prior to incision and monitored intermittently via ultrasound throughout the procedure. To counteract potential uterine irritability induced by surgical stress or anesthesia, prophylactic progesterone was administered intraoperatively (100 mg intramuscularly) and continued daily post-discharge, transitioning to long-acting hydroxyprogesterone caproate until term. This regimen aims to maintain uterine quiescence by stabilizing myometrial cell membranes. Additionally, given the patient's pre-existing iron deficiency anemia exacerbated by intraoperative blood loss (with hemoglobin dropping to 7.5 g/dL), aggressive correction was instituted immediately. This included intravenous ferric carboxymaltose and packed red blood cell transfusions to restore oxygen-carrying capacity, ensuring adequate fetal oxygenation during the critical recovery phase.

Postoperative care extended beyond hemodynamic stabilization to include targeted infection control based on microbiological findings. Although intraoperative cultures of peritoneal fluid were negative, the patient had a confirmed urinary tract infection with *Klebsiella pneumoniae* identified preoperatively. Consequently, antibiotic prophylaxis was escalated from standard cefazolin alone to a combination regimen including clarithromycin, guided by antibiogram sensitivity results. This tailored approach prevented ascending infections that could lead to chorioamnionitis or sepsis. The successful integration of these measures—judicious surgical access, selective resection, rigorous fetal monitoring, hormonal support, and optimized medical management—allowed for the resolution of acute maternal symptoms without compromisingthe pregnancy. The patient was discharged ten days post-operatively and maintained strict obstetric surveillance, ultimately delivering a healthy infant via elective cesarean section at 37 weeks, dictated by the presence of the residual pelvic mass which would have obstructed vaginal delivery.

## Postoperative Course, Delivery Planning, and Long-Term Outcomes

The immediate postoperative period following myomectomy in the second trimester requires vigilant monitoring for obstetric complications, specifically preterm labor and wound integrity, alongside the management of systemic sequelae such as anemia. In this case, despite the successful excision of the twisted, necrotic pedunculated leiomyoma, the patient experienced a decline in hemoglobin to 7.5 g/dL, necessitating aggressive correction with intravenous iron and blood transfusions to restore oxygen-carrying capacity for both maternal recovery and fetal growth. To mitigate the risk of uterine irritability induced by surgical manipulation, a regimen of progesterone prophylaxis was initiated intraoperatively and continued throughout the remainder of the pregnancy. This hormonal support aims to maintain uterine quiescence by reducing myometrial contractility, a critical consideration given that surgical trauma can precipitate preterm contractions. Concurrently, strict surveillance of fetal well-being was maintained through serial ultrasounds every three weeks, confirming appropriate fetal growth at the 86th percentile and adequate amniotic fluid volume, while the absence of infectious markers in the peritoneal fluid validated the efficacy of the targeted antibiotic therapy for the concurrent *Klebsiella pneumoniae* infection.

Delivery planning in pregnancies complicated by residual uterine leiomyomas is dictated primarily by the anatomical relationship between the remaining mass and the birth canal. Although conservative management often allows for vaginal delivery, the presence of a large residual intramural-subserosal myoma in the lower uterine segment or pelvis can create a mechanical obstruction known as "soft tissue dystocia." In this patient, the caudal mass was deliberately left *in situ* during the initial surgery to minimize operative time and hemorrhage risk; however, its fixed position in the lesser pelvis posed a significant barrier to fetal descent during the expulsive phase of labor. Consequently, an elective cesarean section was scheduled at 37 weeks of gestation. Performing the delivery via the previous laparotomy scar allowed for simultaneous access while minimizing additional uterine trauma. This decision underscores a fundamental principle in managing complex fibroids: when a residual mass obstructs the pelvic inlet or lower uterine segment, cesarean delivery is indicated to prevent prolonged labor, uterine rupture, or fetal distress, regardless of the benign nature of the tumor.

Understanding the natural history of leiomyomas during late pregnancy and the puerperium is essential for counseling patients regarding long-term management and the timing of definitive surgery. Uterine fibroids exhibit a non-linear growth pattern characterized by expansion during the first and second trimesters driven by elevated estrogen and progesterone levels, followed by a plateau or reduction in size during the third trimester. More notably, a significant involution occurs postpartum; studies indicate that myoma volume can decrease by approximately 44% within six weeks after delivery due to the abrupt withdrawal of pregnancy hormones and ischemic changes within the tumor. Recognizing this physiological regression prevents unnecessary immediate intervention. In this case, although the residual pelvic myoma remained sizable at 7.5 cm postpartum, it was asymptomatic and had already begun to shrink. Therefore, definitive surgical removal was deferred. This "watchful waiting" approach avoids subjecting the patient to redundant procedures while she is recovering from childbirth and establishes lactation. Future management plans focus on symptom monitoring and re-evaluation after weaning; if symptoms recur or if fertility desires persist with documented distortion of the uterine cavity, a delayed myomectomy can be planned once hormonal influences have fully normalized and postpartum involution is complete.
