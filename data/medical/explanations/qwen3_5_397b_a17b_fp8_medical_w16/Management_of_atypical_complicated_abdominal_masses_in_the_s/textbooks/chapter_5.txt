Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 5: ## Postoperative Course, Delivery Planning, and Long-Term Outcomes

The immediate postoperative period following myomectomy in the second trimester requires vigilant monitoring for obstetric complications, specifically preterm labor and wound integrity, alongside the management of systemic sequelae such as anemia. In this case, despite the successful excision of the twisted, necrotic pedunculated leiomyoma, the patient experienced a decline in hemoglobin to 7.5 g/dL, necessitating aggressive correction with intravenous iron and blood transfusions to restore oxygen-carrying capacity for both maternal recovery and fetal growth. To mitigate the risk of uterine irritability induced by surgical manipulation, a regimen of progesterone prophylaxis was initiated intraoperatively and continued throughout the remainder of the pregnancy. This hormonal support aims to maintain uterine quiescence by reducing myometrial contractility, a critical consideration given that surgical trauma can precipitate preterm contractions. Concurrently, strict surveillance of fetal well-being was maintained through serial ultrasounds every three weeks, confirming appropriate fetal growth at the 86th percentile and adequate amniotic fluid volume, while the absence of infectious markers in the peritoneal fluid validated the efficacy of the targeted antibiotic therapy for the concurrent *Klebsiella pneumoniae* infection.

Delivery planning in pregnancies complicated by residual uterine leiomyomas is dictated primarily by the anatomical relationship between the remaining mass and the birth canal. Although conservative management often allows for vaginal delivery, the presence of a large residual intramural-subserosal myoma in the lower uterine segment or pelvis can create a mechanical obstruction known as "soft tissue dystocia." In this patient, the caudal mass was deliberately left *in situ* during the initial surgery to minimize operative time and hemorrhage risk; however, its fixed position in the lesser pelvis posed a significant barrier to fetal descent during the expulsive phase of labor. Consequently, an elective cesarean section was scheduled at 37 weeks of gestation. Performing the delivery via the previous laparotomy scar allowed for simultaneous access while minimizing additional uterine trauma. This decision underscores a fundamental principle in managing complex fibroids: when a residual mass obstructs the pelvic inlet or lower uterine segment, cesarean delivery is indicated to prevent prolonged labor, uterine rupture, or fetal distress, regardless of the benign nature of the tumor.

Understanding the natural history of leiomyomas during late pregnancy and the puerperium is essential for counseling patients regarding long-term management and the timing of definitive surgery. Uterine fibroids exhibit a non-linear growth pattern characterized by expansion during the first and second trimesters driven by elevated estrogen and progesterone levels, followed by a plateau or reduction in size during the third trimester. More notably, a significant involution occurs postpartum; studies indicate that myoma volume can decrease by approximately 44% within six weeks after delivery due to the abrupt withdrawal of pregnancy hormones and ischemic changes within the tumor. Recognizing this physiological regression prevents unnecessary immediate intervention. In this case, although the residual pelvic myoma remained sizable at 7.5 cm postpartum, it was asymptomatic and had already begun to shrink. Therefore, definitive surgical removal was deferred. This "watchful waiting" approach avoids subjecting the patient to redundant procedures while she is recovering from childbirth and establishes lactation. Future management plans focus on symptom monitoring and re-evaluation after weaning; if symptoms recur or if fertility desires persist with documented distortion of the uterine cavity, a delayed myomectomy can be planned once hormonal influences have fully normalized and postpartum involution is complete.
