Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 3: ## Decision-Making Framework: Conservative Management vs. Surgical Intervention

The management of large abdominal masses during pregnancy requires a nuanced decision-making framework that balances immediate maternal morbidity against significant obstetric risks. In the majority of cases involving uterine leiomyomas or adnexal masses, conservative expectant management is the standard of care. This approach relies on analgesia, rest, and close monitoring, as most masses remain stable or regress postpartum without intervention. However, the threshold for surgical intervention lowers considerably when a mass becomes "complicated." As illustrated in this case, indications for surgery include acute complications such as torsion, rupture, or infection, as well as intractable symptoms that fail to respond to medical therapy. The patient's presentation of severe, localized pain accompanied by restrictive lung disease (dyspnea) due to mechanical compression represented a failure of conservative measures. Furthermore, the imaging findings suggestive of a twisted pedicle and the potential for rapid growth leading to necrosis created an urgent clinical scenario where delaying surgery posed a greater threat to both mother and fetus than the operative procedure itself.

Central to this decision is a rigorous risk-benefit analysis weighing maternal physiological compromise against the dangers of pregnancy loss or preterm delivery. While surgery during pregnancy carries inherent risks—including hemorrhage, spontaneous abortion, preterm labor, and uterine rupture—the consequences of leaving a compromised mass untreated can be catastrophic. In this specific case, the massive size of the lesions threatened further uterine expansion, potentially compromising fetal growth and causing progressive maternal respiratory distress. The slight elevation of CA-125, while nonspecific in pregnancy due to physiological variations and benign conditions like leiomyomas or endometriosis, contributed to the diagnostic uncertainty regarding malignancy. Although tumor markers alone rarely dictate immediate resection in pregnant patients, their trend combined with suspicious imaging features (solid components, thick walls) and clinical deterioration necessitated a definitive histological diagnosis to rule out oncological pathology. The decision to operate was ultimately driven by the convergence of worsening clinical status, mechanical obstruction, and the high probability of acute surgical emergencies like frank necrosis or sepsis if the twisted pedunculated myoma were left in situ.

Executing such complex interventions demands a coordinated multidisciplinary approach involving obstetricians, gynecologic surgeons, anesthesiologists, and neonatologists. This collaboration ensures that every aspect of care addresses both oncological/gynecological safety and fetal preservation. In this case report, the team utilized intraoperative strategies specifically designed to mitigate obstetric risk, including continuous fetal heart rate monitoring via serial ultrasound throughout the procedure and the administration of prophylactic progesterone to suppress uterine contractility. The involvement of infectious disease specialists was also critical given the concurrent *Klebsiella pneumoniae* urinary tract infection, requiring tailored antibiotic prophylaxis that was safe for the fetus yet effective against the identified pathogen. This holistic planning allowed the surgical team to perform a targeted myomectomy of the symptomatic upper mass while deliberately leaving the caudal pelvic mass untouched to minimize uterine trauma and reduce the risk of precipitating labor.

The timing of surgical intervention is another pivotal element of the management algorithm. When surgery is deemed unavoidable during pregnancy, the second trimester (typically between 14 and 24 weeks) is widely regarded as the optimal window for non-emergent procedures. By this stage, organogenesis is complete, reducing the teratogenic risks associated with anesthesia and surgical stress seen in the first trimester. Additionally, the uterus has not yet grown large enough to significantly obscure the surgical field or mechanically interfere with access to abdominal organs, a challenge that becomes pronounced in the third trimester. At 21 weeks gestation, this patient fell squarely within this preferred timeframe. Operating at this juncture allowed for adequate visualization of the pedunculated mass and safe dissection while avoiding the heightened risk of preterm labor associated with late-gestation surgeries. Had the intervention been delayed until term, the enlarging uterus might have exacerbated the compressive symptoms or led to spontaneous torsion during labor; conversely., earlier intervention would have carried higher risks regarding fetal viability and developmental integrity.
