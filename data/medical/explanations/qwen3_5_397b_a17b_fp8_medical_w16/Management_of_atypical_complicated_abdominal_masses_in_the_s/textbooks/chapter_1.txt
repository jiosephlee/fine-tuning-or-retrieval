Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment of Abdominal Masses in Pregnancy

The clinical presentation of large abdominal masses during the second trimester often mimics acute obstetric emergencies, necessitating a high index of suspicion to differentiate between uterine pathology, adnexal disease, and standard pregnancy complications. In this case, a nulliparous woman in her 40s presented at 21 weeks gestation with severe, acute abdominal pain localized to the left upper quadrant and pelvis, accompanied by mild dyspnea. While abdominal pain is a common complaint in pregnancy, the severity and specific localization described here—distinct from the diffuse discomfort of round ligament strain or the rhythmic cramping of preterm labor—should prompt an evaluation for space-occupying lesions. The accompanying dyspnea is a critical clue; in the absence of primary cardiopulmonary disease, as evidenced by the patient's normal oxygen saturation (97%), heart rate, and electrocardiogram, respiratory compromise is likely mechanical. Massive abdominal distension can elevate the diaphragm, reducing functional residual capacity and causing restrictive lung symptoms. Recognizing this "mass effect" is vital, as it distinguishes the pathology from conditions like pulmonary embolism or pneumonia, directing the clinician toward abdominal imaging rather than solely chest-focused diagnostics.

Physical examination in a gravid patient presents unique challenges due to the enlarging uterus obscuring intra-abdominal structures. In this scenario, inspection revealed an abdomen disproportionately enlarged for the gestational age, with a palpable, taut-elastic mass occupying the entire left upper quadrant. While superficial palpation identifies large suprapubic or subphrenic masses, assessing pelvic pathology requires a bimanual vaginal examination. Despite the limitations imposed by the gravid uterus, this maneuver successfully identified a fixed, hard, rounded mass approximately 9 cm in diameter within the lesser pelvis, bulging into the posterior fornix. This finding underscores the necessity of combining abdominal and pelvic examinations; relying on abdominal palpation alone might have missed the distinct caudal component or misidentified it as part of the uterine fundus. The ability to characterize the consistency (hard vs. cystic) and mobility (fixed vs. mobile) of these masses provides immediate diagnostic data regarding their potential origin—whether myomatous, ovarian, or gastrointestinal.

Initial stabilization focuses on symptom management and addressing concurrent physiological stressors while avoiding interventions that could harm the fetus. In this case, conservative measures proved partially effective: placing the patient in a semi-orthostatic position helped alleviate diaphragmatic compression, reducing pain scores and improving respiratory comfort without requiring supplemental oxygen. Analgesia was achieved safely with intravenous paracetamol. However, the clinical picture was complicated by a low-grade fever and laboratory evidence of inflammation. It is crucial to distinguish between fever caused by tumor necrosis (common in rapidly growing or twisting leiomyomas) and infectious etiologies. Here, workup excluded viral respiratory pathogens but confirmed a *Klebsiella pneumoniae* urinary tract infection (UTI). The presence of a UTI in a patient with large pelvic masses requires aggressive management, as infection can precipitate uterine irritability and preterm contractions. Consequently, appropriate antibiotic therapy targeting the identified pathogen became an integral part of the initial stabilization protocol alongside pain control.

Interpreting baseline laboratory results in pregnancy requires adjusting for physiological norms to avoid misdiagnosis. This patient presented with iron deficiency anemia (hemoglobin 9.9 g/L) and leukocytosis (11.29 × 10³/µL) with neutrophilia. While hemodilution naturally lowers hemoglobin levels during pregnancy, a value below 11 g/L warrants investigation for underlying deficits or chronic blood loss, which may be exacerbated by large vascularized tumors. However, the elevation of C-reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL) above the normal non-pregnant range suggests an active inflammatory process beyond normal gestational changes. When combined with the slightly elevated CA-125 (41.8 U/mL), these labs create a complex diagnostic puzzle: while CA-125 can rise physiologically in pregnancy or due to benign conditions like endometriosis and leiomyomas, its upward trend alongside inflammatory markers necessitates ruling out malignancy or acute complications such as torsion or infarction. This nuanced interpretation guides the decision to proceed with advanced imaging rather than adopting a purely expectant approach.
