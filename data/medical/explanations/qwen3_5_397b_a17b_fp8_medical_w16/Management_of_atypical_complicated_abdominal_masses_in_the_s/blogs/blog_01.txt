Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# The Great Mimic: When Pedunculated Leiomyomas Masquerade as Ovarian Malignancy in Pregnancy

We've all been there: a pregnant patient rolls into the ED or OB triage with acute abdominal pain, and the ultrasound screen lights up with a terrifyingly large, complex mass. Your mind immediately jumps to the worst-case scenario—ovarian torsion, ruptured ectopic (though less likely in the second trimester), or worse, an occult malignancy threatening the pregnancy. The stakes feel impossibly high because you aren't just managing a patient; you're managing a dyad.

But what if that "ovarian" nightmare is actually wearing a disguise?

A fascinating case report by De Nola et al. throws a wrench in our diagnostic algorithms, reminding us that in the distorted anatomy of a gravid uterus, even experienced eyes can be fooled. They describe a woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, presenting with severe left upper quadrant pain, dyspnea, and two massive abdominal lesions. On imaging, these looked like giant decidualized endometriomas or perhaps malignant ovarian cysts. There was vascularization, cystic degeneration, and even a mildly elevated CA-125. The team prepared for a potential oncologic disaster.

The twist? When they opened her up, the "upper ovarian mass" wasn't ovarian at all. It was a gigantic, pedunculated uterine leiomyoma that had twisted, leaked, and migrated so far upward it occupied the entire left subphrenic space. The "lower mass" was another myoma adherent to the posterior uterus. This case is a masterclass in why we need to keep our differential diagnosis wide and our interpretation of tumor markers nuanced when dealing with the unique physiology of pregnancy.

## The Diagnostic Fog: Anatomy and Artifacts

The primary challenge in this case—and many like it—is the sheer mechanical distortion caused by the gravid uterus. By the second trimester, the uterus has risen out of the pelvis, pushing bowel loops aside and stretching ligaments. In this patient, a pedunculated fibroid grew on a long stalk, allowing it to ascend well above the uterine fundus into the subphrenic space.

On ultrasound, this created a perfect storm of ambiguity. The mass was unilocular but cystic with peripheral vascularization—a classic look for a decidualized endometrioma or an ovarian neoplasm. Because of the "steric bulk" of the pregnancy, the sonographers couldn't identify a connective stalk linking the mass back to the uterus. Without that visual tether, the default assumption shifts toward an adnexal origin.

This is where MRI becomes your best friend, provided you know its limits. The team utilized non-contrast MRI (gadolinium is generally avoided unless absolutely necessary due to fetal safety concerns). While MRI offered superior soft-tissue resolution showing thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid content consistent with degeneration, it still couldn't definitively rule out an adnexal origin for the cranial mass pre-operatively. The lesson here is that even our most advanced imaging modalities can hit a wall when anatomy is this rearranged. If you can't see the stalk, don't assume it doesn't exist; assume it's just hidden behind 21 weeks of fetal growth.

## The CA-125 Trap: Don't Panic Over Mild Elevations

One of the most anxiety-inducing findings in this case was the CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL. In a non-pregnant post-menopausal woman, this might trigger a full-blown gyn-onc workup. In pregnancy, however, context is everything.

CA-125 is notoriously nonspecific during gestation. Levels physiologically peak in the first trimester (median ~23 U/mL) and again in the third trimester (~21 U/mL), dipping slightly in the second trimester (median ~14 U/mL). However, benign conditions rampant in pregnancy—such as decidualization of peritoneal surfaces, leiomyomas undergoing red degeneration, or even simple mechanical irritation from a twisting mass—can push these numbers above the standard cutoff of 35 U/mL.

In this case series, the slight elevation and upward trend were alarming enough to fuel fears of malignancy. Yet, pathology confirmed benign leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis. Relying on an isolated or mildly trending CA-125 to dictate aggressive surgical intervention in a stable pregnant patient is a dangerous game. Always interpret tumor markers against the backdrop of gestational age and benign inflammatory processes inherent to pregnancy.

## To Cut or Not to Cut: Navigating Surgical Decision Making

So, when do you take them to the OR? Conservative management is usually the gold standard for fibroids in pregnancy because surgery carries risks of hemorrhage, preterm labor, and loss of pregnancy. But this patient crossed several red lines that forced the team's hand:
*   **Worsening Symptoms:** Severe pain refractory to analgesics and restrictive lung disease (dyspnea) due to diaphragmatic compression by the massive subphrenic mass.
*   **Complication Risk:** Evidence suggesting torsion (acute pain onset), leakage/necrosis (fever, localized tenderness), and potential rupture.
*   **Obstructive Potential:** The lower pelvic mass was positioned to block vaginal delivery later in gestation.
