Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# The Partial Solution: Strategic Intraoperative Decision-Making to Preserve Pregnancy

We are taught in surgery that when you find a problem, you fix it. If there's a twisted appendix, out it comes. If there's a gangrenous bowel segment, you resect. But what happens when the "problem" is a massive, symptomatic uterine leiomyoma in a 21-week pregnancy, and fixing *everything* might cost you the pregnancy?

This is the dilemma faced by the team managing a nulliparous woman in her 40s who presented with severe abdominal pain, dyspnea, and two gigantic abdominal masses. One mass was an 18 cm monster occupying the left subphrenic space; the other was a 9 cm fixed lesion in the pouch of Douglas. Initial imaging painted a confusing picture: were these decidualized endometriomas? Ovarian malignancies? The CA-125 was slightly elevated (a notorious red herring in pregnancy), and the patient was deteriorating with pain and restrictive lung disease.

They went to the OR expecting a diagnostic exploration that might turn into a full-blown oncologic debulking or a tragic loss. What they found instead was a masterclass in surgical restraint. The cranial mass turned out to be a pedunculated, twisted, and leaking leiomyoma—the source of her acute agony. The caudal mass, however, was deeply embedded in the posterior uterine torus. Here lies the critical teaching point: the surgeons excised the twisted cranial myoma to resolve the acute crisis but deliberately left the large caudal myoma *in situ*. They traded a future potential obstruction for immediate maternal stabilization and fetal survival. It wasn't an incomplete surgery; it was the *correct* surgery.

## The Diagnostic Fog: When Fibroids Mimic Malignancy

Before we get to the scalpel work, we have to appreciate how tricky these cases are to diagnose. In this case, an obese patient in her second trimester presented with pain and dyspnea. The physical exam revealed an abdomen larger than dates and a tender mass. Ultrasound showed two large cystic structures with peripheral vascularization. To make matters more confusing, her CA-125 was trending upward at 41.8 U/mL.

In a non-pregnant patient over 40, that marker combined with complex adnexal masses screams ovarian cancer until proven otherwise. But pregnancy changes the rules of engagement. CA-125 physiologically rises in the first and third trimesters, and benign conditions like leiomyomas, endometriosis, and even normal placentation can push levels above the standard cutoff of 35 U/mL. Relying on this number alone could have led to overly aggressive management or unnecessary panic.

The real hero here was non-contrast MRI. Gadolinium is generally avoided in pregnancy due to unknown fetal risks, but T1 and T2 weighted sequences provided enough detail to see thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid content without confirming frank malignancy. The imaging suggested decidualized endometriomas or cystic degenerating fibroids. Crucially, no adenopathy was seen. This nuance allowed the team to plan for a myomectomy rather than a radical staging procedure, keeping the goalposts firmly set on pregnancy preservation.

## The Art of Leaving Things Behind

Once inside via a longitudinal laparotomy (chosen due to the sheer size of the masses and gestational age), reality set in quickly. The cranial mass was indeed a pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion and ischemic necrosis—explaining the fever, leukocytosis, and acute pain spectrum. This was the ticking time bomb that needed defusing immediately.

The caudal mass told a different story. It arose from the uterine torus, appeared fixed, and lacked a clear cleavage plane separate from the myometrium attempting to house a growing fetus at 21 weeks dissecting around this posterior intramural-subserosal hybrid would have been akin to walking through a minefield blindfolded. The risk of catastrophic hemorrhage triggering uterine irritability, preterm labor, or outright fetal loss was unacceptably high.
