Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Planning the Exit Strategy: How Antepartum Myomectomy Dictates Mode of Delivery

We often talk about the "exit strategy" in trauma or critical care—how do we get the patient out of the ICU, off the vent, or home safely? But in obstetrics, specifically when managing complex adnexal or uterine pathology during pregnancy, the exit strategy needs to be defined long before the baby arrives. It starts at the moment you make the decision to operate antepartum.

A fascinating case report by De Nola et al. highlights a scenario that keeps many OB/GYNs and emergency physicians up at night: a pregnant patient in her 40s presenting with acute abdominal pain and dyspnea due to massive abdominal masses. What makes this case a perfect teaching tool isn't just the diagnostic dilemma (endometrioma vs. degenerating fibroid), but how the surgical management of one mass fundamentally dictated the mode of delivery for the other.

The patient presented at 21 weeks with two giants: an 18 cm upper mass causing diaphragmatic compression and a 9 cm lower pelvic mass. While initial imaging flirted with the diagnosis of decidualized endometriomas, surgery revealed the truth: these were atypical, cystic leiomyomas. The team performed a delicate balancing act, excising the twisted, necrotic pedunculated upper fibroid to relieve symptoms and save the pregnancy, but wisely leaving the lower, fixed posterior mass *in situ* to avoid excessive uterine manipulation and hemorrhage.

Here is where the clinical pearl shines: **The decision to leave a large posterior myoma behind automatically rewrote the birth plan.** That residual 9 cm mass sitting in the pouch of Douglas wasn't just a bystander; it was a guaranteed obstruction to vaginal delivery. Consequently, the team planned an elective Cesarean section at 37 weeks, utilizing the previous laparotomy scar. This wasn't a reactive decision made in labor; it was a proactive stratification of risk made months prior. The case underscores that antepartum myomectomy is rarely an isolated event; it is the first chapter of a modified delivery narrative.

### Navigating Diagnostic Uncertainty in Pregnancy

Diagnosing abdominal masses in pregnancy is notoriously tricky. Physiologic changes can mimic pathology, and standard markers lose their specificity. In this case, the patient's CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL. In a non-pregnant post-menopausal woman, this raises red flags for malignancy. In pregnancy? It's noise. CA-125 physiologically peaks in the first and third trimesters and can be elevated in benign conditions like endometriosis, PID, and even uncomplicated pregnancies. Relying on a single value here could have led to overtreatment or unnecessary panic.

Instead, the team leaned on high-quality imaging. Ultrasound gave them the initial map, but MRI (without gadolinium contrast) provided the crucial tissue characterization needed to differentiate between a malignant process and a degenerating fibroid. The MRI confirmed thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid content consistent with cystic degeneration rather than frank malignancy. When you are staring down a potential laparotomy at 21 weeks, knowing you aren't cutting into an ovarian carcinoma changes everything—from incision planning to oncologic staging precautions.

### The Surgical Tightrope: To Cut or Not to Cut?

The dogma has long been "leave fibroids alone during pregnancy." And generally, that holds true. The risks of hemorrhage, miscarriage, and preterm labor are real. However, "expectant management" fails when complications arise. This patient wasn't just uncomfortable; she was developing restrictive lung disease from the sheer bulk of the tumors and had signs of torsion and necrosis (fever, leukocytosis, worsening pain).

When conservative measures failed, surgery became mandatory. The operative nuance here was selective resection. Removing *both* masses would have turned a controlled procedure into a chaotic battle against bleeding and uterine irritability. By removing only the symptomatic, twisted pedunculated mass and leaving the broad-based posterior intramural-subserosal fibroid untouched, they achieved symptom relief while minimizing obstetric risk. This "damage control" approach allowed for fetal survival and maternal recovery, proving that sometimes doing less surgically yields more clinically.

### Counseling Beyond Discharge: Regression vs. Resolution

One of the most reassuring aspects of this case for patient counseling is the natural history of fibroids postpartum. Patients often fear that leaving a fibroid means signing up for immediate future surgery once they recover from childbirth and start sleep-deprived parenting. However, leiomyomas follow a non-linear trajectory during and after pregnancy. They tend to grow rapidly in the first trimester but often undergo significant involution postpartum as hormone levels plummet and lactation suppresses estrogen production Studies suggest reductions in size averaging over 40% within six weeks of delivery.

In this case, follow-up showed exactly that: spontaneous regression of both masses without further intervention! This allows us to counsel patients effectively: unless there are persistent symptoms or obstructive issues (like our residual pelvic mass preventing vaginal birth), we can often afford to wait months—or even years—before considering definitive surgical management again let nature do some work for us first!

### Key Take-Home Messages

*   **Don't Trust CA-125 Blindly:** Isolated elevations are common in pregnancy due to physiologic peaks or benign inflammation; rely on serial trends combined with advanced imaging (non-contrast MRI) rather than tumor markers alone for decision-making regarding malignancy!
*   **Surgery Alters Birth Mechanics:** Leaving behind large cervical or low-segment/posterior myomas almost invariably necessitates Cesarean delivery due to dystocia risk! Plan your exit strategy immediately following antepartum interventions!
*   **Selective Resection Saves Pregnancies:** In cases involving multiple giant myomas consider staged approaches! Remove only complicated units (torsion/necrosis) while leaving stable ones intact minimize iatrogenic preterm labor triggers!
*   **Postpartum Involution Is Real:** Counsel patients expecting rapid resolution fears unnecessarily since significant shrinkage occurs naturally during puerperium delaying secondary surgeries until full hormonal recovery completes itself optimizes outcomes overall
