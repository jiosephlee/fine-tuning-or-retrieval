Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# To Cut or To Wait? Navigating the Threshold for Myomectomy in the Second Trimester

We teach our residents that the mantra for managing uterine leiomyomas in pregnancy is simple: *primum non nocere*. Watchful waiting is the gold standard. We tolerate pain, we manage nausea, and we accept that fibroids often grow rapidly in the first half of gestation only to regress postpartum. The risks of surgery—hemorrhage, spontaneous abortion, preterm labor, and uterine rupture—are well-documented and generally outweigh the benefits of removing a benign mass. But what happens when "benign" starts behaving like a catastrophe? When does the risk of leaving a mass in situ surpass the terror of opening a gravid uterus at 21 weeks?

A recent case report by De Nola et al. forces us to confront this exact dilemma. They describe a nulliparous woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, who rolled into the ED with severe left upper quadrant and pelvic pain, accompanied by dyspnea. On exam, she wasn't just uncomfortable; she was compromised. Her abdomen was distended beyond dates, harboring an 18 cm taut-elastic mass pushing up against her diaphragm and a separate 9 cm fixed pelvic mass. Initial imaging painted a confusing picture: two large cystic structures that looked suspiciously for decidualized endometriomas or perhaps something malignant, fueled by a rising CA-125 level (41.8 U/mL). The team did everything right diagnostically—they leveraged ultrasound and non-contrast MRI to map the anatomy while avoiding gadolinium—but the clinical trajectory was heading off a cliff. Despite analgesia and positioning, the patient developed fever, leukocytosis, and worsening restrictive lung disease. The decision was made to cut.

The intraoperative findings were a classic "gotcha" moment that underscores why imaging alone isn't enough. That massive 18 cm "cyst" in the left subphrenic space wasn't an ovarian neoplasm at all; it was a giant, pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion and was actively leaking/necrotic. This explained the fever, the acute pain, and the systemic inflammatory response. The surgical team performed a myomectomy on the twisted pedunculated mass but wisely left the second, deeply adherent pelvic myoma untouched to minimize uterine irritation. The result? A preserved pregnancy, resolution of the septic physiology, and an eventual elective C-section at 37 weeks with a healthy neonate. This case serves as a stark reminder that while conservatism is our default, there is a distinct threshold where surgical intervention becomes the only life-saving option for both mother and fetus.

So how do we identify that threshold before the patient decompensates? In this case, several red flags screamed for intervention despite the gestational age. First was the mechanical compromise: the sheer size of the masses caused restrictive lung disease, evident by her dyspnea and inability to lie flat without support. Second was the evidence of acute complications—specifically torsion and necrosis. The combination of localized tenderness, fever, and rising inflammatory markers (CRP and WBC) in the setting of a known large fibroid should trigger immediate concern for red degeneration progressing to frank necrosis or torsion. Finally, there was the diagnostic uncertainty coupled with oncological anxiety. While CA-125 can physiologically rise in pregnancy, an upward trend alongside complex solid-cystic features on MRI kept malignancy on the differential until proven otherwise. When you combine intractable symptoms, organ compression, suspected torsion/necrosis, and unresolved malignancy concerns, the scale tips decisively toward surgery.

The technical execution in this case also offers vital lessons for the obstetric surgeon. The team opted for a laparotomy rather than laparoscopy—a prudent choice given the massive size of the tumor (18 cm), its high location impinging on the diaphragm, and the need for rapid control of potential hemorrhage from a twisted vascular pedicle. Crucially, they didn't go in blind regarding fetal safety; continuous monitoring of fetal heart tones was maintained throughout the procedure via serial ultrasound checks. Pharmacologic protection was equally aggressive, utilizing intraoperative and postoperative progesterone supplementation (both IM progesterone and hydroxyprogesterone caproate) to suppress uterine irritability. By limiting resection to only the offending pedunculated mass and leaving behind quiescent intramural pathology ("less is more"), they balanced oncologic/surgical clearance with obstetric preservation perfectly.

Ultimately, this case reinforces that management algorithms are guides, not shackles. ### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Surgical Indications Are Rare but Critical:** While expectant management is standard for fibroids in pregnancy, absolute indications for myomectomy include pedunculated fibroid torsion with necrosis/infection (red degeneration refractory to meds), severe mass effect causing organ compromise (e.g., restrictive lung disease), or strong suspicion of malignancy that cannot be resolved conservatively
