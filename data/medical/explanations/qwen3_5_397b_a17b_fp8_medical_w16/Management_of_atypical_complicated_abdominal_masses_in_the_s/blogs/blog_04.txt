Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Mass Effect Mechanics: Recognizing Non-Obstetric Causes of Dyspnea and Abdominal Pain in Late Second Trimester

We often operate on autopilot when a pregnant patient rolls into the ED complaining of abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Our minds immediately jump to the "must-not-miss" obstetric catastrophes: placental abruption, uterine rupture, preterm labor, or perhaps a pulmonary embolism. And rightfully so—these are the killers. But what happens when the vitals are rock solid, the fetal heart trace is reassuring, and yet the patient is in genuine distress with symptoms that feel disproportionately severe for their gestational age?

It's easy to dismiss dyspnea in the second trimester as normal physiological adaptation or attribute vague abdominal discomfort to round ligament pain. However, a recent case report by De Nola et al. serves as a stark reminder that mechanics matter. Sometimes, the problem isn't a clot or a contraction; it's simple, brute-force mass effect. They describe a nulliparous woman in her 40s at 21 weeks gestation presenting with acute左上 quadrant pain and dyspnea. She wasn't hypoxic, her BP was stable, and her ECG was clean. Yet, her abdomen was distended far beyond dates, harboring two massive lesions that were literally pushing her diaphragm up and compressing her lungs. The culprit? Not an ovarian malignancy or an ectopic, but giant, atypical uterine leiomyomas masquerading as adnexal cysts.

The diagnostic journey here highlights a classic trap: assuming complex cystic masses in pregnancy are ovarian in origin until proven otherwise. Initial ultrasound painted a picture of two large unilocular cysts—one towering in the subphrenic space and another lodged deep in the pouch of Douglas. With a mildly elevated CA-125 (41.8 U/mL), the specter of decidualized endometriomas or even malignancy loomed large. But context is king. While CA-125 can physiologically rise in pregnancy, the imaging nuances told a different story. MRI without contrast (the gold standard here to avoid gadolinium risks) revealed thick walls and solid components continuous with the uterine wall. The "cysts" were actually degenerating fibroids. The upper mass was a pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion and ischemic necrosis, explaining the fever and acute pain, while the lower mass was a fixed intramural-subserosal monster obstructing the pelvis.

This case forces us to reconsider our threshold for intervention. The dogma has long been "leave fibroids alone during pregnancy" due to fears of hemorrhage and pregnancy loss. And generally, that's sound advice. But when a mass creates a restrictive lung disease pattern via diaphragmatic splinting, causes intractable pain from torsion/necrosis, or threatens to obstruct labor entirely, conservatism becomes dangerous. The team made the bold call to perform a laparotomy at 21 weeks. They excised the twisted, necrotic upper fibroid—the source of the acute crisis—and wisely left the lower pelvic mass untouched to minimize uterine irritation. The result? Relief of symptoms, preservation of the pregnancy, and a planned C-section at term with a healthy baby. It's a masterclass in balancing oncologic/surgical urgency against obstetric fragility.

The takeaway isn't that we need to start cutting out fibroids in every pregnant patient with belly pain. Rather, it's about maintaining a high index of suspicion for mechanical pathology when the clinical picture doesn't fit the usual obstetric scripts. If your patient looks too big for dates, has localized tenderness that doesn't migrate, or has dyspnea that seems out of proportion to their BMI and gestational week, get your hands dirty with a thorough bimanual exam and don't settle for "physiologic" as an explanation without ruling out space-occupying lesions first.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Don't Anchor on Obstetrics:** In pregnant patients with stable vitals but severe abdominal pain and dyspnea, consider mechanical compression from large uterine or extra-uterine masses if common etiologies (PE, abruption) are ruled out.
*   **Imaging Nuances Matter:** Ultrasound is the first line, but non-contrast MRI is superior for distinguishing between ovarian masses and degenerating/subserosal leiomyomas, specifically looking for continuity with the uterine wall.
*   **CA-125 Interpretation:** Mild elevations in CA-125 are common in pregnancy (peaking in 1st and 3rd trimesters) and can occur with benign conditions like fibroids and endometriosis; do not let a borderline value drive you toward radical surgery without corroborating imaging.
*   **Surgical Indications:** While myomectomy is generally avoided in pregnancy due to bleeding/preterm labor risks, it is indicated for complications such as torsion of a pedunculated fibroid, irreversible necrosis causing sepsis/pain, or severe organ compression (e.g., restrictive lung disease).
*   **Selective Resection:** When surgery is unavoidable, aim for damage control—remove only the symptomatic/complicated mass (like the torsed pedunculated fibroid) and leave stable intramural masses *in situ* to reduce operative time and uterine trauma.
