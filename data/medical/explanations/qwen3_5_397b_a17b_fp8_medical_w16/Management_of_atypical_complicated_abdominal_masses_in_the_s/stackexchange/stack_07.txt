Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] When encountering multiple large uterine masses intraoperatively where one is pedunculated and twisting while another is broad-based and intramural, what strategic decision-making process guides the surgeon to excise one mass while leaving the other in situ?

The decision to excise the pedunculated mass while leaving the broad-based intramural lesion in situ is guided by a risk-benefit analysis prioritizing immediate maternal stabilization against the profound obstetric risks of extensive uterine manipulation. In this case, the cranial mass was identified intraoperatively as a pedunculated leiomyoma that was actively twisted, ischemic, and leaking. This configuration represents an acute surgical emergency; the torsion compromises vascular supply, leading to necrosis and potential infection or rupture, which directly threatens both maternal stability and fetal viability. Consequently, removal of this specific mass via clamping and suturing the stalk was mandatory to resolve the source of acute pain, fever, and hemodynamic stress.

Conversely, the caudal mass was a broad-based, intramural-subserosal leiomyoma firmly integrated into the uterine torus. Attempting to enucleate such a deeply embedded mass during pregnancy carries a prohibitive risk of catastrophic hemorrhage due to the heightened vascularity of the gravid uterus and the difficulty in achieving hemostasis within the myometrial bed. Furthermore, extensive dissection increases the likelihood of triggering preterm labor or compromising uterine integrity, potentially leading to rupture in future pregnancies. As noted in the case report, leaving this asymptomatic (regarding acute torsion) but bulky mass in situ allowed for the preservation of the pregnancy with close monitoring. The strategic compromise accepted the known future need for an elective cesarean delivery—required because the residual pelvic mass would obstruct labor—in exchange for avoiding immediate life-threatening surgical complications and ensuring fetal survival to term. This approach underscores the principle that during pregnancy, myomectomy should be restricted to treating acute complications like torsion, while stable, albeit large, intramural fibroids are managed conservatively until delivery.
