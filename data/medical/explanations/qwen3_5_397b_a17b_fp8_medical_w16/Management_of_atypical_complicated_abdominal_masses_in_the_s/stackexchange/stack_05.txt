Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] In a case of a symptomatic, large pedunculated leiomyoma causing acute pain and respiratory compromise at 21 weeks gestation, what are the primary indications for proceeding with surgical myomectomy during pregnancy rather than adopting an expectant management approach?

Surgical myomectomy during pregnancy is reserved for rare, compelling indications where the immediate threat to maternal physiology or pregnancy viability outweighs the inherent risks of operative intervention, such as hemorrhage, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. Based on the principles illustrated in this case, the primary indications for proceeding with surgery rather than expectant management include **acute mechanical compromise**, **failure of conservative therapy**, and **suspected catastrophic complications** such as torsion or necrosis.

In the presented case, the decision to operate at 21 weeks was driven by the patient's development of **restrictive lung disease (dyspnea)** and **severe, persistent abdominal pain** caused by the "steric bulk" of an 18-cm pedunculated leiomyoma. Crucially, these symptoms did not resolve with standard conservative measures, including analgesia and positional changes; while pain scores improved slightly, the underlying respiratory compromise and mass effect persisted. The clinical picture was further complicated by signs suggestive of **torsion and ischemic necrosis**—specifically acute pain localization, low-grade fever, and leukocytosis—which were confirmed intraoperatively by the finding of a twisted, leaking pedicle with areas of infarction.

Furthermore, surgical intervention becomes necessary when there is a realistic concern that continued fetal growth will exacerbate mass-related hypoxia or precipitate rupture before fetal viability is assured. In this scenario, the multidisciplinary team determined that the risk of leaving a twisting, necrotic, and space-occupying lesion in situ exceeded the surgical risks. Therefore, the hallmark indications for antepartum myomectomy are not merely the presence of a fibroid, but rather the triad of **intractable symptoms**, **evidence of acute degeneration or torsion**, and **threatened maternal organ function** (such as respiratory compromise) that cannot be managed medically. Expectant management remains the standard for asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic cases, but it is contraindicated when the mass poses an imminent threat to maternal stability or pregnancy continuation.
