Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do the sonographic and MRI features of a 'decidualized endometrioma' mimic those of a cystic degenerating leiomyoma, and what specific anatomical clue on imaging helps distinguish a pedunculated uterine myoma from an primary adnexal mass?

Both decidualized endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas present a diagnostic challenge in pregnancy because they share overlapping sonographic and MRI characteristics: both appear as large, complex, predominantly cystic masses with thickened walls, internal hemorrhagic or necrotic debris, and peripheral vascularity. In the setting of pregnancy, the hormonal milieu induces decidualization of endometrial tissue within an endometrioma, creating solid-appearing nodules that mimic the hyperechoic or hyperintense solid components seen in a myoma undergoing red or cystic degeneration. As illustrated in this case, the initial imaging erroneously suggested "huge decidualised endometriomas" due to these shared features of unilocular cystic structures with inhomogeneous fluid content and solid-appearing elements.

The specific anatomical clue that definitively distinguishes a pedunculated uterine myoma from a primary adnexal mass is the identification of a **connective tissue stalk (pedicle) establishing continuity between the mass and the uterine serosa**. While advanced imaging (such as non-contrast MRI) can suggest this relationship by demonstrating the lack of a cleavage plane between the mass and the uterus—as noted in the report where the caudal mass appeared "in continuity with the posterior leiomyoma"—definitive confirmation often requires surgical visualization. In this case, despite the inability of ultrasound and MRI to visualize a feeding vessel or stalk for the upper mass due to the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus, laparotomy revealed a distinct pedicle connecting the cranial mass to the uterine fundus. This finding confirmed the diagnosis of a twisted, pedunculated leiomyoma rather than an ovarian neoplasm, highlighting that while imaging raises suspicion, the physical demonstration of uterine continuity remains the gold standard for differentiation.
