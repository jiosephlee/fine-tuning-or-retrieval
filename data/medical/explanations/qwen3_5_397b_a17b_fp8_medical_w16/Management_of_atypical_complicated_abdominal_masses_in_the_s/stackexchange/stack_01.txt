Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a pregnant patient presenting with an elevated CA-125 level (e.g., 41.8 U/mL) during the second trimester, why is this finding insufficient to diagnose ovarian malignancy, and what benign physiological or pathological conditions commonly cause such elevations?

An isolated elevation of CA-125 in the second trimester, such as the 41.8 U/mL observed in this case, is insufficient to diagnose ovarian malignancy because CA-125 lacks specificity during pregnancy. While typically used as an oncologic marker with a cutoff of ≥35 U/mL in non-pregnant populations, serum CA-125 levels physiologically fluctuate during gestation, often peaking in the first and third trimesters. More importantly, this antigen is expressed by coelomic epithelium and can be elevated by various benign physiological states and pathological conditions unrelated to cancer.

As illustrated in the reported case of a woman with massive abdominal masses ultimately proven to be benign uterine leiomyomas, several non-malignant etiologies can drive CA-125 elevation. These include:
*   **Benign Uterine Pathology:** Large or degenerating leiomyomas (fibroids), particularly those undergoing cystic degeneration, necrosis, or torsion as seen in this patient's pedunculated mass, can cause significant release of CA-125.
*   **Endometriosis and Adenomyosis:** Decidualized endometriomas were a primary differential diagnosis in this case specifically due to the marker's association with ectopic endometrial tissue.
*   **Physiological Pregnancy Changes:** Normal placental development and peritoneal irritation from the expanding uterus can elevate levels.
*   **Inflammation and Infection:** Concurrent conditions such as pelvic inflammatory disease or, as noted in this patient's course, systemic inflammation (evidenced here by leukocytosis and a urinary *Klebsiella* infection) can contribute to rising titers.

Consequently, relying on a single CA-125 measurement risks significant overtreatment. The clinical reasoning must prioritize multimodal assessment—specifically high-resolution ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—to characterize mass morphology (e.g., distinguishing solid vs. cystic components, vascularity, and continuity with the uterus) rather than depending solely on serology. In this case, despite the "slight positivity" of the tumor marker suggesting potential malignancy or endometrioma, the definitive diagnosis relied on surgical pathology confirming benign leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis. Therefore, an elevated CA-125 in pregnancy should be interpreted as a nonspecific indicator of peritoneal or mesothelial irritation rather than a standalone diagnostic criterion for ovarian cancer.
