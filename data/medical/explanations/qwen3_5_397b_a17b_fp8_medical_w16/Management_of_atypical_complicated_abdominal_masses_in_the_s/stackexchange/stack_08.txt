Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] How does the presence and location of a large residual cervical or lower uterine segment leiomyoma influence the determination of the mode of delivery, and what are the risks associated with attempting vaginal birth in this scenario?

The presence of a large residual leiomyoma in the cervix or lower uterine segment fundamentally alters the mechanics of labor, typically necessitating an elective cesarean delivery rather than a trial of labor. As illustrated in the reported case, when a substantial myoma (in this instance, a 7.5 cm residual mass following partial myomectomy) occupies the pelvic inlet or lower uterine segment, it creates a mechanical obstruction known as dystocia. During the expulsive phase of labor, the fetal head requires adequate space to descend and engage; a fixed, bulky mass in this location physically blocks this progression, rendering vaginal delivery impossible and posing a high risk of obstructed labor.

Attempting vaginal birth in the setting of such an obstruction carries severe risks for both mother and fetus. For the mother, prolonged obstruction can lead to uterine inertia, excessive thinning of the lower uterine segment, and ultimately, catastrophic uterine rupture. Furthermore, the pressure exerted by the trapped fetal head against the soft tissues and the myoma itself increases the risk of extensive soft tissue trauma, fistula formation, and postpartum hemorrhage. For the fetus, the inability to descend results in prolonged compression, leading to fetal distress, hypoxia, and potential stillbirth.

In the specific case presented by De Nola et al., despite successful resection of a symptomatic pedunculated fundal myoma during the second trimester, the surgical team deliberately left a large intramural-subserosal myoma in situ within the lesser pelvis to minimize intraoperative obstetric risks. The authors explicitly noted that this residual "caudal myoma would have represented a huge obstacle for the fetal head progression." Consequently, the determination for an elective cesarean section at 37 weeks was driven directly by this anatomical constraint. This approach prioritized safety by avoiding the unpredictability of labor against a known mechanical barrier, resulting in an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome without the complications associated with emergency intervention for obstructed labor. Therefore, when imaging confirms a large leiomyoma impinging on the birth canal or lower uterine segment, cesarean delivery is the indicated management strategy.
