# The Great Mimic: When Pedunculated Leiomyomas Masquerade as Ovarian Malignancy in Pregnancy

We've all been there: a pregnant patient rolls into the ED or OB triage with acute abdominal pain, and the ultrasound screen lights up with a terrifyingly large, complex mass. Your mind immediately jumps to the worst-case scenario—ovarian torsion, ruptured ectopic (though less likely in the second trimester), or worse, an occult malignancy threatening the pregnancy. The stakes feel impossibly high because you aren't just managing a patient; you're managing a dyad.

But what if that "ovarian" nightmare is actually wearing a disguise?

A fascinating case report by De Nola et al. throws a wrench in our diagnostic algorithms, reminding us that in the distorted anatomy of a gravid uterus, even experienced eyes can be fooled. They describe a woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, presenting with severe left upper quadrant pain, dyspnea, and two massive abdominal lesions. On imaging, these looked like giant decidualized endometriomas or perhaps malignant ovarian cysts. There was vascularization, cystic degeneration, and even a mildly elevated CA-125. The team prepared for a potential oncologic disaster.

The twist? When they opened her up, the "upper ovarian mass" wasn't ovarian at all. It was a gigantic, pedunculated uterine leiomyoma that had twisted, leaked, and migrated so far upward it occupied the entire left subphrenic space. The "lower mass" was another myoma adherent to the posterior uterus. This case is a masterclass in why we need to keep our differential diagnosis wide and our interpretation of tumor markers nuanced when dealing with the unique physiology of pregnancy.

## The Diagnostic Fog: Anatomy and Artifacts

The primary challenge in this case—and many like it—is the sheer mechanical distortion caused by the gravid uterus. By the second trimester, the uterus has risen out of the pelvis, pushing bowel loops aside and stretching ligaments. In this patient, a pedunculated fibroid grew on a long stalk, allowing it to ascend well above the uterine fundus into the subphrenic space.

On ultrasound, this created a perfect storm of ambiguity. The mass was unilocular but cystic with peripheral vascularization—a classic look for a decidualized endometrioma or an ovarian neoplasm. Because of the "steric bulk" of the pregnancy, the sonographers couldn't identify a connective stalk linking the mass back to the uterus. Without that visual tether, the default assumption shifts toward an adnexal origin.

This is where MRI becomes your best friend, provided you know its limits. The team utilized non-contrast MRI (gadolinium is generally avoided unless absolutely necessary due to fetal safety concerns). While MRI offered superior soft-tissue resolution showing thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid content consistent with degeneration, it still couldn't definitively rule out an adnexal origin for the cranial mass pre-operatively. The lesson here is that even our most advanced imaging modalities can hit a wall when anatomy is this rearranged. If you can't see the stalk, don't assume it doesn't exist; assume it's just hidden behind 21 weeks of fetal growth.

## The CA-125 Trap: Don't Panic Over Mild Elevations

One of the most anxiety-inducing findings in this case was the CA-125 level of 41.8 U/mL. In a non-pregnant post-menopausal woman, this might trigger a full-blown gyn-onc workup. In pregnancy, however, context is everything.

CA-125 is notoriously nonspecific during gestation. Levels physiologically peak in the first trimester (median ~23 U/mL) and again in the third trimester (~21 U/mL), dipping slightly in the second trimester (median ~14 U/mL). However, benign conditions rampant in pregnancy—such as decidualization of peritoneal surfaces, leiomyomas undergoing red degeneration, or even simple mechanical irritation from a twisting mass—can push these numbers above the standard cutoff of 35 U/mL.

In this case series, the slight elevation and upward trend were alarming enough to fuel fears of malignancy. Yet, pathology confirmed benign leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis. Relying on an isolated or mildly trending CA-125 to dictate aggressive surgical intervention in a stable pregnant patient is a dangerous game. Always interpret tumor markers against the backdrop of gestational age and benign inflammatory processes inherent to pregnancy.

## To Cut or Not to Cut: Navigating Surgical Decision Making

So, when do you take them to the OR? Conservative management is usually the gold standard for fibroids in pregnancy because surgery carries risks of hemorrhage, preterm labor, and loss of pregnancy. But this patient crossed several red lines that forced the team's hand:
*   **Worsening Symptoms:** Severe pain refractory to analgesics and restrictive lung disease (dyspnea) due to diaphragmatic compression by the massive subphrenic mass.
*   **Complication Risk:** Evidence suggesting torsion (acute pain onset), leakage/necrosis (fever, localized tenderness), and potential rupture.
*   **Obstructive Potential:** The lower pelvic mass was positioned to block vaginal delivery later in gestation.

# To Cut or To Wait? Navigating the Threshold for Myomectomy in the Second Trimester

We teach our residents that the mantra for managing uterine leiomyomas in pregnancy is simple: *primum non nocere*. Watchful waiting is the gold standard. We tolerate pain, we manage nausea, and we accept that fibroids often grow rapidly in the first half of gestation only to regress postpartum. The risks of surgery—hemorrhage, spontaneous abortion, preterm labor, and uterine rupture—are well-documented and generally outweigh the benefits of removing a benign mass. But what happens when "benign" starts behaving like a catastrophe? When does the risk of leaving a mass in situ surpass the terror of opening a gravid uterus at 21 weeks?

A recent case report by De Nola et al. forces us to confront this exact dilemma. They describe a nulliparous woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, who rolled into the ED with severe left upper quadrant and pelvic pain, accompanied by dyspnea. On exam, she wasn't just uncomfortable; she was compromised. Her abdomen was distended beyond dates, harboring an 18 cm taut-elastic mass pushing up against her diaphragm and a separate 9 cm fixed pelvic mass. Initial imaging painted a confusing picture: two large cystic structures that looked suspiciously for decidualized endometriomas or perhaps something malignant, fueled by a rising CA-125 level (41.8 U/mL). The team did everything right diagnostically—they leveraged ultrasound and non-contrast MRI to map the anatomy while avoiding gadolinium—but the clinical trajectory was heading off a cliff. Despite analgesia and positioning, the patient developed fever, leukocytosis, and worsening restrictive lung disease. The decision was made to cut.

The intraoperative findings were a classic "gotcha" moment that underscores why imaging alone isn't enough. That massive 18 cm "cyst" in the left subphrenic space wasn't an ovarian neoplasm at all; it was a giant, pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion and was actively leaking/necrotic. This explained the fever, the acute pain, and the systemic inflammatory response. The surgical team performed a myomectomy on the twisted pedunculated mass but wisely left the second, deeply adherent pelvic myoma untouched to minimize uterine irritation. The result? A preserved pregnancy, resolution of the septic physiology, and an eventual elective C-section at 37 weeks with a healthy neonate. This case serves as a stark reminder that while conservatism is our default, there is a distinct threshold where surgical intervention becomes the only life-saving option for both mother and fetus.

So how do we identify that threshold before the patient decompensates? In this case, several red flags screamed for intervention despite the gestational age. First was the mechanical compromise: the sheer size of the masses caused restrictive lung disease, evident by her dyspnea and inability to lie flat without support. Second was the evidence of acute complications—specifically torsion and necrosis. The combination of localized tenderness, fever, and rising inflammatory markers (CRP and WBC) in the setting of a known large fibroid should trigger immediate concern for red degeneration progressing to frank necrosis or torsion. Finally, there was the diagnostic uncertainty coupled with oncological anxiety. While CA-125 can physiologically rise in pregnancy, an upward trend alongside complex solid-cystic features on MRI kept malignancy on the differential until proven otherwise. When you combine intractable symptoms, organ compression, suspected torsion/necrosis, and unresolved malignancy concerns, the scale tips decisively toward surgery.

The technical execution in this case also offers vital lessons for the obstetric surgeon. The team opted for a laparotomy rather than laparoscopy—a prudent choice given the massive size of the tumor (18 cm), its high location impinging on the diaphragm, and the need for rapid control of potential hemorrhage from a twisted vascular pedicle. Crucially, they didn't go in blind regarding fetal safety; continuous monitoring of fetal heart tones was maintained throughout the procedure via serial ultrasound checks. Pharmacologic protection was equally aggressive, utilizing intraoperative and postoperative progesterone supplementation (both IM progesterone and hydroxyprogesterone caproate) to suppress uterine irritability. By limiting resection to only the offending pedunculated mass and leaving behind quiescent intramural pathology ("less is more"), they balanced oncologic/surgical clearance with obstetric preservation perfectly.

Ultimately, this case reinforces that management algorithms are guides, not shackles. # The Partial Solution: Strategic Intraoperative Decision-Making to Preserve Pregnancy

We are taught in surgery that when you find a problem, you fix it. If there's a twisted appendix, out it comes. If there's a gangrenous bowel segment, you resect. But what happens when the "problem" is a massive, symptomatic uterine leiomyoma in a 21-week pregnancy, and fixing *everything* might cost you the pregnancy?

This is the dilemma faced by the team managing a nulliparous woman in her 40s who presented with severe abdominal pain, dyspnea, and two gigantic abdominal masses. One mass was an 18 cm monster occupying the left subphrenic space; the other was a 9 cm fixed lesion in the pouch of Douglas. Initial imaging painted a confusing picture: were these decidualized endometriomas? Ovarian malignancies? The CA-125 was slightly elevated (a notorious red herring in pregnancy), and the patient was deteriorating with pain and restrictive lung disease.

They went to the OR expecting a diagnostic exploration that might turn into a full-blown oncologic debulking or a tragic loss. What they found instead was a masterclass in surgical restraint. The cranial mass turned out to be a pedunculated, twisted, and leaking leiomyoma—the source of her acute agony. The caudal mass, however, was deeply embedded in the posterior uterine torus. Here lies the critical teaching point: the surgeons excised the twisted cranial myoma to resolve the acute crisis but deliberately left the large caudal myoma *in situ*. They traded a future potential obstruction for immediate maternal stabilization and fetal survival. It wasn't an incomplete surgery; it was the *correct* surgery.

## The Diagnostic Fog: When Fibroids Mimic Malignancy

Before we get to the scalpel work, we have to appreciate how tricky these cases are to diagnose. In this case, an obese patient in her second trimester presented with pain and dyspnea. The physical exam revealed an abdomen larger than dates and a tender mass. Ultrasound showed two large cystic structures with peripheral vascularization. To make matters more confusing, her CA-125 was trending upward at 41.8 U/mL.

In a non-pregnant patient over 40, that marker combined with complex adnexal masses screams ovarian cancer until proven otherwise. But pregnancy changes the rules of engagement. CA-125 physiologically rises in the first and third trimesters, and benign conditions like leiomyomas, endometriosis, and even normal placentation can push levels above the standard cutoff of 35 U/mL. Relying on this number alone could have led to overly aggressive management or unnecessary panic.

The real hero here was non-contrast MRI. Gadolinium is generally avoided in pregnancy due to unknown fetal risks, but T1 and T2 weighted sequences provided enough detail to see thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid content without confirming frank malignancy. The imaging suggested decidualized endometriomas or cystic degenerating fibroids. Crucially, no adenopathy was seen. This nuance allowed the team to plan for a myomectomy rather than a radical staging procedure, keeping the goalposts firmly set on pregnancy preservation.

## The Art of Leaving Things Behind

Once inside via a longitudinal laparotomy (chosen due to the sheer size of the masses and gestational age), reality set in quickly. # Mass Effect Mechanics: Recognizing Non-Obstetric Causes of Dyspnea and Abdominal Pain in Late Second Trimester

We often operate on autopilot when a pregnant patient rolls into the ED complaining of abdominal pain and shortness of breath. Our minds immediately jump to the "must-not-miss" obstetric catastrophes: placental abruption, uterine rupture, preterm labor, or perhaps a pulmonary embolism. And rightfully so—these are the killers. But what happens when the vitals are rock solid, the fetal heart trace is reassuring, and yet the patient is in genuine distress with symptoms that feel disproportionately severe for their gestational age?

It's easy to dismiss dyspnea in the second trimester as normal physiological adaptation or attribute vague abdominal discomfort to round ligament pain. However, a recent case report by De Nola et al. serves as a stark reminder that mechanics matter. Sometimes, the problem isn't a clot or a contraction; it's simple, brute-force mass effect. They describe a nulliparous woman in her 40s at 21 weeks gestation presenting with acute左上 quadrant pain and dyspnea. She wasn't hypoxic, her BP was stable, and her ECG was clean. Yet, her abdomen was distended far beyond dates, harboring two massive lesions that were literally pushing her diaphragm up and compressing her lungs. The culprit? Not an ovarian malignancy or an ectopic, but giant, atypical uterine leiomyomas masquerading as adnexal cysts.

The diagnostic journey here highlights a classic trap: assuming complex cystic masses in pregnancy are ovarian in origin until proven otherwise. Initial ultrasound painted a picture of two large unilocular cysts—one towering in the subphrenic space and another lodged deep in the pouch of Douglas. With a mildly elevated CA-125 (41.8 U/mL), the specter of decidualized endometriomas or even malignancy loomed large. But context is king. While CA-125 can physiologically rise in pregnancy, the imaging nuances told a different story. MRI without contrast (the gold standard here to avoid gadolinium risks) revealed thick walls and solid components continuous with the uterine wall. The "cysts" were actually degenerating fibroids. The upper mass was a pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion and ischemic necrosis, explaining the fever and acute pain, while the lower mass was a fixed intramural-subserosal monster obstructing the pelvis.

This case forces us to reconsider our threshold for intervention. The dogma has long been "leave fibroids alone during pregnancy" due to fears of hemorrhage and pregnancy loss. And generally, that's sound advice. But when a mass creates a restrictive lung disease pattern via diaphragmatic splinting, causes intractable pain from torsion/necrosis, or threatens to obstruct labor entirely, conservatism becomes dangerous. The team made the bold call to perform a laparotomy at 21 weeks. They excised the twisted, necrotic upper fibroid—the source of the acute crisis—and wisely left the lower pelvic mass untouched to minimize uterine irritation. The result? Relief of symptoms, preservation of the pregnancy, and a planned C-section at term with a healthy baby. It's a masterclass in balancing oncologic/surgical urgency against obstetric fragility.

The takeaway isn't that we need to start cutting out fibroids in every pregnant patient with belly pain. Rather, it's about maintaining a high index of suspicion for mechanical pathology when the clinical picture doesn't fit the usual obstetric scripts. If your patient looks too big for dates, has localized tenderness that doesn't migrate, or has dyspnea that seems out of proportion to their BMI and gestational week, get your hands dirty with a thorough bimanual exam and don't settle for "physiologic" as an explanation without ruling out space-occupying lesions first.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Don't Anchor on Obstetrics:** In pregnant patients with stable vitals but severe abdominal pain and dyspnea, consider mechanical compression from large uterine or extra-uterine masses if common etiologies (PE, abruption) are ruled out.
*   **Imaging Nuances Matter:** Ultrasound is the first line, but non-contrast MRI is superior for distinguishing between ovarian masses and degenerating/subserosal leiomyomas, specifically looking for continuity with the uterine wall.
*   **CA-125 Interpretation:** Mild elevations in CA-125 are common in pregnancy (peaking in 1st and 3rd trimesters) and can occur with benign conditions like fibroids and endometriosis; do not let a borderline value drive you toward radical surgery without corroborating imaging.
*   **Surgical Indications:** While myomectomy is generally avoided in pregnancy due to bleeding/preterm labor risks, it is indicated for complications such as torsion of a pedunculated fibroid, irreversible necrosis causing sepsis/pain, or severe organ compression (e.g., restrictive lung disease).
*   **Selective Resection:** When surgery is unavoidable, aim for damage control—remove only the symptomatic/complicated mass (like the torsed pedunculated fibroid) and leave stable intramural masses *in situ* to reduce operative time and uterine trauma.



# Planning the Exit Strategy: How Antepartum Myomectomy Dictates Mode of Delivery

We often talk about the "exit strategy" in trauma or critical care—how do we get the patient out of the ICU, off the vent, or home safely? But in obstetrics, specifically when managing complex adnexal or uterine pathology during pregnancy, the exit strategy needs to be defined long before the baby arrives. It starts at the moment you make the decision to operate antepartum.

A fascinating case report by De Nola et al. highlights a scenario that keeps many OB/GYNs and emergency physicians up at night: a pregnant patient in her 40s presenting with acute abdominal pain and dyspnea due to massive abdominal masses. What makes this case a perfect teaching tool isn't just the diagnostic dilemma (endometrioma vs. degenerating fibroid), but how the surgical management of one mass fundamentally dictated the mode of delivery for the other.

The patient presented at 21 weeks with two giants: an 18 cm upper mass causing diaphragmatic compression and a 9 cm lower pelvic mass. While initial imaging flirted with the diagnosis of decidualized endometriomas, surgery revealed the truth: these were atypical, cystic leiomyomas. The team performed a delicate balancing act, excising the twisted, necrotic pedunculated upper fibroid to relieve symptoms and save the pregnancy, but wisely leaving the lower, fixed posterior mass *in situ* to avoid excessive uterine manipulation and hemorrhage.

Here is where the clinical pearl shines: **The decision to leave a large posterior myoma behind automatically rewrote the birth plan.** That residual 9 cm mass sitting in the pouch of Douglas wasn't just a bystander; it was a guaranteed obstruction to vaginal delivery. Consequently, the team planned an elective Cesarean section at 37 weeks, utilizing the previous laparotomy scar. This wasn't a reactive decision made in labor; it was a proactive stratification of risk made months prior. The case underscores that antepartum myomectomy is rarely an isolated event; it is the first chapter of a modified delivery narrative.

### Navigating Diagnostic Uncertainty in Pregnancy

Diagnosing abdominal masses in pregnancy is notoriously tricky. Physiologic changes can mimic pathology, and standard markers lose their specificity. In this case, the patient's CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL. In a non-pregnant post-menopausal woman, this raises red flags for malignancy. In pregnancy? It's noise. CA-125 physiologically peaks in the first and third trimesters and can be elevated in benign conditions like endometriosis, PID, and even uncomplicated pregnancies. Relying on a single value here could have led to overtreatment or unnecessary panic.

Instead, the team leaned on high-quality imaging. Ultrasound gave them the initial map, but MRI (without gadolinium contrast) provided the crucial tissue characterization needed to differentiate between a malignant process and a degenerating fibroid. The MRI confirmed thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid content consistent with cystic degeneration rather than frank malignancy. When you are staring down a potential laparotomy at 21 weeks, knowing you aren't cutting into an ovarian carcinoma changes everything—from incision planning to oncologic staging precautions.

### The Surgical Tightrope: To Cut or Not to Cut?

The dogma has long been "leave fibroids alone during pregnancy." And generally, that holds true. The risks of hemorrhage, miscarriage, and preterm labor are real. However, "expectant management" fails when complications arise. This patient wasn't just uncomfortable; she was developing restrictive lung disease from the sheer bulk of the tumors and had signs of torsion and necrosis (fever, leukocytosis, worsening pain).

When conservative measures failed, surgery became mandatory. The operative nuance here was selective resection. Removing *both* masses would have turned a controlled procedure into a chaotic battle against bleeding and uterine irritability. By removing only the symptomatic, twisted pedunculated mass and leaving the broad-based posterior intramural-subserosal fibroid untouched, they achieved symptom relief while minimizing obstetric risk. This "damage control" approach allowed for fetal survival and maternal recovery, proving that sometimes doing less surgically yields more clinically.

### Counseling Beyond Discharge: Regression vs. Resolution

One of the most reassuring aspects of this case for patient counseling is the natural history of fibroids postpartum. Patients often fear that leaving a fibroid means signing up for immediate future surgery once they recover from childbirth and start sleep-deprived parenting. However, leiomyomas follow a non-linear trajectory during and after pregnancy. They tend to grow rapidly in the first trimester but often undergo significant involution postpartum as hormone levels plummet and lactation suppresses estrogen production Studies suggest reductions in size averaging over 40% within six weeks of delivery.

In this case, follow-up showed exactly that: spontaneous regression of both masses without further intervention! This allows us to counsel patients effectively: unless there are persistent symptoms or obstructive issues (like our residual pelvic mass preventing vaginal birth), we can often afford to wait months—or even years—before considering definitive surgical management again let nature do some work for us first!
