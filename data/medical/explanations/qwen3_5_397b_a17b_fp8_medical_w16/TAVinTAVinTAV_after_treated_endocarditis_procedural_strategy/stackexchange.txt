Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] According to current ESC guidelines, what is the preferred management strategy for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) complicated by heart failure, and what specific factors in this case justified deviating from that standard to pursue a third transcatheter intervention?

According to current European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines, the preferred management strategy for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) complicated by heart failure is early surgical valve replacement. Surgery is mandated in this setting to achieve source control through radical debridement of infected tissue, remove large vegetations, and reconstruct the annulus if abscess formation is present, as medical therapy alone carries a prohibitively high mortality rate in patients with hemodynamic compromise.

However, in this specific case, the clinical team justifiably deviated from the surgical standard to pursue a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) based on three critical factors grounded in the patient's unique trajectory. First, and most fundamentally, the indication for intervention shifted from *active* infection to *structural* valve deterioration following successful eradication of the pathogen. The patient had completed a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy for *Enterococcus faecalis*, and rigorous reassessment—including repeated negative blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT—confirmed the complete resolution of endocarditis with no evidence of active vegetation, abscess, or paravalvular leak. Consequently, the procedural goal was no longer infectious source control but rather the relief of severe mechanical obstruction caused by leaflet fibrosis and restenosis.

Second, the patient presented with prohibitive surgical risk. With a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, significant comorbidities including COPD, diabetes, and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, plus a complex "triple-stented" anatomy where previous valve frames were deeply integrated into the aortic wall, open surgery would have necessitated a hazardous total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement. Third, advanced imaging confirmed anatomical feasibility for a catheter-based approach; preprocedural CT modeling demonstrated that a third valve could be implanted with adequate coronary clearance and commissural alignment to mitigate prosthesis-patient mismatch. Thus, in the absence of active infection and given the extreme operative risk, a tailored transcatheter strategy offered the only viable path to restore hemodynamics and alleviate heart failure.

### [Pathophysiology] In the context of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure, how does the accumulation of multiple overlapping stent frames specifically increase the risk of coronary artery obstruction, and what preprocedural imaging metrics are critical to assess before proceeding?

In a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenario, the accumulation of multiple overlapping stent frames creates a "mesh-within-a-mesh" architecture that significantly elevates the risk of coronary artery obstruction through two primary mechanisms: physical displacement and flow channeling. First, as described in this case, each successive valve implantation pushes the leaflets of the preceding prosthesis outward against the aortic wall. In a triple-layered configuration, this cumulative bulk can form a dense, continuous skirt of tissue and metal struts that extends above the original coronary ostia, effectively sealing them off. Second, the misalignment of commissural posts across three different valve generations can create unpredictable flow channels or directly cover the ostia with thickened leaflet material, particularly if the new valve is deployed too deeply. In this specific patient, who had a Lotus valve followed by an Evolut and then an Acurate neo2, the varying strut designs and heights compounded this risk, necessitating a strategy of high implantation to pin the old leaflets below the coronary level without creating a neo-skirt that would obstruct flow.

To mitigate these risks, preprocedural multislice computed tomography (MSCT) is the diagnostic standard and must focus on specific geometric metrics rather than simple annular sizing. Critical assessments include the **virtual valve-to-coronary distance**, which predicts whether the displaced leaflets of the innermost valves will impinge on the ostia once the new frame expands. Equally important is the analysis of **sinus of Valsalva dimensions** relative to the projected outer diameter of the triple-stent construct; narrow sinuses offer less room for leaflet displacement, increasing obstruction risk. Furthermore, clinicians must evaluate the **coronary ostial height** in relation to the anticipated position of the new valve's sealing skirt and assess **frame interaction** to identify areas where overlapping strats might create a solid barrier to cannulation. In this case report, MSCT was pivotal in confirming adequate coronary clearance and guiding the decision to implant the third valve at a higher plane, aligning it with the existing Evolut leaflets to preserve coronary perfusion without requiring protective wiring or bailout techniques.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why was the Acurate neo2 valve selected over other available transcatheter heart valves for this third implantation, considering the patient's triple-stented anatomy and the need for future coronary access?

The selection of the Acurate neo2 valve for this third transcatheter implantation was driven by its unique architectural features, which specifically addressed the compounded risks of coronary obstruction and prosthesis-patient mismatch inherent in a triple-stented anatomy. Unlike earlier-generation devices or those with taller frames, the Acurate neo2 offers a relatively low profile and supra-annular leaflet positioning. In this case, these characteristics were critical to facilitating a "high implantation" strategy. By deploying the valve at the level of the existing Evolut leaflets, the team could pin the restricted leaflets of the failing prosthesis against the stent frame without creating a dense "neo-skirt" of material below the coronary ostia. This maneuver preserved the sinus space and maintained vital coronary clearance, a paramount concern given the patient's extensive history of coronary artery disease and prior interventions.

Furthermore, the Acurate neo2's design supports precise commissural alignment, which was meticulously planned using preprocedural multislice CT. Aligning the new commissures with those of the underlying valves minimizes the cumulative metal burden overlapping the coronary openings, thereby safeguarding future coronary access—a necessity for a patient with three-vessel disease. Finally, the supra-annular flow dynamics of the Acurate neo2 helped maximize the effective orifice area within the constrained inner diameter of the two previous valves (Lotus and Evolut), mitigating the risk of severe patient-prosthesis mismatch that often plagues valve-in-valve-in-valve procedures. Thus, the device was chosen not merely as another option, but as the only one capable of satisfying the triad of requirements: safe high deployment, preserved coronary accessibility, and optimized hemodynamics in an extremely narrowed landing zone.

### [Pathophysiology] Immediately post-procedure, this patient exhibited a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg consistent with severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM), yet follow-up at one month showed significant hemodynamic improvement. What mechanistic explanation accounts for this delayed reduction in transvalvular gradient?

The delayed reduction in the transvalvular gradient observed in this patient—from a severe mean gradient of 67 mm Hg immediately post-procedure to 27 mm Hg at one month—is best explained by the time-dependent viscoelastic expansion of the underlying constrained valve frames and the resolution of acute procedural edema.

In a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenario, the newly implanted prosthesis (Acurate neo2) must expand within the rigid confines of two previous stent frames (Lotus and Evolut). Immediately following deployment and even after high-pressure post-dilation, the metallic struts of the innermost constrained valve (the Evolut) may not achieve their maximal theoretical diameter due to elastic recoil and the resistance offered by the outer layers. As described in the case discussion, while intentional frame fracture was not performed, the high-pressure balloon post-dilation likely initiated a gradual remodeling process. Over the ensuing weeks, continued radial force from the self-expanding new valve, combined with the viscoelastic properties of the older stent materials and surrounding tissue, allows for further slow expansion of the nested frames. This incremental increase in the effective orifice area significantly reduces turbulence and pressure gradients over time.

Furthermore, immediate post-procedural gradients are often artificially elevated by transient factors such as local tissue edema at the implantation site and hyperdynamic states induced by procedural stress or anesthesia. As these acute inflammatory changes resolve and the patient's hemodynamics stabilize during recovery, the true steady-state gradient emerges. The case report explicitly attributes this phenomenon to "partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame" alongside optimized leaflet motion, confirming that the initial severe prosthesis-patient mismatch was a transient mechanical constraint rather than a permanent failure of valve sizing. This underscores a critical teaching point in complex valve-in-valve interventions: immediate post-deployment hemodynamics may not predict final functional outcomes, and patience with serial imaging is essential before declaring therapeutic failure.

### [Management] What is the rationale for aiming for 'high implantation' and precise commissural alignment when deploying a third transcatheter valve, and how do these techniques mitigate long-term complications in patients with complex prior anatomy?

In the setting of a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), the imperative for **high implantation** and precise **commissural alignment** stems from the cumulative geometric constraints imposed by multiple overlapping stent frames, which critically threaten coronary accessibility and hemodynamic efficiency.

As illustrated in this case of a patient with prior Lotus and Evolut valves, the "nesting" of three prostheses creates a dense metallic lattice within the aortic root. **High implantation**—specifically positioning the new valve at the level of the previous leaflets rather than deep within the annulus—is strategically employed to prevent the formation of a "neo-skirt." If the third valve is deployed too deeply, it pushes the failed leaflets of the preceding valves outward and downward, creating a thick tissue barrier below the coronary ostia that can obstruct flow or render future coronary cannulation impossible. In this report, high implantation allowed the team to fixate the existing leaflets against the stent frame without encroaching on the coronary sinuses, thereby preserving perfusion despite the complex triple-stented anatomy.

Simultaneously, **commissural alignment** is vital to maintain open channels between the struts of the new valve and those of the underlying prostheses. Misalignment in a multi-layered construct exacerbates the "mesh burden," effectively sealing off the spaces required for wire passage during future percutaneous coronary interventions. By aligning the commissures of the Acurate neo2 valve with those of the underlying Evolut frame using CT-guided planning, operators maximize the effective orifice area and minimize prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM). This precision was crucial in this case, where initial post-procedure gradients were high but improved significantly over time as optimal leaflet motion and central flow dynamics were restored through careful alignment and post-dilation. Ultimately, these techniques transform a potentially prohibitive anatomical scenario into a feasible lifetime management strategy, balancing immediate hemodynamic relief with the preservation of future therapeutic options.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Before undertaking a redo TAVI in a patient with a history of Enterococcus faecalis endocarditis, what specific diagnostic modalities and clinical criteria must be met to confidently rule out active infection and ensure procedural safety?

To confidently proceed with a redo transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient with a history of *Enterococcus faecalis* prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), the clinician must establish definitive microbiological cure and exclude residual anatomical sequelae of infection through a multimodal diagnostic approach. As illustrated in the reported case of a triple-TAVI procedure, safety relies on satisfying three concurrent criteria: completion of targeted antimicrobial therapy with negative surveillance cultures, absence of clinical signs of active inflammation, and comprehensive imaging confirmation of sterile fields.

First, microbiological eradication must be documented. The patient must have completed a full course of pathogen-specific intravenous antibiotics—in this instance, a 6-week regimen of ampicillin-based therapy for *E. faecalis*—followed by an appropriate observation period. Crucially, repeated blood cultures obtained after the cessation of therapy must remain negative, and there should be no indication for ongoing suppressive antibiotic therapy. In the referenced case, the decision to intervene was predicated on the completion of this antimicrobial course and the absence of recurrent bacteremia.

Second, advanced imaging is mandatory to rule out occult abscesses, fistulas, or persistent vegetations that blood cultures alone cannot detect. Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) serves as the primary modality to visualize valve leaflets and perivalvular structures; in the described case, preprocedural TEE explicitly confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications. Furthermore, multislice computed tomography (MSCT) is essential not only for procedural planning regarding coronary obstruction and valve sizing but also as a complementary tool to assess perivalvular extension of infection. The combination of TEE and MSCT provides a high-negative predictive value for active tissue invasion.

Finally, these objective data points must be correlated with clinical stability. The patient must be afebrile and demonstrate resolution of systemic inflammatory markers and heart failure symptoms attributable to sepsis rather than pure hemodynamic obstruction. Only when the "Heart Team" confirms that the prior infection has fully resolved—leaving behind structural degeneration rather than active disease—is a transcatheter reintervention considered safe. In this specific case, meeting these rigorous criteria allowed for the successful implantation of a third valve despite the complex history of PVE, demonstrating that prior endocarditis is not an absolute contraindication to redo-TAVI provided strict evidence of cure is established.

### [Pathophysiology] How does the mechanism of structural valve deterioration differ between spontaneous bioprosthetic calcification and degradation secondary to treated infective endocarditis, and how might this influence leaflet mobility in a redo scenario?

Structural valve deterioration (SVD) manifests through distinct pathophysiologic mechanisms depending on whether the primary driver is spontaneous calcific degeneration or sequelae of treated infective endocarditis (PVE), and these differences critically dictate leaflet mobility in redo transcatheter scenarios.

In **spontaneous bioprosthetic calcification**, the dominant mechanism is the gradual deposition of calcium hydroxyapatite within the collagen matrix of the leaflets, leading to intrinsic stiffening and reduced compliance. This process typically results in a symmetric reduction in leaflet excursion and a fixed, narrowed orifice. Conversely, in **degradation secondary to treated PVE**, as seen in this case involving *Enterococcus faecalis*, the mechanism is multifactorial and often more disruptive to the valve architecture. While inflammation resolves with antimicrobial therapy, the healing process induces significant **leaflet fibrosis** and scarring. More importantly, prior infection can cause mechanical restriction where healed vegetations or inflammatory adhesions tether the leaflets to one another or to the stent frame. In this specific patient, the "severe restenosis" observed three months after treating the endocarditis suggests that the dysfunction was driven not merely by tissue stiffening, but by **mechanical restriction** and potential fusion of leaflet edges following the infectious insult.

This distinction profoundly influences **leaflet mobility** during a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure. In spontaneous calcification, a new transcatheter heart valve (THV) generally pushes the old, calcified leaflets outward against the existing stent struts, effectively creating a new conduit. However, in post-PVE scenarios with fibrotic restriction, there is a heightened risk that the diseased leaflets may not displace uniformly; instead, they may fold inward or remain tethered, potentially obstructing the new valve's effective orifice area (EOA). 

The case report illustrates a strategic adaptation to this specific pathophysiology. Recognizing the risk of inward leaflet folding and further EOA reduction due to the fibrotic, restricted nature of the post-endocarditis leaflets, the operators utilized **high implantation** of the third valve (Acurate neo2). By deploying the new prosthesis at the level of the previous Evolut leaflets, they intentionally pinned the fibrotic host leaflets against the outer stent frame. This maneuver prevented the restricted leaflets from prolapsing into the flow stream and mitigated the formation of a "neo-skirt" that could compromise coronary flow. Thus, while spontaneous SVD requires overcoming rigidity, post-PVE SVD requires managing architectural distortion and adhesion, necessitating precise depth control and commissural alignment to ensure adequate leaflet mobility and hemodynamic function in complex redo settings.

### [Management] If this patient were to experience failure of the third prosthesis, why would surgical explantation be considered prohibitively high-risk compared to the initial surgeries, and what emerging catheter-based salvage therapies might theoretically be considered despite their uncertainty in triple-stented configurations?

Surgical explantation in this scenario would be considered prohibitively high-risk due to the cumulative anatomical distortion created by three overlapping transcatheter valve frames deeply integrated into the aortic root. As highlighted in the case discussion, the patient's prior valves have undergone extensive tissue ingrowth and fixation over a decade, effectively fusing the prostheses to the native aortic wall and each other. Attempting surgical removal would not only carry an extreme risk of catastrophic aortic root disruption but would likely necessitate a total root and ascending aorta replacement—a procedure compounded by the patient's prohibitive operative risk profile (EuroSCORE II >27%), significant comorbidities (COPD, diabetes), and hostile mediastinum from prior interventions.

Consequently, if the third prosthesis were to fail, management would almost certainly require a percutaneous approach. Emerging catheter-based salvage therapies that might be theoretically considered include **bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF)** using high-pressure non-compliant balloons to expand the innermost rigid frame and improve hemodynamics, or **leaflet laceration techniques** such as BASILICA (Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to Prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery Obstruction) to mitigate coronary obstruction risks during a fourth valve-in-valve procedure. However, as the authors note, the efficacy and safety of these techniques remain uncertain in a "triple-stented" configuration, where the cumulative metal burden of three distinct stent frames may resist fracture or create unpredictable tear patterns, making pre-procedural CT modeling even more critical than in previous iterations.
