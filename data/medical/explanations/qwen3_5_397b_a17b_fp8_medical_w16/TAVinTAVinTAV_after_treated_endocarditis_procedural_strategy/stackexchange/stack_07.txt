Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Pathophysiology] How does the mechanism of structural valve deterioration differ between spontaneous bioprosthetic calcification and degradation secondary to treated infective endocarditis, and how might this influence leaflet mobility in a redo scenario?

Structural valve deterioration (SVD) manifests through distinct pathophysiologic mechanisms depending on whether the primary driver is spontaneous calcific degeneration or sequelae of treated infective endocarditis (PVE), and these differences critically dictate leaflet mobility in redo transcatheter scenarios.

In **spontaneous bioprosthetic calcification**, the dominant mechanism is the gradual deposition of calcium hydroxyapatite within the collagen matrix of the leaflets, leading to intrinsic stiffening and reduced compliance. This process typically results in a symmetric reduction in leaflet excursion and a fixed, narrowed orifice. Conversely, in **degradation secondary to treated PVE**, as seen in this case involving *Enterococcus faecalis*, the mechanism is multifactorial and often more disruptive to the valve architecture. While inflammation resolves with antimicrobial therapy, the healing process induces significant **leaflet fibrosis** and scarring. More importantly, prior infection can cause mechanical restriction where healed vegetations or inflammatory adhesions tether the leaflets to one another or to the stent frame. In this specific patient, the "severe restenosis" observed three months after treating the endocarditis suggests that the dysfunction was driven not merely by tissue stiffening, but by **mechanical restriction** and potential fusion of leaflet edges following the infectious insult.

This distinction profoundly influences **leaflet mobility** during a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure. In spontaneous calcification, a new transcatheter heart valve (THV) generally pushes the old, calcified leaflets outward against the existing stent struts, effectively creating a new conduit. However, in post-PVE scenarios with fibrotic restriction, there is a heightened risk that the diseased leaflets may not displace uniformly; instead, they may fold inward or remain tethered, potentially obstructing the new valve's effective orifice area (EOA). 

The case report illustrates a strategic adaptation to this specific pathophysiology. Recognizing the risk of inward leaflet folding and further EOA reduction due to the fibrotic, restricted nature of the post-endocarditis leaflets, the operators utilized **high implantation** of the third valve (Acurate neo2). By deploying the new prosthesis at the level of the previous Evolut leaflets, they intentionally pinned the fibrotic host leaflets against the outer stent frame. This maneuver prevented the restricted leaflets from prolapsing into the flow stream and mitigated the formation of a "neo-skirt" that could compromise coronary flow. Thus, while spontaneous SVD requires overcoming rigidity, post-PVE SVD requires managing architectural distortion and adhesion, necessitating precise depth control and commissural alignment to ensure adequate leaflet mobility and hemodynamic function in complex redo settings.
