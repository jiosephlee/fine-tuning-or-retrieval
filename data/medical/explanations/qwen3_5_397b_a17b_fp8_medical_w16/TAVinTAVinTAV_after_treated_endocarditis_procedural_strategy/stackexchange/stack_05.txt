Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] What is the rationale for aiming for 'high implantation' and precise commissural alignment when deploying a third transcatheter valve, and how do these techniques mitigate long-term complications in patients with complex prior anatomy?

In the setting of a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), the imperative for **high implantation** and precise **commissural alignment** stems from the cumulative geometric constraints imposed by multiple overlapping stent frames, which critically threaten coronary accessibility and hemodynamic efficiency.

As illustrated in this case of a patient with prior Lotus and Evolut valves, the "nesting" of three prostheses creates a dense metallic lattice within the aortic root. **High implantation**—specifically positioning the new valve at the level of the previous leaflets rather than deep within the annulus—is strategically employed to prevent the formation of a "neo-skirt." If the third valve is deployed too deeply, it pushes the failed leaflets of the preceding valves outward and downward, creating a thick tissue barrier below the coronary ostia that can obstruct flow or render future coronary cannulation impossible. In this report, high implantation allowed the team to fixate the existing leaflets against the stent frame without encroaching on the coronary sinuses, thereby preserving perfusion despite the complex triple-stented anatomy.

Simultaneously, **commissural alignment** is vital to maintain open channels between the struts of the new valve and those of the underlying prostheses. Misalignment in a multi-layered construct exacerbates the "mesh burden," effectively sealing off the spaces required for wire passage during future percutaneous coronary interventions. By aligning the commissures of the Acurate neo2 valve with those of the underlying Evolut frame using CT-guided planning, operators maximize the effective orifice area and minimize prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM). This precision was crucial in this case, where initial post-procedure gradients were high but improved significantly over time as optimal leaflet motion and central flow dynamics were restored through careful alignment and post-dilation. Ultimately, these techniques transform a potentially prohibitive anatomical scenario into a feasible lifetime management strategy, balancing immediate hemodynamic relief with the preservation of future therapeutic options.
