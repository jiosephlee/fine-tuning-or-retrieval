Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Before undertaking a redo TAVI in a patient with a history of Enterococcus faecalis endocarditis, what specific diagnostic modalities and clinical criteria must be met to confidently rule out active infection and ensure procedural safety?

To confidently proceed with a redo transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient with a history of *Enterococcus faecalis* prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), the clinician must establish definitive microbiological cure and exclude residual anatomical sequelae of infection through a multimodal diagnostic approach. As illustrated in the reported case of a triple-TAVI procedure, safety relies on satisfying three concurrent criteria: completion of targeted antimicrobial therapy with negative surveillance cultures, absence of clinical signs of active inflammation, and comprehensive imaging confirmation of sterile fields.

First, microbiological eradication must be documented. The patient must have completed a full course of pathogen-specific intravenous antibiotics—in this instance, a 6-week regimen of ampicillin-based therapy for *E. faecalis*—followed by an appropriate observation period. Crucially, repeated blood cultures obtained after the cessation of therapy must remain negative, and there should be no indication for ongoing suppressive antibiotic therapy. In the referenced case, the decision to intervene was predicated on the completion of this antimicrobial course and the absence of recurrent bacteremia.

Second, advanced imaging is mandatory to rule out occult abscesses, fistulas, or persistent vegetations that blood cultures alone cannot detect. Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) serves as the primary modality to visualize valve leaflets and perivalvular structures; in the described case, preprocedural TEE explicitly confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications. Furthermore, multislice computed tomography (MSCT) is essential not only for procedural planning regarding coronary obstruction and valve sizing but also as a complementary tool to assess perivalvular extension of infection. The combination of TEE and MSCT provides a high-negative predictive value for active tissue invasion.

Finally, these objective data points must be correlated with clinical stability. The patient must be afebrile and demonstrate resolution of systemic inflammatory markers and heart failure symptoms attributable to sepsis rather than pure hemodynamic obstruction. Only when the "Heart Team" confirms that the prior infection has fully resolved—leaving behind structural degeneration rather than active disease—is a transcatheter reintervention considered safe. In this specific case, meeting these rigorous criteria allowed for the successful implantation of a third valve despite the complex history of PVE, demonstrating that prior endocarditis is not an absolute contraindication to redo-TAVI provided strict evidence of cure is established.
