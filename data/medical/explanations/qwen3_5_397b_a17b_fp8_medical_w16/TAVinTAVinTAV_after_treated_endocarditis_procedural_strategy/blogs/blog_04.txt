Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Chasing Gradients: When a 67 mmHg Mean Gradient Isn't a Disaster

We are taught to fear the number. In the world of aortic stenosis, a mean gradient of 67 mmHg is catastrophic. It screams severe obstruction, impending cardiogenic shock, and procedural failure. If you saw that number pop up on your echo machine immediately after deploying a new transcatheter heart valve, your adrenaline would spike, and the heart team would likely start scrambling for bail-out options.

But what if I told you that in the complex realm of redo TAVI, specifically "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV" procedures, that terrifying number might just be the starting line?

A fascinating case report by Merkulov et al. details the journey of a man in his late 70s who became one of the few patients in the world to undergo a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation. His history is a marathon of structural intervention: a Lotus valve in 2014, an Evolut TAV-in-TAV in 2022, followed by *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis, and finally, a third valve (Acurate neo2) implanted in 2024. The most striking moment of this entire saga wasn't the infection or the triple-stent anatomy; it was the immediate post-procedure echocardiogram showing a mean gradient of **67 mmHg**.

By all conventional metrics, the procedure had failed. Yet, one month later, the gradient plummeted to 27 mmHg. At one year, it held steady at 26 mmHg with the patient completely asymptomatic. How does a patient survive—and thrive—with an initial gradient that looks like critical stenosis? The answer lies in understanding the unique biomechanics of nested valves and distinguishing between acute mechanical constraint and true structural failure.

### The Physics of Nested Frames

When we perform a standard TAVI, we are expanding a stent frame into native calcified tissue. The resistance is predictable. However, when we implant a valve inside another valve (TAV-in-TAV), and then do it *again* (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), we are creating a Russian doll scenario of metal frames. In this specific case, the patient had a Lotus frame, an Evolut frame, and now an Acurate neo2 being deployed into that constrained space.

The immediate post-deployment environment is hostile. You have multiple layers of stent struts pressing against each other. More critically, you have the leaflets of the previous valves (the old Evolut and the ancient Lotus) trapped between the new frame and the old frame. Immediately after deployment, these old leaflets are often immobilized, pinned against the new stent struts, effectively reducing the effective orifice area (EOA). The new valve hasn't fully expanded radially because it is fighting against the rigidity of two prior prostheses.

This creates a phenomenon of acute, severe Prosthesis-Patient Mismatch (PPM). The Doppler velocities skyrocket because the flow is being forced through a temporarily narrowed channel created by folded leaflets and under-expanded frames. In this case report, the indexed AVA was a dismal 0.4 cm²/m² initially. If this were a native valve procedure, we'd be calling cardiothoracic surgery immediately. But here, the "stenosis" was largely artificial—a product of incomplete expansion and leaflet entrapment rather than intrinsic valve dysfunction.

### The Rescue: Post-Dilation and Remodeling

So, how did this patient avoid disaster? The management strategy relied on two pillars: aggressive post-dilation and patience for tissue remodeling.

During the procedure, the team performed high-pressure balloon post-dilation (using a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing). This isn't just about seating the valve; in redo scenarios, it serves to fracture or plastically deform the underlying metal frames slightly and force the immobilized leaflets of the prior valves outward againstthe stent posts. Think of it as "breaking in" the nested assembly. By pushing those old leaflets flat againstthe cage walls, you reclaim lumen area that was initially lost to folds and wrinkles.

However, even with post-dilation, gradients remained high immediately post-op. This is where the biological component kicks in. Over weeks to months, constant shear stress and flow dynamics encourage further expansion of self-expanding nitinol frames (like the Evolut underneath) and remodeling of the trapped tissue. The leaflets that were initially stuck begin to find their groove; micro-fractures in calcium or polymer components may propagate slowly under cyclic loading; and neointimal hyperplasia stabilizes without causing obstruction if alignment was respected during deployment (commissural alignment was meticulously planned via CT in this case).

The trajectory in this case was dramatic but instructive: from 67 mmHg down to 27 mmHg in just four weeks. This rapid normalization suggests that once mechanical constraints are relieved via post-dilation and natural recoil occurs over time ("creep"), hemodynamics can recover significantly even after seemingly disastrous numbers on day zero unless there's thrombus or pannus formation already present before dilation helps push things back toward baseline expectations aren't met right away doesn't always equate to needing emergent surgical explantation—or worse outcomes if recognized early enough as part expected recovery curve!

### Take-Home Messages

*   **Don't Panic at Immediate High Gradients:** In TAV-in-TAV (especially triple-valve) scenarios, an immediate post-procedure mean gradient >50–60 mmHg may reflect transient mechanical constraint from nested frames and immobilized leaflets rather than permanent failure or malposition.
*   **Mechanism Matters:** Acute PPM in redo cases is often driven by "leaflet trapping" where prior valve leaflets fold inward until flow dynamics remodel them outward; distinguish this from thrombosis or undersizing via fluoroscopy/echo mobility checks before considering bailout surgery which carries prohibitive risk here anyway!
*   **Aggressive Post-Dilation Helps:** High-pressure balloon dilatation plays dual role seating plus forcing open restricted channels formed overlapping struts improving initial geometry
