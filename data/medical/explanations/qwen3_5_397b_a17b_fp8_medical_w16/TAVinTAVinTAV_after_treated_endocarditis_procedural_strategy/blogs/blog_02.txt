Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# To Cut or to Cath? Managing Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis in the Prohibitive Surgical Candidate

We all know the dogma: Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE) is a surgical emergency. The 2025 ESC guidelines are clear—when you have a infected bioprosthesis causing heart failure or structural deterioration, you cut it out. Debridement, washout, and a fresh sewn-in valve are the gold standard. But medicine rarely exists in a vacuum of ideal candidates. What happens when your patient has an EuroSCORE II north of 27%, a chest full of scar tissue from multiple prior interventions, and comorbidities that make sternotomy a death sentence? Do we simply offer hospice, or is there a middle ground?

A fascinating case recently published by Merkulov et al. forces us to confront this exact dilemma. They describe a man in his late 70s who didn't just have PVE; he had *recurrent* structural failure on top of it, sitting inside not one, but two previous transcatheter valves. His journey began in 2014 with a Lotus valve, followed by a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut in 2022. Three months later, disaster struck: *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis. After a grueling course of IV antibiotics, the infection cleared, but the damage was done. The valve was destroyed, stenotic, and regurgitant. With surgery deemed prohibitively risky due to his "triple-stented" anatomy and frailty, the team did something bold: they implanted a third valve. A TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV. And remarkably, at one year, he's walking around asymptomatic with a functioning valve.

This case isn't just a technical curiosity; it's a roadmap for managing the "no-option" patient. It challenges us to rethink the absolute contraindication of TAVI in post-PVE scenarios, provided we adhere to strict microbiological and imaging guardrails.

The crux of this decision lies in distinguishing between *active* infection and *sequelae* of treated infection. In this case, the team didn't rush to the cath lab while blood cultures were positive. They waited. They confirmed eradication with repeated negative cultures, Transesophageal Echocardiography (TEE), and Multislice CT (MSCT). Only when there was zero evidence of vegetations, abscesses, or active inflammation did they consider reintervention. The logic is sound: placing a new foreign body into an active septic field is asking for recurrence. Placing it into a sterile, albeit damaged, field is a mechanical solution to a mechanical problem. If you don't intervene here, the alternative isn't surgery; it's certain mortality from pump failure.

The procedural planning here deserves special attention because the anatomy was a nightmare of overlapping metal cages. You aren't just dropping a valve into a native annulus; you are navigating through two previous stent frames that have integrated into the aortic wall. The risk of coronary obstruction skyrockets when you add a third layer of struts and potentially displace old leaflets upward toward the ostia ("neo-skirt" formation). The team utilized high-resolution CT modeling to simulate the final result before making an incision. They specifically chose the ACURATE neo2 platform for its supra-annular design and lower profile, aiming to maximize the effective orifice area while minimizing the height of the new construct relative to the coronaries. They even aligned the commissures deliberately—a nuance often skipped in emergency bailouts but critical for preserving future coronary access in these lifetime-management cases.

Interestingly, the immediate post-procedure hemodynamics looked scary. The mean gradient was initially 67 mmHg with severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM). In many labs, this might trigger panic or immediate rescue maneuvers. However, understanding the physics of expanding a new frame inside two rigid older ones suggests that remodeling takes time. High-pressure post-dilation helped expand the underlying Evolut frame slightly, but true hemodynamic optimization occurred over weeks as the leaflets settled and the geometry stabilized. By one month, gradients dropped significantly, and at one year, they remained stable with no regurgitation. This teaches us patience; instantaneous perfection shouldn't be expected when crushing multiple layers of bioprosthetic tissue.

Of course, this approach is not without peril. We are essentially gambling that the sterilized environment holds and that the new valve won't succumb to early thrombosis or reinfection seeded from microscopic biofilm remnants on the old frames that antibiotics couldn't penetrate.
