Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# To Cut or to Cath? Revisiting Surgical Dogma in Treated Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis

We live in an era where the dogma of "surgery for endocarditis" is being quietly stress-tested by the reality of an aging, high-comorbidity population. The guidelines are clear: for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), especially with heart failure or large vegetations, surgical explantation is the gold standard. But what happens when the "gold standard" carries a mortality risk that approaches certainty? What do you do when the anatomy is so hostile—layered with previous transcatheter frames—that opening the chest means replacing the entire aortic root in a frail octogenarian?

A fascinating case recently published by Merkulov et al. forces us to confront these questions head-on. They describe a man in his late 70s who didn't just undergo one redo TAVI; he survived a **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV** sequence, with the final procedure performed after successfully treated *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis. At one-year follow-up, he is asymptomatic with preserved valve function. This isn't just a technical showcase of stacking valves like Russian nesting dolls; it's a paradigm shift in how we define "cured" infection and manage prohibitive surgical risk.

## The Perfect Storm: A Timeline of Complexity

To appreciate the audacity of this case, you have to look at the timeline. Our patient started his journey in 2014 with a Lotus valve implantation due to severe stenosis and prohibitive surgical risk (three-vessel CAD, COPD, diabetes, GI bleeds). By 2022, that valve degenerated, leading to a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut prosthesis. Things went sideways shortly after: three months post-procedure, he landed back in the hospital with fever and heart failure. Blood cultures grew *E. faecalis*.

This is usually the point where the heart team throws up their hands and calls surgery, or palliative care if surgery is too risky. Instead, they opted for a prolonged course of targeted IV antibiotics (ampicillin-based) followed by oral suppression. Crucially, before considering any reintervention, they waited. They ensured clinical resolution, negative blood cultures, and—most importantly—cleared him via Transoesophageal Echocardiography (TEE) and Multislice CT (MSCT) for any sign of active vegetation or paravalvular abscess.

By mid-2024, despite the infection being dead and buried, the mechanical toll remained. The dual-layered valve apparatus was severely stenotic (mean gradient 49 mmHg) and regurgitant. The EuroSCORE II was a staggering 27%, but the real killer was the anatomy: two deeply integrated stent frames fused to the aortic wall. Surgical explantation would have mandated a total root and ascending aorta replacement—a Herculean task in a patient with recent GI bleeds and COPD. The team made the call: a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2) it is.

## Imaging as the Gatekeeper

The linchpin of this entire strategy wasn't just bravery; it was imaging rigor. You cannot blindly drop a third valve into an infected bed hoping for the best. The authors utilized MSCT not just for sizing, but as a forensic tool to declare the infection "extinct." They looked for abscess cavities, pseudoaneurysms, and vegetations that TTE might miss. Only when TEE and CT concordantly confirmed no active infection did they proceed.

Furthermore, the CT guided the tactical deployment. With two previous frames (Lotus and Evolut) creating a dense metal mesh, coronary obstruction was a looming threat. The team used CT modeling to plan a high implantation strategy for the Acurate neo2. By aligning commissures and deploying high enough to avoid creating a "neo-skirt" below the coronaries, they preserved future access and flow without needing prophylactic coronary wiring or chimneys during the case. It was precision engineering applied to biology.

## The Hemodynamic Tightrope

Let's be honest about hemodynamics: putting a valve inside two other valves invites severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM). Immediately post-procedure, this patient indeed showed signs of severe PPM with a mean gradient of 67 mmHg. This is often where panic sets in in the cath lab. However, physiology takes time to adapt. Over the next month, LV remodeling occurred, gradients dropped to 27 mmHg, and by one year, he was sitting pretty with a mean gradient of 26 mmHg and an EF of 65%.

The success here relied on specific technical nuances: using a self-expanding platform (Acurate neo2) known for favorable hemodynamics in small annuli, aggressive post-dilation to expand the underlying Evolut frame slightly, and meticulous commissural alignment to maximize effective orifice area. They accepted an initial suboptimal gradient knowing that tissue relaxation and remodeling would likely rescue the physiology—and they were right.

## When Guidelines Meet Reality

The 2025 ESC guidelines rightly prioritize surgery for PVE. But guidelines are written for populations; clinicians treat individuals who don't always fit into neat boxes of "low," "intermediate," or "high" risk when their actual risk is "lethal." This case argues that once an infection is microbiologically cured and imaging confirms structural integrity absent of active inflammation, PVE should be managed similarly to structural degeneration if surgery is truly prohibitive due to anatomical hostility rather than just comorbidities alone.

In this patient's world-surgical explantation meant dismantling his entire ascending architecture with layers of scar tissue from three prior interventions plus active healing from recent endocarditis-and sepsis recurrence from such trauma could easily outweigh its benefits-a purely transcatheter bailout became not just reasonable but arguably superior as life saving intervention . It reminds us that lifetime management requires foresight , we must think about next step while doing first . Every TAVI today creates tomorrow challenge ;we owe our patients careful documentation strategic positioning thinking beyond immediate relief toward decades ahead potential needs even if those paths seem improbable now .

## Take-Home Points

*   **"Cured" Must Be Multimodal:** Do not rely on clinical symptoms alone to declare PVE resolved prior to reintervention; require concordance between negative blood cultures, TEE (for vegetations/abscess), and MSCT (for paravalvular extension).
*   **Anatomy Trumps Algorithm:** When layered transcatheter frames make surgical explantation tantamount to root replacement in a frail patient,a carefully planned TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV may offer survival benefit over traditional surgical mandates .
*   **High Implantation Strategy Matters :** In multi-layered procedures , deploy new valves higher within existing frames minimize neo -skirt formation preserve coronary access reduce gradients through better leaflet apposition against old struts .
*   **Expect Delayed Hemodynamic Optimization:** Severe PPM immediately post-deployment does not equal failure ; allow time ventricular remodeling improve gradients weeks following successful multi-valve stacking .
*   **Plan Backwards From Day One:** Lifetime management starts first index procedure consider future options choose devices positions facilitate later retrievals replacements especially younger higher-risk cohorts receiving initial implants .
