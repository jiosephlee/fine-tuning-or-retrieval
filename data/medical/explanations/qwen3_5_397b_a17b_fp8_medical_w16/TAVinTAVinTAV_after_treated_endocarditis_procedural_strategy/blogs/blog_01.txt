Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# The Triple-Stent Dilemma: Navigating TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV Procedures

We often talk about "lifetime management" in structural heart disease as a theoretical concept, something we worry about for the 60-year-old low-risk patient getting their first TAVI today. But what happens when that theory crashes into reality in the form of a septuagenarian needing a *third* transcatheter valve? It sounds like the setup to a medical riddle, but it's becoming our new frontier.

A recent case report from Merkulov et al. throws down the gauntlet: a man in his late 70s who successfully underwent a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure. This isn't just a story of stacking metal; it's a masterclass in navigating the treacherous waters of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM), and the geometric nightmare of three overlapping stent frames. If you think your coronary access worries are bad now, wait until you see what happens when you try to thread a guide through a Russian doll of bioprostheses.

## When One Redo Isn't Enough

Let's set the stage. Our patient is a complex character with a history that reads like a cardiology board exam question: three-vessel CAD with multiple PCIs, COPD, diabetes, and a pacemaker for third-degree AV block. His aortic journey began in 2014 with a 27 mm Lotus valve. Fast forward to 2022, and he developed severe stenosis of that first valve. The team performed a TAV-in-TAV, dropping in a 23 mm Evolut R.

Things looked good initially—his EF bounced back from 42% to 54%. But then came the curveball: *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis just three months later. After six weeks of IV antibiotics and confirmation of sterilization via TEE and CT, the infection cleared, but the damage was done. By early 2024, the dual-valve construct was failing miserably with severe restenosis and regurgitation. The mean gradient was nearly 50 mm Hg, and the valve area had shrunk to a terrifying 0.5 cm².

Surgery was effectively off the table. With an EuroSCORE II over 27%, prior sternotomies (or at least complex vascular surgeries), and two entrenched valve frames fused to the aortic wall, an open explant would likely require total root replacement—a mortality gamble few would take. The only path forward was through the catheter: a third valve inside two existing ones.

## The Geometry of Survival: Planning the Third Layer

Performing a TAV-in-TAV is challenging enough; adding a third layer exponentially increases the risks of coronary obstruction and severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). You are essentially trying to fit a functioning valve inside a narrowing tunnel of metal mesh.

The team's success hinged entirely on multislice CT (MSCT) planning. They weren't just looking at annulus size; they were analyzing the interaction between three distinct frame geometries. The critical question wasn't just "Will it fit?" but "Where will the leaflets sit relative to the coronaries?"

They identified that the minimum lumen diameter within the Evolut frame was only 13.7 mm. To avoid creating a neo-skirt that could bury the coronary ostia or cause immediate obstruction, they devised a strategy of **high implantation**. By targeting deployment at the level of the Evolut leaflets, they aimed to pin those old leaflets against the stent struts, maximizing the effective orifice area (EOA) for the new valve.

Valve selection became paramount here. They chose an Acurate neo2 (Size S). Why? Its supra-annular design places the functional leaflets higher up, away from the constrained inflow of previous frames, and its relatively low profile helped mitigate the risk of further encroaching on coronary space compared to taller alternatives. Commissural alignment was meticulously planned using CT markers to ensure future coronary access remained possible—a crucial consideration for a guy who already has stents in all three major vessels.

## The Procedure and The "Mismatch" Scare

The procedure itself was executed under local anesthesia via femoral and radial access. After predilation under rapid pacing—which confirmed that Coronary flow remained patent despite the crowded anatomy—the Acurate neo2 was deployed.

However, if you stopped reading at the immediate post-op echo, you might think this was a failure. The initial numbers were grim: a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg and an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m². This is classic severe PPM territory. In many contexts, these numbers would send us scrambling for explanations or rescue therapies.

But here is where physiology trumps static numbers. The team recognized that this was likely due to transient edema and incomplete expansion of the innermost layers immediately post-deployment. They held steady. And sure enough, patience paid off dramatically. At one-month follow-up, the mean gradient plummeted to 27 mm Hg, and the AVA opened up to 1.1 cm². By one year, the patient was asymptomatic with stable gradients around 26 mm Hg and no regurgitation. The "failed" hemodynamics were actually just teething pains of a triple-layered system settling into place.

## Lessons from the Edge of Feasibility

This case forces us to confront several hard truths about our evolving field:
*   **Endocarditis doesn't automatically rule out TAVI:** While surgery remains gold standard for PVE, this case demonstrates that in prohibitive surgical candidates with sterilized infections, TAVI can be salvage therapy—even as a third line defense once there is no evidence of vegetations or abscesses on TEE/CT!
*   **High implantation matters:** In multi-layer scenarios where every millimeter counts! Targeting high deployment helps secure old leaflets outwardly prevents them from obstructing flow!
*   **Supra-annular valves win small spaces:** Using devices like Acurate Neo offers better hemodynamic potential when working inside restricted diameters because functional orifice sits above constriction points created by underlying frames!
