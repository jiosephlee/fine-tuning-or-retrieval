Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# The Triple-Stent Dilemma: Navigating TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV Procedures

We often talk about "lifetime management" in structural heart disease as a theoretical concept, something we worry about for the 60-year-old low-risk patient getting their first TAVI today. But what happens when that lifetime gets compressed into a single decade, and the aortic root becomes a Russian nesting doll of metal? We are entering an era where TAV-in-TAV is becoming routine, but TAV-in-TAV-*in*-TAV? That is uncharted territory where the margin for error doesn't just shrink; it virtually disappears.

A recent case report from *BMJ Case Reports* forces us to confront this reality head-on. It describes a man in his late 70s who didn't just survive one or two failed valves; he underwent a third transcatheter implantation (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) after a harrowing course involving structural degeneration and treated prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). By June 2025, one year after his third procedure, he was walking around asymptomatic with a functioning valve. This isn't just a victory for endurance; it's a masterclass in how aggressive imaging planning and strategic valve selection can turn an anatomical nightmare into a survivable pathway.

The patient's journey reads like a gauntlet of modern cardiology's toughest challenges. He received his first valve, a 27 mm Lotus, back in 2014. When that degenerated, he got a 23 mm Evolut inside it in 2022. Then came the curveball: *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis just three months later. After six weeks of IV antibiotics and confirmation of cure, the valve failed again due to restenosis and regurgitation. By 2024, he had two overlapping stent frames, a history of sepsis, and severe symptoms. Surgery was effectively off the table; with an EuroSCORE II over 27%, COPD, diabetes, and deeply integrated prior frames, an open explant would have likely required total root replacement with prohibitive mortality risk. The only way out was through—again.

The genius of this case lies not in the act of dropping a third valve, but in the meticulous preparation that prevented catastrophic coronary obstruction and severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM). When you stack three valves, you aren't just adding metal; you are creating a dense mesh that can seal off the coronaries or create a tiny effective orifice area that leaves the patient in low-flow state despite a "successful" procedure. The team utilized multislice CT (MSCT) not just to measure annulus size, but to model the interaction between the new Acurate neo2 frame and the existing Evolut/Lotus layers. They specifically analyzed the "neo-skirt" height relative to the coronary ostia and planned for high implantation to pin the old leaflets against the wall without blocking flow.

Perhaps most critical was the attention to commissural alignment. In a double-stent scenario, misalignment is annoying; in a triple-stent scenario, it can be fatal for future coronary access. The operators deliberately aligned the new Acurate neo2 commissures with the underlying Evolut frame using fluoroscopic landmarks derived from their CT modeling. They also accepted an initial period of severe PPM immediately post-deployment (mean gradient of 67 mm Hg), trusting that LV remodeling and continued expansion would improve hemodynamics over time—and they were right. By one month, gradients dropped significantly, and at one year, the patient remained stable with no recurrence of infection or heart failure.

This case serves as a stark reminder that as our TAVI population ages and survives longer, we will see more "frankenstein" anatomies. The dogma that PVE always mandates surgery is being challenged by reality; when surgical risk is prohibitive and infection is unequivocally cured, a catheter-based solution can be life-saving. However, this requires moving beyond standard protocols into highly individualized, image-guided strategies where every millimeter of frame height and every degree of rotation matters. We are no longer just treating stenosis; we are navigating complex architectural constraints where the wrong move turns the aortic root into an impenetrable fortress.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is feasible but demands extreme precision:** Sequential reinterventions exponentially increase risks of coronary obstruction and prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM); success relies on detailed MSCT planning to map frame interactions and coronary heights before entering the lab.
*   **Infection history does not automatically preclude redo-TAVI:** In patients with treated prosthetic valve endocarditis who are poor surgical candidates, transcatheter reintervention is a viable option once active infection is ruled out via blood cultures, TEE, and clinical assessment.
*   **Commissural alignment is non-negotiable in multi-layer cases:** Preserving future coronary access in triple-stented anatomy requires deliberate alignment of the new valve commissures with existing frames to prevent locking out future percutaneous coronary interventions.
*   **High implantation strategy can mitigate PPM:** Implanting the new valve slightly higher to pin prior leaflets against the stent struts (rather than letting them fold inward) can maximize the effective orifice area and reduce neo-skirt interference with coronaries.
*   **Expect transient severe PPM:** Immediate post-procedure gradients may be alarmingly high due to restricted expansion within multiple frames; allow time for LV remodeling and gradual frame expansion before declaring procedural failure, provided there is no acute instability.
