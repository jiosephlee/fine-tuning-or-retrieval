# The Triple-Stent Dilemma: Navigating TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV Procedures

We often talk about "lifetime management" in structural heart disease as a theoretical concept, something we worry about for the 60-year-old low-risk patient getting their first TAVI today. But what happens when that lifetime gets compressed into a single decade, and the aortic root becomes a Russian nesting doll of metal? We are entering an era where TAV-in-TAV is becoming routine, but TAV-in-TAV-*in*-TAV? That is uncharted territory where the margin for error doesn't just shrink; it virtually disappears.

A recent case report from *BMJ Case Reports* forces us to confront this reality head-on. It describes a man in his late 70s who didn't just survive one or two failed valves; he underwent a third transcatheter implantation (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) after a harrowing course involving structural degeneration and treated prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). By June 2025, one year after his third procedure, he was walking around asymptomatic with a functioning valve. This isn't just a victory for endurance; it's a masterclass in how aggressive imaging planning and strategic valve selection can turn an anatomical nightmare into a survivable pathway.

The patient's journey reads like a gauntlet of modern cardiology's toughest challenges. He received his first valve, a 27 mm Lotus, back in 2014. When that degenerated, he got a 23 mm Evolut inside it in 2022. Then came the curveball: *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis just three months later. After six weeks of IV antibiotics and confirmation of cure, the valve failed again due to restenosis and regurgitation. By 2024, he had two overlapping stent frames, a history of sepsis, and severe symptoms. Surgery was effectively off the table; with an EuroSCORE II over 27%, COPD, diabetes, and deeply integrated prior frames, an open explant would have likely required total root replacement with prohibitive mortality risk. The only way out was through—again.

The genius of this case lies not in the act of dropping a third valve, but in the meticulous preparation that prevented catastrophic coronary obstruction and severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM). When you stack three valves, you aren't just adding metal; you are creating a dense mesh that can seal off the coronaries or create a tiny effective orifice area that leaves the patient in low-flow state despite a "successful" procedure. The team utilized multislice CT (MSCT) not just to measure annulus size, but to model the interaction between the new Acurate neo2 frame and the existing Evolut/Lotus layers. They specifically analyzed the "neo-skirt" height relative to the coronary ostia and planned for high implantation to pin the old leaflets against the wall without blocking flow.

Perhaps most critical was the attention to commissural alignment. In a double-stent scenario, misalignment is annoying; in a triple-stent scenario, it can be fatal for future coronary access. The operators deliberately aligned the new Acurate neo2 commissures with the underlying Evolut frame using fluoroscopic landmarks derived from their CT modeling. They also accepted an initial period of severe PPM immediately post-deployment (mean gradient of 67 mm Hg), trusting that LV remodeling and continued expansion would improve hemodynamics over time—and they were right. By one month, gradients dropped significantly, and at one year, the patient remained stable with no recurrence of infection or heart failure.

This case serves as a stark reminder that as our TAVI population ages and survives longer, we will see more "frankenstein" anatomies. The dogma that PVE always mandates surgery is being challenged by reality; when surgical risk is prohibitive and infection is unequivocally cured, a catheter-based solution can be life-saving. However, this requires moving beyond standard protocols into highly individualized, image-guided strategies where every millimeter of frame height and every degree of rotation matters. We are no longer just treating stenosis; we are navigating complex architectural constraints where the wrong move turns the aortic root into an impenetrable fortress.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is feasible but demands extreme precision:** Sequential reinterventions exponentially increase risks of coronary obstruction and prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM); success relies on detailed MSCT planning to map frame interactions and coronary heights before entering the lab.
*   **Infection history does not automatically preclude redo-TAVI:** In patients with treated prosthetic valve endocarditis who are poor surgical candidates, transcatheter reintervention is a viable option once active infection is ruled out via blood cultures, TEE, and clinical assessment.
*   **Commissural alignment is non-negotiable in multi-layer cases:** Preserving future coronary access in triple-stented anatomy requires deliberate alignment of the new valve commissures with existing frames to prevent locking out future percutaneous coronary interventions.
*   **High implantation strategy can mitigate PPM:** Implanting the new valve slightly higher to pin prior leaflets against the stent struts (rather than letting them fold inward) can maximize the effective orifice area and reduce neo-skirt interference with coronaries.
*   **Expect transient severe PPM:** Immediate post-procedure gradients may be alarmingly high due to restricted expansion within multiple frames; allow time for LV remodeling and gradual frame expansion before declaring procedural failure, provided there is no acute instability.



# To Cut or to Cath? Managing Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis in the Prohibitive Surgical Candidate

We all know the dogma: Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE) is a surgical emergency. The 2025 ESC guidelines are clear—when you have a infected bioprosthesis causing heart failure or structural deterioration, you cut it out. Debridement, washout, and a fresh sewn-in valve are the gold standard. But medicine rarely exists in a vacuum of ideal candidates. What happens when your patient has an EuroSCORE II north of 27%, a chest full of scar tissue from multiple prior interventions, and comorbidities that make sternotomy a death sentence? Do we simply offer hospice, or is there a middle ground?

A fascinating case recently published by Merkulov et al. forces us to confront this exact dilemma. They describe a man in his late 70s who didn't just have PVE; he had *recurrent* structural failure on top of it, sitting inside not one, but two previous transcatheter valves. His journey began in 2014 with a Lotus valve, followed by a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut in 2022. Three months later, disaster struck: *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis. After a grueling course of IV antibiotics, the infection cleared, but the damage was done. The valve was destroyed, stenotic, and regurgitant. With surgery deemed prohibitively risky due to his "triple-stented" anatomy and frailty, the team did something bold: they implanted a third valve. A TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV. And remarkably, at one year, he's walking around asymptomatic with a functioning valve.

This case isn't just a technical curiosity; it's a roadmap for managing the "no-option" patient. It challenges us to rethink the absolute contraindication of TAVI in post-PVE scenarios, provided we adhere to strict microbiological and imaging guardrails.

The crux of this decision lies in distinguishing between *active* infection and *sequelae* of treated infection. In this case, the team didn't rush to the cath lab while blood cultures were positive. They waited. They confirmed eradication with repeated negative cultures, Transesophageal Echocardiography (TEE), and Multislice CT (MSCT). Only when there was zero evidence of vegetations, abscesses, or active inflammation did they consider reintervention. The logic is sound: placing a new foreign body into an active septic field is asking for recurrence. Placing it into a sterile, albeit damaged, field is a mechanical solution to a mechanical problem. If you don't intervene here, the alternative isn't surgery; it's certain mortality from pump failure.

The procedural planning here deserves special attention because the anatomy was a nightmare of overlapping metal cages. You aren't just dropping a valve into a native annulus; you are navigating through two previous stent frames that have integrated into the aortic wall. The risk of coronary obstruction skyrockets when you add a third layer of struts and potentially displace old leaflets upward toward the ostia ("neo-skirt" formation). The team utilized high-resolution CT modeling to simulate the final result before making an incision. They specifically chose the ACURATE neo2 platform for its supra-annular design and lower profile, aiming to maximize the effective orifice area while minimizing the height of the new construct relative to the coronaries. They even aligned the commissures deliberately—a nuance often skipped in emergency bailouts but critical for preserving future coronary access in these lifetime-management cases.

Interestingly, the immediate post-procedure hemodynamics looked scary. The mean gradient was initially 67 mmHg with severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM). In many labs, this might trigger panic or immediate rescue maneuvers. However, understanding the physics of expanding a new frame inside two rigid older ones suggests that remodeling takes time. High-pressure post-dilation helped expand the underlying Evolut frame slightly, but true hemodynamic optimization occurred over weeks as the leaflets settled and the geometry stabilized. By one month, gradients dropped significantly, and at one year, they remained stable with no regurgitation. This teaches us patience; instantaneous perfection shouldn't be expected when crushing multiple layers of bioprosthetic tissue.

Of course, this approach is not without peril. We are essentially gambling that the sterilized environment holds and that the new valve won't succumb to early thrombosis or reinfection seeded from microscopic biofilm remnants on the old frames that antibiotics couldn't penetrate. # Beyond the Echo: Why MSCT is Non-Negotiable in Complex Valve Reinterventions

We love a good echo. It's portable, dynamic, and gives us real-time hemodynamics that make our brains light up. But if you think transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is going to save you in a "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV" scenario, you are bringing a knife to a nuclear fight.

Consider the case of a man in his late 70s who recently walked out of the cath lab after receiving his *third* transcatheter aortic valve. Yes, you read that right: three valves stacked inside one another. His journey started back in 2014 with a Lotus valve, followed by a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut in 2022, which was then complicated by *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis. After successfully treating the infection, he developed severe restenosis again. The solution? A third valve—an Acurate neo2—implanted inside the previous two.

This isn't just a trivia question for your next board exam; it's a glimpse into the future of structural heart disease. As our TAVI patients live longer and indications expand, we are going to see more "Russian nesting doll" hearts. The takeaway from this incredible case isn't just that it's possible; it's that it would have been impossible without multislice computed tomography (MSCT) acting as the primary roadmap. In complex reinterventions, echo tells you *that* there is a problem, but only CT tells you *how* to fix it without killing the patient.

When you are dealing with three layers of metal mesh, anatomy becomes a guessing game unless you have high-resolution 3D reconstruction. In this case, the team didn't just look at the valve; they measured the minimum lumen diameter through the existing Evolut stent, finding it constricted to 13.7 mm. That number dictated everything. If they had guessed wrong on the sizing or the implantation depth, they risked catastrophic prosthesis-patient mismatch or immediate coronary obstruction. The CT allowed them to visualize the interaction between the old frames and plan a "high implantation" strategy for the new Acurate neo2. By deploying the new valve specifically at the level of the Evolut leaflets, they pinned the old, restricted leaflets against the stent frame, effectively creating a larger effective orifice area and preventing further narrowing.

Perhaps even more critical was the management of coronary risk. In a triple-stented root, the cumulative thickness of the struts creates a formidable barrier to future coronary access and increases the risk of occlusion during deployment. Standard angiography couldn't predict whether pushing a third valve up would create a "neo-skirt" of tissue and metal that would seal off the coronary ostia forever. The pre-procedural CT modeling allowed the team to calculate virtual valve-to-coronary distances and analyze sinus dimensions with precision. They confirmed that despite the layers of metal, there was enough clearance to proceed without prophylactic coronary protection wires—a bold move that paid off because they knew exactly where every millimeter of steel was sitting relative to the left main and RCA.

The result speaks for itself. Immediately post-procedure, there was some expected gradient due to the tight fit (severe prosthesis-patient mismatch initially), but by one month, remodeling occurred, gradients dropped significantly, and at one year, the patient is asymptomatic with preserved ejection fraction and no regurgitation. This wasn't luck; it was engineering guided by imaging. The case proves that while echo is excellent for screening and follow-up, MSCT is non-negotiable for procedural planning in redo TAVI. You cannot navigate a minefield blindfolded, and in these complex anatomies, anything less than a full CT workup is malpractice waiting to happen.

### Key Take-Home Messages

*   **MSCT is Mandatory for Multi-Layer Redos:** In TAV-in-TAV or TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenarios, echocardiography is insufficient for procedural planning; high-resolution CT is required to assess frame interaction, minimum lumen diameter, and true anatomical constraints.
*   **Strategic Implantation Depth Matters:** Precise CT modeling allows operators to choose specific implantation heights (e.g., high implantation) to pin restricted leaflets against existing frames, maximizing effective orifice area and mitigating prosthesis-patient mismatch.
*   **Coronary Access Must Be Modeled Virtually:** Cumulative metal struts increase obstruction risk; CT-derived measurements of valve-to-coronary distance and neo-skirt formation are essential to determine if coronary protection is needed and to preserve future access.
*   **Infection History Doesn't Preclude Redo TAVI:** In patients with treated prosthetic valve endocarditis who are prohibitive surgical candidates, repeat TAVI is feasible provided active infection is ruled out via TEE/CT and inflammatory markers prior to intervention.
*   **Expect Initial Gradients to Improve:** Severe prosthesis-patient mismatch may be present immediately post-deployment in tight anatomies but often resolves with LV remodeling and optimized leaflet motion over weeks to months.



# Chasing Gradients: When a 67 mmHg Mean Gradient Isn't a Disaster

We are taught to fear the number. In the world of aortic stenosis, a mean gradient of 67 mmHg is catastrophic. It screams severe obstruction, impending cardiogenic shock, and procedural failure. If you saw that number pop up on your echo machine immediately after deploying a new transcatheter heart valve, your adrenaline would spike, and the heart team would likely start scrambling for bail-out options.

But what if I told you that in the complex realm of redo TAVI, specifically "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV" procedures, that terrifying number might just be the starting line?

A fascinating case report by Merkulov et al. details the journey of a man in his late 70s who became one of the few patients in the world to undergo a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation. His history is a marathon of structural intervention: a Lotus valve in 2014, an Evolut TAV-in-TAV in 2022, followed by *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis, and finally, a third valve (Acurate neo2) implanted in 2024. The most striking moment of this entire saga wasn't the infection or the triple-stent anatomy; it was the immediate post-procedure echocardiogram showing a mean gradient of **67 mmHg**.

By all conventional metrics, the procedure had failed. Yet, one month later, the gradient plummeted to 27 mmHg. At one year, it held steady at 26 mmHg with the patient completely asymptomatic. How does a patient survive—and thrive—with an initial gradient that looks like critical stenosis? The answer lies in understanding the unique biomechanics of nested valves and distinguishing between acute mechanical constraint and true structural failure.

### The Physics of Nested Frames

When we perform a standard TAVI, we are expanding a stent frame into native calcified tissue. The resistance is predictable. However, when we implant a valve inside another valve (TAV-in-TAV), and then do it *again* (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), we are creating a Russian doll scenario of metal frames. In this specific case, the patient had a Lotus frame, an Evolut frame, and now an Acurate neo2 being deployed into that constrained space.

The immediate post-deployment environment is hostile. You have multiple layers of stent struts pressing against each other. More critically, you have the leaflets of the previous valves (the old Evolut and the ancient Lotus) trapped between the new frame and the old frame. Immediately after deployment, these old leaflets are often immobilized, pinned against the new stent struts, effectively reducing the effective orifice area (EOA). The new valve hasn't fully expanded radially because it is fighting against the rigidity of two prior prostheses.

This creates a phenomenon of acute, severe Prosthesis-Patient Mismatch (PPM). The Doppler velocities skyrocket because the flow is being forced through a temporarily narrowed channel created by folded leaflets and under-expanded frames. In this case report, the indexed AVA was a dismal 0.4 cm²/m² initially. If this were a native valve procedure, we'd be calling cardiothoracic surgery immediately. But here, the "stenosis" was largely artificial—a product of incomplete expansion and leaflet entrapment rather than intrinsic valve dysfunction.

### The Rescue: Post-Dilation and Remodeling

So, how did this patient avoid disaster? The management strategy relied on two pillars: aggressive post-dilation and patience for tissue remodeling.

During the procedure, the team performed high-pressure balloon post-dilation (using a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing). This isn't just about seating the valve; in redo scenarios, it serves to fracture or plastically deform the underlying metal frames slightly and force the immobilized leaflets of the prior valves outward againstthe stent posts. Think of it as "breaking in" the nested assembly. By pushing those old leaflets flat againstthe cage walls, you reclaim lumen area that was initially lost to folds and wrinkles.

However, even with post-dilation, gradients remained high immediately post-op. This is where the biological component kicks in. Over weeks to months, constant shear stress and flow dynamics encourage further expansion of self-expanding nitinol frames (like the Evolut underneath) and remodeling of the trapped tissue. The leaflets that were initially stuck begin to find their groove; micro-fractures in calcium or polymer components may propagate slowly under cyclic loading; and neointimal hyperplasia stabilizes without causing obstruction if alignment was respected during deployment (commissural alignment was meticulously planned via CT in this case).

The trajectory in this case was dramatic but instructive: from 67 mmHg down to 27 mmHg in just four weeks. This rapid normalization suggests that once mechanical constraints are relieved via post-dilation and natural recoil occurs over time ("creep"), hemodynamics can recover significantly even after seemingly disastrous numbers on day zero unless there's thrombus or pannus formation already present before dilation helps push things back toward baseline expectations aren't met right away doesn't always equate to needing emergent surgical explantation—or worse outcomes if recognized early enough as part expected recovery curve!

# Device Selection Strategy: Choosing the Right Tool for a Crowded Aortic Root

We often talk about "lifetime management" in structural heart disease as a theoretical concept, something we discuss at grand rounds for a 60-year-old patient getting their first TAVR. But what does lifetime management actually look like when the clock runs fast, the anatomy gets crowded, and you find yourself staring down the barrel of a *third* transcatheter valve implantation?

Meet a patient in his late 70s who recently underwent a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure. His journey began in 2014 with a Lotus valve, moved to a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut in 2022, and was complicated by *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis. Now, facing severe degeneration of that second prosthesis with a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg and an effective orifice area of just 0.5 cm², his team had to make a critical call. Surgery was prohibitive (EuroSCORE II >27%, hostile chest, fused frames). They had to go back in percutaneously.

The most fascinating part of this case isn't just that they did it; it's *how* they did it. In an aortic root already housing two distinct valve platforms (Lotus and Evolut), the choice of the third device wasn't arbitrary. It was a biomechanical necessity. They selected the Acurate neo2. Why this specific tool for this specific job? Because when space is at a premium, not all valves are created equal.

## The Anatomy of a Crowded Root

Before diving into device selection, we have to appreciate the battlefield. This patient didn't just have a failed valve; he had a "triple-stented" anatomy. The original Lotus valve (known for its unique braided design and active fixation) was overlaid by an Evolut (a self-expanding nitinol frame with tall struts). Adding a third layer creates immediate concerns: coronary obstruction, patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM), and the inability to access coronaries in the future.

Multislice CT (MSCT) revealed a minimum lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm within the existing Evolut frame. That is tight. Any new device needed to navigate this narrow tunnel without crushing the previous frames so hard that it compromised flow, yet provide enough radial force to anchor securely and push the old, infected leaflets out of the way.

## Why the Acurate neo2? A Biomechanical Rationale

In this high-stakes scenario, the Heart Team bypassed other common platforms in favor of the Acurate neo2 (size S). The decision hinged on three specific engineering features that address the unique constraints of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure.

First, consider **frame height and profile**. The Evolut platform has relatively tall cells and struts designed for anchoring in native annuli. Stacking another tall-frame device on top increases the vertical footprint significantly, raising the risk that the new valve's skirt or frame will cover the coronary ostia or create a "neo-skirt" that blocks future catheterization. The Acurate neo2 boasts one of the lowest profile frames available. Its short stent height allows for precise placement within the confines of the previous Evolut frame without extending unnecessarily high into the sinuses or low into the LVOT.

Second is the **supra-annular leaflet positioning**. Unlike intra-annular designs where leaflets sit inside the stent frame (reducing the internal diameter), the Acurate neo2 positions its leaflets abovethe annulus/frame interface. In a tube that is already narrowed to ~13 mm by two previous metal cages, maximizing every millimeter of Effective Orifice Area (EOA) is crucial to avoid severe PPM. By placing the leaflets supra-annularly, the device preserves more internal flow area compared to forcing leaflets inside an already constricted cage.

Finally, **commissural alignment** became non-negotiable. With two layers of mesh already present, adding a third misaligned layer could turn coronary re-access into an impossible puzzle. The Acurate neo2 offers predictable commissural alignment capabilities. In this case, pre-procedural CT modeling allowed the operators to align the new valve's commissures with those of the underlying Evolut frame. This strategic alignment minimized metal overlap at critical points and preserved potential pathways for future coronary engagement—a vital consideration given this patient's extensive history of PCI and three-vessel disease.

The procedure itself was a masterclass in precision planning. Using CT guidance, they aimed for a high implantation depth relative to the Evolut frame. This wasn't just about seating; it was about mechanics.
