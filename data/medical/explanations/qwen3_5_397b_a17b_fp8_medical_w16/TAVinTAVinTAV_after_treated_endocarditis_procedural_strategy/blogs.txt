# The Triple-Stent Dilemma: Navigating TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV Procedures

We often talk about "lifetime management" in structural heart disease as a theoretical concept, something we worry about for the 60-year-old low-risk patient getting their first TAVI today. But what happens when that theory crashes into reality in the form of a septuagenarian needing a *third* transcatheter valve? It sounds like the setup to a medical riddle, but it's becoming our new frontier.

A recent case report from Merkulov et al. throws down the gauntlet: a man in his late 70s who successfully underwent a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure. This isn't just a story of stacking metal; it's a masterclass in navigating the treacherous waters of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM), and the geometric nightmare of three overlapping stent frames. If you think your coronary access worries are bad now, wait until you see what happens when you try to thread a guide through a Russian doll of bioprostheses.

## When One Redo Isn't Enough

Let's set the stage. Our patient is a complex character with a history that reads like a cardiology board exam question: three-vessel CAD with multiple PCIs, COPD, diabetes, and a pacemaker for third-degree AV block. His aortic journey began in 2014 with a 27 mm Lotus valve. Fast forward to 2022, and he developed severe stenosis of that first valve. The team performed a TAV-in-TAV, dropping in a 23 mm Evolut R.

Things looked good initially—his EF bounced back from 42% to 54%. But then came the curveball: *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis just three months later. After six weeks of IV antibiotics and confirmation of sterilization via TEE and CT, the infection cleared, but the damage was done. By early 2024, the dual-valve construct was failing miserably with severe restenosis and regurgitation. The mean gradient was nearly 50 mm Hg, and the valve area had shrunk to a terrifying 0.5 cm².

Surgery was effectively off the table. With an EuroSCORE II over 27%, prior sternotomies (or at least complex vascular surgeries), and two entrenched valve frames fused to the aortic wall, an open explant would likely require total root replacement—a mortality gamble few would take. The only path forward was through the catheter: a third valve inside two existing ones.

## The Geometry of Survival: Planning the Third Layer

Performing a TAV-in-TAV is challenging enough; adding a third layer exponentially increases the risks of coronary obstruction and severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). You are essentially trying to fit a functioning valve inside a narrowing tunnel of metal mesh.

The team's success hinged entirely on multislice CT (MSCT) planning. They weren't just looking at annulus size; they were analyzing the interaction between three distinct frame geometries. The critical question wasn't just "Will it fit?" but "Where will the leaflets sit relative to the coronaries?"

They identified that the minimum lumen diameter within the Evolut frame was only 13.7 mm. To avoid creating a neo-skirt that could bury the coronary ostia or cause immediate obstruction, they devised a strategy of **high implantation**. By targeting deployment at the level of the Evolut leaflets, they aimed to pin those old leaflets against the stent struts, maximizing the effective orifice area (EOA) for the new valve.

Valve selection became paramount here. They chose an Acurate neo2 (Size S). Why? Its supra-annular design places the functional leaflets higher up, away from the constrained inflow of previous frames, and its relatively low profile helped mitigate the risk of further encroaching on coronary space compared to taller alternatives. Commissural alignment was meticulously planned using CT markers to ensure future coronary access remained possible—a crucial consideration for a guy who already has stents in all three major vessels.

## The Procedure and The "Mismatch" Scare

The procedure itself was executed under local anesthesia via femoral and radial access. After predilation under rapid pacing—which confirmed that Coronary flow remained patent despite the crowded anatomy—the Acurate neo2 was deployed.

However, if you stopped reading at the immediate post-op echo, you might think this was a failure. The initial numbers were grim: a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg and an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m². This is classic severe PPM territory. In many contexts, these numbers would send us scrambling for explanations or rescue therapies.

But here is where physiology trumps static numbers. The team recognized that this was likely due to transient edema and incomplete expansion of the innermost layers immediately post-deployment. They held steady. And sure enough, patience paid off dramatically. At one-month follow-up, the mean gradient plummeted to 27 mm Hg, and the AVA opened up to 1.1 cm². By one year, the patient was asymptomatic with stable gradients around 26 mm Hg and no regurgitation. The "failed" hemodynamics were actually just teething pains of a triple-layered system settling into place.

## Lessons from the Edge of Feasibility

This case forces us to confront several hard truths about our evolving field:
*   **Endocarditis doesn't automatically rule out TAVI:** While surgery remains gold standard for PVE, this case demonstrates that in prohibitive surgical candidates with sterilized infections, TAVI can be salvage therapy—even as a third line defense once there is no evidence of vegetations or abscesses on TEE/CT!

# To Cut or to Cath? Revisiting Surgical Dogma in Treated Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis

We live in an era where the dogma of "surgery for endocarditis" is being quietly stress-tested by the reality of an aging, high-comorbidity population. The guidelines are clear: for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), especially with heart failure or large vegetations, surgical explantation is the gold standard. But what happens when the "gold standard" carries a mortality risk that approaches certainty? What do you do when the anatomy is so hostile—layered with previous transcatheter frames—that opening the chest means replacing the entire aortic root in a frail octogenarian?

A fascinating case recently published by Merkulov et al. forces us to confront these questions head-on. They describe a man in his late 70s who didn't just undergo one redo TAVI; he survived a **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV** sequence, with the final procedure performed after successfully treated *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis. At one-year follow-up, he is asymptomatic with preserved valve function. This isn't just a technical showcase of stacking valves like Russian nesting dolls; it's a paradigm shift in how we define "cured" infection and manage prohibitive surgical risk.

## The Perfect Storm: A Timeline of Complexity

To appreciate the audacity of this case, you have to look at the timeline. Our patient started his journey in 2014 with a Lotus valve implantation due to severe stenosis and prohibitive surgical risk (three-vessel CAD, COPD, diabetes, GI bleeds). By 2022, that valve degenerated, leading to a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut prosthesis. Things went sideways shortly after: three months post-procedure, he landed back in the hospital with fever and heart failure. Blood cultures grew *E. faecalis*.

This is usually the point where the heart team throws up their hands and calls surgery, or palliative care if surgery is too risky. Instead, they opted for a prolonged course of targeted IV antibiotics (ampicillin-based) followed by oral suppression. Crucially, before considering any reintervention, they waited. They ensured clinical resolution, negative blood cultures, and—most importantly—cleared him via Transoesophageal Echocardiography (TEE) and Multislice CT (MSCT) for any sign of active vegetation or paravalvular abscess.

By mid-2024, despite the infection being dead and buried, the mechanical toll remained. The dual-layered valve apparatus was severely stenotic (mean gradient 49 mmHg) and regurgitant. The EuroSCORE II was a staggering 27%, but the real killer was the anatomy: two deeply integrated stent frames fused to the aortic wall. Surgical explantation would have mandated a total root and ascending aorta replacement—a Herculean task in a patient with recent GI bleeds and COPD. The team made the call: a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2) it is.

## Imaging as the Gatekeeper

The linchpin of this entire strategy wasn't just bravery; it was imaging rigor. You cannot blindly drop a third valve into an infected bed hoping for the best. The authors utilized MSCT not just for sizing, but as a forensic tool to declare the infection "extinct." They looked for abscess cavities, pseudoaneurysms, and vegetations that TTE might miss. Only when TEE and CT concordantly confirmed no active infection did they proceed.

Furthermore, the CT guided the tactical deployment. With two previous frames (Lotus and Evolut) creating a dense metal mesh, coronary obstruction was a looming threat. The team used CT modeling to plan a high implantation strategy for the Acurate neo2. By aligning commissures and deploying high enough to avoid creating a "neo-skirt" below the coronaries, they preserved future access and flow without needing prophylactic coronary wiring or chimneys during the case. It was precision engineering applied to biology.

## The Hemodynamic Tightrope

Let's be honest about hemodynamics: putting a valve inside two other valves invites severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM). Immediately post-procedure, this patient indeed showed signs of severe PPM with a mean gradient of 67 mmHg. This is often where panic sets in in the cath lab. However, physiology takes time to adapt. Over the next month, LV remodeling occurred, gradients dropped to 27 mmHg, and by one year, he was sitting pretty with a mean gradient of 26 mmHg and an EF of 65%.

The success here relied on specific technical nuances: using a self-expanding platform (Acurate neo2) known for favorable hemodynamics in small annuli, aggressive post-dilation to expand the underlying Evolut frame slightly, and meticulous commissural alignment to maximize effective orifice area. They accepted an initial suboptimal gradient knowing that tissue relaxation and remodeling would likely rescue the physiology—and they were right.

## When Guidelines Meet Reality

The 2025 ESC guidelines rightly prioritize surgery for PVE. But guidelines are written for populations; clinicians treat individuals who don't always fit into neat boxes of "low," "intermediate," or "high" risk when their actual risk is "lethal." This case argues that once an infection is microbiologically cured and imaging confirms structural integrity absent of active inflammation, PVE should be managed similarly to structural degeneration if surgery is truly prohibitive due to anatomical hostility rather than just comorbidities alone.

In this patient's world-surgical explantation meant dismantling his entire ascending architecture with layers of scar tissue from three prior interventions plus active healing from recent endocarditis-and sepsis recurrence from such trauma could easily outweigh its benefits-a purely transcatheter bailout became not just reasonable but arguably superior as life saving intervention . It reminds us that lifetime management requires foresight , we must think about next step while doing first . Every TAVI today creates tomorrow challenge ;we owe our patients careful documentation strategic positioning thinking beyond immediate relief toward decades ahead potential needs even if those paths seem improbable now .

## Take-Home Points

*   **"Cured" Must Be Multimodal:** Do not rely on clinical symptoms alone to declare PVE resolved prior to reintervention; require concordance between negative blood cultures, TEE (for vegetations/abscess), and MSCT (for paravalvular extension).
*   **Anatomy Trumps Algorithm:** When layered transcatheter frames make surgical explantation tantamount to root replacement in a frail patient,a carefully planned TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV may offer survival benefit over traditional surgical mandates .
*   **High Implantation Strategy Matters :** In multi-layered procedures , deploy new valves higher within existing frames minimize neo -skirt formation preserve coronary access reduce gradients through better leaflet apposition against old struts .
*   **Expect Delayed Hemodynamic Optimization:** Severe PPM immediately post-deployment does not equal failure ; allow time ventricular remodeling improve gradients weeks following successful multi-valve stacking .
*   **Plan Backwards From Day One:** Lifetime management starts first index procedure consider future options choose devices positions facilitate later retrievals replacements especially younger higher-risk cohorts receiving initial implants .



# Don't Lose the Coronaries: Advanced CT Planning for Multi-Layered Valve Interventions

We often talk about "lifetime management" in structural heart disease as a buzzword, but rarely do we see what it actually looks like when the clock runs down on multiple layers of metal. Consider the case of a man in his late 70s who didn't just have a failed TAVI; he had *two* failed TAVIs, separated by a bout of *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis. By 2024, this patient presented with severe stenosis inside a Lotus valve, which was inside an Evolut valve. The solution? A third transcatheter valve—an Acurate neo2—implanted via a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV strategy.

What makes this case terrifyingly fascinating isn't just the sheer number of prostheses stacked in the aortic root; it's that he survived a prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) episode only to require another intervention shortly after. In a world where guidelines still lean heavily toward surgery for PVE, this patient was deemed prohibitively high-risk for open repair due to his comorbidities and the "cemented" nature of his previous valves. The team couldn't cut him open; they had to navigate a tunnel of metal without losing the coronaries or creating a valve so small it would never open. The secret weapon wasn't a new gadget, but rigorous, metric-driven CT planning that turned a potential catastrophe into a one-year success story.

The primary enemy in any redo-TAVI, but especially in a triple-stack scenario, is the "neo-skirt." When you deploy a new valve inside old ones, the leaflets of the innermost valve get pushed outward against the stent struts of the outermost valve. If you aren't careful, these displaced leaflets form a curtain that covers your coronary ostia, leading to instant, fatal obstruction. In this case, the team faced two overlapping frames and infected tissue that had likely altered the geometry of the root. They couldn't rely on standard depths or "feel." They needed hard data from multislice CT (MSCT) to measure the exact distance between the anticipated neo-skirt and the coronary buttons. The scan revealed a minimum lumen diameter of just 13.7 mm within the Evolut frame, confirming severe compromise but also providing the roadmap for survival.

The procedural strategy hinged on two critical concepts derived directly from that CT workup: high implantation and commissural alignment. Instead of seating the new Acurate neo2 deep within the annulus, the operators deliberately implanted it higher, aligning it with the leaflets of the existing Evolut valve. This maneuver pinned the old leaflets against the frame in a way that prevented them from billowing out and obstructing flow, effectively managing the neo-skirt risk without needing bail-out techniques like BASILICA or chimney stenting. Furthermore, by aligning the commissures of the new valve with those of the old ones using fluoroscopic landmarks guided by pre-procedural modeling, they preserved future coronary access—a crucial consideration for a patient with three-vessel CAD who has already undergone multiple PCIs.

The immediate post-procedure echo showed what we often fear in these tight spaces: severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM) with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. It looked like a failure. However, thanks to the optimized expansion and alignment strategy, physiology won out over initial numbers. Within one month, remodeling occurred, gradients dropped to 27 mm Hg, and at one year, the patient remains asymptomatic with preserved ejection fraction and no recurrence of infection. This case proves that even in the most hostile anatomical environments—triple-stented roots post-endocarditis—precision imaging can carve out a path where surgery is impossible and standard TAVI would be fatal.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **CT is Non-Negotiable in Redo-TAVI:** You cannot fly blind in multi-layered valves. Multislice CT must be used to calculate valve-to-coronary distance, analyze sinus dimensions, and model frame interactions to predict neo-skirt formation before touching the patient.
*   **High Implantation Strategy:** In TAV-in-TAV scenarios, deploying the new valve at a higher level (aligning with existing leaflets) can pin old leaflets against stent struts, reducing effective orifice area loss and preventing coronary obstruction compared to deep anchoring.
*   **Commissural Alignment Preserves Future Access:** With every added layer of metal, coronary re-access becomes exponentially harder Deliberate commissural alignment during deployment is essential to maintain windows for future PCI or bailout procedures.
*   **Don't Panic Over Immediate Gradients:** Severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM) on immediate post-deployment echo may improve significantly over weeks due to leaflet conditioning and LV remodeling; avoid premature judgment of hemodynamic failure if anatomy was optimized.
*   **PVE Doesn't Always Mean Surgery:** In patients with treated endocarditis who are prohibitive surgical candidates due to complex anatomy (multiple prior valves) and comorbidities, TAVI can be safely performed once infection is confirmed eradicated via TEE and clinical markers.



# The Phantom Gradient: Why You Shouldn't Panic Over Day-1 Gradients in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV

We've all been there. The final angiogram looks pristine, the valve is seated perfectly, and there's zero regurgitation. You breathe a sigh of relief, only to have your breath knocked out by the immediate post-procedure echo. The numbers are terrifying: a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg and an indexed valve area suggesting severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM). In a standard case, this might trigger an immediate call back to the lab or a frantic consult with cardiothoracic surgery. But what if those scary numbers are a mirage?

A fascinating case recently published by Merkulov et al. forces us to rethink our reaction to early hemodynamics in complex "valve-in-valve" scenarios. They describe a man in his late 70s who became perhaps the first reported patient to undergo a **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV** procedure following treated prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). His journey is a marathon of structural intervention: a Lotus valve in 2014, an Evolut TAV-in-TAV in 2022, a bout of *Enterococcus faecalis* endocarditis, and finally, a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2) in 2024.

What makes this case a masterclass in patience and physiology isn't just the technical feat of stacking three valves; it's the bizarre hemodynamic trajectory immediately following that third implant. On day one, the echo screamed severe stenosis. One month later? The gradients had plummeted, and the patient was asymptomatic. At one year, he remains robust with excellent valve function. This "phantom gradient" teaches us that in the world of multi-layered stents, immediate echo data can be misleadingly pessimistic, and premature re-intervention could be a catastrophic error.

## The Perfect Storm: Three Valves and a History of Endocarditis

To appreciate why the gradients behaved so strangely, we have to respect the anatomy they were working with. This wasn't just a failed bioprosthesis; it was a fortress of metal and tissue. The patient had a Lotus valve (known for its unique adaptive seal and rigid frame) overlaid by an Evolut PRO (a self-expanding nitinol frame), which then developed severe restenosis after treated endocarditis.

When you are dealing with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, you aren't just deploying a valve into native tissue or a single failed ring. You are navigating a tunnel of overlapping struts where coronary access is threatened, effective orifice area (EOA) is naturally compromised, and the risk of crushing previous frames unevenly is high. The team faced prohibitive surgical risk (EuroSCORE II >27%) due to COPD, diabetes, prior GI bleeds, and the sheer nightmare of dissecting out three integrated valve frames from the aortic root. Surgery would have likely required total root replacement—a bridge too far for this patient.

The decision to proceed with a third TAVI was bold but grounded in rigorous CT planning. They needed to ensure that adding a third layer wouldn't obliterate coronary flow or create an unsurmountable gradient. Using multislice CT (MSCT), they modeled the interaction between the new Acurate neo2 and the existing Evolut/Lotus stack, specifically looking at the "neo-skirt" height relative to the coronaries. They opted for a high implantation strategy to pin the old leaflets against the wall without creating a new barrier to coronary flow. It was a precision strike in an already crowded room.

## The Phantom Gradient: Day 1 vs. Month 1

Here is where the story gets counterintuitive—and where many clinicians might lose their cool. Immediately after deploying the Acurate neo2 and performing post-dilation, the operators did everything right angiographically: no leak, good position, patent coronaries. Yet, the transthoracic echo told a different story.

The immediate post-op metrics were alarming:
*   **Mean Gradient:** 67 mm Hg
*   **AVA:** 0.9 cm² (Indexed 0.4 cm²/m²)
*   **DVI:** 0.24

By every standard metric (VARC-3 criteria), this looked like severe prosthesis-patient mismatch or even acute valve thrombosis/failure. In a less experienced hand, or without understanding the mechanics of nested frames, this finding could easily prompt emergency balloon fracturing, surgical salvage, or aggressive anticoagulation trials. Instead, the team held steady. They recognized that they were dealing with rigid, overlapping cages that hadn't yet found their equilibrium.

Fast forward four weeks. The patient was asymptomatic with good exercise tolerance. The repeat echo showed a dramatic transformation:
*   **Mean Gradient:** Dropped to 27 mm Hg
*   **AVA:** Improved to 1.1 cm²
*   **Clinical Status:** NYHA Class I

At one year follow-up, these gains were sustained (Mean gradient 26 mm Hg), proving that the initial "stenosis" was transient. So, what happened between Day 1 and Day 30?
