Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 3: ## Strategic Decision-Making: Surgical vs. Transcatheter Reintervention

The management of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) typically adheres to a clear hierarchical algorithm, yet this case exemplifies the critical juncture where strict guideline adherence must yield to individualized risk-benefit analysis. According to the 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines, surgical valve replacement remains the Class I recommendation for PVE, particularly when complicated by heart failure, uncontrolled infection, or large vegetations. Surgery offers the distinct advantage of complete debridement of infected tissue and removal of the foreign material serving as a nidus for bacterial colonization. However, these guidelines explicitly emphasize that management must be tailored to the patient's specific surgical risk and anatomical feasibility. In this case, while the patient had successfully completed a course of targeted antimicrobial therapy for *Enterococcus faecalis* with confirmed microbiological cure and no evidence of active vegetation on transoesophageal echocardiography, the decision to pursue a third transcatheter intervention rather than surgery was driven by prohibitive operative risk and insurmountable anatomical barriers.

Risk stratification provided the quantitative foundation for deviating from the standard surgical pathway. The patient's EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, placing him in a category of extremely high operative mortality. This elevated score was not merely a reflection of his age but was compounded by a heavy burden of comorbidities, including three-vessel coronary artery disease with multiple prior percutaneous interventions, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and a history of recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022. The presence of COPD significantly increases the risk of prolonged postoperative ventilation and respiratory failure, while the history of gastrointestinal bleeding complicates the mandatory perioperative anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy required after surgical valve replacement. Furthermore, the patient's frailty, evidenced by his limited exercise tolerance prior to intervention, suggested a poor likelihood of recovering functional status following a major sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass.

Beyond the numerical risk scores, the anatomical reality of the "triple-stented" aortic root presented a formidable technical barrier to surgical explantation. The patient already harbored two failed transcatheter valves: a Lotus valve implanted in 2014 and an Evolut prosthesis placed in 2022. Over nearly a decade, these devices had become deeply integrated into the aortic wall and native annulus through endothelialization and tissue ingrowth. Attempting to surgically extract these frames would carry an exorbitant risk of catastrophic injury to the aortic root, potentially necessitating a total arch replacement or Bentall procedure (replacement of the aortic valve, ascending aorta, and coronary arteries). Such an extended procedure would drastically increase cross-clamp times and bleeding risks, rendering survival unlikely given the patient's comorbid profile. Consequently, the Heart Team concluded that the morbidity associated with attempting to remove the infected-but-cured prosthetic material outweighed the benefits of source control in an already sterilized field.

With surgery deemed prohibitively dangerous, the feasibility of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV strategy became the focal point of deliberation. The decision to proceed relied heavily on advanced multimodality imaging to ensure that a third valve could be safely deployed without causing coronary obstruction or severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM). Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) was pivotal in this assessment, revealing a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the existing Evolut frame and confirming adequate virtual valve-to-coronary distances. The imaging demonstrated that despite the presence of two previous stent frames, there was sufficient space to anchor a new self-expanding prosthesis (Acurate neo2) while maintaining patency of the coronary ostia. Crucially, the absence of active infection on imaging meant that implanting a new bioprosthesis into a sterile environment was oncologically and microbiologically sound, shifting the primary challenge from infection control to hemodynamic optimization and mechanical stability.

Ultimately, the choice to proceed with transcatheter reintervention was solidified through shared decision-making involving the patient, his family, and the multidisciplinary Heart Team. This process balanced the theoretical ideal of surgical debridement againstthe pragmatic goals of extending life expectancy and preserving quality of life. For a patient in his late 70s with significant comorbidities, the promise of avoiding sternotomy, minimizing recovery time, and rapidly alleviating symptoms of heart failure held greater value than attempting a curative but likely fatal surgical revision. The team transparently discussed the uncertainties inherent in placing a third valve—including future coronary access difficulties and limited options for subsequent failures—but determined that immediate relief from severe symptomatic stenosis offered the best chance for meaningful survival. This case underscores that in complex structural heart disease, particularly following treated endocarditis in high-risk candidates, therapeutic success is defined not just by adherence to guidelines, but by navigating anatomical constraints through precise imaging and collaborative clinical judgment.
