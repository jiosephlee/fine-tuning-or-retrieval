## Clinical Presentation and Complex Patient History

The patient, a man in his late 70s, presented with a clinical picture indicative of severe left ventricular outflow tract obstruction and consequent heart failure. His chief complaints included exertional dyspnea, limiting his walking distance to 200–300 meters, alongside a spastic sensation in the left chest suggestive of angina equivalents. These symptoms were accompanied by dizziness and profound fatigue, reflecting a state of low cardiac output. This symptomatic deterioration occurred against a backdrop of extensive multimorbidity that significantly elevated his operative risk profile. His medical history included three-vessel coronary artery disease managed with multiple percutaneous coronary interventions between 2009 and 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022. Furthermore, the presence of a third-degree atrioventricular block requiring permanent pacemaker implantation in 2014 added another layer of procedural complexity. Collectively, these comorbidities established a "prohibitive surgical risk" status, rendering conventional surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) or redo sternotomy excessively hazardous due to the high probability of perioperative mortality and morbidity.

The trajectory of this patient's valvular disease began a decade prior to the index presentation. In 2014, he underwent his first transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) with a 27 mm Lotus valve to treat severe native aortic stenosis. While initially successful, the bioprosthesis eventually succumbed to structural valve deterioration. By late 2021, the patient experienced worsening dyspnea, and echocardiography revealed severe bioprosthetic stenosis with a precipitous rise in the mean transvalvular gradient to 83 mm Hg and a decline in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) from 54% to 42%. Consequently, in May 2022, he underwent a valve-in-valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedure utilizing a 23 mm Evolut prosthesis. Although this intervention successfully restored hemodynamics and improved LVEF to 54%, it was complicated by a significant vascular injury—a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm—necessitating open vascular surgical repair. This complication further underscored the fragility of his vasculature and the heightened risks associated with any future invasive maneuvers.

The clinical course took a critical turn just three months after the second TAVI when the patient developed persistent fever and worsening heart failure symptoms. Diagnostic workup confirmed prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*, evidenced by positive blood cultures and echocardiographic signs of severe restenosis with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. The management of PVE in the context of recent TAVI presents a formidable challenge; while surgical explantation is often the guideline-recommended standard for PVE complicated by heart failure, this patient's prohibitive surgical risk dictated a conservative approach. He was treated with a rigorous antimicrobial regimen consisting of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy followed by oral suppression, guided by infectious disease specialists. Crucially, prior to considering further mechanical intervention in 2024, comprehensive reassessment—including repeated blood cultures, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), and multislice computed tomography (MSCT)—confirmed the complete resolution of the infection with no evidence of active vegetations or paravalvular abscesses. This confirmation of sterility was an absolute prerequisite for proceeding with another transcatheter intervention, as implanting new hardware into an infected field would carry catastrophic consequences.

Despite the successful eradication of the infection, the structural integrity of the double-layered valve apparatus continued to degrade. By January 2024, the patient exhibited signs of recurrent hemodynamic compromise characterized by severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean gradient had climbed to 49 mm Hg, although his LVEF had recovered robustly to 66%, likely due to optimized medical therapy and myocardial recovery post-infection control. Upon admission for the index procedure in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography documented persistent grade 2–3 intraprosthetic regurgitation, a critically reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², and sustained elevation in transvalvular gradients. The visualization of two overlapping bioprosthetic frames highlighted the anatomical constraint facing the heart team: the need to deploy a third valve within an already crowded annulus without compromising coronary flow or exacerbating patient-prosthesis mismatch. This specific convergence of treated endocarditis, multiple prior failed transcatheter valves, and extreme surgical risk defined the unique clinical scenario necessitating a highly specialized TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV strategy.

## Pathophysiology of Serial Valve Failure and Diagnostic Imaging

The pathophysiology of valve failure in this patient represents a complex convergence of structural degeneration and prior infectious insult within a constrained, multi-layered anatomical environment. In the setting of a "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV" scenario, the mechanisms driving dysfunction extend beyond typical calcific wear. The initial deterioration of the 2014 Lotus valve likely stemmed from standard bioprosthetic aging, but the subsequent failure of the 2022 Evolut prosthesis was accelerated by the episode of *Enterococcus faecalis* prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Even after microbiological cure, PVE can induce permanent leaflet fibrosis and scarring, leading to mechanical restriction that mimics or exacerbates stenosis. When a third valve is implanted inside two existing frames, the cumulative effect of these rigid, overlapping stent structures creates a fixed geometry that limits leaflet excursion. The resultant hemodynamic profile is characterized by severe narrowing, where the effective orifice area (EOA) is compromised not just by tissue overgrowth, but by the physical confinement imposed by the innermost previous valve frame interacting with the new prosthesis.

Accurate hemodynamic assessment in such triple-stented anatomy requires careful interpretation of echocardiographic data to distinguish between true stenosis and prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM). In this case, immediate post-procedural transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed a paradoxically high mean gradient of 67 mm Hg despite the absence of regurgitation, alongside a critically small indexed EOA of 0.4 cm²/m². This finding initially suggested severe PPM, a common risk in valve-in-valve procedures where the internal diameter of the failing valve dictates the maximum possible opening of the new device. However, follow-up imaging at one month demonstrated a significant reduction in gradients to 27 mm Hg and an increase in EOA to 1.1 cm². This temporal improvement highlights a critical physiological concept: immediate post-deployment gradients in multi-layered implants may be artificially elevated due to incomplete expansion of the underlying metal frames. Over time, continued radial force from the self-expanding third valve, potentially aided by post-dilation, allows for gradual remodeling and expansion of the prior stents, thereby optimizing flow dynamics and resolving transient obstruction.

Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) serves as the cornerstone for procedural planning in these complex reinterventions, providing spatial resolution that echocardiography cannot match. For this patient, MSCT was indispensable in quantifying the minimum lumen diameter (MLD) within the existing Evolut prosthesis, which measured only 13.7 mm. This precise measurement confirmed severe hemodynamic compromise and dictated the selection of a smaller-sized Acurate neo2 valve capable of functioning within such a narrow channel. Beyond sizing, MSCT allowed for a detailed analysis of frame interaction, visualizing how the struts of three distinct valves overlapped. This visualization is crucial for predicting potential points of stress concentration and ensuring that the delivery system can traverse the tortuous path created by multiple layers of metal without causing displacement or embolization.

A paramount concern in serial TAVI procedures is the risk of coronary artery obstruction, which increases exponentially with each added layer of valve tissue and stent material. The implantation of a new valve pushes the leaflets of the preceding prosthesis outward against the aortic wall and sinus of Valsalva, creating a "neo-skirt" of tissue that can occlude coronary ostia. In this case, preprocedural CT analysis focused heavily on measuring the virtual valve-to-coronary distance and assessing sinus dimensions to ensure adequate clearance. The evaluation confirmed that despite the triple-stented configuration, there was sufficient space to accommodate the displaced leaflets without compromising flow to the coronary arteries. This assessment permitted the team to proceed without prophylactic coronary protection techniques, such as chimney stenting or wire protection, relying instead on strategic high implantation to keep the neo-skirt below the level of the coronary ostia.

Finally, distinguishing between active infection and sterile structural failure is a prerequisite for any reintervention in a patient with a history of PVE. Proceeding with a transcatheter procedure in the presence of active endocarditis carries an unacceptably high risk of seeding the new prosthesis and causing recurrent sepsis. Therefore, diagnostic rigor required a multimodal approach integrating clinical markers, microbiology, and advanced imaging. In this case, resolution was confirmed through negative serial blood cultures following a completed course of targeted antimicrobial therapy and normalization inflammatory markers Crucially transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) provided high-resolution visualization to exclude vegetations abscesses or paravalvular leaks while MSCT ruled out perivalvular extension or fistula formation Only after satisfying these stringent criteria could safely attribute valvedysfunction purelyto mechanicalanddegenerative causes justifyingthethirdtranscatheterintervention

## Strategic Decision-Making: Surgical vs. Transcatheter Reintervention

The management of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) typically adheres to a clear hierarchical algorithm, yet this case exemplifies the critical juncture where strict guideline adherence must yield to individualized risk-benefit analysis. According to the 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines, surgical valve replacement remains the Class I recommendation for PVE, particularly when complicated by heart failure, uncontrolled infection, or large vegetations. Surgery offers the distinct advantage of complete debridement of infected tissue and removal of the foreign material serving as a nidus for bacterial colonization. However, these guidelines explicitly emphasize that management must be tailored to the patient's specific surgical risk and anatomical feasibility. In this case, while the patient had successfully completed a course of targeted antimicrobial therapy for *Enterococcus faecalis* with confirmed microbiological cure and no evidence of active vegetation on transoesophageal echocardiography, the decision to pursue a third transcatheter intervention rather than surgery was driven by prohibitive operative risk and insurmountable anatomical barriers.

Risk stratification provided the quantitative foundation for deviating from the standard surgical pathway. The patient's EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, placing him in a category of extremely high operative mortality. This elevated score was not merely a reflection of his age but was compounded by a heavy burden of comorbidities, including three-vessel coronary artery disease with multiple prior percutaneous interventions, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and a history of recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022. The presence of COPD significantly increases the risk of prolonged postoperative ventilation and respiratory failure, while the history of gastrointestinal bleeding complicates the mandatory perioperative anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy required after surgical valve replacement. Furthermore, the patient's frailty, evidenced by his limited exercise tolerance prior to intervention, suggested a poor likelihood of recovering functional status following a major sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass.

Beyond the numerical risk scores, the anatomical reality of the "triple-stented" aortic root presented a formidable technical barrier to surgical explantation. The patient already harbored two failed transcatheter valves: a Lotus valve implanted in 2014 and an Evolut prosthesis placed in 2022. Over nearly a decade, these devices had become deeply integrated into the aortic wall and native annulus through endothelialization and tissue ingrowth. Attempting to surgically extract these frames would carry an exorbitant risk of catastrophic injury to the aortic root, potentially necessitating a total arch replacement or Bentall procedure (replacement of the aortic valve, ascending aorta, and coronary arteries). Such an extended procedure would drastically increase cross-clamp times and bleeding risks, rendering survival unlikely given the patient's comorbid profile. Consequently, the Heart Team concluded that the morbidity associated with attempting to remove the infected-but-cured prosthetic material outweighed the benefits of source control in an already sterilized field.

With surgery deemed prohibitively dangerous, the feasibility of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV strategy became the focal point of deliberation. The decision to proceed relied heavily on advanced multimodality imaging to ensure that a third valve could be safely deployed without causing coronary obstruction or severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM). Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) was pivotal in this assessment, revealing a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the existing Evolut frame and confirming adequate virtual valve-to-coronary distances. The imaging demonstrated that despite the presence of two previous stent frames, there was sufficient space to anchor a new self-expanding prosthesis (Acurate neo2) while maintaining patency of the coronary ostia. Crucially, the absence of active infection on imaging meant that implanting a new bioprosthesis into a sterile environment was oncologically and microbiologically sound, shifting the primary challenge from infection control to hemodynamic optimization and mechanical stability.

Ultimately, the choice to proceed with transcatheter reintervention was solidified through shared decision-making involving the patient, his family, and the multidisciplinary Heart Team. This process balanced the theoretical ideal of surgical debridement againstthe pragmatic goals of extending life expectancy and preserving quality of life. For a patient in his late 70s with significant comorbidities, the promise of avoiding sternotomy, minimizing recovery time, and rapidly alleviating symptoms of heart failure held greater value than attempting a curative but likely fatal surgical revision. The team transparently discussed the uncertainties inherent in placing a third valve—including future coronary access difficulties and limited options for subsequent failures—but determined that immediate relief from severe symptomatic stenosis offered the best chance for meaningful survival. This case underscores that in complex structural heart disease, particularly following treated endocarditis in high-risk candidates, therapeutic success is defined not just by adherence to guidelines, but by navigating anatomical constraints through precise imaging and collaborative clinical judgment.

## Procedural Execution: Technical Nuances of Triple-Valve Implantation

The execution of a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) demands a departure from standard protocols, requiring precise adaptation to the constraints imposed by two pre-existing stent frames. In this case, the selection of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis was driven by its unique architectural advantages suited for a "triple-stented" anatomy. Unlike self-expanding valves with tall frames that could exacerbate the cumulative height of the construct, the Acurate neo2 features a relatively low profile and supra-annular leaflet positioning. This design is critical in minimizing the vertical footprint of the new device while maximizing the effective orifice area (EOA) within the narrowed lumen defined by the previous Lotus and Evolut valves. The choice of a size Small device reflected the need to fit within the 13.7 mm minimum lumen diameter identified on preprocedural multislice CT (MSCT), balancing the risk of under-expansion against the danger of annular rupture or excessive pressure on the integrated prior frames.

Access and wire manipulation in this scenario presented heightened challenges due to the tortuosity created by multiple overlapping metal struts. The procedure utilized left femoral access for the primary delivery system and left radial access for monitoring, navigating a Safari guidewire into the left ventricle through the constrained central channel of the failing prostheses. Once access was secured, balloon pre-dilation was performed using a 22/40 mm catheter under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. This step served a dual purpose: it temporarily stabilized cardiac output to prevent embolization during dilation and mechanically expanded the rigid, degenerated leaflets of the underlying Evolut frame. Simultaneous aortography during this phase confirmed that coronary flow remained patent, a vital checkpoint before committing to permanent valve deployment in an anatomy where coronary obstruction carries catastrophic risk.

The core of the procedural strategy lay in the specific implantation depth and commissural alignment. To mitigate the risk of severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM) caused by inward folding of the existing bioprosthetic leaflets, the team employed a high implantation technique. By deploying the Acurate neo2 at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets rather than deep within the annulus, the new stent frame effectively pinned the old, restricted leaflets againstthe outer wall of the prior prosthesis. This maneuver prevented them from obstructing central flow and preserved a functional neo-skirt above the coronary ostia. Concurrently, rigorous attention was paid to commissural alignment. Guided by preprocedural CT modeling and fluoroscopic landmarks, the new valve was oriented to align its commissures with those ofthe underlying frames. This alignment is paramount in multi-layer TAVI procedures to maintain clear pathways for future coronary cannulation, preventingthe "mesh effect" where misaligned struts create an impenetrable barrier to catheters.

Final optimization ofthe hemodynamic result relied on controlled post-dilation. Following deployment, a 20/40 mm balloon was inflated under rapid pacing to ensure full expansion ofthe Acurate neo2 withinthe confining rings ofthe prior valves. This step likely facilitated slight radial expansion ofthe intermediate Evolut frame, further improving leaflet mobility and central flow dynamics without inducing frame fracture or embolization. Final aortography confirmedthe success ofthese technical nuances: correct positioning, absenceof paravalvular leak, and crucially, preserved coronary perfusion. While immediate post-procedure echocardiography initially showed elevated gradients consistent with severe PPM—a common phenomenon in triple-valve constructs due to transient edema and incomplete recoil—the strategic execution laidthefoundationforthe significant hemodynamic improvement observed at one-month follow-up, validatingthe approachofhigh implantationandprecise alignmentinmanagingcomplexreinterventions.
