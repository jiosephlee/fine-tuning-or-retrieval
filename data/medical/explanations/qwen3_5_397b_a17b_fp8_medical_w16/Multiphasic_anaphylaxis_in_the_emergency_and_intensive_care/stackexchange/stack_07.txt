Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Pathophysiology] Why is differentiating between ongoing allergen absorption (e.g., from slow-release medications or food) and true biphasic anaphylaxis critical when determining the necessity for extended observation versus continued active intervention?

Differentiating between ongoing allergen absorption and true biphasic anaphylaxis is fundamental because it dictates whether the clinician must pursue **source control** or **prolonged surveillance**. True biphasic anaphylaxis, as defined in the case report, is characterized by a recurrence of symptoms after a period of complete resolution *without* re-exposure to the allergen. In contrast, ongoing absorption from a depot source—such as residual food in the gut or, theoretically, a slow-release medication formulation—represents a continuation of the initial exposure rather than a distinct immunologic phase.

In the context of the presented case involving clarithromycin, distinguishing these entities was vital for determining the necessity of aggressive interventions like continuous intravenous epinephrine infusions versus an expectant monitoring strategy. Had the patient's recurrent shocks been driven by continued absorption of unmetabolized drug from the gastrointestinal tract (ongoing exposure), the clinical priority would shift toward preventing further uptake (e.g., via activated charcoal if within a viable window) and anticipating a protracted course requiring constant pharmacologic suppression until the reservoir is depleted. Conversely, in true biphasic or multiphasic reactions, the trigger has been removed—as evidenced here by the discontinuation of clarithromycin after a single dose—and recurrences are driven by endogenous mast cell mediator release dynamics.

The case illustrates this distinction clearly: the patient experienced four discrete episodes despite receiving only a single oral dose of clarithromycin, with no evidence of delayed gastric emptying or slow-release formulation issues mentioned. The diminishing intensity of symptoms with each episode suggested a self-limiting cascade of mast cell degranulation typical of multiphasic anaphylaxis, possibly exacerbated by underlying systemic mastocytosis, rather than sustained toxin influx. Recognizing this pattern allowed the team to avoid unnecessary preemptive continuous epinephrine infusion—a high-risk intervention—and instead opt for close observation and rescue dosing, which proved successful as the patient remained stable after 24 hours. Misidentifying ongoing absorption as biphasic anaphylaxis could lead to premature discharge while the allergen reservoir remains active; conversely, misinterpreting biphasic potential as simple ongoing absorption might delay transfer to higher-level care (like the ICU step-up seen in this case) needed for rapid resuscitation during unpredictable relapses.
