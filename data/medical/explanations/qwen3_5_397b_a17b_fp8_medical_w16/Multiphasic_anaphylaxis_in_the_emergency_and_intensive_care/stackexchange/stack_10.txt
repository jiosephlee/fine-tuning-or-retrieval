Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Morning Report] Given the low mortality rate of anaphylaxis in settings with timely care, what is the primary clinical dilemma facing physicians when establishing observation protocols for patients who have resolved from their initial anaphylactic episode?

The primary clinical dilemma lies in the stark disconnect between the statistical rarity of fatal biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis and the complete inability to reliably predict which individual patients will experience a recurrence. As illustrated in the case of the patient with clarithromycin-induced multiphasic anaphylaxis, even an initial robust response to epinephrine and stabilization does not guarantee a resolved course; this patient suffered three distinct recurrences within 12 hours despite standard care.

While overall mortality from anaphylaxis is low in settings with timely access to epinephrine, the consequences of discharging a patient who is destined to have a biphasic reaction are potentially catastrophic. Conversely, because reliable risk stratification tools remain elusive, clinicians cannot confidently identify the "safe" subgroup for early discharge. Current guidelines offer conflicting or vague recommendations on observation duration—ranging from 4 to 24 hours—and established predictors such as severity of initial presentation or need for multiple epinephrine doses lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity to guide precise decision-making.

Therefore, the physician faces a resource-intensive balancing act: determining an observation period long enough to capture the window of recurrence (which can extend up to 78 hours, though typically occurs within 12) without subjecting the vast majority of single-episode patients to unnecessary hospitalization. The case report underscores this uncertainty, noting that even with specialized monitoring and escalated care including ICU admission, the management strategy relied heavily on an expectant approach due to a lack of evidence-based protocols for preventing or preemptively treating multiphasic episodes. Until validated risk scores or biomarkers (such as dynamic tryptase trends) are established, observation protocols must remain conservative, prioritizing safety over efficiency in the face of unpredictable pathophysiology.
