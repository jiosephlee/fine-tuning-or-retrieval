Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Management] Current evidence suggests that corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. Given this lack of efficacy and the potential for harm, what is the primary rationale that still drives their frequent administration in clinical practice, and how has guideline consensus shifted regarding their use?

The persistence of corticosteroid administration in anaphylaxis, despite robust evidence demonstrating their inability to prevent biphasic reactions, is driven primarily by historical inertia and a theoretical appeal to their anti-inflammatory properties. For decades, clinicians operated under the assumption that suppressing the late-phase inflammatory response would mitigate the risk of symptom recurrence after the initial resolution. This rationale, however, has been dismantled by contemporary data; as highlighted in the discussion of this case, a 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found that only one supported steroid use, while 21 showed no reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests potential harm, including an association between prehospital corticosteroid use and increased rates of hospital or ICU admission.

Consequently, guideline consensus has shifted decisively away from routine steroid administration. Current recommendations emphasize that epinephrine remains the sole first-line therapy capable of reversing the life-threatening pathophysiology of anaphylaxis. In the presented case of multiphasic anaphylaxis triggered by clarithromycin, this disconnect between evidence and practice was evident: the patient received both prednisone and dexamethasone following her first relapse solely due to "the traditional role of corticosteroids" and conflicting institutional protocols, rather than proven efficacy. Notably, her clinical course involved four distinct anaphylactic episodes requiring repeated epinephrine doses despite this adjunctive steroid coverage, underscoring that corticosteroids did not abort the multiphasic nature of her reaction. The modern standard of care now prioritizes risk-stratified observation periods and prompt, adequately dosed epinephrine over the prophylactic use of medications that offer no demonstrable benefit in preventing recurrence.
