Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] How is biphasic anaphylaxis definitively distinguished from refractory anaphylaxis and sustained anaphylaxis with transient response to treatment?

Biphasic anaphylaxis is definitively distinguished from refractory and sustained anaphylaxis by the presence of a **completely asymptomatic interval** following the resolution of the initial reaction, occurring in the absence of renewed allergen exposure. As highlighted in the discussion of this case, true biphasic (or multiphasic) anaphylaxis requires that the initial symptoms resolve fully—returning the patient to a baseline state—before a secondary wave of symptoms emerges hours later. In the presented case, the patient experienced distinct episodes separated by periods of stability, necessitating repeated doses of epinephrine only upon recurrence, rather than continuous infusion.

In contrast, **refractory anaphylaxis** is characterized by persistent hemodynamic instability or respiratory compromise that fails to respond adequately to correctly administered epinephrine and supportive care; the patient never achieves clinical stability. Similarly, **sustained anaphylaxis with a transient response** involves a scenario where symptoms improve briefly after treatment but quickly recur because the initial intervention was insufficient or because there is ongoing absorption of the allergen (e.g., from slow-release medications or residual food). In these latter scenarios, there is no true "well" interval; rather, the clinical picture represents a single, protracted event with fluctuating severity. The distinction is critical for management: while refractory cases may require vasopressor infusions and aggressive airway management immediately, biphasic cases demand vigilant observation and readiness to re-administer epinephrine after a period of apparent recovery.

Although rare, clinicians must remain aware that the asymptomatic interval can extend up to three days, even in the absence of re-exposure to the trigger.

### [Management] Current evidence suggests that corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. Given this lack of efficacy and the potential for harm, what is the primary rationale that still drives their frequent administration in clinical practice, and how has guideline consensus shifted regarding their use?

The persistence of corticosteroid administration in anaphylaxis, despite robust evidence demonstrating their inability to prevent biphasic reactions, is driven primarily by historical inertia and a theoretical appeal to their anti-inflammatory properties. For decades, clinicians operated under the assumption that suppressing the late-phase inflammatory response would mitigate the risk of symptom recurrence after the initial resolution. This rationale, however, has been dismantled by contemporary data; as highlighted in the discussion of this case, a 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found that only one supported steroid use, while 21 showed no reduction in biphasic anaphylaxis risk. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests potential harm, including an association between prehospital corticosteroid use and increased rates of hospital or ICU admission.

Consequently, guideline consensus has shifted decisively away from routine steroid administration. Current recommendations emphasize that epinephrine remains the sole first-line therapy capable of reversing the life-threatening pathophysiology of anaphylaxis. In the presented case of multiphasic anaphylaxis triggered by clarithromycin, this disconnect between evidence and practice was evident: the patient received both prednisone and dexamethasone following her first relapse solely due to "the traditional role of corticosteroids" and conflicting institutional protocols, rather than proven efficacy. Notably, her clinical course involved four distinct anaphylactic episodes requiring repeated epinephrine doses despite this adjunctive steroid coverage, underscoring that corticosteroids did not abort the multiphasic nature of her reaction. The modern standard of care now prioritizes risk-stratified observation periods and prompt, adequately dosed epinephrine over the prophylactic use of medications that offer no demonstrable benefit in preventing recurrence.

The recommended intramuscular epinephrine dose for an adult is **0.5 mg** (0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg), not the 0.3 mg contained in many standard auto-injectors. Reliance on fixed-dose 0.3 mg auto-injectors in larger adults can lead to suboptimal outcomes because it results in significant underdosing relative to both weight-based calculations and the established therapeutic ceiling of 0.5 mg. Clinicians must recognize that while auto-injectors offer logistical advantages in time-sensitive settings, the fixed 0.3 mg formulation may be insufficient for adults >50 kg, potentially delaying definitive stabilization in life-threatening anaphylaxis.

### [Clinical Pearls] To accurately diagnose systemic mastocytosis or confirm severe mast cell activation in the context of anaphylaxis, what is the recommended timing protocol for obtaining serial serum tryptase levels?

To accurately diagnose systemic mastocytosis or confirm severe mast cell activation in the setting of anaphylaxis, serum tryptase must be obtained according to a strict temporal protocol that captures both the acute release and the patient's constitutive baseline. As highlighted in the case of multiphasic anaphylaxis presented by Schneider et al., failure to adhere to this timing can obscure the diagnosis; in their patient, the absence of samples during the initial episodes limited the ability to fully characterize the kinetic profile of mast cell degranulation, leaving only suggestive rather than definitive evidence of underlying systemic mastocytosis.

The recommended strategy involves collecting a minimum of two, and ideally three, serial samples. The first sample should be drawn as soon as feasible after resuscitation has commenced, ensuring that life-saving interventions like epinephrine administration are not delayed. The second, and often most critical, sample should be obtained 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset (and no later than 4 hours), as this window corresponds to the peak serum concentration of tryptase following mast cell degranulation. Finally, a third sample must be collected at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution to establish the patient's baseline tryptase level.

This tripartite approach serves two distinct diagnostic purposes. First, comparing the peak level to the baseline allows for the calculation of the "20% plus 2" rule (an increase of >20% above baseline plus 2 ng/mL), which confirms that an acute mast cell activation event occurred—a crucial distinction when clinical signs are ambiguous or when differentiating anaphylaxis from other causes of shock. Second, an elevated baseline tryptase (>20 ng/mL) is a minor criterion for the diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis. In the reported case, while baseline levels were indeed found to be elevated during follow-up, the lack of paired acute-phase samples during the initial severe episodes meant clinicians could not retrospectively validate the magnitude of mediator release against that specific baseline, thereby complicating the immediate assessment of disease severity and etiology. Adherence to this timing protocol is therefore essential not only for confirming the acute event but also for unmasking underlying clonal mast cell disorders that predispose patients to complex, multiphasic reactions.

### [Differential Diagnosis] This patient had elevated baseline tryptase levels and hepatomegaly but did not meet the full WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis. Which specific diagnostic criterion was likely missing or unassessable in this case that prevents a definitive diagnosis?

In this case, the specific diagnostic criterion preventing a definitive diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis was the **major histopathological criterion**: the demonstration of multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells (≥15 mast cells in aggregates) in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification, a diagnosis requires either one major and one minor criterion, or three minor criteria in the absence of the major criterion. This patient exhibited two minor criteria: elevated baseline serum tryptase (>20 ng/mL) and hepatomegaly suggestive of organ involvement. However, as explicitly noted in the case report, the bone marrow biopsy yielded "limited material," rendering the assessment of dense mast cell aggregates impossible. Without visual confirmation of these characteristic clusters to satisfy the major criterion, and lacking a third minor criterion (such as >25% atypical spindle-shaped mast cells, a *KIT* D816V mutation, or aberrant CD2/CD25 expression), the formal diagnostic threshold for systemic mastocytosis could not be met despite the high clinical suspicion.

### [Pathophysiology] Why is differentiating between ongoing allergen absorption (e.g., from slow-release medications or food) and true biphasic anaphylaxis critical when determining the necessity for extended observation versus continued active intervention?

Differentiating between ongoing allergen absorption and true biphasic anaphylaxis is fundamental because it dictates whether the clinician must pursue **source control** or **prolonged surveillance**. True biphasic anaphylaxis, as defined in the case report, is characterized by a recurrence of symptoms after a period of complete resolution *without* re-exposure to the allergen. In contrast, ongoing absorption from a depot source—such as residual food in the gut or, theoretically, a slow-release medication formulation—represents a continuation of the initial exposure rather than a distinct immunologic phase.

In the context of the presented case involving clarithromycin, distinguishing these entities was vital for determining the necessity of aggressive interventions like continuous intravenous epinephrine infusions versus an expectant monitoring strategy. Had the patient's recurrent shocks been driven by continued absorption of unmetabolized drug from the gastrointestinal tract (ongoing exposure), the clinical priority would shift toward preventing further uptake (e.g., via activated charcoal if within a viable window) and anticipating a protracted course requiring constant pharmacologic suppression until the reservoir is depleted. Conversely, in true biphasic or multiphasic reactions, the trigger has been removed—as evidenced here by the discontinuation of clarithromycin after a single dose—and recurrences are driven by endogenous mast cell mediator release dynamics.

The case illustrates this distinction clearly: the patient experienced four discrete episodes despite receiving only a single oral dose of clarithromycin, with no evidence of delayed gastric emptying or slow-release formulation issues mentioned. The diminishing intensity of symptoms with each episode suggested a self-limiting cascade of mast cell degranulation typical of multiphasic anaphylaxis, possibly exacerbated by underlying systemic mastocytosis, rather than sustained toxin influx. Recognizing this pattern allowed the team to avoid unnecessary preemptive continuous epinephrine infusion—a high-risk intervention—and instead opt for close observation and rescue dosing, which proved successful as the patient remained stable after 24 hours. Misidentifying ongoing absorption as biphasic anaphylaxis could lead to premature discharge while the allergen reservoir remains active; conversely, misinterpreting biphasic potential as simple ongoing absorption might delay transfer to higher-level care (like the ICU step-up seen in this case) needed for rapid resuscitation during unpredictable relapses.

### [Management] In managing a patient with documented multiphasic anaphylaxis who continues to relapse despite intermittent intramuscular epinephrine, what advanced therapeutic strategy involving continuous infusion was considered in this case, and what are the risks associated with this approach compared to an expectant management strategy?

In this case of multiphasic anaphylaxis driven by probable underlying systemic mastocytosis, the advanced therapeutic strategy considered was the **continuous intravenous infusion of epinephrine**. This approach was contemplated as a preemptive measure to maintain hemodynamic stability and suppress mediator release during the patient's recurrent episodes, rather than relying solely on reactive intermittent intramuscular boluses.

The clinical reasoning behind considering an infusion stems from the pathophysiology of multiphasic reactions, where mast cell degranulation occurs in waves. In patients with mast cell disorders, the threshold for activation is lowered, and the burden of mediators can overwhelm the transient effect of intramuscular epinephrine. A continuous infusion offers steady-state receptor stimulation, theoretically preventing the "troughs" in drug concentration that allow symptoms to recur between doses.

However, the case report highlights that this strategy carries significant risks compared to an **expectant management** approach. The primary danger of continuous epinephrine infusion is the potential for severe cardiovascular toxicity, including profound tachycardia, arrhythmias, and myocardial ischemia, particularly in a patient without active shock between episodes. Conversely, the risk of expectant management—waiting to treat only upon symptom recurrence—is the possibility of delayed intervention during a rapid deterioration, potentially leading to refractory shock or cardiac arrest before the next dose can be administered.

In this specific instance, the team opted for expectant management because the clinical trajectory showed a natural attenuation of symptom severity with each successive episode. This decision avoided the iatrogenic risks of vasoactive overdose while successfully bridging the patient until spontaneous resolution occurred, underscoring that in the absence of definitive guidelines for multiphasic anaphylaxis, careful observation of clinical trends is a valid and often safer alternative to aggressive pharmacologic escalation.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Aside from delayed epinephrine administration and severity of the initial reaction, what other clinical factors have been suggested as potential predictors for developing a biphasic course, even though reliable risk stratification tools remain elusive?

While delayed epinephrine administration and the severity of the initial reaction are the most consistently cited risk factors for biphasic anaphylaxis, current literature and case analyses suggest several other clinical variables that may predict a complicated course, even in the absence of validated risk stratification tools. As highlighted in the discussion of multiphasic anaphylaxis by Schneider et al., the requirement for **multiple doses of epinephrine** during the initial management is a significant predictor; in their case, the patient experienced four distinct anaphylactic episodes, each necessitating repeat epinephrine administration. Additionally, a **history of prior biphasic reactions** has been suggested as a risk factor, although this specific patient was experiencing her first anaphylactic event.

Crucially, clinicians must consider underlying **mast cell disorders**, such as systemic mastocytosis, as a potential driver for recurrent or multiphasic courses. In the reported case, the patient's multiphasic trajectory prompted further investigation which revealed elevated baseline and acute tryptase levels alongside hepatomegaly, indicating a high probability of systemic mastocytosis despite not meeting all formal WHO diagnostic criteria. This suggests that unexplained recurrent anaphylaxis should trigger a workup for mast cell burden. Finally, while traditionally used for prophylaxis, it is important to note that **corticosteroid administration** has not been proven to prevent biphasic reactions and is no longer routinely recommended; indeed, some data suggest it may be associated with increased rates of admission without altering the recurrence risk. Therefore, when assessing discharge readiness or monitoring needs, physicians should weigh the need for multiple epinephrine doses and suspect underlying mastocytosis rather than relying on steroid coverage or isolated initial severity markers.

### [Morning Report] Given the low mortality rate of anaphylaxis in settings with timely care, what is the primary clinical dilemma facing physicians when establishing observation protocols for patients who have resolved from their initial anaphylactic episode?

The primary clinical dilemma lies in the stark disconnect between the statistical rarity of fatal biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis and the complete inability to reliably predict which individual patients will experience a recurrence. As illustrated in the case of the patient with clarithromycin-induced multiphasic anaphylaxis, even an initial robust response to epinephrine and stabilization does not guarantee a resolved course; this patient suffered three distinct recurrences within 12 hours despite standard care.

While overall mortality from anaphylaxis is low in settings with timely access to epinephrine, the consequences of discharging a patient who is destined to have a biphasic reaction are potentially catastrophic. Conversely, because reliable risk stratification tools remain elusive, clinicians cannot confidently identify the "safe" subgroup for early discharge. Current guidelines offer conflicting or vague recommendations on observation duration—ranging from 4 to 24 hours—and established predictors such as severity of initial presentation or need for multiple epinephrine doses lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity to guide precise decision-making.

Therefore, the physician faces a resource-intensive balancing act: determining an observation period long enough to capture the window of recurrence (which can extend up to 78 hours, though typically occurs within 12) without subjecting the vast majority of single-episode patients to unnecessary hospitalization. The case report underscores this uncertainty, noting that even with specialized monitoring and escalated care including ICU admission, the management strategy relied heavily on an expectant approach due to a lack of evidence-based protocols for preventing or preemptively treating multiphasic episodes. Until validated risk scores or biomarkers (such as dynamic tryptase trends) are established, observation protocols must remain conservative, prioritizing safety over efficiency in the face of unpredictable pathophysiology.
