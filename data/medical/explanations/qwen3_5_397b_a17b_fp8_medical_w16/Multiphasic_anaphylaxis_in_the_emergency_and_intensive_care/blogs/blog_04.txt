# When Anaphylaxis Won't Quit: Managing Refractory and Multiphasic Episodes Without a Playbook

We all know the algorithm for anaphylaxis. It's burned into our muscle memory: recognize the pattern, hit the thigh with epinephrine, flood the veins with fluids, and watch the patient turn the corner. Usually, it works. The hypotension resolves, the wheezing stops, and we breathe a sigh of relief as we move them to a ward bed for a few hours of observation. But what happens when the patient turns the corner, only to plummet back into shock an hour later? And then again? And *again*?

This isn't just "biphasic" anaphylaxis anymore; this is **multiphasic anaphylaxis**, a terrifying clinical entity where the fire refuses to stay out despite adequate fuel removal and standard suppression tactics. Unlike refractory anaphylaxis—where the patient simply never responds to initial epinephrine and requires immediate escalation to infusions—multiphasic cases present a unique psychological and physiological trap. You think you've won, you stand down, and then the mast cells unleash hell all over again.

A recent case report by Schneider et al. highlights this exact nightmare scenario. A woman in her 60s presented with classic anaphylaxis after taking her first dose of clarithromycin for erysipelas. She looked textbook: hypotensive (74/50 mmHg), hypoxic, flushed, and agitated. She got 0.3 mg IM epinephrine via auto-injector, antihistamines, oxygen, and fluids. She responded beautifully. Her blood pressure normalized, her breathing improved, and she was admitted to an acute internal ward. But within 12 hours, she didn't just have one recurrence; she suffered **three more distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock**. Each time, she required rescue epinephrine. By the fourth episode, her team was staring down the barrel of a decision with almost zero literature to guide them: Do we start a prophylactic IV epinephrine drip to clamp down on these waves before they crash her again, or do we trust that the episodes are waning in severity and continue reacting with IM doses as they arise?

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: Is It Biphasic or Just Bad Timing?

Before we dive into management, we have to ensure we aren't mislabeling sustained absorption as multiphasic disease. True biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis is defined by a recurrence of symptoms after complete resolution of the initial reaction, without re-exposure to the trigger. In the case above, the trigger was a single oral dose of clarithromycin taken hours prior. While drug absorption can be variable, the distinct asymptomatic intervals between this patient's shocks suggest a pathophysiological "storm" rather than continued gut absorption.

The incidence of biphasic reactions is notoriously hard to pin down, ranging wildly from 0.4% to 23% in literature, though most robust data suggests it hovers around 4–6%. Multiphasic courses are even rarer and poorly characterized. What makes this specific case fascinating—and instructive—is the underlying substrate. Workup revealed elevated baseline tryptase and hepatomegaly, pointing strongly toward undiagnosed **systemic mastocytosis**. While she didn't meet every formal WHO criterion (likely due to sampling limitations), the clinical picture fits: patients with mast cell disorders have a lower threshold for degranulation and a higher propensity for severe, recurrent reactions. If your anaphylaxis patient keeps bouncing back despite perfect initial care, you need to start thinking about whether their mast cell burden is unusually high.

## The Therapeutic Tightrope: Infusion vs. Expectant Management

This is where the rubber meets the road in terms of clinical judgment. In our case report, the consulting allergists weighed two very different paths once the pattern of recurrence became clear after the second relapse.

**Option A: Preemptive IV Epinephrine Infusion.**
The logic here is aggressive stabilization. If the patient is having cyclic degranulation events causing hemodynamic collapse why wait for the next crash? Starting a titrated IV epinephrine infusion (typically starting at 1–4 mcg/min) creates a floor for vascular tone and bronchodilation that might blunt subsequent waves before they become symptomatic shock states. This is standard for *refractory* anaphylaxis (no response to IM epi), but using it prophylactically in a *responding-but-relapsing* patient is off-label territory born of desperation. The risk? Overtreatment leading to tachyarrhythmias, myocardial ischemia (especially in older adults or those with CAD), or severe hypertension once the endogenous storm finally subsides.

**Option B: Expectant Management with Repeated IM Dosing.**
This approach relies on close monitoring and rapid reaction. You keep the patient in a high-acuity setting (ICU or step-down), have epinephrine drawn up and ready at the bedside, and treat each wave as it breaks. The advantage is avoiding the complications of continuous vasoactive support in a patient who technically stabilizes between episodes. In Schneider's case, they noted that each successive episode was less severe than the last—from profound hypoxia and cyanosis to mere dyspnea and rash. This natural attenuation suggested the mediator release was exhausting itself.

The team chose Option B. They opted against the infusion banked on the decreasing severity of attacks and committed to rapid IM rescue (switching from 0.3 mg auto-injectors to manually drawn 0.5 mg doses). It worked; no further relapses occurred after 24 hours. But let's be honest: that was a high-stakes gamble made in real-time without a randomized controlled trial to back it up.
