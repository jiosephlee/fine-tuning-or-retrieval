Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Acute Stabilization of Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is defined as a severe, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction characterized by the rapid onset of symptoms involving the airway, breathing, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal tract, typically accompanied by cutaneous manifestations. While the identification of a specific trigger supports the diagnosis, clinical recognition relies on the constellation of acute symptoms rather than confirmed allergen exposure. In the presented case, the patient exhibited a textbook presentation of anaphylaxis shortly after ingesting her first dose of clarithromycin. The timeline is clinically significant: symptoms began one hour after ingestion and progressed rapidly, necessitating medical attention within another hour. This latency period is consistent with IgE-mediated reactions to oral medications, distinguishing it from immediate intraoperative reactions or delayed T-cell mediated responses. Although the patient had a history of mild beta-lactam allergy and a recent rash attributed to clindamycin, clarithromycin represented a novel exposure, highlighting that anaphylaxis can occur upon first known contact with a drug class if prior sensitization has occurred through cross-reactivity or unrecognized exposure.

The initial assessment in the Emergency Department revealed the hallmark triad of anaphylactic shock: respiratory compromise, circulatory collapse, and extensive cutaneous involvement. The patient presented with hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxemia (SpO2 88% on room air), indicating profound vasodilation and potential ventilation-perfusion mismatch. These hemodynamic and respiratory derangements were accompanied by generalized erythema, urticaria, facial swelling, pruritus, and agitation—a sign often indicative of cerebral hypoperfusion or severe hypoxia. Notably, while the patient reported a sensation of throat swelling, physical examination did not reveal frank angioedema of the tongue or larynx, nor were there audible wheezes on auscultation. This underscores a critical teaching point: the absence of upper airway obstruction or bronchospasm does not rule out anaphylaxis when distributive shock and mucocutaneous signs are present. The presence of hypotension alone in the context of suspected allergen exposure is sufficient to diagnose anaphylactic shock and mandates immediate intervention.

The cornerstone of acute management is the prompt administration of intramuscular (IM) epinephrine. In this case, the patient received 0.3 mg of epinephrine via auto-injector immediately upon suspicion of a severe allergic reaction. Epinephrine acts rapidly to reverse vasodilation via alpha-1 adrenergic stimulation, improve cardiac output via beta-1 effects, and reduce bronchoconstriction and mediator release via beta-2 effects. The patient's positive response—normalization of blood pressure and reduction in cutaneous symptoms—confirms the efficacy of this first-line therapy. However, the case also illuminates a common discrepancy in dosing practices. Current guidelines recommend an adult dose of 0.5 mg (0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg), yet many commercially available auto-injectors contain only 0.3 mg. For an adult weighing 104 kg, as in this case, a 0.3 mg dose represents significant underdosing. While the auto-injector facilitated rapid delivery and minimized hesitation among staff less familiar with drawing up vasoactive medications, the suboptimal dose may have contributed to the instability that followed. Evidence suggests that adults receiving initial doses below 0.5 mg are more likely to require escalation of care and repeat dosing, a pattern observed here as the patient experienced recurrent episodes despite initial stabilization.

Adjunctive therapies played a supportive but secondary role in this patient's resuscitation. Supplemental oxygen was administered via a non-rebreather mask to correct hypoxemia and was successfully discontinued once perfusion improved following epinephrine administration; however, it required reinstatement during subsequent relapses. Intravenous fluid resuscitation with 500 mL of normal saline helped address relative hypovolemia caused by massive vasodilation and capillary leak. Antihistamines, specifically 2 mg of intravenous clemastine, were administered to alleviate cutaneous symptoms such as pruritus and urticaria. It is vital for learners to recognize that while antihistamines and corticosteroids (which were added later in this case) may provide symptomatic relief for skin findings or theoretically delay late-phase reactions, they have no role in reversing airway obstruction or shock in the acute setting. They must never delay or replace epinephrine. The successful initial stabilization of this patient relied entirely on the timely recognition of shock patterns and the administration of epinephrine, setting the stage for the complex monitoring challenges posed by her subsequent multiphasic course.
