# The Dosing Gap: Why Your Adult Anaphylaxis Auto-Injectors Might Be Underdosing Your Patients

We tend to think of anaphylaxis as a binary event: you give epinephrine, the patient gets better, and you move on. But what happens when the reaction refuses to stay dead? When a patient bounces back from shock not once, but three more times, despite "appropriate" care? That is the terrifying reality of multiphasic anaphylaxis, a clinical beast that tests our protocols and exposes cracks in our standard equipment.

A recent case report by Schneider et al. throws this into sharp relief. They describe a woman in her 60s, weighing 104 kg, who presented with anaphylactic shock after taking clarithromycin. She was hypotensive (74/50 mmHg), hypoxic, and agitated. The team did exactly what the algorithms say: they hit her with intramuscular epinephrine via an auto-injector. She responded beautifully. Blood pressure normalized, oxygenation improved, and she was moved to a ward. Then, she crashed again. And again. And again. Over the next 12 hours, she suffered four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock.

While the case highlights the enigmatic nature of multiphasic reactions—and raises suspicion for underlying systemic mastocytosis given her elevated baseline tryptase and hepatomegaly—the most actionable takeaway isn't about rare mast cell disorders. It's about the syringe in your hand during that first critical minute. This patient received 0.3 mg of epinephrine via auto-injector for her initial resuscitation. For a 104 kg adult, that wasn't just suboptimal; it was likely a setup for the rollercoaster that followed.

## The Math Doesn't Add Up

Let's look at the numbers, because in resuscitation, math matters. Current guidelines universally recommend an epinephrine dose of 0.01 mg/kg for adults, capped at a maximum of 0.5 mg. If you do the quick calculation for our 104 kg patient, the target dose is right at that ceiling: 0.5 mg (technically 1.04 mg based on strict weight scaling, but we cap at 0.5 mg). Yet, she received 0.3 mg.

This isn't an isolated incident; it's a systemic blind spot driven by supply chain convenience rather than physiology. The vast majority of epinephrine auto-injectors stocked in hospitals, schools, and home medicine cabinets come in two flavors: 0.15 mg for children and 0.3 mg for "adults." The label says "adult," so we assume it's correct. But "adult" is a demographic category, not a weight class. In an era where the average adult weight continues to climb, sticking rigidly to a fixed 0.3 mg dose for anyone over puberty leaves our larger patients significantly underdosed from second zero one.

The consequences of this underdosing are becoming harder to ignore while we hide behind tradition. A retrospective study cited in the discussion suggests that patients who initially receive 0.3 mg require escalation of care—including repeat doses and ICU admissions—significantly more often than those who receive the full 0.5 mg upfront. In our case report, the clinical team recognized the inadequacy mid-crisis; for the third and fourth episodes, they abandoned the auto-injector and manually drew up 0.5 mg doses intramuscularly while simultaneously considering an IV infusion drip (though the patient eventually stabilized without it). The pattern was clear: as the dosing became more physiologically appropriate relative to her mass and severity, control was finally achieved.

# Beyond the 4-Hour Rule: Risk-Stratifying Observation Periods for Biphasic and Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

We've all been there. You treat a patient for anaphylaxis in the ED, they respond beautifully to IM epinephrine, their hives fade, and their blood pressure normalizes. The clock starts ticking on the ubiquitous "4-to-6-hour observation rule." Once that timer hits zero, you discharge them with an EpiPen and instructions to follow up. It's clean, it's efficient, and it's baked into our collective muscle memory. But what happens when the clock runs out, the patient goes home (or moves to the ward), and the storm returns? Even worse, what if it returns not just once, but three more times?

A recent case report by Schneider et al. throws a wrench in our standard operating procedure. They describe a woman in her 60s who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single dose of clarithromycin. Despite an initial robust response to epinephrine, she suffered **four distinct episodes** of anaphylactic shock over a 12-hour period. This wasn't a case of refractory anaphylaxis where the first dose simply didn't stick; this was a classic biphasic (or rather, multiphasic) course: resolution, an asymptomatic window, and then a violent recurrence without re-exposure to the trigger. While she eventually stabilized and was found to have probable underlying systemic mastocytosis, her case highlights a terrifying reality: the "safe" window for discharge is far less predictable than our guidelines suggest. With reported rates of biphasic reactions swinging wildly from 0.4% to 23%, relying on a blanket observation time is a gamble we can no longer afford to take blindly.

The definition of biphasic anaphylaxis is straightforward—a recurrence of symptoms after complete resolution and without re-exposure—but the clinical management is anything but. In the reported case, the patient initially received 0.3 mg of epinephrine via auto-injector. While convenient and excellent for reducing hesitation among non-routine users, this dose represents significant underdosing for an adult weighing over 50 kg (guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg). Retrospective data suggests that patients receiving the lower 0.3 mg dose are significantly more likely to require escalation of care. Yet, even after correcting the dose and administering adjunctive therapies like antihistamines and fluids, the cycles continued. The team faced a critical decision point: start a prophylactic IV epinephrine infusion to preempt the next wave, or adopt an expectant approach hoping the waves were dampening? Fortunately, the intensity of her reactions decreased with each episode, sparing her from invasive continuous infusion, but the uncertainty during those 12 hours underscores how ill-equipped we are for multiphasic events.

One of the most persistent myths in anaphylaxis management is the protective role of corticosteroids against biphasic reactions. In this case, following tradition (and conflicting hospital protocols), the patient received both prednisone and dexamethasone after her first relapse. We need to stop doing this reflexively. The evidence simply isn't there. A systematic review examining 22 studies found only one supporting steroid use for preventing biphasic reactions, while 21 showed no benefit whatsoever. Worse still, some data indicates that prehospital steroid administration might actually increase the likelihood of hospital admission, possibly by masking early warning signs or altering immune kinetics in unhelpful ways. Steroids have a place in asthma exacerbations or delayed cutaneous symptoms, but selling them to patients—or ourselves—as insurance against a second hit is medically unsupported and potentially harmful due to side effect profiles.

So, how do we move forward when we can't predict who will bounce back? The answer lies in abandoning the one-size-fits-all observation period in favor of risk stratification. The UK Resuscitation Council has pioneered a tiered approach that makes intuitive sense: low-risk patients get shorter observations, while high-risk patients warrant extended monitoring or ICU admission. In our featured case, risk factors were stacking up rapidly: severe initial presentation with hypotension and hypoxia, the need for multiple doses of epinephrine, and a potential underlying mast cell disorder (evidenced by elevated baseline tryptase). Had we applied a strict risk-stratification model upon her arrival or first relapse, her transfer to ICU-level telemetry monitoring would have happened sooner rather than later. We must also remember that accurate tryptase sampling is crucial for identifying these high-risk substrates like mastocytosis; drawing levels too late or missing the peak window (1–2 hours post-onset) leaves us flying blind regarding long-term risk.

The era of discharging every anaphylaxis patient after exactly four hours needs to end. Instead, we must look at the clinical trajectory. Did they require more than one dose of epinephrine? Was there hypotension or respiratory compromise initially? Is there a history of asthma or mast cell disease? If yes to any of these, your observation window needs to expand significantly—likely beyond 12 hours—and consideration for higher-acuity monitoring should be immediate. For low-risk patients with mild cutaneous-only reactions responding to a single dose, shorter stays remain reasonable resources stewardship. Until we have better predictive biomarkers or large-scale registries to guide us, clinical judgment fueled by aggressive risk stratification is our best defense against the silent return of anaphylaxis.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Abandon rigid time limits:** The traditional 4–6 hour observation rule fails patients with multiphasic anaphylaxis; observation duration must be dictated by individual risk factors rather than a fixed clock.
*   **Dose epinephrine correctly:** Adult patients >50 kg generally require 0.5 mg IM epinephrine; reliance on fixed-dose 0.3 mg auto-injectors in hospital settings may lead to underdosing and increased need for rescue interventions.
*   **Stop using steroids for prophylaxis:** Current evidence does not support corticosteroids for preventing biphasic reactions; their routine use should be discontinued in favor of vigilant monitoring and repeat epinephrine if needed.
*   **Identify high-risk features:** Factors such as hypotension during initial presentation, requirement for >1 dose of epinephrine, severe respiratory symptoms, and underlying mast cell disorders warrant extended observation (>12–24 hours) and potentially ICU-level care.
*   **Optimize tryptase timing:** To evaluate for underlying mastocytosis or confirm diagnosis accurately obtain tryptase levels immediately (without delaying treatment), at peak (1–2 hours post-onset), and at baseline (>24 hours after full resolution).



# The Steroid Myth Persists: Re-evaluating Adjunctive Therapies in Complex Anaphylactic Courses

We love a good "zebra" case, but sometimes the most educational moments come from watching a "horse" get treated like a unicorn. Consider the case of a woman in her 60s who rolled into the ED with textbook anaphylaxis after taking her first dose of clarithromycin. She was hypotensive, hypoxic, flushed, and agitated. She got epinephrine. She improved. And then, because nature loves to keep us on our toes, she crashed again. And again. And again.

This wasn't just a biphasic reaction; it was a **multiphasic** nightmare, with four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock occurring within the first 12 hours despite standard care. While the clinical team navigated this storm brilliantly—escalating monitoring to the ICU and adjusting epinephrine dosing from 0.3 mg auto-injectors to weight-appropriate 0.5 mg IM doses—their management of adjunctive therapies tells a story we see all too often in emergency medicine. Despite the patient already being in the throes of recurrent shock, the team reached for corticosteroids: 30 mg of IV prednisone daily *plus* a one-time hit of 8 mg IV dexamethasone.

Why? Because somewhere deep in our collective medical psyche, we still believe steroids prevent the next wave. But as this harrowing case illustrates—and as the literature has been screaming for years—that belief is a myth that needs to die.

## When One Episode Isn't Enough

The defining feature of this case wasn't just the severity of the initial reaction to clarithromycin, but its stubborn refusal to stay resolved. Biphasic anaphylaxis, defined as a recurrence of symptoms after complete resolution without re-exposure to the allergen, is estimated to occur in 4–6% of cases. Multiphasic reactions, involving multiple relapses, are exponentially rarer and present a unique logistical and physiological challenge.

In this patient's journey, the initial response to IM epinephrine was robust. Blood pressure normalized, and oxygenation improved. Yet, within hours, she plummeted back into shock with cyanosis and severe dyspnea. This cycle repeated three more times. It is crucial to note that with each successive episode, the intensity waned slightly—a glimmer of hope that eventually allowed for discharge after 24 hours of stability. Retrospective analysis suggested an underlying driver: probable systemic mastocytosis. Her baseline tryptase was elevated, and she exhibited other stigmata like hepatomegaly and neuropsychiatric symptoms. While she didn't meet every formal WHO criterion (largely due to limited bone marrow sampling), the clinical picture strongly suggests her mast cells were primed to dump mediators at the slightest provocation.

But here is the pivot point for our education: **The steroids didn't stop the relapses.** The patient received significant steroid coverage *after* the first recurrence began, yet she still suffered two more distinct anaphylactic episodes before finally stabilizing. If steroids were the magic shield against biphasic or multiphasic progression, this patient should have been protected after that double-dose regimen. She wasn't. The resolution came from time, vigilant monitoring, and repeated doses of epinephrine when indicated—not from glucocorticoids.

## The Evidence Gap: Why We Keep Giving Steroids

If you walk into almost any ED or urgent care center today and present with anaphylaxis, you will likely leave with a prescription for prednisone or a dose of dexamethasone. It feels right. It feels proactive. We tell ourselves we are preventing that dangerous "second wave." However, this practice is driven by tradition and pathophysiological plausibility rather than clinical evidence.

The data telling us to stop is not new; it's just inconveniently ignored. A landmark 2017 systematic review examined 22 studies regarding corticosteroid use in acute anaphylaxis. The result? Only **one** study supported their use for preventing biphasic reactions. The other **21** found absolutely no evidence that steroids reduce the risk of symptom recurrence or shorten hospital stays more than placebo or standard care alone (epinephrine and fluids).

More concerning is the potential for harm rather than benefit beyond mere futility. Recent literature highlights that prehospital or early ED administration of corticosteroids may actually be associated with an *increased* likelihood of ICU admission or prolonged ward stays. While causality isn't fully established here—it could be that sicker patients simply get more drugs—the signal is clear: steroids are not benign placeholders. They introduce risks of hyperglycemia (particularly relevant in our diabetic patient), hypertension, insomnia, and psychiatric disturbances without offering the promised insurance policy against relapse.

So why does the habit persist? It's largely about risk mitigation psychology and patient expectation management Clinicians fear litigation if a patient returns in shock after being sent home without "doing everything possible." # When Anaphylaxis Won't Quit: Managing Refractory and Multiphasic Episodes Without a Playbook

We all know the algorithm for anaphylaxis. It's burned into our muscle memory: recognize the pattern, hit the thigh with epinephrine, flood the veins with fluids, and watch the patient turn the corner. Usually, it works. The hypotension resolves, the wheezing stops, and we breathe a sigh of relief as we move them to a ward bed for a few hours of observation. But what happens when the patient turns the corner, only to plummet back into shock an hour later? And then again? And *again*?

This isn't just "biphasic" anaphylaxis anymore; this is **multiphasic anaphylaxis**, a terrifying clinical entity where the fire refuses to stay out despite adequate fuel removal and standard suppression tactics. Unlike refractory anaphylaxis—where the patient simply never responds to initial epinephrine and requires immediate escalation to infusions—multiphasic cases present a unique psychological and physiological trap. You think you've won, you stand down, and then the mast cells unleash hell all over again.

A recent case report by Schneider et al. highlights this exact nightmare scenario. A woman in her 60s presented with classic anaphylaxis after taking her first dose of clarithromycin for erysipelas. She looked textbook: hypotensive (74/50 mmHg), hypoxic, flushed, and agitated. She got 0.3 mg IM epinephrine via auto-injector, antihistamines, oxygen, and fluids. She responded beautifully. Her blood pressure normalized, her breathing improved, and she was admitted to an acute internal ward. But within 12 hours, she didn't just have one recurrence; she suffered **three more distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock**. Each time, she required rescue epinephrine. By the fourth episode, her team was staring down the barrel of a decision with almost zero literature to guide them: Do we start a prophylactic IV epinephrine drip to clamp down on these waves before they crash her again, or do we trust that the episodes are waning in severity and continue reacting with IM doses as they arise?

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: Is It Biphasic or Just Bad Timing?

Before we dive into management, we have to ensure we aren't mislabeling sustained absorption as multiphasic disease. True biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis is defined by a recurrence of symptoms after complete resolution of the initial reaction, without re-exposure to the trigger. In the case above, the trigger was a single oral dose of clarithromycin taken hours prior. While drug absorption can be variable, the distinct asymptomatic intervals between this patient's shocks suggest a pathophysiological "storm" rather than continued gut absorption.

The incidence of biphasic reactions is notoriously hard to pin down, ranging wildly from 0.4% to 23% in literature, though most robust data suggests it hovers around 4–6%. Multiphasic courses are even rarer and poorly characterized. What makes this specific case fascinating—and instructive—is the underlying substrate. Workup revealed elevated baseline tryptase and hepatomegaly, pointing strongly toward undiagnosed **systemic mastocytosis**. While she didn't meet every formal WHO criterion (likely due to sampling limitations), the clinical picture fits: patients with mast cell disorders have a lower threshold for degranulation and a higher propensity for severe, recurrent reactions. If your anaphylaxis patient keeps bouncing back despite perfect initial care, you need to start thinking about whether their mast cell burden is unusually high.

## The Therapeutic Tightrope: Infusion vs. Expectant Management

This is where the rubber meets the road in terms of clinical judgment. In our case report, the consulting allergists weighed two very different paths once the pattern of recurrence became clear after the second relapse.

**Option A: Preemptive IV Epinephrine Infusion.**
The logic here is aggressive stabilization. If the patient is having cyclic degranulation events causing hemodynamic collapse why wait for the next crash? Starting a titrated IV epinephrine infusion (typically starting at 1–4 mcg/min) creates a floor for vascular tone and bronchodilation that might blunt subsequent waves before they become symptomatic shock states. This is standard for *refractory* anaphylaxis (no response to IM epi), but using it prophylactically in a *responding-but-relapsing* patient is off-label territory born of desperation. The risk? Overtreatment leading to tachyarrhythmias, myocardial ischemia (especially in older adults or those with CAD), or severe hypertension once the endogenous storm finally subsides.

**Option B: Expectant Management with Repeated IM Dosing.**
This approach relies on close monitoring and rapid reaction. You keep the patient in a high-acuity setting (ICU or step-down), have epinephrine drawn up and ready at the bedside, and treat each wave as it breaks. The advantage is avoiding the complications of continuous vasoactive support in a patient who technically stabilizes between episodes. In Schneider's case, they noted that each successive episode was less severe than the last—from profound hypoxia and cyanosis to mere dyspnea and rash. This natural attenuation suggested the mediator release was exhausting itself.

The team chose Option B. They opted against the infusion banked on the decreasing severity of attacks and committed to rapid IM rescue (switching from 0.3 mg auto-injectors to manually drawn 0.5 mg doses). It worked; no further relapses occurred after 24 hours. But let's be honest: that was a high-stakes gamble made in real-time without a randomized controlled trial to back it up.

# The Hidden Culprit: Screening for Systemic Mastocytosis in Severe or Atypical Anaphylaxis

We've all been there. You treat anaphylaxis, the patient responds beautifully to epinephrine, vitals normalize, and you breathe a sigh of relief. Then, six hours later, they crash again. It's terrifying, it defies the standard "treat and observe" narrative, and it forces you to question everything about the case. While biphasic anaphylaxis is a known entity—with incidence rates hovering somewhere between 4% and 6% depending on who you ask—true *multiphasic* anaphylaxis, characterized by multiple distinct relapses despite adequate treatment and no re-exposure, is a rare beast that tests even seasoned clinicians.

But what if that recurrent collapse isn't just bad luck or a stubborn allergic reaction? What if it's a clue pointing to an underlying mast cell disorder? A recent case report by Schneider et al. details a harrowing course of a woman in her 60s who suffered four distinct episodes of anaphylactic shock after a single dose of clarithromycin. She didn't just bounce back; she spiraled through repeated cycles of hypotension, hypoxia, and agitation, requiring escalating doses of epinephrine and eventual ICU admission. The twist? This wasn't just a severe allergy; it was likely the first presentation of undiagnosed systemic mastocytosis. This case serves as a stark reminder that when anaphylaxis behaves badly—refusing to stay resolved or occurring without a clear trigger—we need to look deeper than the allergen.

The patient in this case presented with classic signs: flushing, pruritus, throat swelling sensation, and profound hypotension (74/50 mmHg) shortly after taking clarithromycin for erysipelas. She received standard care: IM epinephrine via auto-injector (0.3 mg), antihistamines, fluids, and oxygen. She responded well initially. But then came the relapses. Within 12 hours, she experienced three more episodes of anaphylactic shock. Each required further epinephrine intervention, eventually stepping up from 0.3 mg auto-injectors to manually drawn 0.5 mg doses. The intensity of the attacks waned with each episode, moving from life-threatening hypoxia and cyanosis to mere dyspnea and rash, but the pattern was undeniable. This wasn't refractory anaphylaxis where the drug simply doesn't work; this was multiphasic anaphylaxis, a recurrence of symptoms after full resolution, happening repeatedly without new antigen exposure.

In the heat of the moment, the team faced a dilemma familiar to anyone managing complex allergies: do we start a prophylactic epinephrine infusion to preempt the next hit, or do we wait and see if the storm burns itself out? They chose an expectant approach, supported by corticosteroids (despite growing evidence questioning their utility in preventing biphasic reactions) and close monitoring. Fortunately, the patient stabilized after 24 hours and was discharged three days later with an EpiPen and antihistamines. However, the story didn't end at discharge. The atypical severity and multiphasic nature prompted an allergology workup that uncovered elevated baseline tryptase levels and hepatomegaly. While she didn't meet every formal WHO criterion for systemic mastocytosis (SM)—largely due to limited bone marrow biopsy material—the clinical picture strongly suggested an underlying mast cell burden that predisposed her to this explosive reaction.

This brings us to one of the most critical failures in acute anaphylaxis management: the timing of tryptase sampling. In this case, no tryptase was drawn during the initial chaotic resuscitation in the ED. The first sample wasn't taken until an hour after the *third* episode, and the baseline wasn't established until 27 hours after the final recurrence. Guidelines are explicit: you need an acute sample within 1–2 hours (max 4) of symptom onset to catch the peak mediator release, and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution. Missing that initial window robs us of valuable data regarding mast cell activation magnitude ($\Delta$-tryptase). In patients with suspected SM, baseline tryptase is often chronically elevated (>20 ng/mL), which lowers the threshold for degranulation and increases the risk of severe or multiphasic courses. If we don't draw these labs correctly during the index event, we may miss the diagnosis entirely until the next potentially fatal encounter.

The presence of systemic mastocytosis changes the game entirely for long-term management. These patients aren't just avoiding peanuts or penicillin; they are walking around with a hyper-reactive immune system prone to spontaneous or trivial-trigger degranulation. Features like unexplained depression, memory issues (neuropsychiatric symptoms), hepatosplenomegaly, or severe reactions to insect stings should raise your suspicion index alongside idiopathic or multiphasic anaphylaxis. While confirming SM requires meeting specific WHO criteria—including major criteria like multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells in bone marrow—clinicians must recognize that "probable" SM based on elevated baseline tryptase and compatible clinical features warrants aggressive follow-up. These patients need specialized allergology input, potential cytoreductive therapies, and strict avoidance strategies that go far beyond standard allergy advice.

Furthermore, this case highlights persistent gaps in our immediate pharmacologic interventions. The patient initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector. While convenient and excellent for reducing hesitation among non-routine users, 0.3 mg is technically under-dosing for most adults weighing over 50 kg (this patient weighed 104 kg). Guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg. There is emerging data suggesting that initial under-dosing correlates with higher rates of escalation care and recurrent symptoms.
