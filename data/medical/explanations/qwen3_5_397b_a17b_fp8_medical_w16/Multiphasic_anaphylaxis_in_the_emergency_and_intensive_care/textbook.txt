## Clinical Presentation and Acute Stabilization of Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is defined as a severe, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction characterized by the rapid onset of symptoms involving the airway, breathing, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal tract, typically accompanied by cutaneous manifestations. While the identification of a specific trigger supports the diagnosis, clinical recognition relies on the constellation of acute symptoms rather than confirmed allergen exposure. In the presented case, the patient exhibited a textbook presentation of anaphylaxis shortly after ingesting her first dose of clarithromycin. The timeline is clinically significant: symptoms began one hour after ingestion and progressed rapidly, necessitating medical attention within another hour. This latency period is consistent with IgE-mediated reactions to oral medications, distinguishing it from immediate intraoperative reactions or delayed T-cell mediated responses. Although the patient had a history of mild beta-lactam allergy and a recent rash attributed to clindamycin, clarithromycin represented a novel exposure, highlighting that anaphylaxis can occur upon first known contact with a drug class if prior sensitization has occurred through cross-reactivity or unrecognized exposure.

The initial assessment in the Emergency Department revealed the hallmark triad of anaphylactic shock: respiratory compromise, circulatory collapse, and extensive cutaneous involvement. The patient presented with hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxemia (SpO2 88% on room air), indicating profound vasodilation and potential ventilation-perfusion mismatch. These hemodynamic and respiratory derangements were accompanied by generalized erythema, urticaria, facial swelling, pruritus, and agitation—a sign often indicative of cerebral hypoperfusion or severe hypoxia. Notably, while the patient reported a sensation of throat swelling, physical examination did not reveal frank angioedema of the tongue or larynx, nor were there audible wheezes on auscultation. This underscores a critical teaching point: the absence of upper airway obstruction or bronchospasm does not rule out anaphylaxis when distributive shock and mucocutaneous signs are present. The presence of hypotension alone in the context of suspected allergen exposure is sufficient to diagnose anaphylactic shock and mandates immediate intervention.

The cornerstone of acute management is the prompt administration of intramuscular (IM) epinephrine. In this case, the patient received 0.3 mg of epinephrine via auto-injector immediately upon suspicion of a severe allergic reaction. Epinephrine acts rapidly to reverse vasodilation via alpha-1 adrenergic stimulation, improve cardiac output via beta-1 effects, and reduce bronchoconstriction and mediator release via beta-2 effects. The patient's positive response—normalization of blood pressure and reduction in cutaneous symptoms—confirms the efficacy of this first-line therapy. However, the case also illuminates a common discrepancy in dosing practices. Current guidelines recommend an adult dose of 0.5 mg (0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg), yet many commercially available auto-injectors contain only 0.3 mg. For an adult weighing 104 kg, as in this case, a 0.3 mg dose represents significant underdosing. While the auto-injector facilitated rapid delivery and minimized hesitation among staff less familiar with drawing up vasoactive medications, the suboptimal dose may have contributed to the instability that followed. Evidence suggests that adults receiving initial doses below 0.5 mg are more likely to require escalation of care and repeat dosing, a pattern observed here as the patient experienced recurrent episodes despite initial stabilization.

Adjunctive therapies played a supportive but secondary role in this patient's resuscitation. Supplemental oxygen was administered via a non-rebreather mask to correct hypoxemia and was successfully discontinued once perfusion improved following epinephrine administration; however, it required reinstatement during subsequent relapses. Intravenous fluid resuscitation with 500 mL of normal saline helped address relative hypovolemia caused by massive vasodilation and capillary leak. Antihistamines, specifically 2 mg of intravenous clemastine, were administered to alleviate cutaneous symptoms such as pruritus and urticaria. It is vital for learners to recognize that while antihistamines and corticosteroids (which were added later in this case) may provide symptomatic relief for skin findings or theoretically delay late-phase reactions, they have no role in reversing airway obstruction or shock in the acute setting. They must never delay or replace epinephrine. The successful initial stabilization of this patient relied entirely on the timely recognition of shock patterns and the administration of epinephrine, setting the stage for the complex monitoring challenges posed by her subsequent multiphasic course.

## Navigating Complex Courses: Biphasic and Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

Understanding the trajectory of anaphylaxis is critical for determining observation periods and anticipating clinical deterioration. While the classic presentation involves a single episode that resolves with treatment, a subset of patients experiences complex courses characterized by symptom recurrence. It is essential to distinguish between four distinct clinical patterns: simple resolution, biphasic anaphylaxis, multiphasic anaphylaxis, and refractory or protracted anaphylaxis. Simple resolution occurs when symptoms abate completely after initial therapy and do not return. Biphasic anaphylaxis is defined as the recurrence of symptoms after a period of complete resolution, typically occurring within 1 to 72 hours of the initial event without re-exposure to the trigger. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, as illustrated in this case report, involves two or more recurrences of symptoms following the initial reaction, creating a roller-coaster clinical course that demands prolonged intensive monitoring. In contrast, refractory anaphylaxis refers to persistent symptoms that fail to respond adequately to repeated doses of epinephrine, while protracted anaphylaxis describes a state where symptoms persist continuously for hours or days without a symptom-free interval. Differentiating these entities is not merely semantic; it dictates whether a clinician should anticipate a new wave of mediator release (biphasic/multiphasic) versus escalating support for ongoing shock (refractory/protracted).

The pathophysiology behind symptom recurrence in the absence of renewed allergen exposure remains incompletely understood but is hypothesized to involve several mechanisms. One theory suggests a "second wave" of inflammatory mediator release from mast cells and basophils that were not fully depleted during the initial degranulation event. Another possibility involves the continued absorption of the allergen from a depot site, such as the gastrointestinal tract following oral ingestion or intramuscular tissue following injection, which sustains antigen presentation and immune activation. In the context of this case, where the patient ingested clarithromycin orally, delayed gastric emptying or variable absorption kinetics could theoretically contribute to prolonged antigen availability. However, the distinct asymptomatic intervals observed between this patient's four episodes strongly favor a mechanism of recurrent mast cell destabilization rather than continuous absorption. Furthermore, underlying mast cell disorders, such as the suspected systemic mastocytosis in this patient, may lower the threshold for degranulation, making the mast cell population hyper-reactive and prone to multiple waves of activation even after the initial trigger has been removed.

Epidemiologically, biphasic reactions are reported in approximately 4% to 6% of anaphylaxis cases, though literature estimates vary widely from 0.4% to 23.3% due to inconsistent definitions and observation protocols. True multiphasic anaphylaxis is significantly rarer and lacks robust incidence data. Identifying patients at risk for these complex courses is challenging because reliable predictors remain elusive. Proposed risk factors include severe initial presentation (e.g., hypotension or respiratory compromise), delayed administration of epinephrine, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses during the initial event, and unknown triggers. In this specific case, the patient exhibited several high-risk features: she presented with hypotensive shock (74/50 mmHg) and hypoxemia shortly after ingesting clarithromycin, necessitating immediate epinephrine. Despite this prompt intervention and an initial favorable response, she experienced three subsequent relapses within 12 hours. This progression underscores a crucial clinical lesson: even when initial stabilization appears successful and vital signs normalize, the potential for multiphasic recurrence mandates extended observation in a monitored setting, particularly for patients with severe index presentations or suspected underlying mast cell pathology.

A notable feature of this patient's multiphasic course was the phenomenon of waning severity with each successive episode. The initial presentation involved profound hypotension, cyanosis, and severe dyspnea indicative of frank anaphylactic shock. The subsequent relapses, while still requiring epinephrine rescue, manifested with progressively milder symptoms, eventually limiting themselves to dyspnea, rash, and pruritus without hemodynamic collapse. This pattern suggests a gradual exhaustion of mediators within the mast cell granules or a diminishing pool of recruitable effector cells with each degranulation event. Recognizing this trend is vital for clinical decision-making; while every episode must be treated aggressively as a potential life-threat until proven otherwise, observing a consistent decrease in severity can provide cautious reassurance that the storm is subsiding. In this case, this downward trajectory influenced the multidisciplinary team to consider an expectant management approach rather than initiating high-risk interventions like continuous intravenous epinephrine infusions preemptively.

Finally，准确 identifying true biphasic or multiphasic reactions requires ruling out diagnostic pitfalls related to incomplete initial treatment or pharmacokinetic variables. A common error is misclassifying refractory anaphylaxis—where symptoms never fully resolved due to inadequate dosing—as a biphasic reaction. For instance, this patient initially received 0.3 mg of epinephrine via auto-injector; while effective enough to stabilize her temporarily, this dose represents potential underdosing for an adult weighing 104 kg (recommended dose ~0.5 mg). If symptoms had persisted immediately after this dose without an asymptomatic window, it would constitute refractory shock due to underdosing rather than a biphasic event. Similarly，持续 symptoms caused by ongoing absorption from slow-release medications or large food boluses can mimic recurrence if not carefully timed against symptom resolution windows. True multiphasic anaphylaxis requires documentation of a clear asymptomatic interval where physiological parameters return to baseline before deteriorating again. In this report，严格 adherence to monitoring allowed clinicians to confirm distinct episodes separated by stability validating the diagnosis of multiphasic anaphylaxis driven by clarithromycin in a patient with probable systemic mastocytosis.

## Advanced Management Strategies and Therapeutic Controversies

The management of multiphasic anaphylaxis in the intensive care unit (ICU) requires navigating significant uncertainties, as standard algorithms designed for single-episode reactions often fail to address the complexity of recurrent symptomatology. In the presented case, the patient's transfer to the ICU was triggered by the first relapse of anaphylactic shock within 12 hours of admission, highlighting a critical gap in monitoring protocols: while guidelines suggest observation periods of 4 to 6 hours for uncomplicated cases, nearly half of biphasic reactions occur between 6 and 12 hours post-event. For patients exhibiting severe initial presentations requiring multiple doses of epinephrine, such as this patient who experienced four distinct episodes, standard ward monitoring without telemetry proves insufficient. The decision to escalate care to the ICU allows for continuous hemodynamic surveillance and immediate access to advanced interventions, a necessity when the trajectory of the reaction shifts from a monophasic event to a protracted, multiphasic course.

A central controversy in managing such recurrences is the role of corticosteroids. Historically, steroids like prednisone and dexamethasone were administered routinely under the assumption that they would prevent biphasic or multiphasic reactions by suppressing late-phase inflammatory responses. In this case, the clinical team administered both intravenous prednisone and dexamethasone following the first recurrence, adhering to traditional hospital protocols despite conflicting evidence. Current literature, including systematic reviews cited in recent discourse, indicates no robust evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis; conversely, some data suggest they may prolong hospital stays or increase adverse events without altering the clinical course. The persistence of steroid use in this scenario underscores the disconnect between emerging evidence-based guidelines and entrenched clinical habits, particularly in high-stakes environments where clinicians seek any potential mechanism to halt symptom recurrence.

When standard intermittent intramuscular (IM) epinephrine fails to prevent relapses, clinicians must consider advanced intervention strategies. Two divergent paths were evaluated for this patient: initiating a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to preemptively stabilize mast cells and maintain vascular tone, or adopting an expectant approach reliant on reactive IM dosing as symptoms re-emerged. Continuous infusion offers theoretical advantages in maintaining steady plasma concentrations and avoiding the peaks and troughs associated with IM absorption, which can be erratic in shock states. However, this strategy carries risks of tachyarrhythmias and myocardial ischemia, particularly in a patient with comorbidities like hypertension and diabetes. Conversely, the expectant approach relies on the observation that the intensity of this patient's episodes was diminishing over time, suggesting a self-limiting process. The decision to forego immediate infusion in favor of close monitoring and reactive IM epinephrine (escalating from 0.3 mg auto-injectors to 0.5 mg manual doses) reflected a careful risk-benefit analysis tailored to the patient's improving trend, ultimately proving successful without exposing her to the hazards of vasoactive drips.

Supportive care dynamics also evolve significantly during multiphasic courses. Unlike single-episode anaphylaxis where fluid resuscitation and oxygen therapy are often transient needs, this patient required repeated adjustments. Supplemental oxygen via non-rebreather mask was necessary during multiple episodes due to recurring hypoxemia and dyspnea, while fluid boluses were reiterated to counteract profound vasodilation and capillary leak associated with each shock episode. Notably, the dosage of epinephrine required scrutiny; the initial reliance on 0.3 mg auto-injectors represented a potential under-dosing for an adult weighing 104 kg, as guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg. The transition to manually prepared 0.5 mg doses for subsequent episodes aligned better with pharmacokinetic principles for her body mass, likely contributing to more effective resolution of later attacks. This case illustrates that supportive care in multiphasic anaphylaxis is not static but demands dynamic recalibration based on response patterns and physiological parameters.

Ultimately, this case exposes critical gaps in current guidelines regarding multiphasic anaphylaxis. While definitions for biphasic reactions exist, consensus on managing three or more recourses is absent, leaving clinicians to rely on expert judgment rather than standardized protocols. The lack of validated predictors for identifying which patients will progress to multiphasic courses complicates resource allocation and discharge planning. Furthermore, the inability to perform confirmatory allergy testing due to safety concerns leaves the precise trigger mechanism partially inferential, though the clinical picture strongly suggested clarithromycin-induced anaphylaxis possibly exacerbated by underlying systemic mastocytosis. Until registries accumulate sufficient data on these rare presentations to inform expert consensus, management will remain a delicate balance between aggressive pre-emptive stabilization and conservative reactive care, guided by real-time assessment of symptom severity trends and individual patient risk factors.

## Unmasking Underlying Pathology: The Role of Systemic Mastocytosis

The multiphasic nature of this patient's anaphylactic reaction to clarithromycin served as a critical clinical cue to investigate an underlying mast cell disorder. While most cases of anaphylaxis are isolated events triggered by a specific allergen in otherwise healthy individuals, severe, recurrent, or idiopathic reactions should raise suspicion for systemic mastocytosis (SM). In this condition, the accumulation of neoplastic mast cells in various tissues creates a hyper-reactive state where the threshold for degranulation is lowered, and the magnitude of mediator release is amplified. Clinicians must recognize "red flags" that distinguish simple allergic reactions from those driven by clonal mast cell disease. Key indicators include anaphylaxis triggered by hymenoptera stings or medications without prior sensitization, unusually severe hemodynamic collapse disproportionate to the trigger, and particularly, biphasic or multiphasic courses where symptoms recur despite appropriate epinephrine administration and removal of the inciting agent. In this case, the patient's history of a severe local reaction to a wasp sting and the unprecedented severity of her reaction to a first-time exposure to clarithromycin warranted a deeper etiological search beyond standard allergy testing.

Central to the diagnostic workup of suspected mast cell disorders is the precise measurement and interpretation of serum tryptase levels. Tryptase, a protease stored in mast cell granules, serves as the primary biomarker for mast cell activation. Guidelines dictate a specific sampling strategy to differentiate between acute degranulation and elevated baseline burden: an initial sample should be drawn as soon as feasible during the reaction, a second sample 1 to 2 hours post-onset (when levels peak), and a third sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish the baseline value. In this case report, adherence to these intervals was imperfect; no samples were obtained during the first two episodes. However, a sample taken one hour after the third episode confirmed acute elevation, while a follow-up sample taken 27 hours after the final recurrence demonstrated persistently elevated baseline tryptase. This distinction is vital because while acute elevations confirm anaphylaxis occurred, an elevated baseline tryptase (>20 ng/mL) is a minor criterion for SM and suggests an increased total body mast cell burden rather than merely transient activation.

Definitive diagnosis of Systemic Mastocytosis relies on the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, which require either one major criterion plus one minor criterion, or three minor criteria in the absence of the major one. The major criterion involves identifying multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells (>15 clustered cells) in bone marrow or extracutaneous organs via histology. Minor criteria include: >25% spindle-shaped or atypical mast cells in biopsies; detection of a *KIT* D816V mutation; expression of CD25 and/or CD2 on mast cells; and persistently elevated baseline serum tryptase >20 ng/mL. In this patient's evaluation, bone marrow biopsy results were inconclusive regarding the major criterion due to limited material quality, preventing definitive histological confirmation. Furthermore, only two minor criteria were met: the elevated baseline tryptase and likely morphological abnormalities (though specific immunophenotyping details like CD25 expression were not explicitly detailed as positive in the summary). Consequently, while the clinical picture strongly pointed toward SM, the patient did not strictly fulfill the formal diagnostic thresholds, illustrating the diagnostic challenges posed by sampling limitations and atypical presentations.

Beyond laboratory values, the clinical phenotype of systemic mastocytosis often includes multi-organ involvement resulting from both tissue infiltration and chronic mediator release. This case highlighted several extra-cutaneous findings consistent with SM. Abdominal sonography revealed hepatomegaly, suggesting hepatic infiltration by mast cells, a common manifestation in systemic disease. Additionally, the patient's history included depression and memory problems. Emerging evidence links mast cell disorders to neuropsychiatric symptoms, attributed to the neurotoxic effects of chronically released mediators such as histamine and prostaglandins on the central nervous system. Recognizing this constellation of symptoms—severe recurrent anaphylaxis, organomegaly, neuropsychiatric complaints, and elevated baseline tryptase—is essential for clinicians managing complex allergic presentations.
