Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Pathophysiology] Unlike adult cases where alcohol-induced chronic pancreatitis is the dominant etiology, what are the two primary categories of etiologies responsible for pancreatopleural fistulas in the pediatric population?

In the pediatric population, pancreatopleural fistulas arise primarily from two distinct etiologic categories: **pancreatic trauma** and **underlying chronic pancreatitis**, the latter of which is frequently driven by **genetic mutations** rather than alcohol use.

Unlike adults, where chronic alcoholic pancreatitis accounts for the vast majority (up to 99%) of these fistulas, children lack this exposure. Instead, as illustrated in the case of the boy with a history of blunt abdominal injury from a bicycle handle, **trauma** serves as a critical non-hereditary trigger, representing approximately one-third of pediatric cases. When trauma is absent, the etiology shifts toward **hereditary or idiopathic chronic pancreatitis**. In these instances, genetic anomalies—specifically mutations in *PRSS1*, *SPINK1*, *CFTR*, or *CTRC* genes—are the predominant drivers of ductal disruption. Regardless of whether the inciting event is acute trauma or progressive genetic inflammation, the pathophysiology remains consistent: a rupture in the posterior pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst allows enzyme-rich fluid to track retroperitoneally, ascend through the esophageal or aortic hiatus, and breach the pleura, creating a "leaky pipe" that manifests clinically as recurrent, often hemorrhagic, pleural effusions.
