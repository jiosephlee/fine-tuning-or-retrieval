Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Clinical Presentation and the 'Pulmonary Mask' Phenomenon

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) in the pediatric population frequently presents with a deceptive clinical picture known as the "pulmonary mask," where profound respiratory symptoms obscure the underlying abdominal pathology. In this case, the patient presented not with classic signs of pancreatitis such as epigastric pain, vomiting, or fever, but rather with progressive dyspnea, tachypnea, and right-sided chest pain. This dissociation between the site of injury (the pancreas) and the site of symptom manifestation (the thorax) is characteristic of PPF, occurring because pancreatic enzymes track retroperitoneally through diaphragmatic hiatuses—typically the esophageal or aortic openings—to enter the pleural space. Consequently, nearly 70% of reported pediatric cases present with predominant respiratory complaints, leading clinicians to initially direct their diagnostic workup toward primary pulmonary etiologies such as tuberculosis, malignancy, or empyema. In this specific instance, the presence of a family history of tuberculosis and mildly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels in the pleural fluid further reinforced this misconception, resulting in the empirical initiation of antitubercular therapy before the true diagnosis was established.

The nature of the pleural effusion itself provides critical diagnostic clues that distinguish PPF from other causes of exudative effusions. Unlike typical parapneumonic effusions which are often purulent or serous, the fluid in PPF is characteristically recurrent, massive in volume, and frequently hemorrhagic. This patient experienced three separate episodes of rapid re-accumulation requiring intervention, with each thoracocentesis yielding approximately one liter of dark red fluid. The recurrence despite drainage suggests a continuous high-flow leak rather than a static collection or inflammatory process. While malignancy and esophageal perforation (Boerhaave's syndrome) can also produce amylase-rich or bloody effusions, the absence of weight loss, normal peripheral smears, negative cytology, and lack of vomiting helped rule out these differentials. The defining biochemical feature, which ultimately pierced the "pulmonary mask," was the markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L). Pleural fluid amylase levels exceeding 1000 IU/L are considered pathognomonic for PPF, specifically indicating pancreatic-type isoamylase rather than the salivary-type elevation seen in esophageal rupture or certain malignancies.

Physical examination findings in such cases reflect the mechanics of a large space-occupying lesion rather than intrinsic lung parenchymal disease. The patient exhibited stony dullness to percussion over the right subscapular and axillary regions, significantly diminished vocal fremitus, and absent breath sounds on the affected side. These signs confirm a massive fluid collection compressing the lung tissue, consistent with the hydropneumothorax and multiloculated collection later visualized on imaging. Crucially, the cardiovascular examination revealed tachycardia secondary to respiratory compromise and hypoxia (saturation 88%–89% on room air), yet lacked murmurs or signs of congenital heart disease that might suggest alternative causes of effusion. The disconnect between these severe thoracic findings and a soft, non-tender abdomen underscores why abdominal pathology is easily overlooked; without specific inquiry regarding antecedent trauma, the pancreatic origin remains hidden.

The pivotal moment in resolving this diagnostic dilemma was the meticulous re-evaluation of the patient's history, which uncovered a concealed event: blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar one month prior to symptom onset. Pediatric patients may omit such histories due to fear of reprimand or because they do not associate a past minor injury with current respiratory distress. In PPF caused by trauma, there is often a latency period allowing a pseudocyst to form and subsequently rupture into the mediastinum before respiratory symptoms become apparent. Had this history not been elicited during the third admission, the cycle of recurrent drainage and inappropriate antimicrobial therapy would likely have continued. This case illustrates that in any child presenting with unexplained, recurrent, hemorrhagic pleural effusions with high amylase content, clinicians must actively search for evidence of pancreatic disruption, regardless of whether abdominal symptoms are present at all times.

## Pathophysiology and Etiology in the Pediatric Population

The fundamental pathophysiology of a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) involves the creation of an abnormal communication between the pancreatic ductal system and the pleural cavity, effectively acting as a "leaky pipe" that diverts pancreatic secretions into the thorax. In the case presented, the mechanism was initiated by blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle, which caused a disruption in the pancreatic duct. Unlike anterior ruptures that release enzymatic fluid into the peritoneal cavity resulting in pancreatic ascites, this patient likely sustained an injury to the posterior aspect of the pancreas. When the posterior wall is compromised, leaked pancreatic juice tracks into the retroperitoneal space rather than the peritoneum. Following the path of least resistance, this fluid ascends through the retrocrural space, traversing natural diaphragmatic openings—most commonly the aortic or esophageal hiatuses—to enter the mediastinum and eventually erode through the parietal pleura. While direct trans-diaphragmatic erosion is possible, tracking through these hiatal defects is the predominant anatomical route, explaining why the pathology originates in the abdomen yet manifests primarily as recurrent, massive pleural effusions.

Once established, the fistula is sustained by the potent proteolytic activity of pancreatic enzymes. The continuous flow of enzyme-rich fluid prevents spontaneous closure by causing local tissue necrosis and inflammation along the tract, keeping the channel patent. This dynamic was evident in the patient's presentation of dark red, hemorrhagic pleural fluid with markedly elevated amylase and lipase levels. The high concentration of these enzymes not only confirms the pancreatic origin but also contributes to the chemical irritation of the pleura, driving further exudation and preventing resolution despite repeated thoracenteses. The persistence of this flow creates a cycle where mechanical drainage alone cannot resolve the issue; the upstream source of secretion must be suppressed or diverted to allow the tract to heal.

Etiologically, PPF presents a distinct dichotomy between adult and pediatric populations. In adults, particularly in large series such as those from Japan cited in literature reviews, nearly all cases are secondary to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis leading to ductal strictures and pseudocyst formation. Conversely, in children, alcohol is not a factor; instead, etiologies are dominated by blunt abdominal trauma and hereditary predispositions. Trauma accounts for approximately one-third of pediatric cases, as seen in this report where a specific history of impact preceded symptoms by one month. However, clinicians must remain vigilant for underlying genetic vulnerabilities that may lower the threshold for ductal injury or promote chronic inflammation. Mutations in genes such as *PRSS1* (cationic trypsinogen), *SPINK1* (serine protease inhibitor Kazal type 1), *CFTR* (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator), and *CTRC* (chymotrypsin C) are significant contributors to pediatric pancreatitis. These genetic factors can lead to premature activation of trypsin within the pancreas or impaired inhibition thereof, resulting in autodigestion and ductal weakness even after minor trauma. Therefore, while trauma was the precipitating event in this case, understanding the potential role of hereditary pancreatitis is crucial for long-term management and family counseling in pediatric patients presenting with unexplained or recurrent pancreatic complications.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Multimodal Workup

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates the classic "pulmonary mask" of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF), where respiratory symptoms obscure the underlying abdominal pathology. Initially, the clinical presentation of recurrent right-sided pleural effusion, chest pain, and a history of contact with a grandparent suffering from pulmonary tuberculosis logically directed the workup toward infectious etiologies. The presence of dark red, hemorrhagic fluid and mildly elevated Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) levels further supported an empirical trial of antitubercular therapy. However, the rapid recurrence of symptoms despite drainage and the negative GeneXpert for *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* necessitated a broadening of the differential diagnosis. Clinicians must systematically exclude other causes of exudative or hemorrhagic effusions, including empyema (ruled out by the absence of fever and neutrophilic predominance), malignancy (excluded via normal blood counts, peripheral smears, and negative cytology), and Boerhaave's syndrome. While esophageal perforation can also yield amylase-rich fluid, the patient's lack of vomiting, ability to pass stool normally, and specific imaging findings helped distinguish PPF from this surgical emergency.

The pivotal moment in the diagnostic algorithm occurred when a meticulous re-evaluation of the history uncovered blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior. This clue shifted the focus from the thorax to the abdomen, prompting biochemical analysis of the pleural fluid for pancreatic enzymes. The results revealed massively elevated amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) levels. A pleural fluid amylase concentration exceeding 1000 IU/L is considered pathognomonic for PPF, reflecting the direct communication between the pancreatic ductal system and the pleural space. To ensure specificity, particularly in cases where salivary gland pathology or esophageal rupture might be suspected, amylase isoenzyme typing can be employed. In PPF, the elevation is driven by pancreatic-type amylase, whereas conditions like mumps or esophageal perforation typically demonstrate salivary-type amylase dominance. In this pediatric case, the sheer magnitude of the enzyme elevation alongside the clinical context strongly confirmed a pancreatic origin without immediate need for isoform separation, though understanding this distinction remains vital for complex presentations.

Once biochemical evidence pointed toward a pancreatic source, advanced imaging was required to delineate the anatomical defect and guide management. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest served as an essential intermediate step; it successfully identified a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the paraesophageal region to the pancreatic tail and ruled out diaphragmatic hernia or intrathoracic malignancy. However, CT has significant limitations in visualizing the actual fistulous tract, detecting it in only 33% to 47% of cases due to its lower resolution for soft tissue ductal structures compared to magnetic resonance techniques. Consequently, Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was utilized as the definitive non-invasive modality. MRCP provided superior visualization of the ductal anatomy, confirming bilateral fistulas (right greater than left) and demonstrating a tubular cystic collection extending from the distal pancreatic body and tail through the esophageal hiatus into the pleural cavity. This imaging hierarchy—moving from CT for gross anatomical localization to MRCP for ductal mapping—is critical for avoiding unnecessary invasive procedures while securing a precise diagnosis.

While MRCP confirmed the diagnosis in this instance, Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the gold standard for both definitive diagnosis and therapeutic planning in PPF. ERCP offers direct cannulation of the pancreatic duct, allowing for high-resolution visualization of the leak site and assessment of downstream strictures or disruptions that may perpetuate fistula formation. Although ERCP is invasive and carries risks such as post-procedural pancreatitis, its utility lies in its dual capability: it not only confirms the fistula with higher sensitivity than cross-sectional imaging but also facilitates immediate intervention through transpapillary stenting or sphincterotomy to reduce intraductal pressure. In pediatric populations where radiation exposure is a concern and anesthesia risks exist, ERCP is often reserved for cases where non-invasive imaging is inconclusive or when conservative medical management fails and endoscopic therapy is anticipated. In this case report, MRCP provided sufficient anatomical detail to initiate successful conservative management, reserving ERCP as a potential rescue strategy had the fistula failed to close with bowel rest and octreotide therapy.

## Therapeutic Strategies and Management Algorithms

The management of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) follows a stepwise, tiered algorithm designed to minimize invasiveness while effectively sealing the fistulous tract. Given the rarity of PPF in the pediatric population and the absence of standardized guidelines, treatment strategies are largely extrapolated from adult data and case series, prioritizing conservative measures before escalating to endoscopic or surgical interventions. The primary physiological goal across all modalities is to reduce pancreatic exocrine secretion, thereby lowering intraductal pressure and allowing the disrupted duct or pseudocyst to heal. In the presented case, this hierarchy was strictly adhered to, beginning with aggressive medical management which successfully resolved the fistula within ten days, obviating the need for invasive procedures.

Conservative management serves as the initial cornerstone of therapy for patients without significant ductal disruption or obstruction. This protocol mandates strict bowel rest (nil per os, or NPO) to eliminate cephalic and gastric phase stimulation of pancreatic secretion. Concurrently, Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN) is initiated to maintain adequate caloric intake and nutritional status during the period of fasting. Crucially, effective thoracic drainage via an intercostal drain (ICD) or repeated thoracentesis is required to evacuate the pleural collection, prevent lung compression, and monitor fistula output. In the case report, the patient underwent ICD placement to manage the recurrent hydropneumothorax and dark red effusions. While conservative therapy alone has a reported success rate of only approximately 12% in pediatric cases, it is universally attempted first due to its low morbidity. The decision to persist with this approach relies on close monitoring of drain output; a gradual decrease in volume suggests spontaneous closure is occurring.

Pharmacological adjuncts are routinely employed alongside bowel rest to enhance the likelihood of conservative success. Somatostatin analogues, specifically Octreotide, play a pivotal role by directly inhibiting the release of gastrointestinal hormones (such as secretin and cholecystokinin) that stimulate pancreatic exocrine function. By suppressing enzyme secretion, Octreotide reduces the volume of fluid traversing the fistula, facilitating tract closure. In this pediatric case, Octreotide was administered via continuous infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day, falling within the standard therapeutic range of 10–40 µg/kg/day. Additionally, Pancreatic Enzyme Replacement Therapy (PERT) was initiated. Although counterintuitive when trying to suppress secretion, PERT provides negative feedback to the acinar cells via protease-mediated mechanisms, further diminishing endogenous pancreatic output. The combination of NPO, TPN, chest drainage, Octreotide, and PERT resulted in a progressive decline in pleural fluid output in this patient, leading to ICD removal and discharge within ten days.

When conservative management fails—typically defined as persistent high-output drainage beyond two to three weeks—or if imaging reveals significant ductal anomalies such as strictures or complete disruptions escalation to endoscopic therapy is indicated. Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with trans-papillary stenting has emerged as a highly effective intermediate strategy, boasting success rates exceeding 90% in mixed-age cohorts and 100% in some pediatric series. The rationale for stenting is mechanical: bridging the site of the leak with a stent lowers intraductal pressure upstream of the obstruction or disruption, redirecting pancreatic flow preferentially into the duodenum rather than through the fistula into the pleural space. Had this patient demonstrated continued leakage despite optimal medical therapy, ERCP would have been the logical next step to place a pancreatic stent or perform balloon dilatation if a stricture was identified proximal to the leak.

Surgical intervention remains the final recourse for refractory cases where both medical and endoscopic therapies fail, or when anatomical complexity precludes less invasive options. Surgical options are tailored to the location of the ductal injury and the presence of associated pathology such as pseudocysts or chronic inflammation. Procedures may include distal pancreatectomy for lesions confined to the tail (as suggested by the tracking collection in this case's tail region), cystojejunostomy for large pseudocysts, or longitudinal pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow procedure) for dilated ducts with multiple strictures. While surgery offers a high definitive cure rate (~93%), it carries higher risks including operative mortality (<5%) and potential long-term exocrine or endocrine insufficiency. Therefore, surgery is reserved for scenarios where conservative measures yield no improvement after 3–4 weeks or when severe ductal obstruction prevents successful stenting. The decision-making framework ultimately hinges on balancing the duration of symptoms and daily drain output against the risks of procedural escalation, aiming to resolve the "pulmonary mask" of PPF by addressing its abdominal root cause efficiently.

## Clinical Outcomes, Prognosis, and Key Takeaways

The clinical course of a pediatric pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is defined by the responsiveness of the fistulous tract to pancreatic rest and the anatomical integrity of the pancreatic duct. In this case, the timeline for resolution was rapid once the correct diagnosis was established; following the initiation of conservative management comprising nil per oral status, total parenteral nutrition, and octreotide infusion, the intercostal drain output gradually diminished over ten days before ceasing entirely. This favorable trajectory underscores the importance of monitoring daily drainage volume as the primary metric for therapeutic success during the initial conservative phase. While adult literature often cites higher success rates for medical management due to the prevalence of alcohol-induced chronic pancreatitis where ductal disruption may be less acute, pediatric data suggests a more guarded prognosis for conservative therapy alone. Studies indicate that purely medical management succeeds in only approximately 12% of pediatric cases, largely because underlying etiologies in children frequently involve genetic mutations or significant traumatic ductal disruptions that resist spontaneous closure. Consequently, if drainage does not substantially decrease within two to three weeks, or if the patient exhibits signs of persistent leakage, the clinical algorithm must swiftly transition to endoscopic intervention.

When comparing treatment modalities in the pediatric population, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with trans-papillary stenting emerges as a highly efficacious second-line strategy, boasting success rates approaching 100% in reported pediatric cohorts compared to the modest efficacy of medical management. Stenting serves to bridge the ductal disruption, reducing intraductal pressure and diverting flow away from the fistula, typically achieving closure within two to three weeks. Surgical options, such as distal pancreatectomy or drainage procedures like the Puestow operation, are generally reserved for cases where endoscopic access is failed due to severe strictures or when both medical and endoscopic therapies prove ineffective. Although surgery carries a high success rate of roughly 93%, it is associated with inherent operative risks and potential long-term consequences regarding exocrine and endocrine pancreatic function. Therefore, the prognostic outlook for children with PPF is generally excellent provided there is a timely escalation of care from conservative measures to definitive ductal decompression when indicated.

Long-term follow-up is critical not only to monitor for recurrence of pleural effusion but also to assess for the development of chronic pancreatitis and nutritional deficits. As demonstrated in this case, the patient remained symptom-free post-discharge with no recurrent effusions, yet required ongoing surveillance to ensure sustained recovery. Clinicians must vigilantly evaluate for signs of pancreatic insufficiency, given that the underlying trauma or inflammatory process can compromise acinar cell function over time. Furthermore, because a subset of pediatric pancreatitis cases stems from hereditary factors (such as *PRSS1* or *SPINK1* mutations), patients presenting with idiopathic or trauma-exacerbated PPF should undergo genetic counseling and testing if not previously performed. Nutritional rehabilitation is equally paramount; transitioning from total parenteral nutrition to a balanced diet while potentially maintaining pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy ensures adequate growth and prevents malabsorption-related complications in this vulnerable demographic.

The trajectory of this specific patient highlights how a single piece of historical information—the revelation of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle—fundamentally altered the treatment pathway and prevented further iatrogenic harm. Initially, the clinical focus was misdirected toward pulmonary tuberculosis due to contact history and mildly elevated adenosine deaminase levels, leading to unnecessary empirical antitubercular therapy. It was only after eliciting the history of trauma and correlating it with the characteristic "tracking" collection on CT and massively elevated pleural amylase that the diagnosis shifted from a primary pulmonary disease to an abdominal catastrophe manifesting thoracically. This pivot allowed for the immediate cessation of inappropriate medications and the institution of targeted pancreatic rest and somatostatin analogue therapy, resulting in rapid resolution. Had the trauma history remained concealed due to psychosocial factors like bullying, the patient likely would have faced prolonged diagnostic uncertainty, repeated invasive thoracic procedures, and potential progression to sepsis or respiratory failure.

The core clinical lesson derived from this case is the necessity of maintaining a high index of suspicion for extra-thoracic etiologies in any child presenting with recurrent, unexplained pleural effusions, particularly those that are hemorrhagic or rich in enzymes. PPF acts as a "pulmonary mask," where respiratory distress dominates the clinical picture while the primary pathology resides silently in the abdomen. Diagnostic delay is common because clinicians naturally prioritize investigating lung parenchyma and pleura first. However, when faced with recurrent effusions that re-accumulate rapidly after aspiration, especially in the absence of classic infectious signs like high-grade fever or purulent sputum, measuring pleural fluid amylase becomes a mandatory diagnostic step. A pleural amylase level exceeding 1000 IU/L is virtually pathognomonic for PPF and should immediately trigger abdominal imaging (CT or MRCP) and a meticulous re-evaluation of abdominal history, including subtle or suppressed accounts of trauma. Early recognition breaks cycle of misdiagnosis, sparing patients from futile pulmonary interventions and directing them toward life-saving pancreatic management.
