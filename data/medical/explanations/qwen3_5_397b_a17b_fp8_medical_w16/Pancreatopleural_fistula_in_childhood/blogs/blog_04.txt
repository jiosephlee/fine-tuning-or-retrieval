# Imaging the Invisible Tract: Why MRCP Trumps CT in Suspected Pancreatopleural Fistula

We love a good mystery in the ED, but we hate the kind that sends a kid home three times before we figure out what's actually killing them. Picture this: a morbidly obese 11-year-old boy rolls into your shop with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions, chest pain, and respiratory distress. You tap him, and out comes a liter of dark red fluid. He gets better, goes home, and comes back six days later. Then again five days after that.

Your brain immediately jumps to the usual suspects. With a family history of TB contact and mildly elevated ADA in the fluid, you start anti-tubercular therapy. It feels logical. But when he returns for a *third* admission with the same massive hemorrhagic effusion, something isn't adding up. The lungs are just the victim here; the crime scene is in the abdomen.

This is the classic "pulmonary mask" of a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF), an entity so rare in pediatrics that fewer than 50 cases have ever been reported. In this specific case, the breakthrough didn't come from repeating the chest X-ray or sticking another needle in the pleural space. It came when the terrified child finally admitted to blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar a month prior—a detail hidden due to fear of bullying. Once that door opened, the imaging strategy had to shift instantly from looking for pneumonia to hunting for a leaky pancreatic duct. And this is where many of us stumble: relying on CT when we should be reaching for MRCP.

When the team finally ordered a contrast-enhanced CT of the chest and abdomen, it did its job partially. It showed patchy consolidation, a hydropneumothorax, and a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region down to the tail of the pancreas. That's helpful, sure. It tells you there is fluid moving where it shouldn't be. But here is the rub: CT scans are notoriously bad at actually *seeing* the fistula itself. Literature suggests CT depicts the actual fistulous tract in only 33% to 47% of cases. It sees the aftermath—the pseudocyst, the inflammation, the fluid collection—but it often misses the tiny, torturous pipe connecting the pancreatic duct to the pleural cavity. If you base your entire management plan on a CT that says "maybe," you might find yourself pushing straight toward invasive diagnostics or even surgery without a clear roadmap.

This is why Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is the unsung hero in PPF diagnosis. In our case, once suspicion peaked, MRCP was performed immediately and changed everything. Unlike CT, which relies on density differences that can be obscured by inflammation or adjacent structures, MRCP utilizes heavy T2 weighting to make static fluid glow bright white against a black background. ### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect PPF in Recurrent Effusions:** Any child presenting with recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, particularly if hemorrhagic or high in amylase/lipase (>1000 U/L), warrants evaluation for a pancreatopleural fistula regardless of absent abdominal symptoms.
*   **History is Critical:** Dig deep for history of blunt abdominal trauma (e.g., bicycle handlebars), even if delayed or concealed by the patient; this is a leading non-hereditary cause in pediatrics.
*   **CT Has Limitations:** While excellent for identifying pseudocysts and fluid collections, CT fails to visualize the actual fistulous tract in over half of cases (sensitivity ~33–47%).
*   **MRCP is Diagnostic Gold Standard (Non-Invasive):** MRCP should be obtained early to map ductal anatomy and confirm the fistula tract with higher sensitivity (70–80%) before considering invasive ERCP.
*   **Conservative First:** If no major ductal disruption exists, initial management includes bowel rest (NPO), TPN, octreotide infusion, and adequate drainage; reserve ERCP or surgery for failures of medical management or significant ductal strictures.
