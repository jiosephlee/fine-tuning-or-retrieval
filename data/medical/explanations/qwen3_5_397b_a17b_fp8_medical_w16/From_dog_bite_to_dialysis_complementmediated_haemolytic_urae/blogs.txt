# The Dog Bite That Bit Back: Capnocytophaga canimorsus and the Mask of Sepsis

We've all heard the adage about the dog that didn't bark, but in the ED, we worry far more about the dog that *did*. A bite to the lip seems trivial until four days later your patient rolls in with a temperature of 40°C, mottled extremities, and a blood sugar so low it registers as a lab error. This isn't just a local cellulitis story; this is the classic, terrifying setup for *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis. But here is where this case gets truly fascinating—and dangerous. The infection was treated, the cultures turned positive, and the patient began to stabilize, only to suddenly crash into profound hemolysis and anuria. The sepsis wasn't the whole story; it was merely the curtain raiser for a superimposed complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Our patient, a previously healthy woman in her late 40s, presented with what looked like textbook fulminant sepsis secondary to a dog bite. She had pancytopenia, coagulopathy consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and acute kidney injury (AKI). Initially, the clinical picture was dominated by the storm of septic shock. The team correctly suspected *C. canimorsus*, a fastidious Gram-negative rod found in canine oral flora, and started broad-spectrum antibiotics. Blood cultures eventually confirmed the bug seven days later—a delay typical for this slow-growing organism. By day eight, the inflammatory markers were trending down, platelets were recovering, and the coagulopathy seemed to be resolving. It looked like we had won the battle against the bacteria.

Then, the plot twisted. Just as the sepsis appeared to be under control, the patient's hemoglobin plummeted from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days without any evidence of bleeding. Her platelets crashed again, haptoglobin vanished, and LDH skyrocketed. A peripheral smear revealed 15–20% schistocytes. This wasn't lingering DIC; this was a new beast: Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA). The initial suspicion of autoimmune hemolytic anemia (triggered by a falsely positive Coombs test likely due to prior penicillin exposure) was quickly discarded when steroids failed to halt the destruction. With ADAMTS13 activity at 43.5% (ruling out TTP) and negative Shiga toxin PCR (ruling out STEC-HUS), we were left with a diagnosis of exclusion that required immediate action: Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS).

This case perfectly illustrates the "second hit" phenomenon in TMA. The initial *C. canimorsus* infection acted as the trigger, activating the complement cascade in a host who perhaps had subclinical susceptibility or simply overwhelmed regulatory mechanisms. While standard complement levels (C3/C4) remained normal—a common red herring in CM-HUS—the alternative pathway was running wild, depositing fibrin thrombi in the glomeruli and shredding red blood cells. The renal biopsy confirmed the nightmare: ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and acute tubular necrosis. The patient went from oliguric to completely anuric, requiring dialysis.

Managing this scenario presents a profound therapeutic dilemma. The treatment for CM-HUS is eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that blocks terminal complement component C5. However, blocking C5 leaves patients exquisitely vulnerable to encapsulated organisms—specifically *Neisseria meningitidis*, but also potentially *Capnocytophaga* itself. You are essentially turning off a major arm of innate immunity in a patient who just survived a devastating bacteremia from an encapsulated bug. In this case, the team navigated this tightrope by waiting until blood cultures were unequivocally negative before administering eculizumab. They bridged with plasma exchange (which offers transient benefit in CM-HUS by clearing inflammatory mediators) and ensured meningococcal vaccination coverage alongside antibiotic prophylaxis. The result? Spontaneous diuresis returned, hematologic remission was achieved, and renal function partially recovered.

The takeaway here isn't just about remembering obscure bacteria; it's about maintaining vigilance when the clinical trajectory deviates from the expected recovery curve. If your septic patient starts getting better and then suddenly develops unexplained hemolysis and worsening renal failure, stop thinking about residual infection and start thinking about TMA. Do not let normal C3/C4 levels reassure you; they are frequently normal in CM-HUS. And never underestimate a dog bite in an immunocompetent adult—what starts as a puncture wound can evolve into a systemic catastrophe involving DIC, multiorgan failure, and complement dysregulation.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect *Capnocytophaga* early:** In any patient with sepsis/DIC following dog or cat contact (even minor bites or scratches), consider *C. canimorsus*. Remember it is fastidious and slow-growing; negative early cultures do not rule it out, and molecular methods may be needed if suspicion is high.
*   **Watch for the "Second Hit":** In sepsis-associated TMA, hemolysis and thrombocytopenia may worsen or appear *after* initial signs of septic improvement. A sudden drop in Hb with rising LDH and schistocytes should trigger an immediate TMA workup regardless of improving inflammatory markers.
*   **Normal Complements Don't Rule Out CM-HUS:** Standard C3 and C4 levels are often within normal reference ranges during active complement-mediated HUS rely on excluding TTP (ADAMTS13 >10%) and STEC-HUS (negative toxin PCR) alongside clinical evidence of MAHA and AKI.
*   **The Eculizumab Dilemma:** Treating infection-triggered CM-HUS requires balancing urgent complement blockade against the risk of overwhelming infection from encapsulated organisms. Initiate eculizumab only after bacteremia is cleared whenever possible, ensure appropriate vaccination (MenACWY and MenB), and maintain antibiotic prophylaxis.
*   **Time is Kidney:** Delayed initiation of eculizumab in CM-HUS is strongly associated with poor renal recovery and progression to end-stage kidney disease. Once TTP and STEC are reasonably excluded in a deteriorating patient, do not wait for extensive genetic testing to start therapy if clinical suspicion is high



# Beyond TTP and STEC: Navigating the Diagnostic Labyrinth of Adult TMA

We all know the algorithm. An adult rolls into the ED with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI). Your brain immediately jumps to the big two: Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) and Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* HUS (STEC-HUS). You send the ADAMTS13, you order the stool PCR, and you feel pretty good about your differential. But what happens when those tests come back negative, yet the patient continues to crash? What happens when the "sepsis" seems to be getting better, but the kidneys stop working entirely and the hemoglobin plummets?

This is exactly where a fascinating case reported by Fredriksen et al. forces us to pause and rethink our approach to Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA). They describe a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who went from a dog bite on the lip to dialysis dependence in less than a week. The culprit wasn't just the expected *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis; it was a sinister sequelae known as Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS). This case is a masterclass in why "ruling out" TTP isn't enough—you have to actively hunt for the third horseman of the TMA apocalypse.

The clinical trajectory here was deceptive. Four days after a dog bite, this patient presented with fulminant sepsis, Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC), pancytopenia, and shock. It looked like classic severe bacterial sepsis, which it was. Blood cultures eventually confirmed *C. canimorsus*, a fastidious gram-negative rod found in canine mouths that can kill immunocompetent hosts with frightening speed. She was treated with antibiotics and supportive care. The inflammatory markers downtrended, the leukopenia resolved, and she seemed to be turning the corner. Then, the plot twisted. Just as the sepsis appeared controlled, she developed profound isolated hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, and complete anuria requiring renal replacement therapy. The initial narrative of "sepsis-induced DIC" no longer fit the picture of worsening MAHA in a stabilizing patient.

This divergence is the critical teaching moment. In the early phases of severe sepsis, TMA can be masked by the overwhelming signs of infection and coagulopathy. It's easy to attribute schistocytes and low platelets to DIC or sepsis itself. However, when the infectious storm breaks but the hematologic and renal destruction accelerates, you must suspect a superimposed TMA driven by complement dysregulation. In this case, the team initially chased red herrings: a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) led to a trial of steroids for presumed autoimmune hemolytic anemia, which failed miserably. It wasn't until they scrutinized the peripheral smear—finding 15-20% schistocytes—and noted undetectable haptoglobin with soaring LDH that the penny dropped. This was MAHA, pure and simple.

So, how do you confirm CM-HUS when the textbook markers are missing? This case beautifully illustrates the pitfalls of relying on standard complement panels. Her C3 and C4 levels were normal throughout her course. If you stopped there, you might rule out complement-mediated disease. Don't make that mistake. Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM-HUS; alternative pathway dysregulation often occurs without consuming these specific proteins. Furthermore, her ADAMTS13 activity came back at 43.5%. While low compared to a healthy person, it was well above the <10% threshold required for a diagnosis of TTP. With STEC-HUS ruled out by negative stool PCR and TTP excluded by adequate ADAMTS13 activity, the diagnosis became one of exclusion supported by tissue evidence: CM-HUS.

The definitive proof came from the kidney biopsy, which remains an indispensable tool when serology is equivocal or delayed. The pathology showed severe acute tubular necrosis and ischemic glomeruli packed with fibrin thrombi—the hallmark of TMA—without immune complex deposition. This histologic confirmation allowed them to pull the trigger on targeted therapy: eculizumab. The timing was delicate; blocking terminal complement (C5) in a patient recovering from bacteremia carries a massive risk of recurrent infection with encapsulated organisms like *Neisseria* or *Capnocytophaga*. The team navigated this by ensuring blood cultures were negative, administering meningococcal vaccines (Menveo and Bexsero), providing antibiotic prophylaxis with penicillin, and then initiating eculizumab alongside plasma exchange as a bridge. The result? Hematological remission and return of diuresis, though her renal function remained impaired—a stark reminder that delayed recognition leads to permanent scarring.

What makes this case particularly intriguing for us as clinicians is the trigger mechanism without a clear genetic smoking gun up front. CM-HUS usually requires a "second hit"—an infection or pregnancy—to unmask an underlying genetic predisposition in complement regulatory proteins like Factor H or I. Interestingly, extensive genetic testing in this patient didn't initially reveal pathogenic variants (though reports were pending at publication), suggesting that sometimes severe infections alone can tip the scales into uncontrolled complement activation even without a known hereditary defect. Whether she has an undiscovered variant or simply suffered an extreme infectious trigger, the clinical lesson remains identical: infection-triggered CM-HUS is real, it is deadly, and it hides behind more common diagnoses.

### Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect CM-HUS when MAHA persists or worsens after sepsis control:** Do not dismiss ongoing hemolysis and thrombocytopenia as residual DIC if inflammatory markers are improving; this dissociation is a red flag for complement-mediated TMA.
*   **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM-HUS:** Standard complement levels often remain within reference ranges in alternative pathway dysregulation; rely on clinical criteria and exclusion rather than these labs alone.
*   **ADAMTS13 >10% excludes TTP but demands further workup:** An activity level between 10-70% essentially rules out TTP but leaves STEC-HUS and CM-HUS on the table; do not stop investigating once TTP is excluded.
*   **Renal biopsy is crucial in diagnostic uncertainty:** When serology is inconclusive or delayed, histologic evidence of thrombotic microangiopathy (fibrin thrombi without immune complexes) confirms the diagnosis and justifies expensive targeted therapies like eculizumab.
*   **Manage infection risk aggressively before complement blockade:** Initiating eculizumab during or immediately after bacteremia requires negative cultures, appropriate vaccination against encapsulated bacteria (*N. meningitidis*), and antibiotic prophylaxis to prevent catastrophic recurrence of infection like *Capnocytophaga*.



# The Coombs Conundrum: Avoiding the Steroid Trap in Post-Sepsis Hemolysis

We've all been there. You're managing a complex post-septic patient who finally seems to be turning the corner. The inflammatory markers are trending down, the pressors are off, and then—bam—the hemoglobin crashes. Your brain immediately goes to the usual suspects: occult bleed? Delayed transfusion reaction? Or perhaps autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA)? In the heat of the moment, a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) feels like the smoking gun. You start high-dose steroids, pat yourself on the back for catching Evans syndrome, and wait for the reticulocytes to rise. But what if that positive Coombs test is a liar? What if you're treating the wrong mechanism while the patient's kidneys continue to shut down?

A fascinating case recently published by Fredriksen et al. illustrates this diagnostic pitfall perfectly. A previously healthy woman in her late 40s presented with fulminant sepsis four days after a dog bite to the lip. The culprit was *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a nasty gram-negative rod known for causing rapid deterioration, DIC, and multiorgan failure in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent hosts. She survived the initial storm of septic shock, pancytopenia, and dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI). But just as her coagulopathy resolved and inflammatory markers improved, she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days. Her platelets remained stubbornly low.

Here is where the trap snapped shut. The initial workup showed a positive DAT for IgG. In a vacuum, this screams AIHA. The team appropriately started high-dose prednisolone and transfused blood products. But here's the kicker: she didn't get better. The hemolysis persisted, the platelets stayed depressed, and the clinical picture wasn't fitting a pure autoimmune process. It was only when someone took a hard look at the peripheral smear—revealing 15%–20% schistocytes—that the narrative shifted from "autoimmune" to "microangiopathic." The DAT was likely a false positive driven by her recent penicillin exposure (phenoxymethylpenicillin) and multiple prior transfusions, a well-documented phenomenon that can derail your diagnostic reasoning if you rely on it exclusively.

This pivot point is critical because the treatments for AIHA and Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) are diametrically opposed in terms of urgency and mechanism. While steroids might eventually help an autoimmune process, they do absolutely nothing to stop the complement-mediated endothelial destruction occurring in Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Ureaemic Syndrome (CM-HUS). In this case, every hour spent waiting for steroids to work was an hour of ongoing glomerular thrombosis. Once TMA was suspected based on the smear (schistocytes >1%) and confirmed by ruling out TTP (ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, safely above the <10% cutoff) and STEC-HUS (negative stool PCR), the team initiated plasma exchange as a bridge and promptly started eculizumab. The result? Spontaneous diuresis returned, hematological remission was achieved, and renal function began to recover.

The pathophysiology here is a brutal example of the "two-hit" hypothesis gone wrong. *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated organism that interacts heavily with the host complement system. While complement activation is necessary to clear the bacteria, in susceptible individuals—or perhaps just overwhelmed systems—it can spiral into uncontrolled alternative pathway activation. This leads to widespread endothelial damage, fibrin thrombi formation (seen clearly on her kidney biopsy), and mechanical shearing of red blood cells. The irony is palpable: the very immune mechanism meant to save her from sepsis nearly cost her her kidneys. Interestingly, genetic testing didn't reveal a pathogenic variant in this patient, suggesting this was purely an infection-triggered event rather than a latent genetic disorder unmasked by illness—a reminder that you don't need a known mutation to develop catastrophic CM-HUS.

The takeaway isn't just about dog bites; it's about maintaining a high index of suspicion for TMA in any post-septic patient with falling hemoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia, regardless of what the Coombs test says. Don't let a false-positive DAT lure you into a steroid haze while schistocytes wreck havoc on your patient's microvasculature. Look at the smear. Check the haptoglobin (which was undetectable here) and LDH trends differentiating intravascular destruction from other causes. And remember, in the world of TMA, time is kidney tissue.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Don't Trust the Coombs Blindly:** A positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) can be falsely positive due to recent transfusions, drug exposure (especially penicillins), or severe inflammation; always correlate with peripheral smear findings.
*   **Smear is King:** The presence of schistocytes (>1%) is the hallmark of Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA); if you see them alongside thrombocytopenia and AKI, think TMA first until proven otherwise.
*   **Rule Out TTP Rapidly:** Use ADAMTS13 activity levels to distinguish TTP (<10%) from CM-HUS; do not delay plasma exchange or complement inhibition while waiting for genetics if clinical suspicion is high.
*   **Infection as a Trigger:** Unusual pathogens like *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* can trigger complement-mediated HUS even in patients without identifiable genetic mutations; resolution of sepsis does not guarantee resolution of TMA.
*   **Steroids Won't Fix Complement:** If it's CM-HUS, corticosteroids are ineffective; early initiation of terminal complement blockade (eculizumab/ravulizumab) is crucial for renal salvage once active bacteremia is controlled and meningococcal coverage is established.



# Walking the Tightrope: Initiating Eculizumab in the Shadow of Active Infection

We've all seen the patient who survives the storm of sepsis only to crash into a new, equally terrifying complication once the dust settles. It's a cruel twist of fate where the body's attempt to clear an infection triggers a cascade that destroys the very organs we fought so hard to save. A recent case report by Fredriksen et al. perfectly illustrates this high-stakes clinical paradox: a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who went from a simple dog bite to dialysis-dependent complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), forcing us to walk a therapeutic tightrope between saving her kidneys and inviting a fatal superinfection.

The story begins innocuously enough—a dog bite to the lip. Four days later, she presented with fulminant *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis, complete with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), pancytopenia, and shock. *C. canimorsus* is a nasty bug; it's part of the normal canine flora but can cause rapid, life-threatening illness in humans, often mimicking meningococcemia with its ability to trigger DIC and multiorgan failure. Our patient survived the initial septic onslaught with antibiotics and aggressive supportive care. But just as her inflammatory markers began to trend down and the coagulopathy resolved, she developed something far more insidious: sudden, isolated hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, and worsening anuria.

This is where the diagnostic trap snaps shut. In the early phases of severe sepsis and DIC, distinguishing between consumption coagulopathy and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is notoriously difficult. Schistocytes can be masked by other hematologic abnormalities, and renal failure is expected in septic shock. However, the clue here was the timing and the isolation of symptoms. Once the overt signs of sepsis improved, the persistence of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and thrombocytopenia signaled a shift from DIC to TMA. With ADAMTS13 activity at 43.5% (ruling out TTP) and negative Shiga toxin PCR (ruling out STEC-HUS), the diagnosis landed squarely on complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS). The kidney biopsy confirmed it: fibrin thrombi in glomeruli and ischemic changes consistent with unchecked complement activation.

Now comes the real cliffhanger: treatment. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody against terminal complement component C5, is the gold standard for CM-HUS. It stops the complement cascade dead in its tracks, halting endothelial damage and allowing renal recovery. In this case, it worked beautifully; diuresis returned, and hematologic parameters normalized. But here lies the terrifying irony: *C. canimorsus*, like *Neisseria meningitidis*, is an encapsulated bacterium that relies heavily on the terminal complement pathway for clearance. By administering eculizumab, we essentially induce a functional asplenia, leaving the patient wide open to overwhelming infection by the very organism that nearly killed her days prior—or worse, a new meningococcal invasion.

Initiating terminal complement blockade in a patient recovering from bacteremia feels like defusing a bomb while standing on it. You cannot wait indefinitely for "perfect" clearance because every hour of delay in treating CM-HUS correlates with irreversible renal damage and progression to end-stage kidney disease. Yet, starting too early risks turning a treatable bacteremia into a fatal septic shock that no antibiotic can outrun. In this case report, the team navigated this by ensuring blood cultures were definitively negative before pulling the trigger on eculizumab. They utilized plasma exchange (PEX) as a bridge—a temporizing measure that removes circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators but doesn't fix the underlying dysregulation. PEX bought them time to confirm sterilization of the blood while keeping the TMA somewhat at bay.

The safety net for this maneuver is robust vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis. Standard protocol dictates meningococcal vaccination (covering serogroups A, C, Y, W, and B) at least two weeks prior to eculizumab initiation. However, in acute settings like this where waiting two weeks isn't an option for renal salvage, you vaccinate immediately and cover with prophylactic antibiotics until immunity develops. Our patient received Menveo and Bexsero upon admission to nephrology but started eculizumab before full seroconversion could occur. Consequently, she was placed on prophylactic penicillin (Apocillin) to guard against encapsulated organisms during this vulnerable window. It's a delicate balance of trusting your microbiology data, respecting the pharmacokinetics of vaccine response, and accepting that sometimes you have to treat based on probabilities rather than certainties.

Interestingly, extended genetic testing in this patient revealed no pathogenic complement gene variants. This suggests her CM-HUS was purely infection-triggered—a "second hit" phenomenon where the massive complement activation required to fight *C. canimorsus* overwhelmed her regulatory capacity in a genetically susceptible but previously silent host. While some might argue for stopping eculizumab once genetics come back negative, the severity of her initial presentation and dialysis dependence justified continuing maintenance therapy with plans for future reassessment. The takeaway? Don't let a lack of identified mutation dissuade you from treating active TMA if the clinical picture fits; phenotype trumps genotype in the acute setting.

This case serves as a stark reminder that TMA should remain on our differential list long after sepsis seems to be resolving. # Genotype-Negative, Phenotype-Positive: The Reality of Acquired CM-HUS

We love a clean algorithm in emergency medicine and critical care. If a patient presents with Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI), we run the TTP vs. HUS gauntlet. We check ADAMTS13, we look for Shiga toxin, and if those are negative, we often pivot to "atypical" or Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS). The traditional teaching suggests that CM-HUS is the domain of the genetically predisposed—a patient harboring a silent mutation in Factor H, I, or CD46 waiting for a "second hit" to unleash hell.

But what happens when the genetics come back blank? Do we stop treating? Do we doubt the diagnosis?

A fascinating case report by Fredriksen et al. forces us to confront the reality of **acquired** complement dysregulation. They describe a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who went from a dog bite on the lip to dialysis dependence in less than a week, triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. Despite having no identifiable germline complement mutations, she developed fulminant CM-HUS that responded dramatically to eculizumab. This isn't just a story about a rare bug; it's a reminder that phenotype trumps genotype, and that infection alone can be enough to break the complement brake.

### From Dog Bite to Dialysis Dependence

The clinical trajectory here is terrifyingly rapid. Our patient presented four days after a dog bite with high fever (>40°C), profound hypoglycemia (a classic red flag for severe *Capnocytophaga* sepsis), and mottled extremities. Initially, the picture was dominated by septic shock and Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC). She had pancytopenia, coagulopathy, and AKI. In the fog of early sepsis, distinguishing DIC from Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) is notoriously difficult, and initially, the team rightly focused on source control and hemodynamic support.

She received broad-spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin/tazobactam), and blood cultures eventually confirmed *C. canimorsus*, a fastidious gram-negative rod found in canine oral flora. With aggressive ICU care, her inflammatory markers began to settle, her coagulation profile normalized, and she appeared to be turning the corner on the sepsis front.

Then, the plot twisted. Just as the sepsis seemed to be resolving, she developed sudden, isolated hemolysis. Her hemoglobin plummeted from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days without any evidence of bleeding. Her platelets crashed again and stayed low. Haptoglobin vanished, and LDH skyrocketed. A peripheral smear revealed 15–20% schistocytes. The DIC had resolved, but a new engine of destruction had taken over: pure TMA.

### The Diagnostic Dilemma: Ruling Out the Usual Suspects

When TMA strikes an adult post-sepsis, the differential is narrow but critical. First, you must rule out Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP). Our patient's ADAMTS13 activity came back at 43.5%. Since TTP requires activity <10%, this effectively closed that door. Next, Shiga-toxin producing *E. coli* (STEC-HUS) was ruled out via negative stool PCR and the lack of a diarrheal prodrome.

This left us with Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS). However, the diagnostic waters were muddy by standard labs. Her C3 and C4 levels were normal. In fact, up to 50% of patients with CM-HUS will have normal static complement levels during an acute flare because consumption is balanced by hepatic synthesis or the defect lies in regulation rather than quantity.

The definitive answer came from histology and response to therapy. A kidney biopsy showed fibrin thrombi in glomerular capillaries and ischemic changes consistent with TMA, with no immune complex deposition (ruling out lupus nephritis or other GN). But the true validator was therapeutic: after bridging with plasma exchange (which offers only transient benefit in CM-HUS compared to TTP), she was started on eculizumab. The result? Spontaneous diuresis returned, hemolysis stopped, and platelets recovered.

### The Genetic Paradox: When Panels Come Back Negative

Here is where this case challenges our conventional wisdom. We often think of CM-HUS as a genetic disease triggered by infection. We expect to find a mutation in Factor H or MCP that predisposes the patient to unchecked alternative pathway activation once an inflammatory trigger occurs.

In this case, extensive genetic testing returned **negative**. No pathogenic variants were identified in complement regulatory genes, and anti-Factor H antibodies were absent. So how did she get CM-HUS?

This represents the concept of **purely acquired complement dysregulation**. While 50–70% of CM-HUS patients have identifiable genetic defects, a significant minority do not. In these "genotype-negative" cases, the infectious trigger itself—likely through specific interactions between bacterial surface proteins and host complement regulators—is sufficient to overwhelm the system temporarily. *C. canimorsus* is known to interact variably with serum complement; some strains resist killing by expressing capsules that interfere with regulation, potentially creating a localized storm of alternative pathway activation that spills over into systemic endothelial damage.

The takeaway is crucial: **Do not withhold eculizumab while waiting for genetic results.** Genetics inform long-term prognosis and duration of therapy, but they should never delay acute treatment in a patient with clear clinical evidence of CM-HUS waiting for ADAMTS13 exclusion and STEC negativity. As seen here, delaying blockade risks permanent renal loss or death regardless of whether a mutation exists on paper.

### Navigating Treatment During Active Infection

Treating CM-HUS with eculizumab during or immediately after bacteremia presents a unique philosophical and practical dilemma Eculizumab blocks C5, preventing the formation of the Membrane Attack Complex (MAC). The MAC is our primary defense against encapsulated organisms like *Neisseria meningitidis* and... wait for it... *Capnocytophaga*.

By blocking terminal complement, we theoretically leave the door wide open for recurrent or overwhelming infection with these exact pathogens. The team navigated this tightrope expertly:
*   They ensured blood cultures were negative before administering the first dose of eculizumab ("sterilizing" the bloodstream first).
*   They administered meningococcal vaccines (Menveo and Bexsero) prior to induction when possible (though they had to proceed before full immunity could develop due to urgency).
*   They maintained antibiotic prophylaxis (phenoxymethylpenicillin) throughout the period of complement blockade until vaccine immunity was established weeks later under outpatient follow-up protocols involving Menveo & Bexsero..
  
This balancing act underscores that while infection triggered the TMA in this setting ,the decision block terminall completement requires meticulous planning mitigate super-infection risk without sacrificing organ salvage potential .
