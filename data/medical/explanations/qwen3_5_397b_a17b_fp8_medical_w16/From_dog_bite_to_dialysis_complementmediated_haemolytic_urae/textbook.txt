Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Management of Capnocytophaga Sepsis

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a fastidious, encapsulated, gram-negative rod that resides as commensal flora in the oropharynx of dogs and cats. While human infection typically follows a bite or scratch, transmission can occur through mere close contact, and the organism poses a significant threat even to immunocompetent hosts. Although the majority of dog bites result in localized cellulitis or remain asymptomatic, *C. canimorsus* is uniquely capable of causing fulminant sepsis characterized by rapid progression to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan failure, and death, with mortality rates approaching 30%. The pathophysiology involves the organism's ability to evade innate immunity; its polysaccharide capsule confers resistance to phagocytosis and complement-mediated lysis in some strains, facilitating unchecked bacteremia. In the presented case, a previously healthy woman in her late 40s developed severe septic shock just four days after a dog bite to the upper lip. Her initial failure to fill a prescription for phenoxymethylpenicillin underscores the critical window for early intervention, as delays allow the slow-growing organism to establish a high bacterial load before clinical deterioration becomes apparent.

The clinical spectrum of *C. canimorsus* infection is broad, ranging from mild local inflammation to catastrophic systemic collapse. In severe cases, the presentation mimics other overwhelming infections such as meningococcemia or purpura fulminans. This patient presented with high fever (>40°C), profound malaise, and distinctive cutaneous signs including mottling and cooling of the lower extremities, indicative of peripheral vasoconstriction and microvascular thrombosis. A notable and potentially misleading feature in this case was severe refractory hypoglycemia (nadir 0.6 mmol/L), which persisted despite aggressive glucose administration. While hypoglycemia in sepsis often signals hepatic dysfunction or depleted glycogen stores, its prominence here served as an early marker of metabolic derangement preceding overt hemodynamic collapse. The absence of necrotizing fasciitis on imaging helped focus the differential diagnosis on systemic rather than soft-tissue sources, directing attention toward bacteremia-driven organ injury.

Initial laboratory evaluation revealed a triad of pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), and coagulopathy, creating a complex diagnostic challenge. The combination of fever, thrombocytopenia, anemia, and elevated inflammatory markers initially raised concern for primary hematologic malignancies such as acute leukemia or hyperinflammatory syndromes like hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). However, the clinical context of recent animal exposure and the presence of consumptive coagulopathy strongly favored sepsis-induced DIC over primary bone marrow failure. This distinction was crucial, as evidenced by the subsequent normal flow cytometry and bone marrow findings which ruled out leukemic blasts. Early in the course, the diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) was considered less likely because haptoglobin levels were initially normal and reticulocyte counts were not compensatory; furthermore, significant leukopenia is atypical for classic HUS but common in overwhelming gram-negative sepsis. Recognizing that sepsis itself drives endothelial damage and consumption of clotting factors prevented premature misdiagnosis while allowing for appropriate supportive care.

Diagnosing *C. canimorsus* specifically presents inherent difficulties due to its slow growth rate and fastidious nature in standard culture media. Blood cultures may take several days to turn positive, leading to potential delays in targeted therapy if clinicians rely solely on preliminary Gram stains or rapid automated systems that may miss the organism. In this case, suspicion remained high based on epidemiological clues—the dog bite history—prompting empiric coverage with broad-spectrum antibiotics effective against oral flora of dogs, specifically piperacillin/tazobactam. The eventual confirmation seven days later via both aerobic and anaerobic cultures validates the necessity of maintaining a high index of suspicion when clinical severity outweighs initial microbiological data. Increasingly, molecular methods such as real-time nanopore sequencing are becoming valuable adjuncts to shorten this diagnostic gap, though they were not required here due to sustained clinical vigilance.

Immediate management prioritized hemodynamic stabilization and source control through antimicrobial therapy. Given the severity of the presentation—marked by lactic acidosis (5.7 mmol/L), metabolic acidosis, and impending multiorgan failure—the patient required transfer to the intensive care unit for continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) once anuria developed. Fluid resuscitation was balanced carefully against the risk of volume overload in the setting of septic cardiomyopathy and renal failure. Antibiotic stewardship involved initiating broad coverage immediately upon suspicion of *Capnocytophaga*, later de-escalating to ampicillin once susceptibility was confirmed. Supportive measures included transfusion of platelets and fibrinogen to manage active bleeding and DIC-related coagulopathy. Notably, despite profound shock features like distal ischemia and cyanosis, the patient did not require vasopressors beyond stress-dose corticosteroids, suggesting that while microvascular perfusion was compromised by thrombosis, macro-circulatory tone was relatively preserved—a phenomenon sometimes seen in toxin-mediated or specific gram-negative sepsis patterns. This phase of care laid the groundwork for survival but set the stage for the subsequent emergence of complement-mediated complications once the infectious burden began to resolve.

## Recognizing Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) Amidst Resolving Sepsis

The clinical course of this patient illustrates a deceptive pivot point common in complex sepsis cases: the emergence of a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) just as the primary infectious insult appears to be resolving. Initially, the patient's presentation of pancytopenia, coagulopathy, and acute kidney injury (AKI) was correctly attributed to *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). In typical DIC, hematologic parameters usually parallel the severity of the infection; as inflammation subsides and bacterial loads decrease with appropriate antibiotic therapy, coagulation profiles normalize and cell counts recover. However, in this case, a critical dissociation occurred around day eight of hospitalization. While inflammatory markers improved, leukocyte counts rebounded, and coagulation parameters normalized, the patient experienced a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia. This divergence signals a "secondary hit," where the initial infectious trigger has unleashed a distinct pathological cascade—uncontrolled complement activation—that persists independently of the clearing bacteremia. Recognizing this temporal disconnect is vital, as mistaking these findings for lingering sepsis or simple marrow suppression can delay life-saving targeted therapy.

The hallmark of this transition was the development of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) characterized by non-immune hemolysis and mechanical platelet consumption. Clinically, this manifested as a rapid decline in hemoglobin from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days without evidence of overt bleeding, accompanied by undetectable haptoglobin and rising lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). Crucially, the kidneys remained severely affected, with the patient remaining anuric and dialysis-dependent despite the resolution of systemic shock. This triad of MAHA, persistent thrombocytopenia, and worsening AKI in the setting of resolved coagulopathy strongly suggests TMA rather than pure DIC. In DIC, thrombocytopenia is driven by widespread fibrin clot formation consuming platelets and clotting factors, leading to prolonged PT/aPTT. In contrast, TMA involves localized microvascular thrombosis mediated by endothelial injury and platelet aggregation, typically sparing standard coagulation times. The normalization of this patient's PT and aPTT while her platelet count remained critically low (30–40 × 10⁹/L) provided the first major clue that the pathophysiology had shifted from consumptive coagulopathy to microangiopathic destruction.

Diagnostic reasoning in this phase required navigating potential pitfalls, particularly regarding the Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT). Initially, the patient tested positive for IgG on DAT, raising suspicion for autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) potentially induced by penicillin or recent transfusions. Consequently, high-dose corticosteroids were administered. However, the lack of hematologic response to steroids and the persistence of thrombocytopenia necessitated a re-evaluation. The initial positive DAT was likely a false positive attributable to drug interference or passive acquisition from transfused blood products, a known limitation in post-transfusion settings. Repeat testing proved negative, redirecting focus toward non-immune causes of hemolysis. This underscores a key teaching point: in the context of suspected TMA, a positive DAT must be interpreted cautiously amidst confounding factors like recent transfusion or beta-lactam exposure, and failure to respond to immunosuppression should promptly trigger a search for mechanical hemolysis.

Definitive suspicion for TMA was confirmed through peripheral blood smear analysis, which remains the gold standard for identifying MAHA. The smear revealed 15%–20% schistocytes (fragmented red blood cells), far exceeding the threshold generally accepted for diagnosing MAHA (>1%). Schistocytes result from red blood cells shearing against fibrin strands or damaged endothelium within the microvasculature, a process distinct from the immune-mediated destruction seen in AIHA or the global consumption of DIC. The quantification of schistocytes provided objective evidence of mechanical fragmentation, solidifying the diagnosis of TMA. Furthermore, subsequent renal biopsy findings of fibrin thrombi within glomerular capillaries and ischemic changes confirmed that this microvascular occlusion was occurring at the tissue level, explaining the refractory nature of her AKI compared to the reversible tubular necrosis often seen in septic shock alone.

Differentiating between DIC and TMA is often challenging because they share overlapping features such as thrombocytopenia and organ dysfunction, and they can coexist, as seen early in this patient's course. However, their pathophysiological mechanisms and trajectories differ fundamentally. DIC is a systemic activation of coagulation resulting in both thrombosis and hemorrhage due to factor depletion; it typically resolves as the underlying sepsis is controlled. TMA, conversely, is driven by specific endothelial insults—in this case, likely complement dysregulation triggered by the earlier infection—that lead to predominant platelet-rich microthrombi without significant consumption of soluble clotting factors. In this case report, the persistence of hemolysis and thrombocytopenia after the correction of coagulopathy marked the transition from sepsis-induced DIC to infection-triggered complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS). Understanding this distinction is paramount because while DIC is managed primarily by treating the underlying infection and supportive care CM-HUS requires specific complement inhibition to halt endothelial damage and prevent irreversible renal loss.

## Diagnostic Algorithm for Adult-Onset TMA: Exclusion and Confirmation

The diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in adults begins with recognizing the clinical triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute organ injury. In this case, the patient initially presented with sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), which can obscure the emergence of a primary TMA. The pivotal moment in the diagnostic algorithm occurred when the patient's inflammatory markers improved, yet she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin accompanied by persistent thrombocytopenia. This dissociation between resolving sepsis and worsening hematologic parameters necessitated a shift in focus from infection-driven coagulopathy to a primary thrombotic microangiopathy. Confirmation of MAHA relies on identifying schistocytes on a peripheral blood smear; in this patient, the presence of 15%–20% schistocytes provided the morphological evidence of mechanical red cell fragmentation required to proceed with subtype differentiation. Crucially, while the initial direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive, likely due to penicillin exposure or recent transfusions, the subsequent negative result helped exclude autoimmune hemolytic anemia, reinforcing the diagnosis of a non-immune mediated process.

Once TMA is established, the immediate priority is to exclude thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), a medical emergency requiring distinct management. Differentiation relies heavily on ADAMTS13 activity levels. TTP is defined by a severe deficiency in ADAMTS13 activity, typically less than 10%, whereas other forms of TMA usually present with normal or mildly reduced activity. In this case, the patient's PLASMIC score was calculated at 4/7, indicating a low pre-test probability for TTP, which was definitively confirmed when ADAMTS13 activity returned at 43.5%. This finding effectively ruled out TTP and directed the diagnostic workup toward other etiologies. Simultaneously, Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC)-HUS must be excluded via stool PCR, even in the absence of a diarrheal prodrome. Although STEC-HUS classically follows bloody diarrhea, atypical presentations occur; however, the negative PCR result in this patient eliminated STEC as the causative agent, leaving complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS) as the leading diagnosis by exclusion.

With TTP and STEC-HUS ruled out, the diagnosis of CM-HUS is primarily clinicopathological, often supported by renal biopsy when the clinical picture is complex or overlapped by sepsis. In this patient, a kidney biopsy was performed once platelet counts allowed for safe procedural intervention. Histopathological examination revealed hallmark features of TMA, including fibrin thrombi within glomerular capillaries, ischemic glomeruli, and severe acute tubular necrosis. Importantly, immunofluorescence demonstrated an absence of immune complex deposition, distinguishing this process from immune-complex mediated glomerulonephritis or lupus nephritis. These findings confirmed that the renal failure was driven by endothelial injury and microvascular thrombosis rather than direct bacterial toxicity or immune complex trapping, solidifying the indication for complement-targeted therapy.

A critical teaching point in evaluating CM-HUS is the interpretation of complement studies. Clinicians often mistakenly assume that normal serum C3 and C4 levels exclude complement dysregulation; however, in CM-HUS, standard complement levels frequently remain within reference ranges because the pathology involves dysregulated activation of the alternative pathway rather than wholesale consumption of complement components. As seen in this case, the patient's C3 and C4 levels were normal upon admission and throughout her course. Furthermore, functional assays of the classical, lectin, and alternative pathways were initially reported as normal, likely influenced by recent plasma exchange sessions which replace deficient factors temporarily. Therefore, normal serological complement profiles do not rule out CM-HUS. The diagnosis rests on the exclusion of other causes and the clinical response to complement inhibition. In this instance, despite negative genetic testing for known complement mutations—a scenario occurring in up to 50% of cases—the combination of exclusive criteria fulfillment and rapid hematologic remission following eculizumab administration confirmed infection-triggered CM-HUS secondary to *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*.

## Therapeutic Strategy: Balancing Complement Blockade with Infectious Risk

The management of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) triggered by severe infection represents a delicate clinical equilibrium between halting catastrophic endothelial damage and preserving host immunity against the inciting pathogen. In this case, the decision to initiate eculizumab was contingent upon the resolution of active *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia, illustrating a critical principle: while early complement blockade is the strongest predictor of renal recovery, administering terminal complement inhibitors during uncontrolled sepsis carries a prohibitive risk of overwhelming infection by encapsulated organisms. The patient's trajectory underscores that renal prognosis in CM-HUS is inextricably linked to the timing of therapeutic intervention relative to the onset of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although the patient experienced a delay due to the obscuring presence of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and septic shock, the eventual initiation of eculizumab once cultures cleared allowed for haematological remission and partial return of renal function. Had therapy been withheld indefinitely due to infectious fears, or conversely, started prematurely during peak bacteraemia, the outcome could have ranged from irreversible end-stage kidney disease to fatal recurrent sepsis.

Determining the appropriate duration of eculizumab therapy remains a nuanced challenge, particularly in cases where no underlying genetic complement disorder is identified. In patients with confirmed pathogenic variants in complement regulatory genes, lifelong therapy is often indicated due to the high risk of relapse upon cessation. However, in infection-triggered CM-HUS without demonstrable genetic defects—as seen in this patient—the disease mechanism is hypothesized to be a "two-hit" phenomenon where the infectious insult transiently overwhelms a potentially subclinical or polygenic susceptibility. Consequently, finite courses of therapy are increasingly considered for such acquired forms. In this case, a planned four-month maintenance course was selected, with the intention to reassess for discontinuation if renal function stabilizes and no relapse occurs. This approach balances the need to prevent recurrent TMA during the vulnerable recovery phase against the long-term costs and infectious risks associated with indefinite complement blockade. Nevertheless, close monitoring is mandatory, as the absence of a detected mutation does not entirely eliminate the possibility of an underlying predisposition that might manifest under future stressors.

Long-term follow-up for survivors of severe TMA extends beyond simple monitoring of serum creatinine. Patients who have suffered dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury and widespread microvascular thrombosis remain at elevated risk for chronic kidney disease progression, hypertension, and extra-renal sequelae related to prior ischemic insults. For this patient, ongoing nephrology surveillance is essential to manage residual renal impairment and adjust antihypertensive or proteinuria-reducing therapies as needed. Furthermore, because eculizumab induces a functional hyposplenism by blocking the terminal complement pathway, long-term vigilance against encapsulated bacteria is required. Even after discontinuing the drug, patients must maintain up-to-date vaccinations against *Neisseria meningitidis*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, and *Haemophilus influenzae*, and they should be educated on the imperative need for immediate medical evaluation regarding any febrile illness. The prophylactic antibiotics administered in this case highlight the rigorous protective measures necessary when navigating the intersection of immunosuppression and recent severe infection.

A pivotal clinical lesson from this report is the necessity of maintaining a high index of suspicion for unusual zoonotic triggers in the differential evaluation of TMA, even in immunocompetent adults. While Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC) and thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) dominate the epidemiology of HUS and TMA respectively, rare pathogens like *C. canimorsus* can act as potent activators of the alternative complement pathway. The diagnostic algorithm for sepsis-associated TMA must therefore proceed systematically: first excluding TTP via ADAMTS13 activity assessment and STEC via stool PCR, then evaluating for secondary causes including malignancy, drugs, and specific infections. When these are ruled out and clinical features persist despite source control, CM-HUS becomes the working diagnosis. In such scenarios involving animal exposure, specifically bites or scratches from dogs or cats, clinicians should consider molecular diagnostics or prolonged culture techniques if standard workups are unrevealing early in the course. Recognizing these rare etiologies promptly facilitates targeted antimicrobial therapy while preparing the ground for timely complement inhibition should TMA evolve independently of the resolving sepsis.
