Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] In an adult patient presenting with the triad of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury, what is the specific ADAMTS13 activity threshold used to definitively distinguish Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) from other forms of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)?

In an adult patient presenting with the triad of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury, the definitive laboratory criterion to distinguish Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) from other forms of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is an **ADAMTS13 activity level of less than 10%**.

Severe deficiency of ADAMTS13 (<10%) reflects the presence of inhibitory autoantibodies against the von Willebrand factor-cleaving protease, which is the pathognomonic mechanism of immune-mediated TTP. In contrast, other TMAs, such as complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) or infection-associated TMA, are characterized by ADAMTS13 activity that is typically normal or only mildly to moderately reduced (usually >10%).

This distinction is clearly illustrated in the reported case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-associated CM-HUS. Although the patient presented with classic features of TMA—including schistocytosis, profound thrombocytopenia, and dialysis-dependent renal failure—her ADAMTS13 activity was measured at **43.5%**. This result effectively ruled out TTP, redirecting the diagnostic focus toward complement dysregulation. Had her activity been below 10%, immediate plasma exchange would have been the singular life-saving priority; instead, the preserved enzyme activity supported the diagnosis of CM-HUS and justified the transition to complement inhibition with eculizumab once the active bacteremia was controlled. Thus, while clinical scoring systems like PLASMIC can estimate pretest probability, the <10% ADAMTS13 threshold remains the gold standard for confirming TTP and excluding mimics.

### [Pathophysiology] Why might standard complement levels (C3 and C4) remain within normal reference ranges in a patient with active complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), and why does this not exclude the diagnosis?

Standard complement levels (C3 and C4) often remain within normal reference ranges in complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) because the underlying pathophysiology involves dysregulation of the **alternative complement pathway**, whereas C3 and C4 levels primarily reflect consumption via the classical or lectin pathways. In CM-HUS, genetic defects or acquired autoantibodies impair regulatory proteins (such as Factor H, Factor I, or CD46), leading to unchecked activation of the alternative pathway on endothelial surfaces. This localized consumption occurs without necessarily depleting the total circulating pool of C3 and C4 to below laboratory detection limits, particularly in infection-triggered cases where hepatic acute-phase production may compensate for consumption.

Consequently, normal C3 and C4 levels do not exclude the diagnosis of CM-HUS. As illustrated in this case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-triggered TMA, the patient presented with classic thrombotic microangiopathy (MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI) and a renal biopsy confirming fibrin thrombi, yet her standard C3/C4 levels and functional screening of all three pathways were initially reported as normal. The diagnosis was established only through a process of exclusion—ruling out TTP (ADAMTS13 activity >10%) and STEC-HUS—and confirmed by the clinical response to eculizumab. Definitive evidence of alternative pathway dysregulation in this patient required specialized testing (reduced AH50) performed after the acute phase, underscoring that reliance on standard C3/C4 alone risks missing the diagnosis in up to half of CM-HUS cases.

### [Diagnostic Reasoning] A patient with recent sepsis develops worsening anaemia and thrombocytopenia after inflammatory markers have begun to normalize. How does this temporal relationship help differentiate infection-triggered CM-HUS from sepsis-associated disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)?

The temporal dissociation between the resolution of systemic inflammation and the onset or worsening of hematologic abnormalities is the critical clinical clue distinguishing infection-triggered complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) from sepsis-associated disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).

In classic sepsis-associated DIC, the consumption of platelets and clotting factors, along with microvascular thrombosis, occurs concurrently with the peak of the inflammatory storm. As seen in the early phase of this case, the patient presented with pancytopenia, coagulopathy, and organ failure driven by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia. In uncomplicated DIC, one expects these hematologic parameters to stabilize or improve as the infection is controlled and inflammatory markers (such as CRP or procalcitonin) normalize. Indeed, this patient initially demonstrated exactly that trajectory: resolving coagulopathy and a recovering platelet count as she transitioned from the ICU to the nephrology ward.

However, the hallmark of secondary CM-HUS is a "second hit" phenomenon where microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and persistent thrombocytopenia emerge or worsen *after* the acute infectious insult has subsided. In this case, despite normalized coagulation profiles and improving inflammatory signs, the patient developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin accompanied by rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, and significant schistocytosis. This lag indicates that while the inciting infection triggered an initial complement activation, it unmasked a dysregulated alternative complement pathway that became self-perpetuating independent of the ongoing bacterial load. Unlike DIC, which is a consumptive coagulopathy tied to active inflammation, CM-HUS is a thrombotic microangiopathy driven by unchecked complement-mediated endothelial damage. Therefore, the emergence of MAHA and thrombocytopenia in a clinically stabilizing post-septic patient should immediately shift the differential diagnosis from resolving DIC to superimposed TMA, warranting urgent evaluation for ADAMTS13 activity and consideration of complement inhibition.

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the unique microbiological characteristics of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* that often lead to delays in diagnosis, and what modern diagnostic adjunct is increasingly utilized to detect this pathogen earlier?

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a fastidious, encapsulated, gram-negative rod resident in the oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. Its primary microbiological characteristic leading to diagnostic delay is its notably slow growth in standard blood culture systems; as illustrated in this case, definitive identification via conventional aerobic and anaerobic cultures required seven days despite early clinical suspicion. This latency poses a significant risk, particularly given the organism's potential to trigger fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and, as seen here, complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). To overcome the limitations of phenotypic culture methods, modern diagnostic algorithms are increasingly utilizing molecular adjuncts, specifically real-time nanopore sequencing. These techniques allow for the rapid detection of *C. canimorsus* DNA directly from clinical specimens, bypassing the prolonged incubation period inherent to its fastidious nature and facilitating earlier targeted therapy.

### [Diagnostic Reasoning] In the setting of a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) in a patient with suspected TMA who has recently received penicillin and blood transfusions, what steps should be taken to confirm or refute a diagnosis of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia versus a false-positive result?

When evaluating a patient with suspected thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) and a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT), the clinician must rigorously distinguish between true autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA) and a false-positive result driven by medication or recent transfusion. As illustrated in this case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-associated complement-mediated HUS, a positive DAT should not automatically anchor the diagnosis to AIHA, particularly when the clinical picture suggests microangiopathic haemolysis.

The diagnostic pivot point lies in correlating serology with morphology and therapeutic response. In this patient, the initial DAT positivity for IgG raised suspicion for AIHA, prompting corticosteroid therapy. However, the failure of haemoglobin to recover despite steroids, combined with persistent thrombocytopenia and rising LDH, signaled that the haemolysis was not immune-mediated. Crucially, the peripheral blood smear revealed 15%–20% schistocytes, the hallmark of mechanical fragmentation inherent to TMA, rather than the spherocytes typical of warm AIHA. Furthermore, repeat DAT testing eventually turned negative, confirming that the initial result was likely an artifact caused by recent penicillin exposure (a known cause of drug-induced immune haemolysis or nonspecific coating) and prior blood transfusions.

Therefore, the definitive steps to refute AIHA in this setting are: first, examine the peripheral smear for schistocytes to confirm microangiopathic rather than immune-mediated destruction; second, assess the clinical response to corticosteroids, where a lack of improvement strongly argues against AIHA; and third, repeat the DAT after withholding potential offending agents or allowing time for transfused cells to clear. ### [Management] Compare the mechanisms of action and clinical efficacy of plasma exchange (PLEX) versus eculizumab in the treatment of complement-mediated HUS. Why is PLEX considered only a bridging therapy in this condition?

Plasma exchange (PLEX) and eculizumab operate through fundamentally different mechanisms in complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), resulting in distinct profiles of clinical efficacy. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting terminal complement component C5, provides definitive therapy by blocking the formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9), thereby halting the downstream endothelial injury and microvascular thrombosis characteristic of CM-HUS. In contrast, PEX acts nonspecifically; it temporarily reduces the burden of circulating activated complement fragments, inflammatory cytokines, and potential autoantibodies while supplying functional complement regulators present in donor plasma.

In the reported case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-triggered CM-HUS, this distinction dictated the therapeutic trajectory. The patient underwent three sessions of PEX as a bridging measure while awaiting confirmatory testing and meningococcal vaccination. Although diuresis returned briefly following these exchanges, indicating transient stabilization, sustained hematological remission and renal recovery were achieved only after the initiation of eculizumab. This clinical course underscores why PEX is considered merely a bridging therapy in CM-HUS: unlike in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (where PEX replaces deficient ADAMTS13) or anti-factor H antibody-mediated disease (where it removes the offending antibody), PEX does not correct the underlying dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway inherent to CM-HUS. Once the exogenous supply of regulators from donor plasma is diluted or consumed, unchecked complement activation resumes unless specific blockade is established. Consequently, while PEX can provide a critical window for stabilization and safe vaccine administration, it cannot replace terminal complement inhibition as the standard of care for durable disease control.

### [Management] Initiating eculizumab creates a specific vulnerability to infection by encapsulated organisms. What is the recommended vaccination strategy regarding timing relative to the first dose, and what prophylactic measure is indicated if immediate treatment is required before vaccine immunity is established?

Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits the terminal complement component C5, abolishes the formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9), which is the host's primary defense mechanism against encapsulated bacteria, particularly *Neisseria meningitidis*. Consequently, initiating this therapy creates a profound and specific vulnerability to life-threatening meningococcal sepsis.

The standard of care mandates vaccination against both serogroups A, C, W, Y (e.g., Menveo) and serogroup B (e.g., Bexsero) ideally **at least two weeks prior** to the first dose of eculizumab. This interval is critical to allow sufficient time for the development of protective bactericidal titers before complement blockade is established.

However, in clinical scenarios where the threat of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) necessitates immediate treatment—precluding the two-week waiting period for vaccine efficacy—antibiotic prophylaxis must be instituted concurrently with or prior to the first infusion. As illustrated in the reported case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-triggered CM-HUS, the patient received her initial meningococcal vaccinations but required urgent eculizumab induction before immunity could be established. In this instance, **prophylactic penicillin (phenoxymethylpenicillin)** was administered to bridge the gap until vaccine-induced protection was achieved. While *N. meningitidis* is the paramount concern, clinicians must recognize that this vulnerability extends to other encapsulated organisms relevant to the patient's exposure history, such as *C. canimorsus* in cases involving animal bites. Therefore, if immediate complement inhibition is unavoidable, antibiotic prophylaxis is not optional; it is a mandatory safety measure to prevent overwhelming postsplenectomy-like sepsis during the window of immunologic susceptibility.

### [Pathophysiology] Given that up to 50% of patients with CM-HUS do not have identifiable pathogenic genetic variants in complement regulatory proteins, how does the concept of 'disease penetrance' and 'second hits' explain the pathogenesis in genetically undefined cases triggered by infection?

The apparent paradox of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) occurring in patients without identifiable pathogenic genetic variants is resolved by understanding the concepts of **incomplete disease penetrance** and the **"two-hit" hypothesis**. As illustrated in this case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-triggered CM-HUS, the absence of a detected mutation does not equate to the absence of genetic susceptibility.

Current data suggest that only 50% to 70% of patients with CM-HUS harbor identifiable variants in complement regulatory genes (such as factors H, I, or B). In the remaining "genetically undefined" cases, pathogenesis is driven by the convergence of subclinical genetic vulnerability and a potent environmental trigger. Many patients possess rare genetic variants of uncertain significance, polygenic risk scores, or mutations in non-coding regions that do not manifest as overt disease under homeostatic conditions. This state represents low-penetrance susceptibility where the complement system functions adequately until stressed.

The "second hit"—in this instance, severe *C. canimorsus* bacteremia—provides the necessary threshold to tip the balance toward uncontrolled alternative pathway activation. *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated organism that interacts dynamically with the host complement system; while complement activation is essential for bacterial clearance, certain strains exhibit partial resistance or induce excessive inflammation. In a genetically susceptible host, this infectious surge overwhelms regulatory mechanisms (even those that appear functionally normal at baseline), leading to widespread endothelial injury, thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and organ failure.

This case exemplifies the temporal dissociation characteristic of the two-hit model: the patient initially presented with sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (the first hit/environmental stress), but the classic features of TMA (haemolysis and thrombocytopenia) emerged only later, as the infection began to resolve. This delay underscores that the infection acted as the catalyst for a latent dysregulation rather than a direct toxin-mediated effect like Shiga toxin. Consequently, CM-HUS must be viewed as a clinicopathological diagnosis where a severe infectious trigger can unmask underlying complement dysregulation, regardless of whether standard genetic panels return a positive result.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When evaluating a patient with MAHA and thrombocytopenia, how does the presence or absence of prodromal diarrhoea and stool Shiga toxin PCR results guide the differentiation between STEC-HUS and complement-mediated HUS?

In the evaluation of a patient presenting with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and thrombocytopenia, the distinction between Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* haemolytic uraemic syndrome (STEC-HUS) and complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS) relies heavily on the clinical history of gastrointestinal illness and specific microbiological testing. STEC-HUS is characteristically preceded by a prodrome of bloody or non-bloody diarrhoea, whereas CM-HUS typically lacks this enteric precursor and is often triggered by other insults, such as infection, pregnancy, or autoimmune disease. Definitive differentiation requires stool testing for Shiga toxin; a negative PCR result, particularly in the absence of diarrhoeal symptoms, effectively rules out STEC-HUS and shifts the diagnostic focus toward CM-HUS or other thrombotic microangiopathies.

This diagnostic algorithm was pivotal in the reported case of a woman who developed TMA following *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis secondary to a dog bite. Unlike classic STEC-HUS, this patient had no history of diarrhoea. Furthermore, stool PCR analysis for Shiga toxin was negative, which excluded STEC-HUS from the differential diagnosis. With thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) also ruled out by an ADAMTS13 activity level above 10% (43.5%), and secondary causes like disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) resolving while haemolysis persisted, the diagnosis of CM-HUS was established by exclusion and confirmed by the patient's response to complement blockade with eculizumab. This case underscores that in adults presenting with MAHA and thrombocytopenia without prodromal diarrhoea and with negative Shiga toxin studies, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for complement-mediated mechanisms, even when triggered by rare pathogens like *C. canimorsus*.

### [Management] In a patient with *C. canimorsus* bacteraemia who has cleared the infection but developed TMA, what are the competing risks involved in administering terminal complement inhibitors, and how does the case report suggest balancing the risk of recurrent sepsis against the risk of irreversible renal loss?

In the setting of *C. canimorsus*-associated complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), the administration of terminal complement inhibitors like eculizumab presents a critical therapeutic paradox: the very mechanism required to halt endothelial destruction and preserve renal function simultaneously disables a primary host defense against encapsulated organisms.

The competing risks are distinct and severe. On one hand, unchecked alternative complement pathway activation drives thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to irreversible glomerular thrombosis, cortical necrosis, and permanent dialysis dependence. As illustrated in this case, despite initial sepsis resolution, the patient developed sudden isolated hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia; without complement blockade, this trajectory typically culminates in end-stage kidney disease. On the other hand, terminal complement inhibition (specifically C5 blockade) abolishes the bactericidal activity necessary to clear *Neisseria* species and *Capnocytophaga*. Since *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated gram-negative rod that relies on complement-mediated lysis for clearance, re-administering eculizumab during active bacteremia could precipitate fulminant, fatal recurrent sepsis.

This case report delineates a precise strategy to balance these risks by strictly sequencing therapy based on microbiological status rather than clinical urgency alone. The clinicians delayed eculizumab induction until blood cultures confirmed the eradication of *C. canimorsus*, thereby eliminating the immediate threat of uncontrolled bacterial proliferation. During this window, plasma exchange served as a bridge to temporarily remove circulating complement fragments and mitigate TMA progression without inducing profound, sustained immunosuppression. Once sterility was assured, the team proceeded with aggressive risk mitigation: administering meningococcal vaccination (covering serogroups A, C, Y, W, and B) and initiating prophylactic penicillin to compensate for the vaccine's delayed efficacy and the specific vulnerability to *Capnocytophaga*.

Ultimately, the decision prioritizes preventing irreversible renal loss once the infectious trigger is neutralized. The successful outcome in this patient—hematological remission and partial renal recovery—underscores that while complement inhibition carries inherent infectious risks, those risks are manageable with strict adherence to culture-negative status, vaccination protocols, and antibiotic prophylaxis, whereas the risk of permanent renal failure from untreated CM-HUS is absolute.
