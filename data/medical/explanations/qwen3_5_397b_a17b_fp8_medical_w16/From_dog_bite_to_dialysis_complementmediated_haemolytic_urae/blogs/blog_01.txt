Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# The Dog Bite That Bit Back: Capnocytophaga canimorsus and the Mask of Sepsis

We've all heard the adage about the dog that didn't bark, but in the ED, we worry far more about the dog that *did*. A bite to the lip seems trivial until four days later your patient rolls in with a temperature of 40°C, mottled extremities, and a blood sugar so low it registers as a lab error. This isn't just a local cellulitis story; this is the classic, terrifying setup for *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis. But here is where this case gets truly fascinating—and dangerous. The infection was treated, the cultures turned positive, and the patient began to stabilize, only to suddenly crash into profound hemolysis and anuria. The sepsis wasn't the whole story; it was merely the curtain raiser for a superimposed complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Our patient, a previously healthy woman in her late 40s, presented with what looked like textbook fulminant sepsis secondary to a dog bite. She had pancytopenia, coagulopathy consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and acute kidney injury (AKI). Initially, the clinical picture was dominated by the storm of septic shock. The team correctly suspected *C. canimorsus*, a fastidious Gram-negative rod found in canine oral flora, and started broad-spectrum antibiotics. Blood cultures eventually confirmed the bug seven days later—a delay typical for this slow-growing organism. By day eight, the inflammatory markers were trending down, platelets were recovering, and the coagulopathy seemed to be resolving. It looked like we had won the battle against the bacteria.

Then, the plot twisted. Just as the sepsis appeared to be under control, the patient's hemoglobin plummeted from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days without any evidence of bleeding. Her platelets crashed again, haptoglobin vanished, and LDH skyrocketed. A peripheral smear revealed 15–20% schistocytes. This wasn't lingering DIC; this was a new beast: Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA). The initial suspicion of autoimmune hemolytic anemia (triggered by a falsely positive Coombs test likely due to prior penicillin exposure) was quickly discarded when steroids failed to halt the destruction. With ADAMTS13 activity at 43.5% (ruling out TTP) and negative Shiga toxin PCR (ruling out STEC-HUS), we were left with a diagnosis of exclusion that required immediate action: Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS).

This case perfectly illustrates the "second hit" phenomenon in TMA. The initial *C. canimorsus* infection acted as the trigger, activating the complement cascade in a host who perhaps had subclinical susceptibility or simply overwhelmed regulatory mechanisms. While standard complement levels (C3/C4) remained normal—a common red herring in CM-HUS—the alternative pathway was running wild, depositing fibrin thrombi in the glomeruli and shredding red blood cells. The renal biopsy confirmed the nightmare: ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and acute tubular necrosis. The patient went from oliguric to completely anuric, requiring dialysis.

Managing this scenario presents a profound therapeutic dilemma. The treatment for CM-HUS is eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that blocks terminal complement component C5. However, blocking C5 leaves patients exquisitely vulnerable to encapsulated organisms—specifically *Neisseria meningitidis*, but also potentially *Capnocytophaga* itself. You are essentially turning off a major arm of innate immunity in a patient who just survived a devastating bacteremia from an encapsulated bug. In this case, the team navigated this tightrope by waiting until blood cultures were unequivocally negative before administering eculizumab. They bridged with plasma exchange (which offers transient benefit in CM-HUS by clearing inflammatory mediators) and ensured meningococcal vaccination coverage alongside antibiotic prophylaxis. The result? Spontaneous diuresis returned, hematologic remission was achieved, and renal function partially recovered.

The takeaway here isn't just about remembering obscure bacteria; it's about maintaining vigilance when the clinical trajectory deviates from the expected recovery curve. If your septic patient starts getting better and then suddenly develops unexplained hemolysis and worsening renal failure, stop thinking about residual infection and start thinking about TMA. Do not let normal C3/C4 levels reassure you; they are frequently normal in CM-HUS. And never underestimate a dog bite in an immunocompetent adult—what starts as a puncture wound can evolve into a systemic catastrophe involving DIC, multiorgan failure, and complement dysregulation.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect *Capnocytophaga* early:** In any patient with sepsis/DIC following dog or cat contact (even minor bites or scratches), consider *C. canimorsus*. Remember it is fastidious and slow-growing; negative early cultures do not rule it out, and molecular methods may be needed if suspicion is high.
*   **Watch for the "Second Hit":** In sepsis-associated TMA, hemolysis and thrombocytopenia may worsen or appear *after* initial signs of septic improvement. A sudden drop in Hb with rising LDH and schistocytes should trigger an immediate TMA workup regardless of improving inflammatory markers.
*   **Normal Complements Don't Rule Out CM-HUS:** Standard C3 and C4 levels are often within normal reference ranges during active complement-mediated HUS rely on excluding TTP (ADAMTS13 >10%) and STEC-HUS (negative toxin PCR) alongside clinical evidence of MAHA and AKI.
*   **The Eculizumab Dilemma:** Treating infection-triggered CM-HUS requires balancing urgent complement blockade against the risk of overwhelming infection from encapsulated organisms. Initiate eculizumab only after bacteremia is cleared whenever possible, ensure appropriate vaccination (MenACWY and MenB), and maintain antibiotic prophylaxis.
*   **Time is Kidney:** Delayed initiation of eculizumab in CM-HUS is strongly associated with poor renal recovery and progression to end-stage kidney disease.
