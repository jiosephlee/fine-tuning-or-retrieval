Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Genotype-Negative, Phenotype-Positive: The Reality of Acquired CM-HUS

We love a clean algorithm in emergency medicine and critical care. If a patient presents with Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI), we run the TTP vs. HUS gauntlet. We check ADAMTS13, we look for Shiga toxin, and if those are negative, we often pivot to "atypical" or Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS). The traditional teaching suggests that CM-HUS is the domain of the genetically predisposed—a patient harboring a silent mutation in Factor H, I, or CD46 waiting for a "second hit" to unleash hell.

But what happens when the genetics come back blank? Do we stop treating? Do we doubt the diagnosis?

A fascinating case report by Fredriksen et al. forces us to confront the reality of **acquired** complement dysregulation. They describe a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who went from a dog bite on the lip to dialysis dependence in less than a week, triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. Despite having no identifiable germline complement mutations, she developed fulminant CM-HUS that responded dramatically to eculizumab. This isn't just a story about a rare bug; it's a reminder that phenotype trumps genotype, and that infection alone can be enough to break the complement brake.

### From Dog Bite to Dialysis Dependence

The clinical trajectory here is terrifyingly rapid. Our patient presented four days after a dog bite with high fever (>40°C), profound hypoglycemia (a classic red flag for severe *Capnocytophaga* sepsis), and mottled extremities. Initially, the picture was dominated by septic shock and Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC). She had pancytopenia, coagulopathy, and AKI. In the fog of early sepsis, distinguishing DIC from Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) is notoriously difficult, and initially, the team rightly focused on source control and hemodynamic support.

She received broad-spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin/tazobactam), and blood cultures eventually confirmed *C. canimorsus*, a fastidious gram-negative rod found in canine oral flora. With aggressive ICU care, her inflammatory markers began to settle, her coagulation profile normalized, and she appeared to be turning the corner on the sepsis front.

Then, the plot twisted. Just as the sepsis seemed to be resolving, she developed sudden, isolated hemolysis. Her hemoglobin plummeted from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days without any evidence of bleeding. Her platelets crashed again and stayed low. Haptoglobin vanished, and LDH skyrocketed. A peripheral smear revealed 15–20% schistocytes. The DIC had resolved, but a new engine of destruction had taken over: pure TMA.

### The Diagnostic Dilemma: Ruling Out the Usual Suspects

When TMA strikes an adult post-sepsis, the differential is narrow but critical. First, you must rule out Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP). Our patient's ADAMTS13 activity came back at 43.5%. Since TTP requires activity <10%, this effectively closed that door. Next, Shiga-toxin producing *E. coli* (STEC-HUS) was ruled out via negative stool PCR and the lack of a diarrheal prodrome.

This left us with Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS). However, the diagnostic waters were muddy by standard labs. Her C3 and C4 levels were normal. In fact, up to 50% of patients with CM-HUS will have normal static complement levels during an acute flare because consumption is balanced by hepatic synthesis or the defect lies in regulation rather than quantity.

The definitive answer came from histology and response to therapy. A kidney biopsy showed fibrin thrombi in glomerular capillaries and ischemic changes consistent with TMA, with no immune complex deposition (ruling out lupus nephritis or other GN). But the true validator was therapeutic: after bridging with plasma exchange (which offers only transient benefit in CM-HUS compared to TTP), she was started on eculizumab. The result? Spontaneous diuresis returned, hemolysis stopped, and platelets recovered.

### The Genetic Paradox: When Panels Come Back Negative

Here is where this case challenges our conventional wisdom. We often think of CM-HUS as a genetic disease triggered by infection. We expect to find a mutation in Factor H or MCP that predisposes the patient to unchecked alternative pathway activation once an inflammatory trigger occurs.

In this case, extensive genetic testing returned **negative**. No pathogenic variants were identified in complement regulatory genes, and anti-Factor H antibodies were absent. So how did she get CM-HUS?

This represents the concept of **purely acquired complement dysregulation**. While 50–70% of CM-HUS patients have identifiable genetic defects, a significant minority do not. In these "genotype-negative" cases, the infectious trigger itself—likely through specific interactions between bacterial surface proteins and host complement regulators—is sufficient to overwhelm the system temporarily. *C. canimorsus* is known to interact variably with serum complement; some strains resist killing by expressing capsules that interfere with regulation, potentially creating a localized storm of alternative pathway activation that spills over into systemic endothelial damage.

The takeaway is crucial: **Do not withhold eculizumab while waiting for genetic results.** Genetics inform long-term prognosis and duration of therapy, but they should never delay acute treatment in a patient with clear clinical evidence of CM-HUS waiting for ADAMTS13 exclusion and STEC negativity. As seen here, delaying blockade risks permanent renal loss or death regardless of whether a mutation exists on paper.

### Navigating Treatment During Active Infection

Treating CM-HUS with eculizumab during or immediately after bacteremia presents a unique philosophical and practical dilemma Eculizumab blocks C5, preventing the formation of the Membrane Attack Complex (MAC). The MAC is our primary defense against encapsulated organisms like *Neisseria meningitidis* and... wait for it... *Capnocytophaga*.

By blocking terminal complement, we theoretically leave the door wide open for recurrent or overwhelming infection with these exact pathogens. The team navigated this tightrope expertly:
*   They ensured blood cultures were negative before administering the first dose of eculizumab ("sterilizing" the bloodstream first).
*   They administered meningococcal vaccines (Menveo and Bexsero) prior to induction when possible (though they had to proceed before full immunity could develop due to urgency).
*   They maintained antibiotic prophylaxis (phenoxymethylpenicillin) throughout the period of complement blockade until vaccine immunity was established weeks later under outpatient follow-up protocols involving Menveo & Bexsero..
  
This balancing act underscores that while infection triggered the TMA in this setting ,the decision block terminall completement requires meticulous planning mitigate super-infection risk without sacrificing organ salvage potential .
