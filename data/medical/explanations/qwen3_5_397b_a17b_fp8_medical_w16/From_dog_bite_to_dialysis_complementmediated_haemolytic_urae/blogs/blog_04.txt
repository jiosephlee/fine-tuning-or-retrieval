Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Walking the Tightrope: Initiating Eculizumab in the Shadow of Active Infection

We've all seen the patient who survives the storm of sepsis only to crash into a new, equally terrifying complication once the dust settles. It's a cruel twist of fate where the body's attempt to clear an infection triggers a cascade that destroys the very organs we fought so hard to save. A recent case report by Fredriksen et al. perfectly illustrates this high-stakes clinical paradox: a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who went from a simple dog bite to dialysis-dependent complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), forcing us to walk a therapeutic tightrope between saving her kidneys and inviting a fatal superinfection.

The story begins innocuously enough—a dog bite to the lip. Four days later, she presented with fulminant *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis, complete with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), pancytopenia, and shock. *C. canimorsus* is a nasty bug; it's part of the normal canine flora but can cause rapid, life-threatening illness in humans, often mimicking meningococcemia with its ability to trigger DIC and multiorgan failure. Our patient survived the initial septic onslaught with antibiotics and aggressive supportive care. But just as her inflammatory markers began to trend down and the coagulopathy resolved, she developed something far more insidious: sudden, isolated hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, and worsening anuria.

This is where the diagnostic trap snaps shut. In the early phases of severe sepsis and DIC, distinguishing between consumption coagulopathy and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is notoriously difficult. Schistocytes can be masked by other hematologic abnormalities, and renal failure is expected in septic shock. However, the clue here was the timing and the isolation of symptoms. Once the overt signs of sepsis improved, the persistence of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and thrombocytopenia signaled a shift from DIC to TMA. With ADAMTS13 activity at 43.5% (ruling out TTP) and negative Shiga toxin PCR (ruling out STEC-HUS), the diagnosis landed squarely on complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS). The kidney biopsy confirmed it: fibrin thrombi in glomeruli and ischemic changes consistent with unchecked complement activation.

Now comes the real cliffhanger: treatment. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody against terminal complement component C5, is the gold standard for CM-HUS. It stops the complement cascade dead in its tracks, halting endothelial damage and allowing renal recovery. In this case, it worked beautifully; diuresis returned, and hematologic parameters normalized. But here lies the terrifying irony: *C. canimorsus*, like *Neisseria meningitidis*, is an encapsulated bacterium that relies heavily on the terminal complement pathway for clearance. By administering eculizumab, we essentially induce a functional asplenia, leaving the patient wide open to overwhelming infection by the very organism that nearly killed her days prior—or worse, a new meningococcal invasion.

Initiating terminal complement blockade in a patient recovering from bacteremia feels like defusing a bomb while standing on it. You cannot wait indefinitely for "perfect" clearance because every hour of delay in treating CM-HUS correlates with irreversible renal damage and progression to end-stage kidney disease. Yet, starting too early risks turning a treatable bacteremia into a fatal septic shock that no antibiotic can outrun. In this case report, the team navigated this by ensuring blood cultures were definitively negative before pulling the trigger on eculizumab. They utilized plasma exchange (PEX) as a bridge—a temporizing measure that removes circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators but doesn't fix the underlying dysregulation. PEX bought them time to confirm sterilization of the blood while keeping the TMA somewhat at bay.

The safety net for this maneuver is robust vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis. Standard protocol dictates meningococcal vaccination (covering serogroups A, C, Y, W, and B) at least two weeks prior to eculizumab initiation. However, in acute settings like this where waiting two weeks isn't an option for renal salvage, you vaccinate immediately and cover with prophylactic antibiotics until immunity develops. Our patient received Menveo and Bexsero upon admission to nephrology but started eculizumab before full seroconversion could occur. Consequently, she was placed on prophylactic penicillin (Apocillin) to guard against encapsulated organisms during this vulnerable window. It's a delicate balance of trusting your microbiology data, respecting the pharmacokinetics of vaccine response, and accepting that sometimes you have to treat based on probabilities rather than certainties.

Interestingly, extended genetic testing in this patient revealed no pathogenic complement gene variants. This suggests her CM-HUS was purely infection-triggered—a "second hit" phenomenon where the massive complement activation required to fight *C. canimorsus* overwhelmed her regulatory capacity in a genetically susceptible but previously silent host. While some might argue for stopping eculizumab once genetics come back negative, the severity of her initial presentation and dialysis dependence justified continuing maintenance therapy with plans for future reassessment. The takeaway? Don't let a lack of identified mutation dissuade you from treating active TMA if the clinical picture fits; phenotype trumps genotype in the acute setting.

This case serves as a stark reminder that TMA should remain on our differential list long after sepsis seems to be resolving.
