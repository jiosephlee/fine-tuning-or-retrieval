Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# The Coombs Conundrum: Avoiding the Steroid Trap in Post-Sepsis Hemolysis

We've all been there. You're managing a complex post-septic patient who finally seems to be turning the corner. The inflammatory markers are trending down, the pressors are off, and then—bam—the hemoglobin crashes. Your brain immediately goes to the usual suspects: occult bleed? Delayed transfusion reaction? Or perhaps autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA)? In the heat of the moment, a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) feels like the smoking gun. You start high-dose steroids, pat yourself on the back for catching Evans syndrome, and wait for the reticulocytes to rise. But what if that positive Coombs test is a liar? What if you're treating the wrong mechanism while the patient's kidneys continue to shut down?

A fascinating case recently published by Fredriksen et al. illustrates this diagnostic pitfall perfectly. A previously healthy woman in her late 40s presented with fulminant sepsis four days after a dog bite to the lip. The culprit was *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a nasty gram-negative rod known for causing rapid deterioration, DIC, and multiorgan failure in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent hosts. She survived the initial storm of septic shock, pancytopenia, and dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI). But just as her coagulopathy resolved and inflammatory markers improved, she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days. Her platelets remained stubbornly low.

Here is where the trap snapped shut. The initial workup showed a positive DAT for IgG. In a vacuum, this screams AIHA. The team appropriately started high-dose prednisolone and transfused blood products. But here's the kicker: she didn't get better. The hemolysis persisted, the platelets stayed depressed, and the clinical picture wasn't fitting a pure autoimmune process. It was only when someone took a hard look at the peripheral smear—revealing 15%–20% schistocytes—that the narrative shifted from "autoimmune" to "microangiopathic." The DAT was likely a false positive driven by her recent penicillin exposure (phenoxymethylpenicillin) and multiple prior transfusions, a well-documented phenomenon that can derail your diagnostic reasoning if you rely on it exclusively.

This pivot point is critical because the treatments for AIHA and Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) are diametrically opposed in terms of urgency and mechanism. While steroids might eventually help an autoimmune process, they do absolutely nothing to stop the complement-mediated endothelial destruction occurring in Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Ureaemic Syndrome (CM-HUS). In this case, every hour spent waiting for steroids to work was an hour of ongoing glomerular thrombosis. Once TMA was suspected based on the smear (schistocytes >1%) and confirmed by ruling out TTP (ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, safely above the <10% cutoff) and STEC-HUS (negative stool PCR), the team initiated plasma exchange as a bridge and promptly started eculizumab. The result? Spontaneous diuresis returned, hematological remission was achieved, and renal function began to recover.

The pathophysiology here is a brutal example of the "two-hit" hypothesis gone wrong. *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated organism that interacts heavily with the host complement system. While complement activation is necessary to clear the bacteria, in susceptible individuals—or perhaps just overwhelmed systems—it can spiral into uncontrolled alternative pathway activation. This leads to widespread endothelial damage, fibrin thrombi formation (seen clearly on her kidney biopsy), and mechanical shearing of red blood cells. The irony is palpable: the very immune mechanism meant to save her from sepsis nearly cost her her kidneys. Interestingly, genetic testing didn't reveal a pathogenic variant in this patient, suggesting this was purely an infection-triggered event rather than a latent genetic disorder unmasked by illness—a reminder that you don't need a known mutation to develop catastrophic CM-HUS.

The takeaway isn't just about dog bites; it's about maintaining a high index of suspicion for TMA in any post-septic patient with falling hemoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia, regardless of what the Coombs test says. Don't let a false-positive DAT lure you into a steroid haze while schistocytes wreck havoc on your patient's microvasculature. Look at the smear. Check the haptoglobin (which was undetectable here) and LDH trends differentiating intravascular destruction from other causes. And remember, in the world of TMA, time is kidney tissue.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Don't Trust the Coombs Blindly:** A positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) can be falsely positive due to recent transfusions, drug exposure (especially penicillins), or severe inflammation; always correlate with peripheral smear findings.
*   **Smear is King:** The presence of schistocytes (>1%) is the hallmark of Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia (MAHA); if you see them alongside thrombocytopenia and AKI, think TMA first until proven otherwise.
*   **Rule Out TTP Rapidly:** Use ADAMTS13 activity levels to distinguish TTP (<10%) from CM-HUS; do not delay plasma exchange or complement inhibition while waiting for genetics if clinical suspicion is high.
*   **Infection as a Trigger:** Unusual pathogens like *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* can trigger complement-mediated HUS even in patients without identifiable genetic mutations; resolution of sepsis does not guarantee resolution of TMA.
*   **Steroids Won't Fix Complement:** If it's CM-HUS, corticosteroids are ineffective; early initiation of terminal complement blockade (eculizumab/ravulizumab) is crucial for renal salvage once active bacteremia is controlled and meningococcal coverage is established.
