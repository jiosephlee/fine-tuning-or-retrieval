Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Management of Capnocytophaga Sepsis

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a fastidious, encapsulated, gram-negative rod that resides as commensal flora in the oropharynx of dogs and cats. While human infection typically follows a bite or scratch, transmission can occur through mere close contact, and the organism poses a significant threat even to immunocompetent hosts. Although the majority of dog bites result in localized cellulitis or remain asymptomatic, *C. canimorsus* is uniquely capable of causing fulminant sepsis characterized by rapid progression to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan failure, and death, with mortality rates approaching 30%. The pathophysiology involves the organism's ability to evade innate immunity; its polysaccharide capsule confers resistance to phagocytosis and complement-mediated lysis in some strains, facilitating unchecked bacteremia. In the presented case, a previously healthy woman in her late 40s developed severe septic shock just four days after a dog bite to the upper lip. Her initial failure to fill a prescription for phenoxymethylpenicillin underscores the critical window for early intervention, as delays allow the slow-growing organism to establish a high bacterial load before clinical deterioration becomes apparent.

The clinical spectrum of *C. canimorsus* infection is broad, ranging from mild local inflammation to catastrophic systemic collapse. In severe cases, the presentation mimics other overwhelming infections such as meningococcemia or purpura fulminans. This patient presented with high fever (>40°C), profound malaise, and distinctive cutaneous signs including mottling and cooling of the lower extremities, indicative of peripheral vasoconstriction and microvascular thrombosis. A notable and potentially misleading feature in this case was severe refractory hypoglycemia (nadir 0.6 mmol/L), which persisted despite aggressive glucose administration. While hypoglycemia in sepsis often signals hepatic dysfunction or depleted glycogen stores, its prominence here served as an early marker of metabolic derangement preceding overt hemodynamic collapse. The absence of necrotizing fasciitis on imaging helped focus the differential diagnosis on systemic rather than soft-tissue sources, directing attention toward bacteremia-driven organ injury.

Initial laboratory evaluation revealed a triad of pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), and coagulopathy, creating a complex diagnostic challenge. The combination of fever, thrombocytopenia, anemia, and elevated inflammatory markers initially raised concern for primary hematologic malignancies such as acute leukemia or hyperinflammatory syndromes like hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). However, the clinical context of recent animal exposure and the presence of consumptive coagulopathy strongly favored sepsis-induced DIC over primary bone marrow failure. This distinction was crucial, as evidenced by the subsequent normal flow cytometry and bone marrow findings which ruled out leukemic blasts. Early in the course, the diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) was considered less likely because haptoglobin levels were initially normal and reticulocyte counts were not compensatory; furthermore, significant leukopenia is atypical for classic HUS but common in overwhelming gram-negative sepsis. Recognizing that sepsis itself drives endothelial damage and consumption of clotting factors prevented premature misdiagnosis while allowing for appropriate supportive care.

Diagnosing *C. canimorsus* specifically presents inherent difficulties due to its slow growth rate and fastidious nature in standard culture media. Blood cultures may take several days to turn positive, leading to potential delays in targeted therapy if clinicians rely solely on preliminary Gram stains or rapid automated systems that may miss the organism. In this case, suspicion remained high based on epidemiological clues—the dog bite history—prompting empiric coverage with broad-spectrum antibiotics effective against oral flora of dogs, specifically piperacillin/tazobactam. The eventual confirmation seven days later via both aerobic and anaerobic cultures validates the necessity of maintaining a high index of suspicion when clinical severity outweighs initial microbiological data. Increasingly, molecular methods such as real-time nanopore sequencing are becoming valuable adjuncts to shorten this diagnostic gap, though they were not required here due to sustained clinical vigilance.

Immediate management prioritized hemodynamic stabilization and source control through antimicrobial therapy. Given the severity of the presentation—marked by lactic acidosis (5.7 mmol/L), metabolic acidosis, and impending multiorgan failure—the patient required transfer to the intensive care unit for continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) once anuria developed. Fluid resuscitation was balanced carefully against the risk of volume overload in the setting of septic cardiomyopathy and renal failure. Antibiotic stewardship involved initiating broad coverage immediately upon suspicion of *Capnocytophaga*, later de-escalating to ampicillin once susceptibility was confirmed. Supportive measures included transfusion of platelets and fibrinogen to manage active bleeding and DIC-related coagulopathy. Notably, despite profound shock features like distal ischemia and cyanosis, the patient did not require vasopressors beyond stress-dose corticosteroids, suggesting that while microvascular perfusion was compromised by thrombosis, macro-circulatory tone was relatively preserved—a phenomenon sometimes seen in toxin-mediated or specific gram-negative sepsis patterns. This phase of care laid the groundwork for survival but set the stage for the subsequent emergence of complement-mediated complications once the infectious burden began to resolve.
