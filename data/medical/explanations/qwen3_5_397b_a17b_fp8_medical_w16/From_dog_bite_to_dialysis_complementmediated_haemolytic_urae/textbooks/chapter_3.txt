Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Algorithm for Adult-Onset TMA: Exclusion and Confirmation

The diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in adults begins with recognizing the clinical triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute organ injury. In this case, the patient initially presented with sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), which can obscure the emergence of a primary TMA. The pivotal moment in the diagnostic algorithm occurred when the patient's inflammatory markers improved, yet she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin accompanied by persistent thrombocytopenia. This dissociation between resolving sepsis and worsening hematologic parameters necessitated a shift in focus from infection-driven coagulopathy to a primary thrombotic microangiopathy. Confirmation of MAHA relies on identifying schistocytes on a peripheral blood smear; in this patient, the presence of 15%–20% schistocytes provided the morphological evidence of mechanical red cell fragmentation required to proceed with subtype differentiation. Crucially, while the initial direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive, likely due to penicillin exposure or recent transfusions, the subsequent negative result helped exclude autoimmune hemolytic anemia, reinforcing the diagnosis of a non-immune mediated process.

Once TMA is established, the immediate priority is to exclude thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), a medical emergency requiring distinct management. Differentiation relies heavily on ADAMTS13 activity levels. TTP is defined by a severe deficiency in ADAMTS13 activity, typically less than 10%, whereas other forms of TMA usually present with normal or mildly reduced activity. In this case, the patient's PLASMIC score was calculated at 4/7, indicating a low pre-test probability for TTP, which was definitively confirmed when ADAMTS13 activity returned at 43.5%. This finding effectively ruled out TTP and directed the diagnostic workup toward other etiologies. Simultaneously, Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC)-HUS must be excluded via stool PCR, even in the absence of a diarrheal prodrome. Although STEC-HUS classically follows bloody diarrhea, atypical presentations occur; however, the negative PCR result in this patient eliminated STEC as the causative agent, leaving complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS) as the leading diagnosis by exclusion.

With TTP and STEC-HUS ruled out, the diagnosis of CM-HUS is primarily clinicopathological, often supported by renal biopsy when the clinical picture is complex or overlapped by sepsis. In this patient, a kidney biopsy was performed once platelet counts allowed for safe procedural intervention. Histopathological examination revealed hallmark features of TMA, including fibrin thrombi within glomerular capillaries, ischemic glomeruli, and severe acute tubular necrosis. Importantly, immunofluorescence demonstrated an absence of immune complex deposition, distinguishing this process from immune-complex mediated glomerulonephritis or lupus nephritis. These findings confirmed that the renal failure was driven by endothelial injury and microvascular thrombosis rather than direct bacterial toxicity or immune complex trapping, solidifying the indication for complement-targeted therapy.

A critical teaching point in evaluating CM-HUS is the interpretation of complement studies. Clinicians often mistakenly assume that normal serum C3 and C4 levels exclude complement dysregulation; however, in CM-HUS, standard complement levels frequently remain within reference ranges because the pathology involves dysregulated activation of the alternative pathway rather than wholesale consumption of complement components. As seen in this case, the patient's C3 and C4 levels were normal upon admission and throughout her course. Furthermore, functional assays of the classical, lectin, and alternative pathways were initially reported as normal, likely influenced by recent plasma exchange sessions which replace deficient factors temporarily. Therefore, normal serological complement profiles do not rule out CM-HUS. The diagnosis rests on the exclusion of other causes and the clinical response to complement inhibition. In this instance, despite negative genetic testing for known complement mutations—a scenario occurring in up to 50% of cases—the combination of exclusive criteria fulfillment and rapid hematologic remission following eculizumab administration confirmed infection-triggered CM-HUS secondary to *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*.
