Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 4: ## Therapeutic Strategy: Balancing Complement Blockade with Infectious Risk

The management of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) triggered by severe infection represents a delicate clinical equilibrium between halting catastrophic endothelial damage and preserving host immunity against the inciting pathogen. In this case, the decision to initiate eculizumab was contingent upon the resolution of active *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia, illustrating a critical principle: while early complement blockade is the strongest predictor of renal recovery, administering terminal complement inhibitors during uncontrolled sepsis carries a prohibitive risk of overwhelming infection by encapsulated organisms. The patient's trajectory underscores that renal prognosis in CM-HUS is inextricably linked to the timing of therapeutic intervention relative to the onset of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although the patient experienced a delay due to the obscuring presence of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and septic shock, the eventual initiation of eculizumab once cultures cleared allowed for haematological remission and partial return of renal function. Had therapy been withheld indefinitely due to infectious fears, or conversely, started prematurely during peak bacteraemia, the outcome could have ranged from irreversible end-stage kidney disease to fatal recurrent sepsis.

Determining the appropriate duration of eculizumab therapy remains a nuanced challenge, particularly in cases where no underlying genetic complement disorder is identified. In patients with confirmed pathogenic variants in complement regulatory genes, lifelong therapy is often indicated due to the high risk of relapse upon cessation. However, in infection-triggered CM-HUS without demonstrable genetic defects—as seen in this patient—the disease mechanism is hypothesized to be a "two-hit" phenomenon where the infectious insult transiently overwhelms a potentially subclinical or polygenic susceptibility. Consequently, finite courses of therapy are increasingly considered for such acquired forms. In this case, a planned four-month maintenance course was selected, with the intention to reassess for discontinuation if renal function stabilizes and no relapse occurs. This approach balances the need to prevent recurrent TMA during the vulnerable recovery phase against the long-term costs and infectious risks associated with indefinite complement blockade. Nevertheless, close monitoring is mandatory, as the absence of a detected mutation does not entirely eliminate the possibility of an underlying predisposition that might manifest under future stressors.

Long-term follow-up for survivors of severe TMA extends beyond simple monitoring of serum creatinine. Patients who have suffered dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury and widespread microvascular thrombosis remain at elevated risk for chronic kidney disease progression, hypertension, and extra-renal sequelae related to prior ischemic insults. For this patient, ongoing nephrology surveillance is essential to manage residual renal impairment and adjust antihypertensive or proteinuria-reducing therapies as needed. Furthermore, because eculizumab induces a functional hyposplenism by blocking the terminal complement pathway, long-term vigilance against encapsulated bacteria is required. Even after discontinuing the drug, patients must maintain up-to-date vaccinations against *Neisseria meningitidis*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, and *Haemophilus influenzae*, and they should be educated on the imperative need for immediate medical evaluation regarding any febrile illness. The prophylactic antibiotics administered in this case highlight the rigorous protective measures necessary when navigating the intersection of immunosuppression and recent severe infection.

A pivotal clinical lesson from this report is the necessity of maintaining a high index of suspicion for unusual zoonotic triggers in the differential evaluation of TMA, even in immunocompetent adults. While Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC) and thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) dominate the epidemiology of HUS and TMA respectively, rare pathogens like *C. canimorsus* can act as potent activators of the alternative complement pathway. The diagnostic algorithm for sepsis-associated TMA must therefore proceed systematically: first excluding TTP via ADAMTS13 activity assessment and STEC via stool PCR, then evaluating for secondary causes including malignancy, drugs, and specific infections. When these are ruled out and clinical features persist despite source control, CM-HUS becomes the working diagnosis. In such scenarios involving animal exposure, specifically bites or scratches from dogs or cats, clinicians should consider molecular diagnostics or prolonged culture techniques if standard workups are unrevealing early in the course. Recognizing these rare etiologies promptly facilitates targeted antimicrobial therapy while preparing the ground for timely complement inhibition should TMA evolve independently of the resolving sepsis.
