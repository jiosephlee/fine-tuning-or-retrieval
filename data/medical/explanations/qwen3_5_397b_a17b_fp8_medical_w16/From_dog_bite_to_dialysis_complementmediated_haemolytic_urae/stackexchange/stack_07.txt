Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Management] Initiating eculizumab creates a specific vulnerability to infection by encapsulated organisms. What is the recommended vaccination strategy regarding timing relative to the first dose, and what prophylactic measure is indicated if immediate treatment is required before vaccine immunity is established?

Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits the terminal complement component C5, abolishes the formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9), which is the host's primary defense mechanism against encapsulated bacteria, particularly *Neisseria meningitidis*. Consequently, initiating this therapy creates a profound and specific vulnerability to life-threatening meningococcal sepsis.

The standard of care mandates vaccination against both serogroups A, C, W, Y (e.g., Menveo) and serogroup B (e.g., Bexsero) ideally **at least two weeks prior** to the first dose of eculizumab. This interval is critical to allow sufficient time for the development of protective bactericidal titers before complement blockade is established.

However, in clinical scenarios where the threat of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) necessitates immediate treatment—precluding the two-week waiting period for vaccine efficacy—antibiotic prophylaxis must be instituted concurrently with or prior to the first infusion. As illustrated in the reported case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-triggered CM-HUS, the patient received her initial meningococcal vaccinations but required urgent eculizumab induction before immunity could be established. In this instance, **prophylactic penicillin (phenoxymethylpenicillin)** was administered to bridge the gap until vaccine-induced protection was achieved. While *N. meningitidis* is the paramount concern, clinicians must recognize that this vulnerability extends to other encapsulated organisms relevant to the patient's exposure history, such as *C. canimorsus* in cases involving animal bites. Therefore, if immediate complement inhibition is unavoidable, antibiotic prophylaxis is not optional; it is a mandatory safety measure to prevent overwhelming postsplenectomy-like sepsis during the window of immunologic susceptibility.
