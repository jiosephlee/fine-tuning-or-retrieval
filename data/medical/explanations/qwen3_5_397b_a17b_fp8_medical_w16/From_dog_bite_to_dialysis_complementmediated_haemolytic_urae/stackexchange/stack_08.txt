Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Pathophysiology] Given that up to 50% of patients with CM-HUS do not have identifiable pathogenic genetic variants in complement regulatory proteins, how does the concept of 'disease penetrance' and 'second hits' explain the pathogenesis in genetically undefined cases triggered by infection?

The apparent paradox of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) occurring in patients without identifiable pathogenic genetic variants is resolved by understanding the concepts of **incomplete disease penetrance** and the **"two-hit" hypothesis**. As illustrated in this case of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*-triggered CM-HUS, the absence of a detected mutation does not equate to the absence of genetic susceptibility.

Current data suggest that only 50% to 70% of patients with CM-HUS harbor identifiable variants in complement regulatory genes (such as factors H, I, or B). In the remaining "genetically undefined" cases, pathogenesis is driven by the convergence of subclinical genetic vulnerability and a potent environmental trigger. Many patients possess rare genetic variants of uncertain significance, polygenic risk scores, or mutations in non-coding regions that do not manifest as overt disease under homeostatic conditions. This state represents low-penetrance susceptibility where the complement system functions adequately until stressed.

The "second hit"—in this instance, severe *C. canimorsus* bacteremia—provides the necessary threshold to tip the balance toward uncontrolled alternative pathway activation. *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated organism that interacts dynamically with the host complement system; while complement activation is essential for bacterial clearance, certain strains exhibit partial resistance or induce excessive inflammation. In a genetically susceptible host, this infectious surge overwhelms regulatory mechanisms (even those that appear functionally normal at baseline), leading to widespread endothelial injury, thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and organ failure.

This case exemplifies the temporal dissociation characteristic of the two-hit model: the patient initially presented with sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (the first hit/environmental stress), but the classic features of TMA (haemolysis and thrombocytopenia) emerged only later, as the infection began to resolve. This delay underscores that the infection acted as the catalyst for a latent dysregulation rather than a direct toxin-mediated effect like Shiga toxin. Consequently, CM-HUS must be viewed as a clinicopathological diagnosis where a severe infectious trigger can unmask underlying complement dysregulation, regardless of whether standard genetic panels return a positive result.
