Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# The Pre-Op Cath Trap: Why HIT Can Strike Before Day 5 After Cardiac Surgery

We teach the "Rule of 5s" for Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) so often it becomes dogma: in a patient with no recent heparin exposure, the platelet count drop typically hits between postoperative day (POD) 5 and 10. If your cardiac surgery patient's plates are tanking on POD 3, you look everywhere else first—sepsis, bleeding, dilution, or maybe just the aftermath of cardiopulmonary bypass. But what if that timeline is a trap? What if a "minor" dose of heparin given a week ago during a diagnostic catheterization was actually the primer that turned a routine valve replacement into a perfect storm for catastrophic thrombosis?

A recent case report by St Bernard et al. forces us to rewrite the script on early post-cardiac surgery thrombocytopenia. They describe an elderly man who underwent uncomplicated aortic valve replacement and CABG. His course seemed standard until his platelets began a relentless march downward starting on POD 1, hitting 50k by POD 5, and eventually bottoming out at a terrifying 13k. By the time anyone seriously considered HIT, the patient had already suffered an acute parietal lobe stroke. The twist? He wasn't just exposed to heparin on the table; seven days prior, during a transradial cath, he received 2,000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH) while still therapeutic on apixaban. That small dose was enough to sensitize his immune system, converting what should have been a delayed reaction into an accelerated, fulminant autoimmune disaster.

This isn't just garden-variety HIT; this is **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)**. In classic HIT, antibodies require heparin to bridge platelet factor 4 (PF4) and activate platelets via Fc receptors. In aHIT, the antibodies go rogue—they can activate platelets *without* heparin present and are notoriously resistant to standard blocking mechanisms. Our patient's lab work told this story vividly. While his screening ELISA was strongly positive, the confirmatory Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) looked bizarre. His serum caused massive serotonin release even at zero heparin concentration (the hallmark of aHIT), but unlike typical cases, adding an Fc-receptor blocking antibody failed to shut it down completely. This partial resistance suggests an antibody profile of unusual virulence, explaining why his thrombocytopenia was so profound and why he developed consumptive coagulopathy with schistocytes despite being on therapeutic argatroban.

The clinical lesson here is stark: the clock for HIT starts ticking at the *first* exposure, not the biggest one. We often dismiss intraprocedural cath doses as negligible compared to the tens of thousands of units used during bypass. Immunologically, however, the T-cell help and B-cell priming happen regardless of whether it's 2,000 units or 45,000 units. When that sensitized patient returns for surgery days later, the massive intraoperative heparin load acts as the trigger for an explosive anamnestic response. The result is a platelet crash that violates our comfortable temporal windows and carries a disproportionately high risk of arterial thrombosis, as evidenced by this patient's stroke before POD 6.

Management in these scenarios requires aggressive escalation beyond simply switching anticoagulants. Because aHIT involves persistent platelet activation independent of circulating heparin levels, direct thrombin inhibitors alone may struggle to keep up with the hypercoagulable firestorm. This patient required high-dose IVIG (1 gram/kg for multiple days) alongside anticoagulation to dampen the immune drive, yet even then, his platelet recovery was sluggish and complicated by ongoing microangiopathic hemolysis until they switched to fondaparinux. It serves as a reminder that in suspected aHIT, especially with early onset and severe drops (<20k), we must consider combination therapy earlier rather than waiting for failure of monotherapy.

Don't let the "Day 5" rule blind you to the danger lurking in the pre-op chart. If your post-bypass patient has dropping plates earlier than expected, dig into their history for *any* heparin exposure in the last 100 days—even that tiny flush or cath dose. Recognizing the potential for accelerated autoimmune HIT could be the difference between preventing a stroke and managing one.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **The Timeline Trap:** Recent heparin exposure (even small doses during diagnostic catheterization within the past week) can accelerate HIT onset to **before POD 5**, bypassing the classic 5–10 day window.
*   **Suspect Autoimmune HIT (aHIT):** Profound thrombocytopenia (<20k), early onset, arterial thrombosis (like stroke), and persistent hypercoagulability despite adequate non-heparin anticoagulation should raise suspicion for aHIT rather than classic HIT.
*   **Atypical Lab Profiles:** Be wary of "indeterminate" Serotonin Release Assays (SRA). Strong platelet activation at **zero heparin** confirms aHIT; partial resistance to Fc-receptor blockade indicates an unusually severe antibody phenotype that may correlate with worse clinical outcomes.
*   **Aggressive Management:** Standard anticoagulant switching (e.g., to argatroban) may be insufficient for severe aHIT due to intense ongoing platelet activation; consider early addition of **high-dose IVIG** and monitor closely for consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer).
*   **History Matters:** Always audit pre-operative records for *any* parenteral heparin exposure in patients presenting with unexpected early post-operative thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery.



# Beyond Heparin-Dependence: Recognizing the Serological Signature of Autoimmune HIT

We tend to think of Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) as a binary switch: either the antibodies need heparin to activate platelets, or they don't exist. Classic teaching drills into us that the pathophysiology relies entirely on the formation of large immune complexes between Platelet Factor 4 (PF4) and heparin. Without the polyanion (heparin), there is no cross-linking of Fc receptors, no massive platelet activation, and no thrombotic storm. But what happens when the lab report comes back "indeterminate," yet your patient is throwing clots and their platelet count is plummeting into the single digits?

A fascinating case report by St Bernard et al. forces us to confront a darker, more aggressive variant of this disease: Autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In this scenario, an elderly man underwent cardiac surgery and developed profound thrombocytopenia far earlier than the textbook window, complicated by an acute parietal stroke. The real story, however, lies in the serology. His Serotonin Release Assay (SRA)—the gold standard functional test—didn't look like classic HIT. It looked like something else entirely: strong platelet activation occurring *without* any heparin present, and terrifyingly, partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade. This wasn't just a false positive; it was a serological signature of a hypercoagulable state that refuses to play by the usual rules.

The clinical timeline should have raised red flags immediately. While classic post-cardiac surgery HIT typically manifests between postoperative day (POD) 5 and 10, this patient's platelet count began a steady dive on POD1, hitting a nadir of 13×10⁹/L. Why so early? The culprit was likely a "priming" event: a preoperative cardiac catheterization one week prior where he received unfractionated heparin while still on apixaban. That small intra-procedural dose was enough to sensitize his immune system, so when he hit the operating room for his valve replacement and bypass, the fuse was already lit. By the time he developed delirium and suffered a cerebral infarction, he was deep in the throes of a consumptive coagulopathy that standard anticoagulation struggled to contain.

When the hematologists ran the numbers, the ELISA for PF4/heparin antibodies screamed positive with an optical density of 2.2. But the functional SRA told a confusing story. In classic HIT, you expect zero serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin and massive release at low doses (0.1–0.3 U/mL), with inhibition at high doses (100 U/mL). This patient had the high-dose inhibition, confirming HIT antibodies were present, but he also had 96% serotonin release at *zero* heparin concentration. This heparin-independent activation is the hallmark of aHIT. Even more disturbing was the response to IV.3, a monoclonal antibody that blocks the platelet Fc receptor. In typical HIT cases, adding IV.3 shuts down serotonin release almost completely. In this patient, it only partially inhibited the reaction. The antibodies were so potent and distinct that they bypassed standard inhibitory mechanisms, leading to an initial "indeterminate" label that could have easily led to diagnostic paralysis.

This case underscores a critical lesson for the frontline clinician: do not let an "indeterminate" functional assay dissuade you from treating suspected aHIT when the clinical picture screams hypercoagulability. The management here was aggressive for good reason. The team initiated argatroban and high-dose IVIG, but even then, markers of consumption coagulopathy persisted—fibrinogen dropped, D-dimers soared, and schistocytes appeared on smear—suggesting that direct thrombin inhibition alone wasn't taming the beast. They eventually switched to therapeutic fondaparinux (with antithrombin supplementation), which finally allowed the platelet count to recover over two months. The persistence of these rogue antibodies is notable; serial testing showed they lingered for months before eventually reverting to a more classic heparin-dependent profile as the patient recovered.

Autoimmune HIT represents a distinct clinical entity where antibodies act more like those in spontaneous HIT or lupus anticoagulant syndromes than traditional drug-induced reactions. They can activate platelets without exogenous heparin and drive severe thrombosis despite apparent anticoagulation coverage. When you see early-onset thrombocytopenia after recent heparin exposure, especially if accompanied by arterial thrombosis or refractory low platelets, think beyond classic HIT demand characteristics look at full serological nuance recognizing that sometimes strongest signal danger isn't clear-cut dependence it's autonomous fury autoimmunity gone wrong recognition prompt aggressive multimodal therapy including IVIG alternative anticoagulants potentially saving limb life otherwise lost perfect storm misdiagnosis inertia 

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) in Early Onset:** While classic post-cardiac surgery HIT occurs on POD 5–10, aHIT can present earlier (<POD 5) if there was recent preoperative heparin exposure ("priming").
*   **Decode the "Indeterminate" SRA:** Do not dismiss an indeterminate Serotonin Release Assay if clinical suspicion is high; look specifically for **heparin-independent platelet activation** (strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin), which defines aHIT.
*   **Recognize Atypical Resistance:** Be aware that aHIT antibodies may show **partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade** (e.g., incomplete inhibition with IV.3 monoclonal antibody), distinguishing them from classic HIT and explaining severe clinical courses.
*   **Aggressive Multimodal Therapy is Often Required:** Due to intense hypercoagulability and consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer), treatment often requires combining non-heparin anticoagulants (argatroban or fondaparinux) with high-dose IVIG to suppress antibody production/activation rapidly compared with classical cases



# When Argatroban Isn't Enough: Escalating Therapy in Refractory Autoimmune HIT

We tend to think of Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) as a binary problem: stop the heparin, start a non-heparin anticoagulant (like argatroban), and watch the platelets recover. But what happens when you do everything "right," and the patient keeps consuming clotting factors, dropping platelets, and throwing emboli? 

This isn't just classic HIT; this is **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)**, a distinct, terrifying beast where antibodies activate platelets even in the *absence* of heparin. A recent case report by St Bernard et al. lays bare the clinical nightmare of severe aHIT following cardiac surgery, illustrating a scenario where standard direct thrombin inhibition failed to arrest a runaway hypercoagulable storm. The takeaway? Sometimes argatroban alone isn't enough, and recognizing the subtle lab signs of treatment failure can be the difference between a stroke and a full recovery.

### The Setup: Early Onset and Atypical Labs

The patient was an elderly man undergoing complex cardiac surgery (AVR, ascending aorta replacement, CABG). While HIT typically hits between post-operative day (POD) 5 and 10, his platelet count began a relentless slide on POD1, bottoming out at a staggering 13 × 10⁹/L. Why so early? He had received unfractionated heparin during a heart catheterization one week prior. That priming dose was enough to sensitize his immune system, turning the massive intraoperative heparin load into the trigger for an explosive reaction.

The diagnostic workup was confusing at first glance. The screening ELISA was strongly positive, but the gold-standard Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) came back "indeterminate." Here lies the hallmark of aHIT: the patient's serum caused massive platelet activation *without* any heparin present (96% release at 0 U/mL). Even more bizarrely, adding an Fc-receptor blocking antibody—which usually shuts down classic HIT reactions—only partially inhibited the activation. This wasn't just resistant HIT; it was a novel serological phenotype with heparin-independent activating properties driving profound thrombocytopenia and, inevitably, thrombosis. By POD6, despite stopping all heparin products, the patient suffered an acute parietal lobe stroke.

### The Trap: Recognizing Treatment Failure on Argatroban

Once diagnosed with aHIT, the team did exactly what the textbooks say: they started therapeutic argatroban and hit hard with high-dose IVIG (100g daily) to dampen the antibody storm. You'd expect the bleeding risk to dominate with platelets that low, but in aHIT, the thrombotic drive is often stronger than the hemorrhagic risk. 

Despite aggressive therapy, the patient didn't stabilize immediately. In fact, between POD6 and POD16, while on argatroban, he developed evidence of ongoing disseminated intravascular consumption. This is the critical teaching point for anyone managing refractory HIT: **don't just watch the platelet count.** You have to look at the global coagulation picture. 

In this case, three specific markers signaled that argatroban was failing to control the hypercoagulability:
*   **Falling Fibrinogen:** Levels dropped from an acute-phase high of 6.59 g/L down to 2.49 g/L, suggesting massive consumption rather than just surgical loss.
*   **Rising D-Dimers:** Peaks around POD12 indicated continued fibrin formation and breakdown despite therapeutic anticoagulation.
*   **New Schistocytes:** The peripheral smear suddenly showed red cell fragments days after surgery, confirming microangiopathic hemolysis driven by unchecked thrombus propagation.

The platelets only crept up modestly (from 13 to 54 × 10⁹/L), far less than the brisk recovery usually seen with IVIG in aHIT. The mechanism of injury hadn't been stopped; the direct thrombin inhibitor was putting out some fires, but the factory was still burning.

### The Pivot: Switching Mechanisms and Supplementing Antithrombin

Faced with breakthrough microvascular thrombosis on argatroban, the team made a bold pivot. They switched from the direct thrombin inhibitor to high-dose fondaparinux (10 mg SC daily). This is off-label territory for HIT, but fondaparinux offers potent Factor Xa inhibition via antithrombin. 

There was a catch: fondaparinux requires antithrombin (AT) to work. In states of severe consumption coagulopathy like this patient's AT levels can drop subtherapeutically (his was 0.70 U/mL). Giving fondaparinux without sufficient substrate would be futile. The team correctly identified this gap and administered supplemental antithrombin concentrate (1000 U) prior to initiating fondaparinux. 

The result? The downward spiral halted. Fibrinogen stabilized, D-dimers trended down, schistocytes disappeared, and the platelet count eventually normalized by POD62. The neurological deficits resolved completely. Over time, repeat SRA testing showed his antibodies evolving from that dangerous heparin-independent profile back to a more classic heparin-dependent pattern before finally waning entirely—a serological journey mirroring his clinical recovery.

### Take-Home Messages

*   **Suspect Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) in "Early" or Severe Cases:** If thrombocytopenia starts before POD5 after cardiac surgery or drops below 20 × 10⁹/L with arterial thrombosis, think aHIT regardless of whether heparin exposure seems remote or minimal pre-operatively look for prior exposures like catheterization).
*   **Don't Trust "Indeterminate" Functional Assays Blindly:** An SRA showing strong platelet activation at zero heparin concentration is pathognomonic for aHIT Partial resistance to Fc-receptor blockade indicates an unusually aggressive antibody profile that correlates with severe clinical outcomes
*   **Monitor Beyond Platelets for Treatment Failure:** In refractory cases on DTIs (argatroban/bivalirudin), track fibrinogen trends D-dimer trajectories and peripheral smears Falling fibrinogen and new schistocytes signal ongoing consumptive coagulopathy despite therapeutic anticoagulation levels
*   **Consider Mechanism Switching + Substrate Replacement:** If hypercoagulability persists on direct thrombin inhibitors switching to high-dose fondaparinux may provide better control Ensure antithrombin levels are adequate (>08 U/mL); supplement with AT concentrate if necessary to enable drug efficacy



# Stroke in the Setting of Thrombocytopenia: Navigating the Anticoagulation Paradox

Imagine this: You're managing a post-op cardiac surgery patient. The platelets are crashing, hitting a nadir of 13,000/µL. Your instinct screams "bleeding risk." Then, the CT head comes back: acute parietal infarct. Now you have a terrified team asking if we should hold anticoagulation because the platelets are dangerously low. In almost every other hematologic crisis—ITP, TTP, drug-induced thrombocytopenia—the answer might be hesitation or steroids. But in **Autoimmune Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (aHIT)**, that hesitation can be fatal.

This isn't just theoretical. A recent case report by St Bernard et al. describes exactly this nightmare scenario: an elderly man, days out from valve replacement and aortic repair, who developed a massive ischemic stroke while his platelet count plummeted to levels that usually contraindicate full-dose anticoagulation. The twist? This wasn't classic HIT. It was the rarer, more aggressive cousin: autoimmune HIT. And it teaches us a brutal lesson about the "clotting paradox" where the lower the platelets go, the higher the thrombotic storm rages.

### The Timeline That Didn't Fit (At First Glance)

Classic teaching tells us HIT happens between post-operative day (POD) 5 and 10. If platelets drop earlier, we usually blame surgical consumption, hemodilution, or sepsis. Our patient's platelets started diving on POD1 and hit 50k by POD5. On paper, it looked too early for HIT triggered by the intraoperative heparin during cardiopulmonary bypass.

But here is the pearl: **Look backward, not just forward.** This patient had a cardiac catheterization one week prior involving 2,000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH). That small exposure was enough to prime the immune system. When he got the massive 45,000-unit bolus during surgery, it wasn't the start of the reaction; it was the booster shot that ignited the fire immediately post-op. Early onset thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery shouldn't rule out HIT if there is *any* antecedent heparin exposure in the previous 100 days.

### The Lab Trap: When "Indeterminate" Means "Dangerous"

The diagnostic journey here highlights why we cannot rely solely on screening tests or standard interpretive criteria for atypical cases. The PF4/heparin ELISA was strongly positive (OD > 2.2), which has high sensitivity but poor specificity. The confirmatory Serotonin Release Assay (SRA), however, came back labeled as "indeterminate." Why? Because the antibodies were behaving badly.

In classic HIT, antibodies need heparin to bridge Platelet Factor 4 (PF4) and activate platelets. Add too much heparin, and the reaction stops (the hallmark high-dose inhibition). In **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)**, the antibodies are monstrous—they can activate platelets *without* any heparin present at all. This patient's serum caused 96% serotonin release at zero heparin concentration. Even scarier, these antibodies were so potent they showed **partial resistance to Fc-receptor blockade**. Usually, blocking the Fc receptor shuts down platelet activation in HIT assays; here, it only partially dampened the fire.

If your lab calls an SRA "indeterminate" in a crashing patient with a new clot, do not breathe easy. In the context of aHIT, "indeterminate" often means "unusually aggressive."

### The Anticoagulation Paradox: To Treat or Not to Treat?

This is the crux of the clinical dilemma. We had a patient with active intracranial ischemia and a platelet count of 13k/µL. Standard dogma suggests holding anticoagulants when plates are <20k–30k due to hemorrhagic conversion risk. But aHIT flips the script entirely.

In aHIT, the mechanism isn't just platelet destruction; it's runaway thrombin generation driven by massive immune complex formation and monocyte tissue factor expression. The severe thrombocytopenia is actually a sign of *consumption* due to widespread microvascular thrombosis, not marrow failure or peripheral destruction aloneWithholding anticoagulation doesn't stop the bleeding risk; it guarantees propagating macro-thrombosis (like this patient's stroke) and limb-loss micro-thrombosis.*

The team made the bold but correct call: **Full therapeutic anticoagulation despite profound thrombocytopenia.** They initiated argatroban (chosen for its short half-life given the recent stroke) alongside high-dose IVIG to blunt the antibody production. Did it work instantly? No. The patient continued to consume fibrinogen and generate D-dimers for over a week, proving that even with direct thrombin inhibition, this hypercoagulable state is incredibly stubborn eventually requiring a switch to therapeutic fondaparinux once antithrombin levels were repleted.) The key takeaway is that in suspected aHIT with active thrombosis, **platelet count should not dictate withholding anticoagulation.** The risk of catastrophic clot extension vastly outweighs the risk of hemorrhagic transformation in this specific pathology.

### Take-Home Points

*   **Respect Pre-operative Exposure:** Do not dismiss early-onset thrombocytopenia (<POD5) after cardiac surgery if there was *any* heparin exposure in the preceding weeks (e.g., catheterization, flushes). This "priming" event accelerates typical HIT timelines into autoimmune territory rapidly upon re-exposure during CPB
*   **"Indeterminate" Labs Can Be Deadly:** An SRA showing strong platelet activation at *zero* heparin concentration is pathognomonic for autoimmune HIT (aHIT), even if Fc-blockade studies look weird or incomplete Never let an "indeterminate" label dissuade you from treating if clinical suspicion remains high
*   **Thrombocytopenia ≠ Bleeding Risk in HIT:** In classic ITP or DIC with bleeding diathesis low plates mean bleed caution; In severe/aHIT extremely low plates signify intense consumption due to systemic clotting Withholding anticoagulation in active thrombotic events leads to devastating outcomes regardless of how low counts drop
*   **Escalation May Be Required:** Monotherapy may fail initially due to overwhelming antigen load Combination strategies including non-heparin parenteral agents plus adjunctive therapies like IVIG may be necessary achieve control though response delayed compared classical presentations



# The Consumptive Coagulopathy Mimic: Differentiating Severe aHIT from TTP and DIC

We often teach that Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) is a paradoxical pro-thrombotic state characterized by a drop in platelets, usually hovering above 20,000/µL. We tell residents that if the platelets crash below 20k, they should start looking for sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), or perhaps Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP). But what happens when HIT breaks its own rules? What happens when it presents with profound thrombocytopenia, schistocytes on the smear, and a new stroke, mimicking the very conditions we use to rule it out?

A recent case report by St Bernard et al. forces us to reconsider the boundaries of "classic" HIT. They describe an elderly man who underwent cardiac surgery and subsequently developed a precipitous drop in platelets, bottoming out at a terrifying 13×10⁹/L. To make matters more confusing, his peripheral smear eventually showed schistocytes, his fibrinogen plummeted, and his D-dimer skyrocketed. Clinically, this looked like a consumptive coagulopathy nightmare—either fulminant DIC or TTP. Yet, the culprit was neither. It was Autoimmune HIT (aHIT), a rare and particularly vicious subtype of HIT driven by antibodies that activate platelets even in the *absence* of heparin. This case serves as a critical reminder that severe aHIT can wear the mask of TTP and DIC, and misdiagnosing it leads to ineffective treatments like plasma exchange while the patient continues to clot.

The timeline in this case offers the first major clue that separates this entity from post-operative DIC or delayed TTP. Classic HIT typically manifests between post-operative days (POD) 5 and 10. Our patient's platelet count began its steady decline on POD1, hitting 50k by POD5. At first glance, this seems too early for intraoperative heparin to be the trigger. However, a deep dive into the history revealed a transradial cardiac catheterization one week prior where he received unfractionated heparin (UFH). This pre-exposure primed his immune system, creating a "rapid-onset" scenario upon re-exposure during bypass. While rapid-onset HIT is known, the severity here was unprecedented. By POD6, the patient had suffered a parietal lobe stroke despite prophylactic anticoagulation changes, signaling an antibody-mediated storm that standard algorithms might miss.

Differentiating this from TTP requires a keen eye for hemolysis markers and coagulation profiles. When the team initially evaluated the patient for the stroke and dropping counts, TTP was high on the differential. However, unlike TTP where massive microangiopathic hemolysis is the hallmark, this patient's haptoglobin was actually elevated (an acute phase reactant), and there were no red cell fragments on the initial films. Schistocytes only appeared later (POD9–16) as part of severe secondary consumption rather than primary shearing. Furthermore, while DIC typically presents with deranged PT/PTT and low fibrinogen early on due to systemic activation of the coagulation cascade, this patient maintained normal PT/PTT values until late in the course when fibrinogen finally dropped due to overwhelming consumption. The preservation of these parameters early on, combined with the specific timing relative to heparin exposure, should pivot your suspicion toward aHIT rather than primary DIC or TTP.

The laboratory confirmation in this case reveals why aHIT is so dangerous and difficult to diagnose. Standard screening showed a strongly positive PF4/heparin ELISA, but the functional Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) gave an "indeterminate" result that could have led to dismissal of the diagnosis. In classic HIT, platelet activation requires heparin; add too much heparin, and activation stops. In this patient's serum, however, platelets released serotonin even at zero heparin concentration—a signature of autoimmune HIT. Even more alarming was the finding that adding an Fc-receptor blocking antibody failed to fully inhibit this activation. This suggests an antibody profile with such high avidity and potency that it partially resists standard blockade mechanisms. This isn't just "some" HIT; it is a hyper-aggressive variant that drives continuous platelet destruction and thrombin generation regardless of whether exogenous heparin is present in the tube or the patient's veins.

Management of severe aHIT diverges sharply from TTP and DIC protocols. If you mistake this for TTP and initiate plasma exchange, you are removing antibodies temporarily but failing to stop their production or block their mechanism effectively without targeted therapy. If you treat it as simple DIC with cryoprecipitate or FFP without potent anticoagulation, you are pouring fuel on the fire of thrombin generation. This patient required a dual-pronged attack: switching immediately to a non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban initially) AND administering high-dose IVIG to blunt the immune response.
