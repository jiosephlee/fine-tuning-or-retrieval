Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Challenges in Early Postoperative Thrombocytopenia

In the immediate aftermath of cardiac surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), a decline in platelet count is an anticipated physiological event rather than an automatic alarm for pathology. Typically, platelet numbers drop by 30% to 50% within the first 24 hours due to hemodilution, mechanical shear stress from the bypass circuit, and consumption at sites of surgical trauma. In a standard uncomplicated recovery, this nadir occurs on postoperative day 1 (POD1) or POD2, followed by a gradual rebound beginning around POD3 or POD4 as marrow production compensates for losses. In this case, the patient's initial trajectory appeared consistent with this norm; his platelet count fell from a baseline of 210×10⁹/L to 155×10⁹/L on POD1. However, the critical diagnostic pivot point arose when the count failed to recover and instead continued a steep, progressive descent to 50×10⁹/L by POD5 and eventually to a nadir of 13×10⁹/L. Recognizing the distinction between the expected early dip and a pathological, sustained decline is the primary challenge in post-cardiac surgery monitoring. A failure of the platelet count to begin rising by POD4, or a drop exceeding 50% from baseline after the initial post-bypass period, must trigger an investigation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), even if the timing seems premature.

The temporal onset of HIT is traditionally categorized as "typical" when it occurs between POD5 and POD10 in patients who have not been recently exposed to heparin. This delay represents the time required for the immune system to generate sufficient quantities of IgG antibodies against the platelet factor 4 (PF4)-heparin complex. Consequently, an onset before POD5 is often dismissed as unlikely to be HIT, leading to dangerous diagnostic delays. This case illustrates the phenomenon of "rapid-onset" or "accelerated" HIT, driven by pre-existing sensitization. Although the patient received a massive intraoperative dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH) during CPB, the true inciting event was a cardiac catheterization performed one week prior. During that procedure, despite a reduced dose of UFH (2,000 U) administered via a radial approach while the patient was on apixaban, enough antigenic stimulus occurred to prime his immune system. Because memory B-cells were already present from this recent exposure, the secondary immune response upon re-exposure during surgery was explosive, causing antibody titers to reach pathogenic levels almost immediately post-operatively. Clinicians must therefore meticulously review medication histories for any heparin exposure—including flushes, line locks, or procedural anticoagulation—within the preceding 100 days when evaluating early postoperative thrombocytopenia.

A particularly treacherous aspect of this presentation was the dissociation between the severity of thrombocytopenia and the clinical manifestation of bleeding versus thrombosis. Intuitively, a platelet count plummeting to 26×10⁹/L suggests an imminent risk of spontaneous hemorrhage. However, HIT is fundamentally a pro-thrombotic disorder mediated by potent platelet activation rather than simple platelet destruction. The clinical clue that shifted the diagnostic suspicion toward thrombosis in this patient was the worsening of postoperative delirium in the absence of fever or hemodynamic instability. While delirium is common after cardiac surgery due to microemboli or metabolic shifts, its progression alongside severe thrombocytopenia warranted neuroimaging. The subsequent CT scan revealing an acute parietal lobe infarction confirmed that the falling platelet count was accompanied by a hypercoagulable state resulting in arterial occlusion. This paradox—severe thrombocytopenia concurrent with new thrombotic events—is the hallmark of HIT and distinguishes it from other causes of low platelets where bleeding is the predominant concern.

Establishing the diagnosis required a rigorous exclusion of alternative etiologies that commonly plague post-cardiac surgery patients. Sepsis was considered unlikely given the patient's stable hemodynamics, normal white blood cell differential, and negative blood and urine cultures. Transfusion-related causes were ruled out; post-transfusion purpura was impossible as no blood products were administered, and thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) was excluded by the absence of schistocytes on the initial peripheral smear and normal haptoglobin levels initially suggesting no significant hemolysis. Furthermore, drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia from antibiotics (cefazolin, vancomycin) or other perioperative medications was improbable because these agents were introduced on or after the day of surgery; typical drug-induced mechanisms require at least 5 to 7 days for de novo antibody formation unless there is prior sensitization, which was not documented for these specific drugs. With infectious, transfusion-related, and alternative drug-induced causes effectively eliminated, and given the strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA result combined with the history of recent heparin exposure, HIT emerged as the only plausible diagnosis despite its atypically early onset. This case underscores that in complex surgical patients, "atypical" timing should not preclude testing if pre-operative sensitization is identified and other causes are excluded.

## Pathophysiology and Serological Phenotyping of Autoimmune HIT

To understand the severity and atypical presentation of this patient's condition, one must first distinguish the immunological mechanisms of classic heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) from those of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In classic HIT, the pathogenic process is strictly heparin-dependent. Unfractionated heparin binds to platelet factor 4 (PF4), a cationic protein released by platelets, forming large ultralarge complexes. In susceptible individuals, IgG antibodies recognize neo-epitopes on these PF4/heparin complexes. These immune complexes cross-link Fc-gamma receptors (specifically Fc$\gamma$RIIa) on the platelet surface, triggering massive platelet activation, aggregation, and the release of procoagulant microparticles. Crucially, in classic HIT, this activation ceases if heparin is removed or present in supratherapeutic concentrations (which disrupts the lattice structure of the complexes), and it is reliably inhibited by monoclonal antibodies that block the Fc receptor.

Autoimmune HIT represents a distinct and more aggressive subset of this disorder characterized by antibodies that possess heparin-independent platelet-activating properties. Unlike classic HIT antibodies, which require the presence of exogenous heparin to bridge PF4 and initiate clustering, aHIT antibodies can activate platelets even in the absence of heparin. This fundamental difference explains the clinical phenotype observed in this case: an earlier onset of thrombocytopenia (triggered by preoperative catheterization heparin) and a profoundly lower platelet nadir (13×10⁹/L). The ability of these antibodies to activate platelets without continuous heparin exposure leads to sustained, unchecked thrombin generation and consumptive coagulopathy, manifesting here as falling fibrinogen levels, rising D-dimers, and microangiopathic hemolysis (schistocytes), complications rarely seen in uncomplicated classic HIT.

The diagnostic challenge in this case centered on interpreting the Serotonin Release Assay (SRA), the functional gold standard for HIT diagnosis. Typically, a positive SRA shows significant serotonin release (>20%) at low-to-moderate heparin concentrations (0.1–0.3 U/mL) and suppression (<20%) at high concentrations (100 U/mL). This patient's serum displayed a unique "indeterminate" profile: while it showed the characteristic high-concentration inhibition confirming anti-PF4 specificity, it also demonstrated vigorous serotonin release (96%) at zero heparin concentration. This heparin-independent activation is the serological hallmark of aHIT. However, the profile was further complicated by an unexpected resistance to Fc-receptor blockade. When the laboratory added IV.3, a monoclonal antibody designed to block Fc$\gamma$RIIa clustering, serotonin release was only partially inhibited (dropping from 97% to 76% at 0.1 U/mL heparin) rather than fully suppressed to baseline levels.

This partial resistance to Fc-receptor blockade suggests an unusually potent antibody interaction that may involve alternative pathways of platelet activation or an affinity so high that standard blocking concentrations are insufficient. In typical HIT or even many cases of aHIT, IV.3 effectively abolishes serotonin release by preventing the immune complex from engaging the platelet receptor. The failure of complete inhibition in this patient correlates directly with his refractory clinical course; despite switching to a direct thrombin inhibitor (argatroban) and administering high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)—a therapy intended to saturate Fc receptors and halt antibody-mediated destruction—the patient experienced only a modest rise in platelet counts and persistent markers of hypercoagulability. The serological severity predicted the clinical resistance: because the antibodies could activate platelets independent of heparin and partially bypass standard Fc-receptor blockade mechanisms, the thrombotic drive remained active longer than anticipated, necessitating a prolonged recovery period and a switch to fondaparinux before stabilization occurred. Ultimately, tracking these serological shifts over time confirmed the diagnosis; as the patient recovered months later, repeat testing showed a reversion to a classic heparin-dependent profile with restored sensitivity to Fc-blockade, illustrating the transient yet devastating nature of the autoimmune phenotype.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Laboratory Interpretation Strategy

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates the critical necessity of integrating clinical probability scores with nuanced laboratory interpretation, particularly when standard algorithms yield conflicting results. In the post-cardiac surgery setting, distinguishing heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) from other causes of platelet decline is challenging due to the high baseline frequency of thrombocytopenia. While the "4Ts" scoring system is a standard bedside tool, its application here required careful contextualization. Typically, a platelet count fall beginning before postoperative day 5 (POD5) lowers the pre-test probability for classic HIT, as the immune response usually requires 5 to 10 days to develop after initial heparin exposure. However, in this patient, the timeline was compressed by antecedent heparin exposure during a heart catheterization one week prior to surgery. Recognizing this "booster" effect allowed the clinician to maintain a high index of suspicion despite the early onset (POD1–POD5), correctly identifying the rapid platelet decline and new thrombotic event (parietal stroke) as highly suggestive of HIT rather than dismissing them as routine post-operative phenomena.

The laboratory investigation presented a paradox that demanded advanced interpretive skills: a strongly positive screening immunoassay coupled with an initially "indeterminate" confirmatory functional assay. The PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) returned an optical density of 2.2, well above the threshold for a strong-positive result, which generally correlates with a high likelihood of HIT. However, the gold-standard serotonin-release assay (SRA) produced an atypical profile. Classic HIT antibodies are strictly heparin-dependent; they activate platelets only at therapeutic heparin concentrations (0.1–0.3 U/mL) and are inhibited at supratherapeutic levels (>100 U/mL). In contrast, this patient's serum induced massive serotonin release (96%) even in the complete absence of heparin (0 U/mL). This heparin-independent activation is the serological hallmark of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Furthermore, the addition of an Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3), which typically abolishes platelet activation in classic HIT by preventing immune complex cross-linking, resulted in only partial inhibition (76% release remaining). Without understanding the pathophysiology of aHIT—where antibodies possess intrinsic activating properties independent of heparin binding and may exhibit resistance to Fc blockade—one might erroneously reject the diagnosis based on this "indeterminate" functional result.

Resolving this diagnostic ambiguity required direct communication with the reference laboratory to obtain raw data rather than relying solely on binary positive/negative reports. The clinicians' inquiry revealed the specific nuances of the SRA curve: strong activation at zero heparin, sustained activation at low doses, and appropriate inhibition at very high doses. This pattern confirmed that while the antibodies behaved atypically regarding Fc receptor dependence, they still retained the fundamental characteristic of being anti-PF4/heparin antibodies that dissociate at high antigen excess. This step underscores a vital principle in complex hematology: when clinical suspicion remains high despite equivocal confirmatory testing, reviewing the primary data with laboratory experts can uncover distinct phenotypes like aHIT that automated interpretations might miss or flag as errors.

Before confirming aHIT, it was imperative to rigorously exclude other etiologies of severe thrombocytopenia and microangiopathy that mimic HIT clinically. The differential diagnosis included thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and post-transfusion purpura (PTP). PTP was ruled out immediately as the patient had received no blood transfusions. TTP was considered given the thrombocytopenia and neurological changes, but the peripheral blood smear lacked significant schistocytes initially, and haptoglobin levels were elevated rather than suppressed, arguing against active hemolysis. Similarly, while fibrinogen eventually dropped and D-dimers rose later in the course suggesting consumptive coagulopathy, initial coagulation parameters (normal PT/INR and PTT) and stable hemoglobin made overt DIC unlikely at presentation. The absence of red cell fragments on early smears and normal renal function helped isolate the pathology to an antibody-mediated process rather than a primary microangiopathic disorder or sepsis-induced marrow suppression.

Definitive confirmation of aHIT in this case relied on observing the longitudinal evolution of the serological profile alongside clinical recovery. Autoimmune HIT is characterized by persistent circulating antibodies that often wane slowly over weeks to months. In this patient, serial SRA testing demonstrated a shift in antibody behavior over time. Early samples showed dominant heparin-independent activity consistent with severe aHIT. As the patient's platelet count recovered and hypercoagulability resolved under aggressive immunomodulation and anticoagulation, follow-up testing at POD124 revealed a transition back to a classic heparin-dependent profile: minimal activation at 0 U/mL heparin but robust activation at 0.3 U/mL. This serological migration—from a severe, heparin-independent phenotype to a standard heparin-dependent one—provided retrospective validation of the diagnosis. It highlighted that aHIT exists on a spectrum and that tracking antibody kinetics can serve as both a diagnostic anchor and a marker for disease resolution when initial functional assays present unconventional patterns.

## Management Algorithms for Severe and Refractory aHIT

The management of autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) demands a more aggressive and nuanced approach than classic HIT due to the presence of heparin-independent, platelet-activating antibodies that drive severe thrombocytopenia and profound hypercoagulability. The cornerstone of therapy is the immediate and absolute cessation of all heparin sources. In the context of post-cardiac surgery care, this requires vigilance not only against therapeutic unfractionated heparin (UFH) or low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) but also against trace exposures found in line flushes and catheter locks. As illustrated in the case report, the continuation of dalteparin prophylaxis in the face of declining platelet counts can exacerbate the immune response; thus, upon suspicion of aHIT, all heparin must be discontinued instantly to remove the antigenic trigger, even though aHIT antibodies may retain activity in the absence of exogenous heparin.

Selection of an alternative parenteral anticoagulant is critical, particularly when complicated by acute thrombosis such as the parietal stroke seen in this patient. Direct thrombin inhibitors like argatroban are often the initial choice in patients with recent major surgery or active bleeding risks due to their short half-life and titratability via continuous infusion. In this case, argatroban was initiated to allow for rapid reversal if neurological deterioration or hemorrhagic transformation occurred. However, a unique challenge in aHIT is "refractory" hypercoagulability, where platelet activation persists despite adequate anticoagulation with a direct thrombin inhibitor. Evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy—manifested here by falling fibrinogen levels, rising D-dimer values, and the emergence of schistocytes on peripheral smear while on argatroban—signals treatment failure. In such scenarios, switching to fondaparinux, a synthetic pentasaccharide factor Xa inhibitor, offers a potent alternative. Unlike argatroban, fondaparinux provides sustained subcutaneous coverage, but its efficacy is strictly dependent on antithrombin (AT) levels. Since cardiac surgery and consumptive coagulopathy can deplete AT, as noted in this patient (0.70 U/mL), supplementation with AT concentrate is mandatory prior to initiating fondaparinux to ensure therapeutic effect.

Adjunctive immunomodulation with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is a distinguishing feature of aHIT management compared to classic HIT. Because aHIT antibodies activate platelets via Fc receptors independently of heparin, IVIG serves to block these receptors and neutralize the pathogenic IgG. The standard regimen involves 1 g/kg daily for two days; however, clinicians must recognize that the response in severe aHIT may be delayed or blunted. In this case, the patient required a third dose of IVIG before any significant platelet recovery was observed, highlighting that a lack of immediate response should not prompt discontinuation but rather persistence with therapy alongside optimized anticoagulation. Furthermore, monitoring treatment efficacy in aHIT extends beyond simple platelet count trends. Given the intense thrombin generation inherent to the syndrome, serial measurement of fibrinogen and D-dimer provides essential insight into the resolution of the hypercoagulable state. A normalization of fibrinogen and a downward trend in D-dimer, coupled with the disappearance of schistocytes on blood films, serve as vital biomarkers confirming that the chosen anticoagulant strategy is successfully arresting the microangiopathic process, even before platelet counts fully normalize.

## Clinical Course, Outcomes, and Long-Term Implications

The clinical trajectory of this patient illustrates the severe and protracted nature of autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT), distinguishing it from classic HIT through its resistance to standard interventions and persistent hypercoagulable state. Despite the initiation of therapeutic argatroban and high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), the patient experienced a delayed and incomplete hematologic response. While IVIG typically induces a rapid rise in platelet counts in aHIT by saturating Fc receptors and blocking antibody-mediated platelet activation, this patient's platelet count only modestly increased from a nadir of 13×10⁹/L to 54×10⁹/L over one week. Concurrently, laboratory markers indicated ongoing consumptive coagulopathy, characterized by falling fibrinogen levels, rising D-dimer concentrations, and the transient appearance of schistocytes on peripheral smear. These findings suggested that the initial anticoagulant strategy with argatroban was insufficient to fully suppress the intense thrombin generation driven by the highly reactive, heparin-independent antibodies.

Recognition of this refractory hypercoagulability prompted a strategic shift in management on postoperative day 14 from argatroban to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux. This decision was guided by the need for more potent suppression of factor Xa and the practical advantages of subcutaneous administration, though it required careful physiological optimization; because fondaparinux is an indirect antithrombin-dependent inhibitor, supplemental antithrombin concentrate was administered to correct the patient's mild deficiency prior to initiation. This adjustment proved pivotal. Following the switch, the markers of disseminated intravascular coagulation resolved, and the platelet count began a steady recovery, eventually normalizing to 155×10⁹/L by postoperative day 62. Neurologically, the patient made a complete recovery from the acute parietal stroke, underscoring that while aHIT carries a high risk of catastrophic thrombosis, aggressive and tailored anticoagulation can yield favorable functional outcomes even after significant embolic events.

Long-term management and follow-up provided critical insights into the immunobiology of aHIT. Serological monitoring via the serotonin-release assay (SRA) revealed that the pathogenic antibodies persisted for months, maintaining strong platelet-activating properties long after clinical stabilization. However, the phenotype of these antibodies evolved over time; samples taken four months post-onset demonstrated a reversion to a classic heparin-dependent profile, with loss of the ability to activate platelets in the absence of heparin. This transition confirms that the "autoimmune" variant represents a transient, albeit severe, amplification of the immune response rather than a permanent alteration in antibody specificity. Consequently, long-term anticoagulation planning must account for this prolonged risk window. In this case, fondaparinux was continued until day 65 before transitioning back to oral apixaban. Clinicians must recognize that discontinuation of parenteral anticoagulation prematurely, based solely on platelet count recovery without regard for persistent serological activity or residual hypercoagulability risks, could precipitate recurrent thrombosis in patients with severe aHIT.
