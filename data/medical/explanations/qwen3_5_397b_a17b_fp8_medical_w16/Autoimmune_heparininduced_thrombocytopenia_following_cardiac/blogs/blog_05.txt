Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# The Consumptive Coagulopathy Mimic: Differentiating Severe aHIT from TTP and DIC

We often teach that Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) is a paradoxical pro-thrombotic state characterized by a drop in platelets, usually hovering above 20,000/µL. We tell residents that if the platelets crash below 20k, they should start looking for sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), or perhaps Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP). But what happens when HIT breaks its own rules? What happens when it presents with profound thrombocytopenia, schistocytes on the smear, and a new stroke, mimicking the very conditions we use to rule it out?

A recent case report by St Bernard et al. forces us to reconsider the boundaries of "classic" HIT. They describe an elderly man who underwent cardiac surgery and subsequently developed a precipitous drop in platelets, bottoming out at a terrifying 13×10⁹/L. To make matters more confusing, his peripheral smear eventually showed schistocytes, his fibrinogen plummeted, and his D-dimer skyrocketed. Clinically, this looked like a consumptive coagulopathy nightmare—either fulminant DIC or TTP. Yet, the culprit was neither. It was Autoimmune HIT (aHIT), a rare and particularly vicious subtype of HIT driven by antibodies that activate platelets even in the *absence* of heparin. This case serves as a critical reminder that severe aHIT can wear the mask of TTP and DIC, and misdiagnosing it leads to ineffective treatments like plasma exchange while the patient continues to clot.

The timeline in this case offers the first major clue that separates this entity from post-operative DIC or delayed TTP. Classic HIT typically manifests between post-operative days (POD) 5 and 10. Our patient's platelet count began its steady decline on POD1, hitting 50k by POD5. At first glance, this seems too early for intraoperative heparin to be the trigger. However, a deep dive into the history revealed a transradial cardiac catheterization one week prior where he received unfractionated heparin (UFH). This pre-exposure primed his immune system, creating a "rapid-onset" scenario upon re-exposure during bypass. While rapid-onset HIT is known, the severity here was unprecedented. By POD6, the patient had suffered a parietal lobe stroke despite prophylactic anticoagulation changes, signaling an antibody-mediated storm that standard algorithms might miss.

Differentiating this from TTP requires a keen eye for hemolysis markers and coagulation profiles. When the team initially evaluated the patient for the stroke and dropping counts, TTP was high on the differential. However, unlike TTP where massive microangiopathic hemolysis is the hallmark, this patient's haptoglobin was actually elevated (an acute phase reactant), and there were no red cell fragments on the initial films. Schistocytes only appeared later (POD9–16) as part of severe secondary consumption rather than primary shearing. Furthermore, while DIC typically presents with deranged PT/PTT and low fibrinogen early on due to systemic activation of the coagulation cascade, this patient maintained normal PT/PTT values until late in the course when fibrinogen finally dropped due to overwhelming consumption. The preservation of these parameters early on, combined with the specific timing relative to heparin exposure, should pivot your suspicion toward aHIT rather than primary DIC or TTP.

The laboratory confirmation in this case reveals why aHIT is so dangerous and difficult to diagnose. Standard screening showed a strongly positive PF4/heparin ELISA, but the functional Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) gave an "indeterminate" result that could have led to dismissal of the diagnosis. In classic HIT, platelet activation requires heparin; add too much heparin, and activation stops. In this patient's serum, however, platelets released serotonin even at zero heparin concentration—a signature of autoimmune HIT. Even more alarming was the finding that adding an Fc-receptor blocking antibody failed to fully inhibit this activation. This suggests an antibody profile with such high avidity and potency that it partially resists standard blockade mechanisms. This isn't just "some" HIT; it is a hyper-aggressive variant that drives continuous platelet destruction and thrombin generation regardless of whether exogenous heparin is present in the tube or the patient's veins.

Management of severe aHIT diverges sharply from TTP and DIC protocols. If you mistake this for TTP and initiate plasma exchange, you are removing antibodies temporarily but failing to stop their production or block their mechanism effectively without targeted therapy. If you treat it as simple DIC with cryoprecipitate or FFP without potent anticoagulation, you are pouring fuel on the fire of thrombin generation. This patient required a dual-pronged attack: switching immediately to a non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban initially) AND administering high-dose IVIG to blunt the immune response.
