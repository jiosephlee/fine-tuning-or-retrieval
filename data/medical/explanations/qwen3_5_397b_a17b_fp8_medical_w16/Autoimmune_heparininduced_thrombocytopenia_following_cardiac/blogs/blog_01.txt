Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# The Pre-Op Cath Trap: Why HIT Can Strike Before Day 5 After Cardiac Surgery

We teach the "Rule of 5s" for Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) so often it becomes dogma: in a patient with no recent heparin exposure, the platelet count drop typically hits between postoperative day (POD) 5 and 10. If your cardiac surgery patient's plates are tanking on POD 3, you look everywhere else first—sepsis, bleeding, dilution, or maybe just the aftermath of cardiopulmonary bypass. But what if that timeline is a trap? What if a "minor" dose of heparin given a week ago during a diagnostic catheterization was actually the primer that turned a routine valve replacement into a perfect storm for catastrophic thrombosis?

A recent case report by St Bernard et al. forces us to rewrite the script on early post-cardiac surgery thrombocytopenia. They describe an elderly man who underwent uncomplicated aortic valve replacement and CABG. His course seemed standard until his platelets began a relentless march downward starting on POD 1, hitting 50k by POD 5, and eventually bottoming out at a terrifying 13k. By the time anyone seriously considered HIT, the patient had already suffered an acute parietal lobe stroke. The twist? He wasn't just exposed to heparin on the table; seven days prior, during a transradial cath, he received 2,000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH) while still therapeutic on apixaban. That small dose was enough to sensitize his immune system, converting what should have been a delayed reaction into an accelerated, fulminant autoimmune disaster.

This isn't just garden-variety HIT; this is **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)**. In classic HIT, antibodies require heparin to bridge platelet factor 4 (PF4) and activate platelets via Fc receptors. In aHIT, the antibodies go rogue—they can activate platelets *without* heparin present and are notoriously resistant to standard blocking mechanisms. Our patient's lab work told this story vividly. While his screening ELISA was strongly positive, the confirmatory Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) looked bizarre. His serum caused massive serotonin release even at zero heparin concentration (the hallmark of aHIT), but unlike typical cases, adding an Fc-receptor blocking antibody failed to shut it down completely. This partial resistance suggests an antibody profile of unusual virulence, explaining why his thrombocytopenia was so profound and why he developed consumptive coagulopathy with schistocytes despite being on therapeutic argatroban.

The clinical lesson here is stark: the clock for HIT starts ticking at the *first* exposure, not the biggest one. We often dismiss intraprocedural cath doses as negligible compared to the tens of thousands of units used during bypass. Immunologically, however, the T-cell help and B-cell priming happen regardless of whether it's 2,000 units or 45,000 units. When that sensitized patient returns for surgery days later, the massive intraoperative heparin load acts as the trigger for an explosive anamnestic response. The result is a platelet crash that violates our comfortable temporal windows and carries a disproportionately high risk of arterial thrombosis, as evidenced by this patient's stroke before POD 6.

Management in these scenarios requires aggressive escalation beyond simply switching anticoagulants. Because aHIT involves persistent platelet activation independent of circulating heparin levels, direct thrombin inhibitors alone may struggle to keep up with the hypercoagulable firestorm. This patient required high-dose IVIG (1 gram/kg for multiple days) alongside anticoagulation to dampen the immune drive, yet even then, his platelet recovery was sluggish and complicated by ongoing microangiopathic hemolysis until they switched to fondaparinux. It serves as a reminder that in suspected aHIT, especially with early onset and severe drops (<20k), we must consider combination therapy earlier rather than waiting for failure of monotherapy.

Don't let the "Day 5" rule blind you to the danger lurking in the pre-op chart. If your post-bypass patient has dropping plates earlier than expected, dig into their history for *any* heparin exposure in the last 100 days—even that tiny flush or cath dose. Recognizing the potential for accelerated autoimmune HIT could be the difference between preventing a stroke and managing one.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **The Timeline Trap:** Recent heparin exposure (even small doses during diagnostic catheterization within the past week) can accelerate HIT onset to **before POD 5**, bypassing the classic 5–10 day window.
*   **Suspect Autoimmune HIT (aHIT):** Profound thrombocytopenia (<20k), early onset, arterial thrombosis (like stroke), and persistent hypercoagulability despite adequate non-heparin anticoagulation should raise suspicion for aHIT rather than classic HIT.
*   **Atypical Lab Profiles:** Be wary of "indeterminate" Serotonin Release Assays (SRA). Strong platelet activation at **zero heparin** confirms aHIT; partial resistance to Fc-receptor blockade indicates an unusually severe antibody phenotype that may correlate with worse clinical outcomes.
*   **Aggressive Management:** Standard anticoagulant switching (e.g., to argatroban) may be insufficient for severe aHIT due to intense ongoing platelet activation; consider early addition of **high-dose IVIG** and monitor closely for consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer).
*   **History Matters:** Always audit pre-operative records for *any* parenteral heparin exposure in patients presenting with unexpected early post-operative thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery.
