Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# The Pre-Op Cath Trap: Why Early Post-Cardiac Surgery Thrombocytopenia Isn't Always 'Normal'

We love rules of thumb in emergency medicine and critical care. They keep us sane when the physiology gets messy. When it comes to heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) after cardiac surgery, the golden rule is drilled into us during residency: **"Don't call HIT before Post-Operative Day 5."**

The logic is sound. Classic HIT requires time for the immune system to generate IgG antibodies against the PF4/heparin complex. In a naive patient undergoing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), the massive intraoperative heparin load starts the clock, but the platelet count drop typically doesn't manifest until days 5 through 10. If your patient's plates are crashing on Day 2 or 3, we instinctively reach for other diagnoses: hemodilution, consumption from the pump, sepsis, or drug reactions. We comfortably dismiss HIT because the timeline doesn't fit.

But what if the clock started ticking *before* the patient ever entered the OR?

A fascinating case report by St Bernard et al. describes an elderly gentleman who shattered this dogma, developing severe, life-threatening **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** with onset prior to Day 5. The culprit wasn't just the 45,000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH) used during his valve replacement; it was a mere 2,000 units given a week earlier during a diagnostic heart catheterization. This case forces us to rethink our pre-op history taking and reminds us that "early" thrombocytopenia can still be HIT if the immune system has already been primed.

### The Case: A Perfect Storm of Timing and Severity

Our patient was a man in his 70s scheduled for aortic valve replacement and ascending aorta repair. One week prior to surgery, he underwent a diagnostic cardiac catheterization via a transradial approach. Because he was on apixaban for atrial fibrillation, the interventionalist used a conservative anticoagulation strategy: only 2,000 units of UFH intraprocedurally. The procedure was uneventful, and he went home.

Seven days later, he returned for definitive surgery. He received a standard massive dose of UFH (45,000 units) for CPB. The surgery went perfectly—no transfusions, easy separation from bypass. His pre-op platelets were robust at 210×10⁹/L. On Post-Op Day 1 (POD1), they dipped slightly to 155×10⁹/L, which everyone agreed was normal post-bypass noise. Prophylactic dalteparin was started.

Then things got weird. Instead of stabilizing or rebounding, his platelet count went off a cliff. By POD5, he was down to 50×10⁹/L. By POD6, he plummeted to 24×10⁹/L. Concurrently, his mental status worsened beyond typical post-op delirium. A CT head revealed an acute parietal lobe infarct.

This is where the classic teaching fails us. We have profound thrombocytopenia (<50k) and new arterial thrombosis (stroke) occurring *before* the traditional Day 5 window for post-cardiac surgery HIT. If you strictly adhere to the "Day 5 rule," you might delay testing or dismiss the diagnosis while searching for sepsis or TTP (both of which were unlikely here given normal haptoglobin and negative cultures).

### Unmasking Autoimmune HIT

The team sent off HIT serology anyway. The PF4/polyanion ELISA came back strongly positive (OD > 2.2). However, the confirmatory Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) returned an "indeterminate" result that initially confused the lab.

Here lies the second lesson of this case: **Autoimmune HIT behaves differently in vitro.**

Classic HIT antibodies are strictly heparin-dependent; they activate platelets only in the presence of low-to-moderate heparin concentrations and stop working at high concentrations. This patient's serum did something scarier: it caused massive serotonin release (96%) even in the **absence** of any heparin (0 U/mL). Furthermore, when researchers tried to block this activation using a monoclonal antibody against the platelet Fc receptor (IV.3)—the standard mechanism for classic HIT—it only partially inhibited the reaction.

This profile confirms **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)**. Unlike classic HIT, aHIT antibodies can activate platelets without heparin present and are often resistant to Fc-receptor blockade mechanisms that usually quell classic HIT reactions clinically with IVIG alone explains why this patient had such a brutal course:
*   **Profound Nadir:** His platelets bottomed out at 13×10⁹/L (classic HIT rarely drops below 20k).
*   **Consumptive Coagulopathy:** Despite being on therapeutic argatroban, his fibrinogen dropped and D-dimers skyrocketed, with schistocytes appearing on smear—a sign of microangiopathic hemolysis driven by extreme hypercoagulability often seen in aHIT but rare in classic HIT!
*   **Delayed Recovery:** Even after switching anticoagulants and giving high-dose IVIG (which usually fixes aHIT quickly), his platelet recovery was sluggish and incomplete initially because his antibodies were uniquely aggressive and partially resistant to standard blocking strategies! His recovery took nearly two months! 

### The "Pre-Op Cath" Priming Effect

So how did this happen so fast? Why didn't we wait until Day 7?

The answer is **sensitization**. That tiny 2,000-unit dose of heparin during the cath lab seven days prior was enough to prime his immune system in susceptible individuals! It generated memory B-cells or low-level antibodies that lay dormant until re-exposure.. When he hit! hit! hit! with another bolus of UFH during bypass!, those primed cells exploded into action immediately., skipping lag phase entirely.. This resulted "rapid-onset"or accelerated presentation mimicking someone who had recent prior exposure within last few months,, except here interval between exposures was short enough allow full-blown antibody production before second challenge!.
