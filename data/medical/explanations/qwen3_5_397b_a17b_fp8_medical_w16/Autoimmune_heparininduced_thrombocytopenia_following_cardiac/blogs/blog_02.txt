Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# Beyond Heparin-Dependence: Recognizing the Serological Signature of Autoimmune HIT

We tend to think of Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) as a binary switch: either the antibodies need heparin to activate platelets, or they don't exist. Classic teaching drills into us that the pathophysiology relies entirely on the formation of large immune complexes between Platelet Factor 4 (PF4) and heparin. Without the polyanion (heparin), there is no cross-linking of Fc receptors, no massive platelet activation, and no thrombotic storm. But what happens when the lab report comes back "indeterminate," yet your patient is throwing clots and their platelet count is plummeting into the single digits?

A fascinating case report by St Bernard et al. forces us to confront a darker, more aggressive variant of this disease: Autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In this scenario, an elderly man underwent cardiac surgery and developed profound thrombocytopenia far earlier than the textbook window, complicated by an acute parietal stroke. The real story, however, lies in the serology. His Serotonin Release Assay (SRA)—the gold standard functional test—didn't look like classic HIT. It looked like something else entirely: strong platelet activation occurring *without* any heparin present, and terrifyingly, partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade. This wasn't just a false positive; it was a serological signature of a hypercoagulable state that refuses to play by the usual rules.

The clinical timeline should have raised red flags immediately. While classic post-cardiac surgery HIT typically manifests between postoperative day (POD) 5 and 10, this patient's platelet count began a steady dive on POD1, hitting a nadir of 13×10⁹/L. Why so early? The culprit was likely a "priming" event: a preoperative cardiac catheterization one week prior where he received unfractionated heparin while still on apixaban. That small intra-procedural dose was enough to sensitize his immune system, so when he hit the operating room for his valve replacement and bypass, the fuse was already lit. By the time he developed delirium and suffered a cerebral infarction, he was deep in the throes of a consumptive coagulopathy that standard anticoagulation struggled to contain.

When the hematologists ran the numbers, the ELISA for PF4/heparin antibodies screamed positive with an optical density of 2.2. But the functional SRA told a confusing story. In classic HIT, you expect zero serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin and massive release at low doses (0.1–0.3 U/mL), with inhibition at high doses (100 U/mL). This patient had the high-dose inhibition, confirming HIT antibodies were present, but he also had 96% serotonin release at *zero* heparin concentration. This heparin-independent activation is the hallmark of aHIT. Even more disturbing was the response to IV.3, a monoclonal antibody that blocks the platelet Fc receptor. In typical HIT cases, adding IV.3 shuts down serotonin release almost completely. In this patient, it only partially inhibited the reaction. The antibodies were so potent and distinct that they bypassed standard inhibitory mechanisms, leading to an initial "indeterminate" label that could have easily led to diagnostic paralysis.

This case underscores a critical lesson for the frontline clinician: do not let an "indeterminate" functional assay dissuade you from treating suspected aHIT when the clinical picture screams hypercoagulability. The management here was aggressive for good reason. The team initiated argatroban and high-dose IVIG, but even then, markers of consumption coagulopathy persisted—fibrinogen dropped, D-dimers soared, and schistocytes appeared on smear—suggesting that direct thrombin inhibition alone wasn't taming the beast. They eventually switched to therapeutic fondaparinux (with antithrombin supplementation), which finally allowed the platelet count to recover over two months. The persistence of these rogue antibodies is notable; serial testing showed they lingered for months before eventually reverting to a more classic heparin-dependent profile as the patient recovered.

Autoimmune HIT represents a distinct clinical entity where antibodies act more like those in spontaneous HIT or lupus anticoagulant syndromes than traditional drug-induced reactions. They can activate platelets without exogenous heparin and drive severe thrombosis despite apparent anticoagulation coverage. ### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) in Early Onset:** While classic post-cardiac surgery HIT occurs on POD 5–10, aHIT can present earlier (<POD 5) if there was recent preoperative heparin exposure ("priming").
*   **Decode the "Indeterminate" SRA:** Do not dismiss an indeterminate Serotonin Release Assay if clinical suspicion is high; look specifically for **heparin-independent platelet activation** (strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin), which defines aHIT.
*   **Recognize Atypical Resistance:** Be aware that aHIT antibodies may show **partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade** (e.g., incomplete inhibition with IV.3 monoclonal antibody), distinguishing them from classic HIT and explaining severe clinical courses.
*   **Aggressive Multimodal Therapy is Often Required:** Due to intense hypercoagulability and consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer), treatment often requires combining non-heparin anticoagulants (argatroban or fondaparinux) with high-dose IVIG to suppress antibody production/activation rapidly compared with classical cases
