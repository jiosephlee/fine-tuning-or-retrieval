Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# When the Gold Standard Fails: Decoding Atypical Serotonin Release Assays in Autoimmune HIT

We teach residents that the Serotonin Release Assay (SRA) is the gold standard for diagnosing Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT). It's binary, right? You add heparin, you get massive platelet activation; you add a ton of heparin, it stops. If it doesn't fit that pattern, you breathe a sigh of relief and look for another cause for the dropping platelets. But what happens when the "gold standard" gives you an indeterminate result in a patient who is actively throwing clots?

A fascinating case report by St Bernard et al. forces us to confront a dangerous blind spot in our diagnostic algorithms: **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** with atypical serology. They describe an elderly man post-cardiac surgery whose platelets plummeted to a nadir of 13×10⁹/L, complicated by an acute parietal stroke. The screening ELISA was screaming positive (2.2 OD), but the confirmatory SRA came back "indeterminate." Why? Because his antibodies didn't play by the rules. They activated platelets *without* heparin and, more disturbingly, showed partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade. This isn't just a lab curiosity; it's a recipe for missed diagnoses and catastrophic thrombosis if we rely too rigidly on textbook patterns.

The timeline here should have raised red flags immediately. Classic post-cardiac surgery HIT typically manifests between postoperative day (POD) 5 and 10. Our patient's platelet count began a steady decline on POD1, hitting 50×10⁹/L by POD5. Usually, we blame this early drop on the cardiopulmonary bypass itself or hemodilution. However, this patient had significant pre-operative heparin exposure during a transradial catheterization one week prior. That "priming" event shifted the immunological clock forward, turning what looks like routine post-bypass thrombocytopenia into fulminant aHIT before most of us would even order the labs. When he developed worsening delirium and his platelets crashed further, imaging revealed an acute cerebral infarct. The clinical picture was undeniable: this was HIT, but the lab was hedging its bets.

The crux of this educational pearl lies in understanding why the SRA looked weird. In classic HIT, antibodies are strictly heparin-dependent; they need the PF4-heparin complex to activate platelets via the FcγRIIa receptor. In aHIT, antibodies can activate platelets even in the absence of heparin (heparin-independent activation). This patient's serum caused 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin—a hallmark of aHIT. But here is the novel twist that stumped the initial interpretation: when the lab added IV.3, a monoclonal antibody designed to block the Fc receptor and shut down activation, it only partially worked. Instead of dropping to <20% release (negative), it stayed high at 76%. This partial resistance to Fc blockade suggests an incredibly potent, perhaps multi-mechanistic antibody interaction that overcomes standard inhibitory pathways. If your lab sees "indeterminate" because inhibition wasn't complete, do not dismiss HIT. In the setting of profound thrombocytopenia and new thrombosis, this *is* the diagnosis.

Management in these scenarios requires aggressive escalation because standard protocols often fail. This patient was started on argatroban and high-dose IVIG (1 g/kg for two days), yet his platelets barely budged, rising only to 54×10⁹/L over a week while markers of consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, schistocytes) persisted. The sheer severity of the hypercoagulable state forced a switch to therapeutic fondaparinux (with antithrombin supplementation), which finally allowed for platelet recovery and neurological resolution. The takeaway is stark: aHIT is nastier than classic HIT. It presents earlier if there's prior exposure, drops platelets lower, causes more thrombosis, and responds sluggishly to IVIG. Most importantly, it can break our diagnostic tests. We must remain vigilant when the clinical story screams HIT but the confirmatory test whispers "maybe."

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect aHIT in Early Onset:** While classic post-cardiac surgery HIT peaks at POD 5–10, autoimmune HIT can present earlier (<POD 5) if there was recent pre-operative heparin exposure (e.g., during catheterization).
*   **"Indeterminate" SRA Can Be Positive:** Do not rule out HIT if the Serotonin Release Assay shows heparin-independent activation (positive at 0 U/mL) or fails to show complete inhibition with Fc-receptor blocking antibodies (IV.3). Partial resistance indicates severe aHIT, not a negative test.
*   **Clinical Severity Trumps Lab Nuance:** Profound thrombocytopenia (<20×10⁹/L), falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimers, and new thrombosis in a post-heparin patient constitute a medical emergency regardless of ambiguous serology; treat empirically for HIT/aHIT immediately.
*   **Refractory Cases Need Escalation:** Patients with severe aHIT may not respond briskly to IVIG or direct thrombin inhibitors alone; switching agents (e.g., to fondaparinux) and monitoring consumptive coagulopathy markers are critical for survival.
