Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Challenges in Early Postoperative Thrombocytopenia

In the immediate aftermath of cardiac surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), a decline in platelet count is an anticipated physiological event rather than an automatic alarm for pathology. Typically, platelet numbers drop by 30% to 50% within the first 24 hours due to hemodilution, mechanical shear stress from the bypass circuit, and consumption at sites of surgical trauma. In a standard uncomplicated recovery, this nadir occurs on postoperative day 1 (POD1) or POD2, followed by a gradual rebound beginning around POD3 or POD4 as marrow production compensates for losses. In this case, the patient's initial trajectory appeared consistent with this norm; his platelet count fell from a baseline of 210×10⁹/L to 155×10⁹/L on POD1. However, the critical diagnostic pivot point arose when the count failed to recover and instead continued a steep, progressive descent to 50×10⁹/L by POD5 and eventually to a nadir of 13×10⁹/L. Recognizing the distinction between the expected early dip and a pathological, sustained decline is the primary challenge in post-cardiac surgery monitoring. A failure of the platelet count to begin rising by POD4, or a drop exceeding 50% from baseline after the initial post-bypass period, must trigger an investigation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), even if the timing seems premature.

The temporal onset of HIT is traditionally categorized as "typical" when it occurs between POD5 and POD10 in patients who have not been recently exposed to heparin. This delay represents the time required for the immune system to generate sufficient quantities of IgG antibodies against the platelet factor 4 (PF4)-heparin complex. Consequently, an onset before POD5 is often dismissed as unlikely to be HIT, leading to dangerous diagnostic delays. This case illustrates the phenomenon of "rapid-onset" or "accelerated" HIT, driven by pre-existing sensitization. Although the patient received a massive intraoperative dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH) during CPB, the true inciting event was a cardiac catheterization performed one week prior. During that procedure, despite a reduced dose of UFH (2,000 U) administered via a radial approach while the patient was on apixaban, enough antigenic stimulus occurred to prime his immune system. Because memory B-cells were already present from this recent exposure, the secondary immune response upon re-exposure during surgery was explosive, causing antibody titers to reach pathogenic levels almost immediately post-operatively. Clinicians must therefore meticulously review medication histories for any heparin exposure—including flushes, line locks, or procedural anticoagulation—within the preceding 100 days when evaluating early postoperative thrombocytopenia.

A particularly treacherous aspect of this presentation was the dissociation between the severity of thrombocytopenia and the clinical manifestation of bleeding versus thrombosis. Intuitively, a platelet count plummeting to 26×10⁹/L suggests an imminent risk of spontaneous hemorrhage. However, HIT is fundamentally a pro-thrombotic disorder mediated by potent platelet activation rather than simple platelet destruction. The clinical clue that shifted the diagnostic suspicion toward thrombosis in this patient was the worsening of postoperative delirium in the absence of fever or hemodynamic instability. While delirium is common after cardiac surgery due to microemboli or metabolic shifts, its progression alongside severe thrombocytopenia warranted neuroimaging. The subsequent CT scan revealing an acute parietal lobe infarction confirmed that the falling platelet count was accompanied by a hypercoagulable state resulting in arterial occlusion. This paradox—severe thrombocytopenia concurrent with new thrombotic events—is the hallmark of HIT and distinguishes it from other causes of low platelets where bleeding is the predominant concern.

Establishing the diagnosis required a rigorous exclusion of alternative etiologies that commonly plague post-cardiac surgery patients. Sepsis was considered unlikely given the patient's stable hemodynamics, normal white blood cell differential, and negative blood and urine cultures. Transfusion-related causes were ruled out; post-transfusion purpura was impossible as no blood products were administered, and thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) was excluded by the absence of schistocytes on the initial peripheral smear and normal haptoglobin levels initially suggesting no significant hemolysis. Furthermore, drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia from antibiotics (cefazolin, vancomycin) or other perioperative medications was improbable because these agents were introduced on or after the day of surgery; typical drug-induced mechanisms require at least 5 to 7 days for de novo antibody formation unless there is prior sensitization, which was not documented for these specific drugs. With infectious, transfusion-related, and alternative drug-induced causes effectively eliminated, and given the strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA result combined with the history of recent heparin exposure, HIT emerged as the only plausible diagnosis despite its atypically early onset. This case underscores that in complex surgical patients, "atypical" timing should not preclude testing if pre-operative sensitization is identified and other causes are excluded.
