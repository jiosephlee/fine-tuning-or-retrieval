# Beyond the Triad: Recognizing Telomere Biology Disorders in the Absence of Classic Mucocutaneous Signs

We love a good diagnostic triad. It's clean, it's memorable, and it fits neatly into medical school lectures. For Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC), the prototype Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD), that triad is sacred: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. If your patient doesn't have two out of three, the textbooks imply you should probably keep looking elsewhere.

But real life rarely reads the textbook.

Consider a woman in her late 20s who walks into your clinic with five years of crushing fatigue, progressive weakness, and exertional dyspnea. She looks older than her years—prematurely grey hair, a bald tongue with atrophic papillae, and knuckle hyperpigmentation. Her labs are a mess: pancytopenia with a dangerously low reticulocyte count, severe B12 deficiency, and signs of ineffective erythropoiesis. Imaging reveals a scary combination of hepatic cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis.

Here is the kicker: she lacks the classic DC triad. No frank leukoplakia, no dystrophic nails. If you stop at "B12 deficiency" or "cryptogenic cirrhosis," you miss the unifying diagnosis that changes everything for her and her family. This case, recently detailed by Soni et al., forces us to confront an uncomfortable truth about TBDs: waiting for the mucocutaneous triad means missing the diagnosis until it's too late.

### The Great Mimicker: When Nutrition Isn't the Whole Story

In this case, the initial workup did exactly what it was supposed to do. The peripheral smear showed dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, and hypersegmented neutrophils. Serum B12 was tanked. The team appropriately started replacement therapy. But here is where clinical intuition needs to override algorithmic thinking: **the severity of the multiorgan dysfunction was disproportionate to the nutritional deficit.**

Even after correcting the B12 and folate, the patient remained frail. She had osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, sensorimotor neuropathy, and significant liver stiffness (18.1 kPa on elastography) alongside ground-glass opacities in the lungs. A positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) briefly sent everyone down an autoimmune rabbit hole, but the lack of hemolysis and negative serologies shut that door quickly.

When you see a young adult with "accelerated aging"—grey hair in their 20s, osteoporosis, cryptogenic cirrhosis, and bone marrow failure—you have to stop thinking about acquired conditions and start thinking about telomeres. These disorders affect rapidly dividing tissues: bone marrow, gut mucosa, hair follicles, and hepatocytes. The absence of skin findings does not rule out a telomere crisis; it just means the phenotype is expressing itself viscerally rather than cutaneously.

### The PARN Connection: More Than Just Lungs

Genetic testing eventually revealed a monoallelic missense variant in *PARN* (c.613T>C). While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) strictly by ACMG criteria due to limited population data, the clinical-genetic correlation screamed Telomere Biology Disorder.

Historically, we've pigeonholed *PARN* mutations. Biallelic variants cause severe childhood-onset Dyskeratosis Congenita (DKCB6), while monoallelic variants were thought to primarily drive Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) or bone marrow failure in isolation. This case blows those silos apart. Our patient had monoallelic *PARN* but presented with a devastating cocktail of pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, *and* pulmonary fibrosis.

Recent literature supports this expansion. We are learning that *PARN* haploinsufficiency can manifest as a broad spectrum of TBDs without adhering to strict genotype-phenotype rules previously assumed. The mechanism involves defective deadenylation of TERC RNA, leading to telomerase dysfunction and premature telomere attrition. Essentially, her cells ran out of replication fuel decades before they should have. The fact that she also had severe B12 deficiency likely acted as a "second hit," pushing an already fragile hematopoietic system over the edge into overt failure.

### Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect TBDs in "Premature Aging":** In any young adult (<40) presenting with unexplained bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, or cryptogenic cirrhosis, look for stigmata of accelerated aging such as premature greying (>50% before age 20), early osteoporosis/fractures, or dental issues—even if the classic DC skin/nail triad is absent
*   **Don't Stop at Nutritional Deficiencies:** Severe B12/folate deficiency can coexist with underlying genetic marrow failure; if cytopenias or organ dysfunction persist disproportionately after repletion pursue genetic evaluation for telomere biology disorders
*   **Broaden the Phenotype for PARN:** Monoallelic PARN variants are not limited to isolated IPF they can present with multisystem involvement including hepatic fibrosis pancytopenia and neuropathy mimicking acquired autoimmune or metabolic diseases
*   **Transplant Caution:** Suspected TBD patients require modified conditioning regimens prior to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation standard myeloablative doses carry excessive toxicity risks due to inherent DNA repair defects
*   **Surveillance is Key:** A diagnosis of TBD mandates lifelong screening for solid tumors (especially head neck and GI) regular pulmonary function tests and liver imaging regardless of current symptom status



# The Double Hit: When Nutritional Deficiencies Unmask Cryptic Genetic Bone Marrow Failure

We love a unifying diagnosis. It's the holy grail of internal medicine: one thing explains the fatigue, the pancytopenia, the liver failure, and the lung scarring. But reality is often messier. Sometimes, a patient doesn't have *one* problem; they have a silent genetic time bomb waiting for an acquired trigger to light the fuse.

Consider the case of a woman in her late 20s who rolled into a tertiary center with five years of worsening fatigue and recent functional collapse. On paper, she looked like a textbook case of severe megaloblastic anemia. Her peripheral smear was screaming it: dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and those classic hypersegmented neutrophils. Labs confirmed the suspicion—severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency.

You treat the B12. You expect the reticulocyte count to pop, the hemoglobin to climb, and the patient to walk out feeling human again. But here is where the story gets interesting: even as we corrected the nutritional deficits, the clinical picture remained disproportionately grim. She wasn't just anemic; she had pancytopenia with a shockingly low reticulocyte count (0.39%), suggesting true marrow arrest rather than simple ineffective erythropoiesis. Beyond the blood, she carried signs of premature aging—premature greying, osteoporosis with fractures, neuropathy, and terrifyingly, both hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis.

This wasn't just a vitamin deficiency. This was a "double hit." The severe B12 deficiency acted as a physiological stressor that pushed an already fragile hematopoietic system over the edge, unmasking an underlying Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD).

## The Diagnostic Trap: Acquired vs. Genetic

It is incredibly easy to stop at the first abnormal lab value. When you see hypersegmented neutrophils and low B12, your brain clicks into "pernicious anemia" mode and stops looking. That is exactly what almost happened here. The team correctly identified and treated the B12 deficiency, but the persistence of multisystem failure forced a re-evaluation.

The clue lay in the disproportionality. Yes, B12 deficiency causes pancytopenia and neurological issues. It does *not* typically cause cirrhosis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), and skeletal fragility in a 28-year-old simultaneously. Furthermore, while B12 deficiency leads to ineffective erythropoiesis (cells dying in the marrow), it shouldn't necessarily result in such profound trilineage suppression unless there is an underlying inability of the stem cells to replicate under stress.

In this patient, the bone marrow was trying to respond to the anemia but hit a hard ceiling. The stem cells, burdened by critically short telomeres due to a genetic defect, simply couldn't sustain the high turnover demanded by the recovery from severe anemia. The nutritional deficiency was the match; the genetic defect was the gasoline.

## Connecting the Dots: The PARN Connection

Once the team stepped back and looked at the constellation of symptoms—pancytopenia, premature greying ("canities"), hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis—the differential narrowed sharply toward Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs). These are rare premature aging syndromes caused by defects in telomere maintenance genes. Classically, we think of Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC) with its mucocutaneous triad: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation.

But this patient didn't have the full triad. She had knuckle hyperpigmentation and a bald tongue, but no nail dystrophy or frank leukoplakia. This is a critical teaching point: **adult-onset TBDs often lack classical mucocutaneous features.** They present instead with organ-specific fibrosis (lung/liver) or bone marrow failure that mimics acquired aplastic anemia or myelodysplastic syndrome.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C). While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) strictly by ACMG criteria due to limited prior reports of this exact mutation, the clinical-genetic correlation was undeniable. *PARN* is essential for processing TERC (the RNA component of telomerase). Haploinsufficiency here leads to telomere shortening. While biallelic mutations cause severe childhood-onset DC, monoallelic variants are increasingly recognized as drivers of adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure syndromes (PFBMFT4).

This case expands our understanding of *PARN*-associated disease beyond just lungs or just marrow; it demonstrates that these patients can develop concurrent hepatic fibrosis—a finding historically under-recognized in TBDs but now appearing more frequently as we screen with elastography and genetics together.

# Liver and Lung Fibrosis in a Young Adult: Why You Need to Think Telomeres Before Transplant

We love a unifying diagnosis. There is something deeply satisfying about finding the single thread that ties together pancytopenia, premature greying, neuropathy, and multi-organ fibrosis in a patient barely into their third decade of life. But beyond the intellectual joy of solving the puzzle, missing this specific diagnosis isn't just an academic error—it's potentially fatal.

Consider the case of a woman in her late 20s who rolled into the tertiary center with five years of worsening fatigue and a rapid decline in functional status. On paper, she looked like a mess of unrelated problems: severe vitamin B12 deficiency causing ineffective erythropoiesis, cryptogenic liver cirrhosis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), osteoporosis, and sensorimotor neuropathy. She had the classic stigmata of accelerated aging—premature grey hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald, atrophic tongue—but no one had connected the dots yet.

The initial workup was a masterclass in ruling out the common stuff. Autoimmune panels were negative. Viral hepatitis was out. The bone marrow showed trilineage hypoplasia without malignancy, and while she had a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT), the clinical picture screamed ineffective erythropoiesis rather than pure hemolysis. Even after aggressively repleting her B12 and folate, the systemic dysfunction persisted. The liver was stiff (18.1 kPa on elastography), the lungs were fibrotic on CT, and the bones were brittle.

When a young adult presents with simultaneous hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis alongside bone marrow failure, you have to stop thinking about "bad luck" or environmental toxins and start thinking about **Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs)**.

In this case, clinical exome sequencing revealed the smoking gun: a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C). While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) by strict ACMG criteria due to limited historical data, the clinical-genetic correlation was undeniable. *PARN* is essential for the maturation of TERC (the RNA component of telomerase). When it breaks, telomeres shorten prematurely, leading to cellular senescence in high-turnover tissues like bone marrow, gut mucosa, skin, hepatocytes, and alveolar cells.

Here is where the stakes get real. If this patient had been rushed to transplant evaluation for her "idiopathic" pulmonary fibrosis or cryptogenic cirrhosis without this genetic insight, she could have easily received standard-of-care immunosuppression or conditioning regimens designed for non-TBD patients. That would likely have been catastrophic. TBD patients have exquisitely sensitive hematopoietic stem cells; standard chemotherapy doses used in conditioning can cause profound, irreversible marrow aplasia and death. Furthermore, knowing this is a germline issue changes everything regarding donor selection for potential hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)—you cannot use a related donor who might carry the same silent mutation.

This case serves as a stark reminder that "idiopathic" fibrosis in a young person is a diagnosis of exclusion that must include telomere testing. The classic mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, reticular pigmentation) is often absent in adult presentations, leaving us with isolated organ failure that mimics common diseases until it's too late.

## The Trap of "Idiopathic" Fibrosis

It is easy to silo these patients. The pulmonologist sees IPF; the hepatologist sees cryptogenic cirrhosis; the hematologist sees B12-deficient anemia. In this case report, the patient's severe B12 deficiency acted as a red herring, partially explaining the cytopenias but failing to account for the severity of the fibrosis or the premature aging features once nutrition was optimized.

The *PARN* gene is particularly tricky because it sits at the intersection of different inheritance patterns and phenotypes. Biallelic mutations cause severe childhood-onset Dyskeratosis Congenita (DKCB6) or Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. However, monoallelic variants—which this patient had—are increasingly recognized as drivers of adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). Historically linked mostly to lung disease, emerging data (including registry analyses showing nearly 90% of TBD adults have some degree of hepatic fibrosis) suggests liver involvement is vastly under-recognized.

The danger lies in the treatment algorithm for IPF and cirrhosis. Standard protocols involve immunosuppressants or antifibrotics that don't address the root cause: replicative exhaustion of stem cells. Worse, if transplantation becomes necessary, the conditioning regimen must be drastically reduced in intensity ("telomere-safe" conditioning) to prevent wiping out whatever residual hematopoietic function remains. Missing the diagnosis means applying a sledgehammer to a glass house.

## Clinical Pearls for the Emergency Department and Wards

You don't need to order whole-exome sequencing on every tired patient with anemia, but you do need a high index of suspicion when the clinical picture doesn't fit neatly into one box. Look for the "soft signs" of accelerated aging in any young adult with unexplained organ fibrosis or marrow failure.

*   **Suspect Telomere Biology Disorders (TBD)** in any young adult (<50 years) presenting with "idiopathic" pulmonary fibrosis, cryptogenic liver cirrhosis, or unexplained cytopenias/bone marrow failure.
*   **Look beyond the classic triad:** Do not rule out Dyskeratosis Congenita/TBD if oral leukoplakia or nail dystrophy are absent; many adults present solely with visceral organ fibrosis and premature graying/hair loss.
*   **Check for multisystem involvement:** The combination of lung + liver + marrow issues is a massive red flag for a genetic etiology rather than separate acquired diseases.
*   **Genetic testing is mandatory before transplant:** Identifying *PARN*, *TERT*, *TERC*, or other telomere-related mutations is critical prior to considering solid organ or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation to guide conditioning intensity and donor selection.
*   **Beware the "Red Herring":** Correctable deficiencies (like B12/folate) may coexist with TBDs; improvement in blood counts after supplementation does not exclude an underlying genetic driver if organ fibrosis persists.
*   **Family screening matters:** Since these are germline disorders first-degree relatives may be asymptomatic carriers with short telomeres who are unsafe donors for HSCT or at risk themselves if exposed to marrow-toxic agents later in life



# Navigating the Gray Zone: When a "Uncertain" Gene Tells a Certain Story

We love a good diagnostic mystery, but we hate the answer that comes back from the genetics lab saying, "We don't know." It's the dreaded Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). In the high-stakes world of emergency and acute care medicine, ambiguity feels like failure. We want binary answers: positive or negative, treat or discharge. But genomics rarely plays by those rules.

Consider the case of a woman in her late 20s who walked into a tertiary center looking decades older than her chronologic age. She wasn't just tired; she was crumbling. Over five years, she had developed progressive fatigue, exertional dyspnea, and weakness so severe she needed help with basic self-care. On exam, the clues were screaming at us: premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, a bald atrophic tongue, thoracic scoliosis, and massive hepatosplenomegaly. Her labs were a mess of pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, and severe B12 deficiency. Imaging revealed a terrifying trifecta: hepatic cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and osteoporosis with recurrent fractures.

Initially, the team chased the red herrings. The severe B12 deficiency explained the ineffective erythropoiesis and neuropathy. The positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) hinted at autoimmunity. But here is the clinical pivot point: **the severity of her multiorgan failure was wildly disproportionate to her nutritional deficits.** Even after aggressive repletion of B12 and folate, the systemic dysfunction persisted. The liver was scarred, the lungs were fibrotic, and the bone marrow was failing despite corrected vitamins. This wasn't just malnutrition; this was accelerated aging.

When the clinical exome sequencing finally returned, it didn't give us a slam-dunk "Pathogenic" label. Instead, it handed us a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) and stamped it as a VUS. In many workflows, this result might get filed away as inconclusive, leaving the patient in diagnostic limbo. But this case teaches us a vital lesson in genomic literacy: **a VUS label does not absolve us of the duty to correlate genotype with phenotype.** Sometimes, the "uncertainty" of the variant dissolves when held up against the certainty of the clinical picture.

## The Art of Clinical-Genetic Correlation

So, how do we move from "Uncertain Significance" to a working diagnosis of a Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD)? We have to become detectives again, weighing three specific pillars of evidence that often outweigh the automated classification algorithms used by commercial labs.

First, look at **domain conservation**. The *PARN* gene encodes an exoribonuclease critical for telomerase RNA component (TERC) maturation. The variant in our patient isn't floating in some non-coding wasteland; it hits the R3H domain (residues 178–245), a highly conserved region essential for protein function. There are no known benign variations in this specific pocket. When a change occurs in a critical functional domain where evolution has tolerated zero errors for millions of years, the probability of pathogenicity skyrockets.

Second, leverage **in silico predictions** not as proof, but as powerful corroborating testimony. While no single algorithm is perfect, consensus matters. In this case, four out of five major prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) flagged this specific cysteine-to-arginine swap as damaging. Furthermore, *PARN* is among the top 20% of genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants in population databases. If this mutation were benign, we would likely see it popping up in healthy controls—we don't. Its absence in large cohorts (gnomAD) supports its rarity and potential lethality or severe morbidity.

Finally, and most importantly, apply **phenotype matching**. This is where art meets science. Classic Dyskeratosis Congenita presents with a mucocutaneous triad: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation. Our patient didn't have the full classic triad initially recognized as DC; instead, she presented with what we now recognize as the *PARN*-specific spectrum: bone marrow failure combined with hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis in a young adult. Literature reviews show that monoallelic *PARN* variants are increasingly linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and bone marrow failure syndromes. The fact that our patient has both liver cirrhosis and lung fibrosis alongside cytopenias creates a "phenotypic lock" that fits the *PARN* key perfectly, even if that specific nucleotide change hasn't been published dozens of times before.

## Why This Matters for Your Practice

Diagnosing this patient as having a Telomere Biology Disorder based on strong clinical-genetic correlation changes everything. It stops the endless hunt for autoimmune etiologies or occult infections that will never be found. More critically, it dictates management TBDs carry unique risks that generic supportive care misses. Patients with short telomeres are exquisitely sensitive to standard conditioning regimens used in bone marrow transplantation; giving them full-dose busulfan or cyclophosphamide can be fatal due to catastrophic toxicity in tissues with limited replicative capacity. Recognizing this diagnosis prompts modified transplant protocols and initiates necessary surveillance for other TBD-associated malignancies and organ failures. It also unlocks genetic counseling for the family regarding inheritance patterns and reproductive options.

We must stop treating genetic reports as oracle scripts and start treating them as data points in a larger clinical narrative. When a young patient presents with unexplained multiorgan fibrosis and marrow failure—a "premature aging" phenotype—and carries a rare variant in a telomere-related gene located in a critical domain, you have your diagnosis regardless of whether Bioinformatics Pipeline v4.2 calls it "Pathogenic" or "VUS." Trust your eyes over the algorithm when they align this perfectly advocating for your patient requires interpreting gray zones with confidence derived from first principles physiology deep knowledge human genome constraints .
