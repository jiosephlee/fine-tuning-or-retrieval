Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Differential Diagnosis] When evaluating a patient with severe vitamin B12 deficiency and pancytopenia, what specific clinical or laboratory features should prompt suspicion of an underlying genetic bone marrow failure syndrome rather than attributing all findings solely to nutritional deficiency?

When evaluating a patient with severe vitamin B12 deficiency and pancytopenia, the clinician must maintain a high index of suspicion for an underlying genetic bone marrow failure syndrome—specifically a telomere biology disorder (TBD)—if the clinical picture reveals **multisystem involvement disproportionate to the nutritional deficit** or **features of premature aging**.

In the reported case, while severe B12 deficiency explained the dimorphic anemia, hypersegmented neutrophils, and neuropathy, several "red flags" prompted the search for a genetic etiology despite the correction of nutritional parameters. First, the patient exhibited classic stigmata of accelerated cellular senescence, including **premature graying of hair**, **osteoporosis with recurrent fractures** in a young adult, and **knuckle hyperpigmentation**. Second, and perhaps most critically, there was evidence of **fibrotic organ damage** unrelated to B12 status: specifically, biopsy-proven hepatic fibrosis (cirrhosis) and interstitial pulmonary fibrosis. In TBDs, tissues with high turnover rates—such as bone marrow, skin, gut mucosa, and hepatocytes—are uniquely vulnerable to telomere attrition. Consequently, the coexistence of cytopenias with unexplained liver or lung fibrosis in a young patient should strongly suggest an underlying defect in telomere maintenance (e.g., *PARN*, *TERT*, or *TERC* mutations) rather than an isolated acquired deficiency.

Furthermore, clinicians should be wary when hematologic recovery is incomplete or when the severity of systemic decline (e.g., profound functional impairment, multiorgan failure) exceeds what is typically expected from megaloblastic anemia alone. As illustrated by this case of a monoallelic *PARN* variant, TBDs can present without the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, reticular pigmentation). Instead, they may manifest subtly as "masked" marrow failure exacerbated by metabolic stressors like B12 deficiency. Therefore, the presence of extra-hematologic findings—particularly pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis combined with signs of premature aging—warrants definitive investigation with germline genetic testing and telomere length assessment, even in the setting of a confirmed reversible nutritional cause.
