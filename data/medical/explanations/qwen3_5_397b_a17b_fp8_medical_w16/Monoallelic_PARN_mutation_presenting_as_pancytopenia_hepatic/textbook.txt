Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Clinical Presentation and Multisystem Phenotype of Telomere Biology Disorders

The clinical vignette of a young adult presenting with progressive, multisystem decline serves as a critical entry point for recognizing Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs). In this case, a woman in her late twenties presented with a five-year history of worsening generalized weakness, early fatigability, and exertional dyspnea. While such nonspecific symptoms often prompt a workup for common acquired conditions like nutritional deficiencies or autoimmune diseases, the trajectory of her illness—marked by a steady reduction in functional capacity to the point of requiring assistance with self-care—signals a deeper, systemic pathology. The key pedagogical lesson here is that in TBDs, the cumulative failure of high-turnover tissues manifests as a syndrome of "accelerated aging," where the patient's physiological state appears disproportionately advanced relative to their chronological age. Recognizing this disconnect between the patient's youth and the severity of their organ dysfunction is the first step in shifting the differential diagnosis from isolated acquired diseases to an underlying genetic etiology.

Physical examination in this case revealed hallmark stigmata that bridge disparate organ systems into a unified clinical picture. Beyond the expected pallor and icterus suggestive of hematologic compromise, the patient exhibited distinct features of premature aging, including premature greying of hair and significant musculoskeletal abnormalities such as thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy. Crucially, mucocutaneous findings included a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae and knuckle hyperpigmentation. While classic Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC) is defined by a triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, this case illustrates the phenotypic variability inherent in TBDs. Patients may present with attenuated or partial mucocutaneous features, or "forme fruste" presentations, particularly in adulthood. The presence of hepatosplenomegaly further complicated the picture, indicating involvement beyond the typical dermatologic and hematologic domains often emphasized in textbook descriptions of DC.

The investigative findings confirmed a broad spectrum of organ involvement that defied explanation by a single acquired cause. Hematologically, the patient demonstrated pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count and morphological evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis, initially attributed to severe vitamin B12 deficiency. However, the persistence of systemic dysfunction despite nutritional correction suggested an underlying marrow vulnerability characteristic of bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Concurrently, imaging and elastography uncovered silent but advanced visceral damage: hepatic fibrosis progressing to cirrhosis and pulmonary interstitial changes consistent with fibrosis. Additional skeletal surveying revealed osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, and neurological evaluation identified an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. This constellation—spanning the bone marrow, liver, lungs, skeleton, and nervous system—is highly specific for TBDs when occurring in a young adult without traditional risk factors (e.g., alcohol use for liver disease or smoking for lung disease).

The concept of "accelerated aging" provides the pathophysiological framework necessary to interpret this multisystem phenotype. Telomeres act as protective caps on chromosome ends, shortening with each cell division until they reach a critical length that triggers cellular senescence or apoptosis. In TBDs, germline mutations impair telomere maintenance mechanisms, causing rapidly dividing cells to exhaust their replicative potential prematurely. Consequently, tissues with high turnover rates—such as hematopoietic stem cells, hepatocytes, alveolar epithelium, and basal keratinocytes—fail earlier than expected in life. In this patient, the coexistence of bone marrow failure, hepatic cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and osteoporosis in her late twenties reflects this accelerated biological clock. For the clinician, identifying this pattern is vital; it necessitates looking beyond the most obvious abnormality (in this case, the B12 deficiency) to consider a unifying genetic disorder that dictates long-term surveillance, family counseling, and potential interventions like hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: Differentiating Acquired Deficiencies from Genetic Syndromes

The diagnostic journey in this case exemplifies the critical clinical skill of distinguishing between common acquired pathologies and rare genetic syndromes when presentation features overlap. Initially, the patient's profound fatigue, pancytopenia, and peripheral smear findings strongly suggested a primary nutritional etiology. The presence of dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils is classically indicative of megaloblastic anemia resulting from vitamin B12 or folate deficiency. Laboratory confirmation of severe deficiencies in both vitamins, coupled with bone marrow findings of erythroid hyperplasia and ineffective erythropoiesis, provided a seemingly complete explanation for the hematologic abnormalities. Furthermore, the duodenal biopsy revealing reduced parietal cells offered a plausible mechanism for malabsorption. However, astute clinical reasoning requires evaluating whether the severity of the systemic phenotype aligns with the identified deficit. In this instance, the constellation of premature greying, osteoporosis, neuropathy, and multi-organ fibrosis appeared disproportionately severe for nutritional deficiency alone, prompting a search for concurrent or alternative etiologies once replacement therapy was initiated.

A rigorous exclusion of secondary causes for the patient's organ dysfunction was essential before considering a genetic origin. The evaluation of hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary involvement necessitated ruling out autoimmune, infectious, and neoplastic processes. Autoimmune serologies, including antinuclear antibodies and specific markers for autoimmune hepatitis, were negative, making connective tissue diseases unlikely drivers of the fibrosis. Similarly, an extensive infectious workup excluded active viral hepatitis, HIV, and other common pathogens, with only remote Epstein-Barr virus exposure noted. Imaging studies, specifically PET-CT, were pivotal in excluding occult malignancy or granulomatous diseases such as sarcoidosis, which can mimic fibrotic lung disease and cause cytopenias through marrow infiltration. Although the direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG, suggesting a potential immune-mediated component, the absence of significant hemolysis markers like a high reticulocyte count and the dominance of marrow failure features argued against pure autoimmune hemolytic anemia as the primary driver of the pancytopenia.

The pivotal moment in the diagnostic reasoning occurred when the patient's multisystem dysfunction persisted despite the correction of reversible factors. While vitamin B12 and folate levels normalized with parenteral supplementation, the underlying trajectory of hepatic stiffening, pulmonary fibrosis, and bone marrow hypocellularity did not resolve. This dissociation between the correction of biochemical deficits and the stagnation of clinical progress signaled that the nutritional deficiencies were likely precipitating factors unmasking an underlying vulnerability rather than the sole cause of disease. The persistence of symptoms forced a re-evaluation of the differential diagnosis beyond acquired aplastic anemia or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). The combination of bone marrow failure in a young adult alongside extra-hematologic features—specifically liver cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis without classic mucocutaneous stigmata—narrowed the focus toward inherited bone marrow failure syndromes, particularly Telomere Biology Disorders (TBD).

Formulating the final differential required synthesizing these disparate findings into a unifying genetic hypothesis. While classic Dyskeratosis Congenita presents with a mucocutaneous triad, adult-onset TBDs often manifest solely with organ-specific fibrosis or marrow failure. The specific pattern of pancytopenia co-occurring with hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis in a patient exhibiting signs of premature aging (greying hair, osteoporosis) is highly suggestive of defective telomere maintenance. This clinical picture guided the decision to pursue clinical exome sequencing rather than stopping at symptomatic management. The identification of a monoallelic *PARN* variant provided the molecular basis for the diagnosis, confirming that the patient's susceptibility to severe B12-deficiency-induced marrow stress was rooted in a genetic inability to maintain telomere length in rapidly dividing tissues. This case underscores that when clinical findings are disproportionate to an initial diagnosis, or when "common" conditions fail to explain multisystem involvement clinicians must consider cryptic genetic disorders like TBDs even in the absence of a family history or classic physical stigmata.

## Pathophysiology of Telomere Biology Disorders and the Role of PARN

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) arise from a fundamental failure in the mechanisms that maintain genomic integrity during cell division. Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences (TTAGGG) capping the ends of linear chromosomes, protecting them from degradation and preventing erroneous fusion events. In somatic cells, telomeres naturally shorten with each replication cycle due to the "end-replication problem," eventually triggering a state of irreversible growth arrest known as cellular senescence or apoptosis. To counteract this attrition in high-turnover tissues, the enzyme telomerase adds telomeric repeats to chromosome ends. This ribonucleoprotein complex consists principally of the catalytic reverse transcriptase subunit (TERT) and an RNA template component (TERC). When genetic defects impair the assembly, stability, or function of the telomerase complex, telomeres erode prematurely. This accelerated shortening limits the replicative lifespan of progenitor cells, leading to tissue exhaustion and organ failure in systems with high cellular turnover, such as the bone marrow, gastrointestinal mucosa, skin, and pulmonary epithelium.

While many TBDs involve direct mutations in *TERT* or *TERC*, the *PARN* gene plays a critical, albeit indirect, role in telomere maintenance through post-transcriptional regulation. *PARN* encodes poly(A)-specific ribonuclease, an exoribonuclease responsible for deadenylation—the shortening of poly(A) tails on messenger RNAs to regulate their stability and translation. Crucially, *PARN* is required for the 3'-end maturation of the *TERC* RNA molecule. Newly transcribed *TERC* often acquires aberrant oligo(A) tails that target it for degradation; *PARN* removes these tails, allowing the RNA to fold correctly and associate with TERT to form a functional telomerase holoenzyme. Consequently, loss-of-function variants in *PARN* lead to reduced levels of mature, stable *TERC*. This results in insufficient telomerase activity and accelerated telomere shortening, phenocopying direct defects in the telomerase complex itself.

The clinical manifestation of *PARN*-related TBDs demonstrates a distinct genotype-phenotype correlation governed by the mode of inheritance. Historically, biallelic (homozygous or compound heterozygous) *PARN* mutations have been associated with severe, early-onset forms of dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6) and Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, characterized by profound bone marrow failure, immunodeficiency, cerebellar hypoplasia, and growth retardation in infancy. In contrast, monoallelic (heterozygous) variants are increasingly recognized as a cause of adult-onset disease, specifically idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and bone marrow failure syndromes. This divergence suggests that while biallelic loss leads to catastrophic telomerase deficiency incompatible with normal development, a single defective allele creates a state of haploinsufficiency. In this state, residual telomerase activity is sufficient for embryonic development and childhood growth but inadequate to sustain tissue regeneration over decades under conditions of physiologic stress or aging.

The concept of haploinsufficiency helps explain the variable penetrance and delayed onset seen in patients with monoallelic *PARN* mutations, such as the patient in this case report who presented in her late twenties rather than infancy. The threshold for clinical disease depends on the initial telomere length inherited from parents and the cumulative burden of cellular divisions required by specific organs throughout life. This variability means that individuals with identical mutations may present with vastly different clinical pictures ranging from isolated pulmonary fibrosis to multiorgan involvement including hepatic cirrhosis and cytopenias. Furthermore, environmental factors or concurrent stresses—such as the severe vitamin B12 deficiency observed in this patient—can exacerbate hematopoietic stress. In a marrow already compromised by shortened telomeres and limited replicative reserve, even reversible nutritional insults can precipitate profound pancytopenia that persists or progresses disproportionately compared to individuals with intact telomere biology.

The predilection for specific organ systems in TBDs is directly linked to their proliferative demands. Tissues such as the hematopoietic system require continuous stem cell division to replenish blood cells; similarly, the lung alveolar epithelium and hepatocytes must regenerate following micro-injuries incurred during normal respiration and metabolism. In the setting of *PARN*-mediated telomere attrition, these stem and progenitor pools reach their Hayflick limit prematurely. Once critical telomere length is breached, cells undergo senescence or death faster than they can be replaced. In the bone marrow, this manifests as hypocellularity and cytopenias; in the liver and lungs, repeated injury coupled with failed regeneration drives fibrotic remodeling rather than repair. This pathophysiological framework clarifies why this patient exhibited a triad of bone marrow failure symptoms (pancytopenia), hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, and interstitial lung disease despite lacking the classic mucocutaneous stigmata often seen in pediatric dyskeratosis congenita.

## Genomic Analysis and Interpretation of Variants of Uncertain Significance (VUS)

In cases of unexplained multisystem disease where conventional diagnostic pathways fail to yield a unifying etiology, next-generation sequencing (NGS), specifically clinical exome sequencing, serves as a critical diagnostic tool. The indication for such advanced genomic testing arises when a patient presents with a constellation of symptoms suggestive of a syndromic or genetic disorder, particularly when features of premature aging, organ fibrosis, and bone marrow failure coexist without a clear acquired cause. In the presented case, the patient's combination of pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and premature greying persisted despite the correction of severe vitamin B12 deficiency. This discrepancy between the severity of the clinical phenotype and the explanatory power of nutritional deficits necessitated a shift from evaluating acquired conditions to investigating inherent genetic vulnerabilities. Clinical exome sequencing allowed for the simultaneous evaluation of thousands of genes associated with telomere biology disorders (TBDs), identifying a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene that would have been missed by targeted single-gene testing or standard karyotyping.

The specific variant identified, c.613T>C resulting in the amino acid substitution p.Cys205Arg, requires careful structural and functional interpretation. This mutation affects a cysteine residue located within the R3H domain (residues 178–245) of the PARN protein, a region essential for the enzyme's deadenylase activity and its role in processing the telomerase RNA component (TERC). The R3H domain is highly conserved across species, indicating that alterations in this sequence are likely to disrupt protein function. Bioinformatic predictions using multiple in silico tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) consistently categorized this substitution as damaging. While the presence of a single allele might initially suggest a carrier state rather than a disease-causing genotype, *PARN* is recognized as a gene intolerant to rare disruptive variants, where haploinsufficiency can lead to clinically significant telomere shortening. Consequently, even a monoallelic variant in this critical domain warrants serious consideration as a driver of the patient's phenotype, especially given the established link between monoallelic *PARN* mutations and adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure.

Interpreting this finding within the framework of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) guidelines illustrates how a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) can be leveraged to support a clinical diagnosis through rigorous criterion application. The variant meets criterion **PM1** because it resides in a mutational hot spot or critical functional domain (the R3H domain) where benign variation is essentially absent. It satisfies **PM2** as it is absent or extremely rare in large population control databases, suggesting it is not a common polymorphism. Criterion **PP3** is met due to concordant computational evidence predicting a deleterious effect on the gene product. Furthermore, **PP2** applies because *PARN* is a gene with a low rate of benign missense variation, meaning missense changes are a known primary mechanism of disease for this locus. Although these criteria collectively provide strong supportive evidence for pathogenicity, they do not reach the threshold for a definitive "Pathogenic" classification without functional validation or segregation data from affected family members, hence retaining the VUS label.

Ultimately, the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder in this patient relies on the synthesis of genomic data with robust clinical-genotypic correlation. A VUS should never be interpreted in isolation; rather, its significance is amplified when the patient's phenotype aligns precisely with the known spectrum of disease associated with the gene. In this instance, the identification of a *PARN* variant provided a mechanistic explanation for the concurrent hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis alongside bone marrow susceptibility, features characteristic of TBDs even in the absence of classic dyskeratosis congenita mucocutaneous triads. Recognizing that environmental stressors, such as severe vitamin B12 deficiency, can unmask latent genetic defects in telomere maintenance helps contextualize why the patient presented with such profound cytopenias. This case underscores that while molecular classification may remain technically uncertain pending further research, strong phenotypic alignment allows clinicians to manage the patient according to TBD protocols, including appropriate surveillance for malignancy and organ progression, and offering targeted genetic counseling to family members.
