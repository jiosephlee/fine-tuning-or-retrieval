Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Reasoning: Differentiating Acquired Deficiencies from Genetic Syndromes

The diagnostic journey in this case exemplifies the critical clinical skill of distinguishing between common acquired pathologies and rare genetic syndromes when presentation features overlap. Initially, the patient's profound fatigue, pancytopenia, and peripheral smear findings strongly suggested a primary nutritional etiology. The presence of dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils is classically indicative of megaloblastic anemia resulting from vitamin B12 or folate deficiency. Laboratory confirmation of severe deficiencies in both vitamins, coupled with bone marrow findings of erythroid hyperplasia and ineffective erythropoiesis, provided a seemingly complete explanation for the hematologic abnormalities. Furthermore, the duodenal biopsy revealing reduced parietal cells offered a plausible mechanism for malabsorption. However, astute clinical reasoning requires evaluating whether the severity of the systemic phenotype aligns with the identified deficit. In this instance, the constellation of premature greying, osteoporosis, neuropathy, and multi-organ fibrosis appeared disproportionately severe for nutritional deficiency alone, prompting a search for concurrent or alternative etiologies once replacement therapy was initiated.

A rigorous exclusion of secondary causes for the patient's organ dysfunction was essential before considering a genetic origin. The evaluation of hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary involvement necessitated ruling out autoimmune, infectious, and neoplastic processes. Autoimmune serologies, including antinuclear antibodies and specific markers for autoimmune hepatitis, were negative, making connective tissue diseases unlikely drivers of the fibrosis. Similarly, an extensive infectious workup excluded active viral hepatitis, HIV, and other common pathogens, with only remote Epstein-Barr virus exposure noted. Imaging studies, specifically PET-CT, were pivotal in excluding occult malignancy or granulomatous diseases such as sarcoidosis, which can mimic fibrotic lung disease and cause cytopenias through marrow infiltration. Although the direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG, suggesting a potential immune-mediated component, the absence of significant hemolysis markers like a high reticulocyte count and the dominance of marrow failure features argued against pure autoimmune hemolytic anemia as the primary driver of the pancytopenia.

The pivotal moment in the diagnostic reasoning occurred when the patient's multisystem dysfunction persisted despite the correction of reversible factors. While vitamin B12 and folate levels normalized with parenteral supplementation, the underlying trajectory of hepatic stiffening, pulmonary fibrosis, and bone marrow hypocellularity did not resolve. This dissociation between the correction of biochemical deficits and the stagnation of clinical progress signaled that the nutritional deficiencies were likely precipitating factors unmasking an underlying vulnerability rather than the sole cause of disease. The persistence of symptoms forced a re-evaluation of the differential diagnosis beyond acquired aplastic anemia or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). The combination of bone marrow failure in a young adult alongside extra-hematologic features—specifically liver cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis without classic mucocutaneous stigmata—narrowed the focus toward inherited bone marrow failure syndromes, particularly Telomere Biology Disorders (TBD).

Formulating the final differential required synthesizing these disparate findings into a unifying genetic hypothesis. While classic Dyskeratosis Congenita presents with a mucocutaneous triad, adult-onset TBDs often manifest solely with organ-specific fibrosis or marrow failure. The specific pattern of pancytopenia co-occurring with hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis in a patient exhibiting signs of premature aging (greying hair, osteoporosis) is highly suggestive of defective telomere maintenance. This clinical picture guided the decision to pursue clinical exome sequencing rather than stopping at symptomatic management. The identification of a monoallelic *PARN* variant provided the molecular basis for the diagnosis, confirming that the patient's susceptibility to severe B12-deficiency-induced marrow stress was rooted in a genetic inability to maintain telomere length in rapidly dividing tissues. This case underscores that when clinical findings are disproportionate to an initial diagnosis, or when "common" conditions fail to explain multisystem involvement clinicians must consider cryptic genetic disorders like TBDs even in the absence of a family history or classic physical stigmata.
