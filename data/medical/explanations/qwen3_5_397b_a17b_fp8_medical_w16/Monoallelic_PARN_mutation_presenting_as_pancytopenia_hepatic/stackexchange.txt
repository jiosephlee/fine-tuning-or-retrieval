Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a young adult presenting with pancytopenia, premature greying of hair, and multi-organ fibrosis, why might the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation) lead to a delayed diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder?

The absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad—oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation—often delays the diagnosis of telomere biology disorders (TBDs) because clinicians historically rely on these dermatological hallmarks to identify dyskeratosis congenita, the prototypical TBD. However, as illustrated in this case of a young woman with a *PARN* mutation, TBDs exhibit significant phenotypic heterogeneity and variable penetrance. Many patients, particularly adults or those with specific genetic variants like monoallelic *PARN*, present with "cryptic" disease manifested primarily by internal organ fibrosis (pulmonary and hepatic) and bone marrow failure, while lacking cutaneous stigmata entirely.

In this patient, the clinical picture was dominated by pancytopenia, premature greying, and multi-organ fibrosis, mimicking acquired conditions such as severe vitamin B12 deficiency or autoimmune disease. The lack of the classic triad initially obscured the underlying genetic etiology, leading to a protracted diagnostic journey where the disproportionate severity of her symptoms relative to her nutritional deficits was the key clue. This case underscores that TBDs should be suspected in any young adult presenting with unexplained bone marrow failure combined with solid organ fibrosis or features of premature aging (e.g., premature greying), regardless of whether the mucocutaneous triad is present. Recognizing this expanded phenotype is critical, as it dictates essential management steps including genetic counseling, cancer surveillance, and cautious consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, which requires modified conditioning regimens to avoid fatal toxicity in patients with shortened telomeres.

### [Pathophysiology] How does the pathophysiological role of the PARN gene in TERC RNA 3'-end maturation explain the phenomenon of haploinsufficiency and the development of telomere biology disorders in patients with only a monoallelic variant?

The pathophysiological basis for haploinsufficiency in *PARN*-associated telomere biology disorders lies in the gene's non-redundant, dose-dependent role in the post-transcriptional maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Unlike enzymes where 50% activity might suffice for homeostasis, PARN functions as a critical exoribonuclease responsible for trimming oligo(A) tails added to the 3' end of nascent TERC transcripts. As detailed in the discussion of this case, this deadenylation step is mandatory for the stability and proper accumulation of mature TERC within the nucleus; without it, TERC is targeted for degradation.

In patients with a monoallelic variant, such as the *c.613T>C* missense mutation identified in our patient, the resulting reduction in functional PARN protein falls below a critical threshold required to maintain adequate pools of mature TERC. This "gene dosage" effect leads to insufficient assembly of the active telomerase holoenzyme (comprising TERT and TERC), thereby impairing the cell's ability to counteract physiologic telomere shortening during replication. Consequently, rapidly dividing tissues—specifically hematopoietic stem cells, hepatocytes, and alveolar epithelium—undergo premature senescence and apoptosis. This mechanism explains why a single defective allele is sufficient to cause the multisystem phenotype observed in our patient, including bone marrow failure susceptibility, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis, despite the absence of a second hit. The case illustrates that *PARN* is among the genes least tolerant to disruptive variants, where even partial loss of function disrupts the delicate equilibrium of telomere maintenance, manifesting clinically as a telomere biology disorder rather than requiring biallelic inheritance.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When evaluating a patient with severe vitamin B12 deficiency and pancytopenia, what specific clinical or laboratory features should prompt suspicion of an underlying genetic bone marrow failure syndrome rather than attributing all findings solely to nutritional deficiency?

When evaluating a patient with severe vitamin B12 deficiency and pancytopenia, the clinician must maintain a high index of suspicion for an underlying genetic bone marrow failure syndrome—specifically a telomere biology disorder (TBD)—if the clinical picture reveals **multisystem involvement disproportionate to the nutritional deficit** or **features of premature aging**.

In the reported case, while severe B12 deficiency explained the dimorphic anemia, hypersegmented neutrophils, and neuropathy, several "red flags" prompted the search for a genetic etiology despite the correction of nutritional parameters. First, the patient exhibited classic stigmata of accelerated cellular senescence, including **premature graying of hair**, **osteoporosis with recurrent fractures** in a young adult, and **knuckle hyperpigmentation**. Second, and perhaps most critically, there was evidence of **fibrotic organ damage** unrelated to B12 status: specifically, biopsy-proven hepatic fibrosis (cirrhosis) and interstitial pulmonary fibrosis. In TBDs, tissues with high turnover rates—such as bone marrow, skin, gut mucosa, and hepatocytes—are uniquely vulnerable to telomere attrition. Consequently, the coexistence of cytopenias with unexplained liver or lung fibrosis in a young patient should strongly suggest an underlying defect in telomere maintenance (e.g., *PARN*, *TERT*, or *TERC* mutations) rather than an isolated acquired deficiency.

Furthermore, clinicians should be wary when hematologic recovery is incomplete or when the severity of systemic decline (e.g., profound functional impairment, multiorgan failure) exceeds what is typically expected from megaloblastic anemia alone. As illustrated by this case of a monoallelic *PARN* variant, TBDs can present without the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, reticular pigmentation). Instead, they may manifest subtly as "masked" marrow failure exacerbated by metabolic stressors like B12 deficiency. Therefore, the presence of extra-hematologic findings—particularly pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis combined with signs of premature aging—warrants definitive investigation with germline genetic testing and telomere length assessment, even in the setting of a confirmed reversible nutritional cause.

### [Clinical Pearls] Unlike many other telomere biology disorder genes where biallelic mutations are required for severe phenotypes, how do the clinical presentations typically differ between monoallelic and biallelic PARN mutations?

In telomere biology disorders, the genotype-phenotype correlation for *PARN* mutations is distinct from many other genes in this category. While biallelic *PARN* mutations typically cause severe, early-onset autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (specifically DKCB6) or Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, monoallelic mutations generally present with a later-onset, heterogeneous phenotype characterized primarily by idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (classified as PFBMFT4).

As illustrated in this case report, monoallelic *PARN* variants can manifest in young adults with a multisystem "premature aging" syndrome that extends beyond the classic pulmonary-hematologic axis to include hepatic fibrosis, osteoporosis, and neuropathy, often lacking the full mucocutaneous triad of classic dyskeratosis congenita. This contrasts sharply with the profound developmental delays and severe immunodeficiency seen in biallelic cases. The pathophysiology hinges on *PARN*'s role in the 3'-end maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC); monoallelic loss leads to haploinsufficiency and accelerated telomere attrition in high-turnover tissues, rendering them susceptible to stressors such as nutritional deficiencies or environmental insults. Consequently, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for monoallelic *PARN* defects in patients presenting with unexplained combinations of pulmonary fibrosis, liver disease, and cytopenias, even in the absence of a family history or classic dermatologic stigmata.

### [Morning Report] In this case, the patient had a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG. What specific findings in the reticulocyte count and bone marrow examination helped distinguish ineffective erythropoiesis from autoimmune hemolytic anemia as the primary cause of her anemia?

In this case, the distinction between autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) and ineffective erythropoiesis hinged on the discordance between the serologic finding of a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and the physiologic response of the bone marrow. While a positive DAT for IgG typically raises concern for warm AIHA, the hallmark of immune-mediated peripheral destruction is a robust compensatory increase in red blood cell production. In true AIHA, one expects a significantly elevated reticulocyte count (often >5–10%) and bone marrow findings dominated by marked erythroid hyperplasia as the marrow attempts to match the rate of peripheral loss.

Conversely, this patient presented with a profoundly low reticulocyte count (0.39%), indicating an inadequate marrow response incompatible with active peripheral hemolysis. The bone marrow examination clarified this discrepancy by revealing trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia accompanied by morphologic features of maturation arrest—specifically megaloblastic changes consistent with severe vitamin B12 deficiency. In ineffective erythropoiesis, the marrow is hypercellular and attempting to produce red cells, but intramedullary destruction of precursors prevents their release into circulation, leading to low reticulocytes despite high turnover. Furthermore, the iron studies supported this mechanism; elevated serum iron and ferritin reflected the recycling of iron from destroyed intramedullary precursors rather than the iron consumption patterns sometimes seen in chronic extravascular hemolysis. Thus, the combination of a suppressed reticulocyte index and marrow evidence of dyserythropoiesis confirmed that the anemia was driven primarily by ineffective erythropoiesis due to nutritional deficiency and underlying telomere biology disorder, rendering the positive DAT an incidental or secondary finding rather than the primary driver of cytopenia.

### [Clinical Pearls] Beyond pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure, which other organ system's involvement is increasingly recognized as a core feature of telomere biology disorders, particularly those involving TERT, TERC, and PARN mutations, and may present as cirrhosis in the absence of viral or autoimmune etiologies?

**Answer:**

Beyond the classic triad of bone marrow failure and pulmonary fibrosis, **hepatic involvement**—specifically progressive hepatic fibrosis leading to cirrhosis—is increasingly recognized as a core feature of telomere biology disorders (TBDs), particularly those involving *TERT*, *TERC*, and *PARN* mutations.

In the context of TBDs, the liver is uniquely vulnerable because hepatocytes are rapidly dividing cells that rely heavily on intact telomere maintenance for regeneration. When telomerase function is compromised by germline mutations, hepatocytes undergo premature senescence and apoptosis, driving a fibrotic cascade that can progress to cirrhosis even in the absence of traditional insults such as viral hepatitis, alcohol use, or autoimmunity. As illustrated in the reported case of a young woman with a monoallelic *PARN* variant, this patient presented with unexplained hepatic fibrosis (confirmed by transient elastography showing cirrhosis) alongside pulmonary fibrosis and cytopenias. Crucially, her workup excluded viral and autoimmune etiologies, highlighting that "cryptogenic" cirrhosis in a young adult, especially when accompanied by extrahepatic signs of premature aging or marrow dysfunction, should trigger suspicion for an underlying telomere disorder.

Recent registry data cited in the discussion supports this shift in clinical paradigm: a significant proportion of adults with pathogenic *TERT* or *TERC* mutations demonstrate moderate-to-severe hepatic fibrosis, with up to one-third meeting criteria for cirrhosis. While *PARN* mutations have historically been linked more strongly to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, this case reinforces that the phenotypic spectrum overlaps significantly with *TERT/TERC*-related disease, encompassing multi-organ fibrosis. Therefore, in any patient presenting with non-alcoholic, non-viral cirrhosis concurrent with pulmonary interstitial lung disease or unexplained cytopenias, measurement of leukocyte telomere length and genetic testing for TBDs are indicated to guide management and family counseling.

### [Management] For a patient with a confirmed telomere biology disorder presenting with progressive bone marrow failure, why must hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) be approached with extreme caution regarding conditioning regimens compared to standard aplastic anemia protocols?

In patients with telomere biology disorders (TBDs), hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) demands a fundamentally altered approach to conditioning because the underlying pathophysiology involves a systemic defect in telomere maintenance, not merely an isolated bone marrow failure. As illustrated in this case of a *PARN* mutation presenting with pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis, the genetic defect compromises the replicative capacity of rapidly dividing tissues throughout the body, including the liver, lungs, and gastrointestinal mucosa.

Standard aplastic anemia protocols typically utilize high-dose alkylating agents (such as cyclophosphamide) and intense immunosuppression to eradicate the host immune system and make space for donor cells. However, in TBDs, these regimens are disproportionately toxic. The patient's extra-hematopoietic organs already possess critically short telomeres and diminished reserve due to the germline mutation; exposing them to genotoxic chemotherapy precipitates catastrophic organ failure. Specifically, the hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary interstitial disease noted in this patient represent "ticking time bombs" that are highly susceptible to exacerbation by standard conditioning doses, leading to fatal veno-occlusive disease or irreversible respiratory decline.

Therefore, unlike standard aplastic anemia where full myeloablation is often the goal, HSCT in TBDs requires reduced-intensity or non-myeloablative conditioning regimens. This strategy aims to achieve engraftment while minimizing additional DNA damage to the patient's vulnerable somatic tissues. Furthermore, as this case highlights, the presence of multi-organ involvement (fibrosis) may even contraindicate transplantation entirely if the risk of procedure-related mortality outweighs the benefit, necessitating a careful risk-benefit analysis before proceeding.

### [Morning Report] Given that the identified PARN variant in this case was classified as a 'Variant of Uncertain Significance' (VUS), what combination of clinical and bioinformatic criteria supported the decision to diagnose the patient with a telomere biology disorder despite the lack of definitive genetic classification?

The decision to diagnose this patient with a telomere biology disorder (TBD) despite the *PARN* variant being classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) rests on the principle that clinical diagnosis in genetics often requires synthesizing phenotypic severity with bioinformatic probability, particularly when the alternative explanations have been exhausted.

In this case, the clinical reasoning was driven by a "syndromic convergence" that is highly specific to TBDs. The patient presented with a multisystem constellation involving bone marrow failure (pancytopenia), organ fibrosis (hepatic cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis), and features of premature aging (premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy). Crucially, while severe vitamin B12 deficiency explained the hematologic abnormalities, the persistence of disproportionate systemic dysfunction—including fibrotic liver and lung disease—even after nutritional correction, pointed toward an underlying genetic susceptibility rather than an acquired sole etiology. The absence of classical mucocutaneous triads of dyskeratosis congenita did not exclude the diagnosis, as TBDs are increasingly recognized to present in adulthood with isolated organ failure or subtle aging features.

Bioinformatically, although the specific c.613T>C variant lacked definitive population data for pathogenic classification, it satisfied several supportive ACMG/AMP criteria that elevated its likelihood of being disease-causing. The variant affects a highly conserved residue within the critical R3H functional domain of *PARN*, a region intolerant to benign variation. Furthermore, *in silico* predictive tools consistently flagged the substitution as damaging, and *PARN* itself is a gene where missense variants are a known mechanism of disease (haploinsufficiency). The report notes parallels with other *PARN* missense variants in similar domains that are already classified as likely pathogenic.

Therefore, the diagnosis was established not by the genetic result alone, but by the robust correlation between a biologically plausible genetic hit in a telomere-regulating gene and a classic, albeit complex, TBD phenotype. As highlighted in this case, when a patient presents with unexplained multi-organ fibrosis and marrow failure in young adulthood, a VUS in a high-probability candidate gene should be interpreted as causative in the appropriate clinical context to guide essential surveillance and genetic counseling.
