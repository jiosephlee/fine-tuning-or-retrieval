Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Beyond the Triad: Recognizing Telomere Biology Disorders in the Absence of Classic Mucocutaneous Signs

We love a good diagnostic triad. It's clean, it's memorable, and it fits neatly into medical school lectures. For Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC), the prototype Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD), that triad is sacred: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. If your patient doesn't have two out of three, the textbooks imply you should probably keep looking elsewhere.

But real life rarely reads the textbook.

Consider a woman in her late 20s who walks into your clinic with five years of crushing fatigue, progressive weakness, and exertional dyspnea. She looks older than her years—prematurely grey hair, a bald tongue with atrophic papillae, and knuckle hyperpigmentation. Her labs are a mess: pancytopenia with a dangerously low reticulocyte count, severe B12 deficiency, and signs of ineffective erythropoiesis. Imaging reveals a scary combination of hepatic cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis.

Here is the kicker: she lacks the classic DC triad. No frank leukoplakia, no dystrophic nails. If you stop at "B12 deficiency" or "cryptogenic cirrhosis," you miss the unifying diagnosis that changes everything for her and her family. This case, recently detailed by Soni et al., forces us to confront an uncomfortable truth about TBDs: waiting for the mucocutaneous triad means missing the diagnosis until it's too late.

### The Great Mimicker: When Nutrition Isn't the Whole Story

In this case, the initial workup did exactly what it was supposed to do. The peripheral smear showed dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, and hypersegmented neutrophils. Serum B12 was tanked. The team appropriately started replacement therapy. But here is where clinical intuition needs to override algorithmic thinking: **the severity of the multiorgan dysfunction was disproportionate to the nutritional deficit.**

Even after correcting the B12 and folate, the patient remained frail. She had osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, sensorimotor neuropathy, and significant liver stiffness (18.1 kPa on elastography) alongside ground-glass opacities in the lungs. A positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT) briefly sent everyone down an autoimmune rabbit hole, but the lack of hemolysis and negative serologies shut that door quickly.

When you see a young adult with "accelerated aging"—grey hair in their 20s, osteoporosis, cryptogenic cirrhosis, and bone marrow failure—you have to stop thinking about acquired conditions and start thinking about telomeres. These disorders affect rapidly dividing tissues: bone marrow, gut mucosa, hair follicles, and hepatocytes. The absence of skin findings does not rule out a telomere crisis; it just means the phenotype is expressing itself viscerally rather than cutaneously.

### The PARN Connection: More Than Just Lungs

Genetic testing eventually revealed a monoallelic missense variant in *PARN* (c.613T>C). While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) strictly by ACMG criteria due to limited population data, the clinical-genetic correlation screamed Telomere Biology Disorder.

Historically, we've pigeonholed *PARN* mutations. Biallelic variants cause severe childhood-onset Dyskeratosis Congenita (DKCB6), while monoallelic variants were thought to primarily drive Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) or bone marrow failure in isolation. This case blows those silos apart. Our patient had monoallelic *PARN* but presented with a devastating cocktail of pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, *and* pulmonary fibrosis.

Recent literature supports this expansion. We are learning that *PARN* haploinsufficiency can manifest as a broad spectrum of TBDs without adhering to strict genotype-phenotype rules previously assumed. The mechanism involves defective deadenylation of TERC RNA, leading to telomerase dysfunction and premature telomere attrition. Essentially, her cells ran out of replication fuel decades before they should have. The fact that she also had severe B12 deficiency likely acted as a "second hit," pushing an already fragile hematopoietic system over the edge into overt failure.

Furthermore, we need to talk about the liver Hepatic fibrosis in TBD is notoriously under-recognized because we are usually staring at the lungs or the blood counts Retrospective data suggests nearly 90% of adults with TERT or TERC mutations have some degree of hepatic fibrosis Our patient proves this holds true for PARN as well If you see unexplained cirrhosis in a young person with cytopenias check their telomeres

### Why This Diagnosis Matters

Labeling this as "idiopathic" or purely "nutritional" is dangerous management-wise for several reasons First transplant conditioning regimens designed for standard aplastic anemia or leukemia can be fatal in TBD patients due to their inability to tolerate DNA-damaging agents They require reduced-intensity conditioning Second these patients carry a massively elevated risk of squamous cell carcinomas particularly in head neck and GI tracts requiring aggressive surveillance Third and perhaps most importantly this is genetic Families need counseling Siblings may be carriers or affected asymptomatic individuals who need monitoring before they present with irreversible organ damage

The treatment landscape for TBDs is evolving beyond supportive care Androgens like danazol have shown promise in lengthening telomeres improving blood counts in some cohorts though long term safety profiles are still being mapped Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation remains the only curative option for marrow failure but timing is critical You cannot wait for end stage lung or liver disease to pull that trigger

### Take-Home Points

*   **Suspect TBDs in "Premature Aging":** In any young adult (<40) presenting with unexplained bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, or cryptogenic cirrhosis, look for stigmata of accelerated aging such as premature greying (>50% before age 20), early osteoporosis/fractures, or dental issues—even if the classic DC skin/nail triad is absent
*   **Don't Stop at Nutritional Deficiencies:** Severe B12/folate deficiency can coexist with underlying genetic marrow failure; if cytopenias or organ dysfunction persist disproportionately after repletion pursue genetic evaluation for telomere biology disorders
*   **Broaden the Phenotype for PARN:** Monoallelic PARN variants are not limited to isolated IPF they can present with multisystem involvement including hepatic fibrosis pancytopenia and neuropathy mimicking acquired autoimmune or metabolic diseases
*   **Transplant Caution:** Suspected TBD patients require modified conditioning regimens prior to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation standard myeloablative doses carry excessive toxicity risks due to inherent DNA repair defects
*   **Surveillance is Key:** A diagnosis of TBD mandates lifelong screening for solid tumors (especially head neck and GI) regular pulmonary function tests and liver imaging regardless of current symptom status
