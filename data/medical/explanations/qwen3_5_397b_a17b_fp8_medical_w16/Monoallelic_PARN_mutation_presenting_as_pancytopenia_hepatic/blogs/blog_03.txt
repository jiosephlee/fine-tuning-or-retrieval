Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Liver and Lung Fibrosis in a Young Adult: Why You Need to Think Telomeres Before Transplant

We love a unifying diagnosis. There is something deeply satisfying about finding the single thread that ties together pancytopenia, premature greying, neuropathy, and multi-organ fibrosis in a patient barely into their third decade of life. But beyond the intellectual joy of solving the puzzle, missing this specific diagnosis isn't just an academic error—it's potentially fatal.

Consider the case of a woman in her late 20s who rolled into the tertiary center with five years of worsening fatigue and a rapid decline in functional status. On paper, she looked like a mess of unrelated problems: severe vitamin B12 deficiency causing ineffective erythropoiesis, cryptogenic liver cirrhosis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), osteoporosis, and sensorimotor neuropathy. She had the classic stigmata of accelerated aging—premature grey hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald, atrophic tongue—but no one had connected the dots yet.

The initial workup was a masterclass in ruling out the common stuff. Autoimmune panels were negative. Viral hepatitis was out. The bone marrow showed trilineage hypoplasia without malignancy, and while she had a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT), the clinical picture screamed ineffective erythropoiesis rather than pure hemolysis. Even after aggressively repleting her B12 and folate, the systemic dysfunction persisted. The liver was stiff (18.1 kPa on elastography), the lungs were fibrotic on CT, and the bones were brittle.

When a young adult presents with simultaneous hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis alongside bone marrow failure, you have to stop thinking about "bad luck" or environmental toxins and start thinking about **Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs)**.

In this case, clinical exome sequencing revealed the smoking gun: a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C). While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) by strict ACMG criteria due to limited historical data, the clinical-genetic correlation was undeniable. *PARN* is essential for the maturation of TERC (the RNA component of telomerase). When it breaks, telomeres shorten prematurely, leading to cellular senescence in high-turnover tissues like bone marrow, gut mucosa, skin, hepatocytes, and alveolar cells.

Here is where the stakes get real. If this patient had been rushed to transplant evaluation for her "idiopathic" pulmonary fibrosis or cryptogenic cirrhosis without this genetic insight, she could have easily received standard-of-care immunosuppression or conditioning regimens designed for non-TBD patients. That would likely have been catastrophic. TBD patients have exquisitely sensitive hematopoietic stem cells; standard chemotherapy doses used in conditioning can cause profound, irreversible marrow aplasia and death. Furthermore, knowing this is a germline issue changes everything regarding donor selection for potential hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)—you cannot use a related donor who might carry the same silent mutation.

This case serves as a stark reminder that "idiopathic" fibrosis in a young person is a diagnosis of exclusion that must include telomere testing. The classic mucocutaneous triad of Dyskeratosis Congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, reticular pigmentation) is often absent in adult presentations, leaving us with isolated organ failure that mimics common diseases until it's too late.

## The Trap of "Idiopathic" Fibrosis

It is easy to silo these patients. The pulmonologist sees IPF; the hepatologist sees cryptogenic cirrhosis; the hematologist sees B12-deficient anemia. In this case report, the patient's severe B12 deficiency acted as a red herring, partially explaining the cytopenias but failing to account for the severity of the fibrosis or the premature aging features once nutrition was optimized.

The *PARN* gene is particularly tricky because it sits at the intersection of different inheritance patterns and phenotypes. Biallelic mutations cause severe childhood-onset Dyskeratosis Congenita (DKCB6) or Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. However, monoallelic variants—which this patient had—are increasingly recognized as drivers of adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). Historically linked mostly to lung disease, emerging data (including registry analyses showing nearly 90% of TBD adults have some degree of hepatic fibrosis) suggests liver involvement is vastly under-recognized.

The danger lies in the treatment algorithm for IPF and cirrhosis. Standard protocols involve immunosuppressants or antifibrotics that don't address the root cause: replicative exhaustion of stem cells. Worse, if transplantation becomes necessary, the conditioning regimen must be drastically reduced in intensity ("telomere-safe" conditioning) to prevent wiping out whatever residual hematopoietic function remains. Missing the diagnosis means applying a sledgehammer to a glass house.

## Clinical Pearls for the Emergency Department and Wards

You don't need to order whole-exome sequencing on every tired patient with anemia, but you do need a high index of suspicion when the clinical picture doesn't fit neatly into one box. Look for the "soft signs" of accelerated aging in any young adult with unexplained organ fibrosis or marrow failure.

*   **Suspect Telomere Biology Disorders (TBD)** in any young adult (<50 years) presenting with "idiopathic" pulmonary fibrosis, cryptogenic liver cirrhosis, or unexplained cytopenias/bone marrow failure.
*   **Look beyond the classic triad:** Do not rule out Dyskeratosis Congenita/TBD if oral leukoplakia or nail dystrophy are absent; many adults present solely with visceral organ fibrosis and premature graying/hair loss.
*   **Check for multisystem involvement:** The combination of lung + liver + marrow issues is a massive red flag for a genetic etiology rather than separate acquired diseases.
*   **Genetic testing is mandatory before transplant:** Identifying *PARN*, *TERT*, *TERC*, or other telomere-related mutations is critical prior to considering solid organ or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation to guide conditioning intensity and donor selection.
*   **Beware the "Red Herring":** Correctable deficiencies (like B12/folate) may coexist with TBDs; improvement in blood counts after supplementation does not exclude an underlying genetic driver if organ fibrosis persists.
*   **Family screening matters:** Since these are germline disorders first-degree relatives may be asymptomatic carriers with short telomeres who are unsafe donors for HSCT or at risk themselves if exposed to marrow-toxic agents later in life
