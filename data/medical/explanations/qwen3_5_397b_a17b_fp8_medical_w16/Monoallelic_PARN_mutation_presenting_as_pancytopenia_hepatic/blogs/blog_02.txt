Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# The Double Hit: When Nutritional Deficiencies Unmask Cryptic Genetic Bone Marrow Failure

We love a unifying diagnosis. It's the holy grail of internal medicine: one thing explains the fatigue, the pancytopenia, the liver failure, and the lung scarring. But reality is often messier. Sometimes, a patient doesn't have *one* problem; they have a silent genetic time bomb waiting for an acquired trigger to light the fuse.

Consider the case of a woman in her late 20s who rolled into a tertiary center with five years of worsening fatigue and recent functional collapse. On paper, she looked like a textbook case of severe megaloblastic anemia. Her peripheral smear was screaming it: dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and those classic hypersegmented neutrophils. Labs confirmed the suspicion—severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency.

You treat the B12. You expect the reticulocyte count to pop, the hemoglobin to climb, and the patient to walk out feeling human again. But here is where the story gets interesting: even as we corrected the nutritional deficits, the clinical picture remained disproportionately grim. She wasn't just anemic; she had pancytopenia with a shockingly low reticulocyte count (0.39%), suggesting true marrow arrest rather than simple ineffective erythropoiesis. Beyond the blood, she carried signs of premature aging—premature greying, osteoporosis with fractures, neuropathy, and terrifyingly, both hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis.

This wasn't just a vitamin deficiency. This was a "double hit." The severe B12 deficiency acted as a physiological stressor that pushed an already fragile hematopoietic system over the edge, unmasking an underlying Telomere Biology Disorder (TBD).

## The Diagnostic Trap: Acquired vs. Genetic

It is incredibly easy to stop at the first abnormal lab value. When you see hypersegmented neutrophils and low B12, your brain clicks into "pernicious anemia" mode and stops looking. That is exactly what almost happened here. The team correctly identified and treated the B12 deficiency, but the persistence of multisystem failure forced a re-evaluation.

The clue lay in the disproportionality. Yes, B12 deficiency causes pancytopenia and neurological issues. It does *not* typically cause cirrhosis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), and skeletal fragility in a 28-year-old simultaneously. Furthermore, while B12 deficiency leads to ineffective erythropoiesis (cells dying in the marrow), it shouldn't necessarily result in such profound trilineage suppression unless there is an underlying inability of the stem cells to replicate under stress.

In this patient, the bone marrow was trying to respond to the anemia but hit a hard ceiling. The stem cells, burdened by critically short telomeres due to a genetic defect, simply couldn't sustain the high turnover demanded by the recovery from severe anemia. The nutritional deficiency was the match; the genetic defect was the gasoline.

## Connecting the Dots: The PARN Connection

Once the team stepped back and looked at the constellation of symptoms—pancytopenia, premature greying ("canities"), hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis—the differential narrowed sharply toward Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs). These are rare premature aging syndromes caused by defects in telomere maintenance genes. Classically, we think of Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC) with its mucocutaneous triad: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation.

But this patient didn't have the full triad. She had knuckle hyperpigmentation and a bald tongue, but no nail dystrophy or frank leukoplakia. This is a critical teaching point: **adult-onset TBDs often lack classical mucocutaneous features.** They present instead with organ-specific fibrosis (lung/liver) or bone marrow failure that mimics acquired aplastic anemia or myelodysplastic syndrome.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C). While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) strictly by ACMG criteria due to limited prior reports of this exact mutation, the clinical-genetic correlation was undeniable. *PARN* is essential for processing TERC (the RNA component of telomerase). Haploinsufficiency here leads to telomere shortening. While biallelic mutations cause severe childhood-onset DC, monoallelic variants are increasingly recognized as drivers of adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure syndromes (PFBMFT4).

This case expands our understanding of *PARN*-associated disease beyond just lungs or just marrow; it demonstrates that these patients can develop concurrent hepatic fibrosis—a finding historically under-recognized in TBDs but now appearing more frequently as we screen with elastography and genetics together.
