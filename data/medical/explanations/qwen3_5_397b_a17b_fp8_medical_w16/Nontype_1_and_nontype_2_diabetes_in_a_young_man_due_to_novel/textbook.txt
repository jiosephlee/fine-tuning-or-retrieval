Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Dilemma in Early-Onset Hyperglycemia

The clinical presentation of monogenic diabetes, specifically Hepatocyte Nuclear Factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY), frequently mimics the phenotypes of both Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes, creating a significant diagnostic challenge for clinicians. In this case, a young adult male presented with marked hyperglycemia and glycosuria discovered incidentally during a routine health examination. Despite an HbA1c of 10.2% and fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L—levels often associated with severe insulin deficiency—the patient was notably free of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). This absence of ketosis is a critical differentiator; while Type 1 diabetes typically presents with absolute insulin deficiency leading to ketoacidosis, HNF1A-MODY is characterized by a progressive defect in insulin secretion that usually preserves enough basal insulin to prevent lipolysis and ketone formation, even in the setting of profound hyperglycemia. Furthermore, the presence of glycosuria at glucose levels that might not typically trigger renal spillage in healthy individuals reflects a specific pathophysiological hallmark of HNF1A mutations: a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired expression of the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in the proximal tubule.

The patient's family history provided the strongest clue toward a monogenic etiology, revealing a classic pattern of autosomal dominant inheritance. Diabetes was documented in three consecutive generations: the maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at age 20. This vertical transmission of early-onset diabetes without a history of early cardiovascular complications strongly suggests a single-gene disorder rather than the multifactorial inheritance typical of Type 2 diabetes or the sporadic nature of most Type 1 cases. However, the diagnostic picture was complicated by conflicting biochemical markers. While the glucagon stimulation test revealed insufficient stimulated C-peptide (0.83 nmol/L), indicating beta-cell dysfunction, it also confirmed that endogenous insulin production was not entirely absent, arguing against long-standing Type 1 diabetes. Additionally, the negativity for islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A) further reduced the likelihood of autoimmune destruction, yet the clinical severity initially obscured the possibility of MODY.

Physical examination and metabolic profiling introduced further ambiguity regarding the distinction between MODY and Type 2 diabetes. The patient's Body Mass Index (BMI) was 24.6 kg/m², placing him in a range where obesity-related insulin resistance cannot be immediately ruled out, especially given rising global obesity rates. His lipid profile showed elevated triglycerides (4.31 mmol/L) and low HDL cholesterol, features consistent with metabolic syndrome often seen in Type 2 diabetes. Conversely, the absence of acanthosis nigricans—a cutaneous marker strongly associated with severe insulin resistance—argued against a primary mechanism of peripheral insulin resistance. This case illustrates the limitation of relying solely on BMI or the presence of metabolic syndrome traits to exclude MODY; as obesity becomes more prevalent in the general population, patients with monogenic diabetes may coincidentally exhibit overweight status or dyslipidemia, thereby masking their underlying genetic diagnosis. Consequently, without a high index of suspicion triggered by the specific combination of non-ketotic hyperglycemia, glycosuria out of proportion to blood glucose, and multigenerational family history, such patients are at high risk of being misclassified as having atypical Type 1 or early-onset Type 2 diabetes, delaying appropriate genetic testing and precision therapy.

## Pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY and Mechanisms of Glycosuria

The molecular basis of HNF1A-MODY lies in the disruption of a critical transcriptional regulator encoded by the *HNF1A* gene, located on the long arm of chromosome 12 (12q24). This gene spans approximately 24 kilobases across ten exons and encodes hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1α), a homeodomain-containing protein that functions as a dimeric transcription factor. HNF1α is ubiquitously expressed but plays a disproportionately vital role in tissues central to glucose homeostasis, specifically the pancreatic beta cells, liver, kidney, and intestine. In the context of this case, the patient's novel heterozygous mutation (p.N270S) resides within exon 4, which codes for the DNA-binding domain of the protein. Because HNF1A-MODY follows an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance, the presence of a single mutant allele results in haploinsufficiency or a dominant-negative effect, reducing the total functional pool of HNF1α available to regulate downstream target genes. This genetic defect initiates a cascade of cellular dysfunctions that distinguish MODY from both autoimmune type 1 diabetes and insulin-resistant type 2 diabetes.

In pancreatic beta cells, HNF1α acts as a master switch for genes essential to glucose sensing and insulin secretion. Under normal physiological conditions, HNF1α binds to promoter regions to upregulate the expression of the insulin gene itself, ensuring adequate synthesis of the hormone. Furthermore, it regulates the expression of *SLC2A2*, which encodes GLUT2, the low-affinity glucose transporter responsible for facilitating glucose entry into the beta cell in proportion to ambient blood glucose levels. The mutation identified in this patient compromises these regulatory pathways, leading to diminished GLUT2-mediated glucose uptake and impaired insulin gene transcription. Additionally, HNF1α controls the expression of mitochondrial enzymes, including pyruvate kinase; a deficit here disrupts the glycolytic flux required to generate the ATP surge that triggers calcium influx and subsequent insulin granule exocytosis. Consequently, the hallmark of HNF1A-MODY is a progressive decline in beta-cell function characterized by defective glucose-stimulated insulin secretion rather than absolute insulin deficiency or peripheral resistance. This explains the patient's glucagon stimulation test results, which showed insufficient stimulated C-peptide levels despite negative autoantibodies, reflecting a specific secretory defect rather than beta-cell destruction.

A unique and diagnostically pivotal feature of HNF1A-MODY, clearly illustrated by this patient's presentation of glycosuria alongside hyperglycemia, is the concurrent defect in renal glucose handling. The proximal renal tubules rely heavily on HNF1α for the transcriptional activation of *SLC5A2*, the gene encoding sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2). SGLT2 is responsible for reabsorbing approximately 90% of filtered glucose from the glomerular filtrate back into the bloodstream. In individuals with *HNF1A* mutations, reduced expression of SGLT2 lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. While a healthy individual typically does not spill glucose into the urine until plasma glucose exceeds roughly 180 mg/dL (10 mmol/L), patients with HNF1A-MODY may exhibit glycosuria at significantly lower plasma glucose concentrations. This phenomenon accounts for why glycosuria was detected in this patient during his annual examination; it serves as an early clinical marker that often precedes frank symptomatic hyperglycemia. Clinicians must recognize that in this specific subtype of diabetes, urinary glucose cannot be interpreted solely as a reflection of severe hyperglycemia but also as evidence of intrinsic renal tubular dysfunction driven by the genetic defect.

The natural history of HNF1A-MODY typically progresses from an asymptomatic phase dominated by postprandial hyperglycemia to overt symptomatic diabetes if left untreated. Early in the disease course, fasting glucose levels may remain near normal due to residual beta-cell capacity and hepatic compensation, while post-challenge glucose rises sharply due to the inability to mount a rapid first-phase insulin response. This aligns with literature descriptions where oral glucose tolerance tests reveal significant excursions (>11.1 mmol/L) before fasting criteria are met. Over time, however, as beta-cell mass and function progressively deteriorate—a process accelerated by glucotoxicity—the patient transitions to persistent fasting hyperglycemia and classic symptoms of catabolism, such as polydipsia, polyphagia, and weight loss, as seen in this young man upon referral. Understanding this trajectory is essential for interpreting why initial management strategies designed for type 2 diabetes (targeting insulin resistance) were less optimal than therapies that directly augment insulin secretion or preserve remaining beta-cell function, ultimately guiding the successful transition to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy in this case.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: From Clinical Suspicion to Genetic Confirmation

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates the critical shift from phenotypic observation to genotypic confirmation required for identifying Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY). The initial clinical picture—a young adult with significant hyperglycemia (HbA1c 10.2%), glycosuria, and classic symptoms of polydipsia and weight loss—mimicked both Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes. However, specific features prompted a deviation from standard diagnostic pathways. The absence of acanthosis nigricans and a BMI of 24.6 kg/m² made severe insulin resistance less likely, while the strong multigenerational family history of early-onset diabetes suggested an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern rather than sporadic Type 1 or complex multifactorial Type 2 diabetes. Consequently, the diagnostic strategy prioritized ruling out autoimmune etiologies before proceeding to monogenic testing, adhering to the principle that genetic screening is most yield-effective after excluding common forms of diabetes.

The first pivotal step in this algorithm was the assessment of pancreatic autoimmunity. The patient tested negative for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen-2 autoantibodies (IA-2A). In the context of young-onset diabetes, negative autoantibodies significantly reduce the likelihood of Type 1 diabetes, although they do not entirely exclude it, particularly in early stages or latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA). This seronegative status, combined with the familial clustering of disease, strengthened the pre-test probability of a monogenic disorder. It is important to note that while rare cases of HNF1A-MODY may present with coincidental autoantibodies, the overwhelming majority are antibody-negative; thus, these results served as a necessary gatekeeper to justify further genetic investigation.

Following the exclusion of autoimmunity, evaluating endogenous insulin secretion became essential to characterize the nature of the beta-cell defect. The glucagon stimulation test revealed a baseline C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L rising to 0.83 nmol/L at six minutes. While a stimulated level above 0.60 nmol/L generally indicates preserved beta-cell function inconsistent with long-standing Type 1 diabetes, this patient's response was borderline, reflecting insufficient but detectable secretory capacity. This finding aligns with the pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY, where beta-cell dysfunction is progressive rather than absolute at onset. The presence of glycosuria despite moderate hyperglycemia further supported the diagnosis, as HNF1A mutations impair renal glucose reabsorption by downregulating SGLT2 expression, lowering the renal threshold for glucose. However, reliance on C-peptide alone carries limitations; levels can remain preserved in early Type 1 diabetes ("honeymoon phase") or vary based on glycemic toxicity, necessitating that these biochemical markers be interpreted strictly within the broader clinical context.

To quantify the likelihood of MODY prior to genetic testing, clinicians often utilize risk stratification tools such as the Exeter MODY Probability Calculator. In this case, inputting the patient's demographics and clinical data yielded a probability of 62.4% (approximately 1 in 1.6). While this score strongly supported pursuing genetic analysis, it also highlighted a crucial limitation: the calculator was derived primarily from White European cohorts. Its application in Asian populations may introduce bias due to differing prevalence rates and phenotypic expressions of diabetes subtypes. Furthermore, rising global obesity rates complicate differentiation based on BMI alone, as evidenced by this patient's borderline metabolic profile which might otherwise suggest Type 2 diabetes in less genetically predisposed individuals. Therefore, while probability calculators are valuable heuristics, they serve best as guides rather than definitive diagnostic arbiters in diverse populations.

Definitive diagnosis was achieved through Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) panels, which allow for the simultaneous analysis of multiple genes associated with monogenic diabetes. This approach identified a novel heterozygous variant in exon 4 of the *HNF1A* gene: c.809A>G (p.N270S). Because this specific mutation had not been previously recorded in major databases like ClinVar or VarSome, its classification required rigorous adherence to American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines rather than simple database lookup. The variant was classified as likely pathogenic based on converging lines of evidence: it segregated with the disease phenotype across three generations of the family; it occurred in the DNA-binding domain, a hotspot for pathogenic *HNF1A* mutations; and computational predictive models assigned it a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), indicating a high probability of functional disruption. This comprehensive interpretative framework transformed a "variant of unknown significance" into a confirmed molecular diagnosis, enabling the transition from empirical treatment to precision medicine tailored to HNF1A-MODY physiology.

## Precision Medicine Management Strategies for HNF1A-MODY

The clinical course of this patient illustrates the critical transition from empirical diabetes management to genotype-guided precision medicine. Upon initial presentation, the patient exhibited severe hyperglycemia (HbA1c 10.2%) alongside classic symptoms of catabolism, including polydipsia, polyphagia, and weight loss. Despite a strong family history suggestive of monogenic diabetes, the immediate clinical priority was rapid glycemic stabilization to resolve glucotoxicity and prevent further metabolic decompensation. Consequently, the initial therapeutic regimen empirically mirrored standard protocols for severe Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) or ketosis-prone presentations: basal insulin degludec was initiated to provide exogenous insulin coverage, while metformin and saxagliptin were added to address potential underlying insulin resistance and augment endogenous secretion. This approach is clinically prudent when genetic confirmation is pending, as it ensures safety and efficacy regardless of the final etiology. However, this phase represents a "diagnostic limbo" where treatment is broad-spectrum rather than targeted, potentially exposing the patient to unnecessary injections if the underlying pathology is purely secretory rather than resistive.

Following the identification of the novel *HNF1A* pathogenic variant (p.N270S), the treatment strategy was fundamentally recalibrated to align with the specific pathophysiology of *HNF1A*-MODY. Unlike T2DM, where insulin resistance is paramount, *HNF1A*-MODY is characterized by a progressive defect in pancreatic beta-cell function and a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to reduced SGLT2 expression. Patients with this genotype exhibit exquisite sensitivity to agents that stimulate insulin secretion. While low-dose sulfonylureas have historically been the first-line therapy due to their mechanism of closing ATP-sensitive potassium channels, they carry a significant risk of hypoglycemia, particularly as beta-cell mass declines over time. In this case, the clinical team opted for a Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist (dulaglutide) combined with metformin after discontinuing insulin. This decision leverages the patient's moderately preserved C-peptide response (0.83 nmol/L post-stimulation) while mitigating hypoglycemic risk. Evidence suggests that GLP-1 receptor agonists are highly effective in *HNF1A*-MODY, offering comparable glycemic control to sulfonylureas with a superior safety profile regarding hypoglycemia, making them an ideal choice for long-term maintenance in patients with residual beta-cell function.

Furthermore, precision medicine in *HNF1A*-MODY necessitates not only selecting appropriate therapies but also recognizing specific contraindications inherent to the genetic defect. The *HNF1A* transcription factor regulates the expression of Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in the renal proximal tubule; mutations result in naturally reduced SGLT2 activity and consequent glycosuria even at near-normal blood glucose levels. The use of SGLT2 inhibitors in these patients is theoretically problematic and potentially dangerous. Pharmacologic inhibition of an already compromised transport system can precipitate severe dehydration, volume depletion, and an elevated risk of euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). Although this patient was not prescribed an SGLT2 inhibitor, understanding this pathophysiological nuance is vital to avoid iatrogenic harm. The successful reduction of the patient's HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% within six months, followed by stabilization on oral agents alone, underscores the transformative impact of genetic diagnosis. It allowed for the deprescribing of insulin and the optimization of oral/injectable non-insulin therapies tailored specifically to the molecular defect, thereby improving adherence, quality of life, and long-term prognostic outcomes.
