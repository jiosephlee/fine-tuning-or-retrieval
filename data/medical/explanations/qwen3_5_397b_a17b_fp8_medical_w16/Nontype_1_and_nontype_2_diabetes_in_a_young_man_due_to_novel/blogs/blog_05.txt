Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# Autonomic Neuropathy at Diagnosis? The Hidden Complications of Delayed Monogenic Diabetes Recognition

We tend to treat Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) with a certain degree of clinical complacency. The narrative in our heads usually goes something like this: it's a "mild" form of diabetes, often managed with low-dose sulfonylureas or even just diet, and crucially, it carries a significantly lower risk of microvascular complications compared to Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes. We assume that because the pathophysiology is a specific transcription factor defect rather than autoimmune destruction or severe insulin resistance, the tissues are somehow spared.

But what if that assumption is dangerous? What if the "benign" nature of MODY is actually a myth created by delayed diagnosis and misclassification?

A recent case report by Liu et al. throws a wrench in this comforting narrative. They describe a young man with *HNF1A*-MODY caused by a novel mutation who didn't just present with hyperglycemia; he presented with **severely reduced sudomotor function** indicative of autonomic neuropathy at the very moment of his genetic diagnosis. This isn't just a story about finding a new gene variant; it's a stark reminder that when we miss monogenic diabetes, we aren't just delaying the right pill—we are allowing years of unchecked hyperglycemia to inflict irreversible organ damage under the false guise of a "milder" disease.

### The Case: Not Your Typical "Benign" Presentation

The patient was a young adult referred for evaluation after an annual exam flagged hyperglycemia and glycosuria. On paper, he looked like a classic candidate for monogenic diabetes: lean (BMI 24.6 kg/m²), no acanthosis nigricans, and a striking family history of early-onset diabetes affecting three generations (grandmother, mother, and brother). Yet, his metabolic profile was alarming. His HbA1c was 10.2%, fasting glucose was 12.4 mmol/L, and he had significant postprandial spikes hitting nearly 20 mmol/L.

Initially, the team treated him as Type 2 diabetes, starting basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor. While his sugars improved slightly, the clinical picture nagged at them. He had negative autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A), ruling out classic Type 1, but his C-peptide response to glucagon stimulation was insufficient, suggesting progressive beta-cell failure.

When genetic testing finally returned, it confirmed *HNF1A*-MODY due to a novel heterozygous mutation (p.N270S). But here is the kicker: alongside the genetic confirmation came the results of sudomotor testing via electrochemical skin conductance (Sudoscan). The test revealed **severely reduced conductance in the hands**, signaling significant small fiber autonomic dysfunction.

This finding shatters the illusion that MODY patients are protected from complications until late in life. This young man hadn't just been living with high blood sugar; he had been living with *undiagnosed* high blood sugar for years, likely since adolescence, leading to nerve damage before he ever received the correct label.

### Why Did We Miss It? The Trap of Misclassification

The tragedy in this case—and thousands like it—is the diagnostic limbo. Patients with *HNF1A*-MODY frequently fall through the cracks because they don't fit neatly into binary boxes. They aren't ketotic enough for Type 1 at presentation, yet they are too lean and lack the typical metabolic syndrome features (though this patient did have some dyslipidemia) for classic Type 2.

In this case, the patient's BMI and lipid profile initially muddied the waters, prompting a Type 2 workup. However, the hallmark signs were there if we looked closely:
*   **Glycosuria out of proportion to hyperglycemia:** Due to *HNF1A*'s role in regulating SGLT2 in the renal tubules, these patients have a lowered renal threshold for glucose. They spill sugar in their urine at lower blood glucose levels than usual.
*   **Strong Autosomal Dominant History:** Three generations of early-onset diabetes is the biggest red flag imaginable.
*   **Negative Autoimmunity:** A hard stop for Type 1 diagnosis.

Because clinicians often rely on probability calculators derived from White European populations (which may underestimate risk in Asian cohorts) or hesitate to order expensive genetic panels without "perfect" criteria, these patients languish on suboptimal therapies. In this case, the delay allowed hyperglycemia to silently erode his autonomic nervous system.

### Precision Medicine: More Than Just Swapping Pills

Once the genetic diagnosis was secured, the treatment paradigm shifted dramatically—and effectively. Insulin was discontinued. Given his preserved but declining beta-cell function and the desire to avoid hypoglycemia (a known risk with sulfonylureas as beta-cell mass wanes), he was transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) alongside metformin.

The result? His HbA1c plummeted from 9.1% (post-initial therapy) to 6.0% within six months. He eventually stabilized on metformin and saxagliptin with sustained control below 6.5%.

While the glycemic victory is impressive, we cannot ignore the scar tissue left behind—the autonomic neuropathy detected at baseline Sulfonylureas remain first-line for many *HNF1A* cases due to exquisite sensitivity, but as beta-cell function declines or if hypoglycemia becomes a concern, GLP-1 RAs offer a potent alternative with a favorable safety profile. However, one class of drugs demands extreme caution: **SGLT2 inhibitors**. Since *HNF1A* mutations already downregulate SGLT2 expression in the kidney adding an SGLT2 inhibitor could theoretically precipitate severe dehydration or euglycemic DKA by pushing residual transport mechanisms past their breaking point This case reinforces why knowing the genotype dictates not just efficacy, but safety 

### Take-Home Messages

This case forces us to confront uncomfortable truths about how we manage young-onset diabetes. We can no longer afford to view MODY as a curiosity or a benign entity reserved for textbook examples Timely genetic diagnosis is an emergency intervention for organ preservation 

*   **Challenge the "Benign" Myth**: Do not assume monogenic diabetes protects against microvascular complications Years of undiagnosed hyperglycemia due to misclassification can lead to early autonomic neuropathy retinopathy and nephropathy 
*   **Suspect MODY Early**: Look beyond BMI A strong multigenerational history of early-onset diabetes negative autoantibodies and glycosuria disproportionate to blood glucose levels should trigger immediate genetic referral regardless of ethnicity 
*   **Genotype Guides Therapy**: Confirming *HNF1A*-MODY allows for de-escalation from insulin optimization of oral agents or GLP-1 RAs and avoidance of potentially harmful drugs like SGLT2 inhibitors 
*   **Screen for Complications at Diagnosis**: If there has been any delay in diagnosis perform baseline screening for retinopathy nephropathy and neuropathy immediately Do not wait five years 

The next time you see a lean young person with "Type 2" diabetes and a family tree full of early diagnoses remember this patient The nerves in his hands were already screaming what his chart hadn't yet said get the genetics done now
