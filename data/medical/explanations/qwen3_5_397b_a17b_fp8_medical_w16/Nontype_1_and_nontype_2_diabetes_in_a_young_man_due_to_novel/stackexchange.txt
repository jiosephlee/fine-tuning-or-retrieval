Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Differential Diagnosis] A young adult presents with hyperglycemia, a strong three-generation family history of early-onset diabetes, and negative pancreatic autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A). Despite a BMI suggesting possible metabolic syndrome, why is Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) still the primary diagnostic consideration over Type 2 Diabetes?

While obesity and features of metabolic syndrome traditionally steer the differential diagnosis toward Type 2 Diabetes (T2D), they do not exclude a monogenic etiology in the setting of a compelling autosomal dominant pedigree. In this case, the primary consideration for Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY), specifically *HNF1A*-MODY, rests on the convergence of three critical factors: the pattern of inheritance, the absence of autoimmunity, and the specific pathophysiology of beta-cell dysfunction that transcends BMI classifications.

The patient presents with a classic "three-generation" history of early-onset diabetes affecting the maternal grandmother, mother, and brother, which is the hallmark of autosomal dominant transmission characteristic of MODY. Although the patient's lipid profile suggested possible metabolic syndrome and his BMI was 24.6 kg/m², the background literature explicitly notes that rising global obesity rates have diminished the specificity of BMI as a discriminator between MODY and T2D. Consequently, phenotypic overlap should not deter genetic investigation when the familial pattern is this distinct. Furthermore, the negative pancreatic autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A) effectively rule out Type 1 Diabetes, while the glucagon stimulation test revealing insufficient but preserved endogenous insulin secretion (stimulated C-peptide 0.83 nmol/L) supports a non-autoimmune, non-insulin-deficient state consistent with MODY rather than absolute insulin deficiency.

Crucially, the pathophysiology of *HNF1A* mutations involves a progressive decline in beta-cell function due to impaired insulin gene expression and reduced glucose transport, alongside a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. This explains the patient's presenting symptom of glycosuria despite moderate hyperglycemia—a feature often disproportionate in *HNF1A*-MODY compared to T2D. The definitive resolution in this case required molecular genetic testing, which identified a novel heterozygous pathogenic variant (p.N270S) in exon 4 of *HNF1A*. This confirms that even in the presence of metabolic risk factors, a strong multigenerational history mandates genetic screening to guide precision therapy; indeed, recognizing this genotype allowed for the successful de-escalation from insulin to a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin, achieving optimal glycemic control (HbA1c 6.0%) while avoiding unnecessary lifelong insulin dependence.

### [Pathophysiology] Patients with HNF1A-MODY frequently present with glycosuria even when blood glucose levels are only mildly elevated. What is the specific pathophysiological mechanism linking the HNF1A mutation to this renal finding?

The presence of glycosuria in patients with *HNF1A*-MODY at relatively low blood glucose levels is caused by a **reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption**. This phenomenon stems directly from the tissue-specific expression of the *HNF1A* gene, which encodes a transcription factor critical for the development and function of both pancreatic beta cells and the renal proximal tubule.

Under normal physiological conditions, glucose filtered by the glomerulus is almost entirely reabsorbed in the proximal convoluted tubule via the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2). The expression of the *SLC5A2* gene, which encodes SGLT2, is positively regulated by HNF1A. In patients with heterozygous loss-of-function mutations in *HNF1A*, as seen in the reported case of the young man with the novel p.N270S variant, there is haploinsufficiency of this transcription factor within the kidney. Consequently, the transcriptional drive for SGLT2 is diminished, leading to reduced transporter density on the apical membrane of renal tubular cells.

This reduction in functional SGLT2 lowers the maximum transport capacity ($T_m$) for glucose. While a healthy individual typically does not spill glucose into the urine until plasma concentrations exceed approximately 180 mg/dL (10 mmol/L), patients with *HNF1A*-MODY reach their saturation point at much lower plasma glucose levels. As noted in the discussion of this case report, this "compromised glucose transport in renal tubules" results in significant glycosuria even when hyperglycemia is mild or moderate. Clinically, this finding serves as a vital diagnostic clue; it explains why glycosuria may be detected incidentally in early adolescence before frank diabetes develops and warns against the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in these patients, as further inhibition of an already compromised system can precipitate severe dehydration and euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis.

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient with suspected monogenic diabetes where clinical features overlap with Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes, what constitutes the definitive diagnostic standard, and why are tools like the Exeter MODY probability calculator potentially limited in non-European populations?

The definitive diagnostic standard for monogenic diabetes, particularly when clinical features overlap with Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes, is **molecular genetic testing**. While clinical algorithms incorporating family history, age of onset, negative autoantibodies (such as GADA and IA-2A), and preserved C-peptide secretion provide a strong pre-test probability, they cannot confirm the diagnosis. As illustrated in the reported case of a young man with a novel *HNF1A* mutation, the patient presented with significant hyperglycemia, weight loss, and dyslipidemia—features often mimicking Type 2 diabetes—alongside a strong multigenerational family history. Despite a calculated MODY probability of 62.4% using the Exeter calculator, only next-generation sequencing (NGS) confirmed the pathogenic *HNF1A* variant (p.N270S), allowing for the de-escalation of insulin therapy to a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin regimen.

Tools like the Exeter MODY probability calculator are potentially limited in non-European populations because they were derived and validated primarily using data from White European cohorts. The case report explicitly notes that applying this tool to their patient may have led to an overestimation or inaccurate stratification of risk due to ethnic disparities in genetic architecture and phenotypic expression. Furthermore, rising global rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome blur the traditional anthropometric distinctions between MODY and Type 2 diabetes, reducing the specificity of clinical predictors like BMI in diverse populations. Consequently, reliance on probability calculators without confirmatory genetic testing risks misdiagnosis; molecular analysis remains the requisite gold standard to identify specific variants, guide precision medicine therapies (such as sulfonylureas or GLP-1 agonists instead of insulin), and facilitate accurate familial counseling.

### [Management] While low-dose sulfonylureas are historically the first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY due to their mechanism on ATP-sensitive potassium channels, what specific safety advantage do GLP-1 receptor agonists offer in this population as beta-cell function progressively declines?

While low-dose sulfonylureas remain the historical first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY due to their ability to bypass defective ATP-sensitive potassium channels, they carry an inherent risk of hypoglycemia as endogenous insulin secretory capacity wanes. As illustrated in this case, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a distinct safety advantage by providing potent glucose-dependent insulin secretion, thereby minimizing the risk of hypoglycemia even as beta-cell function progressively declines.

In the reported case of a young man with a novel *HNF1A* mutation (p.N270S), the clinical team initially utilized basal insulin to address severe hyperglycemia (HbA1c 10.2%). However, once genetic confirmation established the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY and acute glucotoxicity resolved, insulin was discontinued in favor of dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist. This transition was strategically chosen because, unlike sulfonylureas which stimulate insulin release regardless of ambient glucose levels, GLP-1 receptor agonists augment insulin secretion only when glucose concentrations are elevated. This mechanism is particularly advantageous in patients with "moderately preserved yet insufficient" beta-cell function, as evidenced by this patient's stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L. By avoiding the forced secretion characteristic of sulfonylureas in the setting of diminishing beta-cell mass, GLP-1 receptor agonists mitigate the danger of iatrogenic hypoglycemia while still achieving robust glycemic control, reducing this patient's HbA1c to 6.0% within six months. Consequently, for HNF1A-MODY patients transitioning away from insulin or those susceptible to hypoglycemic events as their disease evolves, GLP-1 receptor agonists represent a safer physiological alternative that aligns with the principles of precision medicine.

### [Management] Why are SGLT2 inhibitors generally considered hazardous or contraindicated in patients with confirmed HNF1A-MODY, despite their efficacy in typical Type 2 Diabetes?

SGLT2 inhibitors are generally considered hazardous in patients with confirmed *HNF1A*-MODY due to a unique pathophysiologic synergy that predisposes these individuals to severe volume depletion and euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). Unlike typical Type 2 diabetes, where SGLT2 inhibitors act on normally functioning sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 proteins, patients with *HNF1A* mutations possess an intrinsic defect in renal glucose handling. As detailed in the case report, the HNF1A transcription factor regulates the expression of SGLT2 in the renal proximal tubule; consequently, *HNF1A* loss-of-function mutations lead to reduced baseline expression of SGLT2 and a characteristically low renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. This explains why glycosuria is often an early presenting sign in these patients, even before significant hyperglycemia develops.

Administering an SGLT2 inhibitor to a patient who already has genetically diminished SGLT2 activity creates a profound "double hit" on glucose reabsorption. The case discussion explicitly warns that further pharmacological inhibition of this residual SGLT2 activity can precipitate severe dehydration, vascular collapse, and DKA. Because these patients rely heavily on their limited remaining beta-cell function and have altered renal glucose dynamics, the osmotic diuresis induced by these drugs can rapidly outpace fluid intake, leading to hemodynamic instability. Furthermore, the shift toward fat metabolism driven by relative insulin deficiency and caloric loss via urine increases ketone production, raising the risk of DKA even at near-normal blood glucose levels. Therefore, while SGLT2 inhibitors are cornerstone therapies for Type 2 diabetes, their mechanism directly exacerbates the specific renal and metabolic vulnerabilities inherent to *HNF1A*-MODY, making alternatives like sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists—as successfully utilized in the reported case—the preferred management strategy.

### [Clinical Pearls] When a novel genetic variant (e.g., p.N270S in HNF1A) is identified that is absent from major databases like ClinVar or gnomAD, what combination of ACMG guideline criteria supports classifying it as pathogenic rather than a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)?

When a novel genetic variant such as p.N270S in *HNF1A* is absent from major population databases like ClinVar or gnomAD, its classification as pathogenic rather than a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) relies on synthesizing specific American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria that integrate computational predictions, familial segregation, and phenotypic concordance. As illustrated in the reported case of the young man with early-onset diabetes, the absence of a variant in databases satisfies criterion **PM2** (absence in controls), but this alone is insufficient for a definitive pathogenic designation. 

To elevate the classification, one must demonstrate that the variant affects a critical functional domain and aligns with the patient's clinical picture. In this instance, the p.N270S mutation resides within the DNA-binding domain of the *HNF1A* protein, adjacent to previously established pathogenic residues, satisfying criterion **PM1** (located in a mutational hot spot or critical functional domain). Furthermore, advanced in silico modeling provided strong supportive evidence via a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), fulfilling criterion **PP3** (multiple lines of computational evidence support a deleterious effect). Crucially, the variant demonstrated vertical transmission consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance, segregating with the disease phenotype across three generations (maternal grandmother, mother, and sibling), which meets criterion **PP1** (co-segregation with disease in multiple family members). Finally, the patient's specific phenotype—early-onset hyperglycemia, significant glycosuria disproportionate to blood glucose levels due to reduced renal threshold, negative autoantibodies, and preserved yet insufficient beta-cell function—is highly specific for *HNF1A*-MODY, satisfying criterion **PP4** (phenotype highly specific for a gene with a single genetic etiology). The convergence of these independent lines of evidence transforms a novel finding from a VUS into a clinically actionable pathogenic variant, enabling the de-escalation of therapy from insulin to agents targeting residual beta-cell function.

### [Morning Report] How does the glucagon stimulation test assist in distinguishing MODY from long-standing Type 1 Diabetes, and what C-peptide threshold typically suggests preserved endogenous insulin secretion consistent with non-autoimmune etiologies?

The glucagon stimulation test serves as a critical functional assay to quantify residual beta-cell mass, helping to distinguish the absolute insulin deficiency characteristic of long-standing Type 1 Diabetes from the preserved, albeit dysfunctional, secretion seen in Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY). In long-standing Type 1 Diabetes, autoimmune destruction typically results in negligible C-peptide responses (<0.2 nmol/L), whereas patients with *HNF1A*-MODY retain demonstrable endogenous insulin secretory capacity despite significant hyperglycemia.

In clinical practice, a stimulated C-peptide level exceeding **0.60 nmol/L** (approximately 1.8 ng/mL) following glucagon administration is widely accepted as the threshold indicating preserved endogenous insulin secretion consistent with non-autoimmune etiologies like MODY. Levels below this cutoff suggest profound beta-cell failure typical of advanced Type 1 Diabetes.

This distinction is vividly illustrated in the reported case of a young man with a novel *HNF1A* mutation. Despite presenting with severe hyperglycemia (HbA1c 10.2%) and symptoms mimicking Type 1 Diabetes, his glucagon stimulation test revealed a baseline C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L that rose to **0.83 nmol/L** at six minutes. This response, surpassing the 0.60 nmol/L threshold, confirmed moderately preserved beta-cell function. Coupled with negative autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A) and a strong multigenerational family history, this biochemical evidence supported the diagnosis of *HNF1A*-MODY over Type 1 Diabetes. Consequently, the identification of preserved secretion allowed clinicians to safely discontinue insulin therapy and transition the patient to agents targeting residual beta-cell function, such as GLP-1 receptor agonists, achieving excellent glycemic control. Thus, the test acts not as a standalone diagnostic but as a pivotal supportive tool that, when integrated with serology and genetics, guides precision medicine decisions.

### [Pathophysiology] Given that HNF1A acts as a transcription factor regulating genes in the liver, kidney, and pancreas, how does the mutation specifically lead to the progressive beta-cell dysfunction observed clinically, distinct from the absolute insulin deficiency of Type 1 Diabetes?

The distinction between the beta-cell dysfunction in *HNF1A*-MODY and the absolute insulin deficiency of Type 1 Diabetes lies in the underlying mechanism: **progressive transcriptional failure versus autoimmune destruction.**

As detailed in the discussion of this case, *HNF1A* encodes a transcription factor critical for the regulation of genes involved in glucose sensing and insulin secretion within the pancreas. Specifically, mutations such as the novel p.N270S variant identified in this patient impair the expression of key targets, including the insulin gene itself, *GLUT2* (the glucose transporter), and enzymes like pyruvate kinase. This creates a "glucose-sensing defect" where the beta cell fails to appropriately recognize rising glucose levels and secrete insulin, leading to a gradual, progressive decline in function rather than an abrupt cessation.

This pathophysiology explains the clinical findings in our patient: the glucagon stimulation test revealed **moderately preserved yet insufficient** endogenous insulin secretion (stimulated C-peptide 0.83 nmol/L). Unlike Type 1 Diabetes, where autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A) mediate the total obliteration of beta-cell mass resulting in absolute insulin dependence and ketoacidosis risk, *HNF1A*-MODY preserves residual beta-cell capacity. Consequently, these patients do not typically present with diabetic ketoacidosis—as seen in this case—and their hyperglycemia responds dramatically to agents that bypass the defective glucose sensor or potentiate secretion, such as sulfonylureas or, as successfully utilized here, GLP-1 receptor agonists. The presence of glycosuria out of proportion to hyperglycemia further corroborates this mechanism, reflecting concurrent *HNF1A*-mediated downregulation of renal SGLT2 transporters, a feature absent in classic Type 1 Diabetes.
