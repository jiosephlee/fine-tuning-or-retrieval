Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY and Mechanisms of Glycosuria

The molecular basis of HNF1A-MODY lies in the disruption of a critical transcriptional regulator encoded by the *HNF1A* gene, located on the long arm of chromosome 12 (12q24). This gene spans approximately 24 kilobases across ten exons and encodes hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1α), a homeodomain-containing protein that functions as a dimeric transcription factor. HNF1α is ubiquitously expressed but plays a disproportionately vital role in tissues central to glucose homeostasis, specifically the pancreatic beta cells, liver, kidney, and intestine. In the context of this case, the patient's novel heterozygous mutation (p.N270S) resides within exon 4, which codes for the DNA-binding domain of the protein. Because HNF1A-MODY follows an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance, the presence of a single mutant allele results in haploinsufficiency or a dominant-negative effect, reducing the total functional pool of HNF1α available to regulate downstream target genes. This genetic defect initiates a cascade of cellular dysfunctions that distinguish MODY from both autoimmune type 1 diabetes and insulin-resistant type 2 diabetes.

In pancreatic beta cells, HNF1α acts as a master switch for genes essential to glucose sensing and insulin secretion. Under normal physiological conditions, HNF1α binds to promoter regions to upregulate the expression of the insulin gene itself, ensuring adequate synthesis of the hormone. Furthermore, it regulates the expression of *SLC2A2*, which encodes GLUT2, the low-affinity glucose transporter responsible for facilitating glucose entry into the beta cell in proportion to ambient blood glucose levels. The mutation identified in this patient compromises these regulatory pathways, leading to diminished GLUT2-mediated glucose uptake and impaired insulin gene transcription. Additionally, HNF1α controls the expression of mitochondrial enzymes, including pyruvate kinase; a deficit here disrupts the glycolytic flux required to generate the ATP surge that triggers calcium influx and subsequent insulin granule exocytosis. Consequently, the hallmark of HNF1A-MODY is a progressive decline in beta-cell function characterized by defective glucose-stimulated insulin secretion rather than absolute insulin deficiency or peripheral resistance. This explains the patient's glucagon stimulation test results, which showed insufficient stimulated C-peptide levels despite negative autoantibodies, reflecting a specific secretory defect rather than beta-cell destruction.

A unique and diagnostically pivotal feature of HNF1A-MODY, clearly illustrated by this patient's presentation of glycosuria alongside hyperglycemia, is the concurrent defect in renal glucose handling. The proximal renal tubules rely heavily on HNF1α for the transcriptional activation of *SLC5A2*, the gene encoding sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2). SGLT2 is responsible for reabsorbing approximately 90% of filtered glucose from the glomerular filtrate back into the bloodstream. In individuals with *HNF1A* mutations, reduced expression of SGLT2 lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. While a healthy individual typically does not spill glucose into the urine until plasma glucose exceeds roughly 180 mg/dL (10 mmol/L), patients with HNF1A-MODY may exhibit glycosuria at significantly lower plasma glucose concentrations. This phenomenon accounts for why glycosuria was detected in this patient during his annual examination; it serves as an early clinical marker that often precedes frank symptomatic hyperglycemia. Clinicians must recognize that in this specific subtype of diabetes, urinary glucose cannot be interpreted solely as a reflection of severe hyperglycemia but also as evidence of intrinsic renal tubular dysfunction driven by the genetic defect.

The natural history of HNF1A-MODY typically progresses from an asymptomatic phase dominated by postprandial hyperglycemia to overt symptomatic diabetes if left untreated. Early in the disease course, fasting glucose levels may remain near normal due to residual beta-cell capacity and hepatic compensation, while post-challenge glucose rises sharply due to the inability to mount a rapid first-phase insulin response. This aligns with literature descriptions where oral glucose tolerance tests reveal significant excursions (>11.1 mmol/L) before fasting criteria are met. Over time, however, as beta-cell mass and function progressively deteriorate—a process accelerated by glucotoxicity—the patient transitions to persistent fasting hyperglycemia and classic symptoms of catabolism, such as polydipsia, polyphagia, and weight loss, as seen in this young man upon referral. Understanding this trajectory is essential for interpreting why initial management strategies designed for type 2 diabetes (targeting insulin resistance) were less optimal than therapies that directly augment insulin secretion or preserve remaining beta-cell function, ultimately guiding the successful transition to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy in this case.
