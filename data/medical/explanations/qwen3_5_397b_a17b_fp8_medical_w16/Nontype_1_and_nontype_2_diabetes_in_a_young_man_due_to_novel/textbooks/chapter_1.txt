Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Dilemma in Early-Onset Hyperglycemia

The clinical presentation of monogenic diabetes, specifically Hepatocyte Nuclear Factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY), frequently mimics the phenotypes of both Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes, creating a significant diagnostic challenge for clinicians. In this case, a young adult male presented with marked hyperglycemia and glycosuria discovered incidentally during a routine health examination. Despite an HbA1c of 10.2% and fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L—levels often associated with severe insulin deficiency—the patient was notably free of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). This absence of ketosis is a critical differentiator; while Type 1 diabetes typically presents with absolute insulin deficiency leading to ketoacidosis, HNF1A-MODY is characterized by a progressive defect in insulin secretion that usually preserves enough basal insulin to prevent lipolysis and ketone formation, even in the setting of profound hyperglycemia. Furthermore, the presence of glycosuria at glucose levels that might not typically trigger renal spillage in healthy individuals reflects a specific pathophysiological hallmark of HNF1A mutations: a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired expression of the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in the proximal tubule.

The patient's family history provided the strongest clue toward a monogenic etiology, revealing a classic pattern of autosomal dominant inheritance. Diabetes was documented in three consecutive generations: the maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at age 20. This vertical transmission of early-onset diabetes without a history of early cardiovascular complications strongly suggests a single-gene disorder rather than the multifactorial inheritance typical of Type 2 diabetes or the sporadic nature of most Type 1 cases. However, the diagnostic picture was complicated by conflicting biochemical markers. While the glucagon stimulation test revealed insufficient stimulated C-peptide (0.83 nmol/L), indicating beta-cell dysfunction, it also confirmed that endogenous insulin production was not entirely absent, arguing against long-standing Type 1 diabetes. Additionally, the negativity for islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A) further reduced the likelihood of autoimmune destruction, yet the clinical severity initially obscured the possibility of MODY.

Physical examination and metabolic profiling introduced further ambiguity regarding the distinction between MODY and Type 2 diabetes. The patient's Body Mass Index (BMI) was 24.6 kg/m², placing him in a range where obesity-related insulin resistance cannot be immediately ruled out, especially given rising global obesity rates. His lipid profile showed elevated triglycerides (4.31 mmol/L) and low HDL cholesterol, features consistent with metabolic syndrome often seen in Type 2 diabetes. Conversely, the absence of acanthosis nigricans—a cutaneous marker strongly associated with severe insulin resistance—argued against a primary mechanism of peripheral insulin resistance. This case illustrates the limitation of relying solely on BMI or the presence of metabolic syndrome traits to exclude MODY; as obesity becomes more prevalent in the general population, patients with monogenic diabetes may coincidentally exhibit overweight status or dyslipidemia, thereby masking their underlying genetic diagnosis. Consequently, without a high index of suspicion triggered by the specific combination of non-ketotic hyperglycemia, glycosuria out of proportion to blood glucose, and multigenerational family history, such patients are at high risk of being misclassified as having atypical Type 1 or early-onset Type 2 diabetes, delaying appropriate genetic testing and precision therapy.
