# When Angioedema Isn't: The Critical Role of CK in Unexplained Neck Swelling

We love a good diagnostic pivot in the Emergency Department. It's the moment the chart stops making sense as "common thing X" and suddenly clicks into "rare thing Y," changing the entire trajectory of care. But sometimes, that pivot gets delayed because we get stuck in a silo. We call ENT for neck swelling, allergy for hives, or ID for fever, forgetting that systemic diseases often wear local masks.

Consider this: A man in his 40s rolls into your ED with a terrifyingly swollen neck. He looks like he has Ludwig's angina or maybe severe idiopathic angioedema. He isn't febrile, his airway is patent (for now), but the swelling is dramatic, involving the strap muscles and extending posteriorly. You rule out infection. You rule out bradykinin-mediated pathways. C1 esterase inhibitor is normal. Complement levels are fine. No ACE inhibitors on board. So, what's left? If you stop looking at the neck and start looking at the *whole* patient—specifically those fingernails and hip flexors—you might catch a diagnosis that demands immediate, aggressive immunosuppression rather than just observation or antihistamines.

This isn't a hypothetical trap; it's a real case of dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema. While we typically associate dermatomyositis with the classic heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules, this patient presented primarily with non-pitting cervical edema and dysphagia. The swelling wasn't fluid leaking from vessels due to histamine or bradykinin; it was inflammatory edema driven by severe myositis of the neck musculature. The clue that broke the case open wasn't a fancy MRI or a specialized antibody panel ordered in the ED—it was a simple Creatine Kinase (CK) level that came back astronomically elevated at over 16,000 U/L.

The danger here is twofold. First, misdiagnosing this as infectious deep neck space infection could lead to unnecessary surgical exploration or broad-spectrum antibiotics that do nothing for autoimmune inflammation. Second, and more critically, delaying the diagnosis of dermatomyositis allows unchecked muscle destruction and potential respiratory compromise from pharyngeal weakness and aspiration. In this specific case, the patient had anti-SAE1 antibodies, a subset known to carry risks of interstitial lung disease and malignancy, making rapid identification even more vital. The "angioedema" was actually a red flag waving desperately for help from the muscular system, obscured by the dramatic presentation in the neck.

We often relegate CK checks to rhabdomyolysis protocols or obvious trauma cases. However, this case argues forcefully that unexplained cervical edema, particularly when paired with even subtle proximal weakness or nailfold changes (like periungual telangiectasias or microhemorrhages), mandates a CK check immediately. Don't wait for the rheumatology consult three days later when the patient is already intubated for aspiration pneumonia or worsening weakness. The physical exam nuances matter: look beyond the swollen neck to the thighs, check the nailfolds with a magnifier or capillaroscope if available, and ask about difficulty swallowing or climbing stairs. Those subtle findings transform a confusing ENT referral into a clear-cut medical emergency requiring steroids and IVIG.

The takeaway isn't just to memorize SAE1 antibodies; it's to broaden our differential for "swollen neck" beyond infection and allergy. When the story doesn't fit angioedema—no itch, no response to standard therapy, normal complements—and it doesn't fit infection—no fever, sterile cultures—think muscle. Think inflammatory myopathy. A single blood test can prevent weeks of diagnostic odyssey and initiate life-saving treatment before irreversible damage occurs.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Unexplained Cervical Edema Requires a CK:** In any patient presenting with neck swelling where infection (Ludwig's angina) and standard angioedema (histaminergic/bradykinin-mediated) have been ruled out, order a Creatine Kinase level immediately to screen for inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis.
*   **Look Beyond the Neck:** Subtle cutaneous signs such as periungual erythema, nailfold microhemorrhages, or a faint holster sign on the thighs can be the only external clues pointing toward dermatomyositis in patients presenting primarily with visceral or muscular symptoms.
*   **Dysphagia is a Red Flag:** Difficulty swallowing in the context of neck swelling may indicate pharyngeal muscle involvement rather than just mechanical obstruction; this carries a high risk for aspiration pneumonitis and warrants urgent evaluation for myositis.
*   **Pseudoangioedema Exists:** Dermatomyositis can cause significant subcutaneous edema due to muscle inflammation and vascular permeability changes that mimic angioedema but will not respond to epinephrine, antihistamines, or C1-inhibitor concentrates.
*   **Early Immunosuppression Matters:** Prompt recognition allows for early initiation of high-dose corticosteroids and adjunctive therapies (IVIG, rituximab), which are crucial for preserving muscle function and reducing mortality associated with respiratory failure and malignancy risk in subsets like anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis.



# Beyond Gottron's Papules: Decoding Subtle Cutaneous Clues in Anti-SAE1 Dermatomyositis

We are all trained to spot the classics. When you hear "dermatomyositis," your brain immediately pulls up the flashcards: heliotrope rash over the eyelids, Gottron's papules on the knuckles, and the shawl sign across the shoulders. It's a visual diagnosis, or so we think. But what happens when the patient walks into your ED with a massive, terrifyingly swollen neck, no fever, and absolutely none of those textbook rashes? If you're waiting for the classic triad, you might miss a diagnosis that is actively destroying your patient's muscles and threatening their airway.

Consider the case of a man in his 40s who rolled into the Emergency Department looking like he was in the middle of a severe anaphylactic reaction or a deep neck space infection. He had progressive neck swelling for three weeks and difficulty swallowing. The ENT team ruled out Ludwig's angina; the CT scan showed diffuse edema involving the strap muscles but no abscess. The immunology team was consulted for suspected idiopathic angioedema. He wasn't on an ACE inhibitor, his C1 esterase levels were normal, and he had no history of allergic reactions. He was a diagnostic orphan—swollen, weak, and slipping through the cracks of our standard algorithms because he didn't look like the pictures in our textbooks.

The pivot point in this case wasn't a new imaging modality or a exotic genetic test; it was a stubborn clinician who noticed that "angioedema" doesn't typically come with proximal muscle weakness and weird nail changes. While the classic heliotrope eruption and Gottron's papules were conspicuously absent, a closer look revealed subtle periungual erythema, nailfold microhemorrhages, and generalized facial redness that spared nothing—not even the nasolabial folds (a clue that often distinguishes this from lupus). A single Creatine Kinase (CK) level returned at a staggering 16,184 U/L. This wasn't angioedema; it was anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis masquerading as pseudoangioedema.

This case serves as a brutal reminder that dermatomyositis is a chameleon. Specifically, the anti-SAE1 phenotype is notorious for flying under the radar. Unlike other myositis subsets where muscle weakness screams for attention early on, SAE1-positive patients often present with prominent subcutaneous edema—particularly in the face and neck—that mimics angioedema or cellulitis. The swelling isn't mediated by histamine or bradykinin; it's driven by intense inflammation of the underlying musculature and connective tissue leaking fluid into the subcutaneous spaces. In one large cohort study, only about 6% of dermatomyositis patients presented with this "pseudoangioedema," making it a rare but critical trap for the unwary emergency physician.

The danger here lies in the delay. This patient had significant dysphagia and aspiration risk due to pharyngeal muscle involvement before anyone suspected an inflammatory myopathy. Had we continued down the path of treating refractory angioedema or searching for an elusive odontogenic source, we would have allowed unchecked rhabdomyolysis and potential respiratory compromise to progress. The key was connecting the dots between non-pitting neck swelling, subtle cutaneous markers like the "holster sign" (erythema on the lateral thigh), and unexplained weakness. Once the CK was checked and the myositis panel sent (which came back positive for anti-SAE1), the trajectory changed instantly from diagnostic confusion to aggressive immunosuppression with steroids, IVIG, and rituximab.

Don't let the absence of classic signs give you false reassurance. In patients with unexplained neck or facial swelling, especially when accompanied by even mild proximal weakness or dysphagia, you must broaden your differential beyond allergy and infection. Look at the nails. Look at the thighs. Check a CK. Recognizing this "wolf in sheep's clothing" phenotype of dermatomyositis can prevent catastrophic muscle breakdown and secure an airway before it's too late.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Pseudoangioedema is a real entity:** Unexplained neck or facial swelling without urticaria or response to antihistamines/epinephrine should trigger a workup for inflammatory myopathy, particularly if C1 esterase studies are normal.
*   **Look past the classics:** Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis often lacks hallmark heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules; instead, hunt for subtle signs like periungual telangiectasias/microhemorrhages, nonspecific facial erythema involving nasolabial folds, and the "holster sign" on lateral thighs.
*   **CK is your best friend:** In any patient with mysterious soft tissue swelling and concurrent proximal weakness or dysphagia, order a Creatine Kinase immediately; levels can be astronomically elevated (>10,000 U/L) even when cutaneous signs are minimal.
*   **Airway protection is paramount:** Swelling in dermatomyositis involves muscle inflammation leading to dysphagia and aspiration risk; early recognition allows for prompt initiation of high-dose steroids and IVIG to halt progression before respiratory failure occurs.
*   **Malignancy screening is mandatory:** Patients with dermatomyositis (especially males with dysphagia and specific antibody profiles like SAE1) carry an elevated risk of occult malignancy and require comprehensive age-appropriate cancer screening upon diagnosis.



# The Airway Threat You Can't Intubate Away: Managing Dysphagia and Aspiration in Acute Myositis

We spend a lot of time in the ED worrying about the airway that swells shut. We think histamine, we think bradykinin, and we reach for epinephrine, icatibant, or a difficult airway cart. But what happens when the airway isn't obstructed by edema, but rather collapses because the muscles powering it have simply given up?

A recent case report by Chan et al. describes a terrifying diagnostic trap: a man in his 40s presenting with massive neck swelling and dysphagia who was bounced between ENT and Immunology with working diagnoses of Ludwig's angina and idiopathic angioedema. The truth was far more insidious. He didn't have an allergic reaction or a deep space infection; he had **dermatomyositis**. Specifically, an anti-SAE1 antibody-positive variant causing such profound inflammation of the strap muscles and pharyngeal musculature that it mimicked angioedema while silently setting him up for catastrophic aspiration.

This case forces us to confront a brutal reality: a patent glottis on scope does not equal a safe airway. When the pump fails, the pipe doesn't matter.

## The Masquerade: Why It Looked Like Angioedema

The patient arrived with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling and hip flexor weakness. To the initial team, the diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space screamed "deep neck infection" or "angioedema." He was afebrile, his white count was normal, and he wasn't tachypneic, which should have paused the infectious disease train early on. Yet, without a clear infectious source, he was labeled as having idiopathic angioedema.

Here is where clinical gestalt needs to override the anchor bias. True angioedema is mediated by histamine or bradykinin. It is vascular permeability run amok. But this patient had something else: **pseudoangioedema**. In dermatomyositis, intense inflammation of the underlying muscle and subcutaneous tissues can create massive swelling that looks identical to angioedema but responds to none of the standard therapies.

The clues were there if we looked past the neck. He had proximal weakness (4/5 hip flexors), subtle periungual telangiectasias (nailfold microhemorrhages), and generalized facial erythema sparing nothing—not even the nasolabial folds (unlike lupus). But perhaps the most glaring red flag was the disconnect between his "swollen throat" and his exam. ENT scoped him and found no anatomical obstruction in the larynx or vallecula. Instead, they saw increased secretions and coughing. The anatomy was fine; the physiology was broken.

## The Pathophysiology of Bulbar Failure

In classic anaphylaxis or hereditary angioedema, we fear laryngeal edema closing the door. In acute inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis, the threat is **bulbar muscle paralysis**.

The inflammation in this patient wasn't just cosmetic; it was destroying the contractile ability of the pharyngeal constrictors and the upper esophageal sphincter. This leads to severe dysphagia where food and saliva don't go down; they sit in the pyriform fossae until they spill over into the trachea. This is silent aspiration waiting to happen. The patient's CT chest already showed patchy peribronchial consolidation from micro-aspiration before he even received a definitive diagnosis.

The mechanism here is distinct from neuropathic causes like Myasthenia Gravis or Guillain-Barré. This is direct myocyte necrosis driven by complement deposition (C5b-9) in the perifascicular capillaries. The muscle fibers are literally being eaten alive by the immune system, leading to skyrocketing enzymes—this patient's CK hit **16,184 U/L**. That degree of rhabdomyolysis-level destruction in the neck muscles explains why simple anti-edema measures failed miserably.

## Diagnostic Pitfalls: Why Scope Isn't Enough

One ofthe most critical teaching points from this case is the limitation of flexible nasendoscopy in this context. An ENT scope is fantastic for visualizing static anatomy: tumors, abscesses, or frank laryngeal edema. It showed this patient had no structural blockage. However, swallowing is a dynamic event involving complex coordination of over 25 muscle pairs.

You cannot diagnose functional dysphagia with a camera sitting still in the nose. You need to see the swallow in action. This patient required a **video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow study**, which revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with residue pooling in the pyriform fossae and penetration of fluids to the vocal cords. By relying solely on the scope, we risk missing impending respiratory failure caused by aspiration pneumonia rather than upper airway obstruction. If your patient has "neck swelling" and weakness, skip straight to checking CK and ordering a swallow study if they are clearing their throat or coughing with liquids.

## Aggressive Immunosuppression: The Only Way Out

Once the diagnosis of anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis was confirmed via antibody panel and muscle biopsy showing perifascicular necrosis, half-measures were off the table. This is not a condition you watch overnight; it is a race against multiorgan damage and respiratory collapse.

The treatment regimen required was heavy artillery:
*   **Pulse Steroids:** 3 days of IV methylprednisolone 1g daily to halt immediate inflammation.
*   **IVIG:** A loading dose of 2g/kg followed by monthly cycles to modulate the immune response rapidly without waiting for oral agents to kick in.
*   **Steroid-Sparing Agents:** Mycophenolate mofetil and Rituximab were added early to maintain remission and allow for steroid tapering.

Notably, methotrexate was avoided due to concerns regarding interstitial lung disease (ILD), which is common in myositis subsets (though less so with SAE1 than others) and this patient already had radiographic evidence of aspiration pneumonitis complicating his picture.The outcome underscores urgency: despite 21 days inpatient and persistent dysphagia requiring an NG tube at discharge, aggressive therapy eventually normalized his CK within 3 weeks and restored full swallowing function by 7 months Had we hesitated or misdiagnosed this as refractory angioedema, irreversible muscle fibrosis or fatal aspiration could have ensued quickly."/>

# Steroids Alone Aren't Enough: Navigating Escalated Immunosuppression in Severe Dermatomyositis

We've all seen the patient with a swollen neck in the ED. The differential usually narrows quickly to the "big hitters": anaphylaxis, angioedema (histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated), or a deep neck space infection like Ludwig's angina. But what happens when the CT shows diffuse edema without an abscess, the airway is patent but the patient can't swallow, and the allergy workup comes back stone-cold normal?

In a fascinating case recently published by Chan et al., a man in his 40s presented with exactly this conundrum. He arrived with progressive neck swelling and hip weakness, initially bouncing between ENT and Immunology teams under the working diagnosis of idiopathic angioedema. He wasn't on an ACE inhibitor, his C1 esterase levels were normal, and he lacked the classic fever of an infectious process. The clue that cracked the code wasn't in the neck itself, but in the labs: a Creatine Kinase (CK) level that skyrocketed to **16,184 U/L**.

This wasn't angioedema; it was dermatomyositis masquerading as one. Specifically, it was anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis, a rare subset known for severe cutaneous and muscular involvement. While the diagnostic pivot from "swollen neck" to "autoimmune myopathy" is a great story for grand rounds, the real teaching point lies in how this team managed the fallout. With life-threatening dysphagia, massive muscle necrosis, and aspiration pneumonitis, this case perfectly illustrates why high-dose steroids alone are often insufficient for severe induction therapy.

## The Diagnostic Trap: When Angioedema Isn't Angioedema

Before diving into treatment, we have to appreciate how easily this diagnosis can be missed. Dermatomyositis typically presents with Gottron's papules or a heliotrope rash, but these classic signs were absent or subtle in our patient initially. Instead, he presented with "pseudoangioedema"—non-pitting edema caused by profound inflammation of the underlying musculature and subcutaneous tissues rather than vascular permeability issues.

The presence of proximal weakness (4/5 hip flexors) should always trigger a CK check in the setting of unexplained soft tissue swelling. Once the CK came back >16,000 U/L alongside elevated transaminases (AST > ALT), the picture shifted instantly from an allergic reaction to a fulminant inflammatory myopathy. The confirmation came via a positive anti-SAE1 antibody and a muscle biopsy showing perifascicular necrosis and C5b-9 deposition. This specific antibody subtype is notorious for severe skin disease and dysphagia, occurring in nearly half of reported cases, yet it remains obscure enough to delay care if you aren't looking for it.

## Beyond the Steroid Burst: Rationale for Multi-Agent Induction

When facing a CK of 16,000 U/L and a patient who cannot protect their airway due to pharyngeal muscle paralysis, pulling out the prednisone bottle feels inadequate. In this case, the treating team recognized that monotherapy with corticosteroids would likely be too slow to halt the rapid muscle destruction and too risky regarding long-term toxicity at the required doses. They opted for an aggressive, multi-modal induction strategy immediately upon admission.

The backbone of therapy was undeniably heavy steroids: 3 days of IV methylprednisolone at 1 g/day followed by a high-dose oral taper. However, they didn't stop there. They layered on Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG), Rituximab, and Mycophenolate Mofetil simultaneously. This "kitchen sink" approach might seem aggressive to some, but in severe dermatomyositis with visceral involvement (like esophageal striated muscle leading to aspiration), time is muscle—literally. Early combination therapy has been shown to improve outcomes and reduce cumulative steroid exposure compared to stepwise escalation only after failure.

## Agent Selection: Tailoring Therapy to Comorbidities

One of the most clinically nuanced decisions in this case was the choice of steroid-sparing agents. Traditionally, Methotrexate is often the first-line adjunct for dermatomyositis. However, this patient had pulmonary complications—not interstitial lung disease (ILD) directly from the myositis initially, but significant aspiration pneumonitis and ground-glass opacities on CT.

Methotrexate carries a known risk of pneumonitis and can exacerbate existing lung pathology. Given the patient's respiratory compromise and abnormal pulmonary function tests showing a restrictive pattern, the team wisely avoided Methotrexate entirely. Instead, they chose **Mycophenolate Mofetil**, which has a favorable safety profile regarding lung toxicity and is increasingly favored in connective tissue diseases with pulmonary involvement.

Furthermore, they added **Rituximab** early in the course. While often reserved for refractory cases or specific antibody subtypes (like anti-Jo-1 or anti-MDA5), emerging data supports its use in severe refractory dermatomyositis to achieve rapid B-cell depletion. Combined with **IVIG**, which provides immediate passive immunity and modulates Fc receptors to dampen inflammation rapidly, this triple-threat regimen addressed both immediate stabilization and long-term remission induction.

## Managing the Risks: Prophylaxis in Heavy Immunosuppression

Throwing four potent immunomodulators at a patient inevitably opens Pandora's box regarding infectious risks. The combination of high-dose steroids (>20 mg prednisone equivalent), Rituximab (which depletes B-cells for months), Mycophenolate (T-cell suppression), and IVIG creates a perfect storm for opportunistic infections.

The team correctly identified *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia (PJP) as a primary threat and initiated prophylaxis with Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX). However, even standard prophylaxis isn't without pitfalls; this patient developed hemolytic anemia despite having normal G6PD levels—a reminder that sulfa drugs can cause idiosyncratic reactions even outside of enzymatic deficiencies. The team pivoted gracefully to nebulized Pentamidine, ensuring coverage was maintained without compromising hematologic recovery. Additionally, because Rituximab poses a risk for Hepatitis B reactivation and Varicella Zoster reactivation thorough viral screening was mandatory before initiation (all negative in this case), highlighting that you cannot skip your pre-biologic checklist even in urgent scenarios unless absolutely necessary for survival seconds away from intubation!

# The Malignancy Shadow: Risk Stratification and Screening Protocols for New-Onset Dermatomyositis

We often think of "masqueraders" in the ED as infections hiding behind sterile presentations or cardiac issues mimicking gastritis. But sometimes, the masquerade goes deeper. Consider a man in his 40s rolling into your resuscitation bay with a swollen neck, struggling to swallow, and looking like he's having a severe allergic reaction. Your mind jumps to angioedema—maybe bradykinin-mediated? Did he start an ACE inhibitor? Is this Ludwig's angina? You rule out the airway compromise from infection, check the complement levels (normal), and send him to ENT who finds nothing obstructive.

Here is where the plot twists. That "angioedema" isn't histamine or bradykinin driven; it's inflammatory myopathy leaking into the subcutaneous tissues. In a recent striking case report, a patient presented exactly this way: massive cervical edema and dysphagia, initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. It wasn't until someone noticed subtle periungual changes and ordered a creatine kinase (CK) that the true culprit was revealed: **Anti-SAE1 positive Dermatomyositis**. His CK was astronomical at 16,184 U/L. While saving his muscles and airway was the immediate fire fight, lighting that diagnostic fuse ignites a second, more ominous concern: *What cancer is driving this?*

## When Angioedema Isn't Angioedema

Before we dive into the oncology workup, we have to appreciate why this case is such a critical learning point for emergency physicians and hospitalists. Dermatomyositis (DM) is rare, occurring in about 10 per million people, but its ability to mimic other conditions is legendary. The classic textbook description involves Gottron's papules and heliotrope rashes, but reality is messier. As our case demonstrates, you might not see the classic rash immediately. Instead, you see "pseudoangioedema."

This subcutaneous edema results from severe inflammation of the underlying musculature and fascia, particularly in the neck and face. If you miss the connection between unexplained neck swelling, proximal weakness (even if subtle initially), and dysphagia, you delay not just immunosuppression but also vital cancer screening. The clue in this case wasn't just the swelling; it was the constellation of dysphagia, elevated liver enzymes (often a proxy for muscle breakdown when AST > ALT), and those tiny nailfold capillary changes. Once the CK came back hot, the diagnosis shifted from allergy to autoimmunity, opening the door to the most dangerous aspect of new-onset DM: paraneoplastic malignancy.

## The Paraneoplastic Link: Why We Screen Aggressively

Dermatomyositis is not just an autoimmune disease; it is frequently a paraneoplastic syndrome. The association between DM and underlying malignancy is well-documented, with estimates suggesting that up to 20-25% of adult DM cases are associated with cancer. However, not all DM patients carry the same risk. This is where precision medicine meets practical workflow. We don't just throw a "pan-scan" at everyone blindly; we stratify based on specific clinical and serological markers.

Our patient with Anti-SAE1 antibodies presents a fascinating risk profile. Historically, antibodies like Anti-TIF1-γ and Anti-NXP-2 have been flagged as high-risk for malignancy. Anti-SAE1 is less commonly discussed in general circles but carries its own weight. In large cohort studies, SAE1-positive patients show significant rates of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease, with malignancy rates hovering around 18%. While slightly lower than TIF1-γ associations, it is far from negligible. When you combine the antibody status with clinical features like male sex (which generally carries higher cancer risk in DM than female sex) and severe dysphagia, this patient rockets to the top of the risk pyramid. Ignoring this stratification means potentially missing a curable cancer while treating the symptomatology of its immune response.

## Applying the IMACS Guidelines at the Bedside

So, how do we operationalize this fear into action? The International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) has provided robust guidelines for cancer screening in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies. They move us away from vague "age-appropriate screening" toward risk-adjusted protocols. For our patient, the math was clear: he had two high-risk factors (the diagnosis of dermatomyositis itself plus dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (male sex and SAE1 positivity). This categorizes him as **High Risk**, demanding an aggressive diagnostic sweep immediately upon diagnosis.

The workup for high-risk patients extends beyond standard physical exams and basic labs. It requires a multimodal approach including cross-sectional imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis—which in this case revealed pulmonary nodules (later deemed likely aspiration/inflammatory but requiring vigilance) and necessitated ruling out metastasis via PET scan. Crucially, because CT scans can miss mucosal lesions in the GI tract, guidelines recommend endoscopic evaluation (gastroscopy and colonoscopy) even in asymptomatic patients if they fall into high-risk categories. In our case report, despite negative tumor markers (CEA, CA-19-9, PSA) and reassuring initial imaging, the plan rightly included invasive GI scope screening because clinical suspicion outweighed negative biomarkers. This comprehensive net is essential because early detection of the underlying malignancy often leads to remission of the myositis itself once the tumor is resected or treated.
