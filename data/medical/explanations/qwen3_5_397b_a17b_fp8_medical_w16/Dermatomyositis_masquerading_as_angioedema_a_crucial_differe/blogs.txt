Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# When Angioedema Isn't: The 'Pseudo-Angioedema' of Dermatomyositis

We've all been there. A middle-aged patient rolls into the ED with a swollen neck, looking terrifyingly close to losing their airway. Your mind immediately races through the usual suspects: anaphylaxis, ACE-inhibitor induced angioedema, hereditary angioedema, or perhaps a nasty deep neck space infection like Ludwig's angina. You check the lips and tongue—they might be puffy, but the swelling seems centered on the neck straps. The patient isn't wheezing, they aren't hypotensive, and they definitely aren't itching. You consult ENT, who scopes them and finds no abscess, just diffuse edema. You consult Immunology, who runs C1 esterase levels that come back normal. Now what?

Before you label this "idiopathic" and send them home with a prayer and an EpiPen, consider a mimic that hides in plain sight: **dermatomyositis**. Specifically, a rare presentation where severe myositis creates enough inflammation and capillary leak to simulate angioedema. This phenomenon, often termed "pseudo-angioedema," is a diagnostic trap that can delay life-saving immunosuppression. A recent case report highlights exactly how this plays out: a man in his 40s presenting with progressive neck swelling and hip weakness, bounced between ENT and Immunology for suspected angioedema, only to be saved by a single lab test that many of us forget to order in this context: the Creatine Kinase (CK).

The pathophysiology here is fascinating and distinct from the allergic or bradykinin pathways we are used to managing. In classic angioedema, we are dealing with mast cell degranulation (histamine) or unchecked bradykinin production causing vascular permeability. In dermatomyositis-associated pseudo-angioedema, the swelling is mechanical and inflammatory in origin. It stems from severe inflammation of the underlying musculature—specifically the strap muscles of the neck—and the associated subcutaneous tissues. The inflammatory cascade leads to significant capillary leak and edema that mimics the soft tissue swelling of angioedema but lacks the mediators. Consequently, these patients will not respond to epinephrine, antihistamines, steroids (in the short term for allergy), or icatibant. In the case we are reviewing, the patient had diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles extending to the retropharyngeal space, yet he was afebrile with a relatively modest CRP elevation compared to what you'd expect from a fulminant necrotizing fasciitis or deep space abscess. The clue wasn't just the swelling; it was the *context*.

So, how do you spot this before the patient develops frank rhabdomyolysis or respiratory failure? The devil is in the details of the history and physical exam. While our patient lacked the textbook heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules initially—a common occurrence as skin signs can be subtle or appear later—he did have proximal muscle weakness (4/5 in hip flexors) and dysphagia. He also had nailfold changes: periungual erythema and microhemorrhages visible on capillaroscopy. These are your red flags. If a patient presents with "angioedema" but denies pruritus, has normal complement levels, no exposure to offending drugs like ACE inhibitors, and complains of difficulty swallowing or climbing stairs, your differential must expand beyond allergies. The presence of elevated transaminases (AST/ALT) in isolation should also ring alarm bells; remember that AST and ALT are found in skeletal muscle too. An elevated AST out of proportion to ALT, or elevated LDH alongside "liver enzymes," often points to muscle breakdown rather than hepatocellular injury.

The turning point in this case was the decision to check a CK level once infectious and allergic causes were ruled out. The result was staggering: **16,184 U/L**. This isn't just elevated; it's screaming "myositis." Further workup confirmed anti-SAE1 antibodies, a specific marker for a subset of dermatomyositis known to carry higher risks of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease. The MRI confirmed active myositis in the neck and pelvic girdle, and muscle biopsy showed the hallmark perifascicular atrophy and complement deposition (C5b-9) diagnostic of dermatomyositis. This wasn't an allergic reaction; it was an autoimmune assault on the muscles driving fluid into the surrounding tissues. Treatment required heavy artillery: pulse dose IV methylprednisolone, IVIG, rituximab, and mycophenolate mofetil—not benadryl and observation.

This case serves as a potent reminder that not all swelling is angioedema, and not all "liver enzyme" elevations are hepatic. When faced with unexplained neck swelling where infection and allergy have been excluded, particularly in the presence of proximal weakness or dysphagia, you must pivot quickly to evaluate for inflammatory myopathy. Missing this diagnosis delays critical immunosuppression and risks progression to severe respiratory compromise from both muscle weakness and aspiration (as seen in this patient's pneumonitis). A simple CK level is low-cost, high-yield insurance against missing this crucial diagnosis. Don't let dermatomyositas masquerade as angioedema on your watch; look for the weakness beneath the swelling.

### Take-Home Points

*   **Consider "Pseudo-Angioedema" in Unexplained Neck Swelling:** If a patient presents with neck/facial swelling but lacks pruritus, urticaria, or response to standard angioedema therapy (antihistamines/epinephrine), broaden your differential to include inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis.
*   **Don't Ignore Proximal Weakness or Dysphagia:** Even subtle complaints of difficulty rising from a chair or swallowing liquids alongside neck swelling are major red flags for myositis rather than isolated angioedema.
*   **Check a CK Level Early:** In cases of suspected idiopathic angioedema with atypical features (e.g., concurrent weakness, elevated AST/ALT/LDH without liver pathology), obtain a Creatine Kinase (CK) level immediately to rule out rhabdomyolysis secondary to myositis.
*   **Look Beyond Classic Rashes:** Absence of Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash does not rule out dermatomyositis; examine carefully for subtle nailfold changes (periungual erythema/telangiectasia) which may be present even when classic cutaneous signs are absent ("sinus dermatomyositis").
*   **Recognize Anti-SAE1 Risks:** Patients with anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis have a high prevalence of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease; early recognition allows for prompt aggressive immunosuppression (steroids + IVIG/biologics) to prevent airway compromise and aspiration pneumonia.



# Beyond Gottron's Papules: Subtle Cutaneous Clues in Seronegative-Looking Myositis

We love a classic presentation in medicine. The patient walks in with a heliotrope rash, Gottron's papules, and symmetric proximal weakness, and the diagnosis of dermatomyositis practically writes itself. But real life rarely adheres to the textbook illustrations. What happens when the hallmark signs are missing, and the patient presents instead with something that looks suspiciously like an airway emergency?

Consider a man in his 40s who rolls into your Emergency Department with severe, progressive neck swelling. He has no fever, his white count is barely elevated, and he isn't tachypneic, yet his CT scan shows diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space. Your mind immediately goes to the dangerous stuff: Ludwig's angina, deep neck space infection, or perhaps idiopathic angioedema threatening the airway. ENT gets consulted, scopes the throat, finds no abscess, and sends him home. He comes back. The swelling persists. The workup for histaminergic and bradykinin-mediated angioedema is negative. C1 esterase inhibitor levels are normal.

This is exactly where many of us might get stuck in a diagnostic loop of "idiopathic" swelling. But in this specific case, the breakthrough didn't come from repeating the CT scan or trying another dose of epinephrine. It came from a meticulous physical exam that looked beyond the obvious swelling to find the subtle stigmata hiding in plain sight: periungual microhemorrhages, avascular areas on nailfold capillaroscopy, generalized erythema over the nasolabial folds, and a rash on the lateral thigh known as the "holster sign." Despite the complete absence of Gottron's papules or a classic heliotrope eruption, these minor cutaneous clues triggered a creatine kinase (CK) check. The result? A staggering CK of 16,184 U/L. This wasn't angioedema; it was anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis masquerading as a surgical emergency.

The concept of "pseudoangioedema" in dermatomyositis is rare, occurring in less than 7% of patients, yet it represents a critical pitfall for emergency clinicians. When inflammation targets the subcutaneous tissues and underlying musculature of the neck, it mimics fluid-mediated angioedema perfectly. However, the mechanism is entirely different—it's myositis-driven edema, not mast cell degranulation or complement consumption. If you miss this distinction, you delay the intense immunosuppression required to halt rapid muscle destruction and prevent fatal complications like aspiration pneumonitis or respiratory failure due to diaphragmatic involvement. In our patient, the dysphagia was already severe enough to cause micro-aspiration and pulmonary consolidations by the time of diagnosis.

The serology here adds another layer of complexity and learning. This patient was positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies, a subset of dermatomyositis increasingly recognized for its association with severe cutaneous disease and dysphagia, even when classic rashes are muted or absent. While anti-MDA5 or anti-TIF1-γ antibodies are more famously linked to edema and malignancy respectively, SAE1 positivity should still raise your index of suspicion for aggressive disease requiring broad malignancy screening. The takeaway isn't just about knowing the antibody panels; it's about recognizing that a "negative" exam for classic dermatomyositis signs doesn't rule out the disease if you haven't looked at the nails or the lateral thighs.

In the chaos of the ED, it is easy to focus on the life-threatening airway swelling and stop looking once infection and allergic reactions are ruled out. But when faced with unexplained neck edema—especially if accompanied by even vague complaints of fatigue or difficulty swallowing—you must broaden your differential to include inflammatory myopathies. A quick look at the nailfolds for telangiectasias or micro-infarcts and checking proximal strength can pivot your management from antihistamines and antibiotics to high-dose steroids and IVIG before irreversible damage sets in. Don't let the absence of Gottron's papules give you false reassurance; sometimes the devil—and the diagnosis—is in the details you almost missed.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Pseudoangioedema is a real entity:** Unexplained neck or facial swelling without urticaria or response to standard angioedema therapy should trigger consideration of dermatomyositis-induced myositis edema.
*   **Look beyond the classic rash:** Up to 20-30% of dermatomyositis patients may lack pathognomonic signs like Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash at presentation; actively search for subtle markers like periungual telangiectasias, nailfold capillary dropout/microhemorrhages, shawl sign, V-sign, or holster sign (rash on lateral thighs).
*   **Check a CK early:** In any patient with unexplained soft tissue swelling plus proximal weakness (even subtle) or dysphagia, order a creatine kinase immediately regardless of whether they fit the "classic" phenotype.
*   **Don't ignore dysphagia:** Difficulty swallowing in this context is often due to striated muscle involvement (cricopharyngeal dysfunction) rather than mucosal pathology; it carries a high risk for aspiration pneumonia and warrants urgent videofluoroscopy if suspected.
*   **Malignancy screening is mandatory:** Patients with dermatomyositis (particularly those with dysphagia or specific antibody profiles like SAE1) carry an elevated risk of occult malignancy; initiate age-appropriate cancer screening plus extended imaging (CT/PET) per IMACS guidelines upon diagnosis.



# The Anti-SAE1 Phenotype: Dysphagia, Edema, and Malignancy Risk

We've all seen the patient with "idiopathic" neck swelling in the ED. The workup is a reflex: rule out Ludwig's angina, check for anaphylaxis, maybe throw in a C1-esterase inhibitor level if you're feeling fancy about angioedema. But what happens when the CT shows diffuse strap muscle edema, the airway is patent but the patient can't swallow, and the classic allergic or infectious markers are stone cold normal?

Meet a case that should fundamentally change how you approach unexplained cervical edema. A man in his 40s rolled into the ED with three weeks of progressive neck swelling and hip weakness. He wasn't febrile. He wasn't wheezing. ENT scoped him and found nothing obstructing the airway, just some coughing and secretions. He was bounced to Immunology for "suspected idiopathic angioedema."

Here is where the plot twists: this wasn't histamine, and it wasn't bradykinin. It was an autoimmune storm masquerading as a swollen neck. When someone finally ordered a creatine kinase (CK), it came back at a staggering **16,184 U/L**. The diagnosis? Dermatomyositis, specifically the anti-SAE1 positive phenotype. This isn't just a rare curiosity; it represents a distinct clinical entity with a triad of severe dysphagia, cutaneous edema, and a non-trivial risk of underlying malignancy that demands immediate recognition and aggressive management.

## When Angioedema Isn't Angioedema

The term "pseudoangioedema" gets thrown around loosely, but in the context of dermatomyositis (DM), it describes a very specific pathophysiology. Unlike true angioedema driven by mast cell degranulation or kinin release, this swelling is the result of profound inflammation within the muscle and subcutaneous tissues themselves. In our case, the CT didn't show fluid collections typical of infection; it showed diffuse edema involving the strap muscles extending to the retropharyngeal space.

The trap here is focusing solely on the neck. Early in the course, muscle weakness can be subtle or overshadowed by the dramatic soft tissue swelling. Our patient had 4/5 hip flexor strength—easy to miss if you aren't actively testing proximal groups while worrying about his airway. However, the clues were hiding in plain sight on physical exam: periungual telangiectasias (nailfold microhemorrhages) and generalized erythema over the nasolabial folds. While he lacked the textbook heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules initially, these subtle cutaneous markers combined with "atypical" neck swelling should trigger an immediate CK check. If the CK is sky-high and complement levels (C3/C4) are normal, stop chasing angioedema and start thinking myositis.

## Decoding the Anti-SAE1 Phenotype

Not all myositis antibodies paint the same clinical picture. We often group dermatomyositis as a monolith, but serotyping reveals distinct phenotypes with different prognoses and organ involvements. Anti-SAE1 (small ubiquitin-like modifier-activating enzyme) is one of these specific signatures.

While classic DM presents with symmetric proximal weakness and pathognomonic rashes, the anti-SAE1 subset has a reputation for being particularly nasty regarding swallowing and skin integrity. Literature suggests that nearly half of patients with anti-SAE1 antibodies develop severe dysphagia, far exceeding rates seen in other subsets like anti-Mi-2 or anti-Jo-1. In our patient, this manifested as silent aspiration and significant residue in the pyriform fossae, necessitating NG tube feeding for months despite systemic improvement.

Furthermore, this phenotype is associated with prominent cutaneous edema and even ulceration. While facial swelling occurs in various autoimmune conditions, isolated or predominant neck swelling is rare in DM overall (reported in only ~6% of cohorts) but appears disproportionately in specific antibody profiles including anti-SAE1 and anti-MDA5. Recognizing this phenotype matters because these patients often require more aggressive upfront immunosuppression to prevent permanent swallowing dysfunction and respiratory compromise from aspiration pneumonitis.

## The Malignancy Question: To Screen or Not to Screen?

Perhaps the most critical aspect of managing new-onset dermatomyositis, particularly in adults over 40, is addressing the elephant in the room: cancer. Dermatomyositis is a known paraneoplastic syndrome, but risk stratification has become more nuanced with antibody profiling.

Our patient hit several high-risk flags according to IMACS (International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group) guidelines: adult-onset DM, male sex, dysphagia, and specifically, anti-SAE1 positivity. While some antibodies like anti-Mi-2 carry a lower malignancy risk (~10%), anti-SAE1 hovers around **18%**, placing it firmly in the category requiring robust surveillance. Other high-risk markers include anti-TIF1-γ and anti-NXP-2.

The screening strategy must be comprehensive yet pragmatic. A simple chest X-ray won't cut it. Current recommendations for high-risk patients involve age-appropriate cancer screening plus expanded imaging. For our patient, this meant CT Chest/Abdomen/Pelvis, tumor markers (CEA, CA-19-9, PSA), PET scan to hunt for metabolically active occult disease, and planned endoscopies (EGD/Colonoscopy). Interestingly, while his initial PET scan ruled out widespread metastatic disease, he remains under strict surveillance because paraneoplastic DM can precede the detection of the tumor by months or even years. Balancing resource stewardship with patient safety means adhering to these high-risk protocols strictly for the first 3–5 years post-diagnosis when risk is highest.

# Airway Anxiety vs. Muscle Failure: Managing Dysphagia and Aspiration in Acute Myositis

When a patient rolls into your ED with a swollen neck, your brain immediately goes to the worst-case scenarios: Ludwig's angina, epiglottitis, or anaphylaxis. The airway anxiety is real, and rightly so. But what happens when the CT scan rules out a deep space infection, the patient is afebrile, and the ENT scope shows no laryngeal edema? You might find yourself staring at a diagnostic dead end labeled "idiopathic angioedema."

That's exactly where a recent case of anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis left us. A man in his 40s presented with progressive neck swelling and hip weakness. He wasn't obstructing in the traditional sense; his straps were inflamed, and his retropharyngeal space looked angry on imaging, mimicking a surgical emergency. But the real killer here wasn't immediate asphyxiation from soft tissue collapse—it was the silent, insidious threat of bulbar muscle failure leading to massive aspiration.

This case forces us to pivot our mental model from "secure the tube" to "protect the lungs from the inside out." In acute inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis, the neck swelling is often pseudoangioedema driven by underlying myositis and capillary leak, not histamine or bradykinin. The true morbidity comes from dysphagia so severe that every sip of water becomes a potential pneumonia event. Let's break down how we navigated the tension between airway security and aspiration prevention in this unique presentation.

## The Diagnostic Pivot: When Swelling Isn't Angioedema

The initial workup for this patient was textbook for an airway scare: CT neck, ENT consult, and a hunt for infection or allergic mediators. Everything came back negative for infectious or classic angioedema etiologies. C3/C4 and C1-esterase levels were normal. No ACE inhibitors, no family history. Yet, the neck remained diffusely edematous and erythematous.

The clue lay in the details that almost got missed: subtle periungual telangiectasias (nailfold microhemorrhages) and proximal hip flexor weakness graded at 4/5. In the context of unexplained neck swelling, these are screaming red flags for an inflammatory myopathy. Once the Creatine Kinase (CK) returned at a staggering 16,184 U/L, the picture snapped into focus. This wasn't angioedema; it was dermatomyositis with prominent cervical soft tissue involvement—a rare "pseudoangioedema" presentation seen in less than 7% of cases.

The danger here is twofold. First, misdiagnosing this as angioedema delays life-saving immunosuppression. Second, and perhaps more critically for the emergency physician, focusing solely on intubation misses the functional deficit. Our patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia confirmed later by video fluoroscopy, showing residue in the pyriform fossae and penetration of fluids to the vocal cords. His airway was patent, but his protective mechanisms were gone.

## Securing Nutrition vs. Securing the Airway

In standard trauma or sepsis algorithms, if a patient can't protect their airway, we intubate. But in acute myositis with profound bulbar weakness, early intubation isn't always the answer unless there is frank respiratory failure or inability to handle secretions leading to imminent arrest. Intubation carries its own risks—ventilator-associated pneumonia, prolonged ICU stays, and difficult extubation if muscle recovery is slow.

In this case, the team opted for a nuanced approach focused on secretion management and nutritional support without immediate invasive ventilation. The patient was started on aggressive immunosuppression (IV methylprednisolone pulse followed by tapering oral prednisone) alongside IVIG and rituximab to halt the muscle destruction at its source. However, pharmacologic rescue takes time to translate into functional recovery.

While waiting for muscles to heal, we had to bypass the broken swallow mechanism. The decision between Nasogastric (NG) tube versus Percutaneous Endoscopic Gastrostomy (PEG) is often debated in neurology circles. For acute inflammatory myopathies where recovery is anticipated within weeks to months—as evidenced by this patient's full strength recovery at 3 weeks post-discharge—an NG tube is often sufficient for short-term management (<4–6 weeks). It avoids procedural risks and allows for rapid initiation of enteral feeding once dysphagia is identified via bedside screen or formal videofluoroscopy (VFSS).

This patient required NG feeding for two months until his swallow function improved enough for soft solids. Had we jumped straight to PEG or tracheostomy without trialing conservative management alongside aggressive immunotherapy, we might have subjected him to unnecessary procedures given his excellent prognosis for muscular recovery. That said, if dysphagia persists beyond 4-6 weeks despite treatment escalation, early PEG placement should be strongly considered to improve quality of life and reduce aspiration risk during long-term rehabilitation.

In dermatomyositis, particularly with anti-SAE1 antibodies where dysphagia rates hit nearly 50%, aspiration is a leading cause of mortality independent of respiratory muscle weakness alone!

# Escalation Therapy: Navigating Steroid-Sparing Agents in Severe Dermatomyositis

We've all seen the patient with "mystery neck swelling." The workup usually spirals quickly: Is it Ludwig's angina? A deep space abscess? Maybe idiopathic angioedema? But what happens when the CT shows diffuse edema without an abscess, the airway is patent but the patient can't swallow, and the labs come back with a Creatine Kinase (CK) that looks like a typo?

Consider the case of a man in his 40s who rolled into the ED with progressive neck swelling and hip weakness. He was bounced between ENT and Immunology, labeled as a probable deep neck infection, then suspected idiopathic angioedema. He wasn't febrile, his complement levels were normal, and he had no exposure to ACE inhibitors. The clue wasn't in the swelling itself, but in the subtle periungual telangiectasias and a CK that skyrocketed to **16,184 U/L**.

This wasn't angioedema; it was dermatomyositis masquerading as one. Specifically, anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis, a rare subset known for severe cutaneous involvement and, critically, significant dysphagia. While high-dose steroids are the undisputed kickoff for inflammatory myopathies, this case forces us to confront a harder question: When the CK is this high and vital functions like swallowing are compromised, is pulse steroids enough? And perhaps more importantly, how do we choose the right artillery for escalation when traditional options might actually harm the patient?

## The Trap of "Steroids First" in Organ-Threatening Disease

In this case, the team correctly identified the need for aggressive induction therapy. The patient received three days of IV methylprednisolone (1g daily) followed by a high-dose oral taper. This is standard operating procedure. However, looking at the trajectory of severe dermatomyositis—especially with a CK over 10,000 U/L and life-threatening dysphagia leading to aspiration pneumonitis—steroids alone are often too slow or insufficient to halt muscle necrosis.

The danger here isn't just treatment failure; it's the toxicity of prolonged high-dose corticosteroids while waiting for them to work. We need rapid-acting steroid-sparing agents that can induce remission before irreversible muscle damage occurs or before aspiration leads to fatal pneumonia. In this specific case report, the clinicians didn't wait. They immediately layered on Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG), Rituximab, and Mycophenolate Mofetil (MMF). This "kitchen sink" approach might seem aggressive to some, but in the context of anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis with bulbar involvement, hesitation costs function.

## Choosing Your Weapon: Why Not Methotrexate?

If you ask a rheumatologist about first-line steroid-sparing agents for dermatomyositis, Methotrexate (MTX) often tops the list. It's effective, cheap, and familiar. So why did this team explicitly avoid it?

The answer lies in the lungs. Dermatomyositis carries a well-documented association with Interstitial Lung Disease (ILD). While this patient's initial CT showed consolidation likely due to aspiration rather than frank fibrosis, his pulmonary function tests revealed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern. Methotrexate carries a risk of pneumonitis and can exacerbate underlying lung pathology. In a patient already struggling with oxygenation dynamics due to aspiration and potential subclinical ILD (common in myositis spectra), adding a drug with pulmonary toxicity is playing Russian roulette.

Instead, they opted for **Mycophenolate Mofetil (MMF)**. MMF has emerged as a preferred agent in myositis-associated ILD because it lacks the direct pulmonary toxicity of MTX while offering potent immunosuppression. It inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, blocking guanine nucleotide synthesis preferentially in T and B lymphocytes. For the emergency physician or intensivist managing these patients alongside rheumatology, recognizing *why* MTX was skipped is crucial: it signals that lung protection is prioritized alongside muscle recovery.

## The Heavy Hitters: IVIG and Rituximab

When muscle enzymes are in the five figures and the patient cannot protect their airway due to pharyngeal weakness, we need mechanisms that act faster than oral antimetabolites.

**IVIG** acts rapidly through multiple pathways: neutralizing pathogenic autoantibodies, inhibiting complement activation (critical in dermatomyositis where C5b-9 deposition destroys capillaries), and modulating Fc receptors on macrophages. In this case, a loading dose of 2 g/kg was administered over five days. This is not maintenance therapy; this is rescue therapy designed to stop the immune assault immediately while slower-acting agents like MMF ramp up. Evidence supports IVIG particularly in refractory cases or those with severe dysphagia, making it a logical choice here.

Then there is **Rituximab**, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody that depletes B-cells. While traditionally reserved for refractory disease or specific antibody subsets (like anti-synthetase syndrome), its use here reflects the severity of the presentation and the specific profile of anti-SAE1 positivity. Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis can be stubborn; early B-cell depletion may prevent relapse and allow for a more rapid steroid taper. Two doses of 1g given two weeks apart provide sustained depletion, buying time for the muscle fibers to regenerate without ongoing inflammatory insult.

## Practical Considerations: Prophylaxis and Monitoring

Throwing heavy immunosuppression at a patient opens Pandora's box regarding infectious complications. This case serves as a stark reminder that your job isn't done once you order Rituximab; you must anticipate collateral damage.

The patient was started on *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX). This is non-negotiable in patients receiving high-dose steroids plus additional immunosuppressants like Rituximab or MMF. However, medicine is rarely linear: this patient developed hemolytic anemia despite having normal G6PD levels—a known idiosyncratic reaction to sulfonamides in autoimmune populations. The team had to pivot quickly to nebulized pentamidine. This highlights a critical pearl: monitor your CBC closely after initiating prophylaxis don't assume tolerance just because genetic screening was negative earlier or because guidelines say "use TMP-SMX."
