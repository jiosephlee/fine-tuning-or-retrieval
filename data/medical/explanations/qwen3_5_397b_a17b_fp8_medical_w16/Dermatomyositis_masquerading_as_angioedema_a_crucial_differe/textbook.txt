Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Clinical Presentation and the Diagnostic Pitfall: When Neck Swelling is Not Angioedema

The initial presentation of this patient illustrates a classic diagnostic trap where a systemic autoimmune myopathy mimics a localized otolaryngologic or allergic emergency. Upon arrival, the patient's progressive neck swelling and elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 55.6 mg/L) naturally directed the clinical team toward infectious etiologies, specifically Ludwig's angina or a deep neck space infection. This hypothesis was reinforced by CT imaging showing diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal inflammation. However, key clinical features argued against an infectious origin: the patient was afebrile, hemodynamically stable, and lacked the exquisite tenderness typically associated with cellulitis or abscess formation. Furthermore, the failure of the swelling to resolve and the absence of a purulent source on ENT evaluation should have prompted a shift in differential diagnosis away from infection. Recognizing that "cold" inflammation—swelling without fever or focal tenderness—can occur in autoimmune conditions is the first step in avoiding unnecessary surgical interventions or prolonged antibiotic courses.

Once infection was ruled out, the working diagnosis shifted to idiopathic angioedema, yet the clinical picture remained inconsistent with both histaminergic and bradykinin-mediated pathways. True angioedema is characterized by transient, often asymmetric swelling that responds to antihistamines, corticosteroids, or epinephrine (in histaminergic cases) or resolves spontaneously over days (in bradykinin-mediated cases). In contrast, this patient exhibited persistent, non-tender, generalized erythema of the face and neck that did not fluctuate in the manner typical of allergic reactions. Crucially, standard investigations for angioedema were unrevealing; C3 and C4 complement levels were normal, and C1 esterase inhibitor function and quantity were within reference ranges, effectively excluding hereditary and acquired C1-inhibitor deficient angioedema. The persistence of swelling alongside new neurological symptoms signaled that the edema was likely "pseudoangioedema"—a manifestation of underlying tissue infiltration or inflammation rather than vascular permeability changes driven by vasoactive mediators.

The pivot point in this case was the identification of subtle cutaneous stigmata and concurrent neuromuscular deficits that are hallmarks of dermatomyositis but easily overlooked when attention is fixated on airway swelling. While the patient lacked the pathognomonic Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash initially described in textbooks, a meticulous examination revealed periungual telangiectasias with avascular areas on nailfold capillaroscopy, periungual erythema, and a Holster sign (an erythematous rash on the lateral thigh). These findings, though subtle compared to the dramatic neck swelling, are highly specific for inflammatory myopathies. Furthermore, the presence of proximal muscle weakness (4/5 power in hip flexors) and dysphagia provided critical context. Dysphagia in dermatomyositis results from striated muscle involvement in the pharynx and upper esophagus, leading to aspiration risks evidenced here by coughing during nasendoscopy and subsequent radiographic confirmation of penetration. The coexistence of unexplained neck edema, proximal weakness, and dysphagia creates a clinical triad that should immediately trigger suspicion for myositis.

Laboratory data ultimately confirmed the suspicion raised by the physical exam, revealing a pattern of massive muscle injury disproportionate to any localized soft tissue process. The most striking abnormality was the creatine kinase (CK) level, which soared to 16,184 U/L. Such extreme elevation is inconsistent with simple soft tissue edema or mild myalgia and indicates widespread rhabdomyolysis or severe active myositis. Additionally, the patient presented with elevated transaminases (AST 396 U/L > ALT 143 U/L) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH 692 U/L). In the absence of primary liver disease—which was supported by normal bilirubin and synthetic function—these elevations reflect leakage from damaged skeletal muscle rather than hepatocellular injury. The combination of disproportionately high CK, muscle-derived enzyme elevation, negative angioedema workup, and specific cutaneous findings transforms the diagnostic narrative from an allergic or infectious crisis to an urgent autoimmune myopathy requiring immediate immunosuppression.

## Pathophysiology and Serology: Understanding Anti-SAE1 Dermatomyositis

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies represent a heterogeneous group of autoimmune disorders characterized by chronic muscle inflammation and weakness, with dermatomyositis (DM) being a distinct subset defined by characteristic cutaneous findings alongside myopathy. While DM exhibits a bimodal age distribution, peaking in children aged 5–14 and adults between 45 and 64, the presence of specific autoantibodies often dictates the clinical phenotype, severity, and organ involvement more accurately than age alone. In the context of this case, the patient's presentation in his 40s aligns with the adult onset peak, yet his specific serological profile—positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (anti-SAE1) antibodies—defines a unique and aggressive clinical trajectory that diverges from classic DM presentations.

The pathogenic cornerstone of anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis lies in the disruption of post-translational protein modification known as "sumoylation." The SAE1 enzyme functions as part of a heterodimer responsible for activating small ubiquitin-like modifiers (SUMO), which are structurally similar to ubiquitin. Under physiological conditions, sumoylation regulates critical cellular processes, including nuclear transport, transcriptional regulation, and DNA repair. In anti-SAE1 positive DM, autoantibodies target this enzymatic complex, potentially impairing the sumoylation pathway in endothelial cells and myofibers. Although the precise downstream cascade linking this enzymatic inhibition to frank tissue necrosis remains an area of active investigation, the resulting immune dysregulation manifests as a severe inflammatory myopathy with a predilection for specific organ systems not always dominant in other DM subtypes.

Clinically, anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is distinguished by a high frequency of severe dysphagia and interstitial lung disease (ILD). Literature reviews indicate that nearly half of patients with this antibody subtype experience significant swallowing difficulties due to striated muscle involvement in the pharynx and esophagus, while a comparable proportion develop ILD. In the reported case, these features were paramount; the patient exhibited severe pharyngeal dysphagia leading to aspiration pneumonitis and demonstrated pulmonary function deficits consistent with an extrathoracic restrictive pattern. This phenotype underscores the importance of recognizing that anti-SAE1 positivity often signals a disease course complicated by profound bulbar and respiratory muscle weakness, necessitating vigilant monitoring for aspiration and pulmonary compromise beyond simple limb weakness.

The mechanism driving the "pseudoangioedema" seen in this patient differs fundamentally from true angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin. Classic angioedema involves vasoactive peptides causing transient vascular permeability without significant inflammation or tissue destruction. Conversely, the neck swelling in dermatomyositis arises from an underlying complement-mediated microangiopathy. Pathologically, this involves the deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on perifascicular endomysial capillaries, leading to capillary dropout, ischemia, and subsequent muscle fiber necrosis in a perifascicular distribution. As evidenced by this patient's muscle biopsy showing necrotic myofibers and C5b-9 deposition, the visible soft tissue edema is essentially an extension of deep muscular inflammation and fluid extravasation secondary to microvascular injury rather than a primary allergic or kinin-driven event. This distinction is critical because it renders standard angioedema therapies ineffective while mandating potent immunosuppression to halt the destructive myopathic process.

Despite being a recognized feature of dermatomyositis, subcutaneous edema presenting as localized neck or facial swelling is statistically uncommon. Cohort studies suggest that only approximately 6% to 7% of dermatomyositis patients exhibit pseudoangioedema, making it a frequent source of diagnostic confusion with deep neck space infections like Ludwig's angina or idiopathic angioedema. While certain antibodies such as anti-MDA5 or anti-TIF1-γ have been more frequently associated with edematous presentations in historical literature, this case illustrates that anti-SAE1 positivity can also manifest with prominent cervical soft tissue swelling. The rarity of this presentation emphasizes that when faced with unexplained neck edema accompanied by even subtle cutaneous signs like periungual erythema or holstersign rash, clinicians must consider an inflammatory myopathy and pursue serological testing for myositis-specific antibodies to avoid delays in initiating life-saving immunomodulatory therapy.

## Diagnostic Workup and Reasoning: From Bedside to Biopsy

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates a critical shift from excluding life-threatening emergencies to identifying a rare autoimmune etiology through a structured hierarchy of testing. Initially, the patient's presentation of severe neck swelling and elevated inflammatory markers prompted an exclusionary workup focused on infectious and allergic causes. Given the suspicion of angioedema, it was imperative to rule out bradykinin-mediated pathways, which are characteristic of hereditary or acquired angioedema. The normalization of C3 and C4 complement levels, alongside normal C1-esterase inhibitor function and quantification, effectively excluded these common causes of non-histaminergic swelling. Similarly, the absence of fever, normal white cell count relative to the degree of swelling, and negative imaging for abscess formation helped dismiss deep neck space infections like Ludwig's angina. This negative workup for primary otolaryngologic and immunologic emergencies created the necessary clinical space to reconsider the diagnosis based on subtle physical findings, specifically the periungual erythema and proximal muscle weakness, which pointed toward an inflammatory myopathy.

Once the possibility of dermatomyositis was raised, serological confirmation became the cornerstone of the diagnostic process. The patient exhibited a high-titer antinuclear antibody (ANA) with a speckled pattern, a sensitive but non-specific marker for connective tissue diseases. Definitive classification relied on a comprehensive myositis antibody panel, which identified strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (anti-SAE1) antibodies. This specific autoantibody is highly specific for dermatomyositis and often correlates with prominent cutaneous features and dysphagia, even in the absence of classic signs like Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash at initial presentation. The identification of anti-SAE1 guided the subsequent search for visceral involvement and malignancy risk, as this subset carries a distinct clinical profile compared to other myositis-specific antibodies such as anti-Jo-1 or anti-Mi-2.

Advanced imaging played a dual role in this case: confirming active muscle inflammation and screening for paraneoplastic syndromes. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thighs provided anatomical detail, revealing hyperintense signals in the obturator internus, externus, and hip adductor muscles consistent with active myositis. While MRI localized the inflammation, fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) offered a functional assessment of metabolic activity across multiple muscle groups, including the head, neck, and trunk, corroborating the systemic nature of the disease. Crucially, the PET scan also served as a vital malignancy screen; the absence of metabolically active lesions outside the musculoskeletal system helped mitigate immediate concerns regarding an underlying occult tumor, although ongoing surveillance remained necessary given the known association between dermatomyositis and cancer.

Definitive diagnosis was ultimately secured through histopathological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy, which revealed features pathognomonic for dermatomyositis. Microscopic analysis demonstrated perifascicular atrophy—a hallmark finding where muscle fiber degeneration occurs predominantly at the periphery of the fascicle—alongside perimysial inflammation composed of dispersed histiocytes. Perhaps most diagnostically significant was the immunohistochemical staining showing deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on perifascicular endomysial capillaries. This finding confirms that the primary pathology involves complement-mediated microangiopathy leading to muscle ischemia and necrosis, distinguishing dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies like polymyositis or immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, which lack this specific vascular deposition pattern.

Finally, assessing visceral involvement was essential for managing immediate risks and guiding long-term care. The patient's complaint of mild myalgia masked severe pharyngeal dysfunction, which was objectively quantified via a video fluoroscopic swallow study. This test revealed significant residue in the pyriform fossae and penetration of fluids into the vocal cords, explaining the aspiration pneumonitis seen on chest CT and necessitating nasogastric feeding to prevent further pulmonary compromise. Pulmonary function tests further characterized an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, likely secondary to both respiratory muscle weakness and mechanical restriction from neck edema. Together, these assessments confirmed that while the neck swelling was the presenting complaint, it was merely one manifestation of a multisystem autoimmune process requiring aggressive immunosuppression to preserve airway integrity and muscular function.

## Therapeutic Strategy and Malignancy Surveillance in High-Risk Myositis

The management of severe, antibody-defined dermatomyositis requires an aggressive, multimodal approach aimed at rapidly suppressing inflammation to prevent irreversible muscle damage and life-threatening complications such as dysphagia and respiratory failure. In cases presenting with critical airway or swallowing involvement, as seen in this patient with severe pharyngeal dysphagia and cervical edema, induction therapy typically begins with high-dose intravenous corticosteroids. The administration of pulse methylprednisolone (e.g., 1000 mg daily for three days) serves to quickly dampen the cytokine storm and reduce tissue edema, followed by a transition to high-dose oral prednisone with a slow, structured taper to maintain remission while minimizing long-term steroid toxicity. Concurrently, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is often employed as a first-line adjunctive therapy, particularly in severe presentations or where rapid functional recovery is essential. A loading dose of 2 g/kg divided over several days, followed by maintenance cycles, provides immediate passive immunity and modulates the autoimmune response through mechanisms including Fc receptor blockade and complement inhibition. This dual approach of high-dose steroids and IVIG addresses both the acute inflammatory crisis and the underlying autoimmunity.

Selecting appropriate steroid-sparing agents is critical for long-term disease control and must be tailored to the patient's specific organ involvement and comorbidities. While methotrexate is a conventional first-line agent for many forms of dermatomyositis, its use is contraindicated or approached with extreme caution in patients with evidence of interstitial lung disease (ILD) or significant pulmonary compromise due to the risk of methotrexate-induced pneumonitis, which can mimic or exacerbate existing lung pathology. In this case, given the patient's restrictive pulmonary pattern and radiological findings suggestive of aspiration pneumonitis alongside potential ILD risk, mycophenolate mofetil was chosen as the preferred immunomodulator. Mycophenolate offers a favorable safety profile regarding pulmonary toxicity and has demonstrated efficacy in treating both cutaneous and muscular manifestations of dermatomyositis. Furthermore, for patients with severe disease refractory to initial combinations or those with specific high-risk antibody profiles like anti-SAE1, the addition of rituximab—a monoclonal antibody targeting CD20 on B cells—provides a potent mechanism to deplete autoreactive B-cell clones. The integration of rituximab early in the treatment course for severe cases can facilitate faster tapering of corticosteroids and improve overall outcomes in refractory myositis.

Intense immunosuppressive regimens inevitably increase susceptibility to opportunistic infections, necessitating rigorous prophylactic measures. Prophylaxis against *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia (PJP) is standard of care for patients receiving high-dose corticosteroids combined with additional immunosuppressants. Although trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is the first-line agent, clinicians must remain vigilant for adverse reactions; in this instance, the development of hemolytic anemia necessitated a switch to nebulized pentamidine. This adjustment underscores the importance of monitoring hematologic parameters and adapting prophylactic strategies to ensure patient safety without compromising infection protection during the vulnerable induction phase.

Parallel to therapeutic intervention, rigorous malignancy surveillance is mandatory in adult-onset dermatomyositis, particularly in patients identified as high-risk according to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines. Risk stratification integrates demographic factors (such as older age and male sex), clinical features (including dysphagia and cutaneous necrosis), and serological markers. Anti-SAE1 positivity, while sometimes associated with a distinct phenotype, still warrants comprehensive screening given the established association between dermatomyositis and occult malignancy. In this case, the patient possessed multiple high-and intermediate-risk factors, prompting an extensive workup that included cross-sectional imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis via CT and PET scans to detect metabolically active tumors. Normal tumor markers (CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, PSA) provided additional reassurance but did not replace the need for direct visualization. Consequently, planned endoscopic evaluation of the gastrointestinal tract remains a crucial component of the screening algorithm to exclude occult colorectal or gastric cancers that may not be apparent on external imaging. Ongoing surveillance involves regular clinical assessments and repeat imaging at defined intervals, as the risk of malignancy persists beyond the initial diagnosis period.
