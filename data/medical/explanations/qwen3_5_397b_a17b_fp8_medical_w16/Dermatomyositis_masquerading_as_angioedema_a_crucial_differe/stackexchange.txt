Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient presenting with unexplained neck swelling and suspected angioedema, which specific laboratory test is most critical to order immediately to rule out an underlying inflammatory myopathy like dermatomyositis?

In a patient presenting with unexplained neck swelling suspected to be angioedema, the most critical immediate laboratory test to rule out an underlying inflammatory myopathy like dermatomyositis is the **serum creatine kinase (CK)** level.

As illustrated in the reported case of a man in his 40s with progressive neck edema initially misdiagnosed as Ludwig's angina and subsequently idiopathic angioedema, the "swelling" was actually a manifestation of severe myositis involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space—a phenomenon known as pseudoangioedema. Unlike histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, where complement studies (C3, C4, C1 esterase) are the diagnostic mainstay, dermatomyositis-driven edema results from intense muscle inflammation and necrosis. In this case, despite the absence of classic cutaneous stigmata like Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash at presentation, the CK level was markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L, alongside elevated transaminases (AST > ALT) and LDH, confirming massive muscle injury.

The clinical reasoning hinges on recognizing that neck swelling accompanied by even subtle proximal weakness or dysphagia should trigger a search for myogenic causes rather than solely allergic or infectious etiologies. Normal complement levels in the setting of persistent edema should further pivot the differential toward non-bradykinin mechanisms. Prompt ordering of a CK level allows for rapid differentiation between true angioedema and inflammatory myopathy, facilitating the immediate initiation of high-dose immunosuppression necessary to prevent airway compromise and irreversible muscle damage.

### [Pathophysiology] How does the pathophysiology of 'pseudoangioedema' in dermatomyositis differ mechanistically from histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema?

The pathophysiology of "pseudoangioedema" in dermatomyositis differs fundamentally from classic angioedema in both the anatomical compartment involved and the underlying inflammatory mechanism. While histaminergic and bradykinin-mediated angioedema result from increased vascular permeability leading to fluid extravasation into the deep dermis and subcutaneous tissues, the swelling seen in dermatomyositis is driven by active myositis and fasciitis extending into the surrounding soft tissues.

In histaminergic angioedema, mast cell degranulation releases vasoactive mediators that cause rapid, transient leakage of plasma proteins and fluid. Similarly, bradykinin-mediated angioedema (seen in hereditary or ACE-inhibitor induced cases) involves unchecked kinin production causing profound vasodilation and endothelial gap formation. Crucially, both mechanisms primarily affect the interstitial space without direct destruction of muscle fibers, and they typically respond to specific antagonists (antihistamines, icatibant) or resolve as mediator levels drop. Complement studies (C3, C4, C1 esterase) are often abnormal in bradykinin-mediated forms but remain normal in histaminergic types.

In contrast, the case report illustrates that the patient's severe neck swelling was not due to vascular leak alone but was a manifestation of diffuse inflammation involving the strap muscles, retropharyngeal space, and perifascicular regions. The diagnostic pivot point was the markedly elevated creatine kinase (16,184 U/L) and MRI findings confirming active myositis in the neck and proximal muscles. Histopathologically, this process is characterized by perifascicular atrophy, necrotic myofibers, and deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on endomysial capillaries—a hallmark of complement-mediated microangiopathy specific to dermatomyositis. Unlike true angioedema, where muscle enzymes are normal and weakness is absent unless airway compromise occurs, pseudoangioedema in dermatomyositis is invariably accompanied by objective muscle weakness (such as this patient's hip flexor weakness and dysphagia) and signs of cutaneous involvement like periungual telangiectasias. Therefore, the swelling represents an extension of inflammatory myopathy rather than a primary disorder of vascular permeability, explaining why it fails to respond to standard angioedema therapies but resolves with aggressive immunosuppression targeting the underlying autoimmune myositis.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Given a patient with subacute neck swelling, fever, and elevated CRP, what key clinical features on physical examination and history would help distinguish dermatomyositis from Ludwig's angina or deep neck space infection?

To distinguish dermatomyositis from Ludwig's angina or a deep neck space infection in a patient presenting with subacute neck swelling and elevated inflammatory markers, the clinician must pivot from an infectious paradigm to an autoimmune one by identifying specific discordances between the physical exam findings and the expected presentation of cellulitis or abscess.

While both conditions may present with neck swelling and an elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), as seen in our patient whose CRP was 55.6 mg/L, the nature of the swelling and associated systemic signs differ fundamentally. In Ludwig's angina or deep neck infections, the swelling is typically tender, warm, and associated with fever and leukocytosis. In contrast, this case of dermatomyositis presented with **non-tender**, erythematous neck swelling in an **afebrile** patient with a normal white cell count. The absence of fever and tenderness despite significant soft tissue edema should immediately raise suspicion for a non-infectious etiology.

Furthermore, the history and examination must actively search for extra-cervical manifestations of inflammatory myopathy that are absent in odontogenic infections. Key differentiating features include:
1.  **Proximal Muscle Weakness:** Unlike deep neck infections which cause pain-limited movement, dermatomyositis causes true motor weakness. Our patient exhibited 4/5 power in hip flexors, a finding inconsistent with localized infection.
2.  **Dysphagia:** While severe infections can cause odynophagia (painful swallowing) due to mass effect or inflammation, dermatomyositis causes mechanical dysphagia due to striated muscle involvement. The patient's coughing during nasendoscopy and subsequent barium swallow confirmation of pharyngeal residue pointed toward neuromuscular dysfunction rather than simple compression.
3.  **Pathognomonic Cutaneous Signs:** Careful inspection often reveals subtle stigmata missed in the acute setting. This patient demonstrated periungual telangiectasias (confirmed by capillaroscopy showing microhemorrhages), generalized facial erythema involving the nasolabial folds, and a rash on the lateral thigh (Holster sign). The absence of classic Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash does not rule out the diagnosis, as "amyopathic" or hypomyopathic variants exist, but the presence of *any* characteristic rash favors dermatomyositis over infection.

Finally, the trajectory of symptoms offers a clue: the patient's swelling was progressive over three weeks without the rapid deterioration typical of untreated Ludwig's angina, yet failed to resolve with standard supportive care for infection. When faced with "pseudoangioedema"—swelling that mimics allergic or infectious processes but lacks the corresponding fever, tenderness, or response to antibiotics—the immediate ordering of a creatine kinase (CK) level is mandatory. In this case, the CK was massively elevated at 16,184 U/L, confirming rhabdomyolysis secondary to myositis rather than infectious necrosis. Thus, the triad of non-tender edema, proximal weakness/dysphagia, and subtle cutaneous vascular changes distinguishes dermatomyositis from deep neck space infections.

### [Morning Report] What is the characteristic immunofluorescence finding on muscle biopsy that confirms the diagnosis of dermatomyositis, and how does it localize within the muscle fascicle?

The confirmatory immunofluorescence finding in dermatomyositis is the deposition of the membrane attack complex, specifically complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9), within the endomysial capillaries. As illustrated in this case of anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, this deposition follows a distinct **perifascicular** distribution, localizing to the capillaries surrounding the outer edges of the muscle fascicles.

This specific localization reflects the underlying pathophysiology of dermatomyositis as a humoral-mediated microangiopathy. Autoantibodies activate the complement cascade, leading to C5b-9 formation and subsequent destruction of endomysial capillaries. Because blood flow is most tenuous at the periphery of the fascicle, this ischemic injury results in the characteristic perifascicular atrophy and necrosis seen on histology. In the reported case, despite the absence of classic cutaneous signs like Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash initially, the muscle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis by demonstrating both perifascicular pathology and C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, distinguishing it from other inflammatory myopathies such as polymyositis or immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy.

### [Clinical Pearls] This patient tested positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies. Compared to other myositis-specific antibodies (such as anti-MDA5 or anti-TIF1-γ), what are the distinct clinical associations regarding interstitial lung disease, dysphagia, and malignancy risk for anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis?

Anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis presents a distinct clinical phenotype characterized by severe cutaneous disease and prominent muscle involvement, with a specific risk profile that differentiates it from other myositis subsets. Regarding **interstitial lung disease (ILD)**, anti-SAE1 positivity carries a moderate risk; literature cited in this case indicates ILD occurs in approximately 43% of patients. This stands in sharp contrast to anti-MDA5-associated dermatomyositis, where rapidly progressive ILD is the dominant and often life-threatening feature, whereas anti-SAE1 patients typically do not present with the fulminant pulmonary decline seen in MDA5 cases. In the current patient, despite having pulmonary nodules and consolidation, these were attributed to aspiration secondary to dysphagia rather than primary fibrotic ILD.

The association with **dysphagia** is particularly strong in anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis, reported in up to 46% of cases. This reflects the antibody's tendency to cause severe inflammation of the striated muscles of the pharynx and esophagus. In this case, the patient exhibited severe pharyngeal dysphagia leading to micro-aspiration and required prolonged nasogastric feeding, underscoring that swallowing dysfunction is a hallmark complication of this subtype that demands aggressive immunosuppression.

Finally, concerning **malignancy risk**, anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis confers an intermediate probability of paraneoplastic disease, estimated at roughly 18%. This risk is notably lower than that associated with anti-TIF1-γ antibodies, which carry the highest correlation with underlying malignancy (often exceeding 50–70% in adult cohorts). While the patient in this report was classified as "high risk" for cancer due to the combination of male sex, dysphagia, and dermatomyositis itself—warranting comprehensive screening with CT, PET, and planned endoscopy—the specific contribution of the anti-SAE1 antibody to malignancy risk is modest compared to anti-TIF1-γ. Clinicians must therefore maintain vigilance for cancer in all adult dermatomyositis patients but recognize that the immediate threats in anti-SAE1 cases are more likely to be severe myositis, refractory skin disease, and aspiration-related complications rather than an occult tumor.

### [Management] Why was methotrexate specifically avoided in this patient's treatment regimen despite being a common first-line steroid-sparing agent for dermatomyositis?

Methotrexate was specifically avoided in this patient due to the significant risk of exacerbating or inducing interstitial lung disease (ILD) and pulmonary fibrosis, a known adverse effect of the drug. Although methotrexate is frequently employed as a first-line steroid-sparing agent for dermatomyositis, its use requires caution in patients with pre-existing pulmonary involvement. In this specific case, the patient presented with an extrathoracic restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests and CT imaging revealed patchy peribronchial consolidations alongside pulmonary nodules. While the consolidations were attributed to aspiration pneumonitis secondary to severe dysphagia, the presence of abnormal lung function and radiographic findings rendered the lungs vulnerable. Given that anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis carries an inherent association with ILD (reported in approximately 43% of cases), the treating team elected to bypass methotrexate to prevent potential iatrogenic lung injury. Instead, they utilized a regimen of mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and intravenous immunoglobulin, which allowed for effective immunosuppression without compounding the respiratory risks.

### [Management] According to IMACS guidelines, what specific risk factors in this case classified the patient as 'high risk' for paraneoplastic malignancy, and what screening modalities were indicated?

Based on the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines referenced in this case, the patient was stratified as **high risk** for paraneoplastic malignancy due to the convergence of specific clinical and serological features. The guideline framework utilized here identifies two **high-risk factors**: the diagnosis of **dermatomyositis** itself and the presence of **dysphagia**. These were compounded by two **intermediate-risk factors**: **male sex** and positivity for **anti-SAE1 antibodies**. While anti-TIF1-γ and anti-NXP2 are more classically associated with malignancy, the IMACS algorithm in this context aggregates the cumulative burden of these four variables to mandate an aggressive screening protocol.

Consequently, the indicated screening modalities extended beyond routine age-appropriate checks to include comprehensive cross-sectional imaging and endoscopic evaluation. As detailed in the case, the patient underwent **CT imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis**, alongside measurement of **tumor markers** (CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, PSA) and a whole-body **FDG-PET scan** to detect metabolically active occult disease. Notably, despite the negative initial CT of the abdomen and pelvis, the persistence of high-risk classification necessitates invasive mucosal evaluation; therefore, the management plan correctly includes scheduled **gastroscopy and colonoscopy** to rule out gastrointestinal malignancies that may not be apparent on cross-sectional imaging alone. This multimodal approach underscores the principle that in high-risk inflammatory myopathy, a single negative modality does not preclude the need for further investigation until all indicated screens are complete.

### [Management] The patient developed hemolytic anemia while on trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis despite having normal G6PD levels. What alternative prophylactic strategy was employed, and why might sulfa drugs pose a risk in patients with severe systemic inflammation even without G6PD deficiency?

In this case, the patient was switched from trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) to **nebulized pentamidine** (300 mg every 4 weeks) for *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis after developing hemolytic anemia.

Although the patient had normal glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) levels, sulfonamides like TMP-SMX are potent oxidizing agents that can induce hemolysis through mechanisms beyond classic G6PD deficiency. In the setting of severe systemic inflammation—such as the active, untreated dermatomyositis seen in this patient—the body's antioxidant reserves (including glutathione) may be depleted due to the high oxidative burden of the inflammatory state. This creates a functional vulnerability where red blood cells become susceptible to oxidative stress induced by sulfa drugs, leading to hemolysis even in the presence of normal enzymatic activity. Additionally, severe inflammation can alter red cell membrane stability and increase susceptibility to drug-induced oxidative damage. Consequently, when hemolysis occurs in a critically ill or highly inflamed patient on sulfonamides, clinicians must consider discontinuing the agent regardless of G6PD status and utilize non-sulfa alternatives like nebulized pentamidine, which provides effective local prophylaxis without systemic oxidative toxicity.

### [Morning Report] Apart from proximal muscle weakness, what specific bulbar symptom presented early in this case that served as a clue to the severity of muscle involvement and contributed to pulmonary complications?

In this case, the specific bulbar symptom that presented early and served as a critical indicator of severe muscle involvement was **dysphagia**. While the patient's dramatic neck swelling initially mimicked angioedema or a deep neck space infection, the presence of swallowing difficulty signaled that the inflammatory myopathy had extended beyond the skeletal muscles of the limbs to involve the striated muscles of the pharynx and esophagus.

The clinical significance of this symptom is underscored by the findings on flexible nasendoscopy, which revealed increased secretions and coughing, and later confirmed by a video fluoroscopic swallow study showing severe pharyngeal dysphagia with post-swallow penetration of fluids to the vocal cords. This bulbar weakness directly precipitated the patient's pulmonary complications; specifically, the inability to protect the airway led to micro-aspiration, resulting in the patchy peribronchial consolidations observed on his chest CT. Consequently, recognizing dysphagia in the context of suspected dermatomyositis is vital, as it not only confirms the severity of the myositis but also identifies an immediate risk for aspiration pneumonitis that necessitates aggressive immunosuppression and often temporary nutritional support via nasogastric tube, as seen in this patient's prolonged recovery course.

### [Clinical Pearls] In the context of dermatomyositis presenting with predominant soft tissue edema rather than classic rash (e.g., Gottron's papules or heliotrope eruption), what subtle cutaneous signs on the hands or nails should prompt a rheumatologic evaluation?

In the absence of classic pathognomonic rashes like Gottron's papules or a heliotrope eruption, the clinician must scrutinize the **nailfolds** and **periungual regions** for subtle microvascular changes that signal underlying inflammatory myopathy. As illustrated in this case of anti-SAE1-positive dermatomyositis masquerading as idiopathic angioedema, the pivotal physical examination findings were **proximal nailfold microhemorrhages**, **avascular areas** on capillaroscopy, and **periungual erythema**.

These cutaneous signs reflect the fundamental pathophysiology of dermatomyositis: a complement-mediated microangiopathy targeting the endomysial capillaries. Unlike histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, which presents with transient, non-pitting swelling without specific nailbed pathology, dermatomyositis causes chronic vascular injury leading to dilated capillary loops, hemorrhage, and subsequent dropout (avascularity). In this patient, despite the dramatic cervical soft tissue edema and lack of classic truncal or knuckle rashes, the presence of periungual erythema and nailfold capillary abnormalities provided the critical diagnostic clue. When faced with unexplained "pseudoangioedema," particularly when accompanied by dysphagia or proximal weakness, identifying these specific nail unit changes should immediately prompt measurement of creatine kinase and referral for rheumatologic evaluation to prevent delays in initiating life-saving immunosuppression.
