Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Beyond Gottron's Papules: Decoding Subtle Cutaneous Clues in Anti-SAE1 Dermatomyositis

We are all trained to spot the classics. When you hear "dermatomyositis," your brain immediately pulls up the flashcards: heliotrope rash over the eyelids, Gottron's papules on the knuckles, and the shawl sign across the shoulders. It's a visual diagnosis, or so we think. But what happens when the patient walks into your ED with a massive, terrifyingly swollen neck, no fever, and absolutely none of those textbook rashes? If you're waiting for the classic triad, you might miss a diagnosis that is actively destroying your patient's muscles and threatening their airway.

Consider the case of a man in his 40s who rolled into the Emergency Department looking like he was in the middle of a severe anaphylactic reaction or a deep neck space infection. He had progressive neck swelling for three weeks and difficulty swallowing. The ENT team ruled out Ludwig's angina; the CT scan showed diffuse edema involving the strap muscles but no abscess. The immunology team was consulted for suspected idiopathic angioedema. He wasn't on an ACE inhibitor, his C1 esterase levels were normal, and he had no history of allergic reactions. He was a diagnostic orphan—swollen, weak, and slipping through the cracks of our standard algorithms because he didn't look like the pictures in our textbooks.

The pivot point in this case wasn't a new imaging modality or a exotic genetic test; it was a stubborn clinician who noticed that "angioedema" doesn't typically come with proximal muscle weakness and weird nail changes. While the classic heliotrope eruption and Gottron's papules were conspicuously absent, a closer look revealed subtle periungual erythema, nailfold microhemorrhages, and generalized facial redness that spared nothing—not even the nasolabial folds (a clue that often distinguishes this from lupus). A single Creatine Kinase (CK) level returned at a staggering 16,184 U/L. This wasn't angioedema; it was anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis masquerading as pseudoangioedema.

This case serves as a brutal reminder that dermatomyositis is a chameleon. Specifically, the anti-SAE1 phenotype is notorious for flying under the radar. Unlike other myositis subsets where muscle weakness screams for attention early on, SAE1-positive patients often present with prominent subcutaneous edema—particularly in the face and neck—that mimics angioedema or cellulitis. The swelling isn't mediated by histamine or bradykinin; it's driven by intense inflammation of the underlying musculature and connective tissue leaking fluid into the subcutaneous spaces. In one large cohort study, only about 6% of dermatomyositis patients presented with this "pseudoangioedema," making it a rare but critical trap for the unwary emergency physician.

The danger here lies in the delay. This patient had significant dysphagia and aspiration risk due to pharyngeal muscle involvement before anyone suspected an inflammatory myopathy. Had we continued down the path of treating refractory angioedema or searching for an elusive odontogenic source, we would have allowed unchecked rhabdomyolysis and potential respiratory compromise to progress. The key was connecting the dots between non-pitting neck swelling, subtle cutaneous markers like the "holster sign" (erythema on the lateral thigh), and unexplained weakness. Once the CK was checked and the myositis panel sent (which came back positive for anti-SAE1), the trajectory changed instantly from diagnostic confusion to aggressive immunosuppression with steroids, IVIG, and rituximab.

Don't let the absence of classic signs give you false reassurance. In patients with unexplained neck or facial swelling, especially when accompanied by even mild proximal weakness or dysphagia, you must broaden your differential beyond allergy and infection. Look at the nails. Look at the thighs. Check a CK. Recognizing this "wolf in sheep's clothing" phenotype of dermatomyositis can prevent catastrophic muscle breakdown and secure an airway before it's too late.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Pseudoangioedema is a real entity:** Unexplained neck or facial swelling without urticaria or response to antihistamines/epinephrine should trigger a workup for inflammatory myopathy, particularly if C1 esterase studies are normal.
*   **Look past the classics:** Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis often lacks hallmark heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules; instead, hunt for subtle signs like periungual telangiectasias/microhemorrhages, nonspecific facial erythema involving nasolabial folds, and the "holster sign" on lateral thighs.
*   **CK is your best friend:** In any patient with mysterious soft tissue swelling and concurrent proximal weakness or dysphagia, order a Creatine Kinase immediately; levels can be astronomically elevated (>10,000 U/L) even when cutaneous signs are minimal.
*   **Airway protection is paramount:** Swelling in dermatomyositis involves muscle inflammation leading to dysphagia and aspiration risk; early recognition allows for prompt initiation of high-dose steroids and IVIG to halt progression before respiratory failure occurs.
*   **Malignancy screening is mandatory:** Patients with dermatomyositis (especially males with dysphagia and specific antibody profiles like SAE1) carry an elevated risk of occult malignancy and require comprehensive age-appropriate cancer screening upon diagnosis.
