Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Beyond Gottron's Papules: Subtle Cutaneous Clues in Seronegative-Looking Myositis

We love a classic presentation in medicine. The patient walks in with a heliotrope rash, Gottron's papules, and symmetric proximal weakness, and the diagnosis of dermatomyositis practically writes itself. But real life rarely adheres to the textbook illustrations. What happens when the hallmark signs are missing, and the patient presents instead with something that looks suspiciously like an airway emergency?

Consider a man in his 40s who rolls into your Emergency Department with severe, progressive neck swelling. He has no fever, his white count is barely elevated, and he isn't tachypneic, yet his CT scan shows diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space. Your mind immediately goes to the dangerous stuff: Ludwig's angina, deep neck space infection, or perhaps idiopathic angioedema threatening the airway. ENT gets consulted, scopes the throat, finds no abscess, and sends him home. He comes back. The swelling persists. The workup for histaminergic and bradykinin-mediated angioedema is negative. C1 esterase inhibitor levels are normal.

This is exactly where many of us might get stuck in a diagnostic loop of "idiopathic" swelling. But in this specific case, the breakthrough didn't come from repeating the CT scan or trying another dose of epinephrine. It came from a meticulous physical exam that looked beyond the obvious swelling to find the subtle stigmata hiding in plain sight: periungual microhemorrhages, avascular areas on nailfold capillaroscopy, generalized erythema over the nasolabial folds, and a rash on the lateral thigh known as the "holster sign." Despite the complete absence of Gottron's papules or a classic heliotrope eruption, these minor cutaneous clues triggered a creatine kinase (CK) check. The result? A staggering CK of 16,184 U/L. This wasn't angioedema; it was anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis masquerading as a surgical emergency.

The concept of "pseudoangioedema" in dermatomyositis is rare, occurring in less than 7% of patients, yet it represents a critical pitfall for emergency clinicians. When inflammation targets the subcutaneous tissues and underlying musculature of the neck, it mimics fluid-mediated angioedema perfectly. However, the mechanism is entirely different—it's myositis-driven edema, not mast cell degranulation or complement consumption. If you miss this distinction, you delay the intense immunosuppression required to halt rapid muscle destruction and prevent fatal complications like aspiration pneumonitis or respiratory failure due to diaphragmatic involvement. In our patient, the dysphagia was already severe enough to cause micro-aspiration and pulmonary consolidations by the time of diagnosis.

The serology here adds another layer of complexity and learning. This patient was positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies, a subset of dermatomyositis increasingly recognized for its association with severe cutaneous disease and dysphagia, even when classic rashes are muted or absent. While anti-MDA5 or anti-TIF1-γ antibodies are more famously linked to edema and malignancy respectively, SAE1 positivity should still raise your index of suspicion for aggressive disease requiring broad malignancy screening. The takeaway isn't just about knowing the antibody panels; it's about recognizing that a "negative" exam for classic dermatomyositis signs doesn't rule out the disease if you haven't looked at the nails or the lateral thighs.

In the chaos of the ED, it is easy to focus on the life-threatening airway swelling and stop looking once infection and allergic reactions are ruled out. But when faced with unexplained neck edema—especially if accompanied by even vague complaints of fatigue or difficulty swallowing—you must broaden your differential to include inflammatory myopathies. A quick look at the nailfolds for telangiectasias or micro-infarcts and checking proximal strength can pivot your management from antihistamines and antibiotics to high-dose steroids and IVIG before irreversible damage sets in. Don't let the absence of Gottron's papules give you false reassurance; sometimes the devil—and the diagnosis—is in the details you almost missed.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Pseudoangioedema is a real entity:** Unexplained neck or facial swelling without urticaria or response to standard angioedema therapy should trigger consideration of dermatomyositis-induced myositis edema.
*   **Look beyond the classic rash:** Up to 20-30% of dermatomyositis patients may lack pathognomonic signs like Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash at presentation; actively search for subtle markers like periungual telangiectasias, nailfold capillary dropout/microhemorrhages, shawl sign, V-sign, or holster sign (rash on lateral thighs).
*   **Check a CK early:** In any patient with unexplained soft tissue swelling plus proximal weakness (even subtle) or dysphagia, order a creatine kinase immediately regardless of whether they fit the "classic" phenotype.
*   **Don't ignore dysphagia:** Difficulty swallowing in this context is often due to striated muscle involvement (cricopharyngeal dysfunction) rather than mucosal pathology; it carries a high risk for aspiration pneumonia and warrants urgent videofluoroscopy if suspected.
*   **Malignancy screening is mandatory:** Patients with dermatomyositis (particularly those with dysphagia or specific antibody profiles like SAE1) carry an elevated risk of occult malignancy; initiate age-appropriate cancer screening plus extended imaging (CT/PET) per IMACS guidelines upon diagnosis.
