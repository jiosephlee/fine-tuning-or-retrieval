Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# The Airway Threat You Can't Intubate Away: Managing Dysphagia and Aspiration in Acute Myositis

We spend a lot of time in the ED worrying about the airway that swells shut. We think histamine, we think bradykinin, and we reach for epinephrine, icatibant, or a difficult airway cart. But what happens when the airway isn't obstructed by edema, but rather collapses because the muscles powering it have simply given up?

A recent case report by Chan et al. describes a terrifying diagnostic trap: a man in his 40s presenting with massive neck swelling and dysphagia who was bounced between ENT and Immunology with working diagnoses of Ludwig's angina and idiopathic angioedema. The truth was far more insidious. He didn't have an allergic reaction or a deep space infection; he had **dermatomyositis**. Specifically, an anti-SAE1 antibody-positive variant causing such profound inflammation of the strap muscles and pharyngeal musculature that it mimicked angioedema while silently setting him up for catastrophic aspiration.

This case forces us to confront a brutal reality: a patent glottis on scope does not equal a safe airway. When the pump fails, the pipe doesn't matter.

## The Masquerade: Why It Looked Like Angioedema

The patient arrived with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling and hip flexor weakness. To the initial team, the diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space screamed "deep neck infection" or "angioedema." He was afebrile, his white count was normal, and he wasn't tachypneic, which should have paused the infectious disease train early on. Yet, without a clear infectious source, he was labeled as having idiopathic angioedema.

Here is where clinical gestalt needs to override the anchor bias. True angioedema is mediated by histamine or bradykinin. It is vascular permeability run amok. But this patient had something else: **pseudoangioedema**. In dermatomyositis, intense inflammation of the underlying muscle and subcutaneous tissues can create massive swelling that looks identical to angioedema but responds to none of the standard therapies.

The clues were there if we looked past the neck. He had proximal weakness (4/5 hip flexors), subtle periungual telangiectasias (nailfold microhemorrhages), and generalized facial erythema sparing nothing—not even the nasolabial folds (unlike lupus). But perhaps the most glaring red flag was the disconnect between his "swollen throat" and his exam. ENT scoped him and found no anatomical obstruction in the larynx or vallecula. Instead, they saw increased secretions and coughing. The anatomy was fine; the physiology was broken.

## The Pathophysiology of Bulbar Failure

In classic anaphylaxis or hereditary angioedema, we fear laryngeal edema closing the door. In acute inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis, the threat is **bulbar muscle paralysis**.

The inflammation in this patient wasn't just cosmetic; it was destroying the contractile ability of the pharyngeal constrictors and the upper esophageal sphincter. This leads to severe dysphagia where food and saliva don't go down; they sit in the pyriform fossae until they spill over into the trachea. This is silent aspiration waiting to happen. The patient's CT chest already showed patchy peribronchial consolidation from micro-aspiration before he even received a definitive diagnosis.

The mechanism here is distinct from neuropathic causes like Myasthenia Gravis or Guillain-Barré. This is direct myocyte necrosis driven by complement deposition (C5b-9) in the perifascicular capillaries. The muscle fibers are literally being eaten alive by the immune system, leading to skyrocketing enzymes—this patient's CK hit **16,184 U/L**. That degree of rhabdomyolysis-level destruction in the neck muscles explains why simple anti-edema measures failed miserably.

## Diagnostic Pitfalls: Why Scope Isn't Enough

One ofthe most critical teaching points from this case is the limitation of flexible nasendoscopy in this context. An ENT scope is fantastic for visualizing static anatomy: tumors, abscesses, or frank laryngeal edema. It showed this patient had no structural blockage. However, swallowing is a dynamic event involving complex coordination of over 25 muscle pairs.

You cannot diagnose functional dysphagia with a camera sitting still in the nose. You need to see the swallow in action. This patient required a **video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow study**, which revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with residue pooling in the pyriform fossae and penetration of fluids to the vocal cords. By relying solely on the scope, we risk missing impending respiratory failure caused by aspiration pneumonia rather than upper airway obstruction. If your patient has "neck swelling" and weakness, skip straight to checking CK and ordering a swallow study if they are clearing their throat or coughing with liquids.

## Aggressive Immunosuppression: The Only Way Out

Once the diagnosis of anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis was confirmed via antibody panel and muscle biopsy showing perifascicular necrosis, half-measures were off the table. This is not a condition you watch overnight; it is a race against multiorgan damage and respiratory collapse.

The treatment regimen required was heavy artillery:
*   **Pulse Steroids:** 3 days of IV methylprednisolone 1g daily to halt immediate inflammation.
*   **IVIG:** A loading dose of 2g/kg followed by monthly cycles to modulate the immune response rapidly without waiting for oral agents to kick in.
*   **Steroid-Sparing Agents:** Mycophenolate mofetil and Rituximab were added early to maintain remission and allow for steroid tapering.

Notably, methotrexate was avoided due to concerns regarding interstitial lung disease (ILD), which is common in myositis subsets (though less so with SAE1 than others) and this patient already had radiographic evidence of aspiration pneumonitis complicating his picture.The outcome underscores urgency: despite 21 days inpatient and persistent dysphagia requiring an NG tube at discharge, aggressive therapy eventually normalized his CK within 3 weeks and restored full swallowing function by 7 months Had we hesitated or misdiagnosed this as refractory angioedema, irreversible muscle fibrosis or fatal aspiration could have ensued quickly."/>
