Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Steroids Alone Aren't Enough: Navigating Escalated Immunosuppression in Severe Dermatomyositis

We've all seen the patient with a swollen neck in the ED. The differential usually narrows quickly to the "big hitters": anaphylaxis, angioedema (histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated), or a deep neck space infection like Ludwig's angina. But what happens when the CT shows diffuse edema without an abscess, the airway is patent but the patient can't swallow, and the allergy workup comes back stone-cold normal?

In a fascinating case recently published by Chan et al., a man in his 40s presented with exactly this conundrum. He arrived with progressive neck swelling and hip weakness, initially bouncing between ENT and Immunology teams under the working diagnosis of idiopathic angioedema. He wasn't on an ACE inhibitor, his C1 esterase levels were normal, and he lacked the classic fever of an infectious process. The clue that cracked the code wasn't in the neck itself, but in the labs: a Creatine Kinase (CK) level that skyrocketed to **16,184 U/L**.

This wasn't angioedema; it was dermatomyositis masquerading as one. Specifically, it was anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis, a rare subset known for severe cutaneous and muscular involvement. While the diagnostic pivot from "swollen neck" to "autoimmune myopathy" is a great story for grand rounds, the real teaching point lies in how this team managed the fallout. With life-threatening dysphagia, massive muscle necrosis, and aspiration pneumonitis, this case perfectly illustrates why high-dose steroids alone are often insufficient for severe induction therapy.

## The Diagnostic Trap: When Angioedema Isn't Angioedema

Before diving into treatment, we have to appreciate how easily this diagnosis can be missed. Dermatomyositis typically presents with Gottron's papules or a heliotrope rash, but these classic signs were absent or subtle in our patient initially. Instead, he presented with "pseudoangioedema"—non-pitting edema caused by profound inflammation of the underlying musculature and subcutaneous tissues rather than vascular permeability issues.

The presence of proximal weakness (4/5 hip flexors) should always trigger a CK check in the setting of unexplained soft tissue swelling. Once the CK came back >16,000 U/L alongside elevated transaminases (AST > ALT), the picture shifted instantly from an allergic reaction to a fulminant inflammatory myopathy. The confirmation came via a positive anti-SAE1 antibody and a muscle biopsy showing perifascicular necrosis and C5b-9 deposition. This specific antibody subtype is notorious for severe skin disease and dysphagia, occurring in nearly half of reported cases, yet it remains obscure enough to delay care if you aren't looking for it.

## Beyond the Steroid Burst: Rationale for Multi-Agent Induction

When facing a CK of 16,000 U/L and a patient who cannot protect their airway due to pharyngeal muscle paralysis, pulling out the prednisone bottle feels inadequate. In this case, the treating team recognized that monotherapy with corticosteroids would likely be too slow to halt the rapid muscle destruction and too risky regarding long-term toxicity at the required doses. They opted for an aggressive, multi-modal induction strategy immediately upon admission.

The backbone of therapy was undeniably heavy steroids: 3 days of IV methylprednisolone at 1 g/day followed by a high-dose oral taper. However, they didn't stop there. They layered on Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG), Rituximab, and Mycophenolate Mofetil simultaneously. This "kitchen sink" approach might seem aggressive to some, but in severe dermatomyositis with visceral involvement (like esophageal striated muscle leading to aspiration), time is muscle—literally. Early combination therapy has been shown to improve outcomes and reduce cumulative steroid exposure compared to stepwise escalation only after failure.

## Agent Selection: Tailoring Therapy to Comorbidities

One of the most clinically nuanced decisions in this case was the choice of steroid-sparing agents. Traditionally, Methotrexate is often the first-line adjunct for dermatomyositis. However, this patient had pulmonary complications—not interstitial lung disease (ILD) directly from the myositis initially, but significant aspiration pneumonitis and ground-glass opacities on CT.

Methotrexate carries a known risk of pneumonitis and can exacerbate existing lung pathology. Given the patient's respiratory compromise and abnormal pulmonary function tests showing a restrictive pattern, the team wisely avoided Methotrexate entirely. Instead, they chose **Mycophenolate Mofetil**, which has a favorable safety profile regarding lung toxicity and is increasingly favored in connective tissue diseases with pulmonary involvement.

Furthermore, they added **Rituximab** early in the course. While often reserved for refractory cases or specific antibody subtypes (like anti-Jo-1 or anti-MDA5), emerging data supports its use in severe refractory dermatomyositis to achieve rapid B-cell depletion. Combined with **IVIG**, which provides immediate passive immunity and modulates Fc receptors to dampen inflammation rapidly, this triple-threat regimen addressed both immediate stabilization and long-term remission induction.

## Managing the Risks: Prophylaxis in Heavy Immunosuppression

Throwing four potent immunomodulators at a patient inevitably opens Pandora's box regarding infectious risks. The combination of high-dose steroids (>20 mg prednisone equivalent), Rituximab (which depletes B-cells for months), Mycophenolate (T-cell suppression), and IVIG creates a perfect storm for opportunistic infections.

The team correctly identified *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia (PJP) as a primary threat and initiated prophylaxis with Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX). However, even standard prophylaxis isn't without pitfalls; this patient developed hemolytic anemia despite having normal G6PD levels—a reminder that sulfa drugs can cause idiosyncratic reactions even outside of enzymatic deficiencies. The team pivoted gracefully to nebulized Pentamidine, ensuring coverage was maintained without compromising hematologic recovery. Additionally, because Rituximab poses a risk for Hepatitis B reactivation and Varicella Zoster reactivation thorough viral screening was mandatory before initiation (all negative in this case), highlighting that you cannot skip your pre-biologic checklist even in urgent scenarios unless absolutely necessary for survival seconds away from intubation!
