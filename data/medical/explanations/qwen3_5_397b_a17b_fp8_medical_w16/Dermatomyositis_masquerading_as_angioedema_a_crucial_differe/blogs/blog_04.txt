Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Airway Anxiety vs. Muscle Failure: Managing Dysphagia and Aspiration in Acute Myositis

When a patient rolls into your ED with a swollen neck, your brain immediately goes to the worst-case scenarios: Ludwig's angina, epiglottitis, or anaphylaxis. The airway anxiety is real, and rightly so. But what happens when the CT scan rules out a deep space infection, the patient is afebrile, and the ENT scope shows no laryngeal edema? You might find yourself staring at a diagnostic dead end labeled "idiopathic angioedema."

That's exactly where a recent case of anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis left us. A man in his 40s presented with progressive neck swelling and hip weakness. He wasn't obstructing in the traditional sense; his straps were inflamed, and his retropharyngeal space looked angry on imaging, mimicking a surgical emergency. But the real killer here wasn't immediate asphyxiation from soft tissue collapse—it was the silent, insidious threat of bulbar muscle failure leading to massive aspiration.

This case forces us to pivot our mental model from "secure the tube" to "protect the lungs from the inside out." In acute inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis, the neck swelling is often pseudoangioedema driven by underlying myositis and capillary leak, not histamine or bradykinin. The true morbidity comes from dysphagia so severe that every sip of water becomes a potential pneumonia event. Let's break down how we navigated the tension between airway security and aspiration prevention in this unique presentation.

## The Diagnostic Pivot: When Swelling Isn't Angioedema

The initial workup for this patient was textbook for an airway scare: CT neck, ENT consult, and a hunt for infection or allergic mediators. Everything came back negative for infectious or classic angioedema etiologies. C3/C4 and C1-esterase levels were normal. No ACE inhibitors, no family history. Yet, the neck remained diffusely edematous and erythematous.

The clue lay in the details that almost got missed: subtle periungual telangiectasias (nailfold microhemorrhages) and proximal hip flexor weakness graded at 4/5. In the context of unexplained neck swelling, these are screaming red flags for an inflammatory myopathy. Once the Creatine Kinase (CK) returned at a staggering 16,184 U/L, the picture snapped into focus. This wasn't angioedema; it was dermatomyositis with prominent cervical soft tissue involvement—a rare "pseudoangioedema" presentation seen in less than 7% of cases.

The danger here is twofold. First, misdiagnosing this as angioedema delays life-saving immunosuppression. Second, and perhaps more critically for the emergency physician, focusing solely on intubation misses the functional deficit. Our patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia confirmed later by video fluoroscopy, showing residue in the pyriform fossae and penetration of fluids to the vocal cords. His airway was patent, but his protective mechanisms were gone.

## Securing Nutrition vs. Securing the Airway

In standard trauma or sepsis algorithms, if a patient can't protect their airway, we intubate. But in acute myositis with profound bulbar weakness, early intubation isn't always the answer unless there is frank respiratory failure or inability to handle secretions leading to imminent arrest. Intubation carries its own risks—ventilator-associated pneumonia, prolonged ICU stays, and difficult extubation if muscle recovery is slow.

In this case, the team opted for a nuanced approach focused on secretion management and nutritional support without immediate invasive ventilation. The patient was started on aggressive immunosuppression (IV methylprednisolone pulse followed by tapering oral prednisone) alongside IVIG and rituximab to halt the muscle destruction at its source. However, pharmacologic rescue takes time to translate into functional recovery.

While waiting for muscles to heal, we had to bypass the broken swallow mechanism. The decision between Nasogastric (NG) tube versus Percutaneous Endoscopic Gastrostomy (PEG) is often debated in neurology circles. For acute inflammatory myopathies where recovery is anticipated within weeks to months—as evidenced by this patient's full strength recovery at 3 weeks post-discharge—an NG tube is often sufficient for short-term management (<4–6 weeks). It avoids procedural risks and allows for rapid initiation of enteral feeding once dysphagia is identified via bedside screen or formal videofluoroscopy (VFSS).

This patient required NG feeding for two months until his swallow function improved enough for soft solids. Had we jumped straight to PEG or tracheostomy without trialing conservative management alongside aggressive immunotherapy, we might have subjected him to unnecessary procedures given his excellent prognosis for muscular recovery. That said, if dysphagia persists beyond 4-6 weeks despite treatment escalation, early PEG placement should be strongly considered to improve quality of life and reduce aspiration risk during long-term rehabilitation.

## Preventing Aspiration Pneumonitis in the Acute Phase

The chest CT told a grim story: patchy peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration pneumonitis and multiple non-cavitating nodules likely related to chronic micro-aspiration rather than malignancy (which was rigorously screened for). In dermatomyositis, particularly with anti-SAE1 antibodies where dysphagia rates hit nearly 50%, aspiration is a leading cause of mortality independent of respiratory muscle weakness alone!
