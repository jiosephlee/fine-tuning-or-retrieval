Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# When Angioedema Isn't: The Critical Role of CK in Unexplained Neck Swelling

We love a good diagnostic pivot in the Emergency Department. It's the moment the chart stops making sense as "common thing X" and suddenly clicks into "rare thing Y," changing the entire trajectory of care. But sometimes, that pivot gets delayed because we get stuck in a silo. We call ENT for neck swelling, allergy for hives, or ID for fever, forgetting that systemic diseases often wear local masks.

Consider this: A man in his 40s rolls into your ED with a terrifyingly swollen neck. He looks like he has Ludwig's angina or maybe severe idiopathic angioedema. He isn't febrile, his airway is patent (for now), but the swelling is dramatic, involving the strap muscles and extending posteriorly. You rule out infection. You rule out bradykinin-mediated pathways. C1 esterase inhibitor is normal. Complement levels are fine. No ACE inhibitors on board. So, what's left? If you stop looking at the neck and start looking at the *whole* patient—specifically those fingernails and hip flexors—you might catch a diagnosis that demands immediate, aggressive immunosuppression rather than just observation or antihistamines.

This isn't a hypothetical trap; it's a real case of dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema. While we typically associate dermatomyositis with the classic heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules, this patient presented primarily with non-pitting cervical edema and dysphagia. The swelling wasn't fluid leaking from vessels due to histamine or bradykinin; it was inflammatory edema driven by severe myositis of the neck musculature. The clue that broke the case open wasn't a fancy MRI or a specialized antibody panel ordered in the ED—it was a simple Creatine Kinase (CK) level that came back astronomically elevated at over 16,000 U/L.

The danger here is twofold. First, misdiagnosing this as infectious deep neck space infection could lead to unnecessary surgical exploration or broad-spectrum antibiotics that do nothing for autoimmune inflammation. Second, and more critically, delaying the diagnosis of dermatomyositis allows unchecked muscle destruction and potential respiratory compromise from pharyngeal weakness and aspiration. In this specific case, the patient had anti-SAE1 antibodies, a subset known to carry risks of interstitial lung disease and malignancy, making rapid identification even more vital. The "angioedema" was actually a red flag waving desperately for help from the muscular system, obscured by the dramatic presentation in the neck.

We often relegate CK checks to rhabdomyolysis protocols or obvious trauma cases. However, this case argues forcefully that unexplained cervical edema, particularly when paired with even subtle proximal weakness or nailfold changes (like periungual telangiectasias or microhemorrhages), mandates a CK check immediately. Don't wait for the rheumatology consult three days later when the patient is already intubated for aspiration pneumonia or worsening weakness. The physical exam nuances matter: look beyond the swollen neck to the thighs, check the nailfolds with a magnifier or capillaroscope if available, and ask about difficulty swallowing or climbing stairs. Those subtle findings transform a confusing ENT referral into a clear-cut medical emergency requiring steroids and IVIG.

The takeaway isn't just to memorize SAE1 antibodies; it's to broaden our differential for "swollen neck" beyond infection and allergy. When the story doesn't fit angioedema—no itch, no response to standard therapy, normal complements—and it doesn't fit infection—no fever, sterile cultures—think muscle. Think inflammatory myopathy. A single blood test can prevent weeks of diagnostic odyssey and initiate life-saving treatment before irreversible damage occurs.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Unexplained Cervical Edema Requires a CK:** In any patient presenting with neck swelling where infection (Ludwig's angina) and standard angioedema (histaminergic/bradykinin-mediated) have been ruled out, order a Creatine Kinase level immediately to screen for inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis.
*   **Look Beyond the Neck:** Subtle cutaneous signs such as periungual erythema, nailfold microhemorrhages, or a faint holster sign on the thighs can be the only external clues pointing toward dermatomyositis in patients presenting primarily with visceral or muscular symptoms.
*   **Dysphagia is a Red Flag:** Difficulty swallowing in the context of neck swelling may indicate pharyngeal muscle involvement rather than just mechanical obstruction; this carries a high risk for aspiration pneumonitis and warrants urgent evaluation for myositis.
*   **Pseudoangioedema Exists:** Dermatomyositis can cause significant subcutaneous edema due to muscle inflammation and vascular permeability changes that mimic angioedema but will not respond to epinephrine, antihistamines, or C1-inhibitor concentrates.
*   **Early Immunosuppression Matters:** Prompt recognition allows for early initiation of high-dose corticosteroids and adjunctive therapies (IVIG, rituximab), which are crucial for preserving muscle function and reducing mortality associated with respiratory failure and malignancy risk in subsets like anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis.
