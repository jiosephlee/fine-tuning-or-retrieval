Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Escalation Therapy: Navigating Steroid-Sparing Agents in Severe Dermatomyositis

We've all seen the patient with "mystery neck swelling." The workup usually spirals quickly: Is it Ludwig's angina? A deep space abscess? Maybe idiopathic angioedema? But what happens when the CT shows diffuse edema without an abscess, the airway is patent but the patient can't swallow, and the labs come back with a Creatine Kinase (CK) that looks like a typo?

Consider the case of a man in his 40s who rolled into the ED with progressive neck swelling and hip weakness. He was bounced between ENT and Immunology, labeled as a probable deep neck infection, then suspected idiopathic angioedema. He wasn't febrile, his complement levels were normal, and he had no exposure to ACE inhibitors. The clue wasn't in the swelling itself, but in the subtle periungual telangiectasias and a CK that skyrocketed to **16,184 U/L**.

This wasn't angioedema; it was dermatomyositis masquerading as one. Specifically, anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis, a rare subset known for severe cutaneous involvement and, critically, significant dysphagia. While high-dose steroids are the undisputed kickoff for inflammatory myopathies, this case forces us to confront a harder question: When the CK is this high and vital functions like swallowing are compromised, is pulse steroids enough? And perhaps more importantly, how do we choose the right artillery for escalation when traditional options might actually harm the patient?

## The Trap of "Steroids First" in Organ-Threatening Disease

In this case, the team correctly identified the need for aggressive induction therapy. The patient received three days of IV methylprednisolone (1g daily) followed by a high-dose oral taper. This is standard operating procedure. However, looking at the trajectory of severe dermatomyositis—especially with a CK over 10,000 U/L and life-threatening dysphagia leading to aspiration pneumonitis—steroids alone are often too slow or insufficient to halt muscle necrosis.

The danger here isn't just treatment failure; it's the toxicity of prolonged high-dose corticosteroids while waiting for them to work. We need rapid-acting steroid-sparing agents that can induce remission before irreversible muscle damage occurs or before aspiration leads to fatal pneumonia. In this specific case report, the clinicians didn't wait. They immediately layered on Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG), Rituximab, and Mycophenolate Mofetil (MMF). This "kitchen sink" approach might seem aggressive to some, but in the context of anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis with bulbar involvement, hesitation costs function.

## Choosing Your Weapon: Why Not Methotrexate?

If you ask a rheumatologist about first-line steroid-sparing agents for dermatomyositis, Methotrexate (MTX) often tops the list. It's effective, cheap, and familiar. So why did this team explicitly avoid it?

The answer lies in the lungs. Dermatomyositis carries a well-documented association with Interstitial Lung Disease (ILD). While this patient's initial CT showed consolidation likely due to aspiration rather than frank fibrosis, his pulmonary function tests revealed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern. Methotrexate carries a risk of pneumonitis and can exacerbate underlying lung pathology. In a patient already struggling with oxygenation dynamics due to aspiration and potential subclinical ILD (common in myositis spectra), adding a drug with pulmonary toxicity is playing Russian roulette.

Instead, they opted for **Mycophenolate Mofetil (MMF)**. MMF has emerged as a preferred agent in myositis-associated ILD because it lacks the direct pulmonary toxicity of MTX while offering potent immunosuppression. It inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, blocking guanine nucleotide synthesis preferentially in T and B lymphocytes. For the emergency physician or intensivist managing these patients alongside rheumatology, recognizing *why* MTX was skipped is crucial: it signals that lung protection is prioritized alongside muscle recovery.

## The Heavy Hitters: IVIG and Rituximab

When muscle enzymes are in the five figures and the patient cannot protect their airway due to pharyngeal weakness, we need mechanisms that act faster than oral antimetabolites.

**IVIG** acts rapidly through multiple pathways: neutralizing pathogenic autoantibodies, inhibiting complement activation (critical in dermatomyositis where C5b-9 deposition destroys capillaries), and modulating Fc receptors on macrophages. In this case, a loading dose of 2 g/kg was administered over five days. This is not maintenance therapy; this is rescue therapy designed to stop the immune assault immediately while slower-acting agents like MMF ramp up. Evidence supports IVIG particularly in refractory cases or those with severe dysphagia, making it a logical choice here.

Then there is **Rituximab**, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody that depletes B-cells. While traditionally reserved for refractory disease or specific antibody subsets (like anti-synthetase syndrome), its use here reflects the severity of the presentation and the specific profile of anti-SAE1 positivity. Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis can be stubborn; early B-cell depletion may prevent relapse and allow for a more rapid steroid taper. Two doses of 1g given two weeks apart provide sustained depletion, buying time for the muscle fibers to regenerate without ongoing inflammatory insult.

## Practical Considerations: Prophylaxis and Monitoring

Throwing heavy immunosuppression at a patient opens Pandora's box regarding infectious complications. This case serves as a stark reminder that your job isn't done once you order Rituximab; you must anticipate collateral damage.

The patient was started on *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX). This is non-negotiable in patients receiving high-dose steroids plus additional immunosuppressants like Rituximab or MMF. However, medicine is rarely linear: this patient developed hemolytic anemia despite having normal G6PD levels—a known idiosyncratic reaction to sulfonamides in autoimmune populations. The team had to pivot quickly to nebulized pentamidine. This highlights a critical pearl: monitor your CBC closely after initiating prophylaxis don't assume tolerance just because genetic screening was negative earlier or because guidelines say "use TMP-SMX."
