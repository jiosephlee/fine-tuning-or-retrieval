Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# The Malignancy Shadow: Risk Stratification and Screening Protocols for New-Onset Dermatomyositis

We often think of "masqueraders" in the ED as infections hiding behind sterile presentations or cardiac issues mimicking gastritis. But sometimes, the masquerade goes deeper. Consider a man in his 40s rolling into your resuscitation bay with a swollen neck, struggling to swallow, and looking like he's having a severe allergic reaction. Your mind jumps to angioedema—maybe bradykinin-mediated? Did he start an ACE inhibitor? Is this Ludwig's angina? You rule out the airway compromise from infection, check the complement levels (normal), and send him to ENT who finds nothing obstructive.

Here is where the plot twists. That "angioedema" isn't histamine or bradykinin driven; it's inflammatory myopathy leaking into the subcutaneous tissues. In a recent striking case report, a patient presented exactly this way: massive cervical edema and dysphagia, initially misdiagnosed as idiopathic angioedema. It wasn't until someone noticed subtle periungual changes and ordered a creatine kinase (CK) that the true culprit was revealed: **Anti-SAE1 positive Dermatomyositis**. His CK was astronomical at 16,184 U/L. While saving his muscles and airway was the immediate fire fight, lighting that diagnostic fuse ignites a second, more ominous concern: *What cancer is driving this?*

## When Angioedema Isn't Angioedema

Before we dive into the oncology workup, we have to appreciate why this case is such a critical learning point for emergency physicians and hospitalists. Dermatomyositis (DM) is rare, occurring in about 10 per million people, but its ability to mimic other conditions is legendary. The classic textbook description involves Gottron's papules and heliotrope rashes, but reality is messier. As our case demonstrates, you might not see the classic rash immediately. Instead, you see "pseudoangioedema."

This subcutaneous edema results from severe inflammation of the underlying musculature and fascia, particularly in the neck and face. If you miss the connection between unexplained neck swelling, proximal weakness (even if subtle initially), and dysphagia, you delay not just immunosuppression but also vital cancer screening. The clue in this case wasn't just the swelling; it was the constellation of dysphagia, elevated liver enzymes (often a proxy for muscle breakdown when AST > ALT), and those tiny nailfold capillary changes. Once the CK came back hot, the diagnosis shifted from allergy to autoimmunity, opening the door to the most dangerous aspect of new-onset DM: paraneoplastic malignancy.

## The Paraneoplastic Link: Why We Screen Aggressively

Dermatomyositis is not just an autoimmune disease; it is frequently a paraneoplastic syndrome. The association between DM and underlying malignancy is well-documented, with estimates suggesting that up to 20-25% of adult DM cases are associated with cancer. However, not all DM patients carry the same risk. This is where precision medicine meets practical workflow. We don't just throw a "pan-scan" at everyone blindly; we stratify based on specific clinical and serological markers.

Our patient with Anti-SAE1 antibodies presents a fascinating risk profile. Historically, antibodies like Anti-TIF1-γ and Anti-NXP-2 have been flagged as high-risk for malignancy. Anti-SAE1 is less commonly discussed in general circles but carries its own weight. In large cohort studies, SAE1-positive patients show significant rates of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease, with malignancy rates hovering around 18%. While slightly lower than TIF1-γ associations, it is far from negligible. When you combine the antibody status with clinical features like male sex (which generally carries higher cancer risk in DM than female sex) and severe dysphagia, this patient rockets to the top of the risk pyramid. Ignoring this stratification means potentially missing a curable cancer while treating the symptomatology of its immune response.

## Applying the IMACS Guidelines at the Bedside

So, how do we operationalize this fear into action? The International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) has provided robust guidelines for cancer screening in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies. They move us away from vague "age-appropriate screening" toward risk-adjusted protocols. For our patient, the math was clear: he had two high-risk factors (the diagnosis of dermatomyositis itself plus dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (male sex and SAE1 positivity). This categorizes him as **High Risk**, demanding an aggressive diagnostic sweep immediately upon diagnosis.

The workup for high-risk patients extends beyond standard physical exams and basic labs. It requires a multimodal approach including cross-sectional imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis—which in this case revealed pulmonary nodules (later deemed likely aspiration/inflammatory but requiring vigilance) and necessitated ruling out metastasis via PET scan. Crucially, because CT scans can miss mucosal lesions in the GI tract, guidelines recommend endoscopic evaluation (gastroscopy and colonoscopy) even in asymptomatic patients if they fall into high-risk categories. In our case report, despite negative tumor markers (CEA, CA-19-9, PSA) and reassuring initial imaging, the plan rightly included invasive GI scope screening because clinical suspicion outweighed negative biomarkers. This comprehensive net is essential because early detection of the underlying malignancy often leads to remission of the myositis itself once the tumor is resected or treated.
