Chapter 4: ## Therapeutic Strategy and Malignancy Surveillance in High-Risk Myositis

The management of severe, antibody-defined dermatomyositis requires an aggressive, multimodal approach aimed at rapidly suppressing inflammation to prevent irreversible muscle damage and life-threatening complications such as dysphagia and respiratory failure. In cases presenting with critical airway or swallowing involvement, as seen in this patient with severe pharyngeal dysphagia and cervical edema, induction therapy typically begins with high-dose intravenous corticosteroids. The administration of pulse methylprednisolone (e.g., 1000 mg daily for three days) serves to quickly dampen the cytokine storm and reduce tissue edema, followed by a transition to high-dose oral prednisone with a slow, structured taper to maintain remission while minimizing long-term steroid toxicity. Concurrently, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is often employed as a first-line adjunctive therapy, particularly in severe presentations or where rapid functional recovery is essential. A loading dose of 2 g/kg divided over several days, followed by maintenance cycles, provides immediate passive immunity and modulates the autoimmune response through mechanisms including Fc receptor blockade and complement inhibition. This dual approach of high-dose steroids and IVIG addresses both the acute inflammatory crisis and the underlying autoimmunity.

Selecting appropriate steroid-sparing agents is critical for long-term disease control and must be tailored to the patient's specific organ involvement and comorbidities. While methotrexate is a conventional first-line agent for many forms of dermatomyositis, its use is contraindicated or approached with extreme caution in patients with evidence of interstitial lung disease (ILD) or significant pulmonary compromise due to the risk of methotrexate-induced pneumonitis, which can mimic or exacerbate existing lung pathology. In this case, given the patient's restrictive pulmonary pattern and radiological findings suggestive of aspiration pneumonitis alongside potential ILD risk, mycophenolate mofetil was chosen as the preferred immunomodulator. Mycophenolate offers a favorable safety profile regarding pulmonary toxicity and has demonstrated efficacy in treating both cutaneous and muscular manifestations of dermatomyositis. Furthermore, for patients with severe disease refractory to initial combinations or those with specific high-risk antibody profiles like anti-SAE1, the addition of rituximab—a monoclonal antibody targeting CD20 on B cells—provides a potent mechanism to deplete autoreactive B-cell clones. The integration of rituximab early in the treatment course for severe cases can facilitate faster tapering of corticosteroids and improve overall outcomes in refractory myositis.

Intense immunosuppressive regimens inevitably increase susceptibility to opportunistic infections, necessitating rigorous prophylactic measures. Prophylaxis against *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia (PJP) is standard of care for patients receiving high-dose corticosteroids combined with additional immunosuppressants. Although trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is the first-line agent, clinicians must remain vigilant for adverse reactions; in this instance, the development of hemolytic anemia necessitated a switch to nebulized pentamidine. This adjustment underscores the importance of monitoring hematologic parameters and adapting prophylactic strategies to ensure patient safety without compromising infection protection during the vulnerable induction phase.

Parallel to therapeutic intervention, rigorous malignancy surveillance is mandatory in adult-onset dermatomyositis, particularly in patients identified as high-risk according to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines. Risk stratification integrates demographic factors (such as older age and male sex), clinical features (including dysphagia and cutaneous necrosis), and serological markers. Anti-SAE1 positivity, while sometimes associated with a distinct phenotype, still warrants comprehensive screening given the established association between dermatomyositis and occult malignancy. In this case, the patient possessed multiple high-and intermediate-risk factors, prompting an extensive workup that included cross-sectional imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis via CT and PET scans to detect metabolically active tumors. Normal tumor markers (CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, PSA) provided additional reassurance but did not replace the need for direct visualization. Consequently, planned endoscopic evaluation of the gastrointestinal tract remains a crucial component of the screening algorithm to exclude occult colorectal or gastric cancers that may not be apparent on external imaging. Ongoing surveillance involves regular clinical assessments and repeat imaging at defined intervals, as the risk of malignancy persists beyond the initial diagnosis period.
