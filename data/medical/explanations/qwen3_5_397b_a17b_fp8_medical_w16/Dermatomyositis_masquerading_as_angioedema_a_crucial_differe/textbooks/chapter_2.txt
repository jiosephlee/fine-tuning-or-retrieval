Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology and Serology: Understanding Anti-SAE1 Dermatomyositis

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies represent a heterogeneous group of autoimmune disorders characterized by chronic muscle inflammation and weakness, with dermatomyositis (DM) being a distinct subset defined by characteristic cutaneous findings alongside myopathy. While DM exhibits a bimodal age distribution, peaking in children aged 5–14 and adults between 45 and 64, the presence of specific autoantibodies often dictates the clinical phenotype, severity, and organ involvement more accurately than age alone. In the context of this case, the patient's presentation in his 40s aligns with the adult onset peak, yet his specific serological profile—positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (anti-SAE1) antibodies—defines a unique and aggressive clinical trajectory that diverges from classic DM presentations.

The pathogenic cornerstone of anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis lies in the disruption of post-translational protein modification known as "sumoylation." The SAE1 enzyme functions as part of a heterodimer responsible for activating small ubiquitin-like modifiers (SUMO), which are structurally similar to ubiquitin. Under physiological conditions, sumoylation regulates critical cellular processes, including nuclear transport, transcriptional regulation, and DNA repair. In anti-SAE1 positive DM, autoantibodies target this enzymatic complex, potentially impairing the sumoylation pathway in endothelial cells and myofibers. Although the precise downstream cascade linking this enzymatic inhibition to frank tissue necrosis remains an area of active investigation, the resulting immune dysregulation manifests as a severe inflammatory myopathy with a predilection for specific organ systems not always dominant in other DM subtypes.

Clinically, anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is distinguished by a high frequency of severe dysphagia and interstitial lung disease (ILD). Literature reviews indicate that nearly half of patients with this antibody subtype experience significant swallowing difficulties due to striated muscle involvement in the pharynx and esophagus, while a comparable proportion develop ILD. In the reported case, these features were paramount; the patient exhibited severe pharyngeal dysphagia leading to aspiration pneumonitis and demonstrated pulmonary function deficits consistent with an extrathoracic restrictive pattern. This phenotype underscores the importance of recognizing that anti-SAE1 positivity often signals a disease course complicated by profound bulbar and respiratory muscle weakness, necessitating vigilant monitoring for aspiration and pulmonary compromise beyond simple limb weakness.

The mechanism driving the "pseudoangioedema" seen in this patient differs fundamentally from true angioedema mediated by histamine or bradykinin. Classic angioedema involves vasoactive peptides causing transient vascular permeability without significant inflammation or tissue destruction. Conversely, the neck swelling in dermatomyositis arises from an underlying complement-mediated microangiopathy. Pathologically, this involves the deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on perifascicular endomysial capillaries, leading to capillary dropout, ischemia, and subsequent muscle fiber necrosis in a perifascicular distribution. As evidenced by this patient's muscle biopsy showing necrotic myofibers and C5b-9 deposition, the visible soft tissue edema is essentially an extension of deep muscular inflammation and fluid extravasation secondary to microvascular injury rather than a primary allergic or kinin-driven event. This distinction is critical because it renders standard angioedema therapies ineffective while mandating potent immunosuppression to halt the destructive myopathic process.

Despite being a recognized feature of dermatomyositis, subcutaneous edema presenting as localized neck or facial swelling is statistically uncommon. Cohort studies suggest that only approximately 6% to 7% of dermatomyositis patients exhibit pseudoangioedema, making it a frequent source of diagnostic confusion with deep neck space infections like Ludwig's angina or idiopathic angioedema. While certain antibodies such as anti-MDA5 or anti-TIF1-γ have been more frequently associated with edematous presentations in historical literature, this case illustrates that anti-SAE1 positivity can also manifest with prominent cervical soft tissue swelling. The rarity of this presentation emphasizes that when faced with unexplained neck edema accompanied by even subtle cutaneous signs like periungual erythema or holstersign rash, clinicians must consider an inflammatory myopathy and pursue serological testing for myositis-specific antibodies to avoid delays in initiating life-saving immunomodulatory therapy.
