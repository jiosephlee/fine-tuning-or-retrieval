Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Reasoning: From Bedside to Biopsy

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates a critical shift from excluding life-threatening emergencies to identifying a rare autoimmune etiology through a structured hierarchy of testing. Initially, the patient's presentation of severe neck swelling and elevated inflammatory markers prompted an exclusionary workup focused on infectious and allergic causes. Given the suspicion of angioedema, it was imperative to rule out bradykinin-mediated pathways, which are characteristic of hereditary or acquired angioedema. The normalization of C3 and C4 complement levels, alongside normal C1-esterase inhibitor function and quantification, effectively excluded these common causes of non-histaminergic swelling. Similarly, the absence of fever, normal white cell count relative to the degree of swelling, and negative imaging for abscess formation helped dismiss deep neck space infections like Ludwig's angina. This negative workup for primary otolaryngologic and immunologic emergencies created the necessary clinical space to reconsider the diagnosis based on subtle physical findings, specifically the periungual erythema and proximal muscle weakness, which pointed toward an inflammatory myopathy.

Once the possibility of dermatomyositis was raised, serological confirmation became the cornerstone of the diagnostic process. The patient exhibited a high-titer antinuclear antibody (ANA) with a speckled pattern, a sensitive but non-specific marker for connective tissue diseases. Definitive classification relied on a comprehensive myositis antibody panel, which identified strong positivity for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (anti-SAE1) antibodies. This specific autoantibody is highly specific for dermatomyositis and often correlates with prominent cutaneous features and dysphagia, even in the absence of classic signs like Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash at initial presentation. The identification of anti-SAE1 guided the subsequent search for visceral involvement and malignancy risk, as this subset carries a distinct clinical profile compared to other myositis-specific antibodies such as anti-Jo-1 or anti-Mi-2.

Advanced imaging played a dual role in this case: confirming active muscle inflammation and screening for paraneoplastic syndromes. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the thighs provided anatomical detail, revealing hyperintense signals in the obturator internus, externus, and hip adductor muscles consistent with active myositis. While MRI localized the inflammation, fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET) offered a functional assessment of metabolic activity across multiple muscle groups, including the head, neck, and trunk, corroborating the systemic nature of the disease. Crucially, the PET scan also served as a vital malignancy screen; the absence of metabolically active lesions outside the musculoskeletal system helped mitigate immediate concerns regarding an underlying occult tumor, although ongoing surveillance remained necessary given the known association between dermatomyositis and cancer.

Definitive diagnosis was ultimately secured through histopathological examination of a thigh muscle biopsy, which revealed features pathognomonic for dermatomyositis. Microscopic analysis demonstrated perifascicular atrophy—a hallmark finding where muscle fiber degeneration occurs predominantly at the periphery of the fascicle—alongside perimysial inflammation composed of dispersed histiocytes. Perhaps most diagnostically significant was the immunohistochemical staining showing deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on perifascicular endomysial capillaries. This finding confirms that the primary pathology involves complement-mediated microangiopathy leading to muscle ischemia and necrosis, distinguishing dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies like polymyositis or immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, which lack this specific vascular deposition pattern.

Finally, assessing visceral involvement was essential for managing immediate risks and guiding long-term care. The patient's complaint of mild myalgia masked severe pharyngeal dysfunction, which was objectively quantified via a video fluoroscopic swallow study. This test revealed significant residue in the pyriform fossae and penetration of fluids into the vocal cords, explaining the aspiration pneumonitis seen on chest CT and necessitating nasogastric feeding to prevent further pulmonary compromise. Pulmonary function tests further characterized an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, likely secondary to both respiratory muscle weakness and mechanical restriction from neck edema. Together, these assessments confirmed that while the neck swelling was the presenting complaint, it was merely one manifestation of a multisystem autoimmune process requiring aggressive immunosuppression to preserve airway integrity and muscular function.
