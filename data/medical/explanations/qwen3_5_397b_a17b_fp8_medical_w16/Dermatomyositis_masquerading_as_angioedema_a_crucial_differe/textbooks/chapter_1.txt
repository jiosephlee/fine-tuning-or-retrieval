Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and the Diagnostic Pitfall: When Neck Swelling is Not Angioedema

The initial presentation of this patient illustrates a classic diagnostic trap where a systemic autoimmune myopathy mimics a localized otolaryngologic or allergic emergency. Upon arrival, the patient's progressive neck swelling and elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 55.6 mg/L) naturally directed the clinical team toward infectious etiologies, specifically Ludwig's angina or a deep neck space infection. This hypothesis was reinforced by CT imaging showing diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal inflammation. However, key clinical features argued against an infectious origin: the patient was afebrile, hemodynamically stable, and lacked the exquisite tenderness typically associated with cellulitis or abscess formation. Furthermore, the failure of the swelling to resolve and the absence of a purulent source on ENT evaluation should have prompted a shift in differential diagnosis away from infection. Recognizing that "cold" inflammation—swelling without fever or focal tenderness—can occur in autoimmune conditions is the first step in avoiding unnecessary surgical interventions or prolonged antibiotic courses.

Once infection was ruled out, the working diagnosis shifted to idiopathic angioedema, yet the clinical picture remained inconsistent with both histaminergic and bradykinin-mediated pathways. True angioedema is characterized by transient, often asymmetric swelling that responds to antihistamines, corticosteroids, or epinephrine (in histaminergic cases) or resolves spontaneously over days (in bradykinin-mediated cases). In contrast, this patient exhibited persistent, non-tender, generalized erythema of the face and neck that did not fluctuate in the manner typical of allergic reactions. Crucially, standard investigations for angioedema were unrevealing; C3 and C4 complement levels were normal, and C1 esterase inhibitor function and quantity were within reference ranges, effectively excluding hereditary and acquired C1-inhibitor deficient angioedema. The persistence of swelling alongside new neurological symptoms signaled that the edema was likely "pseudoangioedema"—a manifestation of underlying tissue infiltration or inflammation rather than vascular permeability changes driven by vasoactive mediators.

The pivot point in this case was the identification of subtle cutaneous stigmata and concurrent neuromuscular deficits that are hallmarks of dermatomyositis but easily overlooked when attention is fixated on airway swelling. While the patient lacked the pathognomonic Gottron's papules or heliotrope rash initially described in textbooks, a meticulous examination revealed periungual telangiectasias with avascular areas on nailfold capillaroscopy, periungual erythema, and a Holster sign (an erythematous rash on the lateral thigh). These findings, though subtle compared to the dramatic neck swelling, are highly specific for inflammatory myopathies. Furthermore, the presence of proximal muscle weakness (4/5 power in hip flexors) and dysphagia provided critical context. Dysphagia in dermatomyositis results from striated muscle involvement in the pharynx and upper esophagus, leading to aspiration risks evidenced here by coughing during nasendoscopy and subsequent radiographic confirmation of penetration. The coexistence of unexplained neck edema, proximal weakness, and dysphagia creates a clinical triad that should immediately trigger suspicion for myositis.

Laboratory data ultimately confirmed the suspicion raised by the physical exam, revealing a pattern of massive muscle injury disproportionate to any localized soft tissue process. The most striking abnormality was the creatine kinase (CK) level, which soared to 16,184 U/L. Such extreme elevation is inconsistent with simple soft tissue edema or mild myalgia and indicates widespread rhabdomyolysis or severe active myositis. Additionally, the patient presented with elevated transaminases (AST 396 U/L > ALT 143 U/L) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH 692 U/L). In the absence of primary liver disease—which was supported by normal bilirubin and synthetic function—these elevations reflect leakage from damaged skeletal muscle rather than hepatocellular injury. The combination of disproportionately high CK, muscle-derived enzyme elevation, negative angioedema workup, and specific cutaneous findings transforms the diagnostic narrative from an allergic or infectious crisis to an urgent autoimmune myopathy requiring immediate immunosuppression.
