Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Beyond the Valsalva Headache: When "Burning Vertex" Masks a Crowded Foramen Magnum

We teach medical students and residents that Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI) is the classic "Valsalva headache." You know the script: a patient complains of suboccipital pressure that explodes when they cough, sneeze, or strain. It's the board-exam vignette, the hallmark of posterior fossa crowding compressing the cervicomedullary junction. But what happens when the patient doesn't fit the script? What if their primary complaint isn't a headache triggered by exertion, but a bizarre, localized scalp sensitivity so severe that cutting their hair provides genuine analgesic relief?

A recent case report forces us to expand our mental model of Chiari-associated pain. It describes a woman in her early 40s who spent 14 years navigating the healthcare system with chronic vertex headaches and recurrent choking episodes, only to be told she had anxiety or "nummular headache." Her story is a stark reminder that structural brainstem pathology can wear many masks, including cutaneous allodynia and silent hypoxia that mimics Ondine's Curse. If you aren't looking for the crowded foramen magnum in patients with unexplained bulbar symptoms or atypical scalp pain, you might miss it for over a decade.

### The Case That Didn't Fit
This patient presented with a seven-year history of terrifying choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, occurring during meals and sleep. She also suffered from chronic headaches centered on the vertex—the top of her head—not the occiput. These weren't your typical throbbing migraines; they were constant, pressure-like pains accompanied by marked allodynia. The weight of her own hair on her scalp was painful enough to keep her baseline pain at a 6/10. She found that cutting her hair short dropped her pain to a 3/10, a coping mechanism that caused her significant cultural and emotional distress but provided undeniable physiologic relief.

Simultaneously, she was experiencing nocturnal desaturations down to 70–80% without any sense of dyspnea. During an incident at an airport, she was found with an oxygen saturation of 78%, dizzy and nauseous, yet completely unaware she wasn't breathing adequately until supplemental oxygen resolved her symptoms minutes later. Extensive workups by ENT and GI—multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies—were entirely normal. Pulmonary function tests were pristine. The disconnect between her severe hypoxia and her lack of air hunger suggested a failure of automatic respiratory drive, not lung disease.

The breakthrough came only when a clinician dug up a CT scan from 14 years prior, taken after a minor motor vehicle accident. Originally read as normal, a fresh look revealed a crowded foramen magnum with loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction. Subsequent MRI confirmed Chiari Malformation Type I with an 11 mm tonsillar descent. The choking? Compression of lower cranial nerve nuclei. The silent hypoxia? Brainstem compression disrupting automatic respiratory centers (acquired central hypoventilation). And the vertex pain? A likely manifestation of occipital neuralgia or "burning vertex syndrome" referred through disrupted trigeminocervical pathways.

### Rethinking Pain Phenotypes in CMI
The most striking aspect of this case is the pain profile. We are conditioned to look for occipital pain exacerbated by Valsalva maneuvers, which occurs in over 80% of symptomatic CMI patients. However, this patient's pain was localized to the vertex and characterized by cutaneous allodynia—a phenomenon where non-painful stimuli (like hair resting on the scalp) provoke pain. While occipital neuralgia is a known associate of CMI, the specific presentation of "burning vertex" relieved by hair removal is rarely discussed in standard algorithms.

This aligns with emerging concepts like "burning vertex syndrome," which involves small fiber pathways and doesn't fit neatly into migraine or tension-type headache buckets. In CMI, the mechanistic link likely involves irritation of the upper cervical roots (C1-C3) or the spinal trigeminal nucleus due to tonsillar impaction and altered CSF dynamics. When a patient presents with chronic scalp dysesthesia or allodynia that defies dermatological or primary headache explanations, particularly if accompanied by any bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, hoarseness, choking), the posterior fossa must be interrogated. The fact that this patient was misdiagnosed with nummular headache for years underscores how easily we attribute strange sensory phenomena to primary headache disorders when we fail to image the craniocervical junction.

### Silent Hypoxia and the Failure of Automatic Drive
Beyond the pain, this case serves as a masterclass in recognizing acquired central hypoventilation syndrome ("Ondine's Curse") secondary to structural lesions. The patient's ability to maintain normal oxygenation while awake and attentive (voluntary breathing) contrasted sharply with her profound desaturation during sleep or distraction (automatic breathing). This dissociation confirms brainstem involvement specifically affecting the medullary respiratory centers while sparing cortical voluntary pathways.

Critically, she lacked dyspnea during these hypoxic events Dyspnea is an interoceptive sensation generated when afferent signals from chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors reach the anterior insular cortex. In this case, brainstem compression likely interrupted these ascending afferent signals before they could be processed consciously. She literally "forgot to breathe" without feeling air hunger. This is dangerous because it removes the body's primary alarm system for respiratory failure. Clinicians should suspect central hypoventilation in any patient with unexplained hypoxia who appears surprisingly comfortable despite low saturations, especially if they have co-existing neurological signs like dysphagia or atypical headaches.

### Diagnostic Pitfalls and Take-Home Points
The 14-year diagnostic delay in this case was driven by siloed thinking.
