Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# The Silent Desaturation: When 'Ondine's Curse' Isn't Congenital

Imagine a patient sitting in an airport terminal, suddenly becoming dizzy and nauseated. A quick check of their vitals reveals an oxygen saturation of 78%. Yet, here is the kicker: they aren't short of breath. They aren't gasping. They don't feel "air hungry." In fact, without a pulse oximeter clipped to their finger, they wouldn't know anything was wrong at all. Supplemental oxygen fixes the numbers in minutes, but the root cause remains a mystery after years of normal pulmonology and ENT workups.

This isn't a scene from a sci-fi thriller; it's the reality for a woman in her early 40s recently described in the literature who spent 14 years suffering from unexplained choking episodes and silent hypoxia. Her story forces us to confront a terrifying physiological disconnect: the separation of voluntary and automatic breathing controls. While we often reserve terms like "Ondine's Curse" (central hypoventilation syndrome) for congenital genetic mutations or catastrophic brainstem strokes, this case reminds us that structural compression at the craniocervical junction can slowly steal your ability to breathe automatically while leaving your voluntary drive perfectly intact.

The clinical picture here is classic for a diagnostic rabbit hole. For seven years, this patient endured recurrent choking on both solids and liquids, alongside nocturnal episodes that woke her up gasping. She underwent multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies, all of which came back normal. Gastroenterologists ruled out reflux; pulmonologists found clear lungs and normal PFTs. The missing piece wasn't in her throat or her lungs—it was 11 millimeters below her brainstem. A re-review of a CT scan taken 14 years prior (after a minor car accident) finally revealed a crowded foramen magnum. An MRI confirmed Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI) with significant tonsillar descent compressing the medulla.

What makes this case so critical for emergency physicians and hospitalists is the mechanism of the "silent" desaturation. In healthy humans, breathing is governed by two parallel systems: the voluntary cortical pathway (which lets us hold our breath or speak) and the automatic brainstem pathway (which keeps us alive during sleep or distraction). In CMI, the herniated cerebellar tonsils compress the medullary respiratory centers responsible for automatic drive. Consequently, when the patient is distracted, asleep, or simply not thinking about breathing, the automatic signal fails, leading to central apnea and profound hypoxia. Because the cortical pathway remains functional, the patient *can* breathe if reminded to do so, but they lose the autopilot function essential for survival during rest.

Even more unsettling is the absence of dyspnea during these hypoxic events Dyspnea—the subjective feeling of air hunger—is an interoceptive sensation generated when afferent signals from chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors reach the anterior insular cortex. In this patient, the brainstem compression likely disrupted these ascending afferent pathways before they could ever reach consciousness. The body was screaming for oxygen physiologically, but the message never reached the brain's interpretation center. This explains why she sat at an airport gate with an SpO2 of 78% feeling only dizzy and nauseated, rather than panicked and suffocating. It is a literal failure of threat detection, making these patients uniquely vulnerable to fatal arrhythmias or arrest during sleep without warning signs.

The diagnostic delay in this case—14 years—is unfortunately common in CMI presentations dominated by bulbar or respiratory symptoms rather than the classic Valsalva-induced occipital headache. While over 80% of symptomatic CMI patients report headaches worsened by coughing or straining, our patient's headache was a constant vertex pressure with marked allodynia (she found relief only by cutting her hair short). When respiratory symptoms dominate without obvious neurological deficits on a standard cranial nerve exam, we tend to look down at the lungs and GI tract first. However, dysphagia affecting both solids and liquids equally is a red flag for neurogenic causes over mechanical ones. If your workup for choking and hypoxia is unrevealing, you must look up to the craniocervical junction.

Management in these cases hinges on recognizing that this is a structural problem requiring structural solutions. While conservative management with monitoring was chosen initially for this patient due to personal circumstances, posterior fossa decompression is often curative. By relieving the pressure on the medulla and restoring CSF flow dynamics, surgeons can often reverse the central apnea and resolve the dysphagia. Waiting too long risks permanent neuronal damage to the respiratory centers or sudden death during sleep. As foam educators, we need to keep CMI on our differential for any patient presenting with "unexplained" central sleep apnea or paradoxical hypoxia without distress.

**Key Take-Home Points:**

*   **Suspect Structural Brainstem Pathology in "Silent" Hypoxia:** Profound oxygen desaturation (SpO2 <80%) without subjective dyspnea suggests a failure of automatic respiratory drive and/or disrupted afferent interoceptive pathways, hallmark features of acquired central hypoventilation.
*   **Differentiate Voluntary vs. Automatic Breathing:** Patients with Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI) may maintain normal voluntary ventilation (normal PFTs, able to speak) while losing automatic control during sleep or distraction; standard awake exams may miss this deficit entirely.
*   **Re-evaluate "Normal" Imaging in Context:** A crowded foramen magnum can be overlooked on initial trauma scans; if clinical suspicion exists (chronic occipital headache + bulbar/respiratory symptoms), dedicated MRI of the craniocervical junction is mandatory regardless of prior "normal" CT reports.
*   **Neurogenic Dysphagia Mimics GI Disease:** Choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids equally, especially with normal endoscopy/laryngoscopy results should trigger a neurological workup for lower cranial nerve involvement secondary to brainstem compression.
*   **Sleep Study Limitations:** Be wary of Type III home sleep studies in suspected central hypoventilation; they lack EEG to distinguish sleep time from wake time, potentially underestimating the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) and missing pure central events prevalent in CMI.



# Choking on Nothing: The Neurogenic Dysphagia Blind Spot

We love a good diagnostic mystery, but there is nothing quite as frustrating—and potentially dangerous—as watching a patient suffer for years because we're looking in the wrong organ system. Consider the case of a woman in her early 40s who spent seven years living in fear of her own dinner plate. She presented with recurrent, unpredictable choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids. These weren't just occasional slips; they were violent, distressing events that woke her from sleep three to four times a week and forced her into social isolation. She avoided restaurants. She stopped attending gatherings. The psychological toll was immense.

Naturally, the medical machine did what it does best: it scoped her. Three flexible laryngoscopies. Two upper GI endoscopies. All reported as completely normal. No strictures, no masses, no significant reflux esophagitis. With a clean structural workup, the implicit message sent to the patient was that "nothing is wrong," leading to a cascade of anxiety diagnoses and dismissive encounters. One physician even attributed her terrifying nocturnal gasping to panic attacks. But here is the clinical pearl that should have stopped us in our tracks: **dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally is neurogenic until proven otherwise.** Mechanical obstructions (like rings or tumors) typically start with solids and progress to liquids. When a patient chokes on water just as easily as steak, and their scopes are pristine, the problem isn't in the tube; it's in the switchboard controlling the tube.

The plot thickened when we looked beyond the throat. This patient wasn't just choking; she was silently desaturating. During the pandemic, home monitoring revealed oxygen saturations plummeting to 70%–80% without any subjective sense of dyspnea. In one harrowing instance at an international airport, she became dizzy and nauseous, was found to have an O2 sat of 78%, and resolved instantly with supplemental oxygen—yet felt no "air hunger." This dissociation between severe hypoxia and the lack of respiratory distress is the hallmark of failed automatic breathing control, often termed acquired central hypoventilation or "Ondine's Curse." While her lungs were perfect (normal PFTs and ABGs on room air when awake), her brainstem was failing to drive respiration during sleep or moments of distraction.

The unifying diagnosis lay hidden in plain sight for 14 years. A review of a CT head performed after a minor motor vehicle accident over a decade prior revealed a crowded foramen magnum that had been originally signed out as normal. Subsequent MRI confirmed Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI) with an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. The pathophysiology is elegant in its brutality: the herniated tonsils compress the medulla, disrupting both the lower cranial nerve nuclei (causing the neurogenic dysphagia) and the respiratory centers (causing central apnea). The trauma likely either precipitated the herniation or unmasked a congenital vulnerability, kicking off a 14-year odyssey of misdiagnosis that could have been shortened with a single glance at the craniocervical junction.

This case forces us to confront a critical blind spot in our approach to dysphagia and unexplained hypoxia. We are quick to blame GERD or functional disorders when scopes are negative, but we often neglect the posterior fossa. The presence of chronic occipital headaches—even if they don't classically worsen with Valsalva—combined with bulbar symptoms should trigger an immediate neurological workup. Furthermore, this patient's story highlights the danger of relying solely on standard screening tools; her sleep study showed a deceptively mild Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) of 6.0, yet 72% of those events were central. Without recognizing the predominance of central events and correlating them with her anatomy, one might falsely reassure themselves that sleep apnea isn't driving her physiology.

The takeaway here isn't just about remembering Chiari malformation; it's about respecting the pattern of "negative" workups. When a patient tells you they are choking on everything and your scope says everything looks fine, believe the patient, not the scope. The pathology is likely upstream. Delayed diagnosis in CMI can lead to irreversible neurological decline or sudden death from respiratory arrest during sleep. We must broaden our differential for "unexplained" dysphagia to include structural brain abnormalities before subjecting patients to another round of futile GI interventions or labeling them with psychogenic causes.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Solids AND Liquids = Neurogenic:** Dysphagia affecting all consistencies equally strongly suggests a neurological etiology (lower cranial nerve dysfunction) rather than a mechanical obstruction or motility disorder like GERD.
*   **The "Normal" Scope Trap:** Repeated normal laryngoscopies and endoscopies in a patient with persistent choking should mandate neuro-imaging of the brain and cervical spine, specifically evaluating the foramen magnum for Chiari Malformation Type I.
*   **Silent Hypoxia is Red Flag:** Severe oxygen desaturation without subjective dyspnea ("air hunger") indicates a failure of automatic respiratory drive (central hypoventilation), pointing toward brainstem pathology rather than primary lung disease.
*   **Don't Trust Mild AHI Alone:** In suspected central disorders, look at the *type* of apneas, not just the count. A low AHI dominated by central events (>50%) in the setting of anatomical compression is highly significant and may be underestimated by type III home sleep studies lacking EEG.
*   **Re-read Old Imaging:** Historical imaging labeled "normal" may harbor overlooked signs of crowding at the craniocervical junction; always correlate old scans with new clinical findings in complex cases.



# Radiology Retrospective: The Danger of the 'Normal' Report in Post-Traumatic Headaches

We love to hate the phrase "clinical correlation recommended," but there is a far more dangerous two-word phrase lurking in our electronic medical records: **"Report normal."**

Consider the case of a woman in her early 40s who spent **14 years** living with a ticking time bomb in her posterior fossa. Her story begins with a minor motor vehicle accident and a brief loss of consciousness. She walked away with persistent, debilitating occipital and vertex headaches and, over the next decade and a half, developed terrifying episodes of choking on both solids and liquids. She suffered nocturnal gasping fits that woke her from sleep multiple times a week. Home pulse oximetry during the pandemic caught her saturations dipping into the 70s while she sat quietly, completely unaware she wasn't breathing—a literal case of "forgetting to breathe" akin to Ondine's Curse.

She underwent three laryngoscopies, two endoscopies, and countless pulmonary workups. Everything came back normal. Why? Because the answer wasn't in her throat or her lungs; it was in her brainstem, compressed by an 11 mm tonsillar herniation.

The tragedy here isn't just the delay; it's that the diagnosis was sitting in the archive, buried under a "normal" read from 14 years prior. A non-contrast CT head performed immediately after her initial trauma showed a crowded foramen magnum and loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction. It was missed then, and it took a dedicated re-review of those old images to finally connect the dots to Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI). This case serves as a stark reminder: when the clinical picture screams neurological dysfunction but the specialist workup is silent, you must become your own radiologist.

## The Myth of the Negative Trauma Scan

In the chaos of post-traumatic evaluation, our eyes are trained to hunt for the catastrophic: the expanding subdural, the depressed skull fracture, the traumatic SAH. Once we rule out the bleed, we often mentally check out, accepting the radiologist's summary of "no acute intracranial abnormality" as gospel. But in patients with persistent post-concussive headaches, especially those localized to the occiput or vertex, "no acute bleed" does not equal "normal anatomy."

Chiari Malformation Type I is defined by cerebellar tonsils descending more than 5 mm below the foramen magnum. While MRI is the gold standard for measurement, non-contrast CT—the workhorse of trauma bays—can absolutely flag this if you know where to look. In this patient's original scan, the clues were subtle but present: effacement of the basal cisterns and cerebellar tissue extending down to the level of the opisthion. The posterior fossa looked cramped because it *was* cramped.

When you are reviewing a head CT for someone with chronic headaches following trauma, do not stop at ruling out hemorrhage. Scroll down. Look specifically at the craniocervical junction. Is there CSF space surrounding the tonsils, or does the brainstem look squeezed against the bone? A crowded foramen magnum on a trauma CT is a red flag that demands an MRI follow-up, regardless of what the initial report said. Relying solely on the text summary without visual confirmation is how 14-year diagnostic odysseys begin.

## Trauma: Cause or Catalyst?

This case also reignites the debate regarding trauma and Chiari: did the car accident cause the herniation, or did it simply unmask a congenital vulnerability? The literature describes CMI as existing on a spectrum from incidental anatomical variant to symptomatic pathology. Some patients live asymptomatically with mild tonsillar ectopia until a whiplash injury or minor head trauma alters CSF hydrodynamics enough to push them over the threshold into symptomatology.

In our patient, we cannot definitively say if she was born with symptomatic CMI or if the trauma induced downward herniation. However, the temporal relationship is undeniable. The onset of her occipital headaches was immediate post-injury, followed by a slow progression of lower cranial nerve deficits (choking) and respiratory center compression (central hypoventilation). Whether primary or secondary, the result is identical: brainstem compression disrupting automatic respiratory drive and bulbar function. For us as clinicians, etiology matters less than recognition. If a patient develops new-onset Valsalva-induced headaches (though notably absent in this specific case due to atypical presentation), dysphagia, or unexplained hypoxia after head trauma, CMI must be on your differential list.

## When Choking Isn't GERD

Perhaps the most profound teaching point from this case is recognizing neurogenic dysphagia masquerading as GI pathology. This patient endured years of invasive scopes searching for reflux or structural strictures because she choked on food and water. The key discriminator here—and a massive pearl for emergency physicians and hospitalists—is that her dysphagia affected solids and liquids equally from day one. Mechanical obstructions usually start with solids; neurogenic causes hit everything indiscriminately because they represent a failure of coordinated muscle movement driven by cranial nerve compression (specifically CN IX and X).

Furthermore, her respiratory profile was classic for brainstem compromise: severe desaturation without dyspnea. This dissociation occurs because voluntary breathing (cortical control) remained intact while automatic breathing (medullary control) failed during sleep or distraction. The afferent signals telling her brain "you are hypoxic" were blocked bythe very compression causingthe hypoxia. When you see a patient with "anxiety" who drops their sats to 78% while chatting calmly in your department, think brainstem before you think panic attack.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Never trust a "normal" report blindly in complex cases:** If clinical symptoms persist despite negative targeted workups (ENT/GI/Pulm), personally review prior imaging looking for structural anomalies like a crowded foramen magnum that may have been overlooked during acute trauma reads.
*   **Scroll down on every head CT:** In patients with post-traumatic headaches, explicitly evaluatethe craniocervical junction on non-contrast CT for loss of CSF spaces and low-lying tonsils; do not wait for an MRI order to suspect Chiari ifthe CT looks suspicious.
*   **Neurogenic vs. Mechanical Dysphagia:** Choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids equally, particularly with normal endoscopic findings, should trigger immediate consideration of lower cranial nerve pathology and posterior fossa imaging rather than further GI investigation.
*   **Silent Hypoxia implies Brainstem Pathology:** Severe oxygen desaturation inthe absenceof subjective dyspnea ("air hunger") suggests a disruptionofthe automatic respiratory centers inthe medulla ortheir afferent signaling pathways; consider structural lesions like Chiari malformation in unexplained central hypoventilation syndromes ("Ondine's Curse").



# Beyond the Valsalva Headache: When "Burning Vertex" Masks a Crowded Foramen Magnum

We love a good pattern recognition case in emergency medicine and critical care. You know the script: a young patient walks in with an occipital headache that explodes whenever they cough, sneeze, or strain. Your brain immediately jumps to Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI), you order an MRI, and sure enough, there are those cerebellar tonsils hanging out below the foramen magnum like bad tenants who won't leave the basement. It's the classic Valsalva-induced headache, present in over 80% of symptomatic cases.

But what happens when the patient doesn't read the textbook? What if the headache isn't occipital, doesn't worsen with straining, and the primary complaint is actually recurrent choking and silent hypoxia?

A recent case report by Mousa et al. throws a fascinating wrench in our diagnostic heuristics. They describe a woman in her early 40s who spent **14 years** navigating the medical system with undiagnosed CMI. Her story isn't just about a missed radiological finding; it's a masterclass in how structural brainstem compression can masquerade as psychiatric anxiety, primary headache disorders, and even "Ondine's Curse." Most strikingly, her pain phenotype wasn't the classic suboccipital pressure—it was a bizarre, localized vertex allodynia so severe she cut her hair short just to reduce the weight on her scalp.

Let's dig into why this case should change how you look at unexplained respiratory failure and atypical head pain.

## The Case of the Silent Hypoxia and the Haircut Cure

The patient presented with a seven-year history of terrifying choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, occurring day and night. She had undergone multiple normal laryngoscopies and endoscopies. The workup for GERD, myasthenia gravis, and primary pulmonary disease came back empty. Meanwhile, she was living with chronic headaches diagnosed as "nummular headache," characterized by a coin-sized area of pain at the vertex of her skull. The pain was accompanied by marked allodynia; simply having long hair resting on her scalp was excruciating. She found relief only by keeping her hair cropped short—a coping mechanism that caused her significant cultural and emotional distress but was physiologically necessary for her comfort.

The real clincher arrived during the pandemic. While self-monitoring due to global health anxieties, she noticed her oxygen saturation periodically plummeting to 70%–80%. Here is the chilling part: **she felt nothing.** No dyspnea, no air hunger, no panic. Just numbers dropping on a screen. ## Why Didn't She Feel Short of Breath?

This case offers a visceral demonstration of the dissociation between voluntary and automatic breathing controls. In healthy physiology, your cortex handles voluntary breath-holding or deep sighs, while your brainstem manages the autopilot function during sleep or distraction. In CMI, the herniated tonsils compress the medulla, disrupting the respiratory centers responsible for that autopilot.

Even more profound is the mechanism behind her lack of dyspnea despite severe hypoxia. Dyspnea—the subjective sensation of "air hunger"—is an interoceptive experience generated when afferent signals from chemoreceptors reach the anterior insular cortex via ascending brainstem pathways. In this patient, we hypothesize that the cervicomedullary compression interrupted these very pathways. Her body was screaming for oxygen physiologically, but the message never reached her conscious awareness. She literally forgot to breathe because her brain couldn't feel the need to do so. This is acquired central hypoventilation syndrome in its purest, most dangerous form.

Classic teaching tells us to look for suboccipital pain exacerbated by Valsalva maneuvers. This patient had neither. # The AHI Trap: Why Standard Sleep Studies Underestimate Central Apnea in Brainstem Compression

We love a good unifying diagnosis. It's the holy grail of clinical reasoning: taking a scattered constellation of symptoms—chronic headaches, mysterious choking episodes, and terrifying oxygen drops—and snapping them together into a single, treatable entity. But what happens when the "gold standard" test you order to confirm your suspicion comes back essentially normal? Do you trust the number, or do you trust the patient who is turning blue in front of your eyes?

Consider the case of a woman in her early 40s who spent seven years being told nothing was wrong. She presented with recurrent choking on both solids and liquids, nocturnal gasping that woke her from sleep, and a 14-year history of debilitating occipital headaches. The workup was exhaustive and unrewarding: three laryngoscopies, two endoscopies, and countless pulmonary function tests all returned normal. Yet, during the pandemic, her home pulse oximeter told a different story: asymptomatic desaturations dipping into the 70s and 80s. One dramatic episode at an international airport saw her saturation hit 78% with acute dizziness, resolving only with supplemental oxygen, while she remained strangely devoid of air hunger.

The breakthrough came not from a new test, but from digging up a 14-year-old CT scan performed after a minor car accident. A fresh look revealed a crowded foramen magnum that had been missed initially. An MRI confirmed Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI) with an 11 mm tonsillar descent. Finally, a unifying diagnosis: brainstem compression causing lower cranial nerve dysfunction (the choking) and disruption of automatic respiratory centers (the hypoxia). This is acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, often dubbed "Ondine's Curse," where the body forgets to breathe automatically while voluntary breathing remains intact.

But here is where the plot thickens and where many of us might get tripped up. When this patient finally underwent a sleep study to quantify her respiratory failure, the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) came back at 6.0. By conventional standards, that's mild sleep apnea. If you were relying solely on that number, you might dismiss the severity of her condition or attribute her desaturations to something else entirely. How does a patient who stops breathing enough to drop her sats to 70% have a "mild" AHI? The answer lies in the methodology of the test itself and a mathematical trap known as the denominator error.

### The Math Behind the Missed Diagnosis

The devil is in the details of how we classify sleep studies. This patient underwent a Type III home sleep apnea test (HSAT). These devices are fantastic for screening obstructive sleep apnea in high-probability patients; they measure airflow, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, and heart rate. However, they lack one critical component: electroencephalography (EEG). Without EEG leads on the scalp, the machine cannot distinguish between sleep and wakefulness.

This limitation creates a fatal flaw when calculating the AHI for patients with central pathology. The AHI is calculated as the number of apneas and hypopneas divided by the total recording time. In a full Type I in-lab polysomnography (PSG), the denominator is *Total Sleep Time* because the EEG tells us exactly when the patient is asleep. In a Type III study, since we don't know when the patient is asleep, we default to using *Total Recording Time*.

Think about this patient's physiology. Her issue is a failure of *automatic* breathing driven by brainstem compression. When she is awake and distracted (like waiting at an airport gate), she can voluntarily breathe fine. When she falls asleep, that automatic drive fails, leading to central apneas and profound desaturation. However, if she spends a significant portion of the night awake due to anxiety or frequent arousals from choking—a likely scenario given her history—the Type III device counts those hours of wakefulness in the denominator.

If she had severe central apneas occurring exclusively during 4 hours of actual sleep but was monitored for 8 hours total, her event rate is effectively halved in the calculation. An AHI of 6.0 on a home study could easily represent an AHI of 12 or higher if corrected for actual sleep time. More importantly, the *nature* of the events matters. Her study showed that 72% of her events were central. In the context of structural brainstem compression and documented severe hypoxia without dyspnea, a "mild" AHI dominated by central events is a screaming red flag for significant pathology that has been mathematically diluted by wake time.

### Why Capnography Matters Even More

Beyond the AHI calculation issue, there is another layer to peeling back this onion: hypercapnia. Central hypoventilation isn't just about stopping airflow; it's about failing to ventilate CO2 adequately relative to metabolic demand. Patients with brainstem compression may maintain adequate oxygenation through compensatory mechanisms until they suddenly don't, or they may retain CO2 significantly before their oxygen saturations crash visibly on a pulse oximeter (especially if they are young and have good pulmonary reserve).

Standard pulse oximetry lag time can mask rapid desaturation events, and it tells us nothing about ventilation efficiency until late in the game. End-tidal capnography (EtCO2) or transcutaneous CO2 monitoring provides real-time data on ventilation status. In cases like this woman's—who lacked "air hunger" despite severe hypoxia due to disrupted afferent signaling to the cortex—relying on subjective symptoms or simple saturation trends is dangerous. She literally did not feel like she was suffocating because the neural pathway transmitting that distress signal was compressed along with her respiratory center groups. We need objective measures of ventilation failure, which requires upgrading from a basic Type III screen to comprehensive monitoring capable of detecting subtle changes in CO2 trends that precede catastrophic desaturation events during REM sleep or deep NREM stages where muscle tone decreases further compromise already precarious mechanics at level C1-C2/Craniocervical Junction .
