Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient presenting with dysphagia affecting both solids and liquids equally, along with normal laryngoscopy and endoscopy findings, what specific neurological etiology involving the craniocervical junction should be prioritized in the differential diagnosis?

When a patient presents with dysphagia affecting both solids and liquids equally, accompanied by normal laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations, the clinician must prioritize **Chiari malformation type I (CMI)** involving the craniocervical junction in the differential diagnosis.

The equal impairment of solids and liquids is the hallmark of a neuromuscular or motility disorder, distinctly separating it from mechanical obstructions (such as strictures or rings) which typically affect solids first. In the setting of normal structural imaging of the aerodigestive tract, the pathology almost invariably resides in the neural control of swallowing. As illustrated in the case of the 40-year-old woman with a 7-year history of choking episodes, CMI can compress the medulla and lower cranial nerve nuclei (specifically IX, X, and XII), leading to oropharyngeal dysfunction that mimics primary gastrointestinal or ENT disease. Her repeated normal endoscopies and laryngoscopies were classic red flags pointing toward a central neurological etiology rather than a local structural one.

The pathophysiology involves caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils (in this case, 11 mm below the foramen magnum) into a developmentally small posterior fossa. This crowding compromises the cervicomedullary junction, disrupting the brainstem centers responsible for automatic respiratory drive and coordinated swallowing. Consequently, patients may present with a triad of symptoms: dysphagia/choking, central sleep apnea (or acquired central hypoventilation syndrome), and occipital headaches. The case report underscores that these respiratory and bulbar symptoms can precede obvious motor deficits by years, often leading to significant diagnostic delays. Therefore, in any patient with unexplained "neurogenic" dysphagia and concomitant respiratory irregularities or occipital pain, high-resolution MRI of the craniocervical junction is mandatory to rule out tonsillar herniation.
