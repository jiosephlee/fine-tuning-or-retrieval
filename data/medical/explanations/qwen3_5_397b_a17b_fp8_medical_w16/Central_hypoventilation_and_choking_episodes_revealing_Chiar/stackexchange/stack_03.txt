Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Clinical Pearls] Why might a Type III home sleep study significantly underestimate the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) in a patient with suspected central hypoventilation secondary to brainstem compression, and what specific component is missing that leads to this error?

A Type III home sleep study significantly underestimates the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) in this context because it lacks electroencephalogram (EEG) monitoring, which is required to distinguish sleep from wakefulness. As illustrated in the case of the patient with Chiari malformation type I, a Type III device calculates the AHI by dividing the number of respiratory events by the **total recording time** rather than the **total sleep time**. Since patients spend a portion of the recording period awake—during which respiratory events are not counted as apneas or hypopneas—the denominator is artificially inflated, leading to a spuriously low AHI. In this specific case, the reported AHI of 6.0 likely masks a much higher burden of central events occurring strictly during sleep. Furthermore, Type III monitors generally lack capnography, making them insensitive to pure central hypoventilation characterized by hypercapnia without frank apnea, further contributing to the underestimation of disease severity in patients with brainstem compression.
