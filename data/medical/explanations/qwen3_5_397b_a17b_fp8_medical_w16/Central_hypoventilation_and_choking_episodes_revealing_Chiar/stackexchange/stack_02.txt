Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Pathophysiology] How does the pathophysiology of respiratory failure in Chiari malformation type I distinguish between voluntary and automatic breathing control, and how does this explain the phenomenon of severe hypoxemia without subjective dyspnea?

In Chiari malformation type I (CMI), the dissociation between voluntary and automatic breathing control arises from the selective anatomical compression of the medullary respiratory centers by herniated cerebellar tonsils. As illustrated in this case, voluntary breathing—mediated by cortical pathways descending through the corticospinal tracts—often remains intact, allowing the patient to maintain adequate ventilation when consciously focused on respiration. In contrast, automatic breathing, which is governed by the brainstem's chemoreceptive networks and is dominant during sleep or states of distraction, is disrupted by direct mechanical compression or ischemia at the cervicomedullary junction. This creates a clinical phenotype akin to acquired central hypoventilation syndrome ("Ondine's curse"), where the patient effectively "forgets to breathe" when cortical override is absent, leading to the predominant central apneas and severe desaturation seen in this patient's sleep study and airport incident.

The phenomenon of severe hypoxemia (saturation 70%–80%) without subjective dyspnea is explained by the interruption of afferent signaling required for the conscious perception of air hunger. Dyspnea is an interoceptive experience generated when sensory inputs regarding respiratory mechanics and gas exchange ascend via the brainstem to be processed in the anterior insular cortex. In this patient, the same structural pathology causing efferent respiratory failure likely disrupts these ascending afferent pathways before they reach the cortex. Consequently, despite profound physiological distress and hypoxia, the brain fails to register the sensation of breathlessness. This case underscores that in CMI, the absence of dyspnea does not indicate benign physiology but rather reflects a specific neurological disconnection between the body's respiratory status and conscious awareness, placing patients at high risk for unrecognized, life-threatening hypoventilation.
