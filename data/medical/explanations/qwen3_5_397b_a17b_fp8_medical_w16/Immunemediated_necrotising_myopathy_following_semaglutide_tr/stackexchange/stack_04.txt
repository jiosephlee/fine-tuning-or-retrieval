Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Pathophysiology] Why might transaminases (AST and ALT) remain elevated in a patient with IMNM even after CK levels begin to normalize, and how can one determine if the source is hepatic versus muscular in a patient with known fatty liver disease?

Transaminases (AST and ALT) may remain elevated in patients with immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) despite the normalization of creatine kinase (CK) levels when there is a concurrent, independent source of hepatic injury. While both AST and ALT are released from damaged skeletal muscle during active myositis—often leading to transaminitis that parallels CK fluctuations—these enzymes are also abundant in hepatocytes. In the setting of resolving muscle inflammation, CK serves as a specific marker for muscular recovery; therefore, if CK normalizes while AST and ALT remain elevated, the persistence of transaminitis strongly suggests a non-muscular etiology, typically intrinsic liver disease.

Differentiating the source in a patient with known fatty liver disease relies on observing the dissociation between muscle and liver markers over time and confirming hepatic pathology through imaging or biopsy. In the reported case of semaglutide-associated IMNM, the patient had a three-year history of biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with baseline transaminitia. Although her CK peaked at nearly 4000 U/L during the acute phase of her myopathy and subsequently normalized completely following immunosuppression, her AST and ALT levels remained mildly elevated. This clinical trajectory indicated that the initial transaminitis was likely multifactorial (contributed by both muscle necrosis and underlying steatosis), but the persistent elevation after muscular recovery was attributable solely to her chronic hepatic steatosis. This distinction was definitively confirmed by a repeat liver biopsy performed during her admission, which demonstrated progression of macrovesicular steatosis without significant inflammation, ruling out active autoimmune hepatitis or ongoing muscle-derived enzyme leakage as the cause of the persistent lab abnormalities. Thus, in patients with comorbid liver disease, one must interpret transaminases cautiously: normalization of CK confirms resolution of the myopathic component, while residual transaminitis should prompt evaluation of the underlying hepatic condition rather than escalation of immunosuppression for presumed refractory myositis.
