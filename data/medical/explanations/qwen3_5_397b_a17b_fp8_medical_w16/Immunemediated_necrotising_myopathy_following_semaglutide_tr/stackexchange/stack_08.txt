Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Morning Report] Considering the World Health Organization (WHO) Uppsala Monitoring Centre criteria for causality assessment, which three elements in this case support a 'probable' rather than 'possible' link between semaglutide exposure and the development of IMNM?

Based on the World Health Organization Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO-UMC) criteria, the classification of "probable" causality in this case rests on three specific pillars derived from the patient's clinical course, distinguishing it from a mere "possible" association.

First, there is a **reasonable temporal relationship** between the exposure and the event. The patient received a single dose of semaglutide and experienced an immediate adverse reaction requiring hospitalization, followed by a progressive decline in muscle strength and a 12-fold elevation in creatine kinase (CK) over the subsequent four months. This timeline establishes a plausible sequence where the drug exposure precedes the onset and progression of the immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

Second, the case demonstrates a **clinically reasonable response to withdrawal** of the suspected agent combined with appropriate therapy. Unlike direct toxic rhabdomyolysis, which typically resolves rapidly upon discontinuation alone, this patient's condition required immunosuppression (corticosteroids and IVIG) alongside the cessation of semaglutide to achieve resolution. The patient subsequently regained baseline functional status and maintained normal CK levels while off both the drug and long-term immunosuppression, supporting the hypothesis that removing the trigger was essential for recovery.

Third, and crucially for elevating the assessment from "possible" to "probable," is the **unlikelihood of alternative explanations**. The authors systematically excluded other common etiologies: the patient had no history of statin use (the most common trigger for anti-HMGCR IMNM), tested negative for a comprehensive myositis antibody panel (including anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR), and lacked clinical features of underlying connective tissue diseases. Furthermore, her pre-existing transaminitis was attributed to biopsy-proven hepatic steatosis rather than muscle breakdown, and there was no evidence of recent excessive physical exertion or other myotoxic supplements. By ruling out these competing diagnoses, the probability that semaglutide was the precipitating factor increases significantly according to WHO-UMC standards.
