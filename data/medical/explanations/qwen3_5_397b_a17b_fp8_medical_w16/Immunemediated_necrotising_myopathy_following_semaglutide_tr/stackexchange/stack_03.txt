Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Management] Given that approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies, how should a clinician proceed with diagnosis and management when the myositis antibody panel is negative but clinical and histological suspicion remains high?

When faced with a patient presenting with classic features of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)—specifically progressive proximal weakness, markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK), and histological evidence of myofiber necrosis with macrophage predominance—a negative myositis antibody panel for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP should not deter the clinician from pursuing a diagnosis of IMNM or initiating treatment. As illustrated in the reported case of semaglutide-associated IMNM, approximately one-third of patients with this condition are seronegative for known autoantibodies. In such instances, the diagnosis rests firmly on the integration of clinical phenotype and muscle biopsy findings rather than serology alone.

The pathological hallmark of IMNM is the presence of scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with minimal mononuclear cell infiltration, often accompanied by deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on non-necrotic fibers, as seen in this patient's vastus lateralis biopsy. The absence of specific antibodies does not negate the autoimmune nature of the disease, particularly when there is a clear temporal relationship to a potential trigger, such as the single dose of semaglutide described here, and when alternative etiologies like infectious myositis or primary metabolic disorders have been excluded.

Management in seronegative cases should mirror that of seropositive IMNM: prompt initiation of high-dose corticosteroids combined with adjunctive therapies such as intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). In the referenced case, the patient achieved complete remission and normalization of CK levels following a short course of pulse steroids, oral prednisone taper, and a single cycle of IVIG, alongside discontinuation of the offending agent. This outcome underscores that seronegative IMNM can be highly responsive to immunosuppression, especially when driven by a removable trigger. Clinicians must therefore rely on histological confirmation and clinical judgment to guide therapy, avoiding delays in treatment while awaiting further serological discoveries or risking irreversible muscle damage due to diagnostic uncertainty.
