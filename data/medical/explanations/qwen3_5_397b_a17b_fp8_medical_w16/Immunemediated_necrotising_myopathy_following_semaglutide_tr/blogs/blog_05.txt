Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# The Obesity Paradox: Navigating Muscle Loss and Metabolic Therapy in the GLP-1 Era

We are living through a revolution in metabolic medicine. Walk into any emergency department or primary care clinic today, and you'll hear patients discussing "Ozempic" and "Wegovy" with the same familiarity they once reserved for Lipitor. Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have undeniably changed the landscape for obesity and type 2 diabetes, offering profound weight loss and cardiovascular benefits. But as these agents become ubiquitous, we are beginning to see the shadow side of rapid metabolic transformation: a complex interplay between intended fat loss and unintended muscle catabolism.

While we generally accept some degree of lean mass reduction as the price of admission for significant weight loss, a recent case report forces us to draw a hard line between physiological adaptation and pathological catastrophe. A woman in her 60s, previously independent and active aside from her struggle with obesity and fatty liver disease, received a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide. Within hours, she was hospitalized with vomiting and acute kidney injury. But the story didn't end there. Over the next four months, while completely off the drug, she developed progressive, devastating proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. By the time she reached tertiary care, she couldn't sit upright without assistance, and her creatine kinase (CK) had skyrocketed to nearly 4,000 U/L.

This wasn't simple dehydration or expected sarcopenia; this was immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). Her muscle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis with scattered necrotic fibers and macrophage infiltration, yet her extensive autoimmune panel—including the classic anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies associated with statin-induced myopathy—was entirely negative. She required pulse steroids and IVIG to recover, eventually returning to baseline only after avoiding GLP-1 agonists entirely. This case represents the first reported instance of IMNM probably associated with a GLP-1 receptor agonist, serving as a stark reminder that not all muscle weakness in the era of weight-loss drugs is benign.

## Beyond Sarcopenic Obesity: When Adaptation Becomes Autoimmunity

Clinicians are already rightly concerned about "sarcopenic obesity," where rapid weight loss strips away vital muscle mass alongside adipose tissue, leaving frailty in its wake. In typical scenarios, this is a dose-dependent issue managed with protein optimization and resistance training. However, the case described above illustrates a fundamentally different mechanism: an immune-mediated assault triggered by exposure to the drug, persisting long after clearance.

In classic drug-induced rhabdomyolysis (such as that recently reported with tirzepatide), we expect a sharp spike in CK that resolves quickly upon discontinuation and hydration. That is a toxic effect. What happened to this patient was an autoimmune switch flip. Despite stopping semaglutide immediately after her initial reaction, her CK levels climbed twelve-fold over four months, and her weakness progressed relentlessly until immunosuppression was introduced. This trajectory mimics the historical presentation of statin-associated IMNM before we understood the role of anti-HMGCR antibodies. Just as statins can trigger a self-perpetuating autoimmune myopathy in susceptible individuals, it appears GLP-1 agonists may possess a similar, albeit perhaps rarer, potential to ignite an immune fire that burns independently of the inciting agent's presence in the bloodstream.

The diagnostic challenge here is immense because these patients often present with non-specific complaints initially dismissed as "getting out of shape" or residual effects of GI illness. Our patient had negative standard myositis panels, falling into the seronegative category that accounts for roughly one-third of IMNM cases. Without a high index of suspicion—and crucially, without performing a muscle biopsy when clinical severity outweighs serological findings—we risk missing a treatable autoimmune condition while attributing symptoms to rapid weight loss or deconditioning. Furthermore, distinguishing hepatic transaminitis from muscle breakdown is critical; this patient had pre-existing fatty liver disease which muddied the waters, but her rising AST/ALT tracked with her CK elevation rather than her liver pathology alone.

## Practical Management: Screening Red Flags vs. Expected Side Effects

So how do we navigate this in clinical practice? We cannot stop prescribing life-saving medications based on a single case report, nor should we ignore every ache and pain in a patient losing 20 pounds a month. The key lies in recognizing patterns that deviate from the expected course of GLP-1 therapy. Most patients will experience nausea or transient fatigue; they should not develop progressive inability to lift their arms or swallow liquids months after their last dose.

When evaluating a patient on GLP-1 agonists presenting with weakness or elevated enzymes, we must move beyond assuming it's just "muscle loss." If a patient reports difficulty rising from a chair, combing their hair, or swallowing *after* the acute GI phase has passed—or if these symptoms worsen despite stopping the drug—it warrants an aggressive workup including CK levels and consideration for rheumatology/neurology referral. The presence of dysphagia or dysphonia is particularly ominous and should never be attributed solely to dehydration or general malaise in this context. Early recognition allowed this patient to receive IVIG and steroids promptly, leading to full recovery; delay could have resulted in permanent disability or respiratory compromise given the involvement of bulbar muscles.

Ultimately, this case expands our differential diagnosis for new-onset myopathy in the obese population. While we continue to champion protein intake and resistance exercise as pillars of GLP-1 therapy to prevent sarcopenia, we must remain vigilant for signs that suggest an underlying autoimmune process has been unmasked by these potent agents Pharmacovigilance is no longer theoretical; it is happening at our bedside right now as millions start these therapies globally below are key takeaways for managing this emerging clinical dilemma:

*   **Differentiate Toxicity from Autoimmunity:** Unlike direct toxic rhabdomyolysis which resolves rapidly upon drug cessation (>90% improvement within days), immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) progresses over weeks to months even after stopping the offending agent; worsening weakness post-discontinuation is a major red flag requiring immunosuppression evaluation rather than just supportive care
*   **Don't Be Reassured by Seronegativity:** Up to one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative for anti-HMGCR/SRP antibodies especially when triggered by novel agents like GLP-1 agonists so maintain high suspicion if clinical features match (severe proximal weakness + massive CK elevation >10x ULN) regardless of antibody results
*   **Watch for Bulbar Involvement:** Dysphagia/dysphonia combined with limb weakness indicates severe disease progression beyond simple sarcopenia necessitating urgent specialist consultation possible airway protection assessment
*   **Biopsy Remains Gold Standard:** When faced with unexplained progressive myopathy despite negative labs proceed directly toward electromyography/muscle biopsy confirmation since delayed treatment correlates significantly worse outcomes compared early intervention protocols
