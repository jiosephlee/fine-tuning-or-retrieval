Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Short-Course Salvation: Can Brief Immunosuppression Cure Drug-Induced IMNM?

We are living in the era of the GLP-1 agonist. From endocrinology clinics to weight-loss influencers, semaglutide and its cousins are everywhere. We know the script: nausea, vomiting, maybe some gastroparesis if you push the dose too fast. But what happens when a single dose triggers a cascade leading to profound paralysis, dysphagia, and a Creatine Kinase (CK) count nearing 4,000?

A recent case report by Lam et al. throws us a curveball that challenges our understanding of drug-induced myopathies. They describe a woman in her 60s who developed Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM) shortly after her *first* dose of semaglutide. What makes this case truly fascinating—and clinically actionable—isn't just the rare association, but the treatment trajectory. Unlike the grueling, years-long immunosuppressive marathons we typically run for idiopathic IMNM, this patient achieved complete remission with a short, sharp burst of steroids and IVIG once the offending agent was removed. It forces us to ask: Is there an "exit strategy" for drug-triggered autoimmune myopathy that we've been missing?

### The Case: One Dose, Four Months of Decline

Our patient was a woman in her 60s with obesity and NAFLD, previously independent and active. She received a single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose of semaglutide. Within hours, she was hit with severe nausea and vomiting, landing her in the hospital with acute kidney injury and metabolic acidosis from dehydration. At that initial admission, her CK was only mildly elevated at 337 U/L—easy to dismiss as dehydration or minor muscle strain from retching. She was discharged, but the story wasn't over; it was just incubating.

Over the next four months, while completely off the medication, she didn't recover. Instead, she deteriorated rapidly. She developed progressive proximal weakness so severe she couldn't sit upright without help, accompanied by dysphagia and dysphonia. When she finally presented to a tertiary center, her CK had skyrocketed to nearly 4,000 U/L. This wasn't simple rhabdomyolysis; rhabdo from a toxin usually peaks early and resolves quickly once the drug is cleared. This was a slow-burn fire that kept raging long after the spark was gone.

The workup was exhaustive. Viral panels were negative. She had never taken statins (the usual suspect for anti-HMGCR myopathy). Her extensive myositis antibody panel came back negative for both anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP, placing her in that tricky seronegative bucket that accounts for roughly one-third of IMNM cases. The definitive clue came from the muscle biopsy of her vastus lateralis: scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage infiltration (CD68+) and membrane attack complex deposition on non-necrotic fibers. The diagnosis was clear: Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy.

### The Treatment Pivot: Aggressive Start, Rapid Exit

Here is where this case diverges from standard dogma. In classic idiopathic IMNM, we brace ourselves for a long haul. We're talking high-dose steroids combined with methotrexate, azathioprine, rituximab, or ongoing IVIG for months to years. Relapse upon tapering is common, and many patients remain on maintenance therapy indefinitely.

However, recognizing the likely drug-induced nature of this case, the team opted for a different approach. They hit hard initially to stop the immune storm: five days of pulse IV methylprednisolone (1g/day) followed by oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg daily. Given the severity of her bulbar symptoms (dysphagia/dysphonia) and profound weakness, they added a single course of IVIG (2 g/kg total).

The response was dramatic. Within two weeks, her CK plummeted. Even more impressively, because she remained off semaglutide and showed sustained clinical improvement, they were able to taper the prednisone rapidly over just two months. No second-line agents were needed beyond that initial IVIG pulse. Ten months out, she was back to her baseline functional status, off all immunosuppression for eight months, with normalized CK levels and resolved cardiac findings (she had transient myocardial edema noted on CMR).

### Why This Matters: Trigger Removal Changes the Game

This case highlights a critical distinction between *idiopathic* autoimmune disease and *drug-triggered* autoimmunity. In statin-associated IMNM (anti-HMGCR positive), removing the statin often isn't enough; the autoimmune process becomes self-perpetuating because the body is now attacking the HMGCR enzyme itself regardless of statin presence. That's why those patients need heavy-duty suppression.

But what if the drug acts as a unique adjuvant or trigger that kickstarts an immune response which remains dependent on some residual factor or simply lacks the "fuel" to sustain itself once the trigger is gone? In this semaglutide case (and potentially other drug-induced I MNMs), removing the antigenic stimulus might allow a finite course of immunosuppression to fully extinguish the fire rather than just dampen it down temporarily. The fact that our patient remained in remission off therapy suggests that for select drug-induced cases, we might avoid condemning patients to lifelong immunosuppression if we recognize the etiology early and act decisively.

Of course, caution is warranted. We can't definitively prove causality without a rechallenge (which would be unethical here), and we don't know if she had smoldering subclinical disease beforehand. However, the temporal relationship—a massive CK rise starting after exposure and resolving only after withdrawal plus short-course therapy—strongly supports a drug-driven mechanism according to WHO-UMC criteria.

### Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Messages

*   **Don't Dismiss Late-Onset Weakness:** Drug-induced myopathy doesn't always look like acute rhabdomyolysis with immediate collapse watch out for delayed presentations where weakness progresses weeks to months after exposure even if CK was only mildly elevated initially
*   **Seronegativity Doesn't Rule Out IMNM:** Approximately one-third of IMNM cases are negative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies; rely on clinical phenotype (proximal weakness high CK) and biopsy findings (necrosis with minimal inflammation MAC deposition) when antibodies are absent
*   **Consider Finite Therapy for Drug-Triggers:** In cases where IMNM is strongly linked to a specific medication consider whether aggressive short-term immunosuppression combined with strict avoidance of the trigger can lead to complete remission unlike idiopathic cases requiring chronic management
*   **Watch for Bulbar/Cardiac Involvement:** IMNM can affect extraocular muscles rarely but frequently impacts pharyngeal muscles causing dysphagia/aspiration risk; cardiac involvement though less common should be screened for via troponin echo or CMR in severe cases
*   **Biopsy is King:** When facing unexplained proximal weakness with high CK especially in seronegative patients proceed early to muscle biopsy looking for necrotizing features rather than waiting indefinitely for antibody results
