Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Workup: Laboratory, Imaging, and Histopathological Findings

The diagnostic evaluation of this patient required a careful synthesis of longitudinal laboratory trends, advanced imaging, and definitive histopathology to distinguish immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from direct drug toxicity or primary hepatic disease. A critical feature of this case was the trajectory of creatine kinase (CK) levels, which diverged significantly from the pattern expected in simple toxic rhabdomyolysis. Upon her initial hospitalization for semaglutide-induced vomiting and acute kidney injury, the patient's CK was only modestly elevated at 337 U/L. In classic drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, such as that recently described with tirzepatide, CK levels typically peak acutely during the insult and normalize rapidly upon drug cessation and hydration. Conversely, this patient experienced a delayed, progressive surge in CK over four months after stopping the medication, reaching a peak of 3992 U/L. This dissociation between drug exposure and symptom severity—where weakness and enzyme elevation worsened despite the absence of the offending agent—is a hallmark of an autoimmune process rather than direct toxin-mediated muscle damage.

Differentiating the source of transaminase elevation posed an initial challenge given the patient's history of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) are present in both hepatocytes and skeletal muscle; therefore, significant myopathy can mimic liver injury. While the patient had documented hepatic steatosis three years prior, the persistence of elevated AST (191 U/L) and ALT (165 U/L) alongside massively elevated CK suggested a muscular contribution. However, the clinical picture indicated a dual etiology: a repeat liver biopsy confirmed progression of NAFLD (grade 5/8), accounting for some enzymatic leakage, while the magnitude of the CK elevation and subsequent normalization following immunosuppression confirmed that skeletal muscle breakdown was the primary driver of the acute biochemical derangement. This case underscores the necessity of checking CK in any patient with unexplained transaminitis, even when underlying liver disease is known.

Serological testing provided essential negative data that refined the differential diagnosis. The patient tested negative for the canonical autoantibodies associated with IMNM: anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. Approximately one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative, presenting a diagnostic hurdle since these antibodies often guide prognosis and therapeutic intensity. The absence of other myositis-specific antibodies (such as Jo-1 or Mi-2) and connective tissue disease markers (ANA, dsDNA) further isolated the myopathy as a distinct entity rather than part of a systemic lupus erythematosus or dermatomyositis spectrum. An isolated, low-titer positive anti-SS-B antibody was noted; however, in the absence of anti-SS-A positivity, sicca symptoms, or other features of Sjögren's syndrome, this finding is likely nonspecific and unrelated to the myopathic process. The seronegative status necessitated reliance on clinical progression and tissue diagnosis to confirm IMNM.

Radiologic investigations offered supportive evidence of widespread muscle inflammation and assessed potential extra-muscular involvement. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of bilateral femurs revealed symmetric T2 hyperintensity indicative of muscular edema, predominantly affecting the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps. This distribution correlates with the patient's profound proximal weakness and helps localize sites for potential biopsy. Furthermore, given the patient's dyspnea and elevated troponin and pro-BNP levels, cardiac involvement was rigorously evaluated. Cardiac MRI demonstrated mild hypokinesis and myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement, suggesting early myocarditis or stress-induced cardiomyopathy rather than frank infarction or established fibrosis. The resolution of these cardiac abnormalities following treatment supports the hypothesis that the myocardial involvement was part of the same inflammatory cascade driving the skeletal myopathy.

Definitive diagnosis rested on histopathological examination of the right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy, which displayed features classic for IMNM. The specimen showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers accompanied by prominent myophagocytosis—the engulfment of necrotic debris by macrophages. Immunohistochemical staining highlighted a dense infiltration of CD68-positive macrophages within necrotic areas, confirming an active phagocytic response. Crucially, there was a deposition of the membrane attack complex (MAC/C5b-9) on both necrotic and non-necrotic sarcolemma, indicating complement-mediated cytotoxicity central to IMNM pathogenesis. Distinct from polymyositis or dermatomyositis, there was a notable paucity of lymphocytic infiltrate; only rare CD3-positive T-cells were observed primarily in perivascular regions rather than invading non-necrotic fibers. This specific histological triad—necrosis with regeneration, macrophage predominance over lymphocytes, and MAC deposition—in a seronegative patient with a compatible clinical timeline confirms the diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy potentially triggered by semaglutide exposure.
