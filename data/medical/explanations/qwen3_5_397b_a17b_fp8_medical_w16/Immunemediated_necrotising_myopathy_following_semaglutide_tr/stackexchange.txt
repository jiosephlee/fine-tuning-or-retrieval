Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient presenting with progressive proximal muscle weakness and markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK), what specific histological features on muscle biopsy distinguish Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM) from polymyositis or dermatomyositis?

In distinguishing Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM) from polymyositis or dermatomyositis, the critical histological differentiator is the presence of widespread myofiber necrosis and regeneration coupled with a **paucity of mononuclear inflammatory infiltrates**.

As illustrated in the reported case of semaglutide-associated IMNM, the muscle biopsy reveals dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibers with prominent myophagocytosis, evidenced by acid phosphatase and CD68-positive macrophages engulfing debris. Crucially, while there may be evidence of complement deposition (such as Membrane Attack Complex staining on non-necrotic fibers) and rare scattered T-cells (CD3+), the biopsy lacks the dense endomysial lymphocytic invasion of non-necrotic fibers characteristic of polymyositis or the perimysial inflammation and perifascicular atrophy diagnostic of dermatomyositis. In IMNM, the primary driver of injury is humoral-mediated necrosis rather than direct T-cell cytotoxicity; therefore, the relative absence of significant lymphocytic infiltration amidst extensive necrosis is the hallmark feature that separates this entity from the classic inflammatory myopathies.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How does the clinical timeline and response to drug cessation differentiate direct toxic rhabdomyolysis (as seen in some GLP-1 agonist cases) from drug-induced Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy?

The distinction between direct toxic rhabdomyolysis and drug-induced immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) hinges on the trajectory of creatine kinase (CK) elevation relative to drug cessation and the necessity of immunosuppression for resolution.

Direct toxic myopathy, as illustrated in the background discussion regarding a tirzepatide-associated case, presents with an acute, massive release of muscle enzymes—often reaching tens of thousands of units per liter—during active exposure. Crucially, this process is self-limiting upon withdrawal of the offending agent; CK levels typically normalize within days solely through supportive care and hydration, reflecting the clearance of the toxin and damaged muscle fibers without ongoing immune activation.

In contrast, IMNM represents an autoimmune phenomenon where the drug acts as a trigger rather than a continuing toxin. As seen in this patient treated with semaglutide, the clinical course is characterized by a delayed but progressive worsening of symptoms and CK elevation *after* the drug has been discontinued. Despite receiving only a single dose of semaglutide four months prior to her peak presentation, this patient's CK rose 12-fold and her proximal weakness worsened significantly while off the medication. This divergence from the expected rapid recovery of toxic rhabdomyolysis signals a sustained autoimmune attack on muscle fibers, confirmed here by biopsy showing necrosis with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex deposition. Furthermore, unlike toxic myopathy which resolves with cessation alone, IMNM requires targeted immunosuppression (corticosteroids and IVIG in this case) to halt the inflammatory cascade. The fact that this patient required aggressive immunotherapy to achieve remission, rather than improving spontaneously after stopping semaglutide, confirms the diagnosis of an immune-mediated process rather than direct toxicity.

### [Management] Given that approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies, how should a clinician proceed with diagnosis and management when the myositis antibody panel is negative but clinical and histological suspicion remains high?

When faced with a patient presenting with classic features of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)—specifically progressive proximal weakness, markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK), and histological evidence of myofiber necrosis with macrophage predominance—a negative myositis antibody panel for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP should not deter the clinician from pursuing a diagnosis of IMNM or initiating treatment. As illustrated in the reported case of semaglutide-associated IMNM, approximately one-third of patients with this condition are seronegative for known autoantibodies. In such instances, the diagnosis rests firmly on the integration of clinical phenotype and muscle biopsy findings rather than serology alone.

The pathological hallmark of IMNM is the presence of scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with minimal mononuclear cell infiltration, often accompanied by deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on non-necrotic fibers, as seen in this patient's vastus lateralis biopsy. The absence of specific antibodies does not negate the autoimmune nature of the disease, particularly when there is a clear temporal relationship to a potential trigger, such as the single dose of semaglutide described here, and when alternative etiologies like infectious myositis or primary metabolic disorders have been excluded.

Management in seronegative cases should mirror that of seropositive IMNM: prompt initiation of high-dose corticosteroids combined with adjunctive therapies such as intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). In the referenced case, the patient achieved complete remission and normalization of CK levels following a short course of pulse steroids, oral prednisone taper, and a single cycle of IVIG, alongside discontinuation of the offending agent. This outcome underscores that seronegative IMNM can be highly responsive to immunosuppression, especially when driven by a removable trigger. Clinicians must therefore rely on histological confirmation and clinical judgment to guide therapy, avoiding delays in treatment while awaiting further serological discoveries or risking irreversible muscle damage due to diagnostic uncertainty.

### [Pathophysiology] Why might transaminases (AST and ALT) remain elevated in a patient with IMNM even after CK levels begin to normalize, and how can one determine if the source is hepatic versus muscular in a patient with known fatty liver disease?

Transaminases (AST and ALT) may remain elevated in patients with immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) despite the normalization of creatine kinase (CK) levels when there is a concurrent, independent source of hepatic injury. While both AST and ALT are released from damaged skeletal muscle during active myositis—often leading to transaminitis that parallels CK fluctuations—these enzymes are also abundant in hepatocytes. In the setting of resolving muscle inflammation, CK serves as a specific marker for muscular recovery; therefore, if CK normalizes while AST and ALT remain elevated, the persistence of transaminitis strongly suggests a non-muscular etiology, typically intrinsic liver disease.

Differentiating the source in a patient with known fatty liver disease relies on observing the dissociation between muscle and liver markers over time and confirming hepatic pathology through imaging or biopsy. In the reported case of semaglutide-associated IMNM, the patient had a three-year history of biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with baseline transaminitia. Although her CK peaked at nearly 4000 U/L during the acute phase of her myopathy and subsequently normalized completely following immunosuppression, her AST and ALT levels remained mildly elevated. This clinical trajectory indicated that the initial transaminitis was likely multifactorial (contributed by both muscle necrosis and underlying steatosis), but the persistent elevation after muscular recovery was attributable solely to her chronic hepatic steatosis. This distinction was definitively confirmed by a repeat liver biopsy performed during her admission, which demonstrated progression of macrovesicular steatosis without significant inflammation, ruling out active autoimmune hepatitis or ongoing muscle-derived enzyme leakage as the cause of the persistent lab abnormalities. Thus, in patients with comorbid liver disease, one must interpret transaminases cautiously: normalization of CK confirms resolution of the myopathic component, while residual transaminitis should prompt evaluation of the underlying hepatic condition rather than escalation of immunosuppression for presumed refractory myositis.

### [Management] Unlike idiopathic IMNM which often requires prolonged maintenance immunosuppression, why did this patient with suspected semaglutide-associated IMNM achieve sustained remission after a relatively short course of corticosteroids and a single cycle of IVIG?

This patient's ability to achieve sustained remission with a short course of immunosuppression, unlike patients with idiopathic immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), stems from the fundamental difference in disease pathogenesis: the removal of the inciting antigen versus the persistence of a self-sustaining autoimmune loop.

In classic idiopathic IMNM, particularly forms associated with anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP autoantibodies, the immune system generates high-titer, pathogenic autoantibodies that drive continuous muscle fiber necrosis independently of the original trigger (such as statin exposure, which may have ceased years prior). Consequently, these patients typically require prolonged, often multi-agent immunosuppressive therapy to suppress this entrenched humoral response, and relapse is common upon tapering.

In contrast, this case represents a probable drug-induced IMNM triggered by semaglutide. As noted in the discussion, the patient's clinical course—characterized by a 12-fold rise in creatine kinase (CK) over four months following a single dose, rather than immediate resolution as seen in toxic rhabdomyolysis—suggests an immune-mediated mechanism rather than direct toxicity. However, unlike idiopathic disease, the driving force here appears contingent on the presence of the offending agent or the acute inflammatory cascade it initiated. Once semaglutide was discontinued and the acute immune activation was bridged with a finite course of corticosteroids and a single cycle of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), the stimulus for myonecrosis was removed. The patient's muscle biopsy confirmed necrotizing features with macrophage predominance (CD68+) and membrane attack complex deposition but lacked the specific autoantibodies (anti-HMGCR/SRP) that typify refractory idiopathic cases.

Therefore, the successful rapid weaning off prednisone and the absence of further IVIG requirements reflect the natural history of drug-induced autoimmune phenomena: when the trigger is identified and withdrawn early, the immune dysregulation resolves without evolving into a chronic, self-perpetuating autoimmune state. This distinction underscores the critical importance of identifying potential pharmacologic triggers in IMNM, as their removal can fundamentally alter the prognosis and reduce the burden of long-term immunosuppression.

### [Clinical Pearls] This patient presented with dysphagia and dysphonia in addition to limb weakness. What is the significance of bulbar involvement in the context of necrotising myopathies, and does its presence alter the urgency of initiating immunomodulatory therapy?

Bulbar involvement, manifested here as dysphagia and dysphonia alongside profound proximal weakness, signifies a severe and aggressive phenotype of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). While classic IMNM typically presents with limb-girdle weakness, the recruitment of bulbar muscles indicates extensive muscle fiber necrosis and places the patient at immediate risk for aspiration pneumonia and respiratory compromise. In the context of this case, the presence of bulbar symptoms served as a critical marker of disease severity, distinguishing the condition from a self-limiting toxic myopathy or simple rhabdomyolysis, which would not typically progress over four months to involve swallowing and phonation.

The presence of bulbar dysfunction absolutely alters the urgency and intensity of therapeutic intervention. Unlike mild statin-associated myalgia or transient enzyme elevations that resolve with drug cessation alone, bulbar IMNM constitutes a medical emergency requiring rapid escalation to potent immunomodulation. As illustrated in this report, despite the withdrawal of the suspected trigger (semaglutide), the patient's condition continued to deteriorate over months until aggressive therapy was instituted. The treating team correctly recognized that waiting for spontaneous recovery was unsafe; consequently, they initiated high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone pulses combined with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). This dual approach was necessary to halt the ongoing necrosis and restore function quickly, preventing fatal complications such as aspiration or respiratory failure. The patient's subsequent rapid biochemical and functional recovery confirms that early, aggressive immunosuppression is mandatory when bulbar signs are present in necrotising myopathies, regardless of autoantibody serostatus.

### [Differential Diagnosis] The patient had an isolated low-titer positive anti-SS-B antibody with a negative ANA and anti-SS-A. Does this finding support a diagnosis of primary Sjögren's syndrome-associated myositis, or is it likely an incidental finding in the context of drug-induced IMNM?

This isolated low-titer positive anti-SS-B antibody, in the setting of a negative ANA and negative anti-SS-A, is best interpreted as an incidental finding rather than evidence supporting a diagnosis of primary Sjögren's syndrome-associated myositis.

From a serological standpoint, anti-SS-B (La) antibodies rarely occur in isolation; they are typically found in conjunction with anti-SS-A (Ro) antibodies and a positive antinuclear antibody (ANA) test in patients with definitive Sjögren's syndrome or systemic lupus erythematosus. An isolated positive anti-SS-B with a negative ANA has low specificity for connective tissue disease and is frequently observed as a false-positive result or a nonspecific immune activation. Furthermore, the clinical phenotype in this case lacks the cardinal features of Sjögren's syndrome; the patient explicitly denied sicca symptoms, and examination revealed no oral ulcers or other stigmata of autoimmune exocrinopathy.

The weight of the evidence in this case favors a diagnosis of drug-induced immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) triggered by semaglutide. The temporal relationship is striking, with symptom onset following a single dose of the medication, followed by a progressive 12-fold rise in creatine kinase over four months. Histologically, the muscle biopsy demonstrated the hallmark features of IMNM—necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex deposition—but lacked the lymphocytic predominance often seen in polymyositis or specific features of Sjögren's myopathy. Crucially, the patient's clinical course supports a drug-induced etiology: she achieved complete remission and normalization of CK levels after a relatively short course of immunosuppression and discontinuation of semaglutide. In contrast, idiopathic IMNM or myositis secondary to established connective tissue diseases like Sjögren's syndrome typically follows a more refractory course requiring prolonged, aggressive immunosuppressive maintenance. Therefore, the serologic anomaly should not distract from the primary diagnosis of probable semaglutide-associated IMNM.

### [Morning Report] Considering the World Health Organization (WHO) Uppsala Monitoring Centre criteria for causality assessment, which three elements in this case support a 'probable' rather than 'possible' link between semaglutide exposure and the development of IMNM?

Based on the World Health Organization Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO-UMC) criteria, the classification of "probable" causality in this case rests on three specific pillars derived from the patient's clinical course, distinguishing it from a mere "possible" association.

First, there is a **reasonable temporal relationship** between the exposure and the event. The patient received a single dose of semaglutide and experienced an immediate adverse reaction requiring hospitalization, followed by a progressive decline in muscle strength and a 12-fold elevation in creatine kinase (CK) over the subsequent four months. This timeline establishes a plausible sequence where the drug exposure precedes the onset and progression of the immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

Second, the case demonstrates a **clinically reasonable response to withdrawal** of the suspected agent combined with appropriate therapy. Unlike direct toxic rhabdomyolysis, which typically resolves rapidly upon discontinuation alone, this patient's condition required immunosuppression (corticosteroids and IVIG) alongside the cessation of semaglutide to achieve resolution. The patient subsequently regained baseline functional status and maintained normal CK levels while off both the drug and long-term immunosuppression, supporting the hypothesis that removing the trigger was essential for recovery.

Third, and crucially for elevating the assessment from "possible" to "probable," is the **unlikelihood of alternative explanations**. The authors systematically excluded other common etiologies: the patient had no history of statin use (the most common trigger for anti-HMGCR IMNM), tested negative for a comprehensive myositis antibody panel (including anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR), and lacked clinical features of underlying connective tissue diseases. Furthermore, her pre-existing transaminitis was attributed to biopsy-proven hepatic steatosis rather than muscle breakdown, and there was no evidence of recent excessive physical exertion or other myotoxic supplements. By ruling out these competing diagnoses, the probability that semaglutide was the precipitating factor increases significantly according to WHO-UMC standards.
