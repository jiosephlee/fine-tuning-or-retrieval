Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Clinical Presentation and Temporal Association with GLP-1 Agonists

The patient, a woman in her sixth decade of life, presented with a complex medical history significant for obesity, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), hypothyroidism, and hypertension. Notably, she had no history of statin exposure, which is the most common pharmacological trigger identified in immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), nor did she report the use of other supplements associated with myopathy, such as red yeast rice. Her baseline status was one of functional independence until the initiation of semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist prescribed to address her persistent obesity and hepatic steatosis. The absence of traditional risk factors for IMNM in this patient necessitates a careful evaluation of the temporal relationship between the new medication and the onset of neuromuscular symptoms to establish potential causality.

The clinical course unfolded in two distinct phases, distinguishing an acute toxic reaction from a delayed autoimmune process. Immediately following her first subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg), the patient experienced severe gastrointestinal intolerance characterized by nausea and vomiting, leading to hospitalization within 48 hours for hypovolemia-induced acute kidney injury and metabolic acidosis. At this initial presentation, her creatine kinase (CK) level was only mildly elevated at 337 U/L, a finding inconsistent with frank rhabdomyolysis or established necrotizing myopathy. This early phase represents a direct adverse effect of the drug on gastrointestinal motility and volume status rather than primary muscle pathology. Crucially, while her renal function and gastrointestinal symptoms resolved with supportive care, the patient entered a period of progressive clinical deterioration over the subsequent four months while completely off the medication.

The hallmark features of IMNM emerged during this delayed interval, manifesting as severe, progressive proximal muscle weakness and bulbar involvement. By four months post-exposure, the patient had lost the ability to sit or stand without assistance and developed significant dysphagia to both solids and liquids and dysphonia. Laboratory evaluation at this stage revealed a dramatic surge in CK levels to 3992 U/L, representing a twelve-fold increase from her initial admission value. This trajectory is pathophysiologically distinct from direct drug-induced toxic myopathy or acute rhabdomyolysis, where CK elevations typically peak concurrently with drug exposure and normalize rapidly upon discontinuation. Instead, the latency period between the single dose of semaglutide and the zenith of muscle injury, coupled with the relentless progression of weakness despite drug clearance, mirrors the natural history of an autoimmune cascade triggered by an external antigen rather than direct cellular toxicity.

The specific phenotype of weakness further supports the diagnosis of IMNM over other myopathic processes. The predominance of proximal muscle group involvement alongside bulbar symptoms (dysphagia and dysphonia) is classic for necrotizing myopathies, particularly those associated with anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) antibodies, although this patient was seronegative. The separation of the initial systemic insult—characterized by dehydration and acute renal failure—from the later onset of profound myonecrosis suggests that semaglutide may have acted as an inciting event that unleashed an aberrant immune response targeting skeletal muscle. This case highlights the importance of recognizing that drug-associated IMNM can present with a significant delay after exposure, requiring clinicians to maintain a high index of suspicion even when the offending agent was discontinued months prior to the peak of symptomatic presentation.

## Diagnostic Workup: Laboratory, Imaging, and Histopathological Findings

The diagnostic evaluation of this patient required a careful synthesis of longitudinal laboratory trends, advanced imaging, and definitive histopathology to distinguish immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from direct drug toxicity or primary hepatic disease. A critical feature of this case was the trajectory of creatine kinase (CK) levels, which diverged significantly from the pattern expected in simple toxic rhabdomyolysis. Upon her initial hospitalization for semaglutide-induced vomiting and acute kidney injury, the patient's CK was only modestly elevated at 337 U/L. In classic drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, such as that recently described with tirzepatide, CK levels typically peak acutely during the insult and normalize rapidly upon drug cessation and hydration. Conversely, this patient experienced a delayed, progressive surge in CK over four months after stopping the medication, reaching a peak of 3992 U/L. This dissociation between drug exposure and symptom severity—where weakness and enzyme elevation worsened despite the absence of the offending agent—is a hallmark of an autoimmune process rather than direct toxin-mediated muscle damage.

Differentiating the source of transaminase elevation posed an initial challenge given the patient's history of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) are present in both hepatocytes and skeletal muscle; therefore, significant myopathy can mimic liver injury. While the patient had documented hepatic steatosis three years prior, the persistence of elevated AST (191 U/L) and ALT (165 U/L) alongside massively elevated CK suggested a muscular contribution. However, the clinical picture indicated a dual etiology: a repeat liver biopsy confirmed progression of NAFLD (grade 5/8), accounting for some enzymatic leakage, while the magnitude of the CK elevation and subsequent normalization following immunosuppression confirmed that skeletal muscle breakdown was the primary driver of the acute biochemical derangement. This case underscores the necessity of checking CK in any patient with unexplained transaminitis, even when underlying liver disease is known.

Serological testing provided essential negative data that refined the differential diagnosis. The patient tested negative for the canonical autoantibodies associated with IMNM: anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. Approximately one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative, presenting a diagnostic hurdle since these antibodies often guide prognosis and therapeutic intensity. The absence of other myositis-specific antibodies (such as Jo-1 or Mi-2) and connective tissue disease markers (ANA, dsDNA) further isolated the myopathy as a distinct entity rather than part of a systemic lupus erythematosus or dermatomyositis spectrum. An isolated, low-titer positive anti-SS-B antibody was noted; however, in the absence of anti-SS-A positivity, sicca symptoms, or other features of Sjögren's syndrome, this finding is likely nonspecific and unrelated to the myopathic process. The seronegative status necessitated reliance on clinical progression and tissue diagnosis to confirm IMNM.

Radiologic investigations offered supportive evidence of widespread muscle inflammation and assessed potential extra-muscular involvement. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of bilateral femurs revealed symmetric T2 hyperintensity indicative of muscular edema, predominantly affecting the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps. This distribution correlates with the patient's profound proximal weakness and helps localize sites for potential biopsy. Furthermore, given the patient's dyspnea and elevated troponin and pro-BNP levels, cardiac involvement was rigorously evaluated. Cardiac MRI demonstrated mild hypokinesis and myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement, suggesting early myocarditis or stress-induced cardiomyopathy rather than frank infarction or established fibrosis. The resolution of these cardiac abnormalities following treatment supports the hypothesis that the myocardial involvement was part of the same inflammatory cascade driving the skeletal myopathy.

Definitive diagnosis rested on histopathological examination of the right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy, which displayed features classic for IMNM. The specimen showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers accompanied by prominent myophagocytosis—the engulfment of necrotic debris by macrophages. Immunohistochemical staining highlighted a dense infiltration of CD68-positive macrophages within necrotic areas, confirming an active phagocytic response. Crucially, there was a deposition of the membrane attack complex (MAC/C5b-9) on both necrotic and non-necrotic sarcolemma, indicating complement-mediated cytotoxicity central to IMNM pathogenesis. Distinct from polymyositis or dermatomyositis, there was a notable paucity of lymphocytic infiltrate; only rare CD3-positive T-cells were observed primarily in perivascular regions rather than invading non-necrotic fibers. This specific histological triad—necrosis with regeneration, macrophage predominance over lymphocytes, and MAC deposition—in a seronegative patient with a compatible clinical timeline confirms the diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy potentially triggered by semaglutide exposure.

## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Reasoning

Establishing the diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) in this patient required a rigorous exclusion of alternative etiologies and a careful analysis of the clinical timeline relative to semaglutide exposure. The primary diagnostic challenge lay in distinguishing between direct drug toxicity manifesting as rhabdomyolysis and an autoimmune process triggered by the medication. Direct toxic rhabdomyolysis, such as that previously reported with tirzepatide, typically presents with an acute, massive elevation in creatine kinase (CK) shortly after drug ingestion, followed by a rapid normalization of levels upon discontinuation and supportive care alone. In contrast, this patient exhibited a delayed and progressive trajectory: her CK was only modestly elevated (337 U/L) during the initial acute reaction to semaglutide, but rose twelve-fold over the subsequent four months while the drug was absent from her system. This progressive worsening off-drug, coupled with the severity of proximal weakness and dysphagia, argued strongly against a self-limiting toxic myopathy and pointed toward an ongoing autoimmune mechanism requiring immunosuppression for resolution.

Once an autoimmune etiology was suspected, the differential diagnosis expanded to include statin-associated IMNM, viral myositis, and connective tissue diseases. Although statins are the most common trigger for IMNM, specifically associated with anti-HMGCR antibodies, this patient had no history of statin use or exposure to other known myotoxic supplements like red yeast rice. Viral causes were considered unlikely given the absence of fever, prodromal illness, or specific viral serologies, and the chronicity of symptoms exceeded the typical course of post-viral myositis. Furthermore, a comprehensive connective tissue disease workup was largely unrevealing; the patient lacked cutaneous manifestations of dermatomyositis, interstitial lung disease on high-resolution CT, and classic myositis-specific antibodies such as anti-Jo-1 or anti-Mi-2. The isolated low-titer positive anti-SS-B antibody in the setting of negative ANA and anti-SS-A was deemed insufficient to diagnose Sjögren's syndrome or explain the severe necrotizing phenotype, particularly given the lack of sicca symptoms or salivary gland involvement.

A critical nuance in this case was the seronegative status of the patient regarding anti-HMGCR and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) antibodies. While these autoantibodies are hallmarks of IMNM, approximately one-third of patients remain seronegative at presentation. The absence of these markers does not exclude the diagnosis; rather, it necessitates reliance on histopathological evidence. The muscle biopsy provided the definitive confirmation, revealing scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltration—a pattern characteristic of IMNM rather than polymyositis. Immunohistochemistry further supported an immune-mediated process through the presence of macrophage markers (CD68), myophagocytosis, and deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers. These findings confirmed that despite the lack of circulating autoantibodies, the pathophysiology involved complement-mediated muscle destruction consistent with IMNM.

To formally evaluate the link between semaglutide and the development of IMNM, the clinical team applied the World Health Organization-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO-UMC) causality criteria. The assessment yielded a "probable" classification based on three key factors: a reasonable temporal relationship between drug administration and symptom onset; the unlikelihood of the condition being explained by other diseases or concurrent drugs; and a clinically plausible response to withdrawal of the offending agent combined with immunosuppressive therapy. While rechallenge was ethically contraindicated due to the severity of the initial reaction, the patient's sustained remission after stopping semaglutide and completing a finite course of steroids and IVIG reinforced the causal hypothesis. Idiopathic IMNM often requires prolonged, aggressive immunosuppression to achieve control, whereas drug-induced variants frequently resolve more readily once the trigger is removed.

Finally, clinicians had to address the possibility that semaglutide merely unmasked a "smoldering," pre-existing autoimmune condition rather than acting as the primary trigger. This theory was weighed against the patient's baseline functional status; she was fully independent with no prior weakness before receiving her first dose. Additionally, her transaminase elevations predated any muscle symptoms but were attributed to biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), which persisted even after her CK normalized, suggesting hepatic rather than muscular origins for those specific lab abnormalities. The abrupt transition from good health to severe disability immediately following drug exposure makes a latent, subclinical autoimmune myopathy less likely than a de novo drug-induced event. Consequently, integrating the temporal sequence, histological proof of necrotizing myopathy without significant inflammation exclusion criteria for other causes solidified semaglutide as probable precipitant in this case seronegative IMNM

## Therapeutic Management and Clinical Outcomes

The management of this patient's immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) required an aggressive, multimodal immunosuppressive strategy driven by the severity of her clinical presentation. Upon transfer to the tertiary center, the patient exhibited profound proximal muscle weakness rendering her unable to sit or stand independently, accompanied by significant bulbar involvement manifesting as dysphagia and dysphonia. In the context of IMNM, such severe functional impairment and risk of aspiration constitute indications for immediate, high-intensity therapy rather than a stepwise approach. Consequently, the treatment team initiated pulse intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for five days. This "pulse" regimen is designed to rapidly suppress the intense inflammatory cascade and halt ongoing myofiber necrosis, serving as a bridge to slower-acting maintenance therapies. Following the pulse course, the patient was transitioned to high-dose oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg daily to sustain immunosuppression while minimizing the risks associated with prolonged high-dose intravenous steroid exposure.

Given the persistence of severe weakness despite initial steroid pulsing and the critical nature of her bulbar symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered as an adjunctive therapy. The decision to incorporate IVIG reflects its established role in treating refractory or severe cases of necrotising myopathy, particularly when rapid functional recovery is essential to prevent complications such as respiratory failure or permanent disability from immobility. The patient received a total cumulative dose of 2 g/kg, divided over four consecutive days. This dosing strategy leverages the immunomodulatory properties of exogenous IgG to neutralize pathogenic autoantibodies, inhibit complement-mediated damage evidenced by the membrane attack complex deposition on her biopsy, and modulate Fc receptor expression on macrophages. The combination of high-dose corticosteroids and IVIG provided a synergistic effect, targeting both the cellular and humoral arms of the immune response implicated in her muscle injury.

Monitoring therapeutic response relied on a combination of biochemical markers, imaging findings, and functional assessment. Biochemically, the patient demonstrated a marked decline in creatine kinase (CK) levels within two weeks of initiating therapy, dropping from a peak of nearly 4000 U/L toward normalization. This rapid biochemical improvement correlated with clinical gains in muscle strength. Imaging played a complementary role; follow-up cardiac MRI and echocardiography revealed complete resolution of the previously noted myocardial oedema and wall motion abnormalities, confirming that the systemic inflammatory process was responsive to treatment across different tissue beds. Although skeletal muscle MRI was not repeated in the report, the normalization of CK and physical exam findings served as reliable proxies for the resolution of muscular oedema and necrosis seen on initial imaging.

The long-term outcome for this patient was exceptionally favorable, characterized by a swift tapering of immunosuppression and sustained remission. Unlike idiopathic IMNM cases, which often require months to years of maintenance therapy with steroid-sparing agents like methotrexate or rituximab, this patient's condition appeared uniquely responsive to a short course of intervention. Due to her robust clinical improvement and persistently normal CK levels, prednisone was rapidly weaned over two months, and no further doses of IVIG were administered following the initial hospitalization cycle. At ten months post-presentation, she had regained her baseline functional status and remained off all immunosuppressive medications for eight months without evidence of relapse. This trajectory supports the hypothesis that her condition was a drug-triggered event; once the offending agent (semaglutide) was withdrawn and the acute autoimmune flare was suppressed, the underlying drive for autoimmunity ceased, allowing for successful withdrawal of therapy—a distinction that carries significant prognostic implications compared to chronic autoimmune myopathies.

## Pathophysiological Insights and Clinical Implications for GLP-1 Agonist Use

The emergence of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist use represents a critical evolution in understanding the safety profile of these increasingly prescribed agents. While GLP-1 agonists like semaglutide are well-documented to cause gastrointestinal distress and, in some contexts, sarcopenia due to rapid weight loss, this case highlights a distinct pathological entity driven by autoimmunity rather than simple catabolism or direct toxin exposure. Current literature distinguishes between acute rhabdomyolysis, characterized by immediate massive creatine kinase (CK) elevation that resolves rapidly upon drug cessation, and IMNM, which presents with a delayed onset and progressive course despite withdrawal of the offending agent. In the reported case, the patient's CK levels rose twelve-fold over four months following a single dose of semaglutide, a trajectory incompatible with direct toxic myopathy or dehydration-induced rhabdomyolysis. Instead, this pattern mirrors the behavior of established drug-induced autoimmune myopathies, suggesting that GLP-1 agonists may act as an environmental trigger that initiates a self-perpetuating immune response in susceptible individuals.

The pathophysiological mechanism underlying this association likely involves a breach of immune tolerance rather than direct mitochondrial toxicity. In classic statin-associated IMNM, exposure to HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors leads to the upregulation of the target antigen on muscle fibers, triggering an autoimmune cascade mediated by anti-HMGCR antibodies that persists long after the drug is discontinued. Although the patient in this case was seronegative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies—a finding observed in approximately one-third of IMNM cases—the histological presence of necrotic fibers, macrophage infiltration (CD68+), and membrane attack complex deposition confirms an immune-mediated process. It is hypothesized that GLP-1 receptor agonists may modulate the immune microenvironment or alter antigen presentation in skeletal muscle, thereby unmasking cryptic autoantigens or lowering the threshold for autoreactive T-cell activation. The rapid clinical deterioration following a single dose suggests a potent hypersensitivity-like reaction or the acceleration of a smoldering subclinical autoimmune process, distinguishing it from the cumulative dose-dependent toxicity seen in traditional drug-induced myopathies.

Clinically, this case underscores the necessity for heightened vigilance regarding delayed-onset neuromuscular symptoms in patients initiating GLP-1 therapy. Unlike common side effects such as nausea or vomiting which appear within hours to days, IMNM may manifest weeks to months after exposure, creating a diagnostic challenge where the temporal link to the medication is easily overlooked. Providers should maintain a high index of suspicion for any patient presenting with new-onset proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, or dysphonia in the context of recent GLP-1 use, even if the drug was stopped months prior or tolerated initially without issue. The absence of rash and negative standard autoimmune panels (ANA, ENA) should not preclude further investigation; instead, these findings should prompt early measurement of serum CK levels and consideration of specialized myositis antibody testing. As demonstrated in this report, relying solely on routine metabolic panels may miss the diagnosis, as transaminase elevations can be erroneously attributed to underlying hepatic steatosis rather than muscle breakdown.

Management strategies for suspected GLP-1 associated IMNM must prioritize immediate drug discontinuation and prompt initiation of immunosuppressive therapy. Evidence from this case and analogous statin-induced scenarios indicates that cessation of the trigger alone is insufficient to halt disease progression once the autoimmune cascade is activated. Early intervention with high-dose corticosteroids and adjunctive therapies such as intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is crucial to prevent irreversible muscle damage and respiratory compromise. The favorable outcome in this patient—who achieved complete functional recovery and normalization of CK levels after a relatively short course of immunosuppression—suggests that drug-induced IMNM triggered by GLP-1 agonists may be more responsive to treatment than idiopathic forms, provided the offending agent is strictly avoided thereafter. Consequently, pharmacovigilance efforts must expand to include longitudinal monitoring of neuromuscular symptoms in the growing population utilizing these agents for obesity and diabetes management, ensuring that rare but severe immune complications are identified and treated before permanent disability occurs.
