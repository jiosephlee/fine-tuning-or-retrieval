# Beyond the GI Upset: Recognizing Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy as a Rare GLP-1 Agonist Complication

We are currently living through the golden age of GLP-1 receptor agonists. From Ozempic to Wegovy, semaglutide has become a household name, revolutionizing the management of type 2 diabetes and obesity. As emergency physicians and critical care clinicians, we know the drill for adverse events: nausea, vomiting, dehydration, and the occasional case of acute kidney injury or gallstone pancreatitis. We watch for the "washout" patient who hasn't kept food down in three days. But what happens when a patient presents months later with profound weakness that doesn't fit the narrative of simple deconditioning?

A fascinating new case report by Lam et al. forces us to expand our differential diagnosis for patients on these agents. They describe the first reported case of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) temporally associated with semaglutide. This isn't your garden-variety drug-induced rhabdomyolysis that resolves with fluids and stopping the pill. This is an autoimmune firestorm triggered by a single dose, evolving over four months into a life-threatening condition requiring heavy immunosuppression. It serves as a stark reminder that as our prescribing habits change, so too must our vigilance for atypical, delayed toxicities.

## The Case: One Dose, Four Months of Decline

The patient was a woman in her 60s with a history of obesity and NAFLD. Three years prior, she had mild transaminitis attributed to fatty liver, but she was otherwise functional and independent. In an effort to manage her weight and metabolic health, she was prescribed semaglutide. The reaction was immediate and severe. Within hours of her *first* 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose, she developed intractable nausea and vomiting, leading to hospitalization for acute renal failure and metabolic acidosis just two days later.

At that initial admission, her creatine kinase (CK) was mildly elevated at 337 U/L—likely multifactorial given her renal insult and immobilization—but nowhere near the levels suggesting frank rhabdomyolysis. She recovered her renal function, her GI symptoms resolved, and she was discharged. Crucially, she never took another dose of semaglutide.

However, the story didn't end at discharge. Over the subsequent four months, while completely off the medication, she experienced a relentless decline. What began as fatigue progressed to inability to stand or sit upright without assistance. She developed dysphagia and dysphonia, struggling to swallow both liquids and solids. When she finally presented to an outside hospital four months after that single exposure, her CK had skyrocketed to nearly 4,000 U/L. This wasn't a transient spike; it was a progressive climb in a patient who hadn't touched the offending agent in weeks.

## Not Just Rhabdo: Distinguishing Toxicity from Autoimmunity

The distinction between direct drug toxicity and immune-mediated pathology is vital here, as it dictates entirely different management pathways. We are familiar with GLP-1-associated rhabdomyolysis; there are reports involving tirzepatide where patients present with massive CK elevations (often >50,000 U/L) during active treatment. In those classic toxic scenarios, the mechanism is direct cellular injury or ischemia secondary to volume depletion. The fix is straightforward: stop the drug, hydrate aggressively, and watch the CK plummet within days. No steroids needed.

This case looks nothing like that timeline. If this were simple toxicity from semaglutide (which has a half-life of about one week), the muscle injury should have resolved shortly after clearance of the drug. Instead, this patient's weakness worsened and her CK rose twelve-fold over four months *after* cessation. This "smoldering" trajectory is the hallmark of an autoimmune process where the drug acts as a trigger rather than a continuous toxin. Once the immune system is primed against self-antigens in the muscle fiber, removing the trigger doesn't turn off the attack; only immunosuppression can hit the brakes.

The workup reinforced this suspicion while ruling out common mimics. The patient had no history of statin use (the usual suspect for anti-HMGCR myopathy), no recent strenuous exercise, and no infectious prodrome remarkably suggestive of viral myositis. Her extensive autoimmune panel was largely negative—no ANA, no anti-Jo1, and notably negative for both anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies. While roughly one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative, this absence initially clouds the picture until you look at the tissue itself.

# The Seronegative Trap: Diagnosing IMNM When the Autoantibody Panel Comes Back Negative

We live in an era of reflexive medicine. A patient presents with proximal weakness and sky-high creatine kinase (CK)? Order the myositis panel. If anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP comes back positive, you breathe a sigh of relief; the diagnosis of Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM) is confirmed, and you start steroids. But what happens when that panel returns entirely negative? In the rush of the ED or the wards, it is dangerously easy to dismiss an autoimmune etiology and label it "idiopathic," "viral," or purely "toxic," delaying the aggressive immunosuppression these patients desperately need.

A fascinating case report by Lam et al. throws a spotlight on this exact diagnostic pitfall. They describe a woman in her 60s who developed profound, progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia shortly after receiving a single dose of semaglutide for obesity management. Her CK skyrocketed from a modest 337 U/L during her initial admission for dehydration to nearly 4,000 U/L four months later. She was unable to stand or sit without assistance. Yet, when the rheumatology team ran the full gamut of autoantibodies—ANA, ENA, Jo-1, Mi-2, and crucially, the IMNM-specific anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP—the results were negative. She was seronegative. If we stopped there, we might have missed the boat entirely. Instead, the team leaned on histology, identifying dispersed necrotic fibers with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition, confirming IMNM despite the silent serology.

This case serves as a stark reminder that approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative. Relying solely on antibody panels is a trap that can leave patients untreated while their muscle fibers continue to undergo necrosis. The distinction matters because true IMNM, unlike simple toxic myopathy or viral rhabdomyolysis, rarely resolves with drug withdrawal alone; it demands immunosuppression. In this patient's case, despite stopping semaglutide immediately after her initial reaction, her weakness progressed relentlessly over four months until high-dose steroids and IVIG were initiated. Only then did her CK normalize and her function return.

Differentiating seronegative IMNM from its mimics requires a keen eye for clinical trajectory and histological nuance. Pure toxic myopathies (like statin-induced toxicity without autoimmunity) typically improve rapidly upon cessation of the offending agent. Viral myositides usually have an acute onset and self-limiting course. In contrast, IMNm follows an aggressive, subacute progression that persists or worsens even after the trigger is removed. Histologically, the hallmark is necrosis with a *paucity* of lymphocytic infiltrate—a key differentiator from polymyositis, which is defined by endomysial T-cell invasion of non-necrotic fibers. In IMNM, you see dead fibers being eaten by macrophages (myophagocytosis) and MAC deposition on sarcolemma, but few invading lymphocytes. Recognizing this pattern on biopsy is your safety net when serology fails you.

As GLP-1 agonists like semaglutide become ubiquitous in our patient populations for weight loss and diabetes control, we must remain vigilant for atypical presentations. While direct toxicity causing rhabdomyolysis has been reported with this class, this case suggests a potential link to immune-mediated mechanisms that mimic statin-associated IMNM but occur in seronegative individuals. Whether semaglutide was the definitive trigger or merely the straw that broke the camel's back in a predisposed individual remains debated, but the clinical lesson is universal: do not let a negative lab result talk you out of a biopsy when the clinical picture screams autoimmune myopathy.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Seronegativity Does Not Rule Out IMNM:** Approximately 30% of Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy cases are negative for both anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies; a negative myositis panel should never preclude further workup in high-risk clinical scenarios.
*   **Histology is King:** Diagnosis relies on muscle biopsy showing prominent myofiber necrosis and regeneration with macrophage infiltration (myophagocytosis) and Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) deposition, notably lacking significant lymphocytic infiltrates (unlike polymyositis).
*   **Clinical Trajectory Matters:** Unlike toxic myopathies which resolve quickly after drug cessation, IMNM demonstrates progressive weakness and rising CK levels despite removing the potential trigger, necessitating aggressive immunosuppression (steroids + IVIG).
*   **Watch for Bulbar Symptoms:** Dysphagia and dysphonia are red flags for severe myopathy involvement and should prompt urgent evaluation for respiratory compromise and aspiration risk alongside limb weakness.
*   **Emerging Triggers:** While statins are the classic trigger for HMGCR-positive IMNM, clinicians should maintain suspicion for other metabolic agents (including GLP-1 agonists) as potential precipitants of seronegative necrotizing myopathy.



# Short-Course Salvation: Can Brief Immunosuppression Cure Drug-Induced IMNM?

We are living in the era of the GLP-1 agonist. From endocrinology clinics to weight-loss influencers, semaglutide and its cousins are everywhere. We know the script: nausea, vomiting, maybe some gastroparesis if you push the dose too fast. But what happens when a single dose triggers a cascade leading to profound paralysis, dysphagia, and a Creatine Kinase (CK) count nearing 4,000?

A recent case report by Lam et al. throws us a curveball that challenges our understanding of drug-induced myopathies. They describe a woman in her 60s who developed Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM) shortly after her *first* dose of semaglutide. What makes this case truly fascinating—and clinically actionable—isn't just the rare association, but the treatment trajectory. Unlike the grueling, years-long immunosuppressive marathons we typically run for idiopathic IMNM, this patient achieved complete remission with a short, sharp burst of steroids and IVIG once the offending agent was removed. It forces us to ask: Is there an "exit strategy" for drug-triggered autoimmune myopathy that we've been missing?

### The Case: One Dose, Four Months of Decline

Our patient was a woman in her 60s with obesity and NAFLD, previously independent and active. She received a single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose of semaglutide. Within hours, she was hit with severe nausea and vomiting, landing her in the hospital with acute kidney injury and metabolic acidosis from dehydration. At that initial admission, her CK was only mildly elevated at 337 U/L—easy to dismiss as dehydration or minor muscle strain from retching. She was discharged, but the story wasn't over; it was just incubating.

Over the next four months, while completely off the medication, she didn't recover. Instead, she deteriorated rapidly. She developed progressive proximal weakness so severe she couldn't sit upright without help, accompanied by dysphagia and dysphonia. When she finally presented to a tertiary center, her CK had skyrocketed to nearly 4,000 U/L. This wasn't simple rhabdomyolysis; rhabdo from a toxin usually peaks early and resolves quickly once the drug is cleared. This was a slow-burn fire that kept raging long after the spark was gone.

The workup was exhaustive. Viral panels were negative. She had never taken statins (the usual suspect for anti-HMGCR myopathy). Her extensive myositis antibody panel came back negative for both anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP, placing her in that tricky seronegative bucket that accounts for roughly one-third of IMNM cases. The definitive clue came from the muscle biopsy of her vastus lateralis: scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage infiltration (CD68+) and membrane attack complex deposition on non-necrotic fibers. The diagnosis was clear: Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy.

### The Treatment Pivot: Aggressive Start, Rapid Exit

Here is where this case diverges from standard dogma. In classic idiopathic IMNM, we brace ourselves for a long haul. We're talking high-dose steroids combined with methotrexate, azathioprine, rituximab, or ongoing IVIG for months to years. Relapse upon tapering is common, and many patients remain on maintenance therapy indefinitely.

However, recognizing the likely drug-induced nature of this case, the team opted for a different approach. They hit hard initially to stop the immune storm: five days of pulse IV methylprednisolone (1g/day) followed by oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg daily. Given the severity of her bulbar symptoms (dysphagia/dysphonia) and profound weakness, they added a single course of IVIG (2 g/kg total).

The response was dramatic. Within two weeks, her CK plummeted. Even more impressively, because she remained off semaglutide and showed sustained clinical improvement, they were able to taper the prednisone rapidly over just two months. No second-line agents were needed beyond that initial IVIG pulse. Ten months out, she was back to her baseline functional status, off all immunosuppression for eight months, with normalized CK levels and resolved cardiac findings (she had transient myocardial edema noted on CMR).

### Why This Matters: Trigger Removal Changes the Game

This case highlights a critical distinction between *idiopathic* autoimmune disease and *drug-triggered* autoimmunity. In statin-associated IMNM (anti-HMGCR positive), removing the statin often isn't enough; the autoimmune process becomes self-perpetuating because the body is now attacking the HMGCR enzyme itself regardless of statin presence. That's why those patients need heavy-duty suppression.

But what if the drug acts as a unique adjuvant or trigger that kickstarts an immune response which remains dependent on some residual factor or simply lacks the "fuel" to sustain itself once the trigger is gone? In this semaglutide case (and potentially other drug-induced I MNMs), removing the antigenic stimulus might allow a finite course of immunosuppression to fully extinguish the fire rather than just dampen it down temporarily. The fact that our patient remained in remission off therapy suggests that for select drug-induced cases, we might avoid condemning patients to lifelong immunosuppression if we recognize the etiology early and act decisively.

Of course, caution is warranted. We can't definitively prove causality without a rechallenge (which would be unethical here), and we don't know if she had smoldering subclinical disease beforehand. However, the temporal relationship—a massive CK rise starting after exposure and resolving only after withdrawal plus short-course therapy—strongly supports a drug-driven mechanism according to WHO-UMC criteria.

### Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Messages

*   **Don't Dismiss Late-Onset Weakness:** Drug-induced myopathy doesn't always look like acute rhabdomyolysis with immediate collapse watch out for delayed presentations where weakness progresses weeks to months after exposure even if CK was only mildly elevated initially
*   **Seronegativity Doesn't Rule Out IMNM:** Approximately one-third of IMNM cases are negative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies; rely on clinical phenotype (proximal weakness high CK) and biopsy findings (necrosis with minimal inflammation MAC deposition) when antibodies are absent
*   **Consider Finite Therapy for Drug-Triggers:** In cases where IMNM is strongly linked to a specific medication consider whether aggressive short-term immunosuppression combined with strict avoidance of the trigger can lead to complete remission unlike idiopathic cases requiring chronic management
*   **Watch for Bulbar/Cardiac Involvement:** IMNM can affect extraocular muscles rarely but frequently impacts pharyngeal muscles causing dysphagia/aspiration risk; cardiac involvement though less common should be screened for via troponin echo or CMR in severe cases
*   **Biopsy is King:** When facing unexplained proximal weakness with high CK especially in seronegative patients proceed early to muscle biopsy looking for necrotizing features rather than waiting indefinitely for antibody results



# Heart vs. Muscle: Decoding Elevated Troponin and Transaminases in Severe Myopathy

We've all been there. You're staring at a set of labs for a patient with profound weakness, and the numbers are screaming "multi-organ failure." The troponin is up. The liver enzymes are skyrocketing. Your mind immediately races to the worst-case scenarios: fulminant myocarditis, acute hepatic necrosis, or perhaps a catastrophic systemic vasculitis. It's easy to panic and order every scan in the hospital, but sometimes the answer isn't that the heart and liver are failing simultaneously—it's that the skeleton is falling apart, and the labs are just telling tall tales.

A fascinating recent case report by Lam et al. throws this diagnostic dilemma into sharp relief. They describe a woman in her 60s who developed severe immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) shortly after receiving a single dose of semaglutide. Over four months, she progressed from nausea to being unable to sit upright, accompanied by dysphagia and dysphonia. But what really grabs the attention of the emergency physician or intensivist is the lab profile: a creatine kinase (CK) peaking near 4,000 U/L, elevated transaminases (AST 191, ALT 165), and a bumped troponin. On paper, this looks like a patient dying from three different directions. In reality, it was likely one direction with significant collateral damage to our interpretation of biomarkers.

The trap here is assuming organ-specific pathology based on organ-specific markers without considering the source of those markers. AST and ALT are not exclusive to the liver; they are abundant in skeletal muscle. When you have massive myocyte necrosis, as seen in IMNM or rhabdomyolysis, these enzymes spill into the bloodstream indiscriminately. In classic alcoholic liver disease, we look for an AST:ALT ratio greater than 2:1. In pure myopathy, AST is often higher than ALT simply because skeletal muscle contains significantly more AST than ALT. In this patient, despite the alarming transaminitis, the liver biopsy—performed during her admission—showed nothing more than her known background of steatohepatitis (fatty liver). There was no acute hepatitis, no interface inflammation, and no necrosis. The "liver failure" was a phantom created by dying muscle fibers.

The troponin elevation presents an even trickier conundrum. High-sensitivity troponin assays are incredibly specific for cardiac injury, but they aren't infallible in the setting of extreme skeletal muscle turnover. While modern assays have minimized cross-reactivity with skeletal troponins, severe myositis can still lead to false positives or mild elevations due to regeneration of skeletal muscle expressing fetal isoforms, or simply due to supply/demand mismatch from systemic stress and immobility. This patient had a troponin of 62 pg/mL (just above the cutoff) and a sky-high pro-BNP suggesting strain. Naturally, the team worried about myocarditis. However, the cardiac MRI told a different story: mild edema and hypokinesis without late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). The absence of LGE essentially rules out frank myocardial necrosis or established myocarditis. Her cardiac issues were likely secondary to the systemic inflammatory storm and deconditioning rather than a primary viral or autoimmune attack on the heart muscle itself.

This case serves as a potent reminder that context is king. If you see elevated "liver" enzymes and a "cardiac" marker in a patient who cannot lift their arms off the bed, check the CK before you call Gastroenterology and Cardiology for an emergency consult stack. The pattern of enzyme elevation matters. If AST > ALT in a non-drinker with normal bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase, think muscle first. If troponin is mildly elevated but imaging doesn't show infarction or classic myocarditis patterns, consider whether skeletal muscle breakdown is muddying the waters. In this specific case, recognizing IMNM allowed for targeted immunosuppression with steroids and IVIG, leading to complete recovery without needing to treat non-existent liver or heart failure.

Don't let the noise of nonspecific biomarkers drown out the clinical signal. When faced with this triad of weakness, transaminitis, and troponemia, let your hands do the talking during the physical exam before letting the lab computer drive your differential diagnosis wide off the road.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Transaminases are not liver-specific:** Significant skeletal muscle necrosis releases AST and ALT; an AST > ALT ratio in a patient with normal bilirubin/alkaline phos should trigger an immediate CK check to rule out myopathy masquerading as hepatitis.
*   **Troponin elevations require nuance in myositis:** Mild troponin leaks can occur in severe necrotizing myopathy due to assay cross-reactivity with regenerating skeletal fibers or demand ischemia; always correlate with cardiac MRI (looking for Late Gadolinium Enhancement) before diagnosing concurrent myocarditis.
*   **Biopsy remains the gold standard:** When serology is negative (as in seronegative IMNM) and imaging is equivocal, tissue diagnosis distinguishes between true multi-organ failure and single-system disease with misleading biomarkers.
*   **Medication triggers can be subtle:** Be aware that GLP-1 agonists like semaglutide have now been implicated in rare cases of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy; a detailed medication history extending back months is crucial even if symptoms seem delayed relative to dosing onset via



# The Obesity Paradox: Navigating Muscle Loss and Metabolic Therapy in the GLP-1 Era

We are living through a revolution in metabolic medicine. Walk into any emergency department or primary care clinic today, and you'll hear patients discussing "Ozempic" and "Wegovy" with the same familiarity they once reserved for Lipitor. Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have undeniably changed the landscape for obesity and type 2 diabetes, offering profound weight loss and cardiovascular benefits. But as these agents become ubiquitous, we are beginning to see the shadow side of rapid metabolic transformation: a complex interplay between intended fat loss and unintended muscle catabolism.

While we generally accept some degree of lean mass reduction as the price of admission for significant weight loss, a recent case report forces us to draw a hard line between physiological adaptation and pathological catastrophe. A woman in her 60s, previously independent and active aside from her struggle with obesity and fatty liver disease, received a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide. Within hours, she was hospitalized with vomiting and acute kidney injury. But the story didn't end there. Over the next four months, while completely off the drug, she developed progressive, devastating proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. By the time she reached tertiary care, she couldn't sit upright without assistance, and her creatine kinase (CK) had skyrocketed to nearly 4,000 U/L.

Her muscle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis with scattered necrotic fibers and macrophage infiltration, yet her extensive autoimmune panel—including the classic anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies associated with statin-induced myopathy—was entirely negative. She required pulse steroids and IVIG to recover, eventually returning to baseline only after avoiding GLP-1 agonists entirely. This case represents the first reported instance of IMNM probably associated with a GLP-1 receptor agonist, serving as a stark reminder that not all muscle weakness in the era of weight-loss drugs is benign.

## Beyond Sarcopenic Obesity: When Adaptation Becomes Autoimmunity

Clinicians are already rightly concerned about "sarcopenic obesity," where rapid weight loss strips away vital muscle mass alongside adipose tissue, leaving frailty in its wake. In typical scenarios, this is a dose-dependent issue managed with protein optimization and resistance training. However, the case described above illustrates a fundamentally different mechanism: an immune-mediated assault triggered by exposure to the drug, persisting long after clearance.

In classic drug-induced rhabdomyolysis (such as that recently reported with tirzepatide), we expect a sharp spike in CK that resolves quickly upon discontinuation and hydration. That is a toxic effect. What happened to this patient was an autoimmune switch flip. Despite stopping semaglutide immediately after her initial reaction, her CK levels climbed twelve-fold over four months, and her weakness progressed relentlessly until immunosuppression was introduced. This trajectory mimics the historical presentation of statin-associated IMNM before we understood the role of anti-HMGCR antibodies. Just as statins can trigger a self-perpetuating autoimmune myopathy in susceptible individuals, it appears GLP-1 agonists may possess a similar, albeit perhaps rarer, potential to ignite an immune fire that burns independently of the inciting agent's presence in the bloodstream.

The diagnostic challenge here is immense because these patients often present with non-specific complaints initially dismissed as "getting out of shape" or residual effects of GI illness. Our patient had negative standard myositis panels, falling into the seronegative category that accounts for roughly one-third of IMNM cases. Without a high index of suspicion—and crucially, without performing a muscle biopsy when clinical severity outweighs serological findings—we risk missing a treatable autoimmune condition while attributing symptoms to rapid weight loss or deconditioning. Furthermore, distinguishing hepatic transaminitis from muscle breakdown is critical; this patient had pre-existing fatty liver disease which muddied the waters, but her rising AST/ALT tracked with her CK elevation rather than her liver pathology alone.

## Practical Management: Screening Red Flags vs. Expected Side Effects

So how do we navigate this in clinical practice? We cannot stop prescribing life-saving medications based on a single case report, nor should we ignore every ache and pain in a patient losing 20 pounds a month. The key lies in recognizing patterns that deviate from the expected course of GLP-1 therapy. Most patients will experience nausea or transient fatigue; they should not develop progressive inability to lift their arms or swallow liquids months after their last dose.

When evaluating a patient on GLP-1 agonists presenting with weakness or elevated enzymes, we must move beyond assuming it's just "muscle loss." If a patient reports difficulty rising from a chair, combing their hair, or swallowing *after* the acute GI phase has passed—or if these symptoms worsen despite stopping the drug—it warrants an aggressive workup including CK levels and consideration for rheumatology/neurology referral. The presence of dysphagia or dysphonia is particularly ominous and should never be attributed solely to dehydration or general malaise in this context. Early recognition allowed this patient to receive IVIG and steroids promptly, leading to full recovery; delay could have resulted in permanent disability or respiratory compromise given the involvement of bulbar muscles.

Ultimately, this case expands our differential diagnosis for new-onset myopathy in the obese population.
