Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Challenges in Concurrent Systemic and Neurological Behçet's Disease

Behçet's disease (BD) is a chronic, multisystem vasculitis characterized classically by the triad of recurrent oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and uveitis. While mucocutaneous manifestations are the hallmark of the disease, neurological involvement, termed Neuro-Behçet's disease (Neuro-BD), occurs in approximately 10% to 20% of patients. A critical epidemiological feature of Neuro-BD is its typical latency; neurological symptoms usually manifest several years—often around four years—after the initial diagnosis of systemic BD. Consequently, the simultaneous onset of definitive systemic mucocutaneous lesions and acute neurological involvement, as seen in this case, represents a rare and diagnostically precarious clinical scenario. In fewer than 5% of cases do neurological symptoms appear at the very outset of the disease course, creating a situation where clinicians must synthesize non-specific constitutional complaints with emerging dermatological signs to avoid misdiagnosis.

The patient's presentation illustrated the complexity of identifying acute Neuro-BD amidst a backdrop of systemic inflammation. He arrived with a constellation of fever, headache, general malaise, and arthralgia, symptoms that broadly suggest infection, malignancy, or autoimmune crisis. Superimposed on these constitutional symptoms were specific mucocutaneous findings: oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk. Notably, the pathognomonic genital ulcer appeared only on day five of hospitalization, delaying the fulfillment of complete diagnostic criteria. This temporal gap underscores a significant challenge in real-time clinical assessment; without the immediate presence of genital lesions, the specificity for BD decreases, forcing reliance on a broader differential diagnosis that includes viral meningoencephalitis and other connective tissue diseases until the full phenotypic picture emerges.

Among the neurological complaints, persistent hiccups served as a subtle yet crucial localizing sign. While often dismissed as benign or gastrointestinal in origin, intractable hiccups can indicate irritation of the medullary centers in the brainstem or severe metabolic derangement. In this case, the hiccups likely represented a dual pathology: direct inflammatory involvement of the brainstem regions controlling the hiccup reflex arc and severe hyponatremia secondary to the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH). The coexistence of headache, vertigo, and hiccups in a patient with fever should raise suspicion for meningoencephalitis, even in the absence of classic meningeal signs like nuchal rigidity. Recognizing these non-specific neurological cues as potential markers of central nervous system (CNS) inflammation is essential for prompting timely lumbar puncture and neuro-imaging.

Diagnostic confidence in acute Neuro-BD is frequently hampered by normal neuroimaging findings during the early phases of the disease. Unlike chronic or parenchymal Neuro-BD, which typically displays characteristic T2-weighted hyperintensities in the brainstem or basal ganglia, acute meningoencephalic forms may present with a completely normal brain MRI. In this case, despite worsening headache and vertigo suggestive of CNS inflammation, initial imaging revealed no structural abnormalities involving the hypothalamus or brainstem. This radiological silence necessitates a heavy reliance on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis to confirm aseptic meningitis. The presence of lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein in the CSF, combined with negative infectious workups (viral PCR, bacterial cultures), becomes the primary evidence supporting a diagnosis of acute Neuro-BD when MRI is unrevealing. Clinicians must therefore resist ruling out Neuro-BD solely based on normal imaging results in the acute setting.

Definitive diagnosis in such complex presentations relies on the rigorous application of standardized scoring systems, specifically the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD). The ICBD assigns weighted points to various clinical features: two points each for oral and genital aphthosis, one point for skin lesions (such as folliculitis), one point for ocular lesions, one point for vascular manifestations, and one point for a positive pathergy test (though not performed here). A cumulative score of four or more indicates "definite" BD. In this patient's evolving course, the initial presence of oral ulcers (2 points) and skin lesions (1 point) yielded an indeterminate score. However, once genital ulcers manifested (adding 2 points), his total reached five points, confirming definite BD independent of HLA-B51 status or pathergy reactivity. This case reinforces that while HLA-B51 positivity supports susceptibility, it is neither sensitive nor specific enough for standalone diagnosis; instead, serial clinical evaluation to capture evolving mucocutaneous signs remains the gold standard for applying ICBD criteria effectively.

## Pathophysiological Mechanisms Linking CNS Inflammation to SIADH

The development of Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH) in this patient serves as a critical clinical bridge between his systemic inflammation and acute neurological involvement. Diagnosing SIADH requires a systematic interpretation of fluid and electrolyte parameters within the context of the patient's volume status. In this case, the patient presented with euvolemic hyponatremia, evidenced by the absence of peripheral edema, normal blood pressure, and lack of tachycardia suggestive of hypovolemia. The hallmark biochemical profile included low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg) juxtaposed with inappropriately elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), indicating that the kidneys were failing to dilute urine despite systemic hypo-osmolality. Furthermore, the urinary sodium concentration was elevated (>20 mmol/L), reflecting natriuresis driven by volume expansion rather than conservation. Crucially, despite the low serum tonicity which should suppress secretion, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels remained detectable and elevated (22.4 pg/mL). This dissociation confirms the "inappropriate" nature of ADH secretion, distinguishing SIADH from other causes of hyponatremia such as psychogenic polydipsia, where ADH is appropriately suppressed.

The pathophysiology of SIADH in acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) likely stems from the disruption of the hypothalamic-neurohypophyseal axis by inflammatory processes. While classic teaching associates SIADH with structural lesions in the hypothalamus or pituitary stalk, this patient's brain MRI revealed no focal abnormalities in these regions. This suggests that functional disruption, rather than gross anatomical destruction, drove the hormonal dysregulation. In neuro-inflammatory states, meningeal inflammation or parenchymal infiltration can alter the firing thresholds of osmoreceptors or stimulate non-osmotic release of ADH. In Behçet's disease specifically, the mechanism is increasingly attributed to a cytokine-mediated storm. Monocytes in BD patients are prone to spontaneous activation, leading to excessive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β). These cytokines are known potent stimulators of ADH release; IL-6, frequently elevated in the cerebrospinal fluid of acute neuro-BD patients, can cross the blood-brain barrier or act locally to trigger ectopic or dysregulated ADH secretion independent of osmotic stimuli. The patient's markedly elevated C-reactive protein serves as a surrogate marker for this intense IL-6-driven inflammatory state, providing a plausible biological link between his aseptic meningitis and endocrine failure.

Differentiating SIADH from other etiologies of hyponatremia in febrile patients with CNS symptoms is essential for appropriate management. The primary alternative consideration in neurologic injury is Cerebral Salt Wasting (CSW), which also presents with hyponatremia and high urinary sodium. However, CSW is characterized by true hypovolemia due to renal salt loss, necessitating volume replacement—a strategy that would be detrimental in SIADH. The clinical assessment of this patient confirmed euvolemia, effectively ruling out CSW and hypovolemic hyponatremia secondary to poor oral intake or gastrointestinal losses. Additionally, drug-induced SIADH was unlikely given the timeline; the hyponatremia preceded the administration of any potential offending agents like acyclovir or antibiotics, pointing instead to the disease process itself as the culprit. Recognizing this distinction prevented unnecessary fluid resuscitation and guided the decision toward fluid restriction as the cornerstone of therapy.

The severity of the hyponatremia in this case precipitated significant secondary complications, most notably rhabdomyolysis and persistent hiccups. Severe hyponatremia can induce cellular swelling and membrane instability in skeletal muscle, leading to leakage of intracellular enzymes such as creatine kinase (CK), which was markedly elevated at 1349 U/L in this patient. While rhabdomyolysis is often associated with trauma or statin use, it is a recognized but under-appreciated consequence of profound electrolyte disturbance. Similarly, persistent hiccups served as a non-specific but valuable neurological clue pointing toward irritation of the medullary centers or metabolic derangement affecting the phrenic nerve reflex arc. The resolution of both the rhabdomyolysis and hiccups following the normalization of serum sodium via fluid restriction and colchicine therapy underscores that these were downstream effects of the SIADH rather than primary muscular or neuromuscular pathologies. This cascade highlights how untreated SIADH in neuro-BD can extend morbidity beyond neurological symptoms to include systemic metabolic collapse.

## Systematic Differential Diagnosis and Exclusionary Workup

The confirmation of acute neurological Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) in this case relied heavily on a rigorous exclusionary process, as the initial presentation of fever, headache, and hyponatremia overlapped significantly with life-threatening infectious and neoplastic conditions. Given the patient's age and acute onset, the primary diagnostic imperative was to rule out meningoencephalitis caused by common pathogens. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis served as the cornerstone of this evaluation, revealing an "aseptic" pattern characterized by mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) and elevated protein (0.97 g/L) without a neutrophilic predominance. While this profile raised suspicion for viral etiologies such as Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) or Varicella Zoster Virus (VZV), polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing and serology for these viruses were negative. Similarly, bacterial meningitis was excluded based on sterile cultures and the lack of purulent CSF findings. Tuberculous meningitis, a critical consideration given the potential for lymphocytic pleocytosis and basal meningeal involvement, was ruled out through negative acid-fast staining and specific DNA testing. Fungal causes, specifically cryptococcal meningitis, were also dismissed following a negative antigen test. This comprehensive microbiological clearance allowed the clinical team to shift focus from infectious causes to inflammatory or neoplastic processes.

With infection deemed unlikely, the differential diagnosis expanded to include primary central nervous system malignancies, particularly CNS lymphoma, which can present with non-specific neurological symptoms and CSF abnormalities. The diagnostic workup utilized neuroimaging and cytological assessment to address this possibility. Notably, the brain MRI revealed no parenchymal lesions, mass effect, or hypothalamic abnormalities, which is atypical for advanced CNS lymphoma but does not entirely exclude early-stage disease or leptomeningeal involvement. To further investigate, CSF cytology was performed and found to be negative for malignant cells. Additionally, serum soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels were measured; while mildly elevated at 704 U/mL, this finding was interpreted as non-specific. In the context of hematological malignancies like lymphoma, sIL-2R is often markedly higher, whereas in this case, the modest elevation likely reflected the generalized inflammatory state associated with Behçet's disease rather than a clonal lymphoproliferative disorder. Consequently, the absence of radiographic masses and malignant cells in the CSF effectively lowered the probability of a neoplastic etiology.

Granulomatous diseases, specifically neurosarcoidosis, represented another significant diagnostic challenge due to their ability to mimic neuro-BD with meningeal inflammation and cranial nerve or hypothalamic dysfunction. Differentiation relied on a combination of serological markers and clinical phenotyping. Serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) and lysozyme levels were within normal limits, reducing the likelihood of active sarcoidosis, although clinicians must recognize that normal ACE levels do not definitively exclude the disease. More importantly, the patient's clinical trajectory provided distinguishing features: the emergence of painful genital ulcers alongside oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes created a mucocutaneous constellation highly specific to Behçet's disease rather than sarcoidosis. Furthermore, the patient lacked other systemic signs typical of sarcoidosis, such as hilar lymphadenopathy on chest imaging or pulmonary infiltrates. The diagnosis was ultimately solidified by applying the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD), where the presence of oral ulcers, genital ulcers, and skin lesions yielded a score sufficient for a "definite" classification once other causes were excluded.

Interpreting the CSF findings required recognizing the specific pattern of aseptic meningitis inherent to acute neuro-BD. The combination of lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein in the absence of identifiable pathogens is a hallmark of the meningoencephalitic subtype of neuro-BD. It is crucial for learners to appreciate that a normal brain MRI does not preclude this diagnosis; parenchymal lesions typical of chronic neuro-BD (often located in the brainstem or basal ganglia) may be absent in the acute meningeal phase or in cases presenting primarily with meningoencephalitis. Finally, while Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA)-B51 testing was positive in this patient, its role in the acute diagnostic algorithm is supportive rather than definitive. HLA-B51 positivity increases statistical susceptibility to Behçet's disease but lacks the sensitivity and specificity required for standalone diagnosis, as it can be present in healthy individuals or those with other inflammatory conditions. Therefore, in this case series logic HLA-B51 served as corroborative evidence reinforcing a diagnosis already driven by strict clinical criteria and the systematic exclusion of mimics, rather than acting as the primary diagnostic pivot point.

## Therapeutic Decision-Making: Colchicine Monotherapy vs. Standard Immunosuppression

The management of acute neurological Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) traditionally relies on aggressive immunosuppression, yet this case presents a notable deviation where colchicine monotherapy achieved complete remission. Standard guidelines for acute parenchymal neuro-BD, particularly when involving the brainstem or causing significant focal deficits, recommend high-dose intravenous corticosteroids as the first-line induction therapy, often followed by cytotoxic agents such as azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, or tumor necrosis factor inhibitors. This aggressive approach is necessitated by the risk of irreversible neuronal damage and the potentially fulminant course of parenchymal inflammation. However, the clinical phenotype in this patient was distinct; he presented with aseptic meningitis characterized by headache, fever, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pleocytosis, but crucially lacked parenchymal lesions on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and exhibited no focal neurological deficits. This "meningeal-predominant" presentation likely influenced the therapeutic trajectory, suggesting that the inflammatory burden was sufficient to cause systemic symptoms and SIADH but perhaps less destructive than classic parenchymal neuro-BD.

The decision to utilize colchicine instead of corticosteroids requires an understanding of both the drug's pharmacodynamics and the specific risks posed to this patient. Colchicine functions by binding to tubulin, thereby inhibiting microtubule polymerization. This mechanism disrupts neutrophil chemotaxis, degranulation, and superoxide production while also suppressing the inflammasome-mediated release of proinflammatory cytokines like interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and IL-6. Given that IL-6 is implicated in both the pathogenesis of neuro-BD and the dysregulation of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion in SIADH, colchicine offered a targeted anti-inflammatory mechanism that addressed the root cause of both the neurological and endocrine manifestations. Furthermore, avoiding corticosteroids was a prudent strategic choice given the patient's comorbid profile. He presented with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus (HbA1c 8.2%) and active infection concerns initially requiring empirical antibiotics. High-dose steroids would have exacerbated hyperglycemia, complicating metabolic management and increasing susceptibility to secondary infections, whereas colchicine provided a safer side-effect profile for this specific host.

Concurrent with the immunomodulatory strategy, the management of SIADH adhered to conservative physiological principles tailored to the severity of hyponatremia. The patient's serum sodium was 124 mmol/L; while symptomatic with hiccups and malaise, he did not exhibit life-threatening signs such as seizures or coma that would mandate immediate hypertonic saline administration. Consequently, the primary intervention was strict fluid restriction (<800 mL/day), aimed at creating a negative water balance to gradually raise serum sodium concentrations. Careful monitoring ensured that sodium correction proceeded at a safe rate to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome. The avoidance of vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans) reflected the expectation that treating the underlying CNS inflammation would resolve the inappropriate ADH secretion naturally. The normalization of sodium levels within two weeks coincided precisely with the resolution of fever and neurological symptoms following colchicine initiation, reinforcing the causal link between the inflammatory process and the endocrine disturbance.

Ultimately, this case challenges the rigid application of treatment algorithms for neuro-BD by demonstrating that colchicine monotherapy can be effective in selected cases. While systematic reviews generally deem colchicine ineffective for established parenchymal neuro-BD, its success here suggests a viable role in early-stage disease limited to meningeal inflammation without radiographic parenchymal involvement. The rapid resolution of symptoms implies that for patients presenting with mild-to-moderate acute neuro-BD—specifically those where corticosteroids pose significant metabolic or infectious risks—colchicine may serve as a potent alternative rather than merely an adjunctive therapy for mucocutaneous disease. This outcome underscores the necessity of phenotyping neuro-BD accurately; distinguishing between meningeal and parenchymal involvement is critical not only for prognosis but for tailoring immunosuppressive intensity to minimize iatrogenic harm while achieving disease control.

## Clinical Pearls and Red Flags in Neuro-BD with SIADH

Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for acute neurological Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) when a patient presents with classic mucocutaneous findings—specifically oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and folliculitis-like rashes—accompanied by non-specific constitutional and neurological symptoms. While neuro-BD typically manifests years after the initial diagnosis, concurrent presentation is a critical red flag that should not be dismissed. Key warning signs include fever, severe headache, vertigo, and persistent hiccups. In the context of Behçet's disease, persistent hiccups are particularly noteworthy as they may signal severe hyponatremia secondary to central nervous system inflammation rather than a primary gastrointestinal issue. The emergence of these neurological complaints alongside active mucocutaneous lesions warrants an immediate workup for meningoencephalitis, even in the absence of focal deficits or altered mental status.

Unexplained hyponatremia in a patient with suspected or confirmed Behçet's disease serves as a potent diagnostic clue for concurrent syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) driven by CNS inflammation. When evaluating such patients, the presence of euvolemic hypotonic hyponatremia with inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated serum antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels confirms SIADH. It is imperative to recognize this electrolyte disturbance not merely as a metabolic complication but as a marker of active neuro-inflammation. Severe hyponatremia can precipitate secondary complications such as rhabdomyolysis, evidenced by elevated creatine kinase, and exacerbate neurological symptoms like confusion or seizures. Therefore, any new-onset hyponatremia in Behçet's disease should trigger an urgent evaluation for aseptic meningitis and neuro-BD, prompting cerebrospinal fluid analysis regardless of the severity of systemic symptoms.

A common diagnostic pitfall in early acute neuro-BD is relying too heavily on initial neuroimaging to rule out CNS involvement. As illustrated in this case, brain MRI may appear completely normal during the early phases of acute meningeal neuro-BD, lacking the characteristic parenchymal lesions often seen in chronic or advanced disease. The absence of radiological abnormalities, particularly in the brainstem or hypothalamus, does not exclude the diagnosis. Instead, the diagnosis rests on clinical correlation and cerebrospinal fluid findings indicative of aseptic meningitis, such as lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein with negative infectious cultures. Clinicians must avoid false reassurance from a normal scan and proceed with lumbar puncture when clinical suspicion remains high based on mucocutaneous and systemic features.

While high-dose corticosteroids remain the standard first-line therapy for acute neuro-BD, this case highlights a potential modification to the treatment algorithm for select presentations. In cases where acute neuro-BD presents primarily as aseptic meningitis without significant parenchymal involvement and is complicated by SIADH, colchicine monotherapy may offer a viable alternative, particularly when steroid use is undesirable or contraindicated. Colchicine's ability to inhibit neutrophil migration and suppress proinflammatory cytokines like IL-6 likely contributed to the resolution of both neurological symptoms and SIADH in this patient. Although evidence for colchicine as a standalone treatment for neuro-BD is limited compared to its efficacy in mucocutaneous disease, its success here suggests it should be considered in mild-to-moderate meningeal presentations, potentially sparing patients from steroid-related adverse effects while effectively managing endocrine disturbances.

Long-term management requires vigilant follow-up strategies focused on detecting delayed progression to parenchymal disease and monitoring endocrine stability. Patients discharged after an episode of acute meningeal neuro-BD should undergo serial neurological assessments and repeat imaging if new focal deficits develop, as parenchymal lesions can emerge weeks to months after the initial meningeal presentation. Additionally, given the association between CNS inflammation and dysregulated ADH secretion, clinicians should monitor serum sodium levels periodically post-discharge to ensure sustained resolution of SIADH. Education regarding the recurrence of mucocutaneous lesions is also essential, as their return may precede neurological relapse. Continued colchicine therapy appears effective for maintaining remission in such cases, though adherence and regular monitoring for toxicity remain paramount.
