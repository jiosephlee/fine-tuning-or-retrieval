Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] According to the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD), how many points are assigned to genital aphthosis, and why is this specific finding critical for distinguishing Behçet's from other causes of aseptic meningitis in a patient with oral ulcers?

Under the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD), **genital aphthosis is assigned two points**, the highest weighting for any single clinical feature alongside ocular lesions. In the reported case, the patient accumulated a total score of five points—derived from oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and folliculitis-like skin lesions (one point)—thereby meeting the threshold of ≥4 points required for a "definite" diagnosis.

This specific finding is critical for distinguishing Behçet's disease from other etiologies of aseptic meningitis because recurrent genital ulcers possess high specificity for Behçet's, whereas oral aphthae are ubiquitous in the general population and nonspecific. In this case, the patient presented with fever, headache, and cerebrospinal fluid evidence of aseptic meningitis, a picture that initially prompted a broad workup for viral encephalitis, bacterial meningitis, and malignancy. However, infectious and neoplastic causes were excluded via negative cultures, PCR assays, and cytology. The subsequent appearance of a painful scrotal ulcer provided the pivotal diagnostic clue; without this highly specific mucocutaneous marker, linking the sterile meningoencephalitis and concurrent syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) to systemic Behçet's disease would have been exceptionally difficult. Thus, recognizing genital aphthosis allows clinicians to attribute otherwise idiopathic neurological inflammation to neuro-Behçet's disease, guiding appropriate immunomodulatory therapy rather than prolonged empirical antimicrobial treatment.

### [Morning Report] In a patient presenting with fever, headache, and hyponatremia, what specific combination of serum osmolality, urine osmolality, and volume status confirms the diagnosis of SIADH while ruling out hypovolemic hyponatremia or cerebral salt wasting?

To confirm the diagnosis of SIADH and distinguish it from hypovolemic hyponatremia or cerebral salt wasting (CSW), one must demonstrate a specific triad: **low serum osmolality**, **inappropriately elevated urine osmolality**, and **clinical euvolemia**.

In the setting of acute neurological Behçet's disease, as illustrated in this case, the pathophysiology involves CNS inflammation driving non-osmotic release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH). This results in free water retention, diluting serum solutes while concentrating urine. Consequently, the diagnostic hallmark is a mismatch between a hypo-osmolar state and concentrated urine. In our patient, this manifested as a serum osmolality of 267 mOsm/kg (low) paired with a urine osmolality of 900 mOsm/kg (high), alongside elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L) due to volume-expanded natriuresis.

The critical differentiator from hypovolemic hyponatremia and CSW is volume status. Both hypovolemia and CSW present with clinical signs of volume depletion (e.g., tachycardia, orthostasis, dry mucous membranes) driven by true salt and water loss. In contrast, SIADH is a state of euvolemic hyponatremia; although total body water increases slightly, it is insufficient to cause overt edema or hemodynamic instability. Our patient was explicitly assessed as **euvolemic** on physical examination—lacking peripheral edema yet maintaining stable blood pressure despite tachycardia likely secondary to fever and pain rather than volume loss. Furthermore, the resolution of hyponatremia with fluid restriction alone, without the need for saline resuscitation (which would worsen SIADH) or aggressive salt replacement (required for CSW), retrospectively confirms the diagnosis. Thus, the combination of hypo-osmolar serum, hyper-osmolar urine, and a clinically euvolemic exam definitively identifies SIADH while excluding volume-depleted etiologies.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Given that brain MRI was normal in this case of acute neuro-Behçet's disease, what cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) findings support the diagnosis of parenchymal-sparing acute neuro-Behçet's (aseptic meningitis phenotype) versus viral meningoencephalitis?

In this case of acute neuro-Behçet's disease presenting with a normal brain MRI, the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) profile supports a diagnosis of parenchymal-sparing neuro-Behçet's (manifesting as aseptic meningitis) rather than viral meningoencephalitis primarily through the combination of sterile lymphocytic pleocytosis and the specific exclusion of viral pathogens in the context of systemic Behçet's criteria.

The patient's CSF analysis revealed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) and elevated protein (0.97 g/L) with a mildly elevated opening pressure, findings that constitute an aseptic meningitis picture. While this cellular profile overlaps significantly with viral etiologies such as herpes simplex virus (HSV) or varicella-zoster virus (VZV) meningoencephalitis, the distinction here relies on two critical factors grounded in the case report. First, extensive microbiological workup—including CSF cultures and specific testing for HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen—was unequivocally negative, effectively ruling out common infectious causes. Second, and perhaps most importantly, the diagnosis of neuro-Behçet's was secured by the concurrent presence of definitive systemic mucocutaneous manifestations (oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and folliculitis-like rash) fulfilling the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease.

Unlike viral meningoencephalitis, which often presents with focal parenchymal signal changes on MRI (particularly in HSV), this patient exhibited a "parenchymal-sparing" phenotype where inflammation was confined to the meninges, resulting in a normal MRI despite active CNS symptoms. Therefore, in a patient with established or suspected systemic Behçet's disease, the finding of sterile lymphocytic pleocytosis with elevated protein, absent viral markers, and lacking parenchymal lesions on imaging, strongly favors acute neuro-Behçet's over viral infection. This case further illustrates that such meningeal inflammation can be sufficient to trigger downstream complications like SIADH via cytokine-mediated pathways (e.g., IL-6), even in the absence of visible structural brain lesions.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the proposed pathophysiological mechanism linking central nervous system inflammation in acute neuro-Behçet's disease to the development of SIADH, specifically regarding cytokine mediation?

In this case, the proposed pathophysiological mechanism linking acute neuro-Behçet's disease to the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) centers on **interleukin-6 (IL-6)–mediated stimulation of arginine vasopressin (AVP) release**.

Neuro-Behçet's disease is characterized by intense central nervous system inflammation driven by aberrant monocyte activation. As highlighted in the discussion, this activation leads to the spontaneous overproduction of proinflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-6, which has been detected in elevated concentrations within the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with acute neuro-Behçet's. Physiologically, IL-6 acts directly on the hypothalamus and posterior pituitary to stimulate the synthesis and secretion of AVP (also known as antidiuretic hormone), independent of plasma osmolality. This cytokine-driven surge in AVP results in excessive water reabsorption in the renal collecting ducts, manifesting clinically as euvolemic hyponatremia with inappropriately concentrated urine—the hallmark of SIADH.

Although specific IL-6 levels were not quantified in this patient, the authors infer this mechanism based on the markedly elevated C-reactive protein (a downstream marker of IL-6 activity) and the temporal resolution of both neurological symptoms and hyponatremia following colchicine therapy. Colchicine likely facilitated this recovery by inhibiting neutrophil migration and suppressing the production of IL-1β and IL-6, thereby interrupting the cytokine cascade responsible for dysregulated ADH secretion. This case underscores that in the absence of structural hypothalamic lesions on MRI or pharmacological triggers, cytokine-mediated neuroinflammation alone is sufficient to disrupt osmoregulatory homeostasis.

### [Management] While high-dose corticosteroids are the standard first-line therapy for acute neuro-Behçet's disease, what unique outcome was observed in this case with colchicine monotherapy, and how does this challenge current treatment paradigms for neurological involvement?

In this case, the unique outcome was the complete resolution of acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) manifestations—including aseptic meningitis, systemic inflammation, and Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH)—using **colchicine monotherapy**, without the administration of high-dose corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants.

Standard management paradigms dictate that acute neuro-BD, particularly when presenting with meningoencephalitis or parenchymal involvement, requires aggressive immunosuppression with high-dose corticosteroids, often followed by cytotoxic agents like azathioprine or cyclophosphamide. Current literature and systematic reviews generally characterize colchicine as effective for mucocutaneous and articular symptoms but ineffective for neurological involvement, relegating it to an adjunctive role rather than a primary therapy for neuro-BD.

This case challenges that dogma by demonstrating that in a patient presenting with acute neuro-BD manifesting primarily as aseptic meningitis (evidenced by lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein despite a normal brain MRI), colchicine alone was sufficient to halt the inflammatory cascade. The clinical reasoning here hinges on the pathophysiology of colchicine, which inhibits neutrophil chemotaxis and suppresses the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. Given that IL-6 is implicated in both neuro-inflammation and the dysregulated release of ADH leading to SIADH, the rapid normalization of sodium levels and disappearance of neurological symptoms following colchicine initiation suggests that early intervention with this agent may modulate the specific cytokine drive responsible for both the CNS inflammation and the endocrine complication.

While spontaneous resolution of meningoencephalitic neuro-BD cannot be entirely excluded, the temporal correlation between colchicine initiation and symptom abatement suggests that colchicine monotherapy may be a viable therapeutic alternative in select cases of acute neuro-BD, particularly when corticosteroids are contraindicated or when the presentation is limited to meningeal irritation without extensive parenchymal destruction. This case urges clinicians to reconsider the absolute necessity of steroids in every instance of acute neuro-BD and to recognize colchicine as a potential first-line option in specific phenotypic presentations.

### [Clinical Pearls] Beyond neurological symptoms, what two distinct clinical manifestations of severe hyponatremia were present in this patient that served as clues to the severity of the electrolyte disturbance before the diagnosis of SIADH was fully confirmed?

Beyond the expected neurological sequelae of cerebral edema, this patient exhibited **persistent hiccups** and **rhabdomyolysis**, two distinct clinical manifestations that signaled the severity of his hyponatremia prior to the confirmation of SIADH.

The pathophysiology linking severe hyponatremia to these findings lies in cellular swelling due to osmotic shifts. Persistent hiccups (singultus) result from irritation of the hiccup reflex arc—specifically the phrenic and vagus nerves or their centers in the medulla—caused by electrolyte-induced neuronal excitability or localized edema. While often dismissed as benign, in the context of acute CNS inflammation and fever, persistent hiccups should raise immediate suspicion for significant metabolic derangement. Similarly, rhabdomyolysis, evidenced here by a markedly elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L), is an underappreciated complication of profound hyponatremia. The mechanism involves osmotic swelling of skeletal muscle cells, leading to membrane instability, necrosis, and the release of intracellular enzymes.

As highlighted in the discussion of this case, these "unrelated" symptoms served as critical clues. The presence of intractable hiccups alongside unexplained muscle enzyme elevation in a patient with fever and headache should prompt clinicians to urgently evaluate serum sodium, as these may be the earliest harbingers of life-threatening hyponatremia secondary to SIADH before overt seizures or coma develop.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Why is HLA-B51 positivity considered supportive but not diagnostic for Behçet's disease, and how should a negative HLA-B51 result influence the diagnostic workup in a patient meeting clinical criteria?

HLA-B51 positivity is considered supportive rather than diagnostic for Behçet's disease because the allele lacks sufficient specificity; it is present in a significant proportion of healthy individuals within endemic populations (particularly along the Silk Road) and can be found in patients with other inflammatory conditions. Consequently, international diagnostic criteria, such as those applied in this case, rely exclusively on clinical phenotypes—specifically the constellation of recurrent oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and characteristic skin lesions—to establish a diagnosis of "definite" Behçet's. In the reported case, the diagnosis was secured by a clinical score of five points derived from mucocutaneous findings, while HLA-B51 positivity served merely as an ancillary observation that did not drive the diagnostic decision-making.

Conversely, a negative HLA-B51 result should not deter the clinician from pursuing or confirming a diagnosis of Behçet's disease in a patient who meets clinical criteria. As illustrated in this case report's discussion, HLA-B51 testing is not a requisite component of the diagnostic algorithm. A negative result does not exclude the disease, particularly in non-endemic populations where the allele frequency is lower, nor does it invalidate the presence of acute neurological involvement (neuro-Behçet's) when supported by compatible symptoms like aseptic meningitis and systemic inflammation. Therefore, the diagnostic workup must remain anchored in a rigorous exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and other autoimmune mimics, followed by the application of clinical scoring systems. The presence or absence of HLA-B51 should never supersede clinical judgment when the phenotypic evidence for Behçet's disease is robust.

### [Morning Report] In the context of this case, why was the elevation of soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) deemed non-specific, and what pitfalls exist when using this marker to screen for CNS lymphoma in patients with active systemic inflammation?

In this case, the elevation of soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) was deemed non-specific because the degree of elevation was mild (704 U/mL) and occurred in the setting of profound systemic inflammation driven by acute neuro-Behçet's disease, rather than hematologic malignancy. While sIL-2R is a valuable tumor marker for lymphomas—reflecting shedding from activated T-cells and tumor burden—it is fundamentally a marker of immune activation. Consequently, levels can rise in any condition characterized by robust T-cell stimulation, including viral infections, sarcoidosis, and autoimmune flares.

The primary pitfall in using sIL-2R to screen for CNS lymphoma in patients with active systemic inflammation, as illustrated here, is the lack of diagnostic specificity when inflammatory markers are concurrently elevated. This patient presented with a C-reactive protein of 180 mg/L and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 70 mm/hour, indicative of a massive cytokine storm. In such a milieu, a modest increase in sIL-2R likely reflects the generalized immune response to Behçet's disease rather than a clonal lymphoproliferative process. Relying on this marker without corroborating evidence—such as characteristic findings on brain MRI or malignant cells in the cerebrospinal fluid cytology, both of which were absent in this case—can lead to unnecessary anxiety and misdirected workups. As the authors note, sIL-2R should be interpreted strictly within the clinical context; in the presence of definitive mucocutaneous signs of Behçet's and aseptic meningitis, a mild sIL-2R elevation serves as a reflection of disease activity rather than a pointer toward occult lymphoma.
