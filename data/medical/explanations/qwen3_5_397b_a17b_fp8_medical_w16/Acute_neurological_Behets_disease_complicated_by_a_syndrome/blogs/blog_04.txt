Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Colchicine Monotherapy for Acute Neuro-Behçet's: Breaking the Steroid Reflex?

When you see a patient with fever, headache, oral ulcers, and profound hyponatremia, your mind likely races through the usual suspects: viral meningoencephalitis, bacterial meningitis, or perhaps a paraneoplastic syndrome. You probably don't immediately jump to "acute Neuro-Behçet's complicated by SIADH," especially if the brain MRI is completely normal. Even more counter-intuitive? Watching that entire clinical picture resolve with colchicine *alone*, bypassing the high-dose corticosteroids that have become the reflex standard of care for neurological Behçet's.

We tend to operate on autopilot when it comes to acute Neuro-Behçet's disease (NBD). The dogma is clear: parenchymal involvement equals steroids, often plus heavy immunosuppression like cyclophosphamide or azathioprine. But a fascinating case report by Kakehi et al. forces us to pause and question whether we are over-treating a specific subset of these patients. They describe a man in his 60s who presented with the classic triad of mucocutaneous Behçet's (oral aphthae, genital ulcers, folliculitis-like rash) alongside acute neurological symptoms and severe hyponatremia. Despite meeting the criteria for definite Behçet's and having CSF evidence of aseptic meningitis, his MRI was silent. Even more strikingly, he didn't just survive without steroids; he thrived on colchicine monotherapy, with resolution of his neurological deficits, skin lesions, and SIADH within weeks.

The diagnostic journey here is a masterclass in ruling out the mimics before landing on the zebra. Our patient arrived febrile and confused with sodium levels dropping to 124 mmol/L. The workup for infection was exhaustive and negative—no HSV, no VZV, no bacteria, no TB. Malignancy was ruled out despite a mildly elevated sIL-2R, and sarcoidosis didn't fit the lab profile. The clincher was the emergence of a painful genital ulcer on day five, pushing his International Criteria for Behçet's Disease score over the threshold. But here is where it gets tricky: acute NBD typically presents years after the initial diagnosis, not concurrently. Furthermore, while parenchymal NBD usually lights up the brainstem or basal ganglia on MRI, this patient had a scan that looked essentially normal. This is a critical reminder that acute NBD can present as isolated meningoencephalitis with sterile CSF pleocytosis and zero radiographic footprint. If you wait for an MRI lesion to confirm Neuro-Behçet's in a patient with active mucocutaneous disease and meningitis, you might miss the window entirely.

The elephant in the room—and the reason this case breaks the mold—is the management strategy. Standard guidelines scream for high-dose IV methylprednisolone for acute NBD to prevent irreversible neuronal damage. Colchicine is generally relegated to managing mucocutaneous flares and preventing thrombosis; systematic reviews have historically labeled it ineffective for neurological involvement. Yet, in this case, once infectious causes were excluded, the team initiated colchicine alone. The result? Fever broke within 24 hours. Vertigo, hiccups (a subtle but real sign of his hyponatremia/meningeal irritation), and malaise resolved over two weeks. His sodium normalized as his ADH levels plummeted, confirming the SIADH was driven by the neuro-inflammation itself, likely mediated by IL-6 spikes common in Behçet's pathophysiology. Four years later, he remains relapse-free without ever touching a steroid pulse dose.

Does this mean we should throw away our steroid protocols tomorrow? Absolutely not. The distinction between parenchymal and non-parenchymal (vascular/meningeal) NBD remains vital. Parenchymal disease involves direct infiltration of the brain tissue and carries a high risk of permanent deficit; withholding steroids there is dangerous gambling. However, this case suggests that in early-stage acute NBD presenting primarily as aseptic meningitis without focal parenchymal lesions on imaging—and particularly when steroid toxicity is a major concern—colchicine might be more than just an adjunct. Its mechanism involves inhibiting neutrophil chemotaxis and suppressing IL-1β and IL-6 production, potentially dampening the cytokine storm driving both the meningeal inflammation and the ectopic ADH secretion. While we must remain cautious about attributing success solely to colchicine given the possibility of spontaneous remission in mild meningeal cases, ignoring this signal could deny us a valuable steroid-sparing tool in resource-limited settings or for patients with contraindications to high-dose steroids.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Concurrent Diagnosis is Possible:** Do not rule out acute Neuro-Behçet's simply because mucocutaneous symptoms and neurological signs appear simultaneously; while delayed onset is typical, concurrent presentation occurs and requires high vigilance.
*   **Normal MRI Does Not Exclude NBD:** Acute Neuro-Behçet's can present as aseptic meningitis with significant CSF pleocytosis and protein elevation despite a completely normal brain MRI; do not wait for parenchymal lesions to initiate treatment if clinical suspicion is high.
*   **SIADH as a Clue:** Unexplained hyponatremia (SIADH) in a patient with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous ulcers may be the harbinger of Neuro-Behçet's; look for persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis as downstream effects of severe hyponatremia in this context.
*   **Re-evaluating Colchicine:** While corticosteroids remain first-line for parenchymal NBD, colchicine monotherapy may be a viable consideration for acute non-parenchymal presentations (meningoencephalitis without focal deficits), offering potential resolution of neurological and endocrine manifestations via IL-6 suppression.
*   **Diagnostic Discipline:** Avoid indiscriminate testing for malignancy or other autoimmune conditions once clinical criteria for Behçet's are met; focus on excluding infection rapidly to prevent treatment delays.
