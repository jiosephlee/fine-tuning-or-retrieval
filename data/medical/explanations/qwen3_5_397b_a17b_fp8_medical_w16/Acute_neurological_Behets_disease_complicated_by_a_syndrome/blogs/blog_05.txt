Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# The Diagnostic Trap: Avoiding the 'Shotgun Approach' in Fever of Unknown Origin with Hyponatremia

We've all been there. A patient rolls into the ED with fever, headache, altered mental status, and a nasty case of hyponatremia. The differential diagnosis list instantly stretches across the whiteboard: meningitis, encephalitis, malignancy, autoimmune vasculitis, sarcoidosis. The temptation to fire off a "shotgun" panel—ACE levels, soluble interleukin-2 receptors (sIL-2R), extensive viral PCRs, and whole-body imaging—is overwhelming. We want answers *now*. But as a fascinating recent case report illustrates, casting too wide a net can distract us from the clinical clues staring us right in the face, specifically when classic mucocutaneous signs are evolving alongside neurological decline.

Consider the case of a man in his 60s presenting with three days of fever, headache, persistent hiccups, and profound hyponatremia (Na 124 mmol/L). On arrival, he had oral aphthae and a folliculitis-like rash. His workup confirmed Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH) with low serum osmolality and inappropriately concentrated urine. Initially, the team did exactly what many of us would do: they suspected infection or malignancy. They ran the gamut. Soluble IL-2R came back mildly elevated. Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) was checked to rule out neurosarcoidosis. Extensive infectious panels for HSV, VZV, TB, and bacteria were negative. Brain MRI? Completely normal. For several days, the diagnosis remained elusive because the team was chasing non-specific markers and waiting for a smoking gun on imaging that never appeared.

The breakthrough didn't come from a new lab test; it came from watching the patient evolve. On day five, a painful genital ulcer developed. Suddenly, the puzzle pieces snapped together. The combination of recurrent oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and characteristic skin lesions met the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD) with a score of 5 (definite diagnosis requires ≥4). The neurological symptoms and SIADH were not separate entities or side effects of hidden malignancy; they were manifestations of acute Neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD). This case serves as a potent reminder that while ruling out life threats is mandatory, we must resist letting non-specific inflammatory markers like mildly elevated sIL-2R drive our diagnostic bus into a ditch of unnecessary testing for lymphoma or sarcoidosis when the clinical phenotype screams Behçet's.

What makes this case particularly instructive is the presentation of acute neuro-BD itself. Conventionally teaches us that neurological involvement in Behçet's usually happens years after the initial diagnosis of mucocutaneous disease. Concurrent presentation is rare, occurring in less than 5% of cases. Furthermore, we often expect parenchymal brainstem lesions on MRI in neuro-BD. Yet, this patient had a pristine MRI and presented with aseptic meningitis and SIADH as the primary neurological features. The SIADH was likely driven by CNS inflammation and cytokine storms (specifically IL-6), leading to dysregulated ADH secretion. The persistent hiccups and even rhabdomyolysis (indicated by elevated CK) were downstream effects of the severe hyponatremia and neuro-inflammation, red herrings that could easily send you down a GI or muscular dystrophy rabbit hole if you aren't thinking about electrolytes first.

Perhaps the most surprising aspect of this case was the management outcome. Standard dogma dictates that acute neuro-BD requires heavy artillery: high-dose corticosteroids and often cyclophosphamide or azathioprine. Colchicine is typically reserved for mucocutaneous symptoms and is considered ineffective for parenchymal neurological disease. However, this patient was treated with colchicine monotherapy plus conservative fluid restriction for the SIADH. No steroids. No immunosuppressants. And he got better rapidly. His fever broke within 24 hours of starting colchicine; his sodium normalized within two weeks; his neurological symptoms resolved completely. While we must be cautious not to generalize this to every case of severe neuro-BD—and spontaneous resolution cannot be entirely ruled out—it challenges our reflexive reach for steroids in every inflammatory CNS condition and highlights colchicine's potential role in early-stage meningoencephalitic presentations where steroid toxicity is a concern (such as in his uncontrolled diabetes).

The lesson here isn't just about Behçet's disease; it's about diagnostic discipline. When faced with fever of unknown origin and hyponatremia, it is easy to let anxiety drive indiscriminate testing. Mildly elevated sIL-2R is seen in plenty of inflammatory states and doesn't equal lymphoma; normal ACE doesn't rule out sarcoidosis but shouldn't keep it at the top of your list without other supporting evidence. Instead, rely on rigorous clinical scoring systems like ICBD and serial physical examinations. Watch for those evolving mucocutaneous signs—the genital ulcer that appears on day five might be more valuable than any panel you ordered on day one. By grounding ourselves in clinical phenomenology rather than reflexive algorithms, we avoid the trap of over-testing and arrive at the correct diagnosis faster, sparing our patients unnecessary procedures and potentially toxic treatments.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Beware Non-Specific Markers:** Mild elevations in sIL-2R or ACE can occur in benign inflammatory states; do not let these values alone drive a workup for malignancy or sarcoidosis when clinical features point elsewhere.
*   **Neuro-BD Can Be Concurrent:** Do not rule out Neuro-Behçet's disease because neurological symptoms are presenting simultaneously with mucocutaneous signs; while rare (~5%), it happens and requires a high index of suspicion using ICBD criteria scores (≥4 points).
*   **Normal MRI Does Not Exclude Neuro-BD:** Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal brainstem lesions; rely on CSF findings (lymphocytic pleocytosis) and clinical context when imaging is unrevealing early on.
*   **Hyponatremia Clues:** In patients with CNS inflammation unexplained by infection or tumor, consider SIADH secondary to neuro-inflammation (potentially IL-6 mediated); look for associated signs like persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis as indicators of severe electrolyte disturbance rather than primary pathology in those systems
