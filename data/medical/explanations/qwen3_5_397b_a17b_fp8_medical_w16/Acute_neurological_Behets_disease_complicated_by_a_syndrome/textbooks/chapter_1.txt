Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Challenges in Concurrent Systemic and Neurological Behçet's Disease

Behçet's disease (BD) is a chronic, multisystem vasculitis characterized classically by the triad of recurrent oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and uveitis. While mucocutaneous manifestations are the hallmark of the disease, neurological involvement, termed Neuro-Behçet's disease (Neuro-BD), occurs in approximately 10% to 20% of patients. A critical epidemiological feature of Neuro-BD is its typical latency; neurological symptoms usually manifest several years—often around four years—after the initial diagnosis of systemic BD. Consequently, the simultaneous onset of definitive systemic mucocutaneous lesions and acute neurological involvement, as seen in this case, represents a rare and diagnostically precarious clinical scenario. In fewer than 5% of cases do neurological symptoms appear at the very outset of the disease course, creating a situation where clinicians must synthesize non-specific constitutional complaints with emerging dermatological signs to avoid misdiagnosis.

The patient's presentation illustrated the complexity of identifying acute Neuro-BD amidst a backdrop of systemic inflammation. He arrived with a constellation of fever, headache, general malaise, and arthralgia, symptoms that broadly suggest infection, malignancy, or autoimmune crisis. Superimposed on these constitutional symptoms were specific mucocutaneous findings: oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk. Notably, the pathognomonic genital ulcer appeared only on day five of hospitalization, delaying the fulfillment of complete diagnostic criteria. This temporal gap underscores a significant challenge in real-time clinical assessment; without the immediate presence of genital lesions, the specificity for BD decreases, forcing reliance on a broader differential diagnosis that includes viral meningoencephalitis and other connective tissue diseases until the full phenotypic picture emerges.

Among the neurological complaints, persistent hiccups served as a subtle yet crucial localizing sign. While often dismissed as benign or gastrointestinal in origin, intractable hiccups can indicate irritation of the medullary centers in the brainstem or severe metabolic derangement. In this case, the hiccups likely represented a dual pathology: direct inflammatory involvement of the brainstem regions controlling the hiccup reflex arc and severe hyponatremia secondary to the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH). The coexistence of headache, vertigo, and hiccups in a patient with fever should raise suspicion for meningoencephalitis, even in the absence of classic meningeal signs like nuchal rigidity. Recognizing these non-specific neurological cues as potential markers of central nervous system (CNS) inflammation is essential for prompting timely lumbar puncture and neuro-imaging.

Diagnostic confidence in acute Neuro-BD is frequently hampered by normal neuroimaging findings during the early phases of the disease. Unlike chronic or parenchymal Neuro-BD, which typically displays characteristic T2-weighted hyperintensities in the brainstem or basal ganglia, acute meningoencephalic forms may present with a completely normal brain MRI. In this case, despite worsening headache and vertigo suggestive of CNS inflammation, initial imaging revealed no structural abnormalities involving the hypothalamus or brainstem. This radiological silence necessitates a heavy reliance on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis to confirm aseptic meningitis. The presence of lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein in the CSF, combined with negative infectious workups (viral PCR, bacterial cultures), becomes the primary evidence supporting a diagnosis of acute Neuro-BD when MRI is unrevealing. Clinicians must therefore resist ruling out Neuro-BD solely based on normal imaging results in the acute setting.

Definitive diagnosis in such complex presentations relies on the rigorous application of standardized scoring systems, specifically the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD). The ICBD assigns weighted points to various clinical features: two points each for oral and genital aphthosis, one point for skin lesions (such as folliculitis), one point for ocular lesions, one point for vascular manifestations, and one point for a positive pathergy test (though not performed here). A cumulative score of four or more indicates "definite" BD. In this patient's evolving course, the initial presence of oral ulcers (2 points) and skin lesions (1 point) yielded an indeterminate score. However, once genital ulcers manifested (adding 2 points), his total reached five points, confirming definite BD independent of HLA-B51 status or pathergy reactivity. This case reinforces that while HLA-B51 positivity supports susceptibility, it is neither sensitive nor specific enough for standalone diagnosis; instead, serial clinical evaluation to capture evolving mucocutaneous signs remains the gold standard for applying ICBD criteria effectively.
