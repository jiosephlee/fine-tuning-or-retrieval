Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 5: ## Clinical Pearls and Red Flags in Neuro-BD with SIADH

Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for acute neurological Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) when a patient presents with classic mucocutaneous findings—specifically oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and folliculitis-like rashes—accompanied by non-specific constitutional and neurological symptoms. While neuro-BD typically manifests years after the initial diagnosis, concurrent presentation is a critical red flag that should not be dismissed. Key warning signs include fever, severe headache, vertigo, and persistent hiccups. In the context of Behçet's disease, persistent hiccups are particularly noteworthy as they may signal severe hyponatremia secondary to central nervous system inflammation rather than a primary gastrointestinal issue. The emergence of these neurological complaints alongside active mucocutaneous lesions warrants an immediate workup for meningoencephalitis, even in the absence of focal deficits or altered mental status.

Unexplained hyponatremia in a patient with suspected or confirmed Behçet's disease serves as a potent diagnostic clue for concurrent syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) driven by CNS inflammation. When evaluating such patients, the presence of euvolemic hypotonic hyponatremia with inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated serum antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels confirms SIADH. It is imperative to recognize this electrolyte disturbance not merely as a metabolic complication but as a marker of active neuro-inflammation. Severe hyponatremia can precipitate secondary complications such as rhabdomyolysis, evidenced by elevated creatine kinase, and exacerbate neurological symptoms like confusion or seizures. Therefore, any new-onset hyponatremia in Behçet's disease should trigger an urgent evaluation for aseptic meningitis and neuro-BD, prompting cerebrospinal fluid analysis regardless of the severity of systemic symptoms.

A common diagnostic pitfall in early acute neuro-BD is relying too heavily on initial neuroimaging to rule out CNS involvement. As illustrated in this case, brain MRI may appear completely normal during the early phases of acute meningeal neuro-BD, lacking the characteristic parenchymal lesions often seen in chronic or advanced disease. The absence of radiological abnormalities, particularly in the brainstem or hypothalamus, does not exclude the diagnosis. Instead, the diagnosis rests on clinical correlation and cerebrospinal fluid findings indicative of aseptic meningitis, such as lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein with negative infectious cultures. Clinicians must avoid false reassurance from a normal scan and proceed with lumbar puncture when clinical suspicion remains high based on mucocutaneous and systemic features.

While high-dose corticosteroids remain the standard first-line therapy for acute neuro-BD, this case highlights a potential modification to the treatment algorithm for select presentations. In cases where acute neuro-BD presents primarily as aseptic meningitis without significant parenchymal involvement and is complicated by SIADH, colchicine monotherapy may offer a viable alternative, particularly when steroid use is undesirable or contraindicated. Colchicine's ability to inhibit neutrophil migration and suppress proinflammatory cytokines like IL-6 likely contributed to the resolution of both neurological symptoms and SIADH in this patient. Although evidence for colchicine as a standalone treatment for neuro-BD is limited compared to its efficacy in mucocutaneous disease, its success here suggests it should be considered in mild-to-moderate meningeal presentations, potentially sparing patients from steroid-related adverse effects while effectively managing endocrine disturbances.

Long-term management requires vigilant follow-up strategies focused on detecting delayed progression to parenchymal disease and monitoring endocrine stability. Patients discharged after an episode of acute meningeal neuro-BD should undergo serial neurological assessments and repeat imaging if new focal deficits develop, as parenchymal lesions can emerge weeks to months after the initial meningeal presentation. Additionally, given the association between CNS inflammation and dysregulated ADH secretion, clinicians should monitor serum sodium levels periodically post-discharge to ensure sustained resolution of SIADH. Education regarding the recurrence of mucocutaneous lesions is also essential, as their return may precede neurological relapse. Continued colchicine therapy appears effective for maintaining remission in such cases, though adherence and regular monitoring for toxicity remain paramount.
