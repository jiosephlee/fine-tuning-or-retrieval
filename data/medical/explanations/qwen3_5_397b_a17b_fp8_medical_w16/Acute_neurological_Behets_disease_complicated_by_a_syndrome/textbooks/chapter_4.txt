Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 4: ## Therapeutic Decision-Making: Colchicine Monotherapy vs. Standard Immunosuppression

The management of acute neurological Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) traditionally relies on aggressive immunosuppression, yet this case presents a notable deviation where colchicine monotherapy achieved complete remission. Standard guidelines for acute parenchymal neuro-BD, particularly when involving the brainstem or causing significant focal deficits, recommend high-dose intravenous corticosteroids as the first-line induction therapy, often followed by cytotoxic agents such as azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, or tumor necrosis factor inhibitors. This aggressive approach is necessitated by the risk of irreversible neuronal damage and the potentially fulminant course of parenchymal inflammation. However, the clinical phenotype in this patient was distinct; he presented with aseptic meningitis characterized by headache, fever, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pleocytosis, but crucially lacked parenchymal lesions on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and exhibited no focal neurological deficits. This "meningeal-predominant" presentation likely influenced the therapeutic trajectory, suggesting that the inflammatory burden was sufficient to cause systemic symptoms and SIADH but perhaps less destructive than classic parenchymal neuro-BD.

The decision to utilize colchicine instead of corticosteroids requires an understanding of both the drug's pharmacodynamics and the specific risks posed to this patient. Colchicine functions by binding to tubulin, thereby inhibiting microtubule polymerization. This mechanism disrupts neutrophil chemotaxis, degranulation, and superoxide production while also suppressing the inflammasome-mediated release of proinflammatory cytokines like interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and IL-6. Given that IL-6 is implicated in both the pathogenesis of neuro-BD and the dysregulation of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion in SIADH, colchicine offered a targeted anti-inflammatory mechanism that addressed the root cause of both the neurological and endocrine manifestations. Furthermore, avoiding corticosteroids was a prudent strategic choice given the patient's comorbid profile. He presented with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus (HbA1c 8.2%) and active infection concerns initially requiring empirical antibiotics. High-dose steroids would have exacerbated hyperglycemia, complicating metabolic management and increasing susceptibility to secondary infections, whereas colchicine provided a safer side-effect profile for this specific host.

Concurrent with the immunomodulatory strategy, the management of SIADH adhered to conservative physiological principles tailored to the severity of hyponatremia. The patient's serum sodium was 124 mmol/L; while symptomatic with hiccups and malaise, he did not exhibit life-threatening signs such as seizures or coma that would mandate immediate hypertonic saline administration. Consequently, the primary intervention was strict fluid restriction (<800 mL/day), aimed at creating a negative water balance to gradually raise serum sodium concentrations. Careful monitoring ensured that sodium correction proceeded at a safe rate to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome. The avoidance of vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans) reflected the expectation that treating the underlying CNS inflammation would resolve the inappropriate ADH secretion naturally. The normalization of sodium levels within two weeks coincided precisely with the resolution of fever and neurological symptoms following colchicine initiation, reinforcing the causal link between the inflammatory process and the endocrine disturbance.

Ultimately, this case challenges the rigid application of treatment algorithms for neuro-BD by demonstrating that colchicine monotherapy can be effective in selected cases. While systematic reviews generally deem colchicine ineffective for established parenchymal neuro-BD, its success here suggests a viable role in early-stage disease limited to meningeal inflammation without radiographic parenchymal involvement. The rapid resolution of symptoms implies that for patients presenting with mild-to-moderate acute neuro-BD—specifically those where corticosteroids pose significant metabolic or infectious risks—colchicine may serve as a potent alternative rather than merely an adjunctive therapy for mucocutaneous disease. This outcome underscores the necessity of phenotyping neuro-BD accurately; distinguishing between meningeal and parenchymal involvement is critical not only for prognosis but for tailoring immunosuppressive intensity to minimize iatrogenic harm while achieving disease control.
