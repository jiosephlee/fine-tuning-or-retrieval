Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiological Mechanisms Linking CNS Inflammation to SIADH

The development of Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH) in this patient serves as a critical clinical bridge between his systemic inflammation and acute neurological involvement. Diagnosing SIADH requires a systematic interpretation of fluid and electrolyte parameters within the context of the patient's volume status. In this case, the patient presented with euvolemic hyponatremia, evidenced by the absence of peripheral edema, normal blood pressure, and lack of tachycardia suggestive of hypovolemia. The hallmark biochemical profile included low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg) juxtaposed with inappropriately elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), indicating that the kidneys were failing to dilute urine despite systemic hypo-osmolality. Furthermore, the urinary sodium concentration was elevated (>20 mmol/L), reflecting natriuresis driven by volume expansion rather than conservation. Crucially, despite the low serum tonicity which should suppress secretion, antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels remained detectable and elevated (22.4 pg/mL). This dissociation confirms the "inappropriate" nature of ADH secretion, distinguishing SIADH from other causes of hyponatremia such as psychogenic polydipsia, where ADH is appropriately suppressed.

The pathophysiology of SIADH in acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) likely stems from the disruption of the hypothalamic-neurohypophyseal axis by inflammatory processes. While classic teaching associates SIADH with structural lesions in the hypothalamus or pituitary stalk, this patient's brain MRI revealed no focal abnormalities in these regions. This suggests that functional disruption, rather than gross anatomical destruction, drove the hormonal dysregulation. In neuro-inflammatory states, meningeal inflammation or parenchymal infiltration can alter the firing thresholds of osmoreceptors or stimulate non-osmotic release of ADH. In Behçet's disease specifically, the mechanism is increasingly attributed to a cytokine-mediated storm. Monocytes in BD patients are prone to spontaneous activation, leading to excessive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β). These cytokines are known potent stimulators of ADH release; IL-6, frequently elevated in the cerebrospinal fluid of acute neuro-BD patients, can cross the blood-brain barrier or act locally to trigger ectopic or dysregulated ADH secretion independent of osmotic stimuli. The patient's markedly elevated C-reactive protein serves as a surrogate marker for this intense IL-6-driven inflammatory state, providing a plausible biological link between his aseptic meningitis and endocrine failure.

Differentiating SIADH from other etiologies of hyponatremia in febrile patients with CNS symptoms is essential for appropriate management. The primary alternative consideration in neurologic injury is Cerebral Salt Wasting (CSW), which also presents with hyponatremia and high urinary sodium. However, CSW is characterized by true hypovolemia due to renal salt loss, necessitating volume replacement—a strategy that would be detrimental in SIADH. The clinical assessment of this patient confirmed euvolemia, effectively ruling out CSW and hypovolemic hyponatremia secondary to poor oral intake or gastrointestinal losses. Additionally, drug-induced SIADH was unlikely given the timeline; the hyponatremia preceded the administration of any potential offending agents like acyclovir or antibiotics, pointing instead to the disease process itself as the culprit. Recognizing this distinction prevented unnecessary fluid resuscitation and guided the decision toward fluid restriction as the cornerstone of therapy.

The severity of the hyponatremia in this case precipitated significant secondary complications, most notably rhabdomyolysis and persistent hiccups. Severe hyponatremia can induce cellular swelling and membrane instability in skeletal muscle, leading to leakage of intracellular enzymes such as creatine kinase (CK), which was markedly elevated at 1349 U/L in this patient. While rhabdomyolysis is often associated with trauma or statin use, it is a recognized but under-appreciated consequence of profound electrolyte disturbance. Similarly, persistent hiccups served as a non-specific but valuable neurological clue pointing toward irritation of the medullary centers or metabolic derangement affecting the phrenic nerve reflex arc. The resolution of both the rhabdomyolysis and hiccups following the normalization of serum sodium via fluid restriction and colchicine therapy underscores that these were downstream effects of the SIADH rather than primary muscular or neuromuscular pathologies. This cascade highlights how untreated SIADH in neuro-BD can extend morbidity beyond neurological symptoms to include systemic metabolic collapse.
