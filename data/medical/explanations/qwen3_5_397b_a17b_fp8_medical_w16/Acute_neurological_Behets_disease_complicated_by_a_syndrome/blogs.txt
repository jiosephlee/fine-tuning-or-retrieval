Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# When the MRI is Normal: Diagnosing Acute Neuro-Behçet's Without Parenchymal Lesions

We love a good lesion hunt. In neurology and emergency medicine, there is a comforting rhythm to the workup of acute meningoencephalitis: fever and headache hit the door, we get the lumbar puncture showing lymphocytic pleocytosis, and then we wait for the MRI to tell us where the fire is burning. If it's Herpes Simplex, we expect temporal lobe enhancement. If it's classic Neuro-Behçet's (NBD), the textbooks scream at us to look for brainstem or basal ganglia lesions. But what happens when the MRI is pristine? Do we breathe a sigh of relief and go home? Not so fast.

A fascinating case report by Kakehi et al. forces us to confront a diagnostic blind spot: acute Neuro-Behçet's disease presenting as aseptic meningitis with a completely normal brain MRI. The patient, a man in his 60s, rolled into the ED with fever, headache, persistent hiccups, and hyponatremia. He had oral aphthae and a folliculitis-like rash, but no genital ulcers *yet*. His workup was a masterclass in exclusion—viral PCRs negative, bacterial cultures sterile, autoimmune panels largely unrevealing. The brain MRI? Unremarkable. No hypothalamic signal change, no brainstem edema. Yet, this was definite acute Neuro-Behçet's, complicated by SIADH, and it resolved beautifully on colchicine monotherapy without a single dose of steroids. This case shatters the dogma that you need parenchymal lesions to diagnose acute NBD and reminds us that sometimes the most critical imaging isn't radiological—it's dermatological.

The trap here is assuming that "Neuro-Behçet's" equals "parenchymal brainstem syndrome." While chronic or subacute NBD often presents with those classic T2 hyperintensities in the midbrain or pons, acute NBD can manifest purely as meningoencephalitis. In this phenotype, the inflammation is leptomeningeal rather than parenchymal, leaving the MRI looking deceptively normal while the CSF screams otherwise. Our patient had elevated opening pressure, high protein, and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis—the hallmark of aseptic meningitis. Without the visual cue of a glowing brainstem on FLAIR sequences, it is incredibly easy to dismiss Behçet's in favor of a viral etiology or idiopathic inflammation. The kicker in this case was the evolution of symptoms; the genital ulcer appeared days after admission, sealing the diagnosis based on clinical criteria (oral ulcers + genital ulcers + skin lesions) even though the HLA-B51 status was merely supportive and not definitive.

What makes this case truly educational is the electrochemical chaos happening alongside the neurological symptoms: Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH). The patient presented with severe hyponatremia (124 mmol/L), low serum osmolality, and inappropriately concentrated urine. While SIADH is a known companion to CNS inflammation, its presence here offers a mechanistic clue. Behçet's is driven by aberrant monocyte activation and cytokine storms, particularly Interleukin-6 (IL-6). IL-6 is a potent stimulator of ADH release. Even without visible hypothalamic lesions on MRI, the systemic and meningeal inflammatory surge likely triggered this dysregulation. Furthermore, don't ignore the "weird" symptoms like persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK); in the context of hyponatremia and CNS irritation, these are red flags pointing toward significant electrolyte disturbance secondary to neuro-inflammation rather than primary muscle pathology.

Perhaps the most controversial yet successful aspect of this management was the treatment choice. The standard of care for acute parenchymal NBD is high-dose corticosteroids followed by heavy-hitting immunosuppressants like azathioprine or cyclophosphamide. Colchicine is typically reserved for mucocutaneous disease and joint pain, with literature suggesting it doesn't cross into CNS efficacy effectively. Yet, this patient received colchicine alone—and won. Within 24 hours of starting colchicine, his fever broke and appetite returned. Over two weeks, his sodium normalized, his headaches vanished, and his mucocutaneous lesions healed. While we must acknowledge that spontaneous resolution can occur in meningoencephalitic forms of NBD, the rapidity of response suggests colchicine's anti-neutrophil effects may have been sufficient to quell this specific inflammatory cascade. It challenges us to reconsider if steroid-sparing approaches might have a niche in select cases of acute meningoencephalitic NBD where parenchymal involvement is absent.

This case serves as a potent reminder that clinical acumen still trumps advanced imaging. If you see a patient with aseptic meningitis, unexplained hyponatremia, and *any* hint of mucocutaneous weirdness—especially oral ulcers—you cannot rule out Behçet's just because the MRI looks clean. You have to look at the whole patient, wait for those elusive genital ulcers to declare themselves if they haven't already, and recognize that normal imaging does not equal benign disease.

### Take-Home Points

*   **Normal MRI Does Not Rule Out Acute Neuro-Behçet's:** Acute NBD can present as pure meningoencephalitis with aseptic CSF findings despite lacking characteristic brainstem or basal ganglia lesions on imaging.
*   **Look Beyond the Head:** In patients with suspected CNS inflammation actively search for mucocutaneous signs (oral aphthae genital ulcers folliculitis-like rashes); these clinical features carry more diagnostic weight than HLA-B51 testing or early imaging results according to International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD).
*   **Hyponatremia as a Clue:** SIADH is a recognized complication of acute neuro-inflammation in Behçet's potentially mediated by IL-6 driven ADH release; investigate unexplained hyponatremia in febrile patients with headache as part of your neuro-workup rather than attributing it solely to volume depletion or drugs
*   **Don't Dismiss "Minor" Symptoms:** Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be indirect manifestations of severe hyponatremia secondary to CNS inflammation serving as valuable clinical anchors when specific pathogens remain elusive
*   **Colchicine May Have a Role:** While corticosteroids remain first-line for parenchymal NBD colchicine monotherapy demonstrated rapid resolution of both systemic neurological and endocrine manifestations in this case suggesting potential utility in meningoencephalitic phenotypes though further study is needed



# Beyond the Usual Suspects: When Neuro-Behçet's Masquerades as SIADH

We've all been there. You're staring at a febrile patient with a headache and a sodium level of 124 mmol/L. Your mind immediately runs through the "SIADH Big Three": Is there a small cell lung cancer hiding on the CXR? Did they start a new SSRI or carbamazepine? Is this viral meningitis? We order the CTs, the lumbar punctures, and the paraneoplastic panels, bracing ourselves for a malignancy workup that could drag on for weeks.

But what if the driver isn't a tumor, a drug, or a standard bug? What if the hyponatremia is the direct downstream effect of sterile CNS inflammation itself?

A fascinating case report by Kakehi et al. forces us to expand our differential for SIADH beyond the usual suspects. They describe a man in his 60s who presented with fever, headache, persistent hiccups, and profound hyponatremia. He wasn't just sick; he was inflamed. With oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and eventually a painful genital ulcer, he met the criteria for Behçet's disease (BD). But here is the kicker: he had no prior history of BD. He presented with *acute* neurological Behçet's (neuro-BD) and SIADH simultaneously. Even more surprisingly, his brain MRI was completely normal, and he recovered fully on colchicine monotherapy without ever touching a dose of steroids.

This case is a masterclass in connecting disparate dots—mucocutaneous lesions, aseptic meningitis, and electrolyte chaos—to avoid a rabbit hole of unnecessary testing and arrive at a targeted treatment.

### The Diagnostic Trap: Normal Imaging and Missing Ulcers

One of the most treacherous aspects of this case was the initial presentation. The patient arrived with fever, headache, and hyponatremia. In the ED, our instinct is to rule out infection first. His CSF showed lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, classic for aseptic meningitis, but cultures and viral PCRs were negative.

The real curveball was the imaging. We often rely on MRI to confirm neuro-BD, looking for those characteristic brainstem or basal ganglia lesions. In this patient, the MRI was unremarkable. It's easy to dismiss neuro-inflammation when the scan is clean, but acute neuro-BD can present as pure meningoencephalitis without parenchymal lesions visible on early imaging. Furthermore, the pathognomonic genital ulcer didn't appear until day 5 of admission. Without that specific finding early on, the diagnosis of Behçet's hangs in limbo, forcing you to keep malignancy and obscure infections at the top of your list.

The key takeaway here is that a normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD, especially when paired with compatible mucocutaneous findings and CSF evidence of inflammation. If you wait for the "classic" radiological signs or the full triad of symptoms before considering this diagnosis, you may delay critical therapy or subject the patient to invasive cancer screenings they don't need.

### Connecting the Dots: IL-6, Inflammation, and ADH Dysregulation

So, how does Behçet's cause SIADH? The link appears to be cytokine-mediated chaos. Behçet's is driven by aberrant monocyte activation and massive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly Interleukin-6 (IL-6). We know from other neuro-inflammatory conditions that IL-6 can directly stimulate the release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) from the hypothalamus or posterior pituitary.

In this case, while IL-6 levels weren't explicitly measured in the CSF, the sky-high CRP (180 mg/L) serves as a reliable proxy for that intense inflammatory storm. The hypothesis is straightforward: The CNS inflammation triggered an IL-6 surge $\rightarrow$ IL-6 stimulated inappropriate ADH secretion $\rightarrow$ Water retention and dilutional hyponatremia ensued.

This mechanism explains why treating the underlying inflammation resolved the electrolyte disturbance so rapidly once colchicine was started. It also highlights a subtle clinical pearl mentioned in the report: persistent hiccups. While often dismissed as benign irritation, intractable hiccups in this context were likely a sign of severe hyponatremia and CNS irritation acting as an early warning flag before the sodium crashed further or before other focal deficits appeared. Additionally, don't miss the rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK) seen in this patient; severe hyponatremia can cause muscle breakdown independent of trauma or statins, another easily overlooked complication in these complex presentations.

### Treatment Paradigm Shift: Colchicine Monotherapy?

Perhaps the most provocative element of this case is the treatment outcome. Standard teaching dictates that acute neuro-BD requires heavy artillery: high-dose intravenous corticosteroids followed by potent immunosuppressants like azathioprine or cyclophosphamide. Colchicine is typically reserved for mucocutaneous symptoms and joint pain; systematic reviews have historically labeled it ineffective for neurological involvement.

Yet, this patient received *only* colchicine (plus topical care) and saw resolution of fever, neurological symptoms, mucocutaneous lesions, *and* normalization of sodium within two weeks. No steroids were used throughout his month-long admission or his four-year follow-up without recurrence.

Does this mean we should swap prednisone for colchicine in every case of neuro-BD? Absolutely not. This is a single case report where spontaneous resolution cannot be entirely ruled out as a contributing factor alongside anti-inflammatory effects via neutrophil inhibition (colchicine blocks IL-1β and IL-6 production). However, it challenges our dogma that colchicine has zero role in neurological disease control when steroid use might be risky—for instance!



# The Hiccuping Hyponatremic Patient: When Persistent Hiccups Signal a Neuro-Inflammatory Storm

We've all seen the patient in the ED with "viral syndrome." They come in with fever, malaise, maybe some joint pain, and a laundry list of vague complaints. It's easy to anchor on dehydration or a self-limiting bug, especially when the initial workup doesn't scream catastrophe. But what if persistent hiccups and an unexpectedly high creatine kinase (CK) are actually thecanary in the coal mine for a severe electrolyte emergency driven by central nervous system inflammation?

A recent case report throws a fascinating wrench into our standard diagnostic algorithms. An Asian man in his 60s presented with three days of fever, headache, general malaise, and—crucially—persistent hiccups. He also complained of shoulder and ankle arthralgia. On exam, he was tachycardic and febrile, with oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes scattered across his face and trunk. No neck stiffness, no focal deficits. Just a sick guy with a rash and the hiccups that wouldn't quit.

The labs told a different story than "simple viral illness." His sodium was critically low at 124 mmol/L. But this wasn't just any hyponatremia; it was hypotonic hyponatremia with inappropriately concentrated urine (900 mOsm/kg) and high urine sodium, classic for the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH). Even more striking was his CK, which skyrocketed to 1349 U/L, indicating rhabdomyolysis. While we often attribute rhabdo to trauma, seizures, or statins, here it appeared alongside profound hyponatremia and relentless hiccups.

The diagnostic journey was tricky. Infectious meningitis was the primary fear given the fever and headache, but CSF analysis revealed only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis with negative cultures and viral PCRs. MRI of the brain was surprisingly normal, showing no parenchymal lesions or hypothalamic abnormalities that might explain the SIADH structurally. So, where was the fire?

The answer lay in the skin. By day five, the patient developed a painful genital ulcer. Combined with the recurrent oral aphthae and characteristic skin lesions, he met the criteria for definite Behçet's disease (BD). With aseptic meningitis confirmed via LP and no other explanation for his CNS symptoms, he was diagnosed with acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD). This is a rare beast; neurological involvement usually happens years after the initial BD diagnosis, not concurrently at presentation. Furthermore, while SIADH is known in BD patients on immunosuppressants like cyclophosphamide, this patient had never been treated. His SIADH—and consequently his severe hyponatremia—was driven purely by the inflammatory storm of acute neuro-BD.

Here is where the physiology gets elegant and terrifyingly interconnected. The persistent hiccups weren't just annoying; they were likely a sign of central irritation from the meningoencephalitis and/or the electrolyte derangement affecting the hiccup reflex arc in the brainstem. Similarly, while rhabdomyolysis is often mechanical or toxic, severe hyponatremia itself can induce muscle cell swelling and necrosis. In this case, the triad of hiccups, rhabdomyolysis, and hyponatremia wasn't a coincidence of three separate problems; it was a unified presentation of acute neuro-inflammation causing SIADH.

The treatment course challenges our dogma even further. Standard teaching for acute neuro-BD dictates high-dose corticosteroids and often aggressive immunosuppression. Yet, this patient was started on colchicine monotherapy. Within 24 hours, his fever broke and appetite returned. Over two weeks, his neurological symptoms vanished, his skin healed, and his sodium normalized as his ADH levels plummeted. He avoided steroids entirely and remained relapse-free for four years on colchicine alone. While we must be cautious not to overgeneralize from a single case—spontaneous resolution of meningoencephalitic neuro-BD can occur—the rapid temporal association suggests colchicine's anti-inflammatory effects (specifically IL-1β and IL-6 suppression) may have short-circuited the cytokine drive behind both the neuro-BD and the SIADH.

This case serves as a potent reminder that "nonspecific" symptoms are often highly specific if viewed through the right lens. Persistent hiccups in a febrile patient should trigger thoughts of CNS pathology or metabolic disturbance before we dismiss them as gastric reflux. Unexplained rhabdomyolysis in the absence of trauma demands a hard look at electrolytes and inflammatory states. And when you find SIADH in a patient with mucocutaneous ulcers and headache, think beyond infection: acute neuro-Behçet's might be pulling the strings.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Persistent hiccups can be a sentinel sign of CNS pathology:** Intractable hiccups in the setting of fever or altered mental status should raise suspicion for brainstem irritation, meningoencephalitis, or severe metabolic disturbances like profound hyponatremia.
*   **Hyponatremia can cause rhabdomyolysis:** Do not assume trauma or toxins are the sole causes of elevated CK; severe hypotonic hyponatremia can lead to muscle cell lysis independently.
*   **Acute Neuro-Behçet's can present at onset:** Neurological involvement does not always follow years of mucocutaneous disease; concurrent presentation with aseptic meningitis is possible even with a normal initial brain MRI.
*   **SIADH may be inflammatory:** In neuro-Behçet's disease (and potentially other neuro-inflammatory conditions), SIADH can result directly from cytokine-mediated dysregulation (e.g., IL-6) rather than structural hypothalamic damage or medications.
*   **Colchicine monotherapy is a consideration:** While steroids remain first-line for acute neuro-Behçet's emerging evidence suggests colchicine alone may resolve mild meningoencephalitic presentations and associated paraneoplastic-style syndromes like SIADH in select cases.



# Colchicine Monotherapy for Acute Neuro-Behçet's: Breaking the Steroid Reflex?

When you see a patient with fever, headache, oral ulcers, and profound hyponatremia, your mind likely races through the usual suspects: viral meningoencephalitis, bacterial meningitis, or perhaps a paraneoplastic syndrome. You probably don't immediately jump to "acute Neuro-Behçet's complicated by SIADH," especially if the brain MRI is completely normal. Even more counter-intuitive? Watching that entire clinical picture resolve with colchicine *alone*, bypassing the high-dose corticosteroids that have become the reflex standard of care for neurological Behçet's.

We tend to operate on autopilot when it comes to acute Neuro-Behçet's disease (NBD). The dogma is clear: parenchymal involvement equals steroids, often plus heavy immunosuppression like cyclophosphamide or azathioprine. But a fascinating case report by Kakehi et al. forces us to pause and question whether we are over-treating a specific subset of these patients. They describe a man in his 60s who presented with the classic triad of mucocutaneous Behçet's (oral aphthae, genital ulcers, folliculitis-like rash) alongside acute neurological symptoms and severe hyponatremia. Despite meeting the criteria for definite Behçet's and having CSF evidence of aseptic meningitis, his MRI was silent. Even more strikingly, he didn't just survive without steroids; he thrived on colchicine monotherapy, with resolution of his neurological deficits, skin lesions, and SIADH within weeks.

The diagnostic journey here is a masterclass in ruling out the mimics before landing on the zebra. Our patient arrived febrile and confused with sodium levels dropping to 124 mmol/L. The workup for infection was exhaustive and negative—no HSV, no VZV, no bacteria, no TB. Malignancy was ruled out despite a mildly elevated sIL-2R, and sarcoidosis didn't fit the lab profile. The clincher was the emergence of a painful genital ulcer on day five, pushing his International Criteria for Behçet's Disease score over the threshold. But here is where it gets tricky: acute NBD typically presents years after the initial diagnosis, not concurrently. Furthermore, while parenchymal NBD usually lights up the brainstem or basal ganglia on MRI, this patient had a scan that looked essentially normal. This is a critical reminder that acute NBD can present as isolated meningoencephalitis with sterile CSF pleocytosis and zero radiographic footprint. If you wait for an MRI lesion to confirm Neuro-Behçet's in a patient with active mucocutaneous disease and meningitis, you might miss the window entirely.

The elephant in the room—and the reason this case breaks the mold—is the management strategy. Standard guidelines scream for high-dose IV methylprednisolone for acute NBD to prevent irreversible neuronal damage. Colchicine is generally relegated to managing mucocutaneous flares and preventing thrombosis; systematic reviews have historically labeled it ineffective for neurological involvement. Yet, in this case, once infectious causes were excluded, the team initiated colchicine alone. The result? Fever broke within 24 hours. Vertigo, hiccups (a subtle but real sign of his hyponatremia/meningeal irritation), and malaise resolved over two weeks. His sodium normalized as his ADH levels plummeted, confirming the SIADH was driven by the neuro-inflammation itself, likely mediated by IL-6 spikes common in Behçet's pathophysiology. Four years later, he remains relapse-free without ever touching a steroid pulse dose.

Does this mean we should throw away our steroid protocols tomorrow? Absolutely not. The distinction between parenchymal and non-parenchymal (vascular/meningeal) NBD remains vital. Parenchymal disease involves direct infiltration of the brain tissue and carries a high risk of permanent deficit; withholding steroids there is dangerous gambling. However, this case suggests that in early-stage acute NBD presenting primarily as aseptic meningitis without focal parenchymal lesions on imaging—and particularly when steroid toxicity is a major concern—colchicine might be more than just an adjunct. Its mechanism involves inhibiting neutrophil chemotaxis and suppressing IL-1β and IL-6 production, potentially dampening the cytokine storm driving both the meningeal inflammation and the ectopic ADH secretion. While we must remain cautious about attributing success solely to colchicine given the possibility of spontaneous remission in mild meningeal cases, ignoring this signal could deny us a valuable steroid-sparing tool in resource-limited settings or for patients with contraindications to high-dose steroids.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Concurrent Diagnosis is Possible:** Do not rule out acute Neuro-Behçet's simply because mucocutaneous symptoms and neurological signs appear simultaneously; while delayed onset is typical, concurrent presentation occurs and requires high vigilance.
*   **Normal MRI Does Not Exclude NBD:** Acute Neuro-Behçet's can present as aseptic meningitis with significant CSF pleocytosis and protein elevation despite a completely normal brain MRI; do not wait for parenchymal lesions to initiate treatment if clinical suspicion is high.
*   **SIADH as a Clue:** Unexplained hyponatremia (SIADH) in a patient with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous ulcers may be the harbinger of Neuro-Behçet's; look for persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis as downstream effects of severe hyponatremia in this context.
*   **Re-evaluating Colchicine:** While corticosteroids remain first-line for parenchymal NBD, colchicine monotherapy may be a viable consideration for acute non-parenchymal presentations (meningoencephalitis without focal deficits), offering potential resolution of neurological and endocrine manifestations via IL-6 suppression.
*   **Diagnostic Discipline:** Avoid indiscriminate testing for malignancy or other autoimmune conditions once clinical criteria for Behçet's are met; focus on excluding infection rapidly to prevent treatment delays.



# The Diagnostic Trap: Avoiding the 'Shotgun Approach' in Fever of Unknown Origin with Hyponatremia

We've all been there. A patient rolls into the ED with fever, headache, altered mental status, and a nasty case of hyponatremia. The differential diagnosis list instantly stretches across the whiteboard: meningitis, encephalitis, malignancy, autoimmune vasculitis, sarcoidosis. The temptation to fire off a "shotgun" panel—ACE levels, soluble interleukin-2 receptors (sIL-2R), extensive viral PCRs, and whole-body imaging—is overwhelming. We want answers *now*. But as a fascinating recent case report illustrates, casting too wide a net can distract us from the clinical clues staring us right in the face, specifically when classic mucocutaneous signs are evolving alongside neurological decline.

Consider the case of a man in his 60s presenting with three days of fever, headache, persistent hiccups, and profound hyponatremia (Na 124 mmol/L). On arrival, he had oral aphthae and a folliculitis-like rash. His workup confirmed Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH) with low serum osmolality and inappropriately concentrated urine. Initially, the team did exactly what many of us would do: they suspected infection or malignancy. They ran the gamut. Soluble IL-2R came back mildly elevated. Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) was checked to rule out neurosarcoidosis. Extensive infectious panels for HSV, VZV, TB, and bacteria were negative. Brain MRI? Completely normal. For several days, the diagnosis remained elusive because the team was chasing non-specific markers and waiting for a smoking gun on imaging that never appeared.

The breakthrough didn't come from a new lab test; it came from watching the patient evolve. On day five, a painful genital ulcer developed. Suddenly, the puzzle pieces snapped together. The combination of recurrent oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and characteristic skin lesions met the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD) with a score of 5 (definite diagnosis requires ≥4). The neurological symptoms and SIADH were not separate entities or side effects of hidden malignancy; they were manifestations of acute Neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD). This case serves as a potent reminder that while ruling out life threats is mandatory, we must resist letting non-specific inflammatory markers like mildly elevated sIL-2R drive our diagnostic bus into a ditch of unnecessary testing for lymphoma or sarcoidosis when the clinical phenotype screams Behçet's.

What makes this case particularly instructive is the presentation of acute neuro-BD itself. Conventionally teaches us that neurological involvement in Behçet's usually happens years after the initial diagnosis of mucocutaneous disease. Concurrent presentation is rare, occurring in less than 5% of cases. Furthermore, we often expect parenchymal brainstem lesions on MRI in neuro-BD. Yet, this patient had a pristine MRI and presented with aseptic meningitis and SIADH as the primary neurological features. The SIADH was likely driven by CNS inflammation and cytokine storms (specifically IL-6), leading to dysregulated ADH secretion. The persistent hiccups and even rhabdomyolysis (indicated by elevated CK) were downstream effects of the severe hyponatremia and neuro-inflammation, red herrings that could easily send you down a GI or muscular dystrophy rabbit hole if you aren't thinking about electrolytes first.

Perhaps the most surprising aspect of this case was the management outcome. Standard dogma dictates that acute neuro-BD requires heavy artillery: high-dose corticosteroids and often cyclophosphamide or azathioprine. Colchicine is typically reserved for mucocutaneous symptoms and is considered ineffective for parenchymal neurological disease. However, this patient was treated with colchicine monotherapy plus conservative fluid restriction for the SIADH. No steroids. No immunosuppressants. And he got better rapidly. His fever broke within 24 hours of starting colchicine; his sodium normalized within two weeks; his neurological symptoms resolved completely. While we must be cautious not to generalize this to every case of severe neuro-BD—and spontaneous resolution cannot be entirely ruled out—it challenges our reflexive reach for steroids in every inflammatory CNS condition and highlights colchicine's potential role in early-stage meningoencephalitic presentations where steroid toxicity is a concern (such as in his uncontrolled diabetes).

The lesson here isn't just about Behçet's disease; it's about diagnostic discipline. When faced with fever of unknown origin and hyponatremia, it is easy to let anxiety drive indiscriminate testing. Mildly elevated sIL-2R is seen in plenty of inflammatory states and doesn't equal lymphoma; normal ACE doesn't rule out sarcoidosis but shouldn't keep it at the top of your list without other supporting evidence. Instead, rely on rigorous clinical scoring systems like ICBD and serial physical examinations. Watch for those evolving mucocutaneous signs—the genital ulcer that appears on day five might be more valuable than any panel you ordered on day one. By grounding ourselves in clinical phenomenology rather than reflexive algorithms, we avoid the trap of over-testing and arrive at the correct diagnosis faster, sparing our patients unnecessary procedures and potentially toxic treatments.

### Key Take-Home Points

*   **Beware Non-Specific Markers:** Mild elevations in sIL-2R or ACE can occur in benign inflammatory states; do not let these values alone drive a workup for malignancy or sarcoidosis when clinical features point elsewhere.
*   **Neuro-BD Can Be Concurrent:** Do not rule out Neuro-Behçet's disease because neurological symptoms are presenting simultaneously with mucocutaneous signs; while rare (~5%), it happens and requires a high index of suspicion using ICBD criteria scores (≥4 points).
*   **Normal MRI Does Not Exclude Neuro-BD:** Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal brainstem lesions; rely on CSF findings (lymphocytic pleocytosis) and clinical context when imaging is unrevealing early on.
*   **Hyponatremia Clues:** In patients with CNS inflammation unexplained by infection or tumor, consider SIADH secondary to neuro-inflammation (potentially IL-6 mediated); look for associated signs like persistent hiccups or rhabdomyolysis as indicators of severe electrolyte disturbance rather than primary pathology in those systems
