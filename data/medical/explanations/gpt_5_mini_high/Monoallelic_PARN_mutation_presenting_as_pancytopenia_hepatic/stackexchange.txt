Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Morning Report] In a woman in her late 20s with pancytopenia, premature greying, hepatosplenomegaly, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, and no classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita, what unifying inherited condition should you suspect?

Answer: Suspect a telomere biology disorder (TBD) — the dyskeratosis congenita (DC) spectrum — most plausibly due to a PARN‑related telomere maintenance defect.

Why: The patient’s constellation—early hair greying and other premature‑ageing features, pancytopenia with ineffective erythropoiesis, hepatic cirrhosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis—reflects failure of high‑turnover tissues, which is the hallmark of telomere shortening. In TBDs, critically short telomeres drive stem‑cell exhaustion and fibrotic degeneration of lung and liver; classical mucocutaneous findings of DC may be absent in adults because of variable penetrance and age‑dependent expression.

Genetic link in this case: PARN is an exoribonuclease required for 3′ maturation of the telomerase RNA (TERC); haploinsufficiency impairs telomerase and causes telomere attrition. The patient’s clinical exome identified a monoallelic PARN missense change (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) in the conserved R3H domain that in silico tools predict to be deleterious. Although reported as a Variant of Uncertain Significance, the genotype fits the phenotype — monoallelic PARN variants are increasingly recognized causes of adult‑onset pulmonary fibrosis and bone‑marrow/organ failure within the TBD spectrum.

Diagnostic approach: In any young adult with unexplained marrow failure ± pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, test telomere length (flow‑FISH is the standard), and obtain targeted next‑generation sequencing for telomere‑related genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, etc.). In this patient the exome result supports a TBD; formal telomere length testing and family cascade testing would strengthen the diagnosis and inform donor selection if transplant is considered.

Management principles: Treat reversible contributors (the team corrected severe B12 deficiency and provided supportive transfusions). Long‑term care involves surveillance of marrow, lung and liver function, genetic counselling, and avoidance of exposures that accelerate telomere loss. Progressive marrow failure may require hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation, but requires specialist protocols (reduced‑intensity conditioning, careful donor selection) because of higher organ‑toxicity risk in TBD. Emerging therapies (androgens such as danazol/nandrolone) have shown telomere‑lengthening signals but remain investigational.

Clinical pearl: In a young adult with multisystem disease of high‑turnover tissues (pancytopenia, early greying, pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis), think telomere biology disorders even in the absence of the classic DC mucocutaneous triad — PARN‑associated TBD is a unifying diagnosis compatible with this case.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the role of PARN in telomere biology, and how do monoallelic versus biallelic pathogenic PARN variants most commonly present clinically?

Answer (short): PARN is a nuclear deadenylase required for 3′‑end maturation and stabilization of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Loss of PARN function lowers cellular TERC, reduces telomerase activity and accelerates telomere shortening. Biallelic pathogenic PARN variants produce early, severe telomere‑biology disorders (autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita/Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson phenotypes with the mucocutaneous triad and profound childhood marrow failure), whereas monoallelic variants most often present in adults with telomere‑related fibrotic disease (idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis), with variable bone‑marrow insufficiency and sometimes hepatic fibrosis—frequently without the classic mucocutaneous stigmata.

Why: PARN’s deadenylase activity removes post‑transcriptionally acquired oligo(A) tails from nuclear RNAs; this step is required for proper 3′‑end maturation of TERC and for preventing exosome‑mediated degradation of the telomerase RNA. Reduced TERC → reduced telomerase activity → progressive telomere attrition. Rapidly renewing tissues (bone marrow, lung epithelium, liver, skin, hair) are therefore selectively vulnerable.

Clinical distinction and application to the case: complete loss of PARN function on both alleles (biallelic) causes severe, early‑onset TBDs (DKCB6, reported Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson cases) with profound telomere shortening and the classical DC features. Heterozygous (monoallelic) pathogenic variants act largely by haploinsufficiency and present later and variably—most commonly as pulmonary fibrosis and/or adult‑onset bone‑marrow failure (PFBMFT4), and can include hepatic fibrosis, osteoporosis and premature greying. The case illustrates this spectrum: a woman in her late 20s with a monoallelic PARN missense change (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, in the R3H domain, predicted damaging but reported as a VUS) who manifested pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis despite lacking the full mucocutaneous triad. Her severe B12 deficiency likely unmasked or worsened marrow failure in the setting of an underlying telomere‑repair defect.

Diagnostic and management implications (brief): when a TBD is suspected, measure telomere length (flow‑FISH) and pursue targeted genetic sequencing (PARN among known TBD genes). Management is surveillance and organ‑directed care; progressive marrow failure may require hematopoietic stem‑cell transplant (with specialized donor selection) and genetic counselling. Experimental/limited data exist for androgens to increase telomere length, but long‑term benefits are unproven.

Clinical pearl: in young adults with unexplained pulmonary and/or hepatic fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis or cytopenias—even without classic mucocutaneous findings—think telomere biology disorder and test telomere length and telomere‑related genes (including PARN).

### [Differential Diagnosis] A young adult has pancytopenia with very low reticulocyte count, hypersegmented neutrophils, dimorphic red cells with basophilic stippling and Cabot rings, elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin, erythroid hyperplasia on marrow, and a positive IgG direct antiglobulin test. Which features favor ineffective erythropoiesis from severe vitamin B12 deficiency over autoimmune hemolytic anemia?

Short answer
The pattern in this case—especially a very low reticulocyte count despite biochemical evidence of red‑cell destruction, pancytopenia, megaloblastic changes on the smear, marrow erythroid hyperplasia with increased iron stores, and clinical features of B12 deficiency—favors ineffective erythropoiesis from severe vitamin B12 deficiency (intramedullary destruction) rather than peripheral autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA).

Why (clinical reasoning tied to the case)
- Inadequate reticulocyte response: The reticulocyte count was very low (0.39%). In AIHA the marrow is usually intact and responds with reticulocytosis; an inappropriately low reticulocyte count points to marrow failure or intramedullary destruction (ineffective erythropoiesis) as seen in severe B12 deficiency.  
- Smear morphology of dyserythropoiesis: Hypersegmented neutrophils, macro‑ovalocytes/dimorphic red cells, Cabot rings and basophilic stippling are classic for megaloblastic/ineffective erythropoiesis from defective DNA synthesis (B12/folate deficiency) and are not features of warm AIHA (which more typically shows spherocytes and polychromasia).  
- Pancytopenia (trilineage involvement): The patient had pancytopenia and marrow showed trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia. Megaloblastic processes commonly suppress all three cell lines; AIHA generally produces isolated red‑cell loss (unless there is an associated immune process such as Evans syndrome).  
- Bone‑marrow and iron studies: Erythroid hyperplasia with increased marrow iron and the elevated serum iron/ferritin are typical of ineffective erythropoiesis (iron is released but not incorporated into mature RBCs). In AIHA marrow is hypercellular but iron is typically available for increased erythropoiesis and ferritin is not characteristically elevated from failure to utilize iron.  
- Clinical context and response: The patient had clinical signs of B12 deficiency (atrophic tongue/lingual papillae, neuropathy) and reduced parietal cells on biopsy. She improved after parenteral cyanocobalamin—supporting B12 deficiency as the dominant process.

How to interpret the positive DAT in this setting
- A positive IgG DAT (2+) does not by itself establish AIHA. DAT positivity can be incidental or non‑pathogenic and must be interpreted with the reticulocyte response, smear, and marrow findings. In this case the negative indirect test and the constellation above argue that the DAT is either not the primary cause of hemolysis or represents low‑titer/non‑destructive antibodies.

Pathophysiologic summary (one line)
- Severe B12 deficiency → impaired DNA synthesis → nuclear‑cytoplasmic asynchrony → intramedullary apoptosis of erythroid precursors → elevated LDH/indirect bilirubin with an inappropriately low reticulocyte count and the megaloblastic smear/marrow picture (ineffective erythropoiesis), which contrasts with the peripheral destruction and brisk reticulocytosis of AIHA.

Clinical pearl
When laboratory markers suggest hemolysis but the reticulocyte index is low and the smear shows megaloblastic changes (hypersegmented neutrophils, Cabot rings), treat and reassess for B12/folate deficiency before labeling the process as immune hemolysis or commencing immunosuppression.

### [Morning Report] What two complementary tests are most useful to establish a telomere biology disorder, and what telomere-length threshold relative to age-matched controls is typically considered diagnostic?

Short answer
- Two complementary tests: (1) genetic testing for telomere‑related genes (targeted NGS panel / clinical exome or genome) and (2) quantitative telomere‑length measurement by flow cytometry–FISH (flow‑FISH) on peripheral blood leukocyte subsets (especially CD3+ lymphocytes).  
- Diagnostic telomere‑length threshold: age‑adjusted telomere length below the 1st percentile (by flow‑FISH) is generally considered diagnostic of a telomere biology disorder; values between the 1st and 10th percentiles are supportive but less specific (particularly in adults).

Why these two tests and how to use them (clinical reasoning)
- They are complementary: genetic testing identifies a causal variant (genotype) and guides inheritance counseling, surveillance and donor selection; flow‑FISH documents the functional consequence (pathologic telomere attrition) and can help reclassify variants of uncertain significance.  
- Flow‑FISH is the clinical standard because it measures telomeres in defined leukocyte subsets with age‑matched reference percentiles; qPCR approaches are less reproducible and should not be used alone for diagnosis.  
- In the presented case a monoallelic PARN missense VUS was found on exome sequencing — the logical next step is flow‑FISH. Demonstration of lymphocyte telomere length <1st percentile for age would strongly support a TBD diagnosis and strengthen the pathogenic interpretation of the PARN variant; normal telomere length would argue against a telomere‑driven disorder and prompt reconsideration of the variant’s significance.

Practical points
- Always interpret telomere length against an age‑matched reference provided by the laboratory.  
- For adults, <1st percentile is specific; borderline short (1st–10th percentile) is supportive in the right clinical context.  
- These results influence management decisions (surveillance, transplant planning and donor selection) and family testing.

### [Management] For patients with telomere biology disorders who require hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation, what donor screening and conditioning strategy adjustments are recommended to reduce transplant-related toxicity and graft failure?

Short answer
For patients with telomere biology disorders (TBDs) who need hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation (HSCT), two key adjustments reduce toxicity and graft failure: (1) careful donor screening to avoid donors who carry the familial telomere mutation or have abnormally short telomeres; and (2) use of reduced‑intensity / reduced‑toxicity conditioning that minimizes alkylator and radiation exposure and incorporates serotherapy to permit engraftment without myeloablation.

Why this matters (mechanism and clinical relevance)
TBDs (including PARN‑related disease in this case) cause short, dysfunctional telomeres in both haematopoietic stem cells and somatic organs. Tissues with short telomeres are exquisitely sensitive to DNA‑damaging agents (high‑dose alkylators, ionizing radiation) and have impaired regenerative reserve. That explains the high rates of pulmonary, hepatic and mucosal toxicity and of graft failure reported after conventional myeloablative HSCT in TBD patients. The patient in the case already has pulmonary fibrosis and cirrhosis—two predictors of poor transplant tolerance—so these issues are directly relevant before any transplant is contemplated.

Practical recommendations

1) Donor screening
- Genotype any related donor for the known familial variant (here, the PARN change) before selection. First‑degree relatives can be asymptomatic carriers because of variable penetrance.
- Measure donor telomere length by an age‑adjusted method (flow‑FISH is the accepted clinical standard). Avoid donors with abnormally short telomeres for age (telomere length in the “very short” range on age‑adjusted testing) or who carry the pathogenic variant.
- If related donors are unsuitable, preferentially use an unrelated matched donor who tests negative for the familial mutation and has normal age‑adjusted telomere length. Unrelated cord blood with adequate matching is an option (cord units have long telomeres) but has tradeoffs (engraftment risk) and must be chosen carefully.

2) Conditioning strategy
- Avoid myeloablative regimens that include high‑dose alkylators (high‑dose busulfan, melphalan) or high‑dose total body irradiation whenever possible—these agents cause severe organ toxicity in telomere‑deficient patients.
- Use reduced‑intensity or reduced‑toxicity conditioning regimens that emphasize nucleoside backbone agents (e.g., fludarabine) plus lower‑toxicity myeloablative substitutes. Examples used in practice include fludarabine‑based regimens with reduced‑dose busulfan or treosulfan (treosulfan is increasingly used as a less hepatopulmonary‑toxic alternative), together with serotherapy (ATG or alemtuzumab) to facilitate engraftment and reduce GVHD. The exact regimen should be individualized to organ function.
- Tailor doses downward when there is established pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, and avoid TBI when possible.
- Consider strategies to promote engraftment (adequate CD34+ cell dose, early chimerism monitoring) because graft failure risk is increased.

3) Peri‑transplant planning
- Optimize organ function before transplant: full pulmonary evaluation (PFTs, HRCT), hepatology assessment and correction of reversible problems (nutrition, vitamin deficiencies as in this case).
- Counsel patients about increased risks even with modified regimens and refer to centers experienced in TBD‑HSCT.
- Close post‑transplant surveillance for hepatic and pulmonary complications and early chimerism monitoring for graft failure.

Bottom line applied to this case
This young woman with a monoallelic PARN variant plus hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis is at high risk for transplant‑related toxicity. If HSCT becomes necessary for progressive marrow failure, first screen any related donors for the PARN variant and measure telomere length (flow‑FISH)—do not use donors who carry the variant or have very short telomeres. Use a reduced‑intensity / reduced‑toxicity, fludarabine‑based conditioning strategy (avoiding high‑dose alkylators and TBI, with serotherapy), and perform the transplant at a center experienced in TBDs. These steps lower—but do not eliminate—the elevated risks of organ toxicity and graft failure in TBD patients.

### [Clinical Pearls] In adult-onset telomere biology disorders, which organ systems are most commonly affected and which common diagnoses do these presentations frequently mimic?

Direct answer
- Adult-onset telomere biology disorders (TBDs) most commonly affect tissues with high replicative demand — principally the bone marrow (haematopoietic system), the lung (alveolar epithelium → pulmonary fibrosis), and the liver (regenerative hepatocytes → hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis). Mucocutaneous tissues (hair, nails, oral mucosa), skeleton (early osteoporosis, fractures) and, less commonly, peripheral nerves and the gastrointestinal tract may also be involved.

Why this pattern occurs (brief pathophysiology)
- Short or dysfunctional telomeres limit the replicative capacity of stem/progenitor cells. Rapidly dividing compartments therefore undergo premature senescence or apoptosis: haematopoietic stem cells → cytopenias/ marrow failure; alveolar type II cells → fibrotic interstitial lung disease (classically a usual interstitial pneumonia/“idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis” phenotype); hepatocytes → impaired regeneration and progressive fibrosis.

How these presentations commonly masquerade as other diagnoses
- Because adult TBDs often present with a single-organ problem or without the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita, they are frequently mislabelled as common, acquired diseases:
  - Bone marrow involvement → aplastic anemia, myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), nutritional cytopenias (B12/folate deficiency), or autoimmune cytopenias.
  - Pulmonary involvement → idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or other fibrotic interstitial lung diseases.
  - Hepatic involvement → cryptogenic cirrhosis, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease progressing to cirrhosis, viral or autoimmune hepatitis.
  - Mucocutaneous/aging features → ordinary premature aging or isolated dermatologic/nutritional diagnoses; skeletal disease → primary osteoporosis or metabolic bone disease.
- The case illustrates these overlaps: a young woman whose pancytopenia was initially attributed largely to severe B12 deficiency, whose CT showed fibrotic/subpleural lung disease compatible with IPF, and whose transient elastography documented cirrhosis — a constellation ultimately recognized as a TBD associated with a monoallelic PARN variant.

Practical diagnostic and management pearls
- Suspect a TBD when any of the following occur at an unusually young age or together: unexplained cytopenias or marrow failure, early-onset fibrotic ILD (especially familial IPF), cryptogenic cirrhosis, premature greying/nail/oral findings, or a multisystem combination.
- Diagnostic standard: measure telomere length (flow‑FISH) and pursue germline genetic testing for telomere-related genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, DKC1, etc.). Short telomeres for age plus a pathogenic germline variant confirms the diagnosis; note that monoallelic variants can show variable penetrance and may be reported as VUS (as in this case).
- Management is organ-directed and anticipatory: correct reversible contributors (nutritional deficiencies), longitudinal surveillance of marrow, lung and liver, genetic counselling and family screening, and consideration of haematopoietic stem cell transplantation or organ transplant when indicated — using donor-screening and modified conditioning because of increased transplant toxicity in TBD. Androgen therapies have shown telomere-lengthening effects in small studies but require cautious, individualized use.

Clinical pearl
- In adults, TBD most often presents as bone‑marrow failure and/or pulmonary fibrosis and can include hepatic fibrosis; because these presentations mimic common acquired diseases (aplastic anemia/MDS, IPF, cryptogenic cirrhosis), maintain a high index of suspicion for TBD when these problems occur early, are multisystem, or are disproportionate to an apparent single cause — as the PARN-associated case demonstrates.

### [Pathophysiology] Mechanistically, how does critically short telomere length in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells increase susceptibility to cytopenias when exposed to additional stressors such as profound vitamin B12 deficiency?

Short answer
Critically short telomeres in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) reduce the marrow’s replicative reserve and sensitize progenitors to replication/DNA‑damage stress. A superimposed insult that increases proliferative demand and causes DNA synthesis stress—here, severe vitamin B12 deficiency producing ineffective erythropoiesis—tips fragile HSPCs and committed progenitors into p53‑mediated senescence or apoptosis, producing persistent cytopenias rather than a brisk, compensatory response.

Why this happens (mechanistic chain)
- Telomere maintenance and PARN: Telomeres cap chromosome ends and are maintained by telomerase (TERT + TERC). PARN participates in 3′‑end maturation/stability of TERC; loss or reduction of PARN function lowers telomerase RNA and telomerase activity, producing accelerated telomere shortening in highly replicative tissues such as marrow (as suspected in this patient with a monoallelic PARN variant).
- Reduced HSPC reserve: Short telomeres are sensed as DNA damage (uncapped ends → ATM/ATR activation), triggering p53/p21 pathways that drive cell‑cycle arrest, senescence or apoptosis. HSC self‑renewal and long‑term clonogenicity fall, so the absolute pool of HSPCs available to respond to stress is diminished.
- Stress from vitamin B12 deficiency: B12 deficiency impairs nucleotide synthesis (folate cycle dysfunction), causing stalled replication forks, uracil misincorporation, double‑strand breaks and apoptosis of rapidly dividing erythroid precursors — the classic “ineffective erythropoiesis” that produces marrow erythroid hyperplasia with a low reticulocyte output (as in the case: marrow erythroid hyperplasia, low reticulocyte count, hypersegmented neutrophils, Cabot rings).
- Synergy leading to cytopenias: The marrow tries to compensate for defective erythropoiesis by increasing progenitor proliferation, but HSPCs with critically short telomeres cannot sustain additional divisions and instead undergo senescence/apoptosis. The result is failure to produce mature cells despite marrow hyperplasia (ineffective production) and eventual involvement of multiple lineages (pancytopenia) as the HSPC pool is exhausted. Telomere dysfunction also promotes chromosomal instability (end‑to‑end fusions, breakage‑fusion‑bridge cycles), further increasing progenitor loss.

How this explains the case
- The patient had severe B12 deficiency producing marked ineffective erythropoiesis (dimorphic smear, marrow erythroid hyperplasia, low retic), yet cytopenias persisted out of proportion to nutritional deficiency. A monoallelic PARN variant plausibly reduced telomerase function and telomere length in her HSPCs, lowering hematopoietic reserve so that the nutritional “second hit” unmasked marrow failure. The brief improvement after B12 repletion is expected; persistent or recurrent cytopenias reflect the underlying telomere biology disorder and risk of progressive bone‑marrow failure.

Clinical implications (brief)
- Think “two‑hit” biology: inherited telomere shortening lowers reserve; acquired stresses (nutritional deficiencies, infections, cytotoxic drugs) precipitate clinical marrow failure.
- In such patients measure telomere length (flow‑FISH) and monitor counts longitudinally, correct reversible causes (as done), counsel about progressive marrow failure and consider transplant referral when appropriate.

Take‑home pearl
Short telomeres don’t always cause immediate cytopenias — they remove the marrow’s safety margin. Any added replication/DNA‑synthesis stress (for example, severe B12 deficiency) can exhaust that margin, provoking ineffective hematopoiesis and pancytopenia via accelerated progenitor apoptosis and HSC exhaustion.

### [Management] For a young adult diagnosed with or strongly suspected of having a telomere biology disorder, outline key elements of longitudinal surveillance and risk-reduction counseling you would implement across hematologic, pulmonary, hepatic, and cancer risks.

Short answer
In a young adult with a confirmed or strongly suspected telomere biology disorder (TBD) you must adopt a proactive, multidisciplinary surveillance strategy focused on four vulnerable organ systems (bone marrow, lungs, liver, and cancer-prone mucocutaneous/skin sites), coupled with lifestyle and reproductive counselling. The goal is early detection of progressive marrow failure or clonal evolution, timely treatment referral (HSCT, antifibrotic or transplant evaluation), and prevention of avoidable iatrogenic and environmental harms. The patient in the case report—late-20s woman with a monoallelic PARN variant, transient improvement after B12 repletion, established pulmonary fibrosis on CT, and cirrhosis by FibroScan—illustrates why surveillance must be individualized and multidisciplinary.

Practical surveillance and risk‑reduction plan (what to do and why)

1) Baseline characterization (at diagnosis)
- Confirm the diagnosis phenotype and establish objective biomarkers: genetic testing (already done in this case) and telomere length measurement by flow-FISH (gold standard) to document short telomeres if not yet measured.
- Baseline labs and studies: CBC with reticulocyte count and peripheral smear, iron studies, B12/folate (correct reversible contributors as in this case), CMP (AST/ALT, bilirubin, albumin), INR, platelets, viral hepatitis serologies, autoimmune serologies as indicated.
- Hematology work-up: bone marrow biopsy with cytogenetics and a myeloid NGS panel if cytopenias present (the case had a normal marrow initially—this is a baseline to compare).
- Pulmonary: baseline high-resolution CT (HRCT), full pulmonary function tests (spirometry, lung volumes, DLCO), 6-minute-walk test.
- Hepatic: ultrasound + transient elastography (FibroScan—already done: 18.1 kPa consistent with cirrhosis), and baseline HCC-risk assessment.
- Mucocutaneous/oncologic baseline: dermatology and ENT/oral mucosa exam; dental exam.

2) Hematologic surveillance and counseling
- Rationale: TBDs predispose to progressive cytopenias, MDS/AML and transfusion dependence; marrow can be vulnerable even after reversible causes are treated (as in this patient).
- Monitoring:
  - CBC with retic and peripheral smear every 1–3 months while unstable or during work-up, then every 3–6 months if stable. Sooner if new symptoms (bleeding, infections, worsening fatigue).
  - Repeat bone marrow + cytogenetics/NGS for new or worsening cytopenias, transfusion dependence, or abnormal cytogenetics—to detect clonal evolution.
- Management principles:
  - Supportive care (transfusions, infection prevention). Growth factors may be used selectively.
  - Androgen therapy (e.g., danazol) has produced hematologic responses and telomere lengthening in small studies but has hepatotoxicity and uncertain long-term benefit — avoid or use with extreme caution in patients with hepatic fibrosis (this patient’s cirrhosis strongly limits androgen use).
  - Refer early to a hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) center experienced in TBDs for discussion if progressive marrow failure, MDS or leukemia develops. Important transplant principles: screen potential related donors for the familial telomere defect/telomere length and use reduced‑intensity, tailored conditioning because TBD patients are sensitive to myeloablative regimens.

3) Pulmonary surveillance and counseling
- Rationale: monoallelic PARN and other telomere gene carriers commonly develop premature pulmonary fibrosis (this patient already has radiographic fibrosis).
- Monitoring:
  - Baseline HRCT and PFTs (spirometry, lung volumes, DLCO, 6MWT).
  - Reassess PFTs and DLCO every 3–6 months while disease is active or progressive; at least every 6–12 months if stable.
  - Repeat HRCT every 12 months or sooner with clinical decline.
- Management principles and risk reduction:
  - Smoking cessation and avoidance of inhalational occupational exposures are essential.
  - Early referral to an interstitial lung disease (ILD) center; consider antifibrotic therapy (nintedanib or pirfenidone) for progressive fibrotic phenotypes—coordinate with hepatology in this patient because these drugs have hepatic effects.
  - Early lung transplant evaluation in progressive disease is prudent because pulmonary decline can be rapid; pre-transplant assessment must integrate marrow and liver comorbidities.

4) Hepatic surveillance and counseling
- Rationale: hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis are common in adult TBD cohorts; this patient already meets criteria for cirrhosis by FibroScan.
- Monitoring:
  - LFTs (AST/ALT, bilirubin), synthetic function (INR, albumin), and platelets every 3–6 months or sooner with change.
  - Ultrasound (with or without AFP) every 6 months for HCC surveillance when cirrhosis present.
  - Repeat transient elastography periodically (e.g., annually) and hepatology follow-up.
  - Screening endoscopy for varices based on portal hypertension signs (low platelets, splenomegaly, or clinical stigmata).
- Management principles:
  - Abstain from alcohol; avoid hepatotoxic medications (androgens are relatively contraindicated with cirrhosis).
  - Vaccinate for hepatitis A and B if non-immune.
  - Refer to hepatology for cirrhosis management and for transplant assessment if decompensation occurs.

5) Cancer risk reduction and surveillance
- Rationale: TBDs raise risk for MDS/AML and certain solid tumors—especially head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (oral leukoplakia), skin cancers, and lung cancer (esp. with fibrosis).
- Counseling and surveillance:
  - Strong advice to avoid tobacco and heavy alcohol.
  - Oral cavity exams every 3–6 months if mucosal lesions or leukoplakia; low threshold for biopsy of suspicious lesions.
  - Annual dermatology exam (self-skin checks taught to the patient).
  - Age-appropriate standard cancer screening supplemented by heightened vigilance; HCC ultrasound/AFP every 6 months for cirrhosis.
  - Avoid unnecessary ionizing radiation and cytotoxic exposures when alternatives exist; coordinate oncologic therapy in specialized centers if cancer occurs.

6) Reproductive and family counselling
- Offer genetic counselling and cascade testing of first-degree relatives for the PARN variant and telomere length measurement; relatives should be screened before being considered HSCT donors.
- Discuss reproductive options (preimplantation genetic diagnosis) and pregnancy risks — pregnancy may worsen cardiopulmonary or marrow disease; care should be high-risk and multidisciplinary.
- Fertility preservation discussion before cytotoxic therapy or transplant.

7) Vaccination, infection prevention, bone health, and other supportive measures
- Vaccinate against influenza, pneumococcus and SARS-CoV-2; avoid live vaccines if immunosuppressed.
- Screen and treat bone disease (DEXA — this patient has osteoporosis) and supplement vitamin D/calcium; treat per guidelines.
- Provide genetic-disease‑specific psychosocial support and consider referral to a TBD registry or specialized center for longitudinal care and research.

Red flags prompting urgent action
- New transfusion dependence, falling reticulocyte count, new cytogenetic abnormalities, rapidly declining PFTs or DLCO, new hypoxia, hepatic decompensation (ascites, encephalopathy, jaundice), new or enlarging focused mucosal/skin lesions — any of these should prompt expedited specialist review (hematology, ILD center, hepatology, oncology).

Applying this to the reported patient
Because she already has cirrhosis (FibroScan 18.1 kPa) and radiographic pulmonary fibrosis with pancytopenia that initially improved after B12 repletion, immediate steps are: document telomere length (if not done), establish a schedule of CBCs (every 3 months if stable), PFTs every 3–6 months and yearly HRCT, begin HCC surveillance with ultrasound ± AFP every 6 months, refer to ILD and hepatology transplant teams early, avoid androgens given her liver disease, counsel strongly on tobacco/alcohol avoidance, and offer family genetic counselling and testing.

### [Morning Report] A monoallelic PARN missense variant located in a constrained functional domain is reported as a Variant of Uncertain Significance in a patient whose phenotype suggests a telomere biology disorder. What additional evidence would you seek to support reclassification toward likely pathogenic?

Clinical teaching pearl — what would move this monoallelic PARN p.Cys205Arg from VUS toward “likely pathogenic”?

Key principle: you need evidence that links the specific amino‑acid change to PARN loss-of-function (the established disease mechanism for PARN-associated TBDs) or strong genetic evidence that the variant tracks with disease. The most decisive items are (A) well‑established functional/biochemical proof the variant impairs PARN → telomerase/TERC biology (ACMG PS3), or (B) proof the variant arose de novo (ACMG PS2), supplemented by objective evidence of a telomere biology disorder in the patient and case/family data. In this patient the clinical phenotype (early greying, pancytopenia, hepatic cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis) already argues strongly for a TBD, so we should pursue tests that directly connect genotype to mechanism.

Evidence I would seek, in priority order, with why each matters:

1) Age‑adjusted telomere length by flow‑FISH (gold standard).
   - Rationale: profoundly short telomeres (eg, below the ~1st percentile for age in lymphocyte subsets) are a disease biomarker for TBDs and would materially strengthen the genotype–phenotype link (supports ACMG PP4 and the clinical interpretation). The case report already notes flow‑FISH as the diagnostic standard.

2) Well‑validated functional assays of PARN activity in patient cells (or recombinant protein) — the most persuasive molecular evidence.
   - Specific assays: reduced deadenylase activity; defective 3′‑end maturation of TERC (accumulation of oligo(A)‑tailed precursors and reduced mature TERC by northern blot/RT‑qPCR); reduced telomerase activity (TRAP assay); rescue of defects by transfection of wild‑type PARN.  
   - Rationale: such results provide direct mechanistic proof that p.Cys205Arg causes the biochemical defect that produces short telomeres (would satisfy ACMG PS3 — strong evidence).

3) Parental testing and family segregation.
   - De novo: confirm absence in both parents (with confirmed parentage) — would meet PS2 (strong).  
   - Co‑segregation: demonstration that the variant is present in affected relatives (and absent in unaffected) increases PP1 (strength based on number of meioses). Note caveat: PARN variants show variable penetrance and late onset, so absence in an unaffected older parent does not exclude pathogenicity.

4) Recurrence / case‑level corroboration.
   - Search literature/Databases (ClinVar, gnomAD, IPF/TBD cohorts) for the identical variant in unrelated TBD/IPF/BMFS patients or statistical enrichment in cases vs controls (ACMG PS4). Presence in multiple unrelated affected persons but absence in population controls is strong supporting evidence.

5) Additional molecular evidence that the missense alters protein stability/structure/localization.
   - Examples: reduced PARN protein level on western blot, accelerated degradation (cycloheximide chase), abnormal nuclear localization, or structural modelling showing disruption of the R3H RNA‑binding domain (the variant sits in residues 178–245 as noted in the report). These are supportive (can add to PS3/PP3).

6) Rule out or detect second‑allele events.
   - Copy‑number analysis (exon/gene deletion) or deep sequencing for a second PARN pathogenic variant (biallelic loss gives recessive DKCB6). Finding a second pathogenic allele in trans would change interpretation and be decisive for disease causation.

How this changes classification (practical ACMG reasoning):
- Right now the variant meets PM1 (critical domain), PM2 (absent/rare), PP3 and PP2 (in silico/constraint) as in the report. Adding a single strong line of evidence (PS3 from functional studies, or PS2 from confirmed de novo status) combined with existing PMs/PPs would typically be sufficient to reclassify to likely pathogenic under ACMG rules. Recurrence in unrelated affected individuals (PS4) or robust segregation (PP1) would also push toward likely pathogenic if combined appropriately.

Practical next steps for this patient:
- Order age‑adjusted flow‑FISH telomere length.  
- Arrange parental testing through the genetics service (to assess de novo vs inherited).  
- Discuss referral/collaboration with a lab that can perform PARN/TERC functional assays (or research groups studying telomere biology).  
- Re‑query ClinVar and cohort sequencing datasets for the same variant or nearby pathogenic missense changes in the R3H domain.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult with cryptogenic hepatic fibrosis and interstitial lung disease, which inherited conditions beyond telomere biology disorders are in the differential, and what targeted tests would you order to evaluate them?

Bottom line — when a young adult presents with cryptogenic hepatic fibrosis plus interstitial lung disease you must think beyond telomere biology disorders into a short list of inherited, often treatable, multisystem conditions. Prioritize those that explain the combination of hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias, bone disease and neuropathy seen in this case and order quick biochemical/functional tests in parallel with gene sequencing to resolve variants of uncertain significance.

High‑yield alternatives (why it fits this case → targeted tests)

1) Gaucher disease (GBA)
- Why: hepatosplenomegaly, pancytopenia, bone pain/osteoporosis and neuropathic features are classic.
- Tests: leukocyte (or DBS) glucocerebrosidase activity; plasma chitotriosidase or CCL18 as biomarkers; confirmatory GBA sequencing. Bone‑marrow review for Gaucher cells helps.

2) Niemann–Pick disease — acid sphingomyelinase deficiency (SMPD1) and Niemann–Pick type C (NPC1/2)
- Why: hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias and ILD with foamy macrophages (A/B); NPC causes liver disease with neurologic signs.
- Tests: acid sphingomyelinase enzyme assay and SMPD1 sequencing (A/B); for NPC: plasma oxysterols (cholestane‑3β,5α,6β‑triol), filipin staining on fibroblasts and NPC1/2 sequencing.

3) Lysosomal acid lipase deficiency (LIPA — cholesteryl ester storage disease)
- Why: early hepatic steatosis → fibrosis/cirrhosis, hepatosplenomegaly; treatable (sebelipase alfa).
- Tests: LAL enzyme activity (leukocyte/DBS) and LIPA gene sequencing.

4) Alpha‑1 antitrypsin (A1AT) deficiency (SERPINA1)
- Why: genetic cause of early cirrhosis and emphysema/airways disease (can be misread as ILD clinically).
- Tests: serum A1AT concentration (interpret with CRP), Pi‑phenotyping (isoelectric focusing) and SERPINA1 genotyping (Z, S and rarer alleles).

5) Surfactant‑metabolism / familial pulmonary fibrosis genes (SFTPC, SFTPA1/2, ABCA3, NKX2‑1)
- Why: these Mendelian causes present with early ILD and some (NKX2‑1) have extrapulmonary manifestations (thyroid/neurologic; neonatal cholestasis reported).
- Tests: targeted NGS panel for familial interstitial pneumonia / surfactant genes; check thyroid function if NKX2‑1 suspected.

6) Hermansky–Pudlak syndrome (HPS)
- Why: certain HPS subtypes cause progressive pulmonary fibrosis plus bleeding/oculocutaneous signs; liver involvement reported.
- Tests: platelet function and platelet electron microscopy for absent dense granules; HPS gene sequencing; ophthalmologic and bleeding history exam.

7) Cystic fibrosis (CFTR)
- Why: adult or atypical CF can present with bronchiectasis/airways disease and CF‑related liver disease (focal biliary cirrhosis).
- Tests: sweat chloride and CFTR genetic testing; fecal elastase if pancreatic insufficiency suspected.

8) Wilson disease (ATP7B) and hereditary hemochromatosis (HFE/non‑HFE)
- Why: both are inherited causes of young cirrhosis (Wilson particularly important because treatable); pulmonary fibrosis is uncommon but these are mandatory exclusions in young cirrhosis.
- Tests: ceruloplasmin, slit‑lamp exam, 24‑hour urinary copper (and ATP7B sequencing or hepatic copper quantification if needed); transferrin saturation/ferritin and HFE genotyping.

9) Inherited bone‑marrow failure syndromes (Fanconi anemia, Shwachman‑Diamond)
- Why: pancytopenia with extra‑haematologic findings. Fanconi classically has congenital anomalies; SDS has pancreatic and skeletal features.
- Tests: DEB/mitomycin C chromosomal breakage test for Fanconi; SBDS sequencing and pancreatic testing for Shwachman‑Diamond.

10) Pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (hereditary PAP)
- Why: “crazy‑paving” or alveolar filling pattern on HRCT and ILD; rare hereditary forms are due to CSF2RA/CSF2RB mutations.
- Tests: HRCT pattern review, bronchoalveolar lavage (milky fluid, PAS‑positive), anti–GM‑CSF autoantibody (autoimmune PAP); if negative and familial picture, sequence CSF2RA/CSF2RB.

Lower‑yield but consider with supportive clues: congenital disorders of glycosylation (CDG) (transferrin IEF, CDG gene panel), mitochondrial cytopathies (lactate, muscle biopsy, mtDNA/nuclear panels), primary immunodeficiencies such as CVID (Ig levels, vaccine responses, gene panel).

Practical, prioritized approach for this patient
- Because the case has hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias and striking bone disease/neuropathy: order first-line, high‑yield biochemical assays now — leukocyte/DBS assays for glucocerebrosidase, acid sphingomyelinase and LAL activity; serum A1AT level with Pi‑typing/genotyping; ceruloplasmin + 24‑hr urine copper; and CFTR/sweat if clinical lung phenotype suggests bronchiectasis.
- Simultaneously obtain a targeted NGS panel that covers inherited liver disease and familial interstitial pneumonia (GBA, SMPD1, LIPA, SERPINA1, ATP7B, HFE, SFTPC/SFTPA/ABCA3/NKX2‑1, HPS genes, etc.), rather than repeating single‑gene tests serially.
- Use bone‑marrow review (look for Gaucher cells or foamy macrophages) and BAL cytology (for foamy macrophages or PAP) to direct testing.
- Do telomere length testing (flow‑FISH) and familial segregation for any VUS (such as the PARN change in this case) in parallel — biochemical/functional assays can resolve VUS and change management.

Why this matters
- Several of the alternatives (Gaucher, LAL‑D, Wilson) are treatable and their identification changes medical therapy and transplant candidacy. The combination of biochemical assays + gene sequencing prevents misattribution of all findings to a single VUS and guides urgent interventions.

Bottom line in one sentence: in a young patient with cryptogenic hepatic fibrosis + ILD — and especially with hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias, bone pain/osteoporosis and neuropathy as in this report — prioritize storage disorders (Gaucher, Niemann–Pick), LAL deficiency, A1AT deficiency, surfactant‑gene familial pulmonary fibrosis, Wilson disease and cystic fibrosis; order the corresponding enzyme/biochemical screens first and a focused NGS panel, and use functional assays (enzyme activity, telomere length, BAL, marrow review) to resolve genetic variants.
