Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# When B12 Deficiency Isn’t the Whole Story: Untangling Ineffective Erythropoiesis, DAT Positivity, and Telomere Risk

## Why this case grabbed my attention
A woman in her late 20s comes in with a 5‑year history of progressive fatigue and pancytopenia. The peripheral smear reads like classic megaloblastosis (dimorphism, hypersegmented neutrophils) and she has severe vitamin B12 deficiency — a tidy explanation, right? Except she had a low reticulocyte count (0.39%), positive DAT for IgG (2+), elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin, iron overload in the marrow (high ferritin and iron), hepatosplenomegaly with transient elastography consistent with cirrhosis, and CT chest changes compatible with pulmonary fibrosis. She also had premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation and osteoporosis. Genetic testing found a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), labelled a Variant of Uncertain Significance but plausibly relevant to telomere biology.

What made this case educational is the intersection of a reversible cause (B12 deficiency) that could explain much of the marrow picture, a positive DAT that could send you down the autoimmune hemolysis pathway, and clinical red flags suggesting an underlying marrow vulnerability (telomere biology disorder, TBD). The danger is treating the wrong problem — for example, reflexively starting steroids for a “positive DAT” — while missing an inherited marrow disorder that changes surveillance and long‑term management.

## The clinical conundrum — how to reconcile the data
There are three overlapping laboratory/clinical patterns here:

1) Classic features of megaloblastic/ineffective erythropoiesis: dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, hypersegmented neutrophils, erythroid hyperplasia on bone marrow and low reticulocyte response despite anemia. Severe B12/folate deficiency fits this exactly.

2) Biochemical evidence suggesting red‑cell destruction: elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin. Those would normally steer you toward hemolysis.

3) A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT IgG 2+), which usually triggers consideration of warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA).

The key to reconciling these is recognizing intramedullary destruction — ineffective erythropoiesis — as a source of “hemolysis” indices. When erythroid precursors are dysmature and destroyed within the marrow, you see elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin but a low reticulocyte count because the marrow is failing to produce viable reticulocytes for the circulation. Iron is liberated and accumulates (high serum iron and ferritin, increased marrow iron stores). That pattern contrasts with peripheral hemolysis, where reticulocyte count is typically elevated because the marrow responds to increased peripheral loss.

In this patient, the low retic count and marrow picture make ineffective erythropoiesis the dominant mechanism for cytopenia, and the positive DAT becomes a contextual finding rather than definitive proof of clinically significant AIHA.

## DAT: what it really tells you (and what it doesn’t)
The DAT demonstrates immunoglobulin and/or complement bound to a patient’s RBCs. A positive DAT means RBCs are coated with antibody or complement — not that the patient necessarily has brisk, clinically important hemolysis. Always interpret DAT alongside the clinical picture and hemolysis panel.

If you suspect AIHA you should see a constellation of findings: falling hemoglobin, jaundice, high retic response, low haptoglobin, spherocytes on smear, a positive eluate/positive indirect antiglobulin test (if free antibody is present in serum), and often clinical signs of hemolysis. A DAT alone — especially when the retic response is inappropriately low — is insufficient to diagnose AIHA and certainly insufficient to justify systemic immunosuppression.

In this case the indirect test was negative (no free circulating antibody detected), the retic was low, and the marrow showed erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective maturation. The team reasonably deprioritized AIHA as the primary diagnosis.

## What to do at the bedside (a pragmatic approach)
When you face a patient with severe B12 deficiency, pancytopenia, a low retic count and a positive DAT, follow this practical sequence:

1. Treat reversible contributors immediately. Severe B12 deficiency should be corrected promptly (the team used IV cyanocobalamin followed by maintenance) and other nutritional deficiencies addressed. Provide red‑cell transfusion for symptomatic anemia.

2. Put the DAT into context. Order a full hemolysis panel (LDH, indirect bilirubin, haptoglobin, retic count), review the smear for spherocytes vs megaloblastic changes, and consider an eluate if you strongly suspect clinically relevant AIHA. Avoid reflexive steroids unless there is clear, progressive peripheral hemolysis.

3. Evaluate the marrow. If there is an inappropriately low reticulocyte response despite biochemical “hemolysis,” arrange bone marrow examination and iron studies. Evidence of erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective erythropoiesis and increased iron stores points away from peripheral immune destruction.

4. Look for extra‑haematologic clues. Young age with pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, premature greying, mucocutaneous findings, neuropathy, and early osteoporosis should prompt consideration of inherited marrow vulnerability such as a TBD.

5. Escalate to genetic and telomere testing when indicated. In appropriate patients, order targeted next‑generation sequencing panels for telomere genes and consider telomere length assessment (flow‑FISH) and genetics referral. Counseling the patient and family is essential.

6. Plan follow‑up and surveillance. Even when cytopenias improve after correcting deficiencies, patients with suspected TBD need ongoing monitoring of blood counts, pulmonary and hepatic function, and consideration of referrals to centres with experience in telomere disorders if marrow failure or organ fibrosis progresses.

## When to suspect a telomere biology disorder
TBDs are heterogeneous, and the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita is frequently absent. Clues that should raise your suspicion in a young adult include unexplained, disproportionate marrow failure (pancytopenia with low retic), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or early interstitial lung disease, hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis without other cause, premature greying, unexplained osteoporosis and a family history of marrow failure or fibrotic disease. In this patient those flags were present, and sequencing identified a monoallelic PARN variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) affecting the R3H domain — a biologically plausible hit, even though the variant remains classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance.

## Management implications — why the distinction matters
The distinction between AIHA and ineffective erythropoiesis driven by a marrow vulnerability is not academic. Treating presumed AIHA with high‑dose steroids, rituximab or other immunosuppression in a patient with an underlying TBD or evolving aplastic phenotype can cause harm and delay appropriate evaluation. Conversely, identifying a TBD alters counselling, surveillance for pulmonary and hepatic complications, family testing, and how you would approach bone marrow transplantation (patients with short telomeres have higher toxicity from standard conditioning regimens; HSCT decisions should be made in expert centres). There are limited options aimed at telomere biology (androgen therapy has shown some promise in small studies), but most management remains supportive and anticipatory.

In this case the team corrected B12, provided transfusion support and surveillance; the patient improved symptomatically and her counts stabilised at two‑week follow‑up, while a genetic framework was put in place to guide long‑term care.

## Take‑home messages
- A positive DAT indicates RBCs are coated with antibody/complement — it does not, by itself, prove clinically significant autoimmune hemolysis.  
- The reticulocyte count is critical: a brisk retic response points to peripheral hemolysis; an inappropriately low retic with elevated LDH/indirect bilirubin suggests intramedullary destruction/ineffective erythropoiesis.  
- Severe B12 deficiency commonly causes ineffective erythropoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia, low retic, high ferritin/iron and features that can mimic hemolysis. Correct reversible deficiencies first.  
- Don’t reflex to immunosuppression for an isolated positive DAT; treat AIHA only when the clinical and laboratory picture supports active peripheral hemolysis.  
- In young adults with disproportionate cytopenias plus pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, premature greying or other premature‑aging features, consider telomere biology disorders; pursue marrow evaluation, telomere/gene testing and genetics referral.  
- Management of TBDs is largely supportive and requires longitudinal surveillance; HSCT and other interventions should be coordinated with centres experienced in telomere disorders.

This case is a good reminder: treat the reversible causes, interpret tests in clinical context (especially the DAT and retic count), and keep a low threshold for looking for an underlying marrow vulnerability when the phenotype is disproportionate or multisystem.
