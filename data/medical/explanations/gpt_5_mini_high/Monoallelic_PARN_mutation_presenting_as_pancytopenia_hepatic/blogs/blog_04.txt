Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Two Fibroses, One Biology: Hepatic and Pulmonary Fibrosis in Telomere Syndromes

What grabbed our attention in the case above? A woman in her late 20s with 5 years of progressive fatigue, pancytopenia and severe vitamin B12 deficiency who, despite correction of nutrients, still had multisystem disease — premature greying, osteoporosis, hepatosplenomegaly with transient-elastography–proven cirrhosis (FibroScan 18.1 kPa), and CT evidence of pulmonary fibrosis. Whole‑exome testing found a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, VUS). In short: two fibrotic organs (lung and liver) in a young adult with bone‑marrow vulnerability — a classic red flag for a telomere biology disorder (TBD). The “surprise” here is liver disease: hepatic fibrosis is under‑recognized in TBDs, and can coexist with pulmonary fibrosis even when the classical mucocutaneous features of dyskeratosis congenita are absent.

## Why telomere biology links liver and lung fibrosis
Telomeres protect chromosome ends; telomerase (TERT/TERC and accessory factors) maintains them. Defects in telomerase or telomere‑processing genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, DKC1, etc.) lead to accelerated telomere shortening and premature cellular senescence. Tissues with high replicative demand or that undergo repeated injury—bone marrow, mucosa, skin, lung epithelium and hepatocytes—are particularly vulnerable. Inherited telomeropathies classically present as bone‑marrow failure or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), but emerging data (including registry analyses) show that liver fibrosis and cirrhosis are common in adults with telomerase mutations. PARN loss impairs TERC maturation and can present phenotypically across a spectrum from bone‑marrow failure to IPF and now recognized hepatic involvement.

## When to think TBD in a patient with fibrosis
Young age of onset (or fibrosis out of proportion to risk factors), multisystem involvement (cytopenias, premature greying or hair loss, early osteoporosis, mucosal findings or neuropathy), and family history raise suspicion. Nutritional deficiencies (like the severe B12 deficiency in this case) may coexist and can distract you — sustained multisystem dysfunction after correction should prompt a genetic/telomere workup. Importantly, classical mucocutaneous stigmata may be absent.

## A practical screening bundle for suspected/confirmed TBD with concern for liver and lung involvement
Start with a careful baseline assessment and then personalize follow‑up frequency to clinical trajectory and mutation severity. At baseline obtain a full blood count with reticulocyte count and peripheral smear, comprehensive liver biochemical panel (AST, ALT, ALP, GGT, bilirubin, albumin), INR, platelet count and renal function. For the liver, do an abdominal ultrasound to look for hepatosplenomegaly and steatosis, and obtain elastography (transient elastography or MRE where available) to quantify fibrosis and capture steatosis (CAP). Interpret liver‑stiffness values in context—markedly elevated values (for example FibroScan values in the high teens as in this case) support advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis and should trigger portal‑hypertension assessment (platelets, splenic size, and consideration of endoscopy for varices when signs of portal hypertension are present). Follow cirrhosis surveillance guidelines (HCC surveillance, variceal screening) if cirrhosis is confirmed.

For the lungs, get a baseline high‑resolution CT chest and pulmonary function testing with spirometry, lung volumes and DLCO, plus a 6‑minute walk test. Repeat PFTs at least every 6–12 months or sooner for symptoms; repeat HRCT for new/worsening symptoms or when PFTs show decline. Measure telomere length (flow‑FISH is the laboratory standard) and provide genetic testing for telomere‑related genes; involve genetic counselling early. Also screen for osteoporosis (DEXA), and check vitamin D, calcium and PTH given frequent bone disease in TBDs.

## Antifibrotics, androgens and other disease‑modifying therapies — the balance of benefit and organ risk
Antifibrotic therapy (nintedanib or pirfenidone) is the standard of care for progressive fibrosing interstitial lung disease and IPF, irrespective of genetic cause. Observational series and case reports suggest that antifibrotics can slow lung‑function decline in familial and genetic forms of pulmonary fibrosis, but randomized data specifically in telomere mutation carriers are limited. Two practical cautions when the liver is involved: both agents can cause hepatotoxicity and require baseline and serial LFT monitoring; nintedanib dose adjustments/avoidance are advised in significant hepatic impairment and it carries a bleeding risk that matters in portal hypertension/thrombocytopenia; pirfenidone also requires LFT monitoring and has photosensitivity and GI side effects. In patients with advanced hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis, the risk–benefit calculus must be individualized and discussed with hepatology and interstitial lung disease specialists.

Androgens (danazol, nandrolone) have shown biologic activity in telomere disease (telomere length increases and hematologic responses in small trials/series), but androgens are potentially hepatotoxic and therefore are often contraindicated or used with extreme caution in the presence of significant liver disease. This is a key practical tension: an agent that may help marrow disease can injure the liver that is already vulnerable in TBDs.

## Transplantation and procedural considerations
Hematopoietic stem‑cell transplant (HSCT) can cure marrow failure but patients with telomere syndromes are exquisitely sensitive to standard conditioning regimens and at higher risk for organ toxicity (lung and liver). Reduced‑intensity, tailored conditioning regimens and centers experienced with telomere disorders are recommended. Lung transplantation is an option for end‑stage PF, but post‑transplant complications (prolonged cytopenias, infections, poor wound healing) are more common in telomeropathy patients, and pre‑transplant evaluation must screen thoroughly for occult liver disease and marrow reserve. Combined organ transplantation (e.g., lung plus liver) is rarely performed and requires multidisciplinary discussion at referral centers.

## Medications and exposure precautions that matter when both liver and lung are at risk
Avoid smoking and alcohol — both accelerate organ injury. Avoid known pulmonary toxins (bleomycin, certain nitrofurantoin exposures, amiodarone when alternatives exist) and avoid chronic hepatotoxins (methotrexate, high‑dose anabolic steroids, prolonged azathioprine) unless unavoidable and with close monitoring. Be cautious with drugs that can unmask marrow dysfunction or cause idiosyncratic liver injury. Vaccinate (influenza, pneumococcal, COVID, hepatitis B where indicated). When starting antifibrotics or androgens, obtain baseline hepatic tests and monitor regularly. Finally, counsel patients about occupational and environmental exposures (organic dusts, silica, solvents) that might accelerate pulmonary fibrosis.

## Putting it into practice — a simple clinical approach
If you see a young adult with unexplained pulmonary fibrosis, early cirrhosis, unexplained cytopenias or premature greying/osteoporosis: don’t stop at treating reversible contributors (like B12 deficiency); broaden the differential to include telomere biology disorders. Order the screening bundle above, discuss telomere length measurement and genetic testing, involve hepatology and ILD/transplant services early, and tailor treatment choices (antifibrotic and androgen use) to the liver phenotype. Multidisciplinary follow‑up and family counseling are essential because penetrance is variable and genetic results (including VUS) may be reclassified over time.

## Key take‑home messages
- Young age or multisystem disease (bone‑marrow dysfunction + lung or liver fibrosis) should prompt consideration of a telomere biology disorder even when classic mucocutaneous signs are absent.
- Screen both organs: baseline CBC and retic, liver panel, ultrasound ± elastography, and HRCT + full PFTs; measure telomere length (flow‑FISH) and do genetic testing with genetic counselling.
- Antifibrotics (nintedanib, pirfenidone) can be considered for telomere‑related pulmonary fibrosis but require careful LFT monitoring and dose considerations in hepatic impairment; androgens may help marrow but are hepatotoxic and often contraindicated with significant liver disease.
- Pre‑transplant assessment needs to account for marrow reserve and liver disease; HSCT and lung transplant carry higher risks in TBDs and should be done at experienced centers with tailored protocols.
- Avoid smoking, alcohol and drugs/occupational exposures that accelerate lung or liver injury; coordinate care (hepatology, ILD, hematology, genetics) and follow patients longitudinally — telomere syndromes evolve.

This case reminds us that one biology—short, dysfunctional telomeres—can drive fibrosis in more than one organ. Recognizing that link changes how we screen, counsel and treat these patients.
