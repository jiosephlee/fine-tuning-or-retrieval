Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Transplanting on Short Telomeres: Conditioning Choices, Antifibrotics, and Immunosuppression Pitfalls

## Why this case matters
A woman in her late 20s with progressive fatigue, pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (and a monoallelic PARN variant) forces you to think beyond “nutrient deficiency ± autoimmune disease.” This is a classic transplant dilemma in telomere biology disorders (TBDs): the organs that drive the indication for transplant (bone marrow, lungs) are also the organs most vulnerable to transplant-related genotoxic stress, and the patient’s liver disease and poor marrow reserve make standard conditioning and routine immunosuppression potentially catastrophic. The questions this case asks are practical and urgent: when do you refer for hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) or lung transplant, what conditioning and graft choices minimize lethal toxicity, how do you manage CMV and other infections when antiviral drugs suppress marrow, and can androgens buy time without burning bridges to transplant?

## Telomeres and transplant — why short telomeres change the calculus
TBDs -> short/dysfunctional telomeres -> exhausted replicative capacity in high-turnover tissues (haematopoietic stem cells, hepatocytes, pulmonary epithelium, skin). That biology translates directly into greater sensitivity to DNA-damaging agents: full-intensity alkylator regimens and total body irradiation (TBI) produce profound marrow toxicity, severe mucositis, delayed engraftment, hepatic veno-occlusive disease, exaggerated pulmonary toxicity and poor wound healing. Observational series have shown higher early transplant morbidity and mortality when standard myeloablative regimens are used in TBD patients. The practical takeaway: standard “all-comers” conditioning is often too toxic — tailor intensity and agents to the telomere problem.

## HSCT in TBD — whom and when
HSCT remains the only curative option for progressive marrow failure/MDS/AML arising in TBD, but timing is tricky. Indications mirror other marrow-failure syndromes (progressive cytopenias, transfusion dependence, clonal evolution), but you must weigh (a) the severity of pulmonary and hepatic comorbidity, (b) the trajectory of marrow failure, and (c) donor availability. In practice you should involve a multidisciplinary team early (hematology, transplant, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics). In many centres the decision is individualized: transplant earlier for rapidly progressive marrow failure before irreversible lung/liver damage accrues, or delay/bridge therapy if organ disease makes HSCT prohibitively risky.

## Conditioning and graft choices — practical principles
The single most important principle is to reduce genotoxic stress while preserving engraftment.

- Favor reduced-intensity, radiation-sparing regimens. Avoid TBI if possible.
- Avoid or minimize high-dose alkylators (full-dose busulfan, cyclophosphamide). When an alkylator is used, dose-reduce and perform therapeutic drug monitoring (eg, busulfan AUC-guided dosing).
- Consider fludarabine-based reduced-intensity platforms combined with low-exposure alkylator (low-dose busulfan) or treosulfan. Treosulfan has been used as a reduced-toxicity alkylator alternative in fragile patients, but data are limited — discuss with your transplant pharmacist/centre.
- Use serotherapy (ATG or alemtuzumab) judiciously for engraftment/GVHD modulation, balancing infection risk.
- Donor selection matters enormously: do not use related donors without genetic testing for the causative mutation and, when available, telomere length assessment. A phenotypically healthy sibling may still carry the same mutation and short telomeres.
- Prefer bone marrow grafts over peripheral blood when treating marrow failure to reduce chronic GVHD risk.
- Be cautious with post-transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy) for haploidentical grafts — PTCy is an alkylator-based approach and may be poorly tolerated; if haploidentical transplant is the only option, discuss dose-modified strategies with expert centres.

There is no universal “best” conditioning protocol for TBD — choose a centre-experienced regimen that minimizes irradiation and alkylator exposure, and be prepared to individualize drug selection and dosing.

## Infectious risk and CMV vigilance — practical tips
TBD patients are infection-prone because of baseline cytopenias and mucosal fragility, and HSCT further increases that risk. The infection strategy needs to balance antiviral efficacy with marrow-sparing.

- In HSCT recipients who are CMV-seropositive, letermovir prophylaxis is attractive because it reduces CMV reactivation without the marrow suppression associated with valganciclovir/ganciclovir. When available, plan for letermovir prophylaxis and continued PCR surveillance.
- If CMV disease develops or high-level viraemia requires therapy, ganciclovir/valganciclovir can worsen cytopenias; foscarnet is non-marrow-toxic but nephrotoxic — choose according to the patient’s marrow reserve and renal function, with transplant-infectious disease input.
- Pneumocystis prophylaxis: TMP–SMX is the most effective agent but causes cytopenias. If TMP–SMX is not tolerated, use alternatives (monthly inhaled pentamidine, atovaquone or dapsone) with appropriate G6PD screening and clinical monitoring.
- Antifungal prophylaxis should be tailored. Echinocandins are not myelotoxic but are IV-only; azoles (posaconazole/voriconazole) are oral but have drug–drug interactions with calcineurin inhibitors and can affect liver enzymes — important in a patient with hepatic fibrosis. Coordinate with pharmacy.
- Vaccination and infection-prevention measures should be optimized pre-transplant where possible (inactivated vaccines); live vaccines are contraindicated after HSCT until immune reconstitution allows.

## Antifibrotics and the peri-transplant lung patient
For patients with IPF, antifibrotic therapy (nintedanib, pirfenidone) slows progression and can buy time to transplantation. Practical points:

- Continue antifibrotic therapy when it is helping to stabilize disease, but coordinate with the transplant team about perioperative stopping rules. Both drugs can affect liver enzymes, so hepatic dysfunction (as in this case) requires special attention.
- Nintedanib has bleeding signals and carries some perioperative risk; discuss holding it before surgery.
- Antifibrotics do not eliminate the need for transplant in progressive IPF; they are a bridge, not a cure.

## Androgens (danazol/nandrolone) — bridge therapy with limits
Small clinical series and trials have shown that androgens can improve blood counts and, in some cases, increase telomere length. That makes them an attractive bridge to HSCT in patients with transfusion dependence. Caveats:

- Androgens are hepatotoxic and can precipitate or worsen cholestasis and hepatic tumours — a major concern in patients with hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis (like the case patient). Use with great caution or avoid in significant liver disease.
- Side effects include virilization, lipid abnormalities and thrombosis.
- If used as a bridge, closely monitor liver enzymes, synthetic function and hematologic response, and coordinate timing with transplant surgery/risk planning.

## Lung transplant in short telomeres — blunt edges and practical adjustments
Short telomere patients who are candidates for lung transplant have worse perioperative hematologic and infectious complications in multiple series. Practical considerations for lung transplant teams:

- Pre-transplant, screen for telomere gene mutations and measure telomere length where available; perform a full multi-organ assessment (liver fibrosis staging, bone marrow reserve, PFTs, HRCT).
- Expect exaggerated cytopenias post-op, and plan transfusion and hematology support.
- Anastomotic healing may be impaired. Avoid early use of mTOR inhibitors (sirolimus/everolimus) which exacerbate wound-healing problems and have been linked to bronchial anastomotic dehiscence; delay mTOR agents until healing is assured, and prefer calcineurin inhibitor–based regimens initially.
- Avoid azathioprine when marrow reserve is marginal; mycophenolate also suppresses marrow and may need dose adjustments.
- CMV prophylaxis in lung transplant is often valganciclovir; the marrow-suppressive effects complicate management. Discuss individualized strategies with transplant infectious disease — pre-emptive PCR-based therapy, dose adjustments, or alternative agents depending on marrow function.
- Severe concomitant liver disease may push consideration of combined lung–liver transplantation, but that is highly resource- and expertise-intensive and has limited experience — discuss at a centre that does combined transplants.

## A practical pre-transplant checklist for suspected/confirmed TBD
Before contemplating HSCT or lung transplant in a patient like this PARN carrier, practical steps you should ensure are done and documented:

- Refer early to genetics for confirmatory testing, family screening and pre-donation testing of any related donor.
- Measure telomere length (flow-FISH or other validated assay) if available — it informs risk and donor selection.
- Multi-organ staging: high-resolution CT chest and full pulmonary function testing; transient elastography/liver staging and hepatology consult; bone marrow evaluation and cytogenetics; bone density.
- Correct reversible contributors (B12/folate deficiency in this case), optimize nutrition and treat active infections pre-transplant.
- Convene multidisciplinary transplant planning (hematology/transplant, pulmonology, hepatology, infectious disease, pharmacy, genetics).
- Plan conditioning and GVHD prophylaxis that minimizes irradiation and high-dose alkylators; identify an experienced centre and document the rationale for regimen choice.
- Plan infectious prophylaxis strategy up front (consider letermovir for HSCT CMV prophylaxis), and have alternatives for PJP and fungal prophylaxis if TMP–SMX or azoles are poorly tolerated.
- Decide whether androgen therapy is a safe bridge (avoid if significant hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis) and institute rigorous monitoring if used.
- Counsel the patient and family explicitly about the higher risk of post-transplant cytopenias, infection, poor wound healing and the need for intensive follow-up.

## Clinical pearls and take‑home points
- Short telomeres change transplant risk: they increase sensitivity to irradiation and alkylators, worsen mucosal/dermal/hepatic healing, and amplify infectious complications — plan accordingly.  
- For HSCT in TBD, use reduced-intensity, radiation-sparing conditioning and minimize high-dose alkylators; monitor busulfan if used and prefer bone marrow grafts when treating marrow failure.  
- Do not use a related donor without testing for the familial mutation and, when possible, telomere length — an “apparently healthy” sibling may share the risk.  
- CMV management should prioritize marrow-sparing options: letermovir prophylaxis post-HSCT is attractive; when therapy is required, balance marrow toxicity (ganciclovir) against renal toxicity (foscarnet).  
- Androgens can improve counts and sometimes telomere length (useful as a bridge) but are hepatotoxic — avoid or use with extreme caution in cirrhosis.  
- In lung transplant candidates with short telomeres expect exaggerated cytopenias, wound-healing problems and infectious risk; delay mTOR inhibitors and avoid azathioprine when marrow reserve is limited.  
- Early multidisciplinary planning and referral to centres experienced with TBDs (genetics + transplant) materially change outcomes — don’t wait until organ failure is irreversible.

This patient’s story is a reminder: recognizing a TBD in a young adult with pancytopenia plus liver and lung fibrosis changes everything — it forces a more cautious, tailored transplant strategy and a team-based approach.
