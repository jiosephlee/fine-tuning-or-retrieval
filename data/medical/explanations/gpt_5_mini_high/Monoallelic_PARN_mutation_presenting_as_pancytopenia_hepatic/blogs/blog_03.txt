Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# PARN on the Edge: How to Manage a VUS When the Phenotype Fits

This is one of those real-world genetics problems that clinicians increasingly face: a young patient whose clinical story screams “telomere biology disorder,” but whose genetic report hands you a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). Our patient — a woman in her late 20s with progressive pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis and radiographic pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying and osteoporosis — carries a monoallelic PARN missense change (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) located in the R3H domain. In silico tools predict damage, the variant is vanishingly rare in population databases, and the gene itself is intolerant to disruptive variation. Still: VUS. What do you do next? This post walks through a practical, clinician-facing pathway to adjudicate this kind of variant, gather the evidence that matters, and manage the patient while the genetics catch up.

## Why this case is interesting (and uncomfortable)
PARN is a telomere-related gene with a biologically plausible link to this phenotype. Biallelic PARN loss causes severe, early dyskeratosis congenita–type syndromes; monoallelic PARN variants have been reported in adults with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure. The identified variant sits in the R3H domain (aa 178–245), a functionally important region for nucleic acid interactions. In ACMG terms the lab could justify PM1 (critical domain), PM2 (absent/rare), PP3 (multiple in silico predictors), and PP2 (gene constrained for benign missense). Still, without functional data, segregation, telomere-length confirmation or multiple case reports, the variant remains a VUS.

That gap — a strong phenotype and a tantalizing variant that’s not yet “pathogenic” — is what makes the case clinically important. We must balance the risk of over-interpreting a VUS (making irreversible decisions based on uncertain genetics) against the risk of under-recognition (missing an inherited telomeropathy that affects transplant decisions, donor selection, counseling, and surveillance).

## Quick biology recap: why PARN fits the phenotype
PARN is a deadenylase involved in mRNA poly(A) tail shortening and in the 3′-end maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Impaired PARN function can reduce mature TERC, lower telomerase activity, and accelerate telomere attrition — hitting high-turnover tissues (bone marrow, lung, liver, skin, mucosa) hardest. Clinically that tracks with marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis and premature ageing signs — exactly the organ mix in this patient.

## What the lab-level ACMG call tells you (and what it doesn’t)
The lab’s VUS classification reflects the current weight of evidence:
- PM1: the change lies in the R3H domain — a plausible functional hotspot.
- PM2: the variant is absent or extremely rare in population datasets.
- PP3/PP2: multiple computational tools predict deleteriousness and PARN has low benign missense variability.

These are useful pieces of evidence, but they are moderate/supporting; ACMG needs stronger lines (well-validated functional data, robust segregation, de novo occurrence, or statistically significant enrichment of the variant in affected individuals) to move a VUS to likely pathogenic/pathogenic. In short: the genetics are suggestive, not definitive.

## Pragmatic steps to gather more evidence (the clinician’s checklist)
1) Order age‑adjusted telomere length testing (flow‑FISH) as the next step that most directly addresses pathogenicity. Flow‑FISH in lymphocytes and granulocytes is the clinical gold standard: age-adjusted telomere length below the 1st percentile strongly supports a telomeropathy; 1–10th percentile is suggestive; normal length does not fully exclude disease (mosaicism, tissue‑specific effects). If blood yields equivocal results, consider telomere length in fibroblasts (research labs).

2) Pursue focused functional testing if available through a research or reference lab. Useful assays include quantitative TERC measurements, assessment of TERC 3′ processing, telomerase activity (TRAP assay), or PARN enzymatic/deadenylase assays. Robust, replicated functional impairment would meet strong functional evidence (PS3) under ACMG.

3) Family studies (segregation): test parents and available affected or older unaffected relatives. De novo occurrence (with paternity/maternity confirmation) would be strong evidence (PS2); co‑segregation with disease in multiple relatives strengthens the case (PP1 escalating to moderate/strong depending on numbers). Conversely, finding the variant in an unaffected older relative argues against full penetrance — but interpret with caution because telomere disorders often show reduced penetrance and age-dependent expression.

4) Search for additional hits. Re-examine the exome for other rare variants in canonical telomere genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, etc.) or for CNVs that could act as modifiers or second hits. Oligogenic contributions are reported in telomeropathies.

5) Submit and share data. Put the variant and detailed phenotype into ClinVar or disease registries and query whether other labs/clinicians have seen the same variant. Variant reclassification often requires collective evidence from multiple groups.

6) Plan for periodic reanalysis. Genetic knowledge moves fast. Request that the lab re-analyses the VUS every 12–24 months or sooner if new functional data or case reports appear.

## What ordering and interpreting flow‑FISH looks like in practice
Flow‑FISH reports telomere length as an age-adjusted percentile for lymphocytes and granulocytes. Practical points:
- Use a lab experienced in clinical flow‑FISH; pre-analytic handling matters.
- Lymphocyte telomere length <1st percentile for age is highly specific for a telomeropathy in adults (this is the single most clinically actionable test you can order).
- Both lymphocyte and granulocyte shortening increases confidence; discordant results (e.g., normal blood telomeres but short fibroblasts) can reflect mosaicism or tissue‑restricted effects.
- qPCR telomere assays are less reproducible across labs and should not substitute for flow‑FISH when making clinical decisions.

In this patient, a profoundly short age-adjusted telomere length would materially strengthen the argument that the PARN variant is disease‑causing and would justify more telomere‑directed counseling and testing of relatives.

## Managing the patient now — “treat the phenotype, respect the uncertainty”
Don’t let a VUS paralyze clinical care. Manage organ disease according to standard practice, but with telomere‑disorder awareness:

- Continue definitive treatment of reversible contributors (this patient needs ongoing B12 replacement; transfusion support for symptomatic anaemia).
- Institute surveillance: frequent CBC monitoring, baseline and interval pulmonary function testing (including DLCO) and HRCT as indicated, and hepatology follow-up with serial elastography/LFTs given cirrhosis. Frequency is individualized based on clinical trajectory; for many adult TBD patients this means formal pulmonology and hepatology follow-up at least every 6–12 months or sooner if symptomatic change.
- Avoid telomere‑damaging or highly genotoxic exposures where possible: limit alkylator/irradiation exposure and be cautious with cytotoxic chemotherapy. If hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) is being considered, ensure telomere‑aware planning: reduced‑intensity conditioning regimens, experienced transplant center, and careful donor selection (exclude related donors with the variant or with short telomeres).
- Pharmacologic telomere modulation (eg, danazol) has limited, early evidence of telomere elongation in some patients — but carries real risks (hepatotoxicity, thrombosis). In our patient with cirrhosis, danazol is contraindicated; such decisions need a telomere-disease specialist discussion and risk‑benefit judgment.
- Lifestyle counseling: smoking cessation, avoiding excessive alcohol (important with hepatic disease), and vaccinations to reduce pulmonary infection risk.
- If lung transplant or HSCT are being considered, inform the transplant teams about the suspected telomeropathy early — outcomes and conditioning strategies differ for these patients.

## Counseling the patient and the family
Be transparent and precise: explain that the genetic result is a VUS — it is neither proof that the variant causes disease nor proof that it’s benign. Explain what additional evidence could change that (telomere length, family segregation, functional testing). Offer parental testing and targeted family testing to help reclassify the variant; emphasize that predictive testing of asymptomatic relatives (especially children) is generally not recommended for a VUS. Discuss reproductive options only if/when the variant is reclassified to pathogenic/likely pathogenic or if family testing demonstrates cosegregation.

Document the plan: who will order flow‑FISH, when genetic reanalysis will occur, and that the team will recontact the family if the variant is reclassified.

## A practical VUS adjudication workflow you can use tomorrow
1. Order age‑adjusted flow‑FISH (lymphocytes ± granulocytes).  
2. Request targeted parental testing for the variant.  
3. Share the variant + HPO-coded phenotype with the diagnostic lab and submit to ClinVar/registry.  
4. Ask the lab about research or referral options for PARN functional assays.  
5. Continue phenotype-driven care (supportive therapy, surveillance) and avoid telomere‑damaging exposures.  
6. Reanalyze genomic data and the variant classification at least annually or sooner if new data emerge.

## Closing thoughts
A VUS isn’t “no information” — it’s a conditional signal. When the phenotype lines up tightly with the gene’s biology (as with PARN and telomere‑related fibrosis/bone marrow disease), the clinician’s job is to gather orthogonal data (especially telomere length and family segregation), treat the patient’s current problems, and put a process in place for reclassification. That combination — careful phenotyping, directed testing, multidisciplinary management, and data sharing — is how a VUS moves from frustrating ambiguity to clinical clarity.

## Key take‑home messages
- A VUS in a telomere gene does not equal “not important.” If the clinical picture fits, pursue age‑adjusted flow‑FISH as the single most informative next test.  
- Flow‑FISH lymphocyte telomere length <1st percentile for age is strongly suggestive of a telomeropathy; 1–10th percentile is supportive. qPCR is less reliable.  
- PM1/PM2/PP2/PP3 (domain, rarity, gene constraint, in silico) can justify a VUS but are usually insufficient without functional, segregation or population enrichment evidence.  
- Functional assays (TERC processing, telomerase activity, PARN enzymatic assays) and family segregation (including de novo evidence) are the highest‑value evidence to reclassify a VUS.  
- Manage the patient’s phenotype aggressively and avoid genotoxic or hepatotoxic agents until telomere status is clarified; plan HSCT/lung transplant with telomere‑aware teams.  
- Share data: submit the variant and clinical phenotype to ClinVar/registries and plan periodic reanalysis (every 12–24 months) — collective data are what reclassifies VUS into actionable variants.
