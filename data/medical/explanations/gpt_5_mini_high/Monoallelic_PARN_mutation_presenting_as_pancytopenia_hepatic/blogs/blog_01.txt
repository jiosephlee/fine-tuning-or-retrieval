Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# The Telomere Masquerader: Recognizing Adult-Onset TBD Without the Dyskeratosis Triad

## Why this case grabbed my attention
A woman in her late 20s with a 5‑year history of progressive fatigue, pancytopenia and severe B12 deficiency might initially look like a straight nutritional marrow failure—except she also had premature greying, hepatosplenomegaly with cirrhosis on elastography, pulmonary fibrosis on CT, osteoporosis with fractures and axonal neuropathy. Correcting the B12 helped, but the clinical picture remained out of proportion to a simple deficiency. Clinical exome sequencing revealed a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) — a variant of uncertain significance — and the team concluded the overall phenotype fit a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

What’s interesting (and clinically useful) here: adult TBDs often don’t wear the classic dyskeratosis congenita (DC) mucocutaneous triad. Instead they quietly masquerade as multisystem, organ‑specific disease—especially idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or cryptogenic cirrhosis—sometimes with only subtle skin/hair findings. If you only look for the textbook triad, you’ll miss them.

## How TBDs present in adults — the masquerade
Telomere biology disorders arise from defects in telomere maintenance (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, DKC1, PARN and others). Tissues with high turnover—bone marrow, lung epithelium, liver parenchyma, skin and mucosa—are most vulnerable. In adults the presentation is highly variable: isolated early‑onset idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), cryptogenic cirrhosis, progressive cytopenias, early osteoporosis, nail/skin/hair changes or combinations of the above. Classical DC with oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy and reticular pigmentation is the prototype, but adult TBD often lacks that full triad.

PARN is a reminder of this heterogeneity: biallelic PARN loss causes severe childhood syndromes, while monoallelic PARN variants increasingly turn up in adults with IPF and/or bone marrow susceptibility. In this case the monoallelic PARN variant sits in the R3H domain, is predicted damaging by in‑silico tools, and fits the clinical pattern despite being labelled a VUS.

Clinical pearl: early‑onset organ fibrosis (lung or liver), unexplained progressive cytopenias with a low reticulocyte response, premature greying or early osteoporosis in a young adult = think telomere disease—even if the classic mucocutaneous signs are missing.

## Red flags that should prompt you to think TBD (even in the absence of the DC triad)
- Age‑inappropriate fibrosis: IPF, especially if onset <65, or unexpected cirrhosis in a young adult without usual risk factors.
- Progressive unexplained cytopenias (pancytopenia or isolated marrow failure) with inadequate reticulocyte response despite marrow hyperplasia or normal cytogenetics.
- Premature greying, early osteoporosis/fractures, mucosal changes or subtle hyperpigmentation in a young person.
- Multisystem involvement that is disproportionate to an apparent single cause (for example, very severe B12 deficiency but persistent organ fibrosis and cytopenias after correction).
- Family history of early pulmonary fibrosis, bone marrow failure, or liver disease — but remember family history may be absent due to variable penetrance or de novo mutations.

## A pragmatic diagnostic pathway — when to order flow‑FISH and when to escalate to genetic testing
Step 1 — initial stabilization and rule‑outs. Treat reversible problems promptly (correct B12/folate, treat active infections). Do the basic workup: CBC with indices and reticulocyte count, peripheral smear, iron studies, LDH, bilirubin, DAT, bone marrow exam if indicated, LFTs and liver imaging, and HRCT chest if dyspnea or abnormal CXR.

Step 2 — ask the “disproportion” question. If the clinical picture is out of proportion to reversible causes (persistent cytopenias despite nutritional repletion, early fibrosis, multisystem disease, very early greying/osteoporosis), escalate the evaluation for TBD.

Step 3 — measure telomere length with flow‑FISH at a reference lab. Flow‑FISH is the clinical standard for telomere length measurement. Age‑adjusted thresholds help interpretation: telomere length below the 1st percentile for age is strongly suggestive of a TBD; lengths below the 10th percentile are suspicious in the right clinical context. qPCR assays are less reliable for clinical decision making and are not preferred.

Step 4 — genetic testing. If flow‑FISH shows short telomeres or if clinical suspicion is high despite borderline telomere length, order a targeted telomere gene panel or clinical exome that includes TERT, TERC, RTEL1, DKC1, PARN, NAF1 and others. Which to choose? A focused panel is often faster and easier to interpret for TBDs; whole‑exome sequencing can be useful when the phenotype is atypical, but expect more VUS.

Step 5 — interpret results in context. Many variants will be VUS. Correlate genetics with telomere length, family segregation (test relatives where possible), and phenotype. Discuss functional testing or reclassification with a clinical geneticist or a specialized TBD center when needed.

Step 6 — get multidisciplinary input. If confirmed or strongly suspected, involve hematology (for marrow surveillance and HSCT planning if needed), pulmonology, hepatology, genetics and transplant teams when organ replacement is considered.

Clinical pearl: don’t wait for “definitive” genetics to start surveillance and counseling. Short telomeres or a compelling phenotype justify early precautions (smoking cessation, avoid genotoxic exposures) and referral.

## Common pitfalls that delay diagnosis
Anchoring on a single diagnosis. A severe B12 deficiency will explain macrocytic anaemia and neuropathy, but it shouldn’t end the diagnostic workup if other red flags exist. In this case the duodenal biopsy showed reduced parietal cells and B12 malabsorption; treating that was appropriate, but the team kept looking when the overall illness remained disproportionate.

Over‑interpreting a positive DAT. A weakly positive direct antiglobulin test can distract you into autoimmune hemolysis, but a low reticulocyte count and marrow findings consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis favour a marrow problem.

Relying on family history. Absence of a family history does not exclude a germline telomere disorder because of variable penetrance, late onset in relatives, reduced survival, or de novo variants.

Using qPCR telomere assays for clinical decisions. qPCR can be useful in research but is prone to lab and inter‑assay variability. Flow‑FISH at a reference lab is the clinically actionable test.

Assuming normal bone marrow excludes TBD. Early in the course marrow cellularity may be preserved or show erythroid hyperplasia; aplasia or hypocellularity may come later.

## Management and counseling — practical bedside points
Treat reversible contributors immediately (eg, B12 replacement, correct electrolytes). Provide supportive care: transfusions for symptomatic anaemia, bone health optimization, physiotherapy and symptom management. Counsel patients and families about the genetic nature and the implications for relatives.

Avoid or minimize exposures that stress replicative capacity: smoking, excessive alcohol (relevant for liver disease), and unnecessary cytotoxic chemotherapy or radiation when possible. If marrow failure progresses, referral to a centre experienced in HSCT for TBD is essential—these patients tolerate conventional myeloablative regimens poorly and often need tailored, reduced‑intensity conditioning.

Consider experimental or off‑label options carefully. Androgen therapy (eg, danazol, nandrolone) has shown telomere length increases in small series but long‑term safety and efficacy are not settled. Organ transplantation (lung or liver) is not contraindicated but requires specialized evaluation because telomere dysfunction can complicate transplant outcomes and haematologic reserve.

For VUS: pursue segregation testing in relatives, measure their telomere lengths, and re‑evaluate the variant as more evidence accrues. Engage a clinical geneticist early.

Clinical pearl: in a young adult with unexplained organ fibrosis or cytopenias, begin the conversation about genetic testing and family screening early—even if you haven’t confirmed a pathogenic variant yet. Early recognition changes surveillance, avoids harmful exposures, and informs transplant planning.

## Back to our patient — a sensible, conservative course
In this report the team corrected the patient’s severe B12 deficiency and provided supportive care (transfusions, supplements, physiotherapy). Exome testing found a monoallelic PARN variant (p.Cys205Arg) that is predicted damaging but remains a VUS. The constellation—pancytopenia with low retic index, premature greying, hepatic cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis—made a telomere biology disorder the best unifying diagnosis. The patient is on close surveillance (blood counts, liver and lung monitoring) and her family has been offered genetic counselling.

## Take‑home messages
- Consider TBD in young adults with unexplained early pulmonary fibrosis, cryptogenic cirrhosis, progressive cytopenias, premature greying or early osteoporosis—even when the classic DC mucocutaneous triad is absent.  
- Correct reversible causes (eg, B12) quickly, but don’t stop the evaluation if features are disproportionate or persistent.  
- Measure telomere length by flow‑FISH at a reference lab when suspicion is moderate to high; telomere length <1st percentile for age is strongly suggestive.  
- If telomeres are short or clinical suspicion is high, order a targeted telomere gene panel or clinical exome and involve genetics early.  
- Be wary of common traps: anchoring on a single diagnosis (eg, B12 deficiency), over‑reliance on qPCR telomere tests, and assuming no family history rules out a germline disorder.  
- Management is largely supportive; refer to specialized centres for HSCT or transplant considerations, and provide cascade testing and genetic counselling to family members.

If you see a young patient with unexplained fibrosis + cytopenias, put TBD on the differential and pick up the phone to your genetics or telomere clinic early. These are rare diagnoses, but the consequences for surveillance, treatment choices and family planning are big — and catching them sooner changes management.
