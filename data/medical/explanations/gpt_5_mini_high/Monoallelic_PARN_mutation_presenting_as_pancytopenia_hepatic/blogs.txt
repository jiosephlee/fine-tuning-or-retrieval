Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# The Telomere Masquerader: Recognizing Adult-Onset TBD Without the Dyskeratosis Triad

## Why this case grabbed my attention
A woman in her late 20s with a 5‑year history of progressive fatigue, pancytopenia and severe B12 deficiency might initially look like a straight nutritional marrow failure—except she also had premature greying, hepatosplenomegaly with cirrhosis on elastography, pulmonary fibrosis on CT, osteoporosis with fractures and axonal neuropathy. Correcting the B12 helped, but the clinical picture remained out of proportion to a simple deficiency. Clinical exome sequencing revealed a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) — a variant of uncertain significance — and the team concluded the overall phenotype fit a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

What’s interesting (and clinically useful) here: adult TBDs often don’t wear the classic dyskeratosis congenita (DC) mucocutaneous triad. Instead they quietly masquerade as multisystem, organ‑specific disease—especially idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or cryptogenic cirrhosis—sometimes with only subtle skin/hair findings. If you only look for the textbook triad, you’ll miss them.

## How TBDs present in adults — the masquerade
Telomere biology disorders arise from defects in telomere maintenance (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, DKC1, PARN and others). Tissues with high turnover—bone marrow, lung epithelium, liver parenchyma, skin and mucosa—are most vulnerable. In adults the presentation is highly variable: isolated early‑onset idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), cryptogenic cirrhosis, progressive cytopenias, early osteoporosis, nail/skin/hair changes or combinations of the above. Classical DC with oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy and reticular pigmentation is the prototype, but adult TBD often lacks that full triad.

PARN is a reminder of this heterogeneity: biallelic PARN loss causes severe childhood syndromes, while monoallelic PARN variants increasingly turn up in adults with IPF and/or bone marrow susceptibility. In this case the monoallelic PARN variant sits in the R3H domain, is predicted damaging by in‑silico tools, and fits the clinical pattern despite being labelled a VUS.

Clinical pearl: early‑onset organ fibrosis (lung or liver), unexplained progressive cytopenias with a low reticulocyte response, premature greying or early osteoporosis in a young adult = think telomere disease—even if the classic mucocutaneous signs are missing.

## Red flags that should prompt you to think TBD (even in the absence of the DC triad)
- Age‑inappropriate fibrosis: IPF, especially if onset <65, or unexpected cirrhosis in a young adult without usual risk factors.
- Progressive unexplained cytopenias (pancytopenia or isolated marrow failure) with inadequate reticulocyte response despite marrow hyperplasia or normal cytogenetics.
- Premature greying, early osteoporosis/fractures, mucosal changes or subtle hyperpigmentation in a young person.
- Multisystem involvement that is disproportionate to an apparent single cause (for example, very severe B12 deficiency but persistent organ fibrosis and cytopenias after correction).
- Family history of early pulmonary fibrosis, bone marrow failure, or liver disease — but remember family history may be absent due to variable penetrance or de novo mutations.

## A pragmatic diagnostic pathway — when to order flow‑FISH and when to escalate to genetic testing
Step 1 — initial stabilization and rule‑outs. Treat reversible problems promptly (correct B12/folate, treat active infections). Do the basic workup: CBC with indices and reticulocyte count, peripheral smear, iron studies, LDH, bilirubin, DAT, bone marrow exam if indicated, LFTs and liver imaging, and HRCT chest if dyspnea or abnormal CXR.

Step 2 — ask the “disproportion” question. If the clinical picture is out of proportion to reversible causes (persistent cytopenias despite nutritional repletion, early fibrosis, multisystem disease, very early greying/osteoporosis), escalate the evaluation for TBD.

Step 3 — measure telomere length with flow‑FISH at a reference lab. Flow‑FISH is the clinical standard for telomere length measurement. Age‑adjusted thresholds help interpretation: telomere length below the 1st percentile for age is strongly suggestive of a TBD; lengths below the 10th percentile are suspicious in the right clinical context. qPCR assays are less reliable for clinical decision making and are not preferred.

Step 4 — genetic testing. If flow‑FISH shows short telomeres or if clinical suspicion is high despite borderline telomere length, order a targeted telomere gene panel or clinical exome that includes TERT, TERC, RTEL1, DKC1, PARN, NAF1 and others. Which to choose? A focused panel is often faster and easier to interpret for TBDs; whole‑exome sequencing can be useful when the phenotype is atypical, but expect more VUS.

Step 5 — interpret results in context. Many variants will be VUS. Correlate genetics with telomere length, family segregation (test relatives where possible), and phenotype. Discuss functional testing or reclassification with a clinical geneticist or a specialized TBD center when needed.

Step 6 — get multidisciplinary input. If confirmed or strongly suspected, involve hematology (for marrow surveillance and HSCT planning if needed), pulmonology, hepatology, genetics and transplant teams when organ replacement is considered.

Clinical pearl: don’t wait for “definitive” genetics to start surveillance and counseling. Short telomeres or a compelling phenotype justify early precautions (smoking cessation, avoid genotoxic exposures) and referral.

## Common pitfalls that delay diagnosis
Anchoring on a single diagnosis. A severe B12 deficiency will explain macrocytic anaemia and neuropathy, but it shouldn’t end the diagnostic workup if other red flags exist. In this case the duodenal biopsy showed reduced parietal cells and B12 malabsorption; treating that was appropriate, but the team kept looking when the overall illness remained disproportionate.

Over‑interpreting a positive DAT. A weakly positive direct antiglobulin test can distract you into autoimmune hemolysis, but a low reticulocyte count and marrow findings consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis favour a marrow problem.

Relying on family history. Absence of a family history does not exclude a germline telomere disorder because of variable penetrance, late onset in relatives, reduced survival, or de novo variants.

Using qPCR telomere assays for clinical decisions. qPCR can be useful in research but is prone to lab and inter‑assay variability. Flow‑FISH at a reference lab is the clinically actionable test.

Assuming normal bone marrow excludes TBD. Early in the course marrow cellularity may be preserved or show erythroid hyperplasia; aplasia or hypocellularity may come later.

## Management and counseling — practical bedside points
Treat reversible contributors immediately (eg, B12 replacement, correct electrolytes). Provide supportive care: transfusions for symptomatic anaemia, bone health optimization, physiotherapy and symptom management. Counsel patients and families about the genetic nature and the implications for relatives.

Avoid or minimize exposures that stress replicative capacity: smoking, excessive alcohol (relevant for liver disease), and unnecessary cytotoxic chemotherapy or radiation when possible. If marrow failure progresses, referral to a centre experienced in HSCT for TBD is essential—these patients tolerate conventional myeloablative regimens poorly and often need tailored, reduced‑intensity conditioning.

Consider experimental or off‑label options carefully. Androgen therapy (eg, danazol, nandrolone) has shown telomere length increases in small series but long‑term safety and efficacy are not settled. Organ transplantation (lung or liver) is not contraindicated but requires specialized evaluation because telomere dysfunction can complicate transplant outcomes and haematologic reserve.

For VUS: pursue segregation testing in relatives, measure their telomere lengths, and re‑evaluate the variant as more evidence accrues. Engage a clinical geneticist early.

Clinical pearl: in a young adult with unexplained organ fibrosis or cytopenias, begin the conversation about genetic testing and family screening early—even if you haven’t confirmed a pathogenic variant yet. Early recognition changes surveillance, avoids harmful exposures, and informs transplant planning.

## Back to our patient — a sensible, conservative course
In this report the team corrected the patient’s severe B12 deficiency and provided supportive care (transfusions, supplements, physiotherapy). Exome testing found a monoallelic PARN variant (p.Cys205Arg) that is predicted damaging but remains a VUS. The constellation—pancytopenia with low retic index, premature greying, hepatic cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis—made a telomere biology disorder the best unifying diagnosis. The patient is on close surveillance (blood counts, liver and lung monitoring) and her family has been offered genetic counselling.

## Take‑home messages
- Consider TBD in young adults with unexplained early pulmonary fibrosis, cryptogenic cirrhosis, progressive cytopenias, premature greying or early osteoporosis—even when the classic DC mucocutaneous triad is absent.  
- Correct reversible causes (eg, B12) quickly, but don’t stop the evaluation if features are disproportionate or persistent.  
- Measure telomere length by flow‑FISH at a reference lab when suspicion is moderate to high; telomere length <1st percentile for age is strongly suggestive.  
- If telomeres are short or clinical suspicion is high, order a targeted telomere gene panel or clinical exome and involve genetics early.  
- Be wary of common traps: anchoring on a single diagnosis (eg, B12 deficiency), over‑reliance on qPCR telomere tests, and assuming no family history rules out a germline disorder.  
- Management is largely supportive; refer to specialized centres for HSCT or transplant considerations, and provide cascade testing and genetic counselling to family members.

If you see a young patient with unexplained fibrosis + cytopenias, put TBD on the differential and pick up the phone to your genetics or telomere clinic early. These are rare diagnoses, but the consequences for surveillance, treatment choices and family planning are big — and catching them sooner changes management.



# When B12 Deficiency Isn’t the Whole Story: Untangling Ineffective Erythropoiesis, DAT Positivity, and Telomere Risk

## Why this case grabbed my attention
A woman in her late 20s comes in with a 5‑year history of progressive fatigue and pancytopenia. The peripheral smear reads like classic megaloblastosis (dimorphism, hypersegmented neutrophils) and she has severe vitamin B12 deficiency — a tidy explanation, right? Except she had a low reticulocyte count (0.39%), positive DAT for IgG (2+), elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin, iron overload in the marrow (high ferritin and iron), hepatosplenomegaly with transient elastography consistent with cirrhosis, and CT chest changes compatible with pulmonary fibrosis. She also had premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation and osteoporosis. Genetic testing found a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), labelled a Variant of Uncertain Significance but plausibly relevant to telomere biology.

What made this case educational is the intersection of a reversible cause (B12 deficiency) that could explain much of the marrow picture, a positive DAT that could send you down the autoimmune hemolysis pathway, and clinical red flags suggesting an underlying marrow vulnerability (telomere biology disorder, TBD). The danger is treating the wrong problem — for example, reflexively starting steroids for a “positive DAT” — while missing an inherited marrow disorder that changes surveillance and long‑term management.

## The clinical conundrum — how to reconcile the data
There are three overlapping laboratory/clinical patterns here:

1) Classic features of megaloblastic/ineffective erythropoiesis: dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, hypersegmented neutrophils, erythroid hyperplasia on bone marrow and low reticulocyte response despite anemia. Severe B12/folate deficiency fits this exactly.

2) Biochemical evidence suggesting red‑cell destruction: elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin. Those would normally steer you toward hemolysis.

3) A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT IgG 2+), which usually triggers consideration of warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA).

The key to reconciling these is recognizing intramedullary destruction — ineffective erythropoiesis — as a source of “hemolysis” indices. When erythroid precursors are dysmature and destroyed within the marrow, you see elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin but a low reticulocyte count because the marrow is failing to produce viable reticulocytes for the circulation. Iron is liberated and accumulates (high serum iron and ferritin, increased marrow iron stores). That pattern contrasts with peripheral hemolysis, where reticulocyte count is typically elevated because the marrow responds to increased peripheral loss.

In this patient, the low retic count and marrow picture make ineffective erythropoiesis the dominant mechanism for cytopenia, and the positive DAT becomes a contextual finding rather than definitive proof of clinically significant AIHA.

## DAT: what it really tells you (and what it doesn’t)
The DAT demonstrates immunoglobulin and/or complement bound to a patient’s RBCs. A positive DAT means RBCs are coated with antibody or complement — not that the patient necessarily has brisk, clinically important hemolysis. Always interpret DAT alongside the clinical picture and hemolysis panel.

If you suspect AIHA you should see a constellation of findings: falling hemoglobin, jaundice, high retic response, low haptoglobin, spherocytes on smear, a positive eluate/positive indirect antiglobulin test (if free antibody is present in serum), and often clinical signs of hemolysis. A DAT alone — especially when the retic response is inappropriately low — is insufficient to diagnose AIHA and certainly insufficient to justify systemic immunosuppression.

In this case the indirect test was negative (no free circulating antibody detected), the retic was low, and the marrow showed erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective maturation. The team reasonably deprioritized AIHA as the primary diagnosis.

## What to do at the bedside (a pragmatic approach)
When you face a patient with severe B12 deficiency, pancytopenia, a low retic count and a positive DAT, follow this practical sequence:

1. Treat reversible contributors immediately. Severe B12 deficiency should be corrected promptly (the team used IV cyanocobalamin followed by maintenance) and other nutritional deficiencies addressed. Provide red‑cell transfusion for symptomatic anemia.

2. Put the DAT into context. Order a full hemolysis panel (LDH, indirect bilirubin, haptoglobin, retic count), review the smear for spherocytes vs megaloblastic changes, and consider an eluate if you strongly suspect clinically relevant AIHA. Avoid reflexive steroids unless there is clear, progressive peripheral hemolysis.

3. Evaluate the marrow. If there is an inappropriately low reticulocyte response despite biochemical “hemolysis,” arrange bone marrow examination and iron studies. Evidence of erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective erythropoiesis and increased iron stores points away from peripheral immune destruction.

4. Look for extra‑haematologic clues. Young age with pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, premature greying, mucocutaneous findings, neuropathy, and early osteoporosis should prompt consideration of inherited marrow vulnerability such as a TBD.

5. Escalate to genetic and telomere testing when indicated. In appropriate patients, order targeted next‑generation sequencing panels for telomere genes and consider telomere length assessment (flow‑FISH) and genetics referral. Counseling the patient and family is essential.

6. Plan follow‑up and surveillance. Even when cytopenias improve after correcting deficiencies, patients with suspected TBD need ongoing monitoring of blood counts, pulmonary and hepatic function, and consideration of referrals to centres with experience in telomere disorders if marrow failure or organ fibrosis progresses.

## When to suspect a telomere biology disorder
TBDs are heterogeneous, and the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita is frequently absent. Clues that should raise your suspicion in a young adult include unexplained, disproportionate marrow failure (pancytopenia with low retic), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or early interstitial lung disease, hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis without other cause, premature greying, unexplained osteoporosis and a family history of marrow failure or fibrotic disease. In this patient those flags were present, and sequencing identified a monoallelic PARN variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) affecting the R3H domain — a biologically plausible hit, even though the variant remains classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance.

## Management implications — why the distinction matters
The distinction between AIHA and ineffective erythropoiesis driven by a marrow vulnerability is not academic. Treating presumed AIHA with high‑dose steroids, rituximab or other immunosuppression in a patient with an underlying TBD or evolving aplastic phenotype can cause harm and delay appropriate evaluation. Conversely, identifying a TBD alters counselling, surveillance for pulmonary and hepatic complications, family testing, and how you would approach bone marrow transplantation (patients with short telomeres have higher toxicity from standard conditioning regimens; HSCT decisions should be made in expert centres). There are limited options aimed at telomere biology (androgen therapy has shown some promise in small studies), but most management remains supportive and anticipatory.

In this case the team corrected B12, provided transfusion support and surveillance; the patient improved symptomatically and her counts stabilised at two‑week follow‑up, while a genetic framework was put in place to guide long‑term care.

## Take‑home messages
- A positive DAT indicates RBCs are coated with antibody/complement — it does not, by itself, prove clinically significant autoimmune hemolysis.  
- The reticulocyte count is critical: a brisk retic response points to peripheral hemolysis; an inappropriately low retic with elevated LDH/indirect bilirubin suggests intramedullary destruction/ineffective erythropoiesis.  
- Severe B12 deficiency commonly causes ineffective erythropoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia, low retic, high ferritin/iron and features that can mimic hemolysis. Correct reversible deficiencies first.  
- Don’t reflex to immunosuppression for an isolated positive DAT; treat AIHA only when the clinical and laboratory picture supports active peripheral hemolysis.  
- In young adults with disproportionate cytopenias plus pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, premature greying or other premature‑aging features, consider telomere biology disorders; pursue marrow evaluation, telomere/gene testing and genetics referral.  
- Management of TBDs is largely supportive and requires longitudinal surveillance; HSCT and other interventions should be coordinated with centres experienced in telomere disorders.

This case is a good reminder: treat the reversible causes, interpret tests in clinical context (especially the DAT and retic count), and keep a low threshold for looking for an underlying marrow vulnerability when the phenotype is disproportionate or multisystem.



# PARN on the Edge: How to Manage a VUS When the Phenotype Fits

This is one of those real-world genetics problems that clinicians increasingly face: a young patient whose clinical story screams “telomere biology disorder,” but whose genetic report hands you a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). Our patient — a woman in her late 20s with progressive pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis and radiographic pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying and osteoporosis — carries a monoallelic PARN missense change (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) located in the R3H domain. In silico tools predict damage, the variant is vanishingly rare in population databases, and the gene itself is intolerant to disruptive variation. Still: VUS. What do you do next? This post walks through a practical, clinician-facing pathway to adjudicate this kind of variant, gather the evidence that matters, and manage the patient while the genetics catch up.

## Why this case is interesting (and uncomfortable)
PARN is a telomere-related gene with a biologically plausible link to this phenotype. Biallelic PARN loss causes severe, early dyskeratosis congenita–type syndromes; monoallelic PARN variants have been reported in adults with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure. The identified variant sits in the R3H domain (aa 178–245), a functionally important region for nucleic acid interactions. In ACMG terms the lab could justify PM1 (critical domain), PM2 (absent/rare), PP3 (multiple in silico predictors), and PP2 (gene constrained for benign missense). Still, without functional data, segregation, telomere-length confirmation or multiple case reports, the variant remains a VUS.

That gap — a strong phenotype and a tantalizing variant that’s not yet “pathogenic” — is what makes the case clinically important. We must balance the risk of over-interpreting a VUS (making irreversible decisions based on uncertain genetics) against the risk of under-recognition (missing an inherited telomeropathy that affects transplant decisions, donor selection, counseling, and surveillance).

## Quick biology recap: why PARN fits the phenotype
PARN is a deadenylase involved in mRNA poly(A) tail shortening and in the 3′-end maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Impaired PARN function can reduce mature TERC, lower telomerase activity, and accelerate telomere attrition — hitting high-turnover tissues (bone marrow, lung, liver, skin, mucosa) hardest. Clinically that tracks with marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis and premature ageing signs — exactly the organ mix in this patient.

## What the lab-level ACMG call tells you (and what it doesn’t)
The lab’s VUS classification reflects the current weight of evidence:
- PM1: the change lies in the R3H domain — a plausible functional hotspot.
- PM2: the variant is absent or extremely rare in population datasets.
- PP3/PP2: multiple computational tools predict deleteriousness and PARN has low benign missense variability.

These are useful pieces of evidence, but they are moderate/supporting; ACMG needs stronger lines (well-validated functional data, robust segregation, de novo occurrence, or statistically significant enrichment of the variant in affected individuals) to move a VUS to likely pathogenic/pathogenic. In short: the genetics are suggestive, not definitive.

## Pragmatic steps to gather more evidence (the clinician’s checklist)
1) Order age‑adjusted telomere length testing (flow‑FISH) as the next step that most directly addresses pathogenicity. Flow‑FISH in lymphocytes and granulocytes is the clinical gold standard: age-adjusted telomere length below the 1st percentile strongly supports a telomeropathy; 1–10th percentile is suggestive; normal length does not fully exclude disease (mosaicism, tissue‑specific effects). If blood yields equivocal results, consider telomere length in fibroblasts (research labs).

2) Pursue focused functional testing if available through a research or reference lab. Useful assays include quantitative TERC measurements, assessment of TERC 3′ processing, telomerase activity (TRAP assay), or PARN enzymatic/deadenylase assays. Robust, replicated functional impairment would meet strong functional evidence (PS3) under ACMG.

3) Family studies (segregation): test parents and available affected or older unaffected relatives. De novo occurrence (with paternity/maternity confirmation) would be strong evidence (PS2); co‑segregation with disease in multiple relatives strengthens the case (PP1 escalating to moderate/strong depending on numbers). Conversely, finding the variant in an unaffected older relative argues against full penetrance — but interpret with caution because telomere disorders often show reduced penetrance and age-dependent expression.

4) Search for additional hits. Re-examine the exome for other rare variants in canonical telomere genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, etc.) or for CNVs that could act as modifiers or second hits. Oligogenic contributions are reported in telomeropathies.

5) Submit and share data. Put the variant and detailed phenotype into ClinVar or disease registries and query whether other labs/clinicians have seen the same variant. Variant reclassification often requires collective evidence from multiple groups.

6) Plan for periodic reanalysis. Genetic knowledge moves fast. Request that the lab re-analyses the VUS every 12–24 months or sooner if new functional data or case reports appear.

## What ordering and interpreting flow‑FISH looks like in practice
Flow‑FISH reports telomere length as an age-adjusted percentile for lymphocytes and granulocytes. Practical points:
- Use a lab experienced in clinical flow‑FISH; pre-analytic handling matters.
- Lymphocyte telomere length <1st percentile for age is highly specific for a telomeropathy in adults (this is the single most clinically actionable test you can order).
- Both lymphocyte and granulocyte shortening increases confidence; discordant results (e.g., normal blood telomeres but short fibroblasts) can reflect mosaicism or tissue‑restricted effects.
- qPCR telomere assays are less reproducible across labs and should not substitute for flow‑FISH when making clinical decisions.

In this patient, a profoundly short age-adjusted telomere length would materially strengthen the argument that the PARN variant is disease‑causing and would justify more telomere‑directed counseling and testing of relatives.

## Managing the patient now — “treat the phenotype, respect the uncertainty”
Don’t let a VUS paralyze clinical care. Manage organ disease according to standard practice, but with telomere‑disorder awareness:

- Continue definitive treatment of reversible contributors (this patient needs ongoing B12 replacement; transfusion support for symptomatic anaemia).
- Institute surveillance: frequent CBC monitoring, baseline and interval pulmonary function testing (including DLCO) and HRCT as indicated, and hepatology follow-up with serial elastography/LFTs given cirrhosis. Frequency is individualized based on clinical trajectory; for many adult TBD patients this means formal pulmonology and hepatology follow-up at least every 6–12 months or sooner if symptomatic change.
- Avoid telomere‑damaging or highly genotoxic exposures where possible: limit alkylator/irradiation exposure and be cautious with cytotoxic chemotherapy. If hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) is being considered, ensure telomere‑aware planning: reduced‑intensity conditioning regimens, experienced transplant center, and careful donor selection (exclude related donors with the variant or with short telomeres).
- Pharmacologic telomere modulation (eg, danazol) has limited, early evidence of telomere elongation in some patients — but carries real risks (hepatotoxicity, thrombosis). In our patient with cirrhosis, danazol is contraindicated; such decisions need a telomere-disease specialist discussion and risk‑benefit judgment.
- Lifestyle counseling: smoking cessation, avoiding excessive alcohol (important with hepatic disease), and vaccinations to reduce pulmonary infection risk.
- If lung transplant or HSCT are being considered, inform the transplant teams about the suspected telomeropathy early — outcomes and conditioning strategies differ for these patients.

## Counseling the patient and the family
Be transparent and precise: explain that the genetic result is a VUS — it is neither proof that the variant causes disease nor proof that it’s benign. Explain what additional evidence could change that (telomere length, family segregation, functional testing). Offer parental testing and targeted family testing to help reclassify the variant; emphasize that predictive testing of asymptomatic relatives (especially children) is generally not recommended for a VUS. Discuss reproductive options only if/when the variant is reclassified to pathogenic/likely pathogenic or if family testing demonstrates cosegregation.

Document the plan: who will order flow‑FISH, when genetic reanalysis will occur, and that the team will recontact the family if the variant is reclassified.

## A practical VUS adjudication workflow you can use tomorrow
1. Order age‑adjusted flow‑FISH (lymphocytes ± granulocytes).  
2. Request targeted parental testing for the variant.  
3. Share the variant + HPO-coded phenotype with the diagnostic lab and submit to ClinVar/registry.  
4. Ask the lab about research or referral options for PARN functional assays.  
5. Continue phenotype-driven care (supportive therapy, surveillance) and avoid telomere‑damaging exposures.  
6. Reanalyze genomic data and the variant classification at least annually or sooner if new data emerge.

## Closing thoughts
A VUS isn’t “no information” — it’s a conditional signal. When the phenotype lines up tightly with the gene’s biology (as with PARN and telomere‑related fibrosis/bone marrow disease), the clinician’s job is to gather orthogonal data (especially telomere length and family segregation), treat the patient’s current problems, and put a process in place for reclassification. That combination — careful phenotyping, directed testing, multidisciplinary management, and data sharing — is how a VUS moves from frustrating ambiguity to clinical clarity.

## Key take‑home messages
- A VUS in a telomere gene does not equal “not important.” If the clinical picture fits, pursue age‑adjusted flow‑FISH as the single most informative next test.  
- Flow‑FISH lymphocyte telomere length <1st percentile for age is strongly suggestive of a telomeropathy; 1–10th percentile is supportive. qPCR is less reliable.  
- PM1/PM2/PP2/PP3 (domain, rarity, gene constraint, in silico) can justify a VUS but are usually insufficient without functional, segregation or population enrichment evidence.  
- Functional assays (TERC processing, telomerase activity, PARN enzymatic assays) and family segregation (including de novo evidence) are the highest‑value evidence to reclassify a VUS.  
- Manage the patient’s phenotype aggressively and avoid genotoxic or hepatotoxic agents until telomere status is clarified; plan HSCT/lung transplant with telomere‑aware teams.  
- Share data: submit the variant and clinical phenotype to ClinVar/registries and plan periodic reanalysis (every 12–24 months) — collective data are what reclassifies VUS into actionable variants.



# Two Fibroses, One Biology: Hepatic and Pulmonary Fibrosis in Telomere Syndromes

What grabbed our attention in the case above? A woman in her late 20s with 5 years of progressive fatigue, pancytopenia and severe vitamin B12 deficiency who, despite correction of nutrients, still had multisystem disease — premature greying, osteoporosis, hepatosplenomegaly with transient-elastography–proven cirrhosis (FibroScan 18.1 kPa), and CT evidence of pulmonary fibrosis. Whole‑exome testing found a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, VUS). In short: two fibrotic organs (lung and liver) in a young adult with bone‑marrow vulnerability — a classic red flag for a telomere biology disorder (TBD). The “surprise” here is liver disease: hepatic fibrosis is under‑recognized in TBDs, and can coexist with pulmonary fibrosis even when the classical mucocutaneous features of dyskeratosis congenita are absent.

## Why telomere biology links liver and lung fibrosis
Telomeres protect chromosome ends; telomerase (TERT/TERC and accessory factors) maintains them. Defects in telomerase or telomere‑processing genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, DKC1, etc.) lead to accelerated telomere shortening and premature cellular senescence. Tissues with high replicative demand or that undergo repeated injury—bone marrow, mucosa, skin, lung epithelium and hepatocytes—are particularly vulnerable. Inherited telomeropathies classically present as bone‑marrow failure or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), but emerging data (including registry analyses) show that liver fibrosis and cirrhosis are common in adults with telomerase mutations. PARN loss impairs TERC maturation and can present phenotypically across a spectrum from bone‑marrow failure to IPF and now recognized hepatic involvement.

## When to think TBD in a patient with fibrosis
Young age of onset (or fibrosis out of proportion to risk factors), multisystem involvement (cytopenias, premature greying or hair loss, early osteoporosis, mucosal findings or neuropathy), and family history raise suspicion. Nutritional deficiencies (like the severe B12 deficiency in this case) may coexist and can distract you — sustained multisystem dysfunction after correction should prompt a genetic/telomere workup. Importantly, classical mucocutaneous stigmata may be absent.

## A practical screening bundle for suspected/confirmed TBD with concern for liver and lung involvement
Start with a careful baseline assessment and then personalize follow‑up frequency to clinical trajectory and mutation severity. At baseline obtain a full blood count with reticulocyte count and peripheral smear, comprehensive liver biochemical panel (AST, ALT, ALP, GGT, bilirubin, albumin), INR, platelet count and renal function. For the liver, do an abdominal ultrasound to look for hepatosplenomegaly and steatosis, and obtain elastography (transient elastography or MRE where available) to quantify fibrosis and capture steatosis (CAP). Interpret liver‑stiffness values in context—markedly elevated values (for example FibroScan values in the high teens as in this case) support advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis and should trigger portal‑hypertension assessment (platelets, splenic size, and consideration of endoscopy for varices when signs of portal hypertension are present). Follow cirrhosis surveillance guidelines (HCC surveillance, variceal screening) if cirrhosis is confirmed.

For the lungs, get a baseline high‑resolution CT chest and pulmonary function testing with spirometry, lung volumes and DLCO, plus a 6‑minute walk test. Repeat PFTs at least every 6–12 months or sooner for symptoms; repeat HRCT for new/worsening symptoms or when PFTs show decline. Measure telomere length (flow‑FISH is the laboratory standard) and provide genetic testing for telomere‑related genes; involve genetic counselling early. Also screen for osteoporosis (DEXA), and check vitamin D, calcium and PTH given frequent bone disease in TBDs.

## Antifibrotics, androgens and other disease‑modifying therapies — the balance of benefit and organ risk
Antifibrotic therapy (nintedanib or pirfenidone) is the standard of care for progressive fibrosing interstitial lung disease and IPF, irrespective of genetic cause. Observational series and case reports suggest that antifibrotics can slow lung‑function decline in familial and genetic forms of pulmonary fibrosis, but randomized data specifically in telomere mutation carriers are limited. Two practical cautions when the liver is involved: both agents can cause hepatotoxicity and require baseline and serial LFT monitoring; nintedanib dose adjustments/avoidance are advised in significant hepatic impairment and it carries a bleeding risk that matters in portal hypertension/thrombocytopenia; pirfenidone also requires LFT monitoring and has photosensitivity and GI side effects. In patients with advanced hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis, the risk–benefit calculus must be individualized and discussed with hepatology and interstitial lung disease specialists.

Androgens (danazol, nandrolone) have shown biologic activity in telomere disease (telomere length increases and hematologic responses in small trials/series), but androgens are potentially hepatotoxic and therefore are often contraindicated or used with extreme caution in the presence of significant liver disease. This is a key practical tension: an agent that may help marrow disease can injure the liver that is already vulnerable in TBDs.

## Transplantation and procedural considerations
Hematopoietic stem‑cell transplant (HSCT) can cure marrow failure but patients with telomere syndromes are exquisitely sensitive to standard conditioning regimens and at higher risk for organ toxicity (lung and liver). Reduced‑intensity, tailored conditioning regimens and centers experienced with telomere disorders are recommended. Lung transplantation is an option for end‑stage PF, but post‑transplant complications (prolonged cytopenias, infections, poor wound healing) are more common in telomeropathy patients, and pre‑transplant evaluation must screen thoroughly for occult liver disease and marrow reserve. Combined organ transplantation (e.g., lung plus liver) is rarely performed and requires multidisciplinary discussion at referral centers.

## Medications and exposure precautions that matter when both liver and lung are at risk
Avoid smoking and alcohol — both accelerate organ injury. Avoid known pulmonary toxins (bleomycin, certain nitrofurantoin exposures, amiodarone when alternatives exist) and avoid chronic hepatotoxins (methotrexate, high‑dose anabolic steroids, prolonged azathioprine) unless unavoidable and with close monitoring. Be cautious with drugs that can unmask marrow dysfunction or cause idiosyncratic liver injury. Vaccinate (influenza, pneumococcal, COVID, hepatitis B where indicated). When starting antifibrotics or androgens, obtain baseline hepatic tests and monitor regularly. Finally, counsel patients about occupational and environmental exposures (organic dusts, silica, solvents) that might accelerate pulmonary fibrosis.

## Putting it into practice — a simple clinical approach
If you see a young adult with unexplained pulmonary fibrosis, early cirrhosis, unexplained cytopenias or premature greying/osteoporosis: don’t stop at treating reversible contributors (like B12 deficiency); broaden the differential to include telomere biology disorders. Order the screening bundle above, discuss telomere length measurement and genetic testing, involve hepatology and ILD/transplant services early, and tailor treatment choices (antifibrotic and androgen use) to the liver phenotype. Multidisciplinary follow‑up and family counseling are essential because penetrance is variable and genetic results (including VUS) may be reclassified over time.

## Key take‑home messages
- Young age or multisystem disease (bone‑marrow dysfunction + lung or liver fibrosis) should prompt consideration of a telomere biology disorder even when classic mucocutaneous signs are absent.
- Screen both organs: baseline CBC and retic, liver panel, ultrasound ± elastography, and HRCT + full PFTs; measure telomere length (flow‑FISH) and do genetic testing with genetic counselling.
- Antifibrotics (nintedanib, pirfenidone) can be considered for telomere‑related pulmonary fibrosis but require careful LFT monitoring and dose considerations in hepatic impairment; androgens may help marrow but are hepatotoxic and often contraindicated with significant liver disease.
- Pre‑transplant assessment needs to account for marrow reserve and liver disease; HSCT and lung transplant carry higher risks in TBDs and should be done at experienced centers with tailored protocols.
- Avoid smoking, alcohol and drugs/occupational exposures that accelerate lung or liver injury; coordinate care (hepatology, ILD, hematology, genetics) and follow patients longitudinally — telomere syndromes evolve.

This case reminds us that one biology—short, dysfunctional telomeres—can drive fibrosis in more than one organ. Recognizing that link changes how we screen, counsel and treat these patients.



# Transplanting on Short Telomeres: Conditioning Choices, Antifibrotics, and Immunosuppression Pitfalls

## Why this case matters
A woman in her late 20s with progressive fatigue, pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (and a monoallelic PARN variant) forces you to think beyond “nutrient deficiency ± autoimmune disease.” This is a classic transplant dilemma in telomere biology disorders (TBDs): the organs that drive the indication for transplant (bone marrow, lungs) are also the organs most vulnerable to transplant-related genotoxic stress, and the patient’s liver disease and poor marrow reserve make standard conditioning and routine immunosuppression potentially catastrophic. The questions this case asks are practical and urgent: when do you refer for hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) or lung transplant, what conditioning and graft choices minimize lethal toxicity, how do you manage CMV and other infections when antiviral drugs suppress marrow, and can androgens buy time without burning bridges to transplant?

## Telomeres and transplant — why short telomeres change the calculus
TBDs -> short/dysfunctional telomeres -> exhausted replicative capacity in high-turnover tissues (haematopoietic stem cells, hepatocytes, pulmonary epithelium, skin). That biology translates directly into greater sensitivity to DNA-damaging agents: full-intensity alkylator regimens and total body irradiation (TBI) produce profound marrow toxicity, severe mucositis, delayed engraftment, hepatic veno-occlusive disease, exaggerated pulmonary toxicity and poor wound healing. Observational series have shown higher early transplant morbidity and mortality when standard myeloablative regimens are used in TBD patients. The practical takeaway: standard “all-comers” conditioning is often too toxic — tailor intensity and agents to the telomere problem.

## HSCT in TBD — whom and when
HSCT remains the only curative option for progressive marrow failure/MDS/AML arising in TBD, but timing is tricky. Indications mirror other marrow-failure syndromes (progressive cytopenias, transfusion dependence, clonal evolution), but you must weigh (a) the severity of pulmonary and hepatic comorbidity, (b) the trajectory of marrow failure, and (c) donor availability. In practice you should involve a multidisciplinary team early (hematology, transplant, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics). In many centres the decision is individualized: transplant earlier for rapidly progressive marrow failure before irreversible lung/liver damage accrues, or delay/bridge therapy if organ disease makes HSCT prohibitively risky.

## Conditioning and graft choices — practical principles
The single most important principle is to reduce genotoxic stress while preserving engraftment.

- Favor reduced-intensity, radiation-sparing regimens. Avoid TBI if possible.
- Avoid or minimize high-dose alkylators (full-dose busulfan, cyclophosphamide). When an alkylator is used, dose-reduce and perform therapeutic drug monitoring (eg, busulfan AUC-guided dosing).
- Consider fludarabine-based reduced-intensity platforms combined with low-exposure alkylator (low-dose busulfan) or treosulfan. Treosulfan has been used as a reduced-toxicity alkylator alternative in fragile patients, but data are limited — discuss with your transplant pharmacist/centre.
- Use serotherapy (ATG or alemtuzumab) judiciously for engraftment/GVHD modulation, balancing infection risk.
- Donor selection matters enormously: do not use related donors without genetic testing for the causative mutation and, when available, telomere length assessment. A phenotypically healthy sibling may still carry the same mutation and short telomeres.
- Prefer bone marrow grafts over peripheral blood when treating marrow failure to reduce chronic GVHD risk.
- Be cautious with post-transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy) for haploidentical grafts — PTCy is an alkylator-based approach and may be poorly tolerated; if haploidentical transplant is the only option, discuss dose-modified strategies with expert centres.

There is no universal “best” conditioning protocol for TBD — choose a centre-experienced regimen that minimizes irradiation and alkylator exposure, and be prepared to individualize drug selection and dosing.

## Infectious risk and CMV vigilance — practical tips
TBD patients are infection-prone because of baseline cytopenias and mucosal fragility, and HSCT further increases that risk. The infection strategy needs to balance antiviral efficacy with marrow-sparing.

- In HSCT recipients who are CMV-seropositive, letermovir prophylaxis is attractive because it reduces CMV reactivation without the marrow suppression associated with valganciclovir/ganciclovir. When available, plan for letermovir prophylaxis and continued PCR surveillance.
- If CMV disease develops or high-level viraemia requires therapy, ganciclovir/valganciclovir can worsen cytopenias; foscarnet is non-marrow-toxic but nephrotoxic — choose according to the patient’s marrow reserve and renal function, with transplant-infectious disease input.
- Pneumocystis prophylaxis: TMP–SMX is the most effective agent but causes cytopenias. If TMP–SMX is not tolerated, use alternatives (monthly inhaled pentamidine, atovaquone or dapsone) with appropriate G6PD screening and clinical monitoring.
- Antifungal prophylaxis should be tailored. Echinocandins are not myelotoxic but are IV-only; azoles (posaconazole/voriconazole) are oral but have drug–drug interactions with calcineurin inhibitors and can affect liver enzymes — important in a patient with hepatic fibrosis. Coordinate with pharmacy.
- Vaccination and infection-prevention measures should be optimized pre-transplant where possible (inactivated vaccines); live vaccines are contraindicated after HSCT until immune reconstitution allows.

## Antifibrotics and the peri-transplant lung patient
For patients with IPF, antifibrotic therapy (nintedanib, pirfenidone) slows progression and can buy time to transplantation. Practical points:

- Continue antifibrotic therapy when it is helping to stabilize disease, but coordinate with the transplant team about perioperative stopping rules. Both drugs can affect liver enzymes, so hepatic dysfunction (as in this case) requires special attention.
- Nintedanib has bleeding signals and carries some perioperative risk; discuss holding it before surgery.
- Antifibrotics do not eliminate the need for transplant in progressive IPF; they are a bridge, not a cure.

## Androgens (danazol/nandrolone) — bridge therapy with limits
Small clinical series and trials have shown that androgens can improve blood counts and, in some cases, increase telomere length. That makes them an attractive bridge to HSCT in patients with transfusion dependence. Caveats:

- Androgens are hepatotoxic and can precipitate or worsen cholestasis and hepatic tumours — a major concern in patients with hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis (like the case patient). Use with great caution or avoid in significant liver disease.
- Side effects include virilization, lipid abnormalities and thrombosis.
- If used as a bridge, closely monitor liver enzymes, synthetic function and hematologic response, and coordinate timing with transplant surgery/risk planning.

## Lung transplant in short telomeres — blunt edges and practical adjustments
Short telomere patients who are candidates for lung transplant have worse perioperative hematologic and infectious complications in multiple series. Practical considerations for lung transplant teams:

- Pre-transplant, screen for telomere gene mutations and measure telomere length where available; perform a full multi-organ assessment (liver fibrosis staging, bone marrow reserve, PFTs, HRCT).
- Expect exaggerated cytopenias post-op, and plan transfusion and hematology support.
- Anastomotic healing may be impaired. Avoid early use of mTOR inhibitors (sirolimus/everolimus) which exacerbate wound-healing problems and have been linked to bronchial anastomotic dehiscence; delay mTOR agents until healing is assured, and prefer calcineurin inhibitor–based regimens initially.
- Avoid azathioprine when marrow reserve is marginal; mycophenolate also suppresses marrow and may need dose adjustments.
- CMV prophylaxis in lung transplant is often valganciclovir; the marrow-suppressive effects complicate management. Discuss individualized strategies with transplant infectious disease — pre-emptive PCR-based therapy, dose adjustments, or alternative agents depending on marrow function.
- Severe concomitant liver disease may push consideration of combined lung–liver transplantation, but that is highly resource- and expertise-intensive and has limited experience — discuss at a centre that does combined transplants.

## A practical pre-transplant checklist for suspected/confirmed TBD
Before contemplating HSCT or lung transplant in a patient like this PARN carrier, practical steps you should ensure are done and documented:

- Refer early to genetics for confirmatory testing, family screening and pre-donation testing of any related donor.
- Measure telomere length (flow-FISH or other validated assay) if available — it informs risk and donor selection.
- Multi-organ staging: high-resolution CT chest and full pulmonary function testing; transient elastography/liver staging and hepatology consult; bone marrow evaluation and cytogenetics; bone density.
- Correct reversible contributors (B12/folate deficiency in this case), optimize nutrition and treat active infections pre-transplant.
- Convene multidisciplinary transplant planning (hematology/transplant, pulmonology, hepatology, infectious disease, pharmacy, genetics).
- Plan conditioning and GVHD prophylaxis that minimizes irradiation and high-dose alkylators; identify an experienced centre and document the rationale for regimen choice.
- Plan infectious prophylaxis strategy up front (consider letermovir for HSCT CMV prophylaxis), and have alternatives for PJP and fungal prophylaxis if TMP–SMX or azoles are poorly tolerated.
- Decide whether androgen therapy is a safe bridge (avoid if significant hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis) and institute rigorous monitoring if used.
- Counsel the patient and family explicitly about the higher risk of post-transplant cytopenias, infection, poor wound healing and the need for intensive follow-up.

## Clinical pearls and take‑home points
- Short telomeres change transplant risk: they increase sensitivity to irradiation and alkylators, worsen mucosal/dermal/hepatic healing, and amplify infectious complications — plan accordingly.  
- For HSCT in TBD, use reduced-intensity, radiation-sparing conditioning and minimize high-dose alkylators; monitor busulfan if used and prefer bone marrow grafts when treating marrow failure.  
- Do not use a related donor without testing for the familial mutation and, when possible, telomere length — an “apparently healthy” sibling may share the risk.  
- CMV management should prioritize marrow-sparing options: letermovir prophylaxis post-HSCT is attractive; when therapy is required, balance marrow toxicity (ganciclovir) against renal toxicity (foscarnet).  
- Androgens can improve counts and sometimes telomere length (useful as a bridge) but are hepatotoxic — avoid or use with extreme caution in cirrhosis.  
- In lung transplant candidates with short telomeres expect exaggerated cytopenias, wound-healing problems and infectious risk; delay mTOR inhibitors and avoid azathioprine when marrow reserve is limited.  
- Early multidisciplinary planning and referral to centres experienced with TBDs (genetics + transplant) materially change outcomes — don’t wait until organ failure is irreversible.

This patient’s story is a reminder: recognizing a TBD in a young adult with pancytopenia plus liver and lung fibrosis changes everything — it forces a more cautious, tailored transplant strategy and a team-based approach.



# After the Diagnosis: Building a Practical Surveillance and Family Plan for Telomere Biology Disorders

Why this case matters
This patient — a woman in her late 20s with pancytopenia, severe B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis and CT-proven pulmonary fibrosis, ultimately found to carry a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, VUS) — is exactly the kind of presentation that turns a genetic label into clinical urgency. She lacks the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita, yet has multisystem involvement typical of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). The practical question becomes: now that we suspect a TBD, what do we actually do for this patient and her family tomorrow, next month and long term?

This post is a pragmatic playbook for clinicians: concrete surveillance targets for each organ system, infection/vaccine guidance, medication and exposure cautions, when and how to cascade-test relatives, reproductive counseling basics, and the bedside triggers that should prompt early referral to specialist transplant teams.

First conversation: what to tell the patient today
Be honest and actionable. Say something like: “We found a PARN variant and your clinical picture fits a telomere disorder. That doesn’t always mean the variant is definitively disease‑causing, but it does change how we watch you: more frequent blood, lung and liver checks, some extra vaccinations, and we’ll offer genetic counseling and testing for close relatives. We will also avoid drugs and exposures that can hurt the bone marrow or liver, and we’ll involve specialists early so that if transplantation becomes necessary you’ll be referred to teams experienced with telomere disorders.”

Principles to guide a surveillance plan
- Treat reversible contributors aggressively (e.g., the patient’s severe B12 deficiency — already being repleted) but don’t assume full resolution rules out an underlying TBD: marrow vulnerability may be “unmasked” by nutritional stress.
- Use phenotype + laboratory telomere assessment + genetics together. One data point rarely suffices.
- Act early on organ dysfunction and on family testing; outcomes are better when transplant/organ teams are engaged before urgent deterioration.
- Minimize iatrogenic harm: avoid marrow‑toxic or hepatotoxic therapies without specialist input; don’t accept related hematopoietic donors without donor testing.

Build the organ‑specific surveillance plan

Hematology
Start with a baseline panel (already done in this case): CBC with differential and reticulocyte count, peripheral smear, iron studies and ferritin, B12/folate, LDH, bilirubin, DAT if hemolysis suspected, and the bone marrow biopsy/cytogenetics the team already performed. Practical follow-up: while recovering from B12 repletion and if counts are stable, check CBC + retic every 1–3 months initially; if stable at 6–12 months the interval can be lengthened to every 6 months. Repeat bone marrow and cytogenetics (and an MDS/overlap molecular panel) promptly if a new or progressive cytopenia appears, if transfusion dependence develops, or if there are new dysplastic features. Monitor transfusion burden and ferritin regularly if transfusions continue. Consider flow‑FISH telomere length testing on leukocyte subsets if not already done — it helps risk‑stratify marrow failure and can add weight to a VUS. Early hematology/transplant discussion is indicated for progressive marrow failure (transfusion dependence, recurrent severe infections, bleeding from severe thrombocytopenia, or clonal evolution to MDS/AML).

Pulmonology
Get a baseline high‑resolution CT (HRCT) if not already available (this patient has ground‑glass and subpleural fibrosis) and baseline pulmonary function tests with lung volumes and DLCO, plus resting and exertional oxygenation. For monitoring, a pragmatic approach is PFTs every 3–6 months when symptomatic or showing decline, otherwise every 6–12 months; repeat HRCT only for clinical change or unexplained PFT decline. Early referral to a lung transplant center should be made when there is progressive physiological decline (for example, a clinically meaningful fall in FVC or DLCO, worsening hypoxemia, or increasing oxygen requirements) — patients with TBD can progress rapidly and do better when evaluated early by transplant teams familiar with telomere disease. Strong counseling point: complete smoking cessation is essential — smoking accelerates pulmonary fibrosis and worsens outcomes.

Hepatology
This patient’s transient elastography (18.1 kPa) is consistent with cirrhosis. For all patients with TBD and any degree of fibrosis, baseline LFTs, imaging with liver ultrasound and elastography if available, and hepatology input are indicated. If cirrhosis is present, initiate standard cirrhosis surveillance: hepatocellular carcinoma screening (abdominal ultrasound ± AFP) every 6 months, variceal screening as directed by hepatology, and routine monitoring of platelets/INR/albumin for portal hypertension and synthetic dysfunction. Counsel on strict alcohol avoidance and avoid hepatotoxic medications; androgens such as danazol (which have been used experimentally for telomere disorders) require extreme caution or avoidance in cirrhosis.

Bone and neuromuscular health
This case has osteoporosis and axonal polyneuropathy. Baseline DEXA is appropriate; treat osteoporosis with vitamin D, calcium, physiotherapy and consider antiresorptive therapy per standard indications after discussion with endocrinology or bone clinic (coordinate with hematology if transplant is being considered). Neuropathy care is symptomatic plus physiotherapy; monitor for falls and fractures.

Cancer surveillance
TBDs carry elevated risks of hematologic malignancy (MDS/AML) and of mucosal and cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas. Practical monitoring includes periodic oral/dental and dermatologic exams (at least annually and sooner if new lesions), low threshold for ENT evaluation with new oral lesions, and routine hematologic surveillance for evolution to MDS/AML (CBC trends, peripheral smear, marrow/cytogenetics if new cytopenias or blasts). Tailor frequency to findings and family history.

Vaccination and infection prevention
Ensure up‑to‑date inactivated vaccines: annual influenza, pneumococcal series per schedule, and Hepatitis A and B vaccination if the patient is non‑immune (important with liver disease). Use caution with live vaccines: avoid them if the patient becomes significantly immunosuppressed (discuss with immunology/infectious disease). Pre‑transplant vaccination planning is critical — many centers prefer to update vaccine status before conditioning. For patients with splenomegaly or asplenia, follow additional encapsulated‑organism vaccination guidance.

Medications and exposures to avoid or use with caution
Avoid or consult hematology before starting any potentially myelotoxic or hepatotoxic agents. Examples to review with specialists include cytotoxic chemotherapy, alkylators and many immunosuppressants, and certain antibiotics that can cause cytopenias (e.g., linezolid, chloramphenicol); avoid chronic androgen therapy in patients with significant hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis. Advise strict avoidance of tobacco and excessive alcohol and careful assessment of occupational inhalational or chemical exposures.

Family cascade testing and telomere length: who, how and when
Offer genetic counseling to all first‑degree relatives and discuss targeted testing for the family’s PARN variant. If a variant is classified VUS (as in this case), explain the limits: a negative test in a relative won’t fully eliminate risk, and a positive result may not prove causation. Telomere length testing by flow‑FISH in specialized labs is a useful adjunct — very short age‑adjusted telomere length (especially <1st percentile) supports a diagnosis and helps risk‑stratify relatives. Segregation studies (does the variant track with disease in the family?) and telomere length together can sometimes reclassify VUS to likely pathogenic or benign. Testing minors should be discussed with genetics — if knowing the result will change medical management in childhood, testing can be considered; otherwise standard ethical guidance applies.

Donor selection for hematopoietic stem cell transplant
Do not use related donors for HSCT without genetic testing and telomere assessment. Siblings or relatives who carry the same pathogenic variant or who have very short telomeres may be poor donors and may transfer risk. Always involve an experienced transplant program with telomere expertise when planning HSCT.

When to refer to transplant centers (both HSCT and lung transplant)
Refer early, not only when the patient is moribund. Triggers for urgent transplant discussion include transfusion dependence, progressive and symptomatic cytopenias (recurrent severe infections, bleeding), clonal evolution to MDS/AML, rapid pulmonary function decline, or increasing oxygen needs. Teams experienced with TBDs will modify conditioning regimens (often reduced‑intensity), sequencing, and donor selection to reduce organ toxicity.

Interpreting a VUS in PARN — practical next steps
With a VUS (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) that alters a conserved residue and is bioinformatically predicted deleterious, combine all data streams rather than acting on the variant alone. Practical steps: 1) perform flow‑FISH telomere length testing in the proband and available relatives; 2) offer targeted family testing to look for segregation; 3) share data with the laboratory and consider submitting phenotypic and variant data to public repositories (ClinVar) to help future reclassification; 4) manage clinically as you would a TBD in terms of surveillance and drug avoidance while acknowledging uncertainty — i.e., act on phenotype and telomere biology, not solely on the VUS.

Practical checklist for the next outpatient visit
Confirm and document B12 repletion and hematologic response, order or repeat CBC with retic, arrange baseline PFTs/DLCO and HRCT if not already done, repeat LFTs and schedule hepatology review given cirrhosis, check ferritin if transfusions occurred, arrange DEXA follow‑up and bone health measures, refer to clinical genetics for cascade testing and flow‑FISH telomere measurement, update vaccines per recommendations, counsel strongly on smoking/alcohol cessation, and schedule multidisciplinary follow‑up (hematology ± transplant, pulmonology, hepatology and dermatology/oral health).

Clinical pearls
- Short telomeres + compatible phenotype trumps a solitary VUS for clinical decision‑making: monitor and protect the patient while the genetic picture matures.  
- Never select a related HSC donor without genetic/telomere testing — you may be transplanting the disease.  
- Telomere length testing matters: use flow‑FISH in an experienced lab (qPCR is less reliable for clinical decision‑making).  
- Androgens may increase telomere length in small studies but can be hepatotoxic — avoid in cirrhosis and discuss with specialists.  
- Early transplant evaluation (both marrow and lung teams) improves options; don’t wait for end‑stage deterioration.

Take‑home messages
- Suspect a telomere biology disorder in young adults with unexplained cytopenia plus liver or lung fibrosis, even without classic mucocutaneous features.  
- Baseline multi‑organ assessment is mandatory: CBC with retic and smear, bone marrow/cytogenetics if indicated, HRCT + PFTs (with DLCO), LFTs and elastography/US, DEXA, and telomere length testing (flow‑FISH) alongside genetics.  
- Hematology follow‑up should be frequent early (CBC + retic every 1–3 months while unstable) with low threshold for repeat marrow/cytogenetics; refer to transplant for transfusion dependence, progressive failure, or clonal evolution.  
- Pulmonary monitoring: baseline HRCT and PFTs, repeat PFTs every 3–12 months depending on trajectory; early lung transplant referral for progressive decline or increasing oxygen needs. Smoking cessation is non‑negotiable.  
- If cirrhosis is present, enroll in standard cirrhosis care (HCC surveillance every 6 months) and avoid hepatotoxins (including caution with androgens).  
- Vaccinate with inactivated vaccines (influenza, pneumococcus, Hep A/B if non‑immune); avoid live vaccines if immunosuppressed.  
- Offer genetic counseling and cascade testing to first‑degree relatives; use telomere length testing to help interpret a VUS and never use related donors without appropriate testing.  
- Coordinate care in multidisciplinary centers experienced with TBDs — they tailor conditioning for HSCT and manage the multisystem complexities.

If you’re the clinician caring for this patient: set up the genetics referral, arrange flow‑FISH if available, get the pulmonology and hepatology consults on your calendar, and institute the CBC/PFT/LFT monitoring cadence today. Early structure and communication will turn the diagnostic label into better prevention and timely intervention.
