Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 3: ## Telomere Biology and PARN: Focused Pathophysiology for This Case

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences at chromosome ends that progressively shorten with every cell division; when they reach a critically short length the cell undergoes replicative senescence or apoptosis. Telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein enzyme composed chiefly of the catalytic protein TERT and the telomerase RNA component TERC—adds telomeric repeats to offset this loss. Tissues with high proliferative demand (haematopoietic marrow, epithelial surfaces, hair follicles, type II alveolar cells and hepatocytes) depend on telomerase to preserve replicative capacity. Loss of effective telomere maintenance therefore disproportionately impairs these organ systems and produces the premature‑ageing, marrow failure and fibrotic phenotypes seen in telomere biology disorders (TBDs).

PARN (poly(A)-specific ribonuclease) is a 3′→5′ exoribonuclease that participates in the deadenylation and 3′-end maturation of a subset of nuclear RNAs, including TERC. Proper 3′-end processing of TERC is required to produce a stable, functional telomerase RNA. When PARN function is reduced, nascent TERC molecules acquire aberrant oligo(A) tails and are targeted for degradation, leading to lower cellular levels of mature TERC and thus reduced telomerase activity. The net effect is accelerated telomere attrition even in the absence of a primary defect in TERT itself. PARN also influences the metabolism of other RNAs, which may contribute to broader cell‑biologic consequences beyond telomere shortening.

Cellular consequences of shortened telomeres are predictable: stem and progenitor cells with critically short telomeres cannot sustain normal proliferation or repair. In the bone marrow this manifests as diminished haematopoietic stem cell (HSC) reserve and an impaired capacity to expand in response to stress. Clinically this produces cytopenias, a poor reticulocyte response and, over time, frank marrow failure. The marrow findings in this patient—trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia but a low reticulocyte count and evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis—fit a model in which nutritional stress (severe B12 deficiency) provoked increased erythroid demand that an HSC compartment constrained by short telomeres could not meet.

In the lung, alveolar type II epithelial cells play a central role in repair after injury; they are highly replicative and telomerase‑dependent. Telomere dysfunction there leads to premature epithelial cell senescence, impaired re‑epithelialization and abnormal wound healing with activation of fibroblasts and extracellular matrix deposition. The imaging pattern reported—diffuse ground‑glass change with subpleural fibrosis—is typical of telomere‑related pulmonary fibrosis and is a well-described presentation of monoallelic PARN variants in adults.

Hepatic involvement in TBDs reflects a similar principle. Hepatocytes and their progenitors require proliferative capacity for regeneration after injury; shortened telomeres limit this reparative ability, favouring chronic injury pathways, stellate cell activation and progressive fibrosis up to cirrhosis. Increasingly, cirrhosis and clinically significant hepatic fibrosis are recognised components of the adult TBD spectrum and explain the patient’s elastography findings and hepatosplenomegaly.

Cutaneous and hair findings (premature greying, epithelial atrophy or subtle pigmentation changes) result from depletion or dysfunction of epidermal and melanocyte progenitors. Although the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita may be absent in adult‑onset TBD, these subtler premature‑ageing signs help map the systemic effects of telomere shortening in this patient.

Genotype–phenotype relationships for PARN are dose‑sensitive. Biallelic loss‑of‑function PARN variants cause severe, early‑onset DC or Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson syndromes with profound marrow failure and developmental features. Monoallelic PARN variants more commonly present in adulthood with telomere‑related pulmonary fibrosis and variable marrow involvement (the PFBMFT4 spectrum). Nevertheless, penetrance and expressivity are highly variable: environmental insults (smoking, viral infection, chemotherapy, severe nutritional deficiency), coexisting genetic variation in other telomere genes, and somatic events (for example clonal haemopoietic rescue) all modify timing and severity. In this patient a monoallelic missense change affecting the conserved R3H domain of PARN is biologically plausible as a contributor to reduced TERC maturation, and the observed multisystem fibrosis and reduced marrow reserve fit the expected organ‑level consequences even though the specific variant remains classified as a VUS.

Clinically important corollaries of this mechanism are twofold. First, stressors such as the patient’s severe vitamin B12 deficiency can unmask or greatly amplify cytopenias: a marrow whose replicative reserve is already limited by short telomeres is less able to respond to increased demand or to recover after injury. Second, recognition of an underlying TBD changes diagnostic and therapeutic choices—telomere length testing (flow‑FISH) can corroborate the diagnosis, and haematopoietic stem cell transplantation or cytotoxic regimens require modified conditioning because telomere‑deficient tissues tolerate DNA‑damaging therapy poorly. Experimental strategies that aim to increase telomerase activity (for example androgen therapy) have been used in select cases, but current management remains largely supportive and directed at the affected organs, together with genetic counselling and surveillance.

In summary, PARN dysfunction reduces mature TERC, lowers telomerase activity and accelerates telomere shortening. The resultant exhaustion of proliferative compartments explains the patient’s constellation of marrow failure features, premature ageing, pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis, and helps understand why a nutritional insult such as severe B12 deficiency produced disproportionately severe and persistent cytopenias.
