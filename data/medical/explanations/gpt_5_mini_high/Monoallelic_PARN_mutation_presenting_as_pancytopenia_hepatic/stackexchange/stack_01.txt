Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Morning Report] In a woman in her late 20s with pancytopenia, premature greying, hepatosplenomegaly, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, and no classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita, what unifying inherited condition should you suspect?

Answer: Suspect a telomere biology disorder (TBD) — the dyskeratosis congenita (DC) spectrum — most plausibly due to a PARN‑related telomere maintenance defect.

Why: The patient’s constellation—early hair greying and other premature‑ageing features, pancytopenia with ineffective erythropoiesis, hepatic cirrhosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis—reflects failure of high‑turnover tissues, which is the hallmark of telomere shortening. In TBDs, critically short telomeres drive stem‑cell exhaustion and fibrotic degeneration of lung and liver; classical mucocutaneous findings of DC may be absent in adults because of variable penetrance and age‑dependent expression.

Genetic link in this case: PARN is an exoribonuclease required for 3′ maturation of the telomerase RNA (TERC); haploinsufficiency impairs telomerase and causes telomere attrition. The patient’s clinical exome identified a monoallelic PARN missense change (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) in the conserved R3H domain that in silico tools predict to be deleterious. Although reported as a Variant of Uncertain Significance, the genotype fits the phenotype — monoallelic PARN variants are increasingly recognized causes of adult‑onset pulmonary fibrosis and bone‑marrow/organ failure within the TBD spectrum.

Diagnostic approach: In any young adult with unexplained marrow failure ± pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, test telomere length (flow‑FISH is the standard), and obtain targeted next‑generation sequencing for telomere‑related genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, etc.). In this patient the exome result supports a TBD; formal telomere length testing and family cascade testing would strengthen the diagnosis and inform donor selection if transplant is considered.

Management principles: Treat reversible contributors (the team corrected severe B12 deficiency and provided supportive transfusions). Long‑term care involves surveillance of marrow, lung and liver function, genetic counselling, and avoidance of exposures that accelerate telomere loss. Progressive marrow failure may require hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation, but requires specialist protocols (reduced‑intensity conditioning, careful donor selection) because of higher organ‑toxicity risk in TBD. Emerging therapies (androgens such as danazol/nandrolone) have shown telomere‑lengthening signals but remain investigational.

Clinical pearl: In a young adult with multisystem disease of high‑turnover tissues (pancytopenia, early greying, pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis), think telomere biology disorders even in the absence of the classic DC mucocutaneous triad — PARN‑associated TBD is a unifying diagnosis compatible with this case.
