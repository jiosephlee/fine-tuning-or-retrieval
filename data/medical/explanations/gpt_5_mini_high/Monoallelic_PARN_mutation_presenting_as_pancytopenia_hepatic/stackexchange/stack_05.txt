Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Management] For patients with telomere biology disorders who require hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation, what donor screening and conditioning strategy adjustments are recommended to reduce transplant-related toxicity and graft failure?

Short answer
For patients with telomere biology disorders (TBDs) who need hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation (HSCT), two key adjustments reduce toxicity and graft failure: (1) careful donor screening to avoid donors who carry the familial telomere mutation or have abnormally short telomeres; and (2) use of reduced‑intensity / reduced‑toxicity conditioning that minimizes alkylator and radiation exposure and incorporates serotherapy to permit engraftment without myeloablation.

Why this matters (mechanism and clinical relevance)
TBDs (including PARN‑related disease in this case) cause short, dysfunctional telomeres in both haematopoietic stem cells and somatic organs. Tissues with short telomeres are exquisitely sensitive to DNA‑damaging agents (high‑dose alkylators, ionizing radiation) and have impaired regenerative reserve. That explains the high rates of pulmonary, hepatic and mucosal toxicity and of graft failure reported after conventional myeloablative HSCT in TBD patients. The patient in the case already has pulmonary fibrosis and cirrhosis—two predictors of poor transplant tolerance—so these issues are directly relevant before any transplant is contemplated.

Practical recommendations

1) Donor screening
- Genotype any related donor for the known familial variant (here, the PARN change) before selection. First‑degree relatives can be asymptomatic carriers because of variable penetrance.
- Measure donor telomere length by an age‑adjusted method (flow‑FISH is the accepted clinical standard). Avoid donors with abnormally short telomeres for age (telomere length in the “very short” range on age‑adjusted testing) or who carry the pathogenic variant.
- If related donors are unsuitable, preferentially use an unrelated matched donor who tests negative for the familial mutation and has normal age‑adjusted telomere length. Unrelated cord blood with adequate matching is an option (cord units have long telomeres) but has tradeoffs (engraftment risk) and must be chosen carefully.

2) Conditioning strategy
- Avoid myeloablative regimens that include high‑dose alkylators (high‑dose busulfan, melphalan) or high‑dose total body irradiation whenever possible—these agents cause severe organ toxicity in telomere‑deficient patients.
- Use reduced‑intensity or reduced‑toxicity conditioning regimens that emphasize nucleoside backbone agents (e.g., fludarabine) plus lower‑toxicity myeloablative substitutes. Examples used in practice include fludarabine‑based regimens with reduced‑dose busulfan or treosulfan (treosulfan is increasingly used as a less hepatopulmonary‑toxic alternative), together with serotherapy (ATG or alemtuzumab) to facilitate engraftment and reduce GVHD. The exact regimen should be individualized to organ function.
- Tailor doses downward when there is established pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, and avoid TBI when possible.
- Consider strategies to promote engraftment (adequate CD34+ cell dose, early chimerism monitoring) because graft failure risk is increased.

3) Peri‑transplant planning
- Optimize organ function before transplant: full pulmonary evaluation (PFTs, HRCT), hepatology assessment and correction of reversible problems (nutrition, vitamin deficiencies as in this case).
- Counsel patients about increased risks even with modified regimens and refer to centers experienced in TBD‑HSCT.
- Close post‑transplant surveillance for hepatic and pulmonary complications and early chimerism monitoring for graft failure.

Bottom line applied to this case
This young woman with a monoallelic PARN variant plus hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis is at high risk for transplant‑related toxicity. If HSCT becomes necessary for progressive marrow failure, first screen any related donors for the PARN variant and measure telomere length (flow‑FISH)—do not use donors who carry the variant or have very short telomeres. Use a reduced‑intensity / reduced‑toxicity, fludarabine‑based conditioning strategy (avoiding high‑dose alkylators and TBI, with serotherapy), and perform the transplant at a center experienced in TBDs. These steps lower—but do not eliminate—the elevated risks of organ toxicity and graft failure in TBD patients.
