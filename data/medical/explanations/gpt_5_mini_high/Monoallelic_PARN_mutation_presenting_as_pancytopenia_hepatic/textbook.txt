Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Clinical Presentation and Salient Initial Findings

The patient was a woman in her late twenties who reported a 5‑year history of progressive fatigue and generalized weakness with a recent accelerated decline in functional capacity over three months. She also complained of exertional dyspnoea, intermittent chest discomfort, weight loss and anorexia. The prolonged, insidiously progressive course with systemic symptoms suggested a chronic multisystem process rather than an acute isolated illness.

On examination she had pallor and scleral icterus, signs that pointed to anemia with a component of increased red‑cell breakdown or ineffective erythropoiesis. Notable mucocutaneous findings included premature greying of hair and knuckle hyperpigmentation; the tongue was bald with atrophy of lingual papillae (atrophic glossitis), a classic sign of severe vitamin B12 deficiency. Musculoskeletal and neurologic signs included thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy. Abdominal examination revealed hepatomegaly (liver span 18 cm) and chest auscultation demonstrated reduced breath sounds at the bilateral lung bases, correlating with later imaging evidence of interstitial lung disease. Cardiovascular exam was otherwise unremarkable.

Initial haematologic testing documented pancytopenia with an inappropriately low reticulocyte count (0.39%), indicating a poor marrow response to peripheral cytopenia and pointing the differential toward marrow failure or ineffective erythropoiesis rather than a purely peripheral destructive process. Peripheral blood smear showed a dimorphic red‑cell population, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings and hypersegmented neutrophils. Hypersegmented neutrophils and Cabot rings are characteristic of megaloblastic change (classically from B12/folate deficiency), whereas basophilic stippling indicates abnormal ribosomal aggregation or dyserythropoiesis; the overall smear therefore implicated pronounced dyserythropoiesis and ineffective erythropoiesis.

Biochemical evaluation confirmed severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency. Iron studies were notable for elevated serum iron and ferritin—an iron profile more typical of ineffective erythropoiesis than of iron deficiency. In ineffective erythropoiesis (for example, severe megaloblastic anemia) many erythroid precursors are destroyed within the marrow (intramedullary haemolysis), releasing iron that raises serum iron and ferritin while failing to produce circulating reticulocytes and mature erythrocytes.

Bone marrow examination supported this interpretation: there was trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores but no dysplasia, infiltrative disease or malignant clone; cytogenetics were normal. This pattern—an active but ineffective erythroid compartment—explains the paradox of marrow erythroid hyperplasia with peripheral reticulocytopenia and elevated haemolytic markers.

Markers of haemolysis (elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin) were present. The direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test was positive for IgG (2+), while the indirect test was negative. Although a positive DAT raises concern for autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA), the constellation of a low reticulocyte response and marrow findings consistent with intramedullary destruction argued that ineffective erythropoiesis (intramedullary haemolysis) was the dominant mechanism. In clinical practice a DAT must be interpreted alongside reticulocyte count and marrow results, since severe dyserythropoiesis can produce haemolysis markers and a positive DAT may be incidental or secondary.

Investigation of possible causes of vitamin B12 malabsorption included upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and biopsy. Endoscopy was macroscopically normal; duodenal biopsy showed mild chronic gastritis with reduced parietal cells, a finding that can impair intrinsic‑factor–mediated B12 absorption and thereby explain the documented severe B12 deficiency. However, the severity and breadth of the patient’s clinical abnormalities—particularly early‑onset hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis—appeared disproportionate to nutritional deficiency alone and suggested an additional or alternative underlying process.

Hepatic evaluation demonstrated hepatosplenomegaly on ultrasound and significant fibrosis on transient elastography (controlled attenuation parameter 295 dB/m consistent with moderate steatosis; liver stiffness 18.1 kPa consistent with cirrhosis). Common infectious and autoimmune causes were excluded serologically. Cirrhosis in a young adult without an explanatory hepatitis or autoimmune process is a red flag that prompts consideration of systemic, metabolic or genetic fibrotic disorders.

Cross‑sectional imaging (PET–CT) showed inhomogeneous 18F‑FDG uptake in axial and appendicular bone marrow—compatible with marrow activation or inflammatory changes—and diffuse ground‑glass haziness with fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic changes in both lungs, but no FDG‑avid malignancy or granulomatous disease. The pattern of subpleural fibrosis and ground‑glass change is typical of a fibrosing interstitial lung disease and is unusual in this age group, thereby arguing for an underlying predisposition when seen together with marrow and hepatic fibrosis.

Neurologic and bone health assessments documented an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy on EMG/NCS and widespread metabolic bone disease: vitamin D deficiency, secondary hyperparathyroidism, hypocalcaemia and osteoporosis on DEXA with a history of fractures. Severe B12 deficiency can account for some neurologic findings (eg, myeloneuropathy) and nutritional deficits will worsen bone health, but the combination of early osteoporosis, neuropathy, cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis in a young adult again suggested a unifying systemic process rather than isolated nutritional deficiency.

In synthesis, the initial clinical picture combined (1) ineffective erythropoiesis with pancytopenia and marrow erythroid hyperplasia, (2) objective severe B12/folate deficiency explaining many haematologic and mucosal features, and (3) unexplained early‑onset hepatic cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis with multisystem involvement (skin, bone, nerve). Because the affected organs are those with high proliferative demand—bone marrow, lung epithelium, hepatocytes, skin and hair—the findings were disproportionate to nutritional causes alone and prompted correction of reversible deficiencies while escalating evaluation toward inherited or syndromic causes of premature ageing and organ fibrosis (leading to bone marrow biopsy, targeted imaging and genetic investigation).

## Differential Diagnosis and Stepwise Workup

The initial clinical problem was pancytopenia in a young adult with additional multisystem findings (premature greying, neuropathy, osteoporosis, hepatosplenomegaly and interstitial lung changes). The diagnostic approach therefore followed two parallel priorities: (1) rapidly exclude life‑threatening and treatable causes of cytopenia (acute leukaemia, severe hemolysis, fulminant infection, marrow aplasia or infiltration) and (2) treat reversible contributors while assembling evidence to explain the broader syndrome. This stepwise strategy—stabilise and correct obvious reversible factors, obtain marrow assessment and targeted imaging, then escalate to broader serologic and genetic testing when no unifying acquired diagnosis emerges—guided the workup in this case.

Hematologic differentials were considered and tested in a systematic order. Severe megaloblastic anemia from vitamin B12/folate deficiency was an early and plausible explanation: peripheral smear showed hypersegmented neutrophils and dimorphic erythrocytes, and biochemical testing confirmed marked B12 deficiency. The bone marrow showed erythroid hyperplasia, and markers of ineffective erythropoiesis (elevated lactate dehydrogenase, indirect bilirubin and increased marrow iron) were present—patterns all consistent with intramedullary destruction of erythroid precursors that is characteristic of megaloblastic anemia. The low reticulocyte count (0.39%) reflects a hypoproliferative response typical of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than an appropriate marrow recovery response to peripheral hemolysis.

Other marrow disorders were actively excluded. Aplastic anemia was unlikely because the marrow was cellular with trilineage haematopoiesis rather than hypocellular. Myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) was considered because of cytopenias and dyserythropoietic features (Cabot rings, basophilic stippling), but the marrow lacked definitive dysplasia and conventional cytogenetics were normal; while normal karyotype does not absolutely exclude MDS, the combination of findings decreased its probability—particularly in a young adult without clonal cytogenetic abnormalities. Marrow infiltration by malignancy or granulomatous disease was searched for with marrow morphology and PET‑CT; neither study provided evidence of neoplastic or infiltrative disease, making these causes unlikely.

Hemolytic processes required careful interpretation because the direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG (2+). In warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia one would expect reticulocytosis and brisk marrow compensation with elevated circulating reticulocytes; in contrast, this patient had a low reticulocyte count and marrow evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis. Thus the DAT result, although real, was not sufficient alone to diagnose immune hemolysis and could reflect non‑pathogenic antibody binding or a secondary phenomenon. Integrating the DAT with the low reticulocyte response and marrow changes supported ineffective erythropoiesis—principally from B12 deficiency—as the dominant mechanism of anaemia rather than peripheral immune destruction.

Because nutritional deficiency explained many haematologic and neurologic features, the team evaluated causes of malabsorption. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was macroscopically unremarkable, but duodenal biopsy showed loss of parietal cells. This histology is compatible with autoimmune gastritis (resulting in reduced intrinsic factor) and provides a plausible mechanism for chronic B12 deficiency and pernicious anemia. However, while this finding explains the anaemia, glossitis and neuropathy, it does not account for premature greying, early osteoporosis, or the co‑existent hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis—features that pointed beyond an isolated nutritional disorder.

Systemic inflammatory or autoimmune disease was considered because inflammatory markers were raised and liver dysfunction was present. Extensive autoimmune serology, including ANA and autoimmune hepatitis panels, was negative and there were no clinical stigmata of systemic connective tissue disease. Although seronegative autoimmune disease can occur, the absence of corroborating serology, organ‑specific autoantibodies and compatible imaging made an autoimmune unifying diagnosis less likely in this patient.

A broad infectious and infiltrative workup was undertaken because infections can cause cytopenias and organ dysfunction. Viral hepatitis, HIV, cytomegalovirus and other relevant serologies were negative; only EBV IgG was positive, consistent with past exposure. PET‑CT was performed to search for occult malignancy or granulomatous processes and showed heterogeneous bone marrow uptake (most consistent with marrow hyperactivity/expansion) but no focal uptake to suggest malignancy or granulomatous disease. The PET and marrow morphology together argued against an occult neoplasm as the cause of cytopenias.

The combination of early‑onset hepatic cirrhosis and interstitial lung disease in a patient in her twenties was the critical red flag that shifted the differential from acquired isolated conditions to an inherited multisystem disorder. In young adults, cirrhosis and fibrotic interstitial lung disease are unusual and should prompt consideration of metabolic, storage or genetic syndromes—among them telomere biology disorders (TBDs) which classically target highly proliferative tissues (bone marrow, lung epithelium, liver). The lack of classical mucocutaneous features of dyskeratosis congenita does not exclude TBDs in adults, whose initial presentations are often dominated by pulmonary fibrosis, marrow failure or liver disease.

Given the absence of a unifying acquired cause after targeted testing and the presence of multisystem premature‑ageing features, genetic investigation was the appropriate next step. Clinical exome sequencing was chosen because the phenotype could result from pathogenic variants in any of several telomere‑related genes (TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1, DKC1, etc.), and exome‑level testing provides broad coverage to detect single nucleotide variants across these genes. Functional telomere testing (for example flow‑FISH measurement of telomere length) is a complementary diagnostic tool and is often used to confirm a TBD, but in this case exome sequencing was the practical means to look for a monogenic explanation for the constellation of findings. The identification of a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), though classified as a variant of uncertain significance, provided a plausible genetic correlate to the clinical picture and therefore directed ongoing surveillance, family counselling and consideration of future management strategies (including careful planning should haematopoietic stem cell transplantation be required).

In summary, the stepwise workup prioritized exclusion of life‑threatening haematologic and infective causes, identified treatable contributors (B12 deficiency related to parietal cell loss), and then proceeded to broader genetic testing when the persistent multisystem phenotype—early‑onset hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis together with marrow dysfunction—remained unexplained. This integrated approach is essential when a young patient presents with otherwise cryptic organ fibrosis and cytopenia, because confirming a telomere biology disorder has important implications for surveillance, family screening and therapeutic decision‑making.

## Telomere Biology and PARN: Focused Pathophysiology for This Case

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences at chromosome ends that progressively shorten with every cell division; when they reach a critically short length the cell undergoes replicative senescence or apoptosis. Telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein enzyme composed chiefly of the catalytic protein TERT and the telomerase RNA component TERC—adds telomeric repeats to offset this loss. Tissues with high proliferative demand (haematopoietic marrow, epithelial surfaces, hair follicles, type II alveolar cells and hepatocytes) depend on telomerase to preserve replicative capacity. Loss of effective telomere maintenance therefore disproportionately impairs these organ systems and produces the premature‑ageing, marrow failure and fibrotic phenotypes seen in telomere biology disorders (TBDs).

PARN (poly(A)-specific ribonuclease) is a 3′→5′ exoribonuclease that participates in the deadenylation and 3′-end maturation of a subset of nuclear RNAs, including TERC. Proper 3′-end processing of TERC is required to produce a stable, functional telomerase RNA. When PARN function is reduced, nascent TERC molecules acquire aberrant oligo(A) tails and are targeted for degradation, leading to lower cellular levels of mature TERC and thus reduced telomerase activity. The net effect is accelerated telomere attrition even in the absence of a primary defect in TERT itself. PARN also influences the metabolism of other RNAs, which may contribute to broader cell‑biologic consequences beyond telomere shortening.

Cellular consequences of shortened telomeres are predictable: stem and progenitor cells with critically short telomeres cannot sustain normal proliferation or repair. In the bone marrow this manifests as diminished haematopoietic stem cell (HSC) reserve and an impaired capacity to expand in response to stress. Clinically this produces cytopenias, a poor reticulocyte response and, over time, frank marrow failure. The marrow findings in this patient—trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia but a low reticulocyte count and evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis—fit a model in which nutritional stress (severe B12 deficiency) provoked increased erythroid demand that an HSC compartment constrained by short telomeres could not meet.

In the lung, alveolar type II epithelial cells play a central role in repair after injury; they are highly replicative and telomerase‑dependent. Telomere dysfunction there leads to premature epithelial cell senescence, impaired re‑epithelialization and abnormal wound healing with activation of fibroblasts and extracellular matrix deposition. The imaging pattern reported—diffuse ground‑glass change with subpleural fibrosis—is typical of telomere‑related pulmonary fibrosis and is a well-described presentation of monoallelic PARN variants in adults.

Hepatic involvement in TBDs reflects a similar principle. Hepatocytes and their progenitors require proliferative capacity for regeneration after injury; shortened telomeres limit this reparative ability, favouring chronic injury pathways, stellate cell activation and progressive fibrosis up to cirrhosis. Increasingly, cirrhosis and clinically significant hepatic fibrosis are recognised components of the adult TBD spectrum and explain the patient’s elastography findings and hepatosplenomegaly.

Cutaneous and hair findings (premature greying, epithelial atrophy or subtle pigmentation changes) result from depletion or dysfunction of epidermal and melanocyte progenitors. Although the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita may be absent in adult‑onset TBD, these subtler premature‑ageing signs help map the systemic effects of telomere shortening in this patient.

Genotype–phenotype relationships for PARN are dose‑sensitive. Biallelic loss‑of‑function PARN variants cause severe, early‑onset DC or Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson syndromes with profound marrow failure and developmental features. Monoallelic PARN variants more commonly present in adulthood with telomere‑related pulmonary fibrosis and variable marrow involvement (the PFBMFT4 spectrum). Nevertheless, penetrance and expressivity are highly variable: environmental insults (smoking, viral infection, chemotherapy, severe nutritional deficiency), coexisting genetic variation in other telomere genes, and somatic events (for example clonal haemopoietic rescue) all modify timing and severity. In this patient a monoallelic missense change affecting the conserved R3H domain of PARN is biologically plausible as a contributor to reduced TERC maturation, and the observed multisystem fibrosis and reduced marrow reserve fit the expected organ‑level consequences even though the specific variant remains classified as a VUS.

Clinically important corollaries of this mechanism are twofold. First, stressors such as the patient’s severe vitamin B12 deficiency can unmask or greatly amplify cytopenias: a marrow whose replicative reserve is already limited by short telomeres is less able to respond to increased demand or to recover after injury. Second, recognition of an underlying TBD changes diagnostic and therapeutic choices—telomere length testing (flow‑FISH) can corroborate the diagnosis, and haematopoietic stem cell transplantation or cytotoxic regimens require modified conditioning because telomere‑deficient tissues tolerate DNA‑damaging therapy poorly. Experimental strategies that aim to increase telomerase activity (for example androgen therapy) have been used in select cases, but current management remains largely supportive and directed at the affected organs, together with genetic counselling and surveillance.

In summary, PARN dysfunction reduces mature TERC, lowers telomerase activity and accelerates telomere shortening. The resultant exhaustion of proliferative compartments explains the patient’s constellation of marrow failure features, premature ageing, pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis, and helps understand why a nutritional insult such as severe B12 deficiency produced disproportionately severe and persistent cytopenias.

## Diagnostic Synthesis: Assigning a Working TBD Diagnosis with a Monoallelic PARN Variant (VUS)

The clinical team arrived at a working diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) by integrating a high-index clinical phenotype with a monoallelic PARN missense change (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) that, while classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), is biologically plausible in the context of the patient’s multisystem disease. The variant affects a highly conserved cysteine within the R3H nucleic-acid binding domain of PARN, and multiple in-silico tools predict deleterious effect. Clinically, the patient’s young age, progressive cytopenias with marrow vulnerability, premature greying, hepatic fibrosis and early pulmonary fibrosis form a pattern well described in telomere-related disease and in reported carriers of monoallelic PARN variants. Because the genetic result neither proves nor excludes pathogenicity, the diagnosis is usefully framed as a “working TBD”: a clinical diagnosis supported by a suspicious genotype that requires targeted confirmatory testing to refine classification and guide family counselling and management.

Application of ACMG/AMP criteria explains why the variant remains a VUS despite being compelling. The finding meets PM1 because it lies in a critical R3H functional domain, and PM2 because it is absent/very rare in population databases; PP2 applies because PARN is constrained for benign missense variation and missense change is a plausible disease mechanism; and PP3 applies because multiple computational algorithms predict a damaging effect. These are two moderate and two supporting criteria, which are insufficient by themselves for “likely pathogenic” or “pathogenic” assignment under current ACMG rules because stronger lines of evidence are missing: robust functional data demonstrating impaired PARN function (PS3), segregation of the variant with disease in multiple affected relatives (PP1 upgraded), de novo occurrence with confirmed parental status (PS2), or multiple unrelated cases with the same variant (PS4). Recognising the VUS label is important for how the result is used clinically: it supports heightened suspicion and focused testing but should not be the sole basis for predictive testing in asymptomatic relatives or for irreversible interventions without further corroboration.

To move from a VUS to a more definitive classification and to confirm that the patient’s phenotype is driven by telomere dysfunction, a targeted set of adjunct investigations is recommended. Measurement of leukocyte telomere length by flow cytometry–fluorescence in situ hybridisation (flow‑FISH) is the preferred clinical test: age‑adjusted telomere lengths in lymphocyte and granulocyte subsets that are below the first percentile are strongly supportive of a TBD, while lengths below the 10th percentile raise substantial suspicion. Flow‑FISH is more reliable for individual diagnosis than qPCR-based assays and should be performed in an experienced laboratory with appropriate age-matched controls. Functional assays that are informative where available include quantitation of TERC RNA (PARN deficiency decreases mature TERC), telomerase activity assays (TRAP), and direct measures of PARN enzymatic activity or RNA‑processing assays in research laboratories. Because exome sequencing can miss copy‑number changes, deep intronic variants or promoter/UTR abnormalities, targeted methods (MLPA, targeted genomic and RNA sequencing) should be considered to look for a second PARN allele or pathogenic variants in other telomere maintenance genes. Finally, segregation testing and telomere length measurement in first‑degree relatives can provide strong corroborative evidence if the variant co‑segregates with short telomeres and clinical features.

Interpreting how the genotype relates to the phenotype requires distinguishing immediate, reversible causes of marrow dysfunction from a constitutional telomere defect that increases tissue vulnerability. In this patient, severe vitamin B12 deficiency provided a proximate explanation for the profound ineffective erythropoiesis, hypersegmented neutrophils and low reticulocyte output; correction of B12 improved counts, supporting a reversible component. At the same time, the broader pattern—early pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis and multisystem involvement in a young adult—points to an underlying telomere maintenance defect that predisposes tissues with high replicative demand to earlier senescence and to exaggerated response to stressors (nutritional, inflammatory or toxic). In practical terms this means treating reversible causes promptly while pursuing confirmatory testing and instituting longitudinal surveillance for progressive marrow failure and organ fibrosis.

Several diagnostic pitfalls must be actively avoided in this scenario. A positive direct antiglobulin test alone should not be equated with immune haemolysis when marrow indices and a low reticulocyte response point toward ineffective erythropoiesis. The absence of the classic dyskeratosis congenita mucocutaneous triad does not exclude a TBD in adults—many patients present with isolated marrow or organ‑specific disease. Exome‑only genetic results can miss structural or non‑coding pathogenic variants; conversely, computational predictions and domain location, while supportive, do not replace functional or segregation evidence. Recent transfusions, hematopoietic cell transplantation or circulating donor leukocytes may confound telomere length measurements and must be accounted for when timing tests. Finally, a VUS should not be used for asymptomatic predictive family testing or to make irreversible reproductive decisions without reclassification.

For the patient in this report the immediate clinical approach—correction of vitamin deficiencies, supportive transfusions as needed, and multidisciplinary surveillance of liver and lung disease—was appropriate while confirmatory testing was pursued. The next diagnostic steps for definitive classification would be flow‑FISH telomere measurement in lymphocytes and granulocytes, targeted assays for PARN function and TERC levels where available, segregation and telomere studies in relatives, and consideration of more extensive genomic analysis to exclude a second missed PARN hit or involvement of other telomere genes. Results of these studies will determine whether the PARN variant can be reclassified and will directly inform family counselling, the need for tighter haematologic surveillance, and decisions about potential curative therapies such as haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (which, if required, must be planned with telomere‑disease specific conditioning). The practical teaching point is that a VUS in a high‑prior probability setting can justify a working TBD diagnosis and prompt confirmatory testing and counselling, but clinicians must be explicit about the uncertainty and pursue rigorously the adjunct investigations that will resolve it.

## Management Strategy and Long-term Surveillance in TBD

This patient’s care illustrates two complementary elements of managing a telomere biology disorder (TBD): prompt correction of reversible contributors to cytopenia and a structured, organ-focused long-term plan to anticipate and mitigate progressive marrow failure and fibrotic complications. In the short term she received targeted supportive care that addressed the immediate, reversible problems; in the medium and long term management is directed at surveillance for evolving bone‑marrow failure (BMF), organ-specific measures for lung and liver fibrosis, avoidance of additional iatrogenic injury, and genetic counselling to guide family planning and donor selection.

Immediate/supportive care delivered in this case
On presentation the dominant treatable abnormalities were severe vitamin B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, osteoporosis and symptomatic anemia. Corrective measures used here — parenteral cyanocobalamin (1000 µg daily ×5, then weekly for 4 weeks, then monthly maintenance), oral folic acid 1 mg daily, targeted transfusions for symptomatic anemia, high‑dose vitamin D replacement (60,000 IU weekly initially) with elemental calcium, electrolyte repletion and physiotherapy — are appropriate first steps. These interventions address ineffective erythropoiesis and metabolic contributors to weakness and fracture risk and produced symptomatic and hematologic improvement during early follow‑up. It is important to document an expected biological response (for example a reticulocyte rise within 7–10 days after B12 repletion) and to recognise that correction of nutritional deficits does not exclude an underlying genetic marrow vulnerability.

Hematologic surveillance and principles of care
Patients with suspected or confirmed TBD require proactive hematologic follow‑up because telomere shortening lowers haematopoietic stem cell reserve and can produce progressive cytopenias or clonal evolution. Practical steps are: regular complete blood counts (initially monthly until stable, then at least every 3 months or more frequently with evolving cytopenias), periodic reticulocyte counts and peripheral smears, iron studies and ferritin if transfusions occur, and low threshold for bone marrow reassessment (morphology, cytogenetics and targeted NGS panels) if counts fall, dysplastic features appear, or transfusion dependence develops. Avoidance of myelotoxic exposures when feasible is essential (for example unnecessary chemotherapy, alkylators, or drugs known for marrow toxicity such as prolonged chloramphenicol or linezolid). Use of haematopoietic growth factors (G‑CSF for severe neutropenia and recurrent infections, erythropoiesis‑stimulating agents for select anemias) is individualized and should be overseen by a specialist because benefits are modest and there is concern about promoting clonal evolution in some settings. Androgen therapy (eg, danazol) has been reported to improve blood counts and lengthen telomeres in small series, but it carries significant hepatic toxicity and is not appropriate for patients with advanced hepatic fibrosis as in this case.

When to consider haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)
HSCT is the only curative option for BMF arising from a TBD, but it carries higher peritransplant risks because of underlying organ fragility (pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis) and increased sensitivity to DNA‑damaging conditioning. Indications for referral to a transplant centre include progressive, symptomatic pancytopenia or transfusion dependence not controlled by supportive measures, severe or recurrent infections from neutropenia, or evolving marrow failure despite medical therapy. Pretransplant workup must include measurement of age‑adjusted telomere length (flow‑FISH is the reference technique), comprehensive organ assessment (high‑resolution CT chest and pulmonary function tests, liver imaging and elastography, cardiac assessment), and review of prior exposures. Conditioning should avoid full myeloablative regimens; reduced‑intensity or reduced‑toxicity protocols (fludarabine‑based regimens with careful minimisation of alkylator dosing and avoidance of total body irradiation where possible) are preferred to limit pulmonary/hepatic toxicity. Crucially, donor selection requires genetic screening: first‑degree relatives should not be used as donors without targeted genetic testing and telomere length assessment because asymptomatic carriers or relatives with short telomeres may have poor post‑donation haemopoietic reserve. Even with a successful HSCT, extra‑haematopoietic fibrotic disease (pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis) may progress because the underlying germline defect persists in non‑haematopoietic tissues, and this should be discussed during counselling.

Pulmonary fibrosis: assessment and management
Pulmonary involvement is common in monoallelic PARN and other telomeropathies and is an important determinant of morbidity and transplant candidacy. Baseline evaluation should include pulmonary function tests (spirometry, lung volumes, DLCO), a high‑resolution chest CT and assessment of oxygenation. Management is led by a pulmonology/ILD team and includes smoking cessation, vaccination against influenza and pneumococcus, pulmonary rehabilitation and ambulatory oxygen when hypoxaemic. Antifibrotic therapy (nintedanib or pirfenidone) is reasonable to consider when criteria for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or progressive fibrosing interstitial lung disease are met; although evidence for benefit in telomere‑related disease is limited, these agents can slow decline in lung function in broader ILD populations and are frequently used in practice after specialist discussion. Early consideration of lung transplant is important for progressive disease; however, candidates with TBDs require careful assessment because concurrent marrow and liver disease increase perioperative risk and may affect candidacy and timing.

Hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis care
Liver disease in TBD ranges from steatosis to fibrosis and cirrhosis. Co‑management with hepatology is necessary. Actions include optimisation of metabolic risk factors (weight management, glycaemic and lipid control) to treat steatosis, avoidance of hepatotoxic drugs (eg, androgens in this patient), regular surveillance for portal hypertension (clinical assessment, ultrasound, and endoscopy as indicated) and hepatocellular carcinoma screening (ultrasound ± AFP every ~6 months in patients with cirrhosis, following local hepatology guidance). Management of portal hypertension complications (non‑selective beta‑blockers, variceal ligation, diuretics for ascites) and prompt referral for liver transplant evaluation in decompensated disease are required. Interactions between liver and lung disease are clinically important when planning HSCT or lung transplant because both organs influence perioperative risk and long‑term outcomes.

Bone health and neuromuscular issues
Bone fragility and neuropathy are frequent and modifiable contributors to morbidity. After correction of vitamin B12 and vitamin D deficiency (as was done here) reassess neuropathy; some deficits will improve but chronic axonal damage may persist and benefit from neurorehabilitation and symptomatic neuropathic pain management. Osteoporosis should be treated according to standard principles with vitamin D and calcium repletion and consideration of antiresorptive therapy (bisphosphonates or denosumab) when indicated — with attention to liver and renal function and coordination with hepatology. Fall prevention, physiotherapy and orthopaedic input for fractures are important components of care.

Prevention, lifestyle modification and vaccinations
Because lifestyle factors accelerate progression of organ injury in TBD, practical prevention measures matter: complete smoking cessation is essential, alcohol should be minimised (particularly with hepatic fibrosis), and weight and metabolic risk factors should be optimised. Routine immunisations that reduce respiratory and hepatic infections should be up to date (annual influenza, pneumococcal vaccines per adult schedule, hepatitis A and B if susceptible). Live vaccines should be avoided in patients who are or will be immunosuppressed. Infection prophylaxis (eg, PCP prophylaxis) is used on a case‑by‑case basis when immunosuppression or profound neutropenia is present.

Genetic counselling, family testing and reproductive options
A confirmed pathogenic monoallelic PARN variant is most often transmitted in an autosomal dominant fashion with variable penetrance and expressivity; however, this patient’s variant was reported as a variant of uncertain significance and therefore requires careful interpretation. Genetic counselling is essential to explain inheritance patterns, uncertainty surrounding a VUS, implications for relatives and reproductive options. When a pathogenic variant is identified, cascade testing of first‑degree relatives is recommended; when only a VUS is found, testing and interpretation should be done through a clinical genetics service and tied to phenotypic and telomere length assessment. Telomere length measurement (flow‑FISH) is a useful adjunct for relatives and is mandatory when considering related donors for HSCT. Reproductive options — including preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic disorders (PGT‑M) or prenatal testing — should be discussed with a geneticist and reproductive counsellor if a clearly pathogenic variant is identified.

A practical surveillance framework
Longitudinal care must be individualised, but a reasonable framework for a patient like this includes: CBC with reticulocyte count monthly while unstable then every 3 months once stable; annual or more frequent liver biochemistry and elastography if fibrosis is present; baseline PFTs and HRCT chest with at least annual PFT surveillance (or sooner with symptom progression) and interval imaging guided by pulmonology; DEXA scanning at baseline and every 1–2 years thereafter or sooner with therapy; and periodic reassessment of transfusion needs and iron overload (ferritin, MRI when indicated). Any clinical deterioration should trigger expedited multidisciplinary review.

Takeaway
Management of a PARN‑associated TBD is primarily supportive and preventive, directed at correcting reversible contributors (nutritional deficiencies, metabolic disease), close haematologic surveillance for evolving marrow failure, organ‑directed therapy for pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis, and careful timing and preparation for HSCT when progressive BMF emerges. Given the complexities and elevated procedural risks in telomeropathies, care in specialised centres with coordinated haematology, hepatology, pulmonology and clinical genetics input — and thoughtful donor screening and counselling — is essential.

## Outcome, Prognosis, and Practice Pearls

Outcome in this case
The patient improved symptomatically after correction of her severe vitamin B12 deficiency and with supportive care (transfusions, nutritional repletion, physiotherapy); blood counts were stable at the 2‑week follow‑up reported in the case. Importantly, this short‑term improvement did not obviate concern for an underlying telomere biology disorder: the combination of early‑onset hepatic cirrhosis, radiologic pulmonary fibrosis and pancytopenia despite marrow erythroid activity led to genetic testing that revealed a monoallelic PARN missense variant (p.Cys205Arg, VUS). Because telomere disorders can have delayed and progressive manifestations, the team appropriately instituted ongoing surveillance of hepatic, pulmonary and haematologic status rather than assuming a full recovery from the reversible nutritional component.

Prognostic considerations in PARN‑associated TBDs
Prognosis in telomere biology disorders is heterogeneous and determined mainly by three interrelated factors: objectively short telomere length, which correlates with earlier and more severe organ dysfunction; the organ systems involved at presentation (pulmonary fibrosis and cirrhosis carry a worse near‑term prognosis than isolated mild cytopenias); and the trajectory of marrow failure if it develops. Biallelic PARN defects and very short telomeres are typically associated with earlier, more severe multisystem disease, whereas monoallelic PARN variants are more often linked to adult‑onset pulmonary fibrosis but may still present with liver disease or marrow vulnerability. In this patient the presence of cirrhosis (transient elastography 18.1 kPa) and radiographic interstitial lung disease in a young adult increases the likelihood of progressive organ dysfunction over time, even though the cytopenia at presentation had a large reversible nutritional component.

When to suspect a telomere biology disorder
TBDs should be considered whenever a young adult presents with unexplained, otherwise‑unexplained multisystem findings that disproportionately affect high‑turnover tissues. Practical red flags illustrated by this case are: unexplained cytopenias or marrow failure (especially with low reticulocyte response), cryptogenic hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis in a young person, idiopathic interstitial lung disease or early pulmonary fibrosis, and subtle premature‑ageing features such as early greying, mucosal atrophy or nail/skin changes. Importantly, a brisk but incomplete haematologic response to nutritional correction (for example, to vitamin B12) does not exclude an underlying telomere disorder; rather, severe nutritional stress can unmask marrow vulnerability in a genetically predisposed patient.

Common diagnostic and management pitfalls
Several recurrent pitfalls can delay recognition of TBDs. First, attributing the entire clinical picture to an identified reversible cause (for example, vitamin B12 deficiency) without re‑evaluating persistent or discordant findings may miss an underlying genetic vulnerability. Second, isolated laboratory findings can be misleading: a positive direct antiglobulin test does not by itself establish immune‑mediated hemolysis when marrow indices, low reticulocyte count and marrow examination point to ineffective erythropoiesis. Third, absence of a family history or the classic dyskeratosis congenita mucocutaneous triad should not be used to exclude TBD—many adults present without the full textbook phenotype because of incomplete penetrance, de novo variants or later onset. Finally, acting on a single Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) as if it were pathogenic—especially for donor selection or irreversible interventions—can be harmful; VUS findings should be integrated with telomere length testing, segregation data and specialist review.

Operational practice pearls
- Early objective testing: when a young adult has unexplained cytopenia plus cryptogenic hepatic or pulmonary fibrosis, obtain telomere length testing (flow‑FISH is the preferred clinical assay) and a targeted telomere gene panel or clinical exome rather than waiting for classical signs to appear. Short telomeres add diagnostic weight even when a sequence variant is classified as VUS.  
- Multidisciplinary coordination: involve hematology, hepatology, pulmonology, clinical genetics and transplant teams early. Management and surveillance plans (frequency of CBCs, PFTs/DLCO and liver imaging/ elastography) should be tailored to current organ involvement and risk of progression.  
- Donor selection and transplant planning: if haematopoietic stem cell transplantation becomes necessary, screen related donors both genetically and for telomere length—donors with short telomeres or pathogenic variants may transmit risk and worsen recipient outcomes. Transplant decisions for TBD patients are best managed in centres experienced with reduced‑intensity conditioning approaches and telomere disease complications.  
- Handle genetic uncertainty prudently: do not use a VUS alone to exclude or select family members as donors; re‑review VUS classifications periodically as new evidence appears, and consider functional assays or segregation studies when available. Deposit and consult variant databases (ClinVar) and, when possible, enrol the patient in a telomere registry or research protocols to facilitate reclassification.  
- Risk mitigation and counselling: counsel patients to avoid exposures that accelerate tissue injury (smoking, unnecessary hepatotoxins or marrow‑toxic agents) and provide genetic counselling to family members about possible testing, surveillance and reproductive implications.

Takeaway
This case illustrates that short‑term clinical improvement after correction of reversible factors does not rule out an underlying telomere biology disorder. Early recognition—prompted by the combination of young age, unexplained cytopenia and cryptogenic organ fibrosis—permits appropriate surveillance, family counselling, careful donor selection for transplantation and referral to centres with expertise in TBDs. Regular re‑assessment (clinical, genetic and telomere length) is essential because prognostic classification and management recommendations may change as additional data accumulate.
