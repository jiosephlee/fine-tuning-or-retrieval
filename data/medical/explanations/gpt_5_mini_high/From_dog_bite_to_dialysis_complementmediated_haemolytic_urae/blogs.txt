Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Hidden in the Sepsis: When DIC Masks a Complement-Mediated TMA

This case reads like a medicine exam question with a twist. A previously well woman in her late 40s presents 4 days after a dog bite with fulminant sepsis from Capnocytophaga canimorsus, DIC, pancytopenia and dialysis-dependent AKI. After initial improvement on antibiotics and supportive care the team celebrated — until a delayed, dramatic fall in haemoglobin with persistent thrombocytopaenia, undetectable haptoglobin, soaring LDH and a schistocyte‑rich smear revealed a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). ADAMTS13 was not consistent with TTP, kidney biopsy confirmed TMA, and eculizumab ultimately produced haematologic remission and partial renal recovery.

Why is this case worth keeping in your head? Because sepsis and DIC are loud; they can create a smoke screen that hides a second-hit process — infection‑triggered complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). If you stop listening after the first storm, you can miss the second, treatable one.

## Case snapshot (short)

- Dog bite → febrile, hypoglycaemic, mottled extremities; initial labs: pancytopenia, high lactate, coagulopathy consistent with DIC.
- Broad‑spectrum antibiotics started (piperacillin/tazobactam). Blood cultures later grew C. canimorsus.
- Progressed to anuria → CRRT. Coagulation parameters began to normalise and inflammatory markers improved.
- Then a second wave: Hb 111 → 68 g/L over 6 days without bleeding; platelets persistently low (30–40×10^9/L); haptoglobin undetectable; LDH elevated; peripheral smear 15–20% schistocytes.
- DAT initially positive (likely drug/transfusion related), later negative. ADAMTS13 43.5% (rules against TTP). STEC stool PCR negative.
- PEX used as a bridge; kidney biopsy showed TMA (fibrin thrombi, crescents). Meningococcal vaccines given and eculizumab started after cultures were negative. Diuresis returned and kidney function partially recovered (creatinine ~175 μmol/L). Extended complement genetics were negative.

## The diagnostic trap: DIC vs TMA

DIC and TMA overlap clinically and morphologically. Both can produce thrombocytopenia, microvascular thromboses, digital ischemia and schistocytes. During fulminant sepsis the team’s default thinking is DIC — and usually rightly so. But there are red flags that argue for a superimposed TMA rather than (or in addition to) resolving DIC:

- Timing: new or progressive intravascular haemolysis after the infectious parameters and coagulation indices are improving. In this case haemolysis appeared as the sepsis was getting under control.
- Pattern: isolated drop in haemoglobin without overt bleeding, with persistent thrombocytopaenia despite normalising PT/INR and fibrinogen.
- Laboratory signature of MAHA: low/absent haptoglobin, high LDH, increasing indirect bilirubin, rising schistocyte percentage.
- Platelet behaviour: failure to reliably recover when the consumptive coagulopathy is resolving.

DIC can certainly cause schistocytes, but usually the coagulopathy remains abnormal and the picture improves when sepsis control and supportive care succeed. A re-emergent or persistent MAHA phenotype should prompt a rethink.

## Clues from this case that pointed to CM‑HUS

First, the haemoglobin fell sharply without bleeding while the coagulation profile was improving. Second, haptoglobin became undetectable and LDH climbed — classic intravascular haemolysis. Third, the peripheral smear was striking: 15–20% schistocytes (not a few helmet cells). Fourth, platelets stayed low despite correction of PT/INR and fibrinogen. Fifth, ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5% — not TTP (<10%). Sixth, stool Shiga toxin PCR was negative. Finally, the kidney biopsy showed TMA pathology (fibrin thrombi and crescent formation), clinching the diagnosis.

A couple of important nuances from the case: the initial direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive but later became negative — the initial positive DAT can be explained by penicillin or transfusion effects and should not anchor you into autoimmune haemolysis without the full context. Also, standard complement tests (C3/C4) were normal — normal complement levels do not exclude CM‑HUS.

## Practical approach when haemolysis pops up in a recovering septic patient

- Repeat the peripheral blood smear. It’s cheap, rapid and often decisive. If schistocytes are present (even a small percentage), escalate your thinking to MAHA.
- Order a haemolysis panel immediately: LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin (indirect fraction), reticulocyte count and repeat CBC. Look for an isolated Hb fall with biochemical signs of haemolysis.
- Check coagulation studies (PT/INR, aPTT), fibrinogen and D‑dimer to assess whether a consumptive coagulopathy persists.
- Send an urgent ADAMTS13 activity test and calculate a PLASMIC score while you wait. If TTP cannot be excluded clinically and the patient is critically ill, start plasma exchange emergently.
- Send stool Shiga toxin PCR to rule out STEC‑HUS.
- Send basic complement studies (C3, C4) and consider specialized testing (AH50, anti‑factor H antibodies) and genetic testing — but interpret them cautiously (see below).
- Call Haematology and Nephrology early. If AKI is progressive or there’s oliguria/anuria, involve nephrology immediately — this affects biopsy and dialysis decisions.
- If you suspect CM‑HUS and the infection is controlled (cultures negative or improving), consider complement blockade (eculizumab) — but only after careful infection risk mitigation (vaccination and/or antibiotics) and multidisciplinary discussion.

## How to interpret complement and other specialised tests (and timing matters)

Complement testing is seductive but treacherous in the acute setting. C3 and C4 can be normal in CM‑HUS. Functional assays (e.g., AH50) and anti‑factor H antibody testing are helpful but can be altered by plasma exchange and by complement blockade. In this case AH50 was low (6%) — but those samples were taken after PEX and after the first eculizumab dose, so the result likely reflected treatment effects rather than intrinsic depletion. Genetic testing for complement pathway mutations is essential, but results are slow and a negative genetic panel does not rule out CM‑HUS triggered by infection.

In short: send the tests early (before PEX or eculizumab if possible), but don’t wait for them to act if the patient needs treatment now.

## Treatment pragmatics — PEX as a bridge, eculizumab as the specific therapy

- Plasma exchange is lifesaving in TTP and can be used as a bridge in severe undifferentiated TMA while you await ADAMTS13. In CM‑HUS PEX may transiently help by removing circulating complement fragments or autoantibodies, but it does not correct the underlying dysregulation.
- Eculizumab (anti‑C5) is the targeted therapy for CM‑HUS and, when given early, improves haematologic control and renal outcomes. The dilemma in infection‑triggered CM‑HUS is that terminal complement blockade increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms (Neisseria, and concern exists for other encapsulated pathogens like Capnocytophaga). Thus, the usual approach is:
  - Ensure active bacteraemia is controlled (cultures negative and antibiotic therapy effective) if possible.
  - Vaccinate against meningococcus and provide appropriate prophylactic antibiotics if eculizumab cannot be deferred the full 2 weeks.
  - Use PEX as a temporising measure if immediate complement blockade is deemed too risky.
In the described case, PEX produced a transient return of diuresis, but sustained haematologic and renal recovery followed eculizumab given after cultures were negative and after initial meningococcal vaccination doses (plus prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin while vaccines were still taking effect).

## When to biopsy the kidney?

Kidney biopsy can be decisive if the diagnosis is unclear and the biopsy result would change management. Biopsy is best performed once the bleeding risk is acceptable (platelets improved — in this case they waited until ~77×10^9/L) and coagulation is stable. The biopsy here revealed classic TMA histology (fibrin thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli, crescent formation) and helped justify eculizumab despite the absence of a demonstrable genetic complement mutation.

## Common pitfalls and anchoring traps

- Anchoring on DIC: don’t assume every abnormal smear and low platelets in sepsis is “just DIC.” If the pattern changes (new hemolysis while coags normalize), re-open the differential.
- Over‑reading a single positive DAT: penicillins and transfusions can cause a positive DAT without indicating autoimmune haemolysis.
- Relying on C3/C4: normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS.
- Misinterpreting post‑treatment complement tests: PEX and eculizumab confound functional assays; time your specialised sampling if you want interpretable results.
- Delay in involving specialists: early Haematology and Nephrology input shortens time to diagnosis and appropriate therapy.

## Bottom line

Sepsis and DIC are common and often answerable diagnoses — but they can hide a second, treatable process. A recrudescence or new onset of haemolysis (falling Hb without bleeding), persistent thrombocytopaenia despite improving coagulation indices, rising LDH and an schistocyte‑rich smear should trigger an urgent search for TMA. Early repeat smear, early ADAMTS13 and early involvement of Haematology and Nephrology are high‑yield moves. When TTP is unlikely and CM‑HUS is suspected, coordinate safe use of eculizumab (vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis) and use PEX as a bridge if needed.

## Take‑home messages

- Don’t let DIC be the smoke screen: a second wave of haemolysis during improving sepsis is a red flag for TMA.
- Repeat the peripheral smear early. Schistocytes (even small numbers) + low haptoglobin + high LDH = MAHA until proven otherwise.
- If ADAMTS13 <10% → TTP (PEX now). If ADAMTS13 >10% and STEC negative → consider CM‑HUS.
- Normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS; interpret functional and genetic tests with caution, especially after PEX or eculizumab.
- Use PEX as an emergency bridge when TTP cannot be excluded; plan eculizumab for CM‑HUS once infection risk is mitigated (vaccinate and give prophylactic antibiotics when appropriate).
- Involve Haematology and Nephrology early; consider kidney biopsy when safe to confirm TMA and to inform prognosis and management.
- Remember drug and transfusion effects (e.g., penicillin causing positive DAT) — don’t anchor on a single test.



# Capnocytophaga canimorsus: From Dog Bite to Shock—Empiric Coverage and Diagnostic Traps

What makes this case stick in the mind? A previously well woman in her late 40s is bitten on the lip, is given a penicillin V prescription she never filled, and four days later presents with fulminant sepsis: refractory hypoglycaemia, marked pancytopenia, DIC and anuric renal failure requiring dialysis. Blood cultures only turned positive after a week for Capnocytophaga canimorsus. After an initial clinical improvement she then develops sudden microangiopathic haemolysis with schistocytes and dialysis-dependent AKI; renal biopsy shows thrombotic microangiopathy and she ultimately requires plasma exchange then eculizumab, with partial renal recovery. This is not the “typical” dog‑bite cellulitis — it is a reminder that dog mouths can deliver slow‑growing, dangerous bugs that masquerade as routine soft‑tissue infection and that sepsis itself can hide other pathology (here, infection‑triggered complement‑mediated HUS).

## The bug in a nutshell
Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, fastidious gram‑negative rod in the normal oral flora of dogs (and cats). Transmission is usually via bite or scratch but can occur with close contact. Important clinical points:
- It can cause rapid, severe systemic illness — septic shock, DIC, marked leukopaenia, refractory hypoglycaemia and multiorgan failure — even in immunocompetent hosts.
- Cultures are notoriously slow and may require prolonged incubation or molecular methods (broad‑range PCR, targeted sequencing) to identify the organism.
- Many isolates are susceptible to beta‑lactams, but beta‑lactamase production and polymicrobial bite flora (including anaerobes) mean empiric therapy should be broader than penicillin V.

The case above highlights all of those: severe systemic disease, cultures positive only on day 7, and a clinical course complicated by a later complement‑mediated TMA.

## When a patient with a dog bite looks systemically unwell — what to give, immediately
If the bite patient is systemically ill (fever, hypotension, hypoglycaemia, mottling, leukopaenia, coagulopathy) or the wound is high‑risk (face/hand/puncture, delayed presentation, deep wounds, immunocompromise/asplenia), don’t send them home with penicillin V and a dressing. Get blood cultures and start empiric IV therapy that covers Capnocytophaga plus typical bite polymicrobial organisms (streptococci, staphylococci, Pasteurella, anaerobes). Reasonable empiric IV options in severe presentations:
- Piperacillin–tazobactam (broad gram‑negative and anaerobic coverage) — used as empiric therapy in the case and an appropriate initial choice.
- Ampicillin–sulbactam (IV) — another reasonable option where available.
- A carbapenem (eg, meropenem) for very severe infection or if you worry about resistant organisms/beta‑lactamase producers.

Once cultures and susceptibilities are back you can narrow therapy — in the reported case therapy was de‑escalated after culture results. For less severe infections or prophylaxis the oral drug of choice is amoxicillin–clavulanate.

## Why penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin) is often inadequate as empiric therapy
Penicillin V is narrow spectrum: it has limited activity against many gram‑negative or anaerobic organisms you get with animal bites and offers no beta‑lactamase inhibition. Although many C. canimorsus isolates are penicillin‑susceptible, beta‑lactamase producing strains exist and bite wounds are often polymicrobial. In short: penicillin V is not a safe empiric outpatient choice for high‑risk or potentially systemic dog bites. In the case the patient was prescribed penicillin V at the time of the bite but never started it; later, when bacteremia was confirmed, IV broad‑spectrum therapy was appropriately used.

## Diagnostic traps to avoid
- Slow cultures: C. canimorsus can take several days to grow. A “negative” blood culture at 48–72 hours does not rule it out. If you suspect it, tell your lab (some labs will extend incubation or try molecular diagnostics).
- Don’t be falsely reassured by a low qSOFA: qSOFA is insensitive for early severe sepsis. This patient had qSOFA 1 but had rapidly progressive disease with DIC and refractory hypoglycaemia — early clinical judgment beats a single score.
- Early leukopaenia and refractory hypoglycaemia are red flags for severe Capnocytophaga infection; watch for rapid deterioration.
- TMA can be masked by sepsis/DIC: later development of falling Hb, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin and schistocytes should trigger investigation for thrombotic microangiopathy (eg, measure a peripheral smear, DAT, ADAMTS13, hemolysis labs). In this case the first direct antiglobulin test was positive (likely false‑positive due to penicillin or transfusion); repeat testing and peripheral smear with schistocytes led away from immune haemolysis and toward TMA.
- ADAMTS13 is essential to exclude TTP (severe deficiency <10%). This patient’s ADAMTS13 was 43.5% (TTP unlikely).
- Complement levels (C3/C4) may be normal in complement‑mediated HUS; normal C3/C4 does not rule it out. Extended complement testing can be confounded by plasma exchange and by complement blockade (eculizumab).
- Timing of complement blockade: eculizumab is the treatment for CM‑HUS but blocks terminal complement and raises the risk of invasive infections (meningococcus and other encapsulated organisms). In the case they waited for blood cultures to clear, gave meningococcal vaccination (and prophylactic antibiotics), then started eculizumab — a pragmatic balance between treating the TMA and not making ongoing infection worse.

## Practical post‑exposure prophylaxis — who, what and how long
Who gets prophylaxis: give prophylactic antibiotics for high‑risk bites — wounds on the hand or face, puncture wounds, deep or crushed wounds, wounds requiring closure or bone/joint/nerve involvement, delayed presentation >12–24 hours, immunocompromised patients (asplenia, cirrhosis, alcohol use disorder), and in some guidelines bites in diabetics or those with prosthetic implants.

Suggested regimens (adapt to local guidance and allergies):
- Outpatient prophylaxis (high‑risk wounds, not systemically ill): amoxicillin–clavulanate (oral) is first line. Typical adult dosing: 875/125 mg PO twice daily — common prophylaxis courses are short (many ED/trauma guidelines recommend 3–5 days) but follow local guidance; extend if there are signs of established infection.
- Penicillin allergy (non‑severe): doxycycline 100 mg PO/IV twice daily can cover Capnocytophaga and some other bite pathogens; often combined with metronidazole or clindamycin if anaerobic coverage is needed. Avoid doxycycline in pregnancy/children.
- Established bite wound infection or systemic disease: treat with IV therapy. Reasonable IV choices include piperacillin–tazobactam, ampicillin–sulbactam, or a carbapenem. Duration depends on severity: typical soft tissue infections 7–14 days; bacteremia or deep tissue infection often needs at least 10–14 days IV with tailoring to clinical response and culture results.
- Always update tetanus status.

If you are uncertain about alternatives in a truly penicillin‑allergic patient or about duration for bacteremia or osteomyelitis, involve infectious diseases early.

## Small system changes that would help avoid the “prescription not picked up” problem
- For any high‑risk bite, give the first dose of the appropriate antibiotic in the ED/clinic before discharge (and document it). If you decide oral prophylaxis is safe, hand the patient the medication or ensure the prescription is filled on site.
- Don’t rely solely on qSOFA to determine disposition; use clinical red flags (hypoglycaemia, mottling, leukopaenia, coagulopathy, rapid progression).
- Early repeat bloods: in high‑risk or symptomatic bite patients re‑check CBC, coagulation and metabolic panel within 6–12 hours to catch a falling WBC/platelets or new coagulopathy.
- Send blood cultures for febrile or systemically unwell patients and note the clinical suspicion so labs can extend incubation or perform molecular testing if available.
- Make clear written discharge instructions: return sooner for fever, systemic symptoms, new bleeding, increasing pain or neurological symptoms after a bite.

## Bottom line — how I’ll practice after this case
Treat suspicious dog bites with respect. If the patient is systemically unwell or the bite is high risk, get cultures and start IV broad‑spectrum therapy that covers Capnocytophaga and the usual bite polymicrobial flora (piperacillin–tazobactam or ampicillin–sulbactam are good starts; escalate to a carbapenem if indicated). Don’t be reassured by a short‑incubation negative culture. Re‑check bloods early, look for hemolysis and schistocytes, and remember that sepsis/DIC can obscure an evolving TMA. If CM‑HUS is suspected, coordinate hematology/nephrology/ID early — PEX can be a bridge, eculizumab is disease‑specific but requires careful timing around active infection and vaccination.

Key takeaways
- Capnocytophaga canimorsus can cause fulminant sepsis, DIC and even trigger complement‑mediated HUS in immunocompetent people.
- Don’t send high‑risk bite patients home with penicillin V alone; initial empiric therapy for severe presentations should be a beta‑lactam/beta‑lactamase inhibitor (eg, piperacillin–tazobactam or ampicillin–sulbactam) or a carbapenem if needed.
- Amoxicillin–clavulanate is the outpatient prophylactic drug of choice for high‑risk bites; many guidelines recommend short courses (eg, 3–5 days) for prophylaxis and longer courses for established infection.
- Blood cultures can take days to grow C. canimorsus — consider extended incubation or molecular diagnostics if suspicion is high.
- Don’t be falsely reassured by a low qSOFA; use bedside judgment and watch for refractory hypoglycaemia, leukopaenia and rapid deterioration.
- Re‑check CBC/platelets/hemolysis labs early in the course; new schistocytosis, falling Hb and persistent thrombocytopenia should trigger evaluation for TMA (ADAMTS13, complement workup, nephrology/hematology input).
- If CM‑HUS is suspected, plasma exchange may be used as a bridge; eculizumab is the definitive therapy but start it only after controlling active bacteraemia (vaccinate and provide prophylaxis as needed).
- System changes: give the first dose of prophylactic antibiotics before discharge for high‑risk bites, ensure prescriptions are accessible, and communicate clearly with microbiology about suspected fastidious pathogens.



# TMA at the Bedside: A Pragmatic Algorithm for MAHA + Thrombocytopenia + AKI

Why this matters — and why this case stopped us in our tracks
A previously well woman in her late 40s presents after a dog bite with fulminant sepsis from Capnocytophaga canimorsus. She acutely develops DIC, pancytopenia and anuric AKI requiring dialysis. After an initial improvement she then develops new, isolated haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia and schistocytes — a picture that evolves into biopsy-proven thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) and complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). This case is a great reminder of three uncomfortable truths we run into at the bedside: (1) sepsis/DIC can mask an evolving TMA, (2) the diagnosis is time critical and laboratory results (ADAMTS13, complement testing) must be sent and interpreted in context, and (3) therapy (PEX vs complement blockade) must be started empirically and pragmatically while the workup unfolds.

If you look after these patients regularly, you want a short, time-sensitive algorithm you can run mentally in the first hour, the first day, and once ADAMTS13 arrives. Below is the approach we use — grounded in the above case — with practical points for heme–renal collaboration, transfusions, biopsy timing and how to pivot once TTP is ruled out.

## First hour (what you must do immediately)
The most important immediate actions are: (1) establish whether there is microangiopathic haemolysis at the bedside, and (2) send the right tests before you change the blood.

Ask the nurse or lab to prioritise a peripheral blood smear for schistocytes — and look at it yourself. Even a rough report of “schistocytes present” is actionable: schistocytes are the hallmark of MAHA (in this case the smear later showed 15–20%). At the same time draw the following baseline studies (and tell the lab these are urgent): ADAMTS13 activity, CBC, blood film, LDH, total and direct bilirubin, haptoglobin, reticulocyte count, coagulation panel (PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer), creatinine/urinalysis, blood cultures and cross‑match. If infection is suspected get early empiric antibiotics.

If you can, draw complement samples (C3/C4 and functional assays) and a sample for genetic testing before you start plasma exchange or eculizumab — PEX and complement blockade can alter downstream tests. In the case above, basic complement (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 were obtained before PEX; extended assays ended up being affected by therapy.

## First few hours — triage with PLASMIC and act for TTP if it cannot be ruled out
TTP (severe ADAMTS13 deficiency) is the one you cannot miss. While the ADAMTS13 result is pending, apply a pretest score (PLASMIC) to estimate the likelihood of severe deficiency and guide urgency. A high PLASMIC score (6–7) predicts high likelihood and argues for immediate plasma exchange (PEX) and corticosteroids; an intermediate score (5) needs judgment and usually favours early PEX if clinical suspicion is strong; a low score (≤4) lowers the probability but does not eliminate it.

If you cannot confidently exclude TTP clinically, start PEX without delay — it’s life saving in TTP. Start steroids at the time of PEX as per local practice. Remember: ADAMTS13 <10% is the diagnostic threshold for severe deficiency; in our patient ADAMTS13 returned at 43.5% and TTP was effectively ruled out.

## Parallel (same timeline): exclude other causes that need different therapy
While you’re initiating PEX (or if you’ve decided not to because TTP is unlikely), run a focused parallel workup:
- Stool PCR for Shiga toxin to exclude STEC‑HUS.
- Pregnancy test in women of childbearing potential.
- Autoimmune serology (ANA/ANCA) and drug history to look for secondary TMAs.
- Blood pressure assessment and funduscopic exam to exclude malignant hypertension.
- Review for active infection (blood cultures, localising signs) — sepsis can cause DIC that looks like TMA.
In the reported case the patient’s STEC PCR was negative, autoimmune screens were negative, and cultures grew C. canimorsus — an uncommon but real infectious trigger for CM‑HUS.

## Management while you wait for definitive results
PEX should be started emergently if TTP cannot be excluded. If you start PEX, collect ADAMTS13 and complement samples first. Expect that PEX may transiently improve clinical parameters even in CM‑HUS (it did here), but it is not curative for complement-driven disease.

Handle transfusions pragmatically:
- Red cells for symptomatic anaemia or when hemoglobin drops to dangerous levels.
- Avoid platelet transfusion in suspected TTP/TMA unless there is life‑threatening bleeding or an invasive procedure is required; platelet transfusion can worsen microthrombosis in TTP. In contrast, during DIC with active bleeding you will still need platelets and fibrinogen as clinically indicated — this patient received platelets and fibrinogen during the early DIC phase.
Communicate with blood bank early about potential PEX and transfusion needs.

Arrange nephrology early: severe AKI and dialysis planning cannot wait until ADAMTS13 returns. If dialysis is needed, plan access that is compatible with both dialysis and PEX.

## When ADAMTS13 comes back >10% — pivoting away from TTP
An ADAMTS13 activity well above 10% makes TTP unlikely and should prompt a focused pivot:
- Stop chasing TTP as the primary diagnosis.
- If STEC is negative, and the clinical picture is dominated by severe kidney injury and MAHA, consider CM‑HUS.
- Send/interpret complement testing and genetic studies (factor H/I/B, CH50/AH50, anti‑factor H antibodies) — remember many patients with CM‑HUS have normal C3/C4 at presentation. In our case basic C3/C4 were normal and extended tests were affected by therapy; genetic testing later showed no pathogenic variants.
If CM‑HUS is the working diagnosis, discuss eculizumab urgently — earlier therapy is associated with better renal outcomes.

## Eculizumab: timing, vaccinations and infection risk
Eculizumab (terminal complement blockade) is the targeted therapy for CM‑HUS and often necessary when renal injury is severe. Ideal practice is to vaccinate against Neisseria meningitidis at least 2 weeks before starting eculizumab, because terminal complement inhibition greatly increases the risk of invasive meningococcal disease. When delay is unacceptable, give the vaccine and start complement blockade while providing appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis and close monitoring. In the reported patient the treating team waited for sterile blood cultures, administered Menveo and Bexsero, gave prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin because the vaccines had not been in place for 2 weeks, and then started eculizumab (induction 900 mg IV weekly ×4; maintenance 1200 mg IV every 2 weeks as per local protocol).

PEX can be used as a bridge to eculizumab, but persistent or progressive renal failure is unlikely to reverse without complement blockade in true CM‑HUS.

## When (and when not) to biopsy the kidney
Kidney biopsy is useful when the diagnosis is unclear and will change management — for example when distinguishing an immune complex GN from a TMA or confirming TMA in a patient you’re considering for prolonged complement blockade. Don’t biopsy during profound thrombocytopenia or while coagulation is deranged; wait until counts recover (many centres use platelets >50–75×10^9/L) and coagulopathy is corrected. In this case biopsy was performed when platelets had risen to 77×10^9/L and it confirmed fibrin thrombi and ischaemic glomeruli consistent with TMA, supporting the decision to continue complement blockade.

## Heme–nephro collaboration: practical division of labour
Early and repeated multidisciplinary discussion saves time and kidneys. Hematology typically leads the immediate TTP/PEX decision, steroid use and transfusion strategy; nephrology leads renal replacement therapy, biopsy timing and interpretation of kidney histology. Shared priorities: (1) ensure ADAMTS13 and complement samples are taken before PEX/eculizumab, (2) coordinate vascular access for PEX and dialysis, and (3) agree thresholds for platelet transfusion and biopsy timing.

## Practical pitfalls and clinical pearls from the case
- Sepsis/DIC can mask TMA early — recheck the picture as infection is treated. In this patient haemolysis emerged only after the septic picture started to improve.
- A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) is not always autoimmune haemolysis; recent penicillin exposure or transfusions can give false positives (the DAT in this case was initially positive then negative).
- Normal C3/C4 does not exclude complement‑mediated HUS. Functional assays and genetics are helpful but can be altered by PEX and complement blockade.
- Draw ADAMTS13 and complement samples before PEX and eculizumab whenever possible; early sampling was key in this case to exclude TTP.
- Platelet transfusion can worsen outcomes in TTP/TMA and should be given only for bleeding or invasive procedures.
- If you must start eculizumab before vaccination immunity is established, use antibiotic prophylaxis and close follow‑up.

## Case wrap (why the approach mattered)
The patient’s initial picture was dominated by severe sepsis with DIC and pancytopenia following a dog bite; C. canimorsus bacteraemia was confirmed. Hemolysis and schistocytosis appeared later, ADAMTS13 was not consistent with TTP, kidney biopsy showed TMA, and the team used PEX as a bridge to eculizumab. After vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis eculizumab was started, hematological remission was achieved and kidney function partially recovered (the patient remains on maintenance eculizumab with creatinine improved from dialysis dependence to ~175 µmol/L). This sequence illustrates the importance of early, pragmatic decisions: prioritised smear and ADAMTS13, timely PEX when necessary, parallel testing for STEC and complement, and an early multidisciplinary pivot once TTP was excluded.

## Key take‑home messages
- Do a peripheral smear for schistocytes immediately and send ADAMTS13 urgently; treat TTP empirically with plasma exchange if it cannot be excluded.
- Use the PLASMIC score to triage urgency, but treat the patient, not the score.
- Draw complement and ADAMTS13 samples before PEX/eculizumab whenever possible — therapy can alter test results.
- Parallel workup: stool PCR for Shiga, pregnancy test, autoimmune serology, BP review for malignant hypertension, and cultures for infection.
- Avoid routine platelet transfusion in suspected TTP/TMA unless life‑threatening bleeding or a procedure is needed; transfuse RBCs for symptomatic anaemia.
- If ADAMTS13 >10% and STEC is negative, consider CM‑HUS and early discussion about eculizumab; vaccinate for meningococcus and use antibiotic prophylaxis if vaccination timing is insufficient.
- Coordinate early heme–nephro care: PEX, dialysis access, biopsy timing and genetics require rapid collaboration.

If you take one thing home: in adult MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI, act fast, prioritise the smear and ADAMTS13, start PEX when TTP can’t be excluded, and be ready to pivot to complement blockade when the data and clinical course point to CM‑HUS.



# Eculizumab in the Infectious Crossfire: Timing, Vaccines, and Prophylaxis

## Why this case stopped me in my tracks
A previously healthy woman develops fulminant sepsis after a dog bite, grows Capnocytophaga canimorsus, has DIC and dialysis-dependent AKI — then, after initial improvement, develops microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), persistent thrombocytopenia and a biopsy-proven thrombotic microangiopathy. ADAMTS13 was not consistent with TTP, STEC was negative, plasma exchange (PEX) was used as a bridge, meningococcal vaccines were given, prophylactic penicillin started, and eculizumab was started once blood cultures were clear. Haematologic remission and partial renal recovery followed. No pathogenic complement gene variants were found.

What makes the case instructive is the clinical tightrope: complement blockade (eculizumab) is often the kidney-saving therapy for complement-mediated HUS, but blocking the terminal complement pathway in the setting of recent or ongoing bacteraemia—especially with encapsulated organisms—can be dangerous. This case shows a pragmatic pathway through that tension.

## The central dilemma — why timing matters
Complement activity is crucial for opsonization and killing of encapsulated gram-negative organisms. Eculizumab disables C5 cleavage and terminal complement activity: that’s the mechanism that controls TMA but it also removes a key host defense against organisms like Neisseria meningitidis — and, less commonly discussed, other encapsulated or serum-sensitive organisms (including some Capnocytophaga strains). Vaccines reduce but do not eliminate risk; some centres therefore add antibiotic prophylaxis. Waiting for cultures to clear reduces infectious risk but may allow irreversible kidney injury if eculizumab is delayed. How do you balance those risks?

This case illustrates a common, pragmatic strategy: aggressive infection control and supportive care first, PEX as a bridge when needed, vaccinate as early as possible, give prophylactic antibiotics if vaccination can’t precede eculizumab by 2 weeks, and start eculizumab once the acute bacteremia has been controlled — while using laboratory checks (CH50/AH50) and careful clinical follow-up.

## A practical, stepwise framework (grounded in the case)

1) Rapid triage and diagnostics. In any adult with MAHA, thrombocytopenia and AKI rule out TTP emergently (ADAMTS13) and test for STEC. In this case ADAMTS13 was 43.5% (TTP unlikely), and stool STEC PCR was negative; the smear and biopsy pointed to TMA.

2) Treat the infection aggressively. The patient received broad-spectrum therapy (piperacillin–tazobactam then de‑escalation to penicillin after cultures grew C. canimorsus), ICU-level support and renal replacement as needed. Aim for source control and microbiological clearance before complement blockade if the clinical picture allows.

3) Use PEX as a bridge when urgent therapy is needed or diagnosis uncertain. PEX can transiently improve labs and remove inflammatory mediators, and is lifesaving for TTP. In CM‑HUS it is only a temporising measure — in this case multiple PEX sessions produced transient diuresis before sustained improvement after eculizumab. Be mindful that PEX replaces complement (Octaplas/FFP) and will confound complement functional assays and remove monoclonal antibodies (see below).

4) Vaccinate for meningococcus early. Give MenACWY and MenB as soon as feasible before starting eculizumab. Ideally allow 2 weeks for vaccine-induced protection, but real life frequently compresses that window.

5) If you cannot wait 2 weeks after vaccination (or vaccines weren’t given), start antimicrobial prophylaxis immediately and maintain heightened vigilance. In this case the team administered Menveo and Bexsero immediately before eculizumab and started phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) because the 2‑week window was not met. Many centres will continue prophylaxis during C5 blockade because invasive disease can occur despite vaccination.

6) Start eculizumab when the infection is controlled or when the renal/laboratory trajectory makes delay unacceptable. Document shared decision-making between nephrology, haematology, infectious disease and the patient. In the reported patient, blood cultures had cleared and vaccines were given (with prophylactic penicillin) before eculizumab induction; hematologic remission and partial renal recovery followed.

## PEX and eculizumab — operational tips
PEX removes circulating eculizumab and replenishes complement components (if FFP/Octaplas is used). Practical points:
- Give eculizumab after the last planned PEX session when possible.
- If PEX must continue after eculizumab, expect substantial drug removal — coordinate with pharmacy/haematology to give supplemental eculizumab doses and/or check pharmacodynamic markers.
- Remember that plasma products will transiently restore CH50/AH50, so complement functional assays done right after PEX are misleading.

In the case, three PEX sessions with Octaplas were given; AH50 measured after PEX and the first eculizumab dose was low (6%), but the team interpreted that result in the context of recent interventions.

## CH50/AH50 — how to confirm pharmacodynamic blockade
Eculizumab blocks terminal complement; the simplest pharmacodynamic check is a total hemolytic complement assay (CH50). After adequate C5 blockade, CH50 typically becomes undetectable or profoundly suppressed. AH50 (alternative pathway hemolytic activity) can also be suppressed but is more affected by complement factor replacement (PEX/Octaplas) and less standardized across labs.

Practical guidance:
- Obtain baseline CH50/AH50 if feasible before PEX or eculizumab.
- To confirm blockade, measure CH50 at a trough (prior to the next scheduled eculizumab dose). An undetectable/suppressed CH50 supports adequate pharmacodynamic effect.
- Be cautious interpreting CH50/AH50 if PEX, plasma products or recent eculizumab dosing have occurred — coordinate timing with the lab.

## Vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis — the real-world plan
- Vaccinate against meningococcal serogroups (MenACWY) and serogroup B (MenB) as soon as possible before starting eculizumab. Ideally wait 14 days after vaccination before starting complement blockade.
- If you cannot wait 14 days (urgent renal threat or management constraints), give vaccines immediately and start antimicrobial prophylaxis. The team in this case used phenoxymethylpenicillin as prophylaxis.
- Many centres continue prophylactic antibiotics for the duration of complement blockade because of breakthrough infection reports and the potentially catastrophic consequences of meningococcal disease. Local ID guidance and resistance patterns should shape the antibiotic choice.
- Counsel patients that vaccination reduces, but does not eliminate, the risk of meningococcal disease; they must seek immediate care for fever, and clinicians should have a low threshold for empirical antibiotics.

## Counseling on non‑Neisseria pathogens — pet contact and Capnocytophaga
Eculizumab most classically raises risk for Neisseria, but loss of terminal complement function can increase susceptibility to other invasive gram‑negative infections. Capnocytophaga canimorsus—classically associated with dog bites—is an example: it can be encapsulated and variably serum‑susceptible. Practical counseling:
- Advise caution with close contact with dogs/cats; treat bites promptly (irrigation and early antibiotics).
- Ensure access to prompt care for any skin breaks, bites or febrile illness.
- Consider continuing penicillin prophylaxis (if used) since it will also cover Capnocytophaga in many cases.

## Monitoring and outpatient safety
After eculizumab starts monitor hematologic response (CBC, LDH, haptoglobin), renal function and urine output closely. Check CH50 periodically to confirm pharmacodynamic blockade, being mindful of confounders (recent PEX, plasma products). Provide clear safety netting: fever is an emergency. Patients should be given written instructions (alert card/bracelet) stating they are on complement blockade, their vaccine status and recommended immediate actions — and have a low threshold for contacting medical services.

Genetic testing for complement variants should be pursued but does not change the immediate need for blockade; it informs long‑term duration considerations. In the reported case, genetic testing showed no pathogenic variants but therapy was continued given disease severity and partial renal recovery — a common, pragmatic decision.

## When the kidney dictates urgency
Time to eculizumab matters for renal outcome. Severe AKI with dialysis dependence and ongoing hemolysis/TMA is proof that the disease process is active and destructive: delays in complement blockade increase the risk of permanent kidney injury. If the patient is progressively oliguric/anuric, dialysis‑dependent, with ongoing MAHA and thrombocytopenia, the balance shifts toward earlier eculizumab — even in the setting of recent infection — after aggressive antibiotic therapy and with prophylactic measures in place.

In short: if infection is controlled and cultures are negative, start eculizumab. If infection is ongoing but renal injury is catastrophic and irreversible without blockade, consider eculizumab with maximal antimicrobial therapy, PEX bridging where helpful, and detailed multidisciplinary discussion.

## Clinical pearls (short and practical)
Pearl: Normal C3/C4 does not rule out complement‑mediated HUS — complement levels may be normal even in active disease.  
Pearl: ADAMTS13 <10% = TTP; higher values (as in this case, 43.5%) make TTP unlikely and push you to consider CM‑HUS.  
Pearl: PEX can temporise but not cure CM‑HUS; it will remove eculizumab and replenish complement if you use Octaplas/FFP. Coordinate timing.  
Pearl: Vaccinate (MenACWY + MenB) before eculizumab if you can; if you can’t wait, start antibiotics and continue prophylaxis per local practice.  
Pearl: Use CH50 (and AH50 with caveats) to confirm pharmacodynamic blockade — but interpret results in the context of recent PEX/plasma products.

## Take‑home messages
- In infection‑triggered CM‑HUS you must balance the renal benefit of early eculizumab against the infectious risk of terminal complement blockade.  
- Treat the infection aggressively and aim for culture clearance before starting eculizumab when clinically feasible; use PEX as a bridge if needed.  
- Give MenACWY and MenB vaccines as early as possible; if you cannot wait 14 days post‑vaccination, start antimicrobial prophylaxis immediately and maintain close ID follow‑up.  
- CH50 (and AH50, interpreted cautiously) are useful pharmacodynamic checks to confirm complement blockade; timing relative to PEX and plasma products matters.  
- Counsel patients about meningococcal risk and non‑Neisseria pathogens (including Capnocytophaga): provide an action plan for fever, emphasize wound care after animal contact, and consider continuing prophylactic antibiotics during complement inhibition per local guidance.  

This case is a powerful reminder that successful navigation of CM‑HUS in the infectious setting is multidisciplinary, time‑sensitive and logistical — and when done thoughtfully it can save kidneys without losing the patient to infection.



# Reading the Complement Compass: What C3/C4, AH50, and Genetics Really Tell You

Why this case grabs your attention: a previously healthy woman is bitten by her dog, develops fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis with DIC, becomes dialysis-dependent, then—after apparent improvement—suddenly develops microangiopathic hemolysis and thrombocytopenia. ADAMTS13 is not consistent with TTP, kidney biopsy shows TMA, and the team moves to plasma exchange then eculizumab. Yet the complement story is messy: routine C3/C4 were normal, local functional assays were initially unrevealing, and the extended AH50 was markedly reduced only after plasma exchange (PEX) and one dose of eculizumab—exactly the situation that teaches you the hard lesson: timing matters. This patient ultimately did well with complement blockade, but the diagnostic and logistical pitfalls are a real-world how-to for anyone managing suspected complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS).

## The simple headline: normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM‑HUS
C3 and C4 are easy, cheap and often ordered reflexively. But CM-HUS is primarily an alternative pathway problem. C3 can be normal because:
- Complement activation may be localized to microvascular beds and not produce large-scale plasma consumption.
- C3 is an acute-phase reactant and hepatic production can mask consumption.
- Alternative-pathway activation doesn’t consume C4, so a normal C4 is expected.

In this case, early C3/C4 were within reference ranges despite biopsy-proven TMA. Don’t be reassured by "normal" C3/C4 when clinical TMA is present.

## CH50 and AH50: what they measure — and when they lie
CH50 (classical pathway hemolytic assay) and AH50 (alternative pathway hemolytic assay) are functional tests that measure the ability of serum to form the membrane attack complex and lyse indicator erythrocytes. Important practical implications:
- Eculizumab binds C5 and prevents MAC formation; CH50 and AH50 will be suppressed after C5 blockade. A low CH50/AH50 after eculizumab says nothing about the patient's underlying genetic complement competence — it says the drug is doing its job.
- Plasma exchange can remove autoantibodies and dysregulated complement fragments (reducing apparent activation), and replacement with donor plasma (eg, Octaplas, FFP) can transiently normalize or alter functional assays. Conversely, PEX with albumin depletes complement activity.
- Timing matters: a functional assay taken after PEX or after eculizumab is essentially uninterpretable for diagnosing the pre‑treatment complement state.

In the reported patient, an extended AH50 was 6% when measured 8 days after the final PEX and 4 days after an eculizumab dose — most consistent with treatment effect, not the natural history of the disease.

## What to prioritize pre-treatment (if you can)
When you suspect CM‑HUS, try to get baseline tests before you start PEX or eculizumab. These tests are the most informative if collected pre-treatment:
- Serum for C3 and C4 (quick screen, but expect low sensitivity).
- Functional assays: AH50 (alternative pathway) is the highest-yield functional test for CM‑HUS; CH50 (classical) adds information if available.
- Soluble C5b-9 (sC5b-9) when available — a marker of terminal pathway activation and useful adjunctive evidence.
- Complement regulatory proteins: quantitative factor H, factor I, factor B.
- Anti–factor H autoantibodies (important because they are treatable and often removed by PEX).
- ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor (draw before PEX if possible) to exclude TTP (activity <10% = TTP).
- Stool Shiga toxin PCR to exclude STEC-HUS.
- Peripheral smear, LDH, haptoglobin, reticulocyte count, DAT, coagulation panel, urine studies, blood cultures.
- EDTA whole blood (or as your reference lab requests) for CD46 (MCP) expression by flow cytometry and for genetic testing (DNA), and citrated plasma for ADAMTS13 depending on local lab requirements.

Timing note: genetic testing (DNA) is unaffected by PEX or eculizumab and can be drawn anytime, but results take weeks. Anti‑factor H autoantibodies are best obtained before PEX because PEX will remove them.

## If you can’t get pre-treatment samples — what to do
Often the patient needs emergent PEX or eculizumab and you can’t wait. When that happens:
- Document exact timing: record time of blood draw, time and type of PEX (albumin vs plasma replacement like Octaplas/FFP), and time of first eculizumab dose.
- Still send genetic testing (EDTA) and baseline serum for complement (label clearly, because it will be interpreted in light of treatment).
- If PEX uses plasma replacement, expect functional assays to be artificially "better" for a short period; with albumin replacement expect complement depletion.
- If eculizumab has been given, don’t rely on CH50/AH50 to evaluate underlying complement dysregulation — use genetics, autoantibody testing, and clinical trajectory instead.

In the case above, the extended AH50 was low after treatment, and the team correctly interpreted this as treatment effect (they had normal local functional assays earlier and documented recent PEX and eculizumab).

## Genetics: when to send, what it tells you, and why many patients are “variant negative”
- Send a complement gene panel in all suspected CM‑HUS cases. Typical panels include CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, CD46 (MCP), the CFHR gene cluster, thrombomodulin (THBD) and others; the exact genes depend on the reference lab.
- A pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant supports a diagnosis of complement-mediated disease and predicts higher relapse risk — this information is very useful for decisions about long-term eculizumab.
- However, 30–50% (estimates vary; the case report cited 50–70%) of patients with CM‑HUS will not have an identifiable pathogenic variant. Reasons include incomplete knowledge of causative genes, variants of uncertain significance, non-genetic triggers (infections, pregnancy, drugs), or regulatory-region mutations not covered by panels.
- Genetic results rarely change acute management but are pivotal for counseling, relapse risk assessment, and duration-of-therapy decisions.

In our patient the panel returned no pathogenic variants; the team therefore elected a finite maintenance strategy (eculizumab ongoing for months with reevaluation), justified by the severity of the episode even in the absence of a detected mutation.

## How results should influence management
- ADAMTS13 <10%: treat as TTP immediately with urgent PEX (don’t wait for genetic tests).
- Positive stool Shiga PCR: manage as STEC-HUS (supportive care; antibiotic use nuanced).
- Anti‑factor H antibodies: PEX plus immunosuppression is often needed; consider eculizumab in severe cases.
- Pathogenic complement variant in CFH/CFI/CFB/C3/CFHRs/MCP: high relapse risk → strong consideration for long‑term complement blockade.
- No variant identified: consider a time-limited course of eculizumab (commonly months) with planned reassessment; clinical severity and renal recovery guide decisions.
- If you start eculizumab during or soon after sepsis, ensure meningococcal vaccination when feasible, give appropriate antimicrobial prophylaxis, and monitor closely (in the case, antibiotics and vaccinations were used before complement blockade).

Remember: functional assays are not reliable after eculizumab/PEX; genetics and the clinical course are what you will use to make long-term decisions.

## Practical ordering and interpretation checklist (for ED/ICU/nephrology)
Before plasma exchange or complement blockade, if clinically possible, draw and send: serum for C3/C4 and sC5b-9; serum for AH50 (alternative pathway functional assay) and CH50 if available; quantitative factor H, factor I and factor B; anti‑factor H autoantibodies. Draw citrated plasma for ADAMTS13 activity/inhibitor and send stool for Shiga toxin PCR. Collect EDTA whole blood for DNA/genetic testing and for CD46 flow cytometry per your reference lab’s instructions.

If immediate PEX is required and pre-treatment sampling isn’t feasible, document exact times and replacement fluid type (albumin versus plasma/Octaplas), and send genetic testing and any feasible serum samples anyway with labels noting “post‑PEX” or “post‑eculizumab.” Expect that functional assays (AH50/CH50) measured after eculizumab or recent plasma replacement will be confounded.

Coordinate with your reference lab: complement testing and complement genetics are often sent out; confirm sample types (serum vs EDTA vs citrate), processing (centrifuge and freeze vs refrigerate), and courier requirements. Do not use EDTA for functional complement assays (EDTA chelates ions required for complement assays).

When you review results, interpret them in context:
- Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS.
- Low AH50 before treatment supports alternative pathway consumption/activation; low AH50 after eculizumab/PEX is probably treatment effect.
- Positive anti‑factor H antibody (pre‑PEX) points to an acquired cause and suggests immunosuppression + PEX ± eculizumab.
- Genetic findings inform relapse risk and long‑term therapy planning — but many patients will be variant‑negative.

## Back to the dog‑bite patient — what she taught us
Her presentation evolved from sepsis/DIC to biopsy‑proven TMA with clear clinical response only after complement blockade. Yet routine complement tests were normal early, and the extended AH50 was low only after PEX and eculizumab. The team navigated competing priorities: draw ADAMTS13 early (it returned 43.5% and essentially excluded TTP), treat sepsis aggressively (blood cultures later grew C. canimorsus), use PEX as bridging, vaccinate and give prophylactic antibiotics, then start eculizumab after cultures were negative. Genetic testing was negative; the decision was made to continue maintenance eculizumab for months with reassessment because of the severity of renal injury. That sequence is a pragmatic blueprint when clinical urgency and laboratory idealism clash.

## Clinical pearls and take‑home points
- Normal C3/C4 does NOT rule out complement‑mediated HUS; don’t be falsely reassured.
- Prioritize AH50 (alternative pathway) and anti‑factor H antibody testing BEFORE plasma exchange or eculizumab if you can.
- ADAMTS13 MUST be drawn before PEX when possible — a low value (<10%) points to TTP and mandates PEX.
- CH50/AH50 are useless for diagnosing pre‑treatment complement dysregulation after eculizumab or after PEX with recent replacement plasma; always document timing and replacement fluid.
- Send genetic testing in all suspected CM‑HUS cases — results guide long‑term therapy even though they won’t usually alter acute management.
- If you must start eculizumab during/after infection, vaccinate and provide antimicrobial prophylaxis and careful follow‑up; record all treatment timings for later interpretation of labs.



# DAT Positive, But Not AIHA: Avoiding the Steroid Trap in TMA

## Why this case grabbed our attention
A healthy woman develops fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis after a dog bite, with DIC, pancytopenia and dialysis‑dependent AKI. When her haemoglobin suddenly fell, a direct antiglobulin test (DAT) returned IgG positive and high‑dose steroids were started for presumed autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA). The haemolysis did not respond. A blood smear showed 15–20% schistocytes, haptoglobin fell to undetectable, LDH rose, ADAMTS13 was not consistent with TTP and a renal biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). The final diagnosis was complement‑mediated HUS triggered by infection; plasma exchange (PEX) and eculizumab produced sustained improvement.

What’s striking here—and what every clinician should remember—is that a positive DAT in a septic patient can be a red herring. It derailed the diagnostic pathway briefly and exposed the patient to high‑dose steroids at a dangerous time. This is the “steroid trap” we want you to avoid.

## The diagnostic trap: DAT ≠ AIHA (always)
DAT positivity tells you there are antibodies or complement fragments bound to a patient’s red cells. It does not automatically mean warm autoimmune haemolytic anaemia that will respond to prolonged immunosuppression. Infections, recent transfusions, and several drugs (notably penicillin‑class antibiotics and some cephalosporins) can cause a positive DAT without clinically relevant autoimmune haemolysis. In sepsis you can also get nonspecific coating of RBCs. The case illustrates that penicillin exposure and prior transfusions were plausible causes of an initial false‑positive DAT.

Putting a patient with ongoing sepsis and probable TMA on high‑dose steroids based solely on a single positive DAT risks delaying the correct diagnosis and therapy (PEX, complement blockade) and may worsen outcomes.

## How to tell AIHA from MAHA (practical, bedside)
Focus on the pattern, not a single test.

- AIHA (warm IgG): isolated haemolysis, spherocytes on smear, positive DAT (IgG ± C3d), often robust reticulocytosis, platelets and renal function usually preserved unless another process is present.
- MAHA/TMA: fragmentation haemolysis — schistocytes dominate, marked thrombocytopenia, variable reticulocyte response (may be blunted in sepsis), prominent LDH elevation, undetectable haptoglobin, AKI is common. DAT is usually negative — but can be falsely positive in the context of drugs/transfusions/sepsis.

Always get a peripheral smear early. In this case the smear (15–20% schistocytes) was the decisive clue pointing away from AIHA and toward TMA.

## Why the DAT can mislead
Mechanisms behind a positive DAT that are not classic AIHA include:

- Drug‑related RBC coating: penicillin‑type drugs can adsorb to RBCs and produce a positive DAT; cephalosporins (certain agents) can form immune complexes. The presence of antibody on the cell does not necessarily mean clinically significant extravascular haemolysis.
- Recent transfusion: donor RBCs or passively transferred antibodies can leave a positive DAT.
- Sepsis/inflammation: nonspecific complement activation or immune complexes can coat RBCs.
- Laboratory artefact or recent transfusion/immunoglobulin exposure.

In the presented case the team appropriately suspected that the DAT could be a false positive from penicillin exposure or prior transfusions; repeat DAT testing later was negative.

## What to do—an action plan when you see DAT+ in a sick patient
(Keep this as a mental checklist you can run through fast at the bedside.)

1. Don’t reflexively commit to prolonged steroids. If you’re worried about AIHA and decide to give a short steroid trial, reassess within 24–48 hours. Lack of response should prompt immediate reappraisal.
2. Confirm haemolysis with labs: Hb trend, reticulocyte count, LDH, indirect bilirubin, haptoglobin. Interpret reticulocytes cautiously in sepsis.
3. Get a high‑quality peripheral smear reviewed by someone who looks for schistocytes versus spherocytes. A significant schistocyte burden (in this case 15–20%) favors MAHA/TMA.
4. Check platelets and renal function. Thrombocytopenia + MAHA + AKI = think TMA until proven otherwise.
5. Review exposures: recent transfusions, antibiotics (penicillins/cephalosporins), IVIG, or other drugs that can cause a false positive DAT.
6. If TMA is suspected, send ADAMTS13 and start TMA management pathways promptly: use PLASMIC score/clinical judgement to decide whether to start empirical PEX for possible TTP while awaiting ADAMTS13. For suspected complement‑mediated HUS, involve nephrology/hematology early; eculizumab is disease‑specific but consider infection control before starting complement blockade.
7. If drug‑induced immune haemolysis is suspected, stop the culprit drug and request specialized testing (elution studies/drug‑dependent antibody testing) from an immunohematology reference lab.
8. Communicate clearly: a simple phrase to hematology/nephrology can change decisions — e.g., “DAT+ IgG but smear shows prominent schistocytes, platelets low and AKI — TMA is more likely than warm AIHA; please prioritize ADAMTS13/PEX and TMA workup before committing to ongoing steroids.”

## Special considerations about therapy timing (lessons from the case)
- Steroids can be harmful in active sepsis. Use them judiciously and reassess fast.
- PEX is lifesaving in TTP and can temporize in other TMAs, but it is not definitive therapy for complement‑mediated HUS.
- Eculizumab is highly effective for CM‑HUS but blocks terminal complement and increases the risk from encapsulated bacteria. In the case, clinicians waited for negative blood cultures and gave meningococcal vaccines, then started eculizumab with temporary antibiotic prophylaxis since vaccination timing was suboptimal. That sequencing is important when infection is the trigger.

## Clinical pearls
- A positive DAT does not equal AIHA. Consider the whole clinical picture: smear, platelets, renal function and haemolysis pattern.
- Schistocytes are your MVP when diagnosing MAHA/TMA — ask for a smear review early and repeatedly.
- Recent penicillin/cephalosporin exposure or transfusion can produce a positive DAT without AIHA.
- If haemolysis does not respond rapidly to steroids, stop and re‑think: look for TMA and involve hematology/nephrology.
- ADAMTS13 <10% = TTP. Borderline or normal activity should push you to other causes (STEC‑HUS, CM‑HUS, DIC).
- In suspected CM‑HUS triggered by infection, coordinate timing of complement blockade with culture results, vaccination and prophylactic antibiotics.

## Key take‑home messages
- A single positive DAT in a septic patient is not diagnostic of warm AIHA—don’t let it derail the TMA workup.
- Always pair DAT results with a peripheral smear and a full haemolysis panel; schistocytes + thrombocytopenia + AKI should redirect you toward TMA.
- Review recent transfusions and drug exposures (penicillins/cephalosporins are common culprits); stop suspect drugs and repeat DAT/elution testing when appropriate.
- If TMA is likely, prioritize ADAMTS13 testing, empirical PEX when indicated, and early specialist involvement rather than prolonged empiric steroids.
- When infection triggers CM‑HUS, balance the urgency of complement blockade against infectious risks (culture clearance, vaccination, and antimicrobial prophylaxis as needed).
