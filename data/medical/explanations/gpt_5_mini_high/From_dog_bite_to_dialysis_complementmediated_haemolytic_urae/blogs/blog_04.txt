Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Eculizumab in the Infectious Crossfire: Timing, Vaccines, and Prophylaxis

## Why this case stopped me in my tracks
A previously healthy woman develops fulminant sepsis after a dog bite, grows Capnocytophaga canimorsus, has DIC and dialysis-dependent AKI — then, after initial improvement, develops microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), persistent thrombocytopenia and a biopsy-proven thrombotic microangiopathy. ADAMTS13 was not consistent with TTP, STEC was negative, plasma exchange (PEX) was used as a bridge, meningococcal vaccines were given, prophylactic penicillin started, and eculizumab was started once blood cultures were clear. Haematologic remission and partial renal recovery followed. No pathogenic complement gene variants were found.

What makes the case instructive is the clinical tightrope: complement blockade (eculizumab) is often the kidney-saving therapy for complement-mediated HUS, but blocking the terminal complement pathway in the setting of recent or ongoing bacteraemia—especially with encapsulated organisms—can be dangerous. This case shows a pragmatic pathway through that tension.

## The central dilemma — why timing matters
Complement activity is crucial for opsonization and killing of encapsulated gram-negative organisms. Eculizumab disables C5 cleavage and terminal complement activity: that’s the mechanism that controls TMA but it also removes a key host defense against organisms like Neisseria meningitidis — and, less commonly discussed, other encapsulated or serum-sensitive organisms (including some Capnocytophaga strains). Vaccines reduce but do not eliminate risk; some centres therefore add antibiotic prophylaxis. Waiting for cultures to clear reduces infectious risk but may allow irreversible kidney injury if eculizumab is delayed. How do you balance those risks?

This case illustrates a common, pragmatic strategy: aggressive infection control and supportive care first, PEX as a bridge when needed, vaccinate as early as possible, give prophylactic antibiotics if vaccination can’t precede eculizumab by 2 weeks, and start eculizumab once the acute bacteremia has been controlled — while using laboratory checks (CH50/AH50) and careful clinical follow-up.

## A practical, stepwise framework (grounded in the case)

1) Rapid triage and diagnostics. In any adult with MAHA, thrombocytopenia and AKI rule out TTP emergently (ADAMTS13) and test for STEC. In this case ADAMTS13 was 43.5% (TTP unlikely), and stool STEC PCR was negative; the smear and biopsy pointed to TMA.

2) Treat the infection aggressively. The patient received broad-spectrum therapy (piperacillin–tazobactam then de‑escalation to penicillin after cultures grew C. canimorsus), ICU-level support and renal replacement as needed. Aim for source control and microbiological clearance before complement blockade if the clinical picture allows.

3) Use PEX as a bridge when urgent therapy is needed or diagnosis uncertain. PEX can transiently improve labs and remove inflammatory mediators, and is lifesaving for TTP. In CM‑HUS it is only a temporising measure — in this case multiple PEX sessions produced transient diuresis before sustained improvement after eculizumab. Be mindful that PEX replaces complement (Octaplas/FFP) and will confound complement functional assays and remove monoclonal antibodies (see below).

4) Vaccinate for meningococcus early. Give MenACWY and MenB as soon as feasible before starting eculizumab. Ideally allow 2 weeks for vaccine-induced protection, but real life frequently compresses that window.

5) If you cannot wait 2 weeks after vaccination (or vaccines weren’t given), start antimicrobial prophylaxis immediately and maintain heightened vigilance. In this case the team administered Menveo and Bexsero immediately before eculizumab and started phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) because the 2‑week window was not met. Many centres will continue prophylaxis during C5 blockade because invasive disease can occur despite vaccination.

6) Start eculizumab when the infection is controlled or when the renal/laboratory trajectory makes delay unacceptable. Document shared decision-making between nephrology, haematology, infectious disease and the patient. In the reported patient, blood cultures had cleared and vaccines were given (with prophylactic penicillin) before eculizumab induction; hematologic remission and partial renal recovery followed.

## PEX and eculizumab — operational tips
PEX removes circulating eculizumab and replenishes complement components (if FFP/Octaplas is used). Practical points:
- Give eculizumab after the last planned PEX session when possible.
- If PEX must continue after eculizumab, expect substantial drug removal — coordinate with pharmacy/haematology to give supplemental eculizumab doses and/or check pharmacodynamic markers.
- Remember that plasma products will transiently restore CH50/AH50, so complement functional assays done right after PEX are misleading.

In the case, three PEX sessions with Octaplas were given; AH50 measured after PEX and the first eculizumab dose was low (6%), but the team interpreted that result in the context of recent interventions.

## CH50/AH50 — how to confirm pharmacodynamic blockade
Eculizumab blocks terminal complement; the simplest pharmacodynamic check is a total hemolytic complement assay (CH50). After adequate C5 blockade, CH50 typically becomes undetectable or profoundly suppressed. AH50 (alternative pathway hemolytic activity) can also be suppressed but is more affected by complement factor replacement (PEX/Octaplas) and less standardized across labs.

Practical guidance:
- Obtain baseline CH50/AH50 if feasible before PEX or eculizumab.
- To confirm blockade, measure CH50 at a trough (prior to the next scheduled eculizumab dose). An undetectable/suppressed CH50 supports adequate pharmacodynamic effect.
- Be cautious interpreting CH50/AH50 if PEX, plasma products or recent eculizumab dosing have occurred — coordinate timing with the lab.

## Vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis — the real-world plan
- Vaccinate against meningococcal serogroups (MenACWY) and serogroup B (MenB) as soon as possible before starting eculizumab. Ideally wait 14 days after vaccination before starting complement blockade.
- If you cannot wait 14 days (urgent renal threat or management constraints), give vaccines immediately and start antimicrobial prophylaxis. The team in this case used phenoxymethylpenicillin as prophylaxis.
- Many centres continue prophylactic antibiotics for the duration of complement blockade because of breakthrough infection reports and the potentially catastrophic consequences of meningococcal disease. Local ID guidance and resistance patterns should shape the antibiotic choice.
- Counsel patients that vaccination reduces, but does not eliminate, the risk of meningococcal disease; they must seek immediate care for fever, and clinicians should have a low threshold for empirical antibiotics.

## Counseling on non‑Neisseria pathogens — pet contact and Capnocytophaga
Eculizumab most classically raises risk for Neisseria, but loss of terminal complement function can increase susceptibility to other invasive gram‑negative infections. Capnocytophaga canimorsus—classically associated with dog bites—is an example: it can be encapsulated and variably serum‑susceptible. Practical counseling:
- Advise caution with close contact with dogs/cats; treat bites promptly (irrigation and early antibiotics).
- Ensure access to prompt care for any skin breaks, bites or febrile illness.
- Consider continuing penicillin prophylaxis (if used) since it will also cover Capnocytophaga in many cases.

## Monitoring and outpatient safety
After eculizumab starts monitor hematologic response (CBC, LDH, haptoglobin), renal function and urine output closely. Check CH50 periodically to confirm pharmacodynamic blockade, being mindful of confounders (recent PEX, plasma products). Provide clear safety netting: fever is an emergency. Patients should be given written instructions (alert card/bracelet) stating they are on complement blockade, their vaccine status and recommended immediate actions — and have a low threshold for contacting medical services.

Genetic testing for complement variants should be pursued but does not change the immediate need for blockade; it informs long‑term duration considerations. In the reported case, genetic testing showed no pathogenic variants but therapy was continued given disease severity and partial renal recovery — a common, pragmatic decision.

## When the kidney dictates urgency
Time to eculizumab matters for renal outcome. Severe AKI with dialysis dependence and ongoing hemolysis/TMA is proof that the disease process is active and destructive: delays in complement blockade increase the risk of permanent kidney injury. If the patient is progressively oliguric/anuric, dialysis‑dependent, with ongoing MAHA and thrombocytopenia, the balance shifts toward earlier eculizumab — even in the setting of recent infection — after aggressive antibiotic therapy and with prophylactic measures in place.

In short: if infection is controlled and cultures are negative, start eculizumab. If infection is ongoing but renal injury is catastrophic and irreversible without blockade, consider eculizumab with maximal antimicrobial therapy, PEX bridging where helpful, and detailed multidisciplinary discussion.

## Clinical pearls (short and practical)
Pearl: Normal C3/C4 does not rule out complement‑mediated HUS — complement levels may be normal even in active disease.  
Pearl: ADAMTS13 <10% = TTP; higher values (as in this case, 43.5%) make TTP unlikely and push you to consider CM‑HUS.  
Pearl: PEX can temporise but not cure CM‑HUS; it will remove eculizumab and replenish complement if you use Octaplas/FFP. Coordinate timing.  
Pearl: Vaccinate (MenACWY + MenB) before eculizumab if you can; if you can’t wait, start antibiotics and continue prophylaxis per local practice.  
Pearl: Use CH50 (and AH50 with caveats) to confirm pharmacodynamic blockade — but interpret results in the context of recent PEX/plasma products.

## Take‑home messages
- In infection‑triggered CM‑HUS you must balance the renal benefit of early eculizumab against the infectious risk of terminal complement blockade.  
- Treat the infection aggressively and aim for culture clearance before starting eculizumab when clinically feasible; use PEX as a bridge if needed.  
- Give MenACWY and MenB vaccines as early as possible; if you cannot wait 14 days post‑vaccination, start antimicrobial prophylaxis immediately and maintain close ID follow‑up.  
- CH50 (and AH50, interpreted cautiously) are useful pharmacodynamic checks to confirm complement blockade; timing relative to PEX and plasma products matters.  
- Counsel patients about meningococcal risk and non‑Neisseria pathogens (including Capnocytophaga): provide an action plan for fever, emphasize wound care after animal contact, and consider continuing prophylactic antibiotics during complement inhibition per local guidance.  

This case is a powerful reminder that successful navigation of CM‑HUS in the infectious setting is multidisciplinary, time‑sensitive and logistical — and when done thoughtfully it can save kidneys without losing the patient to infection.
