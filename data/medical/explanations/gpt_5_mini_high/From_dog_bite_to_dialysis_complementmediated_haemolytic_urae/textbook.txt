Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## Initial presentation after dog bite: sepsis, DIC, and early management

Four days after a dog bite to her upper lip—when she had received a tetanus injection but did not start the prescribed oral phenoxymethylpenicillin—the patient developed a rapidly progressive systemic illness. Over a short interval she became febrile (>40 °C), anorectic and weak, and developed numbness and discoloration of her feet. At a primary-care emergency visit she was profoundly hypoglycaemic (nadir 0.6 mmol/L) that did not correct with oral glucose or glucagon, prompting urgent transfer. This rapid timeline from exposure to fulminant illness is typical of some invasive zoonotic infections and raised early concern for severe bacterial sepsis.

On arrival the examination showed a patient who was alert but clinically septic with mottling and cool extremities. Initial blood testing demonstrated metabolic acidaemia with lactataemia (pH 7.27, lactate 5.7 mmol/L), marked systemic inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury and abnormal liver enzymes. Urinalysis contained protein and blood. Although her qSOFA score was 1/3 (qSOFA has limited sensitivity and should not replace clinical judgment), the total clinical picture—metabolic derangement, rising lactate and organ dysfunction—was consistent with severe sepsis. The combination of thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, bleeding and acral ischaemic changes led the team to prioritise disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia as the unifying diagnosis in the initial phase.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus was considered early because it is a well-recognised pathogen transmitted from dog and cat bites. C. canimorsus is a slow-growing, encapsulated gram‑negative rod normally carried in canine and feline oropharynxes; invasive disease ranges from local wound infection to fulminant sepsis, DIC/purpura fulminans and meningitis. Diagnosis by conventional culture can be delayed by several days because of the organism’s fastidious growth requirements, so empiric therapy is required while microbiology evolves; molecular methods can accelerate identification when available. Severe invasive disease has substantial mortality and is more common in patients with asplenia, alcohol use disorder or liver disease, although it can occur in previously healthy people as in this case.

Pathophysiologically, severe sepsis provokes systemic endothelial activation and cytokine-driven upregulation of tissue factor, triggering widespread thrombin generation and fibrin deposition. When clot formation in the microvasculature consumes platelets and coagulation factors faster than they can be replaced, the clinical syndrome of DIC develops—paradoxically causing both thrombosis (microvascular ischaemia, acral cyanosis) and bleeding (mucocutaneous oozing, ecchymoses). In parallel, profound sepsis can impair hepatic gluconeogenesis and increase peripheral glucose uptake, explaining refractory hypoglycaemia. Pancytopenia in sepsis may reflect marrow suppression, peripheral consumption or immune-mediated processes, so haematology input is appropriate when cytopenias are severe.

Early diagnostic and management steps reflected these pathophysiologic principles. Surgical causes of the acral changes (for example, necrotising soft‑tissue infection) were actively excluded by clinical and radiological assessment. Haematology was consulted because the degree of pancytopenia broadened the differential to include acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH); flow cytometry was obtained and a bone‑marrow biopsy planned, but the absence of abnormal blasts or phenotypes on initial testing led to cancellation of the biopsy. Initial management focused on treating the presumed severe bacterial infection and supporting failing organs: prompt empiric broad‑spectrum antibiotics, haemodynamic and metabolic support, correction of coagulopathy, and organ replacement where needed.

Empiric antimicrobial therapy with piperacillin–tazobactam was started to cover the spectrum of likely dog‑bite pathogens (including Capnocytophaga, Pasteurella species and anaerobes). This choice is appropriate when the patient is critically ill and culture results are pending; once blood cultures (which in this case took seven days) identified C. canimorsus and susceptibilities were known, therapy was narrowed to ampicillin and later to oral phenoxymethylpenicillin. Concurrent supportive care in the intensive care unit became necessary when the patient developed anuria despite aggressive fluid resuscitation and loop diuretics and then a septic cardiomyopathy with fluid overload. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was instituted both for renal support and to manage fluid balance. Because the patient had active bleeding and laboratory evidence of consumptive coagulopathy, targeted transfusion of platelets and fibrinogen concentrate was given; stress‑dose corticosteroids and albumin were also administered as part of supportive sepsis management.

Key practical lessons from this phase of the case are: (1) a dog bite can lead to rapidly progressive, life‑threatening sepsis—early empiric antibiotics are essential and prophylactic antibiotics for some bites should not be deferred; (2) DIC should be suspected when bleeding, thrombocytopenia, prolonged clotting times and organ ischaemia coexist in sepsis, and management centres on treating the underlying infection while judiciously replacing blood components when there is active bleeding or very low levels; and (3) because Capnocytophaga is fastidious, clinical suspicion must guide early therapy while microbiology confirmation may lag by several days.

## From resolving sepsis to thrombotic microangiopathy: recognizing and diagnosing CM‑HUS

The diagnostic pivot in this case occurred when the patient—clinically improving from an initial episode of C. canimorsus sepsis and resolving coagulopathy—developed a sudden, isolated fall in haemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L over six days while platelets remained low (30–40 × 10^9/L). Concomitant laboratory changes (rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin) and absence of overt bleeding signalled new intravascular haemolysis rather than continued blood loss. That constellation—new microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) developing as inflammatory markers and routine coagulation parameters improved—should immediately raise suspicion for a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) superimposed on or following sepsis.

Early pitfalls in interpretation are illustrated by the initial IgG‑positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) in this patient. A positive DAT can point to autoimmune haemolytic anaemia and reasonably prompted empiric high‑dose corticosteroids. However, the failure of haemolysis to respond, together with the later negative repeat DAT, argued against warm autoimmune haemolysis. Drug‑related DAT positivity (penicillin is a classic example) and recent transfusions can produce false‑positive DAT results; these possibilities must be considered when the clinical picture does not fit or when therapy does not produce the expected response. In this case the team re‑evaluated the diagnosis rather than persisting with steroid therapy.

The peripheral blood smear provided the decisive laboratory clue: 15–20% schistocytes were present. Schistocytes (fragmented red cells) are the morphologic hallmark of MAHA and reflect mechanical red cell destruction across microvascular platelet/fibrin thrombi. Even far lower proportions of schistocytes (laboratory cut‑offs vary, but >1% is commonly considered significant) should prompt urgent TMA evaluation; the very high percentage here made that pathway the leading diagnostic concern.

Distinguishing a TMA from disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is critical yet sometimes difficult because both can coexist in severe infection. Practically, timing and the pattern of laboratory abnormalities help. DIC is characterised by consumptive coagulopathy—prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen and markedly elevated D‑dimer—and typically improves as the underlying sepsis resolves. In contrast, TMA manifests with MAHA and thrombocytopenia while routine coagulation tests (PT/aPTT, fibrinogen) are often near normal. In this patient the coagulation profile had normalised while MAHA and thrombocytopenia persisted, favouring a TMA process rather than ongoing DIC.

Once TMA is suspected in an adult, rapid triage focuses on excluding conditions with specific, time‑sensitive therapies—most importantly thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). Clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score can estimate pretest probability while ADAMTS13 testing is pending; this patient had a low PLASMIC score (4/7) and an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5%, effectively excluding severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (TTP is generally defined by activity <10%). Because ADAMTS13 results may take time, many centres begin plasma exchange (PEX) empirically if TTP cannot be confidently excluded; PEX was used here as a bridging manoeuvre. Other causes of HUS must also be excluded—stool PCR for Shiga toxin was negative in this case, arguing against STEC‑HUS.

Complement testing requires careful interpretation in the diagnostic sequence. Normal C3 and C4 values do not exclude complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS); many patients have normal routine complement levels during active disease. Moreover, therapeutic interventions confound assays: PEX removes complement proteins and autoantibodies, and eculizumab blocks terminal complement activity and will alter functional assays. In this patient, standard C3/C4 were normal early on, and later functional testing (AH50) was markedly reduced after the patient had already received PEX and the first dose of eculizumab—changes most consistent with treatment effects rather than proving baseline complement deficiency. The practical lesson is to obtain baseline ADAMTS13 and complement samples before PEX or complement blockade whenever feasible, but not to delay life‑saving therapy to do so.

Renal biopsy, performed once the platelet count had risen to a safer level (77 × 10^9/L), provided histological confirmation of a TMA: ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, severe acute tubular necrosis and scattered crescent formation. Immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition and electron microscopy revealed no primary glomerulopathy, findings that support a non‑immune‑complex, microangiopathic process—consistent with CM‑HUS rather than an immune‑complex glomerulonephritis.

Putting the pieces together: this patient had biopsy‑proven renal TMA, persistent MAHA with schistocytosis, exclusion of TTP (ADAMTS13 not severely reduced) and STEC‑HUS (negative stool PCR), and a temporal pattern in which haemolysis emerged after the infectious and coagulopathic phase began to resolve. The subsequent marked clinical response to complement blockade (eculizumab) further supported a diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS precipitated by C. canimorsus infection. The absence of pathogenic complement gene variants on extended testing in this patient argues for an infection‑triggered complement dysregulation rather than a clear hereditary complement defect, but such testing does not change the need for the acute diagnostic and therapeutic steps outlined above.

Key practical points for clinicians from this case: re‑assess new anaemia in recovering sepsis patients for haemolysis; examine a peripheral smear early; order ADAMTS13 and stool Shiga testing urgently while recognising that plasma exchange may be appropriate as a bridge; interpret complement assays in light of timing and prior therapies; and obtain renal biopsy when safe to confirm TMA and guide management.

## Pathophysiology of complement-mediated HUS and infectious triggers

The alternative complement pathway provides continuous, low-grade surveillance of the bloodstream through a spontaneous “tick‑over” of C3. Spontaneously hydrolysed C3 (C3(H2O)) or deposited C3b binds factor B, which is cleaved by factor D to form the fluid‑phase C3 convertase; when C3b is fixed to a surface it forms the surface C3 convertase (C3bBb). That convertase is stabilized by properdin and amplifies C3 cleavage, creating a powerful positive‑feedback loop. Continued assembly of C3b-containing complexes yields a C5 convertase, cleavage of C5, release of the inflammatory anaphylatoxin C5a and initiation of the terminal pathway with assembly of C5b–9 (the membrane attack complex, MAC).

Because the amplification loop can rapidly escalate complement deposition, host tissues rely on membrane‑bound and plasma regulators to prevent autologous damage. Factor H (FH) is the principal fluid‑phase regulator: it recognizes polyanionic host markers (for example, glycosaminoglycans and sialic acid), accelerates decay of C3 convertases and serves as a cofactor for factor I (FI)–mediated proteolytic inactivation of C3b. Membrane cofactor protein (CD46/MCP) is a surface cofactor for FI on host cells; FI itself is the serine protease that irreversibly cleaves C3b. Factor B is essential to build the convertase and, together with the other proteins, determines the balance between effective pathogen opsonisation and protection of self surfaces.

When this regulatory balance fails on the microvascular endothelium, the consequences are prothrombotic rather than simply lytic. Sublytic deposition of C5b–9 and local generation of C5a produce endothelial activation: upregulation of adhesion molecules and tissue factor, Weibel–Palade body release of von Willebrand factor multimers, and a locally procoagulant surface that promotes platelet adhesion and aggregation. The resulting platelet‑ and fibrin‑rich microthrombi in arterioles and capillaries shear red cells (producing schistocytes and mechanical haemolysis) and consume platelets (thrombocytopenia), the defining features of a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). The renal microcirculation is particularly vulnerable because glomerular endothelium has high complement exposure and limited reserve of membrane regulators, which explains why haemolytic uraemic syndrome phenotypes are kidney‑predominant.

Genetic and autoimmune abnormalities that impair complement regulation dramatically lower the threshold for this cascade. In adults with complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS), pathogenic variants or disease‑relevant autoantibodies are identified in roughly half to two‑thirds of cases. Typical culprits include variants in CFH, CFI, CFB, C3 and CD46, and anti‑FH autoantibodies (more common in children). Importantly, penetrance is incomplete: many carriers remain well until a precipitating event—a “second hit”—provokes uncontrolled complement activation on the endothelium. Thus, identifying a pathogenic variant increases the risk of relapse and often influences duration of complement blockade, but a negative genetic panel does not rule out a complement‑driven TMA.

Infections are among the common second hits. Respiratory or enteric infections, pregnancy, autoimmune flares, drugs and surgery can all provoke complement activation or direct endothelial injury that unmasks underlying regulatory defects. It is important to distinguish this pathway from Shiga‑toxin HUS (STEC‑HUS), which is directly toxin mediated; CM‑HUS is driven by dysregulated complement even when the trigger is infectious. Rare organisms can be sufficient triggers; the present report documents Capnocytophaga canimorsus, an encapsulated dog‑mouth commensal, as a precipitator of CM‑HUS.

Capnocytophaga’s interaction with complement helps explain the clinical paradox seen in these cases. Complement is a key host defence against C. canimorsus, but capsule expression varies between strains and confers differing degrees of serum resistance. Some isolates are complement‑sensitive and cleared easily; others resist opsonisation and killing. During infection, robust complement activation aimed at clearing the pathogen can spill over onto host endothelia, and in a patient with imperfect regulation this may trigger the endothelial‑complement cycle that produces TMA. A few prior case reports—most notably one in which a factor B variant was identified—demonstrate this pathogen‑triggered pattern, but C. canimorsus can act as a trigger even when no pathogenic complement variant is found.

Interpreting complement laboratory tests in a clinical case requires attention to timing and recent therapy. Serum C3 and C4 may be normal in CM‑HUS because activation is focal (microvascular) rather than global, or because levels recover quickly; therefore normal C3/C4 do not exclude the diagnosis. Functional haemolytic assays (CH50/AH50) measure terminal pathway activity and will be depressed by terminal pathway blockade (eculizumab) or by removal of complement proteins during plasma exchange (PEX). In this patient the markedly reduced AH50 was measured after multiple PEX sessions and after the first dose of eculizumab, so it most likely represents treatment effect rather than intrinsic alternative‑pathway failure. The absence of anti‑factor H antibodies and the lack of identifiable pathogenic variants on extended testing support an infection‑triggered CM‑HUS phenotype in this case, although the limits of genetic testing and incomplete penetrance mean a predisposition cannot be excluded with absolute certainty.

Therapeutically, these pathophysiological principles explain the observed clinical course: PEX can transiently improve parameters by removing inflammatory mediators, autoantibodies and replacing deficient regulators, but it does not abrogate ongoing complement activation in the presence of a dysregulated amplification loop. Eculizumab, by blocking C5 cleavage and preventing formation of C5a and C5b–9, directly interrupts the endothelial injury–thrombosis cycle and is therefore the most effective treatment to achieve sustained haematological remission and allow renal recovery. Because terminal complement is also crucial for defence against encapsulated organisms, vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis are mandatory when initiating eculizumab—an important practical consideration when an encapsulated organism such as C. canimorsus is the trigger.

In summary, CM‑HUS arises when the alternative pathway amplification loop operates unchecked on the microvascular endothelium, producing a procoagulant endothelial phenotype and TMA. Genetic defects or autoantibodies lower the threshold for disease, but infectious triggers—including rare ones like C. canimorsus—can precipitate fulminant complement‑mediated endothelial injury even in the absence of an identifiable mutation. Timing of laboratory tests relative to PEX and complement blockade must be considered when interpreting complement assays, and treatment decisions balance the urgency of stopping complement‑driven microthrombosis against the infectious risks of terminal complement inhibition.

## Therapeutic strategy in CM‑HUS with recent bacteraemia: timing, bridging, and infection risk mitigation

Managing complement‑mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS) that emerges shortly after bacteraemia requires a careful, stepwise approach that balances prompt control of infection with timely interruption of the complement‑driven microangiopathic process. The case presented illustrates the key practical decisions: aggressive, pathogen‑directed antimicrobial therapy; use of plasma exchange (PEX) when thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) cannot be excluded; rapid transition to complement blockade once infection is controlled and TTP is excluded; vaccination and short‑term antibiotic prophylaxis to mitigate infectious risk from complement inhibition; and organ‑supportive measures while the TMA and sepsis resolve.

Antimicrobial management comes first. In a patient with a dog bite followed by fulminant sepsis, broad empiric coverage that includes Capnocytophaga species is appropriate (in this case piperacillin/tazobactam was started). Once blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus and the isolate was clinically susceptible, therapy was narrowed to penicillin/ampicillin and ultimately to oral phenoxymethylpenicillin as the clinical picture allowed. In practice, antibiotic choice and duration should be guided by organism identification, susceptibility, source control and clearance of bacteraemia; controlling the infection reduces the risk of ongoing complement activation and is a prerequisite for safe initiation of complement blockade whenever possible.

Plasma exchange has an important triage role early in the course of any new TMA when TTP cannot be reliably excluded. PEX removes anti‑ADAMTS13 autoantibodies and replaces ADAMTS13 in true TTP, so it is started empirically while ADAMTS13 testing is pending. In CM‑HUS PEX may produce only transient improvement by removing circulating complement fragments, cytokines or autoantibodies, but it is a reasonable bridge when diagnostic uncertainty is high. Practical points illustrated by this case: begin PEX promptly if clinical suspicion of TTP exists, document the timing of samples for ADAMTS13 and complement testing (ideally before PEX), and stop or de‑escalate PEX once TTP is excluded and definitive complement blockade is planned. Also note that PEX will remove therapeutic monoclonal antibodies (including eculizumab) and can confound complement assays, so coordinate the sequence of PEX and eculizumab dosing.

Eculizumab is the specific therapy for CM‑HUS and early initiation is associated with better haematological control and improved renal recovery. Indications in practice are persistent MAHA, thrombocytopenia and progressive AKI attributable to complement‑mediated TMA after reasonable exclusion of TTP and STEC‑HUS. The induction and maintenance regimen used in this patient—900 mg IV weekly for four weeks followed by 1,200 mg IV every two weeks—reflects commonly used adult dosing. Because terminal complement blockade removes a key host defence against encapsulated organisms, the decision to start eculizumab must weigh the severity and trajectory of TMA against the status of the infection and the ability to mitigate infectious risk.

Vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis are essential infection‑risk mitigation strategies when administering eculizumab. Ideally, meningococcal conjugate (MenACWY) and MenB vaccines should be given at least two weeks before starting complement blockade to allow protective antibody responses to develop. If urgent therapy is needed and vaccinations cannot be given sufficiently in advance, give the vaccines as soon as possible and provide immediate antibiotic prophylaxis until two weeks after vaccination. In this case the patient received Menveo (ACWY) and Bexsero (MenB) immediately before eculizumab and was given oral phenoxymethylpenicillin as short‑term prophylaxis because vaccine immunity had not yet matured. Patients and caregivers must be counselled about the need for prompt assessment of any febrile illness during complement blockade and informed about the specific vaccines received and ongoing prophylactic antibiotics.

Timing eculizumab relative to recent bacteraemia requires individualised judgment. When feasible, clinicians should await clearance of the offending organism and negative blood cultures before initiating eculizumab, as occurred here. If the TMA is rapidly progressive and life‑ or kidney‑threatening, however, urgent complement inhibition may be necessary despite ongoing infection; such cases demand aggressive antimicrobial therapy, infectious diseases input, and enhanced surveillance for invasive infections. Coordination between nephrology, haematology and infectious diseases is critical to reach the best risk–benefit decision for the individual patient.

Supportive care remains the backbone while specific therapies are arranged. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is often required for anuric AKI, volume overload or severe metabolic derangements; red blood cell transfusions are used to treat symptomatic anaemia; and fibrinogen/platelet replacement is applied when there is clinically significant bleeding or DIC. Importantly, platelet transfusions should generally be avoided in isolated TMA unless there is active bleeding or an invasive procedure, because they can exacerbate microvascular thrombosis. In the presented patient, initial transfusions were given for DIC and bleeding risk, and a trial of corticosteroids (for a transiently positive direct antiglobulin test) was discontinued once immune haemolysis was ruled out and schistocytes pointed to TMA. Kidney biopsy—performed when platelets improved—provided tissue confirmation of TMA and helped guide definitive therapy.

Monitoring response and safety requires frequent laboratory and clinical reassessment. Hematologic response is tracked with platelet counts, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), haptoglobin and peripheral smear for schistocytes; renal response is monitored by urine output and serum creatinine; and infectious surveillance must be heightened during complement blockade. When ordering complement and ADAMTS13 studies, record the timing of specimen collection relative to PEX and eculizumab because both interventions alter assay results (the extended complement panel in this case was obtained after both PEX and the first eculizumab dose and showed changes most consistent with treatment effect). Vigilance for meningococcal and other encapsulated bacterial infections is mandatory during treatment and for some months after cessation.

Finally, the duration of eculizumab and decisions about discontinuation are individualised. Factors informing the plan include clinical severity, degree of renal recovery, and the presence of pathogenic complement gene variants or anti‑factor H autoantibodies. In patients with demonstrable complement mutations or recurrent disease, long‑term or indefinite therapy is often considered; in others, a time‑limited course with structured reassessment is reasonable. In this patient no pathogenic variants were identified and the team elected to continue maintenance therapy for several months with planned reassessment tied to renal recovery and relapse risk.

In summary, the therapeutic strategy in CM‑HUS emerging after bacteraemia rests on rapid infection control, early empiric PEX when TTP is a realistic possibility, prompt transition to eculizumab once infection is controlled and TTP excluded, and active measures to prevent and detect invasive infections during complement blockade. Successful management requires close multidisciplinary coordination, attention to the timing of diagnostic sampling in relation to PEX/eculizumab, and careful patient counselling about infection risks and follow‑up.

## Outcomes, prognosis, and clinical lessons

This patient’s course illustrates both the potential for recovery in complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) and the factors that determine its extent. Clinically, spontaneous diuresis returned after the final plasma‑exchange (PEX) sessions, but sustained haematological remission and continued renal improvement were achieved only after initiation of complement blockade with eculizumab. She became dialysis independent and, at latest follow‑up, had partial renal recovery with a serum creatinine around 175 µmol/L. Extended genetic testing did not identify pathogenic complement variants; nevertheless, because of the severity of her presentation she was maintained on eculizumab (induction followed by 1,200 mg IV every 2 weeks) with planned outpatient reassessment and individualized consideration of discontinuation if renal function remains stable.

Prognosis in CM‑HUS is determined principally by a few interrelated features that are apparent in this case. Time from clinical recognition to initiation of effective complement blockade is a major determinant of renal outcome: earlier treatment is associated with better recovery, whereas delay increases the risk of irreversible kidney injury. Baseline renal injury—especially the need for dialysis at presentation—predicts a poorer renal prognosis; this patient’s dialysis dependence and severe acute tubular necrosis with ischaemic glomeruli and fibrin thrombi on biopsy indicated a high risk for incomplete recovery, which is why sustained partial recovery is a favorable result. Histologic severity on kidney biopsy (extensive thrombi, crescents, cortical necrosis) correlates with lasting loss of nephron mass. Finally, identification of pathogenic complement‑regulatory gene variants or anti‑factor H antibodies generally implies higher relapse risk and often supports longer or indefinite complement inhibition; the absence of such variants, as in this patient, lowers but does not eliminate the chance of recurrence and therefore does not obviate careful follow‑up.

Several practical clinical lessons emerge from the diagnostic and therapeutic decisions made here. First, maintain a high index of suspicion for CM‑HUS when microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia with schistocytes and persistent thrombocytopenia appear as the inflammatory and DIC features of sepsis begin to improve. Second, routine complement tests (C3/C4) may be normal in CM‑HUS and therefore cannot be used to exclude the diagnosis; functional testing and genetics are helpful but can be confounded by prior PEX or complement blockade. Third, a positive direct antiglobulin test can be false‑positive after penicillin exposure or recent transfusions; in this case an initial IgG‑positive DAT reverted to negative and the peripheral smear with schistocytes clarified the diagnosis. Fourth, because TTP is immediately life‑threatening and cannot be safely excluded clinically in many patients, empiric PEX should be started while urgent ADAMTS13 testing is obtained; PEX may transiently improve parameters in CM‑HUS but does not replace targeted complement blockade. Finally, when CM‑HUS is likely, prompt initiation of eculizumab is indicated—but timing must be balanced against infectious risks.

Managing infection risk when using terminal complement blockade requires specific precautions, as exemplified here. Terminal complement components are critical for defence against encapsulated organisms; therefore, vaccination against meningococcal disease (both conjugate A/C/Y/W and serogroup B where available) should be given before elective initiation if time allows. If eculizumab must be started urgently, give vaccines as soon as feasible and provide short‑term antibiotic prophylaxis until adequate immune response is expected—this patient received first doses of meningococcal vaccines and prophylactic oral antibiotics because vaccination had not preceded therapy by two weeks. When possible, defer complement blockade until active bacteraemia is cleared; in this case eculizumab was started after blood cultures became negative. Patients on complement inhibitors should be counselled to seek immediate medical attention for fever and signs of systemic infection.

There are zoonosis‑specific implications to remember. Capnocytophaga canimorsus, though rare, can produce fulminant sepsis and trigger secondary CM‑HUS even in previously healthy adults. A history of recent dog bite is a crucial diagnostic clue; early, appropriate empiric antimicrobial therapy that covers Capnocytophaga is important after significant dog or cat bites and in suspected invasive infections, and delayed or absent prophylaxis may contribute to severe disease as seen here.

Finally, systems and diagnostic considerations can materially affect outcomes. Capnocytophaga is a fastidious organism and blood cultures may be slow to grow; where available, molecular diagnostics can accelerate identification. Establish and follow a TMA pathway that expedites a focused laboratory bundle (CBC with smear, haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, coagulation studies, creatinine/urinalysis, ADAMTS13, STEC testing, and baseline complement studies) and clarifies who needs immediate PEX versus who can await results. Draw ADAMTS13 and baseline complement studies before PEX or eculizumab when feasible, because both therapies alter test results; perform kidney biopsy when it can be done safely (for example, after platelet recovery) to confirm TMA and assess histologic severity, which helps prognosticate and plan follow‑up. Multidisciplinary coordination among intensive care, infectious disease, haematology and nephrology teams—demonstrated in this case—facilitates timely diagnosis and the complex risk–benefit decisions required in infection‑associated CM‑HUS.

In sum, this case reinforces three practical rules: watch for evolving MAHA as sepsis and DIC improve, exclude TTP urgently and use PEX while doing so, and when CM‑HUS becomes likely pursue complement blockade promptly but with appropriate infection‑risk mitigation and specialist follow‑up.
