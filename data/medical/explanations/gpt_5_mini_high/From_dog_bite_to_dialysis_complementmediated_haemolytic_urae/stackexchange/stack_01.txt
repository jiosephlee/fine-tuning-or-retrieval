Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] Which empiric intravenous antibiotic regimens are preferred for severe sepsis 4 days after a dog bite to reliably cover Capnocytophaga canimorsus, and why might oral phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin V) be inadequate in this setting?

Bottom line: In a patient with severe sepsis after a dog bite you should give IV, broad‑spectrum antibiotics that reliably cover Capnocytophaga, Pasteurella and oral anaerobes — not oral penicillin V. Preferred empiric choices are IV piperacillin–tazobactam or IV ampicillin–sulbactam; for critically ill patients or concern for beta‑lactamase–producing organisms use a carbapenem (eg, meropenem). A third‑generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone or cefotaxime) with metronidazole is an acceptable alternative if anaerobic coverage is added.

Practical examples (adult dosing, adjust for renal function):
- Piperacillin–tazobactam 4.5 g IV every 6–8 h (extended infusion when possible) — empiric workhorse for bite‑associated sepsis.  
- Ampicillin–sulbactam 3 g IV every 6 h — alternative when appropriate.  
- Ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily (or cefotaxime 2 g IV q8 h) plus metronidazole 500 mg IV q8 h if anaerobic coverage is needed.  
- Meropenem 1 g IV q8 h for severe illness or when resistant/beta‑lactamase producers are suspected.

If true severe penicillin anaphylaxis: combine agents that cover Gram‑negatives and anaerobes (eg, aztreonam plus doxycycline or doxycycline plus metronidazole), or tailor to local susceptibilities and allergy severity.

Why these regimens: Capnocytophaga canimorsus is usually susceptible to beta‑lactams but beta‑lactamase–producing strains and encapsulated variants occur; bite wounds are often polymicrobial (Pasteurella, streptococci, staphylococci, anaerobes). Empiric IV beta‑lactam/beta‑lactamase inhibitor combinations or broad cephalosporins/carbapenems give predictable, bactericidal serum concentrations rapidly — critical in sepsis and while awaiting slow‑growing blood culture results (as in this case, where piperacillin–tazobactam was appropriately started).

Why oral phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin V) is inadequate here:
- Route and pharmacokinetics: severe sepsis commonly causes impaired gut perfusion and unpredictable oral absorption; bacteremia and septic shock require reliably high IV bactericidal drug levels.  
- Spectrum and resistance: penicillin V lacks a beta‑lactamase inhibitor and provides limited anaerobic coverage; some Capnocytophaga and other bite pathogens may produce beta‑lactamases.  
- Clinical safety: switching to oral therapy should only occur after clear clinical improvement, source control and culture‑confirmed susceptibility. In the reported case the team started piperacillin–tazobactam empirically (appropriate), and although the isolate later guided de‑escalation, initial IV broad coverage was the correct strategy.

Bottom line for practice: in any patient with systemic illness after a dog bite treat empirically with IV agents that cover Capnocytophaga plus anaerobes (piperacillin–tazobactam or ampicillin–sulbactam; escalate to a carbapenem if severe or resistant organisms suspected). Reserve oral step‑down only after clinical stability and documented susceptibility; do not rely on penicillin V for severe or systemic infections.
