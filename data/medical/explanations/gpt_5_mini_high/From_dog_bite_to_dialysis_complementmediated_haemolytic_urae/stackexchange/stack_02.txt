Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a septic adult with thrombocytopenia, anemia, acute kidney injury, and coagulopathy consistent with DIC, what clinical and laboratory features should prompt suspicion for a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) such as complement-mediated HUS rather than DIC alone?

Short answer
Suspect a superimposed TMA (for example complement‑mediated HUS) in a septic patient with DIC when the picture evolves from a consumptive coagulopathy into a persistent or new microangiopathic haemolytic process — classically: a sudden unexplained fall in haemoglobin with biochemical evidence of intravascular haemolysis, prominent schistocytosis, thrombocytopenia that persists despite resolution of coagulation abnormalities, and renal injury that is out of proportion to, or worsens after, the acute septic/DIC phase.

Why this distinction matters (pathophysiologic rationale)
DIC is driven by systemic activation of the coagulation cascade → fibrin‑rich microthrombi, consumption of clotting factors (prolonged PT/PTT, low fibrinogen) and bleeding. TMA is driven by endothelial injury with platelet‑rich microthrombi → microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia and end‑organ ischemia (kidney is classically affected). Because the treatment and prognosis diverge (supportive care/target infection for DIC versus plasma exchange and possibly complement blockade for CM‑HUS), recognizing when both are present is critical.

Clinical and laboratory features that should prompt suspicion — with illustrations from the case
- New, abrupt, isolated drop in haemoglobin without overt bleeding: in the case Hb fell from 111 → 68 g/L over 6 days.  
- Biochemical evidence of intravascular haemolysis: low/undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, indirect hyperbilirubinaemia and reticulocytosis when bone marrow responsive. In this patient haptoglobin became undetectable and LDH rose as haemolysis emerged.  
- Schistocytes on peripheral smear: their presence (even modest percentages) is a key morphologic clue; this patient had 15–20% schistocytes — strongly suggestive of MAHA/TMA.  
- Persistent or worsening thrombocytopenia despite correction/normalization of coagulation parameters: here platelets remained 30–40×10^9/L while coagulation studies had normalized, arguing against ongoing pure DIC.  
- Renal dysfunction disproportionate to other organ dysfunction or worsening after sepsis control: the patient became anuric and dialysis‑dependent; renal biopsy later showed ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and crescents consistent with TMA.  
- Lack of response to therapies for other causes: failure of presumed autoimmune haemolysis to respond to corticosteroids and a negative repeat DAT (initial DAT likely false positive due to penicillin/transfusion) pointed away from immune haemolysis.  
- Exclusion of other TMAs: ADAMTS13 activity not severely reduced (43.5% here, arguing against TTP), and negative Shiga toxin PCR reduced likelihood of STEC‑HUS.  
- Timing: haemolysis and MAHA emerging or worsening as sepsis/DIC is improving suggests a secondary, infection‑triggered TMA rather than only DIC.

Practical diagnostic approach when you suspect superimposed TMA
- Urgent labs: peripheral smear for schistocytes, haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, reticulocyte count, DAT, CBC, PT/PTT/fibrinogen, D‑dimer, creatinine/urine studies.  
- Send ADAMTS13 and Shiga toxin testing early.  
- Draw complement studies and arrange extended complement/genetic testing, but remember normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS and assays can be confounded by transfusion, plasma exchange or eculizumab. (In the case basic C3/C4 were normal; later AH50 was low after PEX/eculizumab.)  
- Consider kidney biopsy if safe — it can confirm TMA (fibrin thrombi, arteriolar/glomerular endothelial injury).  
- While awaiting results, if TTP cannot be excluded clinically, initiate plasma exchange; if CM‑HUS is suspected and infection controlled, consider prompt eculizumab with meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis.

Clinical takeaway
In a septic patient with initial DIC, new or persistent MAHA (falling Hb, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, schistocytes), ongoing thrombocytopenia despite normalization of coagulation studies, and disproportionate or progressive AKI should raise a high index of suspicion for a superimposed TMA — and trigger ADAMTS13 and Shiga testing, targeted complement evaluation, consideration of biopsy, temporary plasma exchange if TTP is not excluded, and early discussion about complement blockade once infection control and vaccination/prophylaxis considerations are addressed. The Capnocytophaga case neatly illustrates this sequence: initial DIC then delayed MAHA/TMA with biopsy confirmation and clinical response after complement‑targeted therapy.
