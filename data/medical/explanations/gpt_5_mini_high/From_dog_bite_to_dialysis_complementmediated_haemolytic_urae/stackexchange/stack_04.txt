Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] In suspected renal TMA with persistent AKI, when is a kidney biopsy indicated, and which histopathologic findings support a diagnosis of TMA without immune-complex glomerulonephritis?

Short answer
- Perform a kidney biopsy when persistent or dialysis‑dependent AKI remains after initial stabilization and when the histologic result would change management—most importantly to confirm TMA, exclude alternative glomerular diseases (especially immune‑complex GN or vasculitis), and assess chronicity/prognosis.  
- On biopsy, the combination of light‑microscopic features of microvascular thrombosis (fibrin/platelet thrombi in glomerular capillaries and small arterioles, endothelial swelling/subendothelial widening, mesangiolysis, ischemic collapse of glomerular tufts, and often concomitant acute tubular necrosis or crescents) together with negative immunofluorescence for immune‑complexes and absence of electron‑dense deposits on electron microscopy supports TMA without immune‑complex glomerulonephritis.

Clinical reasoning and practical points (teaching pearl)
- Why biopsy? In adults with MAHA, thrombocytopenia and AKI the initial priority is rapid laboratory triage (peripheral smear, ADAMTS13, Shiga‑toxin testing, coagulation panel, basic complement tests) and empiric life‑saving measures (PEX for suspected TTP, supportive care). A renal biopsy is most useful later when the renal injury is prolonged or dialysis‑dependent, when noninvasive tests are inconclusive, or when distinguishing TMA from immune‑complex GN or rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis would alter therapy (for example, complement blockade versus immunosuppression). Biopsy also documents acuity versus chronicity and thus helps prognosis.
- Timing and safety: In patients with recent sepsis/DIC the bleeding risk often mandates delay until the patient is more stable and platelet/coagulation parameters are acceptable (many centres aim for platelet count >50 × 10^9/L and corrected INR/PT). If a high bleeding risk persists, a transjugular renal biopsy can be considered. In the presented case the team waited until platelets rose to 77 × 10^9/L and then obtained a diagnostic biopsy.
- What to look for histologically: Acute TMA — glomerular and arteriolar fibrin thrombi, endothelial swelling with subendothelial “fluffy” material or mesangiolysis, ischemic collapse of glomerular tufts, and frequently acute tubular necrosis; crescents may be seen in severe injury. Chronic TMA changes include GBM double contours and arteriolar intimal thickening. Crucially, absence of granular immunoglobulin/C3 staining on immunofluorescence and absence of electron‑dense deposits on EM argue against immune‑complex GN. In this case the biopsy demonstrated severe ATN, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and scattered crescents, and IF/EM showed no immune‑complex deposition—findings that confirmed TMA and helped support the diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS.
- Caveats: A renal biopsy does not replace targeted laboratory testing (ADAMTS13, Shiga‑toxin, complement genetics/functional assays) and does not itself identify genetic complement defects. Also, normal serum complement levels do not exclude complement‑mediated TMA—histology can be decisive when blood tests are unrevealing or confounded by prior plasma exchange or therapy.
