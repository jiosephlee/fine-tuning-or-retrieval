Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how Capnocytophaga canimorsus can trigger complement activation and, in susceptible hosts, lead to uncontrolled alternative pathway activity, endothelial injury, and thrombotic microangiopathy.

Bottom line: Capnocytophaga canimorsus — like other gram‑negative, encapsulated pathogens — provokes innate complement activation; in a host whose alternative‑pathway (AP) regulation is inadequate (genetic defect, autoantibody, or overwhelming stimulus) this initial activation is amplified by the AP “tick‑over,” producing C5 cleavage, sublytic membrane attack complexes and C5a‑driven inflammation that injure glomerular endothelium and trigger platelet‑rich microthrombi → thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

How this happens (stepwise, clinically useful):
- Triggering the cascade: bacterial surface structures (lipooligosaccharide/outer membrane components and exposed microbial surfaces) activate classical/lectin pathways and provide a platform for AP amplification. C. canimorsus is often capsulated; some strains are relatively complement‑resistant so complement activation persists longer and is more likely to become dysregulated.
- Amplification by the alternative pathway: the AP operates by spontaneous C3 hydrolysis and formation of C3bBb convertases on surfaces; properdin stabilizes these convertases. Normally regulators (factor H, factor I, membrane cofactor protein/CD46, etc.) limit this. A predisposing defect (or antibody), or a very strong infectious stimulus, tips the balance toward runaway AP activity with large amounts of surface C3b and generation of C5 convertase.
- Endothelial injury and thrombosis: C5a is a potent chemoattractant and neutrophil activator; sublytic C5b‑9 (MAC) and C5a‑driven inflammation cause endothelial cell activation (up‑regulation of tissue factor and release of ultra‑large von Willebrand factor), a procoagulant shift and platelet aggregation. Microvascular platelet‑rich thrombi shear red cells (MAHA), consume platelets (thrombocytopenia) and produce ischemic kidney injury — the clinicopathologic picture of complement‑mediated HUS.
- Why the diagnosis can be obscured: sepsis/DIC can dominate early, and standard complement tests (C3/C4) may be normal because activation may be focal or because testing is done at the wrong time or after plasma exchange/therapy. ADAMTS13 activity >10% argues against TTP; renal biopsy showing fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomeruli supports TMA.

Tie to the case:
- The patient developed fulminant C. canimorsus sepsis with DIC and AKI, then—after initial improvement—sudden isolated haemolysis with schistocytes, persistent thrombocytopenia and renal biopsy evidence of fibrin thrombi. ADAMTS13 (43.5%) excluded TTP; C3/C4 were normal; extended AP activity (AH50) was low only after plasma exchange and eculizumab (consistent with treatment effect). Rapid haematologic and partial renal recovery only after complement blockade confirms a pathogenic role for uncontrolled complement activity in her TMA.

Practical implications for clinicians:
- Suspect infection‑triggered, complement‑mediated TMA when MAHA and thrombocytopenia emerge or persist despite control of sepsis, with ADAMTS13 not severely reduced and biopsy/clinical features of TMA.
- Use plasma exchange as a bridge while excluding TTP and securing cultures; start eculizumab promptly once active bacteraemia is controlled (vaccinate and give prophylactic antibiotics) because C5 blockade halts the final common pathway of endothelial injury and often produces rapid hematologic remission.
