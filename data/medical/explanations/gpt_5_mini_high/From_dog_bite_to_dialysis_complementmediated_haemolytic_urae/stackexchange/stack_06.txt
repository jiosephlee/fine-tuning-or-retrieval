Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Pathophysiology] Why do normal C3 and C4 levels not exclude complement-mediated HUS, and how can prior plasma exchange and initiation of eculizumab confound interpretation of downstream complement functional assays such as the AH50?

Short answer
Normal serum C3 and C4 do not rule out complement‑mediated HUS because the pathogenic process is usually local dysregulation of the alternative pathway at the endothelial surface rather than wholesale depletion of circulating complement proteins. And functional haemolytic assays such as the AH50 are easily distorted by therapy: plasma exchange (PEX) changes circulating complement proteins and removes autoantibodies, while eculizumab (anti‑C5) pharmacologically abolishes terminal complement activity and will therefore make AH50 (and CH50) artificially low. In the presented case the timeline illustrates both problems: C3/C4 were measured before PEX and were normal, whereas the AH50 (measured 8 days after the last PEX and 4 days after the first eculizumab dose) was markedly reduced (6%) — a pattern most consistent with treatment effects rather than proving or excluding intrinsic alternative‑pathway consumption.

Why C3/C4 can be normal in CM‑HUS
- CM‑HUS is driven by dysregulated activation of the alternative pathway on endothelial surfaces (microvasculature), producing local C3 activation, endothelial injury and thrombosis. This localized activation frequently does not cause large measurable depletion of circulating C3 or C4.
- C3 is also an acute‑phase reactant and may be preserved or even increased during systemic inflammation or sepsis, masking any modest consumption.
- C4 primarily reflects classical/lectin pathway activity; isolated alternative pathway dysregulation often leaves C4 in the reference range. Therefore normal plasma C3 and C4 are neither sensitive nor specific for diagnosing complement‑mediated TMA.

How PEX and eculizumab confound functional assays (AH50)
- AH50 measures the serum’s alternative‑pathway capacity to produce haemolysis and depends on intact upstream (factor B, D, properdin, C3) and terminal components (C5–C9). Any intervention that removes components or blocks the terminal pathway will change the result.
- Plasma exchange removes circulating complement proteins, activation fragments and autoantibodies (for example anti‑factor H), and replacement with donor plasma (Octaplas or FFP) repletes complement proteins — both actions change measured levels and can produce falsely normal or falsely low functional results depending on timing.
- Eculizumab binds C5 and prevents C5 cleavage and MAC formation. Because AH50 (and CH50) require terminal complement activity to produce lysis, eculizumab will markedly depress these assays regardless of whether upstream complement dysregulation is present. Thus a low AH50 after eculizumab reflects pharmacologic blockade, not diagnostic evidence of prior consumption or genetic deficiency.
- The converse is also important: PEX performed after eculizumab can remove drug and transiently restore AH50, complicating interpretation of both drug effect and complement status.

Practical implications (how to apply this at the bedside)
- If CM‑HUS is suspected, obtain and bank pre‑treatment serum/plasma for C3, C4, CH50/AH50, anti‑factor H antibodies and ADAMTS13 before PEX or complement blockade whenever possible. If urgent therapy is required, at least freeze and label pre‑treatment samples for later reference.
- Do not rely on a single normal C3/C4 to exclude complement‑mediated disease; integrate clinical features (MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI), renal biopsy, exclusion of TTP (ADAMTS13), STEC testing and the clinical response to therapy.
- Recognize that AH50 or CH50 measured after eculizumab will be suppressed and are not diagnostic; anti‑factor H antibodies may be falsely negative after PEX. Genetic testing for complement variants is not affected by PEX/eculizumab and should be pursued but will not be immediately available.

Tie back to the case
In this patient the early normal C3/C4 did not argue against CM‑HUS because the syndrome is commonly a surface‑localized alternative‑pathway disorder and because sepsis can preserve or raise serum complement. The later profoundly reduced AH50 (6%) was measured after several PEX sessions and after starting eculizumab, so it is best interpreted as a treatment effect (terminal pathway blockade and prior plasma manipulation) rather than proof of antecedent systemic complement depletion. Clinical judgement, biopsy evidence of TMA, exclusion of alternative causes and the patient’s sustained response to eculizumab provided the diagnostic and therapeutic rationale.
