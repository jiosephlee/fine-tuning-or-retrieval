Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 3: ## Pathophysiology of complement-mediated HUS and infectious triggers

The alternative complement pathway provides continuous, low-grade surveillance of the bloodstream through a spontaneous “tick‑over” of C3. Spontaneously hydrolysed C3 (C3(H2O)) or deposited C3b binds factor B, which is cleaved by factor D to form the fluid‑phase C3 convertase; when C3b is fixed to a surface it forms the surface C3 convertase (C3bBb). That convertase is stabilized by properdin and amplifies C3 cleavage, creating a powerful positive‑feedback loop. Continued assembly of C3b-containing complexes yields a C5 convertase, cleavage of C5, release of the inflammatory anaphylatoxin C5a and initiation of the terminal pathway with assembly of C5b–9 (the membrane attack complex, MAC).

Because the amplification loop can rapidly escalate complement deposition, host tissues rely on membrane‑bound and plasma regulators to prevent autologous damage. Factor H (FH) is the principal fluid‑phase regulator: it recognizes polyanionic host markers (for example, glycosaminoglycans and sialic acid), accelerates decay of C3 convertases and serves as a cofactor for factor I (FI)–mediated proteolytic inactivation of C3b. Membrane cofactor protein (CD46/MCP) is a surface cofactor for FI on host cells; FI itself is the serine protease that irreversibly cleaves C3b. Factor B is essential to build the convertase and, together with the other proteins, determines the balance between effective pathogen opsonisation and protection of self surfaces.

When this regulatory balance fails on the microvascular endothelium, the consequences are prothrombotic rather than simply lytic. Sublytic deposition of C5b–9 and local generation of C5a produce endothelial activation: upregulation of adhesion molecules and tissue factor, Weibel–Palade body release of von Willebrand factor multimers, and a locally procoagulant surface that promotes platelet adhesion and aggregation. The resulting platelet‑ and fibrin‑rich microthrombi in arterioles and capillaries shear red cells (producing schistocytes and mechanical haemolysis) and consume platelets (thrombocytopenia), the defining features of a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). The renal microcirculation is particularly vulnerable because glomerular endothelium has high complement exposure and limited reserve of membrane regulators, which explains why haemolytic uraemic syndrome phenotypes are kidney‑predominant.

Genetic and autoimmune abnormalities that impair complement regulation dramatically lower the threshold for this cascade. In adults with complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS), pathogenic variants or disease‑relevant autoantibodies are identified in roughly half to two‑thirds of cases. Typical culprits include variants in CFH, CFI, CFB, C3 and CD46, and anti‑FH autoantibodies (more common in children). Importantly, penetrance is incomplete: many carriers remain well until a precipitating event—a “second hit”—provokes uncontrolled complement activation on the endothelium. Thus, identifying a pathogenic variant increases the risk of relapse and often influences duration of complement blockade, but a negative genetic panel does not rule out a complement‑driven TMA.

Infections are among the common second hits. Respiratory or enteric infections, pregnancy, autoimmune flares, drugs and surgery can all provoke complement activation or direct endothelial injury that unmasks underlying regulatory defects. It is important to distinguish this pathway from Shiga‑toxin HUS (STEC‑HUS), which is directly toxin mediated; CM‑HUS is driven by dysregulated complement even when the trigger is infectious. Rare organisms can be sufficient triggers; the present report documents Capnocytophaga canimorsus, an encapsulated dog‑mouth commensal, as a precipitator of CM‑HUS.

Capnocytophaga’s interaction with complement helps explain the clinical paradox seen in these cases. Complement is a key host defence against C. canimorsus, but capsule expression varies between strains and confers differing degrees of serum resistance. Some isolates are complement‑sensitive and cleared easily; others resist opsonisation and killing. During infection, robust complement activation aimed at clearing the pathogen can spill over onto host endothelia, and in a patient with imperfect regulation this may trigger the endothelial‑complement cycle that produces TMA. A few prior case reports—most notably one in which a factor B variant was identified—demonstrate this pathogen‑triggered pattern, but C. canimorsus can act as a trigger even when no pathogenic complement variant is found.

Interpreting complement laboratory tests in a clinical case requires attention to timing and recent therapy. Serum C3 and C4 may be normal in CM‑HUS because activation is focal (microvascular) rather than global, or because levels recover quickly; therefore normal C3/C4 do not exclude the diagnosis. Functional haemolytic assays (CH50/AH50) measure terminal pathway activity and will be depressed by terminal pathway blockade (eculizumab) or by removal of complement proteins during plasma exchange (PEX). In this patient the markedly reduced AH50 was measured after multiple PEX sessions and after the first dose of eculizumab, so it most likely represents treatment effect rather than intrinsic alternative‑pathway failure. The absence of anti‑factor H antibodies and the lack of identifiable pathogenic variants on extended testing support an infection‑triggered CM‑HUS phenotype in this case, although the limits of genetic testing and incomplete penetrance mean a predisposition cannot be excluded with absolute certainty.

Therapeutically, these pathophysiological principles explain the observed clinical course: PEX can transiently improve parameters by removing inflammatory mediators, autoantibodies and replacing deficient regulators, but it does not abrogate ongoing complement activation in the presence of a dysregulated amplification loop. Eculizumab, by blocking C5 cleavage and preventing formation of C5a and C5b–9, directly interrupts the endothelial injury–thrombosis cycle and is therefore the most effective treatment to achieve sustained haematological remission and allow renal recovery. Because terminal complement is also crucial for defence against encapsulated organisms, vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis are mandatory when initiating eculizumab—an important practical consideration when an encapsulated organism such as C. canimorsus is the trigger.

In summary, CM‑HUS arises when the alternative pathway amplification loop operates unchecked on the microvascular endothelium, producing a procoagulant endothelial phenotype and TMA. Genetic defects or autoantibodies lower the threshold for disease, but infectious triggers—including rare ones like C. canimorsus—can precipitate fulminant complement‑mediated endothelial injury even in the absence of an identifiable mutation. Timing of laboratory tests relative to PEX and complement blockade must be considered when interpreting complement assays, and treatment decisions balance the urgency of stopping complement‑driven microthrombosis against the infectious risks of terminal complement inhibition.
