Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 4: ## Therapeutic strategy in CM‑HUS with recent bacteraemia: timing, bridging, and infection risk mitigation

Managing complement‑mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS) that emerges shortly after bacteraemia requires a careful, stepwise approach that balances prompt control of infection with timely interruption of the complement‑driven microangiopathic process. The case presented illustrates the key practical decisions: aggressive, pathogen‑directed antimicrobial therapy; use of plasma exchange (PEX) when thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) cannot be excluded; rapid transition to complement blockade once infection is controlled and TTP is excluded; vaccination and short‑term antibiotic prophylaxis to mitigate infectious risk from complement inhibition; and organ‑supportive measures while the TMA and sepsis resolve.

Antimicrobial management comes first. In a patient with a dog bite followed by fulminant sepsis, broad empiric coverage that includes Capnocytophaga species is appropriate (in this case piperacillin/tazobactam was started). Once blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus and the isolate was clinically susceptible, therapy was narrowed to penicillin/ampicillin and ultimately to oral phenoxymethylpenicillin as the clinical picture allowed. In practice, antibiotic choice and duration should be guided by organism identification, susceptibility, source control and clearance of bacteraemia; controlling the infection reduces the risk of ongoing complement activation and is a prerequisite for safe initiation of complement blockade whenever possible.

Plasma exchange has an important triage role early in the course of any new TMA when TTP cannot be reliably excluded. PEX removes anti‑ADAMTS13 autoantibodies and replaces ADAMTS13 in true TTP, so it is started empirically while ADAMTS13 testing is pending. In CM‑HUS PEX may produce only transient improvement by removing circulating complement fragments, cytokines or autoantibodies, but it is a reasonable bridge when diagnostic uncertainty is high. Practical points illustrated by this case: begin PEX promptly if clinical suspicion of TTP exists, document the timing of samples for ADAMTS13 and complement testing (ideally before PEX), and stop or de‑escalate PEX once TTP is excluded and definitive complement blockade is planned. Also note that PEX will remove therapeutic monoclonal antibodies (including eculizumab) and can confound complement assays, so coordinate the sequence of PEX and eculizumab dosing.

Eculizumab is the specific therapy for CM‑HUS and early initiation is associated with better haematological control and improved renal recovery. Indications in practice are persistent MAHA, thrombocytopenia and progressive AKI attributable to complement‑mediated TMA after reasonable exclusion of TTP and STEC‑HUS. The induction and maintenance regimen used in this patient—900 mg IV weekly for four weeks followed by 1,200 mg IV every two weeks—reflects commonly used adult dosing. Because terminal complement blockade removes a key host defence against encapsulated organisms, the decision to start eculizumab must weigh the severity and trajectory of TMA against the status of the infection and the ability to mitigate infectious risk.

Vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis are essential infection‑risk mitigation strategies when administering eculizumab. Ideally, meningococcal conjugate (MenACWY) and MenB vaccines should be given at least two weeks before starting complement blockade to allow protective antibody responses to develop. If urgent therapy is needed and vaccinations cannot be given sufficiently in advance, give the vaccines as soon as possible and provide immediate antibiotic prophylaxis until two weeks after vaccination. In this case the patient received Menveo (ACWY) and Bexsero (MenB) immediately before eculizumab and was given oral phenoxymethylpenicillin as short‑term prophylaxis because vaccine immunity had not yet matured. Patients and caregivers must be counselled about the need for prompt assessment of any febrile illness during complement blockade and informed about the specific vaccines received and ongoing prophylactic antibiotics.

Timing eculizumab relative to recent bacteraemia requires individualised judgment. When feasible, clinicians should await clearance of the offending organism and negative blood cultures before initiating eculizumab, as occurred here. If the TMA is rapidly progressive and life‑ or kidney‑threatening, however, urgent complement inhibition may be necessary despite ongoing infection; such cases demand aggressive antimicrobial therapy, infectious diseases input, and enhanced surveillance for invasive infections. Coordination between nephrology, haematology and infectious diseases is critical to reach the best risk–benefit decision for the individual patient.

Supportive care remains the backbone while specific therapies are arranged. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is often required for anuric AKI, volume overload or severe metabolic derangements; red blood cell transfusions are used to treat symptomatic anaemia; and fibrinogen/platelet replacement is applied when there is clinically significant bleeding or DIC. Importantly, platelet transfusions should generally be avoided in isolated TMA unless there is active bleeding or an invasive procedure, because they can exacerbate microvascular thrombosis. In the presented patient, initial transfusions were given for DIC and bleeding risk, and a trial of corticosteroids (for a transiently positive direct antiglobulin test) was discontinued once immune haemolysis was ruled out and schistocytes pointed to TMA. Kidney biopsy—performed when platelets improved—provided tissue confirmation of TMA and helped guide definitive therapy.

Monitoring response and safety requires frequent laboratory and clinical reassessment. Hematologic response is tracked with platelet counts, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), haptoglobin and peripheral smear for schistocytes; renal response is monitored by urine output and serum creatinine; and infectious surveillance must be heightened during complement blockade. When ordering complement and ADAMTS13 studies, record the timing of specimen collection relative to PEX and eculizumab because both interventions alter assay results (the extended complement panel in this case was obtained after both PEX and the first eculizumab dose and showed changes most consistent with treatment effect). Vigilance for meningococcal and other encapsulated bacterial infections is mandatory during treatment and for some months after cessation.

Finally, the duration of eculizumab and decisions about discontinuation are individualised. Factors informing the plan include clinical severity, degree of renal recovery, and the presence of pathogenic complement gene variants or anti‑factor H autoantibodies. In patients with demonstrable complement mutations or recurrent disease, long‑term or indefinite therapy is often considered; in others, a time‑limited course with structured reassessment is reasonable. In this patient no pathogenic variants were identified and the team elected to continue maintenance therapy for several months with planned reassessment tied to renal recovery and relapse risk.

In summary, the therapeutic strategy in CM‑HUS emerging after bacteraemia rests on rapid infection control, early empiric PEX when TTP is a realistic possibility, prompt transition to eculizumab once infection is controlled and TTP excluded, and active measures to prevent and detect invasive infections during complement blockade. Successful management requires close multidisciplinary coordination, attention to the timing of diagnostic sampling in relation to PEX/eculizumab, and careful patient counselling about infection risks and follow‑up.
