Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 1: ## Initial presentation after dog bite: sepsis, DIC, and early management

Four days after a dog bite to her upper lip—when she had received a tetanus injection but did not start the prescribed oral phenoxymethylpenicillin—the patient developed a rapidly progressive systemic illness. Over a short interval she became febrile (>40 °C), anorectic and weak, and developed numbness and discoloration of her feet. At a primary-care emergency visit she was profoundly hypoglycaemic (nadir 0.6 mmol/L) that did not correct with oral glucose or glucagon, prompting urgent transfer. This rapid timeline from exposure to fulminant illness is typical of some invasive zoonotic infections and raised early concern for severe bacterial sepsis.

On arrival the examination showed a patient who was alert but clinically septic with mottling and cool extremities. Initial blood testing demonstrated metabolic acidaemia with lactataemia (pH 7.27, lactate 5.7 mmol/L), marked systemic inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury and abnormal liver enzymes. Urinalysis contained protein and blood. Although her qSOFA score was 1/3 (qSOFA has limited sensitivity and should not replace clinical judgment), the total clinical picture—metabolic derangement, rising lactate and organ dysfunction—was consistent with severe sepsis. The combination of thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, bleeding and acral ischaemic changes led the team to prioritise disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia as the unifying diagnosis in the initial phase.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus was considered early because it is a well-recognised pathogen transmitted from dog and cat bites. C. canimorsus is a slow-growing, encapsulated gram‑negative rod normally carried in canine and feline oropharynxes; invasive disease ranges from local wound infection to fulminant sepsis, DIC/purpura fulminans and meningitis. Diagnosis by conventional culture can be delayed by several days because of the organism’s fastidious growth requirements, so empiric therapy is required while microbiology evolves; molecular methods can accelerate identification when available. Severe invasive disease has substantial mortality and is more common in patients with asplenia, alcohol use disorder or liver disease, although it can occur in previously healthy people as in this case.

Pathophysiologically, severe sepsis provokes systemic endothelial activation and cytokine-driven upregulation of tissue factor, triggering widespread thrombin generation and fibrin deposition. When clot formation in the microvasculature consumes platelets and coagulation factors faster than they can be replaced, the clinical syndrome of DIC develops—paradoxically causing both thrombosis (microvascular ischaemia, acral cyanosis) and bleeding (mucocutaneous oozing, ecchymoses). In parallel, profound sepsis can impair hepatic gluconeogenesis and increase peripheral glucose uptake, explaining refractory hypoglycaemia. Pancytopenia in sepsis may reflect marrow suppression, peripheral consumption or immune-mediated processes, so haematology input is appropriate when cytopenias are severe.

Early diagnostic and management steps reflected these pathophysiologic principles. Surgical causes of the acral changes (for example, necrotising soft‑tissue infection) were actively excluded by clinical and radiological assessment. Haematology was consulted because the degree of pancytopenia broadened the differential to include acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH); flow cytometry was obtained and a bone‑marrow biopsy planned, but the absence of abnormal blasts or phenotypes on initial testing led to cancellation of the biopsy. Initial management focused on treating the presumed severe bacterial infection and supporting failing organs: prompt empiric broad‑spectrum antibiotics, haemodynamic and metabolic support, correction of coagulopathy, and organ replacement where needed.

Empiric antimicrobial therapy with piperacillin–tazobactam was started to cover the spectrum of likely dog‑bite pathogens (including Capnocytophaga, Pasteurella species and anaerobes). This choice is appropriate when the patient is critically ill and culture results are pending; once blood cultures (which in this case took seven days) identified C. canimorsus and susceptibilities were known, therapy was narrowed to ampicillin and later to oral phenoxymethylpenicillin. Concurrent supportive care in the intensive care unit became necessary when the patient developed anuria despite aggressive fluid resuscitation and loop diuretics and then a septic cardiomyopathy with fluid overload. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was instituted both for renal support and to manage fluid balance. Because the patient had active bleeding and laboratory evidence of consumptive coagulopathy, targeted transfusion of platelets and fibrinogen concentrate was given; stress‑dose corticosteroids and albumin were also administered as part of supportive sepsis management.

Key practical lessons from this phase of the case are: (1) a dog bite can lead to rapidly progressive, life‑threatening sepsis—early empiric antibiotics are essential and prophylactic antibiotics for some bites should not be deferred; (2) DIC should be suspected when bleeding, thrombocytopenia, prolonged clotting times and organ ischaemia coexist in sepsis, and management centres on treating the underlying infection while judiciously replacing blood components when there is active bleeding or very low levels; and (3) because Capnocytophaga is fastidious, clinical suspicion must guide early therapy while microbiology confirmation may lag by several days.
