Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 5: ## Outcomes, prognosis, and clinical lessons

This patient’s course illustrates both the potential for recovery in complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) and the factors that determine its extent. Clinically, spontaneous diuresis returned after the final plasma‑exchange (PEX) sessions, but sustained haematological remission and continued renal improvement were achieved only after initiation of complement blockade with eculizumab. She became dialysis independent and, at latest follow‑up, had partial renal recovery with a serum creatinine around 175 µmol/L. Extended genetic testing did not identify pathogenic complement variants; nevertheless, because of the severity of her presentation she was maintained on eculizumab (induction followed by 1,200 mg IV every 2 weeks) with planned outpatient reassessment and individualized consideration of discontinuation if renal function remains stable.

Prognosis in CM‑HUS is determined principally by a few interrelated features that are apparent in this case. Time from clinical recognition to initiation of effective complement blockade is a major determinant of renal outcome: earlier treatment is associated with better recovery, whereas delay increases the risk of irreversible kidney injury. Baseline renal injury—especially the need for dialysis at presentation—predicts a poorer renal prognosis; this patient’s dialysis dependence and severe acute tubular necrosis with ischaemic glomeruli and fibrin thrombi on biopsy indicated a high risk for incomplete recovery, which is why sustained partial recovery is a favorable result. Histologic severity on kidney biopsy (extensive thrombi, crescents, cortical necrosis) correlates with lasting loss of nephron mass. Finally, identification of pathogenic complement‑regulatory gene variants or anti‑factor H antibodies generally implies higher relapse risk and often supports longer or indefinite complement inhibition; the absence of such variants, as in this patient, lowers but does not eliminate the chance of recurrence and therefore does not obviate careful follow‑up.

Several practical clinical lessons emerge from the diagnostic and therapeutic decisions made here. First, maintain a high index of suspicion for CM‑HUS when microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia with schistocytes and persistent thrombocytopenia appear as the inflammatory and DIC features of sepsis begin to improve. Second, routine complement tests (C3/C4) may be normal in CM‑HUS and therefore cannot be used to exclude the diagnosis; functional testing and genetics are helpful but can be confounded by prior PEX or complement blockade. Third, a positive direct antiglobulin test can be false‑positive after penicillin exposure or recent transfusions; in this case an initial IgG‑positive DAT reverted to negative and the peripheral smear with schistocytes clarified the diagnosis. Fourth, because TTP is immediately life‑threatening and cannot be safely excluded clinically in many patients, empiric PEX should be started while urgent ADAMTS13 testing is obtained; PEX may transiently improve parameters in CM‑HUS but does not replace targeted complement blockade. Finally, when CM‑HUS is likely, prompt initiation of eculizumab is indicated—but timing must be balanced against infectious risks.

Managing infection risk when using terminal complement blockade requires specific precautions, as exemplified here. Terminal complement components are critical for defence against encapsulated organisms; therefore, vaccination against meningococcal disease (both conjugate A/C/Y/W and serogroup B where available) should be given before elective initiation if time allows. If eculizumab must be started urgently, give vaccines as soon as feasible and provide short‑term antibiotic prophylaxis until adequate immune response is expected—this patient received first doses of meningococcal vaccines and prophylactic oral antibiotics because vaccination had not preceded therapy by two weeks. When possible, defer complement blockade until active bacteraemia is cleared; in this case eculizumab was started after blood cultures became negative. Patients on complement inhibitors should be counselled to seek immediate medical attention for fever and signs of systemic infection.

There are zoonosis‑specific implications to remember. Capnocytophaga canimorsus, though rare, can produce fulminant sepsis and trigger secondary CM‑HUS even in previously healthy adults. A history of recent dog bite is a crucial diagnostic clue; early, appropriate empiric antimicrobial therapy that covers Capnocytophaga is important after significant dog or cat bites and in suspected invasive infections, and delayed or absent prophylaxis may contribute to severe disease as seen here.

Finally, systems and diagnostic considerations can materially affect outcomes. Capnocytophaga is a fastidious organism and blood cultures may be slow to grow; where available, molecular diagnostics can accelerate identification. Establish and follow a TMA pathway that expedites a focused laboratory bundle (CBC with smear, haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, coagulation studies, creatinine/urinalysis, ADAMTS13, STEC testing, and baseline complement studies) and clarifies who needs immediate PEX versus who can await results. Draw ADAMTS13 and baseline complement studies before PEX or eculizumab when feasible, because both therapies alter test results; perform kidney biopsy when it can be done safely (for example, after platelet recovery) to confirm TMA and assess histologic severity, which helps prognosticate and plan follow‑up. Multidisciplinary coordination among intensive care, infectious disease, haematology and nephrology teams—demonstrated in this case—facilitates timely diagnosis and the complex risk–benefit decisions required in infection‑associated CM‑HUS.

In sum, this case reinforces three practical rules: watch for evolving MAHA as sepsis and DIC improve, exclude TTP urgently and use PEX while doing so, and when CM‑HUS becomes likely pursue complement blockade promptly but with appropriate infection‑risk mitigation and specialist follow‑up.
