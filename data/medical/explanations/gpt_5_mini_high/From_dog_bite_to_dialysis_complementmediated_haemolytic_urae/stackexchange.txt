Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] Which empiric intravenous antibiotic regimens are preferred for severe sepsis 4 days after a dog bite to reliably cover Capnocytophaga canimorsus, and why might oral phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin V) be inadequate in this setting?

Bottom line: In a patient with severe sepsis after a dog bite you should give IV, broad‑spectrum antibiotics that reliably cover Capnocytophaga, Pasteurella and oral anaerobes — not oral penicillin V. Preferred empiric choices are IV piperacillin–tazobactam or IV ampicillin–sulbactam; for critically ill patients or concern for beta‑lactamase–producing organisms use a carbapenem (eg, meropenem). A third‑generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone or cefotaxime) with metronidazole is an acceptable alternative if anaerobic coverage is added.

Practical examples (adult dosing, adjust for renal function):
- Piperacillin–tazobactam 4.5 g IV every 6–8 h (extended infusion when possible) — empiric workhorse for bite‑associated sepsis.  
- Ampicillin–sulbactam 3 g IV every 6 h — alternative when appropriate.  
- Ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily (or cefotaxime 2 g IV q8 h) plus metronidazole 500 mg IV q8 h if anaerobic coverage is needed.  
- Meropenem 1 g IV q8 h for severe illness or when resistant/beta‑lactamase producers are suspected.

If true severe penicillin anaphylaxis: combine agents that cover Gram‑negatives and anaerobes (eg, aztreonam plus doxycycline or doxycycline plus metronidazole), or tailor to local susceptibilities and allergy severity.

Why these regimens: Capnocytophaga canimorsus is usually susceptible to beta‑lactams but beta‑lactamase–producing strains and encapsulated variants occur; bite wounds are often polymicrobial (Pasteurella, streptococci, staphylococci, anaerobes). Empiric IV beta‑lactam/beta‑lactamase inhibitor combinations or broad cephalosporins/carbapenems give predictable, bactericidal serum concentrations rapidly — critical in sepsis and while awaiting slow‑growing blood culture results (as in this case, where piperacillin–tazobactam was appropriately started).

Why oral phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin V) is inadequate here:
- Route and pharmacokinetics: severe sepsis commonly causes impaired gut perfusion and unpredictable oral absorption; bacteremia and septic shock require reliably high IV bactericidal drug levels.  
- Spectrum and resistance: penicillin V lacks a beta‑lactamase inhibitor and provides limited anaerobic coverage; some Capnocytophaga and other bite pathogens may produce beta‑lactamases.  
- Clinical safety: switching to oral therapy should only occur after clear clinical improvement, source control and culture‑confirmed susceptibility. In the reported case the team started piperacillin–tazobactam empirically (appropriate), and although the isolate later guided de‑escalation, initial IV broad coverage was the correct strategy.

Bottom line for practice: in any patient with systemic illness after a dog bite treat empirically with IV agents that cover Capnocytophaga plus anaerobes (piperacillin–tazobactam or ampicillin–sulbactam; escalate to a carbapenem if severe or resistant organisms suspected). Reserve oral step‑down only after clinical stability and documented susceptibility; do not rely on penicillin V for severe or systemic infections.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a septic adult with thrombocytopenia, anemia, acute kidney injury, and coagulopathy consistent with DIC, what clinical and laboratory features should prompt suspicion for a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) such as complement-mediated HUS rather than DIC alone?

Short answer
Suspect a superimposed TMA (for example complement‑mediated HUS) in a septic patient with DIC when the picture evolves from a consumptive coagulopathy into a persistent or new microangiopathic haemolytic process — classically: a sudden unexplained fall in haemoglobin with biochemical evidence of intravascular haemolysis, prominent schistocytosis, thrombocytopenia that persists despite resolution of coagulation abnormalities, and renal injury that is out of proportion to, or worsens after, the acute septic/DIC phase.

Why this distinction matters (pathophysiologic rationale)
DIC is driven by systemic activation of the coagulation cascade → fibrin‑rich microthrombi, consumption of clotting factors (prolonged PT/PTT, low fibrinogen) and bleeding. TMA is driven by endothelial injury with platelet‑rich microthrombi → microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia and end‑organ ischemia (kidney is classically affected). Because the treatment and prognosis diverge (supportive care/target infection for DIC versus plasma exchange and possibly complement blockade for CM‑HUS), recognizing when both are present is critical.

Clinical and laboratory features that should prompt suspicion — with illustrations from the case
- New, abrupt, isolated drop in haemoglobin without overt bleeding: in the case Hb fell from 111 → 68 g/L over 6 days.  
- Biochemical evidence of intravascular haemolysis: low/undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, indirect hyperbilirubinaemia and reticulocytosis when bone marrow responsive. In this patient haptoglobin became undetectable and LDH rose as haemolysis emerged.  
- Schistocytes on peripheral smear: their presence (even modest percentages) is a key morphologic clue; this patient had 15–20% schistocytes — strongly suggestive of MAHA/TMA.  
- Persistent or worsening thrombocytopenia despite correction/normalization of coagulation parameters: here platelets remained 30–40×10^9/L while coagulation studies had normalized, arguing against ongoing pure DIC.  
- Renal dysfunction disproportionate to other organ dysfunction or worsening after sepsis control: the patient became anuric and dialysis‑dependent; renal biopsy later showed ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and crescents consistent with TMA.  
- Lack of response to therapies for other causes: failure of presumed autoimmune haemolysis to respond to corticosteroids and a negative repeat DAT (initial DAT likely false positive due to penicillin/transfusion) pointed away from immune haemolysis.  
- Exclusion of other TMAs: ADAMTS13 activity not severely reduced (43.5% here, arguing against TTP), and negative Shiga toxin PCR reduced likelihood of STEC‑HUS.  
- Timing: haemolysis and MAHA emerging or worsening as sepsis/DIC is improving suggests a secondary, infection‑triggered TMA rather than only DIC.

Practical diagnostic approach when you suspect superimposed TMA
- Urgent labs: peripheral smear for schistocytes, haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, reticulocyte count, DAT, CBC, PT/PTT/fibrinogen, D‑dimer, creatinine/urine studies.  
- Send ADAMTS13 and Shiga toxin testing early.  
- Draw complement studies and arrange extended complement/genetic testing, but remember normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS and assays can be confounded by transfusion, plasma exchange or eculizumab. (In the case basic C3/C4 were normal; later AH50 was low after PEX/eculizumab.)  
- Consider kidney biopsy if safe — it can confirm TMA (fibrin thrombi, arteriolar/glomerular endothelial injury).  
- While awaiting results, if TTP cannot be excluded clinically, initiate plasma exchange; if CM‑HUS is suspected and infection controlled, consider prompt eculizumab with meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis.

Clinical takeaway
In a septic patient with initial DIC, new or persistent MAHA (falling Hb, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, schistocytes), ongoing thrombocytopenia despite normalization of coagulation studies, and disproportionate or progressive AKI should raise a high index of suspicion for a superimposed TMA — and trigger ADAMTS13 and Shiga testing, targeted complement evaluation, consideration of biopsy, temporary plasma exchange if TTP is not excluded, and early discussion about complement blockade once infection control and vaccination/prophylaxis considerations are addressed. The Capnocytophaga case neatly illustrates this sequence: initial DIC then delayed MAHA/TMA with biopsy confirmation and clinical response after complement‑targeted therapy.

### [Morning Report] An adult recovering from Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia develops microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and dialysis-dependent AKI. How would you triage the diagnosis using the PLASMIC score and ADAMTS13 activity (what threshold rules in TTP), what immediate therapy should begin while results are pending, and how does management change once TTP is excluded?

Short answer
- Triage with the PLASMIC score to estimate pretest probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency; if the score is high (6–7) treat as TTP immediately. ADAMTS13 activity <10% (severe deficiency) confirms immune or congenital TTP. While ADAMTS13 is pending, start empiric TTP therapy if clinical suspicion is moderate–high: urgent plasma exchange (PEX) plus corticosteroids (and caplacizumab where available). Once ADAMTS13 is not severely deficient (eg, 43.5% in this case) stop TTP-directed therapy and shift management to the alternative TMA diagnosis — in a patient like this with dialysis‑dependent AKI and biopsy and microbiology supporting CM‑HUS, begin complement blockade (eculizumab) after ensuring infection control and administering meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis.

Teaching points and clinical reasoning

1) Use PLASMIC to triage, and draw ADAMTS13 before plasma is given
- The PLASMIC score (Platelet count <30×10^9/L; evidence of Hemolysis; Absence of active cancer; No Solid‑organ/stem‑cell transplant; MCV <90 fL; INR <1.5; Creatinine <2.0 mg/dL) yields 0–7 points. Scores 6–7 = high pretest probability → treat as TTP; 0–4 = low pretest probability → look for alternate causes; 5 = intermediate → use clinical judgment.
- Always obtain an ADAMTS13 sample before starting PEX if feasible because PEX rapidly lowers measurable activity and can remove inhibitors.
- In the reported case the PLASMIC score was 4/7 (low pretest probability) and ADAMTS13 activity returned at 43.5%, effectively excluding TTP.

2) ADAMTS13 threshold to rule in/out TTP
- Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10% activity) is the diagnostic threshold for TTP (usually immune‑mediated). Values well above that make TTP unlikely and prompt evaluation for other TMAs (STEC‑HUS, complement‑mediated HUS, sepsis/DIC‑associated TMA, drug‑ or malignancy‑related TMA).

3) Immediate empiric therapy while results are pending
- If PLASMIC is high or clinical suspicion for TTP is strong (neurologic findings, severe thrombocytopenia, hemolysis) do not wait for ADAMTS13: start daily therapeutic plasma exchange with FFP replacement immediately (PEX is lifesaving in TTP), give high‑dose corticosteroids (eg, prednisone 1 mg/kg PO or IV methylprednisolone), and consider caplacizumab if available.
- Avoid routine platelet transfusions in suspected TTP unless there is life‑threatening bleeding or an invasive procedure is required.
- If PLASMIC is low and the presentation fits sepsis/DIC or infection‑associated TMA, prioritize treatment of the infection and supportive care; PEX may be used as a temporizing measure in severe cases but is not disease‑directed for CM‑HUS.

4) Change in management once TTP is excluded
- If ADAMTS13 activity is >10% (as here), stop TTP‑directed therapies (daily PEX and caplacizumab) directed solely at ADAMTS13 deficiency and redirect the workup and treatment.
- For suspected complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) with ongoing hemolysis and severe AKI: treat the trigger (aggressive antibiotics for bacteremia), and institute complement blockade with eculizumab promptly for severe disease. Do not wait for extended genetic testing to start therapy if clinical severity warrants it.
- Because terminal complement blockade increases risk for Neisseria and other encapsulated organisms, give meningococcal vaccination (MenACWY and MenB) ideally ≥2 weeks before eculizumab. If urgent treatment is required, vaccinate at time of first dose and give prophylactic antibiotics until 2 weeks after vaccination (the team in this report used Apocillin). In this case eculizumab was started after blood cultures became negative and immediately after initial meningococcal vaccination; PEX had been used as a bridge.
- Recognize the role of PEX in CM‑HUS is limited and typically transient; sustained hematologic and renal recovery in CM‑HUS depends on complement blockade (as illustrated in the case where only eculizumab produced durable remission).

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
- Draw ADAMTS13 before plasma when possible. Use the PLASMIC score to decide urgency: high score → immediate PEX ± steroids ± caplacizumab; low score → search for other TMAs. ADAMTS13 <10% = TTP; values well above that (eg 43.5% in this patient) effectively exclude TTP and should prompt transition off TTP therapy and toward targeted treatment (in this case, eculizumab after ensuring infection control and appropriate vaccination/prophylaxis).

### [Clinical Pearls] In suspected renal TMA with persistent AKI, when is a kidney biopsy indicated, and which histopathologic findings support a diagnosis of TMA without immune-complex glomerulonephritis?

Short answer
- Perform a kidney biopsy when persistent or dialysis‑dependent AKI remains after initial stabilization and when the histologic result would change management—most importantly to confirm TMA, exclude alternative glomerular diseases (especially immune‑complex GN or vasculitis), and assess chronicity/prognosis.  
- On biopsy, the combination of light‑microscopic features of microvascular thrombosis (fibrin/platelet thrombi in glomerular capillaries and small arterioles, endothelial swelling/subendothelial widening, mesangiolysis, ischemic collapse of glomerular tufts, and often concomitant acute tubular necrosis or crescents) together with negative immunofluorescence for immune‑complexes and absence of electron‑dense deposits on electron microscopy supports TMA without immune‑complex glomerulonephritis.

Clinical reasoning and practical points (teaching pearl)
- Why biopsy? In adults with MAHA, thrombocytopenia and AKI the initial priority is rapid laboratory triage (peripheral smear, ADAMTS13, Shiga‑toxin testing, coagulation panel, basic complement tests) and empiric life‑saving measures (PEX for suspected TTP, supportive care). A renal biopsy is most useful later when the renal injury is prolonged or dialysis‑dependent, when noninvasive tests are inconclusive, or when distinguishing TMA from immune‑complex GN or rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis would alter therapy (for example, complement blockade versus immunosuppression). Biopsy also documents acuity versus chronicity and thus helps prognosis.
- Timing and safety: In patients with recent sepsis/DIC the bleeding risk often mandates delay until the patient is more stable and platelet/coagulation parameters are acceptable (many centres aim for platelet count >50 × 10^9/L and corrected INR/PT). If a high bleeding risk persists, a transjugular renal biopsy can be considered. In the presented case the team waited until platelets rose to 77 × 10^9/L and then obtained a diagnostic biopsy.
- What to look for histologically: Acute TMA — glomerular and arteriolar fibrin thrombi, endothelial swelling with subendothelial “fluffy” material or mesangiolysis, ischemic collapse of glomerular tufts, and frequently acute tubular necrosis; crescents may be seen in severe injury. Chronic TMA changes include GBM double contours and arteriolar intimal thickening. Crucially, absence of granular immunoglobulin/C3 staining on immunofluorescence and absence of electron‑dense deposits on EM argue against immune‑complex GN. In this case the biopsy demonstrated severe ATN, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and scattered crescents, and IF/EM showed no immune‑complex deposition—findings that confirmed TMA and helped support the diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS.
- Caveats: A renal biopsy does not replace targeted laboratory testing (ADAMTS13, Shiga‑toxin, complement genetics/functional assays) and does not itself identify genetic complement defects. Also, normal serum complement levels do not exclude complement‑mediated TMA—histology can be decisive when blood tests are unrevealing or confounded by prior plasma exchange or therapy.

### [Differential Diagnosis] A patient with falling hemoglobin after sepsis has a positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test after recent beta-lactam exposure and transfusions. What steps help distinguish warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia from MAHA due to TMA, and how can drug exposure or transfusion cause a false-positive Coombs result?

Short answer
Integrate the peripheral smear, platelet trend and organ dysfunction with the DAT result — a positive Coombs test does not prove warm autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA) in a sick, transfused patient. In practice: (1) look for schistocytes versus spherocytes and note the platelet count and presence of AKI; (2) repeat and refine the DAT (monospecific reagents, elution) and send targeted tests (ADAMTS13, complement studies, drug‑dependent antibody workup); and (3) treat the process suggested by the full picture (treat TMA urgently if schistocytes + thrombocytopenia + organ injury), while pursuing confirmation. In the case report the combination of 15–20% schistocytes, persistent severe thrombocytopenia and dialysis‑dependent AKI, failure to respond to steroids and a subsequent negative repeat DAT pointed away from warm AIHA and toward MAHA from TMA (CM‑HUS), and the initial IgG‑positive DAT was probably drug‑ or transfusion‑related.

Why this approach (clinical reasoning)
- Morphology and platelets are decisive. Warm AIHA is an extravascular IgG‑mediated process: peripheral smear usually shows spherocytes, platelets are typically normal (unless Evans syndrome), and patients often show a brisk reticulocyte response and steroid responsiveness. MAHA/TMA is an intravascular, mechanical destruction: schistocytes/helmet cells, marked thrombocytopenia, and end‑organ dysfunction (classically AKI and sometimes neurologic signs). In this patient the dominant findings were schistocytes, low platelets (30–40×10^9/L) and severe AKI — findings much more consistent with TMA than isolated warm AIHA.
- Laboratory context matters. A truly pathogenic DAT should be accompanied by signs of immune haemolysis (spherocytes, reticulocytosis, extravascular haemolysis pattern). The patient had undetectable haptoglobin and rising LDH (consistent with haemolysis) but the morphology and persistent thrombocytopenia argued for MAHA. Steroids were started for suspected AIHA but the lack of response also argues against primary warm AIHA.
- Don’t let a single positive DAT anchor management. Because TTP/HUS are time‑sensitive, if the clinical picture fits TMA (schistocytes + thrombocytopenia ± AKI) you must concurrently pursue TMA protocols — draw ADAMTS13, start plasma exchange if TTP is likely, and consider complement blockade for CM‑HUS — while refining the immunohematology workup.

Practical diagnostic steps to separate warm AIHA from MAHA/TMA
1. Immediate bedside/lab review
   - Peripheral smear: quantify schistocytes vs spherocytes (schistocytes >1% supports MAHA; in this case 15–20% is strongly diagnostic of fragmentation haemolysis).
   - Platelet count trend: marked thrombocytopenia strongly favors TMA.
   - Coagulation panel: DIC causes abnormal PT/aPTT and low fibrinogen; isolated TMA typically has normal coagulation studies.
2. DAT and immunohematology refinement
   - Repeat DAT with monospecific reagents (IgG vs C3d) on a fresh sample.
   - Perform an eluate from RBCs: if eluate reacts broadly with reagent RBCs → true autoantibody; if eluate is non‑reactive → nonspecific coating or passive antibody more likely.
   - Send drug‑dependent antibody testing (specialized lab) if a culprit drug is suspected.
   - Check antibody screen/crossmatch (for transfusion reactions or passive donor antibodies).
3. Targeted TMA workup
   - Urgent ADAMTS13 activity (rule out TTP: activity <10% diagnostic).
   - STEC testing if diarrhoeal prodrome suspected.
   - Complement tests/genetics and consider renal biopsy if needed (as was done in the case).
4. Therapeutic triage
   - If TTP is possible, start plasma exchange immediately while awaiting ADAMTS13.
   - If the clinical picture and biopsy support CM‑HUS, treat with eculizumab once infection control and vaccinations/prophylaxis are addressed.
   - If bona fide warm AIHA is confirmed, treat with corticosteroids ± further immunosuppression; do not delay TMA therapies if the overall picture suggests TMA.

Mechanisms for drug‑ or transfusion‑related false‑positive DAT
- Hapten (drug‑adsorption) mechanism: penicillins (and some beta‑lactams) can bind to RBC membranes; the drug–RBC complex is then targeted by antibodies. This may produce a positive DAT and sometimes true haemolysis; it typically requires the drug to be present.
- Immune‑complex mechanism: drug–antibody complexes can adsorb to RBCs and fix complement (quinidine‑type), producing a complement‑positive DAT and intravascular haemolysis.
- Autoantibody induction: some drugs (eg, α‑methyldopa) induce true RBC autoantibodies that persist after drug cessation.
- Non‑immunologic protein adsorption: certain antibiotics can produce nonspecific adsorption of plasma proteins (including IgG) onto RBCs producing a DAT that may not reflect pathogenic autoimmune haemolysis.
- Passive transfer from plasma products: transfused plasma‑containing products (FFP, Octaplas, platelet units with plasma, IVIG) can deliver donor antibodies that coat recipient RBCs and give a transient positive DAT without true autoimmune haemolysis.
- Sepsis‑related complement deposition: severe infection can deposit complement on RBCs (C3d), producing a positive DAT for complement without classic warm AIHA.

Apply this to the case
- The authors report an initial DAT positive for IgG after exposure to penicillins and after several transfusions. The hemolysis did not respond to steroids; schistocytes were prominent and platelets remained very low. A repeat DAT was negative. These features are classic for a false‑positive DAT from recent drug exposure or transfusion rather than warm AIHA, and the overall picture correctly pointed to MAHA from TMA (CM‑HUS), which ultimately responded to PEX and eculizumab.

Clinical pearl
Always interpret the DAT in the clinical and morphologic context. In a septic, transfused patient with falling haemoglobin, thrombocytopenia and schistocytosis, treat and investigate for TMA while you repeat/refine immunohematology testing — a transient drug‑ or transfusion‑related positive Coombs test is common and can mislead if taken in isolation.

### [Pathophysiology] Why do normal C3 and C4 levels not exclude complement-mediated HUS, and how can prior plasma exchange and initiation of eculizumab confound interpretation of downstream complement functional assays such as the AH50?

Short answer
Normal serum C3 and C4 do not rule out complement‑mediated HUS because the pathogenic process is usually local dysregulation of the alternative pathway at the endothelial surface rather than wholesale depletion of circulating complement proteins. And functional haemolytic assays such as the AH50 are easily distorted by therapy: plasma exchange (PEX) changes circulating complement proteins and removes autoantibodies, while eculizumab (anti‑C5) pharmacologically abolishes terminal complement activity and will therefore make AH50 (and CH50) artificially low. In the presented case the timeline illustrates both problems: C3/C4 were measured before PEX and were normal, whereas the AH50 (measured 8 days after the last PEX and 4 days after the first eculizumab dose) was markedly reduced (6%) — a pattern most consistent with treatment effects rather than proving or excluding intrinsic alternative‑pathway consumption.

Why C3/C4 can be normal in CM‑HUS
- CM‑HUS is driven by dysregulated activation of the alternative pathway on endothelial surfaces (microvasculature), producing local C3 activation, endothelial injury and thrombosis. This localized activation frequently does not cause large measurable depletion of circulating C3 or C4.
- C3 is also an acute‑phase reactant and may be preserved or even increased during systemic inflammation or sepsis, masking any modest consumption.
- C4 primarily reflects classical/lectin pathway activity; isolated alternative pathway dysregulation often leaves C4 in the reference range. Therefore normal plasma C3 and C4 are neither sensitive nor specific for diagnosing complement‑mediated TMA.

How PEX and eculizumab confound functional assays (AH50)
- AH50 measures the serum’s alternative‑pathway capacity to produce haemolysis and depends on intact upstream (factor B, D, properdin, C3) and terminal components (C5–C9). Any intervention that removes components or blocks the terminal pathway will change the result.
- Plasma exchange removes circulating complement proteins, activation fragments and autoantibodies (for example anti‑factor H), and replacement with donor plasma (Octaplas or FFP) repletes complement proteins — both actions change measured levels and can produce falsely normal or falsely low functional results depending on timing.
- Eculizumab binds C5 and prevents C5 cleavage and MAC formation. Because AH50 (and CH50) require terminal complement activity to produce lysis, eculizumab will markedly depress these assays regardless of whether upstream complement dysregulation is present. Thus a low AH50 after eculizumab reflects pharmacologic blockade, not diagnostic evidence of prior consumption or genetic deficiency.
- The converse is also important: PEX performed after eculizumab can remove drug and transiently restore AH50, complicating interpretation of both drug effect and complement status.

Practical implications (how to apply this at the bedside)
- If CM‑HUS is suspected, obtain and bank pre‑treatment serum/plasma for C3, C4, CH50/AH50, anti‑factor H antibodies and ADAMTS13 before PEX or complement blockade whenever possible. If urgent therapy is required, at least freeze and label pre‑treatment samples for later reference.
- Do not rely on a single normal C3/C4 to exclude complement‑mediated disease; integrate clinical features (MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI), renal biopsy, exclusion of TTP (ADAMTS13), STEC testing and the clinical response to therapy.
- Recognize that AH50 or CH50 measured after eculizumab will be suppressed and are not diagnostic; anti‑factor H antibodies may be falsely negative after PEX. Genetic testing for complement variants is not affected by PEX/eculizumab and should be pursued but will not be immediately available.

Tie back to the case
In this patient the early normal C3/C4 did not argue against CM‑HUS because the syndrome is commonly a surface‑localized alternative‑pathway disorder and because sepsis can preserve or raise serum complement. The later profoundly reduced AH50 (6%) was measured after several PEX sessions and after starting eculizumab, so it is best interpreted as a treatment effect (terminal pathway blockade and prior plasma manipulation) rather than proof of antecedent systemic complement depletion. Clinical judgement, biopsy evidence of TMA, exclusion of alternative causes and the patient’s sustained response to eculizumab provided the diagnostic and therapeutic rationale.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how Capnocytophaga canimorsus can trigger complement activation and, in susceptible hosts, lead to uncontrolled alternative pathway activity, endothelial injury, and thrombotic microangiopathy.

Bottom line: Capnocytophaga canimorsus — like other gram‑negative, encapsulated pathogens — provokes innate complement activation; in a host whose alternative‑pathway (AP) regulation is inadequate (genetic defect, autoantibody, or overwhelming stimulus) this initial activation is amplified by the AP “tick‑over,” producing C5 cleavage, sublytic membrane attack complexes and C5a‑driven inflammation that injure glomerular endothelium and trigger platelet‑rich microthrombi → thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

How this happens (stepwise, clinically useful):
- Triggering the cascade: bacterial surface structures (lipooligosaccharide/outer membrane components and exposed microbial surfaces) activate classical/lectin pathways and provide a platform for AP amplification. C. canimorsus is often capsulated; some strains are relatively complement‑resistant so complement activation persists longer and is more likely to become dysregulated.
- Amplification by the alternative pathway: the AP operates by spontaneous C3 hydrolysis and formation of C3bBb convertases on surfaces; properdin stabilizes these convertases. Normally regulators (factor H, factor I, membrane cofactor protein/CD46, etc.) limit this. A predisposing defect (or antibody), or a very strong infectious stimulus, tips the balance toward runaway AP activity with large amounts of surface C3b and generation of C5 convertase.
- Endothelial injury and thrombosis: C5a is a potent chemoattractant and neutrophil activator; sublytic C5b‑9 (MAC) and C5a‑driven inflammation cause endothelial cell activation (up‑regulation of tissue factor and release of ultra‑large von Willebrand factor), a procoagulant shift and platelet aggregation. Microvascular platelet‑rich thrombi shear red cells (MAHA), consume platelets (thrombocytopenia) and produce ischemic kidney injury — the clinicopathologic picture of complement‑mediated HUS.
- Why the diagnosis can be obscured: sepsis/DIC can dominate early, and standard complement tests (C3/C4) may be normal because activation may be focal or because testing is done at the wrong time or after plasma exchange/therapy. ADAMTS13 activity >10% argues against TTP; renal biopsy showing fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomeruli supports TMA.

Tie to the case:
- The patient developed fulminant C. canimorsus sepsis with DIC and AKI, then—after initial improvement—sudden isolated haemolysis with schistocytes, persistent thrombocytopenia and renal biopsy evidence of fibrin thrombi. ADAMTS13 (43.5%) excluded TTP; C3/C4 were normal; extended AP activity (AH50) was low only after plasma exchange and eculizumab (consistent with treatment effect). Rapid haematologic and partial renal recovery only after complement blockade confirms a pathogenic role for uncontrolled complement activity in her TMA.

Practical implications for clinicians:
- Suspect infection‑triggered, complement‑mediated TMA when MAHA and thrombocytopenia emerge or persist despite control of sepsis, with ADAMTS13 not severely reduced and biopsy/clinical features of TMA.
- Use plasma exchange as a bridge while excluding TTP and securing cultures; start eculizumab promptly once active bacteraemia is controlled (vaccinate and give prophylactic antibiotics) because C5 blockade halts the final common pathway of endothelial injury and often produces rapid hematologic remission.

### [Management] When initiating eculizumab for complement-mediated HUS in a patient with recent bacteremia, what are best practices regarding timing relative to negative blood cultures, required meningococcal vaccinations, and the need for antibiotic prophylaxis?

Bottom line
- Whenever possible delay terminal complement blockade until the patient’s bacteraemia is controlled and blood cultures are negative. Before starting eculizumab give both meningococcal conjugate (ACWY) and serogroup B vaccines and, if you cannot wait the recommended 2‑week window for vaccine response, give immediate antibiotic prophylaxis until adequate vaccine-induced protection is expected (commonly 2 weeks after vaccination). Continue definitive therapy for the original infection and involve ID.

Why
- Eculizumab blocks C5 and prevents formation of the membrane attack complex. That dramatically increases the risk of invasive infection with Neisseria meningitidis (and may impair clearance of other encapsulated organisms such as Capnocytophaga). Waiting for clearance of bacteraemia when feasible reduces the risk of uncontrolled infection once complement is inhibited.
- Vaccination against meningococcus is the cornerstone prevention: both MenACWY and MenB are recommended for patients who will receive eculizumab because disease can be caused by either serogroup. Optimal protection develops about 2 weeks after vaccination.
- If eculizumab cannot be safely delayed (life‑threatening TMA), give the vaccines immediately and bridge with antibiotic prophylaxis until vaccine response has had time to develop; also continue appropriate therapeutic antibiotics for the index bacteremia.

Practical points and how this case applied them
- Timing relative to cultures: the team in this report waited until blood cultures were negative before initiating eculizumab — the preferred approach when the infection can be controlled. This balances the urgent need to treat complement‑mediated HUS against the risk of exacerbating an active invasive infection.
- Vaccinations used in the case: Menveo (ACWY) and Bexsero (MenB) were administered prior to the first eculizumab dose. That is concordant with guideline practice of giving both vaccines.
- Antibiotic prophylaxis used in the case: because the vaccines had not been given ≥2 weeks before starting complement blockade, the team gave phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) as prophylaxis — a reasonable approach to cover meningococci until vaccine protection is expected. Local ID/microbiology advice should guide agent choice (e.g., amoxicillin/penicillin if susceptible; alternatives such as a fluoroquinolone or macrolide for true penicillin allergy as directed by local guidance).
- Plasma exchange and testing caveat: PEX can remove administered antibody (eculizumab) and vaccine‑induced antibodies and can confound complement assays; coordinate timing of PEX, vaccination and eculizumab with nephrology/hematology/ID to avoid unintended loss of therapy or misinterpretation of tests.

Counseling and follow‑up
- Educate the patient and family about the signs of meningococcal disease and ensure prompt access to emergency care for fever or sepsis.
- Ensure completion of the recommended vaccine schedule/boosters per local guidance and continued communication between nephrology, ID and immunization services while the patient remains on eculizumab.

In short: treat the bacteremia first if you can, vaccinate against meningococcus (ACWY + B) before starting eculizumab, and if you must start immediately give vaccines at once and provide antibiotic prophylaxis until vaccine protection is established — exactly the strategy used in this case.

### [Management] In complement-mediated HUS, what factors predict renal recovery and inform the duration of complement blockade, and how does identification (or absence) of pathogenic complement gene variants influence long-term management and monitoring for relapse?

Bottom line
- Renal recovery in complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) is determined less by diagnostic labels and more by the severity and chronicity of the kidney injury and by how quickly effective complement blockade is started. Identification of a pathogenic complement variant or anti‑factor H antibody increases the risk of relapse and usually tilts practice toward prolonged or indefinite complement inhibition; absence of a pathogenic variant in a clearly infection‑triggered episode supports consideration of a time‑limited course with close surveillance.

Why these factors matter (clinical reasoning and pathophysiology)
- Pathogenesis: CM‑HUS is driven by dysregulated alternative pathway activation causing endothelial injury, microthrombi and ischaemic kidney damage. Once endothelial injury progresses to irreversible parenchymal loss (global glomerulosclerosis, interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy), recovery is unlikely even if haemolysis is controlled.
- Time to effective therapy: early terminal complement blockade (eculizumab) halts ongoing endothelial injury and microthrombosis, improving the chance of renal recovery. Delays allow sustained microvascular occlusion and necrosis → worse functional recovery.
- Burden and duration of AKI: prolonged anuria/dialysis dependence and higher peak creatinine reflect more severe microvascular/ischaemic injury and predict incomplete recovery.
- Histology: kidney biopsy showing predominantly acute TMA changes (fibrin thrombi, endothelial swelling, acute tubular necrosis) with little chronic scarring predicts better recovery than biopsies dominated by chronic changes.
- Haematologic response is a surrogate: rapid normalization of platelets and clearance of haemolysis after complement blockade correlates with renal improvement; only transient improvement with plasma exchange alone often predicts incomplete renal recovery.

How genetic/autoimmune testing changes management
- Pathogenic complement variants (CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, THBD, etc.) or anti‑factor H antibodies identify patients with an intrinsic predisposition to recurrent complement overactivation. In such patients:
  - Relapse risk is substantially higher and relapse can be severe.
  - Most experts favour prolonged or lifelong complement blockade (or until alternative definitive strategies are available), together with genetic counselling and family screening.
  - Anti‑factor H antibodies are managed with immunosuppression (eg, steroids/rituximab) ± PEX in addition to complement blockade to eradicate antibody production.
- Absence of a pathogenic variant (as in this case) does not exclude CM‑HUS but implies lower baseline relapse risk, particularly when a clear external trigger (here, Capnocytophaga sepsis) is identified. Practical consequences:
  - A time‑limited course of eculizumab (commonly reassessed after 3–6 months) is reasonable if the patient achieves haematologic remission and meaningful renal recovery.
  - Continued therapy decisions should be individualized by severity of the initial insult, pace of renal recovery, and patient preference.

Practical monitoring and criteria for stopping/restarting therapy
- Timing of testing and interpretation: genetic testing is unaffected by therapy but functional complement assays (eg, AH50) and antibody measurements can be confounded by plasma exchange or anti‑C5 therapy — ideally obtain baseline samples before PEX or eculizumab when feasible.
- When considering stopping eculizumab (especially in patients without pathogenic variants):
  - Ensure stable/plateaued renal function, sustained absence of MAHA (stable Hb, platelets, normalizing LDH, no schistocytes), and clinical stability for a defined interval (commonly 3–6 months).
  - Have a protocol for close post‑cessation surveillance and a low threshold to restart therapy.
- Practical surveillance after stopping (or during tapering): frequent early checks (eg, weekly to biweekly for the first month), then every 2–4 weeks for months 2–3, then monthly through the first year — monitor CBC, LDH, haptoglobin, peripheral smear, creatinine/urine output, urinalysis/proteinuria, and blood pressure. Reinitiate eculizumab promptly for recurrence of thrombocytopenia, new schistocytosis/hemolysis, or rising creatinine/proteinuria.
- Infection precautions: vaccinate against Neisseria meningitidis (and consider other encapsulated organisms) before starting anti‑C5 when possible, and give short‑term antibiotic prophylaxis when vaccines are incomplete or when eculizumab must be started during recent/ongoing bacteremia — the team in this case waited for culture clearance, administered vaccines and used prophylactic penicillin.

How this case illustrates the principles
- Predictors: this patient presented with dialysis‑dependent anuria and biopsy‑proven acute TMA (fibrin thrombi, crescents) — features that predict more guarded renal recovery. Her haematologic remission and partial renal improvement followed eculizumab, illustrating the benefit of complement blockade even when started after initial sepsis/DIC.
- Genetics and duration: extended genetic testing found no pathogenic variant, so the treating team elected a finite maintenance strategy (eculizumab every 2 weeks for 4 months) with planned reassessment — a reasonable, individualized approach for a severe but infection‑triggered CM‑HUS without demonstrable complement mutations.
- Monitoring nuance: functional complement assays (AH50) were reduced after PEX and eculizumab, emphasising that timing of testing matters.

Takeaway recommendation
- In CM‑HUS, prioritize rapid control of complement‑mediated injury (eculizumab) to maximize renal recovery; use biopsy and clinical course to gauge reversibility. Perform genetic and antibody testing (ideally before PEX/eculizumab when possible) to stratify relapse risk. If a pathogenic variant or anti‑factor H antibody is present, plan for prolonged or lifelong complement blockade, family counselling, and close follow‑up. If no variant is found and the episode is clearly triggered (for example by infection), a time‑limited course with rigorous post‑cessation monitoring is reasonable.
