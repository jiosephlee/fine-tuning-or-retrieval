Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] Applying the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality criteria, which case features support a “probable/likely” association between semaglutide and IMNM, and why is rechallenge inappropriate?

Answer (clinical pearl): This case meets the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre “probable/likely” category because it combines a plausible time relationship, objective evidence of an immune necrotising myopathy, exclusion of other likely causes, and a clinically convincing recovery after withdrawal and treatment — while a formal rechallenge is neither required nor ethically acceptable.

Why it is “probable/likely” (case features mapped to WHO‑UMC criteria)
- Temporal relationship: the patient had an immediate adverse reaction to a single subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (nausea, vomiting, hypovolaemia, acute renal failure) and then developed progressive proximal weakness with a rising CK over the subsequent 4 months (CK 337 U/L initially, peak 3,992 U/L). The timing is biologically plausible for a drug‑triggered, delayed immune process.
- Objective confirmation of IMNM: muscle biopsy showed necrotic and regenerating myofibres, macrophage (CD68) myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition — classic histopathology for immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy rather than simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.
- Exclusion of competing causes: there was no statin or other known myotoxic exposure, myositis panel was negative for HMGCR and SRP (and other myositis autoantibodies), imaging showed no occult malignancy, and the clinical picture lacked features of an alternative connective‑tissue myositis.
- Positive dechallenge / clinical response: after cessation of semaglutide and treatment with high‑dose corticosteroids and IVIG the patient’s CK fell and her strength and function progressively normalized; she remained off immunosuppression and off GLP‑1 therapy for many months with sustained remission — a clinically meaningful response that supports a drug‑related trigger.
- Limitation preventing “certain”: absence of pre‑drug baseline CK (so a smouldering pre‑existing myopathy cannot be absolutely excluded) and absence of a positive rechallenge. WHO‑UMC requires rechallenge (or incontrovertible evidence) to classify an event as “certain.”

Why rechallenge is inappropriate
- Safety and ethics: deliberate re‑exposure risks provoking a recurrent — and potentially more severe — immune necrotising myopathy with complications already observed in this patient (severe dysphagia, respiratory compromise risk, ICU admission, need for IVIG and high‑dose steroids). Intentionally risking a life‑threatening or disabling recurrence for diagnostic certainty is ethically unjustifiable.
- Not required for clinical management or pharmacovigilance: biopsy‑proven IMNM, the time course, exclusion of likely alternatives, and durable remission after withdrawal and immunotherapy provide sufficient evidence to treat the patient appropriately and to report the suspected adverse reaction. In severe immune adverse events, pharmacovigilance accepts “probable” causality without rechallenge.

Takeaway for clinicians: When a new severe myopathy arises after a drug exposure, seek objective confirmation (CK, MRI, biopsy), exclude common triggers (statins, supplements, malignancy), remove the suspected agent, and treat immune myopathies promptly — but do not attempt rechallenge when the prior reaction was severe.
