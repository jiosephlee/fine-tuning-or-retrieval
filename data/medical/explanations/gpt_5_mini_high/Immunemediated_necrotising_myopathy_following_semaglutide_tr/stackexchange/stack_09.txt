Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Management] Given the expanding use of GLP-1 receptor agonists, what counseling and monitoring steps would you implement before and after initiation to detect potential muscle adverse effects, especially in patients with prior autoimmune risk factors or statin exposure?

Clinical takeaway (two lines)
GLP‑1 receptor agonists are generally safe, but rare severe muscle injury—ranging from typical drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis to immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)—has been reported. Before prescribing, counsel patients and set up pragmatic, risk‑stratified monitoring so that new myalgias or weakness trigger prompt evaluation; if IMNM is suspected, early specialist evaluation and immunotherapy can be limb‑ and life‑saving (as in the presented case).

Practical pre‑initiation steps
- Focused history: ask about prior autoimmune disease, prior unexplained transaminase or CK elevations, personal/family myopathy, current or prior statin (or other myotoxic agent) exposure, and use of supplements (eg, red yeast rice). In the case report the patient had no statin exposure but had baseline transaminase elevations from NAFLD—important because AST/ALT can be confounded by muscle injury.
- Baseline tests for everyone starting a GLP‑1 RA: CK, creatinine, electrolytes, AST/ALT, and TSH. Document functional baseline (gait, proximal strength) and counsel to report changes.
- For higher‑risk patients (prior autoimmune disease, prior CK elevations, active statin therapy): consider baseline myositis serologies including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP and early involvement of rheumatology/neurology in shared decision‑making. Recognize that up to ~1/3 of IMNM can be seronegative (as in this patient), so a negative panel does not exclude immune myopathy.

Counseling to give the patient
- Symptoms to report urgently: new or progressive muscle weakness (especially proximal), new difficulty swallowing or talking (dysphagia/dysphonia), shortness of breath, dark urine, inability to rise from a chair, or any rapidly worsening myalgias.
- Practical advice: treat GI side effects promptly and maintain hydration—severe vomiting/dehydration can precipitate rhabdomyolysis (this patient had severe GI intolerance and AKI immediately after the first dose).
- Advise avoidance or close review of concurrent myotoxic drugs/supplements; plan how and when to stop therapy if problems arise.

Monitoring plan (risk‑stratified and pragmatic)
- Low risk (no autoimmune history, no statin): baseline CK and symptom‑driven CK checks thereafter. Routine serial CKs in asymptomatic low‑risk patients are of low yield.
- Higher risk (prior autoimmune disease, prior CK elevation, active/remote statin exposure): baseline CK, repeat at 4–6 weeks and again at ~3 months, then symptom‑driven thereafter during the first 6–12 months. Consider earlier testing if GI intolerance or dehydration occurs.
- Trigger thresholds to act: any new proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, dark urine or severe myalgias → stop the GLP‑1 RA and obtain CK, renal function, electrolytes, and urinalysis immediately. Consider urgent specialty referral when CK is markedly elevated (>5–10× ULN, or >1000–2000 U/L depending on lab ULN), or with progressive weakness, bulbar symptoms, or renal impairment. In the reported patient CK rose to ~4,000 U/L with progressive weakness and dysphagia—features that prompted hospital admission and immunotherapy.

Evaluation pathway if muscle injury suspected
- Initial labs: CK, AST/ALT (interpret with CK), creatinine, electrolytes, urine myoglobin, ESR/CRP, TSH. Stop potential offending agents (GLP‑1 RA and consider holding statin).
- If CK elevations persist or weakness progresses despite stopping agent: obtain myositis panel including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP, MRI of affected muscles for edema, EMG as needed, and arrange muscle biopsy. Muscle biopsy is diagnostic for IMNM (necrotic/regenerating fibers, macrophage‑predominant inflammation, membrane‑attack‑complex deposition) and was confirmatory in this case despite negative antibodies.
- Don’t be reassured solely by negative serologies: seronegative IMNM occurs and the biopsy pattern plus clinical course can establish the diagnosis.

Management principles
- If classic rhabdomyolysis (acute very high CK, renal impairment, myoglobinuria): stop drug, hospitalize if severe, aggressive IV fluids, monitor electrolytes and urine output; renal replacement therapy if indicated. If CK falls rapidly after withdrawal and hydration, this supports direct toxic rhabdomyolysis rather than autoimmune disease.
- If IMNM suspected (progressive weakness, bulbar involvement, persistent high CK despite drug cessation, biopsy consistent with IMNM): early immunosuppression—high‑dose IV steroids ± IVIG—is indicated and may produce rapid improvement, as in the reported patient. In many immune‑mediated cases (especially anti‑HMGCR positive) prolonged immunomodulation is required; treatment should be guided by rheumatology/neuro‑myology.
- Rechallenge: avoid rechallenge with the suspected GLP‑1 RA if IMNM is suspected; rechallenge is generally contraindicated after a severe immune‑mediated event. For isolated, reversible rhabdomyolysis that clearly improved with withdrawal, rechallenge could be considered cautiously with close monitoring only after multidisciplinary discussion.

Systems and safety items
- Document counseling and monitoring plan in the chart and communicate with the prescribing clinician (endocrinology/primary care).
- Report suspected serious adverse reactions to local pharmacovigilance registries and regulatory bodies (eg, FDA MedWatch) to improve detection of rare events.
- Engage specialists early for progressive weakness, dysphagia, respiratory compromise, CK in the thousands, or biopsy‑proven IMNM.

Bottom line
Be vigilant rather than alarmed. A pragmatic, risk‑stratified approach—baseline CK/renal tests and clear patient instructions to report muscle weakness or dark urine, with low threshold for CK re‑checking and specialty referral in higher‑risk patients—will detect most serious muscle events early. The present case illustrates that an apparently idiosyncratic immune myopathy can present after GLP‑1 RA exposure, may be seronegative, and can respond to prompt immunotherapy once recognised.
