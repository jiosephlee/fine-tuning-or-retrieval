Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] How should an isolated, low-titer anti–SS-B antibody in the setting of negative ANA and anti–SS-A be interpreted in a patient evaluated for myopathy, and does it meaningfully alter your differential diagnosis or management?

Clinical pearl — bottom line
An isolated, low‑titer anti‑SSB (La) in the setting of a negative ANA and negative anti‑SSA (Ro), and no clinical features of Sjögren’s or systemic autoimmune disease, is most likely an incidental or false‑positive finding and should not by itself change the working diagnosis or acute management of suspected immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

Why:
- Test performance and pretest probability: anti‑SSB usually occurs together with anti‑SSA and with a positive ANA. Isolated anti‑SSB is uncommon and has limited positive predictive value when the clinical context lacks sicca or other connective‑tissue signs. Low titers are more likely to be false positives or clinically irrelevant without supportive findings.
- Disease associations: anti‑SSB is classically associated with Sjögren’s syndrome (and sometimes SLE), not with IMNM. The autoantibodies most relevant to IMNM are anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP; about one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative for known myositis autoantibodies.
- Clinical correlation in this case: the patient had no sicca symptoms, no mucocutaneous, pulmonary, or arthritic features to suggest Sjögren’s or another systemic rheumatologic disease; ANA and anti‑SSA were negative; myositis panel (including HMGCR and SRP) was negative; and muscle biopsy showed necrotising myofibres with macrophage/myophagocytosis consistent with IMNM. The treating team’s diagnosis and management were therefore appropriately driven by the clinical picture and histopathology rather than by the isolated SS‑B result.

Practical implications for diagnosis and management
- Do not alter acute treatment on the basis of the isolated low‑titer anti‑SSB. In this patient, the indication for immunosuppression and IVIG rested on the clinical severity, CK elevation, imaging, and biopsy — and she improved with that approach.
- Document the serologic result and counsel the patient but avoid reflexive, extensive Sjögren workup unless new symptoms arise. If clinical suspicion for Sjögren’s increases (new dry eyes/mouth, parotid enlargement, unexplained cytopenias, complement consumption), pursue targeted testing (Schirmer, salivary function studies, repeat ANA/ENA with a different assay or by immunofluorescence, and consider minor salivary gland biopsy).
- If there is concern that the positive anti‑SSB reflects assay variability, you may repeat testing or use a different laboratory/technique; however, this is a confirmatory step rather than one that should change short‑term management.

Tie‑back to the case
The authors of the report likewise considered the isolated low‑titer SS‑B “unexpected,” found no precedent linking isolated SS‑B to IMNM, and treated based on the clinicopathologic syndrome and suspected drug trigger (semaglutide). Their course — rapid improvement after corticosteroids, IVIG, and drug avoidance — supports the interpretation that the SS‑B result did not represent a causative systemic autoimmune disease altering management.

Teaching take‑away
Always interpret specific autoantibody results in the clinical context. Isolated, low‑titer anti‑SSB without ANA or compatible symptoms is of low clinical significance and should not redirect the diagnosis or immediate therapy of biopsy‑proven IMNM.
