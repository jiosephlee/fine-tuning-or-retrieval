Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Morning Report] In a patient with inflammatory myopathy symptoms who has mild high-sensitivity troponin elevation, markedly elevated proBNP, a normal ejection fraction, and cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement, how do you interpret these findings and what follow-up evaluation is appropriate?

Short answer
These findings most likely reflect mild, reversible myocardial inflammation — early myocarditis or focal inflammatory cardiomyopathy — rather than large myocardial necrosis. Myocardial edema on cardiac MRI with preserved LVEF and no late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) suggests active inflammation without significant irreversible injury; the markedly elevated pro‑BNP indicates myocardial stress but is nonspecific (can be raised by atrial fibrillation, renal dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension). The next steps are targeted confirmation, surveillance for arrhythmia or ischemia, and interval imaging rather than immediate invasive biopsy in the absence of hemodynamic compromise.

Why: pathophysiology and test interpretation
- Inflammatory myopathies (including IMNM) can involve the heart. Immune-mediated injury produces complement activation and myocyte membrane injury, which can cause myocardial edema and small troponin leaks. Muscle biopsy in this case showed complement deposition (membrane attack complex), a mechanism that plausibly affects myocardium as well.
- Cardiac MRI is the noninvasive diagnostic standard for suspected myocarditis. The 2018 Lake Louise update requires a T2‑based marker (edema) plus a T1‑based marker (LGE or abnormal native T1/ECV) to make the CMR diagnosis of myocarditis. Edema alone is suggestive of acute inflammation but not definitive; absence of LGE argues against significant necrosis/scar and predicts a better functional recovery.
- Troponin assays matter: high‑sensitivity troponin T can be spuriously elevated in active skeletal myopathy; troponin I is more myocardium-specific. A small troponin rise (here 62 pg/mL just above the lab cutoff) in the setting of normal EF and only MRI edema may therefore represent true minor myocardial injury or assay cross‑reactivity.
- proBNP (4358 pg/mL) is markedly elevated and indicates myocardial strain but is influenced by age, renal function, atrial fibrillation (this patient had paroxysmal AF), and pulmonary pressures (RVSP 46 mm Hg). Its magnitude supports clinically meaningful myocardial stress but is not diagnostic of myocarditis.

Practical next steps (how I would manage/follow)
1. Confirm the cardiac biomarker: determine which troponin isoform was used and obtain a high‑sensitivity cardiac troponin I (hs‑cTnI) and repeat a few hours to document trend.
2. Continuous ECG monitoring and serial 12‑lead ECGs while inpatient; ambulatory (Holter) monitoring if palpitations or to screen for arrhythmia after discharge.
3. Repeat transthoracic echocardiogram to reassess LVEF, regional wall motion and diastolic function; correlate BNP with volume status and treat if overt heart failure.
4. Coronary evaluation only if clinical or imaging features suggest ischemia (typical ischemic chest pain, rising troponin with ischemic ECG changes, or a coronary distribution WMA). In most cases like this one (MRI edema, no LGE, preserved EF) ischemia is less likely.
5. Cardiology consultation for co‑management and to decide whether endomyocardial biopsy is indicated. Reserve biopsy for fulminant heart failure, refractory ventricular arrhythmias, high‑grade AV block, or when histologic diagnosis would change therapy.
6. Interval imaging: repeat cardiac MRI (with T1/T2 mapping and LGE) in ~3 months (earlier if symptoms or biomarker worsening) to document resolution. Return of normal T2 and no LGE confirms reversible inflammation and favorable prognosis.
7. Activity restriction: avoid vigorous/competitive exercise while myocarditis is suspected — typically restrict for ~3 months and until symptoms, ECG, troponin and imaging normalize.
8. Treat the systemic disease: continue rheumatology‑guided immunosuppression if myocarditis is believed to be immune‑mediated; in this case the patient received corticosteroids/IVIG and subsequently had normalization of imaging and no recurrence of AF, supporting a reversible immune process.

How this applies to the reported patient
The case fits this pattern: small troponin rise, very high proBNP, preserved EF, and CMR edema without LGE — an early/limited myocardial inflammatory process. Her clinical course (improvement after immunosuppression, subsequent normalization of echocardiogram and MRI, no recurrent arrhythmia) is what you would expect when inflammation is present without irreversible myocardial injury. Endomyocardial biopsy was not required and would not have been the first‑line step given clinical stability.

Takeaway pearls
- In inflammatory myopathy patients, preferentially obtain hs‑cTnI (less cross‑reactivity with skeletal muscle) and interpret troponin trends, not single values.  
- CMR edema without LGE = active inflammation with a good chance of recovery; LGE indicates scar and worse prognosis.  
- Reserve invasive biopsy for hemodynamic instability, malignant arrhythmia, or when histology will change management; otherwise monitor with ECG, serial biomarkers, echo and repeat CMR at ~3 months.
