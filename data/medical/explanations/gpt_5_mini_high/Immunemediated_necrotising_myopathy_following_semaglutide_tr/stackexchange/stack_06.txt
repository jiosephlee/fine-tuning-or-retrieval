Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] How would you differentiate hepatic versus muscle sources of transaminase elevation (AST/ALT) in a patient with suspected myopathy and known nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, and which ancillary markers or trends are most informative?

Bottom line: In a patient with known NAFLD who develops a suspected myopathy, the single most useful discriminator of whether AST/ALT elevations come from liver versus muscle is the context and temporal correlation with creatine kinase (CK) — i.e., do the aminotransferases rise and fall in parallel with CK? Complement that with “liver‑specific” tests (GGT, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, albumin/INR) and, when needed, muscle‑specific markers and tissue diagnosis.

Why this works (brief physiology):
- AST is abundant in both hepatocytes and skeletal (and cardiac) muscle (cytosolic and mitochondrial); ALT is more liver‑predominant but is present in muscle to a lesser extent. Thus muscle injury tends to raise AST more than ALT and can produce substantial transaminase elevations that mimic liver disease.
- GGT and alkaline phosphatase are produced by the hepatobiliary system (not muscle) and therefore help confirm hepatic origin when they are elevated.
- CK, aldolase, LDH and myoglobin are muscle‑enriched and rise with muscle injury. Troponin/BNP help detect cardiac contribution.

Practical approach (what to do at the bedside)
1. Check CK immediately when AST/ALT are elevated and there are any muscle complaints or weakness. Also order GGT, ALP, bilirubin, albumin and INR (and consider aldolase/LDH, serum/urine myoglobin, troponin if cardiac symptoms).
2. Compare to prior liver tests. A longstanding ALT>AST pattern (as in NAFLD) points to hepatic origin at baseline; a new change in pattern (AST becoming higher or rising sharply) that coincides with CK rise suggests muscle contribution.
3. Follow trends serially (every 24–72 h while acute). Key discriminator: if AST/ALT fall in parallel with CK → muscle source. If AST/ALT remain elevated after CK normalizes → liver source.
4. Use GGT/ALP to corroborate: isolated transaminase rises with normal GGT/ALP favor muscle; elevation of GGT or cholestatic enzymes favors hepatic injury.
5. If ambiguity persists or chronic liver disease is suspected despite normal CK, obtain liver imaging and consider liver biopsy. If muscle disease is suspected clinically or by MRI/EMG, perform muscle imaging/EMG and consider muscle biopsy (diagnostic for IMNM).

How this applies to the case
- She had a pre‑existing, biopsy‑proven NAFLD with mild ALT>AST elevations years before semaglutide — consistent with a hepatic baseline.
- When she presented with progressive weakness, CK rose to 3,992 U/L and AST/ALT were 191/165 U/L (AST slightly > ALT). The sharp CK rise together with the AST predominance pointed to a large muscle contribution, and muscle MRI/biopsy confirmed immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy.
- After immunosuppression her CK normalized and her weakness resolved, but a borderline transaminase elevation persisted — supporting that the persistent transaminase abnormality was hepatic (NAFLD) rather than ongoing muscle necrosis.

Clinical pearl
- Don’t assume elevated AST/ALT in a patient with muscle symptoms and NAFLD is from the liver. Always check CK and liver‑specific markers and rely on the temporal relationship of aminotransferases to CK; muscle and liver biopsies are confirmatory when needed.
