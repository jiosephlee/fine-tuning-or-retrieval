Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# GLP‑1s and the Myopathy Spectrum: Rhabdo or Autoimmune IMNM?

## The hook — why this case made me pause
GLP‑1 receptor agonists are now everywhere in primary care and the ED. We expect nausea, vomiting and weight loss — not an autoimmune muscle disease. The case by Lam et al. describes a woman in her 60s who got a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide, had an immediate GI/volume‑depletion hospitalization (initial CK 337 U/L), then over the next 4 months developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia and a rising CK that peaked at ~4,000 U/L. MRI showed symmetric proximal muscle edema and the vastus lateralis biopsy showed necrotic/regenerating fibers with macrophage‑predominant inflammation and membrane attack complex deposition — classic for immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). She was seronegative for HMGCR and SRP but improved after pulse steroids + IVIG and has remained well off immunosuppression while avoiding GLP‑1 RAs.

Contrast that with a recently reported tirzepatide case of classic toxic rhabdomyolysis (CK >57,000 U/L) that improved rapidly after stopping the drug and fluids. Same drug class — different mechanisms, different time courses, different treatments. That’s the clinical point: not every CK bump in a patient on a GLP‑1 is the same.

## Quick primer — two ends of the myopathy spectrum
Toxic rhabdomyolysis
- Typical timing: acute — hours to days after exposure, exertion or other insult.
- Presentation: severe myalgias, dark urine/pigmenturia, potential AKI and electrolyte abnormalities.
- CK: often enormous (tens to hundreds of thousands).
- Course: CK falls quickly after stopping offending agent and aggressive IV fluids; immunosuppression not indicated.
- Pathology: widespread necrosis from direct muscle injury; not an immune‑mediated process.

Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)
- Typical timing: subacute — weeks to months of progressive proximal weakness; can include bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, dysphonia).
- Presentation: progressive symmetric proximal weakness, possible respiratory/bulbar involvement; myalgias less prominent than in rhabdo.
- CK: elevated, often in the thousands; can rise over time despite stopping a trigger.
- Autoantibodies: classically anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP in many cases, but ~1/3 can be seronegative.
- Imaging/biopsy: MRI shows symmetric muscle edema; biopsy shows necrotic and regenerating fibers, macrophage‑predominant myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition and relatively few lymphocytes.
- Treatment: immunosuppression (steroids ± steroid‑sparing agents) and often IVIG for severe cases.

## The case in one paragraph
A woman in her 60s with NAFLD and no statin exposure received one dose of semaglutide and rapidly developed nausea/vomiting with hypovolemia and AKI. Initial CK was modest (337 U/L). Over 4 months she developed progressive proximal weakness and bulbar symptoms; CK rose to 3,992 U/L. MRI of the thighs showed proximal muscle edema and biopsy revealed necrotising myopathy with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex deposition consistent with IMNM. Myositis autoantibodies (including HMGCR and SRP) were negative. She received pulse methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone plus IVIG (2 g/kg split over 4 days) with marked clinical and CK improvement and returned to baseline off immunosuppression by 10 months while avoiding GLP‑1 therapy.

## How to tell them apart at the bedside
Think time course and phenotype first. An abrupt severe muscle illness with myalgias, pigmenturia and a massive CK spike hours–days after exposure looks like toxic rhabdomyolysis. Expect rapid improvement after stopping the drug and aggressive fluids. Conversely, a subacute, steadily progressive picture — especially with prominent proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, rising CK despite medication cessation, or MRI muscle edema — should raise concern for IMNM.

Other practical bedside clues:
- Urine: frank myoglobinuria supports rhabdo.
- Systemic features: bulbar symptoms and respiratory failure lean toward inflammatory myopathy.
- CK trajectory: falling rapidly after fluids = toxic myonecrosis; rising or persistently elevated despite withdrawal = possible autoimmune process.
- Autoantibodies: positive HMGCR or SRP supports IMNM but absence does not exclude it (seronegative IMNM ≈ one‑third of patients).
- Biopsy: definitive — macrophage‑predominant necrosis and membrane attack complex deposition point to IMNM rather than classic toxic injury.

## What I do when a patient on a GLP‑1 presents with muscle complaints
First, don't reflexively label every CK elevation as ‘rhabdo’ without the clinical context. Initial ED evaluation should include CK, renal function, electrolytes, ECG, urinalysis (for myoglobin), and basic cultures if febrile. If there is an acute toxic picture (massive CK, myalgias, dark urine, AKI), treat with aggressive IV fluids, correct electrolytes, stop the offending drug and monitor urine output/renal function; admit.

If the presentation is subacute or progressive, or there are bulbar symptoms or CK that keeps climbing after withdrawal, get early neurology/rheumatology input and consider MRI of affected muscles, EMG and — importantly — muscle biopsy. Check troponin/BNP and consider cardiology if there are cardiac biomarkers or symptoms (this patient had elevated proBNP and CMR changes suggesting possible myocardial involvement). For suspected IMNM with severe weakness, dysphagia, respiratory involvement or rapidly progressive course, involve specialists early because treatment with high‑dose steroids and IVIG is often indicated and timing matters.

Avoid re‑challenges with the suspected agent; in this case the authors considered the causality “probable” and re‑exposure would be unethical.

## Why this matters — vigilance and pharmacovigilance
GLP‑1 RAs get excellent outcomes for obesity and diabetes, but as their use explodes we will see more off‑target and idiosyncratic events. Muscle loss is already reported with GLP‑1 therapy, and there are case reports of rhabdomyolysis with agents like tirzepatide. Lam et al.’s case is the first reported IMNM temporally associated with semaglutide; causality is not proven, but the clinical course (subacute worsening, biopsy evidence of IMNM, steroid/IVIG response, durable recovery off drug) supports a drug‑triggered autoimmune process. Be prepared to differentiate toxic myonecrosis from autoimmune myopathy because the treatments diverge sharply.

## Clinical pearls (short, not exhaustive)
A single drug exposure can present as very different muscle‑disease phenotypes — toxic rhabdo versus immune‑mediated myopathy — and the history (hours → days vs weeks → months), symptom pattern (myalgias/pigmenturia vs progressive proximal weakness ± bulbar signs), CK trajectory and response to stopping the drug are your best immediate clues. Muscle MRI and biopsy are diagnostic tools for IMNM; autoantibody panels can help but are not definitive. Early specialist involvement changes management and outcomes.

## Take‑home messages
- GLP‑1 RAs can be associated with muscle disorders on a spectrum from acute toxic rhabdomyolysis to subacute immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).
- Toxic rhabdomyolysis: acute onset, severe myalgias, pigmenturia, very high CK, rapid improvement after stopping the drug and IV fluids; immunosuppression not indicated.
- IMNM: subacute progressive proximal weakness, often bulbar involvement (dysphagia/dysphonia), MRI muscle edema, biopsy showing necrosis with macrophage‑predominant inflammation and membrane attack complex; may be seronegative.
- If CK keeps rising or weakness progresses after stopping a GLP‑1 RA, get early neurology/rheumatology input and consider muscle MRI and biopsy — don’t assume “just rhabdo.”
- Check cardiac biomarkers and consider cardiac imaging when IMNM is suspected (myocardial involvement can occur).
- Avoid re‑challenge with the suspected GLP‑1 agent and report suspected cases for pharmacovigilance.

Bottom line: not every CK bump in a patient on a GLP‑1 is the same — parse the time course and phenotype. Early recognition of IMNM changes therapy (immunosuppression/IVIG) and can be the difference between recovery and prolonged disability.
