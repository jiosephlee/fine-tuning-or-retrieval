Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# When the Myositis Panel Is Negative: Making the Diagnosis in Seronegative IMNM

What do you do when the myositis panel comes back negative but your patient is getting weaker, has dysphagia, and the CK keeps climbing? This is the problem at the heart of a striking recent case: a woman in her 60s who received a single dose of semaglutide, had an early episode of severe GI illness, then over 4 months developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia and a peak CK of ~4,000 U/L. The myositis panel (including anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR) was negative, but a targeted muscle biopsy showed the classic histologic signature of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM): dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibres, macrophage myophagocytosis (CD68+), acid phosphatase activity, and membrane attack complex (MAC/C5b-9) deposition — with only rare T cells. She improved on pulse steroids + IVIG and withdrawal of the suspected trigger, and remained off immunosuppression while avoiding GLP‑1 receptor agonists.

This case reminds us of three blunt truths: (1) up to one-third of IMNM is seronegative, (2) the myositis antibody panel is helpful but not definitive, and (3) timely MRI-guided biopsy + the right stains win the day. Below is a pragmatic, case‑grounded pathway for diagnosing seronegative IMNM and avoiding the common traps that delay treatment.

## When to suspect IMNM even if antibodies are negative
Think IMNM when the clinical pattern fits — don't be reassured by a negative panel.

- Subacute to rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness (difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs), often severe.
- Bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia) or respiratory compromise — red flags for urgent evaluation.
- Marked CK elevation (often >1,000 U/L) that is rising or persistent.
- Few or no inflammatory skin findings (distinguishes from classic dermatomyositis).
- A temporal association with a potential trigger (statin is classic; this case raises the possibility of a GLP‑1 receptor agonist trigger).
- Cardiac symptoms or biomarker abnormalities (troponin, BNP) — IMNM can involve the heart, so low threshold for cardiac evaluation.

In the presented case the constellation of progressive proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms, rising CK and MRI evidence of proximal thigh muscle edema made IMNM much more likely than simple drug-related rhabdomyolysis or deconditioning.

## Labs and imaging — what to order right away
Do these immediately while waiting for antibody panels; treat the results as pieces of the puzzle, not the final answer.

- Basic bloods: CK (repeat), CMP (look for renal involvement), AST/ALT (remember muscle can raise transaminases), ESR/CRP, TSH.
- Autoimmune/myositis panel (include anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP and myositis-specific antibodies) — but don’t stop the workup if negative.
- Cardiac: troponin and BNP if any cardiac concern; echo and cardiac MRI if abnormal.
- MRI of affected muscles with fluid‑sensitive sequences (STIR/T2): key for identifying active muscle edema and selecting the optimal biopsy site. In this case MRI of the thighs showed symmetric proximal vastus lateralis/quadriceps edema — the exact area biopsied.
- EMG can be supportive (myopathic motor units, spontaneous activity) and sometimes helps localize but is not necessary if MRI is diagnostic and a biopsy is planned.

Practical point: MRI-guided selection of a biopsy site improves diagnostic yield — choose a muscle with active edema, not an end-stage fatty replaced muscle.

## The biopsy is often the clincher — what to ask the pathologist to look for
If the antibody panel is negative and clinical suspicion remains high, biopsy is indispensable. Tell your pathologist to evaluate with these stains and features:

- H&E: dispersed necrotic myofibres and regenerating fibres (necrosis ± regeneration is the hallmark).
- Acid phosphatase (lysosomal activity) highlighting myophagocytosis.
- CD68 immunostain: macrophage-rich infiltrates with myophagocytosis.
- C5b-9 (MAC) immunostain: membrane attack complex deposition on necrotic and sometimes non‑necrotic fibers — supports complement-mediated necrosis.
- CD3 (and other lymphocyte markers): typically only rare T cells in IMNM (a paucity of lymphocytes helps distinguish IMNM from polymyositis).
- If available, MHC‑I expression and other ancillary stains can be informative.

The case’s biopsy matched this pattern exactly: necrotic and regenerating fibers, acid phosphatase positivity, CD68 macrophages with myophagocytosis, MAC deposition, and only rare CD3+ cells — a textbook IMNM picture even without serologic markers.

A word about timing: ideally obtain the biopsy before prolonged high‑dose steroids because immunosuppression can alter inflammatory infiltrates. However, IMNM’s necrosis, regeneration and MAC deposition are relatively robust, and if the patient has airway/respiratory compromise or severe bulbar disease you should not delay treatment for a biopsy.

## Distinguish IMNM from rhabdomyolysis and other myopathies
Common confounders:

- Drug-induced rhabdomyolysis is typically acute with massive CK elevations (often tens of thousands), myoglobinuria, and improvement with drug withdrawal and hydration over days to weeks. The tirzepatide rhabdo case followed that pattern; the presented case did not.
- Polymyositis shows prominent endomysial lymphocytic infiltrates; dermatomyositis has characteristic skin findings and perivascular/perimysial pathology.
- Inclusion body myositis is chronic, asymmetric, and distal/proximal mixed — different clinical picture.
- Metabolic, endocrine (hypothyroidism), infectious and toxic causes — keep them in the differential and screen appropriately.

## Treatment — when to pull the trigger
Treatment decisions are individualized but guided by severity.

- Remove/stop any suspected offending agent when reasonable (in this case semaglutide was avoided).
- For severe disease (bulbar/respiratory involvement, rapidly progressive weakness, very high CK with ongoing injury): start high‑dose corticosteroids (e.g., IV methylprednisolone pulses) and consider IVIG early (2 g/kg split over a few days as in this case). This patient received 1 g IV methylprednisolone daily x5 days, then prednisone 1 mg/kg, plus IVIG 2 g/kg and improved substantially.
- In idiopathic or refractory IMNM, add steroid‑sparing agents (methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate, rituximab) and consider maintenance IVIG — but drug‑associated IMNM may respond to a shorter course once the trigger is removed.
- Cardiac involvement should trigger cardiology input and appropriate imaging/management.

Remember: seronegative disease may still behave like classic IMNM and requires the same vigilance.

## Avoiding red herrings — lessons from the case
- Isolated low‑titer SS‑B positivity without ANA or SS‑A and without clinical features of Sjögren’s is likely incidental; don’t let it derail your IMNM workup.
- Transaminase abnormalities can be hepatic or muscular. Correlate AST/ALT with CK, check GGT/ALP, and consider prior liver imaging/biopsy. In this patient, a pre‑existing fatty liver biopsy predated the myopathy, and her modest transaminase abnormalities persisted despite normalization of CK — pointing to hepatic origin for those enzymes.
- A single negative antibody panel does not rule out IMNM — up to ~one‑third of IMNM cases are seronegative. If clinical probability is high, proceed to MRI and biopsy.

## Putting it together — a pragmatic pathway
1. Clinical suspicion (proximal weakness ± bulbar symptoms, rising CK) → order CK, CMP, ESR/CRP, TSH, troponin/BNP as indicated, and send myositis panel.
2. Stop any plausible offending medication when safe to do so.
3. Obtain MRI of symptomatic regions with STIR/T2 to localize active edema.
4. Coordinate muscle biopsy targeted to MRI‑active muscle and request H&E, acid phosphatase, CD68, MAC (C5b‑9) and CD3 stains.
5. If severe features present, start empiric high‑dose steroids ± IVIG — don’t let biopsy timing delay potentially life‑saving therapy.
6. If biopsy confirms IMNM, tailor immunosuppression; if drug‑associated, consider short course and close follow‑up; if idiopathic, plan for longer immunotherapy and monitor for relapse.
7. Screen/monitor for cardiac involvement and perform age‑appropriate malignancy screening as clinically indicated.

## Take‑home messages
- Up to one‑third of IMNM cases are seronegative — a negative myositis panel does not exclude disease.
- MRI is invaluable: use fluid‑sensitive sequences to find active muscle and guide biopsy; avoid sampling fatty‑replaced, end‑stage muscle.
- The biopsy signature of IMNM: necrotic/regenerating fibers, macrophage‑predominant myophagocytosis (CD68+), acid phosphatase activity and membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) deposition, with only scant T‑cells.
- Don’t be distracted by isolated low‑titer autoantibodies (eg, SS‑B alone) or by pre‑existing liver enzyme abnormalities — correlate clinically and with CK/GGT/ultrasound/biopsy.
- If the patient is clinically severe, start therapy (steroids ± IVIG) rather than waiting for perfect proof; still try to obtain a targeted biopsy before prolonged immunosuppression when feasible.

Case wrap: this semaglutide‑linked, seronegative IMNM case shows how combining clinical suspicion, targeted MRI, and the right muscle biopsy stains gets you to a diagnosis and to timely treatment — even when the serology says “nothing to see here.”
