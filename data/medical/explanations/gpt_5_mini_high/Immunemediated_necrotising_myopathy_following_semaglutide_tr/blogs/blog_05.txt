Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Reading the Heart in Myositis: Troponin, BNP, and CMR Without Overcalling Myocarditis

## Why this case matters
A woman in her 60s developed immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) after a single dose of semaglutide. Over four months she went from being independent to severe proximal weakness, dysphagia, and a CK that peaked at ~4,000 U/L. Cardiac workup picked up a modest high‑sensitivity troponin rise (62 pg/mL, assay not specified), a markedly elevated pro‑BNP (4,358 pg/mL), transient paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, and an echocardiogram with preserved LVEF (65–70%) but elevated RVSP (≈46 mm Hg). Cardiac MRI showed focal myocardial edema and mild focal hypokinesis but no definite late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). The patient improved on high‑dose corticosteroids + IVIG; repeat imaging normalized and there was no persistent cardiac dysfunction.

Why is this interesting for clinicians? Because the combination of modest troponin elevation, huge BNP, preserved LVEF, transient arrhythmia and CMR edema is exactly the kind of messy signal that tempts us to declare "myocarditis" — and to escalate to invasive or long‑term cardiac therapies — when a more nuanced interpretation is safer and more useful. This case is a great teaching moment on how to read myocardial biomarkers and imaging in patients with inflammatory myopathy.

## The core problem: biomarkers are noisy in myositis
There are three common pitfalls when you see abnormal cardiac tests in a patient with active skeletal muscle disease.

1) Troponin assays are not identical. Cardiac troponin I (cTnI) is generally more specific to myocardium. Cardiac troponin T (cTnT) assays can be confounded by re‑expression of troponin T isoforms in diseased or regenerating skeletal muscle, producing low‑level positive results that don’t reflect myocardial necrosis. If your lab only reports hs‑cTnT and it’s mildly positive in a myositis patient, get a cTnI if available or move to ECG/echo/CMR correlation rather than reflexively diagnosing myocarditis.

2) CK is muscle‑specific but not heart‑specific. A very high CK shows major skeletal muscle injury (as in IMNM) but tells you nothing about the heart. Conversely, a relatively low CK does not exclude cardiac involvement.

3) BNP/pro‑BNP reflect wall stress, stretch and neurohormonal activation — not myocarditis per se. BNP can be profoundly elevated by renal dysfunction, sepsis, atrial arrhythmia, pulmonary hypertension or volume overload. In this case the BNP was very high despite preserved LVEF; that should trigger differential thinking (RV strain, diastolic dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension, atrial arrhythmia) rather than an automatic diagnosis of systolic myocarditis.

## Practical approach at the bedside
When a patient with inflammatory myopathy presents with dyspnea, chest discomfort, syncope, arrhythmia, or any cardiac biomarker abnormality, think "triage, then integrate" rather than "label and treat."

First steps (within the first few hours): symptom assessment, 12‑lead ECG, bedside cardiac monitoring, renal function. Draw high‑sensitivity troponin — preferably cTnI if available — and a natriuretic peptide. Compare to prior values and look for a dynamic rise/fall pattern. Remember that one mildly elevated troponin with a normal ECG and normal echo in a patient with active skeletal muscle disease is not diagnostic of myocarditis.

Echocardiography is the next non‑invasive test: it tells you systolic function, wall motion abnormalities, pericardial effusion and estimated pulmonary pressures. In our case the echo showed preserved LVEF but an elevated RVSP, a clue to RV or pulmonary vascular strain that could explain part of the BNP elevation.

If there are any of the following — persistent or rising troponin, new significant ECG changes, reduced LVEF, unexplained arrhythmia or hemodynamic instability — escalate to cardiology and consider cardiac MRI (CMR). CMR is the single best non‑invasive test to look for myocardial inflammation, but interpret it using the clinical context and the Lake Louise criteria (see below).

Reserve endomyocardial biopsy for fulminant cases with rapidly progressive heart failure, refractory ventricular arrhythmias, high‑grade conduction block, or suspicion for specific treatable pathologies (giant cell myocarditis, eosinophilic, etc.). It is not needed for every myositis patient who has a troponin bump.

## How to read a CMR in this context
CMR gives two major pieces of information: T2‑weighted/T2 mapping for edema (active inflammation) and T1‑based markers (LGE, T1/ECV mapping) for irreversible injury or fibrosis. The 2018 updated Lake Louise criteria require at least one positive T2 marker (edema) and one positive T1 marker (LGE or abnormal T1/ECV) to meet the conventional CMR definition of myocarditis. If you have edema without T1 abnormalities, the study is suggestive but not diagnostic of myocarditis — it can represent early/reversible inflammation, microvascular injury, or even focal stress cardiomyopathy.

Crucially, absence of LGE is a good prognostic sign. LGE in a nonischemic pattern (midwall/epicardial) implies necrosis/fibrosis and correlates with worse outcomes and arrhythmia risk. Edema alone with no LGE — as in this case — is much more likely to be reversible with treatment of the systemic inflammatory process.

CMR can also help separate myocarditis from ischemia. Ischemic injury gives subendocardial or transmural LGE in a coronary distribution; nonischemic patterns argue for inflammatory etiologies.

## Arrhythmias — how worried should we be?
Arrhythmias are common in inflammatory myopathies and myocarditis, ranging from AF to ventricular tachycardia and conduction block. But context matters. In this patient, paroxysmal AF occurred during an acute systemic illness (nausea, vomiting, renal failure) and did not recur after recovery. That pattern fits AF from systemic stress/inflammation rather than persistent myocardial electrical substrate disease.

Management of arrhythmia follows usual cardiology principles: treat hemodynamic instability urgently, control rate/rhythm as appropriate, and assess stroke risk (CHA2DS2‑VASc) to guide anticoagulation. If arrhythmia persists or is associated with reduced LVEF, recurrent syncope, ventricular ectopy or CMR LGE, involve electrophysiology/cardiology for further evaluation and longer‑term planning.

## When to start immunosuppression for suspected myocardial involvement
This is a nuanced decision. If the patient already has a systemic autoimmune myopathy requiring immunosuppression (as with IMNM), and there is concurrent clinical, biomarker, ECG or imaging evidence suggestive of cardiac inflammation, treating the underlying immune disease is reasonable and often effective for both muscle and myocardial disease. High‑dose steroids ± IVIG is a common initial strategy in severe IMNM with suspected myocardial involvement.

If myocarditis is the primary, isolated cardiac problem, you need to exclude infectious etiologies (especially viral) and discuss immunosuppression with cardiology and infectious disease. There is no one‑size‑fits‑all answer; the severity of cardiac involvement rather than a single troponin number should drive therapy.

In our case the patient received pulse steroids and IVIG for severe IMNM and had clinical and imaging recovery — an outcome consistent with immune‑mediated cardiac inflammation that was reversible.

## Follow‑up and return to activity
Once myocarditis is suspected or documented, restrict strenuous activity and competitive sport for at least 3 months; most guidelines suggest 3–6 months depending on recovery and testing. Follow clinically with serial ECGs, troponins (prefer cTnI), BNP as indicated, and repeat echocardiography. Consider repeat CMR at 3–6 months to document resolution of edema and absence of new LGE if the initial study was abnormal.

Coordinate follow‑up between rheumatology and cardiology. Clearance for progressive return to activity should be individualized and based on symptom resolution, normalization of biomarkers, stable rhythm and imaging improvement.

## The takeaways — clinical pearls
- Mild-to-moderate hs‑troponin elevation in a patient with active skeletal myositis does not automatically equal myocarditis; preferentially measure cTnI when available, because cTnT can be falsely positive with skeletal muscle disease.  
- Interpret biomarkers in context: CK = skeletal muscle injury; BNP = wall stress/strain (and affected by renal function, arrhythmia, pulmonary pressures); troponin dynamics and ECG/echo/CMR are needed to attribute myocardial injury.  
- Use bedside triage (ECG, monitor, cTnI, BNP, echo) first; escalate to CMR if there are persistent or significant abnormalities, hemodynamic compromise, arrhythmia, or reduced LVEF.  
- On CMR, edema without LGE suggests active but likely reversible inflammation; LGE (nonischemic pattern) implies necrosis/scar and carries worse prognosis. Apply the Lake Louise criteria rather than calling myocarditis on edema alone.  
- Treat myocarditis in the context of systemic autoimmune myopathy with immunosuppression when clinically warranted; reserve endomyocardial biopsy for fulminant, refractory, or diagnostically unclear cases.  
- Follow with serial ECGs, biomarkers, echocardiography and consider repeat CMR in 3–6 months; restrict vigorous exercise until cardiology clearance.

This case reminds us that the heart in myositis can look scary on paper — a troponin bump, a sky‑high BNP, and a bit of edema on CMR — but the clinical story, assay selection, imaging pattern (edema without scar), and trajectory matter far more than any single number. Read the whole picture before you label, and partner early with cardiology when the signals are persistent or the patient is unstable.
