Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# From Anecdote to Signal: How to Do Pharmacovigilance for GLP‑1 Adverse Events

One biopsy‑proven immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) after a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide is not proof of causation — but it is the kind of case that should trigger careful thinking, documentation and reporting. This patient in her 60s developed immediate GI toxicity and dehydration with acute kidney injury, then over 4 months progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and dysphonia with CK rising from 337 U/L acutely to a peak of 3,992 U/L. Muscle biopsy showed necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophage myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition consistent with IMNM; myositis serologies (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) were negative. She improved after high‑dose steroids and IVIG and remained well off immunosuppression after stopping semaglutide. That temporal pattern, the biopsy, absence of statin use and clinical recovery make a WHO–UMC “probable/likely” causality classification plausible — and they illustrate exactly how clinicians should move from an anecdote toward a safety signal.

## The case — the essentials you need to remember
A woman in her 60s with obesity and NAFLD received a single subcutaneous semaglutide dose. Within hours she vomited, became dehydrated and was hospitalized with AKI and metabolic acidosis; CK was modestly elevated (≈337 U/L). Over the next 4 months she developed progressive symmetric proximal weakness and bulbar symptoms; outside CK was 3,992 U/L. She received pulse methylprednisolone, then prednisone 1 mg/kg, plus IVIG 2 g/kg. Right vastus lateralis biopsy showed necrotic/regenerating fibres, CD68+ macrophage myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition — histology consistent with IMNM. Myositis panel (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) was negative. She improved clinically and biochemically and was back to baseline 10 months later while off GLP‑1 therapy.

## Why a single case matters (but don’t overcall it)
Case reports are the front line of pharmacovigilance. Single events can reveal unexpected harms — especially when an adverse outcome is biologically plausible, documented objectively (labs, biopsy), temporally related to the drug, and other common causes are absent. But association is not causation: the absence of a pre‑treatment CK and the fact ~30% of IMNM cases are seronegative mean we cannot exclude a pre‑existing smouldering process. Still, this case meets several of the criteria that make a report worth generating and sharing: clear timing, objective pathology, absence of alternative triggers (no statin, no myotoxic supplements), clinical severity and a reasonable response after stopping the drug and instituting immunotherapy.

## Applying the WHO–UMC causality categories — a quick practical walk‑through
The WHO–UMC system gives clinicians a pragmatic way to classify causality: Certain, Probable/Likely, Possible, Unlikely, Conditional/Unclassified and Unassessable/Unclassifiable. The key elements are timing, biological plausibility, alternative explanations, dechallenge/rechallenge, and completeness of information.

Applied to this case:
- Timing: onset of muscle disease followed the semaglutide dose (initial GI/AKI immediately, progressive myopathy over months) — compatible.
- Biological plausibility: GLP‑1RAs have been linked to weight/muscle losses and there are prior reports of GLP‑1/GIP agents associated with rhabdomyolysis; a trigger for autoimmune myopathy is biologically plausible though unproven.
- Alternative explanations: statin‑associated IMNM was excluded (no statin use); other myotoxic supplements were denied; pre‑existing IMNM cannot be completely excluded because no baseline CK was available.
- Dechallenge/rechallenge: the patient improved after stopping the drug and receiving immunosuppression; no rechallenge (rechallenge would be unethical for this severity).
Given these points, the case fits “probable/likely” rather than “certain.” Probable means a reasonable time relationship, unlikely alternative causes, and a clinically reasonable response on withdrawal — exactly what the authors concluded.

## What to document when you suspect a serious GLP‑1 adverse event
If you see a patient like this, be meticulous. In the chart and in any safety report include: patient age/sex/comorbidities; exact drug name (generic and brand), dose, route, date/time of each administration and lot/serial number if available; indication; concomitant medications (especially statins, herbal supplements like red yeast rice, antifungals, antiretrovirals); precise timeline of symptom onset (GI, weakness, bulbar symptoms), hospital admissions and treatments; serial labs (CK, AST/ALT, creatinine, electrolytes, troponin/proBNP), urinalysis/myoglobin if available; imaging (muscle MRI, cardiac MRI, echo); biopsy reports and pathology slides if possible; autoantibody panels and results; immunosuppressive therapy given and timing (steroids, IVIG, other agents) and clinical response; whether drug was discontinued and whether any inadvertent re‑exposure occurred. Attach discharge summaries, lab trend graphs, and pathology reports. Obtain patient consent for de‑identified reporting or publication.

## Where and how to report (practical channels)
Do not assume someone else will report. Channels include national regulatory authorities: in the US use FDA MedWatch; in the EU EudraVigilance; in the UK the Yellow Card scheme; in Canada Health Canada’s system; and internationally your national pharmacovigilance centre reports to WHO VigiBase. Also notify the manufacturer (Novo Nordisk for semaglutide brands). Mark the report as “serious” when relevant (hospitalization, life‑threatening, disability). Provide all attached documents and the precise chronology. If you are in an academic center, consider parallel reporting to institutional pharmacovigilance/med safety teams and pursue a case report or letter to a journal (with patient consent) to increase visibility.

## How to talk to patients without fear‑mongering
Most patients starting GLP‑1 RAs will tolerate them well and benefit from metabolic and weight outcomes. Your counseling should be pragmatic and proportionate. A short script: “This medicine commonly causes nausea and sometimes mild GI upset. Very rarely, there have been reports of serious muscle problems. Watch for new, progressive weakness (especially trouble rising from a chair or climbing stairs), difficulty swallowing, changes in your voice, very dark urine or severe muscle pain. If you get any of those, stop the medication and contact us urgently so we can check a blood test (CK) and arrange evaluation.” Emphasize the rarity of severe events, that most side effects are mild, and that prompt reporting and evaluation mitigates risk.

## CK testing — baseline or symptom‑triggered?
There is no current evidence supporting routine baseline CK measurement for all patients starting GLP‑1 therapy. A pragmatic approach many clinicians use is to obtain baseline CK in patients with risk factors: prior unexplained CK elevations, known myopathy or neuromuscular disease, or concurrent high‑risk medications (notably statins or other myotoxins). For all patients, check CK promptly if they develop muscle symptoms, weakness or dark urine. Clinically concerning thresholds commonly used in practice are CK values in the thousands or >10× upper limit of normal (both trigger urgent evaluation for rhabdomyolysis); persistent very high CK with progressive weakness despite stopping the drug should prompt a myositis/IMNM workup (autoantibodies, MRI, biopsy) and specialty involvement.

## When to stop the GLP‑1, admit and escalate
If a patient develops new progressive symmetric proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, dysphonia), respiratory compromise or very high CK, stop the GLP‑1 immediately. If there is significant weakness, bulbar involvement, AKI or very high CK, admit, check CK, renal function and electrolytes, and consult neurology/rheumatology. If biopsy or clinical criteria fit IMNM, immunotherapy (high‑dose steroids, often with IVIG and/or steroid‑sparing agents) is the accepted approach and may be lifesaving. If the patient is on a statin, stop the statin while workup is ongoing and check anti‑HMGCR antibodies.

## From single case to signal — practical next steps for clinicians and researchers
Clinicians: report the event, collect similar cases, and coordinate with institutional safety offices. Pharmacovigilance teams and researchers: perform disproportionality analyses in VigiBase, FAERS and national databases, and design pharmacoepidemiologic studies (cohort or case‑control) to estimate risk. Manufacturers and regulators: review aggregated reports, consider case definitions and post‑marketing studies. Importantly, until more data exist, weigh the well‑documented benefits of GLP‑1 RAs against rare potential harms, and maintain vigilance.

## Clinical pearls and take‑home points
- A single carefully documented case can start a pharmacovigilance signal — report it (FDA MedWatch, Yellow Card, EudraVigilance or your national system) and notify the manufacturer.
- Use WHO–UMC causality reasoning: time course, plausibility, dechallenge/rechallenge (rechallenge is usually unethical for severe events), and absence of alternative explanations guide “probable” vs “possible.”
- Document drug details (brand, dose, lot), exact timing, serial CK and labs, biopsy/pathology, autoantibodies, treatments and outcomes — attach reports when you file.
- Don’t do routine CK for everyone starting a GLP‑1; consider baseline CK in high‑risk patients and check CK promptly for any new muscle symptoms.
- Stop the GLP‑1 and escalate care (admit, check CK/renal function, involve neurology/rheumatology) for progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, respiratory compromise or very high CK.
- Be balanced when counselling patients: emphasize common benign GI side effects, but give clear, actionable instructions for rare but serious muscle or bulbar symptoms.

If you see anything similar: document precisely, report promptly, and get multidisciplinary help. One well‑constructed report may protect the next patient.
