Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# AST vs ALT vs CK: Sorting Out Transaminase Elevations When Myopathy and NAFLD Coexist

What makes this case useful to clinicians is not only the unusual suspected trigger for an immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) — a single dose of semaglutide followed by progressive weakness and CK in the thousands — but the diagnostic trap it highlights: coexisting NAFLD produced a baseline transaminitis that persisted even after the muscle injury and CK normalized. That combination can easily lead you to the wrong organ of interest if you don’t have a tidy bedside framework.

Below I’ll walk through a practical, clinic- and ED-friendly approach to answering the question clinicians ask every day: “Is this AST/ALT elevation coming from the liver or from muscle — or both?” I’ll use the semaglutide/IMNM case as a running example.

## Quick physiologic recap (the part we keep forgetting)
- AST is abundant in liver, but also in skeletal muscle, heart, red cells and other tissues. ALT is much more concentrated in liver, but there is some ALT in muscle.
- CK is the most muscle-specific routine test — when it’s high, think muscle.
- GGT and alkaline phosphatase are hepatic/cholestatic markers and are not elevated by muscle injury. Bilirubin, INR, albumin and platelets speak to hepatic synthetic function and chronicity.
- Aldolase and LDH are additional muscle enzymes but are less specific. Aldolase may be helpful when CK is deceptively normal and clinical suspicion for myositis is high.
- Timing matters: muscle enzymes rise and fall on a different timeline than chronic liver transaminases.

## The clinical problem in one line
If AST/ALT are up in a patient with weakness, you must decide quickly whether this is primary liver disease, primary muscle disease, or both — because that changes your next tests, imaging, need for biopsy, and urgency of treatment.

## A practical bedside approach (stepwise, in words)
First, the history. Look for temporal clues (when did weakness start vs when transaminases were first abnormal?), medication/supplement exposures (statins, supplements like red yeast rice, new drugs — in this case semaglutide), prodromes (nausea, vomiting, oliguria suggesting volume loss and AKI), systemic features (rash, respiratory symptoms, dysphagia), and prior liver tests. In our case the patient already had mild ALT-predominant transaminitis for years from NAFLD; the profound weakness and dysphagia developed after semaglutide.

Second, order targeted labs early. If weakness or myalgias are present, include CK in your initial panel — always. Add AST/ALT, GGT, ALP, bilirubin, INR, albumin, platelets, TSH, CRP/ESR. If available and needed, add aldolase and LDH. In the case above CK was 337 during the first admission, then rose to 3992 four months later — a dramatic muscle signal.

Third, interpret patterns — don’t rely on a single ratio. Some helpful patterns:
- Big CK elevation (hundreds to thousands) with proportionate AST elevation and a normal GGT leans strongly to muscle. AST will often parallel CK.
- AST > ALT can be seen in muscle injury, but AST/ALT ratios are not definitive (they are also seen in alcoholic liver disease and advanced fibrosis).
- ALT > AST classically suggests hepatic origin (early NAFLD often has ALT > AST). In our patient prior labs showed ALT > AST consistent with NAFLD before the myopathy.
- If ALT/AST remain elevated after CK normalize (or fall), suspect liver origin; if they fall with CK in parallel, muscle was the major contributor.

Fourth, use GGT and cholestatic markers as tie-breakers. An elevated GGT with raised ALT implicates liver. In pure muscle injury GGT is usually normal. That makes GGT a very useful discriminator at the bedside when CK and transaminases are both up.

Fifth, add imaging appropriately. Point-of-care or formal liver ultrasound is quick and informative for steatosis and biliary pathology. Muscle MRI helps localize active myositis if you’re planning biopsy. In this case, femoral MRI showed symmetric proximal muscle edema confirming active myositis and liver ultrasound/Doppler and biopsy confirmed hepatosteatosis.

Sixth, decide on biopsy only when it will change management. Muscle biopsy is often required to diagnose IMNM and to guide immunosuppressive therapy when weakness is progressive and CK is high. Liver biopsy is generally reserved for cases where the cause or stage of liver disease is unclear and would change therapy — e.g., suspected NASH requiring staging, unexplained persistent transaminitis despite negative noninvasive workup, suspected autoimmune or drug-induced liver injury that alters therapy. In this patient there was an older liver biopsy showing NAFLD and a repeat biopsy (performed) showed progression — that supports the liver as a chronic contributor to her transaminitis while muscle disease explained the acute CK rise and weakness.

Seventh, temporal behavior helps clinch it. Direct toxic rhabdomyolysis typically improves quickly after stopping the offending agent and hydration (days to a few weeks for CK). Autoimmune myopathies (IMNM) may need immunosuppression and IVIG to bring CK and strength down. Chronic NAFLD/NASH transaminase elevations persist over months to years and won’t resolve quickly when you treat the muscle.

## Laboratory and diagnostic pearls
- Always check CK when you see unexplained AST elevation or when the patient has weakness. Missing CK is the most common error.
- If CK is normal but clinical suspicion for myositis remains high (progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia), aldolase can be helpful — some myositis patients have a relatively normal CK early.
- Use GGT (and ALP) as a hepatic-specific check: normal GGT with markedly elevated CK + AST suggests muscle.
- Don’t overinterpret AST/ALT ratio. It can mislead. Look at the whole panel and the trend.
- Serologies (HMGCR, SRP) help subtype IMNM but ~30% of IMNM patients are seronegative (as in this case), so biopsy and clinical context remain crucial.
- Check TSH (hypothyroid myopathy), hepatitis serologies, and medication/supplement history to rule out reversible contributors.

## When to call specialists
- Rheumatology/neurology immediately if progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, respiratory compromise, CK in the thousands, or concern for IMNM.
- Hepatology if transaminases remain unexplained after noninvasive testing, or if a liver biopsy may affect management.
- Cardiology if troponin, BNP or cardiac MRI suggest myocardial involvement (this case had elevated hs-troponin and pro-BNP and MRI findings that later normalized).

## Applying this back to the semaglutide/IMNM case
- Background: chronic ALT>AST and biopsy-proven NAFLD years before the event.
- New problem: progressive proximal weakness with CK peaking at ~4,000 U/L and biopsy-proven IMNM; treated with high-dose steroids and IVIG with clinical recovery and CK normalization.
- Key diagnostic point: after CK normalized, the patient still had borderline transaminase elevations — explained by her underlying NAFLD rather than ongoing muscle injury. Muscle biopsy confirmed IMNM; repeat liver biopsy documented steatohepatitis progression.
- Takeaway for this case: if the team had assumed every AST/ALT spike was muscle-derived, they might have missed the chronic liver disease and vice versa. Both organs were relevant — one chronic, one acute — and the trends separated them.

## Bottom line (the bedside algorithm in one paragraph)
When faced with AST/ALT elevation plus weakness, get a CK and GGT early, use history/timeline and simple labs to decide: if CK is markedly elevated and GGT is normal, treat and evaluate for muscle disease (and consider muscle MRI/biopsy). If CK is normal or CK normalizes while transaminases persist, think liver and pursue hepatic workup. Reserve biopsies for when results will change therapy.

## Take-home messages
- Always check CK when you encounter unexplained AST/ALT elevation in a patient with weakness — missing CK is a common diagnostic trap.
- AST is not liver-specific; ALT is more liver-predominant. GGT (and ALP) are useful to point toward hepatic origin.
- Follow trends: if CK and AST fall together, muscle is likely the culprit; if CK normalizes but AST/ALT stay high, liver disease (eg, NAFLD) is likely.
- Aldolase can help when CK is unexpectedly normal but clinical suspicion for myositis stays high.
- Muscle biopsy is the diagnostic test for IMNM; liver biopsy should be reserved for cases where staging or alternative liver diagnoses would change management.
- In patients starting drugs associated with muscle effects, take a careful baseline history and consider baseline CK if clinically indicated — and practice pharmacovigilance when novel agents are suspected contributors.
