Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Treating Drug-Triggered IMNM: Steroids, IVIG, and Short Tapers Done Well

Why this case grabbed my attention: a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide preceded a progressive, biopsy‑proven immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) in a woman in her 60s. She developed bulbar symptoms, a CK that climbed from a few hundred to nearly 4,000 U/L over 4 months, and a muscle biopsy with necrosis, macrophage‑predominant inflammation and membrane attack complex deposition — classic IMNM histology. She received high‑dose IV methylprednisolone, early IVIG, and a short prednisone taper, then regained baseline and remained off immunosuppression for months. The flip side: she developed a pseudomonal urosepsis shortly after discharge — a reminder that even a “short” heavy hit of immunosuppression carries real infection risk.

This case is useful because it crystallises a practical approach to suspected drug‑associated IMNM: when to hit fast and hard with IV steroids, when to add IVIG early (especially for bulbar or severe weakness), how to monitor CK and function and justify a rapid taper after drug withdrawal, and how to mitigate infection risk and counsel patients about rechallenge.

## Case in brief
A female in her 60s with obesity and NAFLD received one dose of semaglutide and within hours developed GI intolerance, hypovolaemia and AKI. Initial CK was 337 U/L. Over the next 4 months she developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and dysphonia; CK peaked at 3,992 U/L. Myositis serologies (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) were negative. Thigh MRI showed proximal muscle oedema. Right vastus lateralis biopsy demonstrated necrotic/regenerating fibres, CD68+ macrophages with myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex staining — findings consistent with IMNM. Treatment: IV methylprednisolone 1,000 mg daily ×5, transition to oral prednisone ~1 mg/kg, and IVIG 2 g/kg divided over 4 days. CK fell and strength recovered; prednisone was rapidly tapered over ~2 months and she was off immunosuppression with normal CK at last follow‑up 10 months after presentation. She was rehospitalised 12 days after initial discharge for pseudomonal urosepsis; CK remained normal during that admission.

## Drug‑induced IMNM vs rhabdomyolysis vs idiopathic IMNM — how to think about it
Drug‑associated muscle injury runs a spectrum. Classic toxic rhabdomyolysis presents acutely with massively elevated CK that improves quickly after stopping the drug and supportive care. Idiopathic IMNM is an autoimmune disease often associated with anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP antibodies, frequently requires prolonged immunosuppression, and tends to be refractory to steroids alone.

Drug‑triggered IMNM occupies a middle ground: a drug exposure precedes progressive weakness and rising CK over weeks to months, biopsy shows necrotic fibres with complement deposition and macrophages, autoantibodies may be absent (up to ~1/3 of IMNM are seronegative), and withdrawal of the agent plus a short course of immunotherapy can produce durable remission. In this case the temporal relationship to semaglutide, the histology, the clinical course and the recovery after withdrawal plus brief immunotherapy fulfil criteria for a probable drug‑associated IMNM.

Clinical pearl: don’t dismiss elevated AST/ALT as hepatic when CK is high — muscle can drive transaminase elevation. In this patient, baseline NAFLD muddied interpretation, but biopsy confirmed hepatic steatosis pre‑dating the myopathy.

## When to pulse methylprednisolone
The main rationale for pulse steroids is rapid control of immune‑mediated muscle necrosis when function or life is threatened. Use pulse IV methylprednisolone (commonly 500–1,000 mg IV daily for 3–5 days) when the presentation is severe or rapidly progressive. Indications that push you toward pulsing include:

- Bulbar dysfunction (dysphagia, aspiration risk) or respiratory muscle involvement.
- Inability to ambulate, sit, or stand because of proximal weakness.
- Rapidly rising CK with clinical deterioration over days–weeks.
- Major organ involvement (suspected myocarditis, rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease).

In the reported case, bulbar symptoms and severe proximal weakness justified 1 g methylprednisolone IV daily ×5 days. Pulse therapy should be followed by high‑dose oral prednisone (commonly ~1 mg/kg) while you reassess response.

Clinical pearl: pulse therapy is a bridge. Reassess early for clinical and CK response and plan next steps (IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents) rather than defaulting to prolonged high‑dose steroids.

## When to add IVIG early
IVIG should be considered early in IMNM when there are poor prognostic features or steroid‑sparing is desirable. Typical scenarios include bulbar weakness, severe generalized weakness with functional loss, failure to improve on steroids alone, or antibody profiles that often respond poorly to steroids (anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP). In practice, IVIG dosing is 2 g/kg total, split over 2–5 days; the team in this case used 2 g/kg divided over 4 days.

Practical cautions: IVIG can worsen or cause renal dysfunction (avoid sucrose‑stabilised products; ensure good hydration and monitor creatinine), cause thromboembolism, aseptic meningitis and hemolysis — use slower infusion rates and monitor closely, especially in older or renally vulnerable patients. When renal impairment is present or recent, consider dividing the dose over more days and monitor urine output and creatinine.

Clinical pearl: in drug‑triggered IMNM where you suspect a reversible trigger, early IVIG can short‑circuit a prolonged steroid course and speed functional recovery — often worth the cost and logistics when bulbar dysfunction or severe weakness is present.

## Monitoring CK and function — when a rapid taper is reasonable
The decision to rapidly taper steroids after initial control rests on two pillars: objective muscle injury markers (CK) and objective/subjective functional improvement.

Suggested practical monitoring after initiation of therapy:
- Inpatient: CK daily while acutely ill until trend is clear, plus regular strength assessments and swallow evaluation if bulbar symptoms.
- Early outpatient: CK weekly (or every 2 weeks) until downtrending and normalised, then monthly for several months.
- Functional measures: bedside MRC grades, timed sit‑to‑stand or timed up‑and‑go, and documented improvement in swallowing (formal speech‑language swallow study if indicated).

When to consider a faster taper: if the offending drug has been stopped, CK normalises or returns near normal, strength and bulbar symptoms show clear objective improvement within 2–4 weeks, and there is no evidence of ongoing organ involvement (cardiac, pulmonary). In that setting a clinician may reasonably wean prednisone over weeks rather than many months. That is what happened here: after IV pulse + IVIG the patient’s CK and function improved, permitting a prednisone taper off over ≈2 months with sustained remission.

Contrast that with idiopathic IMNM or seropositive disease with ongoing activity: those typically need slow tapering combined with steroid‑sparing agents and close surveillance.

Clinical pearl: trends trump single numbers. A falling CK plus reproducible functional gains is the justification you need to shorten steroid exposure. Don’t stop steroids abruptly — taper guided by clinical and CK responses.

## Infection risk — learn from the pseudomonas urosepsis
Heavy immunosuppression, even for a short time, alters infection risk. This patient developed pseudomonal urosepsis 12 days after discharge. Whether immunosuppression, deconditioning, urinary stasis or hospital exposures were dominant contributors we cannot know — but it highlights practical prevention steps.

Before, during and after immunosuppression:
- Screen for and treat occult infections before starting high‑dose therapy (urinalysis if symptoms, chest x‑ray if respiratory symptoms, culture if indicated).
- Avoid indwelling urinary catheters when possible and practise strict catheter care when unavoidable.
- Encourage early mobilisation and attention to bladder emptying in deconditioned patients.
- Monitor for fever, dysuria, cough and other infection signals; have a low threshold for evaluation.
- Consider PJP prophylaxis (TMP‑SMX) when prolonged high‑dose steroids (>20 mg prednisone daily for >4 weeks) are planned or when combining steroid with other immunosuppressants.
- Check vaccination status early (influenza, pneumococcus, shingles) and update before immunosuppression if feasible; remember live vaccines are contraindicated during immunosuppression.
- Monitor glucose while on prednisone and manage hyperglycaemia—hyperglycaemia increases infection risk.

Clinical pearl: in people who are frail or deconditioned, even short steroid courses can tip the balance toward serious infection. Prevention and early detection are key.

## Counseling on rechallenge and class crossovers
The authors judged the semaglutide–IMNM link as “probable” by causality criteria. With an adverse event this severe, do not rechallenge the patient with semaglutide. Given the uncertain risk of class cross‑reactivity, the conservative step is to avoid GLP‑1 receptor agonists until more data exist. Document the reaction prominently in the chart, give the patient an adverse‑drug‑reaction card, and report the event to local pharmacovigilance authorities.

Work with endocrinology to discuss alternative weight‑loss or glucose‑lowering strategies (lifestyle, other non‑GLP‑1 medications, referral for bariatric options if appropriate). Decisions about re‑exposure to other agents in the class should only occur after multidisciplinary risk–benefit discussion and with patient informed consent.

Clinical pearl: avoid re‑exposure; err on the side of class avoidance unless proof of safety emerges.

## When to escalate to steroid‑sparing or rescue therapy
If symptoms recur with tapering, CK rises again, or function fails to recover despite steroids and IVIG, escalate therapy. Options include repeating IVIG cycles (many patients respond to serial IVIG), adding steroid‑sparing agents (methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate), or using biologics for refractory disease (rituximab) or cyclophosphamide for life‑threatening manifestations. Anti‑SRP and anti‑HMGCR IMNM can be refractory and often require combination regimens.

Escalation should be guided by specialists (rheumatology/neuromuscular medicine) and tailored to the patient’s comorbidities, infection risk and organ involvement (cardiac or pulmonary disease may require more aggressive immunosuppression).

Clinical pearl: in drug‑associated IMNM, a second IVIG course plus a short steroid resumption often recaptures remission. Persistent disease beyond a few months is the signal to move to steroid‑sparing agents.

## Practical clinical pearls
- Suspect IMNM when proximal weakness progresses over weeks–months with rising CK after a drug exposure; muscle biopsy is often decisive (necrosis, macrophages, MAC deposition).
- Pulse IV methylprednisolone (500–1,000 mg IV/day for 3–5 days) for life‑ or function‑threatening features (bulbar, respiratory, rapidly progressive weakness, suspected myocarditis).
- Add IVIG early (2 g/kg divided over 2–5 days) for bulbar dysfunction, severe weakness, steroid non‑response, or when you want to limit steroid exposure.
- Monitor CK and objective function closely. If CK normalises and function improves after drug withdrawal + early therapy, a more rapid steroid taper (weeks rather than many months) is reasonable — as in this case where prednisone was tapered off over ~2 months.
- Proactively mitigate infection risk: screen for occult infection, avoid indwelling catheters, consider PJP prophylaxis when prolonged immunosuppression is expected, and update vaccinations before therapy when possible.
- Document and report the adverse reaction; counsel patients to avoid rechallenge with the offending drug and consider class avoidance pending more data.
- If relapse or persistent disease occurs, repeat IVIG and add steroid‑sparing agents per neuromuscular or rheumatology guidance; consider rituximab/cyclophosphamide for refractory or organ‑threatening disease.

## Bottom line
Drug‑triggered IMNM behaves differently from classic toxic rhabdomyolysis and from idiopathic IMNM. When a plausible drug precipitant exists, withdraw it, assess severity, and act quickly: pulse steroids for threatened function, add IVIG early for bulbar or severe weakness, and monitor CK and objective function closely — a brisk clinical and lab response can justify a shorter steroid course. Always weigh the infection risks of immunosuppression and enlist a multidisciplinary team early. If you see progressive weakness and rising CK after a GLP‑1 agonist (or any new drug), pick up the phone to get CK checked and involve neurology/rheumatology early — timely therapy can restore function and limit long immunosuppressive courses.
