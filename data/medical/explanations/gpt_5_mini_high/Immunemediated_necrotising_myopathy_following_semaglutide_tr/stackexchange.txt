Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Differential Diagnosis] After a single exposure to a potentially myotoxic drug, what temporal patterns in symptom progression and creatine kinase (CK) values help distinguish immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) from self-limited toxic rhabdomyolysis?

Short answer: timing and trajectory. Toxic (direct) rhabdomyolysis usually produces an acute, large CK rise within hours–days of exposure that peaks within 24–72 hours and then falls predictably after withdrawal and supportive care (CK half‑life ≈36 hours), with clinical recovery over days–weeks. By contrast, immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) presents subacutely to chronically — progressive proximal weakness and bulbar symptoms over weeks–months after the trigger, with CK that is persistently elevated or progressively rising despite drug cessation and supportive care, and that only improves with immunotherapy.

Why this distinction matters (pathophysiology + practical clues)
- Toxic rhabdomyolysis = direct myocyte injury → immediate cell lysis and a rapid CK storm that abates when the offending exposure stops and muscle injury ceases. Clinically you expect myalgias, dark urine/AKI, a sharp early CK peak and clear downward kinetics over days–weeks.
- IMNM = immune‑mediated myofibre necrosis (macrophage predominant, complement deposition) → a smouldering or progressive process that continues after drug withdrawal. Patients develop progressive symmetric proximal weakness, often bulbar involvement, and CK that fails to fall (or rises) over weeks–months unless immunosuppression is given.

How the case illustrates the point
- This patient had only a modest CK (337 U/L) at the time of her index hospitalization immediately after a single semaglutide dose, then developed progressive weakness and a 12‑fold rise in CK to 3992 U/L over the next 4 months. That delayed, progressive rise in CK and worsening weakness despite stopping the drug is typical for IMNM and not for self‑limited toxic rhabdomyolysis (the tirzepatide report cited in the paper had an acute CK >57,000 with rapid improvement after drug withdrawal). Muscle biopsy in this patient showed necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophages and membrane attack complex deposition — the histologic signature of IMNM — and her CK and weakness improved only after corticosteroids + IVIG.

Practical approach (clinical pearl)
- If CK and symptoms peak acutely and decline within days–a few weeks after stopping the drug → think toxic rhabdomyolysis; manage supportively.
- If weakness is progressive and CK remains elevated or rises beyond 2–4 weeks after exposure, or if there are bulbar signs or MRI/biopsy features of inflammatory necrosis → pursue myositis serologies, MRI and muscle biopsy and treat as IMNM (early immunosuppression/IVIG as indicated).

### [Pathophysiology] Which myositis-specific autoantibodies are most strongly associated with IMNM, what common drug exposure is linked to one of them, and how does a seronegative profile influence diagnostic strategy?

Short answer
- Anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP are the two myositis‑specific autoantibodies most strongly linked to immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).  
- Statin exposure is classically associated with anti‑HMGCR–positive IMNM.  
- A seronegative result (≈30% of IMNM cases) does not exclude IMNM — it shifts the diagnostic emphasis onto the clinical picture, imaging and, above all, muscle biopsy, and it mandates a careful search for alternative triggers (including drugs) and early clinicopathologic decision‑making rather than waiting for a positive antibody test.

Why — brief pathophysiology and application to the case
- Anti‑HMGCR antibodies target HMG‑CoA reductase, an antigen that becomes upregulated in regenerating myofibres (often after statin exposure), and anti‑HMGCR disease is therefore strongly linked to prior statin use and tends to persist despite statin withdrawal, requiring immunotherapy. Anti‑SRP is another specific marker of IMNM and is often associated with a more fulminant phenotype (very high CK, refractory weakness, and more frequent cardiac involvement).  
- The case illustrates these principles: the authors correctly tested for anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP (both negative), and the muscle biopsy provided the diagnostic proof — scattered necrotic/regenerating fibres, prominent CD68+ macrophage myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition — all classic for IMNM. The patient’s clinical course (persistent weakness and high CK despite stopping semaglutide, then improvement with corticosteroids + IVIG and avoidance of the drug) highlights that a drug other than a statin (here, semaglutide is a suspected trigger) can be implicated even when standard IMNM antibodies are absent.

Practical diagnostic strategy when seronegative IMNM is suspected
- Do not dismiss IMNM if the myositis panel is negative. Up to ~1/3 of IMNM patients are seronegative.  
- Take a focused medication/exposure history (statins, immune checkpoint inhibitors, other recent drugs — and now consider newer agents such as GLP‑1 RAs if temporally related). Stop any plausible offending agent.  
- Use MRI to localize inflamed muscle for biopsy. Proceed to early muscle biopsy when antibody tests are negative or clinical severity is high — histology is the decisive test for necrotizing myopathy. Look specifically for necrotic/regenerating fibres with macrophage‑predominant inflammation and complement (MAC) deposition.  
- Consider repeat or extended serologic testing if clinical suspicion persists, and perform standard adjuncts (CK, EMG as needed, malignancy screen where appropriate).  
- Management decisions should be guided by severity and biopsy: drug‑induced/seronegative cases may respond to withdrawal of the trigger plus a shorter course of immunotherapy (as in this patient), whereas antibody‑positive IMNM (especially anti‑SRP) often requires prolonged, multi‑agent immunosuppression.

Clinical pearl
- Anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP are the “red flags” for IMNM and statins are the classic trigger for anti‑HMGCR disease — but a negative antibody panel does not rule out IMNM. In seronegative patients, rely on history, MRI and muscle biopsy and treat promptly when the clinicopathologic picture supports immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy.

### [Pathophysiology] What are the characteristic muscle-biopsy findings of IMNM, and how do these differ from polymyositis and dermatomyositis?

Short answer:
Immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) is defined histologically by prominent myofibre necrosis and regeneration with a macrophage‑predominant inflammatory response, scant lymphocytes, and deposition of the terminal complement complex (membrane attack complex, C5b–9) on the sarcolemma. This pattern reflects an antibody‑ and complement‑driven myocytotoxic process and contrasts with the lymphocyte‑dominated pictures of polymyositis and the microvascular/complement‑mediated perifascicular ischemic pattern of dermatomyositis.

How that looks on biopsy (and in this case)
- IMNM: dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibres, abundant CD68+ macrophages with myophagocytosis, positive acid‑phosphatase activity in macrophage debris, and C5b–9 (MAC) deposition on necrotic and non‑necrotic myofibres. Lymphocytes (CD3+) are usually rare or focal. These were precisely the findings in the patient’s vastus lateralis biopsy (figures in the report show necrotic/regenerating fibres, CD68+ myophagocytosis, MAC positivity, and only rare CD3+ cells), and together with the clinical picture supported IMNM despite negative anti‑SRP and anti‑HMGCR serologies (seronegative IMNM occurs in ~30% of cases).

Key differences from polymyositis and dermatomyositis
- Polymyositis (PM): endomysial mononuclear infiltrates composed predominantly of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells that invade non‑necrotic fibres (true fibre invasion). The histology shows more lymphocyte‑driven cytotoxicity than macrophage‑predominant necrosis. Diffuse, macrophage‑dominated necrosis with minimal lymphocytes favors IMNM over PM.
- Dermatomyositis (DM): perivascular and perifascicular inflammation with prominent perifascicular atrophy (small, atrophic fibres at the edges of fascicles) and capillary loss. Complement activation is prominent but directed at the microvasculature — MAC deposition is typically seen on endomysial capillaries rather than predominantly on myofibre sarcolemma. DM also shows a CD4+/B‑cell‑predominant perivascular infiltrate and an interferon‑type I signature (e.g., MxA expression).

Why this matters clinically
These distinct histologic patterns reflect different immune mechanisms (antibody/complement‑mediated in IMNM, CD8 T‑cell cytotoxic in PM, and complement‑mediated microangiopathy in DM) and have implications for serologic testing, prognostication, and treatment choices. IMNM often requires aggressive immunotherapy (steroids plus steroid‑sparing agents and frequently IVIG)—as in this case, where high‑dose steroids and IVIG plus cessation of the suspected trigger produced clinical and CK improvement—whereas management strategies differ for PM and DM. Muscle biopsy therefore remains the diagnostic standard when clinical and serologic data are inconclusive.

### [Morning Report] In a patient presenting with progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and CK around 4000 U/L four months after a single semaglutide dose, outline an initial diagnostic workup (laboratory tests, imaging, and procedures) to confirm IMNM and exclude mimics.

Short answer
In this clinical setting the diagnostic work-up has four simultaneous goals: (1) document a myopathic process and the IMNM phenotype, (2) obtain IMNM‑specific serology, (3) assess severity (bulbar/respiratory/cardiac) so therapy can’t be delayed, and (4) exclude common mimics (toxic/rhabdomyolysis, endocrine, infectious, paraneoplastic, neuromuscular‑junction disease). The essential initial tests are summarized below and illustrated by what was done in the case (MRI showing proximal muscle edema; negative myositis panel; muscle biopsy with necrosis, CD68+ macrophages and membrane attack complex deposition; cardiac MRI/echo evaluation).

Immediate (same day) — safety and baseline
- Clinical: bedside respiratory testing (FVC, maximal inspiratory pressure/negative inspiratory force) and urgent speech‑language pathology assessment for dysphagia/aspiration risk. (Dysphagia here prompted prompt evaluation and therapy.)
- Basic labs: CK (serial), aldolase, AST/ALT, LDH, CBC, CMP (creatinine, electrolytes, Ca/PO4/Mg) and urinalysis/urine myoglobin to assess for ongoing myoglobinuria and renal risk.
- Cardiac screen: ECG, high‑sensitivity troponin and BNP (the case had a mild troponin rise and very high pro‑BNP prompting echo and cardiac MRI).
- Inflammatory markers: ESR, CRP.
- Thyroid function (TSH) and cortisol if clinically indicated (hypothyroidism can mimic myopathy). (TSH was normal in the case.)

Focused diagnostic testing (24–72 hours)
- Myositis‑specific serologies: anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP (these are most closely linked to IMNM), plus an extended myositis panel and ANA/ENA. Interpretation point: up to ~30% of IMNM patients are seronegative — a negative panel does not exclude IMNM (this patient was seronegative).
- Viral/infectious screening: HIV, hepatitis B and C (both potential triggers and important to know before immunosuppression). If prolonged immunosuppression planned, add IGRA for latent TB and hepatitis B surface antigen/core testing.
- Toxic/medication review: careful medication and supplement history (statins, red‑yeast rice, other myotoxic agents). The patient denied statin use in this report.

Imaging and neurophysiology
- MRI of affected muscles with fluid‑sensitive sequences (STIR/T2): detects symmetric proximal muscle edema and helps choose a biopsy site (the case had thigh MRI showing proximal vastus lateralis edema).
- Electromyography/nerve conduction studies (EMG/NCS): demonstrates a myopathic, often “irritable” pattern with fibrillation potentials; can help exclude motor neuron or neuromuscular‑junction disorders (if MG/LEMS suspected perform repetitive stimulation/single‑fiber EMG and AChR/MuSK antibodies).
- Chest imaging and cancer search: high‑resolution chest CT to look for ILD (if relevant) and consider CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or PET‑CT for occult malignancy in older patients or when paraneoplastic disease is a concern. (The case had HRCT chest and abdominal imaging without malignancy.)
- Cardiac imaging: transthoracic echo ± cardiac MRI when troponin/BNP are abnormal or symptoms/signs suggest myocarditis/cardiomyopathy (cardiac MRI in this patient showed myocardial oedema without late gadolinium enhancement).

Definitive diagnostic procedure
- Muscle biopsy (gold standard): perform an open or targeted percutaneous biopsy of a moderately affected muscle (use MRI to avoid end‑stage fatty replaced muscle). Pathology in IMNM classically shows scattered necrotic and regenerating fibres, macrophage‑predominant myophagocytosis, relative paucity of lymphocytes, and complement (C5b‑9/MAC) deposition on sarcolemma — exactly the pattern seen in this case. Arrange frozen tissue for histochemistry and immunostains (CD68, CD3, MHC‑I, MAC).

Differential considerations to exclude and how to test them
- Acute toxic rhabdomyolysis (drug/toxin): usually abrupt, very high CK that falls quickly with hydration and drug withdrawal; test timeline and urine myoglobin, check renal function.
- Statin‑associated IMNM: medication history + anti‑HMGCR serology.
- Inclusion body myositis: older men, finger flexor/ankle dorsiflexor weakness, insidious course, characteristic biopsy (rimmed vacuoles); CK often only mildly elevated.
- Endocrine or metabolic myopathies: TSH, cortisol, electrolytes, targeted metabolic testing if suggested.
- Infectious myositis: fever, focal findings, blood cultures, targeted serologies.
- Neuromuscular junction disorders (e.g., myasthenia gravis): clinical fatigability, normal CK, AChR/MuSK antibodies and electrodiagnostics.

Practical points and next steps
- Because IMNM can be seronegative, muscle MRI + biopsy are crucial when suspicion is high despite negative antibodies — the case’s biopsy was decisive.
- In patients with severe weakness, bulbar dysfunction or rapidly rising CK, do not wait for biopsy to treat: start high‑dose steroids and consider IVIG (as in this patient), after rapidly completing infection/vaccine screening relevant to immunosuppression.
- Always stop suspected offending agents (semaglutide in this report) and document medication/supplement exposure.
- Coordinate care with neurology, rheumatology, pathology, cardiology and speech/swallow therapy.

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
In older patients with subacute progressive proximal weakness, prominent CK elevation and bulbar signs after medication exposure, order urgent safety labs (CK trend, renal, electrolytes), cardiac screening (troponin, echo/CMR if abnormal), myositis serologies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP), muscle MRI to localize disease, and obtain a targeted muscle biopsy — because antibody negativity does not exclude IMNM and biopsy findings (necrosis with macrophages and MAC deposition) are diagnostic, as they were in this case.

### [Management] For severe seronegative IMNM with bulbar involvement, what is your initial treatment plan (including pulse steroids, oral steroid dosing, and intravenous immunoglobulin), how would you monitor for response, and when would you taper or add a steroid-sparing agent?

Short answer
Severe seronegative IMNM with bulbar involvement should be treated promptly and aggressively: immediate high‑dose IV glucocorticoid pulse, early IVIG, transition to full‑dose oral prednisone, close inpatient monitoring of bulbar and respiratory function, and early consideration of steroid‑sparing therapy if there is no clear clinical/biochemical response within 2–4 weeks or if long‑term high‑dose steroids will be required. The case you presented illustrates this approach — methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily ×5 days → prednisone 1 mg/kg PO and IVIG 2 g/kg total (over 4 days) led to marked CK and clinical improvement by 2 weeks and allowed steroid tapering over ~2 months.

Recommended initial plan (practical)
- Immediate inpatient therapy
  - IV methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily for 3–5 days (the case used 5 days).
  - Give IVIG 2 g/kg total divided over 2–5 days (the case used 2 g/kg over 4 days). In patients with bulbar disease IVIG should be started early because it often produces faster functional benefit than steroids alone.
- Transition to oral glucocorticoid
  - After pulse, convert to prednisone ~1 mg/kg/day (usual cap 60–80 mg/day) with GI prophylaxis, bone protection and counseling about infection risk.

How to monitor for response (what and when)
- Clinical:
  - Daily bedside strength exams (proximal limb strength, MRC sum if available).
  - Formal swallow assessment (speech‑language pathology; consider bedside swallow → FEES if concerns). Protect airway; plan for NGT/PEG if prolonged dysphagia.
  - Respiratory monitoring: serial FVC and inspiratory force (NIF/PImax). Escalate to ICU/consider intubation if FVC drops rapidly or NIF becomes inadequate.
  - Cardiac monitoring if troponin/BNP abnormal or symptoms — ECG, troponin trend, echocardiography (the case had elevated proBNP and a cardiac MRI that suggested edema).
- Laboratory:
  - CK daily while inpatient until falling, then every few days to weekly outpatient until stable in normal range. Expect early fall if therapy effective (case had marked CK fall by 2 weeks).
  - CBC, CMP, glucose monitoring while on high‑dose steroids; check renal function prior to IVIG.
- Infection surveillance: fever, cultures as indicated — the case reminds us infections can complicate immunosuppression.

When to taper steroids
- If you see meaningful clinical improvement and falling CK within 1–2 weeks, begin a planned taper. Practical approach:
  - Rapid down‑titration to a moderate dose over the first 2–4 weeks (for example reduce to ~0.5 mg/kg over several weeks) then slower taper once stable (decrease by 5–10 mg every 1–2 weeks to 20 mg, then more gradual).
  - For suspected drug‑induced IMNM that responds briskly (like this patient), a relatively rapid taper over 6–8 weeks can be reasonable; in idiopathic/refractory IMNM tapering is much slower and often requires steroid‑sparing agents.
- Do not stop steroids abruptly; monitor for relapse clinically and by CK.

When to add a steroid‑sparing agent or escalate
- Add or escalate if any of the following:
  - Inadequate clinical or CK response after 2–4 weeks of combined high‑dose steroid + IVIG.
  - Relapse during steroid taper.
  - Need to limit steroid toxicity because prolonged high‑dose steroids would be required.
  - Life‑threatening bulbar or respiratory disease that is not rapidly responding — consider more aggressive agents earlier.
- Choices and rationale:
  - Methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil or azathioprine are common maintenance agents (take weeks to work).
  - Rituximab (or cyclophosphamide in select severe cases) is favored when rapid steroid‑sparing immunosuppression is needed or in refractory disease; rituximab is often used in anti‑SRP disease but is also used empirically in refractory seronegative disease.
  - Continued monthly IVIG is an option for patients who responded to IVIG but relapse when it is stopped.
- Before starting steroid‑sparing biologics: screen for hepatitis B/C, tuberculosis, and update vaccines as appropriate.

Supportive care and safety measures
- Early swallow precautions; nutritional support (NG/PEG) if aspiration risk.
- VTE prophylaxis, PT/OT for deconditioning.
- PJP prophylaxis (TMP‑SMX) if prednisone >20 mg/day expected for >4 weeks or if additional immunosuppressants are started.
- Bone protection (calcium/vitamin D, consider bisphosphonate if prolonged steroids).
- Monitor for and treat infections promptly (the reported patient developed pseudomonal urosepsis after discharge).

Bottom line (teaching pearl)
Severe IMNM with bulbar involvement is a medical emergency: start high‑dose IV steroids plus early IVIG, monitor swallow and respiratory function closely, and reassess at 1–2 weeks. If there is clear clinical and CK improvement (as in this semaglutide‑associated, seronegative case), you can often taper steroids over weeks to a few months and avoid prolonged multi‑agent immunosuppression. If not, add a steroid‑sparing agent or escalate (rituximab, ongoing IVIG or other agents) within 2–4 weeks to prevent permanent weakness and aspiration complications.

### [Clinical Pearls] How would you differentiate hepatic versus muscle sources of transaminase elevation (AST/ALT) in a patient with suspected myopathy and known nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, and which ancillary markers or trends are most informative?

Bottom line: In a patient with known NAFLD who develops a suspected myopathy, the single most useful discriminator of whether AST/ALT elevations come from liver versus muscle is the context and temporal correlation with creatine kinase (CK) — i.e., do the aminotransferases rise and fall in parallel with CK? Complement that with “liver‑specific” tests (GGT, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, albumin/INR) and, when needed, muscle‑specific markers and tissue diagnosis.

Why this works (brief physiology):
- AST is abundant in both hepatocytes and skeletal (and cardiac) muscle (cytosolic and mitochondrial); ALT is more liver‑predominant but is present in muscle to a lesser extent. Thus muscle injury tends to raise AST more than ALT and can produce substantial transaminase elevations that mimic liver disease.
- GGT and alkaline phosphatase are produced by the hepatobiliary system (not muscle) and therefore help confirm hepatic origin when they are elevated.
- CK, aldolase, LDH and myoglobin are muscle‑enriched and rise with muscle injury. Troponin/BNP help detect cardiac contribution.

Practical approach (what to do at the bedside)
1. Check CK immediately when AST/ALT are elevated and there are any muscle complaints or weakness. Also order GGT, ALP, bilirubin, albumin and INR (and consider aldolase/LDH, serum/urine myoglobin, troponin if cardiac symptoms).
2. Compare to prior liver tests. A longstanding ALT>AST pattern (as in NAFLD) points to hepatic origin at baseline; a new change in pattern (AST becoming higher or rising sharply) that coincides with CK rise suggests muscle contribution.
3. Follow trends serially (every 24–72 h while acute). Key discriminator: if AST/ALT fall in parallel with CK → muscle source. If AST/ALT remain elevated after CK normalizes → liver source.
4. Use GGT/ALP to corroborate: isolated transaminase rises with normal GGT/ALP favor muscle; elevation of GGT or cholestatic enzymes favors hepatic injury.
5. If ambiguity persists or chronic liver disease is suspected despite normal CK, obtain liver imaging and consider liver biopsy. If muscle disease is suspected clinically or by MRI/EMG, perform muscle imaging/EMG and consider muscle biopsy (diagnostic for IMNM).

How this applies to the case
- She had a pre‑existing, biopsy‑proven NAFLD with mild ALT>AST elevations years before semaglutide — consistent with a hepatic baseline.
- When she presented with progressive weakness, CK rose to 3,992 U/L and AST/ALT were 191/165 U/L (AST slightly > ALT). The sharp CK rise together with the AST predominance pointed to a large muscle contribution, and muscle MRI/biopsy confirmed immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy.
- After immunosuppression her CK normalized and her weakness resolved, but a borderline transaminase elevation persisted — supporting that the persistent transaminase abnormality was hepatic (NAFLD) rather than ongoing muscle necrosis.

Clinical pearl
- Don’t assume elevated AST/ALT in a patient with muscle symptoms and NAFLD is from the liver. Always check CK and liver‑specific markers and rely on the temporal relationship of aminotransferases to CK; muscle and liver biopsies are confirmatory when needed.

### [Morning Report] In a patient with inflammatory myopathy symptoms who has mild high-sensitivity troponin elevation, markedly elevated proBNP, a normal ejection fraction, and cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement, how do you interpret these findings and what follow-up evaluation is appropriate?

Short answer
These findings most likely reflect mild, reversible myocardial inflammation — early myocarditis or focal inflammatory cardiomyopathy — rather than large myocardial necrosis. Myocardial edema on cardiac MRI with preserved LVEF and no late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) suggests active inflammation without significant irreversible injury; the markedly elevated pro‑BNP indicates myocardial stress but is nonspecific (can be raised by atrial fibrillation, renal dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension). The next steps are targeted confirmation, surveillance for arrhythmia or ischemia, and interval imaging rather than immediate invasive biopsy in the absence of hemodynamic compromise.

Why: pathophysiology and test interpretation
- Inflammatory myopathies (including IMNM) can involve the heart. Immune-mediated injury produces complement activation and myocyte membrane injury, which can cause myocardial edema and small troponin leaks. Muscle biopsy in this case showed complement deposition (membrane attack complex), a mechanism that plausibly affects myocardium as well.
- Cardiac MRI is the noninvasive diagnostic standard for suspected myocarditis. The 2018 Lake Louise update requires a T2‑based marker (edema) plus a T1‑based marker (LGE or abnormal native T1/ECV) to make the CMR diagnosis of myocarditis. Edema alone is suggestive of acute inflammation but not definitive; absence of LGE argues against significant necrosis/scar and predicts a better functional recovery.
- Troponin assays matter: high‑sensitivity troponin T can be spuriously elevated in active skeletal myopathy; troponin I is more myocardium-specific. A small troponin rise (here 62 pg/mL just above the lab cutoff) in the setting of normal EF and only MRI edema may therefore represent true minor myocardial injury or assay cross‑reactivity.
- proBNP (4358 pg/mL) is markedly elevated and indicates myocardial strain but is influenced by age, renal function, atrial fibrillation (this patient had paroxysmal AF), and pulmonary pressures (RVSP 46 mm Hg). Its magnitude supports clinically meaningful myocardial stress but is not diagnostic of myocarditis.

Practical next steps (how I would manage/follow)
1. Confirm the cardiac biomarker: determine which troponin isoform was used and obtain a high‑sensitivity cardiac troponin I (hs‑cTnI) and repeat a few hours to document trend.
2. Continuous ECG monitoring and serial 12‑lead ECGs while inpatient; ambulatory (Holter) monitoring if palpitations or to screen for arrhythmia after discharge.
3. Repeat transthoracic echocardiogram to reassess LVEF, regional wall motion and diastolic function; correlate BNP with volume status and treat if overt heart failure.
4. Coronary evaluation only if clinical or imaging features suggest ischemia (typical ischemic chest pain, rising troponin with ischemic ECG changes, or a coronary distribution WMA). In most cases like this one (MRI edema, no LGE, preserved EF) ischemia is less likely.
5. Cardiology consultation for co‑management and to decide whether endomyocardial biopsy is indicated. Reserve biopsy for fulminant heart failure, refractory ventricular arrhythmias, high‑grade AV block, or when histologic diagnosis would change therapy.
6. Interval imaging: repeat cardiac MRI (with T1/T2 mapping and LGE) in ~3 months (earlier if symptoms or biomarker worsening) to document resolution. Return of normal T2 and no LGE confirms reversible inflammation and favorable prognosis.
7. Activity restriction: avoid vigorous/competitive exercise while myocarditis is suspected — typically restrict for ~3 months and until symptoms, ECG, troponin and imaging normalize.
8. Treat the systemic disease: continue rheumatology‑guided immunosuppression if myocarditis is believed to be immune‑mediated; in this case the patient received corticosteroids/IVIG and subsequently had normalization of imaging and no recurrence of AF, supporting a reversible immune process.

How this applies to the reported patient
The case fits this pattern: small troponin rise, very high proBNP, preserved EF, and CMR edema without LGE — an early/limited myocardial inflammatory process. Her clinical course (improvement after immunosuppression, subsequent normalization of echocardiogram and MRI, no recurrent arrhythmia) is what you would expect when inflammation is present without irreversible myocardial injury. Endomyocardial biopsy was not required and would not have been the first‑line step given clinical stability.

Takeaway pearls
- In inflammatory myopathy patients, preferentially obtain hs‑cTnI (less cross‑reactivity with skeletal muscle) and interpret troponin trends, not single values.  
- CMR edema without LGE = active inflammation with a good chance of recovery; LGE indicates scar and worse prognosis.  
- Reserve invasive biopsy for hemodynamic instability, malignant arrhythmia, or when histology will change management; otherwise monitor with ECG, serial biomarkers, echo and repeat CMR at ~3 months.

### [Clinical Pearls] Applying the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality criteria, which case features support a “probable/likely” association between semaglutide and IMNM, and why is rechallenge inappropriate?

Answer (clinical pearl): This case meets the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre “probable/likely” category because it combines a plausible time relationship, objective evidence of an immune necrotising myopathy, exclusion of other likely causes, and a clinically convincing recovery after withdrawal and treatment — while a formal rechallenge is neither required nor ethically acceptable.

Why it is “probable/likely” (case features mapped to WHO‑UMC criteria)
- Temporal relationship: the patient had an immediate adverse reaction to a single subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (nausea, vomiting, hypovolaemia, acute renal failure) and then developed progressive proximal weakness with a rising CK over the subsequent 4 months (CK 337 U/L initially, peak 3,992 U/L). The timing is biologically plausible for a drug‑triggered, delayed immune process.
- Objective confirmation of IMNM: muscle biopsy showed necrotic and regenerating myofibres, macrophage (CD68) myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition — classic histopathology for immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy rather than simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.
- Exclusion of competing causes: there was no statin or other known myotoxic exposure, myositis panel was negative for HMGCR and SRP (and other myositis autoantibodies), imaging showed no occult malignancy, and the clinical picture lacked features of an alternative connective‑tissue myositis.
- Positive dechallenge / clinical response: after cessation of semaglutide and treatment with high‑dose corticosteroids and IVIG the patient’s CK fell and her strength and function progressively normalized; she remained off immunosuppression and off GLP‑1 therapy for many months with sustained remission — a clinically meaningful response that supports a drug‑related trigger.
- Limitation preventing “certain”: absence of pre‑drug baseline CK (so a smouldering pre‑existing myopathy cannot be absolutely excluded) and absence of a positive rechallenge. WHO‑UMC requires rechallenge (or incontrovertible evidence) to classify an event as “certain.”

Why rechallenge is inappropriate
- Safety and ethics: deliberate re‑exposure risks provoking a recurrent — and potentially more severe — immune necrotising myopathy with complications already observed in this patient (severe dysphagia, respiratory compromise risk, ICU admission, need for IVIG and high‑dose steroids). Intentionally risking a life‑threatening or disabling recurrence for diagnostic certainty is ethically unjustifiable.
- Not required for clinical management or pharmacovigilance: biopsy‑proven IMNM, the time course, exclusion of likely alternatives, and durable remission after withdrawal and immunotherapy provide sufficient evidence to treat the patient appropriately and to report the suspected adverse reaction. In severe immune adverse events, pharmacovigilance accepts “probable” causality without rechallenge.

Takeaway for clinicians: When a new severe myopathy arises after a drug exposure, seek objective confirmation (CK, MRI, biopsy), exclude common triggers (statins, supplements, malignancy), remove the suspected agent, and treat immune myopathies promptly — but do not attempt rechallenge when the prior reaction was severe.

### [Management] Given the expanding use of GLP-1 receptor agonists, what counseling and monitoring steps would you implement before and after initiation to detect potential muscle adverse effects, especially in patients with prior autoimmune risk factors or statin exposure?

Clinical takeaway (two lines)
GLP‑1 receptor agonists are generally safe, but rare severe muscle injury—ranging from typical drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis to immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)—has been reported. Before prescribing, counsel patients and set up pragmatic, risk‑stratified monitoring so that new myalgias or weakness trigger prompt evaluation; if IMNM is suspected, early specialist evaluation and immunotherapy can be limb‑ and life‑saving (as in the presented case).

Practical pre‑initiation steps
- Focused history: ask about prior autoimmune disease, prior unexplained transaminase or CK elevations, personal/family myopathy, current or prior statin (or other myotoxic agent) exposure, and use of supplements (eg, red yeast rice). In the case report the patient had no statin exposure but had baseline transaminase elevations from NAFLD—important because AST/ALT can be confounded by muscle injury.
- Baseline tests for everyone starting a GLP‑1 RA: CK, creatinine, electrolytes, AST/ALT, and TSH. Document functional baseline (gait, proximal strength) and counsel to report changes.
- For higher‑risk patients (prior autoimmune disease, prior CK elevations, active statin therapy): consider baseline myositis serologies including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP and early involvement of rheumatology/neurology in shared decision‑making. Recognize that up to ~1/3 of IMNM can be seronegative (as in this patient), so a negative panel does not exclude immune myopathy.

Counseling to give the patient
- Symptoms to report urgently: new or progressive muscle weakness (especially proximal), new difficulty swallowing or talking (dysphagia/dysphonia), shortness of breath, dark urine, inability to rise from a chair, or any rapidly worsening myalgias.
- Practical advice: treat GI side effects promptly and maintain hydration—severe vomiting/dehydration can precipitate rhabdomyolysis (this patient had severe GI intolerance and AKI immediately after the first dose).
- Advise avoidance or close review of concurrent myotoxic drugs/supplements; plan how and when to stop therapy if problems arise.

Monitoring plan (risk‑stratified and pragmatic)
- Low risk (no autoimmune history, no statin): baseline CK and symptom‑driven CK checks thereafter. Routine serial CKs in asymptomatic low‑risk patients are of low yield.
- Higher risk (prior autoimmune disease, prior CK elevation, active/remote statin exposure): baseline CK, repeat at 4–6 weeks and again at ~3 months, then symptom‑driven thereafter during the first 6–12 months. Consider earlier testing if GI intolerance or dehydration occurs.
- Trigger thresholds to act: any new proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, dark urine or severe myalgias → stop the GLP‑1 RA and obtain CK, renal function, electrolytes, and urinalysis immediately. Consider urgent specialty referral when CK is markedly elevated (>5–10× ULN, or >1000–2000 U/L depending on lab ULN), or with progressive weakness, bulbar symptoms, or renal impairment. In the reported patient CK rose to ~4,000 U/L with progressive weakness and dysphagia—features that prompted hospital admission and immunotherapy.

Evaluation pathway if muscle injury suspected
- Initial labs: CK, AST/ALT (interpret with CK), creatinine, electrolytes, urine myoglobin, ESR/CRP, TSH. Stop potential offending agents (GLP‑1 RA and consider holding statin).
- If CK elevations persist or weakness progresses despite stopping agent: obtain myositis panel including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP, MRI of affected muscles for edema, EMG as needed, and arrange muscle biopsy. Muscle biopsy is diagnostic for IMNM (necrotic/regenerating fibers, macrophage‑predominant inflammation, membrane‑attack‑complex deposition) and was confirmatory in this case despite negative antibodies.
- Don’t be reassured solely by negative serologies: seronegative IMNM occurs and the biopsy pattern plus clinical course can establish the diagnosis.

Management principles
- If classic rhabdomyolysis (acute very high CK, renal impairment, myoglobinuria): stop drug, hospitalize if severe, aggressive IV fluids, monitor electrolytes and urine output; renal replacement therapy if indicated. If CK falls rapidly after withdrawal and hydration, this supports direct toxic rhabdomyolysis rather than autoimmune disease.
- If IMNM suspected (progressive weakness, bulbar involvement, persistent high CK despite drug cessation, biopsy consistent with IMNM): early immunosuppression—high‑dose IV steroids ± IVIG—is indicated and may produce rapid improvement, as in the reported patient. In many immune‑mediated cases (especially anti‑HMGCR positive) prolonged immunomodulation is required; treatment should be guided by rheumatology/neuro‑myology.
- Rechallenge: avoid rechallenge with the suspected GLP‑1 RA if IMNM is suspected; rechallenge is generally contraindicated after a severe immune‑mediated event. For isolated, reversible rhabdomyolysis that clearly improved with withdrawal, rechallenge could be considered cautiously with close monitoring only after multidisciplinary discussion.

Systems and safety items
- Document counseling and monitoring plan in the chart and communicate with the prescribing clinician (endocrinology/primary care).
- Report suspected serious adverse reactions to local pharmacovigilance registries and regulatory bodies (eg, FDA MedWatch) to improve detection of rare events.
- Engage specialists early for progressive weakness, dysphagia, respiratory compromise, CK in the thousands, or biopsy‑proven IMNM.

Bottom line
Be vigilant rather than alarmed. A pragmatic, risk‑stratified approach—baseline CK/renal tests and clear patient instructions to report muscle weakness or dark urine, with low threshold for CK re‑checking and specialty referral in higher‑risk patients—will detect most serious muscle events early. The present case illustrates that an apparently idiosyncratic immune myopathy can present after GLP‑1 RA exposure, may be seronegative, and can respond to prompt immunotherapy once recognised.

### [Clinical Pearls] How should an isolated, low-titer anti–SS-B antibody in the setting of negative ANA and anti–SS-A be interpreted in a patient evaluated for myopathy, and does it meaningfully alter your differential diagnosis or management?

Clinical pearl — bottom line
An isolated, low‑titer anti‑SSB (La) in the setting of a negative ANA and negative anti‑SSA (Ro), and no clinical features of Sjögren’s or systemic autoimmune disease, is most likely an incidental or false‑positive finding and should not by itself change the working diagnosis or acute management of suspected immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

Why:
- Test performance and pretest probability: anti‑SSB usually occurs together with anti‑SSA and with a positive ANA. Isolated anti‑SSB is uncommon and has limited positive predictive value when the clinical context lacks sicca or other connective‑tissue signs. Low titers are more likely to be false positives or clinically irrelevant without supportive findings.
- Disease associations: anti‑SSB is classically associated with Sjögren’s syndrome (and sometimes SLE), not with IMNM. The autoantibodies most relevant to IMNM are anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP; about one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative for known myositis autoantibodies.
- Clinical correlation in this case: the patient had no sicca symptoms, no mucocutaneous, pulmonary, or arthritic features to suggest Sjögren’s or another systemic rheumatologic disease; ANA and anti‑SSA were negative; myositis panel (including HMGCR and SRP) was negative; and muscle biopsy showed necrotising myofibres with macrophage/myophagocytosis consistent with IMNM. The treating team’s diagnosis and management were therefore appropriately driven by the clinical picture and histopathology rather than by the isolated SS‑B result.

Practical implications for diagnosis and management
- Do not alter acute treatment on the basis of the isolated low‑titer anti‑SSB. In this patient, the indication for immunosuppression and IVIG rested on the clinical severity, CK elevation, imaging, and biopsy — and she improved with that approach.
- Document the serologic result and counsel the patient but avoid reflexive, extensive Sjögren workup unless new symptoms arise. If clinical suspicion for Sjögren’s increases (new dry eyes/mouth, parotid enlargement, unexplained cytopenias, complement consumption), pursue targeted testing (Schirmer, salivary function studies, repeat ANA/ENA with a different assay or by immunofluorescence, and consider minor salivary gland biopsy).
- If there is concern that the positive anti‑SSB reflects assay variability, you may repeat testing or use a different laboratory/technique; however, this is a confirmatory step rather than one that should change short‑term management.

Tie‑back to the case
The authors of the report likewise considered the isolated low‑titer SS‑B “unexpected,” found no precedent linking isolated SS‑B to IMNM, and treated based on the clinicopathologic syndrome and suspected drug trigger (semaglutide). Their course — rapid improvement after corticosteroids, IVIG, and drug avoidance — supports the interpretation that the SS‑B result did not represent a causative systemic autoimmune disease altering management.

Teaching take‑away
Always interpret specific autoantibody results in the clinical context. Isolated, low‑titer anti‑SSB without ANA or compatible symptoms is of low clinical significance and should not redirect the diagnosis or immediate therapy of biopsy‑proven IMNM.
