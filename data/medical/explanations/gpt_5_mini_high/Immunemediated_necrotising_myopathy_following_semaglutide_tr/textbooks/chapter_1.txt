Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 1: ## Case Presentation and Clinical Context

The patient was a woman in her 60s with obesity and biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)/steatohepatitis, treated hypothyroidism with a normal TSH, and no history of statin use or exposure to other supplements classically linked to myotoxicity (for example, red yeast rice). These baseline facts matter because they remove common alternative explanations for a necrotising myopathy: statin exposure is strongly associated with anti-HMGCR immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), and a pre-existing hypothyroid myopathy would typically be accompanied by an abnormal TSH. Her chronic, mild transaminase elevation predating semaglutide was documented on liver biopsy and therefore provides a hepatic source for borderline AST/ALT elevations that later complicated interpretation of transaminases during the acute neuromuscular illness.

Semaglutide was started for metabolic disease with a single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose. Within hours she developed severe nausea and vomiting and was hospitalized 2 days later with hypovolaemia, acute kidney injury, and metabolic acidosis. At that admission her CK was only modestly elevated at 337 U/L. During the same hospitalization she had an episode of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation but normal serum troponin measured at that time. The immediate gastrointestinal intolerance and hypovolaemic insult establish a close temporal relationship to drug exposure but do not by themselves define the later myopathy.

What raised concern for IMNM was the subsequent subacute, progressive clinical course over the next four months while off semaglutide. Rather than improving after drug withdrawal, she developed progressive, symmetric proximal limb weakness with new bulbar features — dysphagia and dysphonia — and loss of postural stability to the point of being unable to sit or stand independently. This pattern (progressive, symmetric proximal weakness with bulbar involvement) is characteristic of severe inflammatory myopathies, including IMNM, and contrasts with the usual course expected for an isolated toxic rhabdomyolysis, which is typically more abrupt, often accompanied by myalgias/myoglobinuria, and tends to improve promptly after removal of the offending agent and supportive care.

By the time of tertiary referral her CK had risen substantially, with a reported peak of 3,992 U/L (1237 U/L at one point during evaluation), and inflammatory markers were only modestly elevated (ESR 22 mm/hr, CRP 22.8 mg/L). Her AST and ALT were increased (191 and 165 U/L, respectively), but these values must be interpreted in the context of known NAFLD and concurrent muscle injury, since AST can be muscle-derived. Cardiac testing documented a more complex picture: an early episode of atrial fibrillation with normal troponin, later a mild high-sensitivity troponin elevation and a markedly elevated pro-BNP (4,358 pg/mL), and cardiac MRI demonstrating focal myocardial edema without clear late gadolinium enhancement. These cardiac findings could reflect transient myocardial involvement (inflammatory or stress-related) but were not definitive for myocarditis; nonetheless, cardiac symptoms and biomarker abnormalities are relevant because inflammatory myopathies can rarely involve the heart and because cardiac disease can complicate diagnostic and management decisions.

On focused neuromuscular examination at the referral center she had marked proximal weakness without cutaneous stigmata of dermatomyositis, synovitis, sicca symptoms, or interstitial lung disease signs. The absence of rash helps narrow the differential away from dermatomyositis toward necrotising and other inflammatory myopathies. Laboratory myositis serology was notable for the absence of myositis-specific antibodies (anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR) and a broadly negative autoimmune panel except for a low-titer isolated SS-B (anti–SSA-B) antibody. This seronegativity does not exclude IMNM: about one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative, and isolated low-titer SS-B without clinical features of Sjögren’s syndrome is of uncertain significance.

Crucially, muscle MRI showing symmetric proximal muscle edema and a thigh muscle biopsy demonstrating dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibres with macrophage-predominant myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition provided histopathologic evidence of an immune-mediated necrotising process. Those biopsy features—prominent necrosis with macrophages and complement deposition rather than dense lymphocytic infiltrates—are typical of IMNM and supported an immune mechanism rather than simple toxic muscle breakdown.

Putting the clinical elements together, several red flags favored IMNM over a direct toxic rhabdomyolysis from semaglutide: the subacute, progressive and symmetric proximal (and bulbar) weakness developing after drug exposure rather than resolving; a marked but not immediately massive CK elevation that rose over weeks to months; lack of cutaneous signs of dermatomyositis or other systemic autoimmune features; muscle MRI and muscle biopsy findings consistent with an immune-mediated necrotising myopathy; and clinical improvement only after immunosuppression and IVIG rather than rapid resolution with hydration and drug cessation. Limitations remain—the absence of a pre‑drug baseline CK means a smouldering pre‑existing myopathy cannot be excluded entirely—but the temporal sequence, evolving phenotype, histology, and therapeutic response together made IMNM the leading diagnosis in this patient and prompted immunosuppressive treatment.
