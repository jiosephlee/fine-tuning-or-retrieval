Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 4: ## Management Strategy, Monitoring, and Clinical Course

Because this patient presented with rapidly progressive proximal weakness accompanied by bulbar dysfunction and very high CK, the treating team elected an aggressive, front‑line strategy aimed at rapidly halting immune‑mediated myofibre necrosis. She received intravenous pulse methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 5 days to achieve prompt immunosuppression, followed by oral prednisone approximately 1 mg/kg daily for maintenance. Given the severity of weakness and bulbar involvement, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was added at 2 g/kg total dose, split over four days; the biopsy demonstration of membrane attack complex on myofibres and prominent macrophage myophagocytosis provided a pathophysiologic rationale for IVIG, since IVIG can blunt complement activation and modulate antibody‑mediated injury.

The immediate management priorities were therefore to stop ongoing immune injury, protect the airway and nutritional status, and limit complications of therapy. Pulse steroids are used to produce rapid immunosuppression and then transitioned to high‑dose oral prednisone to maintain control while allowing time for adjunctive therapies such as IVIG to take effect. IVIG was administered in a divided schedule in this patient to reduce infusion‑related adverse events and renal stress (particularly relevant because she had an earlier episode of acute kidney injury). Typical monitoring when delivering these therapies includes frequent CK and renal function checks, vital sign and infusion‑reaction surveillance during IVIG, and metabolic and glucose monitoring while on high‑dose steroids.

Objective assessment of response combined biochemical and functional measures. In this case the patient’s CK fell markedly over the first two weeks after treatment and remained within the normal range during follow‑up. Clinically she had progressive recovery of proximal strength and resolution of dysphagia and dysphonia, permitting discharge and gradual return to baseline function. Cardiac abnormalities that had prompted additional evaluation (elevated pro‑BNP, borderline troponin, and subtle CMR changes) likewise normalized on subsequent echocardiography and repeat cardiac MRI, supporting global improvement and control of the inflammatory process.

Supportive care was an essential adjunct to immunotherapy. Bulbar weakness requires early swallow assessment and speech‑language pathology input, aspiration precautions, diet modification or temporary enteral feeding if needed, and close nursing supervision until safe swallowing is re‑established. Physical and occupational therapy address deconditioning and help restore activities of daily living. While the report does not describe a feeding tube or prolonged respiratory support, the team’s management plan appropriately encompassed these standard supportive measures given the initial severity of bulbar and proximal weakness.

Management of complications and infection risk is integral to care. The patient developed a pseudomonal urinary tract infection that progressed to septic shock shortly after discharge; importantly, her CK remained normal during that episode, indicating that the myopathy remained controlled. Severe infections while receiving immunosuppression require prompt antimicrobial therapy and reassessment of ongoing immunosuppressive dosing — in practice clinicians often reduce or temporarily hold corticosteroids and other agents during life‑threatening infections while maintaining essential supportive care (decisions individualized to the clinical context). Prevention strategies for longer steroid courses (vaccination, screening for latent infections, and prophylaxis when appropriate) are considered when ongoing immunosuppression is anticipated.

The pace of steroid tapering was informed by the robust clinical and biochemical response. Because she achieved rapid improvement, prednisone was tapered relatively quickly over about two months and ultimately discontinued; at last follow‑up she had been off immunosuppression for many months without relapse. This clinical course—substantial recovery after withdrawal of the suspected offending agent and a brief immunosuppressive course—is more consistent with a drug‑associated process than with the protracted courses often required for idiopathic IMNM. Nonetheless, tapering must be guided by serial CK measurements, repeated strength exams, and vigilance for recurrent bulbar or respiratory symptoms.

A pre‑specified contingency plan is necessary for patients who do not respond or who relapse. Options include re‑treatment with IVIG and introduction of steroid‑sparing agents such as methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, or azathioprine; rituximab is considered for refractory disease or certain antibody‑positive phenotypes. The threshold to escalate is persistent or progressive weakness, failure of CK to fall, dependence on high‑dose steroids, or relapse during taper. Management of refractory cases is best undertaken with neuromuscular and rheumatology input and with close monitoring for treatment toxicities.

Finally, counseling and documentation about the suspected trigger were important parts of care. The patient avoided further GLP‑1 receptor agonist exposure and recovered without additional immunosuppression; given the temporal relationship and severity of the reaction, clinicians should document the drug adverse event clearly in the medical record, inform the patient and other care providers, and consider reporting to pharmacovigilance authorities. If weight‑loss or diabetes therapies are reconsidered in the future, coordination with endocrinology and cautious selection of non‑GLP‑1 options with close follow‑up are prudent.

In summary, this patient’s management combined rapid high‑dose corticosteroids with IVIG because of severe, biopsy‑proven necrotising autoimmune myopathy and bulbar involvement. Objective improvement in CK, strength and swallowing over weeks permitted a relatively rapid steroid taper and durable remission off immunosuppression, while supportive care and prompt management of infectious complications were essential to her recovery.
