Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 3: ## IMNM Pathophysiology and Serology in the Context of GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a clinicopathologic syndrome defined by relatively abrupt to subacute, often severe proximal muscle weakness with marked creatine kinase (CK) elevation and by a characteristic biopsy pattern: widespread myofibre necrosis with regenerating fibres, prominent macrophage‑mediated myophagocytosis, and only sparse lymphocytic infiltrates. Clinically IMNM can involve bulbar muscles (dysphagia, dysphonia) and, in some subtypes, the heart or respiratory muscles. Recognition depends on integrating the phenotype (progressive proximal weakness), supportive imaging/EMG, serology where available, and—most importantly—muscle histology showing necrosis out of proportion to lymphocytes.

Pathologically the disease reflects a humoral and innate‑cell dominated process. Deposition of the membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b‑9) on the sarcolemma of both necrotic and non‑necrotic fibres is a recurrent finding in IMNM and points to complement‑mediated membrane injury as a key effector mechanism. In many cases circulating autoantibodies bind targets on myofibres (or on regenerating fibres that upregulate particular antigens), fix complement and lead to MAC formation; macrophages (CD68+) are then recruited to clear necrotic debris and perform myophagocytosis. Small numbers of T cells and perivascular inflammation may be present, but they are not the dominant histologic feature. The pattern—antibody/complement driven injury with macrophage clearance—helps explain why therapies that target humoral effectors (IVIG, plasma exchange) and broad immunosuppression are effective in many patients.

Serology defines important clinical subgroups. Two well‑established autoantibodies are anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP. Anti‑HMGCR IMNM is strongly linked to prior exposure to statins or statin‑like compounds in many patients; mechanistic models propose that statin therapy increases HMGCR expression in regenerating fibres and thereby sustains an antigenic stimulus that breaks tolerance. Anti‑SRP–positive IMNM tends to present with very high CK, more severe weakness, and a higher risk of cardiac or respiratory involvement. Nevertheless, roughly one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative for these known antibodies; they have the same necrotising biopsy pattern but lack currently available disease‑specific autoantibodies, implying either undiscovered autoantibodies or alternative immune triggers.

Some drugs and exposures are established or suspected triggers for IMNM. The clearest example is statins and statin‑containing products (including red yeast rice or certain mushrooms), which are epidemiologically and mechanistically linked to anti‑HMGCR IMNM. Other medications that dysregulate immune checkpoints or provoke broad immune activation have also been associated with necrotising myopathies in case reports. A key clinical point is that drug‑triggered IMNM often does not remit simply with withdrawal of the offending agent; once an autoimmune loop is established the process usually requires immunomodulatory therapy.

Where GLP‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs) fit into this landscape is currently uncertain. GLP‑1 RAs are well known for gastrointestinal side effects and for causing weight loss that can include loss of lean mass. Case reports have also described isolated episodes of rhabdomyolysis temporally linked to newer agents (for example, tirzepatide), and pharmacovigilance datasets are beginning to capture muscular adverse events. Those reported rhabdomyolysis cases behaved like direct toxic or metabolic injury: large acute CK peaks with relatively prompt recovery after stopping the drug and supportive care. That temporal pattern is different from the progressive, immune‑mediated course of IMNM.

Distinguishing IMNM from toxin‑ or metabolism‑mediated rhabdomyolysis is therefore critical. Rhabdomyolysis is typically an acute process with very high CK, myoglobinuria, and rapid biochemical improvement when the toxin or precipitant is removed and supportive care is provided. IMNM, by contrast, is subacute to chronic, with progressive weakness and CK that can remain elevated or even rise after withdrawal of a putative trigger; muscle biopsy in IMNM shows the MAC deposition and macrophage‑dominant necrosis described above, whereas acute rhabdomyolysis lacks the organized complement deposition and often does not show the regenerating fibres and myophagocytosis pattern that point to an ongoing immune response. Importantly, IMNM characteristically responds to immunosuppression (steroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents), whereas uncomplicated rhabdomyolysis does not require or typically benefit from prolonged immune therapy.

Placed in the case context, the patient’s presentation is archetypal for IMNM despite being seronegative. Her biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibres, acid phosphatase activity, prominent CD68+ macrophage myophagocytosis, and MAC on myofibres—histologic hallmarks of IMNM. She had a subacute progression of weakness with dysphagia over months and a CK trajectory that rose after the single semaglutide exposure rather than resolving rapidly. Anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP testing were negative, placing her among the seronegative group, which accounts for roughly a third of cases and may represent illnesses driven by as‑yet‑unidentified antibodies or alternate immune mechanisms. The rapid clinical and biochemical response to corticosteroids plus IVIG, and the sustained recovery after drug avoidance, further support an immune‑mediated process rather than isolated drug toxicity.

How a GLP‑1 RA could plausibly trigger IMNM remains speculative. Possible mechanisms include: (1) an initial direct or ischemic muscle injury (for example, dehydration/AKI) that promotes regeneration and exposes or upregulates otherwise sequestered antigens, facilitating an autoimmune response; (2) modulation of innate or adaptive immune function by GLP‑1 signalling (GLP‑1 receptors are expressed on some immune cells), altering antigen presentation or macrophage polarization in a way that breaks tolerance; or (3) idiosyncratic hapten‑like modification of muscle proteins leading to neoantigen formation. None of these hypotheses has confirmatory experimental or epidemiologic support for IMNM specifically, and a single case cannot establish causality—this remains an area in need of mechanistic and population‑level study.

For clinicians the practical lessons are: when a patient develops progressive proximal weakness and persistently elevated CK after exposure to any new metabolic drug (including GLP‑1 RAs), consider IMNM in the differential. Early CK measurement, myositis serology that includes anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP, muscle MRI/EMG as available, and prompt muscle biopsy when the diagnosis is uncertain are appropriate. If biopsy and clinical features support IMNM, early immunotherapy (steroids, IVIG and other agents as indicated) should be considered rather than assuming a self‑limited toxic rhabdomyolysis. Finally, unusual or severe muscular adverse events after widely used agents like GLP‑1 RAs should be reported to pharmacovigilance systems so that population signals and possible mechanistic links can be sought.
