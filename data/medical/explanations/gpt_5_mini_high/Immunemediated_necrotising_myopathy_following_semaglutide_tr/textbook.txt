Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Case Presentation and Clinical Context

The patient was a woman in her 60s with obesity and biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)/steatohepatitis, treated hypothyroidism with a normal TSH, and no history of statin use or exposure to other supplements classically linked to myotoxicity (for example, red yeast rice). These baseline facts matter because they remove common alternative explanations for a necrotising myopathy: statin exposure is strongly associated with anti-HMGCR immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), and a pre-existing hypothyroid myopathy would typically be accompanied by an abnormal TSH. Her chronic, mild transaminase elevation predating semaglutide was documented on liver biopsy and therefore provides a hepatic source for borderline AST/ALT elevations that later complicated interpretation of transaminases during the acute neuromuscular illness.

Semaglutide was started for metabolic disease with a single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose. Within hours she developed severe nausea and vomiting and was hospitalized 2 days later with hypovolaemia, acute kidney injury, and metabolic acidosis. At that admission her CK was only modestly elevated at 337 U/L. During the same hospitalization she had an episode of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation but normal serum troponin measured at that time. The immediate gastrointestinal intolerance and hypovolaemic insult establish a close temporal relationship to drug exposure but do not by themselves define the later myopathy.

What raised concern for IMNM was the subsequent subacute, progressive clinical course over the next four months while off semaglutide. Rather than improving after drug withdrawal, she developed progressive, symmetric proximal limb weakness with new bulbar features — dysphagia and dysphonia — and loss of postural stability to the point of being unable to sit or stand independently. This pattern (progressive, symmetric proximal weakness with bulbar involvement) is characteristic of severe inflammatory myopathies, including IMNM, and contrasts with the usual course expected for an isolated toxic rhabdomyolysis, which is typically more abrupt, often accompanied by myalgias/myoglobinuria, and tends to improve promptly after removal of the offending agent and supportive care.

By the time of tertiary referral her CK had risen substantially, with a reported peak of 3,992 U/L (1237 U/L at one point during evaluation), and inflammatory markers were only modestly elevated (ESR 22 mm/hr, CRP 22.8 mg/L). Her AST and ALT were increased (191 and 165 U/L, respectively), but these values must be interpreted in the context of known NAFLD and concurrent muscle injury, since AST can be muscle-derived. Cardiac testing documented a more complex picture: an early episode of atrial fibrillation with normal troponin, later a mild high-sensitivity troponin elevation and a markedly elevated pro-BNP (4,358 pg/mL), and cardiac MRI demonstrating focal myocardial edema without clear late gadolinium enhancement. These cardiac findings could reflect transient myocardial involvement (inflammatory or stress-related) but were not definitive for myocarditis; nonetheless, cardiac symptoms and biomarker abnormalities are relevant because inflammatory myopathies can rarely involve the heart and because cardiac disease can complicate diagnostic and management decisions.

On focused neuromuscular examination at the referral center she had marked proximal weakness without cutaneous stigmata of dermatomyositis, synovitis, sicca symptoms, or interstitial lung disease signs. The absence of rash helps narrow the differential away from dermatomyositis toward necrotising and other inflammatory myopathies. Laboratory myositis serology was notable for the absence of myositis-specific antibodies (anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR) and a broadly negative autoimmune panel except for a low-titer isolated SS-B (anti–SSA-B) antibody. This seronegativity does not exclude IMNM: about one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative, and isolated low-titer SS-B without clinical features of Sjögren’s syndrome is of uncertain significance.

Crucially, muscle MRI showing symmetric proximal muscle edema and a thigh muscle biopsy demonstrating dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibres with macrophage-predominant myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition provided histopathologic evidence of an immune-mediated necrotising process. Those biopsy features—prominent necrosis with macrophages and complement deposition rather than dense lymphocytic infiltrates—are typical of IMNM and supported an immune mechanism rather than simple toxic muscle breakdown.

Putting the clinical elements together, several red flags favored IMNM over a direct toxic rhabdomyolysis from semaglutide: the subacute, progressive and symmetric proximal (and bulbar) weakness developing after drug exposure rather than resolving; a marked but not immediately massive CK elevation that rose over weeks to months; lack of cutaneous signs of dermatomyositis or other systemic autoimmune features; muscle MRI and muscle biopsy findings consistent with an immune-mediated necrotising myopathy; and clinical improvement only after immunosuppression and IVIG rather than rapid resolution with hydration and drug cessation. Limitations remain—the absence of a pre‑drug baseline CK means a smouldering pre‑existing myopathy cannot be excluded entirely—but the temporal sequence, evolving phenotype, histology, and therapeutic response together made IMNM the leading diagnosis in this patient and prompted immunosuppressive treatment.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Workup Interpretation

A patient presenting with subacute, symmetric proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms (dysphagia and dysphonia) and an elevated creatine kinase (CK) requires a focused, stepwise evaluation to separate systemic/endocrine, toxic, infectious, metabolic and primary inflammatory causes. In this case the initial work-up excluded several common systemic contributors: thyroid dysfunction was not present (normal TSH), there was no history of statin or other known myotoxic exposures, and prior viral hepatitis serologies and autoimmune liver tests were negative. The history of a single semaglutide dose immediately preceding gastrointestinal illness raised the possibility of an acute toxic/rhabdomyolytic event, but the later clinical course and ancillary testing were inconsistent with that explanation and shifted the pretest probability toward an inflammatory myopathy.

CK kinetics were an early clue to the underlying process. On the index hospitalisation CK was only mildly elevated (337 U/L) in the setting of acute illness, but over the ensuing four months she developed progressive weakness with a delayed peak CK of 3992 U/L. Acute drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis typically produces a very rapid, often dramatic CK rise that falls within days after discontinuation and supportive care; by contrast, inflammatory myopathies tend to show sustained or progressively rising CK over weeks to months. The delayed, progressive pattern in this patient therefore argued against a single brief toxic insult and toward a subacute autoimmune myopathy.

Muscle MRI provided noninvasive confirmation of active myofibre injury and helped localize biopsy targets. The imaging demonstrated symmetric T2/STIR hyperintensity (edema) of the proximal vastus lateralis and quadriceps with adjacent subcutaneous edema. Symmetric proximal thigh edema is a common imaging pattern in inflammatory myopathies and supports an active inflammatory or immune‑mediated process rather than a focal neuropathy, chronic disuse atrophy, or purely metabolic myopathy. MRI is also useful to guide biopsy toward the most affected region, increasing diagnostic yield.

Definitive tissue diagnosis came from the vastus lateralis muscle biopsy. The salient histologic features were dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibers, macrophage (CD68+) myophagocytosis with acid‑phosphatase positivity, membrane attack complex (MAC/C5b‑9) deposition on necrotic and non‑necrotic fibers, only rare CD3+ T cells, and some perivascular inflammatory cells. This pattern—prominent myonecrosis with macrophage‑predominant inflammation, evidence of complement activation on the sarcolemma, and a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates—is the hallmark morphology of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). It contrasts with polymyositis (abundant endomysial CD8+ T cells invading non‑necrotic fibers) and dermatomyositis (perifascicular atrophy and microvascular complement deposition), neither of which were seen here. The presence of regenerating fibers indicates an ongoing subacute process rather than a single, transient toxic injury.

Serologic testing refines subtyping but is not required for diagnosis when clinical and biopsy features are convincing. The patient’s myositis panel was negative for the two antibodies most strongly linked to IMNM—anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP—as well as for other myositis‑specific antibodies. Approximately one‑third of IMNM cases are seronegative; in these patients the histopathologic picture and clinical phenotype (severe proximal weakness, high CK, possible bulbar involvement) establish the diagnosis. The isolated low‑titer SS‑B positivity in this case lacks a clear mechanistic link to IMNM and did not alter the diagnostic interpretation.

Cardiac involvement was considered because IMNM (particularly anti‑SRP–positive disease) can affect the myocardium. The patient had a mildly elevated high‑sensitivity troponin and markedly raised pro‑BNP, but echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular ejection fraction. Cardiac MRI demonstrated mild regional hypokinesis with myocardial edema but no convincing late gadolinium enhancement. These findings are compatible with early or focal myocarditis or a transient stress‑related cardiomyopathy; the subsequent normalization of cardiac imaging and rhythm findings favored a reversible inflammatory or stress response rather than irreversible structural myocardial disease.

Interpretation of transaminases required distinction between muscle and liver sources. Muscle injury releases AST and—less commonly—ALT, but this patient had a documented history of hepatosteatosis and a prior liver biopsy demonstrating NAFLD before semaglutide exposure. Her pattern of transaminase elevation (ALT greater than AST) preceded the myopathy and persisted after CK normalization, supporting a hepatic origin for the chronic transaminase abnormality rather than ongoing muscle breakdown.

Putting the pieces together narrows the differential. Acute toxic rhabdomyolysis due to semaglutide is unlikely because the CK and strength trajectory was progressive over months rather than rapidly resolving after drug withdrawal. Dermatomyositis and polymyositis were not supported by the absence of cutaneous features and by biopsy morphology. Inclusion body myositis was unlikely given the subacute symmetric proximal pattern, lack of characteristic vacuoles or protein aggregates, and the patient’s clinical response to immunotherapy. Endocrine causes such as hypothyroid myopathy were excluded by normal thyroid testing, and steroid myopathy was implausible because weakness predated steroid exposure. Neuromuscular junction disorders characteristically have normal CK and a fatigable pattern, neither of which fit this presentation.

In synthesis, the combination of a compatible clinical phenotype (subacute marked proximal weakness with bulbar involvement), MRI evidence of active symmetric proximal muscle edema, and a muscle biopsy showing macrophage‑predominant necrosis with sarcolemmal complement deposition supports a diagnosis of seronegative IMNM. The timing after a single semaglutide exposure raises the possibility of a drug‑triggered event, but causation cannot be proved from a single case. Importantly, the biopsy‑confirmed immune pathology provided the rationale for immunosuppressive therapy (high‑dose corticosteroids and IVIG), and the subsequent clinical and biochemical recovery further corroborated an immune‑mediated process rather than an isolated toxic myopathy.

## IMNM Pathophysiology and Serology in the Context of GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a clinicopathologic syndrome defined by relatively abrupt to subacute, often severe proximal muscle weakness with marked creatine kinase (CK) elevation and by a characteristic biopsy pattern: widespread myofibre necrosis with regenerating fibres, prominent macrophage‑mediated myophagocytosis, and only sparse lymphocytic infiltrates. Clinically IMNM can involve bulbar muscles (dysphagia, dysphonia) and, in some subtypes, the heart or respiratory muscles. Recognition depends on integrating the phenotype (progressive proximal weakness), supportive imaging/EMG, serology where available, and—most importantly—muscle histology showing necrosis out of proportion to lymphocytes.

Pathologically the disease reflects a humoral and innate‑cell dominated process. Deposition of the membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b‑9) on the sarcolemma of both necrotic and non‑necrotic fibres is a recurrent finding in IMNM and points to complement‑mediated membrane injury as a key effector mechanism. In many cases circulating autoantibodies bind targets on myofibres (or on regenerating fibres that upregulate particular antigens), fix complement and lead to MAC formation; macrophages (CD68+) are then recruited to clear necrotic debris and perform myophagocytosis. Small numbers of T cells and perivascular inflammation may be present, but they are not the dominant histologic feature. The pattern—antibody/complement driven injury with macrophage clearance—helps explain why therapies that target humoral effectors (IVIG, plasma exchange) and broad immunosuppression are effective in many patients.

Serology defines important clinical subgroups. Two well‑established autoantibodies are anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP. Anti‑HMGCR IMNM is strongly linked to prior exposure to statins or statin‑like compounds in many patients; mechanistic models propose that statin therapy increases HMGCR expression in regenerating fibres and thereby sustains an antigenic stimulus that breaks tolerance. Anti‑SRP–positive IMNM tends to present with very high CK, more severe weakness, and a higher risk of cardiac or respiratory involvement. Nevertheless, roughly one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative for these known antibodies; they have the same necrotising biopsy pattern but lack currently available disease‑specific autoantibodies, implying either undiscovered autoantibodies or alternative immune triggers.

Some drugs and exposures are established or suspected triggers for IMNM. The clearest example is statins and statin‑containing products (including red yeast rice or certain mushrooms), which are epidemiologically and mechanistically linked to anti‑HMGCR IMNM. Other medications that dysregulate immune checkpoints or provoke broad immune activation have also been associated with necrotising myopathies in case reports. A key clinical point is that drug‑triggered IMNM often does not remit simply with withdrawal of the offending agent; once an autoimmune loop is established the process usually requires immunomodulatory therapy.

Where GLP‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs) fit into this landscape is currently uncertain. GLP‑1 RAs are well known for gastrointestinal side effects and for causing weight loss that can include loss of lean mass. Case reports have also described isolated episodes of rhabdomyolysis temporally linked to newer agents (for example, tirzepatide), and pharmacovigilance datasets are beginning to capture muscular adverse events. Those reported rhabdomyolysis cases behaved like direct toxic or metabolic injury: large acute CK peaks with relatively prompt recovery after stopping the drug and supportive care. That temporal pattern is different from the progressive, immune‑mediated course of IMNM.

Distinguishing IMNM from toxin‑ or metabolism‑mediated rhabdomyolysis is therefore critical. Rhabdomyolysis is typically an acute process with very high CK, myoglobinuria, and rapid biochemical improvement when the toxin or precipitant is removed and supportive care is provided. IMNM, by contrast, is subacute to chronic, with progressive weakness and CK that can remain elevated or even rise after withdrawal of a putative trigger; muscle biopsy in IMNM shows the MAC deposition and macrophage‑dominant necrosis described above, whereas acute rhabdomyolysis lacks the organized complement deposition and often does not show the regenerating fibres and myophagocytosis pattern that point to an ongoing immune response. Importantly, IMNM characteristically responds to immunosuppression (steroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents), whereas uncomplicated rhabdomyolysis does not require or typically benefit from prolonged immune therapy.

Placed in the case context, the patient’s presentation is archetypal for IMNM despite being seronegative. Her biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibres, acid phosphatase activity, prominent CD68+ macrophage myophagocytosis, and MAC on myofibres—histologic hallmarks of IMNM. She had a subacute progression of weakness with dysphagia over months and a CK trajectory that rose after the single semaglutide exposure rather than resolving rapidly. Anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP testing were negative, placing her among the seronegative group, which accounts for roughly a third of cases and may represent illnesses driven by as‑yet‑unidentified antibodies or alternate immune mechanisms. The rapid clinical and biochemical response to corticosteroids plus IVIG, and the sustained recovery after drug avoidance, further support an immune‑mediated process rather than isolated drug toxicity.

How a GLP‑1 RA could plausibly trigger IMNM remains speculative. Possible mechanisms include: (1) an initial direct or ischemic muscle injury (for example, dehydration/AKI) that promotes regeneration and exposes or upregulates otherwise sequestered antigens, facilitating an autoimmune response; (2) modulation of innate or adaptive immune function by GLP‑1 signalling (GLP‑1 receptors are expressed on some immune cells), altering antigen presentation or macrophage polarization in a way that breaks tolerance; or (3) idiosyncratic hapten‑like modification of muscle proteins leading to neoantigen formation. None of these hypotheses has confirmatory experimental or epidemiologic support for IMNM specifically, and a single case cannot establish causality—this remains an area in need of mechanistic and population‑level study.

For clinicians the practical lessons are: when a patient develops progressive proximal weakness and persistently elevated CK after exposure to any new metabolic drug (including GLP‑1 RAs), consider IMNM in the differential. Early CK measurement, myositis serology that includes anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP, muscle MRI/EMG as available, and prompt muscle biopsy when the diagnosis is uncertain are appropriate. If biopsy and clinical features support IMNM, early immunotherapy (steroids, IVIG and other agents as indicated) should be considered rather than assuming a self‑limited toxic rhabdomyolysis. Finally, unusual or severe muscular adverse events after widely used agents like GLP‑1 RAs should be reported to pharmacovigilance systems so that population signals and possible mechanistic links can be sought.

## Management Strategy, Monitoring, and Clinical Course

Because this patient presented with rapidly progressive proximal weakness accompanied by bulbar dysfunction and very high CK, the treating team elected an aggressive, front‑line strategy aimed at rapidly halting immune‑mediated myofibre necrosis. She received intravenous pulse methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 5 days to achieve prompt immunosuppression, followed by oral prednisone approximately 1 mg/kg daily for maintenance. Given the severity of weakness and bulbar involvement, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was added at 2 g/kg total dose, split over four days; the biopsy demonstration of membrane attack complex on myofibres and prominent macrophage myophagocytosis provided a pathophysiologic rationale for IVIG, since IVIG can blunt complement activation and modulate antibody‑mediated injury.

The immediate management priorities were therefore to stop ongoing immune injury, protect the airway and nutritional status, and limit complications of therapy. Pulse steroids are used to produce rapid immunosuppression and then transitioned to high‑dose oral prednisone to maintain control while allowing time for adjunctive therapies such as IVIG to take effect. IVIG was administered in a divided schedule in this patient to reduce infusion‑related adverse events and renal stress (particularly relevant because she had an earlier episode of acute kidney injury). Typical monitoring when delivering these therapies includes frequent CK and renal function checks, vital sign and infusion‑reaction surveillance during IVIG, and metabolic and glucose monitoring while on high‑dose steroids.

Objective assessment of response combined biochemical and functional measures. In this case the patient’s CK fell markedly over the first two weeks after treatment and remained within the normal range during follow‑up. Clinically she had progressive recovery of proximal strength and resolution of dysphagia and dysphonia, permitting discharge and gradual return to baseline function. Cardiac abnormalities that had prompted additional evaluation (elevated pro‑BNP, borderline troponin, and subtle CMR changes) likewise normalized on subsequent echocardiography and repeat cardiac MRI, supporting global improvement and control of the inflammatory process.

Supportive care was an essential adjunct to immunotherapy. Bulbar weakness requires early swallow assessment and speech‑language pathology input, aspiration precautions, diet modification or temporary enteral feeding if needed, and close nursing supervision until safe swallowing is re‑established. Physical and occupational therapy address deconditioning and help restore activities of daily living. While the report does not describe a feeding tube or prolonged respiratory support, the team’s management plan appropriately encompassed these standard supportive measures given the initial severity of bulbar and proximal weakness.

Management of complications and infection risk is integral to care. The patient developed a pseudomonal urinary tract infection that progressed to septic shock shortly after discharge; importantly, her CK remained normal during that episode, indicating that the myopathy remained controlled. Severe infections while receiving immunosuppression require prompt antimicrobial therapy and reassessment of ongoing immunosuppressive dosing — in practice clinicians often reduce or temporarily hold corticosteroids and other agents during life‑threatening infections while maintaining essential supportive care (decisions individualized to the clinical context). Prevention strategies for longer steroid courses (vaccination, screening for latent infections, and prophylaxis when appropriate) are considered when ongoing immunosuppression is anticipated.

The pace of steroid tapering was informed by the robust clinical and biochemical response. Because she achieved rapid improvement, prednisone was tapered relatively quickly over about two months and ultimately discontinued; at last follow‑up she had been off immunosuppression for many months without relapse. This clinical course—substantial recovery after withdrawal of the suspected offending agent and a brief immunosuppressive course—is more consistent with a drug‑associated process than with the protracted courses often required for idiopathic IMNM. Nonetheless, tapering must be guided by serial CK measurements, repeated strength exams, and vigilance for recurrent bulbar or respiratory symptoms.

A pre‑specified contingency plan is necessary for patients who do not respond or who relapse. Options include re‑treatment with IVIG and introduction of steroid‑sparing agents such as methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, or azathioprine; rituximab is considered for refractory disease or certain antibody‑positive phenotypes. The threshold to escalate is persistent or progressive weakness, failure of CK to fall, dependence on high‑dose steroids, or relapse during taper. Management of refractory cases is best undertaken with neuromuscular and rheumatology input and with close monitoring for treatment toxicities.

Finally, counseling and documentation about the suspected trigger were important parts of care. The patient avoided further GLP‑1 receptor agonist exposure and recovered without additional immunosuppression; given the temporal relationship and severity of the reaction, clinicians should document the drug adverse event clearly in the medical record, inform the patient and other care providers, and consider reporting to pharmacovigilance authorities. If weight‑loss or diabetes therapies are reconsidered in the future, coordination with endocrinology and cautious selection of non‑GLP‑1 options with close follow‑up are prudent.

In summary, this patient’s management combined rapid high‑dose corticosteroids with IVIG because of severe, biopsy‑proven necrotising autoimmune myopathy and bulbar involvement. Objective improvement in CK, strength and swallowing over weeks permitted a relatively rapid steroid taper and durable remission off immunosuppression, while supportive care and prompt management of infectious complications were essential to her recovery.

## Clinical Implications, Causality Assessment, and Practice Points

This case raises an important clinical question: did semaglutide contribute to immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) in this patient, and if so how should clinicians respond? Applying a structured causality approach clarifies the strength of the association and helps translate the case into practical steps for evaluation, management and pharmacovigilance as GLP‑1 receptor agonist (GLP‑1 RA) use expands.

Causality assessment — WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre framework
Using the WHO‑UMC criteria, this case most closely meets the “probable/likely” category. A plausible temporal relationship exists: the patient had an acute systemic reaction within hours of a single subcutaneous semaglutide dose and then developed progressive proximal and bulbar weakness with a marked CK rise (from 337 U/L at initial hospitalization to 3992 U/L four months later). There was objective improvement after stopping semaglutide and receiving immunotherapy (positive dechallenge), and alternative explanations were judged unlikely (no statin exposure, negative myositis serology including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP, and liver biopsy confirming pre‑existing NAFLD rather than ongoing muscle breakdown). The association is not “certain” because there was no rechallenge (and rechallenge would be unethical) and a pre‑drug baseline CK was unavailable, so a smouldering pre‑existing myopathy cannot be absolutely excluded.

Why this presentation favors IMNM rather than classic drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis
Classic toxic rhabdomyolysis is typically acute, produces very large CK spikes (often tens of thousands), may be accompanied by myoglobinuria and acute kidney injury, and usually improves rapidly with drug withdrawal, hydration and supportive care. By contrast, IMNM is immune‑mediated and often follows a subacute course with progressive proximal weakness, CK levels commonly in the thousands, and histology showing necrotic/regenerating fibres with macrophage‑predominant inflammation and complement (membrane attack complex) deposition. In this patient the initial CK was modest during the episode of hypovolaemic illness, but over 4 months she developed progressive weakness with CK rising to nearly 4,000 U/L and biopsy features diagnostic of IMNM; she required corticosteroids and IVIG for recovery. The contrasting tirzepatide case of acute rhabdomyolysis (very high CK that rapidly normalized after stopping the drug) illustrates the different clinical trajectories.

When to suspect IMNM in a patient on a GLP‑1 RA
Suspect IMNM when a patient develops subacute, progressive proximal limb weakness, especially when accompanied by bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, dysphonia), and CK values are markedly elevated (often in the thousands). Supportive findings include myopathic changes or muscle oedema on MRI and a muscle biopsy showing scattered necrotic and regenerating fibres with macrophage‑dominant inflammation, frequent myophagocytosis and complement deposition; lymphocytic infiltrates are typically sparse. Although anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP antibodies are strongly associated with IMNM, up to one‑third of cases are seronegative—so a negative myositis panel does not rule out IMNM, and tissue diagnosis may be required as it was in this case.

Practical evaluation and management approach for GLP‑1 RA users with new weakness
When a patient on a GLP‑1 RA reports new myalgias, weakness, or bulbar symptoms, pause the medication and obtain a focused history (onset, distribution, myoglobinuria, concurrent statin or supplement use, recent exercise, thyroid disease, alcohol) and measurements of CK, basic metabolic panel, AST/ALT and urinalysis. If CK is markedly elevated or weakness is progressive or bulbar/respiratory signs are present, obtain early specialist input (neurology/rheumatology), consider muscle MRI to localize disease and plan biopsy, and arrange timely muscle biopsy and myositis serology. In severe presentations (rapidly progressive weakness, respiratory compromise, severe dysphagia, or very high CK), empiric immunotherapy (for example, pulse corticosteroids with consideration of IVIG) is reasonable while diagnostic workup proceeds because early treatment can be decisive; in milder, static cases close observation with serial CK and strength testing may be appropriate. If IMNM is suspected, avoid rechallenge with the suspected agent; in this report the patient recovered after drug cessation and short immunotherapy and was not re‑exposed.

Interpreting transaminases in the setting of myopathy
AST is present in both liver and muscle, so elevations in AST (and to a lesser extent ALT) can reflect muscle injury when CK is elevated. Correlate transaminases with CK: if AST/ALT fall as CK normalizes, a muscle source is likely; if transaminases remain elevated after CK returns to normal, hepatic pathology should be considered. In this patient, a prior liver biopsy documented NAFLD and AST/ALT remained borderline elevated after CK normalized, consistent with hepatic origin rather than ongoing muscle breakdown.

Monitoring, patient education and preventive considerations
Routine baseline CK testing before starting GLP‑1 RAs is not established as standard practice, but clinicians should review baseline neuromuscular symptoms and counsel patients to report new muscle weakness, severe myalgias, dark urine, difficulty swallowing, or breathing. For patients with additional risk factors for myotoxicity (concurrent statin therapy, known myopathy, or prior unexplained CK elevation), a lower threshold for baseline CK and early monitoring is reasonable. Early recognition and prompt CK testing are the key steps to detecting serious myopathies early.

Reporting, research and wider implications
Given the widespread and growing use of GLP‑1 RAs, single‑case observations like this should prompt careful pharmacovigilance rather than definitive causal claims. Clinicians should report suspected serious adverse reactions to regulatory authorities (for example, FDA MedWatch or equivalent national systems) and consider contributing de‑identified clinical, serologic and histopathologic data to specialty registries or research networks. Systematic case collection will be necessary to estimate incidence, identify predisposing factors, and investigate mechanisms.

Key clinical takeaways
This case illustrates that a temporal association between semaglutide exposure and IMNM is plausible and meets criteria for a probable drug‑related event by WHO‑UMC standards, but causality is not proven. IMNM can be seronegative and requires biopsy for definitive diagnosis; early specialist involvement and prompt immunotherapy (when clinically indicated) can produce rapid and durable recovery. Clinicians prescribing GLP‑1 RAs should be alert to red flags (progressive proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms, very high CK), pause the drug if significant myopathy is suspected, obtain CK and appropriate workup, involve neuromuscular specialists early, and report confirmed or suspected cases to pharmacovigilance systems.
