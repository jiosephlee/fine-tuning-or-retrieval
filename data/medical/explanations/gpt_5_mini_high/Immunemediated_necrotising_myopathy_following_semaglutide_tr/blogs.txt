Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# GLP‑1s and the Myopathy Spectrum: Rhabdo or Autoimmune IMNM?

## The hook — why this case made me pause
GLP‑1 receptor agonists are now everywhere in primary care and the ED. We expect nausea, vomiting and weight loss — not an autoimmune muscle disease. The case by Lam et al. describes a woman in her 60s who got a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide, had an immediate GI/volume‑depletion hospitalization (initial CK 337 U/L), then over the next 4 months developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia and a rising CK that peaked at ~4,000 U/L. MRI showed symmetric proximal muscle edema and the vastus lateralis biopsy showed necrotic/regenerating fibers with macrophage‑predominant inflammation and membrane attack complex deposition — classic for immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). She was seronegative for HMGCR and SRP but improved after pulse steroids + IVIG and has remained well off immunosuppression while avoiding GLP‑1 RAs.

Contrast that with a recently reported tirzepatide case of classic toxic rhabdomyolysis (CK >57,000 U/L) that improved rapidly after stopping the drug and fluids. Same drug class — different mechanisms, different time courses, different treatments. That’s the clinical point: not every CK bump in a patient on a GLP‑1 is the same.

## Quick primer — two ends of the myopathy spectrum
Toxic rhabdomyolysis
- Typical timing: acute — hours to days after exposure, exertion or other insult.
- Presentation: severe myalgias, dark urine/pigmenturia, potential AKI and electrolyte abnormalities.
- CK: often enormous (tens to hundreds of thousands).
- Course: CK falls quickly after stopping offending agent and aggressive IV fluids; immunosuppression not indicated.
- Pathology: widespread necrosis from direct muscle injury; not an immune‑mediated process.

Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)
- Typical timing: subacute — weeks to months of progressive proximal weakness; can include bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, dysphonia).
- Presentation: progressive symmetric proximal weakness, possible respiratory/bulbar involvement; myalgias less prominent than in rhabdo.
- CK: elevated, often in the thousands; can rise over time despite stopping a trigger.
- Autoantibodies: classically anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP in many cases, but ~1/3 can be seronegative.
- Imaging/biopsy: MRI shows symmetric muscle edema; biopsy shows necrotic and regenerating fibers, macrophage‑predominant myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition and relatively few lymphocytes.
- Treatment: immunosuppression (steroids ± steroid‑sparing agents) and often IVIG for severe cases.

## The case in one paragraph
A woman in her 60s with NAFLD and no statin exposure received one dose of semaglutide and rapidly developed nausea/vomiting with hypovolemia and AKI. Initial CK was modest (337 U/L). Over 4 months she developed progressive proximal weakness and bulbar symptoms; CK rose to 3,992 U/L. MRI of the thighs showed proximal muscle edema and biopsy revealed necrotising myopathy with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex deposition consistent with IMNM. Myositis autoantibodies (including HMGCR and SRP) were negative. She received pulse methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone plus IVIG (2 g/kg split over 4 days) with marked clinical and CK improvement and returned to baseline off immunosuppression by 10 months while avoiding GLP‑1 therapy.

## How to tell them apart at the bedside
Think time course and phenotype first. An abrupt severe muscle illness with myalgias, pigmenturia and a massive CK spike hours–days after exposure looks like toxic rhabdomyolysis. Expect rapid improvement after stopping the drug and aggressive fluids. Conversely, a subacute, steadily progressive picture — especially with prominent proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, rising CK despite medication cessation, or MRI muscle edema — should raise concern for IMNM.

Other practical bedside clues:
- Urine: frank myoglobinuria supports rhabdo.
- Systemic features: bulbar symptoms and respiratory failure lean toward inflammatory myopathy.
- CK trajectory: falling rapidly after fluids = toxic myonecrosis; rising or persistently elevated despite withdrawal = possible autoimmune process.
- Autoantibodies: positive HMGCR or SRP supports IMNM but absence does not exclude it (seronegative IMNM ≈ one‑third of patients).
- Biopsy: definitive — macrophage‑predominant necrosis and membrane attack complex deposition point to IMNM rather than classic toxic injury.

## What I do when a patient on a GLP‑1 presents with muscle complaints
First, don't reflexively label every CK elevation as ‘rhabdo’ without the clinical context. Initial ED evaluation should include CK, renal function, electrolytes, ECG, urinalysis (for myoglobin), and basic cultures if febrile. If there is an acute toxic picture (massive CK, myalgias, dark urine, AKI), treat with aggressive IV fluids, correct electrolytes, stop the offending drug and monitor urine output/renal function; admit.

If the presentation is subacute or progressive, or there are bulbar symptoms or CK that keeps climbing after withdrawal, get early neurology/rheumatology input and consider MRI of affected muscles, EMG and — importantly — muscle biopsy. Check troponin/BNP and consider cardiology if there are cardiac biomarkers or symptoms (this patient had elevated proBNP and CMR changes suggesting possible myocardial involvement). For suspected IMNM with severe weakness, dysphagia, respiratory involvement or rapidly progressive course, involve specialists early because treatment with high‑dose steroids and IVIG is often indicated and timing matters.

Avoid re‑challenges with the suspected agent; in this case the authors considered the causality “probable” and re‑exposure would be unethical.

## Why this matters — vigilance and pharmacovigilance
GLP‑1 RAs get excellent outcomes for obesity and diabetes, but as their use explodes we will see more off‑target and idiosyncratic events. Muscle loss is already reported with GLP‑1 therapy, and there are case reports of rhabdomyolysis with agents like tirzepatide. Lam et al.’s case is the first reported IMNM temporally associated with semaglutide; causality is not proven, but the clinical course (subacute worsening, biopsy evidence of IMNM, steroid/IVIG response, durable recovery off drug) supports a drug‑triggered autoimmune process. Be prepared to differentiate toxic myonecrosis from autoimmune myopathy because the treatments diverge sharply.

## Clinical pearls (short, not exhaustive)
A single drug exposure can present as very different muscle‑disease phenotypes — toxic rhabdo versus immune‑mediated myopathy — and the history (hours → days vs weeks → months), symptom pattern (myalgias/pigmenturia vs progressive proximal weakness ± bulbar signs), CK trajectory and response to stopping the drug are your best immediate clues. Muscle MRI and biopsy are diagnostic tools for IMNM; autoantibody panels can help but are not definitive. Early specialist involvement changes management and outcomes.

## Take‑home messages
- GLP‑1 RAs can be associated with muscle disorders on a spectrum from acute toxic rhabdomyolysis to subacute immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).
- Toxic rhabdomyolysis: acute onset, severe myalgias, pigmenturia, very high CK, rapid improvement after stopping the drug and IV fluids; immunosuppression not indicated.
- IMNM: subacute progressive proximal weakness, often bulbar involvement (dysphagia/dysphonia), MRI muscle edema, biopsy showing necrosis with macrophage‑predominant inflammation and membrane attack complex; may be seronegative.
- If CK keeps rising or weakness progresses after stopping a GLP‑1 RA, get early neurology/rheumatology input and consider muscle MRI and biopsy — don’t assume “just rhabdo.”
- Check cardiac biomarkers and consider cardiac imaging when IMNM is suspected (myocardial involvement can occur).
- Avoid re‑challenge with the suspected GLP‑1 agent and report suspected cases for pharmacovigilance.

Bottom line: not every CK bump in a patient on a GLP‑1 is the same — parse the time course and phenotype. Early recognition of IMNM changes therapy (immunosuppression/IVIG) and can be the difference between recovery and prolonged disability.



# When the Myositis Panel Is Negative: Making the Diagnosis in Seronegative IMNM

What do you do when the myositis panel comes back negative but your patient is getting weaker, has dysphagia, and the CK keeps climbing? This is the problem at the heart of a striking recent case: a woman in her 60s who received a single dose of semaglutide, had an early episode of severe GI illness, then over 4 months developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia and a peak CK of ~4,000 U/L. The myositis panel (including anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR) was negative, but a targeted muscle biopsy showed the classic histologic signature of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM): dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibres, macrophage myophagocytosis (CD68+), acid phosphatase activity, and membrane attack complex (MAC/C5b-9) deposition — with only rare T cells. She improved on pulse steroids + IVIG and withdrawal of the suspected trigger, and remained off immunosuppression while avoiding GLP‑1 receptor agonists.

This case reminds us of three blunt truths: (1) up to one-third of IMNM is seronegative, (2) the myositis antibody panel is helpful but not definitive, and (3) timely MRI-guided biopsy + the right stains win the day. Below is a pragmatic, case‑grounded pathway for diagnosing seronegative IMNM and avoiding the common traps that delay treatment.

## When to suspect IMNM even if antibodies are negative
Think IMNM when the clinical pattern fits — don't be reassured by a negative panel.

- Subacute to rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness (difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs), often severe.
- Bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia) or respiratory compromise — red flags for urgent evaluation.
- Marked CK elevation (often >1,000 U/L) that is rising or persistent.
- Few or no inflammatory skin findings (distinguishes from classic dermatomyositis).
- A temporal association with a potential trigger (statin is classic; this case raises the possibility of a GLP‑1 receptor agonist trigger).
- Cardiac symptoms or biomarker abnormalities (troponin, BNP) — IMNM can involve the heart, so low threshold for cardiac evaluation.

In the presented case the constellation of progressive proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms, rising CK and MRI evidence of proximal thigh muscle edema made IMNM much more likely than simple drug-related rhabdomyolysis or deconditioning.

## Labs and imaging — what to order right away
Do these immediately while waiting for antibody panels; treat the results as pieces of the puzzle, not the final answer.

- Basic bloods: CK (repeat), CMP (look for renal involvement), AST/ALT (remember muscle can raise transaminases), ESR/CRP, TSH.
- Autoimmune/myositis panel (include anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP and myositis-specific antibodies) — but don’t stop the workup if negative.
- Cardiac: troponin and BNP if any cardiac concern; echo and cardiac MRI if abnormal.
- MRI of affected muscles with fluid‑sensitive sequences (STIR/T2): key for identifying active muscle edema and selecting the optimal biopsy site. In this case MRI of the thighs showed symmetric proximal vastus lateralis/quadriceps edema — the exact area biopsied.
- EMG can be supportive (myopathic motor units, spontaneous activity) and sometimes helps localize but is not necessary if MRI is diagnostic and a biopsy is planned.

Practical point: MRI-guided selection of a biopsy site improves diagnostic yield — choose a muscle with active edema, not an end-stage fatty replaced muscle.

## The biopsy is often the clincher — what to ask the pathologist to look for
If the antibody panel is negative and clinical suspicion remains high, biopsy is indispensable. Tell your pathologist to evaluate with these stains and features:

- H&E: dispersed necrotic myofibres and regenerating fibres (necrosis ± regeneration is the hallmark).
- Acid phosphatase (lysosomal activity) highlighting myophagocytosis.
- CD68 immunostain: macrophage-rich infiltrates with myophagocytosis.
- C5b-9 (MAC) immunostain: membrane attack complex deposition on necrotic and sometimes non‑necrotic fibers — supports complement-mediated necrosis.
- CD3 (and other lymphocyte markers): typically only rare T cells in IMNM (a paucity of lymphocytes helps distinguish IMNM from polymyositis).
- If available, MHC‑I expression and other ancillary stains can be informative.

The case’s biopsy matched this pattern exactly: necrotic and regenerating fibers, acid phosphatase positivity, CD68 macrophages with myophagocytosis, MAC deposition, and only rare CD3+ cells — a textbook IMNM picture even without serologic markers.

A word about timing: ideally obtain the biopsy before prolonged high‑dose steroids because immunosuppression can alter inflammatory infiltrates. However, IMNM’s necrosis, regeneration and MAC deposition are relatively robust, and if the patient has airway/respiratory compromise or severe bulbar disease you should not delay treatment for a biopsy.

## Distinguish IMNM from rhabdomyolysis and other myopathies
Common confounders:

- Drug-induced rhabdomyolysis is typically acute with massive CK elevations (often tens of thousands), myoglobinuria, and improvement with drug withdrawal and hydration over days to weeks. The tirzepatide rhabdo case followed that pattern; the presented case did not.
- Polymyositis shows prominent endomysial lymphocytic infiltrates; dermatomyositis has characteristic skin findings and perivascular/perimysial pathology.
- Inclusion body myositis is chronic, asymmetric, and distal/proximal mixed — different clinical picture.
- Metabolic, endocrine (hypothyroidism), infectious and toxic causes — keep them in the differential and screen appropriately.

## Treatment — when to pull the trigger
Treatment decisions are individualized but guided by severity.

- Remove/stop any suspected offending agent when reasonable (in this case semaglutide was avoided).
- For severe disease (bulbar/respiratory involvement, rapidly progressive weakness, very high CK with ongoing injury): start high‑dose corticosteroids (e.g., IV methylprednisolone pulses) and consider IVIG early (2 g/kg split over a few days as in this case). This patient received 1 g IV methylprednisolone daily x5 days, then prednisone 1 mg/kg, plus IVIG 2 g/kg and improved substantially.
- In idiopathic or refractory IMNM, add steroid‑sparing agents (methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate, rituximab) and consider maintenance IVIG — but drug‑associated IMNM may respond to a shorter course once the trigger is removed.
- Cardiac involvement should trigger cardiology input and appropriate imaging/management.

Remember: seronegative disease may still behave like classic IMNM and requires the same vigilance.

## Avoiding red herrings — lessons from the case
- Isolated low‑titer SS‑B positivity without ANA or SS‑A and without clinical features of Sjögren’s is likely incidental; don’t let it derail your IMNM workup.
- Transaminase abnormalities can be hepatic or muscular. Correlate AST/ALT with CK, check GGT/ALP, and consider prior liver imaging/biopsy. In this patient, a pre‑existing fatty liver biopsy predated the myopathy, and her modest transaminase abnormalities persisted despite normalization of CK — pointing to hepatic origin for those enzymes.
- A single negative antibody panel does not rule out IMNM — up to ~one‑third of IMNM cases are seronegative. If clinical probability is high, proceed to MRI and biopsy.

## Putting it together — a pragmatic pathway
1. Clinical suspicion (proximal weakness ± bulbar symptoms, rising CK) → order CK, CMP, ESR/CRP, TSH, troponin/BNP as indicated, and send myositis panel.
2. Stop any plausible offending medication when safe to do so.
3. Obtain MRI of symptomatic regions with STIR/T2 to localize active edema.
4. Coordinate muscle biopsy targeted to MRI‑active muscle and request H&E, acid phosphatase, CD68, MAC (C5b‑9) and CD3 stains.
5. If severe features present, start empiric high‑dose steroids ± IVIG — don’t let biopsy timing delay potentially life‑saving therapy.
6. If biopsy confirms IMNM, tailor immunosuppression; if drug‑associated, consider short course and close follow‑up; if idiopathic, plan for longer immunotherapy and monitor for relapse.
7. Screen/monitor for cardiac involvement and perform age‑appropriate malignancy screening as clinically indicated.

## Take‑home messages
- Up to one‑third of IMNM cases are seronegative — a negative myositis panel does not exclude disease.
- MRI is invaluable: use fluid‑sensitive sequences to find active muscle and guide biopsy; avoid sampling fatty‑replaced, end‑stage muscle.
- The biopsy signature of IMNM: necrotic/regenerating fibers, macrophage‑predominant myophagocytosis (CD68+), acid phosphatase activity and membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) deposition, with only scant T‑cells.
- Don’t be distracted by isolated low‑titer autoantibodies (eg, SS‑B alone) or by pre‑existing liver enzyme abnormalities — correlate clinically and with CK/GGT/ultrasound/biopsy.
- If the patient is clinically severe, start therapy (steroids ± IVIG) rather than waiting for perfect proof; still try to obtain a targeted biopsy before prolonged immunosuppression when feasible.

Case wrap: this semaglutide‑linked, seronegative IMNM case shows how combining clinical suspicion, targeted MRI, and the right muscle biopsy stains gets you to a diagnosis and to timely treatment — even when the serology says “nothing to see here.”



# Treating Drug-Triggered IMNM: Steroids, IVIG, and Short Tapers Done Well

Why this case grabbed my attention: a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide preceded a progressive, biopsy‑proven immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) in a woman in her 60s. She developed bulbar symptoms, a CK that climbed from a few hundred to nearly 4,000 U/L over 4 months, and a muscle biopsy with necrosis, macrophage‑predominant inflammation and membrane attack complex deposition — classic IMNM histology. She received high‑dose IV methylprednisolone, early IVIG, and a short prednisone taper, then regained baseline and remained off immunosuppression for months. The flip side: she developed a pseudomonal urosepsis shortly after discharge — a reminder that even a “short” heavy hit of immunosuppression carries real infection risk.

This case is useful because it crystallises a practical approach to suspected drug‑associated IMNM: when to hit fast and hard with IV steroids, when to add IVIG early (especially for bulbar or severe weakness), how to monitor CK and function and justify a rapid taper after drug withdrawal, and how to mitigate infection risk and counsel patients about rechallenge.

## Case in brief
A female in her 60s with obesity and NAFLD received one dose of semaglutide and within hours developed GI intolerance, hypovolaemia and AKI. Initial CK was 337 U/L. Over the next 4 months she developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and dysphonia; CK peaked at 3,992 U/L. Myositis serologies (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) were negative. Thigh MRI showed proximal muscle oedema. Right vastus lateralis biopsy demonstrated necrotic/regenerating fibres, CD68+ macrophages with myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex staining — findings consistent with IMNM. Treatment: IV methylprednisolone 1,000 mg daily ×5, transition to oral prednisone ~1 mg/kg, and IVIG 2 g/kg divided over 4 days. CK fell and strength recovered; prednisone was rapidly tapered over ~2 months and she was off immunosuppression with normal CK at last follow‑up 10 months after presentation. She was rehospitalised 12 days after initial discharge for pseudomonal urosepsis; CK remained normal during that admission.

## Drug‑induced IMNM vs rhabdomyolysis vs idiopathic IMNM — how to think about it
Drug‑associated muscle injury runs a spectrum. Classic toxic rhabdomyolysis presents acutely with massively elevated CK that improves quickly after stopping the drug and supportive care. Idiopathic IMNM is an autoimmune disease often associated with anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP antibodies, frequently requires prolonged immunosuppression, and tends to be refractory to steroids alone.

Drug‑triggered IMNM occupies a middle ground: a drug exposure precedes progressive weakness and rising CK over weeks to months, biopsy shows necrotic fibres with complement deposition and macrophages, autoantibodies may be absent (up to ~1/3 of IMNM are seronegative), and withdrawal of the agent plus a short course of immunotherapy can produce durable remission. In this case the temporal relationship to semaglutide, the histology, the clinical course and the recovery after withdrawal plus brief immunotherapy fulfil criteria for a probable drug‑associated IMNM.

Clinical pearl: don’t dismiss elevated AST/ALT as hepatic when CK is high — muscle can drive transaminase elevation. In this patient, baseline NAFLD muddied interpretation, but biopsy confirmed hepatic steatosis pre‑dating the myopathy.

## When to pulse methylprednisolone
The main rationale for pulse steroids is rapid control of immune‑mediated muscle necrosis when function or life is threatened. Use pulse IV methylprednisolone (commonly 500–1,000 mg IV daily for 3–5 days) when the presentation is severe or rapidly progressive. Indications that push you toward pulsing include:

- Bulbar dysfunction (dysphagia, aspiration risk) or respiratory muscle involvement.
- Inability to ambulate, sit, or stand because of proximal weakness.
- Rapidly rising CK with clinical deterioration over days–weeks.
- Major organ involvement (suspected myocarditis, rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease).

In the reported case, bulbar symptoms and severe proximal weakness justified 1 g methylprednisolone IV daily ×5 days. Pulse therapy should be followed by high‑dose oral prednisone (commonly ~1 mg/kg) while you reassess response.

Clinical pearl: pulse therapy is a bridge. Reassess early for clinical and CK response and plan next steps (IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents) rather than defaulting to prolonged high‑dose steroids.

## When to add IVIG early
IVIG should be considered early in IMNM when there are poor prognostic features or steroid‑sparing is desirable. Typical scenarios include bulbar weakness, severe generalized weakness with functional loss, failure to improve on steroids alone, or antibody profiles that often respond poorly to steroids (anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP). In practice, IVIG dosing is 2 g/kg total, split over 2–5 days; the team in this case used 2 g/kg divided over 4 days.

Practical cautions: IVIG can worsen or cause renal dysfunction (avoid sucrose‑stabilised products; ensure good hydration and monitor creatinine), cause thromboembolism, aseptic meningitis and hemolysis — use slower infusion rates and monitor closely, especially in older or renally vulnerable patients. When renal impairment is present or recent, consider dividing the dose over more days and monitor urine output and creatinine.

Clinical pearl: in drug‑triggered IMNM where you suspect a reversible trigger, early IVIG can short‑circuit a prolonged steroid course and speed functional recovery — often worth the cost and logistics when bulbar dysfunction or severe weakness is present.

## Monitoring CK and function — when a rapid taper is reasonable
The decision to rapidly taper steroids after initial control rests on two pillars: objective muscle injury markers (CK) and objective/subjective functional improvement.

Suggested practical monitoring after initiation of therapy:
- Inpatient: CK daily while acutely ill until trend is clear, plus regular strength assessments and swallow evaluation if bulbar symptoms.
- Early outpatient: CK weekly (or every 2 weeks) until downtrending and normalised, then monthly for several months.
- Functional measures: bedside MRC grades, timed sit‑to‑stand or timed up‑and‑go, and documented improvement in swallowing (formal speech‑language swallow study if indicated).

When to consider a faster taper: if the offending drug has been stopped, CK normalises or returns near normal, strength and bulbar symptoms show clear objective improvement within 2–4 weeks, and there is no evidence of ongoing organ involvement (cardiac, pulmonary). In that setting a clinician may reasonably wean prednisone over weeks rather than many months. That is what happened here: after IV pulse + IVIG the patient’s CK and function improved, permitting a prednisone taper off over ≈2 months with sustained remission.

Contrast that with idiopathic IMNM or seropositive disease with ongoing activity: those typically need slow tapering combined with steroid‑sparing agents and close surveillance.

Clinical pearl: trends trump single numbers. A falling CK plus reproducible functional gains is the justification you need to shorten steroid exposure. Don’t stop steroids abruptly — taper guided by clinical and CK responses.

## Infection risk — learn from the pseudomonas urosepsis
Heavy immunosuppression, even for a short time, alters infection risk. This patient developed pseudomonal urosepsis 12 days after discharge. Whether immunosuppression, deconditioning, urinary stasis or hospital exposures were dominant contributors we cannot know — but it highlights practical prevention steps.

Before, during and after immunosuppression:
- Screen for and treat occult infections before starting high‑dose therapy (urinalysis if symptoms, chest x‑ray if respiratory symptoms, culture if indicated).
- Avoid indwelling urinary catheters when possible and practise strict catheter care when unavoidable.
- Encourage early mobilisation and attention to bladder emptying in deconditioned patients.
- Monitor for fever, dysuria, cough and other infection signals; have a low threshold for evaluation.
- Consider PJP prophylaxis (TMP‑SMX) when prolonged high‑dose steroids (>20 mg prednisone daily for >4 weeks) are planned or when combining steroid with other immunosuppressants.
- Check vaccination status early (influenza, pneumococcus, shingles) and update before immunosuppression if feasible; remember live vaccines are contraindicated during immunosuppression.
- Monitor glucose while on prednisone and manage hyperglycaemia—hyperglycaemia increases infection risk.

Clinical pearl: in people who are frail or deconditioned, even short steroid courses can tip the balance toward serious infection. Prevention and early detection are key.

## Counseling on rechallenge and class crossovers
The authors judged the semaglutide–IMNM link as “probable” by causality criteria. With an adverse event this severe, do not rechallenge the patient with semaglutide. Given the uncertain risk of class cross‑reactivity, the conservative step is to avoid GLP‑1 receptor agonists until more data exist. Document the reaction prominently in the chart, give the patient an adverse‑drug‑reaction card, and report the event to local pharmacovigilance authorities.

Work with endocrinology to discuss alternative weight‑loss or glucose‑lowering strategies (lifestyle, other non‑GLP‑1 medications, referral for bariatric options if appropriate). Decisions about re‑exposure to other agents in the class should only occur after multidisciplinary risk–benefit discussion and with patient informed consent.

Clinical pearl: avoid re‑exposure; err on the side of class avoidance unless proof of safety emerges.

## When to escalate to steroid‑sparing or rescue therapy
If symptoms recur with tapering, CK rises again, or function fails to recover despite steroids and IVIG, escalate therapy. Options include repeating IVIG cycles (many patients respond to serial IVIG), adding steroid‑sparing agents (methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate), or using biologics for refractory disease (rituximab) or cyclophosphamide for life‑threatening manifestations. Anti‑SRP and anti‑HMGCR IMNM can be refractory and often require combination regimens.

Escalation should be guided by specialists (rheumatology/neuromuscular medicine) and tailored to the patient’s comorbidities, infection risk and organ involvement (cardiac or pulmonary disease may require more aggressive immunosuppression).

Clinical pearl: in drug‑associated IMNM, a second IVIG course plus a short steroid resumption often recaptures remission. Persistent disease beyond a few months is the signal to move to steroid‑sparing agents.

## Practical clinical pearls
- Suspect IMNM when proximal weakness progresses over weeks–months with rising CK after a drug exposure; muscle biopsy is often decisive (necrosis, macrophages, MAC deposition).
- Pulse IV methylprednisolone (500–1,000 mg IV/day for 3–5 days) for life‑ or function‑threatening features (bulbar, respiratory, rapidly progressive weakness, suspected myocarditis).
- Add IVIG early (2 g/kg divided over 2–5 days) for bulbar dysfunction, severe weakness, steroid non‑response, or when you want to limit steroid exposure.
- Monitor CK and objective function closely. If CK normalises and function improves after drug withdrawal + early therapy, a more rapid steroid taper (weeks rather than many months) is reasonable — as in this case where prednisone was tapered off over ~2 months.
- Proactively mitigate infection risk: screen for occult infection, avoid indwelling catheters, consider PJP prophylaxis when prolonged immunosuppression is expected, and update vaccinations before therapy when possible.
- Document and report the adverse reaction; counsel patients to avoid rechallenge with the offending drug and consider class avoidance pending more data.
- If relapse or persistent disease occurs, repeat IVIG and add steroid‑sparing agents per neuromuscular or rheumatology guidance; consider rituximab/cyclophosphamide for refractory or organ‑threatening disease.

## Bottom line
Drug‑triggered IMNM behaves differently from classic toxic rhabdomyolysis and from idiopathic IMNM. When a plausible drug precipitant exists, withdraw it, assess severity, and act quickly: pulse steroids for threatened function, add IVIG early for bulbar or severe weakness, and monitor CK and objective function closely — a brisk clinical and lab response can justify a shorter steroid course. Always weigh the infection risks of immunosuppression and enlist a multidisciplinary team early. If you see progressive weakness and rising CK after a GLP‑1 agonist (or any new drug), pick up the phone to get CK checked and involve neurology/rheumatology early — timely therapy can restore function and limit long immunosuppressive courses.



# AST vs ALT vs CK: Sorting Out Transaminase Elevations When Myopathy and NAFLD Coexist

What makes this case useful to clinicians is not only the unusual suspected trigger for an immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) — a single dose of semaglutide followed by progressive weakness and CK in the thousands — but the diagnostic trap it highlights: coexisting NAFLD produced a baseline transaminitis that persisted even after the muscle injury and CK normalized. That combination can easily lead you to the wrong organ of interest if you don’t have a tidy bedside framework.

Below I’ll walk through a practical, clinic- and ED-friendly approach to answering the question clinicians ask every day: “Is this AST/ALT elevation coming from the liver or from muscle — or both?” I’ll use the semaglutide/IMNM case as a running example.

## Quick physiologic recap (the part we keep forgetting)
- AST is abundant in liver, but also in skeletal muscle, heart, red cells and other tissues. ALT is much more concentrated in liver, but there is some ALT in muscle.
- CK is the most muscle-specific routine test — when it’s high, think muscle.
- GGT and alkaline phosphatase are hepatic/cholestatic markers and are not elevated by muscle injury. Bilirubin, INR, albumin and platelets speak to hepatic synthetic function and chronicity.
- Aldolase and LDH are additional muscle enzymes but are less specific. Aldolase may be helpful when CK is deceptively normal and clinical suspicion for myositis is high.
- Timing matters: muscle enzymes rise and fall on a different timeline than chronic liver transaminases.

## The clinical problem in one line
If AST/ALT are up in a patient with weakness, you must decide quickly whether this is primary liver disease, primary muscle disease, or both — because that changes your next tests, imaging, need for biopsy, and urgency of treatment.

## A practical bedside approach (stepwise, in words)
First, the history. Look for temporal clues (when did weakness start vs when transaminases were first abnormal?), medication/supplement exposures (statins, supplements like red yeast rice, new drugs — in this case semaglutide), prodromes (nausea, vomiting, oliguria suggesting volume loss and AKI), systemic features (rash, respiratory symptoms, dysphagia), and prior liver tests. In our case the patient already had mild ALT-predominant transaminitis for years from NAFLD; the profound weakness and dysphagia developed after semaglutide.

Second, order targeted labs early. If weakness or myalgias are present, include CK in your initial panel — always. Add AST/ALT, GGT, ALP, bilirubin, INR, albumin, platelets, TSH, CRP/ESR. If available and needed, add aldolase and LDH. In the case above CK was 337 during the first admission, then rose to 3992 four months later — a dramatic muscle signal.

Third, interpret patterns — don’t rely on a single ratio. Some helpful patterns:
- Big CK elevation (hundreds to thousands) with proportionate AST elevation and a normal GGT leans strongly to muscle. AST will often parallel CK.
- AST > ALT can be seen in muscle injury, but AST/ALT ratios are not definitive (they are also seen in alcoholic liver disease and advanced fibrosis).
- ALT > AST classically suggests hepatic origin (early NAFLD often has ALT > AST). In our patient prior labs showed ALT > AST consistent with NAFLD before the myopathy.
- If ALT/AST remain elevated after CK normalize (or fall), suspect liver origin; if they fall with CK in parallel, muscle was the major contributor.

Fourth, use GGT and cholestatic markers as tie-breakers. An elevated GGT with raised ALT implicates liver. In pure muscle injury GGT is usually normal. That makes GGT a very useful discriminator at the bedside when CK and transaminases are both up.

Fifth, add imaging appropriately. Point-of-care or formal liver ultrasound is quick and informative for steatosis and biliary pathology. Muscle MRI helps localize active myositis if you’re planning biopsy. In this case, femoral MRI showed symmetric proximal muscle edema confirming active myositis and liver ultrasound/Doppler and biopsy confirmed hepatosteatosis.

Sixth, decide on biopsy only when it will change management. Muscle biopsy is often required to diagnose IMNM and to guide immunosuppressive therapy when weakness is progressive and CK is high. Liver biopsy is generally reserved for cases where the cause or stage of liver disease is unclear and would change therapy — e.g., suspected NASH requiring staging, unexplained persistent transaminitis despite negative noninvasive workup, suspected autoimmune or drug-induced liver injury that alters therapy. In this patient there was an older liver biopsy showing NAFLD and a repeat biopsy (performed) showed progression — that supports the liver as a chronic contributor to her transaminitis while muscle disease explained the acute CK rise and weakness.

Seventh, temporal behavior helps clinch it. Direct toxic rhabdomyolysis typically improves quickly after stopping the offending agent and hydration (days to a few weeks for CK). Autoimmune myopathies (IMNM) may need immunosuppression and IVIG to bring CK and strength down. Chronic NAFLD/NASH transaminase elevations persist over months to years and won’t resolve quickly when you treat the muscle.

## Laboratory and diagnostic pearls
- Always check CK when you see unexplained AST elevation or when the patient has weakness. Missing CK is the most common error.
- If CK is normal but clinical suspicion for myositis remains high (progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia), aldolase can be helpful — some myositis patients have a relatively normal CK early.
- Use GGT (and ALP) as a hepatic-specific check: normal GGT with markedly elevated CK + AST suggests muscle.
- Don’t overinterpret AST/ALT ratio. It can mislead. Look at the whole panel and the trend.
- Serologies (HMGCR, SRP) help subtype IMNM but ~30% of IMNM patients are seronegative (as in this case), so biopsy and clinical context remain crucial.
- Check TSH (hypothyroid myopathy), hepatitis serologies, and medication/supplement history to rule out reversible contributors.

## When to call specialists
- Rheumatology/neurology immediately if progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, respiratory compromise, CK in the thousands, or concern for IMNM.
- Hepatology if transaminases remain unexplained after noninvasive testing, or if a liver biopsy may affect management.
- Cardiology if troponin, BNP or cardiac MRI suggest myocardial involvement (this case had elevated hs-troponin and pro-BNP and MRI findings that later normalized).

## Applying this back to the semaglutide/IMNM case
- Background: chronic ALT>AST and biopsy-proven NAFLD years before the event.
- New problem: progressive proximal weakness with CK peaking at ~4,000 U/L and biopsy-proven IMNM; treated with high-dose steroids and IVIG with clinical recovery and CK normalization.
- Key diagnostic point: after CK normalized, the patient still had borderline transaminase elevations — explained by her underlying NAFLD rather than ongoing muscle injury. Muscle biopsy confirmed IMNM; repeat liver biopsy documented steatohepatitis progression.
- Takeaway for this case: if the team had assumed every AST/ALT spike was muscle-derived, they might have missed the chronic liver disease and vice versa. Both organs were relevant — one chronic, one acute — and the trends separated them.

## Bottom line (the bedside algorithm in one paragraph)
When faced with AST/ALT elevation plus weakness, get a CK and GGT early, use history/timeline and simple labs to decide: if CK is markedly elevated and GGT is normal, treat and evaluate for muscle disease (and consider muscle MRI/biopsy). If CK is normal or CK normalizes while transaminases persist, think liver and pursue hepatic workup. Reserve biopsies for when results will change therapy.

## Take-home messages
- Always check CK when you encounter unexplained AST/ALT elevation in a patient with weakness — missing CK is a common diagnostic trap.
- AST is not liver-specific; ALT is more liver-predominant. GGT (and ALP) are useful to point toward hepatic origin.
- Follow trends: if CK and AST fall together, muscle is likely the culprit; if CK normalizes but AST/ALT stay high, liver disease (eg, NAFLD) is likely.
- Aldolase can help when CK is unexpectedly normal but clinical suspicion for myositis stays high.
- Muscle biopsy is the diagnostic test for IMNM; liver biopsy should be reserved for cases where staging or alternative liver diagnoses would change management.
- In patients starting drugs associated with muscle effects, take a careful baseline history and consider baseline CK if clinically indicated — and practice pharmacovigilance when novel agents are suspected contributors.



# Reading the Heart in Myositis: Troponin, BNP, and CMR Without Overcalling Myocarditis

## Why this case matters
A woman in her 60s developed immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) after a single dose of semaglutide. Over four months she went from being independent to severe proximal weakness, dysphagia, and a CK that peaked at ~4,000 U/L. Cardiac workup picked up a modest high‑sensitivity troponin rise (62 pg/mL, assay not specified), a markedly elevated pro‑BNP (4,358 pg/mL), transient paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, and an echocardiogram with preserved LVEF (65–70%) but elevated RVSP (≈46 mm Hg). Cardiac MRI showed focal myocardial edema and mild focal hypokinesis but no definite late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). The patient improved on high‑dose corticosteroids + IVIG; repeat imaging normalized and there was no persistent cardiac dysfunction.

Why is this interesting for clinicians? Because the combination of modest troponin elevation, huge BNP, preserved LVEF, transient arrhythmia and CMR edema is exactly the kind of messy signal that tempts us to declare "myocarditis" — and to escalate to invasive or long‑term cardiac therapies — when a more nuanced interpretation is safer and more useful. This case is a great teaching moment on how to read myocardial biomarkers and imaging in patients with inflammatory myopathy.

## The core problem: biomarkers are noisy in myositis
There are three common pitfalls when you see abnormal cardiac tests in a patient with active skeletal muscle disease.

1) Troponin assays are not identical. Cardiac troponin I (cTnI) is generally more specific to myocardium. Cardiac troponin T (cTnT) assays can be confounded by re‑expression of troponin T isoforms in diseased or regenerating skeletal muscle, producing low‑level positive results that don’t reflect myocardial necrosis. If your lab only reports hs‑cTnT and it’s mildly positive in a myositis patient, get a cTnI if available or move to ECG/echo/CMR correlation rather than reflexively diagnosing myocarditis.

2) CK is muscle‑specific but not heart‑specific. A very high CK shows major skeletal muscle injury (as in IMNM) but tells you nothing about the heart. Conversely, a relatively low CK does not exclude cardiac involvement.

3) BNP/pro‑BNP reflect wall stress, stretch and neurohormonal activation — not myocarditis per se. BNP can be profoundly elevated by renal dysfunction, sepsis, atrial arrhythmia, pulmonary hypertension or volume overload. In this case the BNP was very high despite preserved LVEF; that should trigger differential thinking (RV strain, diastolic dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension, atrial arrhythmia) rather than an automatic diagnosis of systolic myocarditis.

## Practical approach at the bedside
When a patient with inflammatory myopathy presents with dyspnea, chest discomfort, syncope, arrhythmia, or any cardiac biomarker abnormality, think "triage, then integrate" rather than "label and treat."

First steps (within the first few hours): symptom assessment, 12‑lead ECG, bedside cardiac monitoring, renal function. Draw high‑sensitivity troponin — preferably cTnI if available — and a natriuretic peptide. Compare to prior values and look for a dynamic rise/fall pattern. Remember that one mildly elevated troponin with a normal ECG and normal echo in a patient with active skeletal muscle disease is not diagnostic of myocarditis.

Echocardiography is the next non‑invasive test: it tells you systolic function, wall motion abnormalities, pericardial effusion and estimated pulmonary pressures. In our case the echo showed preserved LVEF but an elevated RVSP, a clue to RV or pulmonary vascular strain that could explain part of the BNP elevation.

If there are any of the following — persistent or rising troponin, new significant ECG changes, reduced LVEF, unexplained arrhythmia or hemodynamic instability — escalate to cardiology and consider cardiac MRI (CMR). CMR is the single best non‑invasive test to look for myocardial inflammation, but interpret it using the clinical context and the Lake Louise criteria (see below).

Reserve endomyocardial biopsy for fulminant cases with rapidly progressive heart failure, refractory ventricular arrhythmias, high‑grade conduction block, or suspicion for specific treatable pathologies (giant cell myocarditis, eosinophilic, etc.). It is not needed for every myositis patient who has a troponin bump.

## How to read a CMR in this context
CMR gives two major pieces of information: T2‑weighted/T2 mapping for edema (active inflammation) and T1‑based markers (LGE, T1/ECV mapping) for irreversible injury or fibrosis. The 2018 updated Lake Louise criteria require at least one positive T2 marker (edema) and one positive T1 marker (LGE or abnormal T1/ECV) to meet the conventional CMR definition of myocarditis. If you have edema without T1 abnormalities, the study is suggestive but not diagnostic of myocarditis — it can represent early/reversible inflammation, microvascular injury, or even focal stress cardiomyopathy.

Crucially, absence of LGE is a good prognostic sign. LGE in a nonischemic pattern (midwall/epicardial) implies necrosis/fibrosis and correlates with worse outcomes and arrhythmia risk. Edema alone with no LGE — as in this case — is much more likely to be reversible with treatment of the systemic inflammatory process.

CMR can also help separate myocarditis from ischemia. Ischemic injury gives subendocardial or transmural LGE in a coronary distribution; nonischemic patterns argue for inflammatory etiologies.

## Arrhythmias — how worried should we be?
Arrhythmias are common in inflammatory myopathies and myocarditis, ranging from AF to ventricular tachycardia and conduction block. But context matters. In this patient, paroxysmal AF occurred during an acute systemic illness (nausea, vomiting, renal failure) and did not recur after recovery. That pattern fits AF from systemic stress/inflammation rather than persistent myocardial electrical substrate disease.

Management of arrhythmia follows usual cardiology principles: treat hemodynamic instability urgently, control rate/rhythm as appropriate, and assess stroke risk (CHA2DS2‑VASc) to guide anticoagulation. If arrhythmia persists or is associated with reduced LVEF, recurrent syncope, ventricular ectopy or CMR LGE, involve electrophysiology/cardiology for further evaluation and longer‑term planning.

## When to start immunosuppression for suspected myocardial involvement
This is a nuanced decision. If the patient already has a systemic autoimmune myopathy requiring immunosuppression (as with IMNM), and there is concurrent clinical, biomarker, ECG or imaging evidence suggestive of cardiac inflammation, treating the underlying immune disease is reasonable and often effective for both muscle and myocardial disease. High‑dose steroids ± IVIG is a common initial strategy in severe IMNM with suspected myocardial involvement.

If myocarditis is the primary, isolated cardiac problem, you need to exclude infectious etiologies (especially viral) and discuss immunosuppression with cardiology and infectious disease. There is no one‑size‑fits‑all answer; the severity of cardiac involvement rather than a single troponin number should drive therapy.

In our case the patient received pulse steroids and IVIG for severe IMNM and had clinical and imaging recovery — an outcome consistent with immune‑mediated cardiac inflammation that was reversible.

## Follow‑up and return to activity
Once myocarditis is suspected or documented, restrict strenuous activity and competitive sport for at least 3 months; most guidelines suggest 3–6 months depending on recovery and testing. Follow clinically with serial ECGs, troponins (prefer cTnI), BNP as indicated, and repeat echocardiography. Consider repeat CMR at 3–6 months to document resolution of edema and absence of new LGE if the initial study was abnormal.

Coordinate follow‑up between rheumatology and cardiology. Clearance for progressive return to activity should be individualized and based on symptom resolution, normalization of biomarkers, stable rhythm and imaging improvement.

## The takeaways — clinical pearls
- Mild-to-moderate hs‑troponin elevation in a patient with active skeletal myositis does not automatically equal myocarditis; preferentially measure cTnI when available, because cTnT can be falsely positive with skeletal muscle disease.  
- Interpret biomarkers in context: CK = skeletal muscle injury; BNP = wall stress/strain (and affected by renal function, arrhythmia, pulmonary pressures); troponin dynamics and ECG/echo/CMR are needed to attribute myocardial injury.  
- Use bedside triage (ECG, monitor, cTnI, BNP, echo) first; escalate to CMR if there are persistent or significant abnormalities, hemodynamic compromise, arrhythmia, or reduced LVEF.  
- On CMR, edema without LGE suggests active but likely reversible inflammation; LGE (nonischemic pattern) implies necrosis/scar and carries worse prognosis. Apply the Lake Louise criteria rather than calling myocarditis on edema alone.  
- Treat myocarditis in the context of systemic autoimmune myopathy with immunosuppression when clinically warranted; reserve endomyocardial biopsy for fulminant, refractory, or diagnostically unclear cases.  
- Follow with serial ECGs, biomarkers, echocardiography and consider repeat CMR in 3–6 months; restrict vigorous exercise until cardiology clearance.

This case reminds us that the heart in myositis can look scary on paper — a troponin bump, a sky‑high BNP, and a bit of edema on CMR — but the clinical story, assay selection, imaging pattern (edema without scar), and trajectory matter far more than any single number. Read the whole picture before you label, and partner early with cardiology when the signals are persistent or the patient is unstable.



# From Anecdote to Signal: How to Do Pharmacovigilance for GLP‑1 Adverse Events

One biopsy‑proven immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) after a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide is not proof of causation — but it is the kind of case that should trigger careful thinking, documentation and reporting. This patient in her 60s developed immediate GI toxicity and dehydration with acute kidney injury, then over 4 months progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and dysphonia with CK rising from 337 U/L acutely to a peak of 3,992 U/L. Muscle biopsy showed necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophage myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition consistent with IMNM; myositis serologies (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) were negative. She improved after high‑dose steroids and IVIG and remained well off immunosuppression after stopping semaglutide. That temporal pattern, the biopsy, absence of statin use and clinical recovery make a WHO–UMC “probable/likely” causality classification plausible — and they illustrate exactly how clinicians should move from an anecdote toward a safety signal.

## The case — the essentials you need to remember
A woman in her 60s with obesity and NAFLD received a single subcutaneous semaglutide dose. Within hours she vomited, became dehydrated and was hospitalized with AKI and metabolic acidosis; CK was modestly elevated (≈337 U/L). Over the next 4 months she developed progressive symmetric proximal weakness and bulbar symptoms; outside CK was 3,992 U/L. She received pulse methylprednisolone, then prednisone 1 mg/kg, plus IVIG 2 g/kg. Right vastus lateralis biopsy showed necrotic/regenerating fibres, CD68+ macrophage myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition — histology consistent with IMNM. Myositis panel (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) was negative. She improved clinically and biochemically and was back to baseline 10 months later while off GLP‑1 therapy.

## Why a single case matters (but don’t overcall it)
Case reports are the front line of pharmacovigilance. Single events can reveal unexpected harms — especially when an adverse outcome is biologically plausible, documented objectively (labs, biopsy), temporally related to the drug, and other common causes are absent. But association is not causation: the absence of a pre‑treatment CK and the fact ~30% of IMNM cases are seronegative mean we cannot exclude a pre‑existing smouldering process. Still, this case meets several of the criteria that make a report worth generating and sharing: clear timing, objective pathology, absence of alternative triggers (no statin, no myotoxic supplements), clinical severity and a reasonable response after stopping the drug and instituting immunotherapy.

## Applying the WHO–UMC causality categories — a quick practical walk‑through
The WHO–UMC system gives clinicians a pragmatic way to classify causality: Certain, Probable/Likely, Possible, Unlikely, Conditional/Unclassified and Unassessable/Unclassifiable. The key elements are timing, biological plausibility, alternative explanations, dechallenge/rechallenge, and completeness of information.

Applied to this case:
- Timing: onset of muscle disease followed the semaglutide dose (initial GI/AKI immediately, progressive myopathy over months) — compatible.
- Biological plausibility: GLP‑1RAs have been linked to weight/muscle losses and there are prior reports of GLP‑1/GIP agents associated with rhabdomyolysis; a trigger for autoimmune myopathy is biologically plausible though unproven.
- Alternative explanations: statin‑associated IMNM was excluded (no statin use); other myotoxic supplements were denied; pre‑existing IMNM cannot be completely excluded because no baseline CK was available.
- Dechallenge/rechallenge: the patient improved after stopping the drug and receiving immunosuppression; no rechallenge (rechallenge would be unethical for this severity).
Given these points, the case fits “probable/likely” rather than “certain.” Probable means a reasonable time relationship, unlikely alternative causes, and a clinically reasonable response on withdrawal — exactly what the authors concluded.

## What to document when you suspect a serious GLP‑1 adverse event
If you see a patient like this, be meticulous. In the chart and in any safety report include: patient age/sex/comorbidities; exact drug name (generic and brand), dose, route, date/time of each administration and lot/serial number if available; indication; concomitant medications (especially statins, herbal supplements like red yeast rice, antifungals, antiretrovirals); precise timeline of symptom onset (GI, weakness, bulbar symptoms), hospital admissions and treatments; serial labs (CK, AST/ALT, creatinine, electrolytes, troponin/proBNP), urinalysis/myoglobin if available; imaging (muscle MRI, cardiac MRI, echo); biopsy reports and pathology slides if possible; autoantibody panels and results; immunosuppressive therapy given and timing (steroids, IVIG, other agents) and clinical response; whether drug was discontinued and whether any inadvertent re‑exposure occurred. Attach discharge summaries, lab trend graphs, and pathology reports. Obtain patient consent for de‑identified reporting or publication.

## Where and how to report (practical channels)
Do not assume someone else will report. Channels include national regulatory authorities: in the US use FDA MedWatch; in the EU EudraVigilance; in the UK the Yellow Card scheme; in Canada Health Canada’s system; and internationally your national pharmacovigilance centre reports to WHO VigiBase. Also notify the manufacturer (Novo Nordisk for semaglutide brands). Mark the report as “serious” when relevant (hospitalization, life‑threatening, disability). Provide all attached documents and the precise chronology. If you are in an academic center, consider parallel reporting to institutional pharmacovigilance/med safety teams and pursue a case report or letter to a journal (with patient consent) to increase visibility.

## How to talk to patients without fear‑mongering
Most patients starting GLP‑1 RAs will tolerate them well and benefit from metabolic and weight outcomes. Your counseling should be pragmatic and proportionate. A short script: “This medicine commonly causes nausea and sometimes mild GI upset. Very rarely, there have been reports of serious muscle problems. Watch for new, progressive weakness (especially trouble rising from a chair or climbing stairs), difficulty swallowing, changes in your voice, very dark urine or severe muscle pain. If you get any of those, stop the medication and contact us urgently so we can check a blood test (CK) and arrange evaluation.” Emphasize the rarity of severe events, that most side effects are mild, and that prompt reporting and evaluation mitigates risk.

## CK testing — baseline or symptom‑triggered?
There is no current evidence supporting routine baseline CK measurement for all patients starting GLP‑1 therapy. A pragmatic approach many clinicians use is to obtain baseline CK in patients with risk factors: prior unexplained CK elevations, known myopathy or neuromuscular disease, or concurrent high‑risk medications (notably statins or other myotoxins). For all patients, check CK promptly if they develop muscle symptoms, weakness or dark urine. Clinically concerning thresholds commonly used in practice are CK values in the thousands or >10× upper limit of normal (both trigger urgent evaluation for rhabdomyolysis); persistent very high CK with progressive weakness despite stopping the drug should prompt a myositis/IMNM workup (autoantibodies, MRI, biopsy) and specialty involvement.

## When to stop the GLP‑1, admit and escalate
If a patient develops new progressive symmetric proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, dysphonia), respiratory compromise or very high CK, stop the GLP‑1 immediately. If there is significant weakness, bulbar involvement, AKI or very high CK, admit, check CK, renal function and electrolytes, and consult neurology/rheumatology. If biopsy or clinical criteria fit IMNM, immunotherapy (high‑dose steroids, often with IVIG and/or steroid‑sparing agents) is the accepted approach and may be lifesaving. If the patient is on a statin, stop the statin while workup is ongoing and check anti‑HMGCR antibodies.

## From single case to signal — practical next steps for clinicians and researchers
Clinicians: report the event, collect similar cases, and coordinate with institutional safety offices. Pharmacovigilance teams and researchers: perform disproportionality analyses in VigiBase, FAERS and national databases, and design pharmacoepidemiologic studies (cohort or case‑control) to estimate risk. Manufacturers and regulators: review aggregated reports, consider case definitions and post‑marketing studies. Importantly, until more data exist, weigh the well‑documented benefits of GLP‑1 RAs against rare potential harms, and maintain vigilance.

## Clinical pearls and take‑home points
- A single carefully documented case can start a pharmacovigilance signal — report it (FDA MedWatch, Yellow Card, EudraVigilance or your national system) and notify the manufacturer.
- Use WHO–UMC causality reasoning: time course, plausibility, dechallenge/rechallenge (rechallenge is usually unethical for severe events), and absence of alternative explanations guide “probable” vs “possible.”
- Document drug details (brand, dose, lot), exact timing, serial CK and labs, biopsy/pathology, autoantibodies, treatments and outcomes — attach reports when you file.
- Don’t do routine CK for everyone starting a GLP‑1; consider baseline CK in high‑risk patients and check CK promptly for any new muscle symptoms.
- Stop the GLP‑1 and escalate care (admit, check CK/renal function, involve neurology/rheumatology) for progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, respiratory compromise or very high CK.
- Be balanced when counselling patients: emphasize common benign GI side effects, but give clear, actionable instructions for rare but serious muscle or bulbar symptoms.

If you see anything similar: document precisely, report promptly, and get multidisciplinary help. One well‑constructed report may protect the next patient.
