Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult with hyperglycemia, negative islet autoantibodies (GAD and IA-2), BMI around 25 kg/m2, glycosuria without ketoacidosis, preserved glucagon-stimulated C-peptide, and a multigenerational history of early-onset diabetes, which features most strongly support HNF1A-MODY over type 1 or type 2 diabetes at presentation?

Most convincing features that point to HNF1A‑MODY in this presentation are (in descending order of diagnostic weight): a multigenerational, early‑onset family history of diabetes; absence of islet autoantibodies; preserved stimulated C‑peptide; and glycosuria out of proportion to ketosis or insulin dependence — all seen in this case.

Why each feature matters (and how it applies here)
- Strong autosomal‑dominant family history: HNF1A‑MODY classically shows vertical transmission of early‑onset diabetes. In this patient three successive relatives (maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in late 20s, younger brother at age 20) had early diabetes — the single most powerful clinical clue favoring MODY over T1D or typical T2D.
- Negative pancreatic autoantibodies (GAD, IA‑2): absence of autoimmune markers argues against type 1 diabetes at presentation. This patient was antibody‑negative, which, when coupled with the family history, markedly increases the pretest probability of MODY (recognizing that a minority of T1D patients can be antibody‑negative early on).
- Preserved stimulated C‑peptide: a glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L indicates retained β‑cell function inconsistent with insulin‑deficient T1D. This patient’s stimulated C‑peptide rose to 0.83 nmol/L — supportive of non‑autoimmune, progressive β‑cell dysfunction as seen in HNF1A‑MODY.
- Glycosuria without ketoacidosis and without insulin dependence: HNF1A mutations lower SGLT2 expression and the renal glucose threshold, so glycosuria may occur at relatively modest plasma glucose levels. The patient had glycosuria but no DKA, a pattern that fits HNF1A dysfunction and is less typical of new, severe T1D.
- Lack of overt insulin‑resistance phenotype: a BMI of ~24.6 kg/m2 and no acanthosis nigricans argue against classic young‑onset T2D driven by obesity/insulin resistance (though this is not definitive in isolation).

Caveats and next steps
- No single clinical feature is diagnostic; early T1D can be antibody‑negative with preserved C‑peptide, and family clustering can reflect T2D. The combination of multigenerational early onset + negative antibodies + preserved stimulated C‑peptide + glycosuria is what most strongly points to HNF1A‑MODY in this vignette.
- Genetic testing (NGS panel) is required for confirmation — as in this case, which identified a pathogenic HNF1A variant (p.N270S). The diagnosis has immediate therapeutic implications (e.g., often sulfonylurea responsiveness, alternatives such as GLP‑1RA, and caution with SGLT2 inhibitors).

### [Morning Report] How do you interpret a glucagon-stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L in a newly diagnosed young adult with hyperglycemia, and how should this influence initial insulin use and subsequent de-escalation once a monogenic etiology is confirmed?

Bottom line
A glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L (≈2.5 ng/mL) demonstrates meaningful residual β‑cell secretion. In a young symptomatic patient with negative islet autoantibodies and a strong family history, that result argues against immediate, lifelong type 1 insulin dependence and supports a high pretest probability of MODY (in this case HNF1A). Starting short‑term insulin to treat marked hyperglycaemia was appropriate; once a monogenic diagnosis is confirmed and glycaemia is improving, a supervised, early de‑escalation from insulin to MODY‑tailored therapy is reasonable and often successful.

Why: physiology and thresholds
C‑peptide is secreted equimolarly with endogenous insulin and the glucagon stimulation test (measuring peak C‑peptide at ~6 minutes) probes residual secretory capacity. Many centres use a stimulated C‑peptide threshold of roughly >0.6 nmol/L to indicate preserved β‑cell function; values well below that (eg, <0.2–0.3 nmol/L) are more consistent with severe insulin deficiency as seen in established type 1 diabetes. The patient’s rise from 0.39 → 0.83 nmol/L therefore indicates moderate reserve — enough endogenous insulin to make permanent insulin dependence unlikely and to anticipate responsiveness to insulin secretagogues used in HNF1A‑MODY.

How this should influence initial insulin use
When patients present with symptomatic hyperglycaemia (this case: HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, weight loss) it is appropriate to begin therapy that rapidly reverses glucotoxicity and protects the patient from acute metabolic complications. A modest basal insulin regimen (the case used degludec 8 U/day) with metformin is a pragmatic initial strategy while autoimmune serologies and genetic testing are pending. The preserved stimulated C‑peptide argues that insulin is likely only temporary — but it does not contraindicate its short‑term use to achieve metabolic stabilization.

Principles for de‑escalation after confirming HNF1A‑MODY
1) Use the whole clinical picture: preserved stimulated C‑peptide, negative GADA/IA‑2, and a pathogenic HNF1A variant together justify a trial off insulin.  
2) Choose the targeted alternative: historically low‑dose sulfonylureas are first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY because they augment insulin release; GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an effective and safer alternative (lower hypoglycemia risk) — the case successfully switched to dulaglutide with excellent control.  
3) Practical tapering: if prandial insulin is present, stop prandial doses first and maintain/slowly reduce basal; if only low‑dose basal is used (as in the case), many teams will stop basal after starting the alternative agent with close glucose monitoring. A more cautious approach is to reduce basal by 20–30% every 1–3 days while checking fasting and postprandial glucoses.  
4) Monitoring and safety net: intensify SMBG for several days (fasting + postprandial), review ketone testing if hyperglycemia persists, and reassess HbA1c at ~3 months. Reinstitute insulin promptly if hyperglycaemia becomes uncontrolled or if C‑peptide falls.  
5) Avoid routine use of SGLT‑2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY without caution, because HNF1A‑driven reduction in renal SGLT2 expression increases glycosuria and may heighten the risk of dehydration and ketoacidosis (a concern raised in the report).

How the case illustrates these principles
The team appropriately started modest basal insulin plus metformin/saxagliptin to manage symptomatic hyperglycaemia. The stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L and negative antibodies raised strong suspicion for MODY, and genetic testing confirmed an HNF1A pathogenic variant. On that basis they discontinued insulin at one month and started weekly dulaglutide; HbA1c fell to 6.0% at 6 months and therapy was later simplified to metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor for maintenance — a real‑world example that preserved stimulated C‑peptide allows early, supervised withdrawal of insulin in HNF1A‑MODY.

Teaching pearl
Interpret stimulated C‑peptide in context (clinical severity, antibodies, family history). A value around 0.8 nmol/L indicates useful endogenous insulin and should prompt consideration of temporary insulin for stabilization rather than automatic lifelong insulin; when a monogenic diagnosis is confirmed, transition to MODY‑specific oral or incretin therapies—with close monitoring—is usually possible and often preferable.

### [Pathophysiology] What beta-cell and renal tubular effects of HNF1A loss of function explain progressive hyperglycemia and a low renal threshold for glucose (glycosuria) in HNF1A-MODY?

Teaching point — a single transcription factor (HNF1A) explains both the progressive insulin deficiency and the characteristic low renal glucose threshold in HNF1A‑MODY because it drives key genes in pancreatic β‑cells and in proximal renal tubule cells.

Pathophysiology in the β cell (why hyperglycaemia progresses)
- HNF1A is a transcriptional activator in pancreatic β‑cells. Loss‑of‑function reduces expression of genes required for glucose sensing and insulin production — classically SLC2A2 (GLUT2), insulin itself, and glycolytic enzymes such as pyruvate kinase. 
- Reduced GLUT2 and diminished glycolysis → less glucose entry and less ATP generation in response to glucose. Lower ATP/ADP means less closure of KATP channels and therefore impaired glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion (especially postprandial). Over time reduced insulin gene expression and chronic secretory stress produce progressive β‑cell failure and rising glucose/HbA1c.
- The case illustrates this: the novel heterozygous p.N270S HNF1A variant (in the DNA‑binding domain) produced an inadequate stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L) with marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, 2‑hr OGTT 19.8 mmol/L), consistent with HNF1A‑mediated loss of β‑cell glucose sensing and insulin output.

Pathophysiology in the kidney (why glycosuria occurs at relatively low glucose)
- In the proximal tubule HNF1A transactivates SLC5A2, the gene encoding SGLT2, the main apical sodium‑glucose cotransporter responsible for bulk renal glucose reabsorption. HNF1A LOF → lower SGLT2 expression → reduced Tm for glucose and a lower renal threshold → glucosuria at plasma glucose concentrations that would not cause glycosuria in people with intact SGLT2.
- The report specifically links the patient’s glycosuria to reduced SGLT2 expression and cautions that additional pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition in these patients can precipitate marked glucosuria, osmotic dehydration and even ketoacidosis.

Clinical correlation and implications
- Because the primary defect is impaired glucose sensing (upstream of the KATP channel) rather than a primary channelopathy, patients are typically very sensitive to low‑dose sulfonylureas (which close KATP channels and bypass the defective ATP signal). This mechanistic insight explains why sulfonylureas have been the historical first‑line oral therapy and why GLP‑1 receptor agonists (used in this case) are reasonable alternatives as β‑cell function declines.
- Recognising the dual phenotype (progressive insulin secretory failure plus low renal threshold with early glycosuria) should prompt genetic testing for HNF1A‑MODY because the diagnosis changes therapy choices and family counselling — as the authors demonstrated by switching treatment after genetic confirmation and achieving marked HbA1c improvement.

Bottom line: HNF1A loss‑of‑function causes progressive hyperglycaemia by downregulating GLUT2, insulin and glycolytic enzymes in β‑cells (impairing glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion) and causes low renal glucose threshold by downregulating SGLT2 in proximal tubules (producing glycosuria).

### [Clinical Pearls] When evaluating early-onset diabetes, when should you order MODY genetic testing, and how should the European-derived MODY probability calculator be interpreted or modified in non-European populations?

Bottom line
Order genetic testing for MODY whenever early‑onset diabetes shows features of non‑autoimmune, non‑insulin‑resistant disease with an autosomal‑dominant pattern — the diagnosis changes treatment and prompts family testing. Use the Exeter (MODY) probability calculator as a useful adjunct, but do not let its output replace careful clinical and biochemical triage in non‑European patients: the tool was developed in White Europeans and may overestimate probability outside that population, so interpret its scores in the context of autoantibody results, C‑peptide, family history and local epidemiology.

When to suspect and test for MODY (practical triggers)
- Age and family pattern: diabetes diagnosed in adolescence or young adulthood (classically <25 years, but many present later) with diabetes in multiple successive generations (suggesting autosomal dominant transmission).
- Autoantibodies: negative islet autoantibodies (GAD65, IA‑2; add ZnT8 where available) make type 1 diabetes less likely.
- Endogenous insulin: preserved stimulated C‑peptide (clinical cutoffs vary; many centers use stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L or levels that are inconsistent with absolute insulin deficiency) argues against T1DM.
- Lack of insulin resistance: absence of obesity, acanthosis nigricans or marked dyslipidaemia; however, rising population obesity reduces the specificity of this sign.
- Phenotypic clues: disproportionate post‑prandial hyperglycaemia, marked glycosuria at modest plasma glucose (HNF1A effect on SGLT2), unusually strong response to low‑dose sulfonylureas, or other organ clues.
- A reasonable workflow: in a young patient with diabetes, obtain autoantibodies and a stimulated C‑peptide; if antibodies are negative and C‑peptide preserved (and/or there is multigenerational family history or renal glycosuria), proceed to genetic testing (targeted MODY/monogenic diabetes NGS panel).

How the case illustrates this
- The patient had multigenerational early‑onset diabetes, negative GAD/IA‑2 antibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L), glycosuria and no clear insulin resistance. These features prompted an NGS MODY panel that identified a novel HNF1A variant (p.N270S) classified by ACMG criteria as pathogenic/likely pathogenic after segregation and in‑silico evidence. The molecular diagnosis changed therapy (stopped insulin; GLP‑1 agonist and then oral therapy) and improved HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0%.

Interpreting the Exeter MODY probability calculator in non‑European populations
- What it is: the calculator estimates the pretest probability of a monogenic cause using variables derived from a White European cohort (age at diagnosis, BMI, family history, HbA1c, treatment).
- Limitation in non‑Europeans: because baseline prevalence of young‑onset T2D, patterns of family history and body habitus differ by ethnicity, the calculator’s positive predictive value and calibration can be distorted — several groups (including the authors of this case) have observed overestimation in non‑White populations.
- Practical guidance:
  - Use the calculator as one input, not the arbiter. A high calculated probability in a non‑European patient should prompt corroborating tests (negative antibodies, preserved C‑peptide, multigenerational pedigree) before reflexive sequencing.
  - Conversely, do not exclude genetic testing solely because the calculator yields a low/moderate probability if strong clinical evidence exists (robust autosomal family history, atypical features, or biochemical clues).
  - Where available, incorporate local prevalence data or published cohorts from the same ethnic group to recalibrate your clinical threshold for testing.
  - Given falling costs of NGS and the high clinical yield when suspicion is concordant, many centers will proceed to a targeted monogenic diabetes panel when clinical suspicion is moderate-to-high despite calculator uncertainty.

Practical final points
- Genetic testing (NGS panel) is the diagnostic standard; variant interpretation should follow ACMG guidelines and include family segregation when possible (as in this case).
- A confirmed HNF1A diagnosis has immediate therapeutic and prognostic implications (sulfonylurea sensitivity or GLP‑1 use, caution with SGLT2 inhibitors because of low renal SGLT2 expression, family cascade testing).
- In short: test when clinical and biochemical data point to monogenic disease; use the Exeter calculator as a helpful but not definitive tool in non‑European patients — require concordant evidence or local validation before relying on its probabilistic output.

### [Clinical Pearls] A novel heterozygous HNF1A missense variant (for example, p.N270S) is detected on an NGS panel in a patient with a MODY phenotype. Which ACMG criteria and supporting data (segregation, functional domain, in silico prediction, population frequency, phenotype) would you use to classify its pathogenicity?

Short answer
For a novel HNF1A missense (eg, p.N270S) in a patient with a MODY phenotype you would apply ACMG criteria that emphasize: location in a critical domain (PM1), absence from population databases (PM2), supportive computational evidence (PP3), segregation with disease in the family (PP1), and a highly specific phenotype (PP4). PM1 + PM2 + PP3 + PP1 + PP4 (± PP2) is a combination that generally meets ACMG rules for Likely Pathogenic. Functional data (PS3) or stronger segregation/independent observations (PS4/PP1_strong) would be needed to move to Pathogenic.

Why — mapping the evidence to ACMG (with the case supporting each item)
- PM1 (Moderate): the variant lies in the DNA‑binding domain of HNF1A (exon 4). HNF1A pathogenic missense changes are enriched in the DNA‑binding domain and that domain is functionally critical for transcriptional control of insulin/GLUT2/SGLT2 (the case explicitly notes the variant is in the DNA‑binding domain).
- PM2 (Moderate): the variant is novel/absent from population and curated variant databases (the authors report it was not in ClinVar/VarSome/literature). For a rare, dominant, early‑onset disease, absence from large population datasets is meaningful.
- PP3 (Supporting): multiple in silico tools predict a deleterious effect — the report cites a very high AlphaMissense score (0.995). Per ACMG, computational evidence is supporting only.
- PP1 (Supporting; can be upgraded with more data): the variant “segregates with diabetes in the family” (maternal grandmother, mother, younger brother with early onset disease). Co‑segregation in multiple affected relatives supports PP1; the strength increases with number of informative meioses or an LOD score per ClinGen guidance (genotype the relatives to quantify).
- PP4 (Supporting): the proband’s phenotype is highly consistent with HNF1A‑MODY — young adult onset, pronounced post‑prandial hyperglycaemia and glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide — all features cited in the case.
- PP2 (Supporting, optional): HNF1A is a gene in which pathogenic missense is a common mechanism and the gene has relatively low benign missense rate; this can add supporting weight.

How these criteria combine
- Under ACMG combinatorial rules, 2 Moderate (PM1 + PM2) plus ≥2–3 Supporting (PP1, PP3, PP4, ±PP2) meets the threshold for Likely Pathogenic. That is the conclusion reached by the authors for p.N270S.

What would upgrade to “Pathogenic”
- Well‑established functional evidence that the variant impairs HNF1A function (PS3) — e.g., DNA‑binding or transactivation assays showing loss of function.
- Stronger segregation evidence (PP1_moderate/strong using LOD thresholds) or the same variant observed in multiple unrelated affected probands (PS4).
- Same‑residue pathogenic missense (PM5) if another pathogenic missense at N270 were reported.

Practical next steps (clinical and laboratory)
- Confirm genotype in available affected (and unaffected) relatives to document segregation and calculate LOD/inform strength of PP1.
- Pursue a well‑validated functional assay (transactivation/DNA‑binding) if available to obtain PS3 evidence.
- Deposit the variant and supporting data in ClinVar and re‑evaluate as new cases or functional data accumulate.
- Clinically: a classification of Likely Pathogenic supports cascade testing and gene‑directed management (as in the case: insulin was withdrawn and GLP‑1 agonist/metformin used with marked HbA1c improvement; sulfonylurea/GLP‑1 options and avoidance of SGLT2 inhibitors should be considered).

Clinical pearl
When a novel HNF1A missense variant is found in a patient whose presentation (early non‑autoimmune diabetes, glycosuria, family history, preserved C‑peptide) fits MODY, apply PM1 (critical domain) and PM2 (absent from population data) plus PP3 (strong computational score), PP1 (segregation) and PP4 (phenotype). Together these typically justify a Likely Pathogenic call; targeted family genotyping and functional testing are the two highest‑yield ways to upgrade certainty to Pathogenic.

### [Management] For confirmed HNF1A-MODY, what is the preferred first-line pharmacotherapy, in whom would you favor a GLP-1 receptor agonist over a sulfonylurea, and why should SGLT2 inhibitors be used with caution in this genotype?

Short answer
- First-line pharmacotherapy for confirmed HNF1A‑MODY is historically a low‑dose sulfonylurea because the primary defect is an insulin‑secretory failure that responds well to KATP‑channel stimulation.  
- Favor a GLP‑1 receptor agonist instead of a sulfonylurea when there is a high risk of hypoglycaemia, need for weight loss/obesity, progressive β‑cell decline or when sulfonylurea intolerance/failure has occurred — provided there is some preserved endogenous insulin secretion. The case illustrates this: the patient had a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L and, after insulin was stopped and dulaglutide started, HbA1c fell from 10.2% to 6.0% at 6 months.  
- Use SGLT2 inhibitors with caution because HNF1A regulates renal SGLT2 expression; HNF1A mutations lower the renal glucose threshold (baseline glycosuria), so SGLT2 inhibition can produce exaggerated glycosuria, volume depletion, genital infections and precipitate (euglycaemic) diabetic ketoacidosis. Reports (and this review) describe severe dehydration and DKA in HNF1A‑MODY patients on SGLT2 inhibitors.

Clinical reasoning and practical points
- Pathophysiology explains therapy choice: HNF1A is a transcription factor required for normal β‑cell insulin secretion (and for expression of GLUT2 and SGLT2). The dominant clinical lesion is progressive β‑cell secretory failure rather than insulin resistance. Sulfonylureas directly boost insulin release by closing KATP channels and have long been effective as first‑line therapy in HNF1A‑MODY patients with preserved β‑cell function.  
- When to prefer a GLP‑1 receptor agonist: GLP‑1 RAs augment glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, suppress glucagon, cause weight loss and have a lower risk of hypoglycaemia than sulfonylureas. A randomised crossover trial in HNF1A‑MODY showed comparable glycaemic lowering for liraglutide and glimepiride but less hypoglycaemia with liraglutide. Thus choose a GLP‑1 RA when the patient is obese, at increased hypoglycaemia risk, has higher baseline HbA1c that requires stronger therapy, or when you wish to avoid SU‑related weight gain or hypoglycaemia. Ensure some residual insulin secretion (for example a stimulated C‑peptide in the case was 0.83 nmol/L) before stopping insulin for a GLP‑1 RA transition.  
- Why be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors: HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression in proximal tubules → lower renal threshold and baseline glycosuria. Pharmacologic SGLT2 blockade on top of reduced endogenous SGLT2 can cause marked osmotic diuresis and ketogenesis. Clinical reports (and the discussion in this case) warn of dehydration, genital infection and frank or euglycaemic DKA after SGLT2 use in HNF1A‑MODY. In addition, efficacy for glucose lowering may be blunted if SGLT2 expression is already low. If an SGLT2 inhibitor is contemplated (for a separate cardiorenal indication), it should be done only after careful risk–benefit assessment, with patient education, ketone monitoring and strict sick‑day rules.

Practical algorithm from the case
- Acute severe hyperglycaemia: short course insulin to rapidly control glucose (as in this patient).  
- After genetic confirmation and demonstration of residual β‑cell function, transition off insulin to either low‑dose sulfonylurea or a GLP‑1 RA depending on hypoglycaemia risk, weight goals and patient preference. In this case the team used dulaglutide plus metformin with excellent response, then simplified to metformin + a DPP‑4 inhibitor for maintenance.  
- Avoid routine SGLT2 inhibitor use in HNF1A‑MODY; reserve for exceptional indications with close monitoring.

Takeaway: confirm the diagnosis genetically, assess residual β‑cell function (C‑peptide), start with low‑dose sulfonylurea as the classic first‑line choice but use GLP‑1 RAs when hypoglycaemia, weight or declining β‑cell reserve make them preferable — and avoid or use extreme caution with SGLT2 inhibitors because of reduced renal SGLT2 expression and the attendant risks.

### [Management] What is the rationale for starting basal insulin at diagnosis in some patients with very high HbA1c and suspected MODY, and what clinical or laboratory criteria support safely discontinuing insulin after confirmation of HNF1A-MODY?

Short answer
- Starting basal insulin is a pragmatic, temporary strategy when a young patient with suspected MODY presents with marked hyperglycaemia (symptoms, weight loss, HbA1c ≈10%, fasting glucose ≈12 mmol/L in this case). It rapidly relieves symptoms, prevents ketosis, and reverses glucotoxicity so endogenous β‑cell function can recover while diagnostic testing (autoantibodies, C‑peptide, genetic testing) is completed.  
- Insulin can be safely withdrawn in HNF1A‑MODY when the clinical picture and laboratory data show preserved endogenous insulin secretion (stimulated C‑peptide above a conventional threshold), negative islet autoantibodies, low insulin requirements with improving glycaemia, and confirmatory HNF1A genetics — with a structured, closely monitored trial off insulin and initiation of appropriate non‑insulin therapy (sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA).

Why start basal insulin (rationale)
- Marked hyperglycaemia causes glucotoxicity that suppresses β‑cell insulin secretion; prompt glucose lowering (often easiest and safest with insulin) can restore insulin secretory responsiveness and relieve catabolism and symptoms.  
- Diagnostic uncertainty at presentation is common: pending autoantibody and C‑peptide results do not exclude type 1 diabetes. Short‑term insulin protects against progression to ketosis/DKA if the patient actually has autoimmune insulin deficiency.  
- A low fixed basal insulin dose (as in this patient’s degludec 8 U/day) plus oral agents is often sufficient to control fasting hyperglycaemia and is readily tapered once the diagnosis and β‑cell reserve are clarified.

Which findings support stopping insulin (clinical/laboratory criteria)
Use a combination of clinical, biochemical and genetic information rather than a single test:
- Preserved stimulated C‑peptide (case example: glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide rose from 0.39 to 0.83 nmol/L). Many centres use a stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L as evidence of meaningful endogenous secretion.  
- Negative islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8) supporting a non‑autoimmune process (the patient’s antibodies were negative).  
- Low insulin requirements and early improvement on therapy (this patient required only 8 U basal and HbA1c fell from 10.2% to 9.1% in 1 month). Very low insulin needs argue against absolute insulin deficiency.  
- Confirmatory genetic testing for HNF1A‑MODY (the patient had a pathogenic HNF1A variant) — this changes expected responsiveness (classically good response to sulfonylureas; GLP‑1 RAs are also effective) and supports a trial off insulin.  
- Absence of prior DKA and clinical stability.

How to withdraw insulin safely (practical approach)
- Only after you have the above supportive data and the patient understands the plan. Start or optimize the intended oral/GLP‑1 therapy first (for HNF1A‑MODY: low‑dose sulfonylurea is classically first‑line; GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an acceptable, often safer alternative and were used successfully in this case).  
- Taper basal insulin progressively while monitoring capillary glucose (fasting and postprandial) and ketones if symptomatic. Do not abruptly stop insulin in a patient with uncertain β‑cell reserve without close monitoring.  
- Reassess glycaemia frequently (home glucose checks, early clinic/telephone review) and repeat HbA1c/C‑peptide as needed. If glucose control deteriorates or ketones appear, reinstitute insulin and re‑evaluate.

Clinical caveats
- HNF1A‑MODY is a progressive β‑cell disorder; some patients will require insulin over time. A successful discontinuation now does not preclude future insulin therapy.  
- Exercise caution with SGLT‑2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY because of baseline glycosuria (reduced SGLT2 expression) and reported dehydration/ketosis risk.  
- Genetic confirmation is pivotal for precision therapy and family screening; it also legitimises a therapeutic shift away from insulin when the laboratory and clinical data permit.

Case tie‑in
- In this report, basal insulin was started because of symptomatic severe hyperglycaemia. The patient’s stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L), negative autoantibodies, low insulin need, and the identification of a pathogenic HNF1A variant provided the rationale to stop insulin and switch to dulaglutide plus metformin, with subsequent excellent glycaemic control (HbA1c 6.0% at 6 months). This sequence illustrates the practical and safe use of temporary insulin to manage acute hyperglycaemia while using C‑peptide, antibody testing and genetics to guide definitive, personalised therapy.

### [Management] After confirming HNF1A-MODY in a proband, what are best practices for cascade testing and counseling of family members, including timing of screening and implications for management if they test positive?

Bottom line
- When HNF1A‑MODY is confirmed in a proband you should offer informed, targeted cascade testing to at‑risk relatives (first‑degree first), with pre‑ and post‑test genetic counselling. Testing should be arranged promptly for symptomatic relatives and offered to asymptomatic adults (and to children when the result would change management). A positive result alters surveillance and frequently changes therapy (often from insulin or empiric T2D regimens to sulfonylurea/GLP‑1–based strategies) and carries a 50% transmission risk to offspring.

Why this matters (clinical reasoning grounded in the case)
- HNF1A‑MODY is autosomal dominant with variable age at onset but often presents in adolescence/young adulthood; the proband’s family history (maternal grandmother, mother, younger brother with early‑onset diabetes) illustrates the multigenerational transmission. Identifying the same familial HNF1A variant (in this paper the novel p.N270S, HNF1A:c.809A>G) in relatives 1) confirms who is at genetically conferred risk, 2) helps reclassify treatment (the proband was able to stop insulin and do well on a GLP‑1 agonist ± metformin), and 3) can strengthen pathogenicity evidence by segregation analysis for novel variants.

Practical cascade testing and counselling steps
1. Confirm the proband’s result in a certified laboratory and document the exact variant and its ACMG classification. For novel variants, explain limits of certainty and that segregation data may help.
2. Provide genetic counselling before testing: explain autosomal dominant inheritance, 50% risk to first‑degree relatives, variable penetrance/age at onset, and local implications (privacy/insurance) as appropriate.
3. Offer targeted (single‑variant) testing to first‑degree relatives (parents, siblings, children). Targeted testing is rapid and cost‑effective compared with full panels; it should be performed in an accredited laboratory.
4. Prioritize testing timing:
   - Symptomatic relatives or those with hyperglycaemia: test immediately.
   - Asymptomatic adults: offer testing at the earliest opportunity because a positive result will often change management.
   - Children/adolescents: because HNF1A often manifests in adolescence and treatment decisions can be affected, offer testing in childhood if parents/guardians want it or if early diagnosis would change care; if parents defer genetic testing, perform biochemical screening (annual fasting glucose/HbA1c or OGTT if borderline) starting in adolescence.
5. If a familial variant is classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS), do not use it for predictive cascade testing; instead continue clinical surveillance and consider segregation studies or functional testing.

What to do if a relative tests positive
- Baseline evaluation: fasting glucose, HbA1c (or 2‑hr OGTT if concern for postprandial glycaemia), consider stimulated C‑peptide to document residual β‑cell function, and baseline screening for complications if diabetes is present (urine albumin, retinal exam as indicated).
- Initial treatment and monitoring:
  - If hyperglycaemic: first‑line pharmacotherapy for many HNF1A patients is low‑dose sulfonylurea because of marked insulin secretory responsiveness; GLP‑1 receptor agonists are a reasonable and often safer alternative (lower hypoglycaemia risk) as illustrated by the proband who transitioned successfully from insulin to dulaglutide + metformin with HbA1c fall to 6.0% at 6 months. Choice should be individualized based on glycaemia, C‑peptide, comorbidities and hypoglycaemia risk.
  - If on insulin empirically, consider reassessing need for continued insulin after genetic confirmation — only if C‑peptide and clinical course support preserved endogenous insulin secretion and glycaemia can be maintained off insulin (as in this case).
  - Avoid routine use of SGLT2 inhibitors without careful consideration: HNF1A mutation–associated reduced SGLT2 expression may exaggerate glycosuria and, per the case discussion, could increase risk of dehydration and DKA in some patients.
- Follow‑up: annual glycaemic monitoring (HbA1c ± fasting glucose) for mutation carriers; monitor for progressive β‑cell failure with periodic C‑peptide if management decisions hinge on endogenous insulin secretion. Screen for microvascular complications once diabetes is established per local guidelines.

If a relative tests negative
- Reassure them that they do not carry the familial HNF1A variant and do not require MODY‑directed management. Continue routine diabetes risk counselling and screening per population risk. If clinical suspicion for another monogenic aetiology remains, consider broader genetic testing.

Using family testing to refine variant interpretation
- For novel variants like p.N270S, segregation of the variant with diabetes in multiple affected relatives supports pathogenicity (and was used in this report). Encourage family testing not only for clinical care but also to help classify novel variants.

Communication and logistics
- Encourage the proband to inform at‑risk relatives; offer clinician‑assisted contact if appropriate and permitted. Use targeted testing in accredited labs, and document results in the medical record so that treatment changes are traceable.

Takeaway applied to this case
- For this family (proband with novel pathogenic HNF1A p.N270S and multigenerational early‑onset diabetes), the practical next steps are targeted testing of first‑degree relatives (mother, brother) with counselling; if positive, obtain baseline glycaemia and C‑peptide and consider switching empiric insulin/T2D regimens to sulfonylurea or GLP‑1–based therapy and institute regular surveillance — an approach that both optimizes therapy (as it did for the proband) and refines the genetic interpretation through segregation.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a newly diagnosed young adult with diabetes who already shows markedly reduced sudomotor function on electrochemical skin conductance testing, what is the differential diagnosis for early autonomic dysfunction, and what additional evaluations would you obtain to attribute neuropathy to diabetes versus alternative causes?

Teaching point (short answer)
A markedly reduced electrochemical skin conductance (Sudoscan) in a young person with newly recognized HNF1A‑MODY most likely reflects small‑fiber autonomic injury from hyperglycaemia, but the finding is non‑specific and mandates a short, systematic evaluation for alternative, potentially treatable causes (inherited, immune, infectious, toxic/metabolic, paraneoplastic). The workup should (1) confirm and quantify autonomic/small‑fiber involvement, (2) look for other diabetic microvascular complications and a plausible glycaemic exposure, and (3) screen for alternate causes with focused laboratory, neurophysiologic and—when indicated—genetic or tissue testing.

Why this matters in this case
The patient’s HbA1c of 10.2% at presentation provides a biologically plausible explanation for early small‑fiber/autonomic injury (hyperglycaemia → microvascular ischemia, oxidative stress and metabolic injury to unmyelinated C‑fibers that mediate sweating). But important caveats in this case are: (a) Sudoscan is a screening tool with false positives and site‑dependent variability (hands vs feet); (b) there is no retinopathy or albuminuria yet (absence of other microvascular disease does not exclude diabetic small‑fiber neuropathy but lowers prior probability of long‑standing diabetic microangiopathy); and (c) HNF1A mutations themselves are not classically associated with primary autonomic neuropathy—so alternative causes must be excluded.

Differential diagnosis (concise)
- Diabetic small‑fiber autonomic neuropathy (length‑dependent small‑fiber involvement; most likely given severe hyperglycaemia).
- Hereditary/inherited autonomic neuropathies: familial amyloid polyneuropathy (ATTRv), Fabry disease, familial sensory‑autonomic neuropathies. Look for family history of neuropathy, cardiomyopathy, renal disease, characteristic skin stigmata.
- Autoimmune autonomic ganglionopathy or systemic autoimmune small‑fiber neuropathy (e.g., Sjögren’s).
- Paraneoplastic autonomic neuropathy (anti‑Hu, etc.) — consider with subacute course and systemic symptoms.
- Infectious causes: HIV, hepatitis C, syphilis, Lyme.
- Metabolic/nutritional/toxic: vitamin B12 deficiency (MMA), thiamine, alcohol, chemotherapeutic agents, heavy metals.
- Monoclonal gammopathy / paraproteinemic neuropathy, vasculitic neuropathy (mononeuritis multiplex) — usually non‑length dependent and painful.
- Medication effects or other rare genetic causes.

Targeted evaluation to attribute neuropathy to diabetes versus alternatives
1) Confirm and quantify autonomic/small‑fiber dysfunction
   - Repeat/confirm Sudoscan and obtain formal autonomic reflex testing (cardiovagal and adrenergic tests) including heart‑rate variability with deep breathing, Valsalva ratio, tilt‑table testing. These define the extent (cardiac vs vasomotor vs sudomotor) of autonomic failure.
   - QSART (quantitative sudomotor axon reflex test) or thermoregulatory sweat test — QSART is the reference standard for post‑ganglionic sudomotor function.
   - If exclusive small‑fiber suspicion: skin biopsy for intraepidermal nerve‑fiber density (IENFD) to document small‑fiber neuropathy; QST (thermal thresholds) is complementary.
   - Nerve conduction studies/EMG to evaluate large‑fiber involvement (often normal in isolated small‑fiber neuropathy; abnormal NCS suggests a broader neuropathy or alternative cause).

2) Document other diabetes‑related microvascular disease (supporting diabetic etiology)
   - Formal ophthalmologic exam for diabetic retinopathy.
   - Repeat urinary albumin excretion (ACR) and kidney function. Presence of retinopathy/albuminuria increases likelihood that neuropathy is diabetes‑related.

3) Screen for common and treatable alternative causes (baseline labs)
   - CBC, CMP (renal/hepatic), TSH.
   - Vitamin B12 ± methylmalonic acid, folate, thiamine as indicated.
   - Serum protein electrophoresis with immunofixation and serum free light chains (screen for paraprotein).
   - HIV, hepatitis B and C serologies; syphilis serology.
   - ANA, SSA/SSB (for Sjögren’s) and inflammatory markers (ESR/CRP) if autoimmune features.
   - Anti‑ganglionic acetylcholine receptor antibodies if subacute severe autonomic failure is present.
   - Paraneoplastic antibody panel (anti‑Hu etc.) only if clinical suspicion (smoking, weight loss, subacute course).
   - celiac serology if compatible history.

4) Directed testing when indicated by red flags
   - Alpha‑galactosidase A level / GLA gene testing and lyso‑Gb3 for Fabry disease (young male with neuropathic pain, angiokeratomas, renal/cardiac signs).
   - TTR genetic testing and fat‑pad or nerve biopsy for suspected ATTR amyloidosis (family history, cardiomyopathy, autonomic failure).
   - ANCA/cryoglobulins/vasculitis panel when multifocal painful neuropathy or systemic vasculitis signs are present.
   - Consider nerve biopsy only when vasculitis or amyloid is strongly suspected and will alter management.

How to integrate results
- If autonomic reflex testing shows multi‑domain diabetic pattern, NCS normal (or consistent with length‑dependent polyneuropathy), skin biopsy shows reduced IENFD, and alternative screens are negative → diagnosis most consistent with diabetic small‑fiber autonomic neuropathy.
- If testing reveals autoimmune markers, monoclonal protein, infection, gene mutation, or biopsy‑proven amyloid → treat that specific cause (immunotherapy, hematology referral, enzyme replacement/targeted therapy for Fabry/ATTR).
- If results are ambiguous, repeat testing after glycaemic stabilization (this patient improved HbA1c to 6.0% within months) — early glycaemic control can halt progression and sometimes modestly improve small‑fiber function; lack of improvement despite optimized glycaemia should raise suspicion for non‑diabetic etiologies.

Immediate practical steps for this patient
- Measure orthostatic vital signs now; assess symptoms (orthostatic intolerance, GI dysmotility, erectile/bladder dysfunction) and fall risk.
- Order a formal autonomic reflex screen including QSART and heart‑rate variability, NCS/EMG, and skin biopsy if QSART/QST suggest small‑fiber loss.
- Obtain baseline labs as listed above (B12/MMA, TSH, SPEP/IFE, HIV/HCV, ANA/SSA/SSB, syphilis) and perform a dilated retinal exam.
- If autonomic failure is severe or subacute, add anti‑gAChR and paraneoplastic workup; if clinical features suggest Fabry or ATTR proceed to targeted enzyme/genetic testing.

Bottom line
Markedly reduced sudomotor function in a young person with newly diagnosed HNF1A‑MODY most plausibly reflects hyperglycaemia‑related small‑fiber autonomic neuropathy given the high HbA1c, but Sudoscan is a screening result only. Confirm with formal autonomic and small‑fiber testing, screen for common metabolic/infectious/paraprotein and autoimmune causes, and look for other diabetic microvascular complications to support causality. The evaluation is pragmatic: confirm dysfunction, exclude treatable mimics, and re‑assess after glycaemic control.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Which clinical and biochemical features help distinguish HNF1A-MODY from GCK-MODY at presentation, and which findings in this case argue against GCK-MODY?

Brief answer
HNF1A‑MODY and GCK‑MODY can both present as early, non‑autoimmune diabetes, but they have different natural histories and biochemical fingerprints. GCK‑MODY produces a lifelong, mild, stable fasting hyperglycaemia (little or no symptoms, small OGTT excursions, modestly raised HbA1c, rarely requires treatment). HNF1A‑MODY produces progressive β‑cell failure with larger postprandial excursions, higher HbA1c, symptomatic hyperglycaemia, glycosuria from a low renal glucose threshold, and often the need for glucose‑lowering therapy. Genetic testing is definitive.

Why these differences occur (pathophysiology, briefly)
- GCK (glucokinase) is the pancreatic β‑cell glucose sensor. Heterozygous loss‑of‑function raises the set‑point for insulin secretion so patients have a mild, stable elevation of fasting glucose but preserved, proportionate insulin secretion and little progression or microvascular risk.
- HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in pancreas, liver and kidney; mutations cause progressive β‑cell dysfunction (reduced insulin and GLUT2 expression) and reduce SGLT2 expression in the kidney → lower renal glucose threshold and glycosuria. Hyperglycaemia is therefore more marked and progressive.

Practical clinical/biochemical discriminators (what to look for)
- Degree of hyperglycaemia and HbA1c: GCK typically causes modest fasting hyperglycaemia (commonly ≈5.5–8.0 mmol/L) and a mildly raised, stable HbA1c (usually not markedly elevated). HNF1A typically presents with higher glucose and HbA1c and a progressive rise over time.
- Symptoms and need for therapy: GCK patients are usually asymptomatic and rarely require pharmacologic therapy outside pregnancy; HNF1A patients are more often symptomatic and eventually require treatment (often very sulfonylurea‑sensitive early on).
- OGTT pattern: GCK produces small postprandial excursions; HNF1A shows large 2‑hour rises after glucose load (pronounced postprandial hyperglycaemia).
- Glycosuria/renal threshold: Glycosuria at relatively low glucose levels is characteristic of HNF1A (reduced SGLT2 expression) and not a feature of GCK.
- C‑peptide and autoantibodies: Both are typically autoantibody‑negative. C‑peptide is preserved early in HNF1A but tends to decline with disease progression; GCK shows appropriately preserved secretion for the degree of hyperglycaemia. Thus C‑peptide alone is not definitive.
- Complications and progression: GCK has a low risk of microvascular complications and a non‑progressive course; HNF1A carries the usual diabetes risks because hyperglycaemia is more marked and progressive.
- Family history: Both are autosomal dominant, so family history is supportive for either—details about severity and need for treatment in relatives can help (mild stable hyperglycaemia in relatives → GCK; early progressive diabetes in relatives → HNF1A).

Which findings in this case argue against GCK‑MODY
Referencing the case data: HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, 2‑hour OGTT 19.8 mmol/L, symptomatic polyuria/polydipsia with weight loss, glycosuria on screening, and a glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide that was borderline (0.83 nmol/L).

Specifically:
1. Marked hyperglycaemia and very high HbA1c (10.2%) — far above the mild, stable elevations typical of GCK‑MODY.
2. Symptomatic presentation (polydipsia, weight loss) and requirement for pharmacologic therapy (initial insulin, then GLP‑1 receptor agonist/metformin) — GCK patients are usually asymptomatic and rarely need glucose‑lowering drugs.
3. Large postprandial excursion on OGTT (2‑hour 19.8 mmol/L) — GCK produces only small OGTT rises.
4. Glycosuria noted at presentation — fits HNF1A biology (reduced SGLT2 and lower renal threshold) and is not a characteristic feature of GCK.
5. Evidence of insufficient/declining β‑cell function (borderline stimulated C‑peptide) — supports progressive β‑cell dysfunction as seen in HNF1A rather than the stable physiology of GCK.

Bottom line
The combination of pronounced, symptomatic hyperglycaemia with large OGTT excursions, glycosuria and the need for glucose‑lowering therapy argues strongly against GCK‑MODY and points to a progressive β‑cell defect such as HNF1A‑MODY — a conclusion that was confirmed by genetic testing in this patient. Genetic testing remains the diagnostic gold standard when clinical features suggest MODY.
