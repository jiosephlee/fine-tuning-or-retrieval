Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Case Presentation and Initial Clinical Clues

A young adult man was referred after routine screening detected glycosuria and hyperglycaemia. He reported recent polydipsia, polyphagia and weight loss but had no features of diabetic ketoacidosis. On examination his BMI was 24.6 kg/m2, blood pressure 112/70 mmHg and heart rate 78 bpm. Notably, there was no acanthosis nigricans or other visible stigmata of insulin resistance.

Laboratory testing documented marked hyperglycaemia: HbA1c 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), fasting plasma glucose 12.4 mmol/L (~223 mg/dL) and a 2-hour 75 g OGTT glucose of 19.8 mmol/L (~357 mg/dL). These results confirm diabetes with prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia. The finding of glycosuria on screening, in the absence of ketoacidosis, is an early clinical clue that can point away from classic type 1 diabetes and toward disorders in renal glucose handling or beta‑cell secretory defects; in HNF1A-MODY, reduced expression of renal SGLT2 lowers the renal glucose threshold and commonly produces glycosuria even when insulin deficiency is not absolute.

The metabolic profile was mixed. HDL was low at 1.01 mmol/L (~39 mg/dL) and triglycerides were elevated at 4.31 mmol/L (~382 mg/dL), compatible with a dyslipidaemic/metabolic syndrome pattern. These laboratory features can confound the picture because dyslipidaemia is common in type 2 diabetes, but the absence of clinical insulin‑resistance signs (no acanthosis, only modestly elevated BMI) makes primary insulin resistance less likely as the dominant mechanism. In practice, rising population rates of overweight and dyslipidaemia mean these findings do not exclude monogenic diabetes.

The family history was striking for early-onset diabetes in successive maternal relatives (maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, younger brother at age 20). This three‑generation pattern of early-onset diabetes strongly suggests autosomal dominant inheritance and should raise suspicion for MODY. When present, such a pedigree is one of the most useful clinical triggers to pursue genetic testing for monogenic diabetes rather than assuming type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

Early complication screening in this patient showed no microalbuminuria, but sudomotor testing revealed severely reduced electrochemical skin conductance in the hands, indicating small‑fibre autonomic dysfunction. Sudoscan (electrochemical skin conductance) is a non‑invasive test of sweat-gland (small fibre autonomic) function; severely reduced values suggest autonomic small-fibre involvement, which may reflect prior periods of significant hyperglycaemia. While autonomic dysfunction is more commonly a later complication, very poor glycaemic control at presentation can be associated with early small‑fibre changes; the finding supports the need for comprehensive follow-up but is not diagnostic of diabetes subtype.

Taken together, the combination of young age at onset, pronounced postprandial hyperglycaemia with glycosuria but no ketoacidosis, absence of overt insulin‑resistance stigmata, and a strong autosomal dominant family history provided compelling early clinical evidence to consider monogenic diabetes—particularly HNF1A‑MODY—and to proceed with targeted biochemical testing (autoantibodies, stimulated C‑peptide) and genetic testing.

## HNF1A-MODY: Molecular Pathophysiology and Genetics

HNF1A (hepatocyte nuclear factor‑1 alpha, also called TCF1) is a transcription factor encoded on chromosome 12q24. The gene contains 10 exons and produces a protein of roughly 631 amino acids that is expressed prominently in pancreatic β‑cells, hepatocytes and renal proximal tubule cells. The protein has three functional regions: an N‑terminal dimerization domain (enables homo‑ or heterodimer formation), a central DNA‑binding domain (recognizes and binds promoter sequences of target genes), and a C‑terminal transactivation domain (recruits the transcriptional machinery). Pathogenic variants cluster disproportionately in the DNA‑binding domain, which is critical for correct regulation of downstream targets.

In pancreatic β‑cells HNF1A controls a network of genes required for glucose sensing and stimulus–secretion coupling, including insulin and the glucose transporter GLUT2 (SLC2A2), as well as enzymes involved in glycolysis such as pyruvate kinase. Loss of HNF1A function therefore reduces GLUT2 expression and diminishes glycolytic flux and ATP generation in response to glucose. Because ATP production and the resulting rise in the ATP/ADP ratio are the proximate triggers for closure of ATP‑sensitive K+ channels, membrane depolarization and calcium‑dependent insulin exocytosis, impaired glucose entry and metabolism lead to an attenuated insulin response—particularly to postprandial glucose loads. Clinically this manifests early as exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia with a progressive decline in insulin secretion over time.

HNF1A’s role in the kidney explains a characteristic extra‑pancreatic feature of the syndrome. In proximal tubule cells HNF1A positively regulates SGLT2 (SLC5A2), the major sodium–glucose cotransporter responsible for renal glucose reabsorption. HNF1A loss‑of‑function reduces SGLT2 expression, lowering the renal threshold for glucose and producing glycosuria at lower plasma glucose concentrations than would otherwise be expected. This mechanism accounts for the striking glycosuria observed in the reported patient and is also why clinicians should be cautious using SGLT2 inhibitors in some individuals with HNF1A‑MODY.

The clinical phenotype follows directly from these molecular effects: onset in adolescence or early adulthood, non‑autoimmune hyperglycaemia, and autosomal dominant transmission with variable penetrance. Islet autoantibodies are generally absent and stimulated C‑peptide is often preserved early in the course—reflecting impaired function rather than absolute β‑cell loss. Patients commonly respond well to low‑dose sulfonylureas because these drugs close KATP channels downstream of the defective glucose‑sensing pathway, restoring insulin release; over years β‑cell function typically declines and treatment needs may evolve. The index patient’s family history of early‑onset diabetes, negative GADA/IA‑2 antibodies and a stimulated C‑peptide consistent with residual secretion fit this pattern.

The variant described in this case, heterozygous HNF1A c.809A>G (p.N270S), lies in exon 4 within the central DNA‑binding domain and was not present in public variant repositories (ClinVar/VarSome), making it novel. Multiple lines of evidence support pathogenicity in accordance with ACMG principles as applied by the authors: (1) location in a critical, mutation‑enriched domain (supporting PM1), (2) absence or extreme rarity in population databases (PM2), (3) strong computational prediction of deleteriousness (AlphaMissense score 0.995, supporting PP3), and (4) reported segregation with diabetes in affected family members (PP1). Taken together these data justify a likely‑pathogenic classification in this clinical context, although definitive proof would require functional assays or larger segregation series.

At the mechanistic level most HNF1A‑MODY variants act by loss‑of‑function and haploinsufficiency: reduction to a single functional allele lowers transcriptional activation of key target genes and impairs β‑cell glucose sensing. Variants in the DNA‑binding domain—such as p.N270S—are expected to impair promoter binding directly and therefore have a clear molecular rationale for causing the observed phenotype. Dominant‑negative effects are less common but possible and would require experimental testing to exclude.

Finally, recognizing the gene‑specific signature of HNF1A‑MODY has immediate clinical relevance and distinguishes it from other common MODY subtypes: GCK‑MODY produces mild, stable fasting hyperglycaemia and usually requires no drug therapy; HNF4A‑MODY may be associated with neonatal macrosomia and transient neonatal hypoglycaemia; HNF1B‑MODY typically presents with renal and urogenital malformations and pancreatic hypoplasia. In this patient, the molecular diagnosis of an HNF1A DNA‑binding domain variant coherently explains the early postprandial hyperglycaemia, prominent glycosuria and familial pattern and provided the basis for cascade testing and genotype‑guided therapy.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: From Differential to Genetic Confirmation

When a young adult presents with marked hyperglycaemia and glycosuria, the initial differential diagnosis includes type 1 diabetes (autoimmune β‑cell destruction), type 2 diabetes (insulin resistance with relative β‑cell failure), monogenic diabetes such as MODY, and less common secondary causes (pancreatic disease, endocrinopathies, drug‑induced). The stepwise evaluation is aimed at distinguishing these possibilities using history, examination, targeted laboratory testing and, where warranted, genetic analysis.

Key clinical features in this case pointed away from classic type 1 and type 2 diabetes and toward a monogenic cause. The patient was young at presentation, had no episode of diabetic ketoacidosis, and lacked clinical stigmata of insulin resistance such as acanthosis nigricans despite a body mass index in the overweight range. A striking family history of multigenerational, early‑onset diabetes (maternal grandmother, mother and younger brother affected) suggested autosomal dominant transmission, a hallmark of MODY. Glycosuria that appeared disproportionately early relative to the degree of hyperglycaemia is also a characteristic clue for HNF1A‑MODY because HNF1A mutations reduce renal SGLT2 expression and lower the renal glucose threshold.

Autoimmunity testing is a key early discriminator. In suspected type 1 diabetes, antibodies such as GAD65 and IA‑2 are commonly positive. In this patient both GAD and IA‑2 antibodies were negative, supporting a non‑autoimmune process. It is important to remember that a small proportion of patients with molecularly proven MODY may have positive islet autoantibodies, and conversely a single negative antibody panel does not by itself prove MODY; antibody testing must be interpreted in the whole clinical context.

Assessment of endogenous insulin secretion further refines the differential. This patient’s fasting C‑peptide was 0.39 nmol/L and rose to 0.83 nmol/L after glucagon stimulation. Clinical practice uses stimulated C‑peptide thresholds (commonly cited >0.6 nmol/L) to indicate preserved endogenous insulin secretion; values below this suggest severe β‑cell failure as seen in long‑standing type 1 diabetes. The pattern here—relatively low fasting but a stimulated rise above the threshold—indicated insufficient but present β‑cell function, again arguing against absolute insulin deficiency from type 1 diabetes and fitting with progressive β‑cell dysfunction in MODY. Such dynamic tests are supportive but not definitive and should be combined with other data.

The glycaemic pattern on formal testing was also informative. The 2‑hour glucose after a 75 g OGTT was 19.8 mmol/L, indicating marked postprandial hyperglycaemia. HNF1A dysfunction impairs β‑cell glucose sensing and early insulin secretion, producing large postprandial excursions even when fasting glucose is only moderately elevated. This physiology explains the combination of prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia and early glycosuria seen in the case.

Clinicians often formalize pretest probability for monogenic diabetes using tools such as the Exeter MODY probability calculator, which incorporates age at diagnosis, family history, BMI, treatment and other variables. Entering this patient’s data yielded a high estimated probability (62.4%), which supported proceeding to genetic testing. A caveat is that such calculators were developed in predominantly European cohorts and may misestimate risk in other ethnic groups; they are best used to help select candidates for testing rather than to establish diagnosis.

Genetic confirmation is the decisive step. Indications to order a multigene MODY panel include young onset, strong autosomal dominant family history, preserved endogenous insulin secretion with absent autoimmunity, atypical course for type 1 or type 2 diabetes, or unusual features such as disproportionate glycosuria. Next‑generation sequencing panels screen relevant genes (HNF1A, HNF4A, GCK, HNF1B, etc.) efficiently. Variant interpretation follows ACMG criteria, integrating population frequency, computational predictions, known functional or domain impact, and familial segregation. In this patient a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (p.N270S) was identified; although absent from public databases, its location in the DNA‑binding domain, proximity to known pathogenic residues, strong in silico deleterious prediction and segregation with affected family members supported a pathogenic/likely pathogenic classification and established the diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY.

Baseline complication screening (urine albumin, retinal examination) and focused testing such as the reported sudomotor assessment have an important role in staging disease, counselling and planning follow‑up but are not diagnostic of MODY. In this case microalbuminuria and retinal disease were absent, while sudomotor testing revealed autonomic impairment—findings that guide surveillance and symptomatic management but do not alter the genetic diagnosis.

In summary, the diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY in this patient was reached by integrating the clinical picture (young age, non‑ketotic presentation, multigenerational early diabetes, disproportionate glycosuria, absence of acanthosis), negative islet autoantibodies, evidence of preserved stimulated C‑peptide, characteristic postprandial hyperglycaemia, a high predictive score, and finally genetic confirmation by NGS with ACMG‑guided variant classification. Genetic confirmation had immediate clinical consequences in this case, enabling cessation of insulin and institution of targeted therapy and allowing for focused family testing and counselling—illustrating why a structured, stepwise diagnostic approach is essential when MODY is suspected.

## Precision Therapy in HNF1A-MODY: Rationale and Regimen

Treatment of HNF1A-MODY should be driven by the molecular defect — a primary β-cell secretory problem with progressive decline in insulin output — and by the immediate clinical status of the patient. The two parallel short‑term goals are to reverse glucotoxicity (which itself suppresses insulin secretion) and to rapidly control hyperglycaemia and symptoms, and the long‑term goals are to preserve remaining β‑cell function, avoid hypoglycaemia, prevent complications and tailor therapy to the patient’s lifestyle and comorbidities. In the presented case the patient arrived with marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, 2‑hour OGTT 19.8 mmol/L) and symptomatic hyperglycaemia; these features established the need for prompt glucose lowering while genetic and functional data were obtained.

Immediate management and the role of short‑term insulin. Severe hyperglycaemia produces glucotoxicity that impairs β‑cell responsiveness; a short period of exogenous insulin can restore insulin secretory responsiveness and reduce osmotic symptoms. Using a basal insulin (in this case degludec 8 units/day) is an appropriate, conservative empiric choice while the diagnostic workup is pending: it provides steady background insulin to lower fasting and nocturnal glucose with relatively low hypoglycaemia risk and minimal dosing complexity. The treating team used a modest basal dose rather than immediate intensive prandial regimens because the main objective was to relieve glucotoxicity and stabilize glucose safely, not to assume long‑term insulin dependence prior to genetic results.

Why metformin and a DPP‑4 inhibitor were included initially. Although HNF1A-MODY is principally a secretory defect, adults may have coexisting insulin resistance or features of metabolic syndrome (this patient had elevated triglycerides and a BMI in the overweight range), so metformin was reasonable to address peripheral insulin resistance and to contribute to rapid glycaemic lowering. Saxagliptin (a DPP‑4 inhibitor) was used as an adjunctive, low‑hypoglycaemia oral agent while the diagnosis was uncertain. DPP‑4 inhibitors modestly enhance endogenous incretin action and post‑prandial insulin secretion without provoking hypoglycaemia, making them a pragmatic bridge therapy pending confirmation of MODY.

Therapeutic tailoring after genetic confirmation. Once genetic testing established HNF1A‑MODY and the glucagon stimulation test documented a still‑measurable stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L), the clinicians appropriately de‑escalated insulin and introduced a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide 1.5 mg weekly) while continuing metformin. This strategy matches the pathophysiology: GLP‑1 receptor agonists enhance glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, suppress inappropriate glucagon, reduce postprandial excursions and promote weight loss — all desirable effects in HNF1A‑MODY with preserved but insufficient β‑cell reserve. Importantly, GLP‑1 RAs have a lower risk of hypoglycaemia than sulfonylureas and can produce durable HbA1c reductions; in this patient the change was effective (HbA1c fell to 6.0% at 6 months), allowing subsequent simplification of therapy.

Evidence context and choice between sulfonylureas and incretin‑based therapy. Historically, low‑dose sulfonylureas have been the canonical first‑line therapy for HNF1A‑MODY because they directly close β‑cell KATP channels and increase insulin release, and many patients achieve good glycaemic control on small doses. The trade‑offs are weight gain and a significant risk of hypoglycaemia, especially in lean young patients. Randomized crossover data and case series have shown that GLP‑1 receptor agonists can achieve glycaemic control similar to sulfonylureas but with fewer hypoglycaemic events and favorable effects on weight — making GLP‑1 RAs an attractive alternative when hypoglycaemia risk, obesity, or cardiovascular risk factor modification are considerations. Choice should be individualized to β‑cell reserve, age, comorbidity and patient preference.

Maintenance strategy and future escalation. After achieving stable control with GLP‑1 RA and metformin, the team simplified therapy to metformin plus saxagliptin for long‑term maintenance. This is an acceptable maintenance approach when glycaemia is stable and the patient prefers oral agents; DPP‑4 inhibitors preserve some incretin effect without hypoglycaemia. Clinicians should recognize, however, that DPP‑4 inhibitors are less potent than GLP‑1 RAs. As HNF1A‑MODY is progressive, periodic reassessment of β‑cell function (clinical glycaemia, HbA1c and C‑peptide) is needed; treatment may later require reintroduction of a GLP‑1 RA, initiation of a low‑dose sulfonylurea, or return to insulin if endogenous secretion declines.

Agents to avoid or use with caution. SGLT2 inhibitors deserve special caution in HNF1A‑MODY. HNF1A regulates renal SGLT2 expression; many patients have a reduced renal glucose threshold and prominent glycosuria at a given plasma glucose level. Further pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition can produce excessive glycosuria with heightened risks of volume depletion, genital mycotic infections and (euglycaemic) diabetic ketoacidosis; the case report and current practice advise against routine SGLT2 use in HNF1A‑MODY. Other cautions include the hypoglycaemia potential of sulfonylureas (use low doses, monitor closely) and consideration of heart‑failure safety signals with specific DPP‑4 agents (e.g. saxagliptin) when relevant comorbidity is present.

Non‑pharmacologic measures and patient education. Structured nutrition, regular aerobic and resistance activity, and attention to hydration are important, particularly because glycosuria may promote polyuria and volume loss. Patients must be taught sick‑day rules (when to hold metformin or other agents, when to seek urgent care for dehydration or ketoacidosis symptoms), hypoglycaemia recognition if receiving secretagogues or insulin, and self‑monitoring strategies tailored to their regimen.

Monitoring, complications surveillance and family implications. After regimen changes, HbA1c should be checked every 3 months until stable and then at regular intervals (often every 6 months) with an individualized target (many patients with HNF1A‑MODY are managed to targets near or below 6.5% when achievable without hypoglycaemia, as in this case). Annual screening for albuminuria, retinal examination and neuropathy/autonomic testing should continue; periodic assessment of renal function is required for metformin safety. Because HNF1A‑MODY is autosomal dominant, confirmation of a pathogenic HNF1A variant has immediate family implications — cascade genetic testing and genetic counselling should be offered so relatives can be screened, avoid misclassification as type 1 diabetes, and receive mechanism‑directed therapy.

In summary, precision therapy in HNF1A‑MODY aligns the acute need to relieve glucotoxicity with longer‑term strategies that enhance glucose‑dependent insulin secretion while minimizing hypoglycaemia. The case illustrates an evidence‑based pathway: short‑term basal insulin plus metformin/DPP‑4 inhibitor while awaiting genetic confirmation, followed by transition to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist and eventual simplification to oral therapy with close follow‑up — a practical model of individualized, mechanism‑driven care.

## Outcomes, Prognosis, and Practice Pearls

This patient’s course shows how a molecular diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY can change therapy and outcomes. Glycaemic control improved markedly after targeted treatment: HbA1c fell from 10.2% at presentation to 9.1% after one month on basal insulin plus metformin and saxagliptin, and then to 6.0% at six months after genetic confirmation and transition to dulaglutide (GLP‑1 receptor agonist) with metformin. Once control stabilised the regimen was simplified to metformin plus saxagliptin with sustained HbA1c <6.5%. The pattern of rapid improvement after reducing treatment burden reflects the patient’s preserved but insufficient β‑cell reserve (stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L) and the typical drug responsiveness seen in many HNF1A‑MODY patients.

Complication surveillance to date is reassuring but not complacent. There is no microalbuminuria or retinopathy on follow‑up, which is compatible with a relatively short recognised disease duration and good glycaemic response. In contrast, the severely reduced sudomotor function on electrochemical skin conductance (Sudoscan) indicates early small‑fiber autonomic dysfunction and requires longitudinal monitoring. Practical follow‑up should include periodic symptom assessment for autonomic features (eg, orthostatic intolerance), routine retinal and urinary albumin screening per diabetes surveillance intervals, and repeat neuropathy assessments as clinically indicated.

Prognosis is for a gradual, progressive decline in β‑cell function. Patients with HNF1A‑MODY commonly maintain responsiveness to insulin secretagogues or incretin‑based therapies for years but many eventually need treatment intensification and, in some, insulin. Management should therefore be dynamic: individualize glycaemic targets, monitor HbA1c and stimulated C‑peptide at appropriate intervals (for example every 3 months after treatment changes), and escalate therapy when control or symptoms indicate loss of endogenous insulin secretion. Minimizing hypoglycaemia is important—this influenced the choice of a GLP‑1 receptor agonist in this case as a safer alternative to higher‑dose sulfonylureas in a patient in whom hypoglycaemia risk and quality of life were priorities.

The precision diagnosis had clear, practical benefits for this patient and his family. Genetic confirmation permitted safe de‑intensification from insulin, alignment of drug selection to the underlying pathophysiology (incretin‑based therapy and metformin rather than lifelong insulin), reduced treatment burden and hypoglycaemia risk, and provided the opportunity for cascade testing and counselling of at‑risk relatives. Early identification of mutation carriers allows targeted monitoring and earlier, less burdensome therapy for affected family members.

When to suspect HNF1A‑MODY: key clues are young‑onset diabetes without ketoacidosis, a multigenerational (autosomal dominant) history of early diabetes, prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia with glycosuria (often disproportionate to fasting glucose because of a low renal glucose threshold), negative islet autoantibodies, and preserved stimulated C‑peptide (eg, >0.60 nmol/L). Lack of clinical insulin‑resistance stigmata (eg, acanthosis) supports the diagnosis, although obesity and metabolic syndrome can coexist and should not exclude consideration of MODY.

Pitfalls and safeguards: misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes is common. Clinical calculators (eg, the Exeter MODY probability tool) are useful but were derived largely from European cohorts and can misestimate probability in other ethnic groups. Autoantibody testing is supportive but not definitive—autoantibodies have occasionally been reported in patients with monogenic diabetes—so repeat testing or stimulated C‑peptide measurement can help when the picture is ambiguous. Importantly, exercise caution with certain drug classes: because HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression and lower renal glucose threshold, SGLT2 inhibitors may exacerbate glycosuria, volume depletion and (rarely) euglycaemic ketoacidosis; their risks and benefits should be considered individually and monitored closely.

Research and registry value: the novel p.N270S HNF1A variant described here expands the mutation spectrum and underscores the importance of submitting new variants with segregation data to public databases (ClinVar/VarSome) and enrolling patients in MODY registries. Such data improve variant interpretation under ACMG criteria, strengthen genotype–phenotype correlations, and inform optimal, evidence‑based treatment strategies for future patients.

Takeaway practice pearls: consider monogenic diabetes in young adults with non‑ketotic hyperglycaemia and an autosomal dominant family history; use negative islet antibodies and preserved stimulated C‑peptide to support, but not replace, genetic testing; a confirmed HNF1A diagnosis can allow de‑intensification from insulin and targeted use of incretin‑based or low‑dose secretagogue therapy with reduced hypoglycaemia and improved quality of life; maintain vigilant screening for microvascular and autonomic complications; and contribute novel variant data and family segregation information to registries to advance precision care.
