Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# Young, autoantibody‑negative, and not quite type 2: a pragmatic MODY workup at the bedside

Why this case is worth your attention
A young man turns up in clinic with unexpectedly high glucose (HbA1c 10.2%, fasting 12.4 mmol/L) and glycosuria on routine testing. He isn’t thin and brittle like classic type 1, he’s not obese with obvious insulin resistance, and both GAD and IA‑2 (and ZnT8) antibodies are negative. He has a striking multigenerational history of early‑onset diabetes (grandmother in her 30s, mother late 20s, brother at 20). A glucagon stimulation test showed a preserved stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L) and an OGTT produced marked post‑prandial hyperglycaemia (2‑hour 19.8 mmol/L). Genetic testing found a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (p.N270S), and therapy was changed from low‑dose insulin + metformin to a GLP‑1 agonist + metformin with excellent glycaemic response (HbA1c fell to 6.0% at 6 months).

Why this matters: many patients like this get mislabelled type 1 or type 2 for years. The correct diagnosis — HNF1A‑MODY in this case — changes treatment choices, avoids unnecessary insulin, prompts cascade testing in relatives, and is a paradigm of precision diabetes care.

## The bedside workup — a practical, stepwise approach
You don’t need a geneticist at your elbow to decide whether to pursue MODY testing. Use a focused history, a few targeted tests, and a pragmatic threshold for genetic testing.

Step 1 — history and phenotype
Ask age at diagnosis, age of affected relatives, and pattern of inheritance. Multigenerational, autosomal‑dominant early‑onset diabetes (two or more generations with diagnosis in youth or early adulthood) is a big red flag. Note features arguing for or against insulin resistance (BMI, acanthosis nigricans). In this case the BMI was 24.6 kg/m2 and no acanthosis was present.

Step 2 — basic glycemic physiology
Look for disproportionate post‑prandial hyperglycaemia. If clinically feasible, a 75 g OGTT can be revealing — a 2‑hour glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L is diagnostic of diabetes and, when markedly elevated, common in HNF1A‑MODY. Our patient’s 2‑hour value was 19.8 mmol/L.

Step 3 — rule out autoimmune type 1 disease
Measure GAD, IA‑2 and ZnT8 antibodies. Negative antibodies don’t prove MODY, but positive antibodies usually point to type 1. This patient had negative GAD/IA‑2/ZnT8.

Step 4 — assess endogenous insulin secretion
Measure C‑peptide. A stimulated test (glucagon stimulation or mixed‑meal) is preferable to a single fasting number. A stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L generally indicates substantial residual β‑cell function and argues against classic type 1. Our case rose from 0.39 to 0.83 nmol/L after glucagon — supportive of non‑autoimmune, insulin‑deficient but not absent insulin secretion.

Step 5 — look for HNF1A‑specific clues
Glycosuria out of proportion to plasma glucose (low renal glucose threshold), strong family history, prominent post‑prandial hyperglycaemia, and absence of typical T2D stigmata (marked obesity, acanthosis) all point to HNF1A. The biology: HNF1A regulates SGLT2; mutations lower the renal glucose threshold and produce glycosuria even at lower plasma glucose levels.

Step 6 — integrate probability and decide on genetics
Use a MODY probability tool (e.g. the Exeter MODY calculator) as one data point — it gave 62.4% for this patient — but don’t treat it as the final word. If the clinical picture is compatible and resources permit, order an NGS monogenic diabetes panel (HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, etc.) rather than single‑gene testing when the diagnosis is uncertain.

## Interpreting the signals — when MODY is more likely than T1 or T2
- Positive family history across generations + early onset = big signal.
- Negative islet autoantibodies and preserved stimulated C‑peptide make type 1 unlikely.
- Markedly elevated 2‑hour OGTT glucose with glycosuria and relatively modest BMI makes type 2 less likely (but rising obesity rates can blur this).
- If a patient is young and either doesn’t require insulin long‑term or can be weaned off insulin once glycaemic control improves, MODY should be on your differential.

## The Exeter calculator — useful, but know the limitations
The calculator is a helpful, quick bedside tool to quantify pre‑test probability. Important caveats: it was derived from largely White European cohorts and may overestimate (or underestimate) MODY probability in other ethnic groups. Use it to support — not replace — clinical judgment. In this case the calculator’s high score matched strong clinical signals and helped justify genetic testing.

## When to pull the trigger for an NGS MODY panel (practical triggers)
Consider genetic testing when diabetes onset is young (classically <25 but pragmatically <35 years) and at least one of the following applies: a clear autosomal‑dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes; negative islet autoantibodies with preserved stimulated C‑peptide (>0.6 nmol/L); disproportionate post‑prandial hyperglycaemia on OGTT with glycosuria; low ongoing insulin requirements or an ability to stop insulin without loss of control; or an atypical clinical course for type 1 or type 2. If cost is a concern, prioritize testing when several of these features cluster.

## Treatment implications — the diagnosis changes management
HNF1A‑MODY often responds to low‑dose sulfonylureas, but GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an attractive option when hypoglycaemia is a concern or β‑cell function is declining. In this patient, insulin was stopped after genetic confirmation and dulaglutide (1.5 mg weekly) + metformin produced an impressive fall in HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% over 6 months. Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors: HNF1A patients already have lower SGLT2 expression and accompanying glycosuria — adding an SGLT2 inhibitor may increase dehydration risk and (theoretically) risk of euglycemic DKA in some situations. Importantly, a definitive genetic diagnosis enables cascade testing and tailored counseling for family members.

## Genetics in brief — the variant in this case
Next‑generation sequencing identified a previously unreported heterozygous HNF1A substitution p.N270S (c.809A>G) in exon 4, located in the DNA‑binding domain. The variant was absent from common databases, had a high deleteriousness score, and segregated with diabetes in the family; the authors classified it as pathogenic/likely pathogenic using ACMG criteria. New variants like this expand our recognition of MODY and directly influence patient care.

## Bottom line
When you see a young adult with diabetes, negative autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, a strong multigenerational history, glycosuria, or marked post‑prandial hyperglycaemia — think MODY. Use history, OGTT, antibody testing, and stimulated C‑peptide to build your case. Apply the MODY probability tools judiciously, and when multiple red flags cluster, order an NGS monogenic diabetes panel. Getting the diagnosis right changes therapy, avoids unnecessary insulin, and opens the door to precision care for patients and their families.

Key take‑home messages
- Consider MODY in young adults with diabetes and multigenerational early‑onset disease, especially when autoantibodies are negative and stimulated C‑peptide is preserved (>0.6 nmol/L).
- Marked post‑prandial hyperglycaemia on OGTT and glycosuria out of proportion to plasma glucose are high‑yield clinical clues for HNF1A‑MODY.
- Use the Exeter MODY calculator as an adjunct, not a gatekeeper — it may be less accurate in non‑European populations.
- Order an NGS monogenic diabetes panel when clinical suspicion is high (young onset + family history or negative antibodies + preserved C‑peptide or other red flags).
- Diagnosis matters: HNF1A‑MODY often responds to oral agents (sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 RAs) and may allow stopping insulin; avoid automatic SGLT2 inhibitor use without careful consideration.
- A genetic result permits cascade testing and targeted counseling — precision medicine that changes management for patients and relatives.



# De‑glucotoxicize, then de‑escalate: how a genetic answer changes the diabetes Rx

## Why this case grabbed our attention
A young man walks into clinic after an otherwise routine health check and is found to have an A1c of 10.2% with frank glycosuria but no ketoacidosis. He’s not obese (BMI 24.6), has a strong autosomal‑dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies, a preserved stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L) and—crucially—genetic testing that identified a novel heterozygous HNF1A mutation (p.N270S). What happens next matters: short‑term basal insulin to reverse glucotoxicity, then a precision pivot to targeted therapy (GLP‑1 RA → oral maintenance) that allowed insulin discontinuation and an A1c fall to 6.0% at 6 months. That kind of treatment de‑escalation after a genetic diagnosis is exactly why we should be thinking MODY earlier.

## De‑glucotoxicize first — a pragmatic acute plan
When faced with very high glucose (A1c >9–10%) you have two immediate priorities: protect the patient from metabolic complications and reverse glucose‑mediated β‑cell dysfunction (glucotoxicity). Short‑term basal insulin is an efficient, low‑complexity way to do both. In this case the team started degludec 8 units daily plus metformin 1,500 mg and saxagliptin. Within a month the A1c had fallen from 10.2% to 9.1%—enough improvement to reassess β‑cell function and await genetic results without committing the patient to lifelong intensive insulin.

Why basal insulin? It lowers fasting hyperglycaemia, reduces glucotoxicity, and gives the pancreas a chance to recover insulin secretory capacity. It’s often enough as a bridge while you send off autoantibodies, a C‑peptide and a genetic panel.

Clinical pearl: a stimulated C‑peptide >0.60 nmol/L (as in this patient) is supportive of preserved endogenous insulin secretion and argues against classic type 1 diabetes.

## When to suspect MODY and pull the trigger on genetics
MODY should be on your radar when diabetes presents young, especially with any of these features: an autosomal dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide, disproportionate glycosuria (low renal threshold), and a clinical picture that doesn’t fit type 1 or type 2 cleanly. The Exeter MODY probability calculator can help (it estimated 62.4% in this patient), but remember it was derived from a White-European cohort and can misestimate risk in other populations. If suspicion is moderate–high, pursue molecular testing (NGS panels are now practical).

Genetic confirmation is not academic here—it changes therapy, prognosis and family screening.

## The treatment pivot: HNF1A‑MODY — what to use (and why)
HNF1A mutations produce progressive β‑cell dysfunction but classically retain glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion. Therapy should target the remaining β‑cell reserve.

- Classic teaching: low‑dose sulfonylureas are first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY because patients show marked sensitivity and good initial glycaemic responses. The downside: risk of hypoglycaemia and weight gain, and efficacy may wane as β‑cells decline.
- An increasingly attractive alternative: GLP‑1 receptor agonists. They augment glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, suppress inappropriate glucagon, and carry a lower hypoglycaemia risk than sulfonylureas. In small randomized crossover data in HNF1A‑MODY, liraglutide produced similar glycemic control to glimepiride with fewer hypoglycaemic events. In our case, dulaglutide 1.5 mg weekly plus metformin allowed safe discontinuation of insulin and brought A1c to 6.0% at 6 months.
- DPP‑4 inhibitors (oral) are less potent but are an option for maintenance if GLP‑1 RA injections are unacceptable or if a patient is stable; the case patient was ultimately maintained on metformin plus saxagliptin with A1c <6.5%.

Important mechanistic note: HNF1A regulates renal SGLT2 expression. These patients may have a lower renal threshold for glucose and marked glycosuria at lower plasma glucose levels. That has therapy implications.

## A practical de‑escalation roadmap (how the case unfolded — and how you can apply it)
1) Acute presentation: start short‑term basal insulin to de‑glucotoxicize (low starting dose is often enough), continue metformin, and address postprandial hyperglycaemia if needed (the team used a DPP‑4 inhibitor). Send islet antibodies, stimulated C‑peptide and an NGS MODY panel.

2) Reassess at ~4 weeks: check response (fasting glucose, SMBG logs, A1c trend), review antibody and C‑peptide results. If antibodies are negative and stimulated C‑peptide is preserved (case: 0.83 nmol/L) and genetics confirm HNF1A mutation, you have license to de‑escalate.

3) Transition strategy: one reasonable pathway—stop basal insulin and initiate a GLP‑1 RA (weekly or daily) while continuing metformin; monitor capillary glucose closely for the first 1–2 weeks and have rapid access for dose adjustments. Alternatively, start a low‑dose sulfonylurea if GLP‑1 RA is not acceptable—start low and educate rigorously about hypoglycaemia.

4) Reassess at 3 months: A1c, SMBG patterns, tolerability. If control is robust and β‑cell reserve is enough, some patients can be simplified to oral maintenance (metformin ± DPP‑4 inhibitor), as happened here.

Caveat: discontinuing insulin should be individualized. Do not stop insulin unless you have objective evidence of preserved insulin secretion, genetic confirmation, and a follow‑up plan for close glucose monitoring.

## What to avoid and what to monitor
Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors unless you have a compelling reason—they can dramatically increase urinary glucose losses in patients with already low renal glucose thresholds and have been associated with dehydration and euglycaemic DKA in susceptible patients with HNF1A‑MODY.

Monitor for:
- Hypoglycaemia (if you choose a sulfonylurea)
- GI side effects (with GLP‑1 RA)
- Glycaemic control (SMBG and A1c q3 months initially)
- Microvascular complications (retina, urine albumin, foot exam) despite good short‑term control
- Family cascade testing and genetic counselling—the autosomal dominant risk means screening relatives is high‑yield.

Note from the case: the patient already had markedly reduced sudomotor function on testing, indicating autonomic small‑fiber dysfunction; that’s a reminder to include neuropathy and autonomic symptom screening in follow up.

## The bottom line — precision medicine changes outcomes
This case illustrates a simple, high‑yield sequence: treat the immediate threat (de‑glucotoxicize with short‑term basal insulin), confirm the diagnosis (autoantibodies, C‑peptide, genetic testing), then tailor therapy to pathophysiology (in HNF1A‑MODY choose between low‑dose sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA, and consider oral maintenance with metformin ± DPP‑4 inhibitor). Genetic confirmation allowed this patient to stop insulin and be managed effectively with GLP‑1 RA and later with oral therapy—avoiding unnecessary lifelong insulin and its burdens.

## Clinical pearls and take‑home points
- In young patients with strong autosomal dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide, suspect MODY and send a genetic panel early.  
- If hyperglycaemic (A1c high) at presentation, use short‑term basal insulin to reverse glucotoxicity while awaiting test results—this is often sufficient and reversible.  
- HNF1A‑MODY: classic first‑line is low‑dose sulfonylureas, but GLP‑1 RAs are an effective, lower‑hypoglycaemia alternative as β‑cell function wanes.  
- Small randomized crossover data suggest GLP‑1 RAs and sulfonylureas have similar glycaemic effects in HNF1A‑MODY but GLP‑1 RAs produce less hypoglycaemia.  
- Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors unless you understand the risk—these patients already have reduced SGLT2 expression and can develop marked glycosuria, dehydration and DKA.  
- After genetic confirmation and evidence of preserved insulin secretion, you can often stop insulin and transition to targeted therapy—do this with close SMBG and short‑interval follow up.  
- Cascade testing and genetic counselling for relatives are essential—precision diagnosis changes therapy, prognosis and family screening.



# When SGLT2 is already turned down: why HNF1A‑MODY and SGLT2 inhibitors don’t mix

## Why this case caught my eye
A young adult with early-onset, autosomal-dominant diabetes, baseline glycosuria and negative islet autoantibodies turned out not to have type 1 or type 2 diabetes at all but HNF1A‑MODY from a novel HNF1A DNA‑binding‑domain variant. HNF1A drives renal SGLT2 expression — so these patients already have a “pharmacologic” partial SGLT2 knockdown at baseline. Add an SGLT2 inhibitor and you can super‑charge glucosuria, osmotic diuresis and the very real (and sometimes euglycaemic) risk of DKA. That’s a precision‑medicine moment: the same highly useful drug class we now reach for for cardiorenal protection in T2D may be the wrong choice in a specific genotype.

This case (HNF1A:c.809A>G, p.N270S) illustrates the clinical consequences and gives a good platform to review mechanism, risk and pragmatic management.

## Case in a minute (grounded in the report)
- Young adult referred for hyperglycaemia and glycosuria on health check. BMI 24.6, no acanthosis, strong family history of early‑onset diabetes (maternal-line).
- Labs: HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, 2‑hr OGTT 19.8 mmol/L. No DKA at presentation. GADA and IA‑2 negative. Glucagon stimulation: stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L (suggesting some residual β‑cell function).
- Genetic testing: novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (p.N270S) — HNF1A‑MODY confirmed.
- Initial insulin + metformin + DPP‑4; after genetic confirmation insulin stopped and dulaglutide (GLP‑1 RA) + metformin was used — HbA1c fell to 6.0% at 6 months.
- The report highlights that HNF1A‑MODY patients may already have reduced SGLT2 expression and glycosuria, and that SGLT2 inhibitors have been reported to precipitate severe dehydration and DKA in this setting.

## Quick physiology: HNF1A → SGLT2 → renal glucose threshold
HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in liver, pancreas and renal proximal tubule. It transcriptionally regulates SGLT2 (SLC5A2) expression. Loss‑of‑function HNF1A variants reduce SGLT2 expression → lower the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption → glycosuria at lower plasma glucose levels than expected.

SGLT2 inhibitors work by blocking the same transporter in the proximal tubule, causing glucosuria, osmotic diuresis and natriuresis. In a patient whose SGLT2 is already downregulated by an HNF1A mutation, pharmacologic blockade can be additive (or even synergistic) — resulting in much greater urinary glucose loss, larger volume losses and a higher risk of the downstream metabolic consequences.

## Mechanisms that explain the danger
There are three linked mechanisms to worry about:

1. Exaggerated osmotic diuresis and volume depletion
   - HNF1A‑related lower SGLT2 expression causes baseline urinary glucose loss. Add an SGLT2 inhibitor → more glucosuria → marked osmotic diuresis, hypotension and prerenal kidney injury risk (even in young patients).

2. Promotion of ketogenesis and euglycaemic DKA
   - SGLT2 inhibitors reduce plasma glucose and insulin secretion, increase glucagon, and shift metabolism toward lipolysis and ketone production. HNF1A‑MODY comes with progressive β‑cell dysfunction (relative insulin deficiency). The combination increases the chance of ketoacidosis at lower glucose concentrations (euglycaemic DKA).

3. Infectious and hemodynamic complications
   - The high urinary sugar load increases risk of genital mycotic infections; volume depletion can precipitate significant hypotension and acute kidney injury. Clinical reports have documented severe dehydration and DKA in HNF1A patients exposed to SGLT2 inhibitors.

Put simply: the transporter you are trying to block pharmacologically is already partly off in these patients — blocking it further can push physiology into a dangerous zone.

## Practical clinical guidance

If the patient has a proven HNF1A‑MODY genotype
- Generally avoid SGLT2 inhibitors. The potential CV/renal benefits seen in typical T2D trials need to be balanced against a mechanistically plausible and clinically reported risk of dehydration, hypotension, genital infections and (particularly concerning) euglycaemic DKA.
- Prefer GLP‑1 receptor agonists for glucose lowering with weight and CV benefits (the reported patient did well after switching to dulaglutide + metformin). Low‑dose sulfonylureas are classically effective in HNF1A‑MODY (they increase insulin secretion) but carry hypoglycaemia risk as β‑cell function wanes — discuss risks and monitoring. Metformin remains reasonable for insulin‑sensitive/metabolic benefit.
- If there is a compelling reason to use an SGLT2 inhibitor (rare), discuss risks thoroughly, involve endocrinology, and implement strict monitoring and sick‑day plans.

If you suspect MODY but don’t have genetic confirmation yet
- Be cautious before starting an SGLT2 inhibitor in a young patient with: early onset diabetes, strong autosomal dominant family history, glycosuria disproportionate to blood glucose, negative islet antibodies and preserved C‑peptide. Consider MODY genetic testing (NGS panel) before initiating SGLT2 therapy when feasible.
- If urgent glycaemic control is needed, prefer insulin short‑term or GLP‑1 RA/metformin depending on the clinical scenario, rather than jumping to an SGLT2 inhibitor.

If a patient with HNF1A‑MODY is already taking an SGLT2 inhibitor
- Most clinicians will stop the SGLT2 inhibitor when the diagnosis is established, reassess volume status and renal function, and counsel about ketone monitoring. Check blood β‑hydroxybutyrate if symptomatic (blood ketones preferred over urine). If ketones are elevated or the patient is symptomatic (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, tachypnea, lethargy), treat as DKA.
- If there are strong reasons to continue (rare), do so only after specialist discussion, informed consent, and with a robust plan: daily ketone checks when unwell, clear sick‑day rules, hydration advice, and low threshold for stopping the drug during illness.

Sick‑day rules worth giving every patient on SGLT2 inhibitors — and make them louder for MODY
- Stop SGLT2 inhibitors immediately with intercurrent illness, prolonged poor oral intake, vomiting, fever, diarrhoea, heavy alcohol intake or before major surgery (local guidance varies; many recommend holding the drug ~48–72 hours preop).
- If unwell while on/after SGLT2 use: check capillary blood ketones. If elevated or symptomatic, seek urgent medical care.
- Maintain good hydration and monitor blood pressure.

## How to talk to the patient (a short script)
“Your genetic test shows a mutation that lowers the kidney’s ability to reabsorb sugar. Because SGLT2 inhibitors work by blocking that same kidney transporter, adding one could cause excessive sugar loss in the urine, dehydration and, rarely, diabetic ketoacidosis even when blood sugars look okay. For safety we generally avoid these drugs in people with this mutation and use alternatives such as GLP‑1 receptor agonists, metformin or tailored sulfonylurea therapy. If you’re ever unwell, you should stop any SGLT2 medicine and check ketones.”

## Caveats
- The recommendation to avoid SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY is based on mechanism plus case reports and biologic plausibility; randomized trial data in this specific genotype don’t exist. Still, the risk profile makes avoidance the prudent position in most patients.
- Precision matters: genetic testing in young, atypical diabetes cases changes therapy and outcomes — this case is a concrete example.

## Key take‑home messages
- HNF1A drives renal SGLT2 expression; HNF1A‑MODY commonly produces a lower renal glucose threshold and baseline glycosuria.
- Adding an SGLT2 inhibitor in HNF1A‑MODY can dramatically increase glucosuria → exaggerated osmotic diuresis, hypotension, prerenal AKI and a higher risk of (often euglycaemic) DKA.
- If HNF1A‑MODY is proven: avoid SGLT2 inhibitors in most cases; prefer GLP‑1 receptor agonists, metformin and/or low‑dose sulfonylureas with specialist input.
- If MODY is suspected: pause before starting SGLT2 inhibitors — consider genetic testing, check islet antibodies and stimulated C‑peptide.
- If a patient with HNF1A‑MODY is already on an SGLT2 inhibitor: stop it in most cases, reassess hydration and renal function, and check blood ketones when unwell. Teach strict sick‑day rules.

Precision medicine isn’t just about finding a mutation — it changes which drugs you give and which drugs you avoid. HNF1A‑MODY is one of those diagnoses where the genotype tells you not only what to start, but what to never add.



# Beyond ClinVar: calling a novel HNF1A variant pathogenic without overcalling it

## The hook — why this case is a great teaching moment
A young adult with marked hyperglycaemia, glycosuria and a strong autosomal‑dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes was found to carry a previously unreported HNF1A missense change (p.N270S / c.809A>G). The variant was absent from usual databases but had a very high AlphaMissense score and, importantly, the clinical team was able to stop insulin and move the patient to GLP‑1 agonist + metformin with dramatic glycaemic improvement (HbA1c 10.2% → 6.0% over months). This is precision medicine in action — but it also raises the central practical question: how do you call a novel variant “pathogenic” robustly, without over‑calling, and what should you do clinically while evidence accumulates?

Below I walk through a real‑world ACMG interpretation of p.N270S, the limits of that interpretation, how to communicate it to patients, when it's reasonable to act clinically, and how cascade testing can both improve care and tighten the genetics.

## Quick clinical recap (from the case)
- Young adult male referred for hyperglycaemia and glycosuria on screening. BMI 24.6 kg/m2. No acanthosis nigricans.
- Family history: maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s and a younger brother at 20 — all early‑onset diabetes.
- Labs: HbA1c 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, 2‑hour OGTT 19.8 mmol/L.
- Glucagon stimulation: C‑peptide 0 min 0.39 nmol/L → 6 min 0.83 nmol/L (preserved but insufficient insulin secretion).
- Autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2A) negative. No microalbuminuria. Sudomotor testing abnormal.
- Genetic testing: heterozygous HNF1A c.809A>G (p.N270S), novel—absent from ClinVar/VarSome/literature. AlphaMissense score 0.995. Variant reported to segregate with diabetes in the family.
- Initial Rx: basal insulin + metformin + saxagliptin. After genetic confirmation insulin was discontinued and dulaglutide 1.5 mg weekly was started; at 6 months HbA1c 6.0%, and regimen later simplified to metformin + saxagliptin for maintenance.

## ACMG in action — how the evidence stacks up for p.N270S
Applying ACMG/AMP criteria is a structured way to avoid “gut calls.” Here’s how the case’s data map to evidence codes.

- PM1 (Moderate): The variant sits in HNF1A’s DNA‑binding domain. This domain is a recognized hotspot for pathogenic missense variants in HNF1A, so location contributes moderate evidence.
- PM2 (Moderate): The change is absent from major variant/population resources and not reported in the literature/ClinVar/VarSome as the authors note — supporting PM2 (rare/absent from controls).
- PP1 (Supporting): The variant segregates with diabetes in this family (maternal grandmother, mother, brother have early‑onset disease), giving a supporting segregation signal. (More affected relatives tested increases the strength of this code.)
- PP3 (Supporting): Computational predictors — notably AlphaMissense 0.995 — predict deleterious effect. Computational evidence is supportive but not decisive.
- PP4 (Supporting): The phenotype is highly specific for HNF1A‑MODY: early age of onset, dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, and glycosuria consistent with reduced renal SGLT2 expression.

Combine that: two Moderates (PM1 + PM2) plus multiple Supporting lines (PP1 + PP3 + PP4) is a standard ACMG combination that supports a Likely Pathogenic classification. It’s a defensible, evidence‑based call — but not an ironclad “pathogenic” label yet because we lack very strong functional data (PS3), very large segregation numbers, or independent occurrences of the same variant in unrelated affected individuals (PS4).

Important nuance: different diagnostic labs sometimes lean toward “pathogenic” vs “likely pathogenic” differently. That isn’t malpractice — it’s the reality of judgment calls. The key is transparency about what evidence was used and a plan for follow‑up.

## Why “Likely Pathogenic” matters clinically (and when it’s reasonable to act)
A Likely Pathogenic HNF1A variant in a patient whose clinical picture fits HNF1A‑MODY changes management in a way that is evidence‑based and patient‑centered.

- Rationale: HNF1A‑MODY is a β‑cell secretory defect. Patients often respond well to oral agents (historically low‑dose sulfonylureas) and can often avoid or stop insulin. The case shows a pragmatic approach — insulin weaned, GLP‑1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) started with excellent glycaemic response.
- How to act safely: make decisions with the patient, document the genetic interpretation and uncertainty, and monitor closely. If switching off insulin, plan a supervised taper with frequent glucose checks, education about hypoglycaemia, and readiness to restart insulin if control worsens.
- When to be more conservative: if genetic evidence were weaker (e.g., novel variant with poor phenotypic fit, or variant of uncertain significance), you should not change long‑standing therapy based solely on the variant.

So in short: with an LP call plus a very coherent phenotype (as in this case), it is reasonable to use the genetic result to personalize therapy — but do it transparently and with close follow‑up.

## Communicating this to patients — plain language that helps
Patients want to know two things: what this means for their care now, and what it means for the family.

A succinct script: “We found a change in the HNF1A gene that is very likely to explain your diabetes. That information helps us choose medicines that work better for this type of diabetes, and it means relatives may also carry the same change. There’s still a small amount of uncertainty because this exact change hasn’t been seen before, so we will keep monitoring and re‑evaluate as more genetic or laboratory evidence appears. For now we can safely try changing your diabetes medicines with close follow‑up, and we recommend targeted genetic testing for close relatives.”

Always: (1) document the lab classification and the evidence used, (2) offer genetic counseling, and (3) give the patient a clear plan for follow‑up and recontact.

## Cascade testing — how it tightens classification and improves outcomes
Cascade testing is low‑hanging fruit: targeted testing for a known familial variant is cheaper and faster than broad panels.

- Practical benefits: If the variant is present in multiple affected relatives and absent in unaffected relatives, PP1 becomes stronger and the variant may be upgraded to Likely Pathogenic → Pathogenic. If you find carriers who have been misclassified (for instance, relatives on insulin), you can change therapy and avoid lifelong insulin in some.
- How to proceed: offer targeted testing to first‑degree relatives, with genetic counseling. Provide a family letter summarizing the variant, its implications, and suggested testing. For children/adolescents: discussion is warranted because HNF1A‑MODY can present early and management would change — many teams will test minors when results would alter care.
- Also submit the variant and phenotype data to public resources (ClinVar) so the global evidence base grows.

## What would push p.N270S from “Likely” to “Pathogenic”?
Higher‑confidence upgrades typically require:
- PS3 (Strong): experimental functional evidence showing impaired HNF1A DNA binding or transactivation from an assay.
- Larger segregation datasets (PP1 upgraded from supporting → moderate/strong).
- PS4: observation of the same variant in multiple unrelated affected individuals with appropriate case/control data.
Pursuing functional work or encouraging other centers to report similar findings accelerates this process.

## Practical clinical pearls
- Don’t over‑rely on any single database. “Absent from ClinVar” is informative, but absence is not proof of benignity — and absence plus good phenotype is powerful.
- Computational tools (AlphaMissense, REVEL, CADD, etc.) are supportive (PP3) but cannot substitute for segregation or functional data.
- For HNF1A‑MODY specifically: consider sulfonylureas as historical first‑line therapy; GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an acceptable and often safer alternative as β‑cell function declines (lower hypoglycaemia risk). In this case dulaglutide performed extremely well.
- Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY: SGLT2 expression is transcriptionally regulated by HNF1A — these patients can have baseline glycosuria and there are case reports suggesting dehydration/DKA risk.
- If a lab calls a variant “pathogenic” but you have doubts, ask for the criteria the lab used and request re‑review or second opinion from a clinical geneticist.

## Bottom line
The p.N270S HNF1A variant is a textbook example of how careful phenotype–genotype integration can move a novel missense change from “unknown” to “likely pathogenic” in a way that meaningfully changes patient care. The rule is: be systematic, transparent about uncertainty, use cascade testing aggressively, and act clinically when the genetic evidence coheres with the clinical picture — but do so with shared decision‑making and planned follow‑up.

Key take‑home messages
- A novel HNF1A missense in the DNA‑binding domain (p.N270S) with absence from databases (PM2), domain enrichment (PM1), family segregation (PP1), strong computational support (PP3) and a highly specific phenotype (PP4) reasonably meets ACMG criteria for Likely Pathogenic.
- “Likely pathogenic” is actionable when the clinical phenotype fits — you can change therapy (as in this case) but do it with shared decision‑making, documentation, and close monitoring.
- Cascade (targeted) testing of first‑degree relatives both improves clinical care and strengthens variant classification (moving PP1 from supporting → moderate/strong).
- Computational predictions help but do not replace functional studies or segregation data; pursuing those (and submitting variant data to ClinVar) helps the whole community.
- For HNF1A‑MODY, think oral secretagogues or GLP‑1 RA strategies and be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors; pregnancy and pediatric testing require special counseling.
- If lab classifications differ, ask for the ACMG evidence used, involve genetics colleagues, and keep the patient informed about possible future reclassification.



# MODY in an obesogenic world: why BMI and triglycerides won’t save you from a missed diagnosis

## The case that should change how you think about “typical” MODY
A young man walks into clinic after an occupational health check shows hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. He’s not ketoacidotic, his BMI is only 24.6 kg/m2, but his labs scream metabolic syndrome (triglycerides 4.31 mmol/L, HDL 1.01 mmol/L). He has a striking family history — maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, younger brother at 20 — yet his picture could easily be labelled “type 2” in many settings.

Key objective data from the case:
- HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, 2-hour OGTT 19.8 mmol/L.
- Glycosuria without DKA.
- Islet autoantibodies tested (GADA, IA‑2) — negative.
- Glucagon-stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L (supporting preserved endogenous insulin secretion; cutoff used in the report >0.60 nmol/L).
- Sudomotor testing: severely reduced hand conductance (autonomic involvement).
- Initial therapy: basal insulin degludec (8 units/day) + metformin 1500 mg/day + saxagliptin 5 mg/day.
- Genetic result: novel heterozygous HNF1A variant p.N270S (c.809A>G) — not in ClinVar/VarSome; interpreted as pathogenic/likely pathogenic using ACMG criteria and genotype–phenotype segregation.
- After genetic diagnosis insulin was stopped and dulaglutide 1.5 mg weekly introduced; HbA1c fell to 6.0% at 6 months and therapy was later simplified to metformin + saxagliptin with sustained control <6.5%.

Why is this interesting? Because metabolic syndrome signals (high TG, low HDL) were present alongside a classic autosomal-dominant family pattern of early-onset diabetes and features more in keeping with HNF1A‑MODY. In short: rising background cardiometabolic risk can mask monogenic diabetes.

## Why BMI, acanthosis and dyslipidemia can’t be your exclusion rules anymore
Historically we were taught that MODY patients are “young, lean, non‑insulin resistant” and that obesity, acanthosis nigricans or dyslipidaemia argue for type 2 diabetes. That heuristic is breaking down for three reasons:
- The population prevalence of overweight/obesity and dyslipidaemia has increased everywhere — monogenic diabetes can coexist with environmental/metabolic risk factors.
- Polygenic background (common risk alleles) modifies phenotype. A pathogenic monogenic variant does not exist in isolation; polygenic risk and environment determine the clinical expression.
- Different populations have different baseline cardiometabolic profiles; diagnostic tools and cutoffs derived from European cohorts (eg, Exeter MODY calculator) may misclassify non‑European patients.

The patient above had metabolic-lipid signals that would push many clinicians away from thinking “MODY” — and yet NGS found an HNF1A pathogenic variant. If we rely too heavily on BMI or a single lipid panel, we will miss cases.

## Clues that still work — the practical, bedside triggers
Some features remain highly useful when you think “Is this monogenic diabetes?” — use them together rather than in isolation.
- Strong autosomal‑dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes across 2–3 generations. This case had a clear vertical transmission.
- Negative islet autoantibodies (GADA/IA‑2/ZnT8 when available). Absence of autoimmunity + diabetes in a young adult should raise suspicion.
- Preserved endogenous insulin secretion. A stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L (as used in this report) supports non‑type 1 physiology; the patient’s stimulated C‑peptide was 0.83 nmol/L.
- Disproportionate glycosuria. HNF1A mutations lower SGLT2 expression and the renal glucose threshold — glycosuria at relatively lower glucose levels is a red flag.
- Early, marked sensitivity to insulin secretagogues (sulfonylureas) or good response to GLP‑1 receptor agonists — many HNF1A patients can be managed without long‑term insulin if identified.
- Low hs‑CRP has been reported as an adjunctive clue in HNF1A‑MODY (not measured in this specific patient), but it is neither sensitive nor definitive.

None of these is perfect alone — combine family history, antibody and C‑peptide data, and the clinical course.

## Who to test? Be inclusive (especially outside European cohorts)
There’s no universal one‑size‑fits‑all rule, but consider genetic testing (NGS panel for common MODY genes) when diabetes is diagnosed young and one or more of the following apply:
- Onset at a young age (classically <25, but use judgement up to 35), particularly with an autosomal‑dominant family history.
- Negative islet autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide not consistent with type 1 diabetes.
- Glycosuria out of proportion to blood glucose or early unexplained glycosuria.
- Atypical course for T2D (minimal insulin requirement early on, or unexpectedly strong response to secretagogues).
- Clinical suspicion despite obesity, dyslipidaemia or acanthosis — these do not exclude MODY.

Use the MODY probability calculator as an adjunct, but be cautious: it was derived from a White European population and may misestimate risk in other ethnic groups. In this case the calculator gave a 62.4% probability — useful, but clinical judgement and family pedigree were essential.

## Genetic testing and variant interpretation — don’t over‑trust absence from databases
Next‑generation sequencing panels are the pragmatic test. Expect novel variants. Key points:
- Many HNF1A variants are private or novel; absence from ClinVar/VarSome does not mean benign.
- Variant classification needs integration: segregation in the family, location (DNA‑binding domain vs transactivation domain), functional predictions (eg, high AlphaMissense score as in this case, 0.995), and ACMG criteria.
- A confirmed genetic diagnosis has immediate therapeutic and family implications (cascade testing, reproductive counselling, and tailored therapy).

The index patient’s variant (p.N270S) was novel but met pathogenic/likely pathogenic criteria on multiple grounds and explained the phenotype.

## Treatment implications — the real clinical payoff
Why chase the genotype? Because it changes management:
- HNF1A‑MODY is classically sulfonylurea‑sensitive; many patients can avoid lifelong insulin.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are a reasonable and often safer alternative to sulfonylureas as β‑cell failure progresses — this case responded very well to dulaglutide with insulin withdrawal and later simplification to oral therapy.
- Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY: pre‑existing reduced SGLT2 expression can accentuate glycosuria and may increase risks of dehydration and euglycaemic DKA in vulnerable patients.
- Correct genetic diagnosis enables de‑escalation where appropriate, avoids unnecessary insulin, and directs family screening.

Note: acute hyperglycaemia still requires urgent control. In this case insulin was started initially to rapidly lower glucose while genetic testing proceeded. That’s practical and appropriate.

## A pragmatic in‑clinic workflow
If you see a young person with diabetes and you're unsure:
1. Take a three‑generation family history.
2. Order islet autoantibodies (GADA/IA‑2/ZnT8 if available) and measure C‑peptide (stimulated if possible).
3. Treat the hyperglycaemia as needed (don’t delay acute care).
4. If suspicion persists, order a MODY NGS panel and counsel about implications.
5. Revisit therapy when results return — many HNF1A patients can stop insulin and switch to targeted agents.

## Clinical pearls / Take‑home messages
- Don’t use BMI, acanthosis or a single elevated triglyceride level to exclude MODY — rising obesity blurs classical phenotype boundaries.
- A strong autosomal‑dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes is still one of the most powerful clues.
- Negative islet antibodies + preserved stimulated C‑peptide (>0.6 nmol/L in this report) should prompt consideration of monogenic diabetes.
- Disproportionate glycosuria can point to HNF1A (reduced SGLT2 expression) — and be a reason for caution with SGLT2 inhibitors.
- Genetic testing (NGS panel) is increasingly accessible and should be offered liberally in young-onset diabetes, especially in under‑studied non‑European populations; absence of a variant in ClinVar does not rule out pathogenicity.
- A confirmed HNF1A diagnosis often allows de‑escalation from insulin and enables precision therapy (sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 RAs), with benefits for both patient and family.

## Bottom line
In an obesogenic world, traditional exclusion criteria for MODY are losing their reliability. Think genetics when the story includes early onset, vertical family transmission, negative autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide — even if BMI or lipids look “type 2.” The case above shows the clinical and therapeutic value of casting a wider net: finding a novel HNF1A mutation transformed management and outcomes. Don’t let triglycerides or weight be the reason you miss a diagnosis you can treat precisely.



# The sweat test surprise: small‑fiber autonomic neuropathy at MODY diagnosis

A young man with newly recognized HNF1A‑MODY — HbA1c 10.2% — had an unexpected finding at presentation: severely reduced sudomotor function in the hands on electrochemical skin conductance testing (Sudoscan). No diabetic ketoacidosis, negative islet autoantibodies, a family history of early‑onset diabetes and a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant completed the picture. The surprising bit? Marked small‑fiber autonomic dysfunction at the time of diagnosis in a young patient with “MODY” rather than classic long‑standing type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

This case is a useful clinical reminder: neuropathy (particularly small‑fiber/autonomic) is driven by glycemic exposure and other metabolic risk, not by the diabetes label. Sudomotor testing flagged a problem that may otherwise have gone unnoticed, and it forced a practical discussion: what does an abnormal Sudoscan mean at diagnosis, how should you act, and what should you expect with treatment?

## Why this finding matters
- Many of us mentally reserve neuropathy for long‑standing diabetes. That’s a mistake. High glucose burden (even if under the “MODY” banner) can produce small‑fiber and autonomic dysfunction early.
- HNF1A‑MODY patients can present with substantial hyperglycaemia and glycosuria; their genetic diagnosis steers therapy (sulfonylureas vs GLP‑1 vs insulin) and raises unique drug cautions (SGLT‑2 inhibitors).
- A screening test that detects small‑fiber dysfunction at presentation gives you a chance to act early: tighten glycaemic control, address cardiovascular risk, evaluate symptoms, and plan follow‑up and supportive care.

## Quick primer: what Sudoscan (electrochemical skin conductance) measures
Sudoscan applies a low‑voltage current to palms and soles and measures electrochemical skin conductance (ESC), which reflects chloride ion flux secondary to sweat gland activity. Because sweat glands are innervated by small, unmyelinated sympathetic fibers, ESC provides a rapid, non‑invasive index of sudomotor small‑fiber autonomic function.

Strengths
- Fast (minutes), operator‑independent, painless — good as a screening tool.
- Gives quantitative hand/foot values and can detect subclinical dysfunction.

Limitations
- It’s a screening tool, not a gold‑standard diagnostic test. Results are influenced by skin temperature, hydration, medications (anticholinergics), and device‑specific reference ranges.
- Does not localize lesion or define somatic small‑fiber loss (skin biopsy for intraepidermal nerve fiber density [IENFD] measures somatic small fibers).
- Normal nerve conduction studies do not exclude small‑fiber or autonomic neuropathy; conversely, an abnormal Sudoscan requires clinical correlation and, if important, confirmatory testing (QSART, autonomic reflex testing, or skin biopsy).

## How I interpret an abnormal sudomotor test at diagnosis
1. Context is everything. An abnormal Sudoscan in a patient with HbA1c 10% and family history of early diabetes raises pretest probability that hyperglycaemia explains the finding. Asymptomatic abnormalities are common early.
2. Look for symptoms/signs: burning pain, distal small‑fiber sensory complaints, orthostatic dizziness, heat intolerance, erectile dysfunction, abnormal sweating, or GI dysautonomia. But small‑fiber dysfunction can be subclinical.
3. Check for confounders and alternative causes: alcohol, B12 deficiency (especially if on metformin long term), hypothyroidism, HIV, vasculitis/autoimmune neuropathy, chemotherapy/toxin exposure, or hereditary neuropathy. Basic labs (B12, TSH, creatinine, fasting glucose/HbA1c) are reasonable.
4. Decide on confirmatory testing based on impact: if results will change management or if symptoms are significant, pursue:
   - QSART (quantitative sudomotor axon reflex test) or formal autonomic reflex panel for sudomotor/autonomic confirmation.
   - Skin biopsy for IENFD if somatic small‑fiber neuropathy suspected and diagnosis would change care.
   - Neurology/autonomic referral for multi‑domain testing.

In the reported case, severely reduced hand conductance suggests truly significant autonomic small‑fiber dysfunction (hand involvement is uncommon as an initial isolated finding and may indicate more widespread involvement).

## What to do next — practical, patient‑focused steps
1. Intensify glycaemic control now. The strongest actionable intervention for diabetic neuropathy is reducing cumulative glycaemic exposure. This case shows that moving from insulin to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist + metformin brought HbA1c down to 6.0% at 6 months — the right move for long‑term microvascular risk.
2. Optimize cardiovascular risk factors. Treat blood pressure, lipids and smoking cessation aggressively — neuropathy progression tracks with vascular risk as well as glycaemia.
3. Basic neuropathy and foot care:
   - Do a focused neuropathy screen (symptom questionnaire, pinprick/temperature testing for small fibers, 10‑g monofilament for protective sensation).
   - Educate about foot care even if monofilament is normal (small‑fiber neuropathy can be painful and impair detection of minor injuries).
4. Symptom‑directed therapy:
   - For neuropathic pain: consider first‑line agents such as duloxetine or pregabalin/gabapentin; tricyclics if appropriate and safe.
   - For autonomic symptoms (orthostasis, gastroparesis, erectile dysfunction): treat symptomatically (fluid/salt, compression, fludrocortisone or midodrine for orthostatic hypotension; prokinetics for gastroparesis).
5. Avoid or be cautious with SGLT‑2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY patients. HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT‑2 expression and have been linked to pronounced glycosuria; SGLT‑2 inhibitors may increase risk of volume depletion and euglycaemic DKA in this group.
6. Refer when appropriate: neurology/autonomic center if testing is needed or symptoms are severe; podiatry for foot care if any loss of protective sensation is identified.

## When — and whether — to repeat autonomic/sudomotor testing
There is no high‑quality evidence dictating exact timing. Pragmatic approach:
- If abnormal Sudoscan but patient is asymptomatic and you’ve aggressively optimized glucose and risk factors, consider repeating after 6–12 months to look for signal of recovery or stabilization.
- If symptoms progress or are disabling, repeat testing earlier and refer to an autonomic clinic.
- Use the same testing modality and the same laboratory/device when possible to minimize variability.

Recognize that reversal is possible (especially early and with rapid glycaemic improvement), but timelines are variable and full recovery is not guaranteed.

## Research gaps worth calling out
- Do specific MODY genotypes (eg, HNF1A variants) carry different risks or patterns of small‑fiber/autonomic neuropathy? Data are sparse.
- How reversible is early sudomotor dysfunction after glycaemic control, and what is the typical time course?
- Standardization of Sudoscan cutoffs across devices, and age/ethnicity‑specific normative data, are needed to improve interpretation.
- Prospective studies are needed to define the value of screening newly diagnosed young‑onset diabetes (including MODY) for small‑fiber/autonomic neuropathy.

## Bottom line — clinical pearls
- An abnormal Sudoscan in a young person with new‑onset diabetes is not a lab quirk: it can reveal clinically meaningful small‑fiber autonomic dysfunction even at diagnosis.
- Neuropathy tracks glycaemic exposure (and vascular risk) more than the diagnostic label; early and effective glycaemic control matters in MODY as much as in type 1/2.
- Sudoscan is a useful screening tool but not diagnostic on its own — interpret in clinical context and confirm when results will change management.
- Act on an abnormal result: tighten glucose control, optimize BP/lipids, screen and protect the feet, treat symptoms, and consider neurology/autonomic referral.
- For HNF1A‑MODY, remember genotype implications for therapy (avoid reflexive use of SGLT‑2 inhibitors) and for family screening.

This case is a small but important reminder: don’t let the “MODY” label lull you into complacency about complications. If the sweat test says something is wrong, treat it as a call to action.
