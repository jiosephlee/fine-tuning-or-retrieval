Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## Chiari I Malformation and Respiratory Control—Clinically Relevant Pathophysiology

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is an anatomic diagnosis best established on sagittal MRI: cerebellar tonsils that extend at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum (foramen magnum = McRae line) are conventionally described as a Chiari I malformation. Imaging features that support clinical significance include crowded appearance of the foramen magnum, loss or narrowing of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) spaces at the craniocervical junction, peg‑like tonsils, and secondary findings such as syringomyelia. CT can suggest a crowded posterior fossa, as occurred in this patient’s historic scan, but MRI of the brain and cervical spine is the diagnostic standard because it directly visualises tonsillar position, brainstem anatomy and CSF flow.

Epidemiologically, isolated radiographic tonsillar ectopia is common relative to symptomatic disease: contemporary imaging surveys identify an approximate 1% prevalence of radiographic CMI in adults, whereas symptomatic “Chiari syndrome”—the constellation of signs and symptoms attributable to the malformation—occurs in roughly 0.1% of the population. There is a modest female predominance (about 1.3:1). Importantly, not every radiographic descent is clinically relevant; the clinician must distinguish incidental tonsillar ectopia from pathologic Chiari syndrome by integrating symptoms, signs and imaging.

A useful pathophysiological model centres on a developmentally small posterior fossa that creates a volumetric mismatch between intracranial contents and available space. Tonsillar descent occurs to accommodate this mismatch and can obstruct upward and downward CSF flow through the foramen magnum. That obstruction produces abnormal pressure transmission to the brainstem and spinal cord, can promote syrinx formation in the spinal cord, and leads either to static compression or to dynamic compression with pulsations of CSF and neural tissue. Because these effects exist along a continuum, the modern view emphasises a spectrum—from benign anatomical variants to clinically significant malformations that require intervention—rather than a single binary entity.

Understanding how CMI produces respiratory and bulbar symptoms requires a brief review of brainstem respiratory control. Automatic breathing originates in medullary and pontine circuits: the pre‑Bötzinger complex in the ventrolateral medulla is a principal rhythm‑generating kernel; the retrotrapezoid nucleus/parafacial respiratory group contributes central chemoreception and drives ventilation in response to elevated CO2/H+; the dorsal respiratory group, including the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) in the dorsal medulla, integrates afferent input from peripheral chemoreceptors (via cranial nerves IX and X) and lung stretch receptors. Motor output to inspiratory and expiratory muscles flows through the ventral respiratory group and via spinal respiratory motor neurons (eg, phrenic nucleus C3–C5). Voluntary breathing is mediated separately by cortical motor pathways (corticobulbar and corticospinal tracts) that can activate the same motor pools independently of automatic brainstem rhythms.

Tonsillar descent and crowded craniocervical anatomy can injure or distort these medullary circuits by direct compression, by impaired microvascular perfusion, and by disturbed CSF dynamics. Compression or distortion of the pre‑Bötzinger complex and adjacent ventrolateral medullary structures blunts rhythm generation and the ventilatory response to CO2; involvement of the retrotrapezoid/parafacial region reduces central chemoreception. Dorsal compression of the NTS interferes with integration of peripheral chemoreceptor and vagal afferent signals. The net physiological consequence is reduced automatic respiratory drive—manifest clinically as central hypoventilation and central apnoeas—while voluntary respiratory control may remain relatively preserved because cortical pathways bypass the damaged automatic centres.

These mechanisms explain the clinical picture seen in this case. The patient had dramatic, episodic oxygen desaturations (measured at 70%–80%) often without dyspnoea, normal pulmonary imaging and preserved voluntary respiratory function between events (normal resting arterial blood gas and pulmonary function tests). The predominance of central events on sleep testing (72% central) and anatomic evidence of substantial tonsillar descent (11 mm) are concordant with medullary dysfunction. The type III sleep study used here lacks EEG and therefore underestimates event rates, but the pattern—central events, severe nocturnal desaturations, preserved conscious breathing—fits the pathophysiology of impaired automatic brainstem control.

Bulbar dysfunction in CMI is similarly explained by injury to lower cranial nerve nuclei and their connections. The nucleus ambiguus provides motor innervation to pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles (via cranial nerves IX and X), and the hypoglossal nucleus (XII) controls tongue movement; dorsal and lateral medullary involvement produce incoordination of the pharyngeal swallow, impaired laryngeal closure and a weak cough. These neurogenic impairments cause choking and aspiration that typically affect solids and liquids equally (a hallmark of neuromuscular, rather than obstructive or mechanical, dysphagia). That explains why repeated laryngoscopy and endoscopy in this patient were normal for structure yet symptoms persisted.

A striking clinical consequence of medullary involvement is “silent” hypoxaemia or the absence of expected dyspnoea despite marked hypoxaemia. Sensation of breathlessness depends on peripheral chemoreceptor and vagal afferents relaying to the NTS and then ascending pathways (through parabrachial and thalamic relays to insular and cingulate cortex) that produce conscious interoception. If those afferent relays or their brainstem relays are disrupted—by compression, ischemia or chronic injury—afferent signalling fails to reach cortical centres and the subjective warning of air hunger is blunted or absent. The airport episode in this case, where distraction and severe desaturation occurred without dyspnoea, illustrates this mechanism: automatic drive and afferent awareness were both likely impaired.

Finally, it is important to recognise the distinction between primary (congenital) and secondary (acquired) Chiari I malformations. Primary CMI is usually conceptualised as a congenital posterior fossa hypoplasia, whereas acquired forms can follow trauma, intracranial hypotension, space‑occupying processes, or other perturbations of CSF dynamics. In this patient the temporal association between a motor‑vehicle collision, prompt onset of occipital headache, and a contemporaneous CT scan showing a crowded foramen magnum raises the possibility that trauma either unmasked a pre‑existing tendency or contributed to progressive tonsillar descent. Without pre‑trauma imaging it is impossible to prove causation, but the clinical implication is the same: brainstem compression from tonsillar descent produced the bulbar and respiratory manifestations.

Clinical lessons tied to pathophysiology are direct. Unexplained neurogenic dysphagia, recurrent choking without structural airway lesions, nocturnal desaturation documented outside the sleep laboratory, or central apnoeas on testing should prompt targeted imaging of the craniocervical junction and comprehensive sleep assessment that includes EEG and capnography. Posterior fossa decompression aims to restore CSF flow, relieve medullary compression and can reverse or improve respiratory and bulbar symptoms; meanwhile, supportive measures (sleep‑time ventilatory support, supplemental oxygen or swallowing precautions) are used to reduce immediate risk. In this patient, the combination of characteristic headaches, progressive bulbar symptoms, objective desaturations and marked tonsillar descent coherently links the anatomy to the clinical syndrome of acquired central hypoventilation and neurogenic dysphagia attributable to CMI.

## Case Presentation—From Atypical Choking to Silent Hypoxaemia

The patient’s clinical story unfolded over more than a decade and is best understood as a sequence of symptoms that gradually shifted the diagnostic focus from otolaryngology and gastroenterology toward the brainstem. Fourteen years before presentation she had a minor motor vehicle accident with brief loss of consciousness, after which persistent occipital and vertex headache developed. Seven years before the current consultation she began to experience sudden, distressing choking episodes while eating and, later, during sleep. These nocturnal events became frequent — three to four times per week, sometimes twice in one night — and caused progressive social withdrawal and marked psychological distress because they were unpredictable and embarrassing.

The headache phenotype is important because it differed from the classic Valsalva‑precipitated occipital pain often emphasised in Chiari syndrome. Her pain was constant, localised to a coin‑sized area at the vertex/occiput and accompanied by striking scalp allodynia: even the weight of her hair provoked pain until she shortened it and obtained partial relief. This focal, allodynic presentation pointed to somatic or neuralgic scalp sensitisation rather than the typical paroxysmal cough‑linked posterior headaches that more directly suggest raised intrafamilial posterior fossa pressure.

The choking episodes themselves carried several diagnostic clues. They were abrupt, provoked violent coughing and gasping but never produced complete airway obstruction; they occurred both during meals and during sleep; and they affected solids and liquids equally. Clinically, dysphagia or choking that involves liquids and solids to the same degree, especially when associated with coughing and aspiration, tends to reflect an oropharyngeal (neurogenic) swallowing disorder rather than a mechanical oesophageal lesion, which more often produces progressive solid‑food intolerance. The nocturnal timing of many episodes also implies failure of automatic control of airway protection and coordination of swallowing and respiration — functions that depend on intact brainstem circuits.

Despite the severity and frequency of her symptoms, repeated otolaryngological and gastrointestinal evaluations were unrevealing: three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper endoscopies reported normal anatomy and no obstructing lesions. Those normal investigations, coupled with persistent, intermittent symptoms, unfortunately led to repeated attribution of events to anxiety in emergency settings. This pattern illustrates a common pitfall: intermittent, motor‑orchestrated dysfunction of cranial nerves or brainstem swallow/respiratory centres can be missed on static endoscopic examinations, and normal ENT/GI studies should prompt consideration of a neurological cause when clinical features suggest a neurogenic pattern.

A pivotal objective clue came from peripheral oxygen monitoring. While asymptomatic during clinic visits — with a normal respiratory rate, oxygen saturation at 96% and normal pulmonary function — the patient’s home pulse oximeter repeatedly recorded profound desaturations into the 70%–80% range. The most dramatic example occurred at an airport, where an oxygen saturation of 78% was documented in the absence of dyspnoea; supplemental oxygen produced rapid clinical recovery. Subsequent respiratory workup (chest imaging, spirometry, arterial blood gas while awake) was normal. Severe hypoxaemia without subjective breathlessness — so‑called “silent” hypoxaemia — strongly suggests disruption of afferent chemoreceptive or brainstem interoceptive pathways that normally generate the sensation of air hunger and drive automatic respiration. The clinical pattern — preserved voluntary ventilation during wakefulness but hypoventilatory events during sleep or distraction — is characteristic of acquired central hypoventilation due to brainstem dysfunction.

The final piece that linked these clinical clues was imaging that had been overlooked. A non‑contrast head CT performed after the motor vehicle accident, initially read as normal, showed on re‑review a crowded foramen magnum with loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction. That imaging abnormality, when correlated with the history of focal occipital headache, neurogenic‑pattern choking, nocturnal desaturations and normal ENT/GI/pulmonary tests, raised the suspicion of Chiari malformation with brainstem compression. Subsequent MRI confirmed substantial tonsillar descent and supported a unifying diagnosis.

Taken together, several red flags in this case should alert clinicians away from a purely ENT or gastrointestinal explanation and toward brainstem pathology: focal occipital/vertex pain with scalp allodynia; choking that is unpredictable, affects both solids and liquids, and occurs during sleep; repeated normal endoscopic evaluations; severe, asymptomatic oxygen desaturation episodes with normal daytime respiratory testing; and retrospective evidence of craniocervical crowding on prior imaging. When this pattern is present, targeted neurologic investigation — including high‑resolution MRI of the posterior fossa and craniocervical junction, videofluoroscopic swallowing assessment, and formal sleep studies with capnography — is warranted rather than continued attribution of symptoms to peripheral or psychogenic causes.

## Diagnostic Workup and Reasoning—Linking Structure to Function

This patient’s diagnostic pathway illustrates how careful reappraisal of prior data combined with targeted physiologic testing can convert a collection of otherwise unexplained symptoms into a coherent neurological diagnosis. Her long history of episodic choking (both awake and nocturnal), repeated asymptomatic oxygen desaturations recorded at home (to 70%–80%) and a chronically painful, focal occipital/vertex headache resisted repeated ENT and gastroenterological evaluations. That pattern—intermittent bulbar dysfunction plus nocturnal hypoxaemia with otherwise normal airway and pulmonary exams—raised suspicion for a central (brainstem) process and prompted re-examination of older imaging.

Systematic re‑review of historical imaging
A non‑contrast CT obtained after the motor‑vehicle collision 14 years earlier had been reported as normal but, on focused re‑review, showed a crowded foramen magnum with loss of CSF space at the cranio‑cervical junction and cerebellar tissue extending toward the opisthion. This step is important clinically: subtle indicators on an older CT can generate a new, testable hypothesis and change the diagnostic trajectory. CT can suggest posterior fossa crowding but is less sensitive than MRI for delineating tonsillar position, CSF flow and parenchymal relationships; therefore an abnormal or equivocal CT should prompt dedicated MRI of the brain and craniocervical junction.

MRI confirmation and its implications
The subsequent MRI confirmed Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent and craniocervical crowding, but without a reported syringomyelia. By the usual radiologic criterion (≥5 mm tonsillar descent), 11 mm is clearly abnormal and, combined with the CT evidence of CSF effacement at the foramen magnum, supports significant craniocervical junction crowding. The clinical relevance is that caudal tonsillar displacement can compress the dorsal medulla and adjacent lower cranial nerve nuclei and obstruct pulsatile CSF flow. Those anatomic effects provide a mechanistic link to this patient’s symptoms: compression of medullary respiratory centres and their afferent/efferent connections can impair automatic (brainstem‑mediated) respiration and bulbar motor control, producing nocturnal apnea, choking and dysphagia even when daytime voluntary breathing is preserved. The absence of a syrinx reduces the likelihood of extensive spinal cord involvement but does not exclude clinically important brainstem dysfunction.

Why normal daytime respiratory tests do not exclude nocturnal central hypoventilation
Pulmonary function tests in this patient were within normal limits (FEV1 98% predicted, FVC 96% predicted) with only modestly reduced respiratory pressures (MIP 80% predicted, MEP 75% predicted), and an arterial blood gas on room air during wakefulness was normal (PaCO2 42 mm Hg, PaO2 88 mm Hg). These results document intact lung mechanics and preserved voluntary respiratory muscle function and do not contradict a central cause. The key physiologic concept is that voluntary breathing (cortically mediated) and automatic breathing (brainstem mediated) are dissociable: a structural lesion that injures automatic brainstem respiratory networks can leave voluntary drive intact. Therefore daytime PFTs and ABGs measured during wakefulness can be normal while significant nocturnal hypoventilation or central apnoea occurs during sleep, when automatic control predominates.

Interpretation and limitations of the type III sleep study
A home/type III sleep study in this case reported an AHI of 6.0 with a predominance of centrally classified events (72%). Type III devices record airflow, respiratory effort, oximetry and heart rate and thus can distinguish central from obstructive events by the presence or absence of respiratory effort. That made the finding of mostly central events a physiologically important signal. However, type III studies have important limitations in this clinical setting that explain the apparent mismatch between an only “mild” AHI and the alarming clinical desaturations: they lack EEG, so total sleep time cannot be measured and the AHI is calculated using total recording time (including wake), which underestimates event frequency per hour of true sleep. They also do not record cortical arousals, sleep architecture, or transcutaneous/end‑tidal CO2, so sustained hypoventilation (persistent hypercapnia) can be missed. In addition, intermittent events and signal artifact can lead to event misclassification. Given the patient’s documented severe desaturations (including the airport episode) and the MRI evidence of brainstem crowding, the modest AHI from a type III study should not be taken as definitive evidence of minimal disease.

Why full polysomnography with CO2 monitoring was indicated
Given the clinical picture and the limitations above, arranging a full (type I) polysomnography with EEG, end‑tidal or transcutaneous CO2 monitoring and video was the appropriate next diagnostic step. Full PSG provides true sleep time and staging (so the AHI and event indices are accurate), detects arousals and sleep architecture abnormalities that can both precipitate and result from apnoeas, and—critically—CO2 monitoring identifies sustained nocturnal hypoventilation that will not always produce discrete apnoeas. Transcutaneous CO2 is especially useful for overnight monitoring because it correlates well with arterial CO2 over extended periods. Objective measurement of nocturnal hypercapnia (and of the timing and pattern of central apnoeas) is essential for decisions about nocturnal ventilatory support (such as CPAP vs bilevel or adaptive servo‑ventilation) and for neurosurgical risk–benefit discussions.

Role and interpretation of the videofluoroscopic swallow study
The patient underwent multiple laryngoscopies and upper endoscopies that were structurally normal, which is a common scenario when dysphagia is neurogenic. A videofluoroscopic swallowing study (modified barium swallow) is the appropriate functional test in this context: it evaluates oral, pharyngeal and upper oesophageal phases, quantifies aspiration/penetration and characterises physiologic deficits (delayed pharyngeal trigger, reduced laryngeal elevation, pharyngeal residue, cricopharyngeal dysfunction). In brainstem dysfunction you often see delayed swallow initiation, reduced pharyngeal contraction and silent aspiration because the cough reflex or sensation may be impaired. In this case the swallow study did not reveal a structural obstructive cause; even if it had been nondiagnostic, a normal study does not rule out intermittent brainstem‑mediated dysphagia because episodes can be paroxysmal and dependent on sleep/wake state.

Differential diagnosis and how it was narrowed
Before accepting a unifying central diagnosis, common alternative explanations were actively excluded. Laryngopharyngeal reflux and GERD with laryngospasm were considered but were not supported by pH monitoring, by the lack of endoscopic findings and by failure to respond to acid suppression. Myasthenia gravis was evaluated with acetylcholine receptor and anti‑MuSK antibodies and by clinical assessment for fatigability and diurnal variation; the investigations were negative and the clinical pattern did not fit fatigable weakness. Primary pulmonary disease was ruled out by normal chest imaging and near‑normal PFTs. Psychiatric attribution had been suggested in some emergency visits, but objectively documented severe desaturations with rapid reversibility on supplemental oxygen argue against a purely anxiety‑driven phenomenon. With structural MRI evidence of Chiari I and physiologic signals of central events and hypoxaemia, the balance of probability favoured a brainstem explanation.

Diagnostic synthesis: preserved voluntary breathing with impaired automatic control
Putting the findings together produces a coherent mechanistic diagnosis. The MRI documents anatomical potential for medullary compression and CSF effacement at the foramen magnum; the clinical history shows episodic bulbar dysfunction and striking nocturnal or unperceived hypoxaemia; daytime respiratory testing is intact—consistent with preserved cortical voluntary drive—and the sleep test recorded predominantly central events. These elements fit the phenotype of acquired central alveolar hypoventilation secondary to craniocervical junction pathology (Chiari I) in which automatic respiratory control is selectively impaired while voluntary control remains. This pathophysiologic interpretation also explains the patient’s lack of dyspnoea during severe desaturations: disruption of brainstem afferent pathways can blunt the cortical perception of hypoxaemia and hypercapnia. Important caveats remain—causality cannot be proved without prospective response to intervention, the type III study almost certainly underestimates nocturnal respiratory pathology, and intermittent phenomena may be missed by single studies—but the evidence is sufficient to justify full polysomnography with CO2 monitoring, continued neurosurgical assessment, and targeted swallow management while definitive therapeutic planning proceeds.

Key practical lessons from the workup
For trainees, this case reinforces several pragmatic points: re‑review historical imaging when the clinical picture is unexplained; recognise that normal daytime PFTs/ABGs do not exclude nocturnal central hypoventilation; interpret type III sleep studies cautiously when EEG and CO2 data are absent; use videofluoroscopy to assess functional, neurogenic dysphagia even when structural studies are normal; and integrate imaging and physiologic data to form a testable working diagnosis that guides further definitive testing (full PSG) and multidisciplinary management.

## Management Pathways—Immediate Safety and Definitive Therapy

Management of Chiari I malformation (CMI) with brainstem, lower cranial nerve or respiratory involvement requires two simultaneous priorities: immediate patient safety (preventing life‑threatening hypoxaemia and aspiration) and timely consideration of definitive therapy (posterior fossa decompression). Decisions are individualized, made by a multidisciplinary team (neurosurgery, sleep medicine/pulmonology, ENT/speech–language pathology, anaesthesia) and guided by objective data (MRI including cine flow, full polysomnography with CO₂ monitoring, and functional assessments). In the case described, these pathways were explicitly balanced: neurosurgery recommended decompression based on severe symptomatic and radiologic findings, while the patient chose a period of conservative management with structured monitoring.

Indications for posterior fossa decompression centre on clinical evidence of symptomatic brainstem or lower cranial nerve dysfunction plus corroborating radiology. Indications commonly considered strong are progressive bulbar signs (dysphagia, recurrent aspiration, stridor or persistent choking), documented central hypoventilation with significant nocturnal desaturation or daytime hypercapnia, debilitating Valsalva‑provoked occipital headache refractory to medical measures, and the presence of syringomyelia or progressive neurological deficits. Imaging that supports surgical treatment includes significant tonsillar descent with direct medullary compression and demonstrable CSF flow obstruction at the foramen magnum on cine MRI. In this patient the combination of 11 mm tonsillar descent, repeated severe asymptomatic desaturations to 70%–80% and a high proportion of central events on the type III study made decompression a reasonable recommendation, even though definitive preoperative PSG and cine flow studies were still pending.

Posterior fossa decompression aims to restore normal CSF flow at the craniocervical junction, relieve mechanical compression of the dorsal medulla and lower cranial nerve nuclei, and thereby reduce or reverse syrinx formation and brainstem dysfunction. Clinically, many patients experience improvement in headaches, bulbar symptoms and sleep‑disordered breathing after successful decompression. However, benefits are variable: long‑standing or severe brainstem injury may not fully reverse, central hypoventilation can persist despite radiologic decompression, and symptom improvement may take weeks to months. Surgical approaches differ (suboccipital craniectomy with C1 laminectomy, with or without duraplasty; selective tonsillar reduction) and carry risks—CSF leak, wound infection, pseudomeningocele, new or worsened neurological deficits and, rarely, the need for stabilization if occipitocervical instability is present. These trade‑offs must be discussed with the patient; duraplasty tends to give better decompression and syrinx resolution but at a higher complication rate compared with bone‑only decompression.

While a definitive surgical plan is prepared, conservative and supportive measures focus on symptom control and risk mitigation. Headache management may include neuropathic agents (for occipital neuralgia), targeted occipital nerve blocks when appropriate, judicious use of non‑opioid analgesics, and simple behavioural measures (avoiding hair tension in this patient’s case). Activity modification to reduce repeated Valsalva (avoiding heavy lifting or straining) is reasonable. Importantly, medications and substances that depress respiratory drive (opioids, benzodiazepines, excessive alcohol, illicit sedatives) should be avoided because they can worsen central hypoventilation.

A respiratory safety plan is essential. Objective overnight monitoring (home oximetry as an interim measure and formal overnight polysomnography with EEG and transcutaneous/end‑tidal CO₂ for definitive assessment) should be arranged urgently. Clear escalation thresholds should be communicated: recurrent or sustained oxyhaemoglobin saturations <88% on room air, single severe desaturations to very low levels (for example <80%), new daytime somnolence, morning headaches, syncope or evidence of rising PaCO₂ or daytime hypercapnia warrant prompt evaluation and likely initiation of ventilatory support or urgent intervention. For ventilatory support, the choice depends on the physiology: CPAP is appropriate when an obstructive component is present; for central hypoventilation or predominant central apnoeas, bilevel positive airway pressure with a timed backup rate (S/T or ST modes) or other modes that guarantee minute ventilation are preferred because they support ventilation and correct hypoventilation. Adaptive servo‑ventilation (ASV) can be useful in selected complex central sleep apnoea patterns but must be used cautiously (it is contraindicated in certain heart‑failure populations) and only after specialist review. Titration should include CO₂ monitoring because supplemental oxygen by itself can correct hypoxaemia while worsening hypercapnia and masking the underlying ventilatory failure—therefore oxygen should not be used as sole therapy for central hypoventilation.

Dysphagia management is another immediate priority to reduce aspiration risk. Early involvement of speech–language pathology and instrumental assessment (videofluoroscopic swallow study or FEES) are required to characterise physiology and to teach compensatory strategies (chin‑tuck, head‑turn, pacing, effortful swallow) and to recommend appropriate bolus modifications. Patients with documented aspiration, recurrent pneumonias, marked weight loss or inability to maintain oral intake should be considered for enteral feeding (temporary nasogastric tube or longer‑term gastrostomy) while definitive management is arranged. In many CMI patients dysphagia improves after decompression, but perioperative aspiration risk and anaesthetic airway issues necessitate careful coordination.

Preoperative optimization should aim to define anatomy and physiology and to minimise perioperative risk. Cine MRI to document CSF flow across the foramen magnum helps surgical planning; a diagnostic, attended (type I) polysomnogram with continuous CO₂ monitoring is recommended by current guidance for patients with suspected sleep‑disordered breathing related to CMI. Pulmonary function testing including vital capacity and maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures, arterial blood gas (or baseline transcutaneous CO₂), nutritional assessment and correction of active infections are useful. Anaesthetic teams should be alerted to bulbar dysfunction and aspiration risk so airway plans (rapid‑sequence intubation, avoidance of excessive sedation, readiness for postoperative ventilatory support) and postoperative ICU or high‑dependency monitoring can be arranged. Extubation should only proceed when airway protective reflexes and adequate spontaneous ventilation are documented.

Shared decision‑making is central because the balance of potential benefit, surgical risk and uncertainty about the degree of reversibility varies between patients. Discussions should review the objective data (MRI appearance with tonsillar descent and CSF flow, PSG results including central apnoea index and CO₂ trends, frequency and severity of desaturation events, dysphagia severity and nutritional status), likely outcomes and possible complications, and the patient's values and risk tolerance. Guidelines endorse obtaining formal polysomnography in CMI patients with suspected sleep‑disordered breathing; those data often materially influence the recommendation for surgery and provide baseline measures to judge postoperative improvement. If a patient elects conservative management—as in this report—a formal monitoring plan must be specified (scheduled clinical reviews, serial overnight oximetry or repeat PSG if symptoms progress, prompt reassessment for any life‑threatening desaturation or new bulbar decline), and thresholds for escalation to surgery should be predefined.

In summary, the immediate management emphasis is respiratory and aspiration safety using objective monitoring and, where appropriate, noninvasive ventilatory support rather than supplemental oxygen alone. Definitive therapy—posterior fossa decompression—should be offered when there is symptomatic brainstem or lower cranial nerve dysfunction supported by imaging and physiological testing, recognizing that improvement in central hypoventilation is common but not guaranteed. Multidisciplinary evaluation, careful preoperative optimization (including cine MRI and full PSG with CO₂ monitoring) and shared decision‑making with clear safety nets are the cornerstones of care for patients like the one described.

## Outcome, Prognosis, and Practice Lessons

Case-specific outcome and monitoring plan
The patient elected a period of conservative management while considering definitive neurosurgical treatment. A structured surveillance plan was put in place: monthly clinical reviews, quarterly overnight oximetry (home or laboratory), annual MRI of the brain/cervical spine to screen for syringomyelia or progressive tonsillar descent, and a planned type I polysomnography (full EEG, airflow, effort, oximetry and capnography) at an external sleep centre. These measures are intended to detect early deterioration in respiratory drive, bulbar function or structural progression that would prompt expedited intervention.

Objective metrics to follow and how to use them
Monitoring should combine patient-reported and physiologic data so change can be detected objectively. Maintain a symptom diary documenting frequency, timing (sleep versus wake), and severity of choking episodes and any associated syncope, dizziness or cough; record contextual pulse oximetry readings and the circumstances in which nadirs occur. Objective respiratory measures to trend include nocturnal oxygen nadir, percentage of recording time spent below 90%/88%, and duration/frequency of profound desaturations (the patient’s recorded nadirs of 70–80% fall into a clearly worrisome range). Full (type I) polysomnography will provide central apnoea index (CAI), AHI using true sleep time, oxygen nadirs, arousal index and transcutaneous/end-tidal CO₂ (to detect hypoventilation). Serial neurological examinations should focus on lower cranial nerves (gag, palate elevation, tongue strength and atrophy, voice quality), cerebellar function and long-tract signs; repeat modified barium swallow or videofluoroscopic assessment is appropriate when dysphagia/aspiration changes. Baseline and periodic pulmonary function testing including maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures and daytime arterial blood gas or spot PaCO₂ can help identify evolving daytime hypoventilation.

When to escalate to urgent neurosurgical evaluation
Escalation should be prompt if there is objective evidence of clinical or physiologic deterioration. Clear triggers include new or progressive lower cranial nerve dysfunction with aspiration or recurrent aspiration pneumonia, demonstrable increase in nocturnal central apnoeas or worsening oxygenation on type I polysomnography (rising CAI, lower nadirs, greater time spent <88%), development of daytime hypercapnia or hypoventilation (sustained PaCO₂ above baseline or above commonly accepted normal ranges), evidence of progressive syringomyelia or new long-tract deficits on imaging or exam, or failure of reasonable conservative measures with significant impact on safety or quality of life. Because decisions are individualized, these findings should prompt urgent multidisciplinary review (sleep medicine, neurology, ENT/swallowing and neurosurgery).

Prognosis and what the literature suggests
Published case reports and series—while limited—indicate that posterior fossa decompression can restore CSF flow dynamics, relieve brainstem compression and frequently lead to improvement or resolution of bulbar and respiratory symptoms. Some patients have rapid postoperative improvement in hypoventilation and sleep-disordered breathing, whereas others have partial or no recovery; long-term outcomes are heterogeneous and data are largely observational. Importantly, untreated brainstem compression carries a real risk of life‑threatening hypoxaemia or respiratory failure, so a conservative strategy requires active surveillance and low threshold for reassessment when objective measures worsen.

Practical notes on sleep testing and interpretation
Remember that limited (type III) studies systematically underestimate AHI because they lack EEG to determine true sleep time; a low AHI on a type III study does not exclude clinically significant sleep-related hypoventilation or central apnoeas. In suspected central hypoventilation from craniocervical pathology, definitive assessment requires type I polysomnography with simultaneous CO₂ monitoring (transcutaneous or end-tidal) and video when possible, so that central events, hypoventilation and behavioural correlates (awareness, arousals) can be accurately documented.

Clinical pearls from this case
Several bedside clues point to a neurogenic cause of choking: equal impairment with solids and liquids, normal endoscopic and laryngoscopic evaluations, and a high index of suspicion when dysphagia coexists with chronic occipital headache or historical head trauma. “Silent” hypoxaemia—that is, profound oxygen desaturation without dyspnoea—is a red flag for impaired brainstem afferent signalling and warrants expedited investigation. Always consider re‑reviewing old imaging (films or archived CT/MRI) when the clinical picture is discordant with prior reports; subtle craniocervical crowding may be overlooked on routine reads. Finally, a predominance of central events on any study should not be dismissed even when the overall AHI appears low.

A practical, stepwise diagnostic approach to unexplained choking and hypoxaemia
Begin with targeted evaluation to exclude common local causes: ENT and gastrointestinal investigations (flexible laryngoscopy, endoscopy) and a validated swallowing assessment. If those are negative or the pattern suggests neurogenic dysphagia, screen for neuromuscular disease (clinical exam, antibody testing, electrophysiology as indicated) and obtain baseline respiratory testing (PFTs, MIP/MEP, chest imaging). Concurrently arrange a modified barium swallow to quantify aspiration risk. If otolaryngology/gastroenterology and neuromuscular workup are unrevealing or if there are accompanying headache/brainstem signs, obtain MRI of the brain and cervical spine focused on the craniocervical junction. Perform definitive polysomnography with CO₂ monitoring when sleep-disordered breathing or nocturnal desaturation is suspected. Early multidisciplinary involvement (sleep medicine, neurology, neurosurgery, speech/swallow therapy, respiratory medicine) facilitates timely decision-making.

Unanswered questions and research needs highlighted by this case
Key gaps remain: the precise neural mechanisms responsible for loss of dyspnoea perception in brainstem compression, the extent to which trauma causes versus unmasks Chiari anatomy, standardized thresholds and timing for surgical decompression when respiratory symptoms predominate, and the absence of validated symptom instruments specific to Chiari-related bulbar and respiratory dysfunction. Prospective registries and controlled series that correlate imaging, polysomnography (including CO₂), and clinical outcomes before and after decompression would help define best practices and surveillance intervals.

Bottom line for clinical practice
This case underscores that unexplained choking and episodic or “silent” hypoxaemia merit neurological consideration and targeted imaging of the craniocervical junction. Conservative management is reasonable when the patient prefers it and objective surveillance is rigorous, but worsening physiologic indices, progressive bulbar dysfunction, recurrent aspiration or imaging progression should trigger expedited neurosurgical evaluation because posterior fossa decompression can be corrective in many patients.
