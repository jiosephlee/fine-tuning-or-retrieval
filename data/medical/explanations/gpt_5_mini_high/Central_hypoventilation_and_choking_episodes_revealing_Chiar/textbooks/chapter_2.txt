Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 2: ## Case Presentation—From Atypical Choking to Silent Hypoxaemia

The patient’s clinical story unfolded over more than a decade and is best understood as a sequence of symptoms that gradually shifted the diagnostic focus from otolaryngology and gastroenterology toward the brainstem. Fourteen years before presentation she had a minor motor vehicle accident with brief loss of consciousness, after which persistent occipital and vertex headache developed. Seven years before the current consultation she began to experience sudden, distressing choking episodes while eating and, later, during sleep. These nocturnal events became frequent — three to four times per week, sometimes twice in one night — and caused progressive social withdrawal and marked psychological distress because they were unpredictable and embarrassing.

The headache phenotype is important because it differed from the classic Valsalva‑precipitated occipital pain often emphasised in Chiari syndrome. Her pain was constant, localised to a coin‑sized area at the vertex/occiput and accompanied by striking scalp allodynia: even the weight of her hair provoked pain until she shortened it and obtained partial relief. This focal, allodynic presentation pointed to somatic or neuralgic scalp sensitisation rather than the typical paroxysmal cough‑linked posterior headaches that more directly suggest raised intrafamilial posterior fossa pressure.

The choking episodes themselves carried several diagnostic clues. They were abrupt, provoked violent coughing and gasping but never produced complete airway obstruction; they occurred both during meals and during sleep; and they affected solids and liquids equally. Clinically, dysphagia or choking that involves liquids and solids to the same degree, especially when associated with coughing and aspiration, tends to reflect an oropharyngeal (neurogenic) swallowing disorder rather than a mechanical oesophageal lesion, which more often produces progressive solid‑food intolerance. The nocturnal timing of many episodes also implies failure of automatic control of airway protection and coordination of swallowing and respiration — functions that depend on intact brainstem circuits.

Despite the severity and frequency of her symptoms, repeated otolaryngological and gastrointestinal evaluations were unrevealing: three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper endoscopies reported normal anatomy and no obstructing lesions. Those normal investigations, coupled with persistent, intermittent symptoms, unfortunately led to repeated attribution of events to anxiety in emergency settings. This pattern illustrates a common pitfall: intermittent, motor‑orchestrated dysfunction of cranial nerves or brainstem swallow/respiratory centres can be missed on static endoscopic examinations, and normal ENT/GI studies should prompt consideration of a neurological cause when clinical features suggest a neurogenic pattern.

A pivotal objective clue came from peripheral oxygen monitoring. While asymptomatic during clinic visits — with a normal respiratory rate, oxygen saturation at 96% and normal pulmonary function — the patient’s home pulse oximeter repeatedly recorded profound desaturations into the 70%–80% range. The most dramatic example occurred at an airport, where an oxygen saturation of 78% was documented in the absence of dyspnoea; supplemental oxygen produced rapid clinical recovery. Subsequent respiratory workup (chest imaging, spirometry, arterial blood gas while awake) was normal. Severe hypoxaemia without subjective breathlessness — so‑called “silent” hypoxaemia — strongly suggests disruption of afferent chemoreceptive or brainstem interoceptive pathways that normally generate the sensation of air hunger and drive automatic respiration. The clinical pattern — preserved voluntary ventilation during wakefulness but hypoventilatory events during sleep or distraction — is characteristic of acquired central hypoventilation due to brainstem dysfunction.

The final piece that linked these clinical clues was imaging that had been overlooked. A non‑contrast head CT performed after the motor vehicle accident, initially read as normal, showed on re‑review a crowded foramen magnum with loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction. That imaging abnormality, when correlated with the history of focal occipital headache, neurogenic‑pattern choking, nocturnal desaturations and normal ENT/GI/pulmonary tests, raised the suspicion of Chiari malformation with brainstem compression. Subsequent MRI confirmed substantial tonsillar descent and supported a unifying diagnosis.

Taken together, several red flags in this case should alert clinicians away from a purely ENT or gastrointestinal explanation and toward brainstem pathology: focal occipital/vertex pain with scalp allodynia; choking that is unpredictable, affects both solids and liquids, and occurs during sleep; repeated normal endoscopic evaluations; severe, asymptomatic oxygen desaturation episodes with normal daytime respiratory testing; and retrospective evidence of craniocervical crowding on prior imaging. When this pattern is present, targeted neurologic investigation — including high‑resolution MRI of the posterior fossa and craniocervical junction, videofluoroscopic swallowing assessment, and formal sleep studies with capnography — is warranted rather than continued attribution of symptoms to peripheral or psychogenic causes.
