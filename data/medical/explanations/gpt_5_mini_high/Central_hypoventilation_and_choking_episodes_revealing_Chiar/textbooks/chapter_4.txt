Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 4: ## Management Pathways—Immediate Safety and Definitive Therapy

Management of Chiari I malformation (CMI) with brainstem, lower cranial nerve or respiratory involvement requires two simultaneous priorities: immediate patient safety (preventing life‑threatening hypoxaemia and aspiration) and timely consideration of definitive therapy (posterior fossa decompression). Decisions are individualized, made by a multidisciplinary team (neurosurgery, sleep medicine/pulmonology, ENT/speech–language pathology, anaesthesia) and guided by objective data (MRI including cine flow, full polysomnography with CO₂ monitoring, and functional assessments). In the case described, these pathways were explicitly balanced: neurosurgery recommended decompression based on severe symptomatic and radiologic findings, while the patient chose a period of conservative management with structured monitoring.

Indications for posterior fossa decompression centre on clinical evidence of symptomatic brainstem or lower cranial nerve dysfunction plus corroborating radiology. Indications commonly considered strong are progressive bulbar signs (dysphagia, recurrent aspiration, stridor or persistent choking), documented central hypoventilation with significant nocturnal desaturation or daytime hypercapnia, debilitating Valsalva‑provoked occipital headache refractory to medical measures, and the presence of syringomyelia or progressive neurological deficits. Imaging that supports surgical treatment includes significant tonsillar descent with direct medullary compression and demonstrable CSF flow obstruction at the foramen magnum on cine MRI. In this patient the combination of 11 mm tonsillar descent, repeated severe asymptomatic desaturations to 70%–80% and a high proportion of central events on the type III study made decompression a reasonable recommendation, even though definitive preoperative PSG and cine flow studies were still pending.

Posterior fossa decompression aims to restore normal CSF flow at the craniocervical junction, relieve mechanical compression of the dorsal medulla and lower cranial nerve nuclei, and thereby reduce or reverse syrinx formation and brainstem dysfunction. Clinically, many patients experience improvement in headaches, bulbar symptoms and sleep‑disordered breathing after successful decompression. However, benefits are variable: long‑standing or severe brainstem injury may not fully reverse, central hypoventilation can persist despite radiologic decompression, and symptom improvement may take weeks to months. Surgical approaches differ (suboccipital craniectomy with C1 laminectomy, with or without duraplasty; selective tonsillar reduction) and carry risks—CSF leak, wound infection, pseudomeningocele, new or worsened neurological deficits and, rarely, the need for stabilization if occipitocervical instability is present. These trade‑offs must be discussed with the patient; duraplasty tends to give better decompression and syrinx resolution but at a higher complication rate compared with bone‑only decompression.

While a definitive surgical plan is prepared, conservative and supportive measures focus on symptom control and risk mitigation. Headache management may include neuropathic agents (for occipital neuralgia), targeted occipital nerve blocks when appropriate, judicious use of non‑opioid analgesics, and simple behavioural measures (avoiding hair tension in this patient’s case). Activity modification to reduce repeated Valsalva (avoiding heavy lifting or straining) is reasonable. Importantly, medications and substances that depress respiratory drive (opioids, benzodiazepines, excessive alcohol, illicit sedatives) should be avoided because they can worsen central hypoventilation.

A respiratory safety plan is essential. Objective overnight monitoring (home oximetry as an interim measure and formal overnight polysomnography with EEG and transcutaneous/end‑tidal CO₂ for definitive assessment) should be arranged urgently. Clear escalation thresholds should be communicated: recurrent or sustained oxyhaemoglobin saturations <88% on room air, single severe desaturations to very low levels (for example <80%), new daytime somnolence, morning headaches, syncope or evidence of rising PaCO₂ or daytime hypercapnia warrant prompt evaluation and likely initiation of ventilatory support or urgent intervention. For ventilatory support, the choice depends on the physiology: CPAP is appropriate when an obstructive component is present; for central hypoventilation or predominant central apnoeas, bilevel positive airway pressure with a timed backup rate (S/T or ST modes) or other modes that guarantee minute ventilation are preferred because they support ventilation and correct hypoventilation. Adaptive servo‑ventilation (ASV) can be useful in selected complex central sleep apnoea patterns but must be used cautiously (it is contraindicated in certain heart‑failure populations) and only after specialist review. Titration should include CO₂ monitoring because supplemental oxygen by itself can correct hypoxaemia while worsening hypercapnia and masking the underlying ventilatory failure—therefore oxygen should not be used as sole therapy for central hypoventilation.

Dysphagia management is another immediate priority to reduce aspiration risk. Early involvement of speech–language pathology and instrumental assessment (videofluoroscopic swallow study or FEES) are required to characterise physiology and to teach compensatory strategies (chin‑tuck, head‑turn, pacing, effortful swallow) and to recommend appropriate bolus modifications. Patients with documented aspiration, recurrent pneumonias, marked weight loss or inability to maintain oral intake should be considered for enteral feeding (temporary nasogastric tube or longer‑term gastrostomy) while definitive management is arranged. In many CMI patients dysphagia improves after decompression, but perioperative aspiration risk and anaesthetic airway issues necessitate careful coordination.

Preoperative optimization should aim to define anatomy and physiology and to minimise perioperative risk. Cine MRI to document CSF flow across the foramen magnum helps surgical planning; a diagnostic, attended (type I) polysomnogram with continuous CO₂ monitoring is recommended by current guidance for patients with suspected sleep‑disordered breathing related to CMI. Pulmonary function testing including vital capacity and maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures, arterial blood gas (or baseline transcutaneous CO₂), nutritional assessment and correction of active infections are useful. Anaesthetic teams should be alerted to bulbar dysfunction and aspiration risk so airway plans (rapid‑sequence intubation, avoidance of excessive sedation, readiness for postoperative ventilatory support) and postoperative ICU or high‑dependency monitoring can be arranged. Extubation should only proceed when airway protective reflexes and adequate spontaneous ventilation are documented.

Shared decision‑making is central because the balance of potential benefit, surgical risk and uncertainty about the degree of reversibility varies between patients. Discussions should review the objective data (MRI appearance with tonsillar descent and CSF flow, PSG results including central apnoea index and CO₂ trends, frequency and severity of desaturation events, dysphagia severity and nutritional status), likely outcomes and possible complications, and the patient's values and risk tolerance. Guidelines endorse obtaining formal polysomnography in CMI patients with suspected sleep‑disordered breathing; those data often materially influence the recommendation for surgery and provide baseline measures to judge postoperative improvement. If a patient elects conservative management—as in this report—a formal monitoring plan must be specified (scheduled clinical reviews, serial overnight oximetry or repeat PSG if symptoms progress, prompt reassessment for any life‑threatening desaturation or new bulbar decline), and thresholds for escalation to surgery should be predefined.

In summary, the immediate management emphasis is respiratory and aspiration safety using objective monitoring and, where appropriate, noninvasive ventilatory support rather than supplemental oxygen alone. Definitive therapy—posterior fossa decompression—should be offered when there is symptomatic brainstem or lower cranial nerve dysfunction supported by imaging and physiological testing, recognizing that improvement in central hypoventilation is common but not guaranteed. Multidisciplinary evaluation, careful preoperative optimization (including cine MRI and full PSG with CO₂ monitoring) and shared decision‑making with clear safety nets are the cornerstones of care for patients like the one described.
