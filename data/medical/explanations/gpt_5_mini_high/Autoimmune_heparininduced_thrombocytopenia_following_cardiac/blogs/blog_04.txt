Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# IVIG for Autoimmune HIT: Dosing, Mechanism, and What to Do When It Fizzles

## The hook — why this case matters
Autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) is a beast. In the case above an elderly man who received intra‑arterial UFH at cardiac catheterisation and massive UFH on cardiopulmonary bypass developed a precipitous postoperative platelet fall, an acute left parietal stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA (OD 2.2). The functional assay showed a classic aHIT signature — very high serotonin release even without heparin (96% at 0 U/mL) — but a laboratory wrinkle: platelet activation was only partially inhibited by the FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal IV.3 (76% residual release). Clinically, he was treated with argatroban and high‑dose IVIG (100 g on two consecutive days, then a third 100 g dose) and later switched to fondaparinux plus AT concentrate. Platelets eventually recovered, but only slowly (155×10^9/L on POD62). This is a useful real‑world lesson: IVIG often produces a brisk platelet rise in aHIT, but sometimes it “fizzles.” Here’s how IVIG is supposed to work, when to use it, why it may fail, and what to do next.

## Quick primer — what auto‑immune HIT is and how IVIG helps
HIT is caused by IgG antibodies that bind PF4/heparin complexes and activate platelets via FcγRIIa. In aHIT, the antibodies can activate platelets independent of heparin (heparin‑independent, “super‑activating” antibodies) and tend to cause more severe thrombocytopenia and thrombosis.

IVIG’s principal actions in HIT relevant to acute management are:
- Saturation/functional blockade of platelet and monocyte Fcγ receptors (especially FcγRIIa), preventing immune‑complex‑driven platelet activation.
- Anti‑idiotype interactions and modulation of FcRn, increasing catabolism of pathogenic IgG.
- Broader immunomodulatory effects (complement modulation, upregulation of inhibitory Fc receptors).

Those mechanisms explain why IVIG often yields a rapid platelet rise in aHIT — but they also point to potential failure modes (see below).

## When to consider IVIG in HIT/aHIT
IVIG is not first‑line for every HIT patient. It is indicated or reasonable to consider when:
- You have confirmed or strongly suspected aHIT (heparin‑independent platelet activation on functional assay, very high ELISA OD, or early severe thrombocytopenia with recent heparin exposure).
- There is life‑ or limb‑threatening thrombosis or ongoing consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, schistocytes).
- Platelets fail to recover after 24–72 hours of appropriate non‑heparin anticoagulation.
- You need to blunt platelet activation before platelet transfusion or an urgent invasive procedure (rare).

In the case above the combination of stroke, very low nadir platelets (13×10^9/L), very high ELISA OD and heparin‑independent SRA made a strong case for early IVIG.

## Dosing and practical logistics
The commonly used, evidence‑based regimens are weight‑based:
- 1 g/kg IV daily for 2 days (total 2 g/kg) — the most commonly used “quick” regimen for severe autoimmune phenomena.
- Alternative: 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days (same total dose spread out).

Practical points:
- Dose by body weight (ideal body weight or adjusted weight in obesity per local practice) and be mindful of a total dose ~2 g/kg unless you have a specific reason to deviate.
- Infuse slowly and monitor — premedicate with acetaminophen/antihistamine if needed.
- Prefer non‑sucrose formulations in patients at risk for acute tubular injury; check baseline renal function.
- In the reported case, the team used absolute 100 g doses (two consecutive days, then a third 100 g) — an aggressive, high absolute dosing strategy appropriate to the clinical severity, but one that increases vigilance for toxicity.

## What response to expect — and when to worry
In many published reports and case series, IVIG produces a platelet count rise within 24–48 hours in aHIT. If you do not see a meaningful platelet rise by 48–72 hours after the first IVIG course, re‑evaluate: is the diagnosis correct, is anticoagulation adequate, are antibodies still abundant or unusually potent?

Be aware that IVIG can also interfere with functional platelet assays (by blocking Fc receptors on reagent platelets), sometimes producing false‑negative functional tests shortly after infusion. In this case the SRA remained strongly positive for months despite IVIG, demonstrating persistent pathogenic antibodies.

## Why IVIG can “fizzle” — mechanisms to keep in mind
Several mechanistic reasons explain incomplete or delayed responses:
- Massive antibody burden and high‑affinity antibodies (high ELISA OD like 2.2) can overwhelm FcγR blockade — there’s simply too much pathogenic IgG for the IVIG to neutralize or outcompete.
- Antibodies that are unusually “super‑activating” may partially bypass FcγR blockade or fix complement or engage other platelet activation pathways, making FcγR saturation insufficient. The partial inhibition by IV.3 in the lab supports this idea.
- Ongoing production of pathogenic IgG from active plasmablasts can outpace the temporary neutralizing effect of IVIG.
- Suboptimal IVIG dosing or product selection (e.g., small total dose or product with higher adverse‑event profile) may blunt effect.
- Poor control of hypercoagulability with anticoagulation can perpetuate consumptive platelet loss despite IVIG.

## Practical next steps when IVIG doesn’t deliver
If you’ve given IVIG and the platelets aren’t coming up (or you have ongoing thrombosis/coagulopathy), act promptly and collaboratively with hematology and transfusion medicine.

First, reconfirm the diagnosis with PF4 ELISA and a reference functional assay (inform the lab that aHIT is suspected so they can choose the right tests). Ensure every heparin exposure is stopped (UFH, LMWH, heparin flushes).

Second, optimize non‑heparin anticoagulation. Review anticoagulant choice, dosing, and effect. If you’re on argatroban and there are biochemical signs of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, schistocytes), consider switching agents — options include bivalirudin or fondaparinux (fondaparinux is off‑label but widely used; remember it needs antithrombin activity; check AT level and replace if low as was done in the case with 1,000 U AT concentrate). Keep therapeutic anticoagulation even with very low platelets unless active bleeding mandates otherwise — HIT is a prothrombotic condition.

Third, escalate immunomodulation if necessary. Options include repeating or extending IVIG (additional 1 g/kg doses), or moving to plasma exchange (PEX) for rapid removal of pathogenic antibodies in life‑ or organ‑threatening, IVIG‑refractory cases. PEX frequently gives a fast fall in circulating antibody and can be lifesaving. Rituximab can be considered for refractory cases to blunt antibody production but has a delayed onset.

Fourth, use adjunctive monitoring: daily platelets, PTT/argatroban levels if on direct thrombin inhibitors, fibrinogen, D‑dimer and peripheral blood film for schistocytes. These trends often provide the earliest signal that hypercoagulability is being controlled (or not).

Finally, avoid routine platelet transfusions unless there is life‑threatening bleeding or an urgent procedure — transfused platelets can be consumed and fuel thrombosis unless platelet activation is blocked and anticoagulation is established.

## Safety — IVIG is not benign
High‑dose IVIG has recognized risks that are especially important in HIT:
- Thrombotic events (arterial and venous) — IVIG increases plasma viscosity and has been associated with thrombosis; that risk is additive to the already hypercoagulable HIT patient.
- Renal injury — especially with sucrose‑stabilized products; check renal function and avoid in severe CKD when possible.
- Hemolysis — due to isohemagglutinins in IVIG (watch the hemoglobin).
- Infusion reactions, aseptic meningitis, and rare anaphylaxis (risk in IgA‑deficient patients).
Measures to reduce risk: slow infusion, pre‑hydration, choose non‑sucrose products, monitor creatinine and hemoglobin, and ensure appropriate anticoagulation is in place when feasible.

## Case‑level lessons
This case reinforces several practical points. First, HIT can present early after cardiac surgery if there was recent preoperative heparin exposure (the diagnostic window is not fixed). Second, very high ELISA OD and heparin‑independent SRA activity predict a severe course. Third, IVIG is a powerful tool but not guaranteed — partial resistance to FcγR blockade can occur, and IVIG may need repeating or escalation to PEX. Fourth, anticoagulation choice matters: despite argatroban therapy the patient showed biochemical evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy and benefit was ultimately seen after switching to fondaparinux plus AT replacement. And finally, functional assays can remain positive for months, so treat the patient (not the lab) and monitor trends.

## Key take‑home messages
- Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) is a severe, often heparin‑independent form of HIT; very high ELISA OD and SRA activity without heparin suggest aHIT.  
- IVIG works by saturating Fcγ receptors, neutralizing pathogenic IgG and accelerating IgG catabolism; it often (but not always) produces a rapid platelet rise in aHIT.  
- Typical IVIG dosing: 1 g/kg IV daily × 2 days (or 0.4 g/kg/day × 5 days); dose by weight and monitor for toxicity.  
- Expect a platelet response within 24–72 hours; lack of response should prompt re‑evaluation of diagnosis, anticoagulation efficacy, and consideration of repeat IVIG or plasma exchange.  
- If IVIG “fizzles,” optimize non‑heparin anticoagulation (consider switching agents), consider additional IVIG or plasma exchange for antibody removal, and involve hematology early.  
- Watch for IVIG‑related complications (thrombosis, renal injury, hemolysis) and maintain appropriate anticoagulation while treating the immune process.

If you see a patient like this, get hematology on board urgently, send functional assays to a reference lab and treat aggressively — aHIT can be reversed, but it sometimes requires stepping up immunomodulation beyond a single IVIG course.
