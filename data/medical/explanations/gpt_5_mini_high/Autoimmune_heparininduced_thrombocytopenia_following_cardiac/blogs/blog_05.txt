Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# HIT-Associated Consumptive Coagulopathy: Falling Fibrinogen, Rising D‑Dimer, and the Schistocyte Trap

## Why this case grabbed my attention
A patient after routine cardiac surgery developed dramatic, early-onset thrombocytopenia, an ischemic stroke, and laboratory features that looked more like consumptive coagulopathy than classic heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). PF4 ELISA was strongly positive, but the functional platelet assay behaved oddly — massive platelet activation even without heparin and only partial inhibition by an FcγRIIA‑blocking antibody. In short: autoimmune HIT (aHIT) with a fulminant consumptive phenotype that can masquerade as DIC or TTP — and that misdiagnosis changes everything you do at the bedside.

## Quick case recap (the facts to remember)
An elderly man had transradial cath 1 week before cardiac surgery (2000 U UFH during the cath). Cardiac surgery used cardiopulmonary bypass with 45,000 U UFH. Postop he was put on LMWH prophylaxis (dalteparin 5,000 → 7,500 U). Platelets fell from 210 preop to 155 on POD1, then progressively to 50×10^9/L on POD5 and 24×10^9/L on POD6. CT on POD6 showed a left parietal ischemic stroke. PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive (OD 2.2). The serotonin‑release assay (SRA) showed striking heparin‑independent platelet activation (96% release at 0 U/mL; 97–99% at 0.1–0.3 U/mL; 0% at 100 U/mL) and only partial inhibition with the Fc blocking antibody IV.3 — an atypical aHIT profile. The patient was treated with argatroban (0.75 mcg/kg/min, PTT target 55–85 s), high‑dose IVIG (100 g × 3), then switched to fondaparinux 10 mg daily with AT concentrate 1,000 U because fibrinogen fell (6.59 g/L on POD5 → nadir 2.49 g/L by POD17), d‑dimer rose (peak ≈ POD12), and transient schistocytes appeared (POD9–16). Platelets recovered slowly (155×10^9/L at POD62); long‑term anticoagulation was transitioned back to apixaban.

## What “consumptive coagulopathy” looks like in fulminant aHIT
HIT is primarily a prothrombotic platelet‑activation syndrome driven by anti–PF4 antibodies. In autoimmune HIT the antibodies can activate platelets without added heparin — producing extreme platelet consumption and widespread thrombosis. When platelet activation is intense and widespread, the coagulation system is driven hard: fibrinogen is consumed (fibrinogen falls), fibrin clot turnover increases (d‑dimer rises), and red cells may be sheared in the microvasculature producing schistocytes. That constellation — falling fibrinogen + rising d‑dimer + transient schistocytes — is a consumptive picture that can be mistaken for DIC or TTP unless you connect it to the HIT context.

## How to separate HIT (including aHIT) from DIC and TTP at the bedside
Timing, clinical context, and a few focused labs get you most of the way.

- HIT/aHIT
  - Typical timing: platelet fall days 5–10 after heparin exposure — but can be earlier if there was recent preoperative or perioperative heparin (as in this case).
  - Prominent thrombosis risk; bleeding is uncommon unless platelets are extremely low.
  - Labs: PF4 ELISA may be strongly positive (OD >2 is highly suspicious). SRA confirms platelet‑activating antibodies; aHIT may show heparin‑independent activation.
  - Coagulation tests (PT/INR, PTT) are often normal early; fibrinogen may fall and d‑dimer rise if consumptive coagulation is extensive.
- DIC
  - Usually driven by sepsis, massive tissue injury, obstetric catastrophe, or malignancy.
  - Global coagulopathy: prolonged PT/PTT, low fibrinogen, thrombocytopenia, high d‑dimer, bleeding common.
  - Look for an obvious trigger and global coagulation abnormalities.
- TTP
  - Thrombotic microangiopathy with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and severe thrombocytopenia.
  - Schistocytes and lab evidence of hemolysis are prominent — low haptoglobin, elevated indirect bilirubin, reticulocytosis.
  - ADAMTS13 activity is usually severely reduced (<10%).
  - Treatment is plasma exchange and immunosuppression — not anticoagulation.

In this patient the early coagulation screen was normal (INR 1.1, PTT 34 s) and there were no early hemolysis signals (haptoglobin was high, reticulocyte count normal). That made TTP less likely initially. As fibrinogen fell and schistocytes appeared later, the picture looked consumptive — but the temporal relationship to recent heparin exposure and the strongly positive PF4 ELISA pointed to aHIT rather than primary DIC or TTP.

## The schistocyte trap — don’t let one lab hijack your diagnosis
Schistocytes can pop up in several conditions: TTP, DIC, severe hypertension, mechanical hemolysis, and — importantly — frank microvascular thrombosis from HIT. A few isolated schistocytes on a peripheral smear do not equal TTP. Always pair red cell fragmentation with hemolysis labs (haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, retic count) and consider ADAMTS13 if TTP is in the differential. In this case schistocytes were a transient later feature of consumptive coagulopathy driven by aHIT rather than primary ADAMTS13 deficiency.

## Using serial fibrinogen and D‑dimer to track treatment response
Platelet count is important but often lags. In fulminant aHIT the coagulation markers can move earlier and can be used as dynamic biomarkers of ongoing thrombosis and response to therapy.

- Falling fibrinogen suggests ongoing consumption of fibrin(ogen).
- Rising d‑dimer suggests increased fibrin formation and breakdown (ongoing thrombosis/fibrinolysis).
- In this case fibrinogen fell from 6.59 g/L (POD5) to 2.49 g/L (POD17) while d‑dimer peaked around POD12 — all signs that initial control of hypercoagulability was incomplete.
- After escalation of therapy (IVIG and change in anticoagulant strategy) trends reversed, and platelet recovery followed.

Bottom line: watch trends, not single values. Rising fibrinogen + falling d‑dimer together are reassuring signals that the prothrombotic storm is settling.

## Practical management pearls from this case
Stop all heparin immediately (including flushes). Treat suspected/definite HIT as thrombotic disease — anticoagulate with a non‑heparin agent even when platelets are very low, unless there is life‑threatening bleeding.

- Start a non‑heparin anticoagulant quickly. In this case the team used argatroban (0.75 mcg/kg/min, PTT target 55–85 s) because of the recent ischemic stroke and the desire for short half‑life. When anticoagulation appeared insufficient (falling fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer, schistocytes) they switched to fondaparinux 10 mg daily and gave AT concentrate 1,000 U because fondaparinux requires antithrombin and the AT level was mildly low (0.70 U/mL).
- Autoimmune HIT may benefit from high‑dose IVIG to block FcγRIIA‑mediated platelet activation; this patient received 100 g on two consecutive days followed by a third 100 g dose. Responses can be dramatic — but not always; this patient had only a modest platelet rise and required further adjustments.
- Platelet transfusions generally make HIT worse because they provide activated substrate for antibody complexes. Reserve platelet transfusion for life‑threatening bleeding or when platelets must be corrected for an urgent invasive procedure.
- Don’t wait for a “perfect” functional assay. A strongly positive PF4 ELISA plus a compatible clinical picture should trigger therapy; functional assays (SRA) can be indeterminate or show aHIT patterns (heparin‑independent activation or unusual Fc blockade behavior), as happened here.
- Autoimmune HIT can present earlier than day 5 after surgery if there was recent heparin exposure (the patient had heparin at cath one week earlier). Keep a careful heparin exposure history.
- Plan for follow‑up: HIT serologies (including SRA) can remain positive for months even after clinical recovery; platelet recovery can be slow in severe aHIT (this patient normalized platelet count only by POD62), and long‑term anticoagulation can be transitioned to a DOAC (apixaban in this case) once platelets recover.

## Bottom line — what I want you to remember
- Fulminant autoimmune HIT can produce a consumptive coagulopathy: falling fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer, and transient schistocytes — and it can look like DIC or TTP.
- Always consider recent heparin exposure (procedural or perioperative) when thrombocytopenia appears early after surgery.
- A strongly positive PF4 ELISA with a compatible presentation = treat for HIT while you investigate further; SRA can be atypical in aHIT.
- Trend fibrinogen and d‑dimer (and smear) — they often signal persistent thrombosis before the platelet count recovers.
- Avoid platelet transfusions unless life‑threatening bleeding is present.
- Use non‑heparin anticoagulation promptly; consider IVIG for aHIT; tailor anticoagulant choices to the clinical scenario (bleeding risk, renal function, logistics).

If you see a post‑op patient with inexorable platelet decline plus rising d‑dimer and falling fibrinogen, don’t reflex to plasma exchange for “TTP” or to transfuse platelets — pause, check heparin exposures, send PF4 testing, and treat for HIT while you sort it out.
