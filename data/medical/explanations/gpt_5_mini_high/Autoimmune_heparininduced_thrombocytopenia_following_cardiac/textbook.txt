Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Presentation and early postoperative assessment

This patient’s early course is best understood by lining up the key heparin exposures and the expected pattern of platelet changes after cardiac surgery, then asking whether the observed trajectory fits the usual post‑bypass physiology or instead points to an immune-mediated process such as HIT/aHIT. One week before surgery he received 2000 U of unfractionated heparin (UFH) for a transradial diagnostic catheterization. During cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) he received a large intraoperative UFH dose (45 000 U). Routine postoperative pharmacologic prophylaxis with low‑molecular‑weight heparin (dalteparin) was started on postoperative day (POD) 1 (5000 U), increased to 7500 U on POD2–4, and withheld on POD5 when the platelet count had fallen.

After CPB a predictable, transient thrombocytopenia is common. Mechanisms include hemodilution, platelet activation/consumption on artificial surfaces, sequestration and perioperative drugs; a typical pattern is a 30–50% platelet fall with a nadir around POD2–3 and recovery by about POD4–5. HIT classically produces either a delayed fall beginning on POD5–10 or a “second” drop after an initial postoperative recovery. Distinguishing these patterns is central to early recognition after cardiac surgery, where the routine early drop can obscure an evolving HIT.

In this case the preoperative platelet count was 210 × 10^9/L, and the count fell modestly to 155 × 10^9/L on POD1 (a small early decline that can be attributed to CPB). Instead of recovering, however, the platelet count continued to fall progressively to 50 × 10^9/L by POD5 and to the mid‑20s on POD6. This progressive decline after the immediate post‑bypass period—representing a >50% fall from baseline—is a red flag. The clinical picture was compounded by neurologic deterioration: worsening postoperative delirium prompted brain imaging that demonstrated an acute left parietal infarct. A peripheral blood film on POD7 confirmed true thrombocytopenia (no platelet clumps) and, early on, no schistocytes, making artifact or microangiopathy less likely. Taken together, a progressive platelet fall after the expected CPB nadir plus a new arterial thrombosis is strongly suggestive of HIT rather than routine post‑operative platelet kinetics.

Applying the 4Ts framework in the post‑cardiac surgery setting requires adapting for the baseline CPB effect. The “Thrombocytopenia” element is judged by the relative fall: here the fall exceeded 50% from baseline, which is a high‑risk feature. “Timing” is ordinarily counted as typical when onset is POD5–10, but that rule must be modified when there has been recent heparin exposure: a prior heparin exposure within 30 days can produce an anamnestic (rapid) antibody response and allow HIT to present earlier than the usual window. This patient’s transradial cath with UFH one week before surgery made early onset biologically plausible. The “Thrombosis” element is clearly met by the CT‑confirmed cerebral infarct. Finally, alternative explanations were few: hemodynamics were stable, there were no transfusions to suggest post‑transfusion purpura, cultures were negative and laboratory testing did not support hemolysis or thrombotic microangiopathy. Putting these elements together yields a high pretest probability for HIT/aHIT and appropriately prompted urgent serologic and functional testing.

The clinical teaching point is practical: after CPB one must separate the expected early nadir and recovery from an evolving, progressive or second platelet fall. Early postoperative thrombocytopenia that continues to worsen beyond POD3–4, produces a >50% relative fall, or is temporally associated with new thrombotic events — especially when there has been recent heparin exposure — should prompt immediate consideration of HIT and expedited laboratory evaluation rather than attribution to routine post‑bypass physiology.

## Mechanisms: classic HIT versus autoimmune HIT (aHIT)

Understanding this case requires a focused review of how antibodies against platelet factor 4 (PF4) trigger thrombocytopenia and thrombosis, and how an “autoimmune” variant (aHIT) differs from the more familiar, heparin‑dependent form.

PF4 biology and antigen formation
PF4 (also called CXCL4) is a highly cationic protein stored in platelet α‑granules and released on platelet activation. In solution PF4 forms tetramers whose multiple positive charges bind anionic polyanions — exogenous heparin (especially long-chain unfractionated heparin) or endogenous glycosaminoglycans (eg, heparan, dermatan and chondroitin sulfates) on platelet and endothelial surfaces. When PF4 binds a polyanion it assembles into larger multimolecular lattices and undergoes conformational changes that expose neoepitopes. Those neoepitopes can become the target of IgG antibodies; the geometry, size and stability of PF4–polyanion complexes determine immunogenicity and the potential to form pathogenic immune complexes.

Classic (heparin‑dependent) HIT pathway
In classic HIT, patient IgG recognizes PF4 when it is complexed to heparin (or similar polyanions). The Fc portion of these IgG antibodies then engages platelet FcγRIIa (CD32a). FcγRIIa is a low‑affinity receptor that must be cross‑linked by multivalent immune complexes to generate a signal: clustering of FcγRIIa on the platelet surface triggers platelet activation (dense‑granule release including serotonin, expression of P‑selectin, phosphatidylserine exposure and formation of procoagulant platelet microparticles). Parallel activation of monocytes through their Fc receptors induces tissue‑factor expression, and endothelial and neutrophil activation (including NETosis) further amplify thrombin generation. The net effect is paradoxical thrombosis despite thrombocytopenia. These mechanisms underlie the canonical laboratory SRA pattern: little/no serotonin release with patient serum alone (0 U heparin), strong release at low therapeutic heparin concentrations (eg, 0.1–0.3 U/mL) when PF4–heparin complexes optimally form, and suppression of activation at very high heparin concentrations (which disrupt complex formation).

What defines autoimmune HIT (aHIT)
Autoimmune HIT denotes a subgroup of anti‑PF4 antibodies that can activate platelets without added heparin — either because they recognize PF4 alone (or PF4 bound to endogenous glycosaminoglycans) or because they have evolved higher affinity/avidity and form stable immune complexes on cell surfaces. Clinically this manifests in several atypical phenotypes: “delayed‑onset” HIT after heparin has been stopped, refractory or protracted HIT that is difficult to control, and “spontaneous” HIT that appears without any known heparin exposure. In vitro, aHIT sera typically produce strong platelet activation in the SRA even at 0 U/mL heparin; ELISA optical densities are often very high, reflecting abundant anti‑PF4 antibodies. In the present case the serum caused 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL, a laboratory signature of heparin‑independent platelet activation and therefore of aHIT.

Why aHIT often produces severe thrombosis and consumptive coagulopathy
Because aHIT antibodies form activating immune complexes on platelets, monocytes and endothelium independent of exogenous heparin, immune activation can continue unabated after stopping heparin. Continuous platelet and monocyte activation generates sustained thrombin production and widespread micro‑ and macrovascular thrombosis. Clinically and in the laboratory this ongoing procoagulant state produces falling fibrinogen (consumption), rising D‑dimer (fibrin turnover), appearance of schistocytes (mechanical fragmentation from microthrombi) and sometimes profound thrombocytopenia from massive platelet activation and clearance. Those features — falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer and schistocytes observed in this patient while anticoagulated — are characteristic of the severe consumptive coagulopathy that can accompany aHIT.

Partial resistance to FcγRIIa blockade (IV.3): mechanistic interpretation and implications
In research and diagnostic SRAs, the monoclonal antibody IV.3 is used to block FcγRIIa and thereby confirm that platelet activation is FcγRIIa‑mediated. Typical HIT sera are strongly inhibited by IV.3. Partial resistance to IV.3 blockade, as seen here (serotonin release decreased but remained markedly positive rather than falling <20%), indicates unusually potent immune complexes. Possible explanations include very high‑affinity/avidity anti‑PF4 IgG that can still cluster residual unblocked FcγRIIa, antibody complexes stabilized on PF4 bound to the platelet surface that are sterically less susceptible to receptor blockade, or amplification by other pathways (complement deposition on immune complexes, monocyte‑driven thrombin that secondarily activates platelets, or recruitment of neutrophils/NETs). Genetic variation in Fcγ receptors can also modulate binding and blockade. The practical consequence is twofold: (1) an indeterminate or partially inhibited in‑vitro result does not exclude HIT and, when combined with a heparin‑independent SRA and a strong ELISA, supports an aHIT diagnosis; and (2) therapies that act by blocking Fc receptor–mediated activation (notably IVIG, which competes for Fc receptors) may be less effective or slower to produce platelet recovery when immune complexes are exceptionally potent — a pattern mirrored in this patient’s only modest initial response to high‑dose IVIG.

Laboratory and clinical signposts linking mechanism to management
Mechanistically informed interpretation of laboratory data is critical. Very high PF4/polyanion ELISA OD values plus SRA positivity at 0 U/mL should raise concern for aHIT and predict a greater risk of progressive thrombosis and consumptive coagulopathy. Partial IV.3 inhibition identifies an especially aggressive antibody phenotype. In such situations clinicians should expect prolonged immune activity, close monitoring for thrombosis and evidence of consumption (fibrinogen, D‑dimer, schistocytes), and may need prompt institution of non‑heparin anticoagulation and immunomodulation — as was done in this case.

In short, classic HIT depends on PF4–heparin complexes that require added heparin to form optimally and activate platelets via FcγRIIa clustering; aHIT reflects antibodies that bind PF4 complexes formed on endogenous surfaces or that have such high affinity that platelet activation becomes heparin‑independent. Those mechanistic differences explain why aHIT behaves more aggressively in the clinic, why its laboratory signature differs (SRA positivity at 0 U/mL, high ELISA OD, possible partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade), and why the patient described had early onset, severe thrombocytopenia, persistent hypercoagulability and a delayed platelet recovery despite IVIG.

## Diagnostic workup and reasoning

The diagnostic approach combined careful review of routine postoperative laboratory data and peripheral smear, neuroimaging, and both immunologic and functional HIT testing. The central challenge was distinguishing a true immune-mediated, prothrombotic HIT process from the many non‑immune causes of thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, and then interpreting an initially “indeterminate” functional assay in the clinical context.

Baseline laboratory and peripheral smear findings argued early against non-immune explanations. The platelet fall was isolated: by POD5 the platelet count was 50 × 10^9/L while haemoglobin (97 g/L) and white blood cell count (7.8 × 10^9/L) were stable, and the film (POD7) showed no platelet clumps (ruling out pseudothrombocytopenia) and no early schistocytosis. The pre‑ and early‑postoperative haptoglobin was elevated (an acute‑phase pattern), reticulocyte count was not suggestive of hemolysis, and renal and liver chemistries were normal. Fibrinogen was elevated immediately postoperatively (6.59 g/L), which is typical as an acute phase response to surgery, but over the subsequent week fibrinogen fell (nadir 2.49 g/L) while d‑dimer rose and schistocytes eventually appeared; that temporal pattern—initial postoperative acute‑phase elevation followed by later consumption—favoured a consumptive coagulopathy driven by intravascular thrombosis rather than primary haemorrhage or isolated platelet destruction.

Neuroimaging provided an important clinical correlate. CT demonstrated a left parietal cortical infarct (3.2 × 1.8 cm) identified after worsening delirium. HIT is a well‑recognized, highly prothrombotic disorder that can cause arterial as well as venous thromboses; finding an acute cortical arterial infarct in the setting of falling platelets increased the pretest probability that thrombocytopenia was immune and prothrombotic rather than simply a postoperative or septic phenomenon.

Immunoassay and functional testing were then integrated to confirm the diagnosis. The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive (optical density 2.2; cutoff <0.4). Although ELISAs are sensitive and can give false positives after cardiac surgery, OD magnitude matters: very high ODs correlate with the presence of clinically relevant, platelet‑activating antibodies, particularly when clinical pretest probability is high (progressive platelet fall and new thrombosis). Thus the ELISA result substantially increased the likelihood of true HIT in this patient.

The serotonin‑release assay (SRA), a gold‑standard functional test, provided the decisive pattern. In classic heparin‑dependent HIT the SRA shows platelet activation at low heparin concentrations (typically 0.1–0.3 U/mL) and inhibition at very high heparin concentrations (100 U/mL) because excess heparin prevents the PF4‑heparin complexes required for immune complex formation. This patient’s serum produced very strong serotonin release at 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL (97% and 99%), with complete loss of release at 100 U/mL—findings compatible with HIT. Crucially, the serum also caused strong serotonin release in the absence of heparin (96% at 0 U/mL). Heparin‑independent platelet activation is the characteristic hallmark of autoimmune HIT (aHIT): antibodies that can activate platelets without added heparin, by recognizing PF4 on platelet surfaces or PF4 complexes that form without exogenous heparin.

The only atypical laboratory feature was incomplete inhibition of activation by the FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3. In standard practice addition of IV.3 to the SRA should abolish serotonin release from HIT immune complexes because platelet activation is mediated by FcγRIIa cross‑linking; when IV.3 reduced release only partially (to 76% rather than to <20%), the reporting laboratory initially labelled the SRA “indeterminate.” In this case, direct review and discussion with the reference laboratory—and consideration of the full dataset (very high ELISA OD, strong heparin‑independent activation, inhibition at 100 U/mL, evolving consumptive changes and demonstrable thrombosis)—supported a different interpretation: the patient had a form of aHIT with unusually potent platelet‑activating antibodies that were only partially inhibited by IV.3 in vitro. In other words, the SRA pattern was not reflective of a non‑HIT mechanism but of an unusually severe serological phenotype of aHIT; communication with the reference lab permitted reclassification of the result in the appropriate clinical context.

Systematic exclusion of alternative diagnoses reinforced this conclusion. Common post‑cardiac surgery causes were improbable: CPB‑related platelet consumption typically produces an early nadir (day 1–2) with prompt recovery, not a progressive fall into the first postoperative week; there was no haemodynamic instability to suggest cardiogenic shock; cultures were negative and the white count was normal, making sepsis/DIC unlikely as the primary cause; there had been no transfusion to raise concern for post‑transfusion purpura; the pattern and timing of drug exposures did not support drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia; and early laboratory markers of thrombotic microangiopathy (significant schistocytosis, falling haptoglobin, marked haemolysis) were absent initially, arguing against TTP as the primary process.

Bringing all elements together yielded the diagnostic synthesis. The patient had a recent preoperative heparin exposure (2000 U at cardiac catheterisation one week before surgery), explaining the unusually early postoperative onset of antibody‑mediated platelet destruction. He developed progressive, isolated thrombocytopenia accompanied by a new arterial thrombosis, a very high‑titer PF4/polyanion ELISA, and an SRA that showed both the classic low‑heparin/high‑heparin inhibition profile and, importantly, strong heparin‑independent activation—consistent with autoimmune HIT. The atypical partial resistance to IV.3 blockade represented an unusually aggressive serologic phenotype rather than evidence against HIT. This integrated interpretation, combining clinical course, imaging, ELISA magnitude and the detailed SRA pattern, supported the diagnosis of aHIT and guided urgent anticoagulant and immunomodulatory therapy. The case illustrates that functional assays must be interpreted in context and that early, high‑titer PF4 antibodies with heparin‑independent activity are diagnostic of autoimmune HIT even when assay blockade is incomplete.

## Therapeutic strategy and monitoring in severe aHIT

Management of autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) must be prompt, aggressive and guided by both clinical trends and laboratory markers of ongoing coagulation. In this case the team followed the key principles: immediate cessation of all heparins, avoidance of platelet transfusion except for life‑threatening bleeding, rapid initiation of a non‑heparin anticoagulant that could be tightly controlled because of the recent stroke, and addition of adjunctive immunotherapy for the heparin‑independent, highly activating antibodies. Serial platelet counts and markers of consumptive coagulopathy (fibrinogen, D‑dimer, peripheral smear for schistocytes) were used to judge whether therapy was controlling the prothrombotic process, and changes in anticoagulant strategy were made when those metrics suggested continued hypercoagulability.

Immediate steps
All exposures to unfractionated and low‑molecular‑weight heparin must be stopped as soon as HIT (or aHIT) is suspected; in this patient that meant stopping dalteparin and the prophylactic fondaparinux that had been started on POD5. Platelet transfusions are generally avoided because transfused platelets can be activated by circulating HIT antibodies and may worsen thrombosis; transfusion is reserved for life‑threatening hemorrhage or imminent invasive procedures where hemostasis cannot be otherwise achieved. While heparin is discontinued, a non‑heparin anticoagulant should be started without delay to prevent further thrombosis.

Initial non‑heparin anticoagulant selection and monitoring
In this case argatroban, an intravenous direct thrombin inhibitor with predominantly hepatic clearance and a short half‑life, was chosen first. The rationale was practical and patient‑specific: the recent ischemic stroke and very low platelet count raised concerns about hemorrhagic complications, so an agent that could be titrated rapidly and stopped quickly if bleeding developed was preferred. The treating team targeted an aPTT of 55–85 seconds and used an infusion rate of 0.75 μg/kg/min. (Dose and targets are individualized; in general, monitoring of argatroban is by aPTT aiming for roughly 1.5–3× baseline, and dose reductions are required in hepatic impairment because the drug is hepatically cleared.) Bivalirudin is another parenteral direct thrombin inhibitor that may be used in some settings; DOACs are increasingly employed later in the course but are typically avoided as initial agents in the acute inpatient setting with severe thrombocytopenia and recent stroke because they are less titratable and have less immediate reversibility.

Role of high‑dose IVIG in aHIT
Autoimmune HIT is characterized by antibodies that can activate platelets even without heparin. High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is recommended for severe or refractory aHIT because IVIG can rapidly inhibit FcγRIIa‑mediated platelet activation — one principal mechanism is competitive blockade and functional saturation of Fc receptors, preventing pathogenic IgG from cross‑linking receptors on platelets. Common regimens are 1 g/kg/day for 2 days (or 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days); in this case the team administered 100 g on two consecutive days and added a third 100 g dose when the platelet rise was insufficient. In many patients with aHIT a brisk platelet response is seen within 24–72 hours after IVIG; here the platelet count rose only modestly (from 13×10^9/L to 54×10^9/L over about a week), and that attenuated response was interpreted as a marker of particularly aggressive, persistent antibody activity. Acknowledging that IVIG itself carries prothrombotic risk in some patients, its use in aHIT is a balance of rapidly reducing antibody‑mediated platelet activation vs the small thrombosis risk of IVIG.

Monitoring adequacy of treatment: platelets, fibrinogen, D‑dimer and smear
Serial platelet counts are the primary metric to follow response. In addition, markers of consumptive coagulopathy provide early and actionable signal of ongoing thrombin generation despite anticoagulation. Fibrinogen is an acute‑phase reactant that is typically very high immediately after cardiac surgery; a downward trend in fibrinogen in the postoperative period (as occurred in this patient between POD6–POD17 to a nadir of 2.49 g/L) suggests consumption from ongoing thrombosis. Rising D‑dimer levels and the new appearance of schistocytes on peripheral smear (seen here from about POD9–POD16) similarly indicate active clot formation and red cell fragmentation, and together these changes signaled that argatroban plus IVIG was not fully controlling the hypercoagulable process. Functional assays (serotonin‑release assay) and immunoassays were useful diagnostically, but functional test positivity may lag clinical recovery; therefore therapeutic decisions are driven primarily by clinical trajectory and the dynamic clotting parameters described above. While anticoagulation is ongoing, the aPTT is used to monitor agents such as argatroban; anti‑Xa assays are not routinely used for fondaparinux monitoring.

Rationale for switching to fondaparinux and antithrombin considerations
Because markers suggested ongoing uncontrolled coagulation while on argatroban, and for practical reasons (ease of once‑daily subcutaneous dosing), the team changed to therapeutic‑dose fondaparinux (10 mg once daily) on POD14. Fondaparinux is an indirect factor Xa inhibitor that requires adequate antithrombin (AT) for activity; in this patient a pre‑infusion AT level was mildly low (0.70 U/mL), so 1,000 U of AT concentrate was given prior to starting fondaparinux to ensure its pharmacologic effectiveness. Fondaparinux is renally cleared and should be avoided or dose‑reduced in renal impairment; it is not approved specifically for HIT but is commonly used off‑label because cross‑reactivity with HIT antibodies is uncommon. The switch in this case was driven by laboratory evidence that argatroban was not adequately controlling consumptive coagulopathy and the practical advantages of switching to a stable, once‑daily agent while continuing effective anticoagulation.

Transition to long‑term anticoagulation
The usual criterion to transition from parenteral non‑heparin agents to oral anticoagulation is clinical stabilization and platelet recovery. For HIT with documented thrombosis, anticoagulation is typically continued for at least three months; if HIT occurred without thrombosis a shorter course (about 4 weeks) may be used. In this patient fondaparinux was continued until platelet recovery (fondaparinux stopped on POD65), and apixaban (5 mg twice daily) was resumed for long‑term anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation once the platelet count normalized (155×10^9/L at POD62). Choice of agent and timing should be individualized with hematology input; warfarin, if used, should only be started after platelet recovery and requires overlapping non‑heparin anticoagulation because warfarin initially reduces protein C and can worsen hypercoagulability.

Stroke‑specific balancing of risks
The recent ischemic stroke made the anticoagulant choice and targets more complex. The team preferred argatroban initially because of its short half‑life and ease of rapid cessation if hemorrhagic transformation occurred. The decision to anticoagulate aggressively reflects that aHIT is a highly prothrombotic state and that withholding anticoagulation risks further cerebral or systemic thrombosis. Close neurologic monitoring and prompt imaging if deterioration occurs are essential; platelet transfusions to attempt to reduce hemorrhage risk should be avoided unless there is life‑threatening bleeding or an operative indication, because they can exacerbate HIT‑mediated thrombosis.

Documentation and future precautions
Accurate documentation is critical: the patient should be labeled as having HIT/aHIT and the chart should detail the timing, the serology/functional assay results, and the anticoagulants used. For future procedures that would ordinarily use heparin (including cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass), planning well in advance with hematology is required: options include using alternative intraoperative anticoagulants (for example bivalirudin protocols for CPB) or, in exceptional and carefully controlled circumstances, perioperative plasma exchange and testing to confirm the absence of platelet‑activating antibodies before re‑exposure to heparin. Until functional assays are negative and the patient is deemed safe by hematology, heparin should be avoided.

Takeaway
In severe aHIT the therapeutic priority is rapid interruption of heparin exposure, immediate anticoagulation with a non‑heparin agent chosen to match the clinical context (argatroban in this case because of recent stroke and need for rapid reversibility), early adjunctive high‑dose IVIG for heparin‑independent antibody activity, and tight clinical and laboratory surveillance (platelets, fibrinogen, D‑dimer, smear). Failure to improve clinically or biochemically should prompt reconsideration of the anticoagulant strategy (as occurred here with a switch to fondaparinux after AT correction) and ongoing hematology involvement to guide timing of transition to oral therapy and planning for any future heparin exposures.

## Outcomes, antibody evolution, and clinical lessons

The patient’s clinical course ended favorably: after an initial nadir platelet count of 13×10^9/L and an ischaemic parietal stroke, platelets recovered slowly to 155×10^9/L by postoperative day (POD) 62 and to 202×10^9/L by POD87, and his neurological deficits resolved completely. Anticoagulation was transitioned from argatroban to therapeutic fondaparinux (given until POD65) and finally to oral apixaban (restarted POD65) once platelet recovery permitted outpatient anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation.

Serial serology documented an evolving antibody phenotype. The index serum (POD5) showed a very strong PF4/polyanion ELISA (OD 2.2) and an SRA demonstrating unusually strong, heparin‑independent platelet activation (≈96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; 97–99% at 0.1–0.3 U/mL; inhibition at 100 U/mL). That initial functional profile—strong activation without heparin and only partial inhibition by an FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal antibody—defined autoimmune HIT (aHIT) with unusually potent antibody activity. Over the subsequent months the SRA remained positive but the heparin‑independent component waned: later profiles became more typical of heparin‑dependent HIT, and the last sample tested (POD124) showed minimal activation at 0 U/mL (15%) but 100% release at 0.3 U/mL. Thus, the serologic picture evolved from dominant aHIT activity toward predominantly heparin‑dependent reactivity as the patient recovered clinically.

Several practical diagnostic pearls arise from this case. First, an early postoperative platelet fall (before POD5) after cardiac surgery should prompt review for recent preoperative heparin exposures (for example, catheterization), because such exposures explain early-onset HIT. Second, a very strong PF4/polyanion ELISA (high OD) together with SRA activation in the absence of added heparin is highly suggestive of aHIT. Third, an SRA reported as “indeterminate” because platelet activation is not fully blocked by an FcγRIIa monoclonal antibody requires close liaison with a reference laboratory—partial resistance to Fc blockade can reflect an unusually aggressive aHIT antibody rather than a benign result, and failing to recognise this can delay correct diagnosis and therapy. Finally, falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer and new schistocytes in the setting of HIT are signs of consumptive coagulopathy and ongoing intravascular thrombosis and should heighten concern for inadequate anticoagulant control.

Practical management lessons from the case are these. Stop all heparins promptly when HIT is suspected and start a non‑heparin anticoagulant without delay if the clinical probability is intermediate–high; choice depends on bleeding risk and logistics (argatroban or bivalirudin for short half‑life and intravenous control; fondaparinux is an effective off‑label option but is renally cleared and requires sufficient antithrombin (AT) activity). In this patient argatroban was chosen acutely because of recent stroke, but progressive laboratory evidence of hypercoagulability (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, schistocytes) prompted a change to fondaparinux with AT concentrate because of concerns that argatroban was not controlling the consumptive process. High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is indicated early in aHIT to interrupt Fc‑mediated platelet activation; a blunted platelet response after IVIG—as occurred here—signals a more severe or refractory immunologic phenotype and should trigger close reassessment and readiness to escalate or change anticoagulant strategy. Avoid platelet transfusion unless there is life‑threatening bleeding or an urgent invasive procedure.

A pragmatic, stepwise approach to post‑CPB thrombocytopenia illustrated by this case is: (1) situational assessment of timing (early versus classical POD5–10 window), magnitude of platelet fall and presence of thrombosis; (2) apply the 4Ts in the surgical context while actively excluding common mimics (bleeding/shock, sepsis, transfusion reactions, TTP/DIC); (3) obtain a PF4/polyanion immunoassay as a rapid screen and send a functional assay (SRA) to a reference lab when the immunoassay is positive or clinical suspicion is high; (4) if probability is intermediate–high, stop heparin and start a non‑heparin anticoagulant and involve hematology; (5) if functional testing shows heparin‑independent activation or an unusual profile, add IVIG and monitor fibrinogen, D‑dimer, peripheral smear and platelets closely, escalating therapy if markers worsen.

Finally, implications for future care: document the HIT/aHIT diagnosis prominently in the medical record, warn the patient and care teams about avoiding heparin exposure, and provide a plan for alternative anticoagulants. Because functional and immunologic tests can remain positive for weeks to months even after clinical recovery, avoid routine re‑exposure to heparin until specialist review and, when re‑exposure is unavoidable, consider pre‑procedural antibody testing and hematology consultation to weigh strategies such as use of alternative anticoagulants or antibody‑reducing interventions. This case highlights that early recognition, targeted laboratory interpretation (including reference‑lab consultation), timely non‑heparin anticoagulation, and judicious use of IVIG are key to good outcomes in severe aHIT after cardiac surgery.
