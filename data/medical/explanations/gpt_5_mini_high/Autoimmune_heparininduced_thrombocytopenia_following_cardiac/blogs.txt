Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# Before POD5: The Pre-op Heparin Trap in Post-CPB Thrombocytopenia

Why this case will make you change your knee‑jerk reflex: a small pre‑op heparin dose (2,000 U during a transradial cath a week before surgery) primed a catastrophic autoimmune HIT after on‑pump cardiac surgery. The patient had an early postoperative platelet fall (POD1→POD5), profound thrombocytopenia (nadir 13×10^9/L), a thrombotic stroke, a strongly positive PF4 ELISA (OD 2.2) and an SRA that activated platelets even with no added heparin — classic autoimmune HIT (aHIT) — and (novelly) showed only partial inhibition with FcγRIIa blockade. In short: don’t be reassured by a platelet fall before POD5 after CPB without checking the pre‑op heparin story.

## The case in a paragraph
An elderly man had a transradial cath 1 week before planned valve/graft surgery and received 2,000 U UFH at that time. Surgery on cardiopulmonary bypass used 45,000 U UFH. Platelets fell from 210×10^9/L preop to 155 on POD1 (an expected post‑CPB dip), and LMWH prophylaxis (dalteparin 5,000→7,500 U) was started POD1–POD4. Instead of recovery, platelets declined to 50×10^9/L on POD5 and to 24×10^9/L on POD6 with new delirium; CT found a left parietal infarct. PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive (OD 2.2). The SRA showed striking platelet activation at 0 U/mL (96%), 0.1 U/mL (97%) and 0.3 U/mL (99%) heparin and suppression at 100 U/mL — i.e., heparin‑independent activation consistent with aHIT. The FcγRIIa‑blocking mAb IV.3 produced only partial inhibition (76% remained at 0.1 U/mL), yielding an “indeterminate” SRA result on first report. Heparins were stopped, argatroban started (0.75 μg/kg/min, PTT 55–85 s), high‑dose IVIG was given (100 g ×2, then a third 100 g), later switched to fondaparinux 10 mg daily with prior AT concentrate (AT 0.70 U/mL → 1,000 U AT), and platelets recovered to 155×10^9/L by POD62. Serial SRA remained positive for months before waning.

## What we expect after CPB — and where the trap is
After on‑pump cardiac surgery you expect:
- An immediate platelet drop from hemodilution/consumption/activation with CPB — the nadir is usually around POD1 and then platelet counts recover by POD2–3.
- A second, immune‑mediated drop (HIT) typically shows up POD5–10.

That “first fall” after CPB is so common that clinicians often shrug it off. The trap: if the patient had heparin exposure in the preceding days (even a small cath bolus), the classic timing rules break down — the antibody response is already primed and HIT can present before POD5. In cardiac surgery patients about 10% of HIT cases present before POD5, often because of recent pre‑op heparin.

Pearl: a modest heparin dose a week earlier can be enough to prime pathogenic anti‑PF4 antibodies — don’t assume early timing excludes HIT.

## Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) — the important differences
aHIT is a clinically aggressive subset of HIT where antibodies can activate platelets without heparin (heparin‑independent platelet activation). Clinically this tends to mean:
- Profound thrombocytopenia (very low nadirs),
- Severe hypercoagulability and thrombosis (as in this patient’s stroke),
- Poor or delayed platelet response to standard measures,
- Functional assays (SRA) that release serotonin at 0 U/mL heparin.

This case added another lesson: a severe aHIT antibody can show only partial inhibition with an FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3), producing an “indeterminate” SRA. If the clinical picture fits, an indeterminate SRA does not rule out aHIT — ask the lab for the full heparin‑dose response and Fc blockade data, and involve hematology early.

## How to think about the 4Ts after CPB (practical adaptation)
The 4Ts remain useful, but timing needs context:
- Timing: If the platelet fall is before POD5, actively search for recent heparin exposures in the prior 5–10 days (caths, flushes, peri‑procedural UFH). If recent heparin is present, early timing is “consistent” with HIT.
- Thrombocytopenia: look at the trend vs baseline (fall %), nadir, and whether there is failure to recover after the expected POD2–3 rebound. Very low nadirs (<20×10^9/L) should raise concern for aHIT.
- Thrombosis: any new thrombus (including stroke) strongly increases probability.
- Other causes: surgical blood loss, cardiogenic shock, sepsis, drugs and microangiopathic hemolysis should be considered but are often absent in aHIT.

In short: early postoperative falls are common and often benign — but early + recent heparin exposure + rapid decline or thrombosis = HIT until proven otherwise.

## What to do at the bedside — immediate steps
If you suspect HIT (including early post‑CPB with prior heparin exposure):
- Stop all heparins immediately — stop UFH, stop LMWH prophylaxis, stop heparin flushes.
- Start a non‑heparin anticoagulant without waiting for confirmatory testing: options include argatroban or bivalirudin (institutional choice/availability), fondaparinux is an off‑label option in many centers; consult hematology. In this case argatroban (0.75 μg/kg/min, PTT goal 55–85 s) was used because of recent stroke and the desire for a short half‑life.
- Send PF4/polyanion immunoassay (ELISA) and a functional assay (SRA). Tell the lab if you suspect aHIT so they can run 0 U/mL heparin and an FcγRIIa blockade test (IV.3).
- Consider IVIG early for aHIT (high‑dose regimens were used in this case), and monitor response closely; be aware IVIG responses can be variable.
- Monitor fibrinogen, D‑dimer and peripheral smear for schistocytes as markers of consumptive hypercoagulability.
- Avoid platelet transfusions unless life‑threatening bleeding or invasive procedures are required; they can worsen thrombosis.

## Laboratory nuances you must know
- PF4 ELISA can be strongly positive in a true case — OD values matter; in this case OD = 2.2 (strong).
- The SRA pattern for classic HIT: activation at low heparin concentrations and suppression at very high heparin (100 U/mL). For aHIT you’ll see platelet activation even with no added heparin (0 U/mL).
- If the SRA is reported “indeterminate”, ask for the raw heparin‑dose curve and the FcγRIIa blocking results — some aHIT antibodies show unusual blockade patterns (partial resistance to IV.3), as in this report.

## Why auditing peri‑procedural heparin exposures matters
Small, easily forgotten exposures are the usual culprits that convert an “expected” early post‑op platelet dip into HIT:
- Diagnostic caths (radial or femoral) with heparin boluses,
- Heparinized saline flushes,
- Heparin boluses for line maintenance or device priming,
- Prior therapeutic or prophylactic heparin courses in the days before surgery.

If you don’t ask, you’ll miss the exposure and misclassify timing — and that delays stopping heparins.

## Final words
This was a salvageable, instructive aHIT: early onset, severe thrombocytopenia, thrombotic stroke, a strongly positive ELISA and an SRA that screamed “autoimmune” (activation at 0 U/mL), plus the unusual lab quirk of partial Fc blockade. The clinical lessons are straightforward and actionable: audit heparin history, don’t be falsely reassured by “before POD5,” stop all heparins the moment HIT is in the differential, start non‑heparin anticoagulation after discussing with hematology, and ask the lab for the detailed SRA profile if aHIT is suspected.

Key take‑home messages
- Early post‑CPB platelet falls are common — but if there was any heparin exposure in the prior 5–10 days (even a small cath bolus), early timing can still represent HIT.
- Audit peri‑procedural heparin exposures (caths, flushes, lines) before assuming an early fall is benign.
- Stop all heparins (including LMWH prophylaxis) immediately when HIT is suspected, and start a non‑heparin anticoagulant; don’t wait for confirmatory tests.
- Ask the lab for PF4 ELISA and an SRA that includes a 0 U/mL heparin condition and FcγRIIa (IV.3) blockade if you suspect aHIT.
- aHIT can present with heparin‑independent platelet activation, severe thrombocytopenia and marked hypercoagulability; consider early IVIG and hematology involvement.
- An “indeterminate” SRA report does not rule out aHIT — review the full dose‑response and blockade data with the lab and hematology.



# When “Indeterminate” Is the Red Flag: Reading Functional HIT Assays in aHIT

This case is a reminder that “indeterminate” on a functional HIT assay can be the loudest possible alarm. An elderly man after cardiac surgery developed an early, steep platelet fall, a thrombotic stroke, a very high PF4/polyanion ELISA (OD 2.2) and an SRA that looked odd — massive serotonin release at 0, 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL heparin, inhibition at 100 U/mL, but only partial blockade by the FcγRIIa monoclonal antibody IV.3. The result was labeled “indeterminate.” What that lab shorthand meant in this patient was autoimmune HIT (aHIT) with unusually aggressive, heparin‑independent platelet‑activating antibodies that were partially resistant to FcγRIIa blockade. The clinical course (platelet nadir 13×10^9/L, POD6 parietal stroke, consumptive coagulopathy with falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer and schistocytes) and the eventual response to argatroban + high‑dose IVIG (later fondaparinux) confirm how dangerous—and how treatable—this syndrome can be if recognized.

## A quick refresher on what the assays show (and what you should actually read)
Two tests commonly guide us in HIT workups:

- PF4/polyanion ELISA: sensitive. Optical density (OD) correlates with greater likelihood of clinically relevant, platelet‑activating antibodies. OD >1.0 (and certainly an OD of 2.2 as in this case) is strong evidence of pathogenic antibodies.
- Serotonin‑release assay (SRA): functional gold standard. Classic heparin‑dependent HIT gives a stereotyped pattern:
  - 0 U heparin: little/no release (<20%),
  - low heparin (0.1–0.3 U/mL): strong release (>20%),
  - high heparin (100 U/mL): inhibition of release (<20%).
  - Addition of IV.3 (an FcγRIIa blocker) usually abolishes release, confirming Fc‑mediated platelet activation.

If the SRA shows strong release at 0 U heparin, think autoimmune HIT (aHIT): these antibodies activate platelets without added heparin. If IV.3 fails to fully inhibit activation, don’t interpret that as a laboratory error — interpret it as a biologic clue: unusually aggressive antibodies.

## How aHIT breaks the “rules”
Autoimmune HIT is a subgroup of HIT in which antibodies either don’t require heparin to form platelet‑activating complexes or are able to form complexes at very low heparin/PF4 conditions. Clinically this tends to mean:
- Very early onset if there was recent heparin exposure (as here: 2,000 U at cath 1 week before surgery), because an immune response is already primed.
- Profound thrombocytopenia and a high rate of thrombosis (this patient had a parietal stroke on POD6).
- Functional assays that look atypical: strong serotonin release at 0 U heparin is classic for aHIT. What was novel in this patient was only partial inhibition by IV.3 — a signal of particularly strong/robust immune complexes. Labs may call that “indeterminate.” Clinicians should treat it as dangerous.

## Practical interpretation: read the pattern, not the single word “indeterminate”
When you get SRA output, don’t glance only at the final “positive/negative/indeterminate” label. Look at the numeric release pattern across heparin concentrations and the IV.3 results.

- Red flags for aHIT:
  - High ELISA OD (e.g., 2.2),
  - High serotonin release at 0 U heparin (this patient 96% at 0 U),
  - High release at 0.1–0.3 U heparin (97–99% in this case),
  - Inhibition at 100 U heparin (0% here) — this still fits HIT biology,
  - Partial or absent inhibition with IV.3 — unusual, but may reflect extremely potent antibodies.
- An SRA labeled “indeterminate” because IV.3 only reduced release from 99% to 76% (instead of <20%) should elevate, not lower, your concern for severe aHIT.

## What to ask the lab (copy‑paste friendly)
If the clinical picture is concerning (postoperative platelet drop, thrombosis, strong ELISA) and you receive an “indeterminate” or unusual SRA, call the platelet immunology lab and request additional information/tests. Useful, pragmatic checklist phrasing:

“High clinical suspicion for HIT/aHIT. Please provide the % serotonin release at 0, 0.1, 0.3 and 100 U/mL heparin, and the SRA results with and without IV.3 FcγRIIa blockade, and indicate degree of IV.3 inhibition. If available, please run a PF4‑enhanced SRA or PF4‑dependent PEA and repeat testing across multiple platelet donors. Note recent heparin exposures: 2,000 U UFH at cath one week prior, 45,000 U intraop UFH, LMWH prophylaxis POD1–4. Sample drawn POD5 (pre‑IVIG). Please comment on donor variability, and repeat on a later sample if indicated.”

Also tell the lab whether the sample was obtained after IVIG or plasmapheresis — these therapies can blunt functional assays.

## How the lab can help further
If available, labs can run:
- PF4‑enhanced SRA: adds exogenous PF4 to increase sensitivity for heparin‑independent antibodies.
- PF4‑dependent PEA (platelet activation assays using flow cytometry/P‑selectin): complementary functional testing that may detect aHIT antibodies.
- Repeat SRA with different donor platelets (donor variability can change results).

As in this case, serial sera over weeks helped show an evolving antibody phenotype: the SRA pattern shifted over months from heparin‑independent to a more heparin‑dependent pattern as antibodies waned (POD124 sample showed only 15% release at 0 U but 100% at 0.3 U).

## Clinical actions you should take when SRA is “indeterminate” but the pattern fits aHIT
Do not wait for laboratory perfection if the clinical gestalt and ELISA/SRA pattern point to aHIT. Actions to consider immediately:
- Stop all heparin exposures (UFH, LMWH, heparin flushes, heparin‑coated lines).
- Start an alternative non‑heparin anticoagulant appropriate for the patient’s bleeding risk and thrombotic burden. In this case, argatroban was chosen initially (short half‑life and rapid reversal of effect desirable because of recent stroke and severe thrombocytopenia). When hypercoagulability persisted, the team switched to therapeutic fondaparinux with supplemental antithrombin before initiation because the patient’s AT activity was mildly low (0.70 U/mL).
- Consider early high‑dose IVIG for aHIT. IVIG blocks Fc receptors and often produces rapid platelet rises; a blunted clinical response (as in this patient) may reflect unusually aggressive antibodies but does not negate the diagnosis.
- Monitor platelets, fibrinogen, D‑dimer and peripheral smear (look for schistocytes). Falling fibrinogen and rising D‑dimer with schistocytes should raise concern for consumptive coagulopathy from progressive HIT‑mediated thrombosis.
- Plan for serial serology/functional testing; document dates of heparin exposures clearly for the lab.

## Why this matters for practice
- “Indeterminate” SRA is not a reason to downplay HIT risk. In some settings, it is the laboratory’s way of saying “this is odd — look closer.” In aHIT that oddness can mean more pathogenic antibodies.
- High ELISA OD + serotonin release at 0 U heparin + clinical thrombosis = treat as aHIT even if IV.3 only partially inhibits the SRA.
- Antibody profiles change: early pre‑sensitization can produce early POD platelet drops; antibody phenotype can evolve from heparin‑independent back toward heparin‑dependent as titers fall. Don’t assume that one test at one time gives the whole story.

## Final clinical pearls from this case
- Always ask about recent heparin exposures before cardiac surgery — small doses (e.g., 2,000 U at a cath) can prime for early‑onset HIT/aHIT.
- Read the SRA trace: 0 U release is as telling as low‑heparin release. Big release at 0 U = aHIT until proven otherwise.
- If IV.3 does not fully inhibit, that is not reassurance — it may be the signature of unusually potent antibodies.
- Don’t let a lab’s “indeterminate” label delay stopping heparin and starting non‑heparin anticoagulation when the clinical and serologic picture supports HIT/aHIT.

## Key take‑home points
- A high PF4/polyanion ELISA OD (e.g., 2.2) plus strong serotonin release at 0 U heparin = very suspicious for autoimmune HIT (aHIT).  
- Patterns to memorize: classic HIT = low release at 0 U, high at 0.1–0.3 U, inhibition at 100 U; aHIT = high release at 0 U (± high at low heparin), inhibition at 100 U often preserved.  
- Partial or absent IV.3 inhibition in the SRA should increase your concern for aggressive aHIT rather than reassure you.  
- If clinical probability is high, treat: stop all heparin, start an appropriate non‑heparin anticoagulant, and consider urgent high‑dose IVIG for aHIT.  
- Communicate with the reference lab: request percent release at multiple heparin concentrations, IV.3 inhibition data, PF4‑enhanced SRA/PEA if available, and repeat testing across donors and over time.



# Argatroban, Fondaparinux, or Both? Anticoagulating Fulminant aHIT After Cardiac Surgery

## What made this case surprising
This was not your garden‑variety postoperative drop in platelets. An elderly man who had been exposed to small doses of heparin during a preoperative transradial cath and then large doses of UFH during cardiopulmonary bypass developed a rapidly progressive thrombocytopenia that hit a nadir of 13×10^9/L and—crucially—an ischemic stroke on POD6. PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive (OD 2.2) and the serotonin‑release assay (SRA) showed heparin‑independent platelet activation (very high serotonin release at 0 U heparin) with the unusual laboratory quirk of only partial inhibition by an FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal (IV.3). That serology plus the clinical picture equals autoimmune HIT (aHIT): aggressive, often heparin‑independent, and sometimes refractory to usual maneuvers.

The management dilemma here is classic: you must immediately stop heparin and anticoagulate because of very high thrombotic risk, but the patient also has a fresh ischemic stroke and profound thrombocytopenia—so you want a rapidly reversible, titratable agent that controls hypercoagulability without committing the patient to long-acting anticoagulation if hemorrhagic conversion occurs.

## The initial decision — why argatroban first
In this patient the team stopped all heparins and started intravenous argatroban. The rationale was practical and risk‑based:

- Argatroban is an IV direct thrombin inhibitor with a short biologic half‑life and predictable PTT titration, so it can be stopped quickly if bleeding or hemorrhagic transformation occurs. That short reversibility matters with a fresh ischemic stroke.
- It is hepatically cleared, which can be an advantage if renal function is marginal (this patient had normal creatinine, but hepatic rather than renal clearance remains attractive in some perioperative settings).
- An IV agent lets you titrate to a defined PTT goal (in this case the team used 0.75 mcg/kg/min with a PTT target of 55–85 s), and gives immediate anticoagulant effect while inpatient.

They also gave high‑dose IVIG (multiple 100 g doses) to try to rapidly blunt FcγRIIa‑mediated platelet activation—appropriate for suspected aHIT. Despite this, the platelet count barely budged and markers of consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, appearance of schistocytes) progressed while on argatroban—suggesting ongoing thrombin generation despite treatment.

Clinical pearl: in a patient with fresh intracranial ischemia, choose an anticoagulant you can stop quickly if bleeding occurs. That often favors a short‑acting IV direct thrombin inhibitor as first‑line.

## When argatroban isn’t enough — why they pivoted to fondaparinux
After about a week of argatroban the lab picture suggested persistent hypercoagulability (fibrinogen fell from ~6.6 to 2.5 g/L, D‑dimer peaked, schistocytes appeared) and platelet recovery was minimal. The treating team switched to therapeutic-size fondaparinux (10 mg subcutaneously once daily) on POD14. Reasons:

- Fondaparinux is a once‑daily, subcutaneous, anti‑Xa agent that is effective in HIT in many case series (off‑label use). For an inpatient who is clinically stabilizing, the ease of administration is compelling.
- In this patient there was concern argatroban wasn’t controlling the consumptive coagulopathy; a different mechanism (AT‑dependent factor Xa inhibition) was a reasonable clinical trial.
- Because fondaparinux’s activity depends on antithrombin (AT), the team checked AT and gave 1000 U AT concentrate before starting fondaparinux (pre‑infusion AT was mildly reduced at 0.70 U/mL). That step likely helped the drug perform as intended.

The switch seemed vindicated: over the ensuing weeks platelets gradually recovered to normal (155×10^9/L by POD62) and the patient made a complete neurological recovery.

Clinical pearl: if markers of thrombin generation and fibrin consumption keep trending in the wrong direction while on one non‑heparin anticoagulant, consider switching mechanism of anticoagulation rather than simply increasing dose—especially in fulminant aHIT.

## Using fibrinogen and D‑dimer as bedside signals of anticoagulant adequacy
In extreme aHIT you can’t wait for platelets to be your only signal. Serial fibrinogen and D‑dimer trends helped this team decide whether the anticoagulant strategy was getting ahead of the thrombotic storm.

- Falling fibrinogen in the postoperative period (when you’d otherwise expect high fibrinogen) can mean ongoing consumption from active thrombosis.
- Rising D‑dimer reflects fibrin turnover and active clot formation/breakdown.
- Appearance of schistocytes suggested microangiopathic red cell fragmentation from microthrombi.

Taken together these are practical, rapidly available surrogate markers of uncontrolled hypercoagulability. If fibrinogen keeps falling and D‑dimer keeps rising on therapy, you need to reassess anticoagulation adequacy pronto.

Clinical pearl: use trends, not single values—serial fibrinogen + D‑dimer can be early warning signs that your anticoagulant strategy is failing.

## IVIG — powerful but not magic
High‑dose IVIG is now a standard adjunct for severe aHIT because it blocks FcγRIIa‑mediated platelet activation. Typical immediate expectations are a brisk platelet rise; failure to do so suggests very high‑titer or unusually active antibodies (as in this patient, whose SRA showed strong heparin‑independent activation and only partial inhibition by IV.3). IVIG has risks (volume load, renal injury, thrombotic risk) and it won’t always work in the face of extremely potent pathogenic antibodies.

Clinical pearl: give IVIG early for severe aHIT, but temper expectations and keep monitoring for ongoing thrombosis.

## Practical dosing and monitoring notes (from the case)
This patient’s actual management is a useful template:

- Argatroban: started at 0.75 mcg/kg/min with PTT goal 55–85 s (lower than some standard starting doses because of recent stroke and bleeding risk). Titrate to PTT; remember argatroban prolongs INR and complicates warfarin bridging.
- IVIG: several high doses were used (100 g ×2 then a third 100 g dose) because response was blunted.
- Fondaparinux: switched to therapeutic‑dose 10 mg subcutaneously once daily on POD14. (Standard therapeutic fondaparinux dosing for VTE is weight‑based; this case used 10 mg.) Check renal function—fondaparinux is renally cleared.
- Antithrombin: given 1000 U AT concentrate before fondaparinux because AT was mildly low (0.70 U/mL); fondaparinux requires AT to work.
- Transition to oral anticoagulation: fondaparinux was continued until platelet recovery; apixaban 5 mg twice daily was resumed on POD65 once platelets were stably normal.

## Lab pearl on serology — don’t rely on a single test
This patient’s PF4 ELISA was strongly positive (OD 2.2). The SRA showed classic features of aHIT (strong serotonin release without heparin) plus the unusual feature of only partial inhibition by IV.3 — a novel laboratory phenotype. Bottom line: if the clinical picture fits and immunoassay/SRA are strongly suggestive (or even “indeterminate” in the face of strong ELISA and compatible clinical features), treat for aHIT. Don’t be lulled into inaction by a single odd lab readout.

## Bottom line — practical algorithm from this case
When you have fulminant aHIT after cardiac surgery with fresh stroke:

- Stop all heparin products immediately.
- Start a short‑acting IV non‑heparin anticoagulant (argatroban) to allow rapid cessation if bleeding occurs; consider starting at a conservative infusion rate if bleeding risk is high and titrate by PTT.
- Give high‑dose IVIG early for aHIT.
- Monitor platelets, serial fibrinogen and D‑dimer (and for schistocytes) as early markers of ongoing thrombin generation.
- If anticoagulation on the chosen agent fails to control consumptive coagulopathy, consider switching mechanism (e.g., to therapeutic fondaparinux), and correct low antithrombin if present.
- Transition to an oral DOAC (apixaban used in this case) only after platelet recovery and clinical stability.

Key takeaways
- Early (pre‑POD5) postoperative HIT should prompt review of recent heparin exposures (eg, cath lab heparin).
- In a patient with fresh ischemic stroke and fulminant aHIT, a short‑acting IV anticoagulant (argatroban) is a reasonable first move because it can be stopped quickly if bleeding occurs.
- Persistent falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, and schistocytes on therapy are red flags for ongoing hypercoagulability despite anticoagulation.
- If one non‑heparin anticoagulant appears inadequate, switching to a drug with a different mechanism (fondaparinux’s AT‑dependent anti‑Xa activity in this case) can be effective; check renal function and antithrombin before doing so.
- IVIG helps in most aHIT cases but may be only partially effective when antibodies are unusually potent or partially resistant to Fc blockade.
- Treat the patient, not the test: a suspicious clinical picture of aHIT warrants rapid non‑heparin anticoagulation even when serology is atypical.



# IVIG for Autoimmune HIT: Dosing, Mechanism, and What to Do When It Fizzles

## The hook — why this case matters
Autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) is a beast. In the case above an elderly man who received intra‑arterial UFH at cardiac catheterisation and massive UFH on cardiopulmonary bypass developed a precipitous postoperative platelet fall, an acute left parietal stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA (OD 2.2). The functional assay showed a classic aHIT signature — very high serotonin release even without heparin (96% at 0 U/mL) — but a laboratory wrinkle: platelet activation was only partially inhibited by the FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal IV.3 (76% residual release). Clinically, he was treated with argatroban and high‑dose IVIG (100 g on two consecutive days, then a third 100 g dose) and later switched to fondaparinux plus AT concentrate. Platelets eventually recovered, but only slowly (155×10^9/L on POD62). This is a useful real‑world lesson: IVIG often produces a brisk platelet rise in aHIT, but sometimes it “fizzles.” Here’s how IVIG is supposed to work, when to use it, why it may fail, and what to do next.

## Quick primer — what auto‑immune HIT is and how IVIG helps
HIT is caused by IgG antibodies that bind PF4/heparin complexes and activate platelets via FcγRIIa. In aHIT, the antibodies can activate platelets independent of heparin (heparin‑independent, “super‑activating” antibodies) and tend to cause more severe thrombocytopenia and thrombosis.

IVIG’s principal actions in HIT relevant to acute management are:
- Saturation/functional blockade of platelet and monocyte Fcγ receptors (especially FcγRIIa), preventing immune‑complex‑driven platelet activation.
- Anti‑idiotype interactions and modulation of FcRn, increasing catabolism of pathogenic IgG.
- Broader immunomodulatory effects (complement modulation, upregulation of inhibitory Fc receptors).

Those mechanisms explain why IVIG often yields a rapid platelet rise in aHIT — but they also point to potential failure modes (see below).

## When to consider IVIG in HIT/aHIT
IVIG is not first‑line for every HIT patient. It is indicated or reasonable to consider when:
- You have confirmed or strongly suspected aHIT (heparin‑independent platelet activation on functional assay, very high ELISA OD, or early severe thrombocytopenia with recent heparin exposure).
- There is life‑ or limb‑threatening thrombosis or ongoing consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, schistocytes).
- Platelets fail to recover after 24–72 hours of appropriate non‑heparin anticoagulation.
- You need to blunt platelet activation before platelet transfusion or an urgent invasive procedure (rare).

In the case above the combination of stroke, very low nadir platelets (13×10^9/L), very high ELISA OD and heparin‑independent SRA made a strong case for early IVIG.

## Dosing and practical logistics
The commonly used, evidence‑based regimens are weight‑based:
- 1 g/kg IV daily for 2 days (total 2 g/kg) — the most commonly used “quick” regimen for severe autoimmune phenomena.
- Alternative: 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days (same total dose spread out).

Practical points:
- Dose by body weight (ideal body weight or adjusted weight in obesity per local practice) and be mindful of a total dose ~2 g/kg unless you have a specific reason to deviate.
- Infuse slowly and monitor — premedicate with acetaminophen/antihistamine if needed.
- Prefer non‑sucrose formulations in patients at risk for acute tubular injury; check baseline renal function.
- In the reported case, the team used absolute 100 g doses (two consecutive days, then a third 100 g) — an aggressive, high absolute dosing strategy appropriate to the clinical severity, but one that increases vigilance for toxicity.

## What response to expect — and when to worry
In many published reports and case series, IVIG produces a platelet count rise within 24–48 hours in aHIT. If you do not see a meaningful platelet rise by 48–72 hours after the first IVIG course, re‑evaluate: is the diagnosis correct, is anticoagulation adequate, are antibodies still abundant or unusually potent?

Be aware that IVIG can also interfere with functional platelet assays (by blocking Fc receptors on reagent platelets), sometimes producing false‑negative functional tests shortly after infusion. In this case the SRA remained strongly positive for months despite IVIG, demonstrating persistent pathogenic antibodies.

## Why IVIG can “fizzle” — mechanisms to keep in mind
Several mechanistic reasons explain incomplete or delayed responses:
- Massive antibody burden and high‑affinity antibodies (high ELISA OD like 2.2) can overwhelm FcγR blockade — there’s simply too much pathogenic IgG for the IVIG to neutralize or outcompete.
- Antibodies that are unusually “super‑activating” may partially bypass FcγR blockade or fix complement or engage other platelet activation pathways, making FcγR saturation insufficient. The partial inhibition by IV.3 in the lab supports this idea.
- Ongoing production of pathogenic IgG from active plasmablasts can outpace the temporary neutralizing effect of IVIG.
- Suboptimal IVIG dosing or product selection (e.g., small total dose or product with higher adverse‑event profile) may blunt effect.
- Poor control of hypercoagulability with anticoagulation can perpetuate consumptive platelet loss despite IVIG.

## Practical next steps when IVIG doesn’t deliver
If you’ve given IVIG and the platelets aren’t coming up (or you have ongoing thrombosis/coagulopathy), act promptly and collaboratively with hematology and transfusion medicine.

First, reconfirm the diagnosis with PF4 ELISA and a reference functional assay (inform the lab that aHIT is suspected so they can choose the right tests). Ensure every heparin exposure is stopped (UFH, LMWH, heparin flushes).

Second, optimize non‑heparin anticoagulation. Review anticoagulant choice, dosing, and effect. If you’re on argatroban and there are biochemical signs of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, schistocytes), consider switching agents — options include bivalirudin or fondaparinux (fondaparinux is off‑label but widely used; remember it needs antithrombin activity; check AT level and replace if low as was done in the case with 1,000 U AT concentrate). Keep therapeutic anticoagulation even with very low platelets unless active bleeding mandates otherwise — HIT is a prothrombotic condition.

Third, escalate immunomodulation if necessary. Options include repeating or extending IVIG (additional 1 g/kg doses), or moving to plasma exchange (PEX) for rapid removal of pathogenic antibodies in life‑ or organ‑threatening, IVIG‑refractory cases. PEX frequently gives a fast fall in circulating antibody and can be lifesaving. Rituximab can be considered for refractory cases to blunt antibody production but has a delayed onset.

Fourth, use adjunctive monitoring: daily platelets, PTT/argatroban levels if on direct thrombin inhibitors, fibrinogen, D‑dimer and peripheral blood film for schistocytes. These trends often provide the earliest signal that hypercoagulability is being controlled (or not).

Finally, avoid routine platelet transfusions unless there is life‑threatening bleeding or an urgent procedure — transfused platelets can be consumed and fuel thrombosis unless platelet activation is blocked and anticoagulation is established.

## Safety — IVIG is not benign
High‑dose IVIG has recognized risks that are especially important in HIT:
- Thrombotic events (arterial and venous) — IVIG increases plasma viscosity and has been associated with thrombosis; that risk is additive to the already hypercoagulable HIT patient.
- Renal injury — especially with sucrose‑stabilized products; check renal function and avoid in severe CKD when possible.
- Hemolysis — due to isohemagglutinins in IVIG (watch the hemoglobin).
- Infusion reactions, aseptic meningitis, and rare anaphylaxis (risk in IgA‑deficient patients).
Measures to reduce risk: slow infusion, pre‑hydration, choose non‑sucrose products, monitor creatinine and hemoglobin, and ensure appropriate anticoagulation is in place when feasible.

## Case‑level lessons
This case reinforces several practical points. First, HIT can present early after cardiac surgery if there was recent preoperative heparin exposure (the diagnostic window is not fixed). Second, very high ELISA OD and heparin‑independent SRA activity predict a severe course. Third, IVIG is a powerful tool but not guaranteed — partial resistance to FcγR blockade can occur, and IVIG may need repeating or escalation to PEX. Fourth, anticoagulation choice matters: despite argatroban therapy the patient showed biochemical evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy and benefit was ultimately seen after switching to fondaparinux plus AT replacement. And finally, functional assays can remain positive for months, so treat the patient (not the lab) and monitor trends.

## Key take‑home messages
- Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) is a severe, often heparin‑independent form of HIT; very high ELISA OD and SRA activity without heparin suggest aHIT.  
- IVIG works by saturating Fcγ receptors, neutralizing pathogenic IgG and accelerating IgG catabolism; it often (but not always) produces a rapid platelet rise in aHIT.  
- Typical IVIG dosing: 1 g/kg IV daily × 2 days (or 0.4 g/kg/day × 5 days); dose by weight and monitor for toxicity.  
- Expect a platelet response within 24–72 hours; lack of response should prompt re‑evaluation of diagnosis, anticoagulation efficacy, and consideration of repeat IVIG or plasma exchange.  
- If IVIG “fizzles,” optimize non‑heparin anticoagulation (consider switching agents), consider additional IVIG or plasma exchange for antibody removal, and involve hematology early.  
- Watch for IVIG‑related complications (thrombosis, renal injury, hemolysis) and maintain appropriate anticoagulation while treating the immune process.

If you see a patient like this, get hematology on board urgently, send functional assays to a reference lab and treat aggressively — aHIT can be reversed, but it sometimes requires stepping up immunomodulation beyond a single IVIG course.



# HIT-Associated Consumptive Coagulopathy: Falling Fibrinogen, Rising D‑Dimer, and the Schistocyte Trap

## Why this case grabbed my attention
A patient after routine cardiac surgery developed dramatic, early-onset thrombocytopenia, an ischemic stroke, and laboratory features that looked more like consumptive coagulopathy than classic heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). PF4 ELISA was strongly positive, but the functional platelet assay behaved oddly — massive platelet activation even without heparin and only partial inhibition by an FcγRIIA‑blocking antibody. In short: autoimmune HIT (aHIT) with a fulminant consumptive phenotype that can masquerade as DIC or TTP — and that misdiagnosis changes everything you do at the bedside.

## Quick case recap (the facts to remember)
An elderly man had transradial cath 1 week before cardiac surgery (2000 U UFH during the cath). Cardiac surgery used cardiopulmonary bypass with 45,000 U UFH. Postop he was put on LMWH prophylaxis (dalteparin 5,000 → 7,500 U). Platelets fell from 210 preop to 155 on POD1, then progressively to 50×10^9/L on POD5 and 24×10^9/L on POD6. CT on POD6 showed a left parietal ischemic stroke. PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive (OD 2.2). The serotonin‑release assay (SRA) showed striking heparin‑independent platelet activation (96% release at 0 U/mL; 97–99% at 0.1–0.3 U/mL; 0% at 100 U/mL) and only partial inhibition with the Fc blocking antibody IV.3 — an atypical aHIT profile. The patient was treated with argatroban (0.75 mcg/kg/min, PTT target 55–85 s), high‑dose IVIG (100 g × 3), then switched to fondaparinux 10 mg daily with AT concentrate 1,000 U because fibrinogen fell (6.59 g/L on POD5 → nadir 2.49 g/L by POD17), d‑dimer rose (peak ≈ POD12), and transient schistocytes appeared (POD9–16). Platelets recovered slowly (155×10^9/L at POD62); long‑term anticoagulation was transitioned back to apixaban.

## What “consumptive coagulopathy” looks like in fulminant aHIT
HIT is primarily a prothrombotic platelet‑activation syndrome driven by anti–PF4 antibodies. In autoimmune HIT the antibodies can activate platelets without added heparin — producing extreme platelet consumption and widespread thrombosis. When platelet activation is intense and widespread, the coagulation system is driven hard: fibrinogen is consumed (fibrinogen falls), fibrin clot turnover increases (d‑dimer rises), and red cells may be sheared in the microvasculature producing schistocytes. That constellation — falling fibrinogen + rising d‑dimer + transient schistocytes — is a consumptive picture that can be mistaken for DIC or TTP unless you connect it to the HIT context.

## How to separate HIT (including aHIT) from DIC and TTP at the bedside
Timing, clinical context, and a few focused labs get you most of the way.

- HIT/aHIT
  - Typical timing: platelet fall days 5–10 after heparin exposure — but can be earlier if there was recent preoperative or perioperative heparin (as in this case).
  - Prominent thrombosis risk; bleeding is uncommon unless platelets are extremely low.
  - Labs: PF4 ELISA may be strongly positive (OD >2 is highly suspicious). SRA confirms platelet‑activating antibodies; aHIT may show heparin‑independent activation.
  - Coagulation tests (PT/INR, PTT) are often normal early; fibrinogen may fall and d‑dimer rise if consumptive coagulation is extensive.
- DIC
  - Usually driven by sepsis, massive tissue injury, obstetric catastrophe, or malignancy.
  - Global coagulopathy: prolonged PT/PTT, low fibrinogen, thrombocytopenia, high d‑dimer, bleeding common.
  - Look for an obvious trigger and global coagulation abnormalities.
- TTP
  - Thrombotic microangiopathy with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and severe thrombocytopenia.
  - Schistocytes and lab evidence of hemolysis are prominent — low haptoglobin, elevated indirect bilirubin, reticulocytosis.
  - ADAMTS13 activity is usually severely reduced (<10%).
  - Treatment is plasma exchange and immunosuppression — not anticoagulation.

In this patient the early coagulation screen was normal (INR 1.1, PTT 34 s) and there were no early hemolysis signals (haptoglobin was high, reticulocyte count normal). That made TTP less likely initially. As fibrinogen fell and schistocytes appeared later, the picture looked consumptive — but the temporal relationship to recent heparin exposure and the strongly positive PF4 ELISA pointed to aHIT rather than primary DIC or TTP.

## The schistocyte trap — don’t let one lab hijack your diagnosis
Schistocytes can pop up in several conditions: TTP, DIC, severe hypertension, mechanical hemolysis, and — importantly — frank microvascular thrombosis from HIT. A few isolated schistocytes on a peripheral smear do not equal TTP. Always pair red cell fragmentation with hemolysis labs (haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, retic count) and consider ADAMTS13 if TTP is in the differential. In this case schistocytes were a transient later feature of consumptive coagulopathy driven by aHIT rather than primary ADAMTS13 deficiency.

## Using serial fibrinogen and D‑dimer to track treatment response
Platelet count is important but often lags. In fulminant aHIT the coagulation markers can move earlier and can be used as dynamic biomarkers of ongoing thrombosis and response to therapy.

- Falling fibrinogen suggests ongoing consumption of fibrin(ogen).
- Rising d‑dimer suggests increased fibrin formation and breakdown (ongoing thrombosis/fibrinolysis).
- In this case fibrinogen fell from 6.59 g/L (POD5) to 2.49 g/L (POD17) while d‑dimer peaked around POD12 — all signs that initial control of hypercoagulability was incomplete.
- After escalation of therapy (IVIG and change in anticoagulant strategy) trends reversed, and platelet recovery followed.

Bottom line: watch trends, not single values. Rising fibrinogen + falling d‑dimer together are reassuring signals that the prothrombotic storm is settling.

## Practical management pearls from this case
Stop all heparin immediately (including flushes). Treat suspected/definite HIT as thrombotic disease — anticoagulate with a non‑heparin agent even when platelets are very low, unless there is life‑threatening bleeding.

- Start a non‑heparin anticoagulant quickly. In this case the team used argatroban (0.75 mcg/kg/min, PTT target 55–85 s) because of the recent ischemic stroke and the desire for short half‑life. When anticoagulation appeared insufficient (falling fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer, schistocytes) they switched to fondaparinux 10 mg daily and gave AT concentrate 1,000 U because fondaparinux requires antithrombin and the AT level was mildly low (0.70 U/mL).
- Autoimmune HIT may benefit from high‑dose IVIG to block FcγRIIA‑mediated platelet activation; this patient received 100 g on two consecutive days followed by a third 100 g dose. Responses can be dramatic — but not always; this patient had only a modest platelet rise and required further adjustments.
- Platelet transfusions generally make HIT worse because they provide activated substrate for antibody complexes. Reserve platelet transfusion for life‑threatening bleeding or when platelets must be corrected for an urgent invasive procedure.
- Don’t wait for a “perfect” functional assay. A strongly positive PF4 ELISA plus a compatible clinical picture should trigger therapy; functional assays (SRA) can be indeterminate or show aHIT patterns (heparin‑independent activation or unusual Fc blockade behavior), as happened here.
- Autoimmune HIT can present earlier than day 5 after surgery if there was recent heparin exposure (the patient had heparin at cath one week earlier). Keep a careful heparin exposure history.
- Plan for follow‑up: HIT serologies (including SRA) can remain positive for months even after clinical recovery; platelet recovery can be slow in severe aHIT (this patient normalized platelet count only by POD62), and long‑term anticoagulation can be transitioned to a DOAC (apixaban in this case) once platelets recover.

## Bottom line — what I want you to remember
- Fulminant autoimmune HIT can produce a consumptive coagulopathy: falling fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer, and transient schistocytes — and it can look like DIC or TTP.
- Always consider recent heparin exposure (procedural or perioperative) when thrombocytopenia appears early after surgery.
- A strongly positive PF4 ELISA with a compatible presentation = treat for HIT while you investigate further; SRA can be atypical in aHIT.
- Trend fibrinogen and d‑dimer (and smear) — they often signal persistent thrombosis before the platelet count recovers.
- Avoid platelet transfusions unless life‑threatening bleeding is present.
- Use non‑heparin anticoagulation promptly; consider IVIG for aHIT; tailor anticoagulant choices to the clinical scenario (bleeding risk, renal function, logistics).

If you see a post‑op patient with inexorable platelet decline plus rising d‑dimer and falling fibrinogen, don’t reflex to plasma exchange for “TTP” or to transfuse platelets — pause, check heparin exposures, send PF4 testing, and treat for HIT while you sort it out.



# Stroke on the Heparin Highway: Managing Arterial Thrombosis in HIT After Cardiac Surgery

What makes this case interesting? Two things. First, an elderly patient developed an ischemic cortical stroke in the first week after cardiac surgery — but the “neurologic” sign was worsening postoperative delirium rather than an obvious focal deficit. Second, the stroke occurred in the setting of autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) with an unusually aggressive serologic phenotype: very high PF4 ELISA and a platelet‑activating antibody that triggered platelet activation even without heparin and was only partially inhibited by FcγR blockade. In short: early, severe, prothrombotic HIT after routine perioperative heparin exposures, presenting as delirium and producing an arterial cortical infarct. That combination is a trap for the unwary and forces uncomfortable trade‑offs between treating thrombosis and avoiding hemorrhage.

## The case in a nutshell
A man in his 70s had a transradial coronary angiogram (2000 U UFH) one week before cardiac surgery. During cardiopulmonary bypass he received 45,000 U UFH. Postop he was on LMWH prophylaxis. Platelets fell from 210 preop → 155 on POD1 → 50 on POD5 → mid‑20s on POD6, while mental status worsened. Non‑contrast CT on POD6 showed a 3.2 × 1.8 cm left parietal cortical infarct. PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive (OD 2.2). The serotonin‑release assay was atypical: strong platelet activation at 0 U/mL heparin (heparin‑independent activation, consistent with aHIT) and only partial inhibition by an Fc receptor‑blocking antibody. Heparin exposures were stopped, prophylactic fondaparinux was discontinued, and the team started argatroban (0.75 µg/kg/min targeting aPTT 55–85 s) plus high‑dose IVIG (100 g ×2 then a third 100 g). Ongoing consumptive hypercoagulability prompted a switch to therapeutic fondaparinux (10 mg once daily) with AT concentrate given for a mildly low antithrombin. Platelets recovered to 155 × 10^9/L by POD62, neurologic deficits resolved, and apixaban 5 mg BID was restarted for long‑term anticoagulation on POD65.

## Don’t write off postoperative delirium — think cortical stroke
Postoperative delirium is common after major surgery, but cortical strokes after cardiac procedures (especially embolic cortical infarcts) can present primarily as new or worsening confusion without clear focal weakness. In this case the clue that something else was wrong was the striking platelet fall plus the clinical context of prior heparin exposure. Bottom line: new or worsening delirium in the days after cardiac surgery — particularly when accompanied by an unexplained platelet drop or other hypercoagulable signals — should trigger early neuroimaging.

## When to image
If you see any of the following after cardiac surgery, get neuroimaging without delay:
- New or worsening focal neurologic signs
- New or unexplained deterioration in mental status (persistent delirium)
- Acute decline in level of consciousness
- Unexplained precipitous platelet drop or new thrombosis in a patient at risk for HIT

Start with a non‑contrast head CT to exclude haemorrhage. If ischemia is suspected and you’re within a reperfusion window or suspect large‑vessel occlusion (LVO), add CTA (and CT perfusion if available) to assess candidacy for mechanical thrombectomy.

## Why systemic thrombolysis is usually off the table in this situation
Intravenous alteplase is commonly precluded after recent major surgery and when significant thrombocytopenia is present. In this patient the platelet count was in the 20s and he had very recent major cardiac surgery — both make systemic thrombolysis a high‑risk and generally inappropriate option. Add recent heparin/LMWH/fondaparinux exposure and the picture is even less favorable. In short: pharmacologic thrombolysis was not a safe option for this patient.

## So what do you do when thrombolysis is contraindicated but HIT is causing a stroke?
The clinical imperative in HIT is to stop all heparins and start an alternative anticoagulant promptly because HIT is a highly prothrombotic state and thrombosis will often progress without anticoagulation. But with a recent large cortical infarct and severe thrombocytopenia you must balance the risk of hemorrhagic transformation against the risk of ongoing propagation and new thrombosis.

Practical approach illustrated by the case:
- Stop heparins and heparinoids. The prophylactic fondaparinux the patient had received was stopped.
- Start a short‑acting, titratable non‑heparin parenteral anticoagulant — in this case argatroban (chosen for short half‑life and ease of rapid reversal by stopping infusion). The team targeted an aPTT of 55–85 s (argatroban 0.75 µg/kg/min per local practice).
- Consider adjunctive IVIG for autoimmune HIT when there is life/limb/threatening thrombosis, very severe thrombocytopenia, or failure to improve on anticoagulation. High‑dose IVIG can rapidly block FcγR‑mediated platelet activation in aHIT. This patient received 100 g ×2 followed by a third 100 g dose, though his platelet response was blunted compared with many reported aHIT cases.
- Monitor markers of consumptive coagulopathy (platelet count, fibrinogen, d‑dimer, peripheral blood film for schistocytes) and the neurologic exam closely to watch for hemorrhagic transformation or progression of thrombotic disease.
- If anticoagulation appears inadequate (rising d‑dimer, falling fibrinogen, schistocytes), reassess and consider changing the anticoagulant. The team switched from argatroban to therapeutic fondaparinux (10 mg daily) on POD14 because of persistent hypercoagulability and for practical outpatient dosing; they gave AT concentrate beforehand because antithrombin was mildly low.

## Mechanical thrombectomy — the option when LVO is present
Mechanical thrombectomy remains the primary reperfusion therapy for anterior circulation LVOs, and it can be considered even when IV thrombolysis is contraindicated. Key points:
- If CTA reveals an LVO and the patient is within the treatment window (and otherwise a candidate), discuss MT emergently with neurointerventional colleagues — thrombectomy may be offered despite thrombocytopenia or recent surgery.
- Low platelets complicate procedural decisions. Platelet transfusion to reach a procedural threshold is sometimes considered, but in HIT this is fraught: transfused platelets can be activated by circulating HIT antibodies and may exacerbate thrombosis. Use multidisciplinary discussion — neurointervention, hematology, and anesthesia — to weigh the risks/benefits. In this case the infarct was cortical without an LVO and MT was not performed.

## Neuro‑monitoring while anticoagulating a patient with acute ischemic stroke
Treating thrombosis safely requires close neurologic surveillance. Practical measures:
- Perform frequent neurologic checks (hourly initially) and have a low threshold for repeat CT if new deficits or worsening mental status occur.
- Maintain BP targets appropriate for acute ischemic stroke while anticoagulated (individualize targets with neurology/neurosurgery).
- Keep serial labs: platelets daily until stable, fibrinogen and d‑dimer trends to gauge consumptive activity, peripheral smear for schistocytes.
- Use short‑acting anticoagulants when haemorrhagic risk is high so you can stop infusion quickly if bleeding occurs.

## Transitioning to long‑term anticoagulation
HIT patients require therapeutic non‑heparin anticoagulation until it is safe to switch to an oral strategy. Considerations from the case and common practice:
- Delay transition to oral anticoagulation until platelet recovery (this team resumed apixaban when platelets were 155 × 10^9/L and clinically stable).
- DOACs (eg, apixaban) are reasonable choices for long‑term anticoagulation after HIT once platelets have recovered and the acute phase has passed; in this case the patient was transitioned from fondaparinux to apixaban on POD65.
- Avoid initiating warfarin until platelet recovery and the patient is on a therapeutic non‑heparin anticoagulant (if warfarin must be used, it should be overlapped appropriately) because initiating warfarin alone during active HIT risks microthrombosis from rapid protein C depletion.

## Lab nuance — the aHIT phenotype matters
This case highlights the aHIT pattern: very strong PF4 ELISA positivity and an SRA showing heparin‑independent platelet activation. Important practical points:
- Don’t wait for a “clean” SRA to act if the clinical picture and ELISA strongly suggest HIT — especially when there is thrombosis. The SRA can be atypical in aHIT.
- aHIT antibodies can activate platelets without heparin and may show unusual patterns of inhibition with FcγR blockers — serology can be aggressive and confusing, but the clinical decision to treat should rest on the totality of evidence.

## Lessons learned (short clinical pearls)
- Postop delirium? Think stroke if it’s new/worsening, especially with simultaneous unexplained thrombocytopenia.
- Early‑onset HIT after cardiac surgery can reflect recent preoperative heparin exposure (eg, diagnostic cath).
- In HIT with arterial thrombosis, stop heparin and start a non‑heparin anticoagulant promptly — use a short‑acting, titratable IV agent if hemorrhage risk is high.
- IVIG is a key adjunct for autoimmune HIT with severe thrombocytopenia or life‑threatening thrombosis.
- tPA is typically contraindicated with recent major surgery and severe thrombocytopenia; mechanical thrombectomy may still be an option for LVOs after multidisciplinary discussion.
- Avoid routine platelet transfusion in HIT unless bleeding or required for an invasive procedure; weigh the thrombotic risk if transfusing.
- Transition to an oral anticoagulant (often a DOAC) only after platelet recovery and clinical stability.

Key take‑home messages
- New or worsened delirium after cardiac surgery with a rapid platelet fall should prompt urgent consideration of stroke and HIT.
- For HIT‑associated ischemic stroke, stop heparin, start a non‑heparin anticoagulant (short‑acting IV agent preferred when hemorrhage risk is high), and consider IVIG if an autoimmune phenotype is suspected.
- Systemic thrombolysis is usually contraindicated in the setting of recent major surgery and severe thrombocytopenia; consider mechanical thrombectomy for LVO after multidisciplinary discussion.
- Monitor neurologic status, platelets, fibrinogen and d‑dimer closely; transition to oral anticoagulation only after platelets recover and the acute prothrombotic phase is controlled.

This case is a vivid reminder that HIT is not just a venous problem and that autoimmune HIT can be both early and ferociously prothrombotic — keep a low threshold to image, stop heparin, and treat aggressively but carefully.
