Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Morning Report] How can preoperative exposure to unfractionated heparin during diagnostic catheterization lead to early-onset HIT after cardiac surgery, and how does this timing differ from HIT in heparin-naïve patients?

Short answer
Preoperative exposure to unfractionated heparin (UFH) can “prime” the immune system so that re‑exposure during cardiopulmonary bypass (or shortly afterward) triggers an anamnestic antibody response — producing clinically significant HIT much earlier than the classic 5–10 day window. In the case reported, a 2000‑U UFH dose at diagnostic catheterization 1 week before surgery provided the sensitizing exposure; subsequent intraoperative UFH and postoperative LMWH re‑exposure produced progressive thrombocytopenia before postoperative day (POD) 5 and an arterial stroke on POD6. 

Why this happens (pathophysiology and timing)
- Classic HIT in a truly heparin‑naïve patient is a primary IgG immune response to PF4–heparin complexes and usually requires 5–10 days for pathogenic IgG to appear and cause platelet activation (hence the usual POD5–10 timing after cardiac surgery).  
- If a patient had recent heparin exposure, memory B cells or circulating anti‑PF4/heparin antibodies may already be present (or be rapidly boosted). Re‑exposure therefore produces a much faster (hours to a few days) platelet‑activating response — so thrombocytopenia can appear within 24 hours (rapid‑onset HIT) or otherwise before POD5 (early‑onset HIT). In cardiac surgery series only ≈10% of HIT cases show onset before POD5, and that early group is strongly associated with recent preoperative heparin.  
- Autoimmune HIT (aHIT), as in this patient, adds another layer: antibodies may activate platelets even without heparin (SRA serotonin release at 0 U/mL) and be more potent, producing very early, profound thrombocytopenia and thrombotic complications.

Clinical corollary from the case
- The patient’s 2,000‑U UFH during transradial catheterization one week before surgery provided the sensitizing exposure; the large intraoperative UFH dose plus postoperative LMWH then acted as re‑exposures, provoking an anamnestic/aHIT response with platelet counts falling from 155 on POD1 to 50 by POD5 and stroke on POD6. Laboratory support (strong PF4/polyanion ELISA, SRA serotonin release even at 0 U/mL and only partial inhibition by Fc‑blockade) is typical of aggressive aHIT and explains the unusually early and severe clinical course.

Practice points
- Always ask about any heparin exposure (catheterization, dialysis, flushes, procedural heparin) in the days–weeks before surgery; recent exposure raises the pretest probability of early/rapid HIT.  
- In a patient with recent heparin exposure, do not rely on the “expected” POD5–10 rule — consider HIT early if thrombocytopenia is progressive or accompanied by thrombosis.  
- Diagnosis: PF4 ELISA (sensitive) and functional testing (SRA, recognizing that aHIT may show heparin‑independent activation). Management: stop all heparins, start a non‑heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux as appropriate), and consider IVIG for severe aHIT — as was done in this patient.

Bottom line: recent preoperative UFH can generate an anamnestic anti‑PF4 response so that re‑exposure at surgery produces HIT well before the usual POD5–10 interval; autoimmune HIT variants amplify this effect and can present with very early, severe, and heparin‑independent platelet activation.
