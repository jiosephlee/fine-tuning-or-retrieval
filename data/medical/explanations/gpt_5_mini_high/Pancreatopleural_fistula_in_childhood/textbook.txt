Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Presentation and Immediate Evaluation of a Recurrent Hemorrhagic Pleural Effusion in a Child

A child with a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) will usually present to the emergency department with what appears to be a primary thoracic problem: large, often unilateral pleural effusion, chest pain and varying degrees of dyspnea, but minimal systemic inflammatory signs. The index 24–48 hours are therefore focused on two parallel tasks — stabilizing respiratory function and identifying clues that the pleural process originates outside the chest. In the reported case the boy had progressively worse right-sided chest pain and hypoxemia (SpO2 88%–89%) that promptly improved after therapeutic thoracentesis; this pattern — dramatic symptomatic relief after drainage but rapid re-accumulation — is a typical red flag for an ongoing source of fluid production.

Targeted history in these first hours should go beyond routine pulmonary questions. Ask specifically about recent blunt abdominal trauma (handlebar injuries are classic in children), prior episodes of pancreatitis, family history of hereditary pancreatitis, preceding vomiting or severe retching (raises concern for oesophageal rupture), and constitutional features that would point toward TB or malignancy (weight loss, night sweats, prolonged cough). Because children may omit or minimise abdominal complaints (as in this case), repeat focused questioning and ask the child directly about abdominal pain or injury rather than relying only on parental history.

A focused physical exam documents the physiologic severity and localizes the effusion: asymmetric chest expansion, stony-dullness to percussion, reduced tactile fremitus and markedly decreased breath sounds over the affected hemithorax. These findings should prompt early oxygen therapy and consideration of PICU monitoring when there is hypoxemia, persistent tachypnea, altered mental status, or hemodynamic instability. In this child oxygen by mask and admission to PICU were appropriate while diagnostic steps proceeded.

Therapeutic thoracentesis serves both diagnostic and immediate therapeutic goals and is indicated when a large effusion causes respiratory compromise or pain. Bedside chest radiography confirms side and size; point-of-care lung ultrasound is particularly useful to estimate volume, detect loculations, and guide safe aspiration — in this case ultrasound estimated approximately one litre and guided the procedure. Drainage should be done under ultrasound guidance, removing enough fluid to relieve distress; in children, remove incrementally and monitor for complications (including re-expansion pulmonary edema) rather than removing an arbitrary large volume at once. Indications for a formal chest tube (intercostal drain) include recurrent rapid reaccumulation, hydropneumothorax, frank hemothorax, empyema with loculations or failure of simple aspiration — reasons that led to ICD placement in the reported child after repeated presentations.

What you send with that first pleural tap will materially affect diagnostic yield. In every exudative effusion obtain pleural fluid cell count with differential, pH, glucose, protein and LDH (Light’s criteria), Gram stain and aerobic culture, cytology, triglycerides (if chylothorax suspected), and RBC count/hematocrit (to distinguish hemothorax: fluid hematocrit ≈ blood hematocrit). Importantly, in any haemorrhagic or recurrent effusion include adenosine deaminase (ADA) when TB is a possibility, but also send amylase and lipase (and request amylase isoenzyme typing if available). A markedly raised pleural fluid amylase (classically >1,000 U/L) or very high lipase should immediately broaden the differential to pancreatic sources (PPF), oesophageal rupture or amylase-producing malignancy; in this patient pleural amylase was 6,118 U/L and lipase 7,211 U/L — results that point away from primary pulmonary disease and toward the pancreas. Send simultaneous serum amylase/lipase for comparison.

Be alert to early decision pitfalls. A mild ADA elevation and contact history alone are insufficient to start anti‑tubercular therapy without corroborating microbiological or histologic evidence (pleural fluid GeneXpert/culture, pleural biopsy where indicated). Empiric broad‑spectrum antibiotics should be reserved for clinical or laboratory evidence of bacterial infection (fever, purulent fluid, neutrophil predominance, positive Gram stain). The case illustrates how anchoring on TB exposure and ADA led to an unnecessary empiric TB course that was stopped once pancreatic etiology was demonstrated.

Certain clinical “red flags” should trigger early consideration of an extra‑thoracic source such as PPF: dark red or serosanguinous fluid, very large volumes removed (> expected for simple parapneumonic effusion), rapid re-accumulation after aspiration, and a paucity of systemic infectious signs. In such circumstances obtain cross-sectional imaging of the chest and upper abdomen (contrast-enhanced CT or MRCP when available) early to look for tracking collections or ductal disruption; the CT and MRCP in this case showed a collection extending from the pancreatic tail through the hiatus into the right pleural space and confirmed the diagnosis.

Practical clinical pearls for the first 24–48 hours: (1) stabilize airway/oxygenation and provide analgesia; (2) perform ultrasound‑guided diagnostic/therapeutic thoracentesis for large symptomatic effusions and send an extended pleural panel that always includes amylase/lipase when the fluid is haemorrhagic or recurrent; (3) ask a focused abdominal trauma and pancreatitis history early and repeat it if initial answers are unrevealing; and (4) resist premature empiric anti‑tubercular or antibiotic therapy without microbiologic support, and obtain early cross‑sectional imaging to define possible pancreatic–thoracic communication when clinical clues suggest an extra‑thoracic source. Early recognition in the ED or PICU changes the subsequent management pathway from a purely pulmonary workup to targeted pancreatic therapy (NPO/TPN, somatostatin analogue, endoscopic or surgical drainage), as occurred in this child.

## From Chest to Pancreas: Pathophysiology and Etiology of Pancreatopleural Fistula in Children

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is not a primary chest disease but a mechanical consequence of pancreatic fluid escaping its normal confines and tracking into the mediastinum and pleural space. Understanding how this occurs requires linking pancreatic anatomy and exocrine physiology to the pattern of tissue disruption and the available anatomic corridors between the retroperitoneum and the thorax. The child in this report illustrates the typical sequence: blunt pancreatic injury → ductal disruption and a retroperitoneal collection/pseudocyst → cephalad tracking through a diaphragmatic hiatus → rupture into the pleural cavity (right greater than left).

Pancreatic duct anatomy and exocrine output
The exocrine pancreas produces a large, bicarbonate-rich fluid from ductal cells and digestive enzymes (amylase, lipase, and proteolytic zymogens such as trypsinogen) from acinar cells. These enzymes normally reach the duodenum in inactive forms and are neutralized by duodenal contents. When pancreatic juice is misdirected into surrounding tissues, either as already active enzymes or activated in situ, its potent proteolytic and lipolytic activity causes local tissue breakdown, inflammation and the ability to erode soft tissues and blood vessels. The main pancreatic duct provides the pressurized pathway for this fluid; any ductal disruption or obstruction therefore creates the physical conditions for leakage.

Mechanism of fistula formation: duct disruption and pseudocyst rupture
Two proximate events create a PPF. First, disruption of the pancreatic duct—from blunt trauma, ductal stricturing in chronic pancreatitis, or rupture of a pancreatic pseudocyst—allows enzyme-rich fluid to escape the ductal system. Second, escaped fluid usually collects in the retroperitoneum because most of the pancreas lies in that compartment. A posterior ductal rupture therefore favors a retroperitoneal collection; an anterior rupture tends to spill into the peritoneal cavity and produce pancreatic ascites. Retroperitoneal collections can either become encapsulated pseudocysts or remain uncontained and then extend along the path of least resistance into adjacent compartments.

Anatomic routes from retroperitoneum to chest and the anterior/posterior distinction
Retroperitoneal fluid can ascend into the posterior mediastinum by following fascial planes and vascular sheaths and by traversing natural diaphragmatic openings (most commonly the oesophageal and aortic hiatuses). Once in the posterior mediastinum, the collection may dissect through mediastinal pleura into one or both pleural cavities, or a pseudocyst can erode directly through the diaphragm to the pleural space. Thus posterior leaks favor thoracic complications; anterior leaks favor peritoneal signs and ascites. In the present child, imaging demonstrated a multiloculated collection extending from the distal pancreas posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and then superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity—an archetypal route for PPF following posterior ductal injury.

Laterality: why left is classic but right or bilateral occurs
Because the pancreas (particularly the body and tail) lies predominantly on the left, left-sided pleural effusions are the classic presentation of PPF. However, laterality is determined by the exact site of leak and the retroperitoneal/mediastinal route taken by the fluid. Midline conduits such as the aortic hiatus or variable periesophageal fascial planes allow collections to cross or preferentially track to the right side; pre-existing anatomic variations, pressure gradients in the mediastinum, location of the pseudocyst, and points of least resistance all influence whether the pleural involvement is left, right or bilateral. In this case MRCP and CT showed a dominant right paraesophageal route with a narrow communication to the left pleural space, explaining the right-predominant but bilateral effusions.

Etiologies in children and relative frequency
In children, blunt abdominal trauma—classically “handlebar” injuries that compress the pancreas against the vertebral column—is a common trigger for ductal disruption and subsequent PPF. Hereditary or early-onset chronic pancreatitis (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC mutations) is another important pediatric cause because recurrent ductal inflammation predisposes to leaks and pseudocysts. Idiopathic cases also occur. Overall PPF is uncommon: it complicates only a small fraction of pancreatitis cases (~0.4% overall), and pediatric reports are rare (the literature cited in this case notes about 47 pediatric cases versus several hundred in adults, where alcohol-related chronic pancreatitis predominates).

Biochemical signature and the haemorrhagic appearance of fluid
Pancreatic fluid in the pleural space produces a characteristic biochemical profile: very high amylase and lipase in pleural fluid. Markedly elevated pleural amylase (commonly >1,000 U/L) and concordant lipase elevations strongly support a pancreatic source; isoenzyme typing (pancreatic AMY2 versus salivary AMY1) can distinguish pancreatic origin from other causes of amylase-rich effusions such as oesophageal rupture. The dark red or blood-stained appearance seen repeatedly in this child is also typical: activated proteases and lipases can erode pleural and mediastinal vessels and tissues, producing hemorrhagic fluid rather than a clear transudate.

Why clinical presentation is often thoracic-predominant
Because the pleural cavity is an expansile, low-resistance space, even large volumes of pancreatic fluid preferentially accumulate in the chest and cause dyspnoea, pleuritic chest pain and massive effusions. Retroperitoneal leakage may produce relatively little abdominal pain or free intraperitoneal signs, so the underlying pancreatic injury can be clinically “silent.” This explains why PPF frequently presents with a pulmonary mask and why repeated thoracic-focused workups (and repeated thoracenteses) commonly precede the diagnosis of the pancreatic source—exactly the sequence in this child.

Clinical relevance of the anatomic and pathophysiologic pattern
Recognizing these routes and causes matters for diagnosis and therapy. A history of recent blunt abdominal trauma or recurrent pancreatitis, haemorrhagic recurrent pleural effusion, and very high pleural amylase/lipase should prompt cross-sectional imaging targeted to the pancreas and an MRCP to define ductal anatomy and any pseudocyst or fistulous tract. The site of ductal disruption (and any ductal obstruction or proximal narrowing) governs whether conservative care (bowel rest, somatostatin analogues, drainage) will suffice or whether transpapillary stenting or definitive surgery will be required. In the presented child a trauma-related posterior leak with a retroperitoneal collection that tracked preferentially to the right pleural space produced the clinical picture and, once identified, directed the successful conservative treatment used.

## Diagnostic Workup and Reasoning: Confirming Pancreatic Origin and Excluding Mimics

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a diagnostic trap because it presents with prominent thoracic signs while the source is abdominal. The clinician should suspect PPF whenever a child has a recurrent or massive pleural effusion—especially if the fluid is blood-stained or serosanguinous—with little systemic febrile illness, rapid reaccumulation after thoracentesis, or a history of pancreatitis or recent blunt abdominal trauma. In the presented child the pattern was classic for a nonpulmonary cause: repeated large right-sided, dark‑red effusions that improved briefly after aspiration but recurred, and only on re‑questioning was a bicycle‑handle blunt abdominal injury elicited.

Pleural fluid analysis is the first pivot point that shifts the evaluation from thorax to pancreas. Confirm pleural exudate using Light’s criteria (pleural/serum protein ratio >0.5, pleural/serum LDH ratio >0.6, or pleural LDH >2/3 the upper limit of normal serum LDH), then include amylase and lipase in the initial panel whenever the effusion is large, hemorrhagic, or recurrent. A markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase (commonly cited cutoff ≥1,000 U/L) is strongly suggestive of a pancreatic source; concurrent very high pleural lipase further increases specificity because salivary amylase (from oesophageal sources) will not raise lipase. When available, amylase isoenzyme typing (pancreatic versus salivary) can help distinguish PPF from oesophageal rupture. Additional pleural tests that help discriminate mimics include pH (very low in empyema and oesophageal rupture), glucose (low in empyema or rheumatoid effusion), cytology (to seek malignancy), Gram stain/culture, and ADA.

A single mildly elevated ADA should not be taken as definitive evidence of tuberculous pleuritis. ADA is sensitive for TB in the appropriate clinical context, but low-to-moderate elevations also occur in empyema, malignancy, and other inflammatory effusions. In this child a mildly raised ADA and a family history of tuberculosis initially suggested TB and prompted empirical therapy; however, GeneXpert was negative and the constellation of recurrent haemorrhagic effusions plus very high pleural amylase/lipase and supportive imaging argued against TB and led to stopping antitubercular drugs. In short, ADA must be integrated with microbiology, cytology, imaging, and the clinical picture.

When pleural amylase is high, the differential diagnosis narrows but still requires careful exclusion of other causes. Esophageal perforation (Boerhaave’s syndrome) produces amylase‑rich pleural fluid of salivary origin, typically with a severe history of vomiting, acute chest pain, left‑sided hydropneumothorax and very low pleural pH. Malignancy can elevate pleural amylase and produce haemorrhagic effusions but is usually accompanied by systemic features, positive cytology or abnormal peripheral blood findings. Empyema yields purulent fluid, low pH and glucose, and positive cultures. Hepatic hydrothorax, diaphragmatic hernia, and renal causes have distinguishing clinical and imaging features and metabolic tests that exclude a pancreatic source. Using amylase plus lipase results, pH, cytology and microbiology narrows the field before committing to abdominal imaging or invasive procedures.

Imaging is the next key step, in an ordered sequence. A chest radiograph and bedside ultrasound quantify the effusion and guide safe drainage. Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest and abdomen is useful to identify pancreatitis, pseudocysts, and tracking collections; CT will demonstrate associated lung changes and may show a retroperitoneal collection extending through the diaphragmatic hiatus, but it visualizes the actual fistulous tract in only about one‑third to one‑half of cases. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is the preferred noninvasive study to delineate ductal anatomy and to demonstrate fistulous tracts and duct‑to‑pleura communications, with reported sensitivity around 70–80%. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is the gold standard for ductal assessment and therapeutic planning (and offers transpapillary stenting), with fistula visualization rates in reported pediatric series ranging roughly 38–78%. Practically, MRCP is a high‑yield next test when pleural fluid enzymes point to a pancreatic cause; reserve ERCP for when MRCP is inconclusive or when an endoscopic intervention is being considered.

Applied to this child’s course, the diagnostic reasoning followed these steps: initial chest X‑ray/ultrasound identified large right pleural effusions and guided thoracenteses that yielded dark red fluid. Recurrent reaccumulation despite repeat drainage raised concern for an extrathoracic source. Pleural fluid testing showed very high amylase (6,118 U/L) and lipase (7,211 U/L) and the serum pancreatic enzymes were also elevated, making a pancreatic origin the leading diagnosis. Contrast CT documented a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the retroperitoneum/left upper abdomen into the right paraesophageal pleural space, which suggested a communicating tract. MRCP then confirmed a communication between the distal pancreatic duct/pseudocyst and the right pleural cavity (with a smaller left communication), and showed ductal narrowing proximal to that communication—together definitive for pancreatopleural fistula. These steps allowed the team to stop empirical anti‑tubercular therapy and to direct management at the pancreatic source.

Key practical lessons for trainees are: think pancreas early in recurrent, hemorrhagic, rapidly reaccumulating pleural effusions; order pleural amylase and lipase alongside routine pleural studies; treat mild ADA elevations cautiously and correlate with microbiology and imaging; use chest radiograph/ultrasound for immediate management but obtain cross‑sectional abdominal imaging (CT, then MRCP) to define the ductal anatomy; and involve gastroenterology/ERCP early when ductal therapy may be required. Early recognition prevents prolonged pulmonary workups and unnecessary therapies.

## Management Strategy: Conservative First, Then Endoscopic or Surgical Escalation

Management of a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) in a child is typically stepwise: attempt a carefully supervised conservative trial for appropriately selected patients, and escalate to endoscopic or surgical interventions if the fistula or ductal disruption does not seal. The purpose of the initial conservative approach is to suppress pancreatic exocrine secretion, maintain nutrition, and provide reliable pleural drainage so the fistulous tract can close without immediate invasive repair. The case reported here is a clear illustration: the boy was hemodynamically stable, had no evidence of uncontrolled sepsis, received adequate continuous pleural drainage (an intercostal drain), and was managed with NPO/total parenteral nutrition (TPN), octreotide (30 mcg/kg/day), and pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT); the leak stopped and the chest tube was removed after ten days.

Indications for attempting conservative therapy are clinical stability (no shock or ongoing uncontrolled infection), absence of evidence on imaging for a complete transection or anatomic situation unlikely to close (for example, a large unbridgeable ductal disruption or an infected, enlarging pseudocyst), and ability to provide reliable drainage and close monitoring. When those conditions are met, conservative care is the least invasive and often the appropriate first step in children because many procedures carry greater relative risk in the pediatric population. In the present case MRCP showed a fistulous communication but no requirement for immediate operative drainage or clear-cut high-grade loss of ductal continuity, which supported a conservative first-line plan.

The core components of the conservative regimen are bowel rest (NPO), nutritional support usually via TPN to eliminate enteral stimulation of pancreatic secretion, pharmacologic suppression of exocrine output with somatostatin analogues, adequate and continuous pleural drainage, pain control, and targeted enzyme replacement when indicated. Octreotide is commonly used at 10–40 mcg/kg/day (the case used 30 mcg/kg/day); it may be given as a continuous infusion or by intermittent subcutaneous dosing as determined by local practice and patient factors. PERT was started in this child to support digestion and nutrition while the pancreas rested; dosing and duration should be individualized with pediatric gastroenterology input. For pleural management, a chest tube (intercostal drain) is preferred over repeated large-volume thoracocenteses when effusions are large or recurrent, because continuous drainage reduces respiratory compromise and provides a measurable marker of response; in this case repeated taps led to early recurrences until an ICD was placed and continuous drainage facilitated resolution.

Response to conservative therapy must be closely monitored. Daily measurement of chest-tube output is the most practical indicator of whether the fistula is closing; a progressive fall in drain volume usually precedes imaging confirmation. Serial trends in pleural and serum amylase/lipase and routine clinical parameters — respiratory status and oxygen requirement, fever curve and white cell count, and nutritional markers (weight, albumin/prealbumin, glucose and electrolyte balance while on TPN) — should be followed. If drainage does not fall or if clinical deterioration occurs, interval imaging with MRCP or CT (or planning for diagnostic ERCP) is indicated to reassess ductal anatomy and to look for complications such as enlarging collections or ongoing ductal disruption.

Expected timeline and outcomes with conservative care are variable. A pragmatic trial of 2–3 weeks is customary in the absence of red flags, because many fistulae will either close or declare themselves within this period. Published pediatric series report relatively low overall success for conservative measures (around 12%), so clinicians should be vigilant and ready to escalate. That said, individual patients may respond much sooner — as in this child, whose drainage declined to zero over about ten days — underscoring the importance of individualized assessment rather than rigid timelines.

Endoscopic therapy (ERCP) is the logical next step when conservative measures fail or when imaging shows ductal stenosis or a disruption that is amenable to transpapillary therapy. ERCP serves both diagnostic and therapeutic roles: it can localize a leak and permit transpapillary stent placement to bridge disruptions or to decompress the duct and lower intraductal pressure, which facilitates fistula closure. In mixed-age cohorts endoscopic stenting leads to fistula closure in roughly half of patients within 2–3 weeks; pediatric reports show high technical success of ERCP (near 100% in some series) and long-term success >90% when performed by experienced pediatric endoscopists. Stents are typically left in place for weeks to months (commonly 4 weeks to 6 months) depending on healing. ERCP requires pediatric endoscopic expertise and carries procedure-related risks (post-ERCP pancreatitis, infection, perforation), so selection and timing should be multidisciplinary.

Surgical repair is reserved for children who fail both conservative and endoscopic management, who have inaccessible or irreparable distal ductal disruption, or who develop complications that require operative drainage or resection (for example, a large symptomatic pseudocyst or persistent sepsis related to the pancreatic source). Procedures include distal pancreatectomy (with consideration of splenic preservation in children), pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow) for long-segment ductal dilatation, and cystojejunostomy for symptomatic pseudocysts communicating with the ductal system. Published pediatric series report high success rates for surgery (≈93% in small series), operative mortality under 5%, and recurrence on the order of ~11%; nevertheless, surgery entails lifelong considerations about pancreatic endocrine and exocrine function, so parenchyma-sparing options are preferred when feasible.

Finally, several supportive considerations influence outcomes. Antibiotics should be targeted and reserved for proven or strongly suspected infected collections rather than given routinely; in the reported case, broad-spectrum antibiotics were started empirically but the definitive therapy focused on pancreatic-directed care once the diagnosis was confirmed. TPN requires vigilant glycemic and electrolyte monitoring and planning for transition back to enteral nutrition when safe. Long-term follow-up should include assessment for exocrine insufficiency and diabetes, and counselling on lifestyle and nutrition when pancreatitis is related to trauma or underlying predisposition. Early multidisciplinary involvement — pediatric critical care, gastroenterology/interventional endoscopy, radiology, surgery and nutrition — and access to pediatric ERCP expertise are critical to choose the right time to escalate.

In summary, conservative management is an appropriate first step for a hemodynamically stable child with PPF and no uncontrolled infection or clear high-grade ductal disruption; it combines NPO/TPN, somatostatin analogues (eg, octreotide 10–40 mcg/kg/day), PERT when indicated, and reliable chest-tube drainage. A focused trial of roughly 2–3 weeks is reasonable, with earlier escalation to ERCP or surgery for persistent high output, imaging evidence of an unbridgeable ductal defect, or clinical deterioration. The present case exemplifies successful conservative treatment when these selection principles were applied.

## Outcomes, Follow-up, and Practice Lessons

Outcome in this child was favorable. After the diagnosis of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) was established by MRCP and pleural fluid enzyme analysis, the team treated the patient conservatively with pleural drainage, bowel rest (NPO) with total parenteral nutrition, octreotide infusion and pancreatic enzyme replacement. Pleural output decreased steadily over about 10 days, the intercostal drain was removed when drainage stopped, and the child was discharged. On monthly clinic reviews he remained symptom-free, returned to full school and play activities, and adopted the advised dietary and exercise changes.

Follow-up priorities should be pragmatic and symptom-driven. Early post-discharge reviews should concentrate on respiratory symptoms (recurrence of dyspnea, chest pain, cough), new or worsening abdominal pain, fever suggestive of infection, and nutritional status (weight, growth parameters, signs of malabsorption). Routine monitoring of serum amylase/lipase is not necessary in an asymptomatic patient but is appropriate if symptoms recur. Imaging should be reserved for clinical suspicion of recurrence: a chest radiograph can quickly detect recurrent effusion, and MRCP is the preferred non‑invasive study to reassess pancreatic duct anatomy and to look for persistent fistula or pseudocyst if symptoms return. Nutritional rehabilitation and weight management (dietetic input, gradual reintroduction of oral intake with PERT as indicated) are important components of follow-up—particularly in a child with prior high-calorie intake and obesity—because ongoing pancreatic dysfunction or prolonged catabolic state can impair recovery.

Screening for an underlying predisposition is indicated when pancreatitis or fistula recurs or when imaging shows chronic ductal changes. In that setting, refer to pediatric gastroenterology for evaluation and consideration of genetic testing for hereditary pancreatitis (PRSS1, SPINK1), cystic fibrosis transmembrane regulator (CFTR) mutations, and CTRC variants, and for assessment of metabolic triggers such as hypertriglyceridemia or hypercalcemia. A careful family history and targeted laboratory testing should precede genetic testing; in isolated, clearly trauma-related first episodes (as in this case) routine genetic testing is not required unless events recur.

Prognosis after timely recognition and tiered management is generally good. Conservative treatment (drainage, bowel rest/TPN, somatostatin analogues, PERT) can close fistulae, and escalation to ERCP with transpapillary stenting or to surgery is successful when ductal disruption or obstruction persists. The risk of recurrence is higher when underlying ductal abnormalities, persistent pseudocysts, or chronic pancreatitis are not addressed; long-term sequelae of recurrent or chronic pancreatic injury can include exocrine insufficiency and, less commonly, endocrine dysfunction.

Be vigilant for complications and know when to escalate care. Complications to watch for include secondary infection of the pleural collection (empyema), persistent external pancreatic fluid losses leading to malnutrition, catheter-related problems (infection, displacement), and systemic sepsis. Development of increasing or purulent drainage, fever, rising inflammatory markers, failure to gain weight, or persistent high-volume chest drainage are signals to obtain repeat imaging and involve gastroenterology/endoscopy teams for consideration of ERCP or surgery.

Practice lessons (high‑yield takeaways)
- In any unexplained, recurrent, large or blood‑stained pleural effusion, send pleural fluid amylase and lipase early; pleural amylase markedly above the normal range (classically ≥1,000 U/L) strongly suggests a pancreatic source. Consider amylase isoenzyme typing when differentiation from salivary‑type causes is needed.
- Re-interviewing the child and family is valuable: occult blunt abdominal trauma is a common and easily missed trigger in pediatrics. A single misleading detail (for example a TB contact and mildly raised pleural ADA in this case) can divert the workup unless the pancreatic possibility is actively considered.
- Use MRCP early when PPF is suspected for noninvasive mapping of the fistula and ductal anatomy; ERCP remains the therapeutic tool when stenting is required.
- Follow a stepwise management algorithm: treat respiratory compromise and drain effusion; attempt conservative measures (NPO/TPN, somatostatin analogue, PERT) while reassessing; escalate to endoscopic or surgical interventions if the fistula or ductal leak persists or if ductal obstruction/pseudocyst anatomy indicates intervention.
- Avoid prolonged or invasive thoracic therapies (for example prolonged empirical anti‑tubercular therapy or thoracic surgery) without excluding a pancreatic source when clinical clues (recurrent hemorrhagic effusion, very high pleural amylase, history of abdominal trauma) point toward PPF.

Keeping these priorities in mind should reduce delays in diagnosis, limit unnecessary pulmonary interventions, and improve outcomes for children with this rare but treatable condition.
