Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Clinical Pearls] In children with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis who develop pancreatopleural fistula, which genetic variants are most commonly implicated, and when should genetic testing be pursued?

Short answer
- The commonest genetic contributors in children with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis (and therefore at risk for complications such as pancreatopleural fistula) are PRSS1 and SPINK1 mutations. CFTR and CTRC variants are also important contributors in the pediatric population.  
- Genetic testing should be pursued with a low threshold in any child who has recurrent acute pancreatitis (≥2 discrete episodes), established chronic pancreatitis, idiopathic pancreatitis, or a positive family history — and often before planning definitive endoscopic or surgical therapy. Even when a clear trigger such as blunt trauma is present (as in this case), testing is reasonable because trauma can unmask an underlying genetic susceptibility.

Why this matters (pathophysiology and clinical reasoning)
- Pediatric chronic pancreatitis is often genetic: the case authors note PRSS1 and SPINK1 “feature prominently, accounting for up to 30% of paediatric chronic pancreatitis.” PRSS1 gain‑of‑function mutations (classical examples include R122H and N29I) promote intrapancreatic trypsin activation and ductal injury; SPINK1 loss‑of‑function reduces the pancreatic trypsin inhibitor and lowers the threshold for autodigestion. CFTR defects impair ductal bicarbonate secretion and promote obstruction/stasis; CTRC defects reduce physiologic degradation of trypsinogen. Recurrent ductal injury and pseudocyst formation or duct disruption are the mechanistic steps that predispose to fistula formation into the mediastinum/pleura.
- In the presented child the immediate trigger was blunt abdominal trauma, but the authors explicitly caution that idiopathic or recurrent disease in children often reflects an underlying genetic or ductal predisposition — therefore testing is clinically informative.

Practical testing approach
- Order a pancreatitis gene panel (next‑generation sequencing) that at minimum includes PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR and CTRC (many panels also include CPA1, CTRB1/2 and copy‑number analysis). If CFTR variants are suspected or identified, correlate with sweat chloride and clinical evaluation for cystic fibrosis/CFTR‑related disorder. If a pathogenic familial variant is known, do targeted testing in relatives.
- Timing: testing can be done once the child is stable — results do not change acute PPF management (drainage, bowel rest, octreotide, ERCP/surgery as needed) but they do affect prognosis, surveillance, family counseling and decisions about definitive interventions.

Clinical pearl
- In children with recurrent/ chronic pancreatitis (or an unexplained severe pancreatic complication such as pancreatopleural fistula), think genetics early: PRSS1 and SPINK1 are the most common culprits, and identification of a pathogenic variant changes long‑term care and family counseling even if an acute trigger like trauma was apparent.
