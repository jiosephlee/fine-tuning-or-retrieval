Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Pathophysiology] Explain how a posterior pancreatic duct disruption or pseudocyst after blunt abdominal trauma can lead to a right-dominant pleural effusion, including the anatomic route through the retroperitoneum and diaphragmatic hiatus.

Short answer
A posterior pancreatic-duct disruption or leaking pseudocyst dumps enzyme‑rich fluid into the retroperitoneum; the enzymes dissect along retroperitoneal fascial planes and ascend through natural diaphragmatic openings (most commonly the esophageal or aortic hiatus) into the posterior mediastinum and then into a pleural space. Which hemithorax fills depends on the exact retroperitoneal track and where the collection enters the mediastinum — in this child the MRCP/CT showed a tract to the right para‑esophageal region explaining the right‑dominant pleural effusion.

Pathophysiology and anatomic route (stepwise)
- Site of leak: a posterior disruption of the pancreatic duct or rupture of a pseudocyst in the distal body/tail allows pancreatic juice to escape posterior to the peritoneum into the retroperitoneal/peripancreatic tissues rather than into the peritoneal cavity (anterior ruptures cause ascites).
- Dissection along planes: activated proteolytic enzymes (trypsin, elastase) digest tissue and create a low‑resistance track that follows perivascular and paravertebral fascial planes upward (periaortic, paraesophageal, posterior mediastinal spaces).
- Diaphragmatic passage: the retroperitoneum is continuous with the posterior mediastinum through the aortic and esophageal hiatuses (and less commonly by direct transdiaphragmatic erosion). Fluid ascends through one of these hiatuses into the mediastinum.
- Entry into pleural space: once in the posterior mediastinum the collection may breach the mediastinal/parietal pleura or follow pleural reflections into one hemithorax producing a large pleural effusion. Proteolysis of small vessels accounts for the blood‑stained (dark red) fluid and for high pleural amylase/lipase.

Why right‑dominant in this case
- The sidedness is determined by the precise anatomic route the fluid follows and local anatomic variation, not strictly by “left pancreatic location.” In this patient CT and MRCP explicitly showed a multiloculated collection tracking from the distal body/tail posteriorly into the right paraesophageal region and then superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with only a narrow communication to the left. That anatomic disposition explains the recurrent, large right‑sided haemorrhagic effusions.

Clinical correlation and teaching points
- Recurrent, massive unilateral pleural effusion that is blood‑stained and has very high pleural amylase/lipase should prompt abdominal imaging (CT/MRCP) and consideration of pancreatopleural fistula. In this case pleural fluid amylase 6,118 U/L and lipase 7,211 U/L and MRCP demonstrated the transhiatal track.
- Management follows the pathophysiology: drain the chest, reduce pancreatic output (NPO/TPN, somatostatin analogue), and consider ERCP/stent or surgery if the fistula or ductal disruption does not seal.

Clinical pearl
Always ask about abdominal trauma/pancreatitis and send pleural fluid amylase when you see recurrent, large, unilateral haemorrhagic effusions—retroperitoneal tracking through the esophageal or aortic hiatus can produce a “pulmonary mask” for a pancreatic problem, and the side of the effusion reflects the exact retroperitoneal-to-mediastinal tract, as in this child’s right‑predominant fistula.
