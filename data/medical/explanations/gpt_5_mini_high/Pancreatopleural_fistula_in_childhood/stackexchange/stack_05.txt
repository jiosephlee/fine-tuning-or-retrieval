Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Morning Report] How should a mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA be interpreted in a child with hemorrhagic, culture-negative, recurrent pleural effusion, and which additional tests should be obtained before initiating antitubercular therapy?

Bottom line: a mildly raised pleural ADA in isolation should not be taken as proof of tuberculous pleuritis in a child with a recurrent, blood‑stained, culture‑negative effusion. ADA is most useful when markedly elevated in the right clinical picture (subacute, lymphocyte‑predominant exudate); borderline elevations, hemorrhagic fluid, rapid re‑accumulation, or atypical clinical features should prompt a search for alternative diagnoses (as in this case — pancreatopleural fistula), not reflexive initiation of antitubercular therapy.

Why: ADA is produced by activated T‑lymphocytes and macrophages and is sensitive for TB pleuritis at commonly used cutoffs (≈≥40 U/L), but it is not specific. False‑positive or borderline elevations occur with empyema/parapneumonic effusions, rheumatoid pleurisy, some malignancies, hemothorax or blood contamination, and in other inflammatory states. GeneXpert on pleural fluid has limited sensitivity because pleural TB is pauci‑bacillary, so a negative test does not prove absence of TB — but neither does a mildly elevated ADA prove it.

Apply to the presented child: the effusion was repeatedly dark red, recurred rapidly after aspiration, GeneXpert was negative, and there were no systemic TB features. Those features are atypical for classic tuberculous pleural effusion and should have triggered measurement of pleural amylase/lipase and cross‑sectional imaging earlier. In this report pleural amylase (6118 U/L) and MRCP established a pancreatopleural fistula and antitubercular therapy was appropriately stopped.

What to obtain before committing to full antitubercular therapy
(perform these tests promptly when ADA is only mildly/highly equivocal or the clinical picture is atypical):

- Pleural fluid amylase and lipase (and, if available, amylase isoenzyme typing). A very high pleural amylase (classically ≥1,000 U/L) strongly points to a pancreatopleural fistula or oesophageal rupture rather than TB.

- Pleural fluid cell count with differential (TB: lymphocyte‑predominant; neutrophil‑predominant points to parapneumonic/early inflammatory or pancreatic fluid), total protein and LDH (to classify exudate).

- Routine microbiology: Gram stain and bacterial culture; AFB smear and mycobacterial culture on pleural fluid and, if available, pleural tissue; GeneXpert/NAAT on pleural fluid (useful if positive, but low sensitivity so a negative does not exclude TB).

- Pleural fluid cytology (to exclude malignancy) and biochemical testing as above.

- Pleural interferon‑gamma or ADA isoenzyme (ADA2) testing if available — both increase specificity for tuberculous pleuritis compared with total ADA alone.

- Targeted imaging: chest and abdominal contrast CT and MRCP if pancreatopleural fistula is suspected (history of trauma, large unilateral haemorrhagic effusion, or high amylase). MRCP/CT identified the fistula in this case.

- If TB remains a significant concern after the above noninvasive tests: obtain pleural biopsy (closed Abrams or, preferably, medical thoracoscopy) for histology (caseating granulomas) and culture — this remains the diagnostic gold standard and should usually be done before prolonged empirical ATT when the diagnosis is uncertain.

Practical rule: in a hemorrhagic, rapidly recurrent effusion, always measure pleural amylase and get cross‑sectional imaging before starting TB therapy; reserve empirical long‑course ATT for cases with convincing supportive features or after pleural biopsy/IFN‑γ confirms tuberculosis.
