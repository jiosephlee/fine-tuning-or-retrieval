Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Clinical Pearls] Which bedside and historical features should prompt early consideration of an abdominal source—specifically pancreatopleural fistula—in a child with recurrent unilateral pleural effusions despite appropriate pulmonary evaluation?

Short answer
Suspect a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) early when a child has recurrent, large unilateral pleural effusions that recur rapidly despite appropriate pulmonary evaluation — especially when the effusion is blood‑stained, the child has an antecedent abdominal event (trauma or pancreatitis risk), systemic/infectious signs are absent or minimal, and pleural fluid or serum pancreatic enzymes are markedly elevated.

Why this matters (pathophysiologic rationale)
A disrupted pancreatic duct or ruptured pseudocyst in the retroperitoneum can track pancreatic fluid upward through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus into the mediastinum and pleural space. The fluid is enzyme‑rich and inflammatory, producing large exudative (often haemorrhagic) pleural effusions that present with a “pulmonary mask” while the primary pathology is abdominal.

Bedside and historical features that should raise early suspicion (and why)
- Recurrent large unilateral effusions with rapid reaccumulation after thoracentesis or temporary clinical improvement (suggests a persistent extrathoracic source of fluid). In the case, ≈1 L was removed on multiple occasions with quick recurrence.
- Pleural fluid that is haemorrhagic/dark red or grossly abnormal (pancreatic leakage often produces blood‑stained effusions).
- Effusion that is exudative but lacks convincing infectious features (no high fever, no neutrophilic empyema, negative cultures) or malignant features (negative cytology) despite repeated pulmonary workup.
- Minimal or absent abdominal findings or classical pancreatitis symptoms — in children PPF often presents predominantly with chest symptoms, so a normal abdominal exam does not exclude pancreatic origin (this child’s abdomen was soft and non‑tender).
- Antecedent abdominal trauma, pancreatitis, or pancreatitis risk factors — in the case a bicycle‑handle blunt abdominal injury 1 month earlier was the clue that emerged only after repeat history taking.
- Rapid improvement after drainage but recurrence despite antibiotics or empiric pulmonary treatments (suggests non‑pulmonary cause).
- Any pleural fluid with very high amylase/lipase (send these early). Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is characteristic of PPF; in the case pleural amylase was 6,118 U/L and lipase 7,211 U/L.

Immediate diagnostic steps when you suspect PPF
- Send pleural fluid for amylase and lipase (and type if available — pancreatic vs salivary) and obtain serum amylase/lipase.
- Cross‑sectional imaging: contrast CT chest/abdomen to look for retroperitoneal/pseudocyst collections tracking to the chest; MRCP to define the fistulous tract and duct anatomy (MRCP sensitivity ~70–80% as in this report). ERCP is the invasive gold standard for visualization and therapeutic planning.
- Avoid premature anchoring on tuberculosis or malignancy if pleural amylase is markedly elevated — ADA can be misleadingly elevated, as in this child.

Management principle (brief)
- If confirmed or strongly suspected, coordinate early GI/HPB input: initial conservative therapy (NPO, TPN, octreotide, drainage) is often attempted; if it fails, ERCP‑guided transpapillary stenting or surgery may be required. In the case, conservative measures plus ICD drainage led to resolution over 10 days.

Takeaway (clinical pearl)
In any child with recurrent unilateral, large, rapidly reaccumulating pleural effusions — particularly when the fluid is blood‑stained and pulmonary workup is unrevealing — always ask explicitly about recent abdominal trauma or pancreatitis and send pleural fluid amylase/lipase early. In this case, that combination (recurrent haemorrhagic effusions, prior blunt abdominal trauma, very high pleural amylase, and MRCP demonstration of a tract to the pancreatic tail) made the diagnosis.
