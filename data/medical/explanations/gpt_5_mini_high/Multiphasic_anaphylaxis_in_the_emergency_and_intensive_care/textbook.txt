Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Initial Presentation and Emergency Stabilization

This patient arrived with a history and examination that rapidly pointed to an acute systemic allergic reaction. Her background is clinically relevant: she had remote, limited allergic reactions (facial swelling to amoxicillin in adolescence and a local wasp-sting reaction) but no prior anaphylaxis. Important contemporaneous features that influenced management were her weight (104 kg), diabetes and hypertension, and current medications (metformin, gliclazide, hydrochlorothiazide, omeprazole, sertraline). These comorbidities do not exclude anaphylaxis and, in practical terms, a large body weight increases the epinephrine dose required to reach the usual adult maximum; conversely, she was not on beta‑blockers (which can blunt epinephrine response), an important negative to note.

The exposure timeline supports a drug-triggered immediate reaction. Five days earlier she had been treated with clindamycin and developed a delayed rash, after which her GP switched her to clarithromycin. She took the first 500 mg dose of clarithromycin and developed pruritus, flushing, sweating, agitation and a sensation of throat swelling within one hour; she presented to the ED about one hour after symptom onset. The rapid onset after ingestion makes an immediate hypersensitivity reaction the most likely clinical explanation.

On arrival she had objective multisystem involvement: diffuse erythema and generalized urticaria, facial swelling, marked hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxaemia (SpO2 88% on room air) and tachypnea (RR 22). She reported throat fullness but had no visible tongue or oropharyngeal edema and had normal chest and cardiac auscultation. This pattern meets standard clinical diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis: acute onset with skin–mucosal involvement plus respiratory compromise and/or hypotension, and it also meets the alternate criterion of two or more organ systems involved after exposure to a likely allergen. The temporal relationship to clarithromycin further supports the diagnosis.

Pathophysiologically this presentation reflects sudden mast cell and basophil mediator release (histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, prostaglandins and others) producing vasodilation, increased capillary permeability (causing intravascular volume loss and hypotension), bronchospasm and mucosal edema (causing hypoxaemia and airway symptoms), and cutaneous manifestations. Drug reactions may be IgE‑mediated or via non‑IgE direct mast cell activation; the clinical urgency and organ-system compromise are managed the same way in either case.

Management priorities were airway, breathing and circulation. The first-line and time-critical treatment for anaphylaxis is intramuscular epinephrine: in this patient an auto‑injector delivering 0.3 mg intramuscularly to the anterolateral thigh was used immediately. IM epinephrine provides alpha‑adrenergic vasoconstriction (raising peripheral resistance and blood pressure), beta‑1 inotropy/chronotropy (supporting cardiac output) and beta‑2 bronchodilation plus suppression of further mediator release. Recommended dosing is 0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg in adults; in practice many adult patients will require up to 0.5 mg. In emergent situations an auto‑injector shortens treatment delays and reduces hesitancy, although the 0.3 mg device used here is smaller than the 0.5 mg dose frequently required for larger adults.

Supportive measures performed in the ED followed standard priorities: high‑flow oxygen via a non‑rebreather mask (15 L/min) for hypoxaemia, and an initial bolus of intravenous crystalloid (500 mL 0.9% saline) to address the intravascular volume deficit. Antihistamines (clemastine 2 mg IV slow) were given to control cutaneous symptoms; these are adjunctive and do not replace epinephrine. No airway intervention or intravenous epinephrine infusion was required at that time because there was no stridor, progressive airway compromise or refractory hemodynamic collapse. The suspect drug (clarithromycin) was stopped and no further doses were given.

Clinical indicators of immediate response were clear: blood pressure returned toward normal, oxygenation improved sufficiently that supplemental oxygen could be discontinued, and facial swelling and generalized erythema diminished. These signs indicate effective reversal of the acute mediator effects, but they do not eliminate the risk of recurrence. For that reason, and because the initial presentation involved profound hypotension and hypoxaemia, admission to a monitored setting was appropriate. Patients with severe anaphylaxis should be observed where rapid re-administration of epinephrine, airway management and hemodynamic support are immediately available; in this case she was first admitted to an acute internal ward with increased nursing observation and later required ICU care after relapse. Finally, although serum tryptase measurement is a useful diagnostic adjunct if obtained early (ideally within 1–4 hours of onset), no tryptase was drawn during the initial ED episode in this patient, which limited biochemical confirmation of mast cell activation at that time.

## Multiphasic Anaphylaxis—Definitions, Mechanisms, and Risk Stratification

Anaphylaxis can follow several temporal patterns that have different implications for monitoring and treatment. Biphasic anaphylaxis is defined as recurrence of objective anaphylactic symptoms after an initial clinical improvement or complete resolution, occurring without further exposure to the offending agent. Multiphasic anaphylaxis is a related but less common pattern in which there are two or more discrete relapses separated by asymptomatic intervals. These contrast with refractory or protracted (sustained) anaphylaxis: refractory anaphylaxis denotes persistent or progressive circulatory or respiratory compromise that does not respond adequately to appropriate doses of epinephrine, whereas protracted or sustained reactions represent a continuous, long-lasting anaphylactic state—often because the allergen continues to be present or is slowly released—rather than true relapses separated by symptom-free periods. Distinguishing these entities matters because biphasic/multiphasic reactions imply intermittent re-emergence of mediator-driven pathology (and therefore the need for renewed rescue therapy), whereas sustained or refractory reactions usually require continuous hemodynamic support and different escalation strategies (for example, continuous IV vasopressor or epinephrine infusion).

Several pathophysiological mechanisms have been proposed to explain relapse after an initial resolution. The immediate phase of anaphylaxis is driven by rapid mast cell and basophil degranulation with release of preformed mediators (histamine, tryptase, trypsin-like proteases) that cause vasodilation, increased vascular permeability and bronchospasm. A later phase can follow hours afterwards and is mediated by newly synthesized substances (leukotrienes, prostaglandins, platelet-activating factor and various cytokines) and by recruitment of inflammatory cells; this “late-phase” response can produce a second wave of symptoms. Non-IgE pathways (direct mast cell activation by drugs or complement-derived anaphylatoxins) and abnormalities in mediator metabolism (for example, low activity of enzymes that inactivate platelet-activating factor) have also been implicated in more severe or recurrent courses. Finally, increased mast cell burden or hyperreactivity—as occurs in systemic mastocytosis—lowers the threshold for mediator release and predisposes to severe and recurrent episodes; elevated baseline tryptase and organomegaly in this patient point toward that mechanism as a likely contributor.

Ongoing presence of antigen can mimic or produce a persistent reaction rather than true biphasic relapse. Oral or depot formulations, continued ingestion, or slow release of an injected or implanted substance can maintain antigenic stimulation long enough to cause repeated or sustained mediator release. In the current case the trigger was a single oral dose of clarithromycin; while oral drugs can be absorbed over hours and, in theory, produce prolonged antigen exposure, the clinical pattern here—clear improvement followed by discrete relapses—favours a multiphasic biological process (late-phase mediators or renewed mast cell activation) over uninterrupted antigen exposure. In addition, incomplete initial pharmacologic reversal of mediator effects (for example, underdosing of epinephrine relative to body weight) can allow symptoms to recur when the effect of the epinephrine wanes.

Reported incidence and timing of biphasic reactions vary widely in the literature (0.4%–23%), but most studies cluster around 4–6%. Most relapses occur within the first 24 hours and a substantial proportion within 6–12 hours; late recurrences up to several days have been reported but are uncommon. Multiphasic courses with multiple distinct relapses are rare. In this patient the four anaphylactic episodes (the index event plus three relapses) all occurred within roughly 12 hours and showed progressively less severe physiologic disturbance with each recurrence. That temporal profile is compatible with an early multiphasic course driven by successive waves of mediator activity or a high mast cell reactivity state rather than with refractory continuous shock.

Several clinical features have been associated with higher risk of biphasic or multiphasic courses and therefore inform decisions about monitoring intensity. Predictors suggested in observational studies include a severe initial presentation (marked hypotension, hypoxaemia, shock), the need for multiple doses of epinephrine, delayed administration of epinephrine after symptom onset, comorbid airway or cardiovascular disease, and underlying mast cell disease. Medications that blunt adrenergic responses (notably beta‑blockers) and older age have also been implicated in poorer response to therapy. Applying these to the case: the patient had an objectively severe index event (systolic blood pressure 74 mm Hg, peripheral hypoxaemia), required repeated epinephrine administrations, and was later shown to have persistently elevated tryptase with features suggesting systemic mast cell disease—three established risk elements for recurrence. Although epinephrine was given promptly on ED arrival, the initial intramuscular dose used (0.3 mg) is lower than the commonly recommended 0.5 mg dose for many adults and thus may have been relatively inadequate for a patient weighing 104 kg; inadequate early dosing can contribute to the need for further doses and to relapse.

These considerations determine observation strategy. For patients with mild, clearly resolved anaphylaxis and no risk factors, short observation (often 4–6 hours) may be reasonable in many settings. Patients with severe initial features, need for multiple epinephrine doses, suspected mast cell disorders, or any recurrence should be observed for a longer interval—often at least 24 hours—and monitored more intensively (continuous pulse oximetry, frequent vital signs, IV access, and readiness to give epinephrine). Escalation to ICU-level care is appropriate when there has been recurrent hemodynamic instability, respiratory compromise, the need for repeated boluses or consideration of continuous vasoactive infusions, or when close observation and rapid intervention are otherwise not guaranteed. In this case the decision to admit to intensive care after the first relapse was consistent with these principles: the patient had severe initial shock, early relapse, and required repeated epinephrine administrations, all of which increased the risk of further recurrence and decompensation and justified continuous monitoring and immediate availability of resuscitative therapy.

In summary, biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis reflect re-emergence of mediator-driven pathology after apparent improvement and must be differentiated from refractory or sustained reactions caused by ongoing antigen exposure. The most plausible mechanisms are late-phase mediator release, renewed mast cell activation (amplified in mast cell disorders), and, less commonly, prolonged antigen absorption. Severity at presentation, need for multiple epinephrine doses, and underlying mast cell disease are the key risk features that should trigger prolonged, monitored observation and lower the threshold for ICU-level care—points that directly explain and justify the management choices made in this patient.

## Diagnostic Workup and Reasoning: Tryptase, Culprit Identification, and Mast Cell Disease

The immediate diagnostic goals after a complex anaphylactic episode are threefold: document mast cell activation when possible, identify and document the most likely culprit to prevent re‑exposure, and decide whether the clinical picture warrants investigation for a predisposing mast cell disorder. Tryptase measurement is central to the first and third aims because it is a relatively specific marker of mast cell degranulation and, when persistently elevated, a marker of increased mast cell burden.

Timing and interpretation of tryptase. Tryptase is released from mast cells and typically peaks about 1–2 hours after symptom onset, returning toward baseline over the next 12–24 hours. Recommended sampling therefore includes an acute sample as soon as feasible during or immediately after resuscitation, a second sample at about 1–2 hours (and within 4 hours) from symptom onset to capture the peak, and a third sample at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution to establish the patient’s baseline. A commonly used rule to define a significant mast cell activation is: peak tryptase > (1.2 × baseline) + 2 ng/mL. Separately, a persistent baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL is one of the WHO minor diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis. It is important to remember that normal tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis because sensitivity is imperfect and depends on the trigger and timing of sampling.

How the case deviated and what that means. In this patient the recommended sampling schedule was not followed at the index presentation: no tryptase was drawn during the first emergency department episode. The first available tryptase was obtained about 1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode (an acceptable time window for an acute sample for that episode) and a follow‑up baseline sample was taken 27 hours after the last recurrence. Missing early samples from the first episode limits the ability to demonstrate a tryptase rise from the initial event and to calculate the full dynamic change across the entire clinical course. Nonetheless, an elevated acute value during the recorded episode together with a persistently raised baseline supports both significant mast cell activation during the attacks and the presence of an underlying tendency to higher mast cell burden—evidence that prompted further evaluation for mastocytosis.

Reasoning that clarithromycin was the most likely culprit. Culprit attribution in drug reactions rests mainly on timing and exclusion of alternatives. In this case symptoms began roughly one hour after a first oral 500 mg dose of clarithromycin and progressed rapidly to hypotension, hypoxaemia and diffuse urticaria—features consistent with an immediate systemic hypersensitivity reaction. No subsequent dosing occurred and there were no other strong new exposures temporally linked to the reaction. The earlier rash after several days of clindamycin use was a different clinical pattern (delayed, limited to a rash) and occurred days before the anaphylactic event; chronic maintenance medications had been tolerated previously and were less plausible precipitants. Taken together, the tight temporal relationship to clarithromycin and lack of a more likely trigger make clarithromycin the most probable culprit in this clinical reasoning framework.

Distinguishing multiphasic relapse from sustained antigen-driven anaphylaxis. Clinically, a multiphasic course is characterized by a clear initial response to therapy with a symptom‑free interval followed by one or more recurrences without further exposure to antigen. Sustained anaphylaxis due to ongoing antigen absorption or continued presence of allergen is suggested by failure to achieve a durable response or by continuous fluctuating symptoms despite correct therapy. In this patient the initial prompt and complete response to epinephrine, a subsequent asymptomatic interval, discrete relapses occurring without further dosing, and progressively less severe episodes favor a multiphasic pattern rather than persistent antigen absorption. Sustained absorption from a single oral dose would be less likely to produce well‑demarcated, resolving intervals and progressively milder relapses.

When to pursue evaluation for clonal mast cell disorders. Referral for evaluation of a clonal mast cell disorder (for example, systemic mastocytosis) is indicated when anaphylaxis is unusually severe, recurrent, idiopathic, or when baseline tryptase is persistently elevated. Additional red flags are mediator‑related chronic symptoms or findings such as hepatosplenomegaly, unexplained gastrointestinal symptoms, or bone involvement. The initial workup should include a baseline (asymptomatic) tryptase, repeat confirmatory measurements, and hematology/allergy referral. Specialized testing may include sensitive KIT D816V molecular testing on peripheral blood and, when indicated, a bone marrow aspirate/biopsy with histology, immunophenotyping (CD25/CD2), and molecular studies. In this patient the multiphasic, severe presentation plus persistently elevated tryptase and sonographic hepatomegaly prompted that pathway; the clinicians appropriately escalated to specialist evaluation even though the diagnostic workup could not be completed to a formal microbiologic or histologic endpoint.

WHO criteria and how this patient mapped to them. The WHO diagnosis for systemic mastocytosis requires either the major criterion (multifocal dense mast cell aggregates in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs) plus one minor criterion, or three minor criteria. The four minor criteria are: (1) baseline serum tryptase >20 ng/mL (unless explained by a clonal myeloid disorder), (2) presence of an activating KIT mutation (typically D816V) in blood or bone marrow, (3) aberrant mast cell surface expression of CD2 and/or CD25, and (4) >25% of mast cells showing atypical spindle shape on morphology. The authors report that the patient fulfilled two WHO minor criteria but that the major criterion could not be assessed because the bone marrow specimen was limited and prevented reliable evaluation for multifocal aggregates; thus a formal diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis could not be established, although the constellation of findings made a clonal mast cell disorder likely and justified ongoing follow‑up.

Why confirmatory macrolide allergy testing was deferred and how the allergy was managed. Provocative testing—skin testing, intradermal testing or graded oral challenge—to confirm macrolide allergy carries a real risk of provoking severe recurrent anaphylaxis, and standardized, validated reagents and protocols for macrolide testing are limited. When a history and timing strongly implicate a drug that can readily be avoided and safe alternatives exist, deferring provocative testing is a defensible, patient‑safety–driven choice. In this case the team prioritized avoidance, clear documentation of the suspected allergy in the record, prescription and teaching for an epinephrine auto‑injector, and antihistamine therapy on discharge. If definitive confirmation is later required, testing should be performed by an experienced allergist in a controlled setting, and the presence of a possible mast cell disorder should be taken into account because it raises the risk of severe reactions during challenge.

Takeaway for clinical practice. Capture tryptase samples as early as feasible and always obtain a baseline measurement >24 hours after complete resolution; use the (1.2 × baseline + 2 ng/mL) calculation to assess for significant rises. When anaphylaxis is severe, recurrent, atypical or accompanied by a persistently raised baseline tryptase or organomegaly, involve hematology/allergy for evaluation of clonal mast cell disease. When testing for drug allergy would put the patient at high risk, emphasize avoidance, documentation and emergency preparedness while arranging specialist follow‑up—exactly the pragmatic pathway followed in this case.

## Managing Relapses and Advanced Anaphylaxis Care

Recurrent or multiphasic anaphylaxis requires a structured, stepwise approach that keeps the immediate priorities of airway, breathing and circulation at the centre while anticipating further relapses. Epinephrine remains the single most important intervention: correct dosing, timely repeated administration and escalation to advanced vasoactive support when necessary are the pivotal decisions that determine outcome. In the presented case the patient initially received a single 0.3 mg intramuscular (IM) epinephrine auto‑injector and later required repeated IM doses (including 0.5 mg preparations) for three relapses, and was transferred to the ICU after the first recurrence. Those events illustrate the key practical points below.

Repeat IM epinephrine: timing and dose. IM epinephrine given into the mid‑outer thigh (vastus lateralis) achieves rapid, reliable absorption and should be given promptly for any life‑threatening manifestations (hypotension, respiratory compromise, laryngeal oedema). Recommended dosing is 0.01 mg/kg body weight (maximum 0.5 mg per dose) for adults; many protocols therefore use 0.5 mg as the standard adult dose for patients ≳50 kg. Repeated IM doses may be necessary and should be given promptly for persistent or recurrent features — commonly every 5–10 minutes as clinically required. Because commercially available auto‑injectors are often 0.3 mg, many adults receive a submaximal initial dose; the case illustrates how an initial 0.3 mg IM injection (widely used to reduce hesitation) may be followed by need for additional or larger doses. Where staffing and supply permit, having access to 0.5 mg IM doses for adults >50 kg reduces the risk of underdosing and the need for escalation.

When to escalate to an IV epinephrine infusion. Intravenous epinephrine infusion is reserved for refractory or life‑threatening anaphylaxis that does not respond to repeated adequate IM epinephrine plus aggressive fluid resuscitation, or for patients with recurrent severe episodes despite appropriate IM dosing. Indications include persistent hypotension despite IM epinephrine and crystalloid boluses, inability to maintain perfusion without vasoactive support, or rapidly recurring episodes that cannot be controlled by repeated IM injections. In the present case the treating team debated prophylactic continuous IV epinephrine because of multiphasic recurrences but elected not to start it once the relapses attenuated and resolved; that reflects the clinical judgement required when weighing infusion risks against observed clinical trajectory.

Practical and safety considerations for IV epinephrine. An IV infusion should be initiated only in a monitored environment (ICU or equivalent) with continuous ECG and frequent blood pressure monitoring, ideally using an infusion pump and pharmacy‑standard dilution to avoid dosing errors. Staff must be familiar with titration to clinical effect and vigilant for adrenaline‑related adverse events — arrhythmias, myocardial ischaemia, severe hypertension and local extravasation injury if infusing peripherally. Where prolonged or high‑dose vasopressor support is expected, a secure central venous line and arterial pressure monitoring are appropriate. Small IV boluses are sometimes used in emergency refractory hypotension, but they carry higher arrhythmic risk than IM dosing and should be given only by experienced critical care teams.

Adjunctive therapies and their limitations. H1 antihistamines (oral or parenteral) and H2 blockers can reduce cutaneous symptoms (pruritus, urticaria) and are commonly given, but they do not treat airway obstruction, bronchospasm unresponsive to bronchodilators, or distributive shock and should never delay epinephrine. Inhaled short‑acting beta‑agonists are useful adjuncts for wheeze or bronchospasm that persists after epinephrine. In the case, clemastine (an H1 blocker) was administered for skin symptoms; this is reasonable for symptom control but insufficient for the circulatory collapse that defines severe anaphylaxis.

Corticosteroids: evidence and pragmatic use. Robust evidence does not support routine corticosteroid use to prevent biphasic reactions; steroids have a delayed onset of action and are unlikely to avert early relapses driven by ongoing mediator release. Systematic reviews and guideline updates therefore discourage routine steroid administration for the purpose of biphasic prevention. Steroids can, however, cause harms — hyperglycaemia (important in this patient with type 2 diabetes and an admission glucose of 12.7 mmol/L), possible increased infection risk, psychologic effects and longer hospital observation. In practice, steroids are still widely administered because of historical practice and institutional protocols; this patient received prednisone and dexamethasone after the first relapse for that reason. The key learning point is to acknowledge the lack of evidence for steroid prevention of relapses and to weigh the potential harms in patients with comorbidities.

Supportive care during relapses. High‑flow oxygen and ongoing cardiopulmonary monitoring are essential; oxygen should be titrated to maintain adequate saturations while avoiding delay in definitive treatment. Aggressive intravenous crystalloid boluses are indicated to treat the vasodilatory and capillary‑leak component of anaphylactic shock. Airway preparedness is critical: progressive oropharyngeal or laryngeal oedema can evolve rapidly, and early elective intubation is often safer than emergency attempts in a compromised airway. Frequent reassessment (vital signs, skin, respiratory status, mental state, urine output) is required because relapses in multiphasic courses may be unpredictable. The case demonstrates the practical use of supplemental oxygen and repeated fluid boluses during relapses, and the decision to escalate monitoring by transferring to ICU.

Disposition and duration of observation. Decisions about ICU admission and observation length should be individualized. Indications for ICU level care include refractory hypotension, need for multiple epinephrine doses, requirement for continuous vasopressor infusion, significant airway compromise, severe hypoxaemia, or significant comorbidity (including suspected mast cell disorders). There is no universal consensus on how long to observe after resolution; low‑risk patients are commonly observed for 4–6 hours, but those with severe initial reactions, need for multiple epinephrine doses, or suspected mast cell disease merit prolonged observation. A pragmatic threshold used in severe or multiphasic cases is at least 24 hours symptom‑free and hemodynamically stable before de‑escalation of monitoring, coupled with readiness to intervene if relapse occurs. In this patient, ICU observation after the first relapse and 24 hours without recurrence before transfer to a ward was concordant with a cautious, risk‑based approach.

Discharge planning and follow‑up. Before discharge, patients should be educated on avoidance of the likely trigger, given an epinephrine auto‑injector and instruction in its use, and referred for specialist follow‑up (allergy/allergology and, where appropriate, mast cell disease assessment). For adults >50 kg, guidelines favour access to a 0.5 mg adult dose; the practical availability of auto‑injector strengths varies, so clinicians should aim to supply weight‑appropriate devices when possible. Baseline tryptase measurement after recovery and referral for allergology are important when the course is atypical (severe, multiphasic or recurrent), as in this case where later raised tryptase and other findings raised concern for underlying mast cell disorder.

In summary, managing relapses in anaphylaxis demands prompt, repeated, weight‑appropriate IM epinephrine, aggressive supportive care, clear criteria for escalation to IV infusion and ICU monitoring, and recognition that adjunctive drugs (antihistamines, bronchodilators) do not replace epinephrine. Corticosteroids should not be relied upon to prevent biphasic or multiphasic relapses and their use should be individualized, particularly in patients with comorbidities such as diabetes. The case highlights the practical implications of initial underdosing with a 0.3 mg auto‑injector, the need for rapid reassessment and escalation when relapses occur, and the value of prolonged observation and specialist follow‑up when an underlying mast cell disorder is suspected.

## Outcomes, Follow-up, and Practice Pearls

Clinical course and disposition
The patient experienced four discrete anaphylactic episodes over the first 12 hours after clarithromycin exposure, each promptly reversed with intramuscular epinephrine; the severity of symptoms decreased with each relapse (from severe hypoxaemia and hypotension initially to mainly cutaneous and respiratory symptoms in the final episode). After the final event there were no further recurrences; she remained free of anaphylaxis for approximately 24 hours before being stepped down from the ICU to the ward and was discharged on hospital day three. The team considered but did not need to institute continuous intravenous epinephrine because the relapses ceased and each episode responded to intramuscular dosing.

Discharge medications and emergency equipment
At discharge she was started on daily levocetirizine for symptomatic control of histamine-mediated symptoms and was provided and trained in the use of an epinephrine auto‑injector. Epinephrine remains the first‑line treatment for anaphylaxis and should be carried at all times by patients at risk. Dose selection should be weight‑based (0.01 mg/kg, intramuscular, up to a 0.5 mg adult maximum); many available auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg and may underdose larger adults. Given this patient’s body weight (104 kg) and the need for repeated doses during her admission, clinicians should consider prescribing an adult dose that achieves the recommended maximum intramuscular dose when available and feasible. Patients at higher risk (severe initial reaction, biphasic/multiphasic course, remote access issues) should be given two auto‑injectors and instructed to use a second dose if symptoms persist or recur after 5–15 minutes while awaiting emergency help.

Follow-up pathway for suspected mast cell disease
Because her baseline tryptase remained elevated and other features (hepatomegaly, neuropsychiatric symptoms) raised concern for a mast cell disorder, prompt outpatient follow-up with allergy/immunology (and hematology if advised) is essential. Tryptase sampling is time‑sensitive: obtain an acute sample as soon as feasible during an event, repeat at 1–2 hours (within 4 hours) when levels often peak, and obtain a baseline sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish the baseline value. When both an acute and a baseline value are available, mast cell activation is supported by an acute level > (1.2 × baseline) + 2 ng/mL. If baseline tryptase remains elevated (particularly >20 ng/mL) or clinical suspicion persists, the next steps include targeted testing for KIT D816V (peripheral blood and/or bone marrow), flow cytometry for aberrant mast cell markers (CD2/CD25), and, if indicated, repeat bone marrow assessment with adequate sampling to evaluate the WHO criteria for systemic mastocytosis. In this case the initial marrow material was limited and did not allow full application of the diagnostic criteria, so a repeat specialist evaluation and testing plan is appropriate. Until evaluation is complete, the patient should avoid the implicated trigger (clarithromycin) and other macrolides as advised by the allergy service, and the allergy should be clearly documented in the medical record.

Patient education and emergency planning
Education focuses on early recognition and immediate intramuscular epinephrine administration for any signs of systemic reaction (airway compromise, respiratory distress, circulatory collapse, or progressive mucocutaneous involvement). Patients must be told not to delay epinephrine while attempting to confirm the diagnosis and to call emergency services after using an auto‑injector. Provide a written anaphylaxis action plan that specifies when and how to inject epinephrine, when to repeat a dose, and when to seek emergency care. Training should include demonstration and return‑demonstration of the auto‑injector technique, advice to wear medical identification describing the allergy/mast cell disorder, and discussion of avoidance strategies for the implicated drug class. Given the history of multiphasic events, emphasize vigilance for symptom recurrence over the first 24–72 hours after an event.

System and team practice pearls
This case highlights several system improvements that reduce diagnostic and treatment gaps for complex anaphylaxis. First, standardize ED workflows to ensure timely tryptase draws at the recommended intervals (acute, 1–2 h, and ≥24 h baseline) so that mast cell activation and baseline status can be interpreted accurately. Second, ensure availability of and team familiarity with appropriate adult epinephrine dosing (IM 0.5 mg target for most adults) and local protocols for when to escalate to continuous intravenous infusions in refractory cases—ICU management should be protocolised. Third, adopt a risk‑stratified observation and disposition protocol (longer observation or admission for severe initial reactions, multiple epinephrine doses, or suspected mast cell disease). Fourth, include anaphylaxis discharge checklists that confirm provision of epinephrine auto‑injectors (two devices when indicated), antihistamine prescriptions, written action plans, and specialty follow‑up. Finally, contribute multiphasic and refractory anaphylaxis cases to local or national registries to build evidence for future guideline refinement.

Takeaway
Multiphasic anaphylaxis requires prompt acute treatment, prolonged vigilance, careful follow‑up for underlying mast cell disease when suggested by tryptase and clinical features, weight‑appropriate epinephrine provision and training, and system processes that reliably capture time‑sensitive diagnostic tests and ensure appropriate post‑discharge safety planning.
