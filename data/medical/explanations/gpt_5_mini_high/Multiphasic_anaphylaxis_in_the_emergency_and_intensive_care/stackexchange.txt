Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Differential Diagnosis] How would you distinguish biphasic anaphylaxis from refractory anaphylaxis and from prolonged anaphylaxis due to ongoing allergen absorption in an ED/ICU patient, and what time course and response patterns support each diagnosis?

Short answer — ask three questions at the bedside: (1) Was there a clear symptom‑free interval after an adequate initial response? (2) Did the patient fail to improve after correct epinephrine dosing? (3) Is there a continuing source of allergen? The answers separate biphasic (and multiphasic) reactions, refractory anaphylaxis, and prolonged anaphylaxis from ongoing absorption, and each has a different expected time course and management implication.

How to distinguish them (clinical features and typical time course)

- Biphasic / multiphasic anaphylaxis
  - Definition/clinical pattern: initial anaphylaxis with apparent clinical resolution, followed hours later by spontaneous recurrence of anaphylaxis without further exposure to the trigger. Multiphasic = more than one recurrence.
  - Time course: most relapses occur within 6–12 hours but can occur up to ~72 hours (rare reports beyond that). Literature gives variable ranges; the case’s relapses all occurred within the first 12 hours, a common window.
  - Response pattern: recurrences usually respond to standard bolus therapy (IM epinephrine, oxygen, fluids); severity may be similar or, as in this report, diminish with successive episodes.
  - Supporting features in the case: the patient had an initial improvement after IM epinephrine, then three relapses within 12 hours that each resolved with additional IM epinephrine — a classic multiphasic pattern and not refractory disease.

- Refractory anaphylaxis
  - Definition/clinical pattern: persistent or progressive anaphylaxis despite correct and timely administration of appropriate epinephrine doses.
  - Time course: usually obvious immediately or shortly after initial treatment — failure to reverse hypotension or bronchospasm despite adequate therapy.
  - Response pattern: poor or absent response to repeated IM epinephrine; requires escalation (IV epinephrine boluses and titrated infusion, advanced hemodynamic support, airway control). Check for contributing causes (β‑blocker use — give glucagon; severe mast cell activation; cardiogenic causes).
  - Why the case was not refractory: each relapse was reversed by IM epinephrine. The episodes were responsive, so escalation to IV infusion was not mandatory.

- Prolonged anaphylaxis from ongoing allergen absorption
  - Definition/clinical pattern: continuous or recurrent symptoms driven by continued antigen exposure or slow/extended absorption (e.g., depot injections, retained oral drug, extended‑release formulations).
  - Time course: persists or recurs as long as antigen exposure/absorption continues — hours to days depending on the agent and kinetics.
  - Response pattern: transient improvement after epinephrine that recurs predictably as epinephrine wears off while antigen continues to stimulate mast cells; may require continuous IV epinephrine until the antigen is removed/cleared. Management must include source control (stop medication, remove contact, consider activated charcoal if appropriate and within window).
  - Why this was unlikely in the case: the patient had a single 500‑mg oral dose of clarithromycin and no further exposures; relapses clustered in the early post‑exposure period and were episodic rather than a continuously refractory course. That pattern favors multiphasic biphasic anaphylaxis rather than ongoing antigen absorption.

Practical bedside steps to distinguish and act (ED/ICU algorithm)
1. Verify epinephrine was given correctly (IM 0.01 mg/kg to 0.5 mg in adults; many auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg and may underdose >50 kg). If initial dosing was delayed or underdosed, repeat an appropriate IM dose — delayed/inadequate epinephrine increases risk of biphasic relapse.
2. Look for a symptom‑free interval. If there is a clear period of clinical resolution followed by recurrence, label as biphasic/multiphasic unless ongoing exposure is identified.
3. If there is no meaningful improvement after adequate epinephrine, treat as refractory: escalate to IV epinephrine infusion (and IV boluses as needed), secure airway, invasive monitoring, ICU care, and consider glucagon if the patient is on β‑blockers.
4. If recurrence is due to suspected ongoing absorption (recent oral ingestion, depot injection, retained food), pursue source control (stop the drug, remove contact, consider activated charcoal if within the appropriate time window), and be prepared for prolonged hemodynamic support including a continuous epinephrine infusion until absorption abates.
5. Obtain serial tryptase samples to document mast cell activation and to assess for an underlying mast cell disorder — draw one as soon as feasible, a second at about 1–2 hours from symptom onset (within 4 hours), and a baseline ≥24 hours after full resolution. Persistent elevated baseline tryptase (as in this report) raises suspicion for systemic mastocytosis and a propensity for severe or multiphasic courses.

Management implications from the distinctions
- Biphasic/multiphasic: treat each recurrence with standard anaphylaxis measures (IM epinephrine first), admit for extended observation based on risk (severe initial reaction, multiple epinephrine doses, comorbid asthma or mast cell disorder → lean toward 24 hours ICU/telemetry). Routine corticosteroids do not have solid evidence to prevent biphasic reactions.
- Refractory: immediate escalation to IV epinephrine infusion, ICU care, consider adjuncts (vasopressors, inotropes, glucagon if on β‑blockers), early airway control.
- Ongoing absorption: treat as prolonged antigen stimulation — remove exposure, consider GI decontamination when appropriate, and give continuous hemodynamic support until the antigen is cleared.

Case teaching point
- The patient’s course — rapid onset after first clarithromycin dose, initial response to IM epinephrine, then three relapses within 12 hours each responsive to additional IM epinephrine and with decreasing intensity — fits multiphasic (biphasic) anaphylaxis rather than refractory disease or continued antigen exposure. Contributing factors included initial use of a 0.3‑mg auto‑injector (potential underdosing in a 104‑kg adult) and an elevated baseline tryptase (raising concern for a mast cell disorder that predisposes to recurrent episodes). ICU admission after the first relapse and serial monitoring, with consideration of continuous infusion only if boluses failed, was a reasonable, guideline‑concordant approach.

Bottom line: history of a symptom‑free interval and full responsiveness to appropriate epinephrine favor biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis; failure to respond to correct epinephrine defines refractory anaphylaxis and mandates early escalation; evidence of persistent antigen exposure points toward prolonged anaphylaxis from ongoing absorption and requires source control plus prolonged hemodynamic support. Use serial tryptase and patient risk factors to refine prognosis and disposition.

### [Management] In a 104-kg adult with anaphylactic shock, what is the recommended initial epinephrine dose, route, site, and repeat interval, and what are the clinical implications of using a 0.3 mg auto-injector instead of the guideline-recommended 0.5 mg dose?

Answer (clinical pearl)

- Recommended initial dose: 0.01 mg/kg intramuscularly, with a practical adult cap of 0.5 mg per dose. For a 104‑kg adult this means give 0.5 mg IM (most guidelines simplify to 0.5 mg for adults >50 kg).

- Route and site: intramuscular (IM) into the mid‑anterolateral thigh (vastus lateralis) using epinephrine 1:1,000 (1 mg/mL). IM thigh injection achieves faster, more reliable absorption than subcutaneous or deltoid injections.

- Repeat interval and escalation: repeat the IM dose every 5 minutes as needed until clinical improvement. If there is no meaningful response after 2–3 IM doses, or if the patient remains in shock, escalate to intravenous epinephrine in a monitored setting (small titrated IV boluses or an infusion) and admit to higher‑level care.

Why this matters (clinical reasoning, tied to the case)
- The guideline 0.01 mg/kg dosing is capped at 0.5 mg because most adults weigh >50 kg; therefore a 104‑kg patient should receive the full 0.5 mg IM dose initially. IM epinephrine into the thigh maximizes rapid peak blood levels and reverses airway, bronchospasm and vasodilation more reliably than lower or subcutaneous doses.
- In the published case the patient (104 kg) received a 0.3‑mg auto‑injector in the ED and initially improved, but then had recurrent (multiphasic) episodes requiring further IM doses; later individually prepared 0.5‑mg IM doses were used. The authors note that many commonly available adult auto‑injectors are only 0.3 mg and that retrospective data show an increased need for escalation when 0.3 mg is used as the initial adult dose.

Practical implications of using a 0.3‑mg auto‑injector instead of 0.5 mg
- Underdosing risk: 0.3 mg is below the guideline dose for adults >50 kg. It may produce a partial or temporary response but is more likely to require repeated doses or escalation (additional IM doses, IV epinephrine, ICU care), as illustrated by the case.
- Injection depth matters: in heavier patients an auto‑injector’s needle may deposit drug subcutaneously rather than intramuscularly, further slowing absorption and functionally underdosing the patient.
- Operational trade‑off: do not delay—if a 0.3‑mg auto‑injector is immediately available, give it promptly rather than waiting to draw up a 0.5‑mg syringe. But be prepared to give additional IM epinephrine to reach 0.5 mg and to escalate care if response is incomplete.
- Systems implication: hospitals and prehospital services should consider availability of 0.5‑mg adult devices or ensure rapid ability to draw 0.5 mg IM from ampoules; clinicians should recognize that adults discharged after severe reactions (especially with mast cell disorders) ideally should have access to an appropriate‑dose auto‑injector and specialist follow‑up.

Bottom line: for a 104‑kg adult in anaphylactic shock give 0.5 mg epinephrine IM into the mid‑thigh and repeat q5 minutes as needed. A 0.3‑mg auto‑injector is better than no immediate epinephrine but is likely subtherapeutic in a patient of this weight and may increase the chance of recurrent or refractory episodes, as the case demonstrates.

### [Management] What are your indications to initiate a titrated intravenous epinephrine infusion for anaphylaxis, how would you start and titrate it, and what monitoring and adjunctive therapies are required?

Bottom line
- A titrated intravenous (IV) epinephrine infusion is not first‑line for routine anaphylaxis (IM epinephrine remains first and most important step). Use a continuous IV infusion when the patient has life‑threatening circulatory or respiratory compromise that is not controlled by appropriate IM epinephrine and supportive measures, or when the patient requires repeated urgent IM doses (recurrent/multiphasic or “refractory” anaphylaxis) and needs a reliably titratable vasoactive support in a monitored setting (ICU). The case you read illustrates this dilemma: repeated IM doses (four episodes in 12 hours) prompted discussion of starting an infusion even though it was ultimately not required.

Indications to start an IV epinephrine infusion
- Refractory anaphylaxis: persistent or worsening hypotension, shock, severe bronchospasm, or airway compromise despite
  - appropriate IM epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg for adults; many auto‑injectors are only 0.3 mg and may underdose larger adults),
  - adequate IV fluid resuscitation,
  - repeat IM boluses as needed.  
- Rapidly relapsing or multiphasic anaphylaxis in which the patient requires repeated IM injections in a short period (several injections over hours), making intermittent IM dosing impractical or unsafe.  
- Poor peripheral perfusion such that IM absorption is unreliable (profound shock).  
- Cardiac arrest: IV epinephrine boluses per ACLS (different indication/dosing) — continuous infusion applies to resuscitated or refractory shock, not to the arrest bolus itself.  
- Special situations that lower the threshold for infusion: concurrent beta‑blocker therapy, suspected mast cell disorder with ongoing mediator release (as in the case where systemic mastocytosis was suspected).

How to give it (practical, commonly used approach)
1) Before infusion — immediate steps
  - Continue/rescue with IM epinephrine if not already given (lateral thigh).  
  - If circulatory collapse exists, give small IV boluses while preparing infusion: 50–100 mcg (0.05–0.1 mg) IV of epinephrine (use 1:10,000 = 0.1 mg/mL), given slowly and repeated every 1–2 minutes as needed to restore perfusion. (If cardiac arrest, follow ACLS 1 mg 1:10,000 boluses.)
  - Establish two large‑bore IV lines and give rapid crystalloid resuscitation (adult initial 1–2 L bolus or 20 mL/kg as a guide).

2) Prepare infusion (two common hospital recipes)
  - Option A (more concentrated, smaller fluid volume): 1 mg epinephrine (1:1000, 1 mL) diluted to 100 mL NS → 10 mcg/mL.
  - Option B (less concentrated, easier low‑rate titration): 1 mg in 250 mL NS → 4 mcg/mL.
  - Choose the concentration that fits your pump protocols and desired ml/hr steps.

3) Start and titrate
  - Weight‑based starting guide: 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min (for a 70‑kg adult ≈ 3.5–7 mcg/min).  
  - Absolute starting alternative commonly used: 3–10 mcg/min (start lower for milder shock, higher if severe). Example using 1 mg/100 mL (10 mcg/mL): 5 mcg/min = 0.5 mL/min = 30 mL/hr. Using 1 mg/250 mL (4 mcg/mL), 5 mcg/min = 1.25 mL/min = 75 mL/hr.  
  - Titrate to effect every few minutes (eg, increase by 1–2 mcg/min or by 0.02–0.05 mcg/kg/min), guided by blood pressure, mental status, urine output and oxygenation. Aim for restoration of perfusion (systolic BP >90–100 mm Hg, improving lactate/mental status/urine output).  
  - When stable, down‑titrate gradually. Avoid abrupt cessation if prolonged infusion has been used — monitor for recurrence.

Hazards and cautions
- IV epinephrine carries risk of tachyarrhythmia, myocardial ischemia, severe hypertension and lactic acidosis; use only on a pump in a monitored setting (resuscitation bay or ICU).  
- In patients with coronary artery disease or the elderly, be especially vigilant for ischemia.  
- If patient is receiving beta‑blockers and is unresponsive to epinephrine, give IV glucagon (bolus 1–5 mg IV over 5 minutes then consider infusion) while arranging cardiovascular support — glucagon reverses beta‑blockade‑related epinephrine resistance.

Monitoring required
- Continuous ECG monitoring and continuous pulse oximetry; noninvasive BP every 1–2 minutes while titrating.  
- Consider invasive arterial monitoring for rapidly changing hemodynamics or frequent titration.  
- Frequent clinical reassessment of airway, work of breathing, skin findings, mental status and urine output.  
- Maintain large‑bore IV access; continue aggressive crystalloid resuscitation as needed.  
- Labs: bedside glucose, arterial blood gas/lactate, electrolytes, and troponin if concern for ischemia. Obtain tryptase samples for suspected mast cell activation (ideally ASAP, 1–2 hours after onset, and a baseline ≥24 hours after resolution) — the case highlighted missed early sampling but later elevated baseline tryptase supporting mast cell disorder.

Adjunctive therapies (do not replace epinephrine)
- High‑flow oxygen, repeated IV crystalloid boluses.  
- Nebulized inhaled bronchodilator for bronchospasm.  
- H1 antihistamine (IV) and H2 blocker for cutaneous symptoms (symptom control only).  
- Corticosteroids: commonly given in practice (as in the case) but evidence does not show prevention of biphasic reactions; use selectively for protracted or multiphasic courses or when there are concomitant asthma/COPD exacerbations.  
- If epinephrine alone fails and vasoplegia predominates, consider addition or substitution of norepinephrine in ICU settings per critical care guidance.

Applying this to the presented case
- The patient had multiphasic anaphylaxis with repeated IM epinephrine doses and ICU admission for monitoring. That pattern (multiple recurrences requiring repeated IM doses) is a common and reasonable trigger to consider starting a continuous infusion because it provides reliable, titratable hemodynamic support and reduces the need for repeated boluses. In this patient the episodes diminished in intensity and no infusion was ultimately required; corticosteroids and antihistamines were given after the first relapse and close ICU monitoring allowed rapid rescue with IM epinephrine. In a similar future patient with ongoing or worsening hypotension or continued frequent relapses, I would start small IV boluses (50–100 mcg) to stabilise and then institute a monitored infusion (eg, 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min; adjust to effect), with arterial monitoring, aggressive fluids, consideration of glucagon if on beta‑blockers, and plan for prolonged observation and mast cell workup.

Clinical pearl
- IM epinephrine first; escalate to IV boluses then to a continuous epinephrine infusion when the patient is refractory, relapsing frequently, or has poor IM absorption — but only in a monitored setting with pumps, continuous monitoring and concurrent fluid resuscitation.

### [Management] What does current evidence show about corticosteroids in acute anaphylaxis—do they prevent biphasic reactions or improve outcomes—and in what limited situations, if any, might you still administer them?

Short answer: routine corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic (or multiphasic) anaphylaxis and have not been shown to improve clinically important outcomes. They should not be used as a substitute for, or a reason to delay, first‑line therapy (epinephrine, oxygen, fluids, airway support) or for shortened observation. Steroids remain an occasional, targeted adjunct in a few specific situations only.

Why: corticosteroids act by altering gene transcription and reducing late inflammatory mediator production — an effect that takes hours to develop. Biphasic recurrences are driven by ongoing mediator release or re‑emergence of anaphylactic physiology over the next hours and, importantly, occur unpredictably. High‑quality clinical data do not show that giving systemic corticosteroids at presentation reduces the risk of a later biphasic reaction. As the case authors note, a systematic review of 22 studies found only one study supporting steroid benefit and 21 showing no effect, and observational data have even linked prehospital steroid use with greater downstream resource use (higher odds of ward/ICU admission). Contemporary guidelines therefore advise against routine steroid administration for the purpose of preventing biphasic anaphylaxis.

What the case illustrates: in this patient steroids (prednisone IV daily and a single dose dexamethasone IV) were administered after the first relapse because of traditional practice, yet she still had additional relapses that required repeated IM epinephrine. That clinical course is a practical example of why steroids cannot be relied upon to prevent recurrence and why continued monitoring and readiness to give epinephrine (including escalation to infusion if needed) remain the priorities.

When to consider corticosteroids (limited indications)
- Concomitant severe asthma/bronchospasm: give systemic steroids as you would for an acute asthma exacerbation to reduce risk of late bronchospasm (adjunctive to epinephrine and bronchodilators).  
- Known adrenal insufficiency or chronic corticosteroid therapy: give stress‑dose glucocorticoids to avoid adrenal crisis.  
- Protracted or refractory anaphylaxis as part of specialist‑directed care: in rare, prolonged courses steroids may be used adjunctively while other measures (repeated IM epinephrine, epinephrine infusion, airway management) are implemented. Even here they are adjuncts, not primary therapy.  
- Persistent cutaneous symptoms after stabilization: some clinicians give short steroid courses for protracted urticaria/angioedema, recognizing symptom relief is the goal rather than prevention of biphasic shock.

Practical points for clinical teams
- Never delay epinephrine to give steroids or antihistamines. Epinephrine remains the only life‑saving immediate therapy.  
- If steroids are given, document the indication and dose; do not use them as a replacement for observation. Observation should be risk‑stratified (severe initial reaction, hypotension, respiratory compromise, delayed epinephrine, multiple epinephrine doses → longer monitoring/consider ICU).  
- Be mindful of steroid harms (hyperglycemia — salient here for this patient with diabetes — infection risk, possible increased hospital stays) and local protocol variability (the case received both prednisone and dexamethasone because of differing ward protocols). Standardize local practice to avoid duplication.  
- For recurrent or refractory episodes, escalate treatment promptly (repeat IM epinephrine, consider IV epinephrine infusion and ICU care) rather than relying on corticosteroids to prevent further relapses.

Bottom line (clinical pearl): corticosteroids are not proven to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis or improve outcomes and should not be given routinely for that purpose. Use them only for well‑defined, adjunctive indications (severe asthma, adrenal insufficiency, or as part of specialist management of protracted/refractory cases), and always prioritize rapid epinephrine, appropriate monitoring, and escalation of supportive care.

### [Morning Report] What is the optimal serum tryptase sampling strategy after suspected anaphylaxis (number of samples and timing), and how is mast cell activation confirmed relative to baseline using the 1.2 × baseline + 2 ng/mL rule?

Short answer
- Aim for three serum total‑tryptase samples when feasible: (1) an acute sample drawn as soon as possible after recognition of anaphylaxis (do not delay epinephrine to obtain the blood), (2) a “peak” sample at about 1–2 hours after symptom onset (acceptable up to 4 hours), and (3) a baseline sample taken ≥24 hours after full clinical resolution to define the patient’s baseline. If only two samples are possible, obtain an acute/peak sample and a baseline ≥24 h later. Confirm mast‑cell activation when the acute value ≥ (1.2 × baseline) + 2 ng/mL.

Why and how this is used (clinical reasoning)
- Tryptase is released from mast cells during degranulation; serum tryptase typically rises within minutes and usually peaks around 1–3 hours after symptom onset, then falls toward baseline with a half‑life of a few hours and is usually back to baseline by ~24 hours. Because of this kinetic profile you need at least one early/peak sample and a later baseline to detect a meaningful rise.
- The 1.2 × baseline + 2 ng/mL rule combines a relative (20%) and absolute (2 ng/mL) increment to avoid false negatives in patients with high baseline tryptase and to avoid overcalling tiny changes when baseline is very low. Example: baseline 5 ng/mL → threshold = 1.2×5 + 2 = 8 ng/mL; baseline 50 ng/mL → threshold = 1.2×50 + 2 = 62 ng/mL. An acute value at or above that threshold supports mast‑cell activation consistent with anaphylaxis.

Practical points and limitations
- Do not delay life‑saving treatment to draw blood. Draw the acute sample as soon as feasible and document the exact time of symptom onset and of blood draws.
- Ideally obtain a peak sample at ~1–2 h (acceptable up to 4 h). Then obtain a baseline sample ≥24 h after complete clinical recovery (some clinicians repeat baseline later as well to confirm persistent elevation).
- A normal tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis (certain triggers and less severe reactions may not produce a measurable tryptase rise). Persistent elevation of baseline tryptase (eg, >11–20 ng/mL depending on lab) should prompt evaluation for clonal mast‑cell disease (systemic mastocytosis) with specialist referral.
- Preanalytical handling (serum tube, timely centrifugation/transport per lab instructions) matters for reliable results.

Tie‑back to the case
- The authors note that the recommended sampling strategy was not followed: tryptase was not measured during the initial episodes. In their patient the first tryptase was drawn 1 hour after the third episode (appropriate for a peak sample) and a second sample 27 hours after the last recurrence (appropriate for baseline), which provided useful information but missed earlier acute measurements. The persistent elevation of baseline tryptase thereafter raised the possibility of systemic mastocytosis, illustrating why obtaining properly timed acute and baseline samples is important both to confirm mast‑cell activation during the event and to prompt appropriate downstream evaluation.

### [Pathophysiology] Which clinical and laboratory features in this patient raise suspicion for a clonal mast cell disorder, and what are the WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis? How would you distinguish systemic mastocytosis from other causes of elevated baseline tryptase such as hereditary alpha-tryptasemia or myeloid neoplasms?

Short answer: In this patient the clinical red flags for a clonal mast cell disorder are an unusually severe, multiphasic anaphylactic course requiring repeated epinephrine doses and a persistently elevated baseline serum tryptase, accompanied by organomegaly (hepatomegaly on ultrasound) and non‑specific systemic symptoms (neuropsychiatric complaints). Systemic mastocytosis (SM) is diagnosed by the WHO algorithm: 1 major criterion (multifocal dense mast cell infiltrates ≥15 mast cells/aggregate in bone marrow or extracutaneous tissue) plus ≥1 minor criterion, or ≥3 minor criteria. The four WHO minor criteria are: (1) >25% of mast cells are spindle‑shaped or atypical (or >25% atypical mast cells on aspirate); (2) detection of a activating KIT codon 816 mutation (usually D816V) in marrow, blood, or other extracutaneous organ; (3) aberrant mast cell immunophenotype with expression of CD2 and/or CD25 in addition to CD117; (4) a persistently elevated baseline total serum tryptase >20 ng/mL (this minor criterion is not applied if an associated myeloid neoplasm is present).

Why these features matter (clinical reasoning and pathophysiology)
- Clonal mast cell expansion (most commonly driven by KIT D816V) increases mast cell burden and lowers the threshold for mediator release; patients therefore have higher baseline tryptase (tryptase is released constitutively by a larger mast cell pool) and are at risk for severe or recurrent anaphylaxis. Multiphasic or unusually severe anaphylaxis after trivial exposure should prompt this consideration.  
- Organomegaly (hepatomegaly, splenomegaly), cytopenias or other “B/C‑findings” raise pretest probability for systemic involvement. In this case the team found hepatomegaly and a persistently raised baseline tryptase — both increase suspicion even though the bone marrow specimen was non‑diagnostic.

How to distinguish SM from other causes of elevated baseline tryptase
- Hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia (HαT): germline copy‑number gains of TPSAB1 raise baseline tryptase without clonal mast cell proliferation. HαT is diagnosed by genomic testing for increased TPSAB1 copy number (ddPCR/qPCR or specialized assays). Bone marrow morphology is normal, mast cells lack aberrant CD25/CD2 expression, and KIT D816V is absent. Clinically, HαT can increase anaphylaxis risk and may coexist with SM — so one can have both conditions. Because HαT is familial, a family history of elevated tryptase or similar symptoms is a clue.  
- Myeloid neoplasms and SM‑AHN (SM with an associated hematologic neoplasm): some myeloid neoplasms can raise tryptase and can co‑exist with or produce overlapping findings. If an associated myeloid neoplasm is present, the tryptase >20 ng/mL minor criterion cannot be used to support SM. Distinguish by peripheral blood counts, marrow morphology/cytogenetics/NGS for other myeloid driver mutations, and by demonstrating true mast cell aggregates with aberrant phenotype and/or KIT mutation within the mast cell population. In SM‑AHN the diagnostic workup must document both the mast cell clonality (by WHO criteria) and the independent myeloid neoplasm.

Practical evaluation steps (how this applies to the presented case)
- Confirm a true baseline tryptase: obtain a sample >24 hours after full clinical recovery (the case had imperfect timing — first sample 1 hour after a later episode, second 27 hours after last recurrence — but baseline remained elevated).  
- Document mast cell activation when appropriate: an acute rise consistent with mast cell activation is often defined as an increase > (1.2 × baseline + 2) ng/mL measured within 1–4 hours of symptom onset.  
- Molecular and tissue workup: do sensitive testing for KIT D816V in peripheral blood (allele‑specific PCR or ddPCR) and, if negative or inconclusive, pursue a properly obtained bone marrow trephine with adequate tissue for histology, tryptase/CD117/CD25 immunostains, flow cytometry, and molecular testing. The major WHO criterion requires a marrow trephine showing multifocal dense aggregates (≥15 cells/aggregate). Because the case’s initial marrow was limited and only two minor criteria were documented, repeating marrow with full studies is the appropriate next step.  
- Test for HαT (TPSAB1 copy number) if baseline tryptase is persistently elevated and marrow and KIT testing are negative or discordant, or if there is a family history of elevated tryptase.  
- Evaluate for an associated myeloid neoplasm (CBC, smear, cytogenetics/NGS) because its presence changes interpretation of the tryptase criterion and affects management.

Clinical pearl: persistent baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL or unusually severe/recurrent anaphylaxis should prompt hematologic/mast cell workup; bone marrow trephine with CD25 staining and sensitive KIT testing are required to make (or exclude) SM, and TPSAB1 copy‑number testing will separate hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia (or identify a coexisting condition) — all important because these diagnoses change counseling, monitoring and preventive strategies for future anaphylaxis.

### [Management] How would you determine the duration and level of post-anaphylaxis observation, and which risk factors (eg, delayed epinephrine, severe initial presentation, multiple epinephrine doses, unknown trigger, suspected mast cell disorder) would prompt extended monitoring based on contemporary risk-stratified guidance?

Short answer
Use a risk‑stratified approach. Patients with a single, promptly treated, mild anaphylaxis episode and complete, sustained resolution may be observed 4–6 hours and discharged with an epinephrine auto‑injector and specialist follow‑up. Patients with one or more moderate risk factors (delayed epinephrine, significant respiratory symptoms, need for >1 epinephrine dose, important comorbidity, poor access to emergency care) merit prolonged observation (commonly 12–24 hours on an monitored ward). Patients with high‑risk features (severe initial shock or hypoxaemia, refractory or recurrent/biphasic anaphylaxis, need for repeated epinephrine or vasopressors, suspected mast cell disorder, unknown/ongoing exposure) should have ICU‑level monitoring for at least 24 hours (often 24–48 hours) with readiness for repeat IM epinephrine, IV vasopressors or continuous epinephrine infusion if required.

Why (pathophysiology and evidence base)
Biphasic and multiphasic courses reflect delayed mediator release or ongoing mast cell activation; most clinically important relapses occur within the first 4–12 hours, but relapses have been reported up to 72–78 hours. Evidence for a single “best” observation interval is limited, so contemporary guidance recommends tailoring duration and level of care to objective risk factors rather than one fixed interval.

Risk factors that prompt extended monitoring (and why)
- Severe initial presentation (profound hypotension, hypoxaemia, airway compromise): indicates large mediator burden and higher risk of recurrence and organ injury. In the case the patient presented with BP 74/50 mm Hg and SpO2 88% — a high‑risk feature.
- Delayed administration of epinephrine (>30–60 minutes from symptom onset): associated with worse outcomes and higher likelihood of protracted or biphasic reactions. This case sought care ~1 hour after symptom onset, then received epinephrine — a reason to observe longer.
- Multiple epinephrine doses or refractory response (need for >1–2 IM doses, IV vasopressors, or continuous infusion): marks more severe/recurrent disease and often motivates ward→ICU care. This patient required four IM epinephrine administrations and therefore meets this criterion.
- Recurrent/biphasic or multiphasic course: by definition requires extended monitoring until a prolonged asymptomatic interval (commonly ≥24 hours) is achieved.
- Suspected or confirmed mast cell disorder (elevated baseline tryptase or systemic mastocytosis): mast cell disorders predispose to more severe, prolonged and recurrent mediator release — delay discharge and obtain specialist input. The case had persistently elevated tryptase and high probability of systemic mastocytosis, supporting ICU observation.
- Unknown trigger or ongoing/slow absorption exposure (e.g., extended‑release drugs, dermal exposure): risk of ongoing mediator stimulus.
- Comorbidity or social factors that increase risk (severe asthma, cardiovascular disease, pregnancy, beta‑blocker use, elderly, inability to self‑inject, limited access to care).

Practical monitoring strategy
- Immediate: continuous pulse oximetry and cardiac monitoring for high‑risk; frequent vital signs (q5–15 min while unstable, then q30–60 min) until stable. Maintain IV access for at least several hours in moderate/high‑risk patients.
- Location: ED observation for low risk; monitored ward (telemetry) for moderate risk; ICU for high risk (recurrent shock, need for vasopressors, ongoing airway concerns, suspected mast cell disorder).
- Readiness: epinephrine (ampoules and auto‑injector) immediately available; oxygen; airway equipment; consider glucagon if beta‑blocked and refractory.
- Consider continuous IV epinephrine infusion in refractory/recurrent cases after discussion with ICU/allergy (weigh risks; requires ICU monitoring). In the presented case continuous infusion was considered but not required.
- Diagnostics: draw serum tryptase as soon as feasible (do not delay treatment), then a second sample at ~1–2 hours after onset (within 4 hours) and a baseline sample ≥24 hours after full clinical resolution to assess for mast cell disorders. The case did not have an early sample during the first episodes but later had elevated tryptase supporting mast cell disease workup.

Medications and adjuncts
- IM epinephrine remains the first‑line therapy (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg in adults). Many auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg; adults >50 kg may be undertreated with 0.3 mg. The case highlights this practical dosing issue.
- Antihistamines are for symptomatic relief; corticosteroids do not reliably prevent biphasic reactions and are not recommended routinely to prevent relapse (use case‑by‑case).
  
Discharge criteria and follow‑up
Before discharge the patient should be symptom‑free for the chosen observation interval, hemodynamically stable off oxygen and vasopressors, able to tolerate oral intake, trained and equipped with an epinephrine auto‑injector, given an anaphylaxis action plan, and have prompt allergy/immunology follow‑up (and mast cell specialist if tryptase elevated). Arrange clear instructions about return precautions because late recurrences, while uncommon, can occur.

Application to this case
This patient had four major risk factors — severe hypotension and hypoxaemia on presentation, ~1‑hour delay to epinephrine, multiple epinephrine doses during recurrent episodes, and a high probability of systemic mastocytosis — justifying ICU‑level monitoring. Her relapses within the first 12 hours validated that decision. She required serial IM epinephrine but not a continuous infusion and was observed until 24 hours free of recurrence before transfer out of ICU and eventual discharge after 3 days with an autoinjector and specialist follow‑up.

Bottom line
There is no single evidence‑based “one size fits all” observation period after anaphylaxis. Use objective risk factors to guide level and length of observation: 4–6 hours for low risk, 12–24 hours for intermediate risk, and ICU admission with at least 24 hours (often 24–48) monitoring for patients with severe initial shock, delayed epinephrine, multiple epinephrine doses, recurrent/biphasic courses, unknown trigger, or suspected mast cell disease — as illustrated by this case.

### [Clinical Pearls] What should be included in discharge planning after multiphasic anaphylaxis—number and dosing of epinephrine auto-injectors for adults over 50 kg, antihistamines, a written emergency action plan, trigger avoidance, and allergy/immunology follow-up—particularly when a mast cell disorder is suspected?

Bottom line
After a multiphasic anaphylactic event you must discharge the patient as if they remain at significant short‑term risk of recurrence. A safe discharge bundle therefore includes: appropriately dosed and multiple epinephrine auto‑injectors with hands‑on teaching, short‑term adjunctive antihistamine therapy (but not as a substitute for epinephrine), a concise written emergency action plan, explicit trigger‑avoidance documentation, and expedited allergy/immunology (±hematology) follow‑up for mast‑cell disorder evaluation.

How this applies to the case
The patient in the report weighed 104 kg, received an initial 0.3 mg auto‑injector in the ED and later required 0.5 mg IM epinephrine doses during relapses. Her course (four anaphylactic episodes, ICU admission, persistently raised tryptase) is a classic example of why discharge planning must anticipate repeat dosing and specialist evaluation.

Practical elements to include in every discharge after multiphasic or severe anaphylaxis

1) Epinephrine auto‑injectors
- Dose principle: target 0.01 mg/kg IM up to a maximum of 0.5 mg. For adults who weigh >50 kg the physiologic target dose is therefore 0.5 mg IM.  
- What to prescribe: at least two epinephrine auto‑injectors for all patients at risk of anaphylaxis. For adults >50 kg prescribe two 0.5 mg devices if available. If a 0.5 mg device is not available locally, give at least two 0.3 mg devices and explain that additional doses are often required.  
- When/how to use: teach immediate administration at first signs of anaphylaxis, call emergency services, and repeat a dose if severe symptoms persist or recur after 5–15 minutes while awaiting EMS. Emphasize that auto‑injectors are life‑saving and that antihistamines/steriods do not substitute for epinephrine.  
- Practical teaching: demonstration with a trainer, storage/expiry checks, and wearing/keeping devices accessible. Because this patient required multiple doses during the admission, make special note that recurrent dosing is a real possibility.

2) Antihistamines and adjunctive meds
- Give a non‑sedating H1 blocker at discharge (e.g., cetirizine/levocetirizine) for relief of cutaneous symptoms and prevention of milder mediator symptoms; the case started levocetirizine on discharge. Explain that antihistamines do NOT treat airway or cardiovascular compromise and do not replace epinephrine.  
- Consider H2 blocker as an adjunct in selected patients (often used in mast cell disease), but use under specialist advice.  
- Routine systemic corticosteroids are no longer recommended solely to prevent biphasic reactions; the case received steroids in hospital but current evidence does not support routine steroid use for biphasic prevention.

3) Written emergency action plan and education
- Provide a one‑page action plan that lists: recognition of anaphylaxis, stepwise instructions to inject epinephrine immediately, when to repeat the dose, calling EMS, positioning, and the need to go to the ED after any epinephrine use because of the risk of biphasic or persistent reactions.  
- Ensure the patient (and family/caregiver) can demonstrate auto‑injector use, knows to seek urgent care after use, and receives a written allergy label/message for medical records and a medical‑alert ID.

4) Trigger avoidance and documentation
- Document the suspected trigger clearly in the discharge summary (in this case clarithromycin) and advise strict avoidance. Provide alternatives (e.g., non‑macrolide antibiotics) if appropriate, or advise that antibiotic selection be discussed with the treating clinician/allergist.  
- Discuss common co‑triggers (alcohol, NSAIDs, temperature/exercise in some patients) and wound‑down activities after severe reaction (avoid strenuous activity/alcohol until cleared). In this case, the team appropriately discontinued clarithromycin and avoided skin provocation testing in hospital.

5) Allergy/immunology and mast‑cell workup
- Urgent specialist follow‑up: arrange appointment with allergist/immunologist (and hematology if mast‑cell disorder suspected) before or promptly after discharge. The multiphasic course, severe hypotension and persistently raised tryptase in the case make this necessary.  
- Tryptase testing schedule: ideally obtain a sample as soon as feasible during the acute phase, a second sample about 1–2 hours (but within 4 hours) after symptom onset (when tryptase usually peaks), and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution to establish baseline. Persistent baseline elevation (WHO minor criterion: baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL) or other features should prompt bone‑marrow evaluation for systemic mastocytosis and KIT testing. The case illustrates pitfalls when early samples are missed and why repeat baseline testing is essential.  
- Allergy testing: defer provocative skin or drug‑challenge testing until the patient has been evaluated by allergy in a controlled setting (risk of provoking severe anaphylaxis). In the index case the team reasonably chose not to perform in‑hospital confirmatory testing.

6) Disposition and monitoring recommendation
- Because multiphasic courses and mast‑cell disorders increase relapse risk, consider prolonged observation or admission (often 24 hours or longer) for patients with severe initial presentation, multiple epinephrine doses, or suspected mast cell disease. The patient in the report remained in hospital and was observed for recurrence before discharge. If discharged home, require at least 24 hours symptom‑free stability, clear instructions, and rapid access to care.

Summary statement for the patient in the report
Given her weight (104 kg), multiphasic course and elevated tryptase, she should have been counseled that adult dosing targets 0.5 mg IM and prescribed at least two appropriately dosed auto‑injectors (preferably 0.5 mg if available), provided non‑sedating H1 antihistamine for symptom control, given a written action plan with hands‑on device training, instructed to avoid clarithromycin and wear medical‑alert identification, and seen urgently by allergy/immunology for formal tryptase baseline testing and consideration of a mast‑cell disorder workup.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Given a remote mild reaction to amoxicillin, a delayed rash on clindamycin, and immediate anaphylaxis after first-dose clarithromycin, how would you assess the most likely culprit drug, the expected cross-reactivity across beta-lactams, lincosamides, and macrolides, and how would you document allergies and select future antibiotics safely?

Short answer: Clarithromycin is the most likely culprit. The clindamycin reaction was typical for a delayed (type IV) exanthem and the remote amoxicillin event was mild and remote — both are unlikely to explain this immediate, life‑threatening anaphylaxis. Macrolide allergy does not imply cross‑reactivity to beta‑lactams or lincosamides, but intra‑macrolide cross‑reactivity is possible and penicillin labels should be reassessed rather than accepted as permanent. Document specifically, refer to allergy/immunology for tailored testing, avoid macrolides going forward, and choose future antibiotics from classes without likely cross‑reactivity unless allergy testing permits otherwise.

Why clarithromycin is the most likely culprit
- Timing and phenotype: symptoms began about 1 hour after the first oral dose of clarithromycin and progressed to hypotension, hypoxaemia, generalized urticaria and facial swelling requiring intramuscular epinephrine and ICU care. That pattern (rapid onset after exposure with cardiovascular and cutaneous features) is classic for an immediate (IgE‑mediated or immediate mast cell–mediated) drug anaphylaxis and implicates the most recent drug exposure — clarithromycin — as the proximate trigger.  
- Prior reactions do not match: the clindamycin event occurred after 5 days and was described as a delayed rash (consistent with a delayed T‑cell–mediated exanthem), and the amoxicillin episode was remote and limited to facial swelling in adolescence (mild and nonspecific). Neither history explains the acute hypotensive, hypoxaemic collapse after clarithromycin.  
- Host factors: the case documents elevated tryptase and a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, which increases severity of anaphylaxis and the risk of multiphasic relapses after a triggering exposure — it amplifies the clinical reaction to a culprit drug but does not change the most likely offending agent in this timeline.

Cross‑reactivity across the classes (practical points)
- Macrolides (clarithromycin, azithromycin, erythromycin): true, predictable immunologic cross‑reactivity within macrolides is poorly defined. Structural similarity can sometimes produce cross‑reactivity, but there are no validated commercial skin tests or reliable in‑vitro IgE assays for macrolides. Clinically, after a severe immediate macrolide anaphylaxis you should assume class allergy and avoid other macrolides until specialist evaluation. If a macrolide is essential and no alternatives exist, desensitization or carefully supervised graded challenge under allergist/ICU support is the only option.  
- Lincosamides (clindamycin): structurally distinct from macrolides. The patient’s clindamycin rash after days of therapy is most consistent with a delayed, non‑immediate reaction. Cross‑reactivity between lincosamides and macrolides is uncommon; the prior clindamycin rash does not make clarithromycin an expected cross‑reaction. A future re‑challenge with clindamycin can be considered in consultation with allergy if the prior rash was mild, but avoid re‑exposure if severe or if testing is contraindicated.  
- Beta‑lactams (amoxicillin and cephalosporins, carbapenems, aztreonam): beta‑lactams are a separate chemical class from macrolides and lincosamides — there is no immunologic cross‑reactivity between macrolides and beta‑lactams. The patient’s remote mild facial swelling to amoxicillin is an historical label that should be reassessed: modern data show low cross‑reactivity between penicillins and cephalosporins (especially when side‑chains differ), and carbapenem cross‑reactivity is very low. Practical approach is to pursue formal penicillin allergy evaluation rather than accept an old, nonspecific label.

How to document allergies in the chart (what to write)
- Be specific, not vague. For clarithromycin: “Clarithromycin — severe immediate hypersensitivity/anaphylaxis on [date]: onset ~1 hour after first 500 mg PO dose; hypotension (BP 74/50), hypoxaemia (SpO2 88%), generalized urticaria, facial swelling; required IM epinephrine and ICU admission; multiphasic relapse. Avoid all macrolides pending allergy/immunology evaluation. Epinephrine auto‑injector prescribed.”  
- For clindamycin: “Clindamycin — delayed rash after 5 days of therapy (non‑immediate cutaneous reaction). Document date and description; consider allergy review if future use contemplated.”  
- For amoxicillin: “Amoxicillin — remote facial swelling in adolescence; mild event, limited detail. Recommend penicillin allergy testing for reassessment.”  
- Also document the elevated tryptase and suspected systemic mastocytosis and that this increases risk of severe anaphylaxis and atypical/multiphasic courses. Advise patient to wear medical alert and carry epinephrine.

Recommended next steps for allergy evaluation and safe prescribing
1. Immediate management and safety: continue to avoid clarithromycin and other macrolides; give the patient an epinephrine auto‑injector and education (done in case). Coordinate with allergy/immunology and hematology (mastocytosis workup).  
2. Penicillin/beta‑lactam strategy: refer for formal penicillin allergy testing (validated skin testing with penicilloyl reagents ± amoxicillin followed, if negative, by an oral challenge). If testing is negative, delabel the penicillin allergy and safely use beta‑lactams thereafter. If testing is positive and a beta‑lactam is essential, choose an agent with a different side chain or consider allergy‑guided alternatives.  
3. Macrolide strategy: recognize that validated skin tests are lacking; after a severe anaphylaxis most allergists will recommend avoidance of macrolides. If a macrolide is essential (no acceptable alternatives), discuss hospital‑based graded challenge or desensitization with allergy and perform it in an ICU setting given the prior severity and suspected mast cell disorder.  
4. Clindamycin strategy: the prior delayed rash was likely non‑immediate. If mild, a supervised graded oral challenge can be considered; if re‑exposure needed for a serious infection, choose a different class if feasible.  
5. If future antibiotic therapy is urgent and allergy testing is not immediately available: choose an alternative class with no likely cross‑reactivity (for skin/soft‑tissue infections: doxycycline or a beta‑lactam after risk assessment; avoid macrolides). Consult infectious diseases and allergy if uncertainty.

One‑minute clinical pearls
- The most recent drug taken immediately before anaphylaxis is usually the culprit — here, clarithromycin after 1 hour with classic anaphylaxis is the likely offender.  
- Differentiate immediate (IgE/ mast‑cell mediated) vs delayed (T‑cell mediated) reactions — clindamycin here looks delayed, amoxicillin history is remote and nonspecific.  
- Macrolide allergy should be assumed to include the class until proven otherwise; there are no standardized, reliable macrolide skin tests and drug challenge/desensitization must be specialist‑led after weighing risks.  
- Don’t leave a vague “antibiotic allergy” in the chart — record agent, reaction type, severity, date, treatment, and recommended avoidance; pursue penicillin testing to delabel when history is remote and reaction was mild.  
- In patients with elevated baseline tryptase or suspected mastocytosis, involve allergy/hematology early — they have higher risk of severe and biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis and testing or desensitization should be done in experienced centers.
