Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Differential Diagnosis] How would you distinguish biphasic anaphylaxis from refractory anaphylaxis and from prolonged anaphylaxis due to ongoing allergen absorption in an ED/ICU patient, and what time course and response patterns support each diagnosis?

Short answer — ask three questions at the bedside: (1) Was there a clear symptom‑free interval after an adequate initial response? (2) Did the patient fail to improve after correct epinephrine dosing? (3) Is there a continuing source of allergen? The answers separate biphasic (and multiphasic) reactions, refractory anaphylaxis, and prolonged anaphylaxis from ongoing absorption, and each has a different expected time course and management implication.

How to distinguish them (clinical features and typical time course)

- Biphasic / multiphasic anaphylaxis
  - Definition/clinical pattern: initial anaphylaxis with apparent clinical resolution, followed hours later by spontaneous recurrence of anaphylaxis without further exposure to the trigger. Multiphasic = more than one recurrence.
  - Time course: most relapses occur within 6–12 hours but can occur up to ~72 hours (rare reports beyond that). Literature gives variable ranges; the case’s relapses all occurred within the first 12 hours, a common window.
  - Response pattern: recurrences usually respond to standard bolus therapy (IM epinephrine, oxygen, fluids); severity may be similar or, as in this report, diminish with successive episodes.
  - Supporting features in the case: the patient had an initial improvement after IM epinephrine, then three relapses within 12 hours that each resolved with additional IM epinephrine — a classic multiphasic pattern and not refractory disease.

- Refractory anaphylaxis
  - Definition/clinical pattern: persistent or progressive anaphylaxis despite correct and timely administration of appropriate epinephrine doses.
  - Time course: usually obvious immediately or shortly after initial treatment — failure to reverse hypotension or bronchospasm despite adequate therapy.
  - Response pattern: poor or absent response to repeated IM epinephrine; requires escalation (IV epinephrine boluses and titrated infusion, advanced hemodynamic support, airway control). Check for contributing causes (β‑blocker use — give glucagon; severe mast cell activation; cardiogenic causes).
  - Why the case was not refractory: each relapse was reversed by IM epinephrine. The episodes were responsive, so escalation to IV infusion was not mandatory.

- Prolonged anaphylaxis from ongoing allergen absorption
  - Definition/clinical pattern: continuous or recurrent symptoms driven by continued antigen exposure or slow/extended absorption (e.g., depot injections, retained oral drug, extended‑release formulations).
  - Time course: persists or recurs as long as antigen exposure/absorption continues — hours to days depending on the agent and kinetics.
  - Response pattern: transient improvement after epinephrine that recurs predictably as epinephrine wears off while antigen continues to stimulate mast cells; may require continuous IV epinephrine until the antigen is removed/cleared. Management must include source control (stop medication, remove contact, consider activated charcoal if appropriate and within window).
  - Why this was unlikely in the case: the patient had a single 500‑mg oral dose of clarithromycin and no further exposures; relapses clustered in the early post‑exposure period and were episodic rather than a continuously refractory course. That pattern favors multiphasic biphasic anaphylaxis rather than ongoing antigen absorption.

Practical bedside steps to distinguish and act (ED/ICU algorithm)
1. Verify epinephrine was given correctly (IM 0.01 mg/kg to 0.5 mg in adults; many auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg and may underdose >50 kg). If initial dosing was delayed or underdosed, repeat an appropriate IM dose — delayed/inadequate epinephrine increases risk of biphasic relapse.
2. Look for a symptom‑free interval. If there is a clear period of clinical resolution followed by recurrence, label as biphasic/multiphasic unless ongoing exposure is identified.
3. If there is no meaningful improvement after adequate epinephrine, treat as refractory: escalate to IV epinephrine infusion (and IV boluses as needed), secure airway, invasive monitoring, ICU care, and consider glucagon if the patient is on β‑blockers.
4. If recurrence is due to suspected ongoing absorption (recent oral ingestion, depot injection, retained food), pursue source control (stop the drug, remove contact, consider activated charcoal if within the appropriate time window), and be prepared for prolonged hemodynamic support including a continuous epinephrine infusion until absorption abates.
5. Obtain serial tryptase samples to document mast cell activation and to assess for an underlying mast cell disorder — draw one as soon as feasible, a second at about 1–2 hours from symptom onset (within 4 hours), and a baseline ≥24 hours after full resolution. Persistent elevated baseline tryptase (as in this report) raises suspicion for systemic mastocytosis and a propensity for severe or multiphasic courses.

Management implications from the distinctions
- Biphasic/multiphasic: treat each recurrence with standard anaphylaxis measures (IM epinephrine first), admit for extended observation based on risk (severe initial reaction, multiple epinephrine doses, comorbid asthma or mast cell disorder → lean toward 24 hours ICU/telemetry). Routine corticosteroids do not have solid evidence to prevent biphasic reactions.
- Refractory: immediate escalation to IV epinephrine infusion, ICU care, consider adjuncts (vasopressors, inotropes, glucagon if on β‑blockers), early airway control.
- Ongoing absorption: treat as prolonged antigen stimulation — remove exposure, consider GI decontamination when appropriate, and give continuous hemodynamic support until the antigen is cleared.

Case teaching point
- The patient’s course — rapid onset after first clarithromycin dose, initial response to IM epinephrine, then three relapses within 12 hours each responsive to additional IM epinephrine and with decreasing intensity — fits multiphasic (biphasic) anaphylaxis rather than refractory disease or continued antigen exposure. Contributing factors included initial use of a 0.3‑mg auto‑injector (potential underdosing in a 104‑kg adult) and an elevated baseline tryptase (raising concern for a mast cell disorder that predisposes to recurrent episodes). ICU admission after the first relapse and serial monitoring, with consideration of continuous infusion only if boluses failed, was a reasonable, guideline‑concordant approach.

Bottom line: history of a symptom‑free interval and full responsiveness to appropriate epinephrine favor biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis; failure to respond to correct epinephrine defines refractory anaphylaxis and mandates early escalation; evidence of persistent antigen exposure points toward prolonged anaphylaxis from ongoing absorption and requires source control plus prolonged hemodynamic support. Use serial tryptase and patient risk factors to refine prognosis and disposition.
