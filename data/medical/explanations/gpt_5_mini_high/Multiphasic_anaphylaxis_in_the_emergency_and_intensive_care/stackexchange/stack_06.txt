Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Pathophysiology] Which clinical and laboratory features in this patient raise suspicion for a clonal mast cell disorder, and what are the WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis? How would you distinguish systemic mastocytosis from other causes of elevated baseline tryptase such as hereditary alpha-tryptasemia or myeloid neoplasms?

Short answer: In this patient the clinical red flags for a clonal mast cell disorder are an unusually severe, multiphasic anaphylactic course requiring repeated epinephrine doses and a persistently elevated baseline serum tryptase, accompanied by organomegaly (hepatomegaly on ultrasound) and non‑specific systemic symptoms (neuropsychiatric complaints). Systemic mastocytosis (SM) is diagnosed by the WHO algorithm: 1 major criterion (multifocal dense mast cell infiltrates ≥15 mast cells/aggregate in bone marrow or extracutaneous tissue) plus ≥1 minor criterion, or ≥3 minor criteria. The four WHO minor criteria are: (1) >25% of mast cells are spindle‑shaped or atypical (or >25% atypical mast cells on aspirate); (2) detection of a activating KIT codon 816 mutation (usually D816V) in marrow, blood, or other extracutaneous organ; (3) aberrant mast cell immunophenotype with expression of CD2 and/or CD25 in addition to CD117; (4) a persistently elevated baseline total serum tryptase >20 ng/mL (this minor criterion is not applied if an associated myeloid neoplasm is present).

Why these features matter (clinical reasoning and pathophysiology)
- Clonal mast cell expansion (most commonly driven by KIT D816V) increases mast cell burden and lowers the threshold for mediator release; patients therefore have higher baseline tryptase (tryptase is released constitutively by a larger mast cell pool) and are at risk for severe or recurrent anaphylaxis. Multiphasic or unusually severe anaphylaxis after trivial exposure should prompt this consideration.  
- Organomegaly (hepatomegaly, splenomegaly), cytopenias or other “B/C‑findings” raise pretest probability for systemic involvement. In this case the team found hepatomegaly and a persistently raised baseline tryptase — both increase suspicion even though the bone marrow specimen was non‑diagnostic.

How to distinguish SM from other causes of elevated baseline tryptase
- Hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia (HαT): germline copy‑number gains of TPSAB1 raise baseline tryptase without clonal mast cell proliferation. HαT is diagnosed by genomic testing for increased TPSAB1 copy number (ddPCR/qPCR or specialized assays). Bone marrow morphology is normal, mast cells lack aberrant CD25/CD2 expression, and KIT D816V is absent. Clinically, HαT can increase anaphylaxis risk and may coexist with SM — so one can have both conditions. Because HαT is familial, a family history of elevated tryptase or similar symptoms is a clue.  
- Myeloid neoplasms and SM‑AHN (SM with an associated hematologic neoplasm): some myeloid neoplasms can raise tryptase and can co‑exist with or produce overlapping findings. If an associated myeloid neoplasm is present, the tryptase >20 ng/mL minor criterion cannot be used to support SM. Distinguish by peripheral blood counts, marrow morphology/cytogenetics/NGS for other myeloid driver mutations, and by demonstrating true mast cell aggregates with aberrant phenotype and/or KIT mutation within the mast cell population. In SM‑AHN the diagnostic workup must document both the mast cell clonality (by WHO criteria) and the independent myeloid neoplasm.

Practical evaluation steps (how this applies to the presented case)
- Confirm a true baseline tryptase: obtain a sample >24 hours after full clinical recovery (the case had imperfect timing — first sample 1 hour after a later episode, second 27 hours after last recurrence — but baseline remained elevated).  
- Document mast cell activation when appropriate: an acute rise consistent with mast cell activation is often defined as an increase > (1.2 × baseline + 2) ng/mL measured within 1–4 hours of symptom onset.  
- Molecular and tissue workup: do sensitive testing for KIT D816V in peripheral blood (allele‑specific PCR or ddPCR) and, if negative or inconclusive, pursue a properly obtained bone marrow trephine with adequate tissue for histology, tryptase/CD117/CD25 immunostains, flow cytometry, and molecular testing. The major WHO criterion requires a marrow trephine showing multifocal dense aggregates (≥15 cells/aggregate). Because the case’s initial marrow was limited and only two minor criteria were documented, repeating marrow with full studies is the appropriate next step.  
- Test for HαT (TPSAB1 copy number) if baseline tryptase is persistently elevated and marrow and KIT testing are negative or discordant, or if there is a family history of elevated tryptase.  
- Evaluate for an associated myeloid neoplasm (CBC, smear, cytogenetics/NGS) because its presence changes interpretation of the tryptase criterion and affects management.

Clinical pearl: persistent baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL or unusually severe/recurrent anaphylaxis should prompt hematologic/mast cell workup; bone marrow trephine with CD25 staining and sensitive KIT testing are required to make (or exclude) SM, and TPSAB1 copy‑number testing will separate hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia (or identify a coexisting condition) — all important because these diagnoses change counseling, monitoring and preventive strategies for future anaphylaxis.
