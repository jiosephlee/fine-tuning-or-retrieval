Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Management] How would you determine the duration and level of post-anaphylaxis observation, and which risk factors (eg, delayed epinephrine, severe initial presentation, multiple epinephrine doses, unknown trigger, suspected mast cell disorder) would prompt extended monitoring based on contemporary risk-stratified guidance?

Short answer
Use a risk‑stratified approach. Patients with a single, promptly treated, mild anaphylaxis episode and complete, sustained resolution may be observed 4–6 hours and discharged with an epinephrine auto‑injector and specialist follow‑up. Patients with one or more moderate risk factors (delayed epinephrine, significant respiratory symptoms, need for >1 epinephrine dose, important comorbidity, poor access to emergency care) merit prolonged observation (commonly 12–24 hours on an monitored ward). Patients with high‑risk features (severe initial shock or hypoxaemia, refractory or recurrent/biphasic anaphylaxis, need for repeated epinephrine or vasopressors, suspected mast cell disorder, unknown/ongoing exposure) should have ICU‑level monitoring for at least 24 hours (often 24–48 hours) with readiness for repeat IM epinephrine, IV vasopressors or continuous epinephrine infusion if required.

Why (pathophysiology and evidence base)
Biphasic and multiphasic courses reflect delayed mediator release or ongoing mast cell activation; most clinically important relapses occur within the first 4–12 hours, but relapses have been reported up to 72–78 hours. Evidence for a single “best” observation interval is limited, so contemporary guidance recommends tailoring duration and level of care to objective risk factors rather than one fixed interval.

Risk factors that prompt extended monitoring (and why)
- Severe initial presentation (profound hypotension, hypoxaemia, airway compromise): indicates large mediator burden and higher risk of recurrence and organ injury. In the case the patient presented with BP 74/50 mm Hg and SpO2 88% — a high‑risk feature.
- Delayed administration of epinephrine (>30–60 minutes from symptom onset): associated with worse outcomes and higher likelihood of protracted or biphasic reactions. This case sought care ~1 hour after symptom onset, then received epinephrine — a reason to observe longer.
- Multiple epinephrine doses or refractory response (need for >1–2 IM doses, IV vasopressors, or continuous infusion): marks more severe/recurrent disease and often motivates ward→ICU care. This patient required four IM epinephrine administrations and therefore meets this criterion.
- Recurrent/biphasic or multiphasic course: by definition requires extended monitoring until a prolonged asymptomatic interval (commonly ≥24 hours) is achieved.
- Suspected or confirmed mast cell disorder (elevated baseline tryptase or systemic mastocytosis): mast cell disorders predispose to more severe, prolonged and recurrent mediator release — delay discharge and obtain specialist input. The case had persistently elevated tryptase and high probability of systemic mastocytosis, supporting ICU observation.
- Unknown trigger or ongoing/slow absorption exposure (e.g., extended‑release drugs, dermal exposure): risk of ongoing mediator stimulus.
- Comorbidity or social factors that increase risk (severe asthma, cardiovascular disease, pregnancy, beta‑blocker use, elderly, inability to self‑inject, limited access to care).

Practical monitoring strategy
- Immediate: continuous pulse oximetry and cardiac monitoring for high‑risk; frequent vital signs (q5–15 min while unstable, then q30–60 min) until stable. Maintain IV access for at least several hours in moderate/high‑risk patients.
- Location: ED observation for low risk; monitored ward (telemetry) for moderate risk; ICU for high risk (recurrent shock, need for vasopressors, ongoing airway concerns, suspected mast cell disorder).
- Readiness: epinephrine (ampoules and auto‑injector) immediately available; oxygen; airway equipment; consider glucagon if beta‑blocked and refractory.
- Consider continuous IV epinephrine infusion in refractory/recurrent cases after discussion with ICU/allergy (weigh risks; requires ICU monitoring). In the presented case continuous infusion was considered but not required.
- Diagnostics: draw serum tryptase as soon as feasible (do not delay treatment), then a second sample at ~1–2 hours after onset (within 4 hours) and a baseline sample ≥24 hours after full clinical resolution to assess for mast cell disorders. The case did not have an early sample during the first episodes but later had elevated tryptase supporting mast cell disease workup.

Medications and adjuncts
- IM epinephrine remains the first‑line therapy (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg in adults). Many auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg; adults >50 kg may be undertreated with 0.3 mg. The case highlights this practical dosing issue.
- Antihistamines are for symptomatic relief; corticosteroids do not reliably prevent biphasic reactions and are not recommended routinely to prevent relapse (use case‑by‑case).
  
Discharge criteria and follow‑up
Before discharge the patient should be symptom‑free for the chosen observation interval, hemodynamically stable off oxygen and vasopressors, able to tolerate oral intake, trained and equipped with an epinephrine auto‑injector, given an anaphylaxis action plan, and have prompt allergy/immunology follow‑up (and mast cell specialist if tryptase elevated). Arrange clear instructions about return precautions because late recurrences, while uncommon, can occur.

Application to this case
This patient had four major risk factors — severe hypotension and hypoxaemia on presentation, ~1‑hour delay to epinephrine, multiple epinephrine doses during recurrent episodes, and a high probability of systemic mastocytosis — justifying ICU‑level monitoring. Her relapses within the first 12 hours validated that decision. She required serial IM epinephrine but not a continuous infusion and was observed until 24 hours free of recurrence before transfer out of ICU and eventual discharge after 3 days with an autoinjector and specialist follow‑up.

Bottom line
There is no single evidence‑based “one size fits all” observation period after anaphylaxis. Use objective risk factors to guide level and length of observation: 4–6 hours for low risk, 12–24 hours for intermediate risk, and ICU admission with at least 24 hours (often 24–48) monitoring for patients with severe initial shock, delayed epinephrine, multiple epinephrine doses, recurrent/biphasic courses, unknown trigger, or suspected mast cell disease — as illustrated by this case.
