Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Management] What does current evidence show about corticosteroids in acute anaphylaxis—do they prevent biphasic reactions or improve outcomes—and in what limited situations, if any, might you still administer them?

Short answer: routine corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic (or multiphasic) anaphylaxis and have not been shown to improve clinically important outcomes. They should not be used as a substitute for, or a reason to delay, first‑line therapy (epinephrine, oxygen, fluids, airway support) or for shortened observation. Steroids remain an occasional, targeted adjunct in a few specific situations only.

Why: corticosteroids act by altering gene transcription and reducing late inflammatory mediator production — an effect that takes hours to develop. Biphasic recurrences are driven by ongoing mediator release or re‑emergence of anaphylactic physiology over the next hours and, importantly, occur unpredictably. High‑quality clinical data do not show that giving systemic corticosteroids at presentation reduces the risk of a later biphasic reaction. As the case authors note, a systematic review of 22 studies found only one study supporting steroid benefit and 21 showing no effect, and observational data have even linked prehospital steroid use with greater downstream resource use (higher odds of ward/ICU admission). Contemporary guidelines therefore advise against routine steroid administration for the purpose of preventing biphasic anaphylaxis.

What the case illustrates: in this patient steroids (prednisone IV daily and a single dose dexamethasone IV) were administered after the first relapse because of traditional practice, yet she still had additional relapses that required repeated IM epinephrine. That clinical course is a practical example of why steroids cannot be relied upon to prevent recurrence and why continued monitoring and readiness to give epinephrine (including escalation to infusion if needed) remain the priorities.

When to consider corticosteroids (limited indications)
- Concomitant severe asthma/bronchospasm: give systemic steroids as you would for an acute asthma exacerbation to reduce risk of late bronchospasm (adjunctive to epinephrine and bronchodilators).  
- Known adrenal insufficiency or chronic corticosteroid therapy: give stress‑dose glucocorticoids to avoid adrenal crisis.  
- Protracted or refractory anaphylaxis as part of specialist‑directed care: in rare, prolonged courses steroids may be used adjunctively while other measures (repeated IM epinephrine, epinephrine infusion, airway management) are implemented. Even here they are adjuncts, not primary therapy.  
- Persistent cutaneous symptoms after stabilization: some clinicians give short steroid courses for protracted urticaria/angioedema, recognizing symptom relief is the goal rather than prevention of biphasic shock.

Practical points for clinical teams
- Never delay epinephrine to give steroids or antihistamines. Epinephrine remains the only life‑saving immediate therapy.  
- If steroids are given, document the indication and dose; do not use them as a replacement for observation. Observation should be risk‑stratified (severe initial reaction, hypotension, respiratory compromise, delayed epinephrine, multiple epinephrine doses → longer monitoring/consider ICU).  
- Be mindful of steroid harms (hyperglycemia — salient here for this patient with diabetes — infection risk, possible increased hospital stays) and local protocol variability (the case received both prednisone and dexamethasone because of differing ward protocols). Standardize local practice to avoid duplication.  
- For recurrent or refractory episodes, escalate treatment promptly (repeat IM epinephrine, consider IV epinephrine infusion and ICU care) rather than relying on corticosteroids to prevent further relapses.

Bottom line (clinical pearl): corticosteroids are not proven to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis or improve outcomes and should not be given routinely for that purpose. Use them only for well‑defined, adjunctive indications (severe asthma, adrenal insufficiency, or as part of specialist management of protracted/refractory cases), and always prioritize rapid epinephrine, appropriate monitoring, and escalation of supportive care.
