Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] What should be included in discharge planning after multiphasic anaphylaxis—number and dosing of epinephrine auto-injectors for adults over 50 kg, antihistamines, a written emergency action plan, trigger avoidance, and allergy/immunology follow-up—particularly when a mast cell disorder is suspected?

Bottom line
After a multiphasic anaphylactic event you must discharge the patient as if they remain at significant short‑term risk of recurrence. A safe discharge bundle therefore includes: appropriately dosed and multiple epinephrine auto‑injectors with hands‑on teaching, short‑term adjunctive antihistamine therapy (but not as a substitute for epinephrine), a concise written emergency action plan, explicit trigger‑avoidance documentation, and expedited allergy/immunology (±hematology) follow‑up for mast‑cell disorder evaluation.

How this applies to the case
The patient in the report weighed 104 kg, received an initial 0.3 mg auto‑injector in the ED and later required 0.5 mg IM epinephrine doses during relapses. Her course (four anaphylactic episodes, ICU admission, persistently raised tryptase) is a classic example of why discharge planning must anticipate repeat dosing and specialist evaluation.

Practical elements to include in every discharge after multiphasic or severe anaphylaxis

1) Epinephrine auto‑injectors
- Dose principle: target 0.01 mg/kg IM up to a maximum of 0.5 mg. For adults who weigh >50 kg the physiologic target dose is therefore 0.5 mg IM.  
- What to prescribe: at least two epinephrine auto‑injectors for all patients at risk of anaphylaxis. For adults >50 kg prescribe two 0.5 mg devices if available. If a 0.5 mg device is not available locally, give at least two 0.3 mg devices and explain that additional doses are often required.  
- When/how to use: teach immediate administration at first signs of anaphylaxis, call emergency services, and repeat a dose if severe symptoms persist or recur after 5–15 minutes while awaiting EMS. Emphasize that auto‑injectors are life‑saving and that antihistamines/steriods do not substitute for epinephrine.  
- Practical teaching: demonstration with a trainer, storage/expiry checks, and wearing/keeping devices accessible. Because this patient required multiple doses during the admission, make special note that recurrent dosing is a real possibility.

2) Antihistamines and adjunctive meds
- Give a non‑sedating H1 blocker at discharge (e.g., cetirizine/levocetirizine) for relief of cutaneous symptoms and prevention of milder mediator symptoms; the case started levocetirizine on discharge. Explain that antihistamines do NOT treat airway or cardiovascular compromise and do not replace epinephrine.  
- Consider H2 blocker as an adjunct in selected patients (often used in mast cell disease), but use under specialist advice.  
- Routine systemic corticosteroids are no longer recommended solely to prevent biphasic reactions; the case received steroids in hospital but current evidence does not support routine steroid use for biphasic prevention.

3) Written emergency action plan and education
- Provide a one‑page action plan that lists: recognition of anaphylaxis, stepwise instructions to inject epinephrine immediately, when to repeat the dose, calling EMS, positioning, and the need to go to the ED after any epinephrine use because of the risk of biphasic or persistent reactions.  
- Ensure the patient (and family/caregiver) can demonstrate auto‑injector use, knows to seek urgent care after use, and receives a written allergy label/message for medical records and a medical‑alert ID.

4) Trigger avoidance and documentation
- Document the suspected trigger clearly in the discharge summary (in this case clarithromycin) and advise strict avoidance. Provide alternatives (e.g., non‑macrolide antibiotics) if appropriate, or advise that antibiotic selection be discussed with the treating clinician/allergist.  
- Discuss common co‑triggers (alcohol, NSAIDs, temperature/exercise in some patients) and wound‑down activities after severe reaction (avoid strenuous activity/alcohol until cleared). In this case, the team appropriately discontinued clarithromycin and avoided skin provocation testing in hospital.

5) Allergy/immunology and mast‑cell workup
- Urgent specialist follow‑up: arrange appointment with allergist/immunologist (and hematology if mast‑cell disorder suspected) before or promptly after discharge. The multiphasic course, severe hypotension and persistently raised tryptase in the case make this necessary.  
- Tryptase testing schedule: ideally obtain a sample as soon as feasible during the acute phase, a second sample about 1–2 hours (but within 4 hours) after symptom onset (when tryptase usually peaks), and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution to establish baseline. Persistent baseline elevation (WHO minor criterion: baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL) or other features should prompt bone‑marrow evaluation for systemic mastocytosis and KIT testing. The case illustrates pitfalls when early samples are missed and why repeat baseline testing is essential.  
- Allergy testing: defer provocative skin or drug‑challenge testing until the patient has been evaluated by allergy in a controlled setting (risk of provoking severe anaphylaxis). In the index case the team reasonably chose not to perform in‑hospital confirmatory testing.

6) Disposition and monitoring recommendation
- Because multiphasic courses and mast‑cell disorders increase relapse risk, consider prolonged observation or admission (often 24 hours or longer) for patients with severe initial presentation, multiple epinephrine doses, or suspected mast cell disease. The patient in the report remained in hospital and was observed for recurrence before discharge. If discharged home, require at least 24 hours symptom‑free stability, clear instructions, and rapid access to care.

Summary statement for the patient in the report
Given her weight (104 kg), multiphasic course and elevated tryptase, she should have been counseled that adult dosing targets 0.5 mg IM and prescribed at least two appropriately dosed auto‑injectors (preferably 0.5 mg if available), provided non‑sedating H1 antihistamine for symptom control, given a written action plan with hands‑on device training, instructed to avoid clarithromycin and wear medical‑alert identification, and seen urgently by allergy/immunology for formal tryptase baseline testing and consideration of a mast‑cell disorder workup.
