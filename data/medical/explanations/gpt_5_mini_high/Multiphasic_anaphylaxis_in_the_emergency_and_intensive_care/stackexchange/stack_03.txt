Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Management] What are your indications to initiate a titrated intravenous epinephrine infusion for anaphylaxis, how would you start and titrate it, and what monitoring and adjunctive therapies are required?

Bottom line
- A titrated intravenous (IV) epinephrine infusion is not first‑line for routine anaphylaxis (IM epinephrine remains first and most important step). Use a continuous IV infusion when the patient has life‑threatening circulatory or respiratory compromise that is not controlled by appropriate IM epinephrine and supportive measures, or when the patient requires repeated urgent IM doses (recurrent/multiphasic or “refractory” anaphylaxis) and needs a reliably titratable vasoactive support in a monitored setting (ICU). The case you read illustrates this dilemma: repeated IM doses (four episodes in 12 hours) prompted discussion of starting an infusion even though it was ultimately not required.

Indications to start an IV epinephrine infusion
- Refractory anaphylaxis: persistent or worsening hypotension, shock, severe bronchospasm, or airway compromise despite
  - appropriate IM epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg for adults; many auto‑injectors are only 0.3 mg and may underdose larger adults),
  - adequate IV fluid resuscitation,
  - repeat IM boluses as needed.  
- Rapidly relapsing or multiphasic anaphylaxis in which the patient requires repeated IM injections in a short period (several injections over hours), making intermittent IM dosing impractical or unsafe.  
- Poor peripheral perfusion such that IM absorption is unreliable (profound shock).  
- Cardiac arrest: IV epinephrine boluses per ACLS (different indication/dosing) — continuous infusion applies to resuscitated or refractory shock, not to the arrest bolus itself.  
- Special situations that lower the threshold for infusion: concurrent beta‑blocker therapy, suspected mast cell disorder with ongoing mediator release (as in the case where systemic mastocytosis was suspected).

How to give it (practical, commonly used approach)
1) Before infusion — immediate steps
  - Continue/rescue with IM epinephrine if not already given (lateral thigh).  
  - If circulatory collapse exists, give small IV boluses while preparing infusion: 50–100 mcg (0.05–0.1 mg) IV of epinephrine (use 1:10,000 = 0.1 mg/mL), given slowly and repeated every 1–2 minutes as needed to restore perfusion. (If cardiac arrest, follow ACLS 1 mg 1:10,000 boluses.)
  - Establish two large‑bore IV lines and give rapid crystalloid resuscitation (adult initial 1–2 L bolus or 20 mL/kg as a guide).

2) Prepare infusion (two common hospital recipes)
  - Option A (more concentrated, smaller fluid volume): 1 mg epinephrine (1:1000, 1 mL) diluted to 100 mL NS → 10 mcg/mL.
  - Option B (less concentrated, easier low‑rate titration): 1 mg in 250 mL NS → 4 mcg/mL.
  - Choose the concentration that fits your pump protocols and desired ml/hr steps.

3) Start and titrate
  - Weight‑based starting guide: 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min (for a 70‑kg adult ≈ 3.5–7 mcg/min).  
  - Absolute starting alternative commonly used: 3–10 mcg/min (start lower for milder shock, higher if severe). Example using 1 mg/100 mL (10 mcg/mL): 5 mcg/min = 0.5 mL/min = 30 mL/hr. Using 1 mg/250 mL (4 mcg/mL), 5 mcg/min = 1.25 mL/min = 75 mL/hr.  
  - Titrate to effect every few minutes (eg, increase by 1–2 mcg/min or by 0.02–0.05 mcg/kg/min), guided by blood pressure, mental status, urine output and oxygenation. Aim for restoration of perfusion (systolic BP >90–100 mm Hg, improving lactate/mental status/urine output).  
  - When stable, down‑titrate gradually. Avoid abrupt cessation if prolonged infusion has been used — monitor for recurrence.

Hazards and cautions
- IV epinephrine carries risk of tachyarrhythmia, myocardial ischemia, severe hypertension and lactic acidosis; use only on a pump in a monitored setting (resuscitation bay or ICU).  
- In patients with coronary artery disease or the elderly, be especially vigilant for ischemia.  
- If patient is receiving beta‑blockers and is unresponsive to epinephrine, give IV glucagon (bolus 1–5 mg IV over 5 minutes then consider infusion) while arranging cardiovascular support — glucagon reverses beta‑blockade‑related epinephrine resistance.

Monitoring required
- Continuous ECG monitoring and continuous pulse oximetry; noninvasive BP every 1–2 minutes while titrating.  
- Consider invasive arterial monitoring for rapidly changing hemodynamics or frequent titration.  
- Frequent clinical reassessment of airway, work of breathing, skin findings, mental status and urine output.  
- Maintain large‑bore IV access; continue aggressive crystalloid resuscitation as needed.  
- Labs: bedside glucose, arterial blood gas/lactate, electrolytes, and troponin if concern for ischemia. Obtain tryptase samples for suspected mast cell activation (ideally ASAP, 1–2 hours after onset, and a baseline ≥24 hours after resolution) — the case highlighted missed early sampling but later elevated baseline tryptase supporting mast cell disorder.

Adjunctive therapies (do not replace epinephrine)
- High‑flow oxygen, repeated IV crystalloid boluses.  
- Nebulized inhaled bronchodilator for bronchospasm.  
- H1 antihistamine (IV) and H2 blocker for cutaneous symptoms (symptom control only).  
- Corticosteroids: commonly given in practice (as in the case) but evidence does not show prevention of biphasic reactions; use selectively for protracted or multiphasic courses or when there are concomitant asthma/COPD exacerbations.  
- If epinephrine alone fails and vasoplegia predominates, consider addition or substitution of norepinephrine in ICU settings per critical care guidance.

Applying this to the presented case
- The patient had multiphasic anaphylaxis with repeated IM epinephrine doses and ICU admission for monitoring. That pattern (multiple recurrences requiring repeated IM doses) is a common and reasonable trigger to consider starting a continuous infusion because it provides reliable, titratable hemodynamic support and reduces the need for repeated boluses. In this patient the episodes diminished in intensity and no infusion was ultimately required; corticosteroids and antihistamines were given after the first relapse and close ICU monitoring allowed rapid rescue with IM epinephrine. In a similar future patient with ongoing or worsening hypotension or continued frequent relapses, I would start small IV boluses (50–100 mcg) to stabilise and then institute a monitored infusion (eg, 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min; adjust to effect), with arterial monitoring, aggressive fluids, consideration of glucagon if on beta‑blockers, and plan for prolonged observation and mast cell workup.

Clinical pearl
- IM epinephrine first; escalate to IV boluses then to a continuous epinephrine infusion when the patient is refractory, relapsing frequently, or has poor IM absorption — but only in a monitored setting with pumps, continuous monitoring and concurrent fluid resuscitation.
