Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Watching and Waiting: A Pragmatic, Risk‑Stratified Observation Plan After Anaphylaxis

We recently read a striking case that brings the practical question back to the bedside: a woman in her 60s developed multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single 500 mg dose of oral clarithromycin. She presented in shock with hypoxaemia, improved after intramuscular epinephrine, then had repeated relapses over the next 12 hours requiring additional IM epinephrine (four episodes in total). Tryptase was persistently elevated and systemic mastocytosis was suspected. Teams considered options ranging from continuous IV epinephrine infusion to simply “watching and waiting” — but there are no hard rules. This is exactly the clinical dilemma emergency and inpatient clinicians face: who can safely go home after a single rapid response, and who belongs in monitored beds?

Below I translate common risk factors into a simple, pragmatic observation and disposition plan you can use at the bedside — grounded in the issues raised by that case.

## Why observation matters (briefly)
Biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis is the recurrence of allergic symptoms after an apparent clinical recovery and without further allergen exposure. Reported incidence varies widely, but most series cluster around 4–6%. Recurrences usually occur within 24 hours but have been reported later. Because biphasic/multiphasic courses are unpredictable, observation after anaphylaxis is reasonable — but blanket admission for everyone is neither practical nor necessary. The trick is risk‑stratification.

## A pragmatic, risk‑stratified observation plan
Below is a working approach I use to guide observation time and disposition. The times are practical, not absolute — use clinical judgement and local resources.

Low risk — observe 2–4 hours
- Who: patients with a single, rapid, complete clinical recovery after one correctly administered IM epinephrine dose; clear and removed/avoided trigger (e.g., known food and no ongoing exposure); no airway compromise, no hypotension or hypoxaemia; no significant comorbidity; reliable social support and ability to return.
- What to watch for: stable vitals, no recurrent symptoms during observation, and ability to tolerate oral intake and ambulate.
- Disposition: discharge with instructions, two autoinjectors (dose appropriate for weight), written action plan and early allergy follow‑up.

Intermediate risk — observe 6–12 hours (ED or short‑stay)
- Who: unknown trigger; required a second IM epinephrine dose or needed prolonged supportive care (IV fluids, supplemental oxygen); mild/moderate cardiopulmonary comorbidity (well‑controlled asthma, treated cardiovascular disease); presentation with prominent GI symptoms, systemic urticaria but no sustained shock.
- What to watch for: recurrence in the first 6–12 hours is more likely in this group; consider longer observation if recurrent symptoms are late during the window.
- Disposition: extended observation in ED or short‑stay unit; arrange allergy follow‑up.

High risk — observe 12–24 hours (often admission; telemetry/ICU as indicated)
- Who: initial shock or hypoxaemia (like our case), need for multiple epinephrine doses, delayed epinephrine administration, oral/long‑acting trigger (oral medication, depot preparations), suspected or known mast cell disorder, prior biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis, severe asthma or significant cardiac disease, events occurring at night or in patients who live remotely or cannot return quickly.
- What to watch for: recurrent need for epinephrine, hemodynamic instability, progressive airway involvement. Consider step‑down only after 24 hours symptom‑free and stable.
- Disposition: admit to monitored bed; if repeated relapses or refractory symptoms, escalate to ICU and consider continuous IV epinephrine infusion under experienced supervision.

Use the observation window after "full clinical resolution"
- Practical tip: start your observation clock after the patient has achieved complete clinical resolution (no respiratory distress, normal vitals, no progressive urticaria) following their last epinephrine dose. A patient who has persistent minor urticaria without systemic features isn't necessarily “resolved.” Aim for a symptom‑free period before counting your observation time.

## When to escalate: when “watching” becomes unsafe
If a patient requires multiple additional IM epinephrine doses, has recurrent hypotension or hypoxaemia, progressive airway compromise, or persistent hemodynamic instability despite fluids and IM epinephrine, escalate. That often means ICU, continuous IV epinephrine infusion and invasive monitoring. The case team considered continuous infusion but ultimately did not need it — the patient’s relapses decreased in severity and resolved within the monitoring window. Still, have a low threshold for escalation when episodes are frequent or worsening.

## Practical, evidence‑aware points that matter at the bedside
Epinephrine dosing and delivery
- IM epinephrine is first‑line: 0.01 mg/kg (max 0.5 mg) into the mid‑anterolateral thigh. Many adults should get 0.5 mg. Auto‑injectors are invaluable for rapid administration, but many devices deliver only 0.3 mg; for adults >50 kg this is potentially subtherapeutic — be prepared to give 0.5 mg IM with a syringe if needed in the ED.
- Early epinephrine is key. Delayed administration is associated with worse outcomes and may increase the chance of biphasic reactions.

Steroids and antihistamines
- Antihistamines can help cutaneous symptoms but do NOT treat airway or hemodynamic compromise.
- Corticosteroids have not been convincingly shown to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis and are no longer recommended routinely for that purpose. Use steroids selectively (e.g., for protracted urticaria or coexistent asthma treatment per usual indications), not as a reflex to “prevent” relapse.

Tryptase and mast cell disorders
- If you suspect a mast cell disorder (severe anaphylaxis, idiopathic or recurrent severe reactions, or unusual triggers), measure serum tryptase: obtain an acute sample as soon as feasible, a second sample ~1–2 hours after onset (within 4 hours if possible), and a baseline sample ≥24 hours after complete clinical resolution. Persistent elevation of baseline tryptase merits specialist referral. In the case above, imperfect timing of tryptase samples complicated interpretation; nonetheless persistent elevation raised concern for systemic mastocytosis and influenced the team’s decision to observe and refer.

Practical constraints and shared decision‑making
- Observation decisions are not only clinical: nighttime events, long travel times, poor access to care, or inability to administer epinephrine correctly at home push you toward longer observation or admission.
- Discuss risk and rationale with the patient and family. Document shared decision making if you discharge someone at intermediate risk.

## Discharge essentials (what every patient should leave with)
Before discharge, ensure the patient:
- Has been stable for the planned observation window, symptom‑free and tolerating oral intake.
- Receives at least two epinephrine autoinjectors (dose appropriate for weight) and hands‑on training on their use.
- Has a written anaphylaxis action plan and brief verbal reinforcement of when/how to use epinephrine and when to call emergency services.
- Has antihistamines for symptomatic relief if needed and understands that steroids do not reliably prevent relapse.
- Has timely allergy/immunology follow‑up arranged (to identify trigger and, if indicated, investigate mast cell disorders).
- Understands return precautions and when to come back (any recurrence of respiratory symptoms, hypotension, or symptoms unresponsive to epinephrine).

## Applying this to the clarithromycin case
This patient ticked multiple high‑risk boxes: initial shock with hypoxaemia, repeated relapses requiring additional epinephrine, and a suspected underlying mast cell disorder. ICU monitoring after the first relapse was a reasonable and defensible choice. The team debated continuous IV epinephrine versus continued IM dosing with close observation; the relapses abated and they avoided infusion but arranged specialty follow‑up and education on autoinjector use. This case neatly illustrates why a one‑size‑fits‑all discharge rule would have been unsafe.

## Key take‑home messages
- Low risk (single, rapid, complete response to one IM epinephrine dose, known/removed trigger, no comorbidity) → observe 2–4 hours after full clinical resolution.
- Intermediate risk (unknown trigger, second IM dose, mild comorbidity) → observe 6–12 hours.
- High risk (shock/hypoxaemia, multiple doses, delayed epinephrine, oral/long‑acting trigger, suspected mast cell disease, prior biphasic reaction, night‑time event, remote living) → observe 12–24 hours and often admit to monitored care; escalate to ICU/IV epinephrine for refractory or repetitive relapses.
- IM epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg is the cornerstone; many autoinjectors are 0.3 mg — be ready to give 0.5 mg IM for larger adults in the ED.
- Steroids do not reliably prevent biphasic anaphylaxis and should not be used routinely for that purpose.
- Measure tryptase appropriately (acute and baseline samples) if severe or atypical reaction; persistent elevation requires specialist follow‑up.
- Discharge only with at least two autoinjectors, a written action plan, and arranged allergy follow‑up.

Anaphylaxis is common, biphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon but unpredictable. A simple, risk‑based observation framework lets us safely balance patient safety, resource use and shared decision‑making — and keeps us ready for the rare patient who, like the woman in this case, needs more than a single dose of epinephrine and a quick discharge.
