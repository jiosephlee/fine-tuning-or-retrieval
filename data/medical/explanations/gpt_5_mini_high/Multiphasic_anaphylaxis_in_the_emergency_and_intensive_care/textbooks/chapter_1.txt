Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 1: ## Initial Presentation and Emergency Stabilization

This patient arrived with a history and examination that rapidly pointed to an acute systemic allergic reaction. Her background is clinically relevant: she had remote, limited allergic reactions (facial swelling to amoxicillin in adolescence and a local wasp-sting reaction) but no prior anaphylaxis. Important contemporaneous features that influenced management were her weight (104 kg), diabetes and hypertension, and current medications (metformin, gliclazide, hydrochlorothiazide, omeprazole, sertraline). These comorbidities do not exclude anaphylaxis and, in practical terms, a large body weight increases the epinephrine dose required to reach the usual adult maximum; conversely, she was not on beta‑blockers (which can blunt epinephrine response), an important negative to note.

The exposure timeline supports a drug-triggered immediate reaction. Five days earlier she had been treated with clindamycin and developed a delayed rash, after which her GP switched her to clarithromycin. She took the first 500 mg dose of clarithromycin and developed pruritus, flushing, sweating, agitation and a sensation of throat swelling within one hour; she presented to the ED about one hour after symptom onset. The rapid onset after ingestion makes an immediate hypersensitivity reaction the most likely clinical explanation.

On arrival she had objective multisystem involvement: diffuse erythema and generalized urticaria, facial swelling, marked hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxaemia (SpO2 88% on room air) and tachypnea (RR 22). She reported throat fullness but had no visible tongue or oropharyngeal edema and had normal chest and cardiac auscultation. This pattern meets standard clinical diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis: acute onset with skin–mucosal involvement plus respiratory compromise and/or hypotension, and it also meets the alternate criterion of two or more organ systems involved after exposure to a likely allergen. The temporal relationship to clarithromycin further supports the diagnosis.

Pathophysiologically this presentation reflects sudden mast cell and basophil mediator release (histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, prostaglandins and others) producing vasodilation, increased capillary permeability (causing intravascular volume loss and hypotension), bronchospasm and mucosal edema (causing hypoxaemia and airway symptoms), and cutaneous manifestations. Drug reactions may be IgE‑mediated or via non‑IgE direct mast cell activation; the clinical urgency and organ-system compromise are managed the same way in either case.

Management priorities were airway, breathing and circulation. The first-line and time-critical treatment for anaphylaxis is intramuscular epinephrine: in this patient an auto‑injector delivering 0.3 mg intramuscularly to the anterolateral thigh was used immediately. IM epinephrine provides alpha‑adrenergic vasoconstriction (raising peripheral resistance and blood pressure), beta‑1 inotropy/chronotropy (supporting cardiac output) and beta‑2 bronchodilation plus suppression of further mediator release. Recommended dosing is 0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg in adults; in practice many adult patients will require up to 0.5 mg. In emergent situations an auto‑injector shortens treatment delays and reduces hesitancy, although the 0.3 mg device used here is smaller than the 0.5 mg dose frequently required for larger adults.

Supportive measures performed in the ED followed standard priorities: high‑flow oxygen via a non‑rebreather mask (15 L/min) for hypoxaemia, and an initial bolus of intravenous crystalloid (500 mL 0.9% saline) to address the intravascular volume deficit. Antihistamines (clemastine 2 mg IV slow) were given to control cutaneous symptoms; these are adjunctive and do not replace epinephrine. No airway intervention or intravenous epinephrine infusion was required at that time because there was no stridor, progressive airway compromise or refractory hemodynamic collapse. The suspect drug (clarithromycin) was stopped and no further doses were given.

Clinical indicators of immediate response were clear: blood pressure returned toward normal, oxygenation improved sufficiently that supplemental oxygen could be discontinued, and facial swelling and generalized erythema diminished. These signs indicate effective reversal of the acute mediator effects, but they do not eliminate the risk of recurrence. For that reason, and because the initial presentation involved profound hypotension and hypoxaemia, admission to a monitored setting was appropriate. Patients with severe anaphylaxis should be observed where rapid re-administration of epinephrine, airway management and hemodynamic support are immediately available; in this case she was first admitted to an acute internal ward with increased nursing observation and later required ICU care after relapse. Finally, although serum tryptase measurement is a useful diagnostic adjunct if obtained early (ideally within 1–4 hours of onset), no tryptase was drawn during the initial ED episode in this patient, which limited biochemical confirmation of mast cell activation at that time.
