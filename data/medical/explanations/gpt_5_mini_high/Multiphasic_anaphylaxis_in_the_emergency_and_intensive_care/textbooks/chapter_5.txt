Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 5: ## Outcomes, Follow-up, and Practice Pearls

Clinical course and disposition
The patient experienced four discrete anaphylactic episodes over the first 12 hours after clarithromycin exposure, each promptly reversed with intramuscular epinephrine; the severity of symptoms decreased with each relapse (from severe hypoxaemia and hypotension initially to mainly cutaneous and respiratory symptoms in the final episode). After the final event there were no further recurrences; she remained free of anaphylaxis for approximately 24 hours before being stepped down from the ICU to the ward and was discharged on hospital day three. The team considered but did not need to institute continuous intravenous epinephrine because the relapses ceased and each episode responded to intramuscular dosing.

Discharge medications and emergency equipment
At discharge she was started on daily levocetirizine for symptomatic control of histamine-mediated symptoms and was provided and trained in the use of an epinephrine auto‑injector. Epinephrine remains the first‑line treatment for anaphylaxis and should be carried at all times by patients at risk. Dose selection should be weight‑based (0.01 mg/kg, intramuscular, up to a 0.5 mg adult maximum); many available auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg and may underdose larger adults. Given this patient’s body weight (104 kg) and the need for repeated doses during her admission, clinicians should consider prescribing an adult dose that achieves the recommended maximum intramuscular dose when available and feasible. Patients at higher risk (severe initial reaction, biphasic/multiphasic course, remote access issues) should be given two auto‑injectors and instructed to use a second dose if symptoms persist or recur after 5–15 minutes while awaiting emergency help.

Follow-up pathway for suspected mast cell disease
Because her baseline tryptase remained elevated and other features (hepatomegaly, neuropsychiatric symptoms) raised concern for a mast cell disorder, prompt outpatient follow-up with allergy/immunology (and hematology if advised) is essential. Tryptase sampling is time‑sensitive: obtain an acute sample as soon as feasible during an event, repeat at 1–2 hours (within 4 hours) when levels often peak, and obtain a baseline sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish the baseline value. When both an acute and a baseline value are available, mast cell activation is supported by an acute level > (1.2 × baseline) + 2 ng/mL. If baseline tryptase remains elevated (particularly >20 ng/mL) or clinical suspicion persists, the next steps include targeted testing for KIT D816V (peripheral blood and/or bone marrow), flow cytometry for aberrant mast cell markers (CD2/CD25), and, if indicated, repeat bone marrow assessment with adequate sampling to evaluate the WHO criteria for systemic mastocytosis. In this case the initial marrow material was limited and did not allow full application of the diagnostic criteria, so a repeat specialist evaluation and testing plan is appropriate. Until evaluation is complete, the patient should avoid the implicated trigger (clarithromycin) and other macrolides as advised by the allergy service, and the allergy should be clearly documented in the medical record.

Patient education and emergency planning
Education focuses on early recognition and immediate intramuscular epinephrine administration for any signs of systemic reaction (airway compromise, respiratory distress, circulatory collapse, or progressive mucocutaneous involvement). Patients must be told not to delay epinephrine while attempting to confirm the diagnosis and to call emergency services after using an auto‑injector. Provide a written anaphylaxis action plan that specifies when and how to inject epinephrine, when to repeat a dose, and when to seek emergency care. Training should include demonstration and return‑demonstration of the auto‑injector technique, advice to wear medical identification describing the allergy/mast cell disorder, and discussion of avoidance strategies for the implicated drug class. Given the history of multiphasic events, emphasize vigilance for symptom recurrence over the first 24–72 hours after an event.

System and team practice pearls
This case highlights several system improvements that reduce diagnostic and treatment gaps for complex anaphylaxis. First, standardize ED workflows to ensure timely tryptase draws at the recommended intervals (acute, 1–2 h, and ≥24 h baseline) so that mast cell activation and baseline status can be interpreted accurately. Second, ensure availability of and team familiarity with appropriate adult epinephrine dosing (IM 0.5 mg target for most adults) and local protocols for when to escalate to continuous intravenous infusions in refractory cases—ICU management should be protocolised. Third, adopt a risk‑stratified observation and disposition protocol (longer observation or admission for severe initial reactions, multiple epinephrine doses, or suspected mast cell disease). Fourth, include anaphylaxis discharge checklists that confirm provision of epinephrine auto‑injectors (two devices when indicated), antihistamine prescriptions, written action plans, and specialty follow‑up. Finally, contribute multiphasic and refractory anaphylaxis cases to local or national registries to build evidence for future guideline refinement.

Takeaway
Multiphasic anaphylaxis requires prompt acute treatment, prolonged vigilance, careful follow‑up for underlying mast cell disease when suggested by tryptase and clinical features, weight‑appropriate epinephrine provision and training, and system processes that reliably capture time‑sensitive diagnostic tests and ensure appropriate post‑discharge safety planning.
