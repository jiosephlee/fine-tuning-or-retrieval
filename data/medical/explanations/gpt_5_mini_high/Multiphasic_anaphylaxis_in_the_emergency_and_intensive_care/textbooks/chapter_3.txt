Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Reasoning: Tryptase, Culprit Identification, and Mast Cell Disease

The immediate diagnostic goals after a complex anaphylactic episode are threefold: document mast cell activation when possible, identify and document the most likely culprit to prevent re‑exposure, and decide whether the clinical picture warrants investigation for a predisposing mast cell disorder. Tryptase measurement is central to the first and third aims because it is a relatively specific marker of mast cell degranulation and, when persistently elevated, a marker of increased mast cell burden.

Timing and interpretation of tryptase. Tryptase is released from mast cells and typically peaks about 1–2 hours after symptom onset, returning toward baseline over the next 12–24 hours. Recommended sampling therefore includes an acute sample as soon as feasible during or immediately after resuscitation, a second sample at about 1–2 hours (and within 4 hours) from symptom onset to capture the peak, and a third sample at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution to establish the patient’s baseline. A commonly used rule to define a significant mast cell activation is: peak tryptase > (1.2 × baseline) + 2 ng/mL. Separately, a persistent baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL is one of the WHO minor diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis. It is important to remember that normal tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis because sensitivity is imperfect and depends on the trigger and timing of sampling.

How the case deviated and what that means. In this patient the recommended sampling schedule was not followed at the index presentation: no tryptase was drawn during the first emergency department episode. The first available tryptase was obtained about 1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode (an acceptable time window for an acute sample for that episode) and a follow‑up baseline sample was taken 27 hours after the last recurrence. Missing early samples from the first episode limits the ability to demonstrate a tryptase rise from the initial event and to calculate the full dynamic change across the entire clinical course. Nonetheless, an elevated acute value during the recorded episode together with a persistently raised baseline supports both significant mast cell activation during the attacks and the presence of an underlying tendency to higher mast cell burden—evidence that prompted further evaluation for mastocytosis.

Reasoning that clarithromycin was the most likely culprit. Culprit attribution in drug reactions rests mainly on timing and exclusion of alternatives. In this case symptoms began roughly one hour after a first oral 500 mg dose of clarithromycin and progressed rapidly to hypotension, hypoxaemia and diffuse urticaria—features consistent with an immediate systemic hypersensitivity reaction. No subsequent dosing occurred and there were no other strong new exposures temporally linked to the reaction. The earlier rash after several days of clindamycin use was a different clinical pattern (delayed, limited to a rash) and occurred days before the anaphylactic event; chronic maintenance medications had been tolerated previously and were less plausible precipitants. Taken together, the tight temporal relationship to clarithromycin and lack of a more likely trigger make clarithromycin the most probable culprit in this clinical reasoning framework.

Distinguishing multiphasic relapse from sustained antigen-driven anaphylaxis. Clinically, a multiphasic course is characterized by a clear initial response to therapy with a symptom‑free interval followed by one or more recurrences without further exposure to antigen. Sustained anaphylaxis due to ongoing antigen absorption or continued presence of allergen is suggested by failure to achieve a durable response or by continuous fluctuating symptoms despite correct therapy. In this patient the initial prompt and complete response to epinephrine, a subsequent asymptomatic interval, discrete relapses occurring without further dosing, and progressively less severe episodes favor a multiphasic pattern rather than persistent antigen absorption. Sustained absorption from a single oral dose would be less likely to produce well‑demarcated, resolving intervals and progressively milder relapses.

When to pursue evaluation for clonal mast cell disorders. Referral for evaluation of a clonal mast cell disorder (for example, systemic mastocytosis) is indicated when anaphylaxis is unusually severe, recurrent, idiopathic, or when baseline tryptase is persistently elevated. Additional red flags are mediator‑related chronic symptoms or findings such as hepatosplenomegaly, unexplained gastrointestinal symptoms, or bone involvement. The initial workup should include a baseline (asymptomatic) tryptase, repeat confirmatory measurements, and hematology/allergy referral. Specialized testing may include sensitive KIT D816V molecular testing on peripheral blood and, when indicated, a bone marrow aspirate/biopsy with histology, immunophenotyping (CD25/CD2), and molecular studies. In this patient the multiphasic, severe presentation plus persistently elevated tryptase and sonographic hepatomegaly prompted that pathway; the clinicians appropriately escalated to specialist evaluation even though the diagnostic workup could not be completed to a formal microbiologic or histologic endpoint.

WHO criteria and how this patient mapped to them. The WHO diagnosis for systemic mastocytosis requires either the major criterion (multifocal dense mast cell aggregates in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs) plus one minor criterion, or three minor criteria. The four minor criteria are: (1) baseline serum tryptase >20 ng/mL (unless explained by a clonal myeloid disorder), (2) presence of an activating KIT mutation (typically D816V) in blood or bone marrow, (3) aberrant mast cell surface expression of CD2 and/or CD25, and (4) >25% of mast cells showing atypical spindle shape on morphology. The authors report that the patient fulfilled two WHO minor criteria but that the major criterion could not be assessed because the bone marrow specimen was limited and prevented reliable evaluation for multifocal aggregates; thus a formal diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis could not be established, although the constellation of findings made a clonal mast cell disorder likely and justified ongoing follow‑up.

Why confirmatory macrolide allergy testing was deferred and how the allergy was managed. Provocative testing—skin testing, intradermal testing or graded oral challenge—to confirm macrolide allergy carries a real risk of provoking severe recurrent anaphylaxis, and standardized, validated reagents and protocols for macrolide testing are limited. When a history and timing strongly implicate a drug that can readily be avoided and safe alternatives exist, deferring provocative testing is a defensible, patient‑safety–driven choice. In this case the team prioritized avoidance, clear documentation of the suspected allergy in the record, prescription and teaching for an epinephrine auto‑injector, and antihistamine therapy on discharge. If definitive confirmation is later required, testing should be performed by an experienced allergist in a controlled setting, and the presence of a possible mast cell disorder should be taken into account because it raises the risk of severe reactions during challenge.

Takeaway for clinical practice. Capture tryptase samples as early as feasible and always obtain a baseline measurement >24 hours after complete resolution; use the (1.2 × baseline + 2 ng/mL) calculation to assess for significant rises. When anaphylaxis is severe, recurrent, atypical or accompanied by a persistently raised baseline tryptase or organomegaly, involve hematology/allergy for evaluation of clonal mast cell disease. When testing for drug allergy would put the patient at high risk, emphasize avoidance, documentation and emergency preparedness while arranging specialist follow‑up—exactly the pragmatic pathway followed in this case.
