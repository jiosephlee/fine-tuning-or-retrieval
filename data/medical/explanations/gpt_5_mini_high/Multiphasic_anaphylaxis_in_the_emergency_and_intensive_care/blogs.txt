Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Multiphasic Anaphylaxis at the Bedside: When to Move From Repeat IM to an IV Epinephrine Infusion

This case is the kind of thing that makes you rethink the “one-and-done” anaphylaxis script. A woman in her 60s (104 kg) took a single oral dose of clarithromycin and developed classic anaphylaxis with hypotension, hypoxaemia and widespread urticaria. She responded to intramuscular (IM) epinephrine, only to have multiple recurrences over the next 12 hours — four anaphylactic episodes in total. Two early doses were 0.3 mg IM via auto‑injector; after relapse she required 0.5 mg IM doses. ICU was consulted and the team paused at the fork: keep giving repeat IM doses and watch, or start a continuous IV epinephrine infusion? They chose careful observation and the patient ultimately stabilised, but the dilemma is universal — when do you stop repeating IM epinephrine and move to an infusion?

Below I walk through practical definitions, the clinical triggers that should push you to escalate, safe setup and titration of an epinephrine infusion, and a simple team-friendly algorithm you can use at the bedside.

## Why this matters
- Biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis are uncommon but real. Definitions and incidence vary (reports range widely, though many series cluster around 4–6% for biphasic courses), and guidance about advanced haemodynamic management is sparse.
- Recurrent episodes are stressful for staff and dangerous for patients — oscillating hypotension and hypoxaemia are not benign.
- The case highlights two important operational problems: IM underdosing in large adults (0.3 mg auto‑injectors can be inadequate for a 104‑kg patient) and the clinical uncertainty about when to transition to a continuous IV infusion.

## Operational definitions (to speak the same language)
- Anaphylaxis: acute systemic allergic reaction with airway/breathing/circulatory compromise and usually skin/mucosal features.
- Biphasic anaphylaxis: recurrence of symptoms after apparent clinical resolution, without re‑exposure; usually within 24 hours (reports up to ~78 hours exist).
- Protracted anaphylaxis: symptoms that persist continuously for many hours (often >24 hours).
- Refractory anaphylaxis: ongoing anaphylaxis that is not responding adequately to correctly administered epinephrine.
- Multiphasic anaphylaxis: multiple discrete relapses separated by asymptomatic intervals (this case).

Use these terms to communicate with ED, ICU and allergy teams — they change the calculus.

## The clinical fork: repeat IM or go to an IV infusion?
First principle: give epinephrine early and IM (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg in adults, lateral thigh). Do not delay epinephrine to prepare an infusion. But when repeated IM doses are needed, and when the patient has ongoing hypotension or hypoxaemia despite appropriate IM therapy, it’s time to consider escalation.

Practical triggers that should push you towards a continuous IV infusion (or at least an urgent ICU/vasopressor plan)
- Recurrent hypotension and/or hypoxaemia after ≥2 appropriately dosed IM epinephrine injections given promptly. (In the case the patient received two 0.3 mg doses initially in a 104‑kg person — likely underdosed.)
- Requirement for frequent repeated IM doses in a short time frame (e.g., >2–3 IM injections within hours).
- Severe initial presentation (profound hypotension, need for high‑flow oxygen or assisted ventilation).
- Ongoing / ongoing absorption of allergen (oral medication with continued GI absorption, depot envenomation) where you suspect ongoing mediator stimulus.
- Known or suspected mast cell disease (elevated baseline tryptase, systemic mastocytosis features) — these patients can have protracted or multiphasic reactions.
- Refractory anaphylaxis where IM boluses are not restoring perfusion.

If the patient is stable after a single correctly dosed IM injection, continued observation and supportive care is reasonable. If any of the triggers above are present — get help, prepare for an infusion.

## What an infusion gives you — and what it costs
Why start an infusion? Continuous IV epinephrine removes the peaks-and-troughs of intermittent IM dosing, allows tight titration to haemodynamic targets, and can prevent repeated systemic crashes. It’s particularly helpful when repeated IM doses are being administered, when you’re chasing recurrent hypotension, or when ongoing allergen absorption is suspected.

The downsides: arrhythmias, hypertension, myocardial ischemia, dose errors, and the need for monitored settings and experienced nursing. An infusion is not a “set it and forget it” fix — it requires telemetry, a pump, clear titration orders and readiness to wean.

## How to start and run an epinephrine infusion safely (practical bedside steps)
1. Call for help early: involve ICU/anaesthesia/experienced ED staff. If you meet the triggers above, don’t wait until the patient is crashing again.
2. Environment and monitoring: move the patient to a monitored area/ICU or set up continuous ECG, frequent automated or manual BP checks (every 2–5 minutes during titration), pulse oximetry, and an infusion pump. Consider arterial line if you expect prolonged high-dose vasopressor support.
3. IV access: a large‑bore peripheral IV is acceptable for short-term infusions; central access is preferred if you anticipate prolonged vasopressor use or poor peripheral access.
4. Fluids and adjuncts: give brisk crystalloid boluses to support preload (the case patient received repeated 500 mL boluses). Treat the airway and oxygenation aggressively. Antihistamines and steroids are commonly given for cutaneous inflammation and for tradition — but steroids do not reliably prevent biphasic reactions and are not a substitute for haemodynamic control.
5. Prepare an infusion (examples — adapt to your local pharmacy protocol):
   - A common pragmatic mix: 1 mg epinephrine (1 mL of 1:1000) diluted into 250 mL normal saline → concentration = 4 mcg/mL.
   - Alternatively, 1 mg in 100 mL → 10 mcg/mL for smaller pump rates (useful if you want smaller volumetric rates).
   - Calculation example using 1 mg in 250 mL (4 mcg/mL): target 0.05 mcg/kg/min for a 70‑kg patient = 3.5 mcg/min → pump = 0.875 mL/min = 52.5 mL/h. For a 100‑kg patient at 0.05 mcg/kg/min = 5 mcg/min → ~75 mL/h.
6. Starting dose and titration (examples; adapt to local policy):
   - Start at 0.05 mcg/kg/min (some teams start 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min). For many adults this equals a few micrograms per minute.
   - Titrate to effect: increase by small steps (for example +0.02–0.05 mcg/kg/min or by +2–5 mcg/min) every 3–5 minutes until you achieve target blood pressure and symptom control.
   - Set nurse‑driven limits in the order: e.g., “start 0.05 mcg/kg/min; nurse may increase by 0.02 mcg/kg/min q5min for persistent hypotension to a maximum of 0.2 mcg/kg/min; if more required call physician/ICU.”
7. Avoid push‑dose boluses as routine. Push‑dose epinephrine (boluses of 10–20 mcg) can be used in extreme, transient life‑threatening drops, but boluses produce abrupt haemodynamic swings and arrhythmia risk — the infusion is safer for ongoing needs. If you do use a bolus in a crash, document and then move to an infusion.
8. Watch for complications: arrhythmia, myocardial ischemia, hyperglycaemia, lactic acidosis, hypokalaemia. Continuous ECG is mandatory when vasopressors are running.
9. De‑escalation: once the patient is stable without recurrent symptoms and MAP/BP are maintained, slowly wean the infusion (reduce by small steps over hours). The case team observed 24 hours without relapse before stepping care down; de‑escalation timing depends on the clinical picture and the presumed trigger (longer if suspect mast cell disease or ongoing absorption).

## Practical team-friendly algorithm (words, not a flowchart)
Step 1 — Immediate care: For any suspected anaphylaxis give IM epinephrine immediately (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg). Give oxygen, secure IV access, give crystalloid bolus(es), treat airway, and add antihistamine for cutaneous symptoms. Obtain baseline vitals and assess response.

Step 2 — Reassess after IM epinephrine: If the patient has a single, durable response — observe with appropriate duration per local policy. If they have persistent or recurrent hypotension/hypoxaemia, proceed to Step 3.

Step 3 — Repeat IM if needed while you prepare for escalation: give a repeat IM epinephrine dose promptly if relapse occurs. If the patient requires repeated IM doses (≥2–3 in a short period), or has severe/refractory haemodynamic compromise, activate ICU/vasopressor pathway.

Step 4 — Prepare and start infusion: move to monitored area, get large-bore IV/central access if needed, prepare epinephrine infusion per protocol, start low (eg 0.05 mcg/kg/min) and use nurse-driven titration within pre-defined limits. Avoid routine push boluses.

Step 5 — Monitor and investigate: continuous telemetry, frequent BP and SpO2 checks, labs as indicated. If multiphasic or prolonged course consider mast cell disorder — draw tryptase samples (as soon as practical, ideally a sample at or near peak within 1–2 hours of symptoms, and a baseline sample ≥24 hours after full resolution).

Step 6 — Wean and educate: once stable without relapse for an appropriate observation interval, wean the infusion and transfer out of monitored area. Arrange allergy/immunology follow‑up, provide an epinephrine auto‑injector and teach its use, and consider longer-term investigation for mast cell disease if baseline tryptase remains high.

## Lessons from the case
- Don’t underdose epinephrine in large adults. A 0.3 mg auto‑injector is often inadequate for a 104‑kg adult; guidelines recommend up to 0.5 mg IM in adults.
- Multiphasic anaphylaxis can be associated with underlying mast cell disease — measure tryptase appropriately and plan follow‑up.
- Steroids are commonly given (they were after the first relapse in this case), but steroids have not convincingly been shown to prevent biphasic reactions — they are not a substitute for haemodynamic control.
- Early ICU involvement and a clear plan for infusion/titration reduce hesitation and delay if the patient deteriorates.

## Final thoughts
There is no single “right” time stamp to move from repeat IM to an infusion; you must combine objective triggers (recurrent hypotension/hypoxaemia despite appropriate IM dosing, repeated IM requirements, signs of ongoing absorption, suspected mast cell disease) with practical capabilities (monitoring, experienced nursing, pump availability). An infusion is a tool — powerful when used safely and in the right setting. Make the decision early enough so you aren’t scrambling during the next crash.

Key take‑home messages
- IM epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg) given promptly is first‑line — don’t delay it to prepare an infusion.
- Consider escalation to a continuous IV epinephrine infusion when there is recurrent hypotension/hypoxaemia after ≥2 appropriate IM doses, frequent repeated IM dosing, refractory haemodynamic failure, ongoing allergen absorption, or suspected mast cell disease.
- Move the patient to a monitored area, ensure large‑bore IV (or central access), continuous ECG, and ICU involvement before starting an infusion.
- Practical infusion setup: common mix = 1 mg epinephrine in 250 mL NS (4 mcg/mL); start ~0.05 mcg/kg/min and titrate to effect. Follow local protocols and use explicit nurse‑driven titration orders.
- Avoid routine push‑dose epinephrine; boluses carry arrhythmia and overshoot risk — reserve for true, transient life‑threatening events.
- Measure tryptase appropriately (near peak during reaction and baseline ≥24 h after resolution) and arrange allergist/immunology follow‑up if multiphasic or if tryptase is elevated.

If you take one thing from this: have the conversation early. When a patient needs repeated IM doses, call the ICU/vasopressor team, get pumps and monitoring ready, and make a pre‑defined titration plan. That prevents indecision at 0300 when the patient next crashes.



# 0.5 mg or Bust: Fixing Adult Epinephrine Underdosing in Anaphylaxis

## What woke me up about this case
A woman in her 60s, 104 kg, presents in full anaphylactic shock after a first dose of clarithromycin. She gets a rapid intramuscular epinephrine auto‑injector (0.3 mg) and initially improves — but then has repeated relapses over the next 12 hours, each episode treated with further IM epinephrine. The team ultimately gives two individually prepared 0.5 mg IM doses; the relapses decline in intensity and stop. She is later worked up (persistent elevated tryptase) and suspected to have an underlying mast cell disorder.

Why this matters: guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg IM epinephrine (max 0.5 mg). For many adults that equals 0.5 mg, but the commonly available adult auto‑injector delivers only 0.3 mg. That automatic, human‑factor friendly dose can underdeliver by a wide margin in heavier adults — and mounting evidence (including a 2025 retrospective study cited in the case report) links initial 0.3 mg dosing with more frequent need for rescue doses and escalation of care. This case is a textbook example of how convenience + habit + limited stock can collide with physiology and make an already scary situation harder to manage.

Clinical pearl: for adults weighing >50 kg the guideline target dose (0.01 mg/kg) is at or near 0.5 mg IM. If you default to 0.3 mg in a 100+ kg adult you are giving roughly one‑third of the recommended dose.

## Why the dose matters (quick physiology + evidence)
Epinephrine is the one drug that changes outcomes in anaphylaxis: it reduces laryngeal edema, bronchospasm, vasodilation and capillary leak. The IM route into the mid‑anterolateral thigh gives rapid and reliable absorption without the risks of an IV bolus. The dosing rule — 0.01 mg/kg IM up to 0.5 mg — is simple in theory but frequently muddied in practice by auto‑injector strengths.

A 0.3 mg IM dose in a 100 kg adult equals ~0.003 mg/kg — far below the 0.01 mg/kg target. Observational data and the case above suggest that initial underdosing can translate into incomplete reversal of mediator effects, need for repeated doses, escalation to ICU, and potentially more complications, not fewer. The case report also reminds us that complex presentations (biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis) are unpredictable — but starting with the right epinephrine dose gives you the best chance to stop the cascade early.

Clinical pearl: time to the correct dose matters. A slightly larger dose promptly given IM is better than a “safer‑feeling” smaller dose that forces you to chase the reaction.

## Human factors: autoinjectors are lifesaving — but check the strength
Auto‑injectors reduce delay, reduce hesitation, and standardize delivery. Those are big wins in prehospital and low‑volume settings. The problem is strength mismatch: many institutions and public kits stock the familiar 0.3 mg adult autoinjectors. That strength was historically popular and is still common — but it does not hit the guideline target in many adults.

What to do about it: keep the human‑factor benefits of a single‑step device while matching the dose to the patient. Where 0.5 mg auto‑injectors are available, make them the default adult device. Where they are not available, prefill 0.5 mg syringes for immediate use in the ED and on resuscitation trolleys, or implement a nurse‑initiated protocol that authorizes a quick manual IM injection of 0.5 mg (0.5 mL of 1 mg/mL [1:1000]) into the mid‑thigh.

Clinical pearl: if you must choose between a delayed correct manual 0.5 mg IM and an immediate 0.3 mg autoinjector, the immediate 0.3 mg is better than nothing — but your system should be optimized so that the immediate dose is the correct dose.

## A practical pathway to default 0.5 mg IM for adults >50 kg
Make the decision simple and low‑cognitive load at the bedside:

- Recognition: suspected anaphylaxis → assess airway/breathing/circulation; do not delay epinephrine for tests.
- Dosing rule of thumb: adults >50 kg → 0.5 mg IM (0.5 mL of 1 mg/mL [1:1000]) into the mid‑anterolateral thigh immediately; adults 30–50 kg → 0.3 mg IM; children → 0.01 mg/kg IM (use paediatric autoinjectors or weight‑based syringe dosing). If weight unknown but the patient is clearly a typical adult body habitus, default to 0.5 mg.
- Delivery: prefer a 0.5 mg auto‑injector where available; if not, use a preprepared 0.5 mg syringe or draw 0.5 mL of 1:1000 epinephrine and inject IM without delay. Repeat IM every ~5 minutes as needed while you escalate care if there is no response.
- Escalation: if anaphylaxis is refractory to repeated IM doses or the patient is crashing, move to expert‑led IV epinephrine infusion in a monitored setting — don’t start IV boluses of concentrated epinephrine without expertise.

(Adapt wording and timing to local protocols and supervision.)

## QI tactics to actually change practice (how to make it stick)
Start with the easy wins: stock 0.5 mg adult autoinjectors where they are available to purchase and allowed by regulation. If regulatory or supply barriers exist, create ready‑to‑grab 0.5 mg prefilled syringes (0.5 mL of 1 mg/mL) stored in resuscitation bays, triage rooms and ambulance kits. Update your electronic medical record order sets so “Adult anaphylaxis” defaults to IM epinephrine 0.5 mg for patients ≥50 kg. Empower nursing staff with a standing order or nurse‑initiated protocol that allows immediate administration of 0.5 mg IM in recognized anaphylaxis pending physician review. Label pediatric packs clearly and keep them physically separate to prevent dose errors.

Education and simulation are critical: run short scenario drills where the team practices weight estimation, grabs the correct device, documents time/dose/response, and decides the next step. Pharmacy should be looped in to ensure supply, storage and clear labeling. Finally, measure outcomes: baseline % of adult anaphylaxis patients who receive an initial 0.5 mg IM dose, time to first epinephrine, number of additional doses required, ICU admission rate, and any epinephrine‑related adverse events. Use PDSA cycles to iterate.

Clinical pearl: systems change beats individual vigilance. Make the right dose the easiest option.

## Safety, pushback and practical notes
Anticipate common objections. “Won’t 0.5 mg cause dangerous hypertension or arrhythmia?” Short‑lived tachycardia, palpitations or hypertension can occur, but significant harms from IM epinephrine at guideline doses are rare; underdosing that leaves a patient hypotensive or hypoxaemic is a far greater risk. “We don’t carry 0.5 mg autoinjectors.” Then prefill syringes and implement a nurse protocol; prioritize stocking them in high‑risk areas first (ED, ambulances, wards with frequent allergic patients). “What about obesity and IM absorption?” Ensure mid‑thigh (vastus lateralis) delivery and try to use a needle that will reach muscle for manual injections; if using autoinjectors, press firmly and hold the device per instructions. “What about biphasic/multiphasic reactions?” Correct early dosing reduces the likelihood of ongoing mediator release; steroids have not been convincingly shown to prevent biphasic events and should not substitute for correct epinephrine dosing.

## Back to the case — why this is a systems problem, not an individual mistake
This patient’s relapsing anaphylaxis is a reminder that an early, adequate epinephrine dose matters. The team did the right thing in giving epinephrine without delay; the system handed them a 0.3 mg autoinjector that is commonly stocked and thought of as “adult” — but in a 104 kg adult that dose was suboptimal. Fixing that mismatch at the systems level (default to 0.5 mg for adults >50 kg, stock appropriate devices or prefilled syringes, empower nurses) is a high‑yield safety intervention with low complexity.

## Key takeaways
- Adults >50 kg should generally receive 0.5 mg IM epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg) as the initial dose for anaphylaxis; check weight quickly and default to 0.5 mg when in doubt.
- Auto‑injectors reduce delay and hesitation — but many “adult” autoinjectors are only 0.3 mg and underdose many adults; where possible, stock 0.5 mg adult autoinjectors or provide ready 0.5 mg prefilled syringes.
- Implement simple system fixes: EMR order‑set defaults, nurse‑initiated protocols for immediate 0.5 mg IM in adults ≥50 kg, anaphylaxis kits with correct dosing, and regular simulation training.
- Monitor your change: time to first epinephrine, percent receiving initial 0.5 mg, need for escalation, and safety signals.
- Safety note: IM epinephrine at guideline doses is far safer than underdosing and chasing a worsening anaphylactic cascade; reserve IV boluses for expert‑led rescue in refractory cases.

If your department still reflexively reaches for a 0.3 mg autoinjector for “adults,” consider this case a nudge: make the correct dose the default, and save yourself a second (and a third) adrenaline shot.



# Steroids in Anaphylaxis: Time to Break the Habit

What happened here will sound familiar to many ED and ICU teams: an older woman with new‑onset anaphylaxis after a single dose of clarithromycin gets epinephrine and fluids, stabilises — then has recurrent drops in blood pressure and hypoxaemia over the next 12 hours and is transferred to ICU. After the first relapse she was given corticosteroids — not once, but twice: 30 mg prednisone IV daily for five days and a one‑off 8 mg dexamethasone IV (a duplication born of differing local protocols). She had type 2 diabetes, recent erysipelas treated with antibiotics, and later workup suggested high baseline tryptase with possible systemic mastocytosis — all good reasons to pause before routinely layering on steroids.

This case is a perfect springboard to ask a blunt question: in acute anaphylaxis — especially complex or multiphasic cases — do steroids help, harm, or simply muddy the waters? Short answer: routine steroids to “prevent” biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis are not supported by evidence and can cause real harms. It’s time to stop reflexive steroiding and be purposeful when we reach for them.

## Why steroids became a reflex
Historically steroids were given in anaphylaxis with the rationale that they might blunt a late‑phase inflammatory response and thereby reduce the risk of a biphasic reaction. That rationale is biologically plausible for inflammation that evolves over hours, but corticosteroids take many hours to exert their effects — they are not rescue therapy for airway compromise, hypotension or bronchospasm. Despite this lag and the weak evidence base, a culture of “give a steroid” persists in many EDs and wards.

## What the evidence says (straightforward take)
Systematic reviews and guideline updates over the last decade have failed to show that routine corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. A 2017 systematic review that looked across 22 studies found minimal support for steroids in preventing biphasic reactions; most studies showed no benefit. Observational data have even raised concern that prehospital steroids are associated with higher rates of subsequent admission to hospital or ICU. Because most studies are low quality and randomized trials are scarce, absolute certainty is lacking — but the balance of evidence does not support routine steroid use to prevent relapse.

Two practical implications from the evidence:
- Steroids are slow acting. They do not treat the acute, life‑threatening features of anaphylaxis — epinephrine does.
- There is no reliable evidence that giving steroids after anaphylaxis prevents biphasic or multiphasic relapses.

## Harms matter — not theoretical, real
Giving steroids is not benign. In this case the patient had type 2 diabetes and recent infectious skin disease — two situations where steroids can do harm.

Important harms to consider:
- Hyperglycaemia and worsened glycaemic control (clinically important in diabetics; increases insulin needs and lengthens stay).
- Psychiatric effects (mood changes, agitation, insomnia) — relevant in patients with baseline depression.
- Impaired wound healing and increased infection risk — relevant after recent erysipelas/antibiotic therapy.
- Gastrointestinal upset or bleeding (higher risk with concurrent antiplatelet/NSAID use).
- Masking of infection or fever and iatrogenic adrenal suppression with repeated courses.
- Resource and protocol confusion: duplicative steroid regimens (as in this case) increase exposure without clear benefit.

Beyond patient harms, steroid administration can give a false sense of security that may alter observation decisions or divert attention from monitoring and epinephrine readiness.

## So when — if ever — are steroids reasonable in anaphylaxis?
There are limited, pragmatic situations where steroids have a role as an adjunct, not as primary therapy:
- Coexisting or predominant bronchospasm due to an acute asthma exacerbation. In that setting, systemic steroids reduce the risk of recurrent bronchospasm and hospitalisation for asthma (this is asthma literature, not anaphylaxis literature).
- Protracted allergic airway inflammation when bronchospasm or wheeze persists despite repeated epinephrine and bronchodilators.
- Known adrenal insufficiency or patients on chronic steroid suppression (stress‑dose steroids as indicated).
In all of these scenarios steroids are being used for the coexisting, steroid‑responsive condition — not to “prevent biphasic anaphylaxis.” Even when you decide steroids are appropriate, give a single clearly‑documented dose and plan (and avoid duplicative regimens created by conflicting protocols).

## Clinical pearls from this case
- Epinephrine is first, fast and non‑negotiable. Don’t delay IM epinephrine while debating steroids; steroids are never a substitute for epinephrine.
- Steroids take hours to act. They won’t reverse acute airway obstruction, hypotension or severe bronchospasm.
- Be deliberate: if you give steroids, document the indication (e.g., “severe reactive airways disease with bronchospasm”), choose one agent and dose, and monitor for steroid side effects — especially hyperglycaemia in diabetics.
- Hospital protocols should be harmonised across ED, ward and ICU. Protocol conflict in this case produced unnecessary duplication (prednisone + dexamethasone).
- Consider the patient’s comorbidities before adding steroids. This patient had diabetes and recent cellulitis — two reasons to avoid routine steroid exposure unless clearly indicated.
- Multiphasic or recurrent anaphylaxis should prompt search for risk factors (delayed epinephrine, severe initial reaction, multiple epinephrine doses, possible mast cell disorder). There is no clear evidence that steroids will change the course in these patients.

## Practical bedside approach
- Primary treatment: IM epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg in adults), high‑flow oxygen, IV fluids, airway support as needed.
- Use H1/H2 antihistamines and bronchodilators as adjuncts for cutaneous symptoms and bronchospasm — but do not let these delay epinephrine.
- Do not give steroids routinely to “prevent” biphasic reactions. If a steroid is indicated for a coexisting condition (e.g., asthma), give one appropriate dose and document why.
- If you do give steroids: anticipate and manage predictable side effects (check bedside glucose in diabetics, counsel regarding mood changes, watch wounds/infections).
- For multiphasic or refractory cases, involve allergy/immunology and consider mast cell disorder workup (baseline and acute tryptase sampling, specialist follow‑up). Avoid provocative testing when immediate avoidance is possible.

## Take‑home messages
- Routine corticosteroids do not reliably prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis and should not be a reflexive part of anaphylaxis care.
- Epinephrine is the priority therapy — give it promptly and repeatedly as needed; steroids never replace epinephrine.
- Steroids have real harms (hyperglycaemia, infection risk, psychiatric effects); weigh these before giving them — our case patient had multiple reasons to avoid routine steroids.
- Use steroids only for clear, steroid‑responsive coexisting conditions (eg, uncontrolled asthma), give a single documented dose, and harmonise local protocols to avoid duplication.
- When anaphylaxis is recurrent or severe, seek specialist input and investigate for underlying predispositions (mast cell disorders) rather than reflexively layering on corticosteroids.

We can argue culture change in anaphylaxis management until we get better evidence. For now, the safest move is to stop the reflex steroid, prioritise epinephrine, and be deliberate when steroids are truly needed.



# Tryptase, the 20%+2 Rule, and the Patient You Might Be Missing: Unmasking Mast Cell Disease After Anaphylaxis

Why this case matters: a woman in her 60s walks into the ED with full‑blown anaphylaxis after a single clarithromycin dose. She responds to intramuscular epinephrine, then relapses multiple times over 12 hours — each relapse needing another epinephrine injection. Tryptase was not sampled during the very first event, but later measurements showed an acute rise and an elevated baseline. Workup suggested systemic mast cell disease, but the formal diagnosis could not be completed because bone marrow sampling was limited. This vignette hits two pain points we wrestle with in emergency care: (1) multiphasic/recurrent anaphylaxis is uncommon but scary and poorly guided by existing protocols; and (2) we underuse tryptase testing when it could point to an underlying mast cell disorder that changes long‑term risk and management.

If you see a severe, recurrent, or “out‑of‑proportion” anaphylaxis, start thinking about mast cell disease — and make tryptase part of your anaphylaxis bundle.

## Why tryptase matters (brief physiology and practical value)
Tryptase is a serine protease stored in mast cell granules and released during degranulation. An acute rise in serum tryptase is a useful biomarker of systemic mast cell activation. It’s not perfect — not every anaphylaxis will spike tryptase (cutaneous‑only reactions may not), and other conditions can raise tryptase — but it’s the single best readily available lab test to document mast cell degranulation and to flag an elevated baseline that suggests an underlying mast cell disorder (systemic mastocytosis or hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia).

Two practical points you must always remember: never delay life‑saving treatment (epinephrine) to get a blood draw, and label the sample clearly with the time of symptom onset.

## Make tryptase part of your ED anaphylaxis bundle
Operationally, treat first — then sample. The pragmatic protocol I use and recommend:

- Treat per usual: IM epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg (max ~0.5 mg) into the lateral thigh, oxygen, IV fluids, airway control as needed, remove trigger.
- As soon as feasible (without delaying treatment), draw serum for tryptase and document the time of symptom onset on the lab request.
- Draw a second sample at the 1–2 hour mark (tryptase typically peaks ~1–2 h; still useful up to 4 h).
- Arrange a baseline tryptase ≥24 hours after full clinical resolution (often done as part of follow‑up with allergy/immunology).

Why this timing? Acute tryptase peaks early and falls over the next hours. If you miss the acute window, the later “baseline” sample is still vital: a persistently elevated baseline points toward an underlying mast cell disorder.

In the presented case the team missed tryptase during the very first ED presentation and only drew a sample an hour after the third episode; a baseline was obtained 27 hours after the last recurrence. That kind of imperfect timing is common — but better than not measuring at all.

## How to interpret tryptase — the 20% + 2 rule
Absolute cutoffs are blunt; the consensus tool that clinicians find most useful is the “20% + 2” rule. Calculate:

acute tryptase ≥ (1.2 × baseline tryptase) + 2 ng/mL → supports significant mast cell activation

Example (hypothetical): baseline 5 ng/mL → 1.2×5 + 2 = 8 ng/mL. An acute level ≥8 ng/mL would be considered a true upward change consistent with mast cell activation.

Key interpretation points:
- If you have an acute sample only: an elevated value can support that activation occurred, but you still need a baseline later to apply the 20%+2 rule or to decide if baseline is pathologic.
- If baseline tryptase is persistently elevated (classically >20 ng/mL), that is one of the WHO minor diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis and should prompt referral.
- A normal tryptase does NOT exclude clinically significant anaphylaxis.

## What an elevated baseline suggests — and what to do next
Persistent baseline elevation should trigger specialist referral. The main possibilities to consider are systemic mastocytosis and hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia (HαT); other causes include chronic myeloid disorders and renal impairment.

Initial next steps when baseline tryptase is elevated or when the 20%+2 rule is met:
- Arrange early referral to Allergy/Immunology (and often Hematology) for coordinated workup.
- Order a repeat baseline tryptase (≥24 h after symptom resolution) to confirm persistence.
- Consider KIT D816V mutation testing — this can be done on peripheral blood as an initial, less invasive screen (sensitive assays) but bone marrow testing is often required for definitive diagnosis.
- If suspicion is high or peripheral testing is inconclusive, proceed to bone marrow biopsy with histology, immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry for aberrant mast cell markers (CD25/CD2) and targeted molecular testing (KIT D816V). The WHO major criterion requires dense multifocal mast cell aggregates in marrow or other extracutaneous tissue; minor criteria include baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL, KIT mutation, abnormal mast cell morphology, and expression of CD25/CD2 on mast cells.
- Consider TPSAB1 copy‑number testing for hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia if baseline tryptase is chronically mildly‑moderately elevated — this is a germline finding that increases baseline tryptase and predisposes to mediator symptoms and severe anaphylaxis.

In the case, tryptase was elevated and the patient had hepatomegaly on ultrasound and neuropsychiatric symptoms — all supportive but not sufficient to fulfill WHO criteria because the bone marrow specimen was limited. Real‑world diagnostics are messy; early specialist involvement matters.

## How a diagnosis (or high suspicion) changes care
Detecting mast cell disease changes both acute disposition and long‑term preventive strategies.

Acute and near‑term:
- Consider prolonged observation or ICU monitoring for patients with severe initial reactions, multiple epinephrine doses, or recurrent episodes (as in the case).
- Don’t rely on steroids to prevent biphasic reactions — the evidence for steroids preventing biphasic anaphylaxis is weak. Steroids were given in the reported case, reflecting common practice, but they are not a proven preventive measure.

Long‑term adjustments:
- Prescribe epinephrine autoinjector(s) and educate on prompt use. For adults >50 kg recommend the 0.5 mg dose; many auto‑injectors available are only 0.3 mg and may underdose larger adults. For patients with mast cell disorders consider ensuring access to 0.5 mg devices (where available) and multiple autoinjectors (at least two), with instruction to repeat doses every 5 minutes as needed until emergency services arrive.
- Start daily H1 (eg, cetirizine) and often H2 blockade (eg, famotidine) for symptomatic control of mediator symptoms; many mast cell specialists recommend chronic antihistamine therapy.
- For patients with recurrent, severe, or refractory anaphylaxis despite standard measures, consider omalizumab (anti‑IgE) as an adjunct — clinical series show benefit in reducing spontaneous and triggered anaphylaxis in some patients with mast cell disorders. This is specialist‑directed therapy.
- Counsel about trigger avoidance, peri‑procedural prophylaxis (premedication for radiocontrast, anesthesia, vaccines where appropriate), alert bracelets, and family implications (HαT is inherited).

## Practical ED pearls from this case
The patient weighed 104 kg and initially received a 0.3 mg autoinjector — underdosing is common and real. Auto‑injectors are invaluable for reducing treatment delay, but know their dose limitations and prepare to give repeat or higher IM doses promptly when needed. Multiphasic anaphylaxis (multiple relapses) is uncommon but should trigger consideration of underlying mast cell disease and typically merits longer observation and early specialist input.

## Back to systems: make it a protocol
If you’re an ED lead, embed tryptase into your anaphylaxis order set so it’s a reflexive step: treat first, then draw a stat tryptase with time of onset, automated reminder for the 1–2‑hr draw, and a discharge/follow‑up instruction to repeat baseline >24 h after resolution and refer to Allergy/Immunology if baseline is elevated or the 20%+2 rule is met.

## Take‑home messages
- Make tryptase part of your anaphylaxis toolkit: draw an acute sample as soon as feasible (don’t delay epinephrine), draw again at 1–2 hours (peak), and get a baseline ≥24 hours after full recovery.
- Use the 20% + 2 rule: acute ≥ (1.2 × baseline) + 2 ng/mL supports mast cell activation.
- A persistently elevated baseline tryptase (especially >20 ng/mL) or recurrent/severe/multiphasic anaphylaxis should prompt Allergy/Immunology ± Hematology referral for KIT D816V testing, bone marrow evaluation, and consideration of hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia testing.
- Diagnosis of mast cell disease changes management: longer observation for acute events, ensure appropriate epinephrine dosing (consider 0.5 mg autoinjector for adults >50 kg and provision of multiple devices), daily H1/H2 blockade, and specialist‑directed adjuncts such as omalizumab in select patients.
- Don’t rely on steroids to prevent biphasic reactions; treat promptly with epinephrine and plan observation based on severity and risk factors.

If you only take one systems change from this post: build a simple “anaphylaxis + tryptase” order set in your ED. It makes the difference between a missed opportunity to diagnose an occult mast cell disorder — and a life‑changing diagnosis that allows prevention, specialist care, and safer downstream management.



# The Penicillin Allergy Label That Backfired: How Delabeling Prevents Harm

This case stops you cold: a woman in her 60s with a distant, mild childhood reaction to amoxicillin (facial swelling in adolescence, no airway or circulatory compromise) ends up with severe, multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single dose of clarithromycin. The chain of events is the classic “allergy‑label cascade” — a probably false penicillin allergy label -> second‑line antibiotics (clindamycin/macrolide) -> unexpected, life‑threatening reaction. It’s a perfect example of how an unverified allergy label can do real harm and why systematic delabeling belongs in the ED/inpatient toolkit.

## Quick recap of the case (what actually happened)
A woman in her 60s with a documented beta‑lactam allergy from adolescence presented with erysipelas and was treated by her GP with clindamycin, later switched to clarithromycin. One hour after the first clarithromycin 500 mg oral dose she developed progressive pruritus, flushing, diaphoresis, a sense of throat swelling, hypotension (BP 74/50) and hypoxaemia (SpO2 88% on room air). In the ED she received IM epinephrine (0.3 mg auto‑injector), IV clemastine, oxygen and fluids with an initial good response.

Despite that, she had multiple relapses in the first 12 hours and required additional IM epinephrine doses (the first two doses were 0.3 mg via auto‑injector; subsequent doses were 0.5 mg IM). Steroids were added after the first recurrence. She was admitted to ICU, ultimately stabilized, and discharged after 3 days on levocetirizine and an epinephrine auto‑injector. Further workup showed elevated tryptase levels and a bone marrow biopsy insufficient to meet formal WHO criteria for systemic mastocytosis but suggestive of increased mast cell activation — a possible explanation for unusually severe/multiphasic anaphylaxis. No confirmatory allergy testing to clarithromycin was performed because of the risk.

## Why this matters beyond “another bad allergic reaction”
- Penicillin allergy labels are common and usually wrong. Roughly 1 in 10 patients report a penicillin allergy, but the vast majority are not truly allergic on formal testing (sensitization wanes, errors in childhood history, or misattribution).
- False penicillin labels steer clinicians away from narrow‑spectrum beta‑lactams and toward alternatives (macrolides, clindamycin, fluoroquinolones, vancomycin). That shift drives measurable downstream harms: broader antimicrobial exposure, more Clostridioides difficile and resistant organisms, longer stays, higher costs — and, as this patient shows, the potential for unexpected adverse drug reactions from second‑line agents.
- Delabeling is not just stewardship; it is patient safety. When the label changes prescribing, it can change outcomes.

## A pragmatic, safety‑first approach you can start using tomorrow
You don’t need an allergist appointment for every penicillin allergy story. Use a focused history to stratify risk, then act.

1) Targeted history — the essential questions
Ask: What happened? When did it happen? What symptoms (rash, itching, face/tongue swelling, wheeze, hypotension, mucosal erosions, blistering)? How soon after the drug did it start? Have you taken penicillins/cephalosporins since without problem? Any severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SJS, TEN, DRESS), organ involvement, or ICU admission?

2) Risk stratify (practical categories)
- Low risk: vague history, isolated childhood rash or unknown rash without mucosal/respiratory/cardiovascular features, remote (>10 years) event, or symptoms consistent with intolerance (GI upset) rather than allergy.
- Not low risk / intermediate: clear immediate urticaria/angioedema but not life‑threatening; uncertain timing.
- High risk: documented anaphylaxis with airway/respiratory/circulatory compromise, or severe cutaneous adverse reaction (SJS/TEN/DRESS), or recent reaction within months.

In this case the adolescent facial swelling without airway/circulatory symptoms would typically be considered low risk and a candidate for delabeling pathways.

3) Actionable pathways
- Low‑risk patients: offer a supervised direct oral amoxicillin (or amoxicillin challenge) in a setting with monitoring and resuscitation readiness, and observe (typical protocols use a single supervised dose with at least 60 minutes observation; institutional protocols vary). If the challenge is tolerated, remove the penicillin allergy label and document clearly.
- Intermediate‑risk patients: consider penicillin skin testing ± incremental challenge, often in consultation with allergy or pharmacy depending on local pathways.
- High‑risk patients or those with severe cutaneous reactions, uncertain history, or suspected mast cell disorder: do NOT do a direct challenge in ED — arrange specialist referral (allergy/immunology), and use alternative antibiotics until cleared.

4) When beta‑lactam is needed urgently
If you need to use a cephalosporin and delay is dangerous, choose a cephalosporin with a dissimilar R‑side chain to the implicated penicillin and consider a graded challenge if time allows. For truly high‑risk histories or severe reactions, obtain allergy consultation or use an alternative agent known to be safe for that patient.

5) Safety checklist for bedside challenge
Obtain informed consent, ensure IV access or immediate access to resuscitation meds (epinephrine, oxygen, monitoring), follow a local protocol for dosing and observation, and document result clearly in the chart and in the electronic allergy list.

## Practical implementation in the ED / inpatient setting
- Embed a simple structured allergy history into triage/EMR: the single most valuable thing is a focused history rather than “allergy: penicillin.”
- Create a delabeling pathway with stewardship/pharmacy and allergy: pharmacist‑led screens can flag low‑risk inpatients on alternative antibiotics for a challenge.
- Train ED staff on one standardized protocol for low‑risk direct oral challenge and ensure a clear escalation plan if symptoms appear.
- Make delabeling part of the admission checklist for patients receiving broad‑spectrum agents for infections where beta‑lactams are first‑line (e.g., skin/soft tissue infections).
- Track outcomes: percent of labeled patients delabeled, antibiotic use changes, and any adverse reactions to challenges.

## Special considerations — the tough cases
- Suspected mast cell disorders: higher baseline tryptase and exaggerated anaphylaxis are possible (as in this case). These patients need specialist input; do not attempt routine delabeling without allergy/hematology collaboration.
- Severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SJS/TEN/DRESS): absolute contraindication to direct challenge.
- Pregnancy, severe immunosuppression, or active unstable illness: weigh risks and consult allergy.

## A couple of related clinical pearls (from the case)
- Epinephrine dosing matters. Guidelines recommend IM epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg in adults; many adult patients (and many auto‑injectors) receive only 0.3 mg, which may be suboptimal for heavier adults. In this case the patient (104 kg) initially received 0.3 mg IM and later 0.5 mg IM.
- Corticosteroids are commonly used to “prevent” biphasic reactions, but the evidence for routine steroid use to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis is weak. This patient developed multiphasic anaphylaxis despite steroid administration — a reminder that steroids aren’t reliably protective and that monitoring strategies matter.

## Bottom line
An unverified penicillin allergy label is not benign. It changes prescribing, increases exposure to second‑line drugs, and — as this case painfully illustrates — can lead to unexpected, severe harm. A simple, history‑driven delabeling pathway in the ED/inpatient setting (direct oral challenge for low‑risk patients; targeted testing/referral for others) is practical, safe, and an intervention that improves both antimicrobial stewardship and patient safety.

Key takeaways
- Approximately 1 in 10 patients carry a penicillin allergy label, but most are not truly allergic on testing — this label often causes downstream harm.
- Use a focused history to stratify risk: low‑risk histories are candidates for supervised direct oral amoxicillin challenge.
- Direct oral challenge in low‑risk patients is safe in monitored settings and can meaningfully change prescribing.
- For urgent beta‑lactam needs, choose cephalosporins with dissimilar side‑chains or consult allergy for testing/graded challenge.
- Embed delabeling into ED/inpatient workflows with stewardship/pharmacy and clear protocols — it’s practical, time‑saving, and reduces patient harm.
- Suspected mast cell disorders or severe prior reactions require specialist referral — don’t try routine delabeling in these patients.



# Watching and Waiting: A Pragmatic, Risk‑Stratified Observation Plan After Anaphylaxis

We recently read a striking case that brings the practical question back to the bedside: a woman in her 60s developed multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single 500 mg dose of oral clarithromycin. She presented in shock with hypoxaemia, improved after intramuscular epinephrine, then had repeated relapses over the next 12 hours requiring additional IM epinephrine (four episodes in total). Tryptase was persistently elevated and systemic mastocytosis was suspected. Teams considered options ranging from continuous IV epinephrine infusion to simply “watching and waiting” — but there are no hard rules. This is exactly the clinical dilemma emergency and inpatient clinicians face: who can safely go home after a single rapid response, and who belongs in monitored beds?

Below I translate common risk factors into a simple, pragmatic observation and disposition plan you can use at the bedside — grounded in the issues raised by that case.

## Why observation matters (briefly)
Biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis is the recurrence of allergic symptoms after an apparent clinical recovery and without further allergen exposure. Reported incidence varies widely, but most series cluster around 4–6%. Recurrences usually occur within 24 hours but have been reported later. Because biphasic/multiphasic courses are unpredictable, observation after anaphylaxis is reasonable — but blanket admission for everyone is neither practical nor necessary. The trick is risk‑stratification.

## A pragmatic, risk‑stratified observation plan
Below is a working approach I use to guide observation time and disposition. The times are practical, not absolute — use clinical judgement and local resources.

Low risk — observe 2–4 hours
- Who: patients with a single, rapid, complete clinical recovery after one correctly administered IM epinephrine dose; clear and removed/avoided trigger (e.g., known food and no ongoing exposure); no airway compromise, no hypotension or hypoxaemia; no significant comorbidity; reliable social support and ability to return.
- What to watch for: stable vitals, no recurrent symptoms during observation, and ability to tolerate oral intake and ambulate.
- Disposition: discharge with instructions, two autoinjectors (dose appropriate for weight), written action plan and early allergy follow‑up.

Intermediate risk — observe 6–12 hours (ED or short‑stay)
- Who: unknown trigger; required a second IM epinephrine dose or needed prolonged supportive care (IV fluids, supplemental oxygen); mild/moderate cardiopulmonary comorbidity (well‑controlled asthma, treated cardiovascular disease); presentation with prominent GI symptoms, systemic urticaria but no sustained shock.
- What to watch for: recurrence in the first 6–12 hours is more likely in this group; consider longer observation if recurrent symptoms are late during the window.
- Disposition: extended observation in ED or short‑stay unit; arrange allergy follow‑up.

High risk — observe 12–24 hours (often admission; telemetry/ICU as indicated)
- Who: initial shock or hypoxaemia (like our case), need for multiple epinephrine doses, delayed epinephrine administration, oral/long‑acting trigger (oral medication, depot preparations), suspected or known mast cell disorder, prior biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis, severe asthma or significant cardiac disease, events occurring at night or in patients who live remotely or cannot return quickly.
- What to watch for: recurrent need for epinephrine, hemodynamic instability, progressive airway involvement. Consider step‑down only after 24 hours symptom‑free and stable.
- Disposition: admit to monitored bed; if repeated relapses or refractory symptoms, escalate to ICU and consider continuous IV epinephrine infusion under experienced supervision.

Use the observation window after "full clinical resolution"
- Practical tip: start your observation clock after the patient has achieved complete clinical resolution (no respiratory distress, normal vitals, no progressive urticaria) following their last epinephrine dose. A patient who has persistent minor urticaria without systemic features isn't necessarily “resolved.” Aim for a symptom‑free period before counting your observation time.

## When to escalate: when “watching” becomes unsafe
If a patient requires multiple additional IM epinephrine doses, has recurrent hypotension or hypoxaemia, progressive airway compromise, or persistent hemodynamic instability despite fluids and IM epinephrine, escalate. That often means ICU, continuous IV epinephrine infusion and invasive monitoring. The case team considered continuous infusion but ultimately did not need it — the patient’s relapses decreased in severity and resolved within the monitoring window. Still, have a low threshold for escalation when episodes are frequent or worsening.

## Practical, evidence‑aware points that matter at the bedside
Epinephrine dosing and delivery
- IM epinephrine is first‑line: 0.01 mg/kg (max 0.5 mg) into the mid‑anterolateral thigh. Many adults should get 0.5 mg. Auto‑injectors are invaluable for rapid administration, but many devices deliver only 0.3 mg; for adults >50 kg this is potentially subtherapeutic — be prepared to give 0.5 mg IM with a syringe if needed in the ED.
- Early epinephrine is key. Delayed administration is associated with worse outcomes and may increase the chance of biphasic reactions.

Steroids and antihistamines
- Antihistamines can help cutaneous symptoms but do NOT treat airway or hemodynamic compromise.
- Corticosteroids have not been convincingly shown to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis and are no longer recommended routinely for that purpose. Use steroids selectively (e.g., for protracted urticaria or coexistent asthma treatment per usual indications), not as a reflex to “prevent” relapse.

Tryptase and mast cell disorders
- If you suspect a mast cell disorder (severe anaphylaxis, idiopathic or recurrent severe reactions, or unusual triggers), measure serum tryptase: obtain an acute sample as soon as feasible, a second sample ~1–2 hours after onset (within 4 hours if possible), and a baseline sample ≥24 hours after complete clinical resolution. Persistent elevation of baseline tryptase merits specialist referral. In the case above, imperfect timing of tryptase samples complicated interpretation; nonetheless persistent elevation raised concern for systemic mastocytosis and influenced the team’s decision to observe and refer.

Practical constraints and shared decision‑making
- Observation decisions are not only clinical: nighttime events, long travel times, poor access to care, or inability to administer epinephrine correctly at home push you toward longer observation or admission.
- Discuss risk and rationale with the patient and family. Document shared decision making if you discharge someone at intermediate risk.

## Discharge essentials (what every patient should leave with)
Before discharge, ensure the patient:
- Has been stable for the planned observation window, symptom‑free and tolerating oral intake.
- Receives at least two epinephrine autoinjectors (dose appropriate for weight) and hands‑on training on their use.
- Has a written anaphylaxis action plan and brief verbal reinforcement of when/how to use epinephrine and when to call emergency services.
- Has antihistamines for symptomatic relief if needed and understands that steroids do not reliably prevent relapse.
- Has timely allergy/immunology follow‑up arranged (to identify trigger and, if indicated, investigate mast cell disorders).
- Understands return precautions and when to come back (any recurrence of respiratory symptoms, hypotension, or symptoms unresponsive to epinephrine).

## Applying this to the clarithromycin case
This patient ticked multiple high‑risk boxes: initial shock with hypoxaemia, repeated relapses requiring additional epinephrine, and a suspected underlying mast cell disorder. ICU monitoring after the first relapse was a reasonable and defensible choice. The team debated continuous IV epinephrine versus continued IM dosing with close observation; the relapses abated and they avoided infusion but arranged specialty follow‑up and education on autoinjector use. This case neatly illustrates why a one‑size‑fits‑all discharge rule would have been unsafe.

## Key take‑home messages
- Low risk (single, rapid, complete response to one IM epinephrine dose, known/removed trigger, no comorbidity) → observe 2–4 hours after full clinical resolution.
- Intermediate risk (unknown trigger, second IM dose, mild comorbidity) → observe 6–12 hours.
- High risk (shock/hypoxaemia, multiple doses, delayed epinephrine, oral/long‑acting trigger, suspected mast cell disease, prior biphasic reaction, night‑time event, remote living) → observe 12–24 hours and often admit to monitored care; escalate to ICU/IV epinephrine for refractory or repetitive relapses.
- IM epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg is the cornerstone; many autoinjectors are 0.3 mg — be ready to give 0.5 mg IM for larger adults in the ED.
- Steroids do not reliably prevent biphasic anaphylaxis and should not be used routinely for that purpose.
- Measure tryptase appropriately (acute and baseline samples) if severe or atypical reaction; persistent elevation requires specialist follow‑up.
- Discharge only with at least two autoinjectors, a written action plan, and arranged allergy follow‑up.

Anaphylaxis is common, biphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon but unpredictable. A simple, risk‑based observation framework lets us safely balance patient safety, resource use and shared decision‑making — and keeps us ready for the rare patient who, like the woman in this case, needs more than a single dose of epinephrine and a quick discharge.
