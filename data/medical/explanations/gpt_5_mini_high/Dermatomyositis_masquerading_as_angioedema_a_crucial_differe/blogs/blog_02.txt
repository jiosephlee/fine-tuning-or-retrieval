Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Bulbar Myositis Playbook: Dysphagia, Aspiration, and Airway in Dermatomyositis

What makes this case worth your attention? A previously well man in his 40s walked into the ED with 3 weeks of progressive, non-tender neck swelling that looked — to ENT and the admitting team — like a deep neck process or angioedema. Flexible nasendoscopy was “messy” (increased secretions and coughing) but showed no obstructing lesion. A bright-line test changed everything: a creatine kinase of 16,184 U/L pointed to severe myositis, and further work-up revealed SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis with prominent head/neck muscle involvement, severe pharyngeal dysphagia on videofluoroscopy, and biopsy-proven complement-mediated microangiopathy (C5b-9 deposition). The patient required IV pulse steroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing immunotherapy and temporary nasogastric feeding to protect his lungs — and recovered over months.

If you see “atypical angioedema,” a messy FNE, or unexplained pooling of secretions — think bulbar myositis. Here’s a practical playbook for recognition, immediate bedside steps, how to interpret a “messy” flexible nasendoscopy, why to get videofluoroscopy early, when to protect the airway, and how to feed and monitor recovery.

## Recognize bulbar myositis — the red flags
Bulbar myositis can present subtly and mislead teams toward ENT or allergy diagnoses. In this case the clues were:

- Progressive, non-tender cervical soft‑tissue swelling (CT showed diffuse strap muscle oedema), not typical for infectious cellulitis or histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema.
- New proximal weakness (hip flexors 4/5) and mild myalgias.
- Subtle skin/nail signs (periungual erythema, Holster sign) rather than florid Gottron’s or heliotrope rashes.
- “Messy” nasendoscopy with pooling, secretions and coughing but no obstructing lesion.
- Markedly elevated muscle enzymes (CK 16,184 U/L) and transaminases, MRI/PET showing head-and-neck muscle inflammation, and muscle biopsy with perifascicular necrosis and C5b-9 deposition — the pathophysiology is inflammatory myositis with complement-mediated microangiopathy causing muscle edema and impaired bulbar mechanics.

Clinical pearl: when neck swelling doesn’t behave like ordinary angioedema (no ACEi, no urticaria, normal complement/C1 esterase), check a CK and examine for subtle cutaneous signs. Don’t let a “normal” flexible nasendoscopy lull you into false reassurance.

## The “messy” flexible nasendoscopy — how to read it
Flexible nasendoscopy is a rapid bedside tool, but its findings in bulbar myositis are often functional, not anatomic.

What a “messy” FNE commonly shows in bulbar myositis:
- Increased secretions and pooling in the pharynx.
- Inability to clear secretions on command or with cough.
- Coughing during the exam with evidence of penetration of secretions.
- Larynx and vocal folds may appear structurally normal.

How to act on a messy FNE:
- Treat as evidence of impaired airway protection — NPO pending further assessment.
- Urgently involve speech‑language pathology (SLP), ENT and the admitting medical team.
- Order an instrumental swallow study (videofluoroscopy) rather than relying on bedside tests alone.
- Consider airway monitoring/ICU observation if secretions are worsening or respiratory status is borderline.

Clinical pearl: secretions + poor clearance + cough on FNE = functional bulbar impairment until proven otherwise.

## Videofluoroscopy (VFSS) — get it early
Videofluoroscopic swallow study is the most actionable test for pharyngeal dysphagia and aspiration risk. In this case the VFSS showed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform fossa residue and penetration of thin and mildly thick fluids to the vocal cords — objective evidence that the patient could not safely protect his airway.

Why VFSS early?
- It tells you whether aspiration is occurring, whether it’s silent, and what consistencies are safer.
- It defines the physiologic lesion (reduced hyolaryngeal elevation, delayed swallow trigger, poor pharyngeal clearance) so SLP can recommend targeted compensatory strategies.
- It provides objective data to justify enteral feeding and for later reassessment to time return to PO intake.

Clinical pearl: a messy FNE is a trigger to obtain VFSS without delay — bedside swallow screens are helpful but can miss silent aspiration.

## Airway and aspiration management — when to escalate
Bulbar myositis can threaten the airway by impairing secretion clearance and protective reflexes. The management priorities are the same as in any high‑risk dysphagia: secure the airway, protect the lungs, and treat the underlying disease.

When to consider escalation (ENT/ICU/intubation):
- Rapidly progressive bulbar weakness with inability to manage secretions or maintain oxygenation.
- Stridor, progressive hypoxia, hypercapnia, or signs of respiratory muscle fatigue.
- Repeated aspiration with respiratory compromise, or failure of conservative measures.

In the reported case the patient had significant airway‑muscle involvement and received high‑dose IV methylprednisolone (1000 mg IV daily for 3 days) and prompt IVIG; intubation was not required. Early aggressive immunotherapy may avert the need for intubation if given promptly, but do not delay airway protection if clinical deterioration occurs.

Clinical pearl: balance early immunomodulation with a low threshold for securing the airway. Don’t rely on therapy to avoid intubation if the patient is tiring or losing airway protective reflexes.

## Immunotherapy in dysphagia‑dominant dermatomyositis — why IVIG early?
Severe bulbar involvement is an indication for rapid and aggressive immunotherapy.

What was done in this case:
- Pulse IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg IV daily × 3 days, then high‑dose oral prednisone (60 mg) with taper.
- IVIG loading dose 2 g/kg over 5 days (total 165 g for this patient), followed by repeat monthly cycles.
- Steroid‑sparing therapy: mycophenolate mofetil 1 g twice daily and two doses of rituximab 1000 mg 2 weeks apart.
- Methotrexate avoided because of concern for interstitial lung disease (patient had abnormal PFTs and CT findings consistent with aspiration/not yet fibrosis).

Why IVIG?
- IVIG can produce relatively rapid clinical responses in dermatomyositis and is often prioritized when bulbar dysfunction threatens the airway or nutrition.
- In antibody‑mediated disease with complement deposition (C5b‑9 seen on biopsy), IVIG may help modulate immune mechanisms more quickly than oral steroid‑sparing meds.
- Real‑world practice commonly combines high‑dose steroids + IVIG early for severe cases; steroid‑sparing agents are added for maintenance.

Safety and screening:
- Before potent immunosuppression, screen for HBV/HCV/HIV/CMV/VZV and latent TB (this patient had comprehensive screening).
- Start PJP prophylaxis when indicated — this patient initially received TMP‑SMX but developed hemolytic anaemia and was switched to nebulized pentamidine.
- Be mindful of IVIG adverse effects (volume overload, thrombosis, renal impairment, aseptic meningitis).

Clinical pearl: in dysphagia‑dominant, airway‑threatening dermatomyositis, think pulse steroids + early IVIG while you assemble steroid‑sparing therapy.

## Nutrition: NG vs PEG, and timing return to oral intake
Feeding and medication delivery must be safe and pragmatic.

Strategy used in this case:
- Nasoenteric tube feeding was used to protect the lungs and to deliver oral medications while dysphagia persisted.
- The NG tube remained for approximately 2 months; once VFSS and clinical improvement permitted, the NG was removed and the patient advanced to soft solids.
- PEG was not required.

Practical approach:
- If aspiration risk is high or oral intake unsafe, institute enteral feeding early — NG is quick, reversible and appropriate when you expect recovery in weeks to a few months.
- Consider PEG if dysphagia is likely to be prolonged (>4–6 weeks) or if long‑term enteral access is anticipated.
- Coordinate with SLP and dietetics for caloric/protein targets and a staged reintroduction of PO guided by serial VFSS.

Clinical pearl: use the least invasive enteral route that will safely meet needs while you treat the underlying myositis; reassess frequently.

## Supportive care for aspiration pneumonitis and respiratory monitoring
This patient had CT evidence of peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration pneumonitis. Key points for the team:

- Distinguish chemical aspiration pneumonitis from aspiration pneumonia (superinfection). Treat conservatively unless there is clinical or radiologic evidence of bacterial infection.
- In immunosuppressed patients, have a lower threshold to treat and to obtain sputum cultures, blood cultures and imaging.
- Monitor oxygenation, consider chest physiotherapy and early respiratory therapy involvement.
- Repeat imaging and PFTs can document recovery; in this case consolidation largely resolved by 11 months and PFTs improved.

Clinical pearl: aspiration complications are common with bulbar weakness — preventing aspiration with early NG feeding and SLP strategies is lung‑saving.

## Monitoring recovery and planning return to oral intake
Recovery timelines vary. In this case:
- CK fell from 16,184 U/L to normal (37 U/L) in 3 weeks.
- Proximal muscle strength recovered within 3 weeks.
- Swallowing improved more slowly: NG removed at ~2 months, soft solids, and full dysphagia resolution by 7 months.
- Cervical swelling resolved by 3 months; prednisone was tapered off by 7 months.

Practical monitoring plan:
- Serial CK, AST/ALT, LDH and clinical strength exams to track muscle disease activity.
- Repeat VFSS at intervals guided by clinical improvement (often every 2–6 weeks in the early phase) before advancing diet.
- Ongoing SLP‑led rehabilitation (swallow exercises, compensatory maneuvers, diet modification) and dietetic follow‑up.
- Pulmonary surveillance for ILD and aspiration sequelae; malignancy screening per myositis guidelines when indicated.

Clinical pearl: lab normalization (CK) can precede functional recovery of bulbar muscles — base feeding decisions on objective swallow testing and clinical airway protection, not CK alone.

## Bringing it together — a simple bedside playbook
When you see a patient with unexplained neck swelling, secretions and cough on FNE, or new dysphagia:

- Look beyond ENT: do a quick neuromuscular and skin exam (proximal strength, periungual changes, heliotrope/Gottron signs).
- Order CK STAT and basic inflammatory markers.
- Keep the patient NPO if you suspect impaired airway protection.
- Get urgent VFSS and SLP assessment.
- Involve ENT, rheumatology/immunology and consider ICU if respiratory status is precarious.
- If bulbar myositis is likely and airway or nutrition threatened, initiate high‑dose steroids and early IVIG while arranging steroid‑sparing agents.
- Use NG feeding to protect lungs and deliver meds; consider PEG if prolonged needs anticipated.
- Monitor CK and function serially, repeat VFSS before advancing diet, and follow pulmonary and malignancy surveillance as indicated.

## Take‑home points
- Unexplained, non‑tender neck swelling with a “messy” flexible nasendoscopy (pooled secretions, poor clearance, cough) should prompt consideration of bulbar myositis — get a CK urgently.
- Videofluoroscopic swallow study is the definitive test for pharyngeal dysphagia and should be obtained early when aspiration risk is suspected.
- Protect the airway and lungs first: keep NPO, involve SLP/ENT, and use NG feeding for nutrition and medication delivery while treating the underlying disease.
- Severe bulbar involvement is an indication for rapid immunotherapy — high‑dose IV methylprednisolone and early IVIG are commonly used; add steroid‑sparing agents (eg, mycophenolate, rituximab) for maintenance.
- Monitor recovery with serial VFSS and clinical assessment; CK normalization can precede safe return to oral intake — base feeding decisions on objective swallow studies.
- Coordinate multidisciplinary care (ED/ENT/ICU, rheumatology/immunology, neurology, SLP, dietetics, respiratory) and perform appropriate infection and malignancy screening before and during immunosuppression.

Bulbar myositis can masquerade as ENT disease or angioedema. If the nasendoscopy is messy and the neck swelling doesn’t fit the usual scripts, step back, check CK, and get your swallowing experts involved early — the lungs will thank you.
