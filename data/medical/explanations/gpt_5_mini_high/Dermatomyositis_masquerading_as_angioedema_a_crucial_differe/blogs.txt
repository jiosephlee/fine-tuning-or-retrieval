Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Not All Neck Swelling Is Angioedema: Spotting Dermatomyositis Pseudoangioedema in the ED

## Why this case grabbed our attention
A previously well man in his 40s came to the ED with 3 weeks of progressive, non‑tender neck swelling. The first call was ENT for Ludwig’s angina. CT neck showed diffuse strap‑muscle oedema and retropharyngeal inflammation — not a discrete abscess. He was afebrile, not toxic, had only a mild CRP rise and no clear infectious source. ENT exam was essentially normal. The diagnostic pivot came when clinicians looked for subtle skin/nail findings and ordered a creatine kinase (CK): CK was 16,184 U/L. Final diagnosis — SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis presenting as “pseudoangioedema” with bulbar involvement and severe myositis. Prompt immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab) reversed the swelling and the weakness.

This is the kind of case that can waste days of antibiotics or allergen-directed therapies unless you know when to think outside the angioedema/deep‑neck‑infection box.

## The diagnostic trap
A focal or diffuse neck swelling usually sends ED clinicians down two lanes: infection (cellulitis, deep neck space infection, Ludwig’s) or angioedema (histaminergic or bradykinin mediated). Both are sensible; both can be life‑threatening. But dermatomyositis — an inflammatory myopathy — can produce subcutaneous and muscular oedema of the face/neck, mimic angioedema, cause dysphagia and put airway muscles at risk. It’s uncommon (reported in a small minority of dermatomyositis cohorts), so it’s a low‑probability but high‑impact diagnosis to miss.

In this case the red flags that should have turned suspicion away from primary angioedema or abscess were: the prolonged subacute course (weeks, not hours), afebrile and well appearing, non‑tender swelling, negative ENT focal findings, subtle periungual/nailfold changes and proximal muscle weakness. The CK changed everything.

## What to look for at the bedside
When an atraumatic neck swelling doesn’t behave like classic angioedema or cellulitis, add myositis/dermatomyositis to your differential. Look for:

- Timeline: angioedema is often fast (minutes–hours); infections are often febrile and progressive; dermatomyositis can be subacute (days–weeks).
- Systemic signs: fever, sepsis physiology or toxic appearance favors infection; isolated, painless swelling with preserved vital signs is less typical for abscess.
- Skin and nail clues: inspect periungual areas for erythema, telangiectasia or microhaemorrhages; look for violaceous facial erythema, Holster sign, shawl/V‑neck distribution rashes and any subtle rash on the limbs.
- Neuromuscular signs: ask about dysphagia, voice change, cough with secretions and test proximal strength (hip flexors, shoulder abduction). Bulbar or proximal weakness is a red flag.
- Medication/exposure history: ACE inhibitors or recent new drugs point you toward bradykinin or drug causes; absence of those should widen the differential.

## The single lab that changes the trajectory
If something about the swelling is atypical, draw a CK — early. In this patient, CK was 16,184 U/L. Paired with an AST > ALT and an elevated LDH, the pattern pointed to muscle injury rather than primary hepatic disease. A markedly raised CK should prompt urgent consideration of inflammatory myopathy and early specialty input.

Note: complement and C1‑esterase inhibitor studies were normal in this case — normal results do not rule out dermatomyositis‑related soft tissue oedema. Normal C4/C1‑INH suggests the swelling isn’t bradykinin‑mediated, but it doesn’t diagnose dermatomyositis, so keep looking.

## Practical ED algorithm — when to add myositis to the workup (streamlined)
1) Immediate priorities: secure the airway if there are signs of obstruction, stridor, rapid worsening, or obvious respiratory compromise. Call ENT/anesthesia early. Treat life‑threatening causes first (angioedema, airway compromise, septic neck).

2) Focused history/exam: establish timeline (hours vs days/weeks), ask about dysphagia, voice change, proximal weakness and medication exposures. Inspect skin and periungual areas and test proximal muscle strength.

3) Targeted labs and imaging up front: send CK, AST, ALT, LDH, CBC, CRP, basic chemistry. If infection is a concern, get CT neck with contrast to look for abscess. If angioedema is suspected, send C4 and C1‑INH function/level — but remember normal results don’t exclude a non‑bradykinin inflammatory cause.

4) Interpret results in context:
   - High CK (particularly in the thousands) with AST>ALT and compatible exam = strong signal for myositis → expedite rheumatology/immunology consult and consider inpatient admission.
   - CT showing diffuse strap‑muscle oedema without focal collection + normal ENT mucosal exam = think inflammatory myositis/pseudoangioedema.
   - If CK normal but suspicion remains (eg skin‑predominant disease), discuss with specialists — some dermatomyositis variants can be more cutaneous.

5) Early supportive care: NPO / aspiration precautions if dysphagia; speech/swallow assessment; treat pneumonia/aspiration if present. Avoid unnecessary antibiotics if imaging and clinical picture don’t fit infection — but use clinical judgment.

6) Escalation of therapy: if there is bulbar or airway muscle involvement, very high CK, or rapidly progressive weakness, early high‑dose steroids and urgent specialty input are warranted. In the reported case clinicians gave IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily for 3 days, then a prednisone taper, plus IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab under specialist guidance.

7) Disposition and follow‑up: admit patients with confirmed or strongly suspected inflammatory myopathy for monitoring, initiation of immunosuppression and swallow/airway management. Organize malignancy surveillance and pulmonary evaluation as outpatient/inpatient per specialist recommendations.

## What this case did and why it matters for ED practice
In this patient:
- CT neck showed diffuse muscular oedema rather than a localized collection.
- ENT nasendoscopy showed increased secretions and coughing — a tip toward bulbar weakness rather than mucosal obstruction.
- CK 16,184 U/L with AST 396 U/L and ALT 143 U/L pointed to muscle injury.
- Nailfold capillaroscopy revealed periungual microhaemorrhages.
- Myositis panel was strongly positive for anti‑SAE1 antibody; MRI, PET and muscle biopsy confirmed active dermatomyositis.
- Treatment: IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily × 3, prednisone taper, IVIG 2 g/kg loading and monthly cycles, mycophenolate 1 g twice daily and two doses of rituximab — with PJP prophylaxis attempted (TMP‑SMX stopped for hemolytic anemia; switched to nebulized pentamidine).
- Outcome: marked improvement of neck swelling within weeks, CK normalized at 3 weeks, dysphagia resolved by several months, and prednisone discontinued at 7 months.

The practical message: an early CK saved days of diagnostic wandering and allowed early immunosuppression that likely prevented further airway/muscle damage.

## Pitfalls and “don’t do” moments
Don’t assume normal complement/C1‑INH = “we’re done.” Don’t anchor on infection just because CRP is mildly raised; CRP can be elevated in inflammatory myopathies. Don’t wait for biopsy or antibody panels to start urgent treatment if bulbar/airway muscles are threatened — involve specialists early. And don’t reflexively attribute elevated transaminases to primary liver disease without checking CK and considering muscle as the source.

## Clinical pearls (short list in prose)
Think myositis when neck swelling is subacute, non‑tender and associated with dysphagia or proximal weakness. Early inspection of periungual regions and simple strength testing can reveal subtle dermatomyositis clues. A CK is a cheap, rapid test that often re‑orients the workup; pair it with AST/ALT interpretation (AST often out of proportion) to decide whether the enzymes are muscle‑derived. CT neck that shows diffuse strap‑muscle oedema without abscess and a normal ENT exam is a red flag for inflammatory myopathy. If bulbar/airway involvement is present, bring in rheumatology/immunology and consider early corticosteroid therapy while further workup proceeds.

## Take‑home messages
- If atraumatic neck swelling is subacute, non‑tender, and not behaving like classic angioedema or infection, add inflammatory myopathy/dermatomyositis to your differential.  
- Do the single high‑yield test early: order a CK (with AST/ALT and LDH). Markedly elevated CK with AST>ALT suggests a muscle source.  
- Normal C4/C1‑INH does not exclude dermatomyositis‑related pseudoangioedema.  
- Look for subtle skin/nail findings (periungual changes, facial/limb rashes) and test proximal strength and swallow function.  
- If bulbar or airway muscles appear involved or CK is very high, admit, involve rheumatology/immunology early, and consider urgent immunosuppression under specialist guidance.

If you see a “mysterious” neck swelling in the ED, don’t forget to check the muscles — your CK might be the test that prevents a missed diagnosis.



# Bulbar Myositis Playbook: Dysphagia, Aspiration, and Airway in Dermatomyositis

What makes this case worth your attention? A previously well man in his 40s walked into the ED with 3 weeks of progressive, non-tender neck swelling that looked — to ENT and the admitting team — like a deep neck process or angioedema. Flexible nasendoscopy was “messy” (increased secretions and coughing) but showed no obstructing lesion. A bright-line test changed everything: a creatine kinase of 16,184 U/L pointed to severe myositis, and further work-up revealed SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis with prominent head/neck muscle involvement, severe pharyngeal dysphagia on videofluoroscopy, and biopsy-proven complement-mediated microangiopathy (C5b-9 deposition). The patient required IV pulse steroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing immunotherapy and temporary nasogastric feeding to protect his lungs — and recovered over months.

If you see “atypical angioedema,” a messy FNE, or unexplained pooling of secretions — think bulbar myositis. Here’s a practical playbook for recognition, immediate bedside steps, how to interpret a “messy” flexible nasendoscopy, why to get videofluoroscopy early, when to protect the airway, and how to feed and monitor recovery.

## Recognize bulbar myositis — the red flags
Bulbar myositis can present subtly and mislead teams toward ENT or allergy diagnoses. In this case the clues were:

- Progressive, non-tender cervical soft‑tissue swelling (CT showed diffuse strap muscle oedema), not typical for infectious cellulitis or histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema.
- New proximal weakness (hip flexors 4/5) and mild myalgias.
- Subtle skin/nail signs (periungual erythema, Holster sign) rather than florid Gottron’s or heliotrope rashes.
- “Messy” nasendoscopy with pooling, secretions and coughing but no obstructing lesion.
- Markedly elevated muscle enzymes (CK 16,184 U/L) and transaminases, MRI/PET showing head-and-neck muscle inflammation, and muscle biopsy with perifascicular necrosis and C5b-9 deposition — the pathophysiology is inflammatory myositis with complement-mediated microangiopathy causing muscle edema and impaired bulbar mechanics.

Clinical pearl: when neck swelling doesn’t behave like ordinary angioedema (no ACEi, no urticaria, normal complement/C1 esterase), check a CK and examine for subtle cutaneous signs. Don’t let a “normal” flexible nasendoscopy lull you into false reassurance.

## The “messy” flexible nasendoscopy — how to read it
Flexible nasendoscopy is a rapid bedside tool, but its findings in bulbar myositis are often functional, not anatomic.

What a “messy” FNE commonly shows in bulbar myositis:
- Increased secretions and pooling in the pharynx.
- Inability to clear secretions on command or with cough.
- Coughing during the exam with evidence of penetration of secretions.
- Larynx and vocal folds may appear structurally normal.

How to act on a messy FNE:
- Treat as evidence of impaired airway protection — NPO pending further assessment.
- Urgently involve speech‑language pathology (SLP), ENT and the admitting medical team.
- Order an instrumental swallow study (videofluoroscopy) rather than relying on bedside tests alone.
- Consider airway monitoring/ICU observation if secretions are worsening or respiratory status is borderline.

Clinical pearl: secretions + poor clearance + cough on FNE = functional bulbar impairment until proven otherwise.

## Videofluoroscopy (VFSS) — get it early
Videofluoroscopic swallow study is the most actionable test for pharyngeal dysphagia and aspiration risk. In this case the VFSS showed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform fossa residue and penetration of thin and mildly thick fluids to the vocal cords — objective evidence that the patient could not safely protect his airway.

Why VFSS early?
- It tells you whether aspiration is occurring, whether it’s silent, and what consistencies are safer.
- It defines the physiologic lesion (reduced hyolaryngeal elevation, delayed swallow trigger, poor pharyngeal clearance) so SLP can recommend targeted compensatory strategies.
- It provides objective data to justify enteral feeding and for later reassessment to time return to PO intake.

Clinical pearl: a messy FNE is a trigger to obtain VFSS without delay — bedside swallow screens are helpful but can miss silent aspiration.

## Airway and aspiration management — when to escalate
Bulbar myositis can threaten the airway by impairing secretion clearance and protective reflexes. The management priorities are the same as in any high‑risk dysphagia: secure the airway, protect the lungs, and treat the underlying disease.

When to consider escalation (ENT/ICU/intubation):
- Rapidly progressive bulbar weakness with inability to manage secretions or maintain oxygenation.
- Stridor, progressive hypoxia, hypercapnia, or signs of respiratory muscle fatigue.
- Repeated aspiration with respiratory compromise, or failure of conservative measures.

In the reported case the patient had significant airway‑muscle involvement and received high‑dose IV methylprednisolone (1000 mg IV daily for 3 days) and prompt IVIG; intubation was not required. Early aggressive immunotherapy may avert the need for intubation if given promptly, but do not delay airway protection if clinical deterioration occurs.

Clinical pearl: balance early immunomodulation with a low threshold for securing the airway. Don’t rely on therapy to avoid intubation if the patient is tiring or losing airway protective reflexes.

## Immunotherapy in dysphagia‑dominant dermatomyositis — why IVIG early?
Severe bulbar involvement is an indication for rapid and aggressive immunotherapy.

What was done in this case:
- Pulse IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg IV daily × 3 days, then high‑dose oral prednisone (60 mg) with taper.
- IVIG loading dose 2 g/kg over 5 days (total 165 g for this patient), followed by repeat monthly cycles.
- Steroid‑sparing therapy: mycophenolate mofetil 1 g twice daily and two doses of rituximab 1000 mg 2 weeks apart.
- Methotrexate avoided because of concern for interstitial lung disease (patient had abnormal PFTs and CT findings consistent with aspiration/not yet fibrosis).

Why IVIG?
- IVIG can produce relatively rapid clinical responses in dermatomyositis and is often prioritized when bulbar dysfunction threatens the airway or nutrition.
- In antibody‑mediated disease with complement deposition (C5b‑9 seen on biopsy), IVIG may help modulate immune mechanisms more quickly than oral steroid‑sparing meds.
- Real‑world practice commonly combines high‑dose steroids + IVIG early for severe cases; steroid‑sparing agents are added for maintenance.

Safety and screening:
- Before potent immunosuppression, screen for HBV/HCV/HIV/CMV/VZV and latent TB (this patient had comprehensive screening).
- Start PJP prophylaxis when indicated — this patient initially received TMP‑SMX but developed hemolytic anaemia and was switched to nebulized pentamidine.
- Be mindful of IVIG adverse effects (volume overload, thrombosis, renal impairment, aseptic meningitis).

Clinical pearl: in dysphagia‑dominant, airway‑threatening dermatomyositis, think pulse steroids + early IVIG while you assemble steroid‑sparing therapy.

## Nutrition: NG vs PEG, and timing return to oral intake
Feeding and medication delivery must be safe and pragmatic.

Strategy used in this case:
- Nasoenteric tube feeding was used to protect the lungs and to deliver oral medications while dysphagia persisted.
- The NG tube remained for approximately 2 months; once VFSS and clinical improvement permitted, the NG was removed and the patient advanced to soft solids.
- PEG was not required.

Practical approach:
- If aspiration risk is high or oral intake unsafe, institute enteral feeding early — NG is quick, reversible and appropriate when you expect recovery in weeks to a few months.
- Consider PEG if dysphagia is likely to be prolonged (>4–6 weeks) or if long‑term enteral access is anticipated.
- Coordinate with SLP and dietetics for caloric/protein targets and a staged reintroduction of PO guided by serial VFSS.

Clinical pearl: use the least invasive enteral route that will safely meet needs while you treat the underlying myositis; reassess frequently.

## Supportive care for aspiration pneumonitis and respiratory monitoring
This patient had CT evidence of peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration pneumonitis. Key points for the team:

- Distinguish chemical aspiration pneumonitis from aspiration pneumonia (superinfection). Treat conservatively unless there is clinical or radiologic evidence of bacterial infection.
- In immunosuppressed patients, have a lower threshold to treat and to obtain sputum cultures, blood cultures and imaging.
- Monitor oxygenation, consider chest physiotherapy and early respiratory therapy involvement.
- Repeat imaging and PFTs can document recovery; in this case consolidation largely resolved by 11 months and PFTs improved.

Clinical pearl: aspiration complications are common with bulbar weakness — preventing aspiration with early NG feeding and SLP strategies is lung‑saving.

## Monitoring recovery and planning return to oral intake
Recovery timelines vary. In this case:
- CK fell from 16,184 U/L to normal (37 U/L) in 3 weeks.
- Proximal muscle strength recovered within 3 weeks.
- Swallowing improved more slowly: NG removed at ~2 months, soft solids, and full dysphagia resolution by 7 months.
- Cervical swelling resolved by 3 months; prednisone was tapered off by 7 months.

Practical monitoring plan:
- Serial CK, AST/ALT, LDH and clinical strength exams to track muscle disease activity.
- Repeat VFSS at intervals guided by clinical improvement (often every 2–6 weeks in the early phase) before advancing diet.
- Ongoing SLP‑led rehabilitation (swallow exercises, compensatory maneuvers, diet modification) and dietetic follow‑up.
- Pulmonary surveillance for ILD and aspiration sequelae; malignancy screening per myositis guidelines when indicated.

Clinical pearl: lab normalization (CK) can precede functional recovery of bulbar muscles — base feeding decisions on objective swallow testing and clinical airway protection, not CK alone.

## Bringing it together — a simple bedside playbook
When you see a patient with unexplained neck swelling, secretions and cough on FNE, or new dysphagia:

- Look beyond ENT: do a quick neuromuscular and skin exam (proximal strength, periungual changes, heliotrope/Gottron signs).
- Order CK STAT and basic inflammatory markers.
- Keep the patient NPO if you suspect impaired airway protection.
- Get urgent VFSS and SLP assessment.
- Involve ENT, rheumatology/immunology and consider ICU if respiratory status is precarious.
- If bulbar myositis is likely and airway or nutrition threatened, initiate high‑dose steroids and early IVIG while arranging steroid‑sparing agents.
- Use NG feeding to protect lungs and deliver meds; consider PEG if prolonged needs anticipated.
- Monitor CK and function serially, repeat VFSS before advancing diet, and follow pulmonary and malignancy surveillance as indicated.

## Take‑home points
- Unexplained, non‑tender neck swelling with a “messy” flexible nasendoscopy (pooled secretions, poor clearance, cough) should prompt consideration of bulbar myositis — get a CK urgently.
- Videofluoroscopic swallow study is the definitive test for pharyngeal dysphagia and should be obtained early when aspiration risk is suspected.
- Protect the airway and lungs first: keep NPO, involve SLP/ENT, and use NG feeding for nutrition and medication delivery while treating the underlying disease.
- Severe bulbar involvement is an indication for rapid immunotherapy — high‑dose IV methylprednisolone and early IVIG are commonly used; add steroid‑sparing agents (eg, mycophenolate, rituximab) for maintenance.
- Monitor recovery with serial VFSS and clinical assessment; CK normalization can precede safe return to oral intake — base feeding decisions on objective swallow studies.
- Coordinate multidisciplinary care (ED/ENT/ICU, rheumatology/immunology, neurology, SLP, dietetics, respiratory) and perform appropriate infection and malignancy screening before and during immunosuppression.

Bulbar myositis can masquerade as ENT disease or angioedema. If the nasendoscopy is messy and the neck swelling doesn’t fit the usual scripts, step back, check CK, and get your swallowing experts involved early — the lungs will thank you.



# Decoding Anti‑SAE1 Dermatomyositis: Phenotype, Risks, and What It Means at the Bedside

## Why this case grabbed our attention
A previously well man in his 40s arrives with 3 weeks of progressive, non‑tender neck swelling. The initial workup chased Ludwig’s angina and then idiopathic angioedema — ENT looked inside his throat and found nothing structural to explain the massive soft‑tissue swelling. The clue that changed everything? Subtle cutaneous/nailfold changes, proximal hip‑flexor weakness and a creatine kinase of 16,184 U/L. The diagnosis turned out to be anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis with head/neck muscle inflammation producing “pseudoangioedema.” Muscle MRI, FDG‑PET and a diagnostic muscle biopsy (perifascicular necrosis with C5b‑9 deposition) corroborated the picture. Aggressive immunosuppression (IV methylprednisolone pulses, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab) treated the airway‑threatening muscle involvement; the patient recovered over months.

Why is this useful for the ED/ENT/immunology clinician? Because anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis can present primarily with soft‑tissue swelling of the face/neck that masquerades as angioedema or infection — and missing the diagnosis delays the immunosuppression that these patients often need.

## Anti‑SAE1 in short — what to expect at the bedside
Anti‑SAE1 antibodies target the SUMO activating enzyme (SAE1) and sit in the myositis‑specific antibody (MSA) panel. The clinical phenotype is classically skin‑predominant dermatomyositis but with important strings attached: dysphagia and interstitial lung disease are common, and there’s a nontrivial malignancy signal. In a large pooled series referenced in the case, dysphagia and ILD were reported in roughly half and nearly half of patients respectively (≈46% and 43%), and malignancy in about 18%.

SAE1 disease can therefore look like ‘dermatomyositis with system involvement’ or, as in this case, it can present with prominent head/neck muscle inflammation causing soft‑tissue swelling that mimics angioedema. Classic skin signs (Gottron’s papules, heliotrope) may be absent or subtle — so don’t rule it out just because the obvious rash is missing.

## How SAE1 compares with other MSAs (practical framing)
Different MSAs carry different phenotypic risks. A few practical anchors:
- anti‑MDA5: often associated with severe cutaneous findings and rapidly progressive ILD.
- anti‑TIF1‑γ: linked with an increased malignancy risk.
- antisynthetase antibodies (eg, anti‑Jo‑1): classically have mechanics’ hands and ILD.
The literature and the case series suggest anti‑MDA5 and anti‑TIF1‑γ are more commonly reported with facial/neck swelling than other antibodies — but SAE1 can also produce oedema, albeit less commonly. Bottom line: use the antibody result to sharpen your pretest probabilities, not as a standalone diagnosis.

## Interpreting myositis antibody panels — the line‑blot caveat
Most labs use multiplex line‑blot assays (Euroimmun Euroline is a common example). These generate a signal intensity rather than a simple yes/no. A few practical rules:
- Always interpret the panel in the clinical context. An isolated weak positive in someone without compatible signs should be treated cautiously.
- High signal intensity plus concordant clinical features (very elevated CK, MRI/PET evidence of myositis, classic biopsy findings) is compelling. In this case the SAE1 signal was 173 (normal <35) and matched an explosive clinical and pathologic picture.
- If uncertainty remains and the result would change management, discuss confirmatory testing with your laboratory or specialty team (immunoprecipitation or repeat testing where available).

## Why dermatomyositis can look like angioedema
“Pseudoangioedema” from dermatomyositis is uncommon but real. Mechanistically, perifascicular muscle inflammation and microvascular injury (complement‑mediated — C5b‑9 deposition on capillaries was seen on biopsy in this patient) cause vascular leak and subcutaneous oedema. This is not histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema — it is inflammatory and often more indolent, associated with muscle weakness, and unresponsive to antihistamines or C1‑esterase replacement. Think muscle‑driven swelling when it is persistent, non‑pruritic, and accompanied by weakness or skin/nailfold changes.

## Practical approach when you face unexplained face/neck swelling
Start with ABCs and common causes (infection, airway compromise, medication history like ACE inhibitors). If the swelling is atypical for angioedema (subacute, non‑pruritic, persistent, with systemic clues), expand the differential early to include inflammatory myopathy.

Key steps to consider (in sequence, and tailored to the clinical situation):
- Reassess airway: if there’s bulking from strap muscles, dysphagia or stridor — involve ENT/anesthesia early.
- Medication and family history: ACEi, DPP‑4 inhibitors, family history of hereditary angioedema.
- Focused exam: look for proximal muscle weakness, periungual telangiectasia/microhaemorrhages (nailfold capillaroscopy if available), subtle rashes (shawl/V‑neck/holster signs), Gottron’s/heliotrope.
- Bedside tests: CK (cheap, fast and often diagnostic clue), basic bloods (CBC, CRP/ESR, LFTs, renal function), urinalysis/myoglobin if rhabdo suspected.
- If angioedema workup is being done, check C4 and C1‑esterase function — normal here is a clue against bradykinin‑mediated hereditary angioedema.
- Imaging: CT neck if you suspect deep space infection or to define the distribution of swelling (this case showed strap muscle and retropharyngeal involvement).
- Early specialist input: ENT for airway, rheumatology/immunology for suspected myositis, speech pathology if there is dysphagia.
- If myositis suspected: add myositis antibody panel, MRI muscle (or FDG‑PET if you’re also screening for occult malignancy), and consider biopsy when diagnostic uncertainty persists.
- Swallow assessment: early VFSS or clinical swallow evaluation. This patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia with aspiration on barium swallow and required NG feeding.

## Treatment implications — what this case teaches us
When head/neck muscles are involved, dermatomyositis can be functionally dangerous (airway compromise, aspiration). That changes thresholds for aggressive therapy. This patient received:
- IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days, followed by an oral prednisone taper (started at 60 mg),
- IVIG (2 g/kg loading split over 5 days, then monthly maintenance cycles),
- Mycophenolate mofetil 1 g twice daily,
- Rituximab 1000 mg × 2 doses spaced 2 weeks apart,
- PJP prophylaxis (initial TMP‑SMX, changed to nebulised pentamidine after hemolytic anemia).

Practical treatment points from the case and broader practice:
- Severe dysphagia or respiratory/airway muscle involvement often warrants pulse steroids and consideration of IVIG early.
- Choose steroid‑sparing agents based on comorbidity: methotrexate is often used but may be avoided if ILD is present (as in this case).
- Rituximab is a reasonable escalation for severe or refractory disease.
- Baseline infection screen (HBV/HCV/HIV, TB) and vaccination status is essential before potent immunosuppression.
- PJP prophylaxis is recommended with multi‑agent immunosuppression; monitor for adverse effects and switch agents if needed.
- Multidisciplinary follow‑up (rheumatology/immunology, respiratory, ENT, dietetics/speech and language pathology, physiotherapy) is critical.

## Malignancy surveillance — how aggressively should you search?
Dermatomyositis as a whole carries an association with malignancy. IMACS guidance suggests tailoring screening intensity to risk factors. In this case the team considered the patient high risk for malignancy because he had dermatomyositis and dysphagia (two high‑risk features), plus SAE1 positivity and male sex as intermediate risks. They therefore performed comprehensive screening (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET scan, tumour markers) and planned endoscopic evaluation (gastroscopy/colonoscopy) with ongoing surveillance. Practical takeaway: use a risk‑stratified approach — be more aggressive when there are multiple risk features, and plan follow‑up surveillance rather than a one‑off screen.

## The good news — response and prognosis in this case
This patient’s CK normalized from 16,184 U/L to 37 U/L and proximal strength recovered over weeks. Neck swelling and dysphagia resolved over months, prednisone was stopped by 7 months, and follow‑up imaging showed resolution of aspiration changes. SAE1 disease can be treated effectively with combined immunosuppression, but early recognition matters because dysphagia and ILD are common and can be morbid.

## Clinical pearls and take‑home points
- Consider inflammatory myopathy (including anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis) when face/neck swelling is subacute, non‑pruritic, persistent and accompanied by weakness or subtle cutaneous/nailfold changes.
- Get a creatine kinase early in unexplained neck/face oedema — it’s fast, cheap and often diagnostic.
- Interpret myositis antibody panels in context: high signal intensity on line‑blot + concordant clinical, imaging and histopathology strengthens the diagnosis; isolated weak positives deserve caution.
- “Pseudoangioedema” from dermatomyositis is inflammatory (complement‑mediated capillary injury) not histaminergic/bradykinin‑mediated — it won’t reliably respond to antihistamines or C1‑esterase therapy.
- Early swallow assessment and aspiration precautions are essential; consider NG feeding if severe dysphagia.
- Severe head/neck muscle involvement or life‑threatening dysphagia often needs prompt high‑dose steroids and consideration of IVIG and early steroid‑sparing agents/rituximab.
- Screen for ILD and malignancy according to risk profile (IMACS‑informed approach); use PET/CT and endoscopy selectively based on risk factors.
- Coordinate care early: ENT, speech pathology, rheumatology/immunology, respiratory and sometimes oncology will all be part of the team.

If you see a patient with “angioedema” that doesn’t behave like angioedema, remember this case — a quick CK and a careful search for subtle dermatomyositis signs can change the diagnosis, the treatment and the outcome.



# CT, MRI, PET, and the Biopsy: Imaging and Path Pearls that Separate Myositis from Deep Neck Infection

## The clinical hook — when Ludwig’s angina doesn’t add up
A previously well man in his 40s presented with 3 weeks of progressive neck swelling. CT neck: diffuse oedema of the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space. ENT was worried about Ludwig’s angina but found no drainable collection; flexible nasendoscopy showed no focal anatomic lesion, just increased secretions and coughing. He was afebrile, WCC was normal, CRP only mildly elevated and yet his creatine kinase was 16,184 U/L. Nailfold microhaemorrhages, proximal hip‑flexor weakness and later MRI/PET evidence of widespread muscle inflammation pointed away from infection and toward dermatomyositis. Muscle biopsy with perifascicular necrosis and C5b‑9 deposition sealed the diagnosis (anti‑SAE1 antibody positive). Not your usual ENT case.

This is the scenario I want you to remember: CT shows strap muscle and retropharyngeal edema without a drainable source — think beyond infection. Get a CK, look at the skin and nailfolds, and consider inflammatory myopathy.

## CT — what it's good for, and the red flags that should make you pause
CT remains the first-line imaging when a deep neck space infection is suspected because it reliably finds abscesses that require drainage. But CT is also the test that tells you when NOT to operate.

In infection you usually see:
- Focal low‑attenuation collections with rim enhancement (drainable abscess), possible gas, marked fat stranding, adjacent odontogenic source or destructive bone changes, and often an asymmetric process.

CT patterns that should make you stop and think myositis/inflammatory oedema:
- Diffuse, often symmetric enlargement and low‑attenuation oedema of strap muscles without a rim‑enhancing collection.
- Retropharyngeal or subcutaneous soft tissue swelling without a drainable focus.
- No clear source (no dental abscess, salivary gland focus) and a clinical picture that lacks systemic toxicity (afebrile or low grade, normal/near‑normal WCC).
In the case above, CT excluded a drainable abscess — that was the pivotal moment to broaden the differential.

Remember: CT rules out the surgical target. If there’s no target, additional medical (and rheumatologic) causes must be considered.

## MRI — the muscle map and the biopsy GPS
MRI is the modality of choice for directly assessing muscle inflammation. T2/STIR sequences detect active muscle oedema; T1 can show chronic fatty replacement. MRI tells you:
- Which muscles are inflamed (useful in head & neck myositis where CT is less specific).
- The patchy vs diffuse pattern of involvement.
- Where to target your biopsy to sample active, diagnostic tissue (avoid end‑stage fibrofatty muscle).

In this patient, MRI of the thighs showed active myositis (obturator internus/externus, gracilis, iliopsoas), helping confirm a systemic myositis rather than an isolated neck infection and guiding biopsy planning.

Practical point: if CT excludes an abscess and you suspect myositis, get MRI (if available) to map disease before biopsy.

## FDG‑PET — inflammation mapping with a bonus malignancy screen
FDG‑PET in this case showed diffuse abnormal uptake in multiple muscle groups — head/neck, deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen and pelvis — supporting a systemic inflammatory myopathy rather than a localized infection. PET also found no metabolically active malignancy.

Why this matters:
- PET can simultaneously demonstrate the distribution and burden of muscle inflammation and search for occult malignancy — a relevant concern in dermatomyositis (anti‑SAE1 carries a non‑trivial cancer association in some series).
- A negative PET (in the right clinical context) can be reassuring when cancer workup is part of the diagnostic pathway.

Caveat: PET is not required in every case, but it’s a useful tool when you need both disease mapping and cancer screening.

## The biopsy — the histology that clinches dermatomyositis
Muscle biopsy gave the final answer: perifascicular necrotic and regenerative myofibres, scattered perimysial inflammation with histiocytes, and C5b‑9 (membrane attack complex) deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries. That combination is classic for dermatomyositis and points to a complement‑mediated microangiopathy causing ischemic perifascicular injury.

Why the pathology matters:
- Perifascicular distribution and capillary C5b‑9 deposition differentiate dermatomyositis from other myositides (for example, immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy shows predominant necrosis with scant inflammation; inclusion body myositis has rimmed vacuoles and different inflammatory features).
- The histologic pattern explains the clinical swelling — muscle inflammation and capillary injury produce marked local oedema that can masquerade as angioedema or infection.

## How to think about a swollen neck that has no drainable collection — a practical workflow
When you’re the ED physician or ENT surgeon looking at a CT neck with strap muscle/retropharyngeal oedema and no abscess:
- Pause before the scalpel. If there’s no rim‑enhancing collection, surgery is unlikely to help.
- Reassess the clinical picture: duration (weeks is less typical for acute bacterial infection), systemic signs (fever, severe toxicity), source (dental/salivary), and neuromuscular findings (proximal weakness, dysphagia, increased secretions on nasendoscopy).
- Do some quick, high‑yield bedside tests: check CK (cheap and diagnostic in this setting), look for skin findings (heliotrope, Gottron’s papules, shawl/V‑sign, periungual changes or nailfold microhaemorrhages), and consider nailfold capillaroscopy if available.
- Send directed labs: CK, transaminases (remember AST/ALT can be muscle‑derived), LDH, ANA and myositis serology if available; complement/C1 esterase studies if angioedema is in the differential.
- Get MRI of involved muscle groups to map activity and guide biopsy; consider FDG‑PET if you need both inflammation mapping and cancer screening.
- Involve rheumatology/immunology early if myositis is suspected. If there is significant bulbar or airway muscle involvement, manage airway risk and consider prompt immunosuppression after appropriate infection screening.
- Remember aspiration risk — this patient developed peribronchial consolidation likely from aspiration and had severe pharyngeal dysphagia on video‑swallow.

## Therapeutic and safety reminders from the case
This patient required aggressive immunosuppression (IV methylprednisolone then prednisone taper, IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab) with good recovery. A few practice points to keep in mind:
- If you’re starting high‑dose immunosuppression, do baseline infection screens (HBV, HCV, HIV, TB, VZV/CMV serologies) and consider PCP prophylaxis.
- Tailor steroid‑sparing choices to comorbidities (methotrexate was avoided here because of lung disease concerns; mycophenolate was chosen and tolerated).
- Expect CK and strength to recover over weeks to months; dysphagia may lag and require enteral support.
- Maintain age‑ and risk‑appropriate cancer surveillance — dermatomyositis has a cancer association and in this case IMACS‑guided risk stratification prompted CT/PET and planned endoscopies.

## Pearls — what to remember at 2 a.m.
- If CT neck shows diffuse strap muscle/retropharyngeal oedema without a drainable collection, STOP and get a CK before you scramble toward the OR.
- Look for subtle skin and nailfold clues (periungual changes, capillary microhaemorrhages) — they often tip you off.
- MRI maps active muscle inflammation and directs biopsy; FDG‑PET can show the extent of myositis and help screen for occult malignancy.
- Muscle biopsy with perifascicular necrosis and C5b‑9 deposition is diagnostic for dermatomyositis and distinguishes it from other myositides.
- Dermatomyositis can present with non‑histaminergic, non‑bradykinin neck swelling that mimics angioedema or deep neck infection — dysphagia and bulbar dysfunction are common and can be the dominant early problem.
- Coordinate early with rheumatology/immunology: when myositis is confirmed, prompt immunosuppression (and appropriate infection and malignancy screens) can prevent airway compromise and long‑term morbidity.



# Hitting Hard and Early: Building an Immunosuppressive Strategy for Severe Dermatomyositis with Dysphagia

This case — a previously well man in his 40s who presented with progressive neck swelling thought to be Ludwig’s angina/angioedema but proved to be anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis with severe dysphagia and CK 16,184 U/L — is a great reminder: when dermatomyositis hits the bulbar muscles or threatens the airway, you don’t dither. You treat aggressively, early, and with combinations that bridge the slow‑acting agents.

Below I walk through the pragmatic, stepwise approach used in this case and the rationale behind each move. This is not a guideline document; it’s a practical FOAM-style playbook grounded in what actually worked here.

## Why this presentation deserves “hit hard” therapy
Neck swelling masquerading as angioedema is uncommon in dermatomyositis, but when it represents inflammatory myositis of the cervical/bulbar muscles it can progress to aspiration, frank airway compromise and rapid muscle necrosis. Our patient had objective markers of severe disease: profound CK elevation, MRI/PET evidence of widespread active myositis, severe pharyngeal dysphagia on video fluoroscopy with aspiration physiology, and peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration pneumonitis. Those features — bulbar involvement, high CK and radiographic evidence of aspiration/airway risk — justify immediate, intensive immunomodulation rather than an expectant or single‑agent approach.

## The stepwise regimen used (and the why behind each step)
Pulse intravenous methylprednisolone: this patient received 1000 mg IV daily for 3 days. The purpose of high‑dose IV pulse steroids is rapid suppression of fulminant inflammation — particularly important when airway or swallowing muscles are involved. Pulse therapy can blunt ongoing muscle necrosis and reduce soft tissue oedema more quickly than oral steroids alone.

Rapid transition to oral prednisone and slow taper: after the pulse the team started oral prednisone 60 mg daily (roughly 1 mg/kg) and tapered by 5 mg every 2–3 weeks. The goal: maintain disease control while steroid‑sparing agents and IVIG take effect, but taper slowly enough to avoid relapse. In this patient prednisone was successfully discontinued by 7 months.

Early IVIG for bulbar disease: IVIG was started early — a 2 g/kg loading dose given over 5 days (Gamunex 10% — 165 g total in this patient, ≈35 g/day), followed by monthly maintenance cycles (total 2 g/kg split over each maintenance cycle). IVIG is a key adjunct for severe dermatomyositis with dysphagia or rapidly progressive weakness because it can act sooner than many steroid‑sparing agents and has proven utility in dermatomyositis. Practical caveats: watch renal function, volume status, thrombotic risk and infusion reactions; plan dosing/scheduling with pharmacy.

Introduce a steroid‑sparing immunosuppressant early — mycophenolate as the pragmatic choice here: the patient was started on mycophenolate mofetil 1 g twice daily. Mycophenolate is often favored when there is concomitant lung involvement or concern for methotrexate pulmonary toxicity; it is generally well tolerated and commonly used as a maintenance agent in dermatomyositis. Monitor CBC/LFTs and adjust dosing by clinical response.

Add biologic therapy when disease is severe or not controlled rapidly enough: this patient also received rituximab 1000 mg IV, two doses two weeks apart. Rituximab is commonly used in severe, refractory, or organ‑threatening myositis (bulbar disease, rapidly progressive weakness, or ILD); the B‑cell depletion effect can help patients who have insufficient response to steroids/IVIG and conventional agents. Important safety step: screen for hepatitis B and other infections before rituximab (this case had comprehensive infection screening prior to therapy).

## Why these choices made sense in this case
The combination (pulse steroids → high‑dose prednisone → IVIG + mycophenolate ± rituximab) provided both rapid immunomodulation (steroids + IVIG) and mid‑to‑longer‑term disease control (mycophenolate ± rituximab). That strategy addressed immediate airway and aspiration risk, allowed steroid exposure to be reduced over months, and targeted the underlying autoimmune driver. Clinically this patient had marked improvement in cervical swelling and proximal strength within weeks; CK normalized from 16,184 U/L to 37 U/L by three weeks, while dysphagia recovered more slowly (NG feeding required for ~2 months).

## Monitoring and practical assessments (what to check and when)
Baseline screening before aggressive immunosuppression should include CBC, renal and liver function, CK, viral serologies (HBV/HCV/HIV), varicella/CMV status where relevant, and TB screening (QuantiFERON in this case was indeterminate and AFB sputum negative). Get baseline chest imaging and pulmonary function testing if ILD is a concern — this patient had CT chest, PFTs and PET imaging to characterize lung and systemic involvement.

CK is a useful biomarker for skeletal muscle inflammation and response — in this case it fell dramatically with therapy — but remember CK does not always correlate with bulbar dysfunction; clinical swallowing assessments (formal video fluoroscopy or bedside swallow assessments) are essential. For nutrition and airway safety plan early: consider NG feeding or even temporary enteral access while dysphagia is severe. Repeat imaging (MRI, PET) and muscle biopsy were used diagnostically here; repeat imaging can be helpful when clinical status is unclear or to assess occult malignancy.

## PJP prophylaxis — what to do when TMP‑SMX isn’t tolerated
Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole is first‑line prophylaxis for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia in most immunosuppressed patients, and it was started in this case. The patient developed haemolytic anaemia despite a negative G6PD screen, so TMP‑SMX was stopped. The team used nebulised (inhaled) pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks for PJP prophylaxis — a reasonable alternative when TMP‑SMX cannot be used. Other alternatives include dapsone (requires G6PD testing) or atovaquone (oral), each with pros/cons and variable availability/cost. Choose the alternative that fits the patient’s comorbidities and tolerance, and continue surveillance for opportunistic infections.

## Malignancy screening and follow‑up
Dermatomyositis carries a known malignancy association. In this case the team classified the patient as high risk (dermatomyositis with dysphagia, positive SAE1 antibody, male sex) and performed CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET imaging and tumor markers, with plans for endoscopic evaluation (gastroscopy and colonoscopy). Treating teams should have a low threshold for cancer screening tailored to local guidelines and patient risk factors.

## Clinical pearls from the bedside
- Not all neck “angioedema” is histamine or bradykinin mediated. If angioedema workup is negative and swelling is persistent, look for myositis — examine nailfolds and skin, and check a CK.
- Bulbar involvement (dysphagia, aspiration), very high CK, and MRI/PET evidence of active myositis are red flags that require aggressive, early combination therapy.
- IVIG can be a game changer for severe dysphagia/skin disease and is a bridge while steroid‑sparing agents ramp up.
- When there’s baseline lung disease or concern for ILD, many clinicians favor mycophenolate over methotrexate; methotrexate can cause pulmonary toxicity and is often avoided if ILD is present.
- If TMP‑SMX is not tolerated for PJP prophylaxis, consider alternatives (dapsone if G6PD safe, atovaquone, or inhaled pentamidine as used here).

## Key take‑home points
- Hit hard early when dermatomyositis threatens the airway or causes severe bulbar dysfunction: pulse IV methylprednisolone, then high‑dose oral prednisone with a slow taper.
- Use IVIG early for bulbar disease/aspiration risk — it often works faster than steroid‑sparing agents.
- Start a steroid‑sparing agent early; mycophenolate is a pragmatic choice when lung involvement is a concern.
- Add rituximab for severe, rapidly progressive, or refractory disease after appropriate infection screening.
- Monitor both CK trends and function (especially swallowing) — CK can normalize before bulbar recovery is complete.
- Plan PJP prophylaxis up front; if TMP‑SMX is not tolerated, inhaled pentamidine, dapsone (if G6PD normal) or atovaquone are alternatives.
- Don’t forget malignancy screening and longitudinal follow‑up.

This case is a nice real‑world illustration that when dermatomyositis presents in an unusual way (neck swelling, dysphagia) it’s not a time for conservative monotherapy. It’s a time to pull together rapid immunosuppression, early IVIG, a tolerant steroid‑sparing agent and sensible prophylaxis — and to reassess swallowing and airway constantly until the danger has passed.



# Dermatomyositis and Cancer: How to Operationalize the IMACS Screening Guideline

What jumps out from the case that prompted this post? A previously well man in his 40s presents with progressive neck swelling that looks like angioedema or a deep neck infection, but turns out to be anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis with dysphagia and severe myositis. Because dermatomyositis carries a measurable risk of occult malignancy, the team used an IMACS-style risk approach and launched a targeted cancer screen (CT/PET, tumour markers and planned endoscopy). The result: no cancer found and the patient proceeded to immunosuppression — but the case is a great reminder that cancer screening in myositis is not “one size fits all.” We need a practical bedside algorithm that answers: who do I scan, what do I order first, when do I add PET or endoscopy, and how often do I repeat exams after a negative screen?

## Why cancer screening matters in dermatomyositis (DM)
Dermatomyositis is the inflammatory myopathy most strongly associated with cancer. The excess cancer risk is concentrated close to the time of myositis onset — most new cancers are found within the first 3 years. Different myositis phenotypes and autoantibody profiles carry different risks: classic teaching (supported by cohort data) is that anti‑TIF1‑γ and anti‑NXP2 antibodies confer higher cancer risk; anti‑SAE1 (the antibody in this case) is usually reported as intermediate risk (the case authors quote ≈18% in the SAE literature). Clinical features that bump risk include the dermatomyositis phenotype itself (vs polymyositis), older age, male sex, cutaneous necrosis/ulceration and dysphagia.

The practical consequence: at diagnosis, don’t reflexively order the same battery on everyone. Triage by phenotype + clinical red flags + antibody profile. That’s the principle IMACS asks clinicians to operationalize.

## Translating IMACS risk stratification to the bedside — a pragmatic approach
Start at the bedside: identify the phenotype and look for “red flags” that move a patient into the high‑risk pathway. In the clinic you should document: myositis subtype (dermatomyositis vs polymyositis vs overlap/immune‑necrotizing), age, sex, weight loss/constitutional symptoms, dysphagia, cutaneous ulceration, rapidly progressive symptoms, adenopathy, and the myositis‑specific antibody profile (TIF1‑γ, NXP2, SAE1, MDA‑5, Jo‑1 etc).

If a patient has dermatomyositis plus one or more high‑risk features (dysphagia, age/sex factors, high‑risk antibodies like TIF1‑γ/NXP2), treat them as “high risk” for occult malignancy and pursue a more aggressive baseline screen. If low pretest probability (young patient, non‑dermatomyositis phenotype, low‑risk antibodies) do age‑appropriate cancer screening and be vigilant for new symptoms rather than reflexively ordering whole‑body imaging.

The case illustrates this: dermatomyositis, dysphagia, male sex and anti‑SAE1 placed the patient into the high‑risk category and triggered CT ± PET, tumour markers and plans for endoscopy.

## What to order first (practical first‑line tests)
For a high‑risk patient at diagnosis, a pragmatic first‑line screen is:
- Contrast‑enhanced CT chest/abdomen/pelvis (CT‑CAP) as baseline imaging for common occult cancers.
- Age‑ and sex‑appropriate tests (mammography/pelvic imaging/gynaecology review in women; prostate discussion in men as per local guidance).
- Symptom‑directed exams: ENT exam/nasendoscopy for head & neck symptoms (neck swelling/dysphagia), testicular ultrasound for scrotal mass, pelvic ultrasound for pelvic symptoms, etc.
- Selected tumour markers as adjuncts (e.g., CEA, CA‑125, CA19‑9, PSA) when they would change management — but don’t use markers alone to rule out a malignancy.

Use CT‑CAP as the baseline because it is widely available, relatively specific, and detects most thoraco‑abdominal malignancies. In the published case CT chest and abdomen/pelvis were done and a PET was added; tumour markers were checked and were normal.

## When to add PET‑CT (and the interpretation caveat)
Whole‑body FDG PET‑CT is attractive when you want a single high‑sensitivity search for an occult malignancy. Consider PET‑CT when:
- CT‑CAP is negative but clinical suspicion remains high (multiple high‑risk features).
- You need whole‑body screening and local resources allow it.
- The primary tumour types you’re worried about are FDG‑avid.

Important caveat: active myositis causes diffuse FDG uptake in skeletal muscle and inflamed tissues, which can obscure or generate false positives. The patient in the case had diffuse and prominent FDG muscle uptake consistent with active myositis but no PET‑evidence of cancer. That’s why PET interpretation needs rheumatology/diagnostic radiology input and, when possible, should be contextualized against disease activity (CK, clinical inflammation). Sometimes clinicians will perform PET after brief induction immunosuppression to reduce confounding inflammatory uptake — but weigh that against the desire to find an occult cancer before heavy immunosuppression.

## When to add endoscopy (EGD/colonoscopy)
Endoscopy should be driven by symptoms and risk. In a high‑risk DM patient, add upper GI endoscopy if there is dysphagia, odynophagia, GI bleeding, weight loss, or when regional epidemiology suggests an increased GI cancer prevalence. Colonoscopy is reasonable as part of age‑appropriate screening and may be advanced earlier in high‑risk patients (or when stool tests/symptoms suggest lower GI pathology). In the case, the patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia and the team planned gastroscopy and colonoscopy to exclude occult GI malignancy — an entirely reasonable, symptom‑directed choice.

## How often to repeat screening after a negative initial evaluation
Most cancers associated with DM are discovered within the first 3 years after myositis onset. A pragmatic surveillance schedule that balances sensitivity with resource stewardship is:
- High‑risk patients: baseline CT‑CAP (± PET), then repeat targeted imaging at 6–12 months if baseline negative, and then annually (clinical review ± imaging) up to 3 years. Use earlier re‑imaging if new symptoms appear.
- Intermediate‑risk patients: baseline CT‑CAP or targeted imaging as clinically indicated, then symptom‑driven follow‑up with a low threshold to reimage in the first 12 months.
- Low‑risk patients: age‑appropriate population screening and symptom‑driven investigation; no routine whole‑body imaging required.

Tailor this to local resources and patient preference. Document the plan (“we will re‑image at X months and re‑assess sooner if Y symptoms occur”) so follow‑up is clear.

## What to do if symptoms evolve
Be quick on the trigger: new unexplained weight loss, new or progressive dysphagia, haemoptysis, new lymphadenopathy, or persistent fevers should prompt immediate re‑evaluation and targeted imaging/biopsy. If initial imaging was done during florid muscle inflammation and was non‑diagnostic, consider repeating imaging once inflammation settles or use a different modality (e.g., targeted ultrasound or endoscopy) rather than assuming the negative study was definitive.

If imaging identifies a suspicious lesion, proceed to tissue diagnosis — don’t rely on PET avidity alone. Liaise early with oncology, surgery and relevant subspecialties and use a multidisciplinary team approach.

## How to discuss risk with patients (keep it practical)
Start with the baseline fact: DM increases the risk of an occult cancer, especially early after diagnosis, but most patients will not have a malignancy. Explain the planned, graduated approach: you will do a baseline screen tailored to their risk, then repeat at defined intervals, and investigate promptly if symptoms arise. Discuss the trade‑offs of tests (e.g., PET sensitivity vs cost and false positives in active myositis) and involve the patient in choosing the intensity of the search. Give clear “red flag” symptoms that warrant same‑day contact.

## Practical pearls from the case
- Don’t box every neck swelling into angioedema or infection — look for myositis red flags: skin/nailfold changes, weakness, dysphagia, elevated CK. A low threshold for CK can be diagnostic.
- Use your specialist antibody panel: antibody profile matters for cancer risk stratification (and for prognostication and therapy).
- CT‑CAP is a pragmatic first‑line baseline test for high‑risk patients; PET is useful but may be confounded by active myositis.
- Endoscopy is a targeted, symptom‑driven test — dysphagia in DM is both a morbidity risk and a cancer red flag.
- Most DM‑associated cancers appear within 3 years; focus surveillance in that window while practicing resource stewardship.

## Key take‑home messages
- Dermatomyositis increases the risk of occult cancer; the risk is highest near disease onset and is not uniform across phenotypes and antibody profiles.  
- Triage patients at diagnosis by phenotype, symptoms (especially dysphagia), age/sex and antibody profile; use this to choose a high‑intensity vs low‑intensity screen.  
- For high‑risk patients, baseline contrast CT chest/abdomen/pelvis is a pragmatic first‑line test; add PET‑CT selectively and interpret cautiously in active myositis.  
- Endoscopy (EGD/colonoscopy) is indicated when symptoms or local epidemiology suggest GI malignancy and is reasonable in high‑risk patients.  
- Repeat screening is most useful in the first 3 years; a practical schedule is baseline → repeat at 6–12 months if negative → annual clinical/imaging review up to 3 years, with symptom‑driven earlier re‑assessment.  
- Communicate risk clearly, involve patients in the plan, and coordinate with oncology and relevant specialties for any suspicious findings.

If you’re the clinician at the bedside: document the risk stratification, pick a practical baseline screen that matches the patient’s risk, and set a clear follow‑up plan with alarm symptoms. That’s how IMACS moves from guideline language to bedside action.
