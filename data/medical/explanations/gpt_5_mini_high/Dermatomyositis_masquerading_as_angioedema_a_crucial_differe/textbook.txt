Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Atypical Neck Swelling: Presentation and Initial Clinical Priorities

The patient presented with a subacute, progressive three‑week history of cervical soft‑tissue swelling accompanied by mild myalgia and new proximal hip‑flexor weakness. Vital signs were stable (afebrile, SpO2 99% on room air) and routine blood count was not suggestive of overwhelming sepsis, although CRP was modestly raised. Because neck swelling can rapidly compromise the airway or represent a deep neck infection, the immediate clinical priorities were to assess and secure the airway, to triage for a possible deep neck space infection, and to obtain imaging and ENT review. A CT neck was obtained early and showed diffuse oedema of the strap muscles with extension into the retropharyngeal space, and ENT nasendoscopy excluded a discrete obstructing lesion while noting increased secretions and coughing—an early sign of pharyngeal dysfunction.

An “airway‑and‑infection first” approach is essential with any significant cervical swelling. Ludwig’s angina and other deep neck space infections are surgical emergencies because an expanding collection can produce airway compromise; therefore rapid imaging (contrast CT when safe) and urgent ENT assessment are indicated. In this case the CT showed diffuse inflammatory oedema rather than a focal abscess and the patient remained clinically well without systemic toxicity, findings that lowered the pretest probability of a surgically remediable deep infection but did not eliminate the need for close monitoring and specialist input.

When no infective or structural cause was found, idiopathic angioedema was considered. Angioedema is a common explanation for unexplained soft‑tissue swelling and often prompts immunology involvement. However, several features here were atypical for either histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema: there was no temporal pattern of sudden, self‑limited episodes (the swelling was progressive over weeks), no urticaria or pruritus to suggest a histaminergic mechanism, no medication trigger (for example ACE inhibitors) and no relevant family history to suggest hereditary C1 inhibitor deficiency. Those discordant features argued against conventional angioedema as the primary diagnosis and prompted the team to look for alternative systemic causes.

Careful bedside examination provided the key clues that shifted the differential towards an inflammatory myopathy. The patient had generalized facial erythema extending to the nasolabial folds, periungual capillary changes (microhaemorrhages and avascular areas on capillaroscopy), an erythematous lateral thigh rash (Holster sign), and subtle proximal weakness (hip flexor power 4/5). Nasendoscopy‑documented increased secretions and coughing were consistent with pharyngeal muscle involvement and raised aspiration risk. Importantly, classic dermatomyositis signs such as Gottron’s papules or a heliotrope rash can be absent; therefore the combination of cutaneous nailfold abnormalities plus new proximal weakness should heighten suspicion for dermatomyositis even when neck oedema is the prominent complaint.

Laboratory assessment in this context guided diagnostic reasoning. The modestly elevated CRP and normal white cell count did not point strongly to an acute bacterial deep infection, whereas an urgent creatine kinase (CK) was diagnostic: CK was markedly elevated (16,184 U/L), consistent with active myositis and muscle breakdown. Elevations of AST, ALT and LDH in this setting reflected muscle injury rather than primary hepatobiliary disease. Ordering CK early when neck swelling is unexplained and there are skin or neuromuscular clues was the decisive step that redirected care from an isolated ENT/angioedema pathway towards investigation and immunosuppressive treatment for dermatomyositis.

The clinical lesson for early management is straightforward: secure the airway and exclude life‑threatening deep neck infection first, but if imaging and ENT review do not identify an abscess and the swelling is atypical (subacute, progressive, non‑episodic) look beyond angioedema. A focused skin and nailfold examination, bedside neuromuscular testing, and prompt CK measurement will rapidly distinguish inflammatory myositis from histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated causes and allow timely specialist referral and treatment to reduce risks of dysphagia, aspiration and widespread muscle damage.

## From Suspected Angioedema to Autoimmune Myopathy: Diagnostic Workup and Reasoning

This patient’s pathway from an initial otolaryngology presentation to a unifying diagnosis of dermatomyositis illustrates how targeted investigations and careful synthesis of clinical, laboratory and pathology data change diagnostic probability. Early in the admission the working differentials were infectious deep neck space process (Ludwig’s angina) and angioedema. Several features—lack of fever or localizing signs of abscess on CT, a normal white cell count, a non-tender swollen neck and subtle cutaneous/nailfold findings—made those explanations incomplete and prompted broader testing that ultimately pivoted the team toward inflammatory myopathy.

The single most decisive laboratory trigger was the creatine kinase (CK) drawn because of the combination of neck swelling with proximal weakness and nailfold abnormalities. The CK was profoundly elevated at ~16,000 U/L, a magnitude that is characteristic of active myofibre necrosis rather than a primary dermatologic or simple soft-tissue oedema process. Concomitant transaminase and LDH elevations (AST 396 U/L, ALT 143 U/L, LDH 692 U/L) are best interpreted in this context as muscle-derived rather than as isolated hepatocellular injury; AST in particular is abundant in muscle and often rises in parallel with CK. The absence of renal impairment and a negative urine myoglobin in this patient meant there was no evidence of myoglobinuria–mediated renal injury despite very high CK.

Because angioedema (histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated) was a principal initial consideration, specific tests to evaluate that possibility were performed. Normal C3 and C4 levels and normal C1-esterase inhibitor quantity and function make hereditary or acquired C1-INH deficiency (bradykinin-mediated angioedema) unlikely. Clinically, the patient lacked features typical of histaminergic angioedema (urticaria, pruritus, episodic waxing–waning and response to antihistamines/steroids) and had no medication triggers such as an ACE inhibitor. Taken together, the laboratory and clinical picture reduced the probability that the neck swelling represented true angioedema.

Imaging and functional testing next helped to reframe the neck swelling as part of a systemic myopathic process. CT neck had shown diffuse oedema of strap muscles and retropharyngeal inflammatory change but no drainable collection—findings that argue against an untreated deep neck abscess and are compatible with inflamed muscle. MRI of the thighs demonstrated muscle oedema consistent with active myositis in pelvic and proximal thigh muscles, confirming a multisite inflammatory myopathy rather than isolated neck soft-tissue disease. FDG–PET further mapped active disease by showing diffuse increased muscle uptake most prominent in head/neck, shoulder girdle and pelvic musculature; PET thereby provided two pieces of information at once—anatomic distribution and metabolic activity of myositis, and a concurrent screen that did not identify a focal hypermetabolic malignancy to explain the syndrome.

The patient’s swallowing and respiratory assessments provided both mechanistic explanation for his chest imaging and further support for an inflammatory myopathy affecting bulbar and respiratory muscles. Videofluoroscopic swallow showed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform residue and penetration to the level of the cords, explaining the frequent aspiration events; chest CT findings of peribronchial consolidation were interpreted as aspiration pneumonitis rather than classical interstitial lung disease. Pulmonary function testing showed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern consistent with impaired inspiratory/expiratory muscle function rather than primary parenchymal restriction. Thus the respiratory manifestations were best explained by neuromuscular weakness from myositis with secondary aspiration, not by heart failure, venous congestion or primary pulmonary fibrosis.

Serology narrowed the diagnosis further. A high-titre speckled ANA was non-specific but supportive of autoimmune disease; a targeted myositis antibody panel returned strongly positive anti-SAE1 (signal 173, normal <35) while other myositis-specific antibodies (Mi-2, TIF1-γ, Jo-1, OJ) were negative. Anti-SAE1 is recognized as an autoantibody associated with a dermatomyositis phenotype that frequently includes cutaneous manifestations and has associations with dysphagia and interstitial lung disease in published series. In this clinical context the isolated, high-level anti-SAE1 result increases the likelihood that the inflammatory myopathy is dermatomyositis rather than another serologic subtype.

Definitive tissue evidence was obtained by muscle biopsy. The biopsy showed perifascicular necrosis and regeneration, perimysial inflammation with dispersed histiocytes, and immunohistochemical deposition of complement membrane attack complex (C5b-9) in perifascicular endomysial capillaries. This triad—perifascicular fibre injury, perimysial inflammatory change, and capillary C5b-9 deposition—is characteristic of dermatomyositis and points to a complement-mediated microangiopathy targeting endomysial capillaries as the pathogenic mechanism. The histopathology therefore confirmed what the clinical, serologic and imaging data suggested.

Putting the pieces together also allows clear exclusion of the major competing neuromuscular diagnoses. Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) can produce very high CK but is typically associated with anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP antibodies and histology dominated by widespread myofibre necrosis with minimal perimysial/perifascicular complement capillary deposition—features not seen here. Inclusion body myositis is a clinicopathological diagnosis that usually affects older patients, has characteristic finger‑flexor and distal weakness, and shows rimmed vacuoles and protein aggregates on biopsy; it does not present with this pattern of diffuse CK elevation, bulbar dysphagia with acute neck soft‑tissue oedema, and C5b-9 capillary deposition. Myasthenia gravis can cause severe bulbar weakness and secretion burden but typically has normal CK and is a disorder of the neuromuscular junction rather than a myositis with inflammatory necrosis. Infective explanations for neck swelling and systemic inflammation (cellulitis/abscess, sialadenitis) were not supported by febrile response, leukocytosis, imaging evidence of collection, or positive cultures. Drug hypersensitivity syndromes and urticarial vasculitis were unlikely given the absence of a relevant medication exposure, eosinophilia or hypocomplementaemia, and the presence of objective myofibre necrosis and myositis‑specific serology.

In summary, the diagnostic shift in this case was driven by a very high CK and compatible transaminase/LHD elevation suggesting muscle injury, exclusion of bradykinin- or histamine-mediated angioedema by history and complement testing, corroboration of active systemic myositis on MRI and FDG–PET (including head/neck muscles corresponding to the site of swelling), objective bulbar dysfunction on videofluoroscopy that explained aspiration changes on chest CT, a highly specific anti‑SAE1 autoantibody, and confirmatory muscle biopsy showing perifascicular necrosis with capillary C5b‑9 deposition. Together these findings make anti‑SAE1–positive dermatomyositis the best unifying diagnosis for the patient’s neck swelling, dysphagia and profound myositis, and they explain why conventional angioedema or infectious etiologies were ultimately rejected. The case illustrates the practical value of early CK measurement, focused serology and muscle biopsy when neck or facial oedema is unexplained and accompanied by even subtle neuromuscular or cutaneous signs.

## Dermatomyositis Pathobiology with Emphasis on SAE1-Positive Phenotype and Pseudoangioedema

Dermatomyositis is fundamentally a microvascular and interfacing immune disease rather than a primary disorder of skeletal muscle fibres alone. Two linked pathogenic themes explain both the classic muscle findings and why, in rare cases such as this patient, dermatomyositis can present with what looks like angioedema of the neck: a complement‑mediated microangiopathy that causes perifascicular ischaemic muscle injury and a prominent type I interferon–driven inflammatory milieu that increases vascular permeability in skin and muscle.

The microangiopathy of dermatomyositis is characterized histologically by deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b–9) on endomysial and perifascicular capillaries. Deposition of C5b–9 injures endothelial cells, produces capillary drop‑out and regional hypoperfusion, and therefore preferentially damages fibres at the fascicle periphery (perifascicular atrophy). On muscle biopsy this process is often evident as perifascicular necrosis/atrophy with perimysial or perivascular inflammation, and immunostaining for C5b–9 or the interferon‑induced protein MxA supports the diagnosis. In the reported case the biopsy demonstration of C5b–9 in perifascicular capillaries is the canonical tissue correlate of the disease process and explains the severe, geographically focal muscle injury that underlay the neck swelling.

A type I interferon signature (production of interferon‑α/β by plasmacytoid dendritic cells and downstream upregulation of interferon‑stimulated genes such as MxA) is a second hallmark of dermatomyositis. Type I interferons amplify endothelial activation, upregulate adhesion molecules, and promote local production of cytokines that increase vascular permeability. Together with complement‑mediated capillary damage, this creates a local inflammatory state prone to plasma extravasation and tissue oedema. Importantly, this is a local microvascular phenomenon; systemic complement levels (serum C3/C4) and C1‑inhibitor function are often normal because the complement activation is focused at the tissue level rather than consumed systemically—explaining why routine complement tests can be unremarkable despite marked local MAC deposition.

The soft‑tissue swelling seen in dermatomyositis (pseudoangioedema) therefore arises from inflammatory vascular leak and impaired interstitial/lymphatic drainage in and around inflamed muscles, rather than from mast‑cell histamine release or bradykinin excess. Clinically this distinction matters: histaminergic angioedema is typically abrupt, pruritic and associated with urticaria and responds to antihistamines/steroids/epinephrine; bradykinin‑mediated angioedema is non‑pruritic, often recurrent and associated with low C4 or C1‑inhibitor abnormalities and is not steroid‑responsive. Pseudoangioedema from dermatomyositis tends to be subacute, firm or indurated rather than fluctuant, associated with muscle weakness and cutaneous features (heliotrope rash, Gottron’s changes, periungual capillary change), and does not behave like classic histaminergic or bradykinin angioedema. In the present case the neck swelling was non‑tender, persistent over weeks, accompanied by nailfold changes and marked CK elevation, and the patient had normal C1‑esterase and complement testing—findings that point away from primary angioedema and toward myositis‑related inflammatory leak.

When the disease involves strap muscles, pharyngeal muscles or other head–neck musculature the clinical consequences are twofold. First, direct inflammation and weakness of the suprahyoid/infrahyoid and pharyngeal constrictor muscles impair hyolaryngeal elevation and upper oesophageal sphincter opening, producing oropharyngeal dysphagia with residue in the pyriform fossae and an elevated risk of penetration and aspiration—exactly the physiology demonstrated on the patient’s videofluoroscopic study. Second, inflammation and oedema of neck musculature can produce externally visible swelling that mimics infectious deep‑neck processes or angioedema. Separately, involvement of respiratory muscles (diaphragm, intercostals) or coexistent interstitial lung disease causes restrictive physiology (reduced vital capacity/TLC, reduced maximal inspiratory pressures) and may show upper‑airway/“extrathoracic” flow abnormalities on spirometry; aspiration from dysphagia can in turn produce peribronchial consolidation on imaging, as in this patient.

Anti‑SAE1 antibodies target the SUMO‑activating enzyme (SAE1), the E1 enzyme that initiates protein sumoylation, a post‑translational modification that regulates nuclear trafficking and transcriptional responses. How anti‑SAE1 autoimmunity produces the clinical phenotype is incompletely defined, but cohorts and case series have identified a recognizable SAE1‑positive phenotype: prominent cutaneous disease, a relatively high frequency of severe dysphagia and frequent pulmonary involvement. In the literature review cited in this report SAE1‑positive disease had dysphagia and interstitial lung disease reported in roughly half of patients and an approximately 18% association with malignancy—figures that justify heightened surveillance for swallowing dysfunction, lung disease and occult cancer in SAE1‑positive patients. Muscle enzyme levels and the degree of muscle involvement can be variable in different autoantibody subtypes; this patient’s very high CK reflects florid muscle necrosis in his presentation.

Epidemiologically, pseudoangioedema is an uncommon manifestation of dermatomyositis. Large cohorts have found only a small minority of patients manifesting frank subcutaneous oedema, and neck‑predominant swelling is even less common. That rarity explains why such presentations are often first routed to ENT or infectious disease services and mistaken for Ludwig’s angina, cellulitis or primary angioedema. The practical implication is that clinicians faced with unexplained, subacute cervical swelling—particularly when accompanied by subtle skin changes or muscle weakness—should consider myositis in the differential, check serum muscle enzymes, look for periungual capillary abnormalities, and obtain targeted imaging and myositis serology; a confirmatory muscle biopsy demonstrating perifascicular atrophy and C5b–9 deposition establishes the diagnosis.

Understanding these mechanisms clarifies the therapeutic logic in the case: the swelling and dysphagia were caused by immune‑mediated muscle inflammation and microvascular leak, not by histamine or bradykinin, and therefore improved only after immunomodulation rather than with angioedema‑directed measures. It also explains why local complement deposition can coexist with normal serum complement tests and why SAE1 positivity mandates vigilance for significant dysphagia, pulmonary involvement and cancer screening.

## Management of Severe Dermatomyositis with Airway and Swallow Involvement

Management in this case had three parallel goals: protect the airway and prevent aspiration, rapidly suppress the immune‑mediated muscle inflammation that was causing bulbar dysfunction and neck oedema, and introduce steroid‑sparing therapy while minimizing infectious and treatment‑related complications. The patient’s marked neck swelling, elevated CK, imaging and biopsy findings indicated active, diffuse myositis involving the head and neck musculature; because the oedema reflected inflammation and capillary leak rather than histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema, treatment had to be aimed at quickly stopping immune injury while providing supportive care for swallowing and airway protection.

Acute stabilization and supportive care should be the immediate priority. Early ENT and anaesthetic involvement is essential for direct airway assessment (for example, flexible nasendoscopy as was performed) and for low‑threshold planning for elective intubation or tracheostomy if there are signs of progressive obstruction. Clinically worrying features include stridor, marked pooling of secretions, progressive dysphonia, tachypnoea, hypoxia or inability to protect the airway. Because bulbar weakness markedly increases aspiration risk, patients should be kept nil per os until a formal swallow assessment (videofluoroscopic swallow study or bedside assessment by a speech‑language pathologist) can be performed. Early speech‑language pathology input guides safe diet modification and rehabilitation, and dietetic input secures caloric needs. Short‑term enteral nutrition is usually delivered by nasogastric tube (appropriate for weeks to a few months); percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) is considered if long‑term support (>4–6 weeks) is anticipated. In this patient, severe pharyngeal dysphagia with aspiration was managed with NG feeding, continued ENT surveillance and chest physiotherapy while immunotherapy began.

Glucocorticoid induction is the mainstay for rapid control of severe active myositis. When bulbar or respiratory muscles are threatened, many centres give intravenous methylprednisolone pulses (commonly 500–1000 mg daily for 2–3 days) to achieve quick suppression of inflammation, followed by high‑dose oral prednisone (~1 mg/kg/day up to ~60 mg). The oral dose is then tapered according to clinical response; a common practical approach is slow reduction (eg, 5 mg every 2–3 weeks) to avoid relapse while steroid‑sparing agents take effect. Response is monitored with serial CK and strength testing (MMT‑8 or bedside proximal power), inflammatory markers and repeat swallow assessments. In the reported case the team used methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily for 3 days, then prednisone 60 mg daily with staged tapering; CK and proximal strength improved within weeks.

Steroid‑sparing immunomodulators should be started early in moderate–severe disease to allow steroid reduction and provide sustained control. Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) is commonly chosen for dermatomyositis because of its efficacy against lymphocyte proliferation and generally favourable tolerability; typical dosing is 1–1.5 g twice daily. Baseline and periodic monitoring include CBC for cytopenias, liver and renal function tests, and pregnancy testing/contraception counseling. Rituximab (anti‑CD20) is used for severe, refractory or life‑threatening manifestations (notably severe dysphagia, bulbar weakness, or when rapid steroid minimization is required). Standard rituximab regimens include 1000 mg IV on days 0 and 14 (the regimen used in this patient) or alternative oncology/autoimmune schedules. Before rituximab, screen for hepatitis B (HBsAg, anti‑HBc), HIV and measure baseline immunoglobulins, because of reactivation and hypogammaglobulinaemia risks; monitor IgG levels during follow‑up.

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is particularly valuable in dysphagia‑predominant or refractory dermatomyositis. IVIG has immunomodulatory effects that can improve bulbar and proximal muscle function relatively rapidly and is supported by clinical trial and observational data in this setting. Typical dosing is an initial 2 g/kg total (divided over 2–5 days) followed by maintenance cycles of 1–2 g/kg every 4–6 weeks as needed. In this case the patient received a 2 g/kg loading dose split over 5 days (total ≈165 g for his weight) followed by monthly cycles of 2 g/kg for further control. Monitor renal function, urine output and haemolysis markers during IVIG administration, and be alert for infusion reactions, aseptic meningitis and thromboembolic complications; choose formulations appropriate for renal function.

Some commonly used agents may be deferred depending on comorbidity. Methotrexate, a frequent first‑line steroid‑sparing drug in dermatomyositis, was avoided here because of the patient’s abnormal lung function and radiographic pulmonary changes—methotrexate can cause or exacerbate interstitial lung disease. Azathioprine and tacrolimus are reasonable alternatives when MMF is ineffective or not tolerated; azathioprine requires TPMT assessment and careful CBC monitoring, while tacrolimus requires drug‑level and renal monitoring. The choice of agent should balance disease severity, lung involvement, infection risk and patient preference.

Infection prevention is integral when combining high‑dose steroids, MMF, IVIG and rituximab. Baseline screening performed in this case (HBsAg, anti‑HBc, HCV, HIV, VZV IgG, CMV serology and TB testing) is standard. Hepatitis B core antibody positivity or HBsAg positivity requires antiviral prophylaxis around B‑cell depleting therapy to prevent reactivation. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis is recommended for many patients receiving prolonged high‑dose steroids plus other immunosuppressants; trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole (TMP‑SMX) is first‑line (eg, one double‑strength tablet three times weekly or one single‑strength daily). Alternatives for intolerance include dapsone (after G6PD testing), atovaquone, or monthly aerosolized pentamidine 300 mg; in this case TMP‑SMX was stopped because of haemolytic anaemia and nebulized pentamidine was used. Vaccinate with inactivated vaccines (influenza, pneumococcal, COVID‑19) before planned immunosuppression if possible; live vaccines are contraindicated once the patient is immunosuppressed.

Assessing response and safety requires a combination of laboratory, functional and imaging surveillance. CK, AST/ALT and LDH are useful biochemical monitors that typically fall with effective therapy; in the case presented CK normalized within weeks. Objective strength testing and timed functional measures should be recorded serially. Swallow function must be reassessed with repeat videofluoroscopy when clinically appropriate to guide diet advancement and decisions about ongoing enteral access; recovery of bulbar function often lags behind improvement in CK and proximal strength and may take weeks to months. Pulmonary monitoring (PFTs, repeat chest imaging) is indicated for lung involvement or aspiration risk. Drug safety monitoring includes CBC and LFTs for MMF (initially every 1–2 weeks until stable, then monthly), renal function and urine output with IVIG, and immunoglobulin levels and HBV DNA surveillance with rituximab. Watch for steroid complications (hyperglycaemia, hypertension, osteoporosis—provide bone protection when prolonged steroids are expected) and for infusion‑related or rare but serious complications of biologics (eg, infusion reactions, hypogammaglobulinaemia, reactivation infections).

In practice, expect a staged recovery: biochemical and proximal strength improvements often appear within days to weeks, while bulbar recovery and full resolution of dysphagia can take months and require continued nutritional support and rehabilitation. The reported patient demonstrated this pattern—rapid CK fall and proximal strength recovery within weeks, NG feeding for about two months, and full dysphagia resolution over several months while maintained on MMF and monthly IVIG. Successful outcomes in such cases depend on early recognition, prompt aggressive immunomodulation tailored to comorbidities (eg, avoiding methotrexate with abnormal lung function), and coordinated multidisciplinary care (ENT/anesthesia, speech‑language pathology, dietetics, rheumatology/immunology and infectious disease) to balance disease control with prevention and management of treatment‑related risks.

## Outcomes, Follow-Up Strategy, and Clinical Pearls

Clinical course and recovery trajectory in this case were favorable but staged. After an initial course of high‑dose intravenous methylprednisolone, IVIG, mycophenolate and two doses of rituximab, the patient showed rapid biochemical and clinical improvement: serum creatine kinase fell from >16 000 U/L to 37 U/L within weeks, and proximal hip‑flexor strength recovered to baseline by three weeks after hospital discharge. Cervical soft‑tissue swelling improved markedly early but required months to resolve completely (resolving by about three months). Dysphagia was the slowest to recover: the patient required nasogastric feeding for approximately two months after discharge and attained full resolution of swallowing dysfunction by seven months, at which time prednisone had also been successfully discontinued. Pulmonary abnormalities attributed to aspiration pneumonitis regressed on follow‑up imaging over the first year and pulmonary function tests remained stable.

The long‑term management plan focused on consolidating remission while minimizing cumulative steroid exposure and monitoring for relapse or complications. At discharge the patient continued mycophenolate mofetil and monthly IVIG, with a plan articulated in clinic to taper mycophenolate over the following six months depending on clinical stability; prednisone was tapered off by seven months. In practice, tapering of steroid‑sparing agents is individualized: clinicians in this case used a combination of sustained clinical improvement (strength, swallowing), persistent CK normalization, and stable imaging to justify stepwise reduction. Rehabilitation goals included progressive physiotherapy to restore and maintain proximal strength and safe re‑introduction of oral intake under speech‑language pathology supervision; nutritional support and aspiration precautions were maintained until objective swallow function permitted transition to oral diet.

Defining remission and early detection of relapse require multimodal assessment because no single test is definitive. In this patient, remission was documented by concordant features: sustained normalization of CK, objective recovery of proximal strength and swallowing function, and resolution of inflammatory changes on imaging (MRI/PET and CT chest). It is important to recognize limitations: CK is a sensitive marker of active muscle necrosis in many patients but may be normal in amyopathic disease or when cutaneous disease predominates; conversely CK can lag behind clinical change or be affected by other causes (exercise, statins). Therefore clinical examination (muscle testing, swallow assessment), repeat muscle MRI or FDG‑PET if available, pulmonary function testing for lung involvement, and targeted imaging for previously noted abnormalities should all inform decisions about tapering therapy or escalating treatment if relapse is suspected. Rising CK in conjunction with recurrent weakness, new neck swelling, worsening dysphagia, new cutaneous changes, or fresh pulmonary symptoms should prompt urgent reassessment and consideration of intensifying immunosuppression.

Given the recognized association between dermatomyositis and malignancy and the patient’s IMACS‑based risk profile (two high‑risk features: dermatomyositis and dysphagia; two intermediate features: SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex), a pragmatic cancer surveillance strategy was adopted. This case illustrates a practical bundle used in high‑risk patients: baseline whole‑body evaluation with CT chest/abdomen/pelvis and FDG‑PET to seek occult malignancy, measurement of common tumour markers, and planning of directed investigations such as gastroscopy and colonoscopy when age‑ or symptom‑appropriate. Ongoing surveillance in the absence of identified malignancy relies on regular clinical review (looking for constitutional symptoms or new focal signs), interval imaging guided by symptoms or new findings (and repeat PET/CT selectively if concern arises), and adherence to age‑appropriate screening. Because cancer risk is highest in the early period after myositis diagnosis, surveillance is most intensive in the first 2–3 years and then may be spaced out if no malignancy is found; exact frequency should be individualized and follow IMACS and local protocols.

Distinguishing pseudoangioedema from true histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema is critical when encountering unexplained facial or neck swelling. Key clinical clues that should prompt consideration of dermatomyositis rather than classic angioedema are a subacute progressive course (days to weeks rather than minutes to hours), absence of urticaria or marked pruritus, poor or no response to antihistamines/steroids/epinephrine, associated proximal muscle weakness or dysphagia, and subtle cutaneous signs such as periungual erythema or a shawl/holster‑type rash. Laboratory and bedside tests that help differentiate causes include normal C4 and normal C1‑esterase function (making bradykinin‑driven hereditary or acquired angioedema unlikely), raised CK and muscle‑derived transaminases (AST/ALT) suggesting muscle injury, and nailfold capillaroscopy showing microhaemorrhages or capillary drop‑out. In any “idiopathic” neck or facial swelling with atypical features, obtaining a CK and performing a focused skin and nailfold exam are inexpensive, rapid steps that can redirect the diagnostic pathway to inflammatory myopathy.

Several common pitfalls contributed to diagnostic delay in reported cases and are worth highlighting to learners. First, the absence of classic dermatomyositis stigmata (Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash) does not exclude the disease; cutaneous features may be subtle or atypical. Second, anchoring on ENT or infectious diagnoses for neck swelling — particularly when imaging shows diffuse soft‑tissue oedema without an identifiable abscess and the patient is afebrile with modest inflammatory markers — can lead to unnecessary procedures or inappropriate antibiotic exposure. Third, elevated transaminases are often interpreted as hepatic in origin when they may reflect muscle breakdown; failure to check CK and myositis antibodies in that context is a missed opportunity. To avoid these errors, maintain a broad differential for unexplained neck swelling, perform a quick neuromuscular and skin examination (including nailfolds), and check CK and, if available, myositis serology early when angioedema is not convincingly supported.

This case also underscores the value of coordinated multidisciplinary care. Emergency physicians and ENT specialists are essential for initial assessment and for excluding life‑threatening structural causes of airway compromise; immunology/rheumatology provide disease‑specific diagnosis, serologic testing and long‑term immunosuppression management; neurology and pathology facilitate EMG and muscle biopsy interpretation when needed; radiology interprets neck and chest CT, muscle MRI and PET to define disease extent and screen for malignancy; pulmonology assesses and follows lung involvement and aspiration risk; speech‑language pathology guides swallow assessment, safe feeding strategies and rehabilitation; and gastroenterology/oncology arrange age‑appropriate and symptom‑directed cancer screening. Timely cross‑specialty communication in this patient expedited the pivot from an ENT/infectious pathway to immunosuppressive treatment and rehabilitation, likely preventing further airway or nutritional complications.

In summary, the main clinical lessons are straightforward and actionable: in unexplained or “idiopathic” facial or neck swelling, especially when progressive, non‑pruritic, associated with weakness, dysphagia, or with abnormal transaminases, consider dermatomyositis and obtain a CK and focused skin/nailfold examination early; use a combined assessment of clinical function, CK, imaging and, when indicated, biopsy to judge remission and guide tapering of therapy; apply IMACS‑informed malignancy surveillance tailored to the patient’s risk profile; and involve a multidisciplinary team promptly to optimize outcomes.
