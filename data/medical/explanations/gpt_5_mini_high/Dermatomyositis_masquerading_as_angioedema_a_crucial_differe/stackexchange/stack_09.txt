Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Pathophysiology] How do pulmonary function testing and thoracic imaging connect oropharyngeal muscle involvement in dermatomyositis to aspiration risk, and which PFT pattern supports extrathoracic restriction?

Direct answer: Pulmonary function testing (PFTs) and thoracic imaging tie together oropharyngeal (bulbar) muscle involvement in dermatomyositis and aspiration risk by providing complementary functional and anatomic evidence — PFTs can demonstrate upper‑airway/respiratory muscle dysfunction (an “extrathoracic restrictive” pattern and reduced respiratory pressures) while chest CT shows the downstream consequence of impaired airway protection (peribronchial consolidation and dependent, mid‑to‑lower‑zone air‑space change consistent with aspiration pneumonitis). In this case the combination of an extrathoracic restrictive PFT pattern with no bronchodilator response, CT findings of peribronchial consolidation, and a video‑fluoroscopic study showing severe pharyngeal dysphagia links active oropharyngeal myositis to recurrent microaspiration.

Why this happens (pathophysiology): Dermatomyositis can inflame striated muscles of the oropharynx and larynx → impaired bolus propulsion, reduced laryngeal elevation, poor airway closure and a weak cough. Food or secretions then pool (eg, pyriform fossa residue) and can penetrate past the vocal cords, producing aspiration. Repeated microaspiration causes peribronchial and dependent air‑space consolidation and ground‑glass change on CT, the exact pattern seen in this patient.

Which PFT pattern supports extrathoracic (bulbar/upper‑airway) involvement: classically a restrictive physiology (reduced FVC and reduced TLC with a preserved or increased FEV1/FVC ratio) together with objective signs of respiratory/upper‑airway muscle weakness — low maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures (MIP/MEP) — and flow‑volume loop abnormalities consistent with extrathoracic limitation (flattening of the inspiratory limb for variable extrathoracic obstruction). The absence of bronchodilator response helps exclude obstructive airway disease, and a relatively preserved diffusion capacity (when measured) argues against primary parenchymal ILD as the cause of the low volumes. In the reported patient the PFTs were described as an extrathoracic restrictive pattern with no bronchodilator response, supporting bulbar/upper‑airway muscle dysfunction rather than primary obstructive lung disease.

Clinical pearl: in inflammatory myopathy patients with neck swelling, cough, secretions or any swallowing complaints, pair PFTs (including MIP/MEP and a careful flow‑volume loop) with early swallow evaluation and chest imaging — an “extrathoracic restrictive” PFT pattern plus dependent peribronchial consolidation should raise high suspicion for aspiration from bulbar myositis and prompt airway protection and urgent treatment of the myositis.
