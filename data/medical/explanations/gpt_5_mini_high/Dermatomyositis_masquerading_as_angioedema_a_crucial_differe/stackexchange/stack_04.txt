Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the clinical phenotype typically associated with anti-SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis, and how should this serology influence counseling about dysphagia, interstitial lung disease, and malignancy risk?

Short answer
Anti‑SAE1–positive dermatomyositis is classically a cutaneous‑predominant form of dermatomyositis that frequently involves the pharynx and lungs. In published series (the case report cites a review of 215 patients) roughly 46% had dysphagia, ~43% had interstitial lung disease (ILD), and ~18% had an associated malignancy. Clinically this means you should (1) counsel patients that dysphagia and aspiration are common and may be severe, (2) screen proactively for ILD even when respiratory symptoms are minimal, and (3) regard SAE1 as an intermediate cancer‑risk antibody that, when combined with dermatomyositis and dysphagia (both high‑risk features), justifies comprehensive baseline cancer screening and close surveillance.

Why and how this changes practice (clinical reasoning anchored to the case)
- Phenotype: SAE1 antibodies target the SUMO‑activating enzyme and are associated with striking cutaneous findings (heliotrope, Gottron’s pattern, shawl/V‑neck signs) and sometimes subcutaneous facial/neck oedema that can mimic angioedema. Muscle involvement is variable but can be frank and severe, as in this patient who had very high CK, typical myositis on biopsy and profound pharyngeal weakness. Recognizing skin or nailfold changes and checking CK saved this patient from a delayed diagnosis.

- Dysphagia: Nearly half of reported SAE1 patients have pharyngeal involvement. Pharyngeal weakness leads to residue, penetration/aspiration and aspiration pneumonitis (seen on this patient’s CT and video‑swallow). Action: perform an early bedside swallowing screen and, if any concern, formal instrumental testing (videofluoroscopic swallow or FEES), involve speech‑language pathology, consider ENT evaluation, and be prepared for temporary enteral feeding (NG or PEG) to prevent aspiration while immunosuppression controls the myositis. Treat severe bulbar disease promptly and aggressively (high‑dose steroids, IVIG and steroid‑sparing agents were used successfully in this case).

- ILD: SAE1 carries a substantial rate of lung involvement. Baseline pulmonary function tests (including DLCO) and high‑resolution chest CT are indicated even for minimally symptomatic patients; if ILD is found, pulmonology co‑management is prudent. Importantly, the presence or risk of ILD should influence choice of steroid‑sparing therapy (many centers avoid methotrexate when ILD is present or suspected and favour agents such as mycophenolate, tacrolimus, rituximab or cyclophosphamide)—the treating team in this case avoided methotrexate and used mycophenolate plus IVIG and rituximab.

- Malignancy: SAE1 is an intermediate myositis‑specific autoantibody for cancer risk. The IMACS cancer‑screening framework (used in this report) treats dermatomyositis and dysphagia as high‑risk features; when combined with intermediate factors (SAE1, male sex in this patient) the overall risk profile becomes high enough to justify comprehensive screening. Practical measures include cross‑sectional imaging (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or PET‑CT), age‑ and sex‑appropriate cancer screening (mammography, colonoscopy, pelvic/prostate evaluation), targeted investigations guided by symptoms, and interval surveillance during the first several years after diagnosis. In this case CT, PET, tumour markers and planned endoscopy/colonoscopy were performed and have been reassuring to date.

Bottom line (action checklist for clinicians)
- In any patient with unexplained facial/neck oedema or persistent “angioedema,” examine for cutaneous DM signs and check CK.
- For SAE1‑positive patients: obtain early swallowing evaluation and protect airway/nutrition if abnormal; get baseline PFTs/DLCO and HRCT chest; perform comprehensive baseline malignancy screening per IMACS and individual risk factors; tailor immunosuppression to pulmonary status. Reassure patients that dysphagia and ILD are common but often improve with timely, appropriate therapy—as illustrated by this patient’s recovery after aggressive treatment.
