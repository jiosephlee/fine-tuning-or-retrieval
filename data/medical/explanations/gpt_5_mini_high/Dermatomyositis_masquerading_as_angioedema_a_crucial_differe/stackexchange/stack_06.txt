Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Management] In dermatomyositis with severe dysphagia and significant airway muscle involvement, outline an initial immunomodulatory regimen (agents and typical dosing) to induce remission, and justify avoiding methotrexate when lung involvement is suspected.

Short answer
- Rapid induction for dermatomyositis with severe dysphagia/airway‑muscle involvement aims to (1) rapidly suppress inflammation to reverse bulbar weakness and (2) protect the airway/avoid aspiration. A pragmatic, commonly used induction regimen — and the regimen used in this case — is:
  - High‑dose IV corticosteroid pulse: methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily × 3 days, then switch to oral prednisone ≈1 mg/kg/day (∼60 mg) with gradual taper (eg, decrease by 5 mg every 2–3 weeks as tolerated).
  - IV immunoglobulin (IVIG): 2 g/kg total as a loading dose (commonly given over 2–5 days; in this patient 2 g/kg over 5 days), followed by repeat 2 g/kg cycles every 4 weeks for several months as clinically indicated.
  - Early steroid‑sparing immunosuppression: mycophenolate mofetil 1 g orally twice daily (up‑titrate to 1.5 g BID if needed and tolerated).
  - Add a biologic for severe, antibody‑driven or refractory disease: rituximab 1,000 mg IV × 2 doses, 2 weeks apart (alternative regimen: 375 mg/m2 weekly × 4).
  - PJP prophylaxis while on combination immunosuppression (TMP‑SMX first line; if intolerant, inhaled pentamidine 300 mg monthly as used in this patient).
  - Early supportive measures: swallow assessment (video fluoroscopic swallow), enteral feeding (NG/PEG) until safe oral intake, airway monitoring and ENT/speech pathology involvement.

Why these choices (brief clinical reasoning)
- Bulbar/airway muscle involvement is functionally life‑threatening because inflammation and complement‑mediated microvascular injury produce rapid weakness of pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles. High‑dose IV corticosteroids give the fastest systemic anti‑inflammatory effect and are the immediate cornerstone for induction (the case used methylprednisolone 1 g daily × 3 days then prednisone 60 mg daily).
- IVIG is particularly useful for severe dysphagia and refractory or rapidly progressive disease because it can modulate pathogenic autoantibodies and immune effector mechanisms; this patient received an induction 2 g/kg loading dose (given over 5 days) followed by monthly cycles and had marked clinical improvement of neck swelling and later recovery of swallowing.
- Mycophenolate is an effective, generally well‑tolerated steroid‑sparing agent used in dermatomyositis and in myositis‑associated ILD; it was chosen in this case (1 g twice daily) because of tolerability and activity against both muscle and lung disease.
- Rituximab targets B cells and is reasonable early in severe, antibody‑positive disease (this patient was SAE1‑positive) or when rapid steroid‑sparing is needed; the case used 1,000 mg × 2 doses two weeks apart and attained remission.

Why methotrexate was avoided in this patient
- Methotrexate is frequently used as a steroid‑sparing agent in dermatomyositis but carries a risk of pulmonary toxicity (acute hypersensitivity pneumonitis and potential progression to fibrotic lung disease). In a patient with abnormal pulmonary studies at baseline (this patient had an extrathoracic restrictive pattern on PFTs and CT chest showing nodules and patchy peribronchial consolidation), starting methotrexate risks provoking or worsening lung injury and can complicate the distinction between drug toxicity and myositis‑related ILD.
- Given the substantial baseline lung abnormalities and the known association of SAE1 disease with ILD, selecting an alternative with more favorable pulmonary safety (eg, mycophenolate, IVIG, rituximab, and—if needed—cyclophosphamide for rapidly progressive ILD) is a safer strategy and is exactly the approach taken in this case.

Practical monitoring and safety points
- Baseline infection screening and immunization review before biologics (HBV, HCV, HIV, TB testing as done in the case).
- Serial clinical surveillance: CK, muscle strength, swallow function, PFTs and chest imaging for ILD, and regular assessment for infections and cytopenias.
- PJP prophylaxis during combined immunosuppression (the patient started TMP‑SMX but required switch to nebulized pentamidine 300 mg monthly for hemolytic anemia).
- Early malignancy screening per IMACS risk stratification because dermatomyositis carries an elevated cancer risk.

Bottom line
- When bulbar/airway muscles are severely involved, treat promptly and aggressively with high‑dose IV steroids plus IVIG and an early steroid‑sparing agent (mycophenolate in this patient) and consider rituximab for antibody‑positive or refractory disease. Avoid methotrexate when baseline lung disease or abnormal pulmonary testing is present because of the risk of methotrexate‑related pneumonitis and fibrosis; this rationale guided the safe, effective regimen used in the presented case.
