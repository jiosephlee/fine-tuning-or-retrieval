Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Name three bedside features that argue against histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema and should raise suspicion for dermatomyositis-related pseudoangioedema in a patient with prominent cervical swelling.

Clinical pearl — when “angioedema” involves the neck, three bedside findings that argue against histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema and should prompt consideration of dermatomyositis-related pseudoangioedema are:

1) Dermatomyositis-specific cutaneous signs. Look for periungual changes/nailfold capillary abnormalities (periungual erythema, micro‑haemorrhages), violaceous facial or truncal erythema, shawl/V‑neck/Holster patterns or Gottron-type rash. In this case the patient had proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages, periungual erythema, facial erythema and a positive Holster sign — findings that are not features of allergic or bradykinin angioedema.

2) Concomitant proximal and/or bulbar muscle dysfunction. True myositis produces proximal limb weakness and bulbar signs (dysphagia, pooling of secretions, cough on swallow) — signs that angioedema does not cause. The index patient had reduced hip‑flexor power (4/5), severe pharyngeal dysphagia and increased secretions/cough on nasendoscopy, pointing to muscle involvement rather than isolated vascular permeability.

3) Subacute, persistent, non‑pruritic, non‑tender cervical swelling without urticaria or an obvious trigger. Histaminergic angioedema is usually rapid, itchy and associated with urticaria and response to antihistamines/epinephrine; bradykinin‑mediated attacks have characteristic triggers/family history or medication exposure. This patient’s 3‑week, non‑tender, persistent neck swelling with no ACE‑inhibitor exposure and no urticaria argued against both mechanisms.

Takeaway: if you see any of the above with unexplained neck swelling, examine nailfolds, test a CK and consider inflammatory myopathy/myositis workup rather than treating empirically as isolated angioedema.
