Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Morning Report] When angioedema is suspected, which laboratory tests help diagnose bradykinin-mediated disease, and how would normal results alter your differential for isolated neck swelling?

Short answer
- First‑line laboratory tests to detect bradykinin‑mediated (C1‑inhibitor–related) angioedema are serum C4, C1‑esterase inhibitor (C1‑INH) antigenic level and C1‑INH functional assay. If an acquired form is suspected, add C1q. Specialized genetic testing (eg, F12, PLG and other genes) or research bradykinin assays are reserved for suspected hereditary angioedema with normal C1‑INH (HAE‑nL).

Why these tests
- Pathophysiology: C1‑INH normally restrains the classical complement and kallikrein–kinin pathways. In hereditary (types I and II) or acquired C1‑INH deficiency there is unopposed kallikrein activity → excess bradykinin → vascular permeability and angioedema. Ongoing classical pathway activation consumes C4, so C4 is usually low; C1‑INH antigen level is low in type I, and function is low (sometimes with normal antigen level) in type II. Low C1q supports an acquired (autoantibody/lymphoproliferative) process.

How to use the results in practice
- Algorithmic approach:
  - Start with C4. If C4 is low, measure C1‑INH antigen and function.
  - If C1q is low, look for an acquired cause (autoimmunity, monoclonal/lymphoproliferative disease).
  - If C4 and C1‑INH studies are normal but clinical suspicion remains, repeat tests during an acute attack and consider referral for genetic testing for HAE‑nl.
- Distinguish histaminergic angioedema: measure serum tryptase acutely if mast‑cell activation/anaphylaxis is suspected; histaminergic angioedema typically has urticaria, itching and responds to antihistamines/steroids/epinephrine, whereas bradykinin‑mediated does not.

How normal results change the differential (application to this case)
- Normal C4 and normal C1‑INH level/function (as in the presented patient) make hereditary or acquired C1‑INH deficiency unlikely and should prompt you to broaden the differential away from bradykinin‑mediated angioedema.
- In an isolated, subacute neck swelling with normal complement/C1‑INH, consider:
  - Non‑histaminergic, non‑bradykinin causes of soft‑tissue swelling — inflammatory myopathies (pseudo‑angioedema from muscle inflammation, as here with CK 16,184 U/L and positive SAE1 antibody), cellulitis/deep neck infection, salivary gland disease, venous/lymphatic obstruction, or neoplasm.
  - Histaminergic angioedema (check for urticaria, tryptase, response to antihistamines).
  - HAE with normal C1‑INH (rare): consider only if recurrent, family history, or typical triggers — this requires specialist genetic workup.
- In the case, the normal complement and C1‑INH studies together with the subacute course, periungual/nailfold changes and marked CK elevation correctly shifted the team’s thinking from angioedema toward dermatomyositis causing inflammatory neck swelling (“pseudo‑angioedema”), leading to appropriate immunosuppressive therapy.

Clinical pearl
- When you suspect bradykinin‑mediated angioedema, order C4 and C1‑INH antigen + function (and C1q if acquired disease is a concern). Normal results should make you question C1‑INH–mediated disease and actively search for alternative causes of neck swelling — including inflammatory myopathies such as dermatomyositis, which may mimic angioedema, as illustrated by this case.
