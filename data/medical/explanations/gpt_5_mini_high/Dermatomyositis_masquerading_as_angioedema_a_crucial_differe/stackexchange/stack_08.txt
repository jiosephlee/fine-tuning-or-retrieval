Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] Which subtle cutaneous and nailfold findings, even in the absence of a heliotrope rash or Gottron’s papules, should prompt ordering a myositis workup in a patient with unexplained neck swelling?

Short answer
Even without a heliotrope rash or Gottron’s papules, subtle periungual and nailfold abnormalities and minor cutaneous rashes should lower your threshold to test for myositis in unexplained neck swelling. Specifically look for periungual erythema/telangiectasias, nailfold capillary abnormalities on capillaroscopy (microhaemorrhages, giant loops or capillary dropout/avascular areas), and faint violaceous or distributional rashes (shawl or V‑neck patterns, or a Holster sign on the lateral thigh).

Why this matters (clinical reasoning and pathophysiology)
Dermatomyositis is a complement‑mediated microangiopathy of skin and muscle; nailfold capillary changes reflect the same small‑vessel injury that can precede or occur without classic heliotrope/Gottron lesions. Muscle inflammation and capillary leak produce subcutaneous “pseudo‑angioedema” (non‑histaminergic, non‑bradykinin swelling) that can masquerade as isolated neck/face oedema. In the reported case the absence of typical heliotrope/Gottron findings belied clear nailfold findings (microhaemorrhages and avascular areas) and periungual erythema plus a lateral‑thigh rash (Holster sign) — these clues prompted a CK, which was markedly elevated (16,184 U/L) and led to the diagnosis of SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis.

Practical point: what to do when you see these signs
- Perform a bedside exam of the nailfolds (or formal capillaroscopy) whenever neck/face swelling is unexplained.  
- If periungual erythema, telangiectasias, microhaemorrhages or avascular areas are present, order CK (± aldolase), ANA/myositis‑specific antibody panel (including SAE1), and arrange rheumatology/immunology input.  
- Screen for associated features (proximal weakness, dysphagia, pulmonary involvement) and pursue MRI/EMG and muscle biopsy as indicated.  
- Remember that normal complement and C1‑esterase studies in such a patient favor a myositis‑related pseudo‑angioedema rather than histaminergic or bradykinin angioedema.

Clinical pearl
Periungual erythema/telangiectasias and nailfold microhaemorrhages or capillary dropout are low‑cost, high‑yield signs — in unexplained neck swelling they should prompt a myositis workup (start with CK and myositis antibodies) even when heliotrope rash and Gottron’s papules are absent.
