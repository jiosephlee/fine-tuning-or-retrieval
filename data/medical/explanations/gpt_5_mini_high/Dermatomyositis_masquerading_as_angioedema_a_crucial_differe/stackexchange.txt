Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Morning Report] In an adult with three weeks of progressive, non-tender neck swelling, subtle periungual capillary changes, and new proximal hip-flexor weakness but no urticaria or exposure to ACE inhibitors, which single laboratory test would you order first to redirect the evaluation from angioedema toward an inflammatory myopathy, and what result would you expect if dermatomyositis is the cause?

Order a serum creatine kinase (CK) level first. 

Why: the combination of new proximal hip‑flexor weakness and periungual capillary changes points to an inflammatory myopathy rather than histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema. CK is the single most accessible and sensitive screening test for active muscle injury; dermatomyositis causes complement‑mediated microvascular injury and myofibre necrosis with release of intracellular enzymes (CK, aldolase, AST/ALT, LDH).

What result to expect if dermatomyositis is the cause: a marked CK elevation (commonly >1,000 U/L in active myositis). In the published case the CK was 16,184 U/L, which promptly redirected the team from an angioedema workup to myositis testing (anti‑SAE1 antibody, MRI, muscle biopsy) and led to timely immunosuppression. 

Caveat: CK can be normal in amyopathic dermatomyositis, so if clinical suspicion remains despite a normal CK, proceed to myositis‑specific antibodies and muscle imaging/biopsy.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Name three bedside features that argue against histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema and should raise suspicion for dermatomyositis-related pseudoangioedema in a patient with prominent cervical swelling.

Clinical pearl — when “angioedema” involves the neck, three bedside findings that argue against histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema and should prompt consideration of dermatomyositis-related pseudoangioedema are:

1) Dermatomyositis-specific cutaneous signs. Look for periungual changes/nailfold capillary abnormalities (periungual erythema, micro‑haemorrhages), violaceous facial or truncal erythema, shawl/V‑neck/Holster patterns or Gottron-type rash. In this case the patient had proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages, periungual erythema, facial erythema and a positive Holster sign — findings that are not features of allergic or bradykinin angioedema.

2) Concomitant proximal and/or bulbar muscle dysfunction. True myositis produces proximal limb weakness and bulbar signs (dysphagia, pooling of secretions, cough on swallow) — signs that angioedema does not cause. The index patient had reduced hip‑flexor power (4/5), severe pharyngeal dysphagia and increased secretions/cough on nasendoscopy, pointing to muscle involvement rather than isolated vascular permeability.

3) Subacute, persistent, non‑pruritic, non‑tender cervical swelling without urticaria or an obvious trigger. Histaminergic angioedema is usually rapid, itchy and associated with urticaria and response to antihistamines/epinephrine; bradykinin‑mediated attacks have characteristic triggers/family history or medication exposure. This patient’s 3‑week, non‑tender, persistent neck swelling with no ACE‑inhibitor exposure and no urticaria argued against both mechanisms.

Takeaway: if you see any of the above with unexplained neck swelling, examine nailfolds, test a CK and consider inflammatory myopathy/myositis workup rather than treating empirically as isolated angioedema.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathobiology of tissue edema in dermatomyositis: how does complement-mediated microangiopathy lead to subcutaneous and muscular swelling, and which muscle-biopsy immunohistochemical finding supports this mechanism?

Short answer
Dermatomyositis produces tissue oedema through a complement‑mediated microangiopathy. Antibody‑driven complement activation deposits the terminal complement complex (membrane attack complex, C5b‑9) on endomysial/perifascicular capillary endothelium → endothelial injury, increased capillary permeability and plasma extravasation into perimysial and subcutaneous tissues (producing soft‑tissue/muscle swelling), together with capillary loss and ischemic perifascicular myofibre necrosis. The supportive muscle‑biopsy immunohistochemical finding is deposition of C5b‑9 (MAC) in perifascicular endomysial capillaries — the exact stain reported in this patient.

Short explanation / clinical reasoning
- Mechanism: circulating autoantibodies (myositis‑associated or other autoantibodies) activate complement on the microvasculature. Formation of C5b‑9 on capillary endothelium causes endothelial activation/lysis, loss of barrier function and leakage of protein‑rich fluid into the interstitium (oedema). Progressive capillary dropout produces focal ischemia with perifascicular myofibre necrosis and regeneration.  
- Why it mimics angioedema: the swelling is driven by microvascular leak, not histamine or bradykinin, so it is non‑histaminergic/non‑bradykinin mediated and does not respond to standard angioedema therapy. Localized complement activation can occur without hypocomplementaemia in serum (this patient’s C3/C4 were normal).  
- Diagnostic support in this case: the thigh biopsy showed perifascicular necrotic/regenerative fibres with C5b‑9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries — the classic immunohistochemical signature of dermatomyositis microangiopathy that explains the patient’s neck/subcutaneous swelling and muscle involvement.  

Clinical implication: recognition that oedema reflects complement‑driven vascular injury directs treatment toward immunosuppression (steroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents) rather than antihistamines or C1‑esterase replacement.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the clinical phenotype typically associated with anti-SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis, and how should this serology influence counseling about dysphagia, interstitial lung disease, and malignancy risk?

Short answer
Anti‑SAE1–positive dermatomyositis is classically a cutaneous‑predominant form of dermatomyositis that frequently involves the pharynx and lungs. In published series (the case report cites a review of 215 patients) roughly 46% had dysphagia, ~43% had interstitial lung disease (ILD), and ~18% had an associated malignancy. Clinically this means you should (1) counsel patients that dysphagia and aspiration are common and may be severe, (2) screen proactively for ILD even when respiratory symptoms are minimal, and (3) regard SAE1 as an intermediate cancer‑risk antibody that, when combined with dermatomyositis and dysphagia (both high‑risk features), justifies comprehensive baseline cancer screening and close surveillance.

Why and how this changes practice (clinical reasoning anchored to the case)
- Phenotype: SAE1 antibodies target the SUMO‑activating enzyme and are associated with striking cutaneous findings (heliotrope, Gottron’s pattern, shawl/V‑neck signs) and sometimes subcutaneous facial/neck oedema that can mimic angioedema. Muscle involvement is variable but can be frank and severe, as in this patient who had very high CK, typical myositis on biopsy and profound pharyngeal weakness. Recognizing skin or nailfold changes and checking CK saved this patient from a delayed diagnosis.

- Dysphagia: Nearly half of reported SAE1 patients have pharyngeal involvement. Pharyngeal weakness leads to residue, penetration/aspiration and aspiration pneumonitis (seen on this patient’s CT and video‑swallow). Action: perform an early bedside swallowing screen and, if any concern, formal instrumental testing (videofluoroscopic swallow or FEES), involve speech‑language pathology, consider ENT evaluation, and be prepared for temporary enteral feeding (NG or PEG) to prevent aspiration while immunosuppression controls the myositis. Treat severe bulbar disease promptly and aggressively (high‑dose steroids, IVIG and steroid‑sparing agents were used successfully in this case).

- ILD: SAE1 carries a substantial rate of lung involvement. Baseline pulmonary function tests (including DLCO) and high‑resolution chest CT are indicated even for minimally symptomatic patients; if ILD is found, pulmonology co‑management is prudent. Importantly, the presence or risk of ILD should influence choice of steroid‑sparing therapy (many centers avoid methotrexate when ILD is present or suspected and favour agents such as mycophenolate, tacrolimus, rituximab or cyclophosphamide)—the treating team in this case avoided methotrexate and used mycophenolate plus IVIG and rituximab.

- Malignancy: SAE1 is an intermediate myositis‑specific autoantibody for cancer risk. The IMACS cancer‑screening framework (used in this report) treats dermatomyositis and dysphagia as high‑risk features; when combined with intermediate factors (SAE1, male sex in this patient) the overall risk profile becomes high enough to justify comprehensive screening. Practical measures include cross‑sectional imaging (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or PET‑CT), age‑ and sex‑appropriate cancer screening (mammography, colonoscopy, pelvic/prostate evaluation), targeted investigations guided by symptoms, and interval surveillance during the first several years after diagnosis. In this case CT, PET, tumour markers and planned endoscopy/colonoscopy were performed and have been reassuring to date.

Bottom line (action checklist for clinicians)
- In any patient with unexplained facial/neck oedema or persistent “angioedema,” examine for cutaneous DM signs and check CK.
- For SAE1‑positive patients: obtain early swallowing evaluation and protect airway/nutrition if abnormal; get baseline PFTs/DLCO and HRCT chest; perform comprehensive baseline malignancy screening per IMACS and individual risk factors; tailor immunosuppression to pulmonary status. Reassure patients that dysphagia and ILD are common but often improve with timely, appropriate therapy—as illustrated by this patient’s recovery after aggressive treatment.

### [Management] According to the IMACS cancer-screening framework for idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, which risk factors in an adult with dermatomyositis would categorize them as high risk for malignancy, and what initial screening studies would you obtain?

Short answer
IMACS stratifies cancer risk in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies by phenotype, clinical features and myositis‑specific antibodies. High‑risk features include the dermatomyositis phenotype itself and clinical signs that suggest an occult malignancy (notably dysphagia/oropharyngeal involvement, rapid systemic symptoms or unexplained weight loss) and the presence of cancer‑associated myositis antibodies (classically anti–TIF1‑γ and anti‑NXP2). Intermediate risk features include male sex and some antibodies such as anti‑SAE1 (as in this patient). Because our patient had two high‑risk features (dermatomyositis + dysphagia) plus two intermediate features (male sex, anti‑SAE1), IMACS classed him as high risk and recommended comprehensive cancer screening.

Which risk factors put an adult with dermatomyositis into the IMACS “high‑risk” group
- Dermatomyositis phenotype (vs polymyositis or other IIMs).  
- Clinical features suggesting possible occult cancer: dysphagia or prominent oropharyngeal involvement, constitutional symptoms (weight loss), rapidly progressive disease or severe cutaneous ulceration.  
- Presence of myositis antibodies that are strongly associated with cancer: anti–TIF1‑γ (p155/140) and anti‑NXP2.  
(Intermediate risk examples: male sex, anti‑SAE1 and some other antibodies; older age at onset increases risk and is considered in overall assessment.)

Initial screening studies recommended by IMACS (practical approach)
- Baseline whole‑body cross‑sectional imaging: FDG‑PET/CT is preferred where available because it performs well as a single test for occult malignancy. If PET/CT is not available, do contrast CT chest/abdomen/pelvis.  
- Age‑ and sex‑appropriate cancer screening in addition to imaging:  
  - Women: mammography ± breast exam; pelvic exam with transvaginal ultrasound and CA‑125 where ovarian cancer is a concern (especially with cancer‑associated antibodies).  
  - Men: consider PSA in appropriate age group.  
  - Colonoscopy according to age/symptoms (or earlier if high risk or symptoms).  
- Symptom‑directed endoscopy/imaging: gastroscopy or ENT/nasopharyngeal evaluation for dysphagia or upper‑GI/ENT symptoms.  
- Tumor markers (CEA, CA‑125, CA19‑9, PSA) are optional adjuncts but are insensitive as sole screening tests. Use them adjunctively or to follow up suspicious findings.  
- Careful history and full physical examination (skin, lymph nodes, breast, pelvic) remain essential.

How this applies to the case
- The team appropriately classified this patient as high risk (DM + dysphagia) with intermediate features (male, anti‑SAE1) and proceeded with CT imaging, FDG‑PET, tumour markers and planned upper and lower endoscopy — an approach concordant with the IMACS framework.

Surveillance tip
- Most cancers associated with myositis occur within the first 2–3 years of myositis onset, so concentrate surveillance in that period (repeat targeted imaging/clinical reassessment at intervals determined by local practice — commonly 6–12 months initially, then at least annually through year 2–3).

### [Management] In dermatomyositis with severe dysphagia and significant airway muscle involvement, outline an initial immunomodulatory regimen (agents and typical dosing) to induce remission, and justify avoiding methotrexate when lung involvement is suspected.

Short answer
- Rapid induction for dermatomyositis with severe dysphagia/airway‑muscle involvement aims to (1) rapidly suppress inflammation to reverse bulbar weakness and (2) protect the airway/avoid aspiration. A pragmatic, commonly used induction regimen — and the regimen used in this case — is:
  - High‑dose IV corticosteroid pulse: methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily × 3 days, then switch to oral prednisone ≈1 mg/kg/day (∼60 mg) with gradual taper (eg, decrease by 5 mg every 2–3 weeks as tolerated).
  - IV immunoglobulin (IVIG): 2 g/kg total as a loading dose (commonly given over 2–5 days; in this patient 2 g/kg over 5 days), followed by repeat 2 g/kg cycles every 4 weeks for several months as clinically indicated.
  - Early steroid‑sparing immunosuppression: mycophenolate mofetil 1 g orally twice daily (up‑titrate to 1.5 g BID if needed and tolerated).
  - Add a biologic for severe, antibody‑driven or refractory disease: rituximab 1,000 mg IV × 2 doses, 2 weeks apart (alternative regimen: 375 mg/m2 weekly × 4).
  - PJP prophylaxis while on combination immunosuppression (TMP‑SMX first line; if intolerant, inhaled pentamidine 300 mg monthly as used in this patient).
  - Early supportive measures: swallow assessment (video fluoroscopic swallow), enteral feeding (NG/PEG) until safe oral intake, airway monitoring and ENT/speech pathology involvement.

Why these choices (brief clinical reasoning)
- Bulbar/airway muscle involvement is functionally life‑threatening because inflammation and complement‑mediated microvascular injury produce rapid weakness of pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles. High‑dose IV corticosteroids give the fastest systemic anti‑inflammatory effect and are the immediate cornerstone for induction (the case used methylprednisolone 1 g daily × 3 days then prednisone 60 mg daily).
- IVIG is particularly useful for severe dysphagia and refractory or rapidly progressive disease because it can modulate pathogenic autoantibodies and immune effector mechanisms; this patient received an induction 2 g/kg loading dose (given over 5 days) followed by monthly cycles and had marked clinical improvement of neck swelling and later recovery of swallowing.
- Mycophenolate is an effective, generally well‑tolerated steroid‑sparing agent used in dermatomyositis and in myositis‑associated ILD; it was chosen in this case (1 g twice daily) because of tolerability and activity against both muscle and lung disease.
- Rituximab targets B cells and is reasonable early in severe, antibody‑positive disease (this patient was SAE1‑positive) or when rapid steroid‑sparing is needed; the case used 1,000 mg × 2 doses two weeks apart and attained remission.

Why methotrexate was avoided in this patient
- Methotrexate is frequently used as a steroid‑sparing agent in dermatomyositis but carries a risk of pulmonary toxicity (acute hypersensitivity pneumonitis and potential progression to fibrotic lung disease). In a patient with abnormal pulmonary studies at baseline (this patient had an extrathoracic restrictive pattern on PFTs and CT chest showing nodules and patchy peribronchial consolidation), starting methotrexate risks provoking or worsening lung injury and can complicate the distinction between drug toxicity and myositis‑related ILD.
- Given the substantial baseline lung abnormalities and the known association of SAE1 disease with ILD, selecting an alternative with more favorable pulmonary safety (eg, mycophenolate, IVIG, rituximab, and—if needed—cyclophosphamide for rapidly progressive ILD) is a safer strategy and is exactly the approach taken in this case.

Practical monitoring and safety points
- Baseline infection screening and immunization review before biologics (HBV, HCV, HIV, TB testing as done in the case).
- Serial clinical surveillance: CK, muscle strength, swallow function, PFTs and chest imaging for ILD, and regular assessment for infections and cytopenias.
- PJP prophylaxis during combined immunosuppression (the patient started TMP‑SMX but required switch to nebulized pentamidine 300 mg monthly for hemolytic anemia).
- Early malignancy screening per IMACS risk stratification because dermatomyositis carries an elevated cancer risk.

Bottom line
- When bulbar/airway muscles are severely involved, treat promptly and aggressively with high‑dose IV steroids plus IVIG and an early steroid‑sparing agent (mycophenolate in this patient) and consider rituximab for antibody‑positive or refractory disease. Avoid methotrexate when baseline lung disease or abnormal pulmonary testing is present because of the risk of methotrexate‑related pneumonitis and fibrosis; this rationale guided the safe, effective regimen used in the presented case.

### [Morning Report] When angioedema is suspected, which laboratory tests help diagnose bradykinin-mediated disease, and how would normal results alter your differential for isolated neck swelling?

Short answer
- First‑line laboratory tests to detect bradykinin‑mediated (C1‑inhibitor–related) angioedema are serum C4, C1‑esterase inhibitor (C1‑INH) antigenic level and C1‑INH functional assay. If an acquired form is suspected, add C1q. Specialized genetic testing (eg, F12, PLG and other genes) or research bradykinin assays are reserved for suspected hereditary angioedema with normal C1‑INH (HAE‑nL).

Why these tests
- Pathophysiology: C1‑INH normally restrains the classical complement and kallikrein–kinin pathways. In hereditary (types I and II) or acquired C1‑INH deficiency there is unopposed kallikrein activity → excess bradykinin → vascular permeability and angioedema. Ongoing classical pathway activation consumes C4, so C4 is usually low; C1‑INH antigen level is low in type I, and function is low (sometimes with normal antigen level) in type II. Low C1q supports an acquired (autoantibody/lymphoproliferative) process.

How to use the results in practice
- Algorithmic approach:
  - Start with C4. If C4 is low, measure C1‑INH antigen and function.
  - If C1q is low, look for an acquired cause (autoimmunity, monoclonal/lymphoproliferative disease).
  - If C4 and C1‑INH studies are normal but clinical suspicion remains, repeat tests during an acute attack and consider referral for genetic testing for HAE‑nl.
- Distinguish histaminergic angioedema: measure serum tryptase acutely if mast‑cell activation/anaphylaxis is suspected; histaminergic angioedema typically has urticaria, itching and responds to antihistamines/steroids/epinephrine, whereas bradykinin‑mediated does not.

How normal results change the differential (application to this case)
- Normal C4 and normal C1‑INH level/function (as in the presented patient) make hereditary or acquired C1‑INH deficiency unlikely and should prompt you to broaden the differential away from bradykinin‑mediated angioedema.
- In an isolated, subacute neck swelling with normal complement/C1‑INH, consider:
  - Non‑histaminergic, non‑bradykinin causes of soft‑tissue swelling — inflammatory myopathies (pseudo‑angioedema from muscle inflammation, as here with CK 16,184 U/L and positive SAE1 antibody), cellulitis/deep neck infection, salivary gland disease, venous/lymphatic obstruction, or neoplasm.
  - Histaminergic angioedema (check for urticaria, tryptase, response to antihistamines).
  - HAE with normal C1‑INH (rare): consider only if recurrent, family history, or typical triggers — this requires specialist genetic workup.
- In the case, the normal complement and C1‑INH studies together with the subacute course, periungual/nailfold changes and marked CK elevation correctly shifted the team’s thinking from angioedema toward dermatomyositis causing inflammatory neck swelling (“pseudo‑angioedema”), leading to appropriate immunosuppressive therapy.

Clinical pearl
- When you suspect bradykinin‑mediated angioedema, order C4 and C1‑INH antigen + function (and C1q if acquired disease is a concern). Normal results should make you question C1‑INH–mediated disease and actively search for alternative causes of neck swelling — including inflammatory myopathies such as dermatomyositis, which may mimic angioedema, as illustrated by this case.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which subtle cutaneous and nailfold findings, even in the absence of a heliotrope rash or Gottron’s papules, should prompt ordering a myositis workup in a patient with unexplained neck swelling?

Short answer
Even without a heliotrope rash or Gottron’s papules, subtle periungual and nailfold abnormalities and minor cutaneous rashes should lower your threshold to test for myositis in unexplained neck swelling. Specifically look for periungual erythema/telangiectasias, nailfold capillary abnormalities on capillaroscopy (microhaemorrhages, giant loops or capillary dropout/avascular areas), and faint violaceous or distributional rashes (shawl or V‑neck patterns, or a Holster sign on the lateral thigh).

Why this matters (clinical reasoning and pathophysiology)
Dermatomyositis is a complement‑mediated microangiopathy of skin and muscle; nailfold capillary changes reflect the same small‑vessel injury that can precede or occur without classic heliotrope/Gottron lesions. Muscle inflammation and capillary leak produce subcutaneous “pseudo‑angioedema” (non‑histaminergic, non‑bradykinin swelling) that can masquerade as isolated neck/face oedema. In the reported case the absence of typical heliotrope/Gottron findings belied clear nailfold findings (microhaemorrhages and avascular areas) and periungual erythema plus a lateral‑thigh rash (Holster sign) — these clues prompted a CK, which was markedly elevated (16,184 U/L) and led to the diagnosis of SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis.

Practical point: what to do when you see these signs
- Perform a bedside exam of the nailfolds (or formal capillaroscopy) whenever neck/face swelling is unexplained.  
- If periungual erythema, telangiectasias, microhaemorrhages or avascular areas are present, order CK (± aldolase), ANA/myositis‑specific antibody panel (including SAE1), and arrange rheumatology/immunology input.  
- Screen for associated features (proximal weakness, dysphagia, pulmonary involvement) and pursue MRI/EMG and muscle biopsy as indicated.  
- Remember that normal complement and C1‑esterase studies in such a patient favor a myositis‑related pseudo‑angioedema rather than histaminergic or bradykinin angioedema.

Clinical pearl
Periungual erythema/telangiectasias and nailfold microhaemorrhages or capillary dropout are low‑cost, high‑yield signs — in unexplained neck swelling they should prompt a myositis workup (start with CK and myositis antibodies) even when heliotrope rash and Gottron’s papules are absent.

### [Pathophysiology] How do pulmonary function testing and thoracic imaging connect oropharyngeal muscle involvement in dermatomyositis to aspiration risk, and which PFT pattern supports extrathoracic restriction?

Direct answer: Pulmonary function testing (PFTs) and thoracic imaging tie together oropharyngeal (bulbar) muscle involvement in dermatomyositis and aspiration risk by providing complementary functional and anatomic evidence — PFTs can demonstrate upper‑airway/respiratory muscle dysfunction (an “extrathoracic restrictive” pattern and reduced respiratory pressures) while chest CT shows the downstream consequence of impaired airway protection (peribronchial consolidation and dependent, mid‑to‑lower‑zone air‑space change consistent with aspiration pneumonitis). In this case the combination of an extrathoracic restrictive PFT pattern with no bronchodilator response, CT findings of peribronchial consolidation, and a video‑fluoroscopic study showing severe pharyngeal dysphagia links active oropharyngeal myositis to recurrent microaspiration.

Why this happens (pathophysiology): Dermatomyositis can inflame striated muscles of the oropharynx and larynx → impaired bolus propulsion, reduced laryngeal elevation, poor airway closure and a weak cough. Food or secretions then pool (eg, pyriform fossa residue) and can penetrate past the vocal cords, producing aspiration. Repeated microaspiration causes peribronchial and dependent air‑space consolidation and ground‑glass change on CT, the exact pattern seen in this patient.

Which PFT pattern supports extrathoracic (bulbar/upper‑airway) involvement: classically a restrictive physiology (reduced FVC and reduced TLC with a preserved or increased FEV1/FVC ratio) together with objective signs of respiratory/upper‑airway muscle weakness — low maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures (MIP/MEP) — and flow‑volume loop abnormalities consistent with extrathoracic limitation (flattening of the inspiratory limb for variable extrathoracic obstruction). The absence of bronchodilator response helps exclude obstructive airway disease, and a relatively preserved diffusion capacity (when measured) argues against primary parenchymal ILD as the cause of the low volumes. In the reported patient the PFTs were described as an extrathoracic restrictive pattern with no bronchodilator response, supporting bulbar/upper‑airway muscle dysfunction rather than primary obstructive lung disease.

Clinical pearl: in inflammatory myopathy patients with neck swelling, cough, secretions or any swallowing complaints, pair PFTs (including MIP/MEP and a careful flow‑volume loop) with early swallow evaluation and chest imaging — an “extrathoracic restrictive” PFT pattern plus dependent peribronchial consolidation should raise high suspicion for aspiration from bulbar myositis and prompt airway protection and urgent treatment of the myositis.
