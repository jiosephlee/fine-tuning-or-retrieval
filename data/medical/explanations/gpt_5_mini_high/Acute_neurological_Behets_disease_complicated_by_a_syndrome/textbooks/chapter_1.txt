Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 1: ## Initial presentation and problem representation

An otherwise unwell man in his 60s presented with a short (3‑day) history of systemic and neurological symptoms that together framed three interrelated diagnostic problems: acute central nervous system (CNS) involvement, a newly apparent systemic inflammatory disease, and marked hyponatraemia. His presenting complaints were fever, headache, general malaise, anorexia, persistent hiccups, arthralgia (shoulders and ankles) and oral aphthae; by hospital day 3 he developed worsening headache and vertigo. The acute time course and the combination of fever plus new focal CNS symptoms raised immediate concern for meningoencephalitis or other CNS inflammation. At the same time, the mucocutaneous findings (oral aphthae and folliculitis‑like rash, with a scrotal ulcer appearing later) pointed toward a systemic inflammatory disorder—eventually recognised as Behçet’s disease in this case—which can itself cause CNS inflammation.

On examination he was febrile (38.1°C) and tachycardic (HR 119 bpm) but otherwise haemodynamically stable and without focal motor deficits or overt neck stiffness. Importantly, his volume status was assessed as clinically euvolemic (no peripheral oedema, no obvious dehydration), a key bedside insight that helps classify hyponatraemia and guide immediate management. The mucocutaneous lesions were apparent on inspection and provided an important diagnostic clue linking the systemic inflammatory picture to Behçet’s disease once genital ulceration appeared.

The initial laboratory data framed the physiologic disturbances to prioritise. Serum sodium was 124 mmol/L, and measured serum osmolality was low (267 mOsm/kg), confirming true hypotonic hyponatraemia rather than a translocational or artifactual low sodium. Urine osmolality was very high (900 mOsm/kg) with urine sodium 88 mmol/L, consistent with impaired free water excretion and a euvolemic hypotonic state most compatible with the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Thyroid function (TSH, free T4) and a single serum cortisol were normal, which helped exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency as alternative causes. ADH was reported as elevated, supportive though not required for the clinical diagnosis of SIADH. Other notable abnormalities were a markedly elevated inflammatory response (CRP 180 mg/L, ESR 70 mm/h), a high creatine kinase (CK 1,349 U/L) and mild transaminitis (AST 77, ALT 64 U/L). Plasma glucose was elevated (14.2 mmol/L) with HbA1c 8.2%, but the low serum osmolality confirmed that the hyponatraemia was not simply a hyperglycaemia‑related translocational effect. Renal function was preserved.

Putting these findings together yields a compact problem list for early management: (1) an acute febrile headache syndrome with evolving vertigo—CNS infection or inflammatory meningoencephalitis is highest on the differential and requires rapid exclusion; (2) an underlying systemic inflammatory disorder most consistent with Behçet’s disease given the mucocutaneous lesions; and (3) clinically euvolemic, hypotonic hyponatraemia meeting biochemical criteria for SIADH, with associated muscle injury (elevated CK) that raises concern for rhabdomyolysis and its renal consequences.

Immediate diagnostic and safety priorities flow directly from those problems. Because CNS infection is life‑threatening, clinicians should obtain blood cultures, promptly image the brain (to exclude mass effect before lumbar puncture), and perform lumbar puncture and directed CSF studies (cell count, protein, glucose, Gram stain, cultures and viral PCRs). Empiric antimicrobial coverage for likely pathogens (bacterial meningitis and HSV encephalitis) is appropriate while awaiting results; in this case empiric cefepime and later acyclovir were used until infectious studies were negative. For the hyponatraemia the priorities are to determine acuity and symptom severity (confusion, seizures, coma, respiratory compromise), to establish volume status, and to characterise the biochemical pattern (serum and urine osmolality and urine sodium)—all of which were done here and pointed to SIADH. Immediate treatment decisions depend on symptoms: severe neurologic symptoms mandate urgent hypertonic saline, whereas stable, mild–moderate SIADH is managed conservatively (fluid restriction, treat underlying cause) and with close serial sodium monitoring, as was chosen for this patient.

Finally, this presentation highlights some clinical clues that should raise suspicion for profound hyponatraemia and its complications. Persistent hiccups and nonspecific malaise are common but easily overlooked manifestations of significant electrolyte disturbance and/or brainstem irritation; in this case hiccups accompanied the hyponatraemia. The markedly elevated CK indicates muscle injury (rhabdomyolysis), a recognised but underappreciated complication of severe hyponatraemia; recognising this early prompts measurement of CK and renal function and vigilance for hyperkalaemia and acute kidney injury. Management may require balancing fluid restriction for SIADH against the usual need for aggressive isotonic hydration in rhabdomyolysis—decisions that must be individualised and guided by the severity of the hyponatraemia, renal function, and the overall clinical trajectory. In this patient the multidisciplinary early evaluation and monitoring permitted conservative SIADH management while the diagnostic workup excluded infection and established Behçet’s disease as the likely inflammatory driver.
