Chapter 2: ## Behçet’s disease and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease: focused background

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a relapsing, multisystem inflammatory disorder best conceptualised as a form of systemic vasculitis with prominent neutrophil and cytokine‑mediated inflammation. Because there is no single diagnostic test, BD is a clinical diagnosis based on composite criteria. The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) uses a point system: oral aphthosis (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points), ocular lesions (2 points), skin lesions (1 point), vascular involvement (1 point), neurologic involvement (1 point) and a positive pathergy test (1 point); a total of ≥4 points classifies a patient as having BD. In practice genital aphthosis is relatively specific for BD and often clinches the diagnosis when present. A positive pathergy test can be helpful but has variable sensitivity by region and is not required; likewise HLA‑B51 is a common genetic association that increases disease likelihood but is supportive rather than diagnostic.

Neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) encompasses several distinct neurological phenotypes that reflect the underlying vasculo‑inflammatory biology. The parenchymal form is the prototypical neuro‑BD manifestation and typically presents as meningoencephalitis with inflammatory lesions in the brainstem, diencephalon (including thalami), basal ganglia or spinal cord; these lesions are thought to reflect small‑to‑medium vessel perivascular inflammation and often produce focal neurological deficits. The non‑parenchymal form is dominated by vascular complications such as cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT) and may present with raised intracranial pressure, papilloedema or headache without focal parenchymal signs. A third presentation—aseptic meningitis—manifests as headache, fever, meningitic symptoms and CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis without discrete parenchymal lesions. The clinical phenotype guides urgency and intensity of therapy: parenchymal disease usually requires high‑dose corticosteroids and often additional immunosuppression; non‑parenchymal CVT typically requires combined control of inflammation and consideration of anticoagulation; aseptic meningitis can be self‑limited in some cases but mandates exclusion of infection before withholding immunotherapy.

The investigative pattern in neuro‑BD is characteristic but not pathognomonic. Brain MRI commonly shows T2/FLAIR hyperintense, sometimes enhancing lesions in the brainstem, thalami or basal ganglia in parenchymal disease; however, MRI can be normal, especially early or in meningitic presentations, so a normal scan does not exclude neuro‑BD. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) typically demonstrates mild–moderate lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein; culture and molecular testing are essential to exclude bacterial, mycobacterial and viral causes. Interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) is frequently elevated in CSF during acute neuro‑BD and correlates with inflammatory activity—this cytokine milieu also provides a plausible link to systemic effects (for example, stimulation of ADH secretion in central inflammatory states). Thus the diagnosis of neuro‑BD is often clinical and exclusionary: typical neurological syndrome plus supportive CSF or imaging findings and careful elimination of infectious, neoplastic and other autoimmune mimics.

Epidemiologically, neurological involvement develops in roughly 10–20% of patients over the course of BD, but it usually appears several years after mucocutaneous onset (median intervals of a few years have been reported). Presentation with simultaneous new systemic BD and acute neuro‑BD is uncommon, and isolated neurological presentation at first diagnosis is rare. Because of that timing, clinicians must keep a high index of suspicion when neurological symptoms arise in a patient with mucocutaneous features or when new mucocutaneous lesions appear in someone with unexplained CNS inflammation.

Clinically, neuro‑BD should be suspected in any patient with known or suspected BD who develops a new, persistent headache, meningitic symptoms (fever, neck stiffness, raised opening pressure), unexplained vertigo, acute behavioural or cognitive change, seizures or focal neurological deficits. Practical application to the present case: the patient’s oral ulcers and later genital ulcer satisfied ICBD criteria for BD, and his worsening headache, vertigo and CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis supported an aseptic meningitis variant of acute neuro‑BD despite a normal early MRI. Because neuro‑BD is a diagnosis of exclusion, initial empiric coverage for CNS infection is appropriate until microbiological causes are ruled out. Finally, remember that systemic inflammatory mediators (notably IL‑6) in neuroinflammation can produce endocrine effects such as SIADH, so concurrent unexplained euvolemic hyponatraemia in a patient with suspected neuro‑BD should prompt evaluation for SIADH and coordinated neurologic and endocrine management.
