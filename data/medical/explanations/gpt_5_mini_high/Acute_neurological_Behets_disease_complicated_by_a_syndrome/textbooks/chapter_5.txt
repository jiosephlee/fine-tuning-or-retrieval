Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 5: ## Management: treating acute neuro‑BD and SIADH, monitoring, and when to escalate

This patient’s management illustrates three parallel priorities in the acute setting: (1) treat immediately reversible, potentially life‑threatening causes while diagnostics are pending; (2) manage the electrolyte disturbance and its complications; and (3) choose immunomodulatory therapy for neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) appropriate to disease severity. In the case, empirical broad‑spectrum antibiotics (cefepime) and acyclovir were started while infection was being excluded; SIADH was managed conservatively with fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) and close monitoring; and colchicine monotherapy was begun once the working diagnosis of systemic BD with aseptic meningitis (acute neuro‑BD) was established. The patient improved without corticosteroids or other immunosuppression. Below I explain the reasoning behind each decision, contrast it with standard practice, and specify monitoring and triggers for escalation.

Initial empiric therapy during diagnostic uncertainty
When a patient presents with fever, headache and CSF pleocytosis, prompt empirical coverage for common and treatable CNS infections is standard because delays in therapy for bacterial meningitis or herpes encephalitis increase morbidity and mortality. Empiric regimens should be chosen for the clinical context and local microbiology (community‑acquired regimens commonly include a third‑generation cephalosporin plus vancomycin ± ampicillin for Listeria in older or immunocompromised patients; cefepime is used in some institutions for broad gram‑negative coverage). Acyclovir should be started empirically when HSV encephalitis is suspected. These agents should be discontinued once objective data lower the probability of infection: negative CSF Gram stain and cultures after an appropriate interval (typically 48–72 hours for cultures), negative highly sensitive CSF PCRs for HSV and VZV, a clinical course incompatible with bacterial infection, and an alternative non‑infectious diagnosis that explains the presentation. Exercise caution before stopping antivirals if PCR testing was performed very early, if the patient is immunocompromised, or if clinical suspicion remains high — in those situations repeat testing or continued treatment until the diagnosis is secure is reasonable.

Standard acute neuro‑BD therapy and indications to escalate
Parenchymal neuro‑BD (brainstem, hemispheric or spinal cord involvement with focal deficits) is usually treated promptly with high‑dose corticosteroids because of the risk of permanent neurologic injury. A commonly used regimen is intravenous methylprednisolone (for example, 1 g daily) for 3–5 days followed by an oral prednisone taper. Steroid‑sparing or adjunctive immunosuppressants (for example, azathioprine, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide for severe vasculitic disease, or biologics such as anti‑TNF agents for refractory cases) are added for severe, progressive, relapsing, or steroid‑dependent disease. By contrast, patients with a mild, self‑limited aseptic meningitic presentation, normal brain MRI and no focal deficits—like this patient—may occasionally be managed conservatively or with colchicine for mucocutaneous disease while observing the neurologic course. That said, guidelines and expert opinion support a low threshold to initiate high‑dose steroids when there is any neurological deterioration, development of focal deficits, MRI evidence of parenchymal or brainstem lesions, spinal cord involvement, seizures, or progressive CSF abnormalities. Early consultation with neurology and rheumatology/immunology is appropriate before committing to prolonged immunosuppression or to select second‑line agents.

Colchicine: mechanism, dosing considerations, evidence and safety
Colchicine acts by binding tubulin, impairing microtubule formation and neutrophil chemotaxis, and suppressing inflammasome activity and cytokine release (including IL‑1 and IL‑6). It is effective for many mucocutaneous and joint manifestations of BD and is widely used in that setting. Typical maintenance dosing for BD mucocutaneous disease is about 0.5 mg twice daily (total ≈1 mg/day), with lower doses (0.5 mg once daily) for elderly patients or those with significant renal or hepatic impairment; doses and adjustments should follow local formularies and patient tolerance. The major adverse effects are dose‑related gastrointestinal intolerance (diarrhea, nausea); more serious toxicities include myopathy and rhabdomyolysis—especially when combined with statins, in renal impairment, or with strong CYP3A4/P‑glycoprotein inhibitors (clarithromycin, itraconazole, cyclosporine)—and rare bone‑marrow suppression in overdose. Drug–drug interactions must be checked before starting colchicine.

Randomized data support colchicine for mucocutaneous BD manifestations, but evidence for efficacy in parenchymal neuro‑BD is sparse. Most case series and reviews recommend immunosuppression rather than colchicine alone for parenchymal CNS disease. Therefore, colchicine monotherapy may be reasonable in selected situations—mild aseptic meningitic presentations without focal deficits, normal imaging, contraindications to steroids, or when close inpatient monitoring is feasible—recognizing that spontaneous recovery is possible and that clinicians must remain ready to escalate to high‑dose steroids and additional immunosuppression if neurologic status worsens.

SIADH management: when to restrict fluids, give hypertonic saline, use salt/urea or vaptans
Management of SIADH proceeds in two linked directions: treat the underlying cause (here, neuroinflammation) and correct the hyponatraemia safely. Initial therapy for euvolemic SIADH is fluid restriction tailored to urine osmolality and patient tolerance; in practice many teams start with <800–1000 mL/day as was done for this patient. If restriction fails, the next options include increasing solute intake (oral urea or salt tablets with a loop diuretic to encourage free water excretion) or use of vasopressin‑receptor antagonists (vaptans) in the hospital; vaptans (e.g., tolvaptan) are effective but require close monitoring because of the risk of overly rapid correction and, for tolvaptan, potential hepatotoxicity with longer courses.

Hypertonic (3%) saline is indicated for severe or life‑threatening hyponatraemia with neurologic symptoms (seizure, profound somnolence, coma) or when sodium is very low and symptoms are present; small boluses are typically used to achieve an initial modest rise (for example, an initial target rise of 4–6 mmol/L to relieve cerebral edema) rather than aiming for a rapid normalization. Correction must be conservative overall: aim for a rise of no more than about 8–10 mmol/L in 24 hours (and many experts prefer ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours for patients at high risk of osmotic demyelination such as those with chronic hyponatraemia, alcoholism, malnutrition or severe hypokalemia). If overcorrection occurs, desmopressin coupled with administration of free water (D5W) can be used to safely re‑lower serum sodium.

Monitoring response and safety during therapy
Close and protocolized monitoring is essential. When hypertonic saline or vaptans are used, measure serum sodium every 2–4 hours and monitor urine output continuously; when active correction is not underway and conservative measures are used, check sodium at least every 6–12 hours until trends are clear. Record urine osmolality and urine sodium to judge the likelihood of response to fluid restriction. Perform frequent neurological examinations (hourly to several times daily as indicated) to detect new focal signs. Because severe hyponatraemia can cause rhabdomyolysis and because colchicine and statins can cause myopathy, monitor creatine kinase and urine myoglobin if patients develop myalgias, weakness, or dark urine; in this case CK was elevated initially and normalized with correction. Monitor renal and liver function tests, full blood count and inflammatory markers (CRP/ESR) regularly while treating neuro‑inflammation and when using agents with hepatic or marrow toxicity. Review medication interactions (notably CYP3A4 and P‑glycoprotein inhibitors with colchicine) and adjust doses accordingly.

Comorbidity considerations: hyperglycaemia and diabetes
Interpret serum sodium in the light of hyperglycaemia because elevated plasma glucose causes osmotic movement of water from cells to the extracellular space and lowers measured sodium. Use a sodium correction formula (a commonly used adjustment is to add ~1.6 mmol/L to measured sodium for each 100 mg/dL [≈5.6 mmol/L] rise in glucose above ~100 mg/dL) to estimate the true hypotonic component; treating hyperglycaemia will partially correct the sodium concentration and avoids unnecessary or excessive sodium repletion. Diabetes management should be optimized once the acute phase is controlled because glycaemic instability can confound sodium interpretation and complicate fluid and electrolyte management.

When to escalate and which specialists to involve
Escalate therapy and seek multidisciplinary input for any neurologic deterioration (new or worsening focal deficits, seizures, reduced consciousness), new or progressive parenchymal lesions on imaging (especially brainstem or spinal cord), persistent or worsening hyponatraemia despite conservative measures, or when considering potent or prolonged immunosuppression or vaptan therapy. Early involvement of neurology and rheumatology/immunology is advisable when neuro‑BD is suspected or prior to initiating long‑term disease‑modifying therapy. Nephrology or endocrinology should be consulted for complicated or refractory SIADH, for initiation of vaptans, or for controlled hypertonic saline protocols.

In summary, the team’s approach in this case—empiric antimicrobial/antiviral coverage while excluding infection, conservative SIADH care with fluid restriction and correction of hyperglycaemia, and initiation of colchicine for BD with very close observation—was defensible given the mild aseptic meningitic presentation and normal MRI. However, clinicians must individualize treatment, be ready to begin high‑dose corticosteroids and add steroid‑sparing immunosuppression for parenchymal or progressive neuro‑BD, and escalate SIADH therapy promptly when sodium or neurologic status requires it.
