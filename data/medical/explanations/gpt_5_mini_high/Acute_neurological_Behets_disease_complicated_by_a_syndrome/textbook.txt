Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Initial presentation and problem representation

An otherwise unwell man in his 60s presented with a short (3‑day) history of systemic and neurological symptoms that together framed three interrelated diagnostic problems: acute central nervous system (CNS) involvement, a newly apparent systemic inflammatory disease, and marked hyponatraemia. His presenting complaints were fever, headache, general malaise, anorexia, persistent hiccups, arthralgia (shoulders and ankles) and oral aphthae; by hospital day 3 he developed worsening headache and vertigo. The acute time course and the combination of fever plus new focal CNS symptoms raised immediate concern for meningoencephalitis or other CNS inflammation. At the same time, the mucocutaneous findings (oral aphthae and folliculitis‑like rash, with a scrotal ulcer appearing later) pointed toward a systemic inflammatory disorder—eventually recognised as Behçet’s disease in this case—which can itself cause CNS inflammation.

On examination he was febrile (38.1°C) and tachycardic (HR 119 bpm) but otherwise haemodynamically stable and without focal motor deficits or overt neck stiffness. Importantly, his volume status was assessed as clinically euvolemic (no peripheral oedema, no obvious dehydration), a key bedside insight that helps classify hyponatraemia and guide immediate management. The mucocutaneous lesions were apparent on inspection and provided an important diagnostic clue linking the systemic inflammatory picture to Behçet’s disease once genital ulceration appeared.

The initial laboratory data framed the physiologic disturbances to prioritise. Serum sodium was 124 mmol/L, and measured serum osmolality was low (267 mOsm/kg), confirming true hypotonic hyponatraemia rather than a translocational or artifactual low sodium. Urine osmolality was very high (900 mOsm/kg) with urine sodium 88 mmol/L, consistent with impaired free water excretion and a euvolemic hypotonic state most compatible with the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Thyroid function (TSH, free T4) and a single serum cortisol were normal, which helped exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency as alternative causes. ADH was reported as elevated, supportive though not required for the clinical diagnosis of SIADH. Other notable abnormalities were a markedly elevated inflammatory response (CRP 180 mg/L, ESR 70 mm/h), a high creatine kinase (CK 1,349 U/L) and mild transaminitis (AST 77, ALT 64 U/L). Plasma glucose was elevated (14.2 mmol/L) with HbA1c 8.2%, but the low serum osmolality confirmed that the hyponatraemia was not simply a hyperglycaemia‑related translocational effect. Renal function was preserved.

Putting these findings together yields a compact problem list for early management: (1) an acute febrile headache syndrome with evolving vertigo—CNS infection or inflammatory meningoencephalitis is highest on the differential and requires rapid exclusion; (2) an underlying systemic inflammatory disorder most consistent with Behçet’s disease given the mucocutaneous lesions; and (3) clinically euvolemic, hypotonic hyponatraemia meeting biochemical criteria for SIADH, with associated muscle injury (elevated CK) that raises concern for rhabdomyolysis and its renal consequences.

Immediate diagnostic and safety priorities flow directly from those problems. Because CNS infection is life‑threatening, clinicians should obtain blood cultures, promptly image the brain (to exclude mass effect before lumbar puncture), and perform lumbar puncture and directed CSF studies (cell count, protein, glucose, Gram stain, cultures and viral PCRs). Empiric antimicrobial coverage for likely pathogens (bacterial meningitis and HSV encephalitis) is appropriate while awaiting results; in this case empiric cefepime and later acyclovir were used until infectious studies were negative. For the hyponatraemia the priorities are to determine acuity and symptom severity (confusion, seizures, coma, respiratory compromise), to establish volume status, and to characterise the biochemical pattern (serum and urine osmolality and urine sodium)—all of which were done here and pointed to SIADH. Immediate treatment decisions depend on symptoms: severe neurologic symptoms mandate urgent hypertonic saline, whereas stable, mild–moderate SIADH is managed conservatively (fluid restriction, treat underlying cause) and with close serial sodium monitoring, as was chosen for this patient.

Finally, this presentation highlights some clinical clues that should raise suspicion for profound hyponatraemia and its complications. Persistent hiccups and nonspecific malaise are common but easily overlooked manifestations of significant electrolyte disturbance and/or brainstem irritation; in this case hiccups accompanied the hyponatraemia. The markedly elevated CK indicates muscle injury (rhabdomyolysis), a recognised but underappreciated complication of severe hyponatraemia; recognising this early prompts measurement of CK and renal function and vigilance for hyperkalaemia and acute kidney injury. Management may require balancing fluid restriction for SIADH against the usual need for aggressive isotonic hydration in rhabdomyolysis—decisions that must be individualised and guided by the severity of the hyponatraemia, renal function, and the overall clinical trajectory. In this patient the multidisciplinary early evaluation and monitoring permitted conservative SIADH management while the diagnostic workup excluded infection and established Behçet’s disease as the likely inflammatory driver.

## Behçet’s disease and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease: focused background

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a relapsing, multisystem inflammatory disorder best conceptualised as a form of systemic vasculitis with prominent neutrophil and cytokine‑mediated inflammation. Because there is no single diagnostic test, BD is a clinical diagnosis based on composite criteria. The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) uses a point system: oral aphthosis (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points), ocular lesions (2 points), skin lesions (1 point), vascular involvement (1 point), neurologic involvement (1 point) and a positive pathergy test (1 point); a total of ≥4 points classifies a patient as having BD. In practice genital aphthosis is relatively specific for BD and often clinches the diagnosis when present. A positive pathergy test can be helpful but has variable sensitivity by region and is not required; likewise HLA‑B51 is a common genetic association that increases disease likelihood but is supportive rather than diagnostic.

Neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) encompasses several distinct neurological phenotypes that reflect the underlying vasculo‑inflammatory biology. The parenchymal form is the prototypical neuro‑BD manifestation and typically presents as meningoencephalitis with inflammatory lesions in the brainstem, diencephalon (including thalami), basal ganglia or spinal cord; these lesions are thought to reflect small‑to‑medium vessel perivascular inflammation and often produce focal neurological deficits. The non‑parenchymal form is dominated by vascular complications such as cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT) and may present with raised intracranial pressure, papilloedema or headache without focal parenchymal signs. A third presentation—aseptic meningitis—manifests as headache, fever, meningitic symptoms and CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis without discrete parenchymal lesions. The clinical phenotype guides urgency and intensity of therapy: parenchymal disease usually requires high‑dose corticosteroids and often additional immunosuppression; non‑parenchymal CVT typically requires combined control of inflammation and consideration of anticoagulation; aseptic meningitis can be self‑limited in some cases but mandates exclusion of infection before withholding immunotherapy.

The investigative pattern in neuro‑BD is characteristic but not pathognomonic. Brain MRI commonly shows T2/FLAIR hyperintense, sometimes enhancing lesions in the brainstem, thalami or basal ganglia in parenchymal disease; however, MRI can be normal, especially early or in meningitic presentations, so a normal scan does not exclude neuro‑BD. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) typically demonstrates mild–moderate lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein; culture and molecular testing are essential to exclude bacterial, mycobacterial and viral causes. Interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) is frequently elevated in CSF during acute neuro‑BD and correlates with inflammatory activity—this cytokine milieu also provides a plausible link to systemic effects (for example, stimulation of ADH secretion in central inflammatory states). Thus the diagnosis of neuro‑BD is often clinical and exclusionary: typical neurological syndrome plus supportive CSF or imaging findings and careful elimination of infectious, neoplastic and other autoimmune mimics.

Epidemiologically, neurological involvement develops in roughly 10–20% of patients over the course of BD, but it usually appears several years after mucocutaneous onset (median intervals of a few years have been reported). Presentation with simultaneous new systemic BD and acute neuro‑BD is uncommon, and isolated neurological presentation at first diagnosis is rare. Because of that timing, clinicians must keep a high index of suspicion when neurological symptoms arise in a patient with mucocutaneous features or when new mucocutaneous lesions appear in someone with unexplained CNS inflammation.

Clinically, neuro‑BD should be suspected in any patient with known or suspected BD who develops a new, persistent headache, meningitic symptoms (fever, neck stiffness, raised opening pressure), unexplained vertigo, acute behavioural or cognitive change, seizures or focal neurological deficits. Practical application to the present case: the patient’s oral ulcers and later genital ulcer satisfied ICBD criteria for BD, and his worsening headache, vertigo and CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis supported an aseptic meningitis variant of acute neuro‑BD despite a normal early MRI. Because neuro‑BD is a diagnosis of exclusion, initial empiric coverage for CNS infection is appropriate until microbiological causes are ruled out. Finally, remember that systemic inflammatory mediators (notably IL‑6) in neuroinflammation can produce endocrine effects such as SIADH, so concurrent unexplained euvolemic hyponatraemia in a patient with suspected neuro‑BD should prompt evaluation for SIADH and coordinated neurologic and endocrine management.

## Hyponatraemia and SIADH in neuroinflammation: mechanisms and diagnostic framework

Hyponatraemia in a patient with acute central nervous system (CNS) inflammation should immediately prompt consideration of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). SIADH is a common cause of hypotonic hyponatraemia and is mechanistically plausible in meningitis, encephalitis or any disorder that irritates or inflames the hypothalamic–pituitary axis. In the reported case the combination of profound hyponatraemia (Na+ 124 mmol/L), low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), highly concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg), urinary sodium 88 mmol/L, and a clinically euvolemic examination led the team to diagnose SIADH after excluding other endocrine causes — a useful example of how the diagnostic framework is applied at the bedside.

Diagnostic framework and application to the case
Diagnosing SIADH is fundamentally a diagnosis of hypotonic euvolemic hyponatraemia after exclusion of alternative explanations. The commonly used criteria are: (1) true hypotonic hyponatraemia (serum osmolality <275 mOsm/kg); (2) inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality typically >100 mOsm/kg and often >300 mOsm/kg when ADH is active); (3) elevated urine sodium (usually >30–40 mmol/L) with normal salt intake; (4) clinical euvolemia without signs of hypovolaemia or oedema; and (5) normal thyroid and adrenal function and no recent diuretic use. Measurement of ADH itself is not required for diagnosis because assays are technically difficult and results can be variable; when available, an elevated ADH level can be supportive. Applied to this patient: serum osmolality was low (267 mOsm/kg), urine was markedly concentrated (900 mOsm/kg), urine Na was high (88 mmol/L), there were no clinical features of volume depletion or overload, and endocrine testing (thyroid and cortisol) did not suggest hypothyroidism or adrenal failure — fulfilling the criteria for SIADH. The recorded ADH elevation (22.4 pg/mL) therefore provided additional corroboration but was not essential.

Mechanistic links between CNS inflammation and ADH release
Physiologically, ADH (vasopressin) secretion is controlled by hypothalamic osmoreceptors and by non‑osmotic stimuli such as decreased effective arterial blood volume or certain neurohumoral signals. CNS inflammation can provoke non‑osmotic ADH release by several mechanisms. Direct inflammatory involvement of the hypothalamus or medulla can stimulate magnocellular neurons, and inflammatory cytokines—particularly interleukin‑6 (IL‑6)—have been shown to stimulate ADH secretion or magnify its release in meningitis and other neuroinflammatory states. A related concept is the “reset osmostat,” in which CNS disease lowers the threshold at which ADH is released, producing a stable, mild hyponatraemia. In acute neuroinflammatory syndromes (as in this patient), a cytokine‑driven non‑osmotic ADH surge is the likelier mechanism. In this case IL‑6 was not measured, but a markedly raised CRP argued for active systemic/CNS inflammation consistent with that mechanism.

Key differential diagnoses to consider and practical pitfalls
A structured approach to hyponatraemia begins with confirmation that the low sodium represents true hypotonic hyponatraemia. Pseudohyponatraemia from severe hyperproteinaemia or hyperlipidaemia is excluded by measuring serum osmolality, as was done here (serum osmolality was low). Translocational hyponatraemia from hyperglycaemia can also lower measured sodium by osmotic water shift; the usual correction formula is: corrected Na ≈ measured Na + 1.6 × ((glucose mg/dL − 100)/100). In this patient glucose was 14.2 mmol/L (≈256 mg/dL), so the corrected Na would increase by roughly 2.5 mmol/L (to ≈126–127), which does not explain the degree of hyponatraemia alone and therefore does not negate SIADH. Other important differentials are hypovolaemic hyponatraemia (usually low urine Na <20 mmol/L unless the patient is on diuretics), hypervolaemic states (heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome), hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency (both should be excluded by TSH and cortisol testing — the latter often by a morning cortisol or stimulation testing if suspicious). Drug-induced SIADH (eg, SSRIs, carbamazepine, cyclophosphamide, thiazides) must be considered; in this case the medication history did not implicate such agents.

Clinical manifestations and complications to watch for
Symptoms depend on the degree and rapidity of sodium change. Mild or chronic hyponatraemia may be asymptomatic or cause subtle cognitive dysfunction and gait instability; more acute or severe drops produce headache, nausea, vomiting, confusion, seizures and coma due to cerebral edema. Certain features may point specifically to CNS causes: persistent hiccups and intractable nausea have been reported as clues to meningitis‑related SIADH and were present here. Hyponatraemia can also lead to less widely recognized complications such as rhabdomyolysis: hypotonic swelling of muscle cells (or indirect effects such as seizures) can cause muscle breakdown and a rise in CK, as was documented in this patient (CK 1,349 U/L). Recognising these downstream consequences is important because they require targeted monitoring (renal function, CK, urine output) and supportive care.

Principles of safe treatment and monitoring
Management has two simultaneous aims: (1) safe and controlled correction of serum sodium to prevent cerebral oedema or neurological deterioration, and (2) treatment of the underlying cause so that inappropriate ADH stimulus resolves. For euvolemic SIADH without severe neurologic symptoms, first‑line therapy is conservative: fluid restriction (often 800–1,000 mL/day or less, tailored to patient size and ongoing losses) and monitoring. In acute severe neurologic symptoms (seizures, coma, severe obtundation) prompt administration of hypertonic saline is indicated to raise serum sodium rapidly by a small, controlled amount (an initial target increase of approximately 4–6 mmol/L to relieve life‑threatening cerebral oedema), followed by slower correction. After the emergent phase, most guidelines recommend limiting total rise in serum sodium to ≤8–10 mmol/L in the first 24 hours (some sources advise ≤8 mmol/L in high‑risk patients) and ≤18 mmol/L in 48 hours to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome. Serum sodium should be checked frequently during active correction (eg, every 2–4 hours when hypertonic saline is being given, or at least every 6–12 hours with fluid restriction and ongoing correction). If overcorrection occurs, desmopressin and free water can be used to re‑lower sodium and reduce ODS risk. Second‑line or adjunctive therapies for chronic or refractory SIADH include oral urea, loop diuretics with salt supplementation, and vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans) in selected patients; these options require familiarity with contraindications and monitoring.

How this applies to the case
In this patient the team applied the stepwise diagnostic framework—confirmed hypotonic hyponatraemia, documented inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated urine sodium, excluded hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency, and accounted for hyperglycaemia—leading to a diagnosis of SIADH attributable to CNS inflammation from acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease. Management was conservative: fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) and treatment of the underlying inflammatory disorder (colchicine), with frequent sodium monitoring and avoidance of rapid correction. The serum sodium normalized over two weeks without hypertonic saline or vaptans, supporting the principle that control of the primary inflammatory stimulus will often resolve secondary SIADH.

Clinical takeaways
When hyponatraemia accompanies CNS symptoms, use a structured approach: confirm hypotonicity, assess volemic status, measure urine osmolality and sodium, and exclude thyroid and adrenal disease and common drugs. Recognise that CNS inflammation can provoke non‑osmotic ADH release (often cytokine‑mediated), and that clues such as persistent hiccups or unexplained rhabdomyolysis can point to clinically significant hyponatraemia. Treat emergent neurological complications promptly with controlled hypertonic therapy but start with conservative water restriction and directed therapy against the underlying neuroinflammatory process in most cases.

## Stepwise diagnostic workup and reasoning toward acute neuro-BD with SIADH

The clinical challenge in this case was to move from a broad, urgent differential—infectious meningoencephalitis, parenchymal CNS disease, systemic inflammatory disorders or malignancy—to a unifying diagnosis that also explained marked hyponatraemia. The team followed a pragmatic, stepwise approach: (1) rapidly exclude treatable and immediately life‑threatening infections, (2) characterise the CNS process with CSF and imaging, (3) recognise and classify the hyponatraemia, (4) seek systemic clues (the mucocutaneous lesions) that could link CNS inflammation to a multisystem disease, and (5) avoid over‑interpreting non‑specific screening tests. The sequence of investigations and how each finding narrowed the differential are described below.

Because the patient presented febrile with headache and evolving neurological symptoms, initial management appropriately prioritized infectious causes. CSF analysis showed a mildly raised opening pressure (17 cmH2O), lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) and elevated protein (0.97 g/L). These features point to an aseptic (non‑purulent) meningeal process and argue against typical acute bacterial meningitis, which usually produces a neutrophil‑predominant pleocytosis, markedly elevated protein and low glucose. Accordingly, blood cultures were obtained and empirical broad‑spectrum antibacterial coverage (cefepime) was started on admission. When headache and vertigo progressed, empiric acyclovir was added because herpes simplex and varicella zoster encephalitis are treatable causes whose outcomes improve with early antiviral therapy. Serial CSF testing (bacterial cultures, PCR for HSV/VZV, tests for mycobacterial and fungal pathogens, and cryptococcal antigen) were negative; with these results and clinical improvement, antiviral therapy was discontinued. This exemplifies the principle of early empirical therapy for high‑risk infections followed by de‑escalation once microbiology is unrevealing.

Neuroimaging was an important next step but required nuanced interpretation. Brain MRI in this patient showed no focal parenchymal or hypothalamic abnormalities. In neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD), however, imaging can be normal in early or purely meningeal presentations; typical parenchymal lesions (when present) often involve the brainstem, basal ganglia or deep white matter and are best seen on contrast‑enhanced T2/FLAIR sequences. Thus, a normal MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD in a patient with compatible CSF findings and systemic BD features. Practically, if clinical evolution produced new focal deficits or worsening consciousness, repeating MRI (with dedicated sequences and contrast) or expanding to spinal imaging would be indicated to seek delayed or evolving lesions. In the absence of new focal signs, however, normal early MRI combined with aseptic CSF should not divert attention from inflammatory CNS diagnoses.

Because lymphocytic CSF can also reflect neoplastic or granulomatous processes, the team screened for malignancy and other inflammatory mimics. CSF cytology was negative and systemic tests included soluble interleukin‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R), serum angiotensin‑converting enzyme (ACE) and lysozyme. These markers proved unhelpful: the sIL‑2R was mildly elevated, and ACE/lysozyme were within reference ranges. It is important to recognise their limitations—sIL‑2R can be elevated in inflammatory states as well as lymphoma, and ACE/lysozyme have limited sensitivity for neurosarcoidosis. Negative cytology and imaging made CNS lymphoma unlikely in this case, but persistent clinical concern would prompt repeat CSF studies, flow cytometry, or directed tissue sampling rather than reliance on a single non‑specific blood test.

Parallel to the CNS workup, careful dermatologic and mucosal assessment provided the diagnostic key. The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) allocate points for recurrent oral aphthae (2), genital aphthosis (2), and characteristic skin lesions such as folliculitis‑like eruptions (1); a score of ≥4 denotes “definite” BD. In this patient the appearance of oral ulcers, folliculitis‑type rashes and a painful scrotal ulcer yielded a total of five points, meeting the ICBD threshold. Ophthalmologic examination was negative for uveitis and HLA‑B51 was positive; the latter is supportive but not diagnostic. Because BD is a clinical syndrome, the presence of these mucocutaneous manifestations shifts pre‑test probability heavily toward BD as the underlying systemic disorder linking the aseptic meningitis and other systemic inflammation.

Concurrently, the hyponatraemia was rigorously classified using biochemical and clinical criteria. The patient was hypotonic (serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg) with inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg) and elevated urine sodium (88 mmol/L) while clinically euvolemic; thyroid and adrenal function were normal. These findings satisfy standard diagnostic criteria for the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). An elevated ADH level (22.4 pg/mL) provided additional physiological confirmation, and the prompt fall in ADH (to 1.8 pg/mL) and normalization of serum sodium with anti‑inflammatory therapy and fluid restriction supports an ADH‑mediated, inflammation‑linked process. CNS inflammation can provoke inappropriate ADH release—possibly mediated by cytokines such as interleukin‑6—so SIADH in this context is biologically plausible. Management principles followed were conservative: fluid restriction, careful serial sodium checks, and avoidance of overly rapid correction.

A final, pragmatic element of the diagnostic reasoning here was test stewardship. Early in the admission a broad exclusionary approach was necessary, but the team limited specialty tests to those with a clear impact on immediate decisions: microbiology for treatable infections, CSF cytology if malignancy suspected, measurements to categorise hyponatraemia (TSH, cortisol), and clinical dermatologic assessment to establish BD. Tests of low sensitivity or specificity for the suspected conditions (ACE, lysozyme, sIL‑2R) were interpreted cautiously and not allowed to override the clinical synthesis. This avoided anchoring on non‑specific abnormalities and kept the working diagnosis aligned with the most coherent explanation of the total clinical picture.

Putting the pieces together: the most parsimonious diagnosis in this patient was acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease manifesting as aseptic meningitis, complicated by SIADH. This conclusion rested on three pillars—definite systemic BD by ICBD criteria, CSF evidence of CNS inflammation without microbiologic proof of infection, and biochemical confirmation of SIADH in a euvolemic patient—plus the temporal association of clinical and biochemical recovery after anti‑inflammatory treatment (colchicine) and conservative hyponatraemia management. Alternative diagnoses (occult CNS infection, sarcoidosis, lymphoma) were not completely dismissed but were rendered less likely by negative microbiology, normal imaging and non‑diagnostic serologic markers; they would require new or progressive findings to re‑emerge as leading considerations. The case illustrates the value of integrating focused laboratory and bedside assessments with clinical pattern recognition, using empiric therapy appropriately and de‑escalating when data permit, and reserving broader or invasive testing for situations in which clinical uncertainty persists.

## Management: treating acute neuro‑BD and SIADH, monitoring, and when to escalate

This patient’s management illustrates three parallel priorities in the acute setting: (1) treat immediately reversible, potentially life‑threatening causes while diagnostics are pending; (2) manage the electrolyte disturbance and its complications; and (3) choose immunomodulatory therapy for neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) appropriate to disease severity. In the case, empirical broad‑spectrum antibiotics (cefepime) and acyclovir were started while infection was being excluded; SIADH was managed conservatively with fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) and close monitoring; and colchicine monotherapy was begun once the working diagnosis of systemic BD with aseptic meningitis (acute neuro‑BD) was established. The patient improved without corticosteroids or other immunosuppression. Below I explain the reasoning behind each decision, contrast it with standard practice, and specify monitoring and triggers for escalation.

Initial empiric therapy during diagnostic uncertainty
When a patient presents with fever, headache and CSF pleocytosis, prompt empirical coverage for common and treatable CNS infections is standard because delays in therapy for bacterial meningitis or herpes encephalitis increase morbidity and mortality. Empiric regimens should be chosen for the clinical context and local microbiology (community‑acquired regimens commonly include a third‑generation cephalosporin plus vancomycin ± ampicillin for Listeria in older or immunocompromised patients; cefepime is used in some institutions for broad gram‑negative coverage). Acyclovir should be started empirically when HSV encephalitis is suspected. These agents should be discontinued once objective data lower the probability of infection: negative CSF Gram stain and cultures after an appropriate interval (typically 48–72 hours for cultures), negative highly sensitive CSF PCRs for HSV and VZV, a clinical course incompatible with bacterial infection, and an alternative non‑infectious diagnosis that explains the presentation. Exercise caution before stopping antivirals if PCR testing was performed very early, if the patient is immunocompromised, or if clinical suspicion remains high — in those situations repeat testing or continued treatment until the diagnosis is secure is reasonable.

Standard acute neuro‑BD therapy and indications to escalate
Parenchymal neuro‑BD (brainstem, hemispheric or spinal cord involvement with focal deficits) is usually treated promptly with high‑dose corticosteroids because of the risk of permanent neurologic injury. A commonly used regimen is intravenous methylprednisolone (for example, 1 g daily) for 3–5 days followed by an oral prednisone taper. Steroid‑sparing or adjunctive immunosuppressants (for example, azathioprine, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide for severe vasculitic disease, or biologics such as anti‑TNF agents for refractory cases) are added for severe, progressive, relapsing, or steroid‑dependent disease. By contrast, patients with a mild, self‑limited aseptic meningitic presentation, normal brain MRI and no focal deficits—like this patient—may occasionally be managed conservatively or with colchicine for mucocutaneous disease while observing the neurologic course. That said, guidelines and expert opinion support a low threshold to initiate high‑dose steroids when there is any neurological deterioration, development of focal deficits, MRI evidence of parenchymal or brainstem lesions, spinal cord involvement, seizures, or progressive CSF abnormalities. Early consultation with neurology and rheumatology/immunology is appropriate before committing to prolonged immunosuppression or to select second‑line agents.

Colchicine: mechanism, dosing considerations, evidence and safety
Colchicine acts by binding tubulin, impairing microtubule formation and neutrophil chemotaxis, and suppressing inflammasome activity and cytokine release (including IL‑1 and IL‑6). It is effective for many mucocutaneous and joint manifestations of BD and is widely used in that setting. Typical maintenance dosing for BD mucocutaneous disease is about 0.5 mg twice daily (total ≈1 mg/day), with lower doses (0.5 mg once daily) for elderly patients or those with significant renal or hepatic impairment; doses and adjustments should follow local formularies and patient tolerance. The major adverse effects are dose‑related gastrointestinal intolerance (diarrhea, nausea); more serious toxicities include myopathy and rhabdomyolysis—especially when combined with statins, in renal impairment, or with strong CYP3A4/P‑glycoprotein inhibitors (clarithromycin, itraconazole, cyclosporine)—and rare bone‑marrow suppression in overdose. Drug–drug interactions must be checked before starting colchicine.

Randomized data support colchicine for mucocutaneous BD manifestations, but evidence for efficacy in parenchymal neuro‑BD is sparse. Most case series and reviews recommend immunosuppression rather than colchicine alone for parenchymal CNS disease. Therefore, colchicine monotherapy may be reasonable in selected situations—mild aseptic meningitic presentations without focal deficits, normal imaging, contraindications to steroids, or when close inpatient monitoring is feasible—recognizing that spontaneous recovery is possible and that clinicians must remain ready to escalate to high‑dose steroids and additional immunosuppression if neurologic status worsens.

SIADH management: when to restrict fluids, give hypertonic saline, use salt/urea or vaptans
Management of SIADH proceeds in two linked directions: treat the underlying cause (here, neuroinflammation) and correct the hyponatraemia safely. Initial therapy for euvolemic SIADH is fluid restriction tailored to urine osmolality and patient tolerance; in practice many teams start with <800–1000 mL/day as was done for this patient. If restriction fails, the next options include increasing solute intake (oral urea or salt tablets with a loop diuretic to encourage free water excretion) or use of vasopressin‑receptor antagonists (vaptans) in the hospital; vaptans (e.g., tolvaptan) are effective but require close monitoring because of the risk of overly rapid correction and, for tolvaptan, potential hepatotoxicity with longer courses.

Hypertonic (3%) saline is indicated for severe or life‑threatening hyponatraemia with neurologic symptoms (seizure, profound somnolence, coma) or when sodium is very low and symptoms are present; small boluses are typically used to achieve an initial modest rise (for example, an initial target rise of 4–6 mmol/L to relieve cerebral edema) rather than aiming for a rapid normalization. Correction must be conservative overall: aim for a rise of no more than about 8–10 mmol/L in 24 hours (and many experts prefer ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours for patients at high risk of osmotic demyelination such as those with chronic hyponatraemia, alcoholism, malnutrition or severe hypokalemia). If overcorrection occurs, desmopressin coupled with administration of free water (D5W) can be used to safely re‑lower serum sodium.

Monitoring response and safety during therapy
Close and protocolized monitoring is essential. When hypertonic saline or vaptans are used, measure serum sodium every 2–4 hours and monitor urine output continuously; when active correction is not underway and conservative measures are used, check sodium at least every 6–12 hours until trends are clear. Record urine osmolality and urine sodium to judge the likelihood of response to fluid restriction. Perform frequent neurological examinations (hourly to several times daily as indicated) to detect new focal signs. Because severe hyponatraemia can cause rhabdomyolysis and because colchicine and statins can cause myopathy, monitor creatine kinase and urine myoglobin if patients develop myalgias, weakness, or dark urine; in this case CK was elevated initially and normalized with correction. Monitor renal and liver function tests, full blood count and inflammatory markers (CRP/ESR) regularly while treating neuro‑inflammation and when using agents with hepatic or marrow toxicity. Review medication interactions (notably CYP3A4 and P‑glycoprotein inhibitors with colchicine) and adjust doses accordingly.

Comorbidity considerations: hyperglycaemia and diabetes
Interpret serum sodium in the light of hyperglycaemia because elevated plasma glucose causes osmotic movement of water from cells to the extracellular space and lowers measured sodium. Use a sodium correction formula (a commonly used adjustment is to add ~1.6 mmol/L to measured sodium for each 100 mg/dL [≈5.6 mmol/L] rise in glucose above ~100 mg/dL) to estimate the true hypotonic component; treating hyperglycaemia will partially correct the sodium concentration and avoids unnecessary or excessive sodium repletion. Diabetes management should be optimized once the acute phase is controlled because glycaemic instability can confound sodium interpretation and complicate fluid and electrolyte management.

When to escalate and which specialists to involve
Escalate therapy and seek multidisciplinary input for any neurologic deterioration (new or worsening focal deficits, seizures, reduced consciousness), new or progressive parenchymal lesions on imaging (especially brainstem or spinal cord), persistent or worsening hyponatraemia despite conservative measures, or when considering potent or prolonged immunosuppression or vaptan therapy. Early involvement of neurology and rheumatology/immunology is advisable when neuro‑BD is suspected or prior to initiating long‑term disease‑modifying therapy. Nephrology or endocrinology should be consulted for complicated or refractory SIADH, for initiation of vaptans, or for controlled hypertonic saline protocols.

In summary, the team’s approach in this case—empiric antimicrobial/antiviral coverage while excluding infection, conservative SIADH care with fluid restriction and correction of hyperglycaemia, and initiation of colchicine for BD with very close observation—was defensible given the mild aseptic meningitic presentation and normal MRI. However, clinicians must individualize treatment, be ready to begin high‑dose corticosteroids and add steroid‑sparing immunosuppression for parenchymal or progressive neuro‑BD, and escalate SIADH therapy promptly when sodium or neurologic status requires it.

## Outcomes, prognosis, and practice points

This patient experienced a brisk clinical recovery after colchicine was started: fever and appetite improved within 24 hours, mucocutaneous lesions and neurological symptoms (headache, vertigo, hiccups) resolved over the next two weeks, and serum sodium normalized from 124 mmol/L to 136 mmol/L by day 16. Measured ADH fell in parallel (22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL), and the elevated CK (1349 U/L) returned to normal. No systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants were required; the patient was discharged after about one month and remained free of BD flares or neurologic recurrence while on colchicine for approximately four years. The temporal association between colchicine initiation, clinical improvement and biochemical recovery is notable, but causality cannot be proved from a single case.

Prognosis in acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) depends importantly on the pattern of CNS involvement. Parenchymal disease—classically brainstem or deep hemispheric lesions—carries a higher risk of recurrent attacks and permanent neurologic deficit; by contrast, non‑parenchymal presentations (including aseptic meningitis or isolated meningoencephalitis without focal lesions) often follow a more benign, sometimes self‑limited course. Because relapses are common in BD overall, long‑term follow‑up is required regardless of the initial severity. Practically, follow‑up should be individualized but include early clinical reassessment (weeks after discharge), ongoing surveillance for new neurological, ocular or vascular symptoms, and periodic review several times during the first year. Repeat MRI or CSF testing is indicated when there is new or worsening headache, emergence of focal deficits, cognitive decline, seizures, failure to improve on therapy, or concern for an alternative diagnosis (infection, malignancy). In patients with parenchymal lesions at baseline, interval imaging to document evolution or resolution is reasonable; in those with initially normal imaging, re‑imaging is prompted by persistent or new symptoms rather than routine scheduling.

Several clinical pearls emerge from this case. First, a normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro‑BD early in the disease course—diagnosis may rest on clinical features and supportive CSF findings (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein) combined with characteristic mucocutaneous signs. Second, hyponatraemia in a patient with BD and CNS symptoms should prompt evaluation for SIADH: confirm euvolemic hypotonic hyponatraemia (low plasma osmolality), inappropriately concentrated urine (high urine osmolality and urine sodium), and exclude adrenal and thyroid dysfunction. Inflammatory CNS disease can drive ADH secretion (possible cytokine‑mediated mechanisms), so correction focuses on treating the underlying CNS process in addition to conventional SIADH measures—initial conservative management with fluid restriction and careful monitoring was effective here. Third, laboratory or genetic markers such as HLA‑B51 are supportive but not diagnostic and should not replace careful clinical assessment; mucocutaneous findings (including genital ulcers) can appear after initial presentation, so repeated physical examinations are important. Fourth, apparently unrelated symptoms—persistent hiccups or an elevated CK suggesting rhabdomyolysis—may be clinical clues to significant hyponatraemia and merit prompt electrolyte evaluation. Finally, although colchicine is a mainstay for mucocutaneous BD, it is not the established standard for parenchymal neuro‑BD; its apparent efficacy here in an aseptic meningoencephalitic presentation is noteworthy but should be interpreted cautiously.

Important knowledge gaps remain. The mechanistic link between neuroinflammation and dysregulated ADH secretion (for example an IL‑6 → ADH pathway) is plausible but not proven; prospective studies measuring cytokines, ADH and clinical course would help clarify causality. Evidence is also lacking to guide which patients with early neuro‑BD can safely be managed without systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants, and whether colchicine can be a steroid‑sparing strategy in selected non‑parenchymal presentations. Multi‑centre registries and controlled trials that capture presentation subtype, biomarkers, treatment strategies and long‑term neurologic outcomes are needed to refine management algorithms and to define which therapies best prevent relapse and disability.

Takeaway: in patients with newly identified BD who present with headache, fever or other CNS symptoms, screen for hyponatraemia and consider SIADH as a manifestation of CNS inflammation. Do not rely solely on a normal early MRI or on HLA‑B51 to exclude neuro‑BD. Manage SIADH conservatively while treating the underlying neuroinflammatory disorder, follow patients closely for relapse or new organ involvement, and recognize that single cases suggesting benefit from colchicine alone require confirmation in larger series before changing standard practice.
