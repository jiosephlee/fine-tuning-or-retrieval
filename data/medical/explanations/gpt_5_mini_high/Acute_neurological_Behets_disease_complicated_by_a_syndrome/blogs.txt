Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# MRI-Negative Neuro-Behçet’s: Don’t Let a Normal Scan Fool You

This case surprised a lot of teams: an otherwise-fit man in his 60s presenting with fever, headache, hiccups and mucocutaneous lesions (oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rash, then a scrotal ulcer) who had marked hyponatraemia. Brain MRI was unremarkable, but lumbar puncture showed aseptic meningitis (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein). He met the clinical criteria for definite Behçet’s disease and was diagnosed with acute neuro-Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) presenting as meningitis. SIADH explained the hyponatraemia; conservative management (fluid restriction) plus colchicine alone (no corticosteroids or other immunosuppression) led to rapid clinical recovery and normalization of sodium over two weeks.

Why this matters: neuro‑BD usually conjures images of brainstem/parenchymal lesions on MRI and aggressive immunotherapy. But early neuro‑BD can present as aseptic meningitis with a normal MRI. Missing it — or assuming “the MRI is clean, so it’s not neuro‑BD” — is an easy trap. Add hyponatraemia/SIADH to the mix and the case becomes a reminder that CNS inflammation need not leave an early radiographic footprint.

## The clinical pattern to watch for
Think neuro‑BD when several pieces line up:
- Recent or concurrent mucocutaneous findings consistent with Behçet’s (recurrent oral ulcers, genital ulcers, folliculitis-like lesions, uveitis) — ICBD score ≥4 = definite BD.
- New CNS symptoms: headache, fever, behavioural change, persistent hiccups, vertigo, or more overt meningoencephalitic signs.
- CSF consistent with aseptic meningitis: lymphocytic pleocytosis (mild in early disease), elevated protein, possibly mildly increased opening pressure.
- Systemic inflammation (high CRP/ESR) and exclusion of infectious, neoplastic and other autoimmune causes.
- Hyponatraemia with biochemical picture of SIADH (low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine, euvolemia, normal thyroid/adrenal function).

The presence of these features should raise suspicion even when MRI is normal.

## How to make the call when imaging is unrevealing — a practical, stepwise approach
Step 1 — Suspect it. Ask specifically about oral/genital ulcers, skin lesions and eye symptoms in any patient with aseptic meningitis or unexplained CNS symptoms. A normal MRI does not rule out early neuro‑BD.

Step 2 — Stabilize and exclude infection. In febrile patients or when encephalitis/meningitis is in your differential, collect blood cultures and do an LP before antibiotics if safe. Start empiric antimicrobials/acyclovir based on the clinical picture and discontinue when negative microbiology/PCR excludes pathogens.

Step 3 — Do the right CSF studies. Opening pressure, cell count with differential, protein, glucose, bacterial/fungal cultures, PCRs for HSV/VZV/enterovirus and TB if indicated, and CSF cytology when malignancy is a concern. Neuro‑BD often gives mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein — enough to support CNS inflammation even when MRI is blind.

Step 4 — Targeted imaging, but don’t over-rely on it. Obtain a contrast-enhanced brain MRI with brainstem- and hypothalamic-focused sequences (FLAIR/T2, DWI, gadolinium T1). If MRI is normal and the clinical picture strongly suggests neuro‑BD, proceed with the rest of the diagnostic workup — and plan for early repeat imaging if things don’t settle.

Step 5 — Exclude mimics. Use clinical judgment to test for TB, fungal infections, CNS lymphoma (CSF cytology, PET/CT as appropriate), sarcoidosis (ACE/lysozyme only if clinically suspected), and endocrine causes of hyponatraemia (TSH, cortisol). HLA‑B51 can support the diagnosis but is neither necessary nor definitive.

Step 6 — Decide on therapy and monitoring. If the patient has only aseptic meningitis with a stable exam and no red flags, close observation plus colchicine (for mucocutaneous BD) and supportive care may be reasonable — with very close monitoring. If there are focal deficits, encephalopathy, seizures, rapid progression, parenchymal MRI lesions, severe SIADH with neurological symptoms, or other “high-risk” features, start early immunomodulation (high‑dose corticosteroids ± steroid-sparing agents) and involve neuroimmunology/rheumatology.

Step 7 — Reassess frequently. Repeat CSF/imaging and escalate therapy if the clinical course worsens or fails to improve. Arrange prompt outpatient follow-up and secondary prevention planning.

## When and how often to repeat MRI
MRI-negative early disease may “light up” later. Practical guidance:
- Repeat MRI within 7–14 days if the diagnosis is uncertain and the patient remains symptomatic or shows any worsening.
- Repeat sooner (within 24–72 hours) if new focal deficits, seizure, reduced consciousness or other red flags develop.
- When repeating, ensure high-quality contrast-enhanced imaging with thin cuts through the brainstem and hypothalamus (sites classically involved in BD).
- Consider spinal imaging if symptoms/localizing signs suggest myelitis.

## Risk‑stratify: who needs immediate immunomodulation?
Absolute decisions should be individualized, but these are useful heuristics.

Features that push toward early aggressive therapy (corticosteroids ± additional immunosuppression):
- Focal neurological deficits or brainstem signs.
- Altered level of consciousness, seizures, rapidly progressive course.
- Parenchymal lesions on MRI (especially brainstem, basal ganglia).
- Severe CSF inflammation or evidence of ongoing, progressive CNS dysfunction.
- Recurrent or relapsing attacks, or other major organ involvement.

Features that may allow cautious observation with close monitoring (and consider colchicine/supportive care):
- Isolated aseptic meningitis presentation (headache/fever) with normal MRI.
- Mild CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis and only modest systemic inflammation.
- Stable, improving clinical course after exclusion of infection.
Note: colchicine alone is not the standard for parenchymal neuro‑BD — the case here did well without steroids, but that may represent a milder phenotype or spontaneous resolution. Don’t delay steroids when red flags are present.

## SIADH in neuro‑Behçet’s — don’t miss it
SIADH can be triggered by CNS inflammation (and cytokines such as IL‑6), and neuro‑BD is not exempt. In the case described:
- Serum sodium was 124 mmol/L with low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), very concentrated urine (900 mOsm/kg) and urine sodium 88 mmol/L; TSH and morning cortisol were normal — consistent with SIADH.
- ADH was measured and elevated, but routine diagnosis relies on the biochemical pattern, not ADH assays.

Management principles:
- Treat the underlying CNS inflammation.
- Fluid restriction is first‑line for mild–moderate SIADH (in the case, restriction <800 mL/day was effective).
- For severe symptomatic hyponatraemia (seizures, obtundation), give hypertonic saline and monitor sodium closely.
- Avoid overly rapid correction (general target: avoid increasing serum Na by >8–10 mmol/L in 24 hours in chronic hyponatraemia; be even more conservative in high-risk patients).
- Tolvaptan and other vaptans are niche tools and require specialist input.

Watch for clinical clues of significant hyponatraemia: persistent hiccups, nausea, confusion — and sometimes rhabdomyolysis (CK was elevated in this patient), which can be an underappreciated complication.

## Practical pearls from the case
- A normal MRI early in the course does not exclude neuro‑BD. CSF is your friend.
- Look hard for mucocutaneous clues — they may be the diagnostic anchor.
- Start empiric antimicrobials/acyclovir when meningitis/encephalitis is possible, but narrow when tests are negative.
- Measure TSH and morning cortisol when evaluating hyponatraemia to exclude other causes before attributing to SIADH.
- Hiccups and unexplained rhabdomyolysis may be clues to severe hyponatraemia.
- Colchicine can help mucocutaneous disease and may have anti‑cytokine effects, but it is not a substitute for immunosuppression in fulminant parenchymal neuro‑BD.

## Closing thought
Neuro‑Behçet’s can masquerade as “just” aseptic meningitis with a blank scan. If the clinical picture points toward Behçet’s — mucocutaneous signs, CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis, systemic inflammation and exclusion of infection — treat the patient, not the MRI. Involve neurology/rheumatology early, watch sodium closely, and have a low threshold to repeat imaging and escalate therapy if the course moves in the wrong direction.

Take-home messages
- Don’t rule out neuro‑Behçet’s just because MRI is normal early on — CSF + mucocutaneous criteria + exclusion of infection/malignancy are key.
- In suspected neuro‑BD with meningitic presentation, perform LP, start empiric antimicrobials if indicated, and consider colchicine for mucocutaneous disease while monitoring closely.
- Repeat MRI in 7–14 days (or sooner with deterioration); use contrast and brainstem/hypothalamic sequences.
- Escalate to high‑dose steroids ± steroid‑sparing agents for focal/parenchymal disease, encephalopathy, seizures, rapid progression or severe SIADH with neurological symptoms.
- Assess and manage SIADH promptly (fluid restriction, treat underlying inflammation, avoid rapid sodium correction); hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be red flags.



# Hyponatremia as a Signal: SIADH in Neuroinflammation and Behçet’s

Why this vignette jumps off the page
A man in his 60s arrives with fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions and persistent hiccups. He has marked hyponatraemia (Na 124 mmol/L), low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), very concentrated urine (Uosm 900 mOsm/kg) and high urine sodium (88 mmol/L). CSF shows aseptic meningitis (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein) and MRI is unrevealing. Genital ulcers appear on day 5 and he scores “definite” Behçet’s by clinical criteria. The treating team diagnoses SIADH complicating acute neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD). Colchicine monotherapy was started and—remarkably—mucocutaneous symptoms, neurologic complaints and the hyponatraemia all improved without steroids; sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L by hospital day 16.

That constellation is useful to study because: (1) neuro‑BD may present with aseptic meningitis and a normal early MRI, (2) CNS inflammation can produce SIADH (often via cytokine‑driven ADH release), and (3) the endocrine disturbance may be the most obvious abnormality at presentation — a real diagnostic clue rather than an incidental laboratory finding.

Short physiology refresher — how CNS inflammation drives SIADH
Antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin) is released in response to osmotic and non‑osmotic triggers. CNS inflammation (infection, haemorrhage, tumour, autoimmune meningoencephalitis) can trigger non‑osmotic ADH release through pain, nausea, direct hypothalamic irritation and cytokine signalling. Interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) is a key cytokine often elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions and has been shown to stimulate vasopressin release. In neuro‑Behçet’s, CSF IL‑6 elevations have been reported, offering a biologically plausible link between CNS inflammation and SIADH. (In this case IL‑6 was not measured; the association is inferential but consistent with the temporal course.)

A practical bedside framework to diagnose SIADH
At the bedside you want a short, sequential checklist — is this true hypotonic hyponatraemia, and is the kidney inappropriately conserving water?

1. Confirm true hypotonic hyponatraemia
   - Serum sodium low and measured serum osmolality low (the case: Na 124 mmol/L, Osm 267 mOsm/kg → hypotonic). Important caveat: hyperglycaemia causes translocational hyponatraemia, so correct Na if glucose is very high (this patient had glucose 14.2 mmol/L, but measured serum osmolality was still low, so the hyponatraemia was real).

2. Clinical volume assessment
   - SIADH patients are usually clinically euvolemic (no oedema, no orthostatic hypotension). The case was clinically euvolemic.

3. Urine studies to confirm inappropriate ADH effect
   - Urine osmolality not maximally dilute (typically >100 mOsm/kg and often >300 mOsm/kg in SIADH). This patient’s Uosm was 900 mOsm/kg — very concentrated.
   - Urine sodium usually >30 mmol/L (case: 88 mmol/L) when dietary sodium intake is normal.

4. Exclude mimics
   - Hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, renal failure and diuretic use can mimic SIADH. Check TSH and cortisol (this patient had normal TSH and a clearly non‑low cortisol), review meds (no offending drugs), and confirm normal renal function.

5. ADH (vasopressin) assay?
   - Rarely required. Assays are not standardized and do not usually change management. In this report ADH was measured (22.4 pg/mL and later fell to 1.8 pg/mL), but that’s more a confirmatory/academic data point than a practical requirement.

Management — when to be conservative and when to escalate
Think first about severity of symptoms and chronicity. The general approach:

- Severe or life‑threatening neurologic symptoms (seizures, obtundation, severe encephalopathy): treat emergently with hypertonic (3%) saline. Use 100 mL boluses (repeat as needed) to achieve a prompt small rise (aim +4–6 mmol/L quickly to relieve symptoms) and then slow down.

- Mild–moderate or asymptomatic SIADH: start with conservative measures.
  - Fluid restriction is first‑line (this case used <800 mL/day and sodium normalized over ~2 weeks). Success requires adherence; inpatient monitoring makes it safer.
  - Increase solute intake if restriction is ineffective (oral urea, salt tablets — local practice varies).
  - Loop diuretics (with saline) can be used to increase free‑water excretion in selected patients.

- Tolvaptan (oral V2 receptor antagonist):
  - Effective in raising serum sodium in euvolemic/hypervolemic hyponatraemia but carries risk of overly rapid correction and hepatotoxicity. Reserve for specialist‑supervised cases when fluid restriction and conservative measures fail, and only with close inpatient monitoring of sodium.

- Avoid rapid correction
  - Target safe correction limits: generally ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours (some authorities recommend ≤6–8 mmol/L in high‑risk patients) and ≤18 mmol/L in 48 hours. Check sodium every 2–4 hours during active correction. If overcorrection occurs, consider re‑lowering serum sodium (desmopressin + free water) to reduce risk of osmotic demyelination.

Tie back to the Behçet’s case — what to watch for and pitfalls
- Neuro‑Behçet’s can present early with aseptic meningitis and a normal MRI. Don’t exclude CNS involvement simply because imaging is unrevealing.
- Hyponatraemia was the most striking objective abnormality and provided an early clue to CNS dysfunction. Non‑specific symptoms such as hiccups and generalized malaise were important adjuncts; hiccups can be a subtle sign of brainstem irritation and have been reported in SIADH/CNS inflammation.
- Complications of hyponatraemia may be unexpected: the patient had an elevated CK (1349 U/L), consistent with rhabdomyolysis sometimes seen with severe hyponatraemia — check CK if muscle pain, weakness or very low sodium.
- Drug causes matter: SIADH has been reported in BD patients on cyclophosphamide, SSRIs, anticonvulsants and others. This patient had no provoking medication, strengthening the link to CNS inflammation.
- Treat the brain. Controlling the underlying neuroinflammation is central; in neuro‑BD steroids and other immunosuppressants are classically used. This case is notable because colchicine alone coincided with rapid clinical improvement and normalization of sodium — an atypical but instructive outcome. Don’t assume colchicine will replace steroids/immunosuppression for typical neuro‑BD.

When to involve colleagues
- Endocrinology/nephrology: severe hyponatraemia, rapid or difficult corrections, consideration of vaptans, or diagnostic uncertainty.
- Neurology/rheumatology: suspected neuro‑Behçet’s or other neuroinflammatory causes that will require immunomodulatory therapy.

Clinical pearls / take‑home points
- SIADH is a common endocrine footprint of CNS inflammation — hyponatraemia in a patient with headache/fever and mucocutaneous signs should raise the possibility of neuro‑Behçet’s when the clinical picture fits.
- Diagnose SIADH by confirming hypotonic hyponatraemia, clinically euvolaemic state, inappropriately concentrated urine (Uosm usually >300 mOsm/kg) with urine Na >30 mmol/L, and exclusion of hypothyroidism/adrenal insufficiency; ADH assays are rarely required.
- At the bedside: check serum osmolality, urine osmolality and urine sodium early; review medications and volume status; correct sodium for marked hyperglycaemia if present.
- Start with fluid restriction for mild/asymptomatic SIADH (<800–1000 mL/day). Reserve hypertonic saline for severe neurologic symptoms and vaptans for specialist‑managed, refractory cases — always monitor sodium closely.
- Avoid overly rapid correction (generally ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours); have a plan to relower sodium (desmopressin + free water) if overcorrection occurs.
- Treat the underlying CNS problem — in neuro‑BD that usually means steroids/immunosuppression; this case’s colchicine response is interesting but atypical and should not change usual practice without specialist input.

Bottom line: hyponatraemia can be more than a lab oddity — in the right clinical context it’s a red flag for CNS disease. Recognize SIADH quickly, manage safely, and treat the underlying neuroinflammation.



# Hiccups and Rhabdo from Salt Trouble: The Hyponatremia Clues You’ll Miss if You Don’t Look

## Why this case grabbed my attention
A man in his 60s presents with fever, headache and mucocutaneous ulcers and ends up being diagnosed with Behçet’s disease and aseptic meningitis. That’s already an eyebrow-raiser. What made the case stick with me were the “small” clues that should have pushed sodium to the top of the problem list: persistent hiccups and an elevated CK (1,349 U/L). Labs showed a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L, a low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg and urine sodium 88 mmol/L — a picture consistent with SIADH. The team treated conservatively (fluid restriction <800 mL/day) while treating the underlying inflammatory disease with colchicine; the patient’s symptoms, hyponatraemia and CK all normalized over the next two weeks, and ADH fell from 22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL. No steroids, hypertonic saline or vaptans were required.

This case is a great reminder that quiet symptoms — hiccups, myalgias, malaise — can be sentinel clues to clinically important hyponatraemia, and that hyponatraemia itself can cause downstream complications (rhabdomyolysis) that change management priorities.

## Persistent hiccups: an under‑appreciated red flag
Hiccups are usually benign. Persistent hiccups (days) are not. They can be triggered by irritation of central structures in the brainstem or by meningeal/CNS inflammation, and they turn up in case series of meningitis, encephalitis and other neuroinflammatory states. In this patient the hiccups appeared alongside headache and aseptic meningitis, and they improved as the inflammatory process and hyponatraemia resolved.

The practical take-home: when hiccups are persistent and unexplained, check a basic metabolic panel — don’t forget sodium. If the patient has any CNS symptoms (headache, altered behaviour, neck pain), think neuro causes that could be driving inappropriate ADH release.

## Rhabdomyolysis from low salt — when to check CK
Hyponatraemia can cause muscle cell swelling and injury, producing CK elevations and frank rhabdomyolysis. The mechanism is cellular swelling and membrane dysfunction from hypotonic extracellular fluid; clinically this can be worsened by seizures or prolonged immobility. In this case CK was 1,349 U/L on presentation, with normal renal function, and normalized as sodium corrected — consistent with hyponatraemia-associated muscle injury rather than a primary myopathy or traumatic cause.

When should you check CK in hyponatraemia? I consider CK when:
- Na is very low (for many, <120 mmol/L) or falling fast,
- the patient has muscle pain, weakness or dark urine,
- there was a seizure, prolonged immobilization, or a history of intoxication,
- or when empiric evaluation of systemic symptoms (fever, myalgias) includes CK anyway.

If CK is markedly elevated or there is evidence of myoglobinuria/AKI, treatment priorities shift: preventing renal injury via judicious isotonic fluids becomes urgent — and that may conflict with fluid restriction for SIADH, so early nephrology input is wise.

## Correct the sodium for glucose — every time
Hyperglycaemia causes a translocational hyponatraemia (osmotic water shift from cells to ECF). Always correct sodium for hyperglycaemia to avoid misclassification. The common correction formula is: add ~1.6 mEq/L to measured sodium for every 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) of glucose above 100 mg/dL. Some use 2.4 mEq/L per 100 mg/dL for very high glucose states; both are approximations.

Apply it to this patient: glucose 14.2 mmol/L × 18 = ~256 mg/dL. Glucose is 156 mg/dL above 100. Using 1.6/mEq per 100 mg/dL: correction ≈ 1.6 × 1.56 ≈ +2.5 → corrected Na ≈ 126–127 mmol/L. Even after correction, sodium remains low. Importantly, this patient had a low measured serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), confirming true hypotonic hyponatraemia rather than hypertonic (glucose‑driven) hyponatraemia.

So: correct for glucose, but don’t stop there — check serum osmolality. If osmolality is low, you’re dealing with true hypotonic hyponatraemia that needs a specific workup.

## Diagnosing SIADH — the bedside confirmation
SIADH remains a diagnosis made from a cluster of findings, not a single test. The classic elements are:
- Hypotonic hyponatraemia (low serum osmolality),
- Inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality >100–300 mOsm/kg; in this case 900 mOsm/kg),
- Elevated urine sodium (>30–40 mmol/L) with normal salt intake (88 mmol/L here),
- Clinical euvolemia,
- Normal thyroid and adrenal function (this patient had TSH and cortisol within acceptable ranges),
- No recent diuretic use or other clear explainers.

Measuring ADH is not required for diagnosis and is often unhelpful in real time, but in this case an elevated ADH supported the pathophysiology. Given the concurrent aseptic meningitis/meningoencephalitis and marked systemic inflammation (CRP 180 mg/L), neuroinflammation was a plausible SIADH trigger. IL‑6–driven ADH release is a biologically plausible mechanism in neuroinflammatory states like neuro‑Behçet’s.

## Management — how aggressively (or not) to correct and where desmopressin fits
Management decisions depend on severity and acuity.

If the patient has severe neurologic symptoms (seizure, coma, severe obtundation), treat immediately with hypertonic saline (institutional protocol) and monitor closely. This case did not have severe neuro compromise, and conservative management with fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) plus treatment of the underlying inflammatory condition was sufficient; sodium rose slowly and safely from 124 to 136 mmol/L over ~2 weeks.

Targets matter: to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS), limit correction. Reasonable, commonly used targets are <8 mmol/L increase in the first 24 hours and <18 mmol/L in 48 hours for most patients. For those at high risk of ODS (Na very low, malnourished, alcoholic, hypokalaemic, chronic hyponatraemia), tighter goals (4–6 mmol/L/day) are prudent.

The desmopressin clamp is your tool to prevent or treat rapid overcorrection caused by spontaneous water diuresis. There are two uses:
- Prophylactic clamp (planned): give desmopressin (commonly 1–2 μg IV/SC; institutional dosing varies) to halt free‑water diuresis while you correct sodium at a controlled rate with hypertonic saline. Check sodium every 2 hours.
- Rescue clamp: if sodium has overcorrected (e.g., >8 mEq/L in 24 h or especially >10–12 in 24 h), give desmopressin and administer hypotonic fluid (D5W) to re‑lower the sodium to safe levels.

Dosing practices vary — check local protocols and pharmacy. The critical point is: if the patient is at risk of brisk water diuresis, anticipate it and use desmopressin to avoid an uncontrolled leap in sodium.

## The balancing act: rhabdo versus SIADH
This case neatly illustrates a therapeutic tension. SIADH wants fluid restriction; rhabdomyolysis wants aggressive isotonic fluids to prevent myoglobinuric AKI. The approach is pragmatic: severity drives therapy. Mild CK elevations with preserved renal function can be watched with conservative hyponatraemia management and serial monitoring (BMP, CK, urine). Significant rhabdo (very high CK, dark urine, rising creatinine) requires prioritizing renal protection — give isotonic fluids, monitor sodium closely, and use desmopressin (clamp) and frequent sodium checks to avoid overcorrection.

## How the team did it (and what I’d remember)
They recognized SIADH in the setting of neuro‑Behçet’s (aseptic meningitis), treated the underlying inflammatory disease with colchicine, enforced fluid restriction, monitored sodium and CK, and allowed a slow, safe correction. Everything resolved without steroids, hypertonic saline or vaptans. The temporal association between treating neuroinflammation and falling ADH/sodium normalization is a useful clinical reminder: fix the trigger when you can.

## Quick bedside checklist and take‑home pearls
- Always check a sodium when hiccups are persistent or unexplained, especially with CNS symptoms.  
- Correct serum sodium for hyperglycaemia before classifying hyponatraemia (add ~1.6 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL glucose >100 mg/dL; this patient’s Na 124 → ~126–128 when corrected).  
- Confirm hypotonic hyponatraemia with measured serum osmolality — low osmolality = true hyponatraemia.  
- Diagnose SIADH by the cluster: hypotonic hyponatraemia, inappropriately concentrated urine (high urine Osm), urine Na >30–40 mmol/L, euvolaemia, normal adrenal/thyroid function, and no diuretics.  
- Consider neuroinflammatory causes (meningitis, encephalitis, neuro‑Behçet’s) as triggers for SIADH; IL‑6 mediated ADH release is a plausible mechanism.  
- Check CK in severe hyponatraemia (<120), muscle symptoms, seizures, dark urine or prolonged immobility — hyponatraemia can cause rhabdomyolysis.  
- Manage asymptomatic/moderate SIADH with fluid restriction and treat the underlying cause; reserve hypertonic saline for severe neurologic symptoms.  
- Safe correction targets: generally ≤8 mmol/L in 24 h (≤18 mmol/L in 48 h); tighter (4–6 mmol/L/day) for high‑risk patients to avoid ODS.  
- Use desmopressin (the “clamp”) prophylactically or as rescue when there’s risk of rapid water diuresis or overcorrection — check local dosing and monitor sodium at least every 2 hours during active management.  
- When rhabdomyolysis and SIADH collide, prioritize renal protection for significant rhabdo (isotonic fluids) and use desmopressin and close monitoring to manage the sodium safely; early nephrology consult is helpful.

If you take one practical habit from this case: when hiccups are persistent and the patient looks off, check a BMP. A single sodium can prevent a cascade.



# Colchicine-Only for Acute Neuro-Behçet’s? Steroid-Sparing Lessons from an N-of-1

## The twist
Conventional teaching: acute neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) — especially meningoencephalitis or parenchymal brain disease — gets high‑dose corticosteroids ± long‑term immunosuppression. This case report gives us a curveball: an Asian man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, mucocutaneous Behçet’s stigmata and aseptic meningitis, had a normal brain MRI, and developed SIADH with symptomatic hyponatraemia. Instead of steroids, the team used colchicine monotherapy (plus conservative SIADH management) and the patient improved quickly — fever resolved the day after colchicine, neurologic complaints and mucocutaneous lesions settled over 2 weeks, sodium normalized by day 16, and colchicine was continued long‑term with no relapse over ~4 years.

Why is that interesting? Because neuro‑BD is usually treated aggressively with steroids (and often immunosuppressants), and colchicine is typically considered ineffective for neuro‑BD in systematic reviews. This single patient recovered without steroids — offering a practical stimulus to think about when a cautious, steroid‑sparing approach might be reasonable.

## The case in one paragraph
A man in his 60s with untreated type 2 diabetes presented with fever, headache, hiccups, arthralgia and oral aphthae. Exam later revealed folliculitis‑like rash and a painful genital ulcer. Labs: Na 124 mmol/L, CK 1,349 U/L, CRP 180 mg/L. Endocrine workup supported SIADH (low serum osmolality, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine Na 88 mmol/L, ADH elevated at 22.4 pg/mL). Brain MRI was normal. CSF showed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) and raised protein — aseptic meningitis. Infectious tests were negative. A diagnosis of definite Behçet’s and acute neuro‑BD was made. Colchicine was started on day 8; antibiotics/acyclovir were stopped after negative tests. Fever resolved the next day; neurologic symptoms and hyponatraemia improved over 2 weeks (Na 136 mmol/L on day 16, ADH fell to 1.8 pg/mL). No corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants were used; the patient did well long term.

## Why this matters (and why you shouldn’t change practice based on one case)
- Neuro‑BD is heterogeneous. Parenchymal brain disease (brainstem, basal ganglia) has higher morbidity and generally needs aggressive immunosuppression. Acute aseptic meningitis‑predominant presentations, especially early and MRI‑negative, can be more self‑limited.
- Colchicine is established for mucocutaneous Behçet’s but historically has not been shown to be effective for neuro‑BD in systematic reviews. That said, the drug has anti‑inflammatory effects (neutrophil inhibition, decreased IL‑1/IL‑6) and might plausibly help in milder neuroinflammatory phenotypes.
- This report is an N‑of‑1. It does not establish colchicine as an evidence‑based replacement for steroids in neuro‑BD, but it does show a scenario where a steroid‑sparing approach worked — and might be appealing when steroids are high risk (uncontrolled diabetes, active infection risk, etc.).

## When a steroid‑sparing, colchicine‑first approach might be reasonable (candidate features)
Consider this only in a tightly monitored, inpatient setting and only when all the following are present:
- Mild neurologic phenotype: headache, meningitis‑like symptoms, preserved mentation, no focal deficits, no seizures.
- MRI brain without parenchymal lesions (no brainstem/hypothalamic/parenchymal signal).
- CSF consistent with aseptic meningitis (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, modest protein elevation) and infectious causes have been reasonably excluded (PCRs/cultures pending but empiric antivirals/antibiotics started).
- Behçet’s diagnosis clear from mucocutaneous findings (here, oral + genital aphthae and folliculitis‑like rash).
- Comorbidities that increase steroid risk (e.g., poorly controlled diabetes) OR patient preference after informed discussion.
- Ability to keep the patient hospitalized for close neurological and biochemical monitoring and to rapidly escalate therapy if they deteriorate.

## A cautious steroid‑sparing pathway (practical steps)
1. Initial management
   - Do not reflexively withhold empiric antimicrobials/acyclovir while awaiting CSF/PCR: exclude infection first. In the reported case cefepime and acyclovir were started and later stopped.
   - Baseline labs: serum Na, osmolality, urine osmolality and urine Na, renal and liver function, CK, CRP, glucose/HbA1c.
   - MRI brain with contrast and ophthalmology exam to look for uveitis.
2. SIADH management (if present)
   - Fluid restriction (the team used <800 mL/day). Monitor Na closely (at least daily; more frequently if Na <125 or if symptomatic).
   - Avoid rapid correction. Reserve hypertonic saline or vaptans for severe/symptomatic hyponatraemia per usual protocols.
3. Start colchicine (used in this case) while continuing close observation. Adjust dose for renal/hepatic impairment and be mindful of drug interactions (CYP3A4 inhibitors, statins).
4. Monitoring while on colchicine‑first strategy
   - Frequent neuro exams (q4–6 h initially), daily vitals.
   - Serum sodium at least daily until stable; CK and renal function every 48–72 h initially.
   - Repeat MRI/LP if clinical deterioration.
5. Clear escalation triggers — start steroids promptly if any of the following:
   - New or worsening focal neurologic deficit, altered mental status, or seizures.
   - MRI showing parenchymal lesion (especially brainstem/hypothalamus) or new mass‑effect lesion.
   - Worsening or refractory CSF inflammation by repeat testing with clinical decline.
   - Failure to improve or clinical deterioration within 48–72 hours of colchicine start.
   - Severe, symptomatic hyponatraemia (seizures, obtundation) or sodium falling despite conservative measures.
6. If escalation required
   - Typical practice: high‑dose IV corticosteroids (e.g., IV methylprednisolone pulses) then oral taper; add steroid‑sparing immunosuppressants (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, anti‑TNF agents) for severe/parenchymal disease or recurrent course. Use established neuro‑Behçet’s protocols and specialist input.

## SIADH and neuro‑BD — a reminder
CNS inflammation can drive ADH release (likely cytokine mediated — IL‑6 has been implicated). In this case ADH was high (22.4 pg/mL) with a urine osmolality of 900 mOsm/kg and urine Na 88 mmol/L; treating the underlying inflammatory process (colchicine) and fluid restriction coincided with normalization of ADH and Na. Watch for subtle clues of hyponatraemia — hiccups and rhabdomyolysis (CK 1,349 U/L in this patient) were present here and may be underappreciated indicators.

## Limitations — and the bottom line
This is an instructive single case. It shows that:
- Early, MRI‑negative, meningitic presentations of neuro‑BD can be self‑limited.
- In patients where steroids are risky, a monitored colchicine‑first approach (with infection adequately excluded and with a low threshold to escalate) is a reasonable, pragmatic consideration.
But it should not replace standard care for patients with parenchymal neuro‑BD or those with clear neurologic compromise — those patients still need prompt high‑dose steroids and specialist immunosuppression.

## Take‑home bullets
- Acute neuro‑Behçet’s is heterogeneous: MRI‑negative aseptic meningitis can be milder and occasionally self‑limited; parenchymal brain disease warrants aggressive immunosuppression.
- Colchicine monotherapy was associated with rapid clinical and biochemical recovery in this single case — useful as hypothesis‑generating, not practice‑changing.
- Consider a steroid‑sparing, colchicine‑first pathway only for carefully selected patients (mild symptoms, normal MRI, infection reasonably excluded, ability for close inpatient monitoring) and with explicit escalation triggers.
- Monitor frequently: neuro checks q4–6 h initially, serum Na daily (more if severe), CK/renal/hepatic tests, and repeat imaging/LP if clinical status changes.
- SIADH is a recognized complication of CNS inflammation; manage with fluid restriction and treat the underlying cause; be alert for hiccups, rhabdomyolysis and symptomatic hyponatraemia.
- If any sign of neurological deterioration, new focal deficits, parenchymal lesions on MRI, seizures, or refractory hyponatraemia — escalate promptly to high‑dose steroids and involve neuro/rheumatology.

Clinical bottom line: this N‑of‑1 shows colchicine can work in an early, mild neuro‑Behçet’s phenotype — but only use a steroid‑sparing strategy with close monitoring and a low threshold to escalate.



# Stop Shotgunning the Workup: Use Behçet’s Criteria and Pretest Probability

## Why this case grabbed my attention
A man in his 60s walks into the ED with fever, headache and a handful of non-specific findings — oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like rashes and later a painful genital ulcer. He’s hyponatraemic (Na 124 mmol/L) with headache and vertigo, MRI is normal, lumbar puncture shows a mild aseptic meningitis, and endocrine testing meets criteria for SIADH. The team ordered ACE and soluble IL‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) as broad screens; ACE was normal and sIL‑2R was mildly elevated — neither helped. Ultimately the patient met the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) and was diagnosed with concurrent systemic Behçet’s disease and acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD). Colchicine alone (no steroids) coincided with resolution of mucocutaneous disease, neurological symptoms and hyponatraemia.

This vignette highlights two common traps: 1) reflexively ordering low‑yield “omnibus” tests (ACE, sIL‑2R, broad autoimmune panels) without clinical signals, and 2) over‑relying on imaging to exclude neuro‑inflammatory disease. If you anchor your workup on clinical probability — the ICBD and focused bedside exam — you’ll get faster to the right questions, avoid noisy results that spawn more tests, and still treat time‑sensitive infectious mimics without delay.

## The case in a paragraph
Male in his 60s with 3 days of fever, headache, malaise and persistent hiccups. Exam: oral aphthae and folliculitis‑like rashes; a scrotal ulcer appeared on day 5. Labs: Na 124 mmol/L, CRP 180 mg/L, ESR 70, CK 1,349 U/L, glucose 14.2 mmol/L. Endocrine: TSH/T4 normal, cortisol adequate; serum osmolality low (267 mOsm/kg), urine osmolality high (900 mOsm/kg), urine Na 88 mmol/L, ADH elevated (22.4 pg/mL) → SIADH. Neuro: MRI normal; CSF LP with opening pressure 17 cm H₂O, protein 0.97 g/L, 11 lymphocytes/µL; CSF bacterial/fungal/HSV/VZV testing negative. ACE and lysozyme normal, sIL‑2R mildly up (704 U/mL); HLA‑B51 positive. Empiric cefepime started day 1, empiric acyclovir day 3 (stopped after negative viral testing), colchicine began day 8, symptoms improved rapidly and Na normalized to 136 mmol/L by day 16. No systemic steroids used; discharged after 1 month, colchicine continued long term with no recurrence.

## The common ED impulse — and why it hurts
When fever, headache and hyponatraemia coexist you must think about CNS infection, autoimmune CNS disease, malignancy and drug effects. That diagnostic breadth drives the temptation to “cast a wide net” with labs like ACE, lysozyme, sIL‑2R, broad autoantibody panels and even HLA typing. The problem: these tests have modest sensitivity/specificity and a low positive predictive value when pretest probability is low. Inflammation alone (high CRP/ESR) can nudge sIL‑2R up; ACE can be normal early in sarcoid. A mildly abnormal screen result can create a cascade of additional imaging, biopsies and consultations — and delay clarity.

In this case ACE and sIL‑2R were ordered as part of a broad exclusion approach. They didn’t change management and distracted from the pragmatic path: 1) rapidly rule out or treat infection, 2) apply clinical criteria for Behçet’s, and 3) pursue targeted testing based on clinical clues.

## Anchor on the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD)
Don’t forget the tools you already have. The ICBD is simple and practical for estimating pretest probability at the bedside:

- Oral aphthosis = 2 points  
- Genital aphthosis = 2 points  
- Ocular lesions = 2 points  
- Skin lesions = 1 point  
- Neurological manifestations = 1 point  
- Vascular manifestations = 1 point  
- Positive pathergy test = 1 point

Score ≥4 → classification as Behçet’s disease.

In this patient the mucocutaneous signs alone (oral 2 + genital 2 + skin 1) reached 5 points — “definite” Behçet’s by the ICBD — before HLA‑B51 or expansive rheumatologic or oncologic testing added anything. Using the criteria early changes the prior probabilities and should focus your next steps.

## A practical, streamlined workup for suspected Behçet’s with neurological symptoms
Use clinical probability to direct testing; here’s a pragmatic approach that mirrors what worked in the case:

Step 1 — Triage and treat the time‑sensitive things fast: If meningitis/encephalitis is on the table, don’t wait for a perfect diagnostic picture. Obtain blood cultures and perform neuroimaging (CT if needed for LP safety, then MRI). Start empiric antimicrobials (broad antibiotics and acyclovir when indicated) promptly.

Step 2 — Do the focused bedside diagnosis: check for oral/genital ulcers, skin lesions, uveitis, vascular signs and use the ICBD. If the ICBD score is ≥4, your pretest probability for Behçet’s is high and the differential shifts.

Step 3 — Target your investigations based on what you find: if the ICBD suggests Behçet’s and LP shows aseptic meningitis, prioritize CSF viral PCRs, bacterial cultures, TB testing as clinically appropriate. Avoid broad sarcoid/lymphoma screening unless you have clinical clues (chest X‑ray/CT abnormalities, uveitis suspicious for sarcoid, lymphadenopathy, cytopenias).

Step 4 — Be wary of over‑interpreting single lab bumps: sIL‑2R can rise with inflammation; ACE has limited sensitivity for sarcoid; HLA‑B51 supports but does not define BD.

Step 5 — Treat and monitor: once infection is reasonably excluded, manage neuro‑BD per specialty guidance (steroids/immunosuppression are mainstays for many parenchymal neuro‑BD cases). For mucocutaneous disease colchicine is appropriate. In this case colchicine monotherapy was temporizing and coincided with clinical recovery, but remember this is not the usual trajectory for moderate–severe neuro‑BD.

Step 6 — Watch electrolytes and complications: check serum and urine osmolality and urine sodium when hyponatraemia is present; manage SIADH with fluid restriction and cautious correction, and investigate the cause in a focused manner.

## When ACE, lysozyme or sIL‑2R are reasonable — and when they’re not
Those tests have a role when you have specific clinical reasons: abnormal chest imaging or respiratory symptoms suggesting sarcoidosis (then ACE/lysozyme are adjuncts), or clear radiologic/clinical suspicion for lymphoma (then sIL‑2R may help). They are poor as broad screening tests for every febrile patient with CNS symptoms.

In the case above, chest X‑ray/CT were normal and there were no ocular findings to raise sarcoid suspicion — so ACE and lysozyme were unlikely to be informative. sIL‑2R was mildly elevated, which could be explained by active inflammation and led nowhere diagnostically.

## HLA‑B51: supportive, not diagnostic
HLA‑B51 was positive in this patient and is a helpful epidemiologic clue — it increases the probability of Behçet’s in the right clinical context — but it’s not part of the diagnostic ICBD scoring and it doesn’t prove disease. Use it as confirmatory information in ambiguous cases, not as a trigger for an expansive workup.

## SIADH and neuro‑Behçet’s — don’t miss the endocrine link
CNS inflammation can dysregulate ADH release. In this case the patient fulfilled classic biochemical criteria for SIADH (hypotonic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated urine sodium) and had normal thyroid and adrenal tests. Symptoms such as persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis (CK 1,349 U/L) were additional clinical hints that the hyponatraemia was clinically significant. Treat SIADH appropriately (fluid restriction, slow correction, use hypertonic saline only for severe neurologic symptoms) and reassess as the inflammatory process is controlled.

Mechanistically IL‑6 and other cytokines can stimulate ADH — a plausible link between neuro‑inflammation and SIADH — but measuring cytokines is rarely necessary for clinical decision making.

## How this case should change your practice (a short list)
- When you see mucocutaneous lesions, use the ICBD right away — it changes your pretest probabilities and focuses testing.  
- If neuro infection is possible, treat empirically and get LP/MRI rapidly; don’t delay empiric antimicrobials while ordering a panel of low‑yield screens.  
- Order ACE, sIL‑2R, broad autoimmune panels and HLA typing only when specific clinical findings make those diagnoses plausible. Avoid shotgun screens that generate noise.  
- Normal MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD; CSF and clinical context matter.  
- Monitor sodium closely in neuro‑inflammatory disease — hiccups or unexplained CK elevations may be clues to significant hyponatraemia.

## Clinical pearls
A normal MRI in early neuro‑Behçet’s does not rule out CNS involvement — LP matters.  
sIL‑2R and ACE are not “general inflammation” tests; their interpretation depends on the clinical context. Mild sIL‑2R elevations are non‑specific.  
HLA‑B51 may help support a diagnosis in ambiguous cases but does not substitute for clinical criteria.  
When meningitis/encephalitis is a live possibility, start empiric antimicrobials promptly — you can still avoid scattershot immunologic or oncologic screens while the infection workup is ongoing.

## Take‑home points
- Apply the ICBD early — oral 2, genital 2, skin 1, neuro 1, ocular 2; score ≥4 = “definite” Behçet’s by criteria.  
- Don’t use ACE, lysozyme or sIL‑2R as routine screening tests without clinical clues (abnormal chest imaging, uveitis, lymphadenopathy, cytopenias).  
- MRI can be normal in early neuro‑BD; perform LP and target infectious testing rapidly. Start empiric antimicrobials when meningitis/encephalitis is possible.  
- HLA‑B51 is supportive but non‑diagnostic — order it selectively.  
- Watch for SIADH in neuro‑inflammatory disease; manage with fluid restriction and slow correction, and treat the underlying inflammation once infection is excluded.

Keep tests purposeful. Anchor on history and the ICBD, treat the time‑sensitive threats first, then let targeted clinical findings guide the rest of your workup.



# Two Diagnoses, One Visit: Catching Concurrent Behçet’s and Neuro-Behçet’s

What makes this case worth bookmarking? An otherwise healthy man in his 60s presented with fever and headache and—crucially—new mucocutaneous lesions (oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rash and a scrotal ulcer). He had hyponatraemia (Na 124 mmol/L), a CSF picture of aseptic meningitis, a normal brain MRI, and laboratory evidence of SIADH (serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine Na 88 mmol/L, ADH 22.4 pg/mL). After infections, malignancy and other causes were excluded, clinicians diagnosed definite Behçet’s disease with acute neuro-Behçet’s and treated with colchicine alone — and the patient’s mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms and hyponatraemia all resolved without steroids. Rare? Yes. Teach-worthy? Absolutely.

## Quick case snapshot
A man in his 60s came in with 3 days of fever, headache, malaise, persistent hiccups and arthralgia. Exam showed oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes; a painful scrotal ulcer appeared on day 5. Important labs: Na 124 mmol/L, CK 1349 U/L, CRP 180 mg/L, HbA1c 8.2%. LP: opening pressure 17 cmH2O, protein 0.97 g/L, lymphocytes 11/µL; CSF cultures and viral PCRs were negative. Brain MRI was unremarkable. Workup met International Criteria for Behçet’s (oral 2 + genital 2 + skin 1 = 5). Diagnosis: systemic Behçet’s with acute neuro-Behçet’s complicated by SIADH. Treatment: colchicine started on day 8; conservative SIADH care (fluid restriction <800 mL/d), no steroids. By day 16 Na normalized and ADH fell to 1.8 pg/mL; symptoms resolved and the patient remained well on colchicine long term.

## Why this is tricky (and why you’ll miss it)
- Neuro-Behçet’s usually emerges years after mucocutaneous disease. Simultaneous presentation is uncommon, so the default reflex is often “infection first.”
- Acute neuro-Behçet’s may present as aseptic meningitis or meningoencephalitis and can have a completely normal MRI in the early phase. A normal MRI does not rule out neuro-BD.
- Headache is common in Behçet’s patients and is usually benign. Distinguishing a primary headache from true neuro-Behçet’s requires attention to context and additional red flags.
- SIADH can be a clue — CNS inflammation can drive inappropriate ADH release. In this case hyponatraemia produced hiccups and rhabdomyolysis (CK elevation), which might otherwise be attributed to other causes.

## Bedside heuristics to raise (or lower) suspicion for neuro-Behçet’s
If you see new mucocutaneous lesions (oral and/or genital ulcers, folliculitis-like rash) in a patient with acute headache/fever, pause and ask: is this only a systemic inflammatory illness with a coincidental headache, or is there CNS involvement?

Things that should push you toward neuro-Behçet’s:
- New mucocutaneous BD features alongside acute new headache and systemic inflammation (high CRP/ESR).
- Neurological red flags: altered mental status, persistent severe headache, meningitic signs, focal deficits, new seizures, or vestibular symptoms (e.g., vertigo) that progress.
- Aseptic CSF pleocytosis and elevated protein without an identified pathogen.
- Otherwise unexplained euvolemic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine (think SIADH in the setting of CNS inflammation).
- HLA-B51 positive is supportive but not diagnostic; clinical criteria carry the day (International Criteria for Behçet’s: ≥4 = definite).

Things that make neuro-Behçet’s less likely:
- Long-standing, recurrent headaches with a stable pattern and no systemic inflammatory markers.
- Normal neuro exam, normal LP, and no evolving neurologic features over serial evaluations—monitor rather than jump to immunosuppression.

## Distinguishing “BD-associated headache” from true neuro-Behçet’s
Most headaches in Behçet’s are primary (tension-type or migraine-like). Reserve the label neuro-Behçet’s for patients with:
- A new, atypical, or worsening headache pattern,
- Concomitant systemic features (fever, new mucocutaneous lesions) or neurological signs,
- LP abnormalities (lymphocytic pleocytosis, high protein) or supportive MRI findings — remembering that MRI can be normal early.

When in doubt, do the basics: neuro exam, basic labs including Na, imaging (head CT if concerned for mass/bleed; MRI with contrast if available), and LP when safe and clinically indicated. If infection is on the differential, start empiric antimicrobial/antiviral therapy while pursuing diagnostics.

## SIADH in neuro-Behçet’s — pathophysiology & practical management
CNS inflammation can upregulate cytokines (IL‑6 among them) that drive ADH release. The picture is classic: hypotonic hyponatraemia, euvolemia, low serum osmolality, inappropriately high urine osmolality and urine sodium, normal thyroid/adrenal function. In this case ADH was elevated (22.4 pg/mL) and normalized as the inflammatory process abated.

Practical management points:
- Identify and treat the underlying cause (here: control neuroinflammation).
- Conservative first-line: fluid restriction and careful monitoring of serum sodium and neurologic status.
- Avoid rapid correction. Aim for conservative correction limits (generally ≤8–10 mmol/L in 24 hours — be even more cautious in high-risk patients).
- Reserve hypertonic saline for severe neurologic symptoms (seizure, coma) and consider specialist input for vaptans or other measures.
- Watch for complications of hyponatraemia: hiccups, nausea, altered mental status, and secondary rhabdomyolysis (measure CK if clinically warranted).

## Treatment takeaways for neuro-Behçet’s
- Standard teaching: acute neuro-Behçet’s, especially parenchymal disease, is treated with high-dose corticosteroids and often additional immunosuppression (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, biologics depending on severity).
- Colchicine is first‑line for mucocutaneous disease and can reduce cytokine release, but it is not considered sufficient for most cases of neuro-Behçet’s. This case is an exception: the patient’s aseptic meningitis–like presentation improved with colchicine alone. Don’t generalize this as standard of care.
- If the neurologic picture is severe or progressive (focal deficits, significant MRI lesions, deterioration), do not delay steroids and specialist referral while waiting for serologies.

## Practical ED/internal medicine checklist when this pattern shows up
- Thorough mucocutaneous exam (look for oral/genital ulcers, acneiform or folliculitis-like rash).
- Basic labs: CMP (check Na), CRP/ESR, CK if muscle symptoms or unexplained weakness, glucose/HbA1c.
- Evaluate hyponatraemia with serum osmolality, urine osmolality, urine sodium; get TSH and cortisol to exclude other causes.
- Neuro imaging (MRI brain with contrast) and LP if safe. Send CSF PCRs/cultures to exclude HSV, VZV, TB and common bacterial pathogens.
- Start empiric antibacterial/antiviral therapy if infection can’t be confidently excluded.
- Involve neurology and rheumatology early if neuro-Behçet’s is suspected.
- If SIADH: fluid restriction and close monitoring; escalate only if severe symptoms.

## Caveats
This is a single case report. It shows a temporal association between neuroinflammation and SIADH and suggests colchicine may sometimes suffice in mild, aseptic neuro-Behçet’s, but it does not overturn standard therapy recommendations. Keep a low threshold to treat aggressively when the neurologic disease is severe.

## Take-home points
- New mucocutaneous BD lesions (oral +/- genital ulcers, folliculitis-like rash) plus acute fever/headache should raise suspicion for concurrent neuro-Behçet’s — even if MRI is normal.
- Neuro-Behçet’s may present as aseptic meningitis; CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein are important diagnostic clues.
- Unexplained euvolemic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine in this context should prompt evaluation for SIADH as a complication of CNS inflammation.
- Manage SIADH conservatively first (fluid restriction, monitor), avoid overly rapid sodium correction, and treat the underlying neuroinflammatory disorder.
- Colchicine treats mucocutaneous Behçet’s and may help systemic inflammation, but most acute neuro-Behçet’s cases require corticosteroids ± additional immunosuppression — don’t withhold aggressive therapy for progressive neurologic disease.

If you see a patient with new oral/genital ulcers and a “red flag” headache or meningitic features — don’t file it as “just another headache” until you’ve checked sodium, looked for CSF inflammation, and considered neuro-Behçet’s alongside infection.
