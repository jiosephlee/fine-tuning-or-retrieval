Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Colchicine-Only for Acute Neuro-Behçet’s? Steroid-Sparing Lessons from an N-of-1

## The twist
Conventional teaching: acute neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) — especially meningoencephalitis or parenchymal brain disease — gets high‑dose corticosteroids ± long‑term immunosuppression. This case report gives us a curveball: an Asian man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, mucocutaneous Behçet’s stigmata and aseptic meningitis, had a normal brain MRI, and developed SIADH with symptomatic hyponatraemia. Instead of steroids, the team used colchicine monotherapy (plus conservative SIADH management) and the patient improved quickly — fever resolved the day after colchicine, neurologic complaints and mucocutaneous lesions settled over 2 weeks, sodium normalized by day 16, and colchicine was continued long‑term with no relapse over ~4 years.

Why is that interesting? Because neuro‑BD is usually treated aggressively with steroids (and often immunosuppressants), and colchicine is typically considered ineffective for neuro‑BD in systematic reviews. This single patient recovered without steroids — offering a practical stimulus to think about when a cautious, steroid‑sparing approach might be reasonable.

## The case in one paragraph
A man in his 60s with untreated type 2 diabetes presented with fever, headache, hiccups, arthralgia and oral aphthae. Exam later revealed folliculitis‑like rash and a painful genital ulcer. Labs: Na 124 mmol/L, CK 1,349 U/L, CRP 180 mg/L. Endocrine workup supported SIADH (low serum osmolality, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine Na 88 mmol/L, ADH elevated at 22.4 pg/mL). Brain MRI was normal. CSF showed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) and raised protein — aseptic meningitis. Infectious tests were negative. A diagnosis of definite Behçet’s and acute neuro‑BD was made. Colchicine was started on day 8; antibiotics/acyclovir were stopped after negative tests. Fever resolved the next day; neurologic symptoms and hyponatraemia improved over 2 weeks (Na 136 mmol/L on day 16, ADH fell to 1.8 pg/mL). No corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants were used; the patient did well long term.

## Why this matters (and why you shouldn’t change practice based on one case)
- Neuro‑BD is heterogeneous. Parenchymal brain disease (brainstem, basal ganglia) has higher morbidity and generally needs aggressive immunosuppression. Acute aseptic meningitis‑predominant presentations, especially early and MRI‑negative, can be more self‑limited.
- Colchicine is established for mucocutaneous Behçet’s but historically has not been shown to be effective for neuro‑BD in systematic reviews. That said, the drug has anti‑inflammatory effects (neutrophil inhibition, decreased IL‑1/IL‑6) and might plausibly help in milder neuroinflammatory phenotypes.
- This report is an N‑of‑1. It does not establish colchicine as an evidence‑based replacement for steroids in neuro‑BD, but it does show a scenario where a steroid‑sparing approach worked — and might be appealing when steroids are high risk (uncontrolled diabetes, active infection risk, etc.).

## When a steroid‑sparing, colchicine‑first approach might be reasonable (candidate features)
Consider this only in a tightly monitored, inpatient setting and only when all the following are present:
- Mild neurologic phenotype: headache, meningitis‑like symptoms, preserved mentation, no focal deficits, no seizures.
- MRI brain without parenchymal lesions (no brainstem/hypothalamic/parenchymal signal).
- CSF consistent with aseptic meningitis (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, modest protein elevation) and infectious causes have been reasonably excluded (PCRs/cultures pending but empiric antivirals/antibiotics started).
- Behçet’s diagnosis clear from mucocutaneous findings (here, oral + genital aphthae and folliculitis‑like rash).
- Comorbidities that increase steroid risk (e.g., poorly controlled diabetes) OR patient preference after informed discussion.
- Ability to keep the patient hospitalized for close neurological and biochemical monitoring and to rapidly escalate therapy if they deteriorate.

## A cautious steroid‑sparing pathway (practical steps)
1. Initial management
   - Do not reflexively withhold empiric antimicrobials/acyclovir while awaiting CSF/PCR: exclude infection first. In the reported case cefepime and acyclovir were started and later stopped.
   - Baseline labs: serum Na, osmolality, urine osmolality and urine Na, renal and liver function, CK, CRP, glucose/HbA1c.
   - MRI brain with contrast and ophthalmology exam to look for uveitis.
2. SIADH management (if present)
   - Fluid restriction (the team used <800 mL/day). Monitor Na closely (at least daily; more frequently if Na <125 or if symptomatic).
   - Avoid rapid correction. Reserve hypertonic saline or vaptans for severe/symptomatic hyponatraemia per usual protocols.
3. Start colchicine (used in this case) while continuing close observation. Adjust dose for renal/hepatic impairment and be mindful of drug interactions (CYP3A4 inhibitors, statins).
4. Monitoring while on colchicine‑first strategy
   - Frequent neuro exams (q4–6 h initially), daily vitals.
   - Serum sodium at least daily until stable; CK and renal function every 48–72 h initially.
   - Repeat MRI/LP if clinical deterioration.
5. Clear escalation triggers — start steroids promptly if any of the following:
   - New or worsening focal neurologic deficit, altered mental status, or seizures.
   - MRI showing parenchymal lesion (especially brainstem/hypothalamus) or new mass‑effect lesion.
   - Worsening or refractory CSF inflammation by repeat testing with clinical decline.
   - Failure to improve or clinical deterioration within 48–72 hours of colchicine start.
   - Severe, symptomatic hyponatraemia (seizures, obtundation) or sodium falling despite conservative measures.
6. If escalation required
   - Typical practice: high‑dose IV corticosteroids (e.g., IV methylprednisolone pulses) then oral taper; add steroid‑sparing immunosuppressants (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, anti‑TNF agents) for severe/parenchymal disease or recurrent course. Use established neuro‑Behçet’s protocols and specialist input.

## SIADH and neuro‑BD — a reminder
CNS inflammation can drive ADH release (likely cytokine mediated — IL‑6 has been implicated). In this case ADH was high (22.4 pg/mL) with a urine osmolality of 900 mOsm/kg and urine Na 88 mmol/L; treating the underlying inflammatory process (colchicine) and fluid restriction coincided with normalization of ADH and Na. Watch for subtle clues of hyponatraemia — hiccups and rhabdomyolysis (CK 1,349 U/L in this patient) were present here and may be underappreciated indicators.

## Limitations — and the bottom line
This is an instructive single case. It shows that:
- Early, MRI‑negative, meningitic presentations of neuro‑BD can be self‑limited.
- In patients where steroids are risky, a monitored colchicine‑first approach (with infection adequately excluded and with a low threshold to escalate) is a reasonable, pragmatic consideration.
But it should not replace standard care for patients with parenchymal neuro‑BD or those with clear neurologic compromise — those patients still need prompt high‑dose steroids and specialist immunosuppression.

## Take‑home bullets
- Acute neuro‑Behçet’s is heterogeneous: MRI‑negative aseptic meningitis can be milder and occasionally self‑limited; parenchymal brain disease warrants aggressive immunosuppression.
- Colchicine monotherapy was associated with rapid clinical and biochemical recovery in this single case — useful as hypothesis‑generating, not practice‑changing.
- Consider a steroid‑sparing, colchicine‑first pathway only for carefully selected patients (mild symptoms, normal MRI, infection reasonably excluded, ability for close inpatient monitoring) and with explicit escalation triggers.
- Monitor frequently: neuro checks q4–6 h initially, serum Na daily (more if severe), CK/renal/hepatic tests, and repeat imaging/LP if clinical status changes.
- SIADH is a recognized complication of CNS inflammation; manage with fluid restriction and treat the underlying cause; be alert for hiccups, rhabdomyolysis and symptomatic hyponatraemia.
- If any sign of neurological deterioration, new focal deficits, parenchymal lesions on MRI, seizures, or refractory hyponatraemia — escalate promptly to high‑dose steroids and involve neuro/rheumatology.

Clinical bottom line: this N‑of‑1 shows colchicine can work in an early, mild neuro‑Behçet’s phenotype — but only use a steroid‑sparing strategy with close monitoring and a low threshold to escalate.
