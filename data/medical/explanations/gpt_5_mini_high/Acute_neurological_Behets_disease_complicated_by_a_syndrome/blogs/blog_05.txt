Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Stop Shotgunning the Workup: Use Behçet’s Criteria and Pretest Probability

## Why this case grabbed my attention
A man in his 60s walks into the ED with fever, headache and a handful of non-specific findings — oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like rashes and later a painful genital ulcer. He’s hyponatraemic (Na 124 mmol/L) with headache and vertigo, MRI is normal, lumbar puncture shows a mild aseptic meningitis, and endocrine testing meets criteria for SIADH. The team ordered ACE and soluble IL‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) as broad screens; ACE was normal and sIL‑2R was mildly elevated — neither helped. Ultimately the patient met the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) and was diagnosed with concurrent systemic Behçet’s disease and acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD). Colchicine alone (no steroids) coincided with resolution of mucocutaneous disease, neurological symptoms and hyponatraemia.

This vignette highlights two common traps: 1) reflexively ordering low‑yield “omnibus” tests (ACE, sIL‑2R, broad autoimmune panels) without clinical signals, and 2) over‑relying on imaging to exclude neuro‑inflammatory disease. If you anchor your workup on clinical probability — the ICBD and focused bedside exam — you’ll get faster to the right questions, avoid noisy results that spawn more tests, and still treat time‑sensitive infectious mimics without delay.

## The case in a paragraph
Male in his 60s with 3 days of fever, headache, malaise and persistent hiccups. Exam: oral aphthae and folliculitis‑like rashes; a scrotal ulcer appeared on day 5. Labs: Na 124 mmol/L, CRP 180 mg/L, ESR 70, CK 1,349 U/L, glucose 14.2 mmol/L. Endocrine: TSH/T4 normal, cortisol adequate; serum osmolality low (267 mOsm/kg), urine osmolality high (900 mOsm/kg), urine Na 88 mmol/L, ADH elevated (22.4 pg/mL) → SIADH. Neuro: MRI normal; CSF LP with opening pressure 17 cm H₂O, protein 0.97 g/L, 11 lymphocytes/µL; CSF bacterial/fungal/HSV/VZV testing negative. ACE and lysozyme normal, sIL‑2R mildly up (704 U/mL); HLA‑B51 positive. Empiric cefepime started day 1, empiric acyclovir day 3 (stopped after negative viral testing), colchicine began day 8, symptoms improved rapidly and Na normalized to 136 mmol/L by day 16. No systemic steroids used; discharged after 1 month, colchicine continued long term with no recurrence.

## The common ED impulse — and why it hurts
When fever, headache and hyponatraemia coexist you must think about CNS infection, autoimmune CNS disease, malignancy and drug effects. That diagnostic breadth drives the temptation to “cast a wide net” with labs like ACE, lysozyme, sIL‑2R, broad autoantibody panels and even HLA typing. The problem: these tests have modest sensitivity/specificity and a low positive predictive value when pretest probability is low. Inflammation alone (high CRP/ESR) can nudge sIL‑2R up; ACE can be normal early in sarcoid. A mildly abnormal screen result can create a cascade of additional imaging, biopsies and consultations — and delay clarity.

In this case ACE and sIL‑2R were ordered as part of a broad exclusion approach. They didn’t change management and distracted from the pragmatic path: 1) rapidly rule out or treat infection, 2) apply clinical criteria for Behçet’s, and 3) pursue targeted testing based on clinical clues.

## Anchor on the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD)
Don’t forget the tools you already have. The ICBD is simple and practical for estimating pretest probability at the bedside:

- Oral aphthosis = 2 points  
- Genital aphthosis = 2 points  
- Ocular lesions = 2 points  
- Skin lesions = 1 point  
- Neurological manifestations = 1 point  
- Vascular manifestations = 1 point  
- Positive pathergy test = 1 point

Score ≥4 → classification as Behçet’s disease.

In this patient the mucocutaneous signs alone (oral 2 + genital 2 + skin 1) reached 5 points — “definite” Behçet’s by the ICBD — before HLA‑B51 or expansive rheumatologic or oncologic testing added anything. Using the criteria early changes the prior probabilities and should focus your next steps.

## A practical, streamlined workup for suspected Behçet’s with neurological symptoms
Use clinical probability to direct testing; here’s a pragmatic approach that mirrors what worked in the case:

Step 1 — Triage and treat the time‑sensitive things fast: If meningitis/encephalitis is on the table, don’t wait for a perfect diagnostic picture. Obtain blood cultures and perform neuroimaging (CT if needed for LP safety, then MRI). Start empiric antimicrobials (broad antibiotics and acyclovir when indicated) promptly.

Step 2 — Do the focused bedside diagnosis: check for oral/genital ulcers, skin lesions, uveitis, vascular signs and use the ICBD. If the ICBD score is ≥4, your pretest probability for Behçet’s is high and the differential shifts.

Step 3 — Target your investigations based on what you find: if the ICBD suggests Behçet’s and LP shows aseptic meningitis, prioritize CSF viral PCRs, bacterial cultures, TB testing as clinically appropriate. Avoid broad sarcoid/lymphoma screening unless you have clinical clues (chest X‑ray/CT abnormalities, uveitis suspicious for sarcoid, lymphadenopathy, cytopenias).

Step 4 — Be wary of over‑interpreting single lab bumps: sIL‑2R can rise with inflammation; ACE has limited sensitivity for sarcoid; HLA‑B51 supports but does not define BD.

Step 5 — Treat and monitor: once infection is reasonably excluded, manage neuro‑BD per specialty guidance (steroids/immunosuppression are mainstays for many parenchymal neuro‑BD cases). For mucocutaneous disease colchicine is appropriate. In this case colchicine monotherapy was temporizing and coincided with clinical recovery, but remember this is not the usual trajectory for moderate–severe neuro‑BD.

Step 6 — Watch electrolytes and complications: check serum and urine osmolality and urine sodium when hyponatraemia is present; manage SIADH with fluid restriction and cautious correction, and investigate the cause in a focused manner.

## When ACE, lysozyme or sIL‑2R are reasonable — and when they’re not
Those tests have a role when you have specific clinical reasons: abnormal chest imaging or respiratory symptoms suggesting sarcoidosis (then ACE/lysozyme are adjuncts), or clear radiologic/clinical suspicion for lymphoma (then sIL‑2R may help). They are poor as broad screening tests for every febrile patient with CNS symptoms.

In the case above, chest X‑ray/CT were normal and there were no ocular findings to raise sarcoid suspicion — so ACE and lysozyme were unlikely to be informative. sIL‑2R was mildly elevated, which could be explained by active inflammation and led nowhere diagnostically.

## HLA‑B51: supportive, not diagnostic
HLA‑B51 was positive in this patient and is a helpful epidemiologic clue — it increases the probability of Behçet’s in the right clinical context — but it’s not part of the diagnostic ICBD scoring and it doesn’t prove disease. Use it as confirmatory information in ambiguous cases, not as a trigger for an expansive workup.

## SIADH and neuro‑Behçet’s — don’t miss the endocrine link
CNS inflammation can dysregulate ADH release. In this case the patient fulfilled classic biochemical criteria for SIADH (hypotonic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated urine sodium) and had normal thyroid and adrenal tests. Symptoms such as persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis (CK 1,349 U/L) were additional clinical hints that the hyponatraemia was clinically significant. Treat SIADH appropriately (fluid restriction, slow correction, use hypertonic saline only for severe neurologic symptoms) and reassess as the inflammatory process is controlled.

Mechanistically IL‑6 and other cytokines can stimulate ADH — a plausible link between neuro‑inflammation and SIADH — but measuring cytokines is rarely necessary for clinical decision making.

## How this case should change your practice (a short list)
- When you see mucocutaneous lesions, use the ICBD right away — it changes your pretest probabilities and focuses testing.  
- If neuro infection is possible, treat empirically and get LP/MRI rapidly; don’t delay empiric antimicrobials while ordering a panel of low‑yield screens.  
- Order ACE, sIL‑2R, broad autoimmune panels and HLA typing only when specific clinical findings make those diagnoses plausible. Avoid shotgun screens that generate noise.  
- Normal MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD; CSF and clinical context matter.  
- Monitor sodium closely in neuro‑inflammatory disease — hiccups or unexplained CK elevations may be clues to significant hyponatraemia.

## Clinical pearls
A normal MRI in early neuro‑Behçet’s does not rule out CNS involvement — LP matters.  
sIL‑2R and ACE are not “general inflammation” tests; their interpretation depends on the clinical context. Mild sIL‑2R elevations are non‑specific.  
HLA‑B51 may help support a diagnosis in ambiguous cases but does not substitute for clinical criteria.  
When meningitis/encephalitis is a live possibility, start empiric antimicrobials promptly — you can still avoid scattershot immunologic or oncologic screens while the infection workup is ongoing.

## Take‑home points
- Apply the ICBD early — oral 2, genital 2, skin 1, neuro 1, ocular 2; score ≥4 = “definite” Behçet’s by criteria.  
- Don’t use ACE, lysozyme or sIL‑2R as routine screening tests without clinical clues (abnormal chest imaging, uveitis, lymphadenopathy, cytopenias).  
- MRI can be normal in early neuro‑BD; perform LP and target infectious testing rapidly. Start empiric antimicrobials when meningitis/encephalitis is possible.  
- HLA‑B51 is supportive but non‑diagnostic — order it selectively.  
- Watch for SIADH in neuro‑inflammatory disease; manage with fluid restriction and slow correction, and treat the underlying inflammation once infection is excluded.

Keep tests purposeful. Anchor on history and the ICBD, treat the time‑sensitive threats first, then let targeted clinical findings guide the rest of your workup.
