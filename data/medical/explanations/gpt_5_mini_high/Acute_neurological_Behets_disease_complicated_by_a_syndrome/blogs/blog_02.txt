Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Hyponatremia as a Signal: SIADH in Neuroinflammation and Behçet’s

Why this vignette jumps off the page
A man in his 60s arrives with fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions and persistent hiccups. He has marked hyponatraemia (Na 124 mmol/L), low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), very concentrated urine (Uosm 900 mOsm/kg) and high urine sodium (88 mmol/L). CSF shows aseptic meningitis (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein) and MRI is unrevealing. Genital ulcers appear on day 5 and he scores “definite” Behçet’s by clinical criteria. The treating team diagnoses SIADH complicating acute neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD). Colchicine monotherapy was started and—remarkably—mucocutaneous symptoms, neurologic complaints and the hyponatraemia all improved without steroids; sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L by hospital day 16.

That constellation is useful to study because: (1) neuro‑BD may present with aseptic meningitis and a normal early MRI, (2) CNS inflammation can produce SIADH (often via cytokine‑driven ADH release), and (3) the endocrine disturbance may be the most obvious abnormality at presentation — a real diagnostic clue rather than an incidental laboratory finding.

Short physiology refresher — how CNS inflammation drives SIADH
Antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin) is released in response to osmotic and non‑osmotic triggers. CNS inflammation (infection, haemorrhage, tumour, autoimmune meningoencephalitis) can trigger non‑osmotic ADH release through pain, nausea, direct hypothalamic irritation and cytokine signalling. Interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) is a key cytokine often elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions and has been shown to stimulate vasopressin release. In neuro‑Behçet’s, CSF IL‑6 elevations have been reported, offering a biologically plausible link between CNS inflammation and SIADH. (In this case IL‑6 was not measured; the association is inferential but consistent with the temporal course.)

A practical bedside framework to diagnose SIADH
At the bedside you want a short, sequential checklist — is this true hypotonic hyponatraemia, and is the kidney inappropriately conserving water?

1. Confirm true hypotonic hyponatraemia
   - Serum sodium low and measured serum osmolality low (the case: Na 124 mmol/L, Osm 267 mOsm/kg → hypotonic). Important caveat: hyperglycaemia causes translocational hyponatraemia, so correct Na if glucose is very high (this patient had glucose 14.2 mmol/L, but measured serum osmolality was still low, so the hyponatraemia was real).

2. Clinical volume assessment
   - SIADH patients are usually clinically euvolemic (no oedema, no orthostatic hypotension). The case was clinically euvolemic.

3. Urine studies to confirm inappropriate ADH effect
   - Urine osmolality not maximally dilute (typically >100 mOsm/kg and often >300 mOsm/kg in SIADH). This patient’s Uosm was 900 mOsm/kg — very concentrated.
   - Urine sodium usually >30 mmol/L (case: 88 mmol/L) when dietary sodium intake is normal.

4. Exclude mimics
   - Hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, renal failure and diuretic use can mimic SIADH. Check TSH and cortisol (this patient had normal TSH and a clearly non‑low cortisol), review meds (no offending drugs), and confirm normal renal function.

5. ADH (vasopressin) assay?
   - Rarely required. Assays are not standardized and do not usually change management. In this report ADH was measured (22.4 pg/mL and later fell to 1.8 pg/mL), but that’s more a confirmatory/academic data point than a practical requirement.

Management — when to be conservative and when to escalate
Think first about severity of symptoms and chronicity. The general approach:

- Severe or life‑threatening neurologic symptoms (seizures, obtundation, severe encephalopathy): treat emergently with hypertonic (3%) saline. Use 100 mL boluses (repeat as needed) to achieve a prompt small rise (aim +4–6 mmol/L quickly to relieve symptoms) and then slow down.

- Mild–moderate or asymptomatic SIADH: start with conservative measures.
  - Fluid restriction is first‑line (this case used <800 mL/day and sodium normalized over ~2 weeks). Success requires adherence; inpatient monitoring makes it safer.
  - Increase solute intake if restriction is ineffective (oral urea, salt tablets — local practice varies).
  - Loop diuretics (with saline) can be used to increase free‑water excretion in selected patients.

- Tolvaptan (oral V2 receptor antagonist):
  - Effective in raising serum sodium in euvolemic/hypervolemic hyponatraemia but carries risk of overly rapid correction and hepatotoxicity. Reserve for specialist‑supervised cases when fluid restriction and conservative measures fail, and only with close inpatient monitoring of sodium.

- Avoid rapid correction
  - Target safe correction limits: generally ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours (some authorities recommend ≤6–8 mmol/L in high‑risk patients) and ≤18 mmol/L in 48 hours. Check sodium every 2–4 hours during active correction. If overcorrection occurs, consider re‑lowering serum sodium (desmopressin + free water) to reduce risk of osmotic demyelination.

Tie back to the Behçet’s case — what to watch for and pitfalls
- Neuro‑Behçet’s can present early with aseptic meningitis and a normal MRI. Don’t exclude CNS involvement simply because imaging is unrevealing.
- Hyponatraemia was the most striking objective abnormality and provided an early clue to CNS dysfunction. Non‑specific symptoms such as hiccups and generalized malaise were important adjuncts; hiccups can be a subtle sign of brainstem irritation and have been reported in SIADH/CNS inflammation.
- Complications of hyponatraemia may be unexpected: the patient had an elevated CK (1349 U/L), consistent with rhabdomyolysis sometimes seen with severe hyponatraemia — check CK if muscle pain, weakness or very low sodium.
- Drug causes matter: SIADH has been reported in BD patients on cyclophosphamide, SSRIs, anticonvulsants and others. This patient had no provoking medication, strengthening the link to CNS inflammation.
- Treat the brain. Controlling the underlying neuroinflammation is central; in neuro‑BD steroids and other immunosuppressants are classically used. This case is notable because colchicine alone coincided with rapid clinical improvement and normalization of sodium — an atypical but instructive outcome. Don’t assume colchicine will replace steroids/immunosuppression for typical neuro‑BD.

When to involve colleagues
- Endocrinology/nephrology: severe hyponatraemia, rapid or difficult corrections, consideration of vaptans, or diagnostic uncertainty.
- Neurology/rheumatology: suspected neuro‑Behçet’s or other neuroinflammatory causes that will require immunomodulatory therapy.

Clinical pearls / take‑home points
- SIADH is a common endocrine footprint of CNS inflammation — hyponatraemia in a patient with headache/fever and mucocutaneous signs should raise the possibility of neuro‑Behçet’s when the clinical picture fits.
- Diagnose SIADH by confirming hypotonic hyponatraemia, clinically euvolaemic state, inappropriately concentrated urine (Uosm usually >300 mOsm/kg) with urine Na >30 mmol/L, and exclusion of hypothyroidism/adrenal insufficiency; ADH assays are rarely required.
- At the bedside: check serum osmolality, urine osmolality and urine sodium early; review medications and volume status; correct sodium for marked hyperglycaemia if present.
- Start with fluid restriction for mild/asymptomatic SIADH (<800–1000 mL/day). Reserve hypertonic saline for severe neurologic symptoms and vaptans for specialist‑managed, refractory cases — always monitor sodium closely.
- Avoid overly rapid correction (generally ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours); have a plan to relower sodium (desmopressin + free water) if overcorrection occurs.
- Treat the underlying CNS problem — in neuro‑BD that usually means steroids/immunosuppression; this case’s colchicine response is interesting but atypical and should not change usual practice without specialist input.

Bottom line: hyponatraemia can be more than a lab oddity — in the right clinical context it’s a red flag for CNS disease. Recognize SIADH quickly, manage safely, and treat the underlying neuroinflammation.
