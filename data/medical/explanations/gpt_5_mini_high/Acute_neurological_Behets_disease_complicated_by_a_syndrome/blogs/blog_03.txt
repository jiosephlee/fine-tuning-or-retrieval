Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Hiccups and Rhabdo from Salt Trouble: The Hyponatremia Clues You’ll Miss if You Don’t Look

## Why this case grabbed my attention
A man in his 60s presents with fever, headache and mucocutaneous ulcers and ends up being diagnosed with Behçet’s disease and aseptic meningitis. That’s already an eyebrow-raiser. What made the case stick with me were the “small” clues that should have pushed sodium to the top of the problem list: persistent hiccups and an elevated CK (1,349 U/L). Labs showed a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L, a low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg and urine sodium 88 mmol/L — a picture consistent with SIADH. The team treated conservatively (fluid restriction <800 mL/day) while treating the underlying inflammatory disease with colchicine; the patient’s symptoms, hyponatraemia and CK all normalized over the next two weeks, and ADH fell from 22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL. No steroids, hypertonic saline or vaptans were required.

This case is a great reminder that quiet symptoms — hiccups, myalgias, malaise — can be sentinel clues to clinically important hyponatraemia, and that hyponatraemia itself can cause downstream complications (rhabdomyolysis) that change management priorities.

## Persistent hiccups: an under‑appreciated red flag
Hiccups are usually benign. Persistent hiccups (days) are not. They can be triggered by irritation of central structures in the brainstem or by meningeal/CNS inflammation, and they turn up in case series of meningitis, encephalitis and other neuroinflammatory states. In this patient the hiccups appeared alongside headache and aseptic meningitis, and they improved as the inflammatory process and hyponatraemia resolved.

The practical take-home: when hiccups are persistent and unexplained, check a basic metabolic panel — don’t forget sodium. If the patient has any CNS symptoms (headache, altered behaviour, neck pain), think neuro causes that could be driving inappropriate ADH release.

## Rhabdomyolysis from low salt — when to check CK
Hyponatraemia can cause muscle cell swelling and injury, producing CK elevations and frank rhabdomyolysis. The mechanism is cellular swelling and membrane dysfunction from hypotonic extracellular fluid; clinically this can be worsened by seizures or prolonged immobility. In this case CK was 1,349 U/L on presentation, with normal renal function, and normalized as sodium corrected — consistent with hyponatraemia-associated muscle injury rather than a primary myopathy or traumatic cause.

When should you check CK in hyponatraemia? I consider CK when:
- Na is very low (for many, <120 mmol/L) or falling fast,
- the patient has muscle pain, weakness or dark urine,
- there was a seizure, prolonged immobilization, or a history of intoxication,
- or when empiric evaluation of systemic symptoms (fever, myalgias) includes CK anyway.

If CK is markedly elevated or there is evidence of myoglobinuria/AKI, treatment priorities shift: preventing renal injury via judicious isotonic fluids becomes urgent — and that may conflict with fluid restriction for SIADH, so early nephrology input is wise.

## Correct the sodium for glucose — every time
Hyperglycaemia causes a translocational hyponatraemia (osmotic water shift from cells to ECF). Always correct sodium for hyperglycaemia to avoid misclassification. The common correction formula is: add ~1.6 mEq/L to measured sodium for every 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) of glucose above 100 mg/dL. Some use 2.4 mEq/L per 100 mg/dL for very high glucose states; both are approximations.

Apply it to this patient: glucose 14.2 mmol/L × 18 = ~256 mg/dL. Glucose is 156 mg/dL above 100. Using 1.6/mEq per 100 mg/dL: correction ≈ 1.6 × 1.56 ≈ +2.5 → corrected Na ≈ 126–127 mmol/L. Even after correction, sodium remains low. Importantly, this patient had a low measured serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), confirming true hypotonic hyponatraemia rather than hypertonic (glucose‑driven) hyponatraemia.

So: correct for glucose, but don’t stop there — check serum osmolality. If osmolality is low, you’re dealing with true hypotonic hyponatraemia that needs a specific workup.

## Diagnosing SIADH — the bedside confirmation
SIADH remains a diagnosis made from a cluster of findings, not a single test. The classic elements are:
- Hypotonic hyponatraemia (low serum osmolality),
- Inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality >100–300 mOsm/kg; in this case 900 mOsm/kg),
- Elevated urine sodium (>30–40 mmol/L) with normal salt intake (88 mmol/L here),
- Clinical euvolemia,
- Normal thyroid and adrenal function (this patient had TSH and cortisol within acceptable ranges),
- No recent diuretic use or other clear explainers.

Measuring ADH is not required for diagnosis and is often unhelpful in real time, but in this case an elevated ADH supported the pathophysiology. Given the concurrent aseptic meningitis/meningoencephalitis and marked systemic inflammation (CRP 180 mg/L), neuroinflammation was a plausible SIADH trigger. IL‑6–driven ADH release is a biologically plausible mechanism in neuroinflammatory states like neuro‑Behçet’s.

## Management — how aggressively (or not) to correct and where desmopressin fits
Management decisions depend on severity and acuity.

If the patient has severe neurologic symptoms (seizure, coma, severe obtundation), treat immediately with hypertonic saline (institutional protocol) and monitor closely. This case did not have severe neuro compromise, and conservative management with fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) plus treatment of the underlying inflammatory condition was sufficient; sodium rose slowly and safely from 124 to 136 mmol/L over ~2 weeks.

Targets matter: to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS), limit correction. Reasonable, commonly used targets are <8 mmol/L increase in the first 24 hours and <18 mmol/L in 48 hours for most patients. For those at high risk of ODS (Na very low, malnourished, alcoholic, hypokalaemic, chronic hyponatraemia), tighter goals (4–6 mmol/L/day) are prudent.

The desmopressin clamp is your tool to prevent or treat rapid overcorrection caused by spontaneous water diuresis. There are two uses:
- Prophylactic clamp (planned): give desmopressin (commonly 1–2 μg IV/SC; institutional dosing varies) to halt free‑water diuresis while you correct sodium at a controlled rate with hypertonic saline. Check sodium every 2 hours.
- Rescue clamp: if sodium has overcorrected (e.g., >8 mEq/L in 24 h or especially >10–12 in 24 h), give desmopressin and administer hypotonic fluid (D5W) to re‑lower the sodium to safe levels.

Dosing practices vary — check local protocols and pharmacy. The critical point is: if the patient is at risk of brisk water diuresis, anticipate it and use desmopressin to avoid an uncontrolled leap in sodium.

## The balancing act: rhabdo versus SIADH
This case neatly illustrates a therapeutic tension. SIADH wants fluid restriction; rhabdomyolysis wants aggressive isotonic fluids to prevent myoglobinuric AKI. The approach is pragmatic: severity drives therapy. Mild CK elevations with preserved renal function can be watched with conservative hyponatraemia management and serial monitoring (BMP, CK, urine). Significant rhabdo (very high CK, dark urine, rising creatinine) requires prioritizing renal protection — give isotonic fluids, monitor sodium closely, and use desmopressin (clamp) and frequent sodium checks to avoid overcorrection.

## How the team did it (and what I’d remember)
They recognized SIADH in the setting of neuro‑Behçet’s (aseptic meningitis), treated the underlying inflammatory disease with colchicine, enforced fluid restriction, monitored sodium and CK, and allowed a slow, safe correction. Everything resolved without steroids, hypertonic saline or vaptans. The temporal association between treating neuroinflammation and falling ADH/sodium normalization is a useful clinical reminder: fix the trigger when you can.

## Quick bedside checklist and take‑home pearls
- Always check a sodium when hiccups are persistent or unexplained, especially with CNS symptoms.  
- Correct serum sodium for hyperglycaemia before classifying hyponatraemia (add ~1.6 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL glucose >100 mg/dL; this patient’s Na 124 → ~126–128 when corrected).  
- Confirm hypotonic hyponatraemia with measured serum osmolality — low osmolality = true hyponatraemia.  
- Diagnose SIADH by the cluster: hypotonic hyponatraemia, inappropriately concentrated urine (high urine Osm), urine Na >30–40 mmol/L, euvolaemia, normal adrenal/thyroid function, and no diuretics.  
- Consider neuroinflammatory causes (meningitis, encephalitis, neuro‑Behçet’s) as triggers for SIADH; IL‑6 mediated ADH release is a plausible mechanism.  
- Check CK in severe hyponatraemia (<120), muscle symptoms, seizures, dark urine or prolonged immobility — hyponatraemia can cause rhabdomyolysis.  
- Manage asymptomatic/moderate SIADH with fluid restriction and treat the underlying cause; reserve hypertonic saline for severe neurologic symptoms.  
- Safe correction targets: generally ≤8 mmol/L in 24 h (≤18 mmol/L in 48 h); tighter (4–6 mmol/L/day) for high‑risk patients to avoid ODS.  
- Use desmopressin (the “clamp”) prophylactically or as rescue when there’s risk of rapid water diuresis or overcorrection — check local dosing and monitor sodium at least every 2 hours during active management.  
- When rhabdomyolysis and SIADH collide, prioritize renal protection for significant rhabdo (isotonic fluids) and use desmopressin and close monitoring to manage the sodium safely; early nephrology consult is helpful.

If you take one practical habit from this case: when hiccups are persistent and the patient looks off, check a BMP. A single sodium can prevent a cascade.
