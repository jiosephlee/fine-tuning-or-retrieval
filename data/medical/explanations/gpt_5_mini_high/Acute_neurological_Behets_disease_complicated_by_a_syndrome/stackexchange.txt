Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Morning Report] Using the 2014 International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD), what are the point assignments for oral aphthosis, genital aphthosis, and typical skin lesions, what is the minimum score required for classification, and which of these findings is most specific for BD?

Short answer: In the 2014 International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) oral aphthosis = 2 points, genital aphthosis = 2 points, and typical skin lesions = 1 point. A total score of ≥4 points is required to classify a patient as having Behçet’s disease. Of those three findings, genital aphthosis is the most specific for BD.

Clinical reasoning and application to the case: The 2014 ICBD assigns weighted points to key manifestations (oral, genital, and ocular lesions carry 2 points each; skin, neurologic and vascular manifestations and a positive pathergy test carry 1 point each; pathergy is scored only if performed). Classification requires ≥4 points and is intended to standardize case definition while still relying on clinical judgement. In this patient the mucocutaneous findings were oral aphthae (2), a folliculitis‑like rash (1) and, when it appeared, a painful scrotal (genital) ulcer (2) — total 5 points, meeting the ICBD threshold for “definite” BD. Practically, oral ulcers are sensitive but common and relatively nonspecific; genital ulcers are less common outside BD and therefore carry greater specificity and diagnostic weight.

### [Clinical Pearls] What laboratory criteria define SIADH in a patient with hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, and how do these results help you distinguish SIADH from hypovolemic hyponatremia or endocrine causes (adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism)?

Direct answer — the laboratory definition and how to use the results to separate SIADH from hypovolemia or endocrine causes:

1) Laboratory criteria that define SIADH in a patient with hypo‑osmolar hyponatraemia
- Confirm true hypotonic hyponatraemia: low serum sodium with low plasma osmolality. (In this case: Na 124 mmol/L with serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg.)
- Inappropriately concentrated urine for a hypotonic state: urine osmolality is not maximally suppressed (classically >100 mOsm/kg; many clinicians use >300 mOsm/kg as the cut point to indicate active ADH). (Case: urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg.)
- Renal natriuresis despite hyponatraemia: elevated urine sodium on a normal salt intake (commonly >30–40 mmol/L; the report uses >20 mmol/L as reference). (Case: urine Na 88 mmol/L.)
- Clinical euvolemia (no signs of volume depletion or overload).
- Exclusion of other causes that can mimic SIADH: normal renal function, no recent diuretic use, and normal adrenal and thyroid function. (Case: creatinine normal, no diuretics reported, TSH 1.11 mIU/L and free T4 1.25 ng/dL normal, morning cortisol 42 µg/dL.)

Supportive findings that strengthen the diagnosis: low BUN and low uric acid (not reported in this paper), and — if available — an inappropriately high plasma ADH (or copeptin). In this patient ADH was elevated (22.4 pg/mL) when hyponatraemic and fell to 1.8 pg/mL as sodium normalized, which is biochemical confirmation of inappropriate ADH activity.

2) How these results distinguish SIADH from hypovolemic hyponatraemia or endocrine causes
- Hypovolemic hyponatraemia (extrarenal losses): ADH is appropriately high because of volume depletion. Key lab differences:
  - Urine Na is typically low (<20 mmol/L) because the kidney avidly retains sodium (unless there is renal salt wasting or recent diuretic use, in which case urine Na can be high).
  - Clinical findings of hypovolemia (orthostasis, tachycardia, dry mucosa, low JVP) are usually present.
  - BUN and uric acid are often elevated.
  In contrast, SIADH shows euvolemia, high urine Na (natriuresis) and low BUN/uric acid. In the case the combination of euvolemia and urine Na 88 mmol/L pointed away from extrarenal hypovolemia.

- Adrenal insufficiency (secondary or primary): cortisol deficiency removes tonic suppression of ADH and may produce a biochemical picture similar to SIADH (hyponatraemia with concentrated urine and natriuresis). Important distinguishing tests:
  - Measure morning serum cortisol and consider a cosyntropin stimulation test if the cortisol is not clearly normal/high. Primary adrenal insufficiency often causes hyperkalemia and hypotension; urine Na may be high because of mineralocorticoid loss.
  - In this patient a high morning cortisol (42 µg/dL) effectively excluded adrenal insufficiency as the cause of the hyponatraemia.

- Hypothyroidism: severe hypothyroidism can cause hyponatraemia via decreased cardiac output/GFR and increased ADH. Distinguish by measuring TSH and free T4. In this case TSH and free T4 were normal, excluding hypothyroidism.

Practical diagnostic sequence (apply to the case):
1. Confirm hypotonic hyponatraemia (done).
2. Assess volume status clinically.
3. Measure urine osmolality and urine sodium. If urine osmolality is high (>100–300) and urine Na is not low, think SIADH (after excluding diuretics and renal failure).
4. Exclude endocrine causes (TSH, morning cortisol ± cosyntropin).
5. If all criteria are met, label SIADH and treat underlying cause and fluid‑restrict. In this patient the laboratory pattern (serum osmolality 267, urine osmolality 900, urine Na 88), clinical euvolemia, normal thyroid and adrenal tests and an elevated ADH level pointed to SIADH; conservative management with fluid restriction and treating the underlying neuroinflammatory disorder led to normalization of sodium (136 mmol/L) and fall in ADH.

Teaching pearl: always exclude adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism before diagnosing SIADH, and interpret urine sodium in the context of diuretics and clinical volume status — the combination of low plasma osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine, natriuresis, euvolemia, and normal endocrine tests is the hallmark of SIADH, as illustrated in this Behçet’s/neuro‑inflammatory case.

### [Management] Outline an evidence-based empiric treatment strategy for an older adult presenting with fever, headache, and suspected meningoencephalitis, and specify which CSF and PCR/culture results would allow you to safely discontinue acyclovir and broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Clinical pearl
In any older adult with fever, headache and concern for meningoencephalitis you must (1) obtain blood cultures and CSF studies promptly if safe, (2) start empiric antimicrobials immediately — covering the common bacterial pathogens and Listeria in older adults — and (3) start acyclovir empirically when encephalitis (altered mental status, seizures, focal deficits or strong clinical concern) is possible. Early, directed CSF testing (Gram stain, culture, cell count/differential, glucose, protein, and multiplex/HSV/VZV PCRs) is what allows safe, early de‑escalation.

Recommended empiric strategy (practical, evidence‑based)
- Before antibiotics: draw blood cultures. If no contraindication, do LP promptly. If LP is delayed (mass effect, anticoagulation, unstable patient), do CT first but do not delay empiric therapy for imaging.
- Empiric antimicrobials for community‑acquired disease in an adult ≥50 years:
  - Vancomycin IV (dose to target appropriate exposure) PLUS
  - A third‑generation cephalosporin that penetrates CSF (ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h or cefotaxime) OR cefepime if local epidemiology/hospital‑acquired organisms are a concern, PLUS
  - Ampicillin 2 g IV q4h for Listeria coverage (important in adults >50, immunocompromised).
- Empiric antiviral:
  - Acyclovir 10 mg/kg IV q8h (adjust for renal function) should be given whenever encephalitis is suspected (altered mental status, focal deficits, seizures) because HSV encephalitis is time‑sensitive.
- Adjuncts:
  - Dexamethasone is indicated when bacterial meningitis due to S. pneumoniae is likely (give before or with first antibiotic dose).
- Reassess at 24–72 hours with microbiology and imaging and narrow therapy as soon as it is safe.

Which CSF / PCR / culture results allow you to stop acyclovir
- A reliably negative CSF HSV PCR in the appropriate clinical context is the key test:
  - If CSF HSV PCR is negative and the clinical picture and MRI do not suggest HSV encephalitis (no temporal‑lobe changes, no focal seizure onset, no marked RBCs in CSF) and the patient is improving or stable, it is reasonable to stop acyclovir.
  - If initial PCR is negative but clinical suspicion remains high (classic temporal‑lobe MRI changes, focal neurologic signs, seizures, or a deteriorating patient), continue acyclovir and repeat CSF HSV PCR in ~48 hours rather than stopping immediately.
- Negative VZV PCR or other viral PCRs support stopping empiric antiviral therapy directed at those pathogens.

Which CSF / PCR / culture results allow you to stop broad‑spectrum antibiotics
- De‑escalation or stopping of empiric antibiotics can be considered when all of the following are present:
  - CSF Gram stain is negative and CSF bacterial cultures remain negative at 48–72 hours (culture incubation usually detects common pathogens in this window), AND
  - Blood cultures are not growing a bacterial pathogen, AND
  - CSF profile is not consistent with bacterial meningitis (no neutrophilic predominance, CSF glucose is not low relative to serum, protein only mildly elevated) — i.e., a lymphocytic/aseptic picture, AND
  - A non‑infectious diagnosis is established or strongly suspected (autoimmune/aseptic meningitis, neuro‑Behçet’s disease as in this case), or a viral etiology is confirmed by PCR.
- If a multiplex CSF PCR panel is available and negative for bacterial targets, that provides additional reassurance and can permit earlier narrowing, but negative cultures remain the usual practical milestone at 48–72 hours.
- Special note for Listeria: in older adults keep ampicillin until cultures have had adequate time to become negative (generally 48–72 hours) unless an alternative diagnosis is secure; Listeria may not be seen on Gram stain.

How this maps to the presented case
- The patient in the report was an older adult with fever and headache. The team drew cultures and started empiric cefepime and, when CNS symptoms progressed, acyclovir. CSF showed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, mildly elevated protein, and opening pressure within a modest range; CSF testing for bacterial/mycobacterial pathogens and HSV/VZV (PCR) were negative. Those negative microbiologic results together with a CSF picture more consistent with aseptic meningitis, normal brain MRI, the emergence of characteristic mucocutaneous findings of Behçet’s disease, and clinical improvement after anti‑inflammatory therapy allowed the clinicians to stop antimicrobials and focus on immunotherapy (colchicine).
- This illustrates the practical rule: start broad empiric coverage early, obtain targeted CSF PCR/culture testing quickly, and stop acyclovir when HSV PCR is reliably negative (unless strong clinical/MRI evidence argues otherwise); stop broad‑spectrum antibiotics when Gram stain/cultures remain negative at 48–72 h and the CSF and clinical course point away from bacterial meningitis.

Bottom line
Treat early and broadly; use CSF Gram stain, culture and multiplex/HSV PCR to guide rapid de‑escalation. Stop acyclovir when CSF HSV PCR is negative and clinical/MRI data do not support HSV (repeat PCR in 48 h if suspicion remains). Stop empiric antibiotics when Gram stain and cultures are negative at 48–72 h, the CSF profile is not bacterial, blood cultures are negative, and an alternative diagnosis is established.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the proposed pathophysiologic link between CNS inflammation in acute neuro-Behçet’s disease and the development of SIADH, and which cytokine is most implicated in stimulating ADH release?

Answer (brief): Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease can provoke SIADH by CNS inflammation–driven, non‑osmotic stimulation of antidiuretic hormone release. The cytokine most implicated in this process is interleukin‑6 (IL‑6).

Why — pathophysiologic reasoning:
- Neuro‑BD produces intrathecal and systemic inflammation (in this case: CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, and a marked systemic inflammatory response with CRP 180 mg/L). Even when MRI is normal, microscopic or functional inflammation of hypothalamic/brainstem regions (or activation of circumventricular organs) can alter ADH regulation.
- Proinflammatory cytokines—chiefly IL‑6—are known to rise in neuroinflammatory states and to stimulate non‑osmotic ADH release from magnocellular neurons and the posterior pituitary (and/or act via circumventricular organs that lack a blood–brain barrier). The net effect is inappropriate water retention: hypotonic hyponatraemia with low serum osmolality and inappropriately concentrated urine.
- The clinical data in this case support that mechanism: serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine Na 88 mmol/L, clinically euvolemic, normal TSH and cortisol, and an elevated ADH (22.4 pg/mL) — all diagnostic of SIADH. IL‑6 itself was not measured here, but the very high CRP and the known biology of BD make an IL‑6–mediated pathway plausible.
- Therapeutic corroboration: treatment directed at inflammation (colchicine, which suppresses IL‑6 and other cytokines) coincided with clinical improvement, normalization of sodium, and a fall in ADH to 1.8 pg/mL — consistent with cytokine‑driven, reversible ADH excess.

Clinical pearl: In patients with BD and new hyponatraemia, think SIADH from neuroinflammation (often IL‑6 mediated). Manage acutely with standard SIADH measures (fluid restriction, avoid rapid correction) and treat the underlying CNS inflammation — this can reverse the inappropriate ADH secretion.

### [Management] In a euvolemic patient with SIADH-related hyponatremia without severe neurologic symptoms, what are the initial management steps, and what are the recommended maximum safe increases in serum sodium over 24 and 48 hours to minimize the risk of osmotic demyelination?

Short answer
- Initial management of euvolemic SIADH without severe neurologic symptoms: confirm the diagnosis and volume status, treat the underlying cause, institute free‑water restriction (≈800 mL/day), monitor serum sodium and urine studies frequently, and escalate only if sodium fails to rise or symptoms worsen (oral solute such as urea or salt tablets, loop diuretic ± urea; reserve vaptans or hypertonic saline for refractory or frankly symptomatic cases).
- Safe correction limits to minimise osmotic demyelination: aim for an increase of ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours and ≤18 mmol/L in 48 hours (use even more conservative targets—≈4–6 mmol/L/24 h—in high‑risk patients such as chronic hyponatraemia of unknown duration, alcoholism, malnourishment, advanced liver disease or hypokalemia).
  
Clinical reasoning and practical points
- Why fluid restriction first: SIADH is characterised by inappropriate ADH with concentrated urine (high urine osmolality and urine Na). Restricting free water reduces water retention and is the simplest effective initial therapy for a euvolemic patient. In the reported case the team used fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) and avoided aggressive measures — an approach that matched the patient’s mild–moderate symptoms and resulted in gradual, safe correction.
- Treat the cause: correcting the underlying inflammatory CNS process (here, neuro‑Behçet’s treated with colchicine) may reverse ADH excess and lead to spontaneous improvement in serum sodium; anticipate a brisk water diuresis as inflammation resolves and monitor closely.
- Monitoring and escalation: check serum sodium frequently while therapy is being adjusted (at least every 6–8 hours during active correction); follow urine output, urine osmolality and urine sodium. If fluid restriction fails, consider measures to increase solute excretion (oral urea, salt tablets) or a loop diuretic to induce free‑water loss. Vasopressin receptor antagonists (tolvaptan) can correct SIADH but carry risks (overcorrection, hepatic toxicity) and are generally reserved for selected, closely monitored cases.
- Avoiding osmotic demyelination: the brain adapts to chronic hyponatraemia by losing intracellular osmolytes; overly rapid correction creates an osmotic gradient that can cause demyelination (central pontine and extrapontine). For most patients the internationally accepted ceilings are ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours and ≤18 mmol/L in 48 hours. For patients at especially high risk (chronic hyponatraemia, alcoholism, malnutrition, severe liver disease, severe hypokalemia) aim for a much smaller daily increase (≈4–6 mmol/L/day).
- Rescue if overcorrection occurs: if serum Na rises too quickly (e.g., >8 mmol/L in 24 h), promptly give desmopressin (to stop free‑water diuresis) and administer hypotonic fluid (D5W) to re‑lower serum sodium toward the safe target.

Takeaway from the case
- The team recognised euvolemic SIADH (Na 124 mmol/L, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine Na 88 mmol/L), treated the underlying neuro‑Behçet’s and used conservative management (fluid restriction, close monitoring). Sodium normalized slowly (to 136 mmol/L by day 16) without hypertonic therapy or vasopressin antagonists and without evidence of osmotic demyelination—illustrating the appropriate, safety‑first approach for a euvolemic SIADH patient without severe neurologic symptoms.

### [Management] For acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, what is the usual first-line immunotherapy, and in what clinical scenarios might a trial of colchicine monotherapy be reasonable despite limited evidence for efficacy in neuro-BD?

First‑line immunotherapy for acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease
- High‑dose systemic corticosteroids are the standard first‑line treatment for acute neuro‑BD — typically IV methylprednisolone pulses followed by an oral prednisone taper — because the condition is driven by CNS inflammation and early suppression of that inflammation reduces tissue injury and short‑term morbidity. For parenchymal, life‑threatening, relapsing, or steroid‑dependent disease, early addition of steroid‑sparing immunosuppressants (eg, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide) or biologics (eg, anti‑TNF agents) is generally recommended.

When a trial of colchicine monotherapy might be reasonable (despite limited evidence)
- Evidence: systematic reviews and series generally show colchicine is ineffective for acute neuro‑BD and it is usually used as an adjunct for mucocutaneous disease. The report you provided is a single, instructive exception: an older man with concurrent systemic BD and an aseptic meningitis–type neuro‑BD (normal brain MRI, mild lymphocytic CSF pleocytosis, no focal deficits) and poorly controlled diabetes improved after colchicine alone, with resolution of symptoms and SIADH.
- Practical circumstances in which a carefully supervised trial of colchicine monotherapy can be considered:
  - Presentation limited to non‑parenchymal neuro‑BD (aseptic meningitis/meningoencephalitis) with normal MRI and no focal neurologic deficits.
  - Mild clinical course, haemodynamic stability, and only modest CSF/serologic inflammation.
  - Significant reasons to avoid systemic steroids (eg, uncontrolled diabetes, active infection risk, patient refusal).
  - Ability to hospitalize and closely monitor neurologic status, electrolytes (especially sodium), and inflammatory markers, with a predefined, low threshold to escalate therapy.
- Caveats and monitoring/escape plan:
  - Treat any colchicine trial as provisional. Because spontaneous recovery can occur and because the evidence for colchicine in neuro‑BD is weak, monitor frequently (serial neurologic exams, daily sodium and basic labs, repeat MRI or CSF if clinically indicated).
  - Escalate promptly to high‑dose corticosteroids (and add immunosuppression) for any clinical deterioration: new or worsening focal deficits, decreased level of consciousness, new MRI parenchymal lesions, progressive CSF pleocytosis or inflammatory markers, persistent fever or failure to improve within 48–72 hours, or worsening SIADH.
  - In the reported case, colchicine likely helped mucocutaneous and CNS inflammation (it inhibits neutrophil activity and cytokines including IL‑6), and the patient’s diabetes provided a clinically relevant reason to avoid steroids; nonetheless this remains a single favourable outcome and not proof of efficacy.

Bottom line: start with high‑dose corticosteroids for most cases of acute neuro‑BD, and reserve colchicine monotherapy only for carefully selected, mild non‑parenchymal presentations or when steroids are contraindicated — with inpatient monitoring and a low threshold to escalate to standard immunotherapy.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How can acute neuro-Behçet’s disease present when brain MRI is normal, and which CSF profile (cells, protein, microbiologic testing) would support this diagnosis over viral meningitis?

Bottom line
- Acute neuro‑Behçet’s can present as an aseptic meningo‑encephalitic syndrome (headache, fever, malaise, hiccups, vertigo, mild cognitive symptoms) with a completely normal brain MRI, especially early or when inflammation is limited to the meninges. In that situation the diagnosis rests on the clinical phenotype (concurrent mucocutaneous BD features) plus an “aseptic” CSF picture and exclusion of infection.
Why MRI may be normal
- Neuro‑BD is a vasculoinflammatory disorder that commonly produces focal parenchymal lesions (brainstem, basal ganglia) but can present as meningeal or diffuse perivascular inflammation that is radiographically occult in the early phase. The case illustrates this: normal brain MRI despite clear CNS symptoms. A normal MRI therefore does not exclude neuro‑BD.
Which CSF profile supports neuro‑BD over viral meningitis
- Cells: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis. In the reported patient the CSF WBC was 11 cells/µL (lymphocyte predominant) — consistent with aseptic meningitis from neuro‑BD. Viral meningitis can also produce lymphocytic pleocytosis, but very high counts or a clear temporal evolution with encephalitic features point more toward viral causes.
- Protein: moderately elevated. The case had CSF protein ~0.97 g/L (~97 mg/dL). A raised protein with only mild lymphocytosis favors an inflammatory/autoimmune aseptic meningitis such as neuro‑BD rather than an untreated bacterial process; viral meningitis often has only mild protein elevation but there is overlap.
- Microbiologic testing: sterile CSF with negative bacterial culture, negative mycobacterial tests and negative PCR/antigen testing for common neurotropic viruses (HSV, VZV, enterovirus) favors neuro‑BD. In this patient CSF cultures and tests for HSV/VZV and cryptococcal antigen were negative — a critical part of excluding viral (and other infectious) causes. A positive pathogen PCR would argue for viral meningitis and should prompt antiviral therapy.
- Glucose/opening pressure/cytology: normal CSF glucose and absence of malignant cells further support an inflammatory (noninfectious) process. In the case the opening pressure was only mildly raised (17 cm H2O) and cytology was negative.
Clinical reasoning (how to apply this at the bedside)
1. In any patient with fever/headache and mucocutaneous findings of Behçet’s (oral/genital ulcers, folliculitis‑like rash), consider neuro‑BD even if MRI is normal.
2. Perform an LP early: cell count with diff, protein, glucose, opening pressure, Gram stain/culture, targeted PCRs (HSV/VZV/enterovirus), TB testing and cytology as indicated. Neuro‑BD is a diagnosis of exclusion — you must rule out treatable infections.
3. If CSF shows mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, moderately elevated protein, normal glucose and negative microbiology in the correct clinical context, this pattern supports neuro‑BD over viral meningitis.
4. Treat based on severity and probability: corticosteroids ± additional immunosuppression are standard for most acute neuro‑BD, but some meningeal presentations may improve with colchicine or even spontaneously (as in this case). Always start empiric antimicrobials/antivirals until infectious causes are reasonably excluded.
Pearl
- Normal MRI does not rule out acute neuro‑Behçet’s. The combination of sterile (aseptic) CSF with mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and raised protein, negative pathogen PCR/culture, and concurrent BD mucocutaneous signs is the key to favouring neuro‑BD over viral meningitis — and explains the favourable response to anti‑inflammatory therapy in the reported patient.

### [Clinical Pearls] How should HLA-B51 testing influence your diagnostic approach to Behçet’s disease—what does a positive or negative result add to classification or clinical decision-making?

Bottom line: HLA‑B51 is a susceptibility marker for Behçet’s disease (BD) — a positive result raises the probability of BD in a patient with compatible features, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient to make the diagnosis. A negative test does not rule out BD and should not by itself alter classification or treatment decisions.

Why: HLA‑B51 is an MHC class I allele consistently associated with increased risk of BD in many populations (the strength of association varies by ethnicity). It therefore has modest diagnostic value at the population level (it increases the odds of disease several‑fold in meta‑analyses) but limited positive or negative predictive value for an individual patient, because the allele is present in a substantial minority of healthy people in some regions and absent in many BD patients. Current international classification schemes and clinical practice rely on characteristic clinical findings (oral/genital ulcers, ocular and skin lesions, vascular or neurologic involvement, pathergy) rather than HLA status; HLA‑B51 is not required for classification.

How to use the test in practice: order HLA‑B51 only as an adjunct when the clinical picture is equivocal and you need supportive evidence (intermediate pretest probability), or for epidemiologic/research purposes. Interpret results in the context of pretest probability and local allele prevalence. Do not use HLA‑B51 to:
- make the diagnosis in the absence of sufficient clinical criteria,
- exclude BD when negative,
- justify initiation of major immunosuppression or to decide intensity of therapy by itself.

Prognostic and management nuances: some studies link HLA‑B51 to particular phenotypes (earlier onset, more ocular or severe disease in some cohorts), so a positive result may prompt closer surveillance (for example, early ophthalmologic follow‑up), but this is not an established trigger to change therapy. Clinical severity and organ involvement should drive treatment choices.

Application to the presented case: the patient met international criteria for definite BD on clinical grounds (oral and genital aphthae, characteristic skin lesions) and had acute neuro‑BD with SIADH. The authors correctly report that although HLA‑B51 was positive, it did not contribute meaningfully to establishing the diagnosis or determining management — a useful example of HLA‑B51’s limited role in individual clinical decision‑making.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which atypical symptoms or laboratory abnormalities should prompt you to suspect profound hyponatremia in neuroinflammatory illnesses, and what is the mechanism by which hyponatremia can cause rhabdomyolysis?

Clinical pearl — in neuroinflammatory syndromes, think “profound hyponatraemia” not just when patients are obtunded or seizing, but when you see otherwise unexplained constitutional or non‑specific findings and certain laboratory hints.

Which atypical symptoms or lab abnormalities should raise suspicion?
- Persistent hiccups and anorexia/poor oral intake. In the reported case of acute neuro‑Behçet’s the patient’s persistent hiccups and loss of appetite were early, prominent complaints and have been reported as a useful clinical clue to severe hyponatraemia from CNS inflammation.
- New or out‑of‑proportion nonspecific CNS symptoms: headache, vertigo, behavioural change or confusion when imaging is unrevealing (this patient had headache/vertigo with a normal brain MRI).
- Unexplained myalgias or elevated “hepatic” enzymes with no liver cause, especially AST>ALT. Rhabdomyolysis can present silently; in the case CK was 1,349 U/L with modest AST/ALT elevations despite no objective muscle weakness.
- Dark urine, oliguria, or any evidence of muscle injury (rise in CK, myoglobinuria).
- Classic laboratory signature of SIADH: euvolaemic hyponatraemia with low serum osmolality (case: 267 mOsm/kg), inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality very high — 900 mOsm/kg here), elevated urine sodium (88 mmol/L), and normal thyroid/adrenal testing. An elevated ADH level (22.4 pg/mL in this patient) supports inappropriate ADH secretion when available.

Why does hyponatraemia cause rhabdomyolysis?
- Hyponatraemia lowers extracellular osmolality → water moves into myocytes → cellular swelling. Swelling stresses and can disrupt the sarcolemma.
- Ionic dysregulation amplifies injury: a reduced transmembrane Na+ gradient impairs Na+/K+‑ATPase and Na+/Ca2+ exchange, leading to intracellular Ca2+ accumulation. Raised intracellular Ca2+ activates proteases and phospholipases, damages mitochondria, depletes ATP and culminates in membrane breakdown and myocyte necrosis.
- Clinically, this sequence produces release of CK and myoglobin (risking acute kidney injury). Seizures, prolonged muscle activity or coexisting electrolyte problems (hypokalaemia, hypophosphataemia) can exacerbate the process.

Practical takeaways
- In any patient with CNS inflammation who has hiccups, anorexia, unexplained transaminase elevations or elevated CK, check a serum sodium (and urine studies) promptly.
- Diagnose SIADH by the standard criteria (euvolaemia, hypoosmolar serum, inappropriately concentrated urine, elevated urine Na, normal adrenal/thyroid function) and seek an underlying CNS cause (neuroinflammation — IL‑6 mediated ADH release is a plausible mechanism, as discussed in the report).
- If rhabdomyolysis is present, monitor CK, renal function and electrolytes closely. Management must balance the need to treat SIADH (fluid restriction, treat the underlying inflammation — colchicine improved sodium and CK in this patient) against the usual rhabdomyolysis strategy of volume repletion; this frequently requires individualized care and nephrology input. Always avoid overly rapid correction of chronic hyponatraemia to prevent osmotic demyelination.

Bottom line: persistent hiccups or unexplained myocyte injury in a patient with CNS inflammation should trigger an early check of sodium and urine studies — recognizing SIADH and the risk of hyponatraemia‑induced rhabdomyolysis can prevent missed diagnosis and guide safe, coordinated management.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with new Behçet’s disease and hyponatremia, list three non-CNS precipitants of SIADH that should be actively excluded (e.g., medications, pulmonary disease, malignancy), and identify a key screening test or history element for each.

Short answer
After you confirm hypotonic, euvolemic hyponatraemia and exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency (as was done in this case), actively exclude three high‑yield non‑CNS precipitants of SIADH: (1) medications, (2) pulmonary disease/infection, and (3) malignancy (classically small‑cell lung cancer). For each I list the practical screening step and the rationale.

1) Medications — screening element: focused, time‑linked medication reconciliation (prescription, OTC, herbal, illicit, and recent chemotherapy exposures).  
- Why: many drugs either increase ADH release or potentiate its renal effect (classic culprits: SSRIs/SNRIs/TCAs, carbamazepine/oxcarbazepine, opioids, some antipsychotics, NSAIDs, and cytotoxics such as cyclophosphamide). Drug‑induced SIADH commonly appears days–weeks after starting or changing dose.  
- Case tie‑in: the authors specifically note cyclophosphamide has been reported to cause SIADH in BD; in their patient no such agents preceded hyponatraemia and colchicine was started only after the low sodium was present.

2) Pulmonary disease/infection (pneumonia, tuberculosis, empyema, COPD exacerbation) — screening element: chest radiograph as an immediate screen and targeted respiratory history (cough, sputum, fever, dyspnea, night sweats); if clinical suspicion persists, chest CT and sputum studies (including AFB/IGRA for TB).  
- Why: pulmonary pathology and hypoxia can stimulate ADH release through pulmonary afferent reflexes and inflammatory cytokines. Pneumonia and TB are common non‑CNS causes of SIADH.  
- Case tie‑in: the team obtained chest X‑ray and chest CT, which were unremarkable, appropriately reducing the likelihood of a pulmonary cause.

3) Malignancy (particularly small‑cell lung cancer causing ectopic ADH) — screening element: focused oncologic history (smoking, weight loss, hemoptysis, constitutional symptoms) and directed imaging — chest CT (or PET/CT) rather than relying solely on CXR when suspicion is high.  
- Why: small‑cell lung carcinoma commonly secretes ADH ectopically; other tumors can also produce paraneoplastic SIADH. Imaging is the most sensitive initial screen.  
- Case tie‑in: the authors performed CT imaging of chest/abdomen and found no malignant focus; a mildly elevated sIL‑2R was non‑specific and did not indicate malignancy.

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
SIADH is a diagnosis of exclusion — before ascribing it to neuro‑Behçet’s (or other CNS inflammation and cytokine‑mediated ADH release), do a rapid, high‑yield workup: thorough medication reconciliation (recent starts/dose changes), chest X‑ray/CT and focused pulmonary history (plus TB testing if indicated), and targeted cancer screening with chest CT when paraneoplastic SIADH is a concern. In the reported patient these non‑CNS causes were actively searched for and not found, supporting attribution to neuro‑BD.
