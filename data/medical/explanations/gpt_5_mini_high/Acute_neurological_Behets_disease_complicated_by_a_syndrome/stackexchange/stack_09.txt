Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] Which atypical symptoms or laboratory abnormalities should prompt you to suspect profound hyponatremia in neuroinflammatory illnesses, and what is the mechanism by which hyponatremia can cause rhabdomyolysis?

Clinical pearl — in neuroinflammatory syndromes, think “profound hyponatraemia” not just when patients are obtunded or seizing, but when you see otherwise unexplained constitutional or non‑specific findings and certain laboratory hints.

Which atypical symptoms or lab abnormalities should raise suspicion?
- Persistent hiccups and anorexia/poor oral intake. In the reported case of acute neuro‑Behçet’s the patient’s persistent hiccups and loss of appetite were early, prominent complaints and have been reported as a useful clinical clue to severe hyponatraemia from CNS inflammation.
- New or out‑of‑proportion nonspecific CNS symptoms: headache, vertigo, behavioural change or confusion when imaging is unrevealing (this patient had headache/vertigo with a normal brain MRI).
- Unexplained myalgias or elevated “hepatic” enzymes with no liver cause, especially AST>ALT. Rhabdomyolysis can present silently; in the case CK was 1,349 U/L with modest AST/ALT elevations despite no objective muscle weakness.
- Dark urine, oliguria, or any evidence of muscle injury (rise in CK, myoglobinuria).
- Classic laboratory signature of SIADH: euvolaemic hyponatraemia with low serum osmolality (case: 267 mOsm/kg), inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality very high — 900 mOsm/kg here), elevated urine sodium (88 mmol/L), and normal thyroid/adrenal testing. An elevated ADH level (22.4 pg/mL in this patient) supports inappropriate ADH secretion when available.

Why does hyponatraemia cause rhabdomyolysis?
- Hyponatraemia lowers extracellular osmolality → water moves into myocytes → cellular swelling. Swelling stresses and can disrupt the sarcolemma.
- Ionic dysregulation amplifies injury: a reduced transmembrane Na+ gradient impairs Na+/K+‑ATPase and Na+/Ca2+ exchange, leading to intracellular Ca2+ accumulation. Raised intracellular Ca2+ activates proteases and phospholipases, damages mitochondria, depletes ATP and culminates in membrane breakdown and myocyte necrosis.
- Clinically, this sequence produces release of CK and myoglobin (risking acute kidney injury). Seizures, prolonged muscle activity or coexisting electrolyte problems (hypokalaemia, hypophosphataemia) can exacerbate the process.

Practical takeaways
- In any patient with CNS inflammation who has hiccups, anorexia, unexplained transaminase elevations or elevated CK, check a serum sodium (and urine studies) promptly.
- Diagnose SIADH by the standard criteria (euvolaemia, hypoosmolar serum, inappropriately concentrated urine, elevated urine Na, normal adrenal/thyroid function) and seek an underlying CNS cause (neuroinflammation — IL‑6 mediated ADH release is a plausible mechanism, as discussed in the report).
- If rhabdomyolysis is present, monitor CK, renal function and electrolytes closely. Management must balance the need to treat SIADH (fluid restriction, treat the underlying inflammation — colchicine improved sodium and CK in this patient) against the usual rhabdomyolysis strategy of volume repletion; this frequently requires individualized care and nephrology input. Always avoid overly rapid correction of chronic hyponatraemia to prevent osmotic demyelination.

Bottom line: persistent hiccups or unexplained myocyte injury in a patient with CNS inflammation should trigger an early check of sodium and urine studies — recognizing SIADH and the risk of hyponatraemia‑induced rhabdomyolysis can prevent missed diagnosis and guide safe, coordinated management.
