Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] How should HLA-B51 testing influence your diagnostic approach to Behçet’s disease—what does a positive or negative result add to classification or clinical decision-making?

Bottom line: HLA‑B51 is a susceptibility marker for Behçet’s disease (BD) — a positive result raises the probability of BD in a patient with compatible features, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient to make the diagnosis. A negative test does not rule out BD and should not by itself alter classification or treatment decisions.

Why: HLA‑B51 is an MHC class I allele consistently associated with increased risk of BD in many populations (the strength of association varies by ethnicity). It therefore has modest diagnostic value at the population level (it increases the odds of disease several‑fold in meta‑analyses) but limited positive or negative predictive value for an individual patient, because the allele is present in a substantial minority of healthy people in some regions and absent in many BD patients. Current international classification schemes and clinical practice rely on characteristic clinical findings (oral/genital ulcers, ocular and skin lesions, vascular or neurologic involvement, pathergy) rather than HLA status; HLA‑B51 is not required for classification.

How to use the test in practice: order HLA‑B51 only as an adjunct when the clinical picture is equivocal and you need supportive evidence (intermediate pretest probability), or for epidemiologic/research purposes. Interpret results in the context of pretest probability and local allele prevalence. Do not use HLA‑B51 to:
- make the diagnosis in the absence of sufficient clinical criteria,
- exclude BD when negative,
- justify initiation of major immunosuppression or to decide intensity of therapy by itself.

Prognostic and management nuances: some studies link HLA‑B51 to particular phenotypes (earlier onset, more ocular or severe disease in some cohorts), so a positive result may prompt closer surveillance (for example, early ophthalmologic follow‑up), but this is not an established trigger to change therapy. Clinical severity and organ involvement should drive treatment choices.

Application to the presented case: the patient met international criteria for definite BD on clinical grounds (oral and genital aphthae, characteristic skin lesions) and had acute neuro‑BD with SIADH. The authors correctly report that although HLA‑B51 was positive, it did not contribute meaningfully to establishing the diagnosis or determining management — a useful example of HLA‑B51’s limited role in individual clinical decision‑making.
