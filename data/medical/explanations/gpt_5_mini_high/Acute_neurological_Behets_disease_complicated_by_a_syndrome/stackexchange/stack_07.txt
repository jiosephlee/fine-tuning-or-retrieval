Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Differential Diagnosis] How can acute neuro-Behçet’s disease present when brain MRI is normal, and which CSF profile (cells, protein, microbiologic testing) would support this diagnosis over viral meningitis?

Bottom line
- Acute neuro‑Behçet’s can present as an aseptic meningo‑encephalitic syndrome (headache, fever, malaise, hiccups, vertigo, mild cognitive symptoms) with a completely normal brain MRI, especially early or when inflammation is limited to the meninges. In that situation the diagnosis rests on the clinical phenotype (concurrent mucocutaneous BD features) plus an “aseptic” CSF picture and exclusion of infection.
Why MRI may be normal
- Neuro‑BD is a vasculoinflammatory disorder that commonly produces focal parenchymal lesions (brainstem, basal ganglia) but can present as meningeal or diffuse perivascular inflammation that is radiographically occult in the early phase. The case illustrates this: normal brain MRI despite clear CNS symptoms. A normal MRI therefore does not exclude neuro‑BD.
Which CSF profile supports neuro‑BD over viral meningitis
- Cells: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis. In the reported patient the CSF WBC was 11 cells/µL (lymphocyte predominant) — consistent with aseptic meningitis from neuro‑BD. Viral meningitis can also produce lymphocytic pleocytosis, but very high counts or a clear temporal evolution with encephalitic features point more toward viral causes.
- Protein: moderately elevated. The case had CSF protein ~0.97 g/L (~97 mg/dL). A raised protein with only mild lymphocytosis favors an inflammatory/autoimmune aseptic meningitis such as neuro‑BD rather than an untreated bacterial process; viral meningitis often has only mild protein elevation but there is overlap.
- Microbiologic testing: sterile CSF with negative bacterial culture, negative mycobacterial tests and negative PCR/antigen testing for common neurotropic viruses (HSV, VZV, enterovirus) favors neuro‑BD. In this patient CSF cultures and tests for HSV/VZV and cryptococcal antigen were negative — a critical part of excluding viral (and other infectious) causes. A positive pathogen PCR would argue for viral meningitis and should prompt antiviral therapy.
- Glucose/opening pressure/cytology: normal CSF glucose and absence of malignant cells further support an inflammatory (noninfectious) process. In the case the opening pressure was only mildly raised (17 cm H2O) and cytology was negative.
Clinical reasoning (how to apply this at the bedside)
1. In any patient with fever/headache and mucocutaneous findings of Behçet’s (oral/genital ulcers, folliculitis‑like rash), consider neuro‑BD even if MRI is normal.
2. Perform an LP early: cell count with diff, protein, glucose, opening pressure, Gram stain/culture, targeted PCRs (HSV/VZV/enterovirus), TB testing and cytology as indicated. Neuro‑BD is a diagnosis of exclusion — you must rule out treatable infections.
3. If CSF shows mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, moderately elevated protein, normal glucose and negative microbiology in the correct clinical context, this pattern supports neuro‑BD over viral meningitis.
4. Treat based on severity and probability: corticosteroids ± additional immunosuppression are standard for most acute neuro‑BD, but some meningeal presentations may improve with colchicine or even spontaneously (as in this case). Always start empiric antimicrobials/antivirals until infectious causes are reasonably excluded.
Pearl
- Normal MRI does not rule out acute neuro‑Behçet’s. The combination of sterile (aseptic) CSF with mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and raised protein, negative pathogen PCR/culture, and concurrent BD mucocutaneous signs is the key to favouring neuro‑BD over viral meningitis — and explains the favourable response to anti‑inflammatory therapy in the reported patient.
