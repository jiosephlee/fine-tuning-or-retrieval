Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Management] In a euvolemic patient with SIADH-related hyponatremia without severe neurologic symptoms, what are the initial management steps, and what are the recommended maximum safe increases in serum sodium over 24 and 48 hours to minimize the risk of osmotic demyelination?

Short answer
- Initial management of euvolemic SIADH without severe neurologic symptoms: confirm the diagnosis and volume status, treat the underlying cause, institute free‑water restriction (≈800 mL/day), monitor serum sodium and urine studies frequently, and escalate only if sodium fails to rise or symptoms worsen (oral solute such as urea or salt tablets, loop diuretic ± urea; reserve vaptans or hypertonic saline for refractory or frankly symptomatic cases).
- Safe correction limits to minimise osmotic demyelination: aim for an increase of ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours and ≤18 mmol/L in 48 hours (use even more conservative targets—≈4–6 mmol/L/24 h—in high‑risk patients such as chronic hyponatraemia of unknown duration, alcoholism, malnourishment, advanced liver disease or hypokalemia).
  
Clinical reasoning and practical points
- Why fluid restriction first: SIADH is characterised by inappropriate ADH with concentrated urine (high urine osmolality and urine Na). Restricting free water reduces water retention and is the simplest effective initial therapy for a euvolemic patient. In the reported case the team used fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) and avoided aggressive measures — an approach that matched the patient’s mild–moderate symptoms and resulted in gradual, safe correction.
- Treat the cause: correcting the underlying inflammatory CNS process (here, neuro‑Behçet’s treated with colchicine) may reverse ADH excess and lead to spontaneous improvement in serum sodium; anticipate a brisk water diuresis as inflammation resolves and monitor closely.
- Monitoring and escalation: check serum sodium frequently while therapy is being adjusted (at least every 6–8 hours during active correction); follow urine output, urine osmolality and urine sodium. If fluid restriction fails, consider measures to increase solute excretion (oral urea, salt tablets) or a loop diuretic to induce free‑water loss. Vasopressin receptor antagonists (tolvaptan) can correct SIADH but carry risks (overcorrection, hepatic toxicity) and are generally reserved for selected, closely monitored cases.
- Avoiding osmotic demyelination: the brain adapts to chronic hyponatraemia by losing intracellular osmolytes; overly rapid correction creates an osmotic gradient that can cause demyelination (central pontine and extrapontine). For most patients the internationally accepted ceilings are ≤8 mmol/L in 24 hours and ≤18 mmol/L in 48 hours. For patients at especially high risk (chronic hyponatraemia, alcoholism, malnutrition, severe liver disease, severe hypokalemia) aim for a much smaller daily increase (≈4–6 mmol/L/day).
- Rescue if overcorrection occurs: if serum Na rises too quickly (e.g., >8 mmol/L in 24 h), promptly give desmopressin (to stop free‑water diuresis) and administer hypotonic fluid (D5W) to re‑lower serum sodium toward the safe target.

Takeaway from the case
- The team recognised euvolemic SIADH (Na 124 mmol/L, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine Na 88 mmol/L), treated the underlying neuro‑Behçet’s and used conservative management (fluid restriction, close monitoring). Sodium normalized slowly (to 136 mmol/L by day 16) without hypertonic therapy or vasopressin antagonists and without evidence of osmotic demyelination—illustrating the appropriate, safety‑first approach for a euvolemic SIADH patient without severe neurologic symptoms.
