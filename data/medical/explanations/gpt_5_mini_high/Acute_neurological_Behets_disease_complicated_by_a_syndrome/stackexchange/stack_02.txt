Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] What laboratory criteria define SIADH in a patient with hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, and how do these results help you distinguish SIADH from hypovolemic hyponatremia or endocrine causes (adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism)?

Direct answer — the laboratory definition and how to use the results to separate SIADH from hypovolemia or endocrine causes:

1) Laboratory criteria that define SIADH in a patient with hypo‑osmolar hyponatraemia
- Confirm true hypotonic hyponatraemia: low serum sodium with low plasma osmolality. (In this case: Na 124 mmol/L with serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg.)
- Inappropriately concentrated urine for a hypotonic state: urine osmolality is not maximally suppressed (classically >100 mOsm/kg; many clinicians use >300 mOsm/kg as the cut point to indicate active ADH). (Case: urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg.)
- Renal natriuresis despite hyponatraemia: elevated urine sodium on a normal salt intake (commonly >30–40 mmol/L; the report uses >20 mmol/L as reference). (Case: urine Na 88 mmol/L.)
- Clinical euvolemia (no signs of volume depletion or overload).
- Exclusion of other causes that can mimic SIADH: normal renal function, no recent diuretic use, and normal adrenal and thyroid function. (Case: creatinine normal, no diuretics reported, TSH 1.11 mIU/L and free T4 1.25 ng/dL normal, morning cortisol 42 µg/dL.)

Supportive findings that strengthen the diagnosis: low BUN and low uric acid (not reported in this paper), and — if available — an inappropriately high plasma ADH (or copeptin). In this patient ADH was elevated (22.4 pg/mL) when hyponatraemic and fell to 1.8 pg/mL as sodium normalized, which is biochemical confirmation of inappropriate ADH activity.

2) How these results distinguish SIADH from hypovolemic hyponatraemia or endocrine causes
- Hypovolemic hyponatraemia (extrarenal losses): ADH is appropriately high because of volume depletion. Key lab differences:
  - Urine Na is typically low (<20 mmol/L) because the kidney avidly retains sodium (unless there is renal salt wasting or recent diuretic use, in which case urine Na can be high).
  - Clinical findings of hypovolemia (orthostasis, tachycardia, dry mucosa, low JVP) are usually present.
  - BUN and uric acid are often elevated.
  In contrast, SIADH shows euvolemia, high urine Na (natriuresis) and low BUN/uric acid. In the case the combination of euvolemia and urine Na 88 mmol/L pointed away from extrarenal hypovolemia.

- Adrenal insufficiency (secondary or primary): cortisol deficiency removes tonic suppression of ADH and may produce a biochemical picture similar to SIADH (hyponatraemia with concentrated urine and natriuresis). Important distinguishing tests:
  - Measure morning serum cortisol and consider a cosyntropin stimulation test if the cortisol is not clearly normal/high. Primary adrenal insufficiency often causes hyperkalemia and hypotension; urine Na may be high because of mineralocorticoid loss.
  - In this patient a high morning cortisol (42 µg/dL) effectively excluded adrenal insufficiency as the cause of the hyponatraemia.

- Hypothyroidism: severe hypothyroidism can cause hyponatraemia via decreased cardiac output/GFR and increased ADH. Distinguish by measuring TSH and free T4. In this case TSH and free T4 were normal, excluding hypothyroidism.

Practical diagnostic sequence (apply to the case):
1. Confirm hypotonic hyponatraemia (done).
2. Assess volume status clinically.
3. Measure urine osmolality and urine sodium. If urine osmolality is high (>100–300) and urine Na is not low, think SIADH (after excluding diuretics and renal failure).
4. Exclude endocrine causes (TSH, morning cortisol ± cosyntropin).
5. If all criteria are met, label SIADH and treat underlying cause and fluid‑restrict. In this patient the laboratory pattern (serum osmolality 267, urine osmolality 900, urine Na 88), clinical euvolemia, normal thyroid and adrenal tests and an elevated ADH level pointed to SIADH; conservative management with fluid restriction and treating the underlying neuroinflammatory disorder led to normalization of sodium (136 mmol/L) and fall in ADH.

Teaching pearl: always exclude adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism before diagnosing SIADH, and interpret urine sodium in the context of diuretics and clinical volume status — the combination of low plasma osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine, natriuresis, euvolemia, and normal endocrine tests is the hallmark of SIADH, as illustrated in this Behçet’s/neuro‑inflammatory case.
