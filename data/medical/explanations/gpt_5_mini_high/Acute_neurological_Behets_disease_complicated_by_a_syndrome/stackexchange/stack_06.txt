Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Management] For acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, what is the usual first-line immunotherapy, and in what clinical scenarios might a trial of colchicine monotherapy be reasonable despite limited evidence for efficacy in neuro-BD?

First‑line immunotherapy for acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease
- High‑dose systemic corticosteroids are the standard first‑line treatment for acute neuro‑BD — typically IV methylprednisolone pulses followed by an oral prednisone taper — because the condition is driven by CNS inflammation and early suppression of that inflammation reduces tissue injury and short‑term morbidity. For parenchymal, life‑threatening, relapsing, or steroid‑dependent disease, early addition of steroid‑sparing immunosuppressants (eg, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide) or biologics (eg, anti‑TNF agents) is generally recommended.

When a trial of colchicine monotherapy might be reasonable (despite limited evidence)
- Evidence: systematic reviews and series generally show colchicine is ineffective for acute neuro‑BD and it is usually used as an adjunct for mucocutaneous disease. The report you provided is a single, instructive exception: an older man with concurrent systemic BD and an aseptic meningitis–type neuro‑BD (normal brain MRI, mild lymphocytic CSF pleocytosis, no focal deficits) and poorly controlled diabetes improved after colchicine alone, with resolution of symptoms and SIADH.
- Practical circumstances in which a carefully supervised trial of colchicine monotherapy can be considered:
  - Presentation limited to non‑parenchymal neuro‑BD (aseptic meningitis/meningoencephalitis) with normal MRI and no focal neurologic deficits.
  - Mild clinical course, haemodynamic stability, and only modest CSF/serologic inflammation.
  - Significant reasons to avoid systemic steroids (eg, uncontrolled diabetes, active infection risk, patient refusal).
  - Ability to hospitalize and closely monitor neurologic status, electrolytes (especially sodium), and inflammatory markers, with a predefined, low threshold to escalate therapy.
- Caveats and monitoring/escape plan:
  - Treat any colchicine trial as provisional. Because spontaneous recovery can occur and because the evidence for colchicine in neuro‑BD is weak, monitor frequently (serial neurologic exams, daily sodium and basic labs, repeat MRI or CSF if clinically indicated).
  - Escalate promptly to high‑dose corticosteroids (and add immunosuppression) for any clinical deterioration: new or worsening focal deficits, decreased level of consciousness, new MRI parenchymal lesions, progressive CSF pleocytosis or inflammatory markers, persistent fever or failure to improve within 48–72 hours, or worsening SIADH.
  - In the reported case, colchicine likely helped mucocutaneous and CNS inflammation (it inhibits neutrophil activity and cytokines including IL‑6), and the patient’s diabetes provided a clinically relevant reason to avoid steroids; nonetheless this remains a single favourable outcome and not proof of efficacy.

Bottom line: start with high‑dose corticosteroids for most cases of acute neuro‑BD, and reserve colchicine monotherapy only for carefully selected, mild non‑parenchymal presentations or when steroids are contraindicated — with inpatient monitoring and a low threshold to escalate to standard immunotherapy.
