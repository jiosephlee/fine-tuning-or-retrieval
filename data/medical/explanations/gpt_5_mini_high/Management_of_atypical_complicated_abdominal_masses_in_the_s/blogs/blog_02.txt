Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Second-Trimester Myomectomy for Torsion and Mass Effect: Doing the Minimum Necessary

## Why this case is worth your attention
We think of myomectomy in pregnancy as a last-resort move — most are managed expectantly because surgery risks bleeding, pregnancy loss and preterm labour. This case flips that script in a sensible way: a nulliparous woman at 21+ weeks presented with two huge, cystic abdominal masses causing pain and dyspnoea. Imaging and CA‑125 were equivocal, and the working differential included decidualised endometriomas versus cystic degeneration of fibroids. At laparotomy the “upper adnexal” lesion turned out to be a pedunculated, twisted, leaking, highly vascular uterine leiomyoma. The team removed only that offending lesion (double‑clamp, cut the stalk, close the root with an absorbable stitch), left the intramural/subserosal pelvic myoma in situ, protected the pregnancy, and the patient subsequently delivered a healthy baby at term by elective cesarean for mechanical obstruction from the residual myoma. The pathology confirmed ischemic necrosis around the twisted pedicle — exactly the intraoperative risk the team had targeted.

This is a great example of a focused, risk‑proportionate strategy: when surgery is necessary in pregnancy, do the minimum that treats the threat while minimizing uterine trauma.

## The clinical picture (the facts)
A woman in her 40s, BMI 33, G1P0 at 21 weeks presented with acute upper‑left abdominal pain (VAS 5) and mild dyspnoea. Exam showed an 18 cm taut mass in the left subphrenic region and a fixed 9 cm pelvic mass on bimanual exam. Labs: Hb ~9.9 g/dL with iron deficiency, WBC and CRP mildly raised; urine culture grew Klebsiella. Ultrasound showed two large unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularity (upper 18×12×7 cm; lower ~9.5 cm adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma ~58×71 mm). Tumour markers were otherwise normal but CA‑125 was mildly elevated and rising (36 → 41.8 U/mL). Non‑contrast MRI (performed without gadolinium) showed thick‑walled, inhomogeneous cystic lesions with focal solid components; cleavage of the pelvic mass from the uterus was unclear. Worsening pain, mass effect with restrictive symptoms, diagnostic uncertainty and concern for torsion/infection prompted surgical exploration.

Intraoperatively the cranial mass was a pedunculated, twisted, leaking uterine leiomyoma; the surgeon excised it with a double clamp and closed the stalk with an absorbable suture. The pelvic lesion was fixed and left in place. Peritoneal fluid cultures/cytology were negative; pathology showed benign leiomyoma with ischemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle. The patient required IV iron and two transfusions post‑op but recovered and had an uncomplicated pregnancy follow‑up; elective C‑section at 37 weeks (same scar) for mechanical obstruction from the residual myoma gave an excellent neonatal outcome.

## When to consider surgery in pregnancy: practical indications
Surgery in pregnancy should be exceptional, but the following scenarios justify operative management — and were specifically relevant in this case:
- Torsion of a pedunculated fibroid (vascular compromise → necrosis, fever, leakage)  
- Persistent, severe pain or progressive restrictive symptoms (eg, diaphragmatic compromise causing dyspnoea) not responding to conservative care  
- Evidence or strong suspicion of complication (rupture, infection, ongoing hemorrhagic degeneration)  
- Diagnostic uncertainty with concern for malignancy (serial markers and imaging escalation can help)  
- Mass effect that will prevent uterine expansion or threaten maternal/fetal physiology

Key point: the decision should be multidisciplinary (obstetrics, gynecology, surgery, anesthesia, neonatology) and individualized to gestational age, lesion type and maternal condition.

## Imaging and tumour markers — how to interpret them in pregnancy
Ultrasound with Doppler is the first-line tool. Non‑contrast MRI at 1.5T is a safe, high‑yield adjunct after the first trimester and often clarifies relationships between masses and the uterine wall. Avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary.

CA‑125 can be misleading in pregnancy — it physiologically rises in the first and near‑term third trimester and small elevations alone are not diagnostic. Serial trends are more informative than a single value. In this case a rising CA‑125 added concern but did not make the diagnosis.

## Surgical strategy: do the minimum necessary
This case illustrates a simple principle: remove the lesion doing most harm and avoid creating new problems.

Practical intraoperative strategy used here
- Confirm fetal viability before and during the operation (the team documented fetal cardiac activity and used serial transvaginal ultrasound intraoperatively).  
- Sample peritoneal fluid for culture/cytology if there is diagnostic uncertainty.  
- Target pedunculated lesions first: control the stalk (double clamp was used here), transect, and close the uterine base with an absorbable suture. That eliminates the source of ischemia and sepsis risk while avoiding a large myometrial incision.  
- Minimize uterine manipulation and keep the operation as short as possible. This team limited the procedure to the cranial lesion; the fixed pelvic myoma was left in situ to avoid massive bleeding and additional uterine trauma.  
- Perioperative planning is crucial: have blood products available and be ready to transfuse (this patient required two transfusions post‑op), use antibiotic prophylaxis and tailor postoperative antibiotics to cultures, and document plans for fetal monitoring and obstetric follow‑up.

Why pedunculated lesions are the best targets
Pedunculated fibroids are the closest thing to “easy wins” in pregnancy: the stalk provides a clear control point, they can be removed without entering the uterine cavity, and the operation often restores anatomy and relieves mass effect quickly. Intramural or sessile lesions often require larger myometrial incisions, carry much higher hemorrhage risk and are usually best left until after delivery unless absolutely necessary.

## Laparoscopy vs laparotomy — choosing the approach
Laparoscopic myomectomy in pregnancy is possible in selected patients (small, pedunculated lesions, early gestation, experienced laparoscopic team, open Hasson entry), and offers faster recovery. However, large lesions, multiple masses, intramural/critical locations, significant vascularity, or advanced gestational age frequently push the balance toward laparotomy for safety and exposure. In this case the size (18 cm), uncertain anatomy and second‑trimester uterus made a longitudinal xipho‑pubic laparotomy the pragmatic choice.

## Hemostasis and perioperative preparedness
Anticipate bleeding: type and crossmatch blood and be ready to transfuse. Hemostatic options include careful clamping/traction of the stalk, prompt suturing of the myometrial base, electrosurgery as appropriate, topical agents and, where available and indicated, uterine artery control techniques — but any adjunct should be chosen with pregnancy physiology in mind. Keep surgery focused and time on uterine manipulation to a minimum.

## Obstetric counseling and downstream planning
Counsel patients that operative intervention adds risk: miscarriage, preterm labour, hemorrhage and potential need for transfusion. Also explain the alternative — expectant management with conservative measures — and the reasons surgery may still be the safer path. Residual myomas can obstruct labour or cause complications later; in this case the remaining pelvic myoma was expected to block the fetal head, so an elective cesarean at term was planned and executed with an excellent result. Note that many fibroids partially regress postpartum, which may alter future surgical decisions.

The team in this case also used intraoperative and continued progesterone prophylaxis and performed close obstetric surveillance; these are management choices taken to support the pregnancy but not universally standardized.

## The bottom line
This case shows that a disciplined, minimalist surgical approach can be the right answer in pregnancy when the lesion is causing true harm. Pedunculated, twisted, leaking or infected leiomyomas are legitimate indications for targeted myomectomy in experienced hands. The aim is not to “clear all lesions” but to treat the immediate threat while protecting the pregnancy.

## Key take‑home messages
- Consider surgery in pregnancy only when there is torsion, frank ischemic degeneration with systemic signs, relentless pain/dyspnoea from mass effect, infection/rupture risk, or strong concern for malignancy not resolved by imaging.  
- Pedunculated fibroids are the safest targets: control the stalk, transect and close the base; this avoids large uterine incisions and preserves the pregnancy.  
- Leave fixed intramural or adherent masses in situ when possible — overtreatment increases hemorrhage and obstetric risk.  
- Use ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI (1.5T) for anatomical detail; interpret CA‑125 cautiously (physiologically variable in pregnancy). Serial measurements beat a single value.  
- Be prepared: multidisciplinary planning, fetal monitoring before/during/after surgery, blood products available, perioperative antibiotics guided by cultures, and a plan for postpartum follow‑up.  
- Counsel patients on the risks (miscarriage, preterm labour, bleeding) and on realistic downstream obstetric plans (residual myomas may necessitate elective cesarean).  

This is not about heroic surgery — it’s about doing the minimum necessary to defuse an immediate threat and keep a pregnancy safe.
