Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Morning Report] In a 21-week pregnancy with severe abdominal pain, dyspnoea, and two large abdominal masses on ultrasound, which imaging modalities are safe and most informative for further characterization, and what contrast or field-strength considerations apply during pregnancy?

Direct answer: Start with targeted ultrasound (transabdominal ± transvaginal) with colour Doppler as the first-line, and use non‑contrast MRI as the second‑line problem‑solving study. During pregnancy avoid gadolinium contrast and, when possible, limit MRI field strength to 1.5‑T (the team in this case specifically avoided >1.5‑T and performed a non‑enhanced MRI).

Why: 
- Ultrasound is safe throughout pregnancy, bedside, and excellent for determining cystic versus solid components, vascularity of a mass, presence of a pedicle, and relationship to the uterus and adnexa. In this patient US showed two large unilocular cystic lesions with peripheral vascularisation and raised the differential (ovarian endometrioma versus uterine myoma with cystic degeneration). Transvaginal scanning is useful for pelvic masses and for defining distance from the endometrial cavity.
- MRI without contrast provides superior soft‑tissue characterisation and anatomic mapping for large, posterior, multiple or indeterminate masses (T1/T2, fat‑suppressed sequences, and diffusion sequences can identify haemorrhage, necrosis, cystic degeneration and solid nodules). In the case report a non‑contrast MRI (T1/T2 fat‑sat) demonstrated thick walls and focal hyperintense components and guided multidisciplinary decision‑making.

Contrast and field strength considerations:
- Gadolinium crosses the placenta and is avoided in pregnancy unless absolutely necessary because of possible fetal retention and associations with adverse outcomes in observational data; thus non‑contrast MRI is preferred.
- Many centers prefer 1.5‑T MRI in pregnancy because higher field strengths (3‑T) increase specific absorption rate and acoustic noise (theoretical risks of heating); the team here avoided >1.5‑T. If 3‑T is the only available option, weigh urgency vs theoretical risk and follow institutional MRI‑in‑pregnancy protocols.
- CT and iodinated contrast involve ionising radiation and placental transfer of iodide; CT should be reserved for situations where MRI/US cannot answer the clinical question (trauma, suspected bowel perforation, or when vascular mapping is essential). If iodinated contrast is used, neonatal thyroid function should be checked.

Clinical takeaways:
- Use bimanual exam + targeted US with colour Doppler first; proceed to non‑contrast MRI for large, posterior, or indeterminate masses.
- Avoid gadolinium in pregnancy and preferentially use 1.5‑T MRI when available; reserve CT/contrast studies for exceptional circumstances after multidisciplinary discussion.

### [Clinical Pearls] How should a CA-125 level of approximately 42 U/mL in the second trimester be interpreted in the evaluation of adnexal-appearing masses, and what is the added value of serial trends versus a single measurement during pregnancy?

Direct answer — a CA‑125 of ≈42 U/mL in the second trimester should be viewed as a mild, non‑specific elevation: it is higher than the typical second‑trimester median (about 14 U/mL in the series cited) and above the conventional nonpregnant cut‑off (≥35 U/mL), but it is not diagnostic of malignancy in pregnancy. A single value has limited discriminatory power in pregnant, premenopausal patients; the marker must be interpreted in the clinical and imaging context. Serial trends add meaningful information: a stable or falling CA‑125 favors a benign or pregnancy‑related cause, whereas a clear upward trajectory (especially if rapid or to very high levels) increases concern and should prompt re‑imaging and multidisciplinary reassessment.

Why: CA‑125 is produced by coelomic and mesothelial-derived tissues (peritoneum, endometrium, pleura) and rises with decidualisation, peritoneal irritation, inflammation or tissue necrosis. Pregnancy itself causes physiologic variation (peaks in the first and third trimesters, a nadir in the second trimester). Benign gynecologic conditions that commonly raise CA‑125 include endometriosis/decidualised endometriomas, adenomyosis, degenerating or torsed fibroids, pelvic infection and peritoneal irritation — all of which are more likely explanations than ovarian cancer in a symptomatic pregnant patient in her 40s with adnexal‑appearing masses.

How to use trends in practice:
- Always interpret CA‑125 alongside high‑quality ultrasound (and non‑contrast MRI when needed), symptoms (pain, fever), and signs of complication (ascites, rapid growth, solid papillary components, marked vascularity).  
- If imaging is reassuring and the patient is asymptomatic, a short interval repeat (about 1–2 weeks) can help: a small, stable elevation that does not rise further usually supports conservative management and surveillance.  
- If CA‑125 is rising over serial measurements, particularly with suspicious imaging features or worsening symptoms, the likelihood of a pathologic process (decidualised endometrioma, torsion/degeneration, or less commonly neoplasm) increases and stronger consideration for intervention is warranted.  
- Very high or rapidly rising values are more worrisome but no pregnancy‑validated CA‑125 cutoff reliably distinguishes benign from malignant disease; decisions should be multidisciplinary.

Tie to the case: in this patient CA‑125 rose from 36 U/mL to 41.8 U/mL over 2 weeks — a modest upward trend that, together with imaging showing large vascularised cystic masses with mixed solid components and the patient’s pain/dyspnoea, increased concern and supported operative management. Operative findings (a twisted, leaking pedunculated leiomyoma with ischaemic necrosis) explain the CA‑125 elevation (peritoneal irritation/necrosis) and illustrate the teaching point: a single mildly elevated CA‑125 in the second trimester is not diagnostic, but trend data combined with imaging and clinical deterioration can legitimately influence the decision to proceed to surgery.  

Bottom line (clinical pearl): CA‑125 ≈42 U/mL in the second trimester is a red flag that requires context, not a diagnosis. Serial measurements increase specificity by showing whether the marker is stable (favoring conservative care) or rising (prompting re‑evaluation and possible intervention), but management should always be guided primarily by symptoms and imaging.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What sonographic and MRI features help distinguish a pedunculated cystic-degenerating uterine leiomyoma from a decidualised endometrioma or ovarian neoplasm in pregnancy?

Short answer
Distinguishing a pedunculated, cystic‑degenerating uterine leiomyoma from a decidualised endometrioma or an ovarian neoplasm in pregnancy relies on demonstrating uterine origin (stalk or myometrial continuity, bridging vessels, “claw/beak” sign) and on characteristic tissue signals on non‑contrast MRI (residual low‑T2 fibrous rim and central high‑T2 fluid in degenerating fibroid vs T1‑hyperintense blood products and T2‑shading in endometrioma; papillary/irregular solid projections, diffusion restriction, ascites or nodes raise concern for ovarian malignancy). When the gravid uterus and very large masses obscure these signs imaging may be equivocal and clinical course guides management — as in this case, where the upper mass lacked a visible pedicle on imaging but proved to be a twisted pedunculated cystic leiomyoma at surgery.

Key sonographic features
- Evidence of uterine origin
  - Visible pedicle/stalk or continuity of myometrium into the lesion on transvaginal/transabdominal views favors a subserosal/pedunculated leiomyoma.
  - Bridging‑vessel sign on colour Doppler: vessels coursing from the uterine wall into the mass strongly suggests a fibroid.
  - Claw or beak sign: myometrium wraps around the lesion edge.
- Internal echotexture
  - Cystic degeneration of leiomyoma → large unilocular or multilocular anechoic/hypoechoic spaces with a thick peripheral wall and residual solid hypoechoic fibrous rim.
  - Endometrioma → “ground‑glass” homogeneous low‑level internal echoes on ultrasound (classically), sometimes with vascularised mural nodules when decidualised in pregnancy.
  - Ovarian neoplasm → complex septations, papillary projections, irregular solid components, bilateral disease more common for metastatic/malignant lesions.
- Vascular pattern
  - Leiomyoma: peripheral/serosal feeding vessels entering via a stalk.
  - Decidualised endometrioma: vascular mural nodules but lacking a uterine feeding vessel.

Key non‑contrast MRI features
- Myometrial continuity/pedicle
  - A T2 hypointense band or direct continuity between myometrium and mass (stalk or “bridging” tissue) identifies uterine origin (most specific sign for pedunculated fibroid).
- Signal characteristics
  - Fibroid with cystic degeneration: residual solid fibrous tissue typically low T2 signal; cystic/necrotic areas are T2 hyperintense and usually not T1 hyperintense unless hemorrhagic.
  - Endometrioma (including decidualised): high T1 signal on fat‑saturated sequences from blood products; the T2 shading sign (low T2 signal of chronic blood) supports endometrioma. Decidualised mural nodules are often T2 hyperintense and (if contrast were used) enhancing — without gadolinium they remain suspicious but their T1/T2 behavior plus ovarian location helps.
  - Ovarian malignancy: irregular solid components, papillary projections, thick septa; diffusion restriction (high b‑value DWI / low ADC) and associated ascites/adenopathy increase likelihood of malignancy.
- Ancillary findings
  - Absence of ascites, implants, or nodes lowers suspicion for ovarian cancer.
  - Lack of gadolinium in pregnancy limits assessment of enhancement; rely on morphology, T1 fat‑sat for blood products, and DWI when available.

Practical application and limitations (illustrated by the case)
- In the reported patient ultrasound showed two large unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularization and the caudal lesion appeared continuous with a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma — a clue to leiomyomatous origin. MRI without contrast showed thick walls, inhomogeneous T1/T2 fluid and focal hyperintense components; the caudal mass appeared continuous with the uterine myoma while the stalk of the cranial mass could not be visualized because the gravid uterus and huge lesions obscured anatomy. That imaging ambiguity is common in pregnancy and explains why the cranial mass was misinterpreted preoperatively as ovarian; laparotomy demonstrated a pedunculated, twisted cystic leiomyoma.
- CA‑125 is unreliable in pregnancy (physiologic rises), so imaging pattern and clinical course (acute pain, fever, torsion/necrosis, mass effect) drive decisions.

Bottom line (teaching pearl)
Systematically search for uterine connection (pedicle/bridging vessels, claw sign) on ultrasound and MRI and use T1 fat‑sat and T2 patterns to detect blood‑product–rich endometrioma versus fibroid degeneration. If imaging is inconclusive and the patient has worsening symptoms or mass effect in pregnancy, multidisciplinary evaluation and timely surgical intervention are appropriate, recognizing that large gravid uteri can mask the very imaging clues that would make a nonoperative diagnosis certain.

### [Management] What clinical features in a pregnant patient with large pelvic or abdominal masses justify proceeding with operative management in the second trimester rather than expectant care?

Short answer: operate in the second trimester when the mass is large and symptomatic, when there are signs of acute or impending complications, when imaging or biomarkers cannot exclude neoplasm, or when the mass is likely to compromise uterine expansion or the route of delivery. The second trimester is the preferred window for necessary non‑obstetric abdominal surgery because it carries lower risk of miscarriage than the first trimester and lower risk of preterm labour than the third.

Key clinical features that justify operative management (with links to the case)

- Progressive or severe maternal symptoms despite conservative measures.  
  - Rationale: persistent/worsening pain, significant dyspnoea, or inability to tolerate oral intake/positioning indicate ongoing physiologic compromise from mass effect and argued for surgery in this case (VAS 5 pain, dyspnoea from diaphragmatic compression by an 18‑cm mass).

- Signs of complication or impending complication: torsion, rupture, haemorrhage, infection, or ischemic degeneration.  
  - Rationale: pedunculated masses are at high risk for torsion → ischemia, fever and acute abdomen. In the case the cranial lesion was highly vascularized, twisted and leaking with ischemic necrosis on pathology, justifying resection.

- Systemic inflammatory signs or sepsis workup suggestive of infected/degenerating lesion.  
  - Rationale: fever, leukocytosis and rising CRP (even if a concomitant UTI existed in the case) increase concern for an infected/necrotic mass that may not respond to expectant care.

- Mass effect causing cardiopulmonary, urinary, or gastrointestinal compromise.  
  - Rationale: very large abdominal/pelvic masses can restrict diaphragmatic excursion (dyspnoea), compress ureters (hydronephrosis) or bowel; the patient’s dyspnoea and mechanical compression were decisive.

- Imaging features that are suspicious, inconclusive, or evolving: large size, mixed solid–cystic components, thickened irregular walls, increased vascularity, or inability to define organ of origin.  
  - Rationale: ultrasound + non‑contrast MRI in the case showed thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid, focal solid areas and hypertrophied vessels; these raised the threshold for operative diagnosis and treatment.

- Rising or persistently abnormal tumor markers that cannot be explained by pregnancy alone.  
  - Rationale: CA‑125 is physiologically variable in pregnancy, but a rising trend above expected second‑trimester values (case: 36 → 41.8 U/mL) increased concern and contributed to the decision to operate.

- Anticipated obstetric hindrance (mass likely to obstruct labour or limit uterine growth).  
  - Rationale: a fixed posterior pelvic mass that would obstruct fetal descent was left in situ in the case but was the reason to plan a cesarean; removal of obstructing lesions in the second trimester can be considered when they jeopardize the pregnancy course or delivery.

- Failure of conservative management combined with multidisciplinary assessment concluding that expectant care poses higher risk.  
  - Rationale: analgesics/positioning only partially relieved symptoms in this patient; the team weighed maternal risk, diagnostic uncertainty, and future growth of the uterus and mass.

Practical management principles (as applied in the case)
- Use targeted imaging (US with Doppler; non‑contrast MRI) to characterize lesions and avoid gadolinium in pregnancy.  
- Multidisciplinary informed consent: obstetrics, gynecologic surgery, anaesthesia, neonatology.  
- Plan fetal monitoring before/during/after surgery and minimize operative time and uterine manipulation.  
- Remove only lesions that are symptomatic, complicated, or suspicious; leave other masses in situ to minimize obstetric risk.  
- Prepare for blood loss, transfusion, postoperative fetal surveillance and close obstetric follow‑up.

Takeaway: in pregnancy the threshold for surgery rises, but second‑trimester operative management is warranted when mass‑related maternal compromise, suspected or evolving complications, diagnostic uncertainty about malignancy, or likely obstruction of labour make expectant care unsafe — exactly the combination of symptoms, imaging findings and risks that prompted laparotomy in this case.

### [Management] If non-obstetric abdominal surgery is undertaken at around 22 weeks’ gestation, what perioperative steps minimize maternal–fetal risk, including fetal assessment before, during, and after the procedure and intraoperative strategies to reduce uterine stimulation and hypoperfusion?

Short answer
When non‑obetric abdominal surgery is required in the second trimester the perioperative priorities are (1) optimize maternal physiology to preserve uteroplacental perfusion, (2) minimise direct uterine stimulation/manipulation, and (3) document fetal viability before, during and after the procedure with contingency plans if the fetus is viable. The case you read illustrates these principles: multidisciplinary planning, pre‑op ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI, intra‑operative verification of fetal cardiac activity by ultrasound, limited removal of only the symptomatic pedunculated myoma, intra‑operative progesterone given by the team, meticulous haemostasis and early correction of anaemia — all measures that contributed to a good maternal and fetal outcome.

Key elements and the rationale

1) Pre‑operative preparation and planning
- Multidisciplinary team (obstetrics/MFM, anaesthesia, surgery, neonatology when the fetus is potentially viable) and explicit informed consent that includes fetal risk. In the report a multidisciplinary evaluation guided decision making.
- Confirm fetal viability, gestational age and placental location with ultrasound immediately before surgery. Non‑contrast MRI is safe after the first trimester and useful for surgical planning (they used MRI without gadolinium).
- Correct reversible maternal problems that reduce uteroplacental oxygen delivery: treat infection, optimise haemoglobin (type & screen/crossmatch blood available), correct fluid/electrolyte abnormalities. In the case the team treated the UTI and addressed iron deficiency/transfusion post‑op.
- Anticipate blood loss and have blood products and haemorrhage plan available; choose the surgical approach that minimises operative time and uterine manipulation (team chose a vertical midline laparotomy and limited the procedure to the symptomatic pedunculated myoma).

2) Fetal assessment: before, during and after surgery
- Before: document fetal cardiac activity and estimated GA by ultrasound; if fetus is at or above institution’s viability threshold, notify neonatology and discuss availability for immediate delivery if needed.
- During: document fetal heart activity before incision, intermittently during the procedure if continuous monitoring is not feasible, and immediately after the procedure. If the fetus is potentially viable (commonly ≥24 weeks, institutional thresholds vary) continuous fetal heart monitoring in the OR is recommended when technically possible and when there is the capability to perform an emergency cesarean. At 21+6 weeks (as in this case) continuous monitoring would not be helpful; the team used serial/intermittent transvaginal ultrasound to document fetal cardiac activity intra‑operatively, consistent with published recommendations (Zhao et al).
- After: perform immediate postoperative fetal assessment (ultrasound or cardiotocography if viable), observe for uterine contractions or signs of preterm labour, and arrange short‑interval obstetric follow‑up (the case used strict follow‑up every 3 weeks with growth scans).

3) Intra‑operative strategies to preserve uteroplacental perfusion and limit uterine stimulation
- Positioning: left uterine displacement (15–30°) or right hip wedge to avoid aortocaval compression from 20 weeks onward — preserves venous return and cardiac output.
- Maintain maternal oxygenation and ventilation: keep SpO2 ≥95%, avoid hypoxia; maintain normocapnia (avoid hyperventilation which reduces uterine blood flow via vasoconstriction, and avoid hypoventilation which causes fetal acidosis).
- Maintain maternal blood pressure and intravascular volume: uteroplacental flow is pressure‑dependent. Treat hypotension promptly (vasopressors such as phenylephrine are commonly used and effective), give fluids judiciously, transfuse early when indicated.
- Minimise uterine manipulation and traction, keep the operation as brief as feasible, and use careful, rapid haemostasis. The surgeons in the case excised only the pedunculated lesion and left the adherent pelvic mass in situ to reduce uterine trauma and operative time.
- Avoid routine prophylactic uterotonics; avoid drugs known to provoke contractions unless clinically required. Use tocolytics only selectively for active uterine contractions (evidence for routine prophylactic tocolysis is limited).
- Anaesthetic considerations: rapid‑sequence induction (pregnancy increases aspiration risk), avoid teratogens in the first trimester (less relevant in second trimester), invasive monitoring (arterial line) when large blood loss is anticipated, and temperature control.
- Infection prophylaxis and thromboprophylaxis: give perioperative antibiotics (case used cefazolin), and institute VTE prophylaxis appropriate to bleeding risk and gestation.

4) Role of pharmacologic prophylaxis for preterm labour
- There is no high‑quality evidence to mandate routine tocolysis for all operative pregnancies. Some teams use progesterone prophylaxis or tocolytics selectively. In this report the team administered intramuscular progesterone intra‑operatively and continued parenteral progesterone thereafter; that was a centre‑specific strategy rather than an evidence‑based universal requirement.

5) Postoperative care and delivery planning
- Monitor maternal haemodynamics, correct anaemia and infection promptly (patient required IV iron and transfusion post‑op). Early mobilisation and VTE prophylaxis.
- Reassess fetal wellbeing (CTG or ultrasound) and watch for preterm labour; if delivery becomes likely, give antenatal corticosteroids and magnesium sulphate according to gestational‑age thresholds used locally (typically recommended when delivery is imminent and fetus is within the window of benefit).
- Individualise delivery planning based on residual pathology (the retained pelvic myoma in this case led to elective cesarean at term).

Practical checklist (brief)
- Multidisciplinary plan + informed consent
- Pre‑op US to confirm FHR, GA, placenta; non‑contrast MRI if needed
- Type & screen/crossmatch, treat infection, correct anaemia
- Anaesthesia plan: aspiration prophylaxis, left tilt, invasive monitoring if indicated
- Fetal monitoring strategy decided pre‑op (continuous if viable & feasible; intermittent US if previable or if continuous monitoring impossible)
- Minimise uterine manipulation, limit operative time, meticulous haemostasis
- Perioperative antibiotics, thromboprophylaxis, neonatal backup if viable
- Post‑op: immediate fetal reassessment, serial obs, treat maternal problems, arrange obstetric follow‑up

Bottom line
Successful non‑obstetric abdominal surgery in the mid‑second trimester rests on multidisciplinary preparation, strict maternal physiologic support to preserve uteroplacental perfusion, judicious minimisation of uterine manipulation, and documentation of fetal viability before/during/after the case with readiness to deliver if the fetus is viable and distressed. The presented case is a practical example of those principles: targeted surgery, intra‑op fetal checks by ultrasound, optimisation of maternal status, and close obstetric follow‑up leading to a favourable outcome.

### [Pathophysiology] What mechanisms explain acute pain, low-grade fever, and CRP elevation in a twisted, leaking pedunculated subserosal fibroid, and how does this differ from the pathophysiology of red (carneous) degeneration in pregnancy?

Short answer
- In a twisted pedunculated subserosal leiomyoma acute pain, low‑grade fever and a raised CRP are produced by sudden vascular compromise of the stalk → rapid venous congestion, hemorrhagic infarction and ischemic necrosis with release of inflammatory mediators into the peritoneal cavity (chemical peritonitis ± secondary infection). By contrast, red (carneous) degeneration in pregnancy is an intramural hemorrhagic infarction due to venous thrombosis within the fibroid related to rapid growth/hormonal changes; it causes pain and a systemic inflammatory response but is usually contained within the uterine wall and is managed conservatively.

Pathophysiology explained
- Torsion of a pedunculated subserosal fibroid is a mechanical event: twisting of the vascular pedicle first obstructs venous outflow (congestion, edema), then compromises arterial inflow → progressive ischemia, hemorrhagic infarction and necrosis of the tumour. Ischaemic cells and interstitial hemorrhage generate intense visceral and somatic pain (stretch of the serosa, activation of nociceptors by bradykinin, prostaglandins and other mediators) and can produce peritoneal irritation if necrotic/hemorrhagic material “leaks” from the mass.
- The necrotic tissue and activated inflammatory cells release cytokines (notably IL‑6) that drive a hepatic acute‑phase response with elevation of CRP and can produce low‑grade fever and neutrophilia. If necrotic material reaches the peritoneal cavity the reaction is predominantly chemical (sterile) peritonitis — cultures may be negative — but secondary bacterial infection is possible and would amplify fever and CRP.

How this applies to the case
- In this patient the intraoperative finding of a highly vascularized, initially twisted and leaking pedunculated myoma and the pathology report of ischemic necrosis at the twisted pedicle directly explain her acute left‑upper‑quadrant and pelvic pain, the mild fever and the modest CRP rise. The negative peritoneal cultures support a primarily sterile inflammatory response from necrosis/leakage; the documented Klebsiella UTI could have contributed to fever and neutrophilia and prompted antibiotic therapy.

How red (carneous) degeneration differs
- Red degeneration (classically seen in pregnancy) results from venous thrombosis within an enlarging intramural fibroid → hemorrhagic infarction of the fibroid substance. Clinically it causes localized uterine pain, low‑grade fever and often mild leukocytosis/CRP rise, but the process is intramural and contained (less likely to cause peritonitic signs or free necrotic leak). Management is usually conservative (analgesia, rest); surgery is reserved for complications. By contrast, torsion of an extra‑uterine pedunculated fibroid is a mechanical ischemic emergency more often requiring operative treatment to relieve pain and prevent infection.

Clinical pointers
- Think torsion when a subserosal/pedunculated lesion causes sudden focal pain, peritoneal signs or imaging suggests an avascular stalk; red degeneration should be suspected when a pregnant patient with known intramural fibroid develops subacute localized uterine pain with systemic symptoms but no peritoneal signs. In this case the operative findings and pathology confirmed torsion with ischemic necrosis — the explanation for the clinical picture and the indication for myomectomy despite pregnancy.

### [Pathophysiology] How do uterine fibroids typically change in size across pregnancy and the early postpartum period, and how should this trajectory inform the timing of any deferred myomectomy?

Direct answer
Uterine leiomyomas typically enlarge during pregnancy (most rapidly in early gestation) and then commonly regress after delivery. Published pregnancy-series summarized in the case report show the fastest growth between the first and second trimesters (≈6% increase per week), a slower but continued gain between the second and third trimesters (≈3% per week), and a marked postpartum involution — a mean size reduction of ≈44% by 6 weeks after delivery (wide variability, SD ≈36%). Clinically, that trajectory argues strongly for deferring elective myomectomy until the uterus has involuted and myoma size has stabilized unless there is an urgent indication.

Why this matters (pathophysiology and clinical reasoning)
- Hormonal milieu and increased uterine blood flow in pregnancy (estrogen/progesterone, growth factors, decidual changes) drive non‑linear myoma growth early in pregnancy; growth slows later in gestation.  
- After delivery the abrupt fall in circulating hormones and uterine involution commonly produces substantial shrinkage of leiomyomas within weeks. Because of that natural regression, many lesions that are sizable or symptomatic in pregnancy become smaller and asymptomatic postpartum — avoiding unnecessary surgery.  
- Operative risk is higher when the uterus is enlarged and hypervascular (greater bleeding risk), so postponing elective myomectomy reduces perioperative hemorrhage and technical difficulty.

How this informs timing of deferred myomectomy (practical guidance)
- Urgent surgery during pregnancy is reserved for complications (torsion of a pedunculated myoma, infection, intractable pain, suspicion of malignancy, or mass effect threatening maternal/fetal well‑being). The case illustrates this principle: the twisted, leaking pedunculated myoma at 21+6 weeks was appropriately excised, whereas the fixed caudal myoma was left in situ.  
- If no urgent indication exists, re‑evaluate after delivery. A first postpartum reassessment at about 6 weeks is reasonable because many myomas show substantial involution by then (mean ≈44% reduction).  
- If symptoms persist or the myoma remains obstructive/large at 6 weeks, plan definitive, elective myomectomy once size has stabilized — commonly at 6–12 weeks to 3 months postpartum in clinical practice. Waiting longer (3 months) may give maximal involution and allows optimization (iron repletion, breastfeeding counselling, surgical planning).  
- Document size, number and location with the same imaging modality (serial ultrasound; MRI reserved for complex mapping) before scheduling surgery; optimize hemoglobin and blood availability.

Application to the presented case
- The team removed the pedunculated, torsed upper myoma during pregnancy because of acute complication (fever, leak, vascularised pedicle) — an accepted indication. The caudal intramural–subserosal myoma was left in place; on follow‑up it had slightly reduced to 7.5 cm and the patient was asymptomatic, supporting conservative postpartum management and continued observation rather than immediate surgery.

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
Because myomas often grow during pregnancy—especially early—and commonly regress substantially by about 6 weeks postpartum, elective myomectomy is best deferred until after puerperal involution and reassessment (usually ≥6 weeks, often targeted around 6–12 weeks or 3 months), unless acute complications demand earlier intervention.

### [Management] What factors determine mode and timing of delivery when a large posterior intramural–subserosal fibroid occupies the pelvis, and why might an elective caesarean at 37 weeks be chosen?

Short answer
Mode and timing of delivery are driven by whether the fibroid will obstruct the birth canal or impair safe uterine contraction, and by the balance between fetal maturity and the maternal/fetal risks of allowing spontaneous labour. When a large posterior intramural–subserosal myoma occupies the pelvis and is predicted to physically block descent of the fetal head (as in this case), an elective caesarean planned once the fetus is term (here at 37 weeks) is appropriate to avoid obstructed labour, emergency interventions and the risk of uterine rupture or severe haemorrhage.

Clinical reasoning and principles (grounded in the case)
- Key determinants of mode/timing: size, number and exact anatomic location of the myoma (particularly proximity to the cervix or lower uterine segment), whether it is fixed in the pelvis or pedunculated, its relationship to the uterine cavity and placenta, the anticipated effect on fetal lie/presentation and on cervical dilatation/descent during the expulsive phase, prior uterine surgery and any maternal symptoms or complications (pain, degeneration, infection, torsion, bleeding). The team used ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI in this patient to define these relationships.

- Pathophysiology: a large posterior intramural–subserosal mass occupying the lesser pelvis reduces the pelvic outlet and distorts the lower uterine segment and cervix, mechanically preventing engagement and descent of the presenting part and producing labour dystocia or arrest (and thereby increasing the risk of uterine rupture, haemorrhage and emergency surgery). Pedunculated or vascular tumours also risk torsion, necrosis or infection (the upper mass in this patient had already twisted and was excised).

- Diagnostic standard to guide planning: imaging (transvaginal/transabdominal ultrasound with Doppler and, when needed, non‑contrast MRI) to determine size, relation to the uterine cavity and lower segment, and whether the mass is likely to be an obstacle to vaginal delivery. In this case the caudal mass was clinically fixed in the pouch of Douglas, bulged into the posterior fornix and appeared adherent to a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma (~58×71 mm), predicting mechanical obstruction.

Why an elective caesarean at 37 weeks was chosen in this case
- The caudal myoma “would have represented a huge obstacle for the fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labour,” making vaginal birth unlikely and risky. An elective caesarean avoids failed labour and emergency surgery with uncontrolled bleeding.
- Timing at 37 weeks balanced risk: by 37 weeks the fetus is term with low risk of major respiratory morbidity, while delaying delivery risks spontaneous labour with potentially catastrophic obstructed labour or uterine scar complications (the patient had undergone myomectomy of the pedunculated lesion earlier in pregnancy and had a large residual pelvic myoma). Delivering as soon as fetal maturity was reached minimized intrapartum risk while preserving neonatal outcome.
- An elective, planned procedure also permits optimal preparation (blood products, experienced surgical/anesthesia team, same abdominal access as previous surgery) and avoids attempting myomectomy of the fixed pelvic mass at the time of delivery (which would have markedly increased intraoperative haemorrhage risk).

Takeaway (clinical pearl)
Most fibroids do not mandate caesarean delivery, but a large, fixed lower‑segment or pelvic myoma that mechanically obstructs the birth canal—especially when associated with prior uterine surgery or ongoing symptoms—warrants planned caesarean before spontaneous labour; timing should balance fetal maturity (term) against the maternal risk of intrapartum complications, as illustrated by the elective caesarean at 37 weeks in this patient.

### [Management] How should symptomatic iron-deficiency anaemia compounded by perioperative blood loss be managed in mid-pregnancy, and what thresholds guide the use of intravenous iron versus red blood cell transfusion?

Bottom line
- In mid‑pregnancy iron‑deficiency anaemia should be corrected promptly to protect maternal oxygen delivery and fetal growth. If iron deficiency is proven and the patient is haemodynamically stable, give intravenous iron for rapid repletion; reserve red‑cell transfusion for haemodynamic instability, ongoing major bleeding, or severe/symptomatic anaemia when an immediate rise in oxygen‑carrying capacity is required.  

Why (clinical reasoning)
- Pregnancy increases iron requirements and reduces physiologic reserve; perioperative blood loss causes an acute drop in haemoglobin (Hb) and cannot be corrected quickly by oral iron. Intravenous (IV) iron supplies the substrate for erythropoiesis and restores iron stores over days–weeks; red‑cell transfusion raises Hb immediately but carries risks (alloimmunisation, transfusion reactions, volume overload) and does not replenish stores. The choice between the two depends on acuity, severity, symptoms, maternal comorbidity and time available before delivery or further procedures.

Practical thresholds and approach
- Assess stability first: heart rate, blood pressure, ongoing bleeding, symptoms (dyspnoea, chest pain, syncope), and fetal status.
- Haemodynamically unstable or ongoing major haemorrhage → transfuse immediately (type‑specific/crossmatch), resuscitate, then reassess. Don’t delay for iron studies.
- Stable patient:
  - Hb <7 g/dL (severe) → generally transfuse (restrictive transfusion strategy used in most adults; pregnancy practice is similar when urgent correction is needed).
  - Hb 7–8 g/dL → consider transfusion if symptomatic, evidence of inadequate oxygen delivery or cardiac disease; otherwise treat with IV iron if iron deficiency proven.
  - Hb ≥8 g/dL with ferritin <30 µg/L (iron deficiency) → IV iron is preferred when rapid correction is desirable (late pregnancy, preoperative) or oral iron is ineffective/intolerable; oral iron can be used for mild anaemia when there is time and tolerability.
- Typical targets: transfusion usually given to achieve Hb sufficient to relieve symptoms and stabilise (often 8–10 g/dL depending on circumstances); 1 unit RBC ≈ +1 g/dL Hb in an average adult.
- IV iron dosing: ferric carboxymaltose 1 g single dose (as used in many centres) or iron sucrose 200 mg serial doses; calculate total deficit (e.g., Ganzoni formula) if full repletion is required. Observe for hypersensitivity.

Application to this case
- The patient presented with iron deficiency (preoperative Hb ≈9.9 g/dL, ferritin 25 µg/L) and suffered perioperative blood loss with a postoperative Hb of 7.5 g/dL. Because she had symptomatic compromise and an acute drop in Hb, the team gave a single 1‑g infusion of ferric carboxymaltose and two units of packed red cells, raising Hb to 10.0 g/dL. That combination is appropriate: transfusion provided immediate correction for oxygen delivery and IV iron treated the underlying iron‑deficit to support ongoing erythropoiesis and limit further transfusion needs.

Monitoring and safety
- Monitor vitals and fetal heart rate during and after interventions. Check Hb 6–24 hours after transfusion and again at 2–4 weeks after IV iron to document response and replete further if needed. Be aware of transfusion risks and observe for infusion reactions with IV iron.

Clinical pearl
- Screen and treat iron deficiency early in pregnancy to reduce the likelihood of perioperative or peripartum transfusion; when acute correction is needed (instability, active bleeding or severe/symptomatic anaemia) transfuse red cells, and when time allows use IV iron to correct stores and support sustained recovery — the management in this case (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g + two RBC units for postoperative Hb 7.5 g/dL) exemplifies that principle.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Provide a broad differential diagnosis for large, unilocular cystic abdominal masses detected in the second trimester with dyspnoea and pain, and identify imaging or clinical features that would raise oncologic concern.

Direct answer
Large, unilocular cystic abdominal masses in the second trimester with pain and dyspnoea have a broad differential that includes uterine, adnexal, and non‑gynecologic etiologies. The key clinical and imaging features that should raise oncologic concern are solid/papillary nodules, irregular thick septations or walls, internal (low‑resistance) vascularity, ascites or peritoneal implants, bilateral ovarian involvement, rapidly progressive growth, and a rising trend in tumor markers that is unexplained by pregnancy.

Differential diagnosis (practical groups and distinguishing clues)
- Uterine
  - Pedunculated or exophytic leiomyoma with cystic/red degeneration. Can be large, unilocular, and highly vascular; torsion of a pedicle causes acute pain, fever and necrosis (exactly what occurred in this case). Clue: continuity with the uterus or a bridging vascular pedicle on Doppler/MRI favors uterine origin.
- Adnexal/ovarian
  - Endometrioma (decidualised in pregnancy): typically T1‑hyperintense from blood products; decidualisation can produce vascularised papillary nodules that mimic malignancy.
  - Benign epithelial cystadenoma (serous/mucinous) and mucinous cystadenoma (often very large, multilocular).
  - Borderline or invasive epithelial ovarian tumors: commonly have mural nodules/papillary projections, irregular septa, ascites and internal vascularity.
  - Mature cystic teratoma: fat signal on MRI/fat‑suppression sequences or calcifications on ultrasound.
  - Paraovarian/paratubal cysts (unilocular, mobile, thin‑walled).
- Inflammatory / infectious
  - Tubo‑ovarian abscess: complex cystic mass with systemic infection signs (fever, elevated CRP/WBC)—but pregnancy can distort the picture.
- Mesenteric/omental/retroperitoneal cysts and lymphatic malformations
  - Large unilocular cysts, typically without internal vascularised solid nodules.
- Visceral cysts (less likely here but anatomically relevant)
  - Splenic cyst, pancreatic pseudocyst or subphrenic collections — location, relation to spleen/pancreas and history help.
- Rare malignant possibilities
  - Cystic degeneration in sarcoma or metastatic cystic lesions (Krukenberg metastases are usually solid/bilateral).

Imaging and clinical features that should raise oncologic concern
- Sonography
  - Any internal solid component, papillary projections or mural nodularity.
  - Thick, irregular septations or walls (septations > a few millimetres are suspicious).
  - Internal colour Doppler flow within solid components (particularly low‑resistance flow).
  - Bilaterality, associated ascites, peritoneal implants or pleural effusion.
- MRI (non‑contrast sequences useful in pregnancy)
  - Solid nodules that show restricted diffusion on DWI (malignant tissue often restricts).
  - Irregular, thickened walls or mural nodules; lack of a clear cleavage plane with peritoneal disease or lymphadenopathy.
  - T1 hyperintensity consistent with fat (teratoma) versus blood (endometrioma/red degeneration)—interpret in context.
  - Enhancing features on contrast would be most specific but gadolinium is avoided in pregnancy; non‑contrast DWI and detailed anatomic T1/T2/fat‑sat sequences are the workhorse.
- Laboratory / clinical
  - A rising trend in CA‑125 in the second trimester is atypical (the case had CA‑125 41.8 U/mL, up from 36 two weeks earlier) and warrants cautious evaluation; absolute CA‑125 has limited specificity in pregnancy.
  - Progressive symptoms (worsening pain, dyspnoea from mass effect), unexplained systemic signs or rapid growth despite conservative measures increase urgency.
  - New ascites, cachexia, or evidence of metastatic disease on imaging.

How to apply this in practice (diagnostic and management principles)
- Start with careful pelvic bimanual and US (transvaginal + transabdominal) with colour Doppler; apply IOTA features to stratify risk (solid nodules, papillary projections, thick septa, ascites increase concern).
- When US is inconclusive, obtain non‑contrast MRI at 1.5 T with T1, T2, fat‑sat and DWI sequences to look for continuity with the uterus (bridging vessel sign), blood/fat signal, cystic degeneration patterns, and diffusion characteristics.
- Interpret tumor markers in context: CA‑125 is frequently elevated in the first and third trimesters and with benign conditions (endometriosis, myoma, inflammation); trends are more informative than a single value.
- Indications for operative management in pregnancy: strong suspicion of malignancy, acute complications (torsion, rupture, haemorrhage, infection), or progressive mass effect compromising maternal respiration or the ability of the uterus to expand — balance risks to fetus and mother. Second trimester is preferred timing for elective surgery; large symptomatic masses (as in this case) may require laparotomy.
- If surgery is undertaken, minimize operative time and blood loss, perform fetal monitoring as feasible, obtain peritoneal washings if malignancy is suspected, and aim to preserve pregnancy when safe.

Application to the presented case — why surgery was reasonable
- The patient had two very large masses producing pain and dyspnoea from mass effect, a low‑grade fever and leukocytosis, MRI features that included thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid and focal T1/T2 hyperintense solid components, and a rising CA‑125 (41.8 U/mL). These combined factors produced both symptomatic imperative and diagnostic uncertainty (endometrioma, cystic ovarian neoplasm, or a degenerating/twisted myoma).
- Laparotomy identified a torsed, pedunculated cystic leiomyoma (explaining pain, fever and ischemic necrosis) and an adherent cystic intramural–subserosal myoma left in situ to minimize obstetric risk — an outcome that illustrates why degenerating fibroids can mimic adnexal neoplasms and why clinical context plus imaging features guide the decision to operate.

Clinical pearls
- Large, unilocular cystic masses in pregnancy demand integration of clinical signs, serial imaging and cautious use of tumor markers; non‑contrast MRI with DWI is the safest next step when ultrasound is indeterminate.
- Features that heighten oncologic concern are internal solid/papillary nodules, irregular thick septa/wall, internal vascularity, ascites or peritoneal disease, bilateral involvement, and an unexplained rising tumor‑marker trend.
- Pedunculated or degenerating fibroids commonly mimic adnexal masses in pregnancy; look for uterine continuity (bridging vessels/cleavage plane) but remember that torsion/degeneration may alter typical appearances and prompt surgery when maternal compromise or diagnostic uncertainty exists — as illustrated by this case.
