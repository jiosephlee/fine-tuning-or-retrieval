Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Provide a broad differential diagnosis for large, unilocular cystic abdominal masses detected in the second trimester with dyspnoea and pain, and identify imaging or clinical features that would raise oncologic concern.

Direct answer
Large, unilocular cystic abdominal masses in the second trimester with pain and dyspnoea have a broad differential that includes uterine, adnexal, and non‑gynecologic etiologies. The key clinical and imaging features that should raise oncologic concern are solid/papillary nodules, irregular thick septations or walls, internal (low‑resistance) vascularity, ascites or peritoneal implants, bilateral ovarian involvement, rapidly progressive growth, and a rising trend in tumor markers that is unexplained by pregnancy.

Differential diagnosis (practical groups and distinguishing clues)
- Uterine
  - Pedunculated or exophytic leiomyoma with cystic/red degeneration. Can be large, unilocular, and highly vascular; torsion of a pedicle causes acute pain, fever and necrosis (exactly what occurred in this case). Clue: continuity with the uterus or a bridging vascular pedicle on Doppler/MRI favors uterine origin.
- Adnexal/ovarian
  - Endometrioma (decidualised in pregnancy): typically T1‑hyperintense from blood products; decidualisation can produce vascularised papillary nodules that mimic malignancy.
  - Benign epithelial cystadenoma (serous/mucinous) and mucinous cystadenoma (often very large, multilocular).
  - Borderline or invasive epithelial ovarian tumors: commonly have mural nodules/papillary projections, irregular septa, ascites and internal vascularity.
  - Mature cystic teratoma: fat signal on MRI/fat‑suppression sequences or calcifications on ultrasound.
  - Paraovarian/paratubal cysts (unilocular, mobile, thin‑walled).
- Inflammatory / infectious
  - Tubo‑ovarian abscess: complex cystic mass with systemic infection signs (fever, elevated CRP/WBC)—but pregnancy can distort the picture.
- Mesenteric/omental/retroperitoneal cysts and lymphatic malformations
  - Large unilocular cysts, typically without internal vascularised solid nodules.
- Visceral cysts (less likely here but anatomically relevant)
  - Splenic cyst, pancreatic pseudocyst or subphrenic collections — location, relation to spleen/pancreas and history help.
- Rare malignant possibilities
  - Cystic degeneration in sarcoma or metastatic cystic lesions (Krukenberg metastases are usually solid/bilateral).

Imaging and clinical features that should raise oncologic concern
- Sonography
  - Any internal solid component, papillary projections or mural nodularity.
  - Thick, irregular septations or walls (septations > a few millimetres are suspicious).
  - Internal colour Doppler flow within solid components (particularly low‑resistance flow).
  - Bilaterality, associated ascites, peritoneal implants or pleural effusion.
- MRI (non‑contrast sequences useful in pregnancy)
  - Solid nodules that show restricted diffusion on DWI (malignant tissue often restricts).
  - Irregular, thickened walls or mural nodules; lack of a clear cleavage plane with peritoneal disease or lymphadenopathy.
  - T1 hyperintensity consistent with fat (teratoma) versus blood (endometrioma/red degeneration)—interpret in context.
  - Enhancing features on contrast would be most specific but gadolinium is avoided in pregnancy; non‑contrast DWI and detailed anatomic T1/T2/fat‑sat sequences are the workhorse.
- Laboratory / clinical
  - A rising trend in CA‑125 in the second trimester is atypical (the case had CA‑125 41.8 U/mL, up from 36 two weeks earlier) and warrants cautious evaluation; absolute CA‑125 has limited specificity in pregnancy.
  - Progressive symptoms (worsening pain, dyspnoea from mass effect), unexplained systemic signs or rapid growth despite conservative measures increase urgency.
  - New ascites, cachexia, or evidence of metastatic disease on imaging.

How to apply this in practice (diagnostic and management principles)
- Start with careful pelvic bimanual and US (transvaginal + transabdominal) with colour Doppler; apply IOTA features to stratify risk (solid nodules, papillary projections, thick septa, ascites increase concern).
- When US is inconclusive, obtain non‑contrast MRI at 1.5 T with T1, T2, fat‑sat and DWI sequences to look for continuity with the uterus (bridging vessel sign), blood/fat signal, cystic degeneration patterns, and diffusion characteristics.
- Interpret tumor markers in context: CA‑125 is frequently elevated in the first and third trimesters and with benign conditions (endometriosis, myoma, inflammation); trends are more informative than a single value.
- Indications for operative management in pregnancy: strong suspicion of malignancy, acute complications (torsion, rupture, haemorrhage, infection), or progressive mass effect compromising maternal respiration or the ability of the uterus to expand — balance risks to fetus and mother. Second trimester is preferred timing for elective surgery; large symptomatic masses (as in this case) may require laparotomy.
- If surgery is undertaken, minimize operative time and blood loss, perform fetal monitoring as feasible, obtain peritoneal washings if malignancy is suspected, and aim to preserve pregnancy when safe.

Application to the presented case — why surgery was reasonable
- The patient had two very large masses producing pain and dyspnoea from mass effect, a low‑grade fever and leukocytosis, MRI features that included thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid and focal T1/T2 hyperintense solid components, and a rising CA‑125 (41.8 U/mL). These combined factors produced both symptomatic imperative and diagnostic uncertainty (endometrioma, cystic ovarian neoplasm, or a degenerating/twisted myoma).
- Laparotomy identified a torsed, pedunculated cystic leiomyoma (explaining pain, fever and ischemic necrosis) and an adherent cystic intramural–subserosal myoma left in situ to minimize obstetric risk — an outcome that illustrates why degenerating fibroids can mimic adnexal neoplasms and why clinical context plus imaging features guide the decision to operate.

Clinical pearls
- Large, unilocular cystic masses in pregnancy demand integration of clinical signs, serial imaging and cautious use of tumor markers; non‑contrast MRI with DWI is the safest next step when ultrasound is indeterminate.
- Features that heighten oncologic concern are internal solid/papillary nodules, irregular thick septa/wall, internal vascularity, ascites or peritoneal disease, bilateral involvement, and an unexplained rising tumor‑marker trend.
- Pedunculated or degenerating fibroids commonly mimic adnexal masses in pregnancy; look for uterine continuity (bridging vessels/cleavage plane) but remember that torsion/degeneration may alter typical appearances and prompt surgery when maternal compromise or diagnostic uncertainty exists — as illustrated by this case.
