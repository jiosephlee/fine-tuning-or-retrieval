Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Pathophysiology] How do uterine fibroids typically change in size across pregnancy and the early postpartum period, and how should this trajectory inform the timing of any deferred myomectomy?

Direct answer
Uterine leiomyomas typically enlarge during pregnancy (most rapidly in early gestation) and then commonly regress after delivery. Published pregnancy-series summarized in the case report show the fastest growth between the first and second trimesters (≈6% increase per week), a slower but continued gain between the second and third trimesters (≈3% per week), and a marked postpartum involution — a mean size reduction of ≈44% by 6 weeks after delivery (wide variability, SD ≈36%). Clinically, that trajectory argues strongly for deferring elective myomectomy until the uterus has involuted and myoma size has stabilized unless there is an urgent indication.

Why this matters (pathophysiology and clinical reasoning)
- Hormonal milieu and increased uterine blood flow in pregnancy (estrogen/progesterone, growth factors, decidual changes) drive non‑linear myoma growth early in pregnancy; growth slows later in gestation.  
- After delivery the abrupt fall in circulating hormones and uterine involution commonly produces substantial shrinkage of leiomyomas within weeks. Because of that natural regression, many lesions that are sizable or symptomatic in pregnancy become smaller and asymptomatic postpartum — avoiding unnecessary surgery.  
- Operative risk is higher when the uterus is enlarged and hypervascular (greater bleeding risk), so postponing elective myomectomy reduces perioperative hemorrhage and technical difficulty.

How this informs timing of deferred myomectomy (practical guidance)
- Urgent surgery during pregnancy is reserved for complications (torsion of a pedunculated myoma, infection, intractable pain, suspicion of malignancy, or mass effect threatening maternal/fetal well‑being). The case illustrates this principle: the twisted, leaking pedunculated myoma at 21+6 weeks was appropriately excised, whereas the fixed caudal myoma was left in situ.  
- If no urgent indication exists, re‑evaluate after delivery. A first postpartum reassessment at about 6 weeks is reasonable because many myomas show substantial involution by then (mean ≈44% reduction).  
- If symptoms persist or the myoma remains obstructive/large at 6 weeks, plan definitive, elective myomectomy once size has stabilized — commonly at 6–12 weeks to 3 months postpartum in clinical practice. Waiting longer (3 months) may give maximal involution and allows optimization (iron repletion, breastfeeding counselling, surgical planning).  
- Document size, number and location with the same imaging modality (serial ultrasound; MRI reserved for complex mapping) before scheduling surgery; optimize hemoglobin and blood availability.

Application to the presented case
- The team removed the pedunculated, torsed upper myoma during pregnancy because of acute complication (fever, leak, vascularised pedicle) — an accepted indication. The caudal intramural–subserosal myoma was left in place; on follow‑up it had slightly reduced to 7.5 cm and the patient was asymptomatic, supporting conservative postpartum management and continued observation rather than immediate surgery.

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
Because myomas often grow during pregnancy—especially early—and commonly regress substantially by about 6 weeks postpartum, elective myomectomy is best deferred until after puerperal involution and reassessment (usually ≥6 weeks, often targeted around 6–12 weeks or 3 months), unless acute complications demand earlier intervention.
