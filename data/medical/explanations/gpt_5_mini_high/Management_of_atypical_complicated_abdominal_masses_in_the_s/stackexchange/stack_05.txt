Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] If non-obstetric abdominal surgery is undertaken at around 22 weeks’ gestation, what perioperative steps minimize maternal–fetal risk, including fetal assessment before, during, and after the procedure and intraoperative strategies to reduce uterine stimulation and hypoperfusion?

Short answer
When non‑obetric abdominal surgery is required in the second trimester the perioperative priorities are (1) optimize maternal physiology to preserve uteroplacental perfusion, (2) minimise direct uterine stimulation/manipulation, and (3) document fetal viability before, during and after the procedure with contingency plans if the fetus is viable. The case you read illustrates these principles: multidisciplinary planning, pre‑op ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI, intra‑operative verification of fetal cardiac activity by ultrasound, limited removal of only the symptomatic pedunculated myoma, intra‑operative progesterone given by the team, meticulous haemostasis and early correction of anaemia — all measures that contributed to a good maternal and fetal outcome.

Key elements and the rationale

1) Pre‑operative preparation and planning
- Multidisciplinary team (obstetrics/MFM, anaesthesia, surgery, neonatology when the fetus is potentially viable) and explicit informed consent that includes fetal risk. In the report a multidisciplinary evaluation guided decision making.
- Confirm fetal viability, gestational age and placental location with ultrasound immediately before surgery. Non‑contrast MRI is safe after the first trimester and useful for surgical planning (they used MRI without gadolinium).
- Correct reversible maternal problems that reduce uteroplacental oxygen delivery: treat infection, optimise haemoglobin (type & screen/crossmatch blood available), correct fluid/electrolyte abnormalities. In the case the team treated the UTI and addressed iron deficiency/transfusion post‑op.
- Anticipate blood loss and have blood products and haemorrhage plan available; choose the surgical approach that minimises operative time and uterine manipulation (team chose a vertical midline laparotomy and limited the procedure to the symptomatic pedunculated myoma).

2) Fetal assessment: before, during and after surgery
- Before: document fetal cardiac activity and estimated GA by ultrasound; if fetus is at or above institution’s viability threshold, notify neonatology and discuss availability for immediate delivery if needed.
- During: document fetal heart activity before incision, intermittently during the procedure if continuous monitoring is not feasible, and immediately after the procedure. If the fetus is potentially viable (commonly ≥24 weeks, institutional thresholds vary) continuous fetal heart monitoring in the OR is recommended when technically possible and when there is the capability to perform an emergency cesarean. At 21+6 weeks (as in this case) continuous monitoring would not be helpful; the team used serial/intermittent transvaginal ultrasound to document fetal cardiac activity intra‑operatively, consistent with published recommendations (Zhao et al).
- After: perform immediate postoperative fetal assessment (ultrasound or cardiotocography if viable), observe for uterine contractions or signs of preterm labour, and arrange short‑interval obstetric follow‑up (the case used strict follow‑up every 3 weeks with growth scans).

3) Intra‑operative strategies to preserve uteroplacental perfusion and limit uterine stimulation
- Positioning: left uterine displacement (15–30°) or right hip wedge to avoid aortocaval compression from 20 weeks onward — preserves venous return and cardiac output.
- Maintain maternal oxygenation and ventilation: keep SpO2 ≥95%, avoid hypoxia; maintain normocapnia (avoid hyperventilation which reduces uterine blood flow via vasoconstriction, and avoid hypoventilation which causes fetal acidosis).
- Maintain maternal blood pressure and intravascular volume: uteroplacental flow is pressure‑dependent. Treat hypotension promptly (vasopressors such as phenylephrine are commonly used and effective), give fluids judiciously, transfuse early when indicated.
- Minimise uterine manipulation and traction, keep the operation as brief as feasible, and use careful, rapid haemostasis. The surgeons in the case excised only the pedunculated lesion and left the adherent pelvic mass in situ to reduce uterine trauma and operative time.
- Avoid routine prophylactic uterotonics; avoid drugs known to provoke contractions unless clinically required. Use tocolytics only selectively for active uterine contractions (evidence for routine prophylactic tocolysis is limited).
- Anaesthetic considerations: rapid‑sequence induction (pregnancy increases aspiration risk), avoid teratogens in the first trimester (less relevant in second trimester), invasive monitoring (arterial line) when large blood loss is anticipated, and temperature control.
- Infection prophylaxis and thromboprophylaxis: give perioperative antibiotics (case used cefazolin), and institute VTE prophylaxis appropriate to bleeding risk and gestation.

4) Role of pharmacologic prophylaxis for preterm labour
- There is no high‑quality evidence to mandate routine tocolysis for all operative pregnancies. Some teams use progesterone prophylaxis or tocolytics selectively. In this report the team administered intramuscular progesterone intra‑operatively and continued parenteral progesterone thereafter; that was a centre‑specific strategy rather than an evidence‑based universal requirement.

5) Postoperative care and delivery planning
- Monitor maternal haemodynamics, correct anaemia and infection promptly (patient required IV iron and transfusion post‑op). Early mobilisation and VTE prophylaxis.
- Reassess fetal wellbeing (CTG or ultrasound) and watch for preterm labour; if delivery becomes likely, give antenatal corticosteroids and magnesium sulphate according to gestational‑age thresholds used locally (typically recommended when delivery is imminent and fetus is within the window of benefit).
- Individualise delivery planning based on residual pathology (the retained pelvic myoma in this case led to elective cesarean at term).

Practical checklist (brief)
- Multidisciplinary plan + informed consent
- Pre‑op US to confirm FHR, GA, placenta; non‑contrast MRI if needed
- Type & screen/crossmatch, treat infection, correct anaemia
- Anaesthesia plan: aspiration prophylaxis, left tilt, invasive monitoring if indicated
- Fetal monitoring strategy decided pre‑op (continuous if viable & feasible; intermittent US if previable or if continuous monitoring impossible)
- Minimise uterine manipulation, limit operative time, meticulous haemostasis
- Perioperative antibiotics, thromboprophylaxis, neonatal backup if viable
- Post‑op: immediate fetal reassessment, serial obs, treat maternal problems, arrange obstetric follow‑up

Bottom line
Successful non‑obstetric abdominal surgery in the mid‑second trimester rests on multidisciplinary preparation, strict maternal physiologic support to preserve uteroplacental perfusion, judicious minimisation of uterine manipulation, and documentation of fetal viability before/during/after the case with readiness to deliver if the fetus is viable and distressed. The presented case is a practical example of those principles: targeted surgery, intra‑op fetal checks by ultrasound, optimisation of maternal status, and close obstetric follow‑up leading to a favourable outcome.
