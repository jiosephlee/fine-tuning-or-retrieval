Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Morning Report] In a 21-week pregnancy with severe abdominal pain, dyspnoea, and two large abdominal masses on ultrasound, which imaging modalities are safe and most informative for further characterization, and what contrast or field-strength considerations apply during pregnancy?

Direct answer: Start with targeted ultrasound (transabdominal ± transvaginal) with colour Doppler as the first-line, and use non‑contrast MRI as the second‑line problem‑solving study. During pregnancy avoid gadolinium contrast and, when possible, limit MRI field strength to 1.5‑T (the team in this case specifically avoided >1.5‑T and performed a non‑enhanced MRI).

Why: 
- Ultrasound is safe throughout pregnancy, bedside, and excellent for determining cystic versus solid components, vascularity of a mass, presence of a pedicle, and relationship to the uterus and adnexa. In this patient US showed two large unilocular cystic lesions with peripheral vascularisation and raised the differential (ovarian endometrioma versus uterine myoma with cystic degeneration). Transvaginal scanning is useful for pelvic masses and for defining distance from the endometrial cavity.
- MRI without contrast provides superior soft‑tissue characterisation and anatomic mapping for large, posterior, multiple or indeterminate masses (T1/T2, fat‑suppressed sequences, and diffusion sequences can identify haemorrhage, necrosis, cystic degeneration and solid nodules). In the case report a non‑contrast MRI (T1/T2 fat‑sat) demonstrated thick walls and focal hyperintense components and guided multidisciplinary decision‑making.

Contrast and field strength considerations:
- Gadolinium crosses the placenta and is avoided in pregnancy unless absolutely necessary because of possible fetal retention and associations with adverse outcomes in observational data; thus non‑contrast MRI is preferred.
- Many centers prefer 1.5‑T MRI in pregnancy because higher field strengths (3‑T) increase specific absorption rate and acoustic noise (theoretical risks of heating); the team here avoided >1.5‑T. If 3‑T is the only available option, weigh urgency vs theoretical risk and follow institutional MRI‑in‑pregnancy protocols.
- CT and iodinated contrast involve ionising radiation and placental transfer of iodide; CT should be reserved for situations where MRI/US cannot answer the clinical question (trauma, suspected bowel perforation, or when vascular mapping is essential). If iodinated contrast is used, neonatal thyroid function should be checked.

Clinical takeaways:
- Use bimanual exam + targeted US with colour Doppler first; proceed to non‑contrast MRI for large, posterior, or indeterminate masses.
- Avoid gadolinium in pregnancy and preferentially use 1.5‑T MRI when available; reserve CT/contrast studies for exceptional circumstances after multidisciplinary discussion.
