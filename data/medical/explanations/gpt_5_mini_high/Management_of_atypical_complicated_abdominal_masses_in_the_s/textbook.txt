Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Initial Presentation, Stabilization, and Context

A nulliparous woman in her 40s, with a body mass index of 33, presented in the second trimester at 21 weeks’ gestation. Her routine prenatal screening and non‑invasive prenatal testing had shown low risk for aneuploidy and prior fetal morphology scans were normal. These demographic details are important when interpreting the clinical picture: advanced maternal age and obesity can both complicate the physical examination and imaging, and the patient’s nulliparity and desire to preserve the pregnancy framed subsequent risk–benefit decisions.

Her acute complaint on arrival was concurrent left upper‑quadrant and pelvic pain with associated dyspnoea; pain was scored 5/10 on the Visual Analogue Scale. In pregnant patients with new, localized abdominal pain and large palpable masses, the immediate differential includes torsion of a pedunculated lesion, acute degeneration or necrosis of a fibroid, rupture or infection of an adnexal cyst, and non‑gynaecological causes. The combination of pelvic and LUQ pain in this patient raised concern for one or more bulky intra‑abdominal masses producing local ischemia or mechanical complications. Symptoms were temporally responsive to initial conservative measures: intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) and semi‑orthostatic positioning reduced her pain score to 1/10 and eased dyspnoea without need for supplemental oxygen.

Initial bedside assessment prioritized exclusion of cardiopulmonary causes of dyspnoea and documentation of maternal stability. Pulse oximetry showed median oxygen saturation of 97%, heart rate was 74 beats per minute and blood pressure 100/60 mm Hg; ECG and thoracic examination were unremarkable. In the absence of hypoxaemia, tachycardia, abnormal ECG or lung findings, dyspnoea was attributed to mechanical restriction from the gravid uterus combined with the large abdominal masses. Physiologically, enlarging intra‑abdominal contents elevate the diaphragm and decrease functional residual capacity, producing a restrictive pattern of breathlessness that is common in late pregnancy or with large intra‑abdominal lesions and that is managed primarily by relieving mass effect and optimizing position.

The abdominal and pelvic examination provided key localization clues despite limitations imposed by pregnancy and obesity. The abdomen appeared larger than expected for gestational age and palpation revealed a taut‑elastic, tender mass approximately 18 cm occupying the left upper quadrant, without clinical signs of ascites. Bimanual pelvic examination identified a fixed, rounded, firm 9 cm mass that partially bulged into the posterior fornix. A posterior fornix bulge typically indicates a pelvic (adnexal or posterior uterine) lesion; a firm, fixed mass suggests a leiomyomatous origin or an adherent adnexal mass, whereas a taut‑elastic consistency can reflect cystic degeneration. Nonetheless, physical examination alone cannot reliably distinguish uterine from adnexal masses in pregnancy, so these findings were used to prioritize imaging and to counsel regarding likely diagnoses.

Laboratory testing was obtained to guide management and to search for an infectious trigger of fever and leukocytosis. The patient had iron‑deficiency anaemia (haemoglobin reported 9.9, ferritin 25 µg/L), a mild rise in C‑reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL) and a leukocytosis with neutrophil predominance (WBC 11.29×10^3/µL, neutrophils 9.27×10^3/µL). It is important to interpret white blood cell counts and inflammatory markers in pregnancy in the appropriate physiologic context because mild leukocytosis and modest CRP elevations are common; nevertheless, the development of a low‑grade fever prompted directed infection testing. A nasopharyngeal SARS‑CoV‑2 PCR was negative, while urine culture grew Klebsiella pneumoniae. Identification of a urinary tract infection in pregnancy changes management: asymptomatic bacteriuria and symptomatic UTI carry risks for pyelonephritis and preterm labour and therefore warrant organism‑directed antibiotics chosen with fetal safety and culture sensitivity in mind. In this case the urine culture result informed perioperative and inpatient antibiotic choices.

Summarizing the immediate priorities: the team focused on maternal stabilization (adequate analgesia and positioning to relieve mass effect and dyspnoea), documentation of baseline maternal and fetal status, and targeted laboratory evaluation to detect infection and quantify anaemia ahead of further imaging or intervention. These measures reduced symptoms, identified a treatable UTI, and provided the necessary clinical context to proceed with diagnostic imaging and multidisciplinary planning for definitive management while preserving the pregnancy.

## Pregnancy-Safe Diagnostics and Differential Diagnosis of Large Abdominal Masses

Evaluating large intra‑abdominal masses in pregnancy requires balancing diagnostic accuracy against fetal safety and the anatomic distortion produced by the gravid uterus. In practice this means a stepwise approach that begins with clinical examination and ultrasound, adds non‑contrast MRI when sonography is inconclusive or the lesion is large or posteriorly located, and interprets laboratory tests such as CA‑125 with caution. The present case—two large, cystic, vascularised abdominal masses in the second trimester—illustrates both the strengths and the limitations of the available tools.

Ultrasound with colour Doppler is the indispensable first‑line test in pregnancy. It is safe, widely available and allows assessment of size, internal architecture (unilocular versus multilocular, septations, solid mural nodules), wall thickness, vascularity and the spatial relationship to the uterus and adnexa. Transvaginal examination and careful bimanual palpation remain important supplements: in this patient the posterior fornix bulge on bimanual exam correctly localized the caudal lesion to the pouch of Douglas and argued for uterine adjacency. Ultrasound can, however, be limited by the “steric bulk” of an enlarging uterus and by very large lesions; feeding pedicles or thin stalks of pedunculated subserosal fibroids may be obscured, as happened with the upper mass here.

When ultrasound is insufficient to determine origin or to guide management, non‑contrast MRI after the first trimester is the next best modality. MRI adds soft‑tissue contrast and multiplanar detail that can clarify whether a mass is contiguous with the myometrium (favouring leiomyoma) or separate in the adnexa (favouring ovarian/paraovarian origin). In pregnancy the preferred practice is non‑contrast MRI on a 1.5‑T scanner; gadolinium is avoided unless there is a compelling maternal indication because it crosses the placenta and carries into‑utero exposure. In this case MRI confirmed two rounded, unilocular cysts, demonstrated a thick (1.4 cm) wall and inhomogeneous fluid on T1 and T2 fat‑saturated sequences, visualised focal T1/T2 hyperintense solid components and hypertrophied vessels, and suggested continuity between the caudal lesion and posterior uterine tissue—findings that narrowed the differential but did not provide absolute certainty.

Interpreting signal characteristics helps generate diagnostic hypotheses. T1 hyperintensity that persists on fat‑saturation most often reflects blood products or proteinaceous material rather than fat; this is a classic feature of endometriomas (old hemorrhage) and can also be seen with haemorrhagic or red degeneration of a fibroid. Heterogeneous T2 signal reflects varying content (liquid, blood, necrosis, fibrous tissue). Thickened walls and prominent peripheral or mural vascularity on Doppler or MRI suggest an active vascular supply (for example a pedicle to a subserosal fibroid) or vascularised mural nodules (which can be seen with decidualised endometriomas in pregnancy). The absence of pelvic or para‑aortic adenopathy on MRI argues against invasive ovarian malignancy but does not completely exclude it.

Serum tumour markers have important limitations in pregnancy and must be interpreted in context. CA‑125 is the marker most commonly considered for adnexal masses; physiologically it can be elevated in the first and third trimesters and is usually lower in the second trimester. A single, modest elevation (the patient’s CA‑125 was 41.8 U/mL, just above the conventional 35 U/mL cut‑off and higher than her prior 36 U/mL) is not diagnostic of malignancy in pregnancy and may reflect endometriosis, peritoneal irritation or fibroid degeneration. Serial measurements that show a rising trend are more informative than isolated values. Other markers (CA 19‑9, CA 15‑3, CEA, β2‑microglobulin, LDH) were normal in this case and therefore provided no support for a specific germ‑cell, mucinous or other marker‑positive ovarian malignancy.

A short pathophysiology review helps reconcile the imaging patterns. Endometriomas in pregnancy may undergo decidualisation under progesterone influence: the cyst contents remain blood‑stained (T1 hyperintense) and mural nodules can develop that are vascular on Doppler and that may mimic solid tumour on imaging. Conversely, leiomyomas commonly change during pregnancy: red (hemorrhagic/ischemic) degeneration causes acute pain, low‑grade fever and T1 hyperintense signal due to blood; cystic degeneration produces fluid‑filled areas and can render fibroids predominantly cystic. Pedunculated subserosal fibroids can undergo torsion, producing a painful, vascular‑compromised mass that may appear extra‑uterine on imaging. Any of these processes can produce overlapping sonographic and MR appearances.

Putting these elements together yields a focused differential. For a large unilocular cystic lesion with peripheral vascularity and T1 hyperintense contents, a decidualised endometrioma is a leading consideration in a reproductive‑age woman because of the combination of blood‑product signal and vascularised mural nodules; cystic/hemorrhagic degeneration of a leiomyoma—particularly a pedunculated subserosal fibroid—can produce an identical appearance if the uterine stalk is not visualised. Benign simple ovarian or paraovarian cysts tend to be thin‑walled, anechoic and avascular; complex ovarian neoplasms show multilocularity, papillary projections, solid components with persistent vascularity, ascites and sometimes nodal disease—features that raise concern for malignancy. In pregnancy the key malignant “red flags” remain mural solid nodules with aggressive features, rapid growth, ascites, lymphadenopathy and markedly abnormal tumour markers; the absence of these in this patient reduced but did not eliminate concern.

In this case the pieces were mixed: the mild upward trend in CA‑125 and the T1/T2 signal characteristics favoured an endometrioma for the upper lesion, but the caudal lesion’s anatomic continuity with the posterior myometrium on MRI and the bulge on bimanual exam suggested a myomatous origin. The patient’s dyspnoea was plausibly explained by mass effect and diaphragmatic limitation rather than cardiopulmonary disease. Because of severe pain, fever and the risk of torsion, ischaemia and further uterine compromise with fetal growth, the team proceeded to diagnostic and therapeutic laparotomy; surgical pathology ultimately confirmed a pedunculated, cystic, torsed leiomyoma cranially and a large intramural–subserosal leiomyoma caudally. The case highlights three practical points for clinical care: always start with ultrasound and careful pelvic examination; use non‑contrast MRI after the first trimester when origin or surgical planning is unclear; and interpret CA‑125 and other markers cautiously—serial trends and the whole clinical picture are far more useful than single values. When imaging and clinical findings conflict and the patient is symptomatic or at risk of complication, multidisciplinary discussion and timely surgical intervention remain appropriate despite the diagnostic uncertainty that pregnancy can impose.

## Operative Indications, Planning, and Technique in Mid-Trimester Myomectomy

Deciding to operate on uterine or adnexal masses during pregnancy requires weighing maternal benefits against fetal risks. In this case the team moved from conservative management to surgery because multiple converging concerns made expectant care unsafe: severe, refractory abdominal pain and mechanical dyspnoea from very large masses; clinical and laboratory signs suggesting complication (fever, a rising CA‑125 and leukocytosis); imaging that could not exclude a complicated or neoplastic process; and the realistic prospect that continued uterine and tumour growth would worsen compression and increase the risk of acute events (torsion, rupture, infection). These indications — symptomatic refractory pain and respiratory compromise, suspected torsion/necrosis, diagnostic uncertainty with oncological concern, and space‑limiting mass effect during a period of ongoing uterine enlargement — are the standard justifications for intervening in pregnancy when conservative measures fail or the mother’s clinical course is worsening.

Understanding the pathophysiology that drove the decision helps clarify the urgency. Pedunculated subserosal leiomyomas can twist on their stalk and occlude their vascular supply; torsion produces ischaemia, necrosis and often fever and increasing pain, and leaking/necrotic tissue can promote local inflammation or infection that threatens maternal health. Large intra‑abdominal masses also cause upward displacement of the diaphragm and restricted lung volumes, producing dyspnoea that can compromise maternal oxygenation and uteroplacental perfusion. Imaging in this patient showed mixed cystic and solid components and an enlarging CA‑125, creating diagnostic ambiguity (degenerating myoma versus decidualised endometrioma or less likely malignancy) and providing an additional rationale for obtaining a tissue diagnosis.

Timing and route of surgery were chosen to minimize fetal risk while maximizing maternal safety. The second trimester (after organogenesis and before the peak risk of preterm labour in the third trimester) is generally the preferred window for elective non‑obstetric abdominal surgery in pregnancy when intervention is needed. Within that window, the surgical approach is individualized. Laparoscopy is appropriate in early pregnancy for smaller, well‑located lesions, but large or posteriorly situated masses, an advanced gestational uterus, multiple lesions, distorted anatomy, or inability to identify a clear pedicle favour an open approach. In this case the dominant mass measured roughly 18 cm, the pelvis contained an adherent intramural–subserosal myoma, the fetus and gravid uterus occupied substantial intra‑abdominal space, and preoperative imaging could not demonstrate a stalk for the cranial lesion. Those factors, together with the need for rapid access to control bleeding if needed, supported a longitudinal xipho‑pubic laparotomy rather than laparoscopy.

Preoperative planning emphasized multidisciplinary discussion, informed consent, and contingency preparation. The obstetrician, maternal–fetal medicine specialist, surgical team, anesthesiologist and neonatal staff should participate in the planning meeting. The consent discussion must include the risks of pregnancy loss, preterm labour, haemorrhage with possible transfusion, need for hysterectomy in the event of uncontrollable bleeding, and neonatal implications. Practical preparations include baseline and cross‑matched blood, correction of anaemia as feasible, treatment of active infection (the team initiated culture‑directed antibiotics for the Klebsiella urinary infection), and availability of neonatal resuscitation. Because continuous fetal cardiotocography is not reliable in mid‑second trimester, the team documented fetal cardiac activity before entering the abdomen and monitored viability intraoperatively with serial intermittent transvaginal ultrasound, documenting fetal heart rate before, during and after the procedure as part of standard perioperative obstetric preparedness.

The intraoperative strategy followed a conservative, targeted plan: obtain diagnostic samples, remove only tissue that posed imminent maternal risk, and minimize uterine handling. On entry the surgeons sampled free peritoneal fluid for culture and cytology because imaging had not definitively excluded a neoplasm. They confirmed fetal cardiac activity, then inspected the lesions. The upper mass proved to be a large, pedunculated, atypical cystic leiomyoma that had become twisted and was leaking — findings consistent with the preoperative fever and pain. The team controlled the pedicle with direct mechanical clamping (double clamp), resected the mass, and closed the uterine implantation site with an absorbable suture. These maneuvers achieve rapid vascular control and secure closure of the uterine defect while keeping operative time and uterine trauma to a minimum. The caudal lesion, in contrast, was a fixed, soft intramural–subserosal myoma adherent to the posterior uterine wall; after intraoperative assessment the team elected to leave it in place to avoid extensive dissection that would have increased bleeding, uterine irritability and the risk of pregnancy loss.

Selective myomectomy — removing only the lesion that is symptomatic or at high risk of acute complication and leaving deep intramural or adherent myomas in situ — is a pragmatic approach in pregnant patients when removal of all lesions would cause disproportionate risk. In this patient the twisted pedunculated tumor represented an immediate threat (ischaemia, infection, ongoing leakage), while removal of the fixed caudal myoma would have required deeper uterine incisions and prolonged surgery, with a substantially higher chance of haemorrhage and uterine contraction. By addressing the imminently dangerous lesion only, the team treated the acute problem, obtained tissue for definitive pathology (which later confirmed benign leiomyoma with ischaemic necrosis), and preserved the pregnancy.

Adjunctive and perioperative measures in this case reflect accepted tactics to protect the pregnancy and control infection and bleeding. The team administered single‑dose perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis (cefazolin) and continued culture‑directed antibiotics postoperatively because of the urinary Klebsiella. To reduce the risk of uterine irritability and support pregnancy continuation they began intramuscular progesterone intraoperatively and continued it through discharge, then switched to hydroxyprogesterone caproate for maintenance until planned delivery. Practical intraoperative strategies to limit fetal risk included minimizing uterine manipulation, keeping the operation as brief as possible (the procedure lasted 2 hours 25 minutes), and maintaining strict hemodynamic control to preserve uteroplacental perfusion. Despite these efforts the patient did develop post‑operative anaemia requiring intravenous iron and transfusion; this highlights the importance of preoperative cross‑matching and readiness for blood replacement.

Pregnancy‑specific risk mitigation is integral to operative planning and post‑operative care. Maternal hemodynamic stability must be prioritized to ensure uteroplacental perfusion, and meticulous haemostasis is required in the hypervascular pregnant uterus to limit blood loss. Teams should be prepared to manage preterm contractions should they develop (tocolytics are an option in selected circumstances) and to provide intensive fetal‑neonatal support if early delivery becomes necessary. Documentation of fetal heart rate before, during and after surgery is mandatory, and regular obstetric follow‑up postoperatively is required; in this case the patient had serial ultrasounds and ultimately an elective caesarean delivery at 37 weeks because the residual pelvic myoma would have mechanically impeded vaginal birth.

In summary, mid‑trimester myomectomy can be appropriate when maternal symptoms, evidence of tumour complication, diagnostic uncertainty, or rapid growth create unacceptable risks for the mother or fetus. The safest course is individualized: operate in the second trimester when possible, choose an approach (open versus minimally invasive) that provides safe exposure and rapid haemorrhage control for the anatomy encountered, plan carefully with a multidisciplinary team, limit the operation to what is necessary (selective myomectomy), and institute perioperative measures to protect the pregnancy. This case illustrates that such a focused, cautious approach can resolve an acute maternal problem, secure a tissue diagnosis, and allow continuation of a healthy pregnancy to term.

## Postoperative Care, Ongoing Obstetric Management, and Delivery

After intra‑abdominal surgery in pregnancy, immediate priorities are maternal stabilization, optimization of oxygen‑carrying capacity, reassurance of fetal well‑being, and a clear plan for ongoing obstetric surveillance and timing of delivery. In this case those priorities were addressed sequentially and illustrate the principles that guide postoperative care for pregnant patients with complicated uterine masses.

The first postoperative problem was anaemia. The patient’s haemoglobin fell from 9.9 g/dL preoperatively to 7.5 g/dL (HCT 24.3%) after surgery, with iron deficiency (ferritin 25 mcg/L). This degree of anaemia in the perioperative pregnant patient justifies rapid correction because maternal anaemia reduces oxygen delivery to the placenta and fetus and impairs maternal recovery. The team used a combined approach: two units of packed red blood cells to restore haemoglobin promptly and intravenous iron (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) to replete iron stores and support ongoing erythropoiesis. Hemoglobin was reassessed and rose to 10.0 g/dL (HCT 30.4%) after therapy. Teaching points for trainees are to recognize when oral iron is too slow or inadequate (acute blood loss, significant symptomatic anaemia, or limited time before anticipated delivery) and to follow haemoglobin and iron indices after transfusion/IV iron (typically recheck within 24–72 hours and again at outpatient follow‑up) to document response and guide further therapy.

Pathology and peritoneal fluid analysis guided longer‑term decisions. The excised cranial mass proved to be a benign leiomyoma with areas of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle, correlating with the preoperative pain, low‑grade fever and inflammatory markers. Torsion of a pedunculated myoma produces venous congestion, ischaemia and sterile inflammation, which explains the clinical picture in this patient. Peritoneal fluid was negative for bacterial culture and cytology, which lowered concern for intra‑abdominal infection or malignant dissemination and supported a conservative strategy for the residual pelvic mass. In practice, tissue diagnosis and negative cytology after surgical exploration allow teams to avoid unnecessary additional oncologic interventions during pregnancy.

Following recovery from surgery, the obstetric plan emphasized close surveillance. Because uterine surgery and large pelvic masses can affect placental perfusion and increase the risk of preterm birth, the team instituted serial ultrasound assessments every three weeks focusing on fetal growth and amniotic fluid. In this case the fetus tracked well (86th percentile) and amniotic fluid remained normal. Fetal cardiac activity had been documented before, during and after the laparotomy, consistent with recommended practice to confirm ongoing viability when intervening surgically in the second trimester. The multidisciplinary team also administered progesterone prophylaxis starting intraoperatively (intramuscular progesterone 100 mg daily while inpatient, then intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate per the institutional regimen until delivery). The rationale was to support uterine quiescence and reduce the risk of preterm contractions after uterine manipulation; protocols vary between centres, but the goal is prevention of preterm labour when a uterine procedure has occurred.

Delivery planning was individualized to the anatomical and obstetric risks posed by the remaining pelvic myoma. The caudal lesion originated from the uterine torus/posterior lower segment and was large enough to be expected to impede descent of the fetal head; attempting a trial of labour carried a significant risk of obstructed labour and potential uterine rupture given prior myomectomy. For these reasons the team planned an elective cesarean delivery once fetal maturity was achieved, at 37 weeks. Performing the cesarean on the existing midline (xipho‑pubic) abdominal scar avoided a new abdominal incision and allowed the obstetric and surgical teams to manage adhesions and any bleeding safely. Timing at 37 weeks balanced minimizing the risks of spontaneous labour and scar stress against the risks of neonatal immaturity. Practical considerations for such deliveries include ensuring availability of blood products (because of prior anaemia/transfusion and the potential for haemorrhage), arranging surgical expertise for potential difficult dissection around large fibroids, and clear preoperative counselling about the likelihood of cesarean and deferred myomectomy.

The short‑term outcome in this case was excellent: the patient recovered without surgical complications, the neonate was healthy, and the retained pelvic myoma showed partial postpartum regression (to approximately 7.5 cm). Postpartum shrinkage of fibroids is commonly seen as hormones fall and the uterus involutes; breastfeeding and lactational amenorrhoea may accentuate this effect. Because the patient was asymptomatic and the mass had decreased in size, she and the team elected to defer further surgical intervention. This shared decision reflects the key principle of weighing current symptoms, degree of mass effect, future fertility desires and surgical risks when counselling patients after pregnancy.

Long‑term considerations to discuss with patients in this situation include the natural history of fibroid regression postpartum, indications for delayed myomectomy (persistent pain, mass effect, rapid regrowth, heavy bleeding or oncologic concern), and the timing of any definitive surgery once the uterus has involuted and breastfeeding is complete. Future pregnancy planning should include preconception imaging to reassess size/location and discussion of whether a planned preconception myomectomy would be advisable if the fibroid remains obstructive. Finally, trainees should be aware of adjunctive perioperative considerations in similar patients—thromboembolism prophylaxis in obese or immobile postoperative patients, and careful anaesthetic and blood‑product planning for the cesarean delivery—even though these specifics were not detailed in this report.

In summary, this case illustrates a coordinated approach: prompt correction of clinically significant postoperative anaemia with transfusion plus IV iron, use of pathology and peritoneal fluid results to exclude malignancy and infection, structured fetal surveillance with interval ultrasound, targeted progesterone prophylaxis after uterine surgery, and delivery timing and route determined by the mechanical and scar‑related risks posed by the residual myoma. Shared decision‑making and planned reassessment after the immediate postpartum period permitted conservative, pregnancy‑preserving care with an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome.

## Key Teaching Points and Evidence-Guided Practice

Atypical or large abdominal masses in pregnancy require a structured, multidisciplinary approach that balances maternal and fetal safety with the low absolute risk of malignancy. The case presented—two large cystic masses in the second trimester, one ultimately proven to be a twisted pedunculated leiomyoma—illustrates three recurring themes: (1) careful bedside assessment combined with targeted imaging narrows the differential, (2) tumour markers in pregnancy are only adjunctive and must be interpreted in context, and (3) surgery should be reserved for clear maternal–fetal indications or diagnostic uncertainty that threatens pregnancy, with techniques aimed at preserving the pregnancy and minimizing morbidity.

Structured evaluation algorithm. Begin with a focused history (onset, severity and character of pain; fever; prior known myomas or endometriosis; reproductive history) and a targeted physical examination including careful abdominal inspection and bimanual pelvic palpation to assess size, mobility and whether a pelvic mass bulges into a fornix. First-line investigations are laboratory tests (CBC, CRP, urine culture) and a transabdominal/transvaginal ultrasound with colour Doppler. If ultrasound is inconclusive because of posterior location, multiple large lesions or poor windows, obtain a non‑contrast MRI after the first trimester using 1.5 T where possible. Tumour markers (most commonly CA‑125) may be measured at baseline, but trends over time are more informative than a single value. Practically, this becomes a stepwise decision tree: if the patient is stable and imaging favors benign leiomyoma, manage conservatively with symptom control and serial imaging; if symptoms are severe, there is clinical or imaging evidence of complication (torsion, rupture, infection, rapid expansion causing compromise), or imaging raises concern for malignancy, escalate to specialist review and consider surgery.

Differentiating fibroids from adnexal masses: imaging pearls and pitfalls. On ultrasound and MRI, look for continuity with the myometrium (bridging vessels or a visible stalk) and for the “whorled” internal architecture that favors leiomyoma. Pedunculated subserosal fibroids may be highly vascularized and can lose an identifiable stalk on imaging when the gravid uterus displaces anatomy; cystic degeneration produces fluid-filled cavities that can mimic adnexal cysts. Endometriomas classically have high T1 signal and “shading” on T2, but in pregnancy decidualization can produce vascularized mural nodules that mimic neoplasm. On MRI, typical leiomyomas are low signal on T2 unless degenerated (which produces heterogeneous or hyperintense fluid signals and thickened walls). Worrisome imaging features prompting escalation include irregular solid mural nodules, papillary excrescences, thick irregular walls, ascites or adenopathy—but in pregnancy the absence of gadolinium limits assessment of enhancement, so interpretation must integrate clinical context. The present case exemplifies the pitfall: a large, cystic, well‑vascularized pedunculated myoma that appeared adnexal because a stalk was not identified preoperatively.

Interpreting CA‑125 in pregnancy. CA‑125 values vary by trimester (median values reported higher in the first and third trimesters than in the second), so an isolated mild elevation in pregnancy is nonspecific. CA‑125 can rise with benign conditions including endometriosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, myomas and early/late pregnancy. Serial measurements that show a rising trend, especially when accompanied by suspicious imaging or clinical deterioration, should prompt deeper evaluation. Conversely, a single modest elevation in an otherwise benign-appearing lesion should not alone mandate surgery.

When to operate: maternal–fetal indications and risk balance. Indications for operative intervention during pregnancy are primarily maternal or fetal compromise (severe uncontrolled pain, respiratory compromise from mass effect, active infection, torsion or rupture), strong concern for neoplasm that cannot be reasonably observed, or anatomic factors that will almost certainly obstruct later gestation uterine expansion or delivery. Surgery carries risks (bleeding, preterm labour, pregnancy loss), so the threshold should be higher than outside pregnancy. In this case the combination of increasing pain and dyspnoea from mass effect, fever and possible torsion of a vascular pedunculated mass, a rising CA‑125 trend, and the projected future space-limitation for the growing uterus justified a timely laparotomy and selective myomectomy.

Choosing laparoscopy versus laparotomy. The choice depends on gestational age, size and location of the lesion, number of masses, and surgeon experience. Laparoscopy (with an open/Hasson entry) is generally favored for smaller, accessible pedunculated lesions and in early pregnancy when the uterus is smaller; it reduces recovery time and adhesions. Large, posterior, intramural or multiple myomas, or very large adnexal masses, commonly require laparotomy for safe exposure and haemorrhage control—this was the pragmatic choice in the case because of lesion size and the second‑trimester gravid uterus. Always plan anesthesia and monitoring with obstetric and neonatal teams available.

Operative pearls in pregnancy. Keep the operation as short as possible and minimize uterine manipulation. For pedunculated myomas, secure the pedicle (for example, double clamp and ligation) before division and close the uterine defect with meticulous suturing to reduce bleeding and preserve uterine integrity. Be prepared with blood products and correct preoperative anaemia if possible; intra‑ and postoperative antibiotics should be guided by cultures. If resection of an additional lesion would substantially increase obstetric risk (bleeding, prolonged uterine opening), consider leaving it in situ, with planned postpartum reassessment—exactly the approach taken for the caudal mass in this patient. Note that the use of intraoperative progesterone was part of the team’s strategy here, but evidence for routine progesterone prophylaxis around non‑obstetric surgery is limited and institutional practice varies.

Fetal monitoring standards for non‑obstetric surgery. Document fetal heart activity before, intermittently during (as feasible), and immediately after the procedure. Continuous intraoperative fetal monitoring is ideal for viable gestations when logistic conditions allow; if not possible, pre‑ and post‑operative ultrasound or Doppler assessment is mandatory. Coordinate perioperative care with anesthesia and neonatal resuscitation teams and have a plan for emergent delivery if fetal compromise or uterine rupture occurs.

Delivery planning when fibroids are present. Mode of delivery should be individualized according to the size, number and location of myomas and obstetric indications. A myoma located in the lower uterine segment or torus that is likely to obstruct labor often leads to planned cesarean delivery, whereas many patients with non‑obstructing myomas can have a trial of labor. Myomectomy at the time of cesarean can be performed selectively; evidence from tertiary series indicates it can be done safely in experienced hands without significant increases in transfusion rates but with longer operative time and hospital stay. Counseling should emphasize these trade‑offs and the possibility of postpartum myoma regression.

Natural history and counseling to avoid overtreatment. Leiomyomas commonly enlarge early in pregnancy (reported growth acceleration most marked in the first trimester) and may slow or regress in the third trimester and postpartum (mean reported postpartum reductions can be substantial). This dynamic argues for expectant management when the maternal–fetal situation permits, with re‑evaluation after delivery before committing to definitive surgical management. In this patient the residual pelvic myoma decreased in size postpartum and the patient elected to defer further surgery.

Imaging safety principles. Ultrasound with colour Doppler is the first‑line imaging modality in pregnancy. When ultrasound is inconclusive, non‑contrast MRI with a 1.5‑T system after the first trimester provides useful anatomic detail without ionising radiation; gadolinium should generally be avoided in pregnancy unless the clinical benefits clearly outweigh potential fetal risks.

In summary, the practical approach to an atypical abdominal mass in pregnancy is to integrate careful history and examination with stepwise imaging and selective laboratory evaluation; reserve surgery for clear maternal or fetal indications or when diagnostic uncertainty cannot be resolved non‑operatively; choose the least invasive and safest operative approach that achieves haemostasis and preserves the pregnancy; and plan delivery and postpartum reassessment with an expectation that some lesions will regress after delivery. Multidisciplinary discussion at every decision point is essential to tailor management to the individual patient and to avoid both undertreatment of complications and unnecessary intervention.
