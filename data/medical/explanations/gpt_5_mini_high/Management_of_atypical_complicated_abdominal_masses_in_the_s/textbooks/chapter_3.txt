Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 3: ## Operative Indications, Planning, and Technique in Mid-Trimester Myomectomy

Deciding to operate on uterine or adnexal masses during pregnancy requires weighing maternal benefits against fetal risks. In this case the team moved from conservative management to surgery because multiple converging concerns made expectant care unsafe: severe, refractory abdominal pain and mechanical dyspnoea from very large masses; clinical and laboratory signs suggesting complication (fever, a rising CA‑125 and leukocytosis); imaging that could not exclude a complicated or neoplastic process; and the realistic prospect that continued uterine and tumour growth would worsen compression and increase the risk of acute events (torsion, rupture, infection). These indications — symptomatic refractory pain and respiratory compromise, suspected torsion/necrosis, diagnostic uncertainty with oncological concern, and space‑limiting mass effect during a period of ongoing uterine enlargement — are the standard justifications for intervening in pregnancy when conservative measures fail or the mother’s clinical course is worsening.

Understanding the pathophysiology that drove the decision helps clarify the urgency. Pedunculated subserosal leiomyomas can twist on their stalk and occlude their vascular supply; torsion produces ischaemia, necrosis and often fever and increasing pain, and leaking/necrotic tissue can promote local inflammation or infection that threatens maternal health. Large intra‑abdominal masses also cause upward displacement of the diaphragm and restricted lung volumes, producing dyspnoea that can compromise maternal oxygenation and uteroplacental perfusion. Imaging in this patient showed mixed cystic and solid components and an enlarging CA‑125, creating diagnostic ambiguity (degenerating myoma versus decidualised endometrioma or less likely malignancy) and providing an additional rationale for obtaining a tissue diagnosis.

Timing and route of surgery were chosen to minimize fetal risk while maximizing maternal safety. The second trimester (after organogenesis and before the peak risk of preterm labour in the third trimester) is generally the preferred window for elective non‑obstetric abdominal surgery in pregnancy when intervention is needed. Within that window, the surgical approach is individualized. Laparoscopy is appropriate in early pregnancy for smaller, well‑located lesions, but large or posteriorly situated masses, an advanced gestational uterus, multiple lesions, distorted anatomy, or inability to identify a clear pedicle favour an open approach. In this case the dominant mass measured roughly 18 cm, the pelvis contained an adherent intramural–subserosal myoma, the fetus and gravid uterus occupied substantial intra‑abdominal space, and preoperative imaging could not demonstrate a stalk for the cranial lesion. Those factors, together with the need for rapid access to control bleeding if needed, supported a longitudinal xipho‑pubic laparotomy rather than laparoscopy.

Preoperative planning emphasized multidisciplinary discussion, informed consent, and contingency preparation. The obstetrician, maternal–fetal medicine specialist, surgical team, anesthesiologist and neonatal staff should participate in the planning meeting. The consent discussion must include the risks of pregnancy loss, preterm labour, haemorrhage with possible transfusion, need for hysterectomy in the event of uncontrollable bleeding, and neonatal implications. Practical preparations include baseline and cross‑matched blood, correction of anaemia as feasible, treatment of active infection (the team initiated culture‑directed antibiotics for the Klebsiella urinary infection), and availability of neonatal resuscitation. Because continuous fetal cardiotocography is not reliable in mid‑second trimester, the team documented fetal cardiac activity before entering the abdomen and monitored viability intraoperatively with serial intermittent transvaginal ultrasound, documenting fetal heart rate before, during and after the procedure as part of standard perioperative obstetric preparedness.

The intraoperative strategy followed a conservative, targeted plan: obtain diagnostic samples, remove only tissue that posed imminent maternal risk, and minimize uterine handling. On entry the surgeons sampled free peritoneal fluid for culture and cytology because imaging had not definitively excluded a neoplasm. They confirmed fetal cardiac activity, then inspected the lesions. The upper mass proved to be a large, pedunculated, atypical cystic leiomyoma that had become twisted and was leaking — findings consistent with the preoperative fever and pain. The team controlled the pedicle with direct mechanical clamping (double clamp), resected the mass, and closed the uterine implantation site with an absorbable suture. These maneuvers achieve rapid vascular control and secure closure of the uterine defect while keeping operative time and uterine trauma to a minimum. The caudal lesion, in contrast, was a fixed, soft intramural–subserosal myoma adherent to the posterior uterine wall; after intraoperative assessment the team elected to leave it in place to avoid extensive dissection that would have increased bleeding, uterine irritability and the risk of pregnancy loss.

Selective myomectomy — removing only the lesion that is symptomatic or at high risk of acute complication and leaving deep intramural or adherent myomas in situ — is a pragmatic approach in pregnant patients when removal of all lesions would cause disproportionate risk. In this patient the twisted pedunculated tumor represented an immediate threat (ischaemia, infection, ongoing leakage), while removal of the fixed caudal myoma would have required deeper uterine incisions and prolonged surgery, with a substantially higher chance of haemorrhage and uterine contraction. By addressing the imminently dangerous lesion only, the team treated the acute problem, obtained tissue for definitive pathology (which later confirmed benign leiomyoma with ischaemic necrosis), and preserved the pregnancy.

Adjunctive and perioperative measures in this case reflect accepted tactics to protect the pregnancy and control infection and bleeding. The team administered single‑dose perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis (cefazolin) and continued culture‑directed antibiotics postoperatively because of the urinary Klebsiella. To reduce the risk of uterine irritability and support pregnancy continuation they began intramuscular progesterone intraoperatively and continued it through discharge, then switched to hydroxyprogesterone caproate for maintenance until planned delivery. Practical intraoperative strategies to limit fetal risk included minimizing uterine manipulation, keeping the operation as brief as possible (the procedure lasted 2 hours 25 minutes), and maintaining strict hemodynamic control to preserve uteroplacental perfusion. Despite these efforts the patient did develop post‑operative anaemia requiring intravenous iron and transfusion; this highlights the importance of preoperative cross‑matching and readiness for blood replacement.

Pregnancy‑specific risk mitigation is integral to operative planning and post‑operative care. Maternal hemodynamic stability must be prioritized to ensure uteroplacental perfusion, and meticulous haemostasis is required in the hypervascular pregnant uterus to limit blood loss. Teams should be prepared to manage preterm contractions should they develop (tocolytics are an option in selected circumstances) and to provide intensive fetal‑neonatal support if early delivery becomes necessary. Documentation of fetal heart rate before, during and after surgery is mandatory, and regular obstetric follow‑up postoperatively is required; in this case the patient had serial ultrasounds and ultimately an elective caesarean delivery at 37 weeks because the residual pelvic myoma would have mechanically impeded vaginal birth.

In summary, mid‑trimester myomectomy can be appropriate when maternal symptoms, evidence of tumour complication, diagnostic uncertainty, or rapid growth create unacceptable risks for the mother or fetus. The safest course is individualized: operate in the second trimester when possible, choose an approach (open versus minimally invasive) that provides safe exposure and rapid haemorrhage control for the anatomy encountered, plan carefully with a multidisciplinary team, limit the operation to what is necessary (selective myomectomy), and institute perioperative measures to protect the pregnancy. This case illustrates that such a focused, cautious approach can resolve an acute maternal problem, secure a tissue diagnosis, and allow continuation of a healthy pregnancy to term.
