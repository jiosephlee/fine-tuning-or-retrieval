Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 2: ## Pregnancy-Safe Diagnostics and Differential Diagnosis of Large Abdominal Masses

Evaluating large intra‑abdominal masses in pregnancy requires balancing diagnostic accuracy against fetal safety and the anatomic distortion produced by the gravid uterus. In practice this means a stepwise approach that begins with clinical examination and ultrasound, adds non‑contrast MRI when sonography is inconclusive or the lesion is large or posteriorly located, and interprets laboratory tests such as CA‑125 with caution. The present case—two large, cystic, vascularised abdominal masses in the second trimester—illustrates both the strengths and the limitations of the available tools.

Ultrasound with colour Doppler is the indispensable first‑line test in pregnancy. It is safe, widely available and allows assessment of size, internal architecture (unilocular versus multilocular, septations, solid mural nodules), wall thickness, vascularity and the spatial relationship to the uterus and adnexa. Transvaginal examination and careful bimanual palpation remain important supplements: in this patient the posterior fornix bulge on bimanual exam correctly localized the caudal lesion to the pouch of Douglas and argued for uterine adjacency. Ultrasound can, however, be limited by the “steric bulk” of an enlarging uterus and by very large lesions; feeding pedicles or thin stalks of pedunculated subserosal fibroids may be obscured, as happened with the upper mass here.

When ultrasound is insufficient to determine origin or to guide management, non‑contrast MRI after the first trimester is the next best modality. MRI adds soft‑tissue contrast and multiplanar detail that can clarify whether a mass is contiguous with the myometrium (favouring leiomyoma) or separate in the adnexa (favouring ovarian/paraovarian origin). In pregnancy the preferred practice is non‑contrast MRI on a 1.5‑T scanner; gadolinium is avoided unless there is a compelling maternal indication because it crosses the placenta and carries into‑utero exposure. In this case MRI confirmed two rounded, unilocular cysts, demonstrated a thick (1.4 cm) wall and inhomogeneous fluid on T1 and T2 fat‑saturated sequences, visualised focal T1/T2 hyperintense solid components and hypertrophied vessels, and suggested continuity between the caudal lesion and posterior uterine tissue—findings that narrowed the differential but did not provide absolute certainty.

Interpreting signal characteristics helps generate diagnostic hypotheses. T1 hyperintensity that persists on fat‑saturation most often reflects blood products or proteinaceous material rather than fat; this is a classic feature of endometriomas (old hemorrhage) and can also be seen with haemorrhagic or red degeneration of a fibroid. Heterogeneous T2 signal reflects varying content (liquid, blood, necrosis, fibrous tissue). Thickened walls and prominent peripheral or mural vascularity on Doppler or MRI suggest an active vascular supply (for example a pedicle to a subserosal fibroid) or vascularised mural nodules (which can be seen with decidualised endometriomas in pregnancy). The absence of pelvic or para‑aortic adenopathy on MRI argues against invasive ovarian malignancy but does not completely exclude it.

Serum tumour markers have important limitations in pregnancy and must be interpreted in context. CA‑125 is the marker most commonly considered for adnexal masses; physiologically it can be elevated in the first and third trimesters and is usually lower in the second trimester. A single, modest elevation (the patient’s CA‑125 was 41.8 U/mL, just above the conventional 35 U/mL cut‑off and higher than her prior 36 U/mL) is not diagnostic of malignancy in pregnancy and may reflect endometriosis, peritoneal irritation or fibroid degeneration. Serial measurements that show a rising trend are more informative than isolated values. Other markers (CA 19‑9, CA 15‑3, CEA, β2‑microglobulin, LDH) were normal in this case and therefore provided no support for a specific germ‑cell, mucinous or other marker‑positive ovarian malignancy.

A short pathophysiology review helps reconcile the imaging patterns. Endometriomas in pregnancy may undergo decidualisation under progesterone influence: the cyst contents remain blood‑stained (T1 hyperintense) and mural nodules can develop that are vascular on Doppler and that may mimic solid tumour on imaging. Conversely, leiomyomas commonly change during pregnancy: red (hemorrhagic/ischemic) degeneration causes acute pain, low‑grade fever and T1 hyperintense signal due to blood; cystic degeneration produces fluid‑filled areas and can render fibroids predominantly cystic. Pedunculated subserosal fibroids can undergo torsion, producing a painful, vascular‑compromised mass that may appear extra‑uterine on imaging. Any of these processes can produce overlapping sonographic and MR appearances.

Putting these elements together yields a focused differential. For a large unilocular cystic lesion with peripheral vascularity and T1 hyperintense contents, a decidualised endometrioma is a leading consideration in a reproductive‑age woman because of the combination of blood‑product signal and vascularised mural nodules; cystic/hemorrhagic degeneration of a leiomyoma—particularly a pedunculated subserosal fibroid—can produce an identical appearance if the uterine stalk is not visualised. Benign simple ovarian or paraovarian cysts tend to be thin‑walled, anechoic and avascular; complex ovarian neoplasms show multilocularity, papillary projections, solid components with persistent vascularity, ascites and sometimes nodal disease—features that raise concern for malignancy. In pregnancy the key malignant “red flags” remain mural solid nodules with aggressive features, rapid growth, ascites, lymphadenopathy and markedly abnormal tumour markers; the absence of these in this patient reduced but did not eliminate concern.

In this case the pieces were mixed: the mild upward trend in CA‑125 and the T1/T2 signal characteristics favoured an endometrioma for the upper lesion, but the caudal lesion’s anatomic continuity with the posterior myometrium on MRI and the bulge on bimanual exam suggested a myomatous origin. The patient’s dyspnoea was plausibly explained by mass effect and diaphragmatic limitation rather than cardiopulmonary disease. Because of severe pain, fever and the risk of torsion, ischaemia and further uterine compromise with fetal growth, the team proceeded to diagnostic and therapeutic laparotomy; surgical pathology ultimately confirmed a pedunculated, cystic, torsed leiomyoma cranially and a large intramural–subserosal leiomyoma caudally. The case highlights three practical points for clinical care: always start with ultrasound and careful pelvic examination; use non‑contrast MRI after the first trimester when origin or surgical planning is unclear; and interpret CA‑125 and other markers cautiously—serial trends and the whole clinical picture are far more useful than single values. When imaging and clinical findings conflict and the patient is symptomatic or at risk of complication, multidisciplinary discussion and timely surgical intervention remain appropriate despite the diagnostic uncertainty that pregnancy can impose.
