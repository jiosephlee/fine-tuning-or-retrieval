Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Bridging Vessels, Not Ovaries: Diagnosing Pedunculated Fibroids That Mimic Adnexal Cysts in Pregnancy

## Why this case grabbed our attention
A woman in her 40s at 21 weeks’ gestation presented with acute upper‑left abdominal pain, pelvic pain and mild dyspnoea. Bedside imaging showed two huge, unilocular cystic masses that read like ovarian endometriomas; CA‑125 was mildly elevated and non‑contrast MRI was inconclusive. At laparotomy the “ovarian cyst” occupying the left subphrenic space was actually a giant pedunculated, cystically degenerating uterine leiomyoma — twisted, leaking and ischemic. The team removed only that symptomatic mass and left the deep posterior myoma in situ to preserve the pregnancy; mother and baby did well.

This is the kind of diagnostic trap you’ll see in pregnancy: a cystic, vascular mass that looks ovarian on imaging — but it’s connected to the uterus by a stalk or vascular bridge. Knowing the bedside and imaging clues that point to uterine origin (and their limits in pregnancy) avoids both under‑ and overtreatment.

## Short case recap (key facts)
- Patient: nulliparous woman in her 40s, BMI 33, GA 21 weeks.
- Presentation: acute upper‑left abdominal and pelvic pain (VAS 5), mild dyspnoea, low‑grade fever; bimanual exam found a fixed ~9 cm mass bulging the posterior fornix and a taut 18 cm upper abdominal mass.
- Imaging: ultrasound — two large unilocular cystic masses (upper 18×12×7 cm; lower 9.5×8×8 cm) with peripheral vascularisation; MRI (no gadolinium) showed thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid and focal T1/T2 hyperintense solid components; cleavage plane for caudal mass not clear.
- Labs: CA‑125 elevated to 41.8 U/mL (up from 36 two weeks earlier); other tumour markers normal; iron deficiency anaemia.
- Management: multidisciplinary decision for laparotomy at 21w+6d because of pain, respiratory restriction, concern for torsion/degeneration and uncertainty about origin. The cranial mass was a pedunculated, cystic leiomyoma (twisted, leaking) and was excised; caudal myoma left in situ. Pathology: benign leiomyomas with ischaemic necrosis at the pedicle.
- Outcome: postoperative transfusion and iron, strict obstetric follow‑up, elective caesarean at 37 weeks for the residual pelvic myoma, excellent maternal and neonatal outcome.

## Why fibroids can masquerade as ovarian cysts in pregnancy
Leiomyomas are common and can behave atypically in pregnancy. When a fibroid outgrows its blood supply it can undergo degeneration (hyaline, cystic, or red/haemorrhagic degeneration), producing large cystic spaces with thin walls that look exactly like ovarian cysts on ultrasound or MRI. Pedunculated subserosal fibroids develop a stalk; that stalk can twist (torsion) causing acute pain, fever and ischemic necrosis — again mimicking an infected or complicated adnexal cyst.

Add pregnancy‑specific confounders: decidualisation of endometriomas produces vascular mural nodules that can look solid or enhancing, CA‑125 physiologically fluctuates in pregnancy, and the gravid uterus and large masses can physically obscure a pedicle or feeding vessel on imaging. That’s why a mass that looks “ovarian” in a pregnant patient should still be interrogated carefully for uterine origin.

## Bedside and ultrasound clues that point to uterine origin
Start with the physical exam. A fixed posterior fornix mass that bulges into the vagina is a warning sign that the lesion is attached to the pelvis/uterus rather than freely mobile ovary. In this case the bimanual exam detected a fixed 9 cm pelvic mass bulging posteriorly — a clue that the caudal lesion was contiguous with the uterus.

On ultrasound:
- Use both transvaginal and transabdominal approaches. Large masses need transabdominal windows but transvaginal probes often better show uterine continuity for pelvic lesions.
- Colour/power Doppler: look for a feeding vessel or "bridging‑vessel sign" — vessels running from the myometrium into the mass. When present, this is a strong pointer to uterine origin (pedunculated fibroid).
- Look for myometrial echotexture extending to the mass margin or a "claw" of myometrium wrapping around the lesion.
- Observe motion: does the mass move with the uterus when you push the probe? Does it remain fixed relative to the uterus?
- Beware false‑negatives: a very large mass, awkward orientation or the steric bulk of the gravid uterus can hide a thin pedicle or feeding vessels. In our patient the upper mass lacked an identifiable stalk on US because the gravid uterus and huge lesion obscured the pedicle.

## MRI (no gadolinium) — what to look for and pitfalls
Non‑contrast MRI is a powerful problem‑solving tool in pregnancy when ultrasound is equivocal, but you must know what to search for:
- Claw (or beak) sign / myometrial continuity: uterine myometrium wraps or "beaks" around the lesion, or you can see a band of myometrium leading into the mass. This favors fibroid.
- Pedicle or stalk: sometimes visible as a low‑signal band on T2 connecting mass to uterus.
- Degeneration signal patterns:
  - Cystic degeneration → T2 bright fluid, variable T1 depending on protein/haemorrhage.
  - Red (haemorrhagic) degeneration → T1 hyperintense due to blood products.
  - Endometrioma → classically T1 hyperintense and T2‑shading (low T2 signal); decidualised mural nodules can appear solid and vascular.
- Thick walls or mural nodules on MRI may push you toward decidualised endometrioma or neoplasm; in pregnancy you cannot rely on gadolinium enhancement for clarification — so correlate with Doppler ultrasound and clinical picture.

In the case MRI showed thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid and focal T1/T2 hyperintense components — features that are not specific and led to diagnostic uncertainty; intraop inspection resolved the diagnosis.

Safety note: avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary. Non‑contrast MRI at ≤1.5 T after the first trimester is considered reasonable for problem solving.

## CA‑125 in pregnancy — use with caution
CA‑125 is notoriously nonspecific in pregnancy. Physiologic rises occur (median reported values roughly: first trimester ~23 U/mL, second ~14 U/mL, third ~21 U/mL), so a single mildly raised value is of limited diagnostic value. In this case CA‑125 was 41.8 U/mL in the second trimester and rising — that contributed to concern, but it was not diagnostic. Serial trends can help, but interpret CA‑125 only as a piece of the puzzle.

## A pragmatic imaging algorithm (pregnant patient with a large cystic “adnexal” mass)
1. Start with a careful history, bimanual exam and obstetric assessment (fetal viability, signs of compromise).
2. Targeted ultrasound: transvaginal + transabdominal as needed, with colour/power Doppler. Try to identify bridging vessels, stalk, myometrial continuity or a claw sign. Document size, wall thickness, septations, solid components and vascularity.
3. If ultrasound is diagnostic for uterine fibroid and patient is stable → conservative management and obstetric follow‑up unless there are symptoms or complication risks.
4. If ultrasound is equivocal, or there are worrying features (thick wall >1 cm, new/enlarging solid components, rising CA‑125 trend, significant pain, fever, or respiratory compromise from mass effect) → non‑contrast MRI (≤1.5 T) to look for myometrial continuity/claw sign and degeneration patterns.
5. If MRI still cannot rule out an adnexal tumour OR the patient has complications suggesting torsion/rupture/infection or significant mass effect → multidisciplinary discussion and consider surgical intervention (timing usually in second trimester where possible). Always document fetal heart rate before, during and after any procedure and prepare for transfusion and hemostatic challenges.

## Surgical lessons from the case
- Pedunculated fibroids that have twisted (torsion) can present with acute pain, fever and raised inflammatory markers and they may require timely surgery.
- When you operate: minimize uterine trauma, control the pedicle (double clamp/ligate), close the uterine defect with absorbable suture, and keep the operation as short as possible to protect the pregnancy.
- Consider leaving asymptomatic or high‑risk lesions in situ if removal would increase obstetric risk — the team in this case removed the twisted pedunculated mass and left the deep posterior myoma to preserve uterine integrity and reduce preterm risk.
- Be ready for postpartum shrinkage: many fibroids regress after delivery and planned definitive surgery can often wait.

## Clinical pearls (from this case)
- A large cystic mass in pregnancy that looks “ovarian” on first glance may still be a uterine fibroid; always correlate the bimanual exam with targeted ultrasound.
- The bridging‑vessel sign and claw sign are your friends — but they can be hidden by the gravid uterus or very large lesions.
- Decidualized endometriomas can develop vascularized mural nodules that mimic solid masses; correlate MRI signal patterns (T1/T2) with Doppler.
- CA‑125 can mislead in pregnancy — interpret trends, not single values.
- When in doubt and the patient is symptomatic (pain, dyspnoea, fever, signs of torsion) escalate to non‑contrast MRI and early multidisciplinary discussion; surgery in the second trimester can be safe when indicated.

## Key take‑home messages
- Don't assume “ovarian cyst” = ovary in pregnancy; always ask whether the lesion is continuous with the uterus.
- Combine bimanual exam + transvaginal/transabdominal ultrasound with colour/power Doppler to look for bridging vessels and myometrial continuity.
- Non‑contrast MRI (≤1.5 T) is the next step when ultrasound is equivocal — look for claw/beak sign and degeneration patterns; avoid gadolinium.
- CA‑125 is of limited specificity in pregnancy; use serial trends and clinical context rather than a single value.
- Manage conservatively unless there are complications (torsion, rupture, infection, severe mass effect) or strong oncologic concern; if operating, plan with a multidisciplinary team, monitor the fetus and minimize uterine trauma.
- Large pedunculated fibroids can twist and present as acute, infected‑looking cysts — surgical myomectomy of the offending mass can be pregnancy‑sparing and successful when carefully executed.

If you see a massive “ovarian cyst” in a pregnant patient, pause — look for the bridge. It will save you from unnecessary ovary‑directed interventions and guide safer, pregnancy‑preserving care.



# Second-Trimester Myomectomy for Torsion and Mass Effect: Doing the Minimum Necessary

## Why this case is worth your attention
We think of myomectomy in pregnancy as a last-resort move — most are managed expectantly because surgery risks bleeding, pregnancy loss and preterm labour. This case flips that script in a sensible way: a nulliparous woman at 21+ weeks presented with two huge, cystic abdominal masses causing pain and dyspnoea. Imaging and CA‑125 were equivocal, and the working differential included decidualised endometriomas versus cystic degeneration of fibroids. At laparotomy the “upper adnexal” lesion turned out to be a pedunculated, twisted, leaking, highly vascular uterine leiomyoma. The team removed only that offending lesion (double‑clamp, cut the stalk, close the root with an absorbable stitch), left the intramural/subserosal pelvic myoma in situ, protected the pregnancy, and the patient subsequently delivered a healthy baby at term by elective cesarean for mechanical obstruction from the residual myoma. The pathology confirmed ischemic necrosis around the twisted pedicle — exactly the intraoperative risk the team had targeted.

This is a great example of a focused, risk‑proportionate strategy: when surgery is necessary in pregnancy, do the minimum that treats the threat while minimizing uterine trauma.

## The clinical picture (the facts)
A woman in her 40s, BMI 33, G1P0 at 21 weeks presented with acute upper‑left abdominal pain (VAS 5) and mild dyspnoea. Exam showed an 18 cm taut mass in the left subphrenic region and a fixed 9 cm pelvic mass on bimanual exam. Labs: Hb ~9.9 g/dL with iron deficiency, WBC and CRP mildly raised; urine culture grew Klebsiella. Ultrasound showed two large unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularity (upper 18×12×7 cm; lower ~9.5 cm adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma ~58×71 mm). Tumour markers were otherwise normal but CA‑125 was mildly elevated and rising (36 → 41.8 U/mL). Non‑contrast MRI (performed without gadolinium) showed thick‑walled, inhomogeneous cystic lesions with focal solid components; cleavage of the pelvic mass from the uterus was unclear. Worsening pain, mass effect with restrictive symptoms, diagnostic uncertainty and concern for torsion/infection prompted surgical exploration.

Intraoperatively the cranial mass was a pedunculated, twisted, leaking uterine leiomyoma; the surgeon excised it with a double clamp and closed the stalk with an absorbable suture. The pelvic lesion was fixed and left in place. Peritoneal fluid cultures/cytology were negative; pathology showed benign leiomyoma with ischemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle. The patient required IV iron and two transfusions post‑op but recovered and had an uncomplicated pregnancy follow‑up; elective C‑section at 37 weeks (same scar) for mechanical obstruction from the residual myoma gave an excellent neonatal outcome.

## When to consider surgery in pregnancy: practical indications
Surgery in pregnancy should be exceptional, but the following scenarios justify operative management — and were specifically relevant in this case:
- Torsion of a pedunculated fibroid (vascular compromise → necrosis, fever, leakage)  
- Persistent, severe pain or progressive restrictive symptoms (eg, diaphragmatic compromise causing dyspnoea) not responding to conservative care  
- Evidence or strong suspicion of complication (rupture, infection, ongoing hemorrhagic degeneration)  
- Diagnostic uncertainty with concern for malignancy (serial markers and imaging escalation can help)  
- Mass effect that will prevent uterine expansion or threaten maternal/fetal physiology

Key point: the decision should be multidisciplinary (obstetrics, gynecology, surgery, anesthesia, neonatology) and individualized to gestational age, lesion type and maternal condition.

## Imaging and tumour markers — how to interpret them in pregnancy
Ultrasound with Doppler is the first-line tool. Non‑contrast MRI at 1.5T is a safe, high‑yield adjunct after the first trimester and often clarifies relationships between masses and the uterine wall. Avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary.

CA‑125 can be misleading in pregnancy — it physiologically rises in the first and near‑term third trimester and small elevations alone are not diagnostic. Serial trends are more informative than a single value. In this case a rising CA‑125 added concern but did not make the diagnosis.

## Surgical strategy: do the minimum necessary
This case illustrates a simple principle: remove the lesion doing most harm and avoid creating new problems.

Practical intraoperative strategy used here
- Confirm fetal viability before and during the operation (the team documented fetal cardiac activity and used serial transvaginal ultrasound intraoperatively).  
- Sample peritoneal fluid for culture/cytology if there is diagnostic uncertainty.  
- Target pedunculated lesions first: control the stalk (double clamp was used here), transect, and close the uterine base with an absorbable suture. That eliminates the source of ischemia and sepsis risk while avoiding a large myometrial incision.  
- Minimize uterine manipulation and keep the operation as short as possible. This team limited the procedure to the cranial lesion; the fixed pelvic myoma was left in situ to avoid massive bleeding and additional uterine trauma.  
- Perioperative planning is crucial: have blood products available and be ready to transfuse (this patient required two transfusions post‑op), use antibiotic prophylaxis and tailor postoperative antibiotics to cultures, and document plans for fetal monitoring and obstetric follow‑up.

Why pedunculated lesions are the best targets
Pedunculated fibroids are the closest thing to “easy wins” in pregnancy: the stalk provides a clear control point, they can be removed without entering the uterine cavity, and the operation often restores anatomy and relieves mass effect quickly. Intramural or sessile lesions often require larger myometrial incisions, carry much higher hemorrhage risk and are usually best left until after delivery unless absolutely necessary.

## Laparoscopy vs laparotomy — choosing the approach
Laparoscopic myomectomy in pregnancy is possible in selected patients (small, pedunculated lesions, early gestation, experienced laparoscopic team, open Hasson entry), and offers faster recovery. However, large lesions, multiple masses, intramural/critical locations, significant vascularity, or advanced gestational age frequently push the balance toward laparotomy for safety and exposure. In this case the size (18 cm), uncertain anatomy and second‑trimester uterus made a longitudinal xipho‑pubic laparotomy the pragmatic choice.

## Hemostasis and perioperative preparedness
Anticipate bleeding: type and crossmatch blood and be ready to transfuse. Hemostatic options include careful clamping/traction of the stalk, prompt suturing of the myometrial base, electrosurgery as appropriate, topical agents and, where available and indicated, uterine artery control techniques — but any adjunct should be chosen with pregnancy physiology in mind. Keep surgery focused and time on uterine manipulation to a minimum.

## Obstetric counseling and downstream planning
Counsel patients that operative intervention adds risk: miscarriage, preterm labour, hemorrhage and potential need for transfusion. Also explain the alternative — expectant management with conservative measures — and the reasons surgery may still be the safer path. Residual myomas can obstruct labour or cause complications later; in this case the remaining pelvic myoma was expected to block the fetal head, so an elective cesarean at term was planned and executed with an excellent result. Note that many fibroids partially regress postpartum, which may alter future surgical decisions.

The team in this case also used intraoperative and continued progesterone prophylaxis and performed close obstetric surveillance; these are management choices taken to support the pregnancy but not universally standardized.

## The bottom line
This case shows that a disciplined, minimalist surgical approach can be the right answer in pregnancy when the lesion is causing true harm. Pedunculated, twisted, leaking or infected leiomyomas are legitimate indications for targeted myomectomy in experienced hands. The aim is not to “clear all lesions” but to treat the immediate threat while protecting the pregnancy.

## Key take‑home messages
- Consider surgery in pregnancy only when there is torsion, frank ischemic degeneration with systemic signs, relentless pain/dyspnoea from mass effect, infection/rupture risk, or strong concern for malignancy not resolved by imaging.  
- Pedunculated fibroids are the safest targets: control the stalk, transect and close the base; this avoids large uterine incisions and preserves the pregnancy.  
- Leave fixed intramural or adherent masses in situ when possible — overtreatment increases hemorrhage and obstetric risk.  
- Use ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI (1.5T) for anatomical detail; interpret CA‑125 cautiously (physiologically variable in pregnancy). Serial measurements beat a single value.  
- Be prepared: multidisciplinary planning, fetal monitoring before/during/after surgery, blood products available, perioperative antibiotics guided by cultures, and a plan for postpartum follow‑up.  
- Counsel patients on the risks (miscarriage, preterm labour, bleeding) and on realistic downstream obstetric plans (residual myomas may necessitate elective cesarean).  

This is not about heroic surgery — it’s about doing the minimum necessary to defuse an immediate threat and keep a pregnancy safe.



# Nonobstetric Surgery in Pregnancy: A Practical Perioperative Playbook at 20–24 Weeks

## The case that inspired this playbook
A nulliparous woman in her 40s, BMI 33, at 21 weeks GA presented with upper‑left quadrant pain and pelvic pain with mild dyspnoea. Ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI suggested two large cystic abdominal masses and a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma. CA‑125 was mildly raised. Because of worsening symptoms and concern for torsion/necrosis, the multidisciplinary team performed a laparotomy at 21+6 weeks. Intra‑op the cranial lesion proved to be a highly vascular, twisted, pedunculated cystic leiomyoma and was excised; the caudal mass was left in situ to minimize obstetric risk. The patient required IV iron and two transfusions post‑op, received intraoperative progesterone (a strategy of uncertain benefit), had strict obstetric follow‑up and ultimately an elective cesarean at 37 weeks with an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome.

This case is a great microcosm of second‑trimester nonobstetric surgery: diagnostic uncertainty, a need to balance maternal indications against fetal risk, a decision to operate to prevent acute complications, and careful perioperative planning that prioritized pregnancy preservation.

## Why the 20–24 week window matters
When nonobstetric surgery cannot safely wait, the early–mid second trimester is often the pragmatic window. Organogenesis is complete (lower teratogenic risk than first trimester) and the risk of spontaneous preterm labour is lower than later pregnancy. That doesn’t make surgery “safe” without planning — it simply gives the best balance when maternal indications mandate intervention.

## The perioperative playbook (consent → closure)

## Pre‑op
Start with a focused multidisciplinary huddle (obstetrics, surgical team, anaesthesia, radiology, blood bank, neonatology if near viability). Document fetal cardiac activity and obtain baseline obstetric ultrasound; use ultrasound + non‑contrast MRI for anatomy when US is limited (as in this case). Explain to the patient the specific maternal indication for surgery, the risks to the fetus (including pregnancy loss and preterm delivery), the possibility of transfusion and even hysterectomy if uncontrollable haemorrhage occurs, and the plan for neonatal support if delivery becomes imminent.

Obtain targeted labs: CBC with iron studies, type & screen/crossmatch, coagulation studies, basic metabolic panel and any culture specimens if infection is suspected (this patient had a Klebsiella UTI). Optimize anaemia pre‑op when feasible (oral iron, or IV iron if time and severity demand). If haemoglobin is low and surgery is not emergent, correct it pre‑operatively; if urgent, ensure crossmatched blood is available (this case went to theatre with Hb 9.9 and required transfusion afterwards).

Plan venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis: mechanical prophylaxis intra‑op (SCDs) and a clear strategy for LMWH post‑op based on bleeding risk. Coordinate timing around neuraxial techniques if these are planned.

Antibiotic strategy: give standard surgical prophylaxis with pregnancy‑safe agents (cefazolin is appropriate for abdominal/gynecologic surgery; adjust dosing for obesity per local protocol). If active infection is present or cultures are positive, tailor therapy to sensitivities — culture‑directed therapy was used here.

Decide about fetal lung maturity interventions only if preterm delivery is likely and the fetus is within local thresholds for viable care; routine antenatal steroids are not indicated for a single planned maternal surgery at 21 weeks.

Discuss tocolytics and progesterone: routine prophylactic tocolysis is not recommended. The team in this case used intraoperative progesterone and continued hydroxyprogesterone caproate — an approach used in some centers — but evidence for routine perioperative progesterone to prevent preterm birth after nonobstetric surgery is limited; individualize this choice with obstetrics.

Logistics: schedule when senior staff and blood products are available; ensure portable ultrasound for intra‑op fetal checks, warmed fluids, uterotonics available, and neonatal team on call if approaching viability.

## Intra‑op
Positioning and physiology: tilt the table left (≈15°) to avoid aortocaval compression; maintain maternal oxygenation and perfusion — keep SpO2 high (>95%), avoid hypotension (treat promptly), and avoid profound hyperventilation. Vasopressors (phenylephrine or ephedrine) are safe when needed to maintain uterine perfusion.

Monitoring and fetal checks: document fetal heart rate before incision and immediately after surgery; for a previable fetus intermittent transabdominal or transvaginal ultrasound to confirm viability is the practical approach (this team used serial ultrasound checks). Continuous fetal monitoring can be considered if the fetus is at or beyond the center’s viability threshold and resources/positioning allow.

Surgical principles: keep the procedure focused and as short as possible. Minimize uterine manipulation. When removing uterine masses, achieve secure hemostasis and repair myometrium with absorbable sutures. For pedunculated, twisted fibroids, ligate the stalk and remove — that was the life‑saving move in this case. Have uterotonics and haemorrhage control strategies immediately available.

Bleeding preparedness: crossmatch and have blood products available for myomectomy or other operations at high risk of blood loss. Plan for interventional radiology or senior surgical help if available. This patient required postoperative transfusion, illustrating the real haemorrhage risk.

Antibiotic redosing: for long cases or significant blood loss, re‑dose prophylactic antibiotics per local protocol. The team gave cefazolin 2 g at induction.

Keep anaesthesia teamwork tight: coordinate timing for ultrasound checks, limit surgical duration, and maintain normothermia.

## Post‑op (first 48–72 hours and beyond)
Immediate post‑op: reconfirm fetal cardiac activity and monitor maternal haemodynamics closely. Check bleeding, urine output, pain control, and early mobilisation. If anaemia worsens, correct it promptly — this patient’s Hb fell to 7.5 g/dL and she received IV iron and two transfusions.

VTE prophylaxis: resume mechanical prophylaxis immediately and start pharmacologic prophylaxis (LMWH) when haemostasis is secure; tailor duration to patient risk.

Analgesia: use multimodal analgesia — acetaminophen, regional/local techniques where possible, and judicious opioids. Avoid routine NSAIDs late in gestation; while a short course in mid‑trimester has lower risk, avoid NSAIDs as gestation advances because of ductus arteriosus and oligohydramnios concerns.

Tocolysis and progesterone: do not use routine prophylactic tocolytics. Treat uterine contractions if they occur. Discuss perioperative progesterone with obstetrics — the evidence is inconclusive for preventing preterm labour after nonobstetric surgery.

Infection management: continue or tailor antibiotics based on cultures and clinical course — the team continued culture‑directed therapy for the Klebsiella UTI.

Follow‑up and delivery planning: arrange close obstetric follow‑up (serial ultrasound fetal growth and amniotic fluid assessment). Reassess mode of delivery in the third trimester based on the location and size of residual uterine masses — in this case the residual pelvic myoma posed an obstruction risk and the patient had elective cesarean at 37 weeks with an excellent outcome.

Discharge teaching: warn about fever, new or worsening abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, contractions, decreased fetal movement once viable, shortness of breath, calf pain/leg swelling, or signs of wound infection — any of these should prompt immediate re‑evaluation.

## Special scenarios & myomectomy lessons from the case
Leiomyomas can masquerade as adnexal masses and can decidualize or undergo cystic degeneration; CA‑125 is unreliable in pregnancy and can be mildly elevated. MRI without gadolinium is a powerful adjunct when ultrasound is equivocal (as shown here). Myomectomy in pregnancy is a high‑risk but potentially necessary operation when a pedunculated or torsed myoma is symptomatic, infected or leaking — the surgical goal is to treat the maternal complication and preserve the pregnancy whenever safe. Expect significant blood loss risk and plan accordingly.

## Case reflections / clinical pearls (short prose)
This case reinforces several practical truths: document fetal cardiac activity before, during and after surgery; use non‑contrast MRI when ultrasound is limited; prepare for haemorrhage with crossmatched blood if operating on uterine masses; avoid routine prophylactic tocolysis — treat contractions if they occur; and plan delivery mode later based on residual anatomy (the caudal myoma in this patient ultimately drove a planned cesarean).

## Take‑home messages
- When nonobstetric surgery is required in pregnancy, the early–mid second trimester (≈20–24 wks) is often the best pragmatic window, but meticulous planning is mandatory.  
- Multidisciplinary consent must document maternal indications, fetal risks (loss/preterm birth), blood transfusion risk and the possibility of hysterectomy.  
- Pre‑op: confirm fetal cardiac activity, optimize anaemia if time allows, obtain type & screen/crossmatch, and tailor antibiotics to pregnancy and cultures. Use ultrasound + non‑contrast MRI for anatomy; avoid gadolinium.  
- Intra‑op: left lateral tilt to avoid aortocaval compression; maintain maternal oxygenation and BP; keep operative time short; monitor fetal viability intermittently if previable and continuously if viable and feasible.  
- Be prepared for haemorrhage with blood products available and senior surgical support — myomectomy carries real transfusion risk.  
- Post‑op: individualized tocolysis only for contractions; routine prophylactic progesterone is of uncertain benefit (the team in this case used it but evidence is limited); employ multimodal analgesia and avoid NSAIDs late in pregnancy.  
- Clear triggers for obstetric re‑evaluation: fever, bleeding, new or worsening pain, contractions, decreased fetal movement once viable, or haemodynamic instability.  
- Always aim to treat the maternal problem while preserving pregnancy when safe — limited, targeted surgery (remove the lesion causing immediate risk; leave others in situ) is often the right move.

If you take away one practical rule from this case: fix what is actively threatening the mother or the pregnancy, prepare for the common complications (haemorrhage, anaemia, VTE), and document fetal status before and after — that approach preserved both mother and baby in a high‑stakes scenario.



# Birth Planning With Large Fibroids: Vaginal Trial or Elective Caesarean?

What makes this case interesting: a nulliparous woman in her 40s at 21 weeks presented with two huge, cystic abdominal masses and dyspnoea. Imaging and tumour markers were ambiguous (one mass looked like an endometrioma on ultrasound/MRI, CA‑125 was mildly raised), but at laparotomy the “ovarian” lesion proved to be a pedunculated, twisted, highly vascular uterine leiomyoma and was excised. A second large posterior/intramural myoma was left in situ. The pregnancy continued and—because the pelvic myoma was judged likely to block head descent—the team elected an uncomplicated caesarean at 37 weeks with an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome.

This case highlights three clinical realities:
- Large fibroid-like masses in pregnancy can masquerade as adnexal pathology on imaging and even show mildly raised CA‑125.
- Pedunculated subserosal fibroids can twist and present with acute pain/fever and may require mid‑pregnancy surgery.
- Crucially for birth planning, the location of residual fibroid(s) — especially lower‑segment/isthmic or posterior torus lesions — often drives the decision toward elective caesarean rather than a trial of labour.

## Assessment: what to do when you find large masses in pregnancy
Start with careful clinical mapping. A thorough abdominal exam and bimanual vaginal exam often give the best clue to whether a pelvic mass is fixed to the uterus or mobile (and therefore more likely adnexal).

Imaging strategy in pregnancy:
- Ultrasound (with colour Doppler) is first line for morphology, vascularity and relation to the uterus and cervix.
- If ultrasound is inconclusive, non‑contrast MRI (≤1.5 T) can clarify relationships between leiomyomas and the uterine cavity/placenta; avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary.
- Tumour markers: CA‑125 can be physiologically elevated in pregnancy (peaks in 1st and 3rd trimesters). A single mildly elevated value is not diagnostic; serial trends are more helpful. In this case CA‑125 rose from 36 to 41.8 U/mL in the second trimester — a red flag but not proof of malignancy.

Don’t forget basic testing: infection markers and urine culture (this patient had a Klebsiella UTI), and baseline haemoglobin (this patient was iron‑deficient and later required transfusion).

## When to consider surgery in pregnancy
The default is conservative management because myomectomy in pregnancy carries risks (bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labour). Indications for surgery are limited but include:
- Acute complications — torsion of a pedunculated fibroid, rupture, severe infection or intractable pain not responsive to medical therapy.
- Mass effect causing respiratory compromise or severe functional limitation.
- High suspicion of malignancy (rare).

If you operate:
- Keep the operation as short and blood‑sparing as possible.
- Ensure multidisciplinary coordination (obstetrics, gynecologic surgery, anaesthesia, neonatology, blood bank).
- Monitor the fetus before, during and after the procedure (document fetal heart rate), and have a plan for perioperative tocolysis or fetal support as per local protocols. In this case the team also used progesterone prophylaxis intra‑ and post‑operatively (practice varies and evidence is limited).

## Deciding route of delivery: the pragmatic rule
Labeling a lesion “fibroid” is not enough. Three features matter most for route of delivery planning:
1. Location relative to the lower uterine segment, cervix and pelvic inlet — lower‑segment, isthmic or posterior torus masses that impinge on the pelvic brim or cervical canal are the highest risk for obstructing the fetal head.
2. Size and number — very large or multiple adjacent lesions increase the chance of mechanical obstruction and malpresentation.
3. Symptom trajectory and growth trend — rapid growth, persistent pain or new mass effect argues for a planned approach.

If a fibroid is judged likely to obstruct labour (as in this case, where the caudal myoma would have blocked fetal head descent), an elective caesarean is reasonable. Many teams choose to schedule delivery as soon as fetal maturity is achieved (the case team elected 37 weeks) to avoid spontaneous labour and emergency surgery. Weigh this against neonatal respiratory and other risks of early‑term delivery and individualize the plan.

If the fibroid is small, anterior/subserosal and well away from the presenting part and the placenta, a planned trial of labour is often appropriate.

## Cesarean myomectomy: selective, not routine
There is an active debate about removing fibroids at the time of CS:
- Potential benefits: single operation, avoid later surgery.
- Downsides: increased operative time, potentially higher blood loss, longer hospital stay.

The case authors cite a tertiary‑centre series where CS + myomectomy did not show worse haemoglobin or transfusion rates than CS alone, but did have longer surgery and hospital stay. The bottom line: cesarean myomectomy can be safe in selected patients in experienced hands with a clear haemorrhage‑control plan — but routine removal of large intramural posterior lesions is often deferred because postpartum involution frequently reduces their size.

In this case the team removed the twisted pedunculated lesion mid‑pregnancy (needed) and left the posterior isthmic lesion in situ; they later performed elective CS without myomectomy, and the patient elected to defer further surgery postpartum after partial regression and no symptoms.

## Hemorrhage mitigation — plan for the worst, hope for the best
If you anticipate a high bleeding risk (large myoma, intramural or multiple lesions), prepare:
- Crossmatch blood and have a massive transfusion protocol available.
- Consider cell salvage if your centre supports it.
- Have uterotonics, tranexamic acid (TXA), uterine balloon tamponade and materials for uterine or internal iliac artery ligation ready.
- Temporary uterine tourniquet or bilateral uterine artery clamping can reduce blood loss when performing myomectomy.
- Interventional radiology (uterine artery embolization) is useful postpartum but is generally not a first‑line option during pregnancy if fertility preservation is desired.
- Experienced surgical and anaesthetic teams improve outcomes.

## Postpartum course and deferred surgery
Leiomyomas often regress after delivery. The literature and this case show significant postpartum shrinkage (the case patient’s residual pelvic myoma reduced to ~7.5 cm and she remained asymptomatic). This justifies a conservative postpartum watchful approach if symptoms are absent and the patient prefers to avoid further surgery.

Address anaemia and iron deficiency aggressively in the postpartum period — this patient needed iron infusion and two transfusions to restore haemoglobin.

## Clinical pearls
- Do not let the word “fibroid” alone dictate delivery route — location (lower segment/isthmus/posterior torus) is the decisive factor for head descent obstruction.
- Combine bimanual exam + targeted ultrasound + non‑contrast MRI to map the lesion relative to the cervix and pelvic inlet.
- CA‑125 is unreliable in pregnancy; serial trends are more informative than a single mildly elevated value.
- Pedunculated fibroids can torsion and present with acute pain/fever — consider surgical removal if complicated.
- Cesarean myomectomy can be safe in selected cases, but plan for hemorrhage mitigation and counsel about longer operative time and recovery.

## Take‑home points
- Individualize route of delivery based on size, number and — above all — anatomical location of fibroids in relation to the presenting part and lower uterine segment.
- Reserve prenatal myomectomy for acute complications (torsion, intractable pain, infection, mass effect); otherwise, conservative care and planned delivery are preferred.
- If a fibroid is likely to obstruct labour, elective caesarean after fetal maturity (this team used 37 weeks) is a reasonable strategy to avoid emergency surgery and improve predictability.
- Cesarean myomectomy should be selective and performed where there is surgical expertise and a robust hemorrhage plan; many large posterior/intramural lesions are safely deferred to the postpartum period when regression often occurs.
- Multidisciplinary counselling and shared decision‑making with the patient are essential — and don’t forget to plan blood products, anesthesia, fetal monitoring and neonatal support.

This case underscores a practical mantra: location matters more than the label “fibroid” when planning birth.



# Interpreting CA-125 in Pregnancy: Trends, Traps, and When to Worry

## Why this case grabbed my attention
A pregnant patient in her 40s at 21 weeks presented with two very large abdominal cystic masses and a CA‑125 of 41.8 U/mL (it had been 36 two weeks earlier). The usual alarm bell—CA‑125 above the classic ovarian‑cancer cutoff of 35 U/mL—made ovarian neoplasm a real concern. Imaging was ambiguous (large unilocular cystic lesions, thick walls, focal solid components) and the differential included decidualized endometriomas versus cystic‑degenerating leiomyomas. The team elected laparotomy because of worsening pain and dyspnea and concern for torsion/rupture/infection. Intra‑operatively the “ovarian” mass turned out to be a pedunculated, twisted cystic leiomyoma with ischemic necrosis. Pathology was benign; pregnancy and neonatal outcomes were excellent.

This is a classic teaching case: a mildly raised CA‑125 in the second trimester almost nudged the diagnostic pendulum towards malignancy, but the clinical picture and imaging told a different story. The take‑home: CA‑125 in pregnancy is a treacherous single number. Use it — but in context.

## CA‑125 in pregnancy — a quick primer
CA‑125 is widely used as an adjunct in evaluating adnexal masses, but pregnancy changes the baseline and the interpretation:

- Pregnancy produces physiologic variation in CA‑125. Reported medians (from registry/study data discussed in the case) are roughly 23 U/mL in the first trimester, about 14 U/mL in the second trimester, and about 21 U/mL in the third trimester. So values that would be unusual in non‑pregnant women can be seen during pregnancy.
- The conventional oncologic cut‑off (≥35 U/mL) remains in common use, but its specificity drops sharply in premenopausal and pregnant patients.
- CA‑125 is non‑specific — it rises with a wide range of benign pelvic processes (endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic infection), physiologic states (menstruation, early and late pregnancy), and with some uterine pathologies including fibroids.

In short: expect trimester‑dependent variation and substantial overlap between benign and malignant causes.

## Why a single CA‑125 can mislead
A lone mildly elevated CA‑125 in a pregnant patient is poor proof of cancer. Small absolute values above 35 U/mL (like 40–50) can reflect physiologic pregnancy change, benign gynecologic disease, or inflammation/degeneration of a leiomyoma. The case illustrates two common traps:

- Pedunculated or cystic‑degenerating leiomyomas can mimic adnexal cysts on ultrasound and MRI.
- Decidualized endometriomas and degenerating fibroids can both show cystic areas and solid components on imaging, and both can be associated with modest CA‑125 elevations.

Therefore, do not let a single mildly elevated CA‑125 drive an aggressive plan without integrating the clinical and imaging picture.

## How I use CA‑125 in pregnant patients — a pragmatic approach
1) Start with the clinical context. Symptoms (pain, fever, dyspnea), mass size, and evidence of mass effect or complication (torsion, rupture, infection, restrictive lung mechanics) weigh heavily. In the case, progressive pain + dyspnea pushed the team toward intervention despite diagnostic uncertainty.

2) Examine thoroughly. Bimanual exam and a careful abdominal exam can give clues about uterine origin versus adnexal origin (a fixed pelvic mass, bulging posterior fornix, or a mass that moves with the uterus).

3) Image first. Transvaginal and transabdominal ultrasound with colour Doppler are first‑line. Key ultrasound clues: continuity with the uterine wall or a feeding vessel/stalk suggests a pedunculated myoma. If ultrasound is inconclusive, obtain a non‑contrast MRI (after the first trimester and preferably ≤1.5 T), which provides superior anatomy for posterior or intramural lesions. Gadolinium should be avoided in pregnancy unless absolutely necessary.

4) Use CA‑125 as an adjunct — and favour serial measurements when there is diagnostic uncertainty. A small absolute change over a short interval (for example, 36 → 41.8 U/mL over two weeks as in this case) is not, by itself, diagnostic of malignancy.

5) Multidisciplinary decision‑making. Bring together obstetrics, gynecology, radiology and surgical colleagues. If the patient is symptomatic, has signs of complication (torsion, infection, rupture), or imaging shows worrisome features, operative management may be necessary even in pregnancy.

6) If you operate, document fetal status pre, intra and post‑op, keep surgery as short and targeted as possible, and involve obstetric anesthesia and neonatal backup. In this case the team sampled peritoneal fluid, removed the twisted pedunculated mass and deliberately left the adherent caudal myoma in situ to limit risk to the pregnancy.

## Imaging pitfalls — leiomyoma vs adnexal mass
Pedunculated fibroids, cystic degeneration of myomas, and decidualized endometriomas are the real mimickers. Imaging clues that favour myoma include a visible stalk or continuity with the myometrium, a myometrial cleavage plane, and features suggesting degeneration rather than papillary solid nodules. But in advanced gestation the gravid uterus and “steric bulk” may obscure those clues — MRI without contrast can help but is not foolproof. In this patient the upper mass lacked an obvious stalk on imaging and looked adnexal; surgically it was a pedunculated fibroid.

## When should CA‑125 prompt an oncologic workup in pregnancy?
CA‑125 alone rarely mandates oncologic surgery in pregnancy, but some features should raise real concern and prompt early multidisciplinary oncology involvement:

- Markedly elevated CA‑125 or a rapidly rising trend over serial measurements (much larger changes than seen with physiologic pregnancy).
- Imaging with frank malignant features — solid papillary projections into a cyst, multiple vascularized solid nodules, diffuse peritoneal implants, ascites, suspicious lymphadenopathy.
- Patient factors that increase pretest probability (older age, post‑reproductive, concerning clinical course).

If these red flags are present, the balance may tip toward more aggressive diagnostic or therapeutic surgery even during pregnancy.

## The case, revisited — what was managed well
This case used CA‑125 appropriately: it was measured, trended, and interpreted alongside imaging and the clinical picture. The decision for laparotomy was driven by symptoms (pain, dyspnea), concern for torsion/necrosis/infection, and the risk that rapid uterine growth would worsen the situation — not by the CA‑125 alone. Intra‑op fetal monitoring, targeted myomectomy (removing the twisted pedunculated mass) and leaving a risky-to‑remove pelvic myoma in situ were pragmatic choices that preserved the pregnancy and resulted in a good perinatal outcome. Post‑op management (iron, transfusion, antibiotics guided by culture) and delivery planning (elective CS at 37 weeks because the residual myoma could obstruct labor) were appropriate downstream decisions.

## Clinical pearls
Clinical pearl: CA‑125 is a useful data point — not a diagnosis. In pregnancy it loses specificity. Always ask: does this single number change management?

Clinical pearl: Serial CA‑125 trends can be more informative than a single value. Small absolute rises are often noise.

Clinical pearl: Always integrate bimanual exam and high‑quality ultrasound (look for continuity with the myometrium). If uncertain, get a non‑contrast MRI and discuss the case in a multidisciplinary team.

Clinical pearl: Reserve surgery in pregnancy for symptomatic lesions, complications, or strong imaging/clinical suspicion of malignancy. If you operate, minimize the procedure to what’s necessary to treat the problem and preserve the pregnancy.

## Take‑home points
- CA‑125 normally varies by trimester (medians reported ~23 U/mL first trimester, ~14 U/mL second, ~21 U/mL third); pregnancy alters the baseline.
- A single mildly elevated CA‑125 in pregnancy is non‑specific — interpret it in the clinical and imaging context.
- Ultrasound (with Doppler) ± non‑contrast MRI are the mainstays for anatomical diagnosis; gadolinium is generally avoided.
- Serial CA‑125 can help, but small rises are frequently non‑diagnostic.
- Surgery in pregnancy is indicated for symptomatic or complicated masses or when malignancy is strongly suspected; decision‑making should be multidisciplinary.
- Don’t let a mildly elevated CA‑125 alone drive an aggressive operative plan when anatomy and imaging point to benign myomatous disease — but do escalate when clinical and imaging red flags align.



# Short of Breath From Below: Recognizing Restrictive Mechanics From Giant Pelvic Masses in Pregnancy

## Why this case grabbed our attention
Dyspnoea in pregnancy usually triggers a cardiopulmonary workup. This case reminded us that the cause can be literally mechanical — “short of breath from below.” A nulliparous woman in her 40s at 21 weeks presented with abdominal pain and mild dyspnoea. Imaging suggested two large cystic lesions; tumour markers were equivocal. The surprising intraoperative finding was that the upper lesion was a huge, pedunculated, twisted and leaking uterine leiomyoma. A limited myomectomy produced rapid symptomatic improvement and allowed the pregnancy to continue to term with an excellent outcome. The case illustrates how massive intra‑abdominal/ pelvic masses can produce true restrictive physiology in pregnancy, how to evaluate it safely, and when limited decompression is appropriate.

## The case in a nutshell
At 21 weeks’ gestation the patient had left‑upper‑quadrant and pelvic pain (VAS 5) and mild dyspnoea; vitals were reassuring (SpO2 ~97%, HR 74, BP 100/60). Abdominal exam showed enlargement beyond expected gestational size and a tense 18 cm upper quadrant mass; bimanual exam found a fixed 9 cm pelvic mass bulging into the posterior fornix. Labs showed iron deficiency anaemia (Hb 9.9 g/dL), mild CRP elevation and a Klebsiella UTI. Ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI described two large unilocular cystic lesions with peripheral vascularity; CA‑125 rose from 36 to 41.8 U/mL. Because of worsening pain, dyspnoea attributed to mass effect, risk of torsion/leak/infection and limited room for uterine growth, the team performed a longitudinal laparotomy at 21+6 weeks. The cranial mass proved to be a pedunculated, twisted, highly vascular leiomyoma that was excised; the caudal mass adherent to the posterior uterine wall was left in place. The postoperative course required IV iron and blood transfusion; pathology confirmed benign leiomyoma with ischemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle. The pregnancy continued, and an elective caesarean at 37 weeks produced a healthy neonate.

## How giant pelvic/abdominal masses create “restrictive” dyspnoea
Pregnancy already alters respiratory mechanics: upward displacement of the diaphragm by the gravid uterus reduces functional residual capacity, while progesterone‑driven hyperventilation increases minute ventilation. Add massive subphrenic or uterine‑adjacent lesions and you can further compress the diaphragm, reduce tidal volumes, increase the work of breathing and provoke the sensation of breathlessness without primary cardiopulmonary disease.

Key physiologic consequences:
- Reduced diaphragmatic excursion and lung volumes (especially supine).
- Increased work of breathing and shallow, rapid respirations.
- Symptoms may occur with preserved oxygen saturation because of increased minute ventilation; early hypoxaemia is not inevitable.
- Position matters — upright/semi‑orthostatic positioning unloads the diaphragm and often relieves symptoms.

The rapid improvement of dyspnoea after removing the pedunculated myoma in this case is a classic diagnostic clue that the problem was mechanical compression rather than intrinsic lung or heart disease.

## Practical ED/ward approach — don’t get tunnel‑visioned
When a pregnant patient complains of dyspnoea, always prioritize life‑threatening cardiopulmonary causes (PE, pneumonia, cardiomyopathy). Once those are reasonably excluded, consider mechanical causes — especially when there is abdominal fullness, disproportionate uterine enlargement, palpable masses, positional improvement of breathlessness, or normal cardiopulmonary testing.

A practical approach:
1. Rapid triage and ABCs; obtain pulse oximetry, vital signs and a brief cardiopulmonary exam.
2. Focused history: onset, triggers, positional changes, associated abdominal pain or fullness, obstetric history and prior imaging.
3. Focused physical exam: palpate the abdomen for discrete masses or a tense subphrenic mass; do a bimanual pelvic exam when appropriate (in this case it revealed a fixed pelvic mass).
4. Basic labs: Hb (anaemia worsens dyspnoea), infection markers and urine culture if UTI suspected.
5. Bedside ultrasound: maternal abdominal and transvaginal US is safe and invaluable to look for large uterine masses or adnexal pathology.
6. If imaging is inconclusive and additional characterisation is needed, non‑contrast MRI (≤1.5 T) after the first trimester is the next step; avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary.
7. Involve obstetrics/gynaecology early and discuss multidisciplinary options.

In this case the combination of a tense subphrenic mass, a posterior pelvic mass on bimanual exam, normal chest exam/ECG and preserved oxygenation pointed to a mechanical cause.

## Imaging and tumour markers — interpretation reminders
Ultrasound with colour Doppler is first‑line for suspected pelvic masses in pregnancy; it’s excellent for identifying pedunculated and anterior myomas. MRI without contrast improves characterisation for posterior, intramural or multiple lesions and helps surgical planning. Gadolinium is generally avoided in pregnancy.

Tumour markers are noisy in pregnancy. CA‑125 rises in the first and third trimesters and can be elevated in benign conditions (endometriosis, PID, myomas, pregnancy). A single mildly elevated CA‑125 in the second trimester is not diagnostic; trends and the whole clinical picture matter. In this patient CA‑125 rose from 36 to 41.8 U/mL over two weeks, a red flag that prompted definitive imaging and multidisciplinary discussion, but it was not diagnostic of malignancy.

## Temporizing measures that matter
Before committing to surgery, several low‑risk measures can substantially improve symptoms and buy time:
- Semi‑orthostatic positioning (head‑up, sitting or inclined) to reduce diaphragmatic compression.
- Targeted analgesia to reduce splinting and shallow breathing — in this case IV paracetamol 1 g twice daily markedly reduced pain and symptom burden.
- Treat reversible contributors: correct anaemia, treat urinary infection and control fever.
- Supplemental oxygen if hypoxaemic, but note that oxygen saturation may be normal with significant subjective dyspnoea from compression.
- Serial bedside ultrasound and bimanual exams to monitor for progression.
These temporizing steps improved symptoms in this patient and clarified that definitive intervention was needed when pain, dyspnoea and the risk of complication escalated.

## When to escalate to decompression / surgery
Surgical intervention in pregnancy is not routine and carries risks (bleeding, preterm labour, pregnancy loss), but it is warranted for specific, urgent problems. Reasonable indications include:
- Torsion of a pedunculated lesion, ongoing ischemia or leaking (risk of sepsis/necrosis).
- Intractable pain not controlled with conservative therapy.
- Mass‑related mechanical compromise (respiratory restriction that is progressive or causing hypoxaemia).
- Suspicion of malignancy that cannot be excluded non‑operatively.
- Threat to uterine space with anticipated compromise of fetal growth or impending obstetric complications.

In our patient the combination of a tense, vascularised mass with mixed solid/cystic features, worsening pain and dyspnoea, a rising CA‑125 and concern for torsion/leak tipped the balance in favor of surgical myomectomy. The team limited exposure by removing only the symptomatic pedunculated mass and leaving the adherent pelvic myoma in place — a tailored approach to relieve compression while minimizing uterine injury and surgical time.

## Operative and perioperative considerations (lessons from the case)
- Multidisciplinary planning is essential (obstetrics, gynaecology, anaesthesia, neonatology).
- Obtain informed consent discussing maternal and fetal risks and the contingency of preterm delivery.
- Document fetal heart activity before, during and after surgery. In this case fetal cardiac activity was verified intraoperatively and the team used serial transvaginal ultrasound monitoring.
- Minimize operative time and uterine manipulation; choose laparotomy over laparoscopy when lesions are large, vascular, posteriorly located or numerous.
- Prepare for blood loss: cross‑matched blood, IV iron and transfusion plan if anaemia worsens.
- Perioperative antibiotics tailored to infections (here cefazolin then cefazolin + clarithromycin because of Klebsiella UTI sensitivity).
- Progesterone support was used in this case (intraoperative 100 mg IM then ongoing progesterone, later hydroxyprogesterone caproate) — practices vary and evidence is not definitive, but prophylactic measures to reduce uterine activity are commonly employed.
- Pathology confirmation is important; in this patient the resected mass was benign leiomyoma with ischemic necrosis.

## What this case teaches us
1. Don’t anchor on cardiopulmonary causes when a pregnant patient has dyspnoea — examine the abdomen. Large subphrenic or uterine‑adjacent masses can produce true restrictive physiology.
2. Positioning and simple analgesia can be powerful temporizers; they also help you test the mechanism (positional improvement supports mechanical compression).
3. Use safe imaging: ultrasound first, then non‑contrast MRI (≤1.5 T) if needed. Interpret tumour markers in the context of pregnancy and trends, not absolute cutoffs.
4. Surgical decompression (limited myomectomy) is justified for torsion, leakage, intractable symptoms or progressive mechanical compromise and can preserve pregnancy if done with multidisciplinary planning.
5. Tailor the operation — remove what is necessary to relieve compression and minimize obstetric risk; leaving adherent lesions in situ can be the safest choice.

## Key take‑home messages
- Large pelvic or subphrenic masses in pregnancy can cause restrictive dyspnoea by mechanically limiting diaphragmatic excursion; oxygenation may remain normal early on.
- Look for abdominal disproportion, palpable masses and positional relief; use bimanual exam and bedside ultrasound early.
- Temporize with semi‑orthostatic positioning, targeted analgesia (e.g., IV paracetamol), and treatment of reversible contributors (anaemia, infection).
- Use non‑contrast MRI (after first trimester) to characterise unclear masses; interpret CA‑125 with caution — trends matter more than single values in pregnancy.
- Escalate to surgical decompression for torsion, leaking/necrosis, refractory pain or progressive respiratory compromise — aim for focused resection to relieve compression while minimizing uterine trauma.
- Multidisciplinary planning, fetal monitoring and readiness for transfusion/critical care are essential for safe outcomes.

If you see a pregnant patient “short of breath,” put your hands on the belly — sometimes the fix is from below.
