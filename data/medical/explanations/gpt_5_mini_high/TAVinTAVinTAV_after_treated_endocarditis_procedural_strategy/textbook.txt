Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Focused background: TAVI durability, redo-TAVI, and post-endocarditis considerations

As transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has moved from high‑risk patients into lower‑risk and younger populations, clinicians increasingly face the downstream problem of structural valve deterioration (SVD) and the need for repeat interventions. The timeline in this case—initial Lotus implantation in 2014, a TAV‑in‑TAV with an Evolut in 2022, prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) later in 2022, and a third transcatheter implant in 2024—illustrates two common drivers of reintervention: time‑dependent bioprosthetic degeneration and accelerated dysfunction after infection. Understanding the mechanisms of SVD, the physiologic consequences of serial valve layering, and the specific implications of prior endocarditis is essential to judge the indication, plan imaging, and choose a device and implantation strategy.

Mechanisms and timing of transcatheter SVD are multifactorial. Classic degenerative processes—leaflet fibrosis, calcification and mechanical wear—tend to accrue over years and are the dominant cause of mid‑ to late‑term failure. Superimposed processes can accelerate dysfunction: thrombosis can cause early leaflet immobilization, and PVE can produce leaflet destruction, scarring or fusion that produces either stenosis, regurgitation or a mixed lesion. Frame factors also matter: underexpansion of a stent frame at initial implantation or subsequent compression by an inner valve concentrates stress on leaflets and can promote earlier failure. In this patient the first valve functioned for several years (consistent with typical mid‑term durability), while the episode of Enterococcus faecalis PVE after the 2022 TAV‑in‑TAV likely contributed to the more rapid deterioration that prompted the 2024 reintervention.

Prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) is a central concept when considering redo‑TAVI. PPM exists when the effective orifice area (EOA) of a prosthetic valve is too small for the patient’s body size; it is usually quantified as indexed EOA (iEOA = EOA / body surface area). Common thresholds are: iEOA ≤0.85 cm²/m² denotes at least moderate PPM and iEOA ≤0.65 cm²/m² severe PPM (with some adjustment for obesity). The Doppler velocity index (DVI)—the ratio of LVOT to transvalvular velocity—is another practical measure; a DVI ≤0.25 is consistent with severe obstruction. Clinically, PPM is important because it is associated with persistently elevated gradients, less symptomatic improvement, adverse left ventricular remodeling and worse long‑term outcomes. Serial TAVI amplifies the risk of PPM because each added valve and its leaflets occupy lumen, reducing the inner diameter and EOA; the result can be very high transvalvular gradients unless the chosen device or post‑dilatation strategy can restore acceptable orifice area. This case highlights that immediate post‑deployment gradients may be very high (mean gradient 67 mm Hg, DVI 0.24, iEOA 0.4 cm²/m²), and that careful post‑dilatation and time for frame expansion can substantially improve haemodynamics (mean gradient falling to 27 mm Hg at 1 month).

Repeat transcatheter procedures create unique hazards. Coronary obstruction is a leading immediate threat in TAV‑in‑TAV scenarios: implantation of an inner valve can displace or splay the leaflets of the outer valve outward, forming a “neo‑skirt” that can cover coronary ostia. The risk increases when sinuses are small, coronary heights are low, or valve‑to‑coronary distances are reduced by multiple frames. Reduced EOA and severe PPM are physiologic consequences of frame stacking and leaflet confinement. Overlapping stent frames also impede future coronary access because frame struts and commissural misalignment can block catheter passage or make selective cannulation difficult. Some bail‑out or preventive techniques (coronary protection with guidewires/stents, BASILICA leaflet laceration, or bioprosthetic valve fracture) may be limited or technically more hazardous in a triple‑stented anatomy, so preprocedural planning must explicitly evaluate these scenarios.

Guideline‑driven considerations are particularly important after PVE. The 2025 ESC guidance reiterates that active, complicated PVE is usually managed surgically, and that any decision to pursue transcatheter treatment in a patient with prior PVE requires careful individualisation: infection must be controlled, blood cultures should be negative, and imaging (transoesophageal echocardiography and CT) must exclude active vegetation, abscess or paravalvular disruption. A heart‑team discussion weighing surgical risk, anatomical feasibility and the durability and risks of a transcatheter redo is mandatory. In the reported case these prerequisites were met (completed antibiotic therapy, negative cultures and imaging without active infection), and the patient’s prohibitive operative risk supported a CT‑guided transcatheter strategy.

Device design and interaction of frames strongly influence strategy and outcome. Mechanically expanded valves such as the Lotus (implanted in 2014) have high radial strength and a rigid frame that tends to become firmly integrated into the annulus and aortic root; that permanence can limit the ability of subsequent devices to expand the inner lumen. Self‑expanding nitinol frames such as the Evolut are tall, supra‑annular devices with good haemodynamic performance but a high frame that can contribute to reduced valve‑to‑coronary distances when nested. Supra‑annular leaflets (as on Evolut and Acurate neo2) generally provide larger EOAs for a given internal diameter compared with intra‑annular designs and thus are often preferred in small lumens or redo situations to mitigate PPM. The Acurate neo2 combines supra‑annular leaflet position with a relatively lower frame height and a design that facilitates commissural alignment; in this case those features were chosen to maximise effective orifice area, to minimise neo‑skirt encroachment on coronaries and to preserve future coronary access. Post‑dilatation with balloons can sometimes partially expand a previously implanted self‑expanding frame (as was performed here), improving leaflet excursion and reducing gradients even without intentional frame fracture.

In summary, the decision to perform a third transcatheter valve in this patient reflects the intersection of three realities: expanding TAVI use and longer patient survival, multifactorial SVD (with superadded PVE accelerating deterioration), and prohibitive surgical risk. Safe and effective redo‑TAVI in this setting depends on documented eradication of infection, meticulous MSCT and TEE assessment of residual lumen and coronary relationships, device selection that prioritises supra‑annular EOA and manageable frame height, and procedural strategies (high implantation, commissural alignment, and planned post‑dilatation) aimed at minimising PPM and preserving coronary access.

## Case chronology, presentation, and diagnostic synthesis

This patient’s course is best understood as a stepwise progression from an initial transcatheter treatment for native aortic stenosis to recurrent prosthetic dysfunction complicated by an episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), followed by further structural deterioration that ultimately prompted consideration of a repeat transcatheter intervention. Key dates and turning points were: implantation of a 27 mm Lotus transcatheter valve in 2014 for severe native aortic stenosis; symptomatic prosthetic degeneration with a very high mean gradient (≈83 mm Hg) and a fall in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) to 42% by early 2022; a TAV‑in‑TAV procedure with a 23 mm Evolut valve in May 2022 (complicated by a femoral pseudoaneurysm); diagnosis of Enterococcus faecalis PVE three months later (sept 2022) treated with a complete course of targeted intravenous antibiotics and subsequent oral therapy; and recurrent symptomatic prosthetic dysfunction documented during 2023–2024 with severe stenosis and mixed regurgitation that led to re‑evaluation and planning for a third transcatheter valve in mid‑2024.

Several baseline features shaped the risk–benefit calculus throughout this timeline. The patient had advanced age, poorly tolerated cardiovascular disease (three‑vessel coronary artery disease with multiple prior percutaneous coronary interventions), third‑degree atrioventricular block treated with a pacemaker, type 2 diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022. Each of these comorbid conditions increases perioperative risk; together they contributed to a very high calculated surgical risk (EuroSCORE II 27.36%) and argued against conventional surgical explant and root replacement when the prosthetic valves later failed.

The interval event of PVE was a critical diagnostic and management pivot. Three months after the 2022 TAV‑in‑TAV the patient presented with fever and worsening heart failure; transthoracic echo showed severe restenosis and blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis. He completed an appropriate course of targeted intravenous therapy (6 weeks) and subsequent oral management under infectious disease supervision. For the purposes of planning a further transcatheter intervention, the team required and obtained objective evidence that infection had been eradicated: repeated negative blood cultures, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) without vegetations or paravalvular complications, and no clinical or laboratory signs of ongoing sepsis. Establishing absence of active infection is essential because placing a new prosthesis into an infected field is associated with high risk of recurrent infection and poor outcomes.

The clinical presentation that precipitated re‑evaluation in 2024 was progressive exertional dyspnea with limited exercise tolerance (walking 200–300 m), intermittent left‑sided chest spasm on exertion, dizziness and fatigue. These symptoms reflect a reduction in forward stroke volume and increasing transvalvular obstruction and/or regurgitation from the failing prosthesis; angina‑type chest discomfort in this context can represent demand ischemia from low cardiac output in a patient with known coronary disease.

Echocardiography documented the haemodynamic severity at several stages and guided urgency. Before the 2022 redo the mean transvalvular gradient had risen to ≈83 mm Hg with an LVEF fall to 42%, and LVEF improved to ~54% after the May 2022 TAV‑in‑TAV—demonstrating the expected recovery in ventricular function when outflow obstruction is relieved. By January–June 2024 progressive prosthetic dysfunction was documented: mean gradient ≈49 mm Hg with moderate–severe intraprosthetic regurgitation and a calculated aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm² (the AVA here was derived by the continuity equation using LVOT dimensions and Doppler velocity‑time integrals). These measurements indicate severe prosthetic obstruction despite preserved or recovered LVEF. After the third implantation, immediate post‑procedure Doppler metrics showed a Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24 and a small calculated AVA (0.9 cm²) with a very high mean gradient (67 mm Hg) consistent with severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM) at that time point; the DVI is the ratio of LVOT to transvalvular velocity indices and values ≲0.25 indicate severe obstruction in prosthetic valves.

Multimodality imaging—particularly transesophageal echocardiography and multislice CT (MSCT)—was used both to exclude active endocarditis and to characterise the anatomic constraints that determined feasibility and strategy for further intervention. TEE confirmed the absence of vegetations or paravalvular abscess before proceeding. MSCT provided critical measurements for procedural planning: it documented two overlapping transcatheter frames and measured a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut stent, a narrow residual flow channel that has important implications for expected effective orifice area after any additional valve, for the risk of coronary ostial obstruction, and for the potential need to position a new valve high to avoid creating a neo‑skirt that would impinge on coronary flow. CT assessment of sinus of Valsalva size, coronary ostial heights and valve‑to‑coronary distances informed the heart team’s judgement that coronary obstruction risk could be managed without routine prophylactic coronary protection in this specific anatomy.

Bringing these data together supported a clear clinical problem statement: the patient had severe prosthetic valve dysfunction manifested as combined stenosis and regurgitation in a double‑stented (TAV‑in‑TAV) aortic valve, occurring after a treated episode of Enterococcus faecalis PVE, in the setting of prohibitive surgical risk and anatomical constraints that complicated open explantation. Multimodality imaging confirmed both the absence of active infection and the anatomic feasibility (albeit with elevated risks of residual PPM and potential coronary access difficulties) of a strategy involving a further transcatheter valve‑in‑valve intervention rather than surgical replacement.

## CT-guided planning for a third TAVI: goals, measurements, and strategy

A third transcatheter valve inside two existing stented prostheses presents competing priorities: restore adequate valve area and low gradients, avoid coronary obstruction now and preserve future coronary access, and minimise procedural risk (embolisation, paravalvular leak, or acute haemodynamic collapse). In this patient, preprocedural multislice CT (MSCT) became the central tool to reconcile those goals by defining the residual intraluminal geometry inside the prior devices and by predicting how a new frame and skirt would interact with the aortic root and coronary ostia. The planning process therefore focused on five practical objectives—preserve coronary flow and future coronary access; maximise effective orifice area (EOA) to reduce patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM); define an implantation depth and rotational orientation (commissural alignment) that limit interference with prior leaflets; assess and plan for radial expandability of the existing frames; and prepare bailout strategies should CT identify high obstruction risk.

MSCT measurements and interpretation
For TAV-in-TAV planning MSCT is performed as an ECG‑gated contrast study with centerline reconstructions and multiplanar reformatting. Key measurements are made relative to the native annular plane and along the axis of the prior valve frames: coronary ostial heights (measured perpendicular to the annular plane); dimensions of the sinuses of Valsalva and the sinotubular junction; and internal diameters of the lumen through the previously implanted stents at multiple levels (landing zone, valve leaflet level and supra-annular levels). In reinterventions it is also essential to measure valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance—that is, the distance between the anticipated final skirt (neo‑skirt) of the new transcatheter valve and each coronary ostium—and to estimate the vertical height of the neo‑skirt relative to the coronary ostia and sinus floor.

In this case MSCT documented a markedly reduced internal lumen within the Evolut frame (minimum lumen diameter 13.7 mm). That single figure carried several implications: the residual flow area was small and therefore risk of PPM and high gradients after another valve was high unless a device with favorable supra‑annular EOA was chosen; the limited internal diameter constrained valve size selection; and the small lumen increased the importance of predicting where the new skirt would sit relative to the coronaries. The team therefore used the CT to simulate a virtual implant and to confirm that, with a high implant strategy, the neo‑skirt would remain below the coronary ostia and leave adequate VTC distances.

Frame interaction mapping and implantation depth
When prior transcatheter frames are present, MSCT is used to map the position of prior commissures and leaflet nadirs relative to the coronary ostia. Radiopaque frame elements and leaflet attachment points are identified, and a virtual valve is positioned within the existing frames to predict leaflet displacement and skirt location. Two mechanistic problems commonly drive strategy in TAV‑in‑TAV: (1) inward folding of degenerated prior leaflets into the orifice (which narrows EOA), and (2) creation of a neo‑skirt (new valve fabric plus pinned leaflets) that can obstruct a coronary ostium or isolate the sinus (sinus sequestration). To avoid both, the team planned a relatively high implant—positioning the new prosthesis so its frame would pin the prior Evolut leaflets outward against the Evolut stent rather than allow them to prolapse inward. The CT-based overlay allowed selection of a target fluoroscopic projection and a precise depth relative to the Evolut leaflet nadir so that the neo‑skirt would fall below the coronaries while still giving adequate anchoring and sealing.

Valve selection and sizing informed by CT
Because the effective inner diameter inside the Evolut was small, the group selected a valve designed to maximise EOA in narrow geometries: a self‑expanding, supra‑annular Acurate neo2 in size S. CT measurements guide sizing in redo procedures by matching the device to the smallest luminal diameter and by anticipating how the new valve will expand within the layered frames, not simply the original annulus diameter. A supra‑annular leaflet design increases the functional orifice for a given inner stent diameter. In addition, the relatively low height of the Acurate neo2 frame and its constructive features facilitating commissural alignment reduced the expected mesh burden above the sinuses and improved the chances of preserving coronary access—features that MSCT overlay modelling indicated were favourable in this patient.

Coronary protection: decision framework and bailouts
MSCT provides the anatomical thresholds commonly used to decide whether to preemptively protect coronaries: low coronary height, small sinus diameters, or valve‑to‑coronary distances that predict the neo‑skirt will impinge on an ostium. Although numeric thresholds vary in the literature, operators typically regard a VTC of only a few millimetres, coronary heights in the low teens (mm) or very narrow sinuses as high‑risk features. In this patient, CT modelling showed adequate clearance of the neo‑skirt from the coronaries with the proposed high implant, and therefore the team elected not to perform routine prophylactic coronary wiring or chimney stenting. CT did, however, guide preparation for bailouts: coronary guidewires and stents were available on standby, and the team had contingency plans (chimney/stent snorkel or emergency coronary intervention). Importantly, the team used balloon pre‑dilation with simultaneous aortography as a real‑time check of coronary flow—a practical step when CT suggests borderline clearance—confirming on table that coronary perfusion remained intact before final valve deployment. Techniques such as BASILICA (leaflet laceration) are additional pre‑emptive options when CT predicts a high likelihood of obstruction, but their feasibility is limited in multi‑stented anatomies and was not required here.

PPM mitigation and intra‑procedural testing of expandability
Mitigating PPM in serial TAV procedures revolves around maximising the effective orifice within the constrained lumen. CT directed several complementary measures: selection of a supra‑annular valve to increase EOA, planning commissural alignment to favour central flow, and a strategy of measured pre‑ and post‑dilation to assess and improve expandability of the existing Evolut frame. Balloon pre‑dilation with a large balloon not only fractures or repositions obstructing leaflet tissue but also serves as a functional test—if the balloon waist is severe and persistent it signals limited radial expandability and higher residual gradients. In this patient the team performed a 22/40 mm balloon predilation under rapid pacing and observed preserved coronary flow on aortography; after valve implantation they performed post‑dilation with a 20/40 mm balloon to further expand the construct. The operators deliberately avoided intentional frame fracture (a manoeuvre used for some surgical bioprostheses) because self‑expanding transcatheter frames behave differently; instead they relied on high‑pressure ballooning to achieve incremental expansion. This combination of device choice, alignment and balloon techniques is what likely explained the clinical course: an initially high immediate post‑implant gradient (reflecting residual stiffness and incomplete early expansion) followed by progressive reduction in gradient and improved EOA over the first month as the frames and leaflets accommodated.

How imaging translated into the executed plan
In summary, MSCT supplied the critical answers: the minimal residual lumen (13.7 mm) narrowed device choices, virtual overlays showed that a high implant would keep the neo‑skirt clear of the coronaries, and commissural mapping enabled an orientation strategy to preserve future coronary access. These imaging‑derived decisions led to selection of the Acurate neo2 S, a high implantation technique to pin prior leaflets, a stepwise dilation strategy (predilation to test coronary patency and expandability, then post‑dilation for optimisation), and readiness with bailout tools despite choosing not to deploy prophylactic coronary protection. The result—a technically successful deployment with immediate elimination of regurgitation and progressive haemodynamic improvement over the following weeks—illustrates how targeted CT planning can balance haemodynamics, coronary safety and lifetime access in even the most complex, triple‑stented anatomies.

Clinical takeaways
For learners, the practical lesson is that MSCT in repeat TAVI is not a perfunctory annular measurement but a three‑dimensional engineering study: it defines the available internal lumen, predicts the neo‑skirt and leaflet behaviour, and permits virtual positioning of a candidate device so operators can choose implantation depth, rotational orientation and adjunctive ballooning strategies that optimise both short‑term haemodynamics and long‑term coronary access. In redo and trifecta procedures, careful CT‑guided planning paired with staged on‑table testing (balloon predilation with aortography) and prepared bailouts is the safest way to navigate the competing demands of EOA restoration and coronary protection.

## Procedural execution and intraprocedural decision points

This case required a stepwise, image‑guided approach because three concentric transcatheter frames greatly increase the risks of coronary obstruction, patient–prosthesis mismatch and malposition. The team’s intraprocedural strategy therefore aimed to (1) create and maintain a safe fluoroscopic and haemodynamic environment, (2) confirm coronary patency before committing to the definitive implant, (3) deploy the new valve in a preplanned high position with careful commissural alignment to maximise effective orifice area and preserve future coronary access, and (4) have clearly defined bailouts and equipment immediately available.

Access and monitoring were selected to balance procedural control and patient safety. The procedure was performed under local anaesthesia with left femoral arterial access for the large‑bore delivery system and left radial access for angiography and potential coronary interventions. Continuous invasive haemodynamic monitoring was used throughout, and preparations for rapid ventricular pacing (RVP) were made to provide brief periods of very low cardiac output and ventricular motion for controlled balloon inflation and post‑dilatation; in this procedure RVP at ≈180 beats per minute was applied during balloon inflations. A pacing setup and conversion plan (including conversion to general anaesthesia and surgical backup) are routine preparatory steps in complex redo TAVI.

After vascular access and baseline checks, an initial aortogram with a pigtail catheter confirmed the dominant lesion—severe intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation—and provided angiographic landmarks for valve orientation. A Safari guidewire was advanced and positioned in the left ventricle to provide stable rail support for device delivery; this wire type is commonly chosen because it combines a preformed curve for safe crossing and sufficient stiffness to support controlled device manipulation in the LV outflow tract.

A key intraprocedural decision point was whether coronary protection would be required. Preprocedural MSCT predicted adequate valve‑to‑coronary distances and favourable sinus anatomy, so the team elected not to prophylactically wire the coronaries. To confirm that prediction in real time, a controlled balloon pre‑dilation was performed with a 22×40 mm balloon under RVP (≈180 bpm) while simultaneous aortography was performed. This “balloon test” simulates the displacement of leaflet tissue and any potential neo‑skirt that the definitive valve would produce; observing preserved coronary filling during inflation was the critical real‑time check that supported proceeding without coronary stenting or BASILICA. Balloon size and pacing were chosen based on the CT‑derived internal diameters of the prior prostheses and the need to open the stenosed valve sufficiently to allow safe device passage and to challenge coronary flow without causing trauma.

Deployment of the self‑expanding Acurate neo2 (size S) followed the preprocedural CT plan for commissural alignment and a deliberate high implant. CT modelling identified the relationship between the Evolut leaflets, the coronary ostia and the sinuses; these landmarks were reproduced fluoroscopically during delivery so that the neo‑leaflets of the Acurate would sit at the level of the Evolut leaflets rather than deep within the prior frames. The rationale for high implantation in this triple‑stented anatomy was twofold: to fix the existing, inward‑folded leaflets against the stent frame and thereby avoid further reduction of the central orifice, and to minimise the formation of a neo‑skirt below the coronary ostia that could obstruct flow or hinder future coronary access. Controlled, stepwise release under fluoroscopic guidance and reference to the preplanned orientation markers allowed the operators to achieve the intended final depth and rotational alignment.

After deployment, a planned post‑dilatation was performed with a 20×40 mm balloon under RVP to optimise radial expansion of the new prosthesis and to promote additional expansion of the underlying Evolut frame. The objectives of post‑dilatation were to improve leaflet excursion, maximise the effective orifice area within the constraints of three frames, and reduce paravalvular leak if present. Intraprocedural angiographic endpoints used to accept the result were: stable valve position without migration, absence of angiographic paravalvular or transvalvular regurgitation, and preserved coronary perfusion on aortic and selective injections. In addition to angiography, invasive arterial pressure traces and clinical stability guided decisions about performing further dilatation.

Throughout the procedure the team maintained predefined bailout plans. If the balloon test had demonstrated coronary compromise, immediate coronary protection measures were to be executed—wiring the affected coronary and performing chimney stenting if necessary—or emergent conversion to surgery considered if stenting was not feasible. For malposition or embolisation the operators had tools for snaring, delivery of an additional valve or conversion to surgical explant on standby. Because the patient had a prior femoral vascular complication, vascular‑surgery contingencies and equipment for large‑bore access repair (covered stents, surgical instruments) were specifically part of the resource plan. Contrast and radiation exposure were actively managed by limiting cine runs to essential checks and relying on the detailed preprocedural MSCT plan to reduce intraprocedural imaging needs.

In summary, the intraprocedural conduct combined CT‑driven preplanning with staged angiographic tests (balloon simulation of leaflet displacement), controlled self‑expanding valve deployment in a high position with commissural alignment, and focused post‑dilatation to optimise expansion. Real‑time confirmation of coronary patency and the absence of significant regurgitation were the decisive checks that allowed the team to accept the implant; explicit bailout readiness and conservative contrast/radiation strategies were integral to safe execution in this complex redo setting.

## Outcomes, haemodynamic interpretation, and longitudinal surveillance

On procedural completion the case met the key VARC‑3 procedural endpoints for device deployment: the Acurate neo2 was correctly positioned within the prior valves, there was no residual paravalvular or transvalvular regurgitation on angiography or immediate imaging, and there were no acute access or vascular catastrophes. Hemodynamically, however, the immediate transthoracic Doppler study showed a very high mean transvalvular gradient (67 mm Hg), an indexed aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.4 cm²/m² (absolute AVA 0.9 cm²) and a Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24 — findings consistent with severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) or prosthetic obstruction despite the otherwise successful implant.

Interpreting those early abnormal Doppler numbers requires attention to how prosthetic gradients are generated and measured. The continuity equation (AVA = LVOT area × LVOT VTI / aortic VTI) and DVI (ratio of LVOT to aortic valve velocity or VTI) are the standard Doppler tools for prosthetic stenosis assessment; in valve‑in‑valve situations these measurements can be more technically challenging because LVOT sizing, Doppler alignment and the presence of multiple frames alter flow geometry. Practically, DVI is less dependent on an accurate LVOT area and is therefore useful when continuity‑equation inputs are uncertain. Commonly used cutoffs are: DVI ≤0.25 and mean gradient ≥40 mm Hg (or AVA ≤1.0 cm², indexed EOA ≤0.65–0.85 cm²/m² depending on reference) to define severe prosthetic obstruction or severe PPM; by these standards the immediate post‑implant study in this patient indicated severe functional obstruction.

Why did the Doppler gradient fall from 67 mm Hg immediately to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and remain ≈26 mm Hg at 1 year? The clinical team’s explanation — and the pathophysiology underlying it — is instructive. Immediately after deployment a new leaflet assembly can be mechanically constrained by the pre‑existing frames, producing “leaflet pinning” and a reduced effective orifice area (EOA). Even when an appropriately sized valve is delivered, nested stents and remnants of prior leaflets can limit full leaflet excursion and produce very high instantaneous velocities across a small orifice. The team performed high‑pressure post‑dilatation at the index procedure; that, together with the force of the new device and ongoing hemodynamic cycles, likely produced partial mechanical expansion of the underlying Evolut frame over the following hours to weeks. As the composite stent geometry improved (better radial expansion and commissural alignment), leaflet mobility and central flow improved, producing a larger functional EOA. From fluid dynamics, for a given forward stroke volume an increase in EOA produces a disproportionate fall in transvalvular velocity and therefore a fall in the velocity‑derived gradient (gradient ∝ velocity²). In short, modest increases in EOA can more than offset concomitant increases in stroke volume (the patient’s EF and forward flow also recovered after the relief of obstruction), yielding the net decrease in measured gradient observed between the immediate and follow‑up studies. Other contributors that can transiently elevate early gradients — measurement artifacts, tachycardia, low systemic pressure or leaflet edema/thickening — were considered and were not the dominant explanation here because the improvement persisted and correlated with clinical recovery.

The serial echocardiographic course mirrors this pathophysiology and supports a durable midterm result. At 1 month the mean gradient was 27 mm Hg with AVA 1.1 cm² and no regurgitation; the patient’s symptoms abated and left ventricular volumes remodelled favourably. At 3.5 months he remained asymptomatic and haemodynamics stable, and at 1 year the mean gradient was 26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm² and EF 65% with no recurrence of endocarditis. These findings represent sustained functional benefit despite a triple‑stented anatomy: the residual gradients place this valve functionally in the moderate stenosis / limited EOA range rather than severe obstruction, and the clinical improvement and preserved EF provide the most important corroboration that the haemodynamic result is acceptable at 1 year.

Given the uncertainty of durability in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV construct and the history of prior prosthetic endocarditis, a structured surveillance plan is essential. Reasonable elements of that plan — and the triggers for escalation used in this case — are:

- Early imaging: baseline TTE before discharge or within 48–72 hours, and a formal TTE at ~30 days to document early haemodynamic trajectory and detect complications (thrombosis, residual obstruction, regurgitation).  
- Short‑term follow‑up: repeat TTE at 3 months and 6 months to confirm stability during the period of ongoing leaflet and frame remodelling. The centre obtained and documented imaging at 1 month and 3.5 months with a 1‑year study showing durable results.  
- Long‑term schedule: annual clinical review and TTE thereafter if stable. More frequent imaging is warranted for new/worsening symptoms, a sustained rise in mean gradient (for example an increase >10 mm Hg from prior study), a decline in AVA, or new regurgitation.  
- When to use advanced imaging: gated multislice CT should be obtained if there is unexplained gradient increase, suspected hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT)/thrombosis, concern for underexpansion or for preprocedural planning of any future coronary or transcatheter intervention; TEE and blood cultures should be obtained promptly for any febrile illness or concern for recurrent PVE. The team’s pre‑procedure CT images in this case were indispensable for planning and should be retained for future coronary or reintervention planning.  
- Coronary access planning: because overlapping frames can impede future coronary cannulation, documentation of commissural alignment, fluoroscopic implant landmarks and CT reconstructions should be stored; any future need for PCI should be planned with CT guidance and heart‑team discussion.  
- Reintervention thresholds and decision process: symptomatic severe prosthetic stenosis or severe regurgitation, haemodynamic compromise, or imaging evidence of structural failure should prompt multidisciplinary reassessment. In triple‑stented anatomy the balance of surgical risk, feasibility of another transcatheter approach and patient goals of care must be individualized.

Finally, vigilance for recurrence of prosthetic valve endocarditis is warranted given the prior E. faecalis infection. Any unexplained fever, constitutional symptoms or new murmurs should prompt immediate blood cultures and echocardiography and rapid infectious‑disease and heart‑team input. In this patient the combination of imaging confirmation of infection resolution before the third TAVI, a stable clinical course and durable echocardiographic performance at 1 year provides a reassuring early durability signal — but long‑term uncertainty remains, and follow‑up must be proactive and multidisciplinary.

## Clinical lessons, decision frameworks, and future directions

This case of a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) illustrates several practical lessons for trainees and heart teams who must balance infection control, complex anatomy, coronary safety and the inevitability of structural valve deterioration when planning repeat transcatheter interventions. The patient’s course—Lotus (2014) → Evolut TAV‑in‑TAV (2022) → Enterococcus faecalis PVE treated with antibiotics → Acurate neo2 TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV (2024) with good 1‑year outcome—serves as a running example for how rigorous documentation, CT‑led planning and strategy-driven implantation can make a high‑risk reintervention feasible.

Heart‑team checklist for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after PVE
Before contemplating a repeat transcatheter valve in a patient with prior PVE the multidisciplinary team must confirm eradication of infection and document the absence of anatomic complications. Practical checkpoints that were followed in this case include completed and appropriate antibiotic therapy, persistently negative blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography showing no vegetations or paravalvular abscess, and multislice CT (MSCT) excluding paravalvular complications. Equally important is a thorough hemodynamic assessment by TTE (gradients, continuity‑equation AVA, indexed AVA and Doppler velocity index), and objective surgical risk quantification (eg, EuroSCORE II) combined with clinical judgement about frailty and comorbidities. Device selection, the possibility of off‑label use and its institutional approval should be discussed openly with the patient and family; documented shared decision‑making and formal informed consent are essential, as was done in this case for the Acurate neo2 implantation.

Choosing transcatheter versus surgical redo
Surgical explantation and root/ascending aorta reconstruction remain the standard when PVE is active, when there is root destruction, or when durable debridement is required. However, prior TAVI frames can become deeply integrated into the aortic wall and sinotubular junction, making explantation technically complex and high risk. In such circumstances—especially when the patient has prohibitive comorbidity burden, as here (EuroSCORE II >20%, COPD, recurrent GI bleeding, multiple prior coronary interventions, pacemaker)—a CT‑guided TAV‑in‑TAV strategy is frequently the only realistic option. The decision must weigh the risks of leaving multiple frames in place (eg, coronary access issues, PPM) against the immediate procedural risk of surgery, and it should be individualized by the heart team.

Lifetime management beginning at the index TAVI
This case reinforces that the first TAVI should be performed with a “lifetime” mindset. When possible, index implantation should aim for commissural alignment and documented valve orientation so future reinterventions can better preserve coronary access. Device choice at the index procedure (supra‑annular vs intra‑annular, frame height, cell design) influences the ease of future valve‑in‑valve work and the risk of PPM. Procedural notes and fluoroscopic landmarks should be recorded and available for later CT synthesis. In patients likely to outlive a first bioprosthesis (younger or lower surgical risk), these considerations should be discussed pre‑procedurally.

Coronary safety strategies in serial TAVR
The greatest immediate catastrophe in a redo TAVR is coronary obstruction. Meticulous MSCT assessment of coronary ostial height, sinus dimensions, valve‑to‑coronary distance and the predicted neo‑skirt is mandatory; in this case a minimum lumen of 13.7 mm inside the Evolut frame and CT modelling indicated acceptable coronary clearance and supported the decision to proceed without routine coronary protection. Procedural techniques that mitigate coronary risk include high implantation of the new valve (positioning the skirt above prior leaflet tissue to avoid creating a neo‑skirt that covers the ostia), careful commissural alignment to avoid leaflet commissures lying in front of the coronaries, and selection of valve types with favorable open cells or low frame height. When MSCT suggests borderline risk, prophylactic coronary protection (wiring the target coronary with a balloon/stent ready to deploy) or leaflet modification techniques such as BASILICA can be considered; however, BASILICA and chimney stenting become technically more complex and less well validated in the setting of multiple overlapping frames, and their feasibility must be evaluated on a case‑by‑case basis.

Prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM): prediction, intra‑procedural mitigation and interpretation
PPM is a frequent and clinically important concern in redo TAVR because the inner diameter available for flow is reduced by prior frames and leaflets. Preprocedural MSCT measurement of the effective inner lumen (13.7 mm in this patient) should feed directly into valve selection: supra‑annular devices typically provide larger effective orifice areas and were chosen here (Acurate neo2) to maximise hemodynamics. Where feasible, bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) can enlarge rigid surgical rings and some transcatheter frames to reduce PPM, but its utility is limited or uncertain in stacked transcatheter frames and must be planned carefully. Intra‑procedurally, aggressive but controlled pre‑dilation and high‑pressure post‑dilation can help expand prior frames; this case used post‑dilation and noted partial mechanical expansion of the Evolut frame that likely accounted for the marked haemodynamic improvement seen between immediate and 1‑month TTE. Immediate post‑implant metrics require nuanced interpretation: the patient’s immediate mean gradient was high (67 mm Hg) with an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² and DVI 0.24—figures consistent with severe mismatch early after deployment—yet these values improved substantially by 1 month (mean gradient 27 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm²). Doppler velocity index is less flow‑dependent than absolute gradient and is useful in prosthetic valves; very low values (as in this case) should prompt reassessment and tailored optimisation rather than reflex surgical referral.

Evidence gaps and research priorities
Serial TAVR and especially TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV procedures are becoming more common but remain under‑studied. Key research needs include large registries and prospective studies of long‑term clinical and hemodynamic outcomes after triple‑stented constructs, validation of advanced 3D computational modelling to predict frame expansion and neo‑skirt formation, and evidence‑based recommendations for managing PVE risk and antibiotic strategies after serial TAVI. Comparative data on BVF, BASILICA and other adjunctive techniques in multilayered frames are particularly sparse; trainees should recognise the value of contributing cases to registries and publications to build this evidence base.

Practical takeaways
For trainees involved in complex redo TAVR after treated PVE: always confirm microbiological and imaging evidence of infection resolution before reintervention; involve a multidisciplinary heart team early; obtain high‑quality MSCT for valve‑to‑coronary and inner‑lumen planning; anticipate and quantify the risk of PPM and select devices and implantation strategies accordingly; plan coronary protection when anatomy is borderline; document shared decision‑making and any off‑label device use; and arrange close early and mid‑term echocardiographic follow‑up, recognising that early haemodynamics can improve after optimisation. This case demonstrates that, when these elements are addressed, a carefully planned third transcatheter valve can produce sustained symptomatic and haemodynamic benefit in a patient who would otherwise face prohibitively high surgical risk.
