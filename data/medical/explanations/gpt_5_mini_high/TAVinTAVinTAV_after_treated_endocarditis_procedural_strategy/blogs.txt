Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Start With the End in Mind: Lifetime TAVI Strategy When a Third Valve Might Be in the Cards

## Why this case grabbed my attention
A man in his late 70s with a Lotus valve in 2014, an Evolut TAV‑in‑TAV in 2022, prosthetic valve endocarditis (Enterococcus faecalis) that was treated and sterilised, and then—yes—a third transcatheter valve placed in 2024 (Acurate neo2) with a good 1‑year clinical and echocardiographic outcome. Triple‑stented anatomy, prior PVE, a minimum residual lumen of 13.7 mm inside the Evolut and a EuroSCORE II of 27%: this is the kind of real‑world problem that forces teams to “start with the end in mind.”

This case is useful because it forces us to translate abstract lifetime‑TAVI concepts into practical choices: which valve do you pick first, how do you orient and implant it, how do you use CT to anticipate later valve‑in‑valve constraints, and how do you counsel patients that today’s elegant solution may create tomorrow’s technical bottleneck. The team in this report used CT modelling, commissural alignment, high implantation strategy and post‑dilation to preserve coronary flow and maximise effective orifice area (EOA) — and the patient was asymptomatic with mean gradient 26 mmHg and AVA 1.1 cm² at 1 year.

## Case in brief (the essentials)
- 2014: 27 mm Lotus implanted for severe AS.  
- 2022: Degeneration → TAV‑in‑TAV with 23 mm Evolut; vascular complication (pulsatile femoral haematoma → pseudoaneurysm → open vascular surgery).  
- Sep 2022: PVE with Enterococcus faecalis, treated with 6 weeks IV ampicillin; infection resolved by imaging and blood cultures.  
- Jan–Jun 2024: Recurrent prosthetic dysfunction (mean gradient 49 mmHg, AVA 0.5 cm²) → MSCT minimum lumen inside Evolut 13.7 mm. Multidisciplinary decision for third TAVI.  
- Jun 2024: CT‑guided implantation of Acurate neo2 (size S) with balloon pre‑ and post‑dilatation; no coronary protection needed after CT assessment. Immediate post‑procedure mean gradient 67 mmHg (AVA 0.9 cm²), improved to 27 mmHg and AVA 1.1 cm² at 1 month; at 1 year mean gradient 26 mmHg, EF 65%, clinically well.

## What this case teaches about “start with the end in mind”
Every valve you implant affects the anatomical and hemodynamic real estate left for the next one. The features that matter most for future‑proofing are simple concepts, but they must be applied at the time of the first (and every) implant:

- Frame height and cell geometry: tall, dense frames with small cells can bury the coronaries and make later coronary access and leaflet laceration (BASILICA) more difficult. Conversely, valves with more open cells or lower stent heights preserve options for future PCI and leaflet modification.
- Leaflet position (supra‑ versus intra‑annular): supra‑annular leaflets generally give larger EOA for a given annulus—important if you anticipate future valve‑in‑valve procedures in a small annulus.
- Commissural alignment capability: valves and deployment techniques that allow predictable commissural alignment will help preserve coronary access later.
- Radial expandability and options for post‑dilatation or frame fracture: some strategies can expand prior frames and improve EOA; knowing what is feasible for the valve you are implanting matters.
- Documentation: record valve platform, model, size and fluoroscopic orientation (commissural markers) so future teams know exactly what they're dealing with.

In this patient the cumulative stent layers produced a very small internal lumen (13.7 mm). CT modelling allowed the team to choose an Acurate neo2 with supra‑annular leaflets and relatively low frame height, plan a high implant to avoid creating a neo‑skirt that would obstruct the coronaries, and use post‑dilatation to mechanically expand the previous Evolut frame — tactics that converted a near‑impossible anatomy into a durable clinical result.

## Imaging and procedural strategy: how CT mattered here
Multislice CT was central to every key decision. The team used MSCT to measure the residual lumen, to compute valve‑to‑coronary distances, to simulate the neo‑skirt and to plan implantation depth and commissural alignment. Because of that planning they were comfortable proceeding without prophylactic coronary protection.

Procedurally they:
- Used rapid pacing and balloon pre‑dilatation (22/40 mm under 180 bpm) to assess behaviour and coronary flow under balloon expansion.
- Implanted the Acurate neo2 aligned to Evolut leaflets to maximise flow through the supra‑annular space and preserve coronary access.
- Performed post‑dilatation (20/40 mm) to optimise expansion and improve gradients.

The immediate post‑implant gradient was high (mean 67 mmHg), but the team had anticipated underexpansion and accepted early higher gradients knowing that post‑dilatation, mechanical expansion of the older frame and ventricular remodelling can produce meaningful improvement (in this case to 27 mmHg at 1 month and 26 mmHg at 1 year).

## Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE): does it rule out repeat TAVI?
Not automatically — but it changes the calculus. Key steps before considering TAV‑in‑TAV (or TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) after PVE:
- Ensure the infection is fully treated (completed recommended antimicrobial course with negative blood cultures).
- Demonstrate absence of active infection or paravalvular complication on TEE and CT.
- Involve infectious diseases, imaging specialists, interventional cardiology and cardiac surgery in the decision — this case was discussed in a heart team and the patient/family were engaged in shared decision‑making.
- Weigh the surgical risk carefully: in this report the EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, prior stent fixation to the aortic wall made explantation and root replacement technically hazardous, and the team judged surgery prohibitive.

This case shows that after eradication of infection and careful imaging, a third transcatheter valve can be a reasonable option when surgery is not feasible.

## Practical framework for counseling patients about lifetime TAVI strategy
When you consent patients for their first TAVI (and at every follow‑up), make lifetime planning explicit:
- Explain that transcatheter valves can degenerate and that future valve‑in‑valve procedures are often possible, but each additional valve reduces the effective lumen and limits options.
- Discuss valve‑specific tradeoffs: some devices have better short‑term gradients, others allow easier future coronary access or commissural alignment — there is rarely a one‑size‑fits‑all choice.
- Document implant orientation and include it in the discharge summary and imaging archive.
- Revisit the plan periodically: as the patient ages and comorbidities evolve, the balance between surgical and transcatheter reintervention may change.

In short: build a “plan A, B and C” for the patient’s valve lifetime and involve the patient in the decision early.

## Procedural pearls from the case
- CT‑guided modelling for valve‑to‑coronary distance and neo‑skirt is indispensable for redo TAVI planning. In uncertain cases, don't hesitate to plan coronary protection.
- Commissural alignment matters: when you can orient a valve to avoid covering a coronary ostium, you preserve future PCI and decrease the chance of obstruction.
- High implantation in a TAV‑in‑TAV context can deliberately fix the prior leaflets outward and reduce leaflet infolding / neo‑skirt formation that would otherwise worsen EOA.
- Expect that immediate post‑implant gradients can be worse than final: balloon post‑dilatation and subsequent mechanical frame expansion and ventricular remodelling often lower the gradient over weeks.
- PVE is not an absolute contraindication to repeat TAVI once infection is eradicated, but it mandates strict multidisciplinary evaluation.

## What this case does not solve
A successful 1‑year result is encouraging, but the long‑term trajectory beyond that is uncertain. Options after a failed triple‑stent construct are limited: surgical explantation and root replacement are technically demanding; technologies like BASILICA or dedicated leaflet modification may be harder or less predictable in triple‑stented anatomy; bioprosthetic valve fracture techniques are device‑dependent and may not be feasible or safe in every situation. This is why the “start with the end in mind” philosophy matters at the first implant.

## Clinical pearls
This case is a practical reminder that TAVIs are not isolated procedures — they are the first step in a lifetime pathway. Use CT early and often. When you can, pick implants and implant depths that preserve coronary access and maximise EOA for future valve‑in‑valve options. Treat PVE aggressively and only consider repeat percutaneous intervention once infection is proven controlled by imaging and cultures and the heart team judges surgery prohibitive. Finally, document everything — valve platform, size and fluoroscopic orientation — so the next team has the best possible starting point.

## Take‑home messages
- Think lifetime at the time of the first TAVI: frame height, cell geometry, leaflet position and commissural alignment will determine future options.  
- Use MSCT to model valve‑to‑coronary distances, neo‑skirt and residual lumen before any redo TAVI; plan coronary protection when risk remains.  
- In triple‑stented anatomies, consider high implantation, commissural alignment and post‑dilatation to maximise EOA and preserve coronary access.  
- PVE is not an absolute contraindication to repeat TAVI when infection is clearly eradicated, but multidisciplinary evaluation and full antimicrobial therapy are mandatory.  
- Document valve type/size/orientation and discuss a clear lifetime plan with the patient — today’s choice often shapes tomorrow’s rescue options.



# Coronary Safety in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV: Neo-skirts, Valve-to-Coronary Distance, and When to Forgo Protection

Why this case grabbed my attention: a man in his late 70s with two prior TAVIs (Lotus 27 mm in 2014, then an Evolut 23 mm TAV‑in‑TAV in 2022), who developed Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis that was successfully treated — and then underwent a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2 S) in 2024. The team used MSCT-guided planning, deliberately implanted the new valve high to “pin” the old leaflets (minimising the neo‑skirt over the coronaries), performed an intraprocedural balloon test under rapid pacing, and — importantly — proceeded without prophylactic coronary protection. Coronary perfusion was preserved on angiography and the patient is asymptomatic with stable valve function at 1 year.

If you’ve ever worried about coronary obstruction in valve‑in‑valve (ViV) procedures, triple‑stented anatomy is the stuff of nightmares: every frame increases the chance that displaced leaflet tissue will form a neo‑skirt that covers an ostium. This case is a tidy example of how careful CT assessment, targeted device choice and an intra‑procedural balloon test can allow you to safely proceed without wiring the coronaries — but only when the anatomy really supports that decision.

## The anatomy of obstruction in serial ViV

With serial TAVs the mechanism is straightforward but unforgiving: the old valve leaflets get pushed outwards and can form a “neo‑skirt” — it’s that skirt, not the stent metal per se, that blocks the coronary. The key job of preprocedural imaging is to predict where that skirt will sit relative to the coronary ostia once the new valve is deployed.

What you need to focus on on MSCT (and why each matters)
- Coronary ostial height from the annular plane: low ostia increase risk, but height alone is insufficient in ViV.
- Sinus of Valsalva (SOV) diameter and sinus depth: small or shallow sinuses have less space to accommodate displaced leaflets.
- Sinotubular junction (STJ) diameter and height: a narrow STJ can trap leaflet tissue and push it toward the ostia.
- Valve‑to‑coronary distance (VTC): the shortest distance between the intended neo‑skirt/top of pinned leaflet and the coronary ostium. This is the most direct predictor for ViV obstruction risk.
- The internal lumen of the existing THV (minimum lumen diameter): limits the size of the new valve and impacts final effective orifice area.
- Frame geometry and cell size of prior THVs and leaflet heights/material: these determine whether the leaflets will fold predictably and whether commissural alignment is feasible.

A practical point from the case: MSCT here showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm inside the Evolut frame — that constrained device choice and implantation depth, and the CT modelling suggested adequate clearance of the ostia once the third valve was seated.

Rule‑of‑thumb thresholds (use as guidance, not absolutes)
- VTC < 4 mm = high risk for coronary obstruction.
- VTC 4–6 mm = “borderline” — consider protection or adjuncts.
- VTC > 6–8 mm = lower risk (but interpret with sinus size, STJ, leaflet height).
- Coronary height < ~10–12 mm plus small SOV (<30 mm) = high risk in many ViV scenarios.

These thresholds are simplified — always interpret in context of valve type, leaflet morphology and frame stacking.

## What is the predicted neo‑skirt and how to measure it

Think of the neo‑skirt as the new leading edge of leaflet tissue once displaced by the new valve. On CT you’re trying to virtually implant the planned valve and ask: “where will the top of the pinned leaflets sit relative to each coronary ostium?” Dedicated 3D modelling tools make this easier, but even careful multiplanar MSCT measurements and fluoroscopic projection planning (as in this case) can be sufficient to decide whether the neo‑skirt will clear the ostia.

In this patient the team’s CT modelling (they did not use advanced expansion software) suggested that a high implant of an Acurate neo2 would pin the Evolut leaflets low, keeping any neo‑skirt above the coronaries — a deliberate strategy to preserve flow and future access.

## The intra‑procedural balloon test — what it shows and how they used it here

A balloon test is the most pragmatic intraprocedural check when CT is borderline or you want to confirm real‑time coronary patency before committing to a valve that could make things worse. The technique used in this case is illustrative:

- Position a balloon across the valve (size approximating intended final expansion).
- Rapid ventricular pacing (they used 180 bpm) to stabilise the balloon.
- Inflate the balloon and perform simultaneous aortography to assess coronary opacification and flow.

If an ostium fills unobstructed during the inflation/test, it’s strong reassurance that deployment won’t immediately occlude flow. In this patient, balloon aortic valvuloplasty with a 22/40 mm balloon and simultaneous aortography demonstrated preserved coronary perfusion, and the team proceeded without prophylactic coronary wiring.

Important caveat: a negative balloon test is reassuring but not perfect. Final leaflet and skirt geometry after full valve deployment (and after post‑dilatation) can differ from balloon inflation. That said, in ViV settings the balloon often replicates the displacement effect reasonably well because you’re directly testing leaflet displacement.

## Device choice, implantation strategy and commissural alignment

Three practical levers you can use to reduce coronary risk in serial ViV:

1) Device selection: favour valves that give you the best chance to preserve EOA and not create a long neo‑skirt. In this case the team chose Acurate neo2 (supra‑annular leaflet position, relatively low frame height) for its haemodynamic profile in narrow landing zones and for commissural alignment features.

2) Implant depth: a deliberate high implant can pin the prior leaflet tissue against the previous stent frame, preventing an extended neo‑skirt that would lie between coronary ostia and flow. That is exactly what was done here — the third valve was aligned to pin the Evolut leaflets and maximise effective orifice area.

3) Commissural alignment: aim to orient the new valve so that its commissures and frame cells don’t sit in front of coronaries. This preserves both immediate flow and future coronary access.

## BASILICA, chimney stents, prophylactic wiring — when to choose what

BASILICA (laceration of the leaflet) is an elegant, planned solution when CT predicts that displaced leaflet tissue will obstruct an ostium and laceration is technically feasible. It’s particularly useful when you’re dealing with native or surgical leaflets that can be lacerated to create a flow channel. In TAV‑in‑TAV scenarios, and especially in triple‑stented anatomy, BASILICA can be more challenging — the leaflet target may be synthetic or difficult to access between metal frames, and its efficacy is less certain.

Chimney stenting (prophylactic stent parked across the ostium protruding into the aorta) is a bail‑out that reliably preserves an ostium but has long‑term consequences: stent jailing, altered flow, thrombosis risk, and difficulties with future interventions. In multilayered frame situations geometry can lead to stent compression or stent‑metal interplay with unpredictable results.

Practical approach used by many centres (and compatible with this case):
- If CT predicts high risk (VTC very small, low ostia, small SOV) and BASILICA is feasible — plan BASILICA.
- If CT is borderline — pre‑wire the coronary(s) and park a stent on the wire (a low‑threshold step). Perform a balloon test and decide: if the balloon test is clearly negative you may proceed without deploying the parked stent; if the test shows impaired flow, you deploy the stent as a chimney or as needed.
- If CT predicts very high risk and BASILICA is not feasible, consider surgical options if the patient can tolerate them; if not, prepare for chimney stenting or hybrid approaches.

In this case the team judged CT and balloon test to be sufficiently reassuring to forgo protection — a defensible choice because they had a clear plan, equipment ready, and the patient’s surgical risk made open options unattractive.

## Practical preparation and bailout planning

Triple‑frame anatomy severely restricts rescue options, so preparation matters more than ever. Before you start:
- Have coronary guidewires and stents ready (parked on the bench) even if you hope to not use them.
- Ensure your imaging orthogonal views are planned from the CT and readily available for intraprocedural checks.
- Plan for rapid conversion to coronary protection if the balloon test or deployment suggests compromise.
- Have experienced operators for BASILICA if you intend to use it — the technique requires specialised skill and equipment.
- Recognise that in patients with prohibitive surgical risk (this patient had a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%), your bailout surgical options may be limited — that should lower your threshold for careful preprocedural planning.

A final practical note from the case: the team post‑dilated the implanted Acurate neo2 with a 20/40 balloon and observed that immediate post‑implant mean gradient was high (67 mm Hg) but improved markedly over the subsequent month (27 mm Hg) — likely from partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame and haemodynamic improvement as the valve leaflets seated and flow dynamics stabilised. So don’t overreact to an immediately high gradient if the valve position and angiography look good, but of course monitor closely.

## Take‑home clinical pearls
- Coronary obstruction risk increases with each additional transcatheter valve because of the neo‑skirt formed by displaced leaflet tissue — MSCT prediction of the neo‑skirt relative to the ostia is the pivotal assessment.
- On CT prioritise: valve‑to‑coronary distance (VTC), coronary height, SOV diameter/depth, STJ dimensions, prior THV internal lumen and frame geometry.
- Use VTC as your primary ViV metric: VTC <4 mm = high risk; 4–6 mm = borderline; >6–8 mm = lower risk (interpret with sinus size and leaflet morphology).
- When borderline, perform an intraprocedural balloon test (inflate under rapid pacing with simultaneous aortography); a negative test is strong reassurance but not infallible.
- For borderline cases pre‑wire and park a stent — it’s a low‑cost safety net. For clear high risk, favour planned BASILICA when feasible; chimney stenting is a true bailout with significant long‑term trade‑offs.
- Device choice, high implantation to pin prior leaflets, and commissural alignment are actionable strategies to minimise neo‑skirt formation and preserve coronary access.
- Always have a documented bailout plan and the right equipment ready — in triple‑stented anatomy your surgical options may be limited, so planning is everything.

This case shows that with rigorous CT‑driven planning, careful intra‑procedural testing and deliberate implant technique you can sometimes safely forgo prophylactic coronary protection even in TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV. But the margin for error is thin — be methodical, measure everything, and have your bailout ready.



# Redo TAVI After Treated Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis: Navigating 2025 ESC Guidance and Real-World Trade-offs

## Why this case grabbed my attention
A man in his late 70s, with two prior TAVIs and a documented episode of Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), underwent a third transcatheter valve implant (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) after successful antimicrobial therapy and multimodality imaging showed no active infection. Surgery was guideline‑preferred for PVE, but was prohibitive here (EuroSCORE II 27.36%, multiple comorbidities, prior stent frames integrated into the aortic wall). CT‑guided planning, commissural alignment and valve choice (Acurate neo2) were used to preserve coronaries and maximimise orifice area. The patient was asymptomatic with good valve function at 1 year. This is the kind of real‑world trade‑off we’ll discuss: guideline framing, what “resolution of infection” really means, how imaging drives the decision, the limits of suppressive antibiotics — and how to talk about uncertainty with patients and the heart team.

## Case snapshot (the facts)
- Initial TAVI: 27 mm Lotus, 2014.  
- Redo (TAV‑in‑TAV): 23 mm Evolut, May 2022 — complicated by a femoral pseudoaneurysm needing open repair.  
- PVE: Sept 2022, blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis → 6 weeks intravenous ampicillin‑based therapy plus subsequent oral therapy per ID recommendations. At the time of the third procedure antimicrobial therapy had been completed and no long‑term suppressive antibiotics were ongoing; blood cultures, TEE and MSCT excluded active infection.  
- Clinical decline: by Jan 2024 severe prosthetic dysfunction (mean gradient 49 mm Hg; AVA 0.5 cm²). MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm inside the Evolut frame.  
- Third procedure (June 2024): Acurate neo2 S implanted under CT‑guided strategy (high implantation, commissural alignment); predilation and postdilation performed. Immediate post‑op mean gradient 67 mm Hg (severe PPM), but gradients improved to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and 26 mm Hg at 1 year; AVA 1.1 cm² at 1 year; patient asymptomatic, EF 65%.

## What the 2025 ESC guidance says — and how to interpret it here
The 2025 ESC documents reiterate that surgical valve replacement remains the recommended strategy for PVE, especially with heart failure or uncontrolled infection. Importantly, the guidelines also stress individualised decision‑making: the presence or absence of active infection, the patient’s surgical risk and anatomical feasibility must all be weighed. This case sits squarely in that “exceptional, individually tailored” zone — infection treated and documented as resolved, surgery deemed prohibitive, and CT showing a feasible transcatheter plan.

## A practical pathway to consider redo TAVI after treated PVE
1) Confirm eradication of infection. Complete a targeted antimicrobial course and get objective proof of control: repeat negative blood cultures, clinical stability, TEE without vegetations/paravalvular abscess and cross‑sectional imaging (MSCT) that excludes periannular complications. This case had all of those before proceeding.

2) Get CT planning right. High‑quality MSCT is non‑negotiable. For redo or triple‑stented anatomy you need measurements of valve‑to‑coronary distance, sinus sizes, and minimum internal lumen (13.7 mm here). Use CT modelling (or 3D printing if available) to predict the neo‑skirt, plan implantation depth, and decide whether coronary protection or BASILICA is needed.

3) Convene the full heart team (including infectious disease and imaging). The calculus is multidisciplinary: the risks of surgery (technical complexity, need for root replacement, high EuroSCORE) versus the risks of leaving the infected prosthesis in situ and doing another TAVI.

4) Choose valve and implantation strategy deliberately. In narrow, layered frames a supra‑annular valve with a lower frame height (Acurate neo2 in this case) can preserve effective orifice area. Intentional high implantation to pin prior leaflets against the frame can reduce neo‑skirt and help preserve coronary flow. Aim for commissural alignment to protect future coronary access.

5) Be ready for intra‑procedural contingencies. Have coronary protection gear ready if CT shows borderline valve‑to‑coronary distances. Recognise that in triple‑stent anatomies tools like BASILICA or intentional valve‑fracture may be technically challenging or of uncertain feasibility — the case team elected not to perform BASILICA and did not intentionally fracture the Evolut frame, but high‑pressure post‑dilation likely produced partial radial expansion and haemodynamic improvement.

6) Involve ID for antibiotic strategy. If eradication is clear you may proceed without suppressive therapy (as in this case). If there is any uncertainty about eradication or the prosthesis cannot be removed and the microbial burden is thought to persist, long‑term suppressive antibiotics can be considered — this is an individualized trade‑off with limited evidence, potential adverse effects and resistance risk.

7) Plan rigorous follow‑up. Early TTE (before discharge), 1 month, 3 months, then periodic surveillance; low threshold for blood cultures and repeat imaging if fever or symptoms recur. The team in this report documented symptomatic and echocardiographic stability at 1 year — encouraging but not definitive.

## Procedural and clinical pearls from the case
- Don’t shortcut infection control: objective microbiology and imaging clearance were central to the decision.  
- MSCT is the procedural fulcrum for feasibility (VTC, sinus size, frame interaction, min lumen).  
- Valve selection and implantation depth matter: high implantation + supra‑annular leaflets = strategy to preserve area and coronaries.  
- Immediate post‑implant gradients can be misleading; early haemodynamics may improve after post‑dilation and frame expansion (immediate 67 mm Hg → 27 mm Hg at 1 month in this patient).  
- Have honest conversations about salvage options: if the new valve fails, surgical explant in a triple‑stented aortic root is a very high‑risk operation.

## How to discuss this with patients and families (and the heart team)
Start from the guidelines: “Surgery is preferred for infected prosthetic valves, but in your situation surgery is extremely high risk and infection has been successfully treated. We can offer a carefully planned transcatheter option that minimises immediate risk but carries uncertainty about long‑term outcomes and carries a small but real risk of infection recurrence. If infection recurs or the valve fails, options will be limited and high risk. We will involve infectious disease specialists, use CT planning, and follow you closely after the procedure.” Document shared decision‑making and ensure the patient understands the trade‑offs.

## Limitations and unknowns
TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after PVE is sparsely reported. This case shows feasibility and a good 1‑year result, but generalisability is unknown. Long‑term infection recurrence risk, durability in triple‑stented anatomy, and strategies for future coronary access remain uncertain.

Clinical pearls (short)
- Don’t proceed without ID sign‑off: microbiologic and imaging proof of eradication is essential.  
- MSCT drives strategy — valve choice, implant depth, and whether coronary protection is needed.  
- Commissural alignment and high implantation are practical tools to preserve coronaries and orifice area.  
- Immediate haemodynamics may evolve — post‑dilation and frame expansion can improve gradients over weeks.

## Take‑home points
- The 2025 ESC guidance favours surgery for PVE, but patient‑centred, multidisciplinary decisions can justify TAVI when infection is documented as resolved and surgery is prohibitive.  
- Objective infection control (completed targeted antibiotics, negative cultures, TEE and MSCT without active infection) is a prerequisite for considering redo TAVI.  
- High‑quality MSCT and CT‑based modelling are indispensable to assess coronary obstruction risk, frame interactions and minimal lumen — they often determine whether coronary protection is required.  
- Valve selection and implantation strategy (supra‑annular valve, high implantation, commissural alignment) are critical in layered frames to reduce PPM and preserve future coronary access.  
- Suppressive antibiotic therapy should be individualized with ID input; absence of suppressive therapy is reasonable when eradication is convincingly documented.  
- Counselling must be explicit: explain guideline preferences, the reasons for deviating from surgery, the uncertainties about recurrence and durability, and the limited rescue options if the transcatheter solution fails.  
- Close post‑procedural surveillance is mandatory; one successful case with 1‑year follow‑up is reassuring but not definitive — we need more data.

If you’re about to face this scenario in your centre: get ID and surgical sign‑off, prioritise MSCT/3D planning, have coronary protection strategies on standby, and document a clear shared decision‑making process. This case shows it can be done — but it’s still an exception, not the rule.



# Making Peace With Moderate Gradients: Interpreting Early Hemodynamics and PPM After Serial Valve‑in‑Valve

## Why this case catches your eye
A patient with two prior transcatheter aortic valves and treated prosthetic valve endocarditis underwent a third TAVI (Acurate neo2 inside an Evolut inside a Lotus). Immediately after the implant the TTE showed a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg (AVA 0.9 cm², indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m², DVI 0.24) — alarmingly “severe” — yet at 1 month the mean gradient had fallen to 27 mm Hg (AVA 1.1 cm²) and stayed ~26 mm Hg at 1 year, with the patient asymptomatic and EF 65%. How do you reconcile an immediate post‑implant severe gradient with a durable, clinically good result a month later? That is the practical problem this post unpacks.

The facts I’ll use are those from the report: Lotus in 2014, Evolut TAV‑in‑Lotus in 2022, Enterococcus faecalis PVE treated in 2022, and a CT‑planned Acurate neo2 S implanted in June 2024 with balloon pre‑ and post‑dilation (22/40 pre, 20/40 post) and deliberate high implantation and commissural alignment. No residual regurgitation was seen after the final implant.

## Short answer — the likely drivers for this dramatic fall in gradient
There isn’t a single magic bullet. In this triple‑stented anatomy the most plausible contributors are mechanical and hemodynamic, working together:

- Mechanical constraint and incremental expansion: nesting multiple stent frames (Lotus → Evolut → Acurate) creates a constrained landing zone. Even after deployment and on‑table post‑dilation, the composite frame can be underexpanded or the new valve leaflets initially constrained. High‑pressure ballooning during the procedure will increase valve area, but additional mechanical expansion (plastic deformation or relaxation of nitinol frames), improved leaflet coaptation and leaflet mobility can continue in the hours–weeks after the procedure, producing further increase in effective orifice area (EOA).
- Commissural alignment and central flow restoration: aligning the new valve to the existing leaflet commissures and fixing prior leaflets outward (via high implantation) can convert eccentric/turbulent flow to a larger central orifice, improving EOA disproportionately to small geometric changes.
- Measurement and flow effects: small increases in EOA produce large drops in velocities and mean gradients because gradient ∝ velocity². Immediate post‑implant Doppler assessment can be affected by transducer angle, conduction disturbances, heart rate and stroke volume; repeating imaging in a few days–weeks often clarifies the picture.
- What to consider but not evident here: early leaflet thrombosis (HALT) can raise gradients but would be suspected if gradients rose later or if CT/TEE showed leaflet immobility; infection recurrence was excluded in this patient.

The case authors explicitly attribute the improvement to optimized commissural alignment, effective post‑dilation and partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame — all consistent with the mechanisms above.

## What this teaches us about interpreting early post‑implant haemodynamics
After valve‑in‑valve (ViV), and especially after multiple nested valves, expect the unexpected. Practical guidance:

- Immediate high gradients do not always mean procedural failure. If valve is well seated, there is no residual regurgitation, coronaries are patent and the patient is hemodynamically stable, gradients may fall over days–weeks as the frame/leaflets settle and as the hemodynamics normalize.
- Don’t over‑rely on a single number. Look at the pattern: valve position on fluoroscopy/angiography, presence/absence of paravalvular leak, leaflet motion on TEE if needed, valve morphology on gated CT if uncertainty persists, and trends on serial echocardiography.
- Use DVI (dimensionless velocity index) as your friend in prosthetic valves. DVI = VTI_LVOT / VTI_aortic_jet. It avoids LVOT diameter measurement error (a common problem after TAVI). A DVI ≤0.25 is consistent with severe obstruction; in our case DVI 0.24 matched the immediate severe classification.
- Indexed EOA (EOAi = AVA/BSA) defines prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM). Common thresholds: EOAi ≤0.65 cm²/m² = severe PPM; 0.65–0.85 = moderate PPM (note: lower thresholds apply in obese patients). In this patient the immediate indexed AVA was 0.4 cm²/m² (severe) and improved to an AVA of 1.1 cm² at 1 month (indexed value not provided, but the absolute increase and clinical response mattered).
- Acceleration time (AT) and AT/ejection time can add supportive data — AT > ~100 ms often indicates significant obstruction — but they are flow‑dependent and less commonly used than DVI and EOAi in prosthetic assessments.

## Practical approach when you see a high immediate gradient after ViV
1) First, confirm the basics: was the valve deployed where planned? Is there transvalvular or paravalvular regurgitation? Are the coronaries patent? Is the hemodynamic status acceptable? If malposition or severe regurgitation is present — act promptly. If the valve sits well and there is no leak, you have time to observe.

2) Check measurement quality: repeat Doppler with optimal alignment, measure LVOT VTI carefully and calculate DVI and EOAi. If possible, perform TEE in the cath lab before rushing to additional steps.

3) If a clear mechanical underexpansion is suspected and the patient is unstable or gradients extreme with clinical compromise, consider re‑ballooning on the table (weigh benefit vs risk of annular injury / coronary obstruction). In these nested frames, ballooning can expand prior frames (as in this case) but carries risk — only do it with full planning and coronary protection ready if needed.

4) If the patient is stable: observe and reimage in days–weeks. Many gradients fall as frames settle and leaflet motion improves. Consider anticoagulation only if you suspect leaflet thrombosis (diagnose with 4D CT/TEE).

5) If gradient remains high and symptomatic despite these measures, consider heart‑team options: valve fracture is sometimes possible for surgical rings (bioprosthetic valve fracture), further ViV with a different platform, or surgical explant — but with triple‑stented anatomy and prior PVE, surgical options may be prohibitive, so decisions must be individualized.

## The pragmatics of a “win” in triple‑frame anatomy
Absolute haemodynamic “perfection” is often unattainable when you are dealing with three overlapping frames. In this case the pragmatic success markers were:

- Elimination of regurgitation (grade 0 immediately post‑implant)
- A large drop in gradient from 67 mm Hg → 27 mm Hg with persistent stability at 1 year
- Symptomatic improvement and preserved EF (65% at 1 year)
- No recurrent infection

Accepting a moderate residual gradient (mid‑20s mm Hg) and moderate PPM may be a clinically appropriate win when the alternatives (repeat surgery, unacceptable coronary risk) are worse. The goal is symptom relief, preservation of ventricular function, and prevention of regurgitation or hemolysis — not necessarily a “textbook” gradient <20 mm Hg in every high‑risk triple‑stented patient.

## Bottom line — a clinician’s rational strategy
- Expect higher gradients after ViV, particularly after multiple implants.
- Use DVI and indexed EOA to interpret severity (less dependent on geometry than AVA alone).
- Don’t reflex to immediate reintervention for elevated gradients if position is good, no leak, and the patient is stable — allow time for mechanical settling and recheck echocardiography.
- Be ready to act if there is malposition, severe regurgitation, hemodynamic instability, or persistent severe obstruction despite optimized management.
- Planning (CT, valve choice, commissural alignment) and thoughtful peri‑procedural maneuvers (targeted ballooning and careful implantation depth) are the difference between “trial of watchful waiting” vs “crisis.”

## Take‑home messages
- Immediate post‑implant high gradients after valve‑in‑valve (even >60 mm Hg) can fall substantially over days–weeks due to additional mechanical expansion, improved leaflet motion and hemodynamic settling — don’t reflexively reintervene if the valve is well positioned and the patient is stable.
- Use DVI (VTI_LVOT / VTI_aortic_jet) and EOAi (AVA/BSA) to judge prosthetic obstruction and PPM; DVI ≤0.25 and EOAi ≤0.65 cm²/m² indicate severe obstruction/PPM.
- Expect higher baseline gradients after ViV and plan accordingly: choose supra‑annular designs and attempt commissural alignment when coronary access or EOA preservation matters.
- If you suspect underexpansion and the patient is unstable, targeted post‑dilatation can help; if stable, observe and reimage before committing to further intervention.
- In complex triple‑stented patients, a pragmatic “win” is symptom relief, no regurgitation, stable mid‑20s mean gradient and preserved LV function — not necessarily a gradient <20 mm Hg.



# Why Acurate neo2 for the Third Round? Device Selection and High-Implant Technique in Triple-Frame Anatomy

This case is the kind of “medical gymnastics” that will become more common as TAVI ages with patients: a man in his late 70s with a 2014 Lotus, a 2022 Evolut TAV‑in‑TAV, intervening Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (treated), and then severe restenosis in 2024 with a minimum lumen inside the Evolut of 13.7 mm. The team elected a CT‑guided, strategy‑driven third transcatheter implantation (Acurate neo2 size S) with a deliberate high implant to “pin” old leaflets and minimise a neo‑skirt — no coronary protection was required after balloon test‑dilatation — and the patient is asymptomatic with stable valve function at 1 year (mean gradient ~26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm², EF 65%).

Why this is interesting: triple‑stented anatomy + prior prosthetic valve endocarditis + a very small residual lumen. Device choice and precise implant strategy (depth, orientation, and post‑dilation) were the key to getting an acceptable haemodynamic result while preserving coronary flow and future coronary access.

## The strategy in one sentence
Use CT to model how a supra‑annular, low‑frame, alignment‑friendly valve can be oriented and implanted high at the leaflet level to pin old leaflets against the frame, minimise neo‑skirt, and maximise effective orifice area — verify coronary safety with a balloon test under rapid pacing, then optimise expansion with targeted post‑dilation.

## Why Acurate neo2 here?
The team chose Acurate neo2 (self‑expanding, size S) for three practical reasons that matter more as layers accumulate:

- Supra‑annular leaflet position — gives better effective orifice area in tight internal diameters versus intra‑annular designs. In a lumen narrowed to ~13.7 mm this helps rescue transvalvular area.
- Relatively low frame height and an alignment‑friendly design — reduces the risk that a bulky skirt or a tall frame will create a neo‑skirt above the coronary ostia or block coronary access.
- Predictable leaflet plane relative to the Evolut leaflets — permitted CT modelling to align leaflet tips to avoid further restriction and to position the new leaflets to function in the most open part of the stacked construct.

All of these considerations supported a high, leaflet‑level implant to “pin” prior leaflets against the Evolut frame and avoid inward folding that would further reduce the EOA.

## What the CT did for you (and what to measure)
Preprocedural multislice CT was the backbone of decision‑making in this case:

- Measure the minimum internal lumen (here 13.7 mm) — drives valve sizing and feasibility.
- Valve‑to‑coronary distances, sinus of Valsalva dimensions and coronary ostial heights — to estimate obstruction risk and whether coronary protection is required.
- Map the prior valve commissures and leaflet hinge points — to plan where the new commissural posts should sit relative to the old leaflets.
- Virtually model the intended neo‑skirt and choose an implantation depth/rotation that places posts in front of interleaflet zones (maximising open cell flow) rather than in front of coronary ostia.

In this patient the CT model suggested sufficient clearance and a strategy that made coronary protection unnecessary — but it also dictated a very specific implant depth and rotational orientation.

## Stepwise procedural pearls from the case
These are the practical, reproducible steps the team used — useful when you face multiple overlapping frames.

1. Team prep and mindset
   - Multidisciplinary heart‑team decision (surgery prohibitive, infection resolved).
   - Have coronary protection equipment ready (wires, balloons, ready stents) even if CT suggests low risk.

2. Access and support
   - Local anaesthesia, left radial + left femoral arterial access in this case.
   - Pigtail aortography to confirm baseline AR and coronary flow.
   - Safari wire into LV for support.

3. Balloon valvuloplasty as a rehearsal / coronary test
   - Pre‑dilate with a balloon (here 22/40 mm) under rapid ventricular pacing (180 bpm) and perform simultaneous aortography to confirm unobstructed coronaries. This is the simplest “BASILICA‑rehearsal” — if coronaries are pinched by the balloon–leaflet complex, you’ll see it.

4. Precise implantation: high and aligned
   - Use the CT plan and fluoroscopic landmarks to rotate the delivery so commissural posts will sit between the prior valve leaflet hinges.
   - Aim for a higher implant at the level of the Evolut leaflets to pin old leaflets against the frame — this reduces the neo‑skirt that sits below the coronaries and helps preserve the largest possible central flow area.

5. Controlled post‑dilation
   - Post‑dilate (20/40 mm balloon in this case) under rapid pacing to optimise expansion. Do not attempt indiscriminate “frame fracture” of transcatheter frames; rather, use targeted high‑pressure ballooning to coax radial expansion while monitoring for coronary compromise or annular injury.
   - Expect some immediate mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame; this can improve leaflet mobility and central flow.

6. Check immediate haemodynamics and imaging
   - In this patient immediate TTE showed no regurgitation but high mean gradient (67 mm Hg) — monitor closely as gradients may improve once the new valve and prior frames settle and after the LV remodels.

7. Follow‑up
   - Repeat echo at 1 month (here mean gradient improved to 27 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm²) and ongoing surveillance for PVE recurrence (blood cultures, TTE/TEE if indicated).

## Why the immediate gradient fell over time
It’s important to set expectations: immediate post‑implant gradients can be high in multi‑stented constructs because the new valve and old frames may not be fully expanded or may have restricted leaflet motion initially. In this case optimization (post‑dilation), partial mechanical expansion of the Evolut, and LV remodeling (improved stroke volume) produced marked gradient improvement by 1 month — and this improvement was durable at 1 year.

Do not reflexively assume immediate high gradient equals failure; combine hemodynamics, leaflet motion on echo, and clinical status before deciding on re‑intervention.

## Risks, alternatives and pitfalls
- Coronary obstruction risk is amplified with layered frames; always plan and be ready with coronary protection or BASILICA if CT suggests borderline clearance.
- Multiple frames reduce future options — explant surgery later would be very complex. Lifetime planning from the first TAVI matters.
- Aggressive ballooning risks annular rupture, valve embolisation or conduction disturbance (less concerning here because the patient already had a pacemaker).
- PVE history: only proceed if infection is confidently eradicated (negative cultures, no vegetations on TEE, completed antibiotics) — this patient had completed therapy and imaging confirmed no active endocarditis.

## Bottom line — practical takeaways
- In triple‑stented anatomy with a narrow internal lumen, favour supra‑annular, low‑frame valves that allow predictable commissural alignment to maximise effective orifice area and preserve coronary access.
- Use MSCT modelling to plan implantation depth and rotation — plan the implant so the new leaflets work in the largest available supra‑leaflet space and pin old leaflets against the frame to avoid a neo‑skirt.
- Always perform a balloon test under rapid pacing with simultaneous angiography to assess coronary safety before committing; have coronary protection ready.
- Use targeted post‑dilation to optimise expansion; expect early haemodynamic improvement over days–weeks as frames settle and LV remodels.
- Document shared decision‑making and ensure infection is eradicated before reintervention in patients with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis.

Key take‑home messages
- Device choice becomes critical as layers accumulate: supra‑annular + low frame + alignment capability = better chance of acceptable EOA in small lumens.
- CT-guided leaflet‑level alignment and a deliberate high implant can pin old leaflets, minimise neo‑skirt, and preserve coronary flow/access.
- Balloon pre‑dilation under rapid pacing with angiography is a simple, effective coronary safety test in TAV‑in‑TAV (and TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) scenarios.
- Immediate high gradients do not always require reintervention — re‑evaluate after optimisation and clinical follow‑up.
- Plan for the life‑time strategy at first TAVI: commissural alignment and coronary access preservation matter for future interventions.



# Coronary Reaccess Through Triple Mesh: Practical Strategies for Tomorrow’s PCI After Today’s TAVI

Three transcatheter valves stacked together turn future coronary work into wire archaeology. The case that inspired this post is a perfect example: a man in his late 70s with a 2014 Lotus TAVI, a 2022 Evolut TAV‑in‑TAV, treated Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, and a CT‑planned 2024 Acurate neo2 implanted as a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV. Careful CT modelling, commissural alignment and a high implant strategy preserved coronary flow — no coronary protection was required — and the patient was doing well at 1 year (mean gradient ~26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm², EF 65%). That outcome didn’t happen by luck. It was the result of thinking about coronary access before, during, and after the procedure.

If you do TAVI now, your cath lab colleagues will be doing PCI years later. Here’s a pragmatic playbook for preserving coronary access and for getting back in when you’re looking through two, or three, layers of metal.

## The problem — why coronary access matters after repeat TAVI
- Coronary artery disease is common in TAVI patients and many will need PCI later. Each additional transcatheter frame increases metal density across the sinuses and around the coronary ostia, narrowing “windows” through which catheters and wires must pass.
- Risks of lost or difficult access: inability to treat acute or chronic coronary lesions, emergency inability to wire a threatened left main, prolonged procedure time, higher radiation/contrast, and more complex hardware requirements (mother‑and‑child, microcatheters, exotic curves).
- The bigger the stack, the more planning you must do up front.

## Before you implant (or re‑implant): map the coronary “windows”
CT is not optional. Use MSCT to do two jobs:
1) Risk of coronary obstruction: measure coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, sinotubular junction and calculate valve‑to‑coronary distance (VTC) for the planned device. Many teams treat VTC < ~4 mm and low coronary height (~10–12 mm) as high risk and plan protection.
2) Coronary windows for future PCI: reconstruct the prior frame(s) and map where ostia sit relative to stent cells and commissures. Look for the largest cell openings or gaps where catheter passage will be easiest.

Practical imaging tips
- Ask for a virtual THV overlay and VTC measurements for your candidate device(s). If available, run simulation software (3D modelling or vendor tools) to predict skirt position and ostial coverage.
- Export CT projection angles and use them in the lab (fusion overlay if your system supports it) so fluoroscopy matches the CT roadmap.

Case tie‑in: the team measured a minimum lumen of 13.7 mm inside the Evolut, checked valve‑to‑coronary relationships, and used CT modelling to confirm they could implant an Acurate neo2 without coronary protection.

## Device and implant strategy — choose and place to preserve access
If you care about future PCI, pick and place with that goal in mind.

Device characteristics that help future access
- Open‑cell frame geometry and larger cell windows are friendlier for catheter passage than tight, closed-cell designs.
- Lower frame height and shorter skirts reduce metal burden at the level of the coronary ostia.
- Supra‑annular leaflets (when combined with low frame/skirt) can give better haemodynamics while still leaving space for access.
- Recapturable valves and delivery systems that allow controlled rotation make commissural alignment possible.

Implant tactics that preserve windows
- Commissural alignment: orient the valve so commissural posts do not sit in front of coronary ostia. Use device‑specific radiopaque markers and the manufacturer’s recommended delivery orientation; check alignment fluoroscopically before final deployment. Think of commissural alignment as cheap insurance for future access.
- High implantation (when anatomically safe): in redo TAV scenarios it can be used to press the old leaflets against the frame, minimizing the formation of a bulky neo‑skirt below the coronaries. High implantation reduces the mesh between ostia and catheter tip, but balance this with anchoring and embolization concerns.
- If CT predicts high risk for coronary occlusion (small VTC, low ostia, small sinuses), plan prophylactic protection (pre‑wired wire ± stent, or BASILICA when indicated).

Case tie‑in: the operators chose Acurate neo2 for its low frame and commissural alignment capability, performed high implantation aligned with the Evolut leaflets, and preserved coronary perfusion without prophylactic stenting.

## If you need PCI through multilayer mesh — a practical, stepwise approach
When you’re faced with one or more valve frames, adopt a systematic workflow rather than improvising.

Pre‑procedure prep
- Review the most recent MSCT (post‑implant CT if available) and bring the CT projection angles into the lab. Identify the best “window” (largest cell opening) into each ostium.
- Have a PCI tray ready: a variety of guiding catheter shapes, a couple of workhorse wires, hydrophilic/penetration wires, low‑profile microcatheters, small balloons, and at least one guide‑extension device (GuideLiner/Guidezilla or equivalent). Consider radial + femoral access options depending on guide support needs.
- If the patient had prior CABG or critical left main disease, be prepared for urgent coronary protection or chimney stenting.

Crossing the mesh — technique
1. Roadmap the ostium in the CT‑derived working projection(s). Use aortography to correlate fluoroscopic anatomy with CT windows.
2. Start with a neutral, non‑aggressive guiding catheter. Work to achieve the best possible coaxial approach to the ostium using catheter rotation and small adjustments — avoid forceful deep seating against the stent mesh.
3. Use a small, low‑profile microcatheter (plus a soft, hydrophilic wire) to probe the ostium through the chosen window. Microcatheters let you manipulate the wire tip precisely inside the mesh and protect the wire during exchanges.
4. Once the wire is in the coronary and in a safe distal position, exchange for a definitive workhorse wire and step up support as needed.
5. If you need more support for device delivery, advance a guide‑extension catheter carefully through the stent cells to sit just proximal to or within the coronary ostium; use a low‑profile extension to minimize entanglement risk. Keep a close eye on resistance and fluoroscopy — withdraw if snagging occurs.
6. Use balloon anchoring (small balloon inflated distally) to enhance backup if necessary, but be mindful of vessel trauma and avoid aggressive maneuvers that could damage the valve frame.
7. Deploy stents using the guide‑extension for pushability as required. Confirm final result carefully.

Tools and tricks
- Microcatheters: invaluable for negotiating small cell openings and exchanging wires.
- Guide‑extension catheters: often the difference between success and failure — use them but respect entrapment risk.
- Be flexible with guide shape: JL, AL, extra‑backup curves, or even Amplatz‑like shapes may be needed depending on ostial orientation through the mesh.
- CT‑fluoro fusion: where available, this speeds localization of ostia and window targeting.
- If you cannot reach the ostium through any reasonable window and revascularization is urgent, consider chimney stenting as a bailout; recognize the long‑term tradeoffs.

Warnings and failure modes
- Guide‑extension entrapment in stent frames is a real hazard — don’t force it; withdraw and re‑plan.
- Catheter manipulation through multiple frames can cause coronary dissection — move slowly, use soft wires first.
- Multiple layers of metal complicate future reinterventions and may limit options like rotational atherectomy or complex bifurcation techniques.
- Always have a plan B: surgical backup (if feasible), chimney stent kit, or consult the heart team.

## Make this a lifetime strategy — not a one‑off decision
The best time to protect future coronary access is before the first TAVI. If a patient has known coronary disease or is likely to need interventions in future, select devices and deployment strategies that preserve access: choose valves with favourable cell geometry when possible, plan commissural alignment, and document CT windows and projection angles in the record. When re‑intervention is inevitable (this patient’s journey shows that), reuse the same CT roadmap and prioritise commissural alignment/high implant strategies to keep the corridors open.

## Case takeaway — what worked here
- Thorough MSCT mapping guided valve choice and implantation strategy.
- Acurate neo2 was selected for supra‑annular leaflets, lower frame height and ability to align commissures.
- High implantation aligned to the prior Evolut leaflets reduced neo‑skirt formation and preserved coronary perfusion — the team performed no coronary protection during the third implant.
- Immediate transvalvular gradients were high after deployment but improved substantially after post‑dilation and frame expansion; clinically the patient was asymptomatic at 1 year.

Clinical pearls
- Think “coronary windows,” not just coronary height. VTC and 3D relationships matter.
- Commissural alignment is a small extra step during TAVI that pays huge dividends later.
- Keep a PCI kit ready for every TAVI — even when CT says the coronaries are safe.
- When faced with triple mesh, expand your toolbox: microcatheters, guide extensions, CT‑fluoro fusion and patience.

Key take‑home messages
- Pre‑TAVI MSCT mapping (including VTC) is essential — plan the implant for future coronary access, not just acute valve function.
- Commissural alignment and high implantation (when safe) preserve coronary windows and reduce ostial coverage by skirts/leaflets.
- Choose valve platforms with future access in mind: open‑cell frames, lower height and commissural alignment capability when the clinical scenario allows.
- For PCI through multilayer frames, use CT‑guided projections, microcatheters to probe ostia, and guide‑extension catheters for support — advance cautiously to avoid entrapment or dissection.
- If CT predicts high obstruction risk (small VTC, low coronaries, small SOV), plan coronary protection (wires/stent) or pre‑emptive strategies rather than hoping for the best.
- Treat lifetime planning as part of every TAVI discussion — today’s implant decisions are tomorrow’s PCI challenges.
