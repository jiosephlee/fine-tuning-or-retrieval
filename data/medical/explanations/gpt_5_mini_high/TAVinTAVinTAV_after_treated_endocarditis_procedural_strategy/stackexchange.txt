Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] What clinical and imaging criteria should be documented to justify a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVR) in a patient with prior TAVR-in-TAVR and previously treated Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, and which tests would you obtain to confirm eradication of infection before reintervention?

Bottom line: before implanting a third transcatheter aortic valve in a patient with prior TAV‑in‑TAV and treated Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), you must document (1) a clear clinical indication for reintervention and prohibitive surgical risk, (2) microbiologic and clinical eradication of infection, and (3) imaging evidence that there is no active prosthetic infection and that anatomy is favourable for a safe TAV‑in‑TAV re‑intervention. If any doubt about eradication remains, re‑implantation of a new prosthesis should be deferred.

What to document (practical checklist)
1. Clinical indication and risk/consent
 - Symptomatic severe prosthetic valve dysfunction meeting intervention thresholds (symptoms + objective echo/hemodynamic criteria: high mean gradient, very small AVA, low DVI). In the case report the patient had dyspnea, mean gradient 49 mm Hg and AVA 0.5 cm2 — clear indication.  
 - Formal documentation of prohibitive surgical risk and multidisciplinary heart‑team decision, informed consent, and any institutional approval for off‑label device use (all recorded in the report).

2. Microbiologic and infectious‑disease documentation (must be explicit in the chart)
 - Completion of a full, organism‑directed antibiotic course appropriate for Enterococcus faecalis (the patient received a 6‑week IV ampicillin‑based course and oral follow‑up). Record regimen, dates, and susceptibilities.  
 - Microbiologic clearance: serial negative blood cultures after completion of therapy (practice: multiple sets — typically ≥2–3 sets over 24–72 hours off antibiotics — documented with dates). The authors report “repeated blood cultures … confirmed resolution.”  
 - Infectious disease (ID) consultation note stating clinical eradication, recommendation regarding need (or not) for chronic suppressive therapy, and endorsement of timing for reintervention.

3. Imaging demonstrating absence of active PVE and absence of destructive perivalvular disease
 - Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE): no vegetations, no new mobile masses, no perivalvular abscess, no prosthetic dehiscence or fistula. The report states pre‑procedural TEE “confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.”  
 - Cross‑sectional imaging (multislice CT): no perivalvular contrast collection, abscess, pseudoaneurysm or other signs of active infection; and CT used for procedural planning (annulus, sinus size, coronary heights, valve‑to‑coronary distance, minimum inner lumen of prior valve—in this case MSCT showed a 13.7 mm minimum lumen and guided feasibility).  
 - FDG‑PET/CT (or radiolabelled leukocyte SPECT/CT) when TEE/CT are equivocal: absence of focal increased uptake around the prosthesis supports eradication. Note caveat: FDG uptake may be falsely positive early after implantation; timing and expertise in interpretation are required.

4. Laboratory evidence and clinical stability
 - Afebrile and no systemic features of infection for an appropriate interval.  
 - Trend of inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) returning towards baseline; normalizing WBC; document values and trend.

5. Procedural planning documentation
 - CT‑based valve‑in‑valve planning metrics explicitly documented (coronary heights, sinus dimensions, valve‑to‑coronary distances, predicted neo‑skirt, minimum stent lumen) and a plan for coronary protection or BASILICA if risk judged high. In the report CT modelling supported a strategy without coronary protection.  
 - Heart‑team note that balances infection status, anatomy, and surgical risk and documents rationale for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV versus surgery.

Which tests to obtain to confirm eradication (recommended panel)
 - Serial blood cultures (≥2–3 sets) after completion of antibiotics and while off suppressive therapy; if any positive culture, delay intervention.  
 - Repeat TEE focused on vegetations, abscess, dehiscence and prosthetic motion.  
 - Multislice cardiac CT (contrast) to exclude perivalvular collections/abscesses and to obtain detailed valve‑in‑valve planning measurements (annulus, sinuses, coronary ostia, valve‑to‑coronary distance, minimum inner lumen).  
 - FDG‑PET/CT (or leukocyte SPECT/CT) when available and especially if TEE/CT are inconclusive — absence of pathological uptake strengthens confidence in eradication.  
 - Routine labs: CRP, ESR, CBC documented to be improving/normal.  
 - Formal ID clearance note stating eradication and recommendation against suppressive therapy or, if suppressive therapy is required, a plan for how that will be managed around reintervention.

Why this is essential (pathophysiology and clinical reasoning)
 - Prosthetic endocarditis involves organisms in biofilm on prosthetic material; implanting additional prosthetic material into an incompletely treated infected field risks persistent or recurrent PVE with very high morbidity and mortality. TEE has the best sensitivity for vegetations but is limited by prosthetic shadowing, hence the additive value of CT and FDG‑PET/CT. Microbiologic clearance (negative blood cultures) and ID confirmation are central—imaging alone is not sufficient.  
 - The procedural decision must balance the risk of leaving a failed infected prosthesis untreated against the risk of re‑seeding a new prosthesis; the multidisciplinary heart team and ID clearance are therefore required to document a favorable risk–benefit assessment.

How the case illustrates these principles
 - The treating team documented completion of targeted 6‑week therapy for E. faecalis, no long‑term suppressive antibiotics, repeated negative blood cultures, preprocedural TEE and MSCT showing no active endocarditis or paravalvular disease, and CT measurements that made a third TAVR anatomically feasible (no need for coronary protection). Heart‑team review and institutional approval for off‑label device use were recorded; the procedure was performed with CT‑guided strategy and the patient remained free of recurrent infection at 1 year.

Practical pearl
 - If you cannot document microbiologic clearance and clear imaging evidence of no perivalvular infection, do not implant another valve. When documented eradication, detailed CT planning, ID clearance and heart‑team consensus are present, a highly selected patient at prohibitive surgical risk can be considered for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV as was done successfully in this report.

### [Clinical Pearls] During CT planning for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, which measurements most strongly predict coronary obstruction (e.g., coronary height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and valve-to–sinotubular junction distance), what practical high-risk thresholds do you use, and how do these values guide valve selection, implant depth, and the decision to use coronary protection?

Short answer
- The single most predictive CT-derived measurement for coronary obstruction in valve‑in‑valve (and especially valve‑in‑valve‑in‑valve) procedures is the virtual valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance measured after “virtually” implanting the planned transcatheter valve. VTC must be interpreted together with coronary ostial height and the size/volume of the sinuses of Valsalva (SOV) and with the valve‑to–sinotubular junction (VT‑STJ) relationship to judge the risk of direct ostial coverage versus sinus sequestration.

How I measure and why these numbers matter (pathophysiology in one line)
- Coronary height: distance from the annular plane to the coronary ostium. Low heights (<10 mm) mean displaced leaflets or a neo‑skirt can reach and cover the ostium.  
- Sinus of Valsalva diameter/volume: small or shallow sinuses cannot accommodate outwardly displaced leaflets or stacked frames, increasing risk of both ostial coverage and sinus sequestration.  
- Virtual valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance: the shortest distance from the anticipated prosthetic skirt/leaflet line to the coronary ostium on a CT virtual‑implant. This directly predicts whether the new neo‑skirt/leaflet will impinge the ostium.  
- Valve‑to–sinotubular junction (VT‑STJ) / neo‑skirt vs STJ: if the neo‑skirt will reach or sit above the STJ (VT‑STJ very small or negative), sinuses can become sealed (sinus sequestration) and coronaries may be ischemic despite unobstructed ostia.

Practical thresholds I use (practical, evidence‑based, and conservative for re‑interventions)
- Coronary height: <10 mm = high risk; 10–12 mm = borderline; >12 mm = lower risk.  
- SOV diameter (mid‑sinus): <30 mm = high risk; 30–34 mm = borderline; >34 mm = lower risk. Consider sinus volume as an adjunct (small volume = higher risk).  
- VTC (virtual valve‑to‑coronary): ≤4 mm = high risk; 4–6 mm = intermediate; >6 mm = low risk. In TAV‑in‑TAV and especially TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV I use a more conservative cutoff (treat VTC ≤6 mm as actionable) because stacked frames reduce effective sinus space.  
- VT‑STJ (predicted distance between neo‑skirt and STJ): ≤2 mm (or neo‑skirt projected above STJ) = high risk for sinus sequestration; 2–4 mm = borderline.

How these measurements change strategy (selection, depth, protection)
- Valve selection: prefer devices with the lowest possible skirt height and favorable commissural‑alignment features when coronary protection is a concern. A device with a lower frame/skirt or the ability to achieve truly “high” implantation (and reliably align commissures) reduces the neo‑skirt height and helps preserve coronary access — this is exactly why the team in the case selected the Acurate neo2 to reduce frame/skirt impact and to facilitate commissural alignment. Avoid tall‑skirt, high‑leaflet devices when VTC is small unless you will use prophylactic strategies.  
- Implant depth: plan a higher implant so the new skirt sits as low as possible relative to the coronaries (or aligns the leaflets to fold against prior leaflets), but balance this against anchoring and embolization risk. In re‑interventions you should plan the implant depth on the CT virtual implant and target the depth that optimizes VTC and VT‑STJ. In the reported case the team intentionally implanted high at the level of the Evolut leaflets to minimise neo‑skirt reaching the coronaries and to preserve EOA.  
- Coronary protection (when to do it and what):  
  - Low risk (VTC >6 mm, coronary height >12 mm, SOV roomy): routine protection not required; standard precautions.  
  - Intermediate risk (VTC 4–6 mm, coronary height 10–12 mm, small/intermediate SOV): strongly consider prophylactic wiring of the coronary(s) with an undeployed stent ready (balloon/stent parked) and have bail‑out equipment ready; consider intraprocedural balloon test.  
  - High risk (VTC ≤4 mm or coronary height <10 mm with SOV <30 mm, or predicted neo‑skirt above STJ): plan definitive prevention — BASILICA if leaflet anatomy and prior valve leaflet access allow (to lacerate the leaflet and prevent it acting as a sealed neo‑skirt) or pre‑positioned chimney/stent strategy if BASILICA not feasible. In TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, recognize BASILICA may be technically limited; many teams therefore favour having wires/stents parked as primary protection and a low threshold to bail out.  
- Intraprocedural verification: aortic angiography during balloon predilatation or with a partially deployed device is a practical check — in this case the team performed balloon predilation with simultaneous aortography and observed unobstructed coronary flow and therefore proceeded without protection.

A practical workflow I use on CT planning day
1. Perform a virtual implant of the planned valve size/type at the intended implantation depth; measure VTC and VT‑STJ for that configuration.  
2. Record coronary heights and SOV diameters/volumes. Check leaflet/previous‑valve leaflet heights relative to coronaries.  
3. Stratify into low/intermediate/high risk using the thresholds above; in redo and multi‑frame cases shift thresholds conservatively.  
4. If intermediate/high risk, decide between BASILICA (if anatomy allows), prophylactic coronary wiring/undeployed stent, or abort/choose alternate valve strategy. Plan commissural alignment and high implantation if feasible.  
5. Perform an intraprocedural balloon test (BAV) and angiography prior to final deployment as a final safety check.

How this applies to the published case
- The team used MSCT virtual modelling to confirm adequate coronary clearance and favorable VT‑STJ relationships, chose the Acurate neo2 because it has a relatively low skirt/frame and good commissural alignment potential, planned a high implant (to fold prior leaflets and avoid a neo‑skirt at the coronary level), and performed balloon predilation with aortography that demonstrated preserved coronary flow — so they proceeded without preemptive coronary protection and had an excellent outcome at 1 year. That sequence — CT virtual implant → conservative valve choice → high implant strategy → intraprocedural angiographic check — is the practical template I teach.

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
- VTC on CT virtual implant is the single best predictor of coronary obstruction; combine it with coronary height and SOV size and be more conservative for repeat‑TAV procedures. Use thresholds (VTC ≤4 mm, coronary height <10 mm, SOV <30 mm) to trigger prophylactic measures; in TAV‑in‑TAV and TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV lower thresholds (i.e., treat VTC ≤6 mm as concerning) and favour valve choices and implant depths that minimize the neo‑skirt and preserve coronary access. When in doubt, wire the coronaries and have a bailout stent ready — and remember an intraprocedural balloon test is a valuable real‑time check before committing to final deployment.

### [Pathophysiology] In a transcatheter bioprosthesis that has failed after a prior episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis, explain the pathophysiologic mechanisms by which infection can lead to late restenosis and/or regurgitation, and contrast these with the mechanisms and imaging signatures of transcatheter valve thrombosis.

Short answer
In a transcatheter bioprosthesis that previously had prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), late restenosis and/or regurgitation most commonly result from infection‑mediated tissue injury followed by scarring and anatomic disruption. This is an inflammatory‑destructive → fibrotic process (leaflet destruction, perforation, fibrosis and leaflet retraction; annular/abscess‑related dehiscence) that produces fixed mechanical obstruction or loss of coaptation. By contrast, transcatheter valve thrombosis is a thrombotic process that produces hypo‑attenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and reduced leaflet motion (RLM) on 4D CT, is usually reversible with anticoagulation, and lacks the systemic and perivalvular signs of infection.

Pathophysiology — how infection causes late restenosis or regurgitation
- Direct tissue injury during active PVE: vegetations and bacterial invasion destroy cusp tissue and can produce leaflet perforation or commissural disruption → acute regurgitation.
- Healing with fibrosis: after successful antibiotic therapy the destroyed or inflamed bioprosthetic tissue commonly heals by scar. Scarred, thickened, shortened leaflets have reduced excursion and central orifice area, producing progressive stenosis (restenosis). Chronic inflammation also accelerates degenerative change of the bioprosthetic tissue.
- Annular/perivalvular damage: abscess formation or annular involvement can produce paravalvular dehiscence or pseudoaneurysm → paravalvular leak and regurgitation.
- Interaction with TAVI frames: in transcatheter valves the stent frames constrain leaflets; infection‑related thickening/fibrosis or adherent healed vegetations can cause inward folding or fixation of leaflets against the frame, magnifying effective orifice loss. In layered (TAV‑in‑TAV) configurations this effect is amplified and can produce severe, fixed gradients.

Imaging and diagnostic signatures that favour healed/infectious structural failure
- Clinical context: recent fever, positive blood cultures, systemic inflammation point to active PVE; healed PVE presents with sterile cultures and absence of systemic signs but persistent mechanical dysfunction.
- Echocardiography (TEE): gold standard for detecting vegetations, leaflet perforation, dehiscence or abscess cavities. Persistent immobile, thickened leaflets after treated PVE suggest fibrotic restriction.
- Multislice CT: shows leaflet thickening, frame geometry and residual lumen; CT cannot always distinguish fresh thrombus from fibrotic scarring but is essential for planning and for detecting perivalvular collections or annular disruption.
- FDG‑PET/CT: increased FDG uptake around the valve supports active infection; absence of uptake supports healed, sterile scarring.
- Temporal behaviour: structural change from infection is fixed and will not promptly reverse; it often evolves over weeks–months after the infection.

How thrombosis differs — mechanism and imaging signature
- Mechanism: thrombus forms on leaflets or frame in zones of low shear, stasis or hypercoagulability (including after valve undersizing, low cardiac output or lack of anticoagulation). Thrombus causes leaflet immobility and obstruction but is not inflammatory tissue destruction.
- Imaging signature: 4D (multiphase) CT classically shows hypo‑attenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and reduced leaflet motion (RLM). Transthoracic/ transesophageal echo may show rising Doppler gradients but is less sensitive for early HALT. HALT/RLM do not have FDG uptake and blood cultures are negative.
- Clinical course and reversibility: valve thrombosis often responds to therapeutic anticoagulation with reduction of HALT/RLM and improvement in gradients over weeks to months—this therapeutic response is both diagnostic and therapeutic.

Putting it together for clinical decision‑making (practical algorithm)
1. New or worsening prosthetic valve dysfunction → immediately obtain blood cultures and TEE.
2. If fever/bacteremia or TEE shows vegetations/abscess → treat as PVE (targeted antibiotics ± surgery depending on complications).
3. If no systemic infection but rising gradients or leaflet immobility → add multislice 4D CT to look for HALT/RLM (thrombosis) versus fixed anatomic restriction, and consider FDG‑PET if concern persists for low‑grade active infection.
4. If HALT/RLM — institute therapeutic anticoagulation and reimage; if thrombus resolves, no further structural intervention is needed.
5. If fixed fibrotic restriction, annular destruction, or healed PVE with persistent mechanical obstruction/regurgitation (and no active infection) — structural reintervention (TAV‑in‑TAV or surgery) is required because anticoagulation will not reverse scar.

How this applies to the reported case
- The patient had culture‑positive Enterococcus faecalis PVE in 2022 treated with a full antibiotic course. Preprocedural TEE and MSCT at the time of planning for the third TAVI showed no active infection or vegetations and cultures were sterile. Despite eradication of infection, he developed severe restenosis with moderate–severe regurgitation and an extremely small residual lumen within the Evolut frame (minimum diameter 13.7 mm). The authors concluded — appropriately — that the dominant mechanism was infection‑related leaflet injury followed by fibrosis and mechanical restriction (and progressive structural degeneration), not thrombosis. That distinction justified proceeding to CT‑guided TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV rather than a trial of anticoagulation. The case also illustrates how layered frames and healed leaflet fixation can produce fixed prosthetic failure that will not respond to anticoagulation.

Clinical pearl
When a TAVI patient with prior PVE presents with valve dysfunction, integrate clinical (fever, cultures), TEE, 4D‑CT and (if needed) FDG‑PET: reversible thrombus (HALT/RLM) calls for anticoagulation; infection requires antibiotics (± surgery); healed infectious damage produces fixed leaflet fibrosis/annular injury and usually requires structural reintervention as in this case.

### [Management] In a triple-stented aortic valve with an in-valve minimum lumen diameter of approximately 14 mm, what procedural strategies can maximize effective orifice area and reduce prosthesis–patient mismatch, and how do device features (supra-annular leaflet position, frame height, commissural alignment capability) influence your choice of valve?

Bottom line
- In a triple‑stented valve with an in‑valve minimum lumen diameter ≈14 mm, your procedural goal is to preserve or enlarge the flow lumen and avoid creating an additional obstructing “neo‑skirt.” Practically this is achieved by CT‑guided valve selection (favoring supra‑annular leaflets), high implantation that pins prior leaflets outward, aggressive but cautious balloon dilation to expand prior frames, and deliberate commissural alignment to minimise strut/leaflet overlap of the coronaries. These steps were exactly the strategy used in the reported case (MSCT MLD 13.7 mm → Acurate neo2 S, high implant, 22×40 mm predilation, 20×40 mm post‑dilation, commissural alignment) and led to durable improvement in gradients (mean gradient fell from an immediate post‑implant 67 mm Hg to 26–27 mm Hg at 1 month–1 year).

Why the problem is hard
- Each added transcatheter frame reduces the internal lumen and can fold or tether prior leaflets inward, producing a small effective orifice area (EOA) and prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM). In small residual lumens (~14 mm) these effects are magnified: even small changes in how the new valve seats and how much the prior frame expands materially change transvalvular flow.

Practical procedural strategies (what to do and why)
1) Preprocedural CT/3D modelling
   - Measure the minimum in‑valve lumen, valve‑to‑coronary distances, leaflet positions and the geometry of stacked frames. In this case MSCT (MLD 13.7 mm) determined feasibility and guided selection, implant depth and the decision that coronary protection was not required.

2) Choose a valve with supra‑annular leaflet position when EOA is the priority
   - Supra‑annular valves (leaflets open above the stent frame) typically deliver larger EOA in small annuli than intra‑annular designs. That advantage matters when the in‑valve lumen is already constrained. The team selected Acurate neo2 for this reason.

3) Prefer a valve with lower frame height and predictable commissural alignment
   - A relatively low frame reduces additional stacking height and the chance of covering coronary ostia; predictable commissural alignment allows the new commissures to sit between prior posts and coronary ostia, preserving coronary access and reducing neo‑skirt effects. The Acurate neo2 was chosen here for a combination of supra‑annular leaflets, low frame and alignment capability.

4) High implantation to “pin” prior leaflets outward
   - Deploying the new valve at or slightly above the level of the prior valve leaflets fixes those leaflets against the stent, preventing them from prolapsing inward and further reducing the EOA. The case explicitly used this strategy to minimise neo‑skirt formation.

5) Balloon pre‑dilation and high‑pressure post‑dilation (targeted frame expansion)
   - Pre‑dilation assesses compliance and creates room; high‑pressure post‑dilation can partially expand earlier self‑expanding frames (as happened in the report) and improve leaflet motion and central orifice area. The operators used 22×40 mm pre‑dilation and 20×40 mm post‑dilation; post‑dilation likely produced partial mechanical expansion of the Evolut frame and contributed to the improved gradients noted over weeks to months.
   - Caveats: balance benefit against risks (annular injury, coronary obstruction, conduction disturbances). The patient already had a pacemaker, which affects the risk calculus for additional conduction block.

6) Coronary protection / adjuncts when indicated
   - If CT suggests threatened coronaries, plan for coronary wiring, chimney stent strategy, or leaflet laceration (BASILICA/LAMPOON) to prevent obstruction. In this case CT showed adequate clearance and no prophylactic protection was used.

7) Avoid assuming you can “fracture” transcatheter frames
   - Bioprosthetic valve fracture is established for many stented surgical valves but is not a routine or validated technique for transcatheter frames. Rely instead on controlled dilation and valve selection.

How device features influence choice (summary)
- Supra‑annular leaflet position: maximises EOA in small lumens → preferred when residual lumen is small.
- Frame height/profile: lower frames reduce stacked height, limit neo‑skirt formation and preserve coronary access; high/tall frames increase risk of coronary obstruction and difficult re‑access.
- Commissural alignment capability: predictable orientation reduces overlap of struts with coronary ostia and makes future coronary access more likely; important in patients with coronary disease or when lifetime management is a concern.
- Radial force: balloon‑expandable valves have high radial force and can expand previous frames but are intra‑annular (less EOA) and may create larger skirts; choice requires weighing expansion benefit against loss of EOA and coronary risk.

Clinical expectation and counseling
- Even with optimal technique, some degree of residual PPM may be unavoidable with an in‑valve diameter ≈14 mm. Aim to minimise it (supra‑annular valve + high implant + aggressive but safe dilation) and plan long‑term follow‑up. In the reported patient this strategy produced substantial symptomatic and hemodynamic benefit with stable valve function at 1 year.

Teaching pearl
- In layered TAV‑in‑TAV scenarios the two most impactful choices for maximising EOA are (1) a supra‑annular valve that places the functional leaflets outside the tightest ring of prior frames and (2) a deployment strategy (high implant + targeted post‑dilation) that pins existing leaflets outward and expands earlier frames—coupled with commissural alignment to protect coronary access. The Moscow case provides a clear, real‑world example of these principles in action.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Immediately after a technically successful third TAVR with no aortic regurgitation on aortography, the mean gradient is 67 mm Hg. Provide a prioritized differential diagnosis for this finding and outline a stepwise evaluation (hemodynamics, echocardiography, fluoroscopy/CT) to distinguish high flow, measurement artifact, underexpansion/malapposition, leaflet immobility, and true severe prosthesis–patient mismatch, including initial management for each scenario.

Clinical pearl — what to think and what to do when a technically successful TAVR shows an unexpectedly high mean gradient immediately after implantation

Short answer (priority differential, in order)
1) Measurement/ Doppler artifact (including LVOT measurement error, beam misalignment, and pressure‑recovery effects)  
2) High transvalvular flow state (transient hyperdynamic output, tachycardia, inotrope effect, anemia, fever)  
3) Under‑expansion or malapposition of the new frame (or residual constrained prior frame limiting EOA)  
4) Leaflet immobility/entrapment (mechanical entrapment of new or old leaflets, acute thrombosis or pannus)  
5) True severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (fixed small effective orifice after final configuration)

Stepwise evaluation (practical, in the cath lab → echo → fluoroscopy/CT)
1. Immediate cath‑lab checks (do these first; fast and decisive)
   - Confirm aortography (you already did: no AR and coronary flow preserved). Aortography excludes gross regurgitation but is insensitive for stenosis.
   - Obtain simultaneous LV and aortic pressure tracings (true gold standard in the moment). Interpret carefully: if invasive mean LV–Ao gradient is low while Doppler mean is very high → measurement artifact or pressure recovery likely. If invasive gradient is concordantly high → mechanical obstruction.
   - Review transduction zeroing and pressure waveform morphology (damping, catheter whip or drift can create false readings).

2. Assess flow and global haemodynamics
   - Measure stroke volume / cardiac output (thermodilution or echo-derived stroke volume index). A high cardiac index/ high stroke volume can raise instantaneous velocities and gradients; conversely, low flow states can mask stenosis.
   - Check heart rate, rhythm, blood pressure, inotrope use, hemoglobin, temperature — treat reversible causes that increase cardiac output.

3. Focused echocardiography (TTE immediately, then TEE if discordant or unclear)
   - Reacquire continuous‑wave Doppler parallel to highest jet; compute mean gradient, peak velocity, DVI (VTI LVOT / VTI aortic jet). DVI is less sensitive to LVOT diameter measurement errors: DVI ≤0.25 indicates severe obstruction.
   - Re‑measure LVOT VTI and diameter carefully (use 3‑D or TEE if prosthetic frames distort 2‑D measurement). Small LVOT diameter underestimation will artifactually lower calculated AVA; conversely, wrong placement of the CW beam (sampling a high‑velocity eccentric jet) can overcall gradients.
   - Compare Doppler findings to invasive gradients. If Doppler >> invasive, suspect measurement artifact or pressure recovery.

4. Fluoroscopy
   - Cine review for stent shape: look for waist, noncircular expansion, frame recoil or visible leaflet excursion. Self‑expanding nitinol frames (and layered frames) can be constrained by prior frames and may show a waist.
   - If leaflets are radiopaque enough, assess opening excursion; restricted motion on fluoro supports mechanical obstruction.

5. Multislice CT (4D CT) when immediate anatomy clarification is necessary / feasible
   - Quantify minimal lumen diameter (MLD), frame expansion (eccentricity), and residual orifice. Look for hypo‑attenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) or thrombus if leaflet immobility suspected.
   - CT is especially useful when layered frames make echo measurements unreliable — the case’s preprocedural MSCT already showed an Evolut MLD of 13.7 mm (a substrate for high gradients).

How the steps distinguish the five diagnoses and immediate management for each

1) Measurement artifact (most common and immediately remediable)
   - Clues: Doppler mean >> invasive mean; DVI not consistent with severe stenosis; normal leaflet motion on TEE/fluoro; AVA changes after careful remeasurement of LVOT.
   - What to do: do not re‑balloon. Re‑acquire Doppler with optimal beam alignment, use TEE/3‑D echo or CT to measure LVOT diameter, compute EOAi, calculate energy‑loss index if pressure recovery suspected. If artifact confirmed — no further immediate intervention; document and repeat echo in 24–48 hours.

2) High‑flow state (transient, reversible)
   - Clues: high cardiac index / stroke volume, tachycardia, inotrope use, fever/anemia; Doppler gradients high but DVI may be preserved or only mildly reduced; invasive gradient may be proportionally high but resolves when flow normalizes.
   - What to do: treat underlying cause (control pain/fever, correct anemia, reduce inotropes, rate control for tachyarrhythmia). Recheck gradients after haemodynamics normalize. If patient is stable, observe — immediate invasive interventions usually unnecessary.

3) Underexpansion / malapposition of valve frame(s)
   - Clues: concordant high invasive and Doppler gradients; fluoroscopy shows waist or constrained frame; CT demonstrates small MLD (as in this patient’s pre‑TAVR MSCT and constrained Evolut frame). Supra‑annular implant design can mitigate but not always eliminate the problem.
   - What to do acutely: if invasive gradient is high and patient hemodynamically compromised or symptomatic, consider repeat balloon post‑dilatation (stepwise, with appropriate pacing and coronary protection if needed). In layered frames be prepared for incomplete expansion and for coronary obstruction risk — coronary protection (wires/balloon/snare) may be necessary. If post‑dilatation improves gradient, observe; if not feasible/unsafe, multidisciplinary discussion about further strategies (bioprosthetic fracture where appropriate, surgical explant — recognizing very high risk in a triple‑stented aortic root).

4) Leaflet immobility / entrapment / acute thrombus
   - Clues: restricted leaflet opening on TEE or fluoro; CT with HALT or thrombus; rapid clinical deterioration may indicate obstruction by thrombus or entrapped tissue; a history of subtherapeutic anticoagulation favors thrombus.
   - What to do: if thrombus suspected and patient stable, start systemic anticoagulation (IV heparin / start warfarin/DOAC per local protocol) and repeat imaging in 24–72 h. If massive obstruction with instability, urgent mechanical intervention (balloon valvuloplasty to free leaflets), surgical removal, or catheter thrombectomy may be required. If entrapment of prior leaflets is suspected, targeted balloon valvuloplasty or additional implantation strategy may be effective.

5) True severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (fixed small EOA)
   - Clues: persistent high invasive gradients after correction of measurement errors and after attempts at expansion; EOAi (indexed EOA) ≤0.65 cm²/m² generally defines severe PPM; DVI persistently low; imaging shows anatomic limitation (small residual orifice despite fully expanded frames).
   - What to do: options limited in the immediate setting. If the patient is unstable, consider redo strategy with surgical explantation (often high risk) or advanced transcatheter maneuvers (valve‑in‑valve fracture where possible, BASILICA or leaflet modification are not solutions for PPM). If patient stable, optimise medical therapy and plan multidisciplinary discussion for definitive management; some patients can have gradual haemodynamic improvement as frames expand or as LV dynamics normalize — short interval follow‑up is reasonable.

How this relates to the reported case
- The team implanted an Acurate neo2 S into an Evolut constrained frame with pre‑procedural MSCT MLD 13.7 mm — a setting at high risk for an elevated immediate gradient (anatomic constraint → small EOA). The immediate post‑procedure TTE showed mobile leaflets, no AR, but mean gradient 67 mm Hg, AVA 0.9 cm² and DVI 0.24 (indexes consistent with severe obstruction). Over the first month the mean gradient fell to 27 mm Hg and AVA increased to 1.1 cm².
- Interpretation: in this multi‑stent situation the most likely contributors were (a) mechanical constraint/underexpansion of prior frame(s) and partial improvement after balloon post‑dilation and progressive radial expansion of nitinol frames, plus (b) haemodynamic and measurement factors. The team’s CT‑guided strategy, high implantation and post‑dilatation likely produced progressive improvement without need for surgery — exactly what the stepwise strategy above would recommend: confirm, attempt safe post‑dilatation as indicated, and observe with early re‑imaging when patient haemodynamically stable.

Take‑home points
- Always confirm an unexpectedly high immediate gradient with simultaneous LV/Ao pressures before escalating therapy.  
- Use DVI and 3‑D/TEE or CT to resolve LVOT measurement and pressure‑recovery errors.  
- If invasive gradients are high and frame underexpansion is evident, carefully consider repeat balloon dilation (with coronary protection if indicated). If thrombus is suspected, anticoagulate promptly.  
- In complex layered valve anatomy allow for the possibility of progressive expansion and short‑interval haemodynamic improvement; if the patient is stable, a brief period of observation with repeat imaging (24–72 h and 1 month) is reasonable and may avoid unnecessary high‑risk reintervention.

### [Clinical Pearls] Define echocardiographic criteria for prosthetic aortic valve stenosis and for prosthesis–patient mismatch after TAVR (including thresholds for mean gradient, AVA/indexed AVA, and Doppler velocity index), and interpret the following values from this case at two time points: preprocedure mean gradient 49 mm Hg, AVA 0.5 cm², DVI 0.24; immediate postprocedure AVA 0.9 cm², indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m², DVI 0.24.

Clinical answer (clinical pearl)

Principles and thresholds
- The same Doppler parameters used for native aortic stenosis are applied to prosthetic valves, but interpretation must account for prosthesis geometry, flow dependence and measurement artefact. Use a combination of mean transvalvular gradient, continuity-equation AVA (and indexed AVA), Doppler velocity index (DVI), and imaging of leaflet motion/structure (TEE/fluoroscopy/CT).
- Common echocardiographic thresholds used in practice:
  - Severe prosthetic aortic stenosis: mean gradient ≥40 mm Hg (peak velocity ≥4.0 m/s), AVA ≤1.0 cm², and/or DVI ≤0.25. Moderate stenosis: mean gradient ~20–39 mm Hg, DVI ~0.25–0.35.
  - Prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) is defined by indexed effective orifice area (EOA or AVA indexed to BSA). Conventional cutoffs: severe PPM ≤0.65 cm²/m², moderate PPM 0.65–0.85 cm²/m² (adjust lower for obesity: severe ≤0.55 cm²/m², moderate 0.55–0.70 cm²/m²). Some TAVR literature uses a severe cutoff ≈0.6 cm²/m²; either way an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² is clearly in the severe range.
- Practical points: mean gradient is flow‑dependent; continuity-derived AVA relies on accurate LVOT measurement (often difficult with stented valves). DVI (LVOT VTI ÷ aortic VTI) avoids LVOT area and is particularly useful in prosthetic valves—DVI ≤0.25 is strongly suggestive of severe obstruction/very small orifice.

Interpretation in this case
- Preprocedure (presented values): mean gradient 49 mm Hg, AVA 0.5 cm², DVI 0.24.
  - Interpretation: concordant evidence of severe prosthetic valve stenosis. Mean gradient >40 mm Hg, AVA markedly reduced (0.5 cm²) and DVI at the severe threshold (≤0.25) all point to severe obstruction of the prior prosthesis.
- Immediate postprocedure (presented values): AVA 0.9 cm², indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m², DVI 0.24 (the report also notes an immediate mean gradient of 67 mm Hg).
  - Interpretation: although the non‑indexed AVA rose to 0.9 cm², the indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² places this unequivocally in the severe PPM range. The DVI remained ≤0.25 and the mean gradient was very high (67 mm Hg), consistent with severe residual obstruction from a small effective orifice (patient‑prosthesis mismatch and/or underexpansion of the new valve within layered frames). In other words: the implanted valve was functioning with a small effective orifice for this patient’s body size (severe PPM), producing high gradients immediately after deployment.

Why the apparent discrepancy (AVA up but gradients higher)?
- Continuity‑equation AVA can change with altered flow and is sensitive to LVOT measurement errors (and measurement variability immediately post‑procedure). DVI and gradients reflect the instantaneous hemodynamic burden and are less dependent on LVOT area. Here DVI stayed low and gradients remained very high, so the haemodynamic reality was persistent severe obstruction despite a higher calculated AVA.
- The case explains the clinical course: immediate high gradients were attributed to PPM/underexpansion in the triple‑stented anatomy; post‑dilatation and mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame (and settling of the new valve) led to improved gradients and AVA at 1 month (mean gradient 27 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm²).

Take‑away teaching points
- Always integrate mean gradient, AVA (and indexed AVA), and DVI when assessing prosthetic valves; in stented valves give particular weight to DVI and to comparison with prior baseline studies.
- Indexed EOA is the metric that defines PPM; an iAVA of 0.4 cm²/m² is severe PPM.
- Immediate post‑TAVR haemodynamics may overestimate obstruction; in valve‑in‑valve scenarios early high gradients may improve after post‑dilatation or as frames expand — but severe PPM identified by low iEOA predicts a substantial haemodynamic burden and should be anticipated at planning.

### [Management] For Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, what antibiotic regimens and durations are guideline-supported, how do you choose between ampicillin–gentamicin versus ampicillin–ceftriaxone combinations, and what is the role (if any) for suppressive oral therapy once cultures clear and imaging shows no ongoing infection prior to redo TAVR?

Short answer
- For Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE) the accepted, guideline‑supported strategy is 6 weeks of targeted intravenous therapy using an ampicillin‑based combination: either ampicillin + gentamicin or ampicillin + ceftriaxone. Choice is driven by organism characteristics (high‑level aminoglycoside resistance, HLAR) and the patient’s risk of aminoglycoside toxicity (age, renal function). Routine long‑term oral suppressive antibiotics are not recommended once blood cultures are cleared and imaging shows no active infection; suppression is reserved for the unusual situation in which infection cannot be eradicated or prosthetic material cannot be removed.

Why these regimens work (brief pathophysiology)
- Enterococci are relatively tolerant to beta‑lactams alone; bactericidal activity is usually achieved by combining a cell‑wall active agent (ampicillin) with either an aminoglycoside (gentamicin) that gains intracellular access after cell‑wall damage or a second beta‑lactam (ceftriaxone) that provides complementary penicillin‑binding‑protein coverage. The dual‑beta‑lactam (ampicillin+ceftriaxone) regimen achieves synergy without the nephrotoxicity of aminoglycosides.

What guidelines and outcome data support
- Large comparative series and guideline statements endorse two validated approaches for ampicillin‑susceptible E. faecalis IE:
  - Ampicillin (eg, 2 g IV q4h or equivalent) plus gentamicin (traditional dosing ~3 mg/kg/day in divided doses) with gentamicin given for the early part of therapy and ampicillin continued for 6 weeks for PVE.
  - Ampicillin plus ceftriaxone (eg, ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h) for 6 weeks. Multiple studies have shown similar cure/relapse rates for ampicillin+ceftriaxone versus ampicillin+gentamicin with substantially less nephrotoxicity in the dual‑beta‑lactam arm — a frequent reason to prefer it in older or renally impaired patients.

How to choose between ampicillin–gentamicin and ampicillin–ceftriaxone
- Use ampicillin–ceftriaxone when:
  - The isolate demonstrates HLAR to aminoglycosides (no aminoglycoside synergy).
  - The patient is elderly, has baseline renal insufficiency, or is at high risk for aminoglycoside toxicity.
  - You wish to avoid drug‑induced nephrotoxicity (common concern in PVE patients with comorbidities).
- Ampicillin–gentamicin remains an option when:
  - The isolate is ampicillin‑susceptible, aminoglycoside synergy is demonstrable (no HLAR), and the patient has good renal function and low toxicity risk.
  - If used, gentamicin is typically limited to the early phase (many centers give it for the first 2 weeks) with careful level and renal monitoring; ampicillin is continued to complete a full 6‑week course for PVE.
- Practical caveat: in modern practice many centers favour ampicillin+ceftriaxone for elderly/fragile patients because it preserves efficacy and avoids aminoglycoside harm.

Monitoring and adjunctive issues
- Always obtain HLAR testing (high‑level gentamicin resistance) — if present, aminoglycoside synergy is unreliable.
- Monitor renal function closely if gentamicin is used; check drug levels per institutional protocol.
- Rifampin is not routinely used for enterococcal PVE (its benefit is established for staphylococcal prosthetic infections but not for enterococci).
- For penicillin‑allergic patients, ID involvement for desensitization or alternative regimens (eg, vancomycin‑based) is required.

Role of suppressive oral therapy before redo TAVR
- If the patient has completed an appropriate 6‑week course, serial blood cultures are negative, and TEE/MSCT show no residual vegetations or perivalvular complications, continued chronic oral suppressive antibiotics are not indicated and are not standard practice prior to re‑implantation.
- Exceptions in which chronic suppressive therapy might be considered (after multidisciplinary discussion with ID and cardiac surgery) include:
  - Persistent bacteremia or relapse despite appropriate therapy.
  - Retained infected hardware that cannot be removed and where definitive cure is not achievable.
  - Recurrent infection or host factors that preclude clearing infection.
- Peri‑procedural planning: for redo valve procedures after treated PVE, multidisciplinary confirmation of completed therapy, repeated negative blood cultures, and imaging documentation of cure (TEE/MSCT) are the prerequisites for proceeding — as was done in the presented case. Routine long‑term suppressive antibiotics were not given in that case, and the patient remained free of recurrent infection at 1 year.

How this applies to the case report
- The patient had E. faecalis PVE treated with a 6‑week IV ampicillin‑based course followed by oral treatment per ID; at the time of the third TAVR antimicrobial therapy had been completed, repeat blood cultures and imaging (TEE and MSCT) showed resolution, and no long‑term suppressive therapy was continued. This approach reflects contemporary guideline practice: complete a guideline‑recommended course, confirm sterilization and absence of active infection, involve ID and the heart team, and proceed with reintervention without routine chronic oral suppression.

### [Management] What planning principles and implant techniques help preserve future coronary access in sequential TAVR (e.g., commissural alignment, high implantation to minimize the neo-skirt, leaflet-to-ostium relationship), and in what scenarios would you escalate to BASILICA, leaflet modification, or prophylactic coronary protection?

Short answer
Preserve future coronary access in sequential TAVR by rigorous CT-based planning (virtual valve implantation), choosing a valve and an implantation depth/orientation that minimize a “neo‑skirt” at the coronary level, and by actively pursuing commissural alignment. Escalate to leaflet‑laceration (BASILICA), leaflet modification or prophylactic coronary protection when CT modeling (and intra‑procedural tests) predict borderline or high risk of coronary obstruction, sinus sequestration, or when the patient has critical coronary disease that would be catastrophic to lose.

Why (the physiology and the problem)
In valve‑in‑valve and especially repeat TAV‑in‑TAV procedures the main mechanism of coronary compromise is displacement/pinning of the existing bioprosthetic leaflets and creation of a neo‑skirt or sealed neo‑sinus that either directly covers the coronary ostium or prevents coronary flow (sinus sequestration). Multiple concentric stent frames also reduce frame cell size and make later coronary re‑engagement difficult. Every additional valve amplifies these risks, so procedural strategy must anticipate leaflet-to‑ostium geometry and the final position of the neo‑skirt.

Planning and implant techniques that preserve coronary access (practical principles)
1) High‑quality CT with virtual valve implantation is mandatory.
   - Measure coronary ostial heights, sinus of Valsalva (SOV) diameters, STJ height, and valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance; perform 3D modeling to predict where leaflets and skirt will sit relative to the ostia. (The team in the reported case used MSCT and CT modelling to guide valve choice, implantation depth and to decide against routine coronary protection.)
2) Choose the valve to match the lifetime plan.
   - Prefer lower frame height / larger cell design and supra‑annular leaflets when coronary access and haemodynamics are a priority. The operators chose an Acurate neo2 for its supra‑annular leaflets and relatively low frame height to optimise orifice and access in a triple‑stented anatomy.
3) Commissural alignment.
   - Aim to orient the new commissural posts between coronary ostia so ostia fall in large frame cells, not behind posts or a leaflet skirt. Use pre‑CT mapping and delivery system markers/controlled rotation during deployment. The case explicitly used commissural alignment to preserve access.
4) High implantation to minimise the neo‑skirt.
   - Implant the new valve at a level that pins prior leaflets against the frame and keeps the bulk of any neo‑skirt above (or away from) the coronary ostia, reducing the chance of ostial coverage. The case used a high implant aligned with the Evolut leaflets for this reason.
5) Intra‑procedural testing and staged decisions.
   - Balloon pre‑dilatation with simultaneous aortography or selective coronary angiography can confirm coronary patency before permanent implantation (as was done in this patient). If flow remains clear you may proceed without pre‑wiring.
6) Post‑dilatation strategy.
   - Post‑dilatation can improve expansion and gradients (and in this case likely expanded the Evolut frame), but recognize it may also alter leaflet geometry — use only after confirming coronary patency or with protection in place if risk is present.
7) Document orientation and fluoroscopic landmarks for future coronary access.

When to escalate to BASILICA, leaflet modification, or prophylactic coronary protection
Use CT‑guided risk stratification. Common practical thresholds (from contemporary practice):
   - VTC distance < 4 mm, coronary height < ~10–12 mm, SOV diameter small relative to valve size (often < 30–32 mm), or predicted leaflet/neo‑skirt overlap with the ostium → high risk for coronary obstruction/sinus sequestration.
Escalation strategy:
1) BASILICA (intentional leaflet laceration)
   - Indication: CT modelling predicts that intact leaflet tissue (native or bioprosthetic) will obstruct coronary flow (high risk VTC <4 mm, leaflet tip projecting over ostium, sinus sequestration) and the leaflet can be safely crossed/lacerated. BASILICA is most attractive when laceration will create a flow channel and commissural alignment alone is insufficient.
   - Caveat: BASILICA in re‑interventions with multiple stent frames is technically more challenging and sometimes not feasible—this is acknowledged by the authors as uncertain in triple‑stented configurations.
2) Prophylactic coronary protection (pre‑wire ± parked balloon/stent; chimney/stent if needed)
   - Indication: borderline anatomy where BASILICA is not feasible, operator/centre lacks BASILICA experience, or patient has critical coronary disease (left main or proximal LAD lesion) such that temporary loss of that vessel is unacceptable. Pre‑wiring allows immediate stent deployment (chimney) if obstruction occurs.
3) Leaflet/frame modification (bioprosthetic valve fracture or other strategies)
   - Indication: primarily to treat severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch / high residual gradients (to increase effective orifice area), rather than to prevent coronary obstruction; consider when prior valve frame is amenable and fracture is safe. In multiple overlapping transcatheter frames, feasibility and safety are less certain.
4) Surgical or hybrid approach
   - Indicated when CT shows near‑certain sinus sequestration or coronary obstruction risk that cannot be mitigated percutaneously, or when anatomy and comorbidity make percutaneous rescue high risk.

A practical intra‑procedural algorithm (short)
   - Pre‑procedural CT + virtual implantation → classify risk (low / borderline / high).
   - Low risk: aim for commissural alignment + high implant + careful angiographic check (as in the case); proceed without routine pre‑wiring.
   - Borderline risk: plan pre‑wiring of target coronaries; consider readiness to perform chimney stent; consider BASILICA if anatomically feasible.
   - High risk (predictable obstruction / sinus sequestration): consider BASILICA if feasible and team experienced; if not feasible, discuss prophylactic chimney stent or surgical options.

Takeaway from the case
This patient illustrates the core principles: MSCT modelling guided valve choice (Acurate neo2), commissural alignment and a high implant were used deliberately to minimise the neo‑skirt and preserve coronary flow; balloon pre‑dilation + aortography confirmed patency and allowed the team to avoid prophylactic coronary protection. The result was durable at 1 year. However, the report also highlights that in other anatomies — particularly with low coronaries, small sinuses or predicted sinus sequestration — escalation to BASILICA or prophylactic protection remains essential and must be anticipated during lifetime TAV planning.

### [Morning Report] Given a EuroSCORE II of about 27% and two prior transcatheter frames integrated into the aortic wall after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, construct the heart-team argument for choosing a third TAVR rather than surgical explant and root replacement, citing key elements from contemporary ESC valvular and endocarditis guidelines that inform this decision.

Short answer
Given (1) proven resolution of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), (2) exceptionally high predicted surgical mortality (EuroSCORE II ≈ 27%), (3) two transcatheter frames deeply integrated into the aortic wall making explant + root replacement technically hazardous, and (4) MSCT-confirmed anatomic feasibility with strategies to mitigate coronary obstruction and patient–prosthesis mismatch, the heart team can reasonably choose a third transcatheter valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) rather than surgical explant and root replacement. This approach is fully consonant with contemporary ESC principles: surgery remains the treatment of choice for complicated or active PVE, but management must be individualized by a multidisciplinary heart team when infection is controlled and surgical risk or anatomy makes surgery prohibitive.

Key elements of the heart‑team argument (with case evidence and guideline rationale)

1) Infection status — key diagnostic standard
- ESC endocarditis guidance: active infection, paravalvular extension (abscess, dehiscence), uncontrolled sepsis or persistent bacteremia are indications for surgery; TAVI is contraindicated in active IE. By contrast, once infection is eradicated, reintervention decisions may follow the pathway for structural valve dysfunction and should be individualized.
- Case evidence: Enterococcus faecalis PVE was treated with a full course (6 weeks IV ampicillin-based therapy), subsequent blood cultures were negative, and both TEE and MSCT showed no vegetations or paravalvular complications before the reintervention. This satisfies the guideline requirement that infection be controlled before considering a transcatheter approach.

2) Operative risk and technical feasibility favor a percutaneous strategy
- ESC valvular guidance: heart‑team decision-making must weigh operative mortality, comorbidity burden and technical feasibility of surgery versus catheter therapy.
- Case evidence: EuroSCORE II ≈27% predicts very high operative mortality. The patient has age in the late 70s, COPD, diabetes, prior vascular complications and prior device/pacemaker history—factors that increase perioperative risk. The report explicitly notes that explantation of two embedded frames would likely require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement, a procedure with substantially higher morbidity and mortality than percutaneous reintervention. These facts make surgery prohibitive in this individual.

3) Imaging‑guided anatomic suitability and procedural planning (ESC recommendation)
- ESC VHD guidance emphasizes preprocedural MSCT to assess coronary ostia, sinus anatomy, valve‑to‑coronary distance and feasibility of valve‑in‑valve procedures and to plan coronary protection/commissural alignment.
- Case evidence: MSCT measured a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm, and detailed CT modelling showed acceptable valve‑to‑coronary relationships permitting high implantation and selection of a supra‑annular Acurate neo2 valve. The team planned commissural alignment and assessed coronary obstruction risk, permitting the procedure without prophylactic coronary protection.

4) Expected benefit vs. surgical risk and limited incremental gain from surgery
- Guidelines support individualized choice when surgery offers no clear survival or functional advantage but exposes the patient to excess risk.
- Case evidence: The third TAVR immediately eliminated regurgitation and by 1 month improved transvalvular gradients (and by 1 year the patient remained asymptomatic with mean gradient ~26 mm Hg and EF 65%). Given the good clinical and hemodynamic result and the high operative risk of explant + root replacement, the balance favors a percutaneous approach.

5) Heart‑team process, ID involvement and informed consent (guideline imperative)
- ESC guidance mandates multidisciplinary decision‑making (cardiology, cardiac surgery, imaging, infectious diseases, anesthesia) and shared decision‑making with the patient when deviating from standard surgical recommendations for PVE.
- Case evidence: This case was reviewed by a multidisciplinary heart team, included infectious disease input (no long‑term suppressive therapy required), and the patient/family participated in shared decision‑making. The team documented contingency planning (coronary protection strategy, high implantation, post‑dilation).

Practical teaching points / clinical pearl
- The ESC position is not “TAVI vs surgery” dogma; it is a framework: (a) rule out active infection and perivalvular complications, (b) quantify operative risk and surgical technical difficulty, (c) use MSCT to assess coronary risk and valve‑in‑valve feasibility, and (d) have the multidisciplinary heart team and patient agree on the strategy. In this patient the combination of resolved PVE, prohibitive EuroSCORE II (~27%), embedded stent frames necessitating root replacement if explanted, and CT‑confirmed feasibility of a carefully planned TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV produced a guideline‑consistent, patient‑centered justification for percutaneous reintervention.

Bottom line
When PVE is cured and imaging shows safe anatomy, but surgery carries prohibitive mortality and would require complex root explantation, contemporary ESC guidance supports individualized heart‑team selection of transcatheter reintervention. The described case exemplifies this pathway: infection control, CT‑guided planning, explicit risk–benefit appraisal, multidisciplinary approval, and informed consent led to a successful and guideline‑aligned choice of a third TAVR.

### [Management] In a TAVR recipient with prior remote PCI and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, no atrial fibrillation, and a permanent pacemaker, what postprocedural antithrombotic regimen would you select, and how would your plan change if new-onset atrial fibrillation or a recent (<3 months) coronary stent were present?

Recommendation (direct): For this patient — remote PCI (years earlier), permanent pacemaker, no atrial fibrillation, and a history of recurrent gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding — I would use single antiplatelet therapy (low‑dose aspirin 75–100 mg daily) alone after the TAVR, together with GI‑protective measures (proton‑pump inhibitor, urgent/definitive GI evaluation and treatment of bleeding sources). I would avoid routine dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin + clopidogrel) or empiric anticoagulation because his bleeding risk clearly outweighs incremental thrombotic benefit in the absence of another indication.

Why: evidence and case‑specific reasoning
- Randomized TAVR trials and guideline updates show that, in patients without an indication for oral anticoagulation and without a recent coronary stent, single antiplatelet therapy after TAVR reduces bleeding compared with short-term dual antiplatelet therapy without increasing ischemic events. In this case the patient’s PCIs were remote (2009–2014), so there is no contemporary stent-related indication for DAPT.  
- He has recurrent GI bleeding since 2022 — a dominant clinical problem that predicts major bleeding on DAPT or on anticoagulation. The pacemaker and prior infective endocarditis are not indications for chronic anticoagulation. Hence aspirin monotherapy + PPI and active GI management is the safest evidence‑based approach.

How my plan would change if new‑onset atrial fibrillation (AF) developed
- New AF with a CHA2DS2‑VASc in this patient (age ≥75, diabetes, vascular disease) would create a clear indication for oral anticoagulation. In that situation I would stop antiplatelet monotherapy and start oral anticoagulation alone (DOAC preferred over VKA in most patients) rather than combine OAC with antiplatelet therapy. Trials in the TAVR population have shown that OAC alone (vs OAC + single antiplatelet) reduces bleeding without excess thrombotic events.  
- Because this patient has a history of recurrent GI bleeding, I would preferentially choose a DOAC with comparatively lower GI‑bleeding signal (for many clinicians apixaban is preferred unless dose‑reduction criteria or renal function dictate otherwise), ensure PPI therapy and urgent gastroenterology reassessment. If anticoagulation is absolutely contraindicated because of recurrent, refractory GI bleeding, then percutaneous left atrial appendage occlusion (after multidisciplinary review) is a reasonable alternative for stroke prevention.

How my plan would change if a recent (<3 months) coronary stent were present
- A recent stent changes priorities because early stent thrombosis is catastrophic. If the patient has a new-generation DES placed in the prior 1–3 months and no AF, guideline‑directed dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin + clopidogrel) is indicated for the minimum recommended duration for that clinical context, tempered by bleeding risk: for stable PCI this may be 3–6 months but can be shortened to 1 month in high‑bleeding‑risk patients after multidisciplinary discussion. Use clopidogrel as the P2Y12 agent (avoid prasugrel/ticagrelor when possible if bleeding risk high) and maintain PPI prophylaxis. Reassess aggressively; when the stent‑mandated DAPT period ends, convert to aspirin monotherapy.  
- If both recent stent and new AF are present, avoid prolonged triple therapy. The contemporary strategy is short‑term combination therapy (DOAC + single antiplatelet — usually clopidogrel — for the briefest necessary period, e.g., ~1 month) followed by DOAC alone. This minimizes bleeding while protecting the stent during the highest risk window.

Practical points for this patient
- Start aspirin once procedural hemostasis is secure; add a PPI and coordinate with GI to identify and treat bleeding source(s) and optimize hemoglobin.  
- If AF arises, favor DOAC monotherapy (standard dosing unless dose reduction criteria met) and stop aspirin. If DOAC not possible, discuss LAA occlusion.  
- If a recent PCI exists, coordinate timing/duration of DAPT with the interventional cardiologist and accept a tailored, shortened DAPT strategy when bleeding risk is high.  
- Reassess antithrombotic strategy frequently (1–3 months) and document shared decision‑making with the heart team and gastroenterology.

Bottom line: In the presented patient the balance of risks favors aspirin monotherapy plus GI protection and active evaluation of bleeding sources. New AF converts the indication to anticoagulation (OAC alone, DOAC preferred); a recent stent mandates prioritizing stent protection with short, guideline‑directed DAPT (or DOAC+clopidogrel if AF is also present) for the minimum necessary duration.
