Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 2: ## Case chronology, presentation, and diagnostic synthesis

This patient’s course is best understood as a stepwise progression from an initial transcatheter treatment for native aortic stenosis to recurrent prosthetic dysfunction complicated by an episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), followed by further structural deterioration that ultimately prompted consideration of a repeat transcatheter intervention. Key dates and turning points were: implantation of a 27 mm Lotus transcatheter valve in 2014 for severe native aortic stenosis; symptomatic prosthetic degeneration with a very high mean gradient (≈83 mm Hg) and a fall in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) to 42% by early 2022; a TAV‑in‑TAV procedure with a 23 mm Evolut valve in May 2022 (complicated by a femoral pseudoaneurysm); diagnosis of Enterococcus faecalis PVE three months later (sept 2022) treated with a complete course of targeted intravenous antibiotics and subsequent oral therapy; and recurrent symptomatic prosthetic dysfunction documented during 2023–2024 with severe stenosis and mixed regurgitation that led to re‑evaluation and planning for a third transcatheter valve in mid‑2024.

Several baseline features shaped the risk–benefit calculus throughout this timeline. The patient had advanced age, poorly tolerated cardiovascular disease (three‑vessel coronary artery disease with multiple prior percutaneous coronary interventions), third‑degree atrioventricular block treated with a pacemaker, type 2 diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022. Each of these comorbid conditions increases perioperative risk; together they contributed to a very high calculated surgical risk (EuroSCORE II 27.36%) and argued against conventional surgical explant and root replacement when the prosthetic valves later failed.

The interval event of PVE was a critical diagnostic and management pivot. Three months after the 2022 TAV‑in‑TAV the patient presented with fever and worsening heart failure; transthoracic echo showed severe restenosis and blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis. He completed an appropriate course of targeted intravenous therapy (6 weeks) and subsequent oral management under infectious disease supervision. For the purposes of planning a further transcatheter intervention, the team required and obtained objective evidence that infection had been eradicated: repeated negative blood cultures, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) without vegetations or paravalvular complications, and no clinical or laboratory signs of ongoing sepsis. Establishing absence of active infection is essential because placing a new prosthesis into an infected field is associated with high risk of recurrent infection and poor outcomes.

The clinical presentation that precipitated re‑evaluation in 2024 was progressive exertional dyspnea with limited exercise tolerance (walking 200–300 m), intermittent left‑sided chest spasm on exertion, dizziness and fatigue. These symptoms reflect a reduction in forward stroke volume and increasing transvalvular obstruction and/or regurgitation from the failing prosthesis; angina‑type chest discomfort in this context can represent demand ischemia from low cardiac output in a patient with known coronary disease.

Echocardiography documented the haemodynamic severity at several stages and guided urgency. Before the 2022 redo the mean transvalvular gradient had risen to ≈83 mm Hg with an LVEF fall to 42%, and LVEF improved to ~54% after the May 2022 TAV‑in‑TAV—demonstrating the expected recovery in ventricular function when outflow obstruction is relieved. By January–June 2024 progressive prosthetic dysfunction was documented: mean gradient ≈49 mm Hg with moderate–severe intraprosthetic regurgitation and a calculated aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm² (the AVA here was derived by the continuity equation using LVOT dimensions and Doppler velocity‑time integrals). These measurements indicate severe prosthetic obstruction despite preserved or recovered LVEF. After the third implantation, immediate post‑procedure Doppler metrics showed a Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24 and a small calculated AVA (0.9 cm²) with a very high mean gradient (67 mm Hg) consistent with severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM) at that time point; the DVI is the ratio of LVOT to transvalvular velocity indices and values ≲0.25 indicate severe obstruction in prosthetic valves.

Multimodality imaging—particularly transesophageal echocardiography and multislice CT (MSCT)—was used both to exclude active endocarditis and to characterise the anatomic constraints that determined feasibility and strategy for further intervention. TEE confirmed the absence of vegetations or paravalvular abscess before proceeding. MSCT provided critical measurements for procedural planning: it documented two overlapping transcatheter frames and measured a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut stent, a narrow residual flow channel that has important implications for expected effective orifice area after any additional valve, for the risk of coronary ostial obstruction, and for the potential need to position a new valve high to avoid creating a neo‑skirt that would impinge on coronary flow. CT assessment of sinus of Valsalva size, coronary ostial heights and valve‑to‑coronary distances informed the heart team’s judgement that coronary obstruction risk could be managed without routine prophylactic coronary protection in this specific anatomy.

Bringing these data together supported a clear clinical problem statement: the patient had severe prosthetic valve dysfunction manifested as combined stenosis and regurgitation in a double‑stented (TAV‑in‑TAV) aortic valve, occurring after a treated episode of Enterococcus faecalis PVE, in the setting of prohibitive surgical risk and anatomical constraints that complicated open explantation. Multimodality imaging confirmed both the absence of active infection and the anatomic feasibility (albeit with elevated risks of residual PPM and potential coronary access difficulties) of a strategy involving a further transcatheter valve‑in‑valve intervention rather than surgical replacement.
