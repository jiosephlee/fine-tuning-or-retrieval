Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 3: ## CT-guided planning for a third TAVI: goals, measurements, and strategy

A third transcatheter valve inside two existing stented prostheses presents competing priorities: restore adequate valve area and low gradients, avoid coronary obstruction now and preserve future coronary access, and minimise procedural risk (embolisation, paravalvular leak, or acute haemodynamic collapse). In this patient, preprocedural multislice CT (MSCT) became the central tool to reconcile those goals by defining the residual intraluminal geometry inside the prior devices and by predicting how a new frame and skirt would interact with the aortic root and coronary ostia. The planning process therefore focused on five practical objectives—preserve coronary flow and future coronary access; maximise effective orifice area (EOA) to reduce patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM); define an implantation depth and rotational orientation (commissural alignment) that limit interference with prior leaflets; assess and plan for radial expandability of the existing frames; and prepare bailout strategies should CT identify high obstruction risk.

MSCT measurements and interpretation
For TAV-in-TAV planning MSCT is performed as an ECG‑gated contrast study with centerline reconstructions and multiplanar reformatting. Key measurements are made relative to the native annular plane and along the axis of the prior valve frames: coronary ostial heights (measured perpendicular to the annular plane); dimensions of the sinuses of Valsalva and the sinotubular junction; and internal diameters of the lumen through the previously implanted stents at multiple levels (landing zone, valve leaflet level and supra-annular levels). In reinterventions it is also essential to measure valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance—that is, the distance between the anticipated final skirt (neo‑skirt) of the new transcatheter valve and each coronary ostium—and to estimate the vertical height of the neo‑skirt relative to the coronary ostia and sinus floor.

In this case MSCT documented a markedly reduced internal lumen within the Evolut frame (minimum lumen diameter 13.7 mm). That single figure carried several implications: the residual flow area was small and therefore risk of PPM and high gradients after another valve was high unless a device with favorable supra‑annular EOA was chosen; the limited internal diameter constrained valve size selection; and the small lumen increased the importance of predicting where the new skirt would sit relative to the coronaries. The team therefore used the CT to simulate a virtual implant and to confirm that, with a high implant strategy, the neo‑skirt would remain below the coronary ostia and leave adequate VTC distances.

Frame interaction mapping and implantation depth
When prior transcatheter frames are present, MSCT is used to map the position of prior commissures and leaflet nadirs relative to the coronary ostia. Radiopaque frame elements and leaflet attachment points are identified, and a virtual valve is positioned within the existing frames to predict leaflet displacement and skirt location. Two mechanistic problems commonly drive strategy in TAV‑in‑TAV: (1) inward folding of degenerated prior leaflets into the orifice (which narrows EOA), and (2) creation of a neo‑skirt (new valve fabric plus pinned leaflets) that can obstruct a coronary ostium or isolate the sinus (sinus sequestration). To avoid both, the team planned a relatively high implant—positioning the new prosthesis so its frame would pin the prior Evolut leaflets outward against the Evolut stent rather than allow them to prolapse inward. The CT-based overlay allowed selection of a target fluoroscopic projection and a precise depth relative to the Evolut leaflet nadir so that the neo‑skirt would fall below the coronaries while still giving adequate anchoring and sealing.

Valve selection and sizing informed by CT
Because the effective inner diameter inside the Evolut was small, the group selected a valve designed to maximise EOA in narrow geometries: a self‑expanding, supra‑annular Acurate neo2 in size S. CT measurements guide sizing in redo procedures by matching the device to the smallest luminal diameter and by anticipating how the new valve will expand within the layered frames, not simply the original annulus diameter. A supra‑annular leaflet design increases the functional orifice for a given inner stent diameter. In addition, the relatively low height of the Acurate neo2 frame and its constructive features facilitating commissural alignment reduced the expected mesh burden above the sinuses and improved the chances of preserving coronary access—features that MSCT overlay modelling indicated were favourable in this patient.

Coronary protection: decision framework and bailouts
MSCT provides the anatomical thresholds commonly used to decide whether to preemptively protect coronaries: low coronary height, small sinus diameters, or valve‑to‑coronary distances that predict the neo‑skirt will impinge on an ostium. Although numeric thresholds vary in the literature, operators typically regard a VTC of only a few millimetres, coronary heights in the low teens (mm) or very narrow sinuses as high‑risk features. In this patient, CT modelling showed adequate clearance of the neo‑skirt from the coronaries with the proposed high implant, and therefore the team elected not to perform routine prophylactic coronary wiring or chimney stenting. CT did, however, guide preparation for bailouts: coronary guidewires and stents were available on standby, and the team had contingency plans (chimney/stent snorkel or emergency coronary intervention). Importantly, the team used balloon pre‑dilation with simultaneous aortography as a real‑time check of coronary flow—a practical step when CT suggests borderline clearance—confirming on table that coronary perfusion remained intact before final valve deployment. Techniques such as BASILICA (leaflet laceration) are additional pre‑emptive options when CT predicts a high likelihood of obstruction, but their feasibility is limited in multi‑stented anatomies and was not required here.

PPM mitigation and intra‑procedural testing of expandability
Mitigating PPM in serial TAV procedures revolves around maximising the effective orifice within the constrained lumen. CT directed several complementary measures: selection of a supra‑annular valve to increase EOA, planning commissural alignment to favour central flow, and a strategy of measured pre‑ and post‑dilation to assess and improve expandability of the existing Evolut frame. Balloon pre‑dilation with a large balloon not only fractures or repositions obstructing leaflet tissue but also serves as a functional test—if the balloon waist is severe and persistent it signals limited radial expandability and higher residual gradients. In this patient the team performed a 22/40 mm balloon predilation under rapid pacing and observed preserved coronary flow on aortography; after valve implantation they performed post‑dilation with a 20/40 mm balloon to further expand the construct. The operators deliberately avoided intentional frame fracture (a manoeuvre used for some surgical bioprostheses) because self‑expanding transcatheter frames behave differently; instead they relied on high‑pressure ballooning to achieve incremental expansion. This combination of device choice, alignment and balloon techniques is what likely explained the clinical course: an initially high immediate post‑implant gradient (reflecting residual stiffness and incomplete early expansion) followed by progressive reduction in gradient and improved EOA over the first month as the frames and leaflets accommodated.

How imaging translated into the executed plan
In summary, MSCT supplied the critical answers: the minimal residual lumen (13.7 mm) narrowed device choices, virtual overlays showed that a high implant would keep the neo‑skirt clear of the coronaries, and commissural mapping enabled an orientation strategy to preserve future coronary access. These imaging‑derived decisions led to selection of the Acurate neo2 S, a high implantation technique to pin prior leaflets, a stepwise dilation strategy (predilation to test coronary patency and expandability, then post‑dilation for optimisation), and readiness with bailout tools despite choosing not to deploy prophylactic coronary protection. The result—a technically successful deployment with immediate elimination of regurgitation and progressive haemodynamic improvement over the following weeks—illustrates how targeted CT planning can balance haemodynamics, coronary safety and lifetime access in even the most complex, triple‑stented anatomies.

Clinical takeaways
For learners, the practical lesson is that MSCT in repeat TAVI is not a perfunctory annular measurement but a three‑dimensional engineering study: it defines the available internal lumen, predicts the neo‑skirt and leaflet behaviour, and permits virtual positioning of a candidate device so operators can choose implantation depth, rotational orientation and adjunctive ballooning strategies that optimise both short‑term haemodynamics and long‑term coronary access. In redo and trifecta procedures, careful CT‑guided planning paired with staged on‑table testing (balloon predilation with aortography) and prepared bailouts is the safest way to navigate the competing demands of EOA restoration and coronary protection.
