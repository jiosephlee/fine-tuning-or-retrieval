Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 6: ## Clinical lessons, decision frameworks, and future directions

This case of a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) illustrates several practical lessons for trainees and heart teams who must balance infection control, complex anatomy, coronary safety and the inevitability of structural valve deterioration when planning repeat transcatheter interventions. The patient’s course—Lotus (2014) → Evolut TAV‑in‑TAV (2022) → Enterococcus faecalis PVE treated with antibiotics → Acurate neo2 TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV (2024) with good 1‑year outcome—serves as a running example for how rigorous documentation, CT‑led planning and strategy-driven implantation can make a high‑risk reintervention feasible.

Heart‑team checklist for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after PVE
Before contemplating a repeat transcatheter valve in a patient with prior PVE the multidisciplinary team must confirm eradication of infection and document the absence of anatomic complications. Practical checkpoints that were followed in this case include completed and appropriate antibiotic therapy, persistently negative blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography showing no vegetations or paravalvular abscess, and multislice CT (MSCT) excluding paravalvular complications. Equally important is a thorough hemodynamic assessment by TTE (gradients, continuity‑equation AVA, indexed AVA and Doppler velocity index), and objective surgical risk quantification (eg, EuroSCORE II) combined with clinical judgement about frailty and comorbidities. Device selection, the possibility of off‑label use and its institutional approval should be discussed openly with the patient and family; documented shared decision‑making and formal informed consent are essential, as was done in this case for the Acurate neo2 implantation.

Choosing transcatheter versus surgical redo
Surgical explantation and root/ascending aorta reconstruction remain the standard when PVE is active, when there is root destruction, or when durable debridement is required. However, prior TAVI frames can become deeply integrated into the aortic wall and sinotubular junction, making explantation technically complex and high risk. In such circumstances—especially when the patient has prohibitive comorbidity burden, as here (EuroSCORE II >20%, COPD, recurrent GI bleeding, multiple prior coronary interventions, pacemaker)—a CT‑guided TAV‑in‑TAV strategy is frequently the only realistic option. The decision must weigh the risks of leaving multiple frames in place (eg, coronary access issues, PPM) against the immediate procedural risk of surgery, and it should be individualized by the heart team.

Lifetime management beginning at the index TAVI
This case reinforces that the first TAVI should be performed with a “lifetime” mindset. When possible, index implantation should aim for commissural alignment and documented valve orientation so future reinterventions can better preserve coronary access. Device choice at the index procedure (supra‑annular vs intra‑annular, frame height, cell design) influences the ease of future valve‑in‑valve work and the risk of PPM. Procedural notes and fluoroscopic landmarks should be recorded and available for later CT synthesis. In patients likely to outlive a first bioprosthesis (younger or lower surgical risk), these considerations should be discussed pre‑procedurally.

Coronary safety strategies in serial TAVR
The greatest immediate catastrophe in a redo TAVR is coronary obstruction. Meticulous MSCT assessment of coronary ostial height, sinus dimensions, valve‑to‑coronary distance and the predicted neo‑skirt is mandatory; in this case a minimum lumen of 13.7 mm inside the Evolut frame and CT modelling indicated acceptable coronary clearance and supported the decision to proceed without routine coronary protection. Procedural techniques that mitigate coronary risk include high implantation of the new valve (positioning the skirt above prior leaflet tissue to avoid creating a neo‑skirt that covers the ostia), careful commissural alignment to avoid leaflet commissures lying in front of the coronaries, and selection of valve types with favorable open cells or low frame height. When MSCT suggests borderline risk, prophylactic coronary protection (wiring the target coronary with a balloon/stent ready to deploy) or leaflet modification techniques such as BASILICA can be considered; however, BASILICA and chimney stenting become technically more complex and less well validated in the setting of multiple overlapping frames, and their feasibility must be evaluated on a case‑by‑case basis.

Prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM): prediction, intra‑procedural mitigation and interpretation
PPM is a frequent and clinically important concern in redo TAVR because the inner diameter available for flow is reduced by prior frames and leaflets. Preprocedural MSCT measurement of the effective inner lumen (13.7 mm in this patient) should feed directly into valve selection: supra‑annular devices typically provide larger effective orifice areas and were chosen here (Acurate neo2) to maximise hemodynamics. Where feasible, bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) can enlarge rigid surgical rings and some transcatheter frames to reduce PPM, but its utility is limited or uncertain in stacked transcatheter frames and must be planned carefully. Intra‑procedurally, aggressive but controlled pre‑dilation and high‑pressure post‑dilation can help expand prior frames; this case used post‑dilation and noted partial mechanical expansion of the Evolut frame that likely accounted for the marked haemodynamic improvement seen between immediate and 1‑month TTE. Immediate post‑implant metrics require nuanced interpretation: the patient’s immediate mean gradient was high (67 mm Hg) with an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² and DVI 0.24—figures consistent with severe mismatch early after deployment—yet these values improved substantially by 1 month (mean gradient 27 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm²). Doppler velocity index is less flow‑dependent than absolute gradient and is useful in prosthetic valves; very low values (as in this case) should prompt reassessment and tailored optimisation rather than reflex surgical referral.

Evidence gaps and research priorities
Serial TAVR and especially TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV procedures are becoming more common but remain under‑studied. Key research needs include large registries and prospective studies of long‑term clinical and hemodynamic outcomes after triple‑stented constructs, validation of advanced 3D computational modelling to predict frame expansion and neo‑skirt formation, and evidence‑based recommendations for managing PVE risk and antibiotic strategies after serial TAVI. Comparative data on BVF, BASILICA and other adjunctive techniques in multilayered frames are particularly sparse; trainees should recognise the value of contributing cases to registries and publications to build this evidence base.

Practical takeaways
For trainees involved in complex redo TAVR after treated PVE: always confirm microbiological and imaging evidence of infection resolution before reintervention; involve a multidisciplinary heart team early; obtain high‑quality MSCT for valve‑to‑coronary and inner‑lumen planning; anticipate and quantify the risk of PPM and select devices and implantation strategies accordingly; plan coronary protection when anatomy is borderline; document shared decision‑making and any off‑label device use; and arrange close early and mid‑term echocardiographic follow‑up, recognising that early haemodynamics can improve after optimisation. This case demonstrates that, when these elements are addressed, a carefully planned third transcatheter valve can produce sustained symptomatic and haemodynamic benefit in a patient who would otherwise face prohibitively high surgical risk.
