Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 5: ## Outcomes, haemodynamic interpretation, and longitudinal surveillance

On procedural completion the case met the key VARC‑3 procedural endpoints for device deployment: the Acurate neo2 was correctly positioned within the prior valves, there was no residual paravalvular or transvalvular regurgitation on angiography or immediate imaging, and there were no acute access or vascular catastrophes. Hemodynamically, however, the immediate transthoracic Doppler study showed a very high mean transvalvular gradient (67 mm Hg), an indexed aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.4 cm²/m² (absolute AVA 0.9 cm²) and a Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24 — findings consistent with severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) or prosthetic obstruction despite the otherwise successful implant.

Interpreting those early abnormal Doppler numbers requires attention to how prosthetic gradients are generated and measured. The continuity equation (AVA = LVOT area × LVOT VTI / aortic VTI) and DVI (ratio of LVOT to aortic valve velocity or VTI) are the standard Doppler tools for prosthetic stenosis assessment; in valve‑in‑valve situations these measurements can be more technically challenging because LVOT sizing, Doppler alignment and the presence of multiple frames alter flow geometry. Practically, DVI is less dependent on an accurate LVOT area and is therefore useful when continuity‑equation inputs are uncertain. Commonly used cutoffs are: DVI ≤0.25 and mean gradient ≥40 mm Hg (or AVA ≤1.0 cm², indexed EOA ≤0.65–0.85 cm²/m² depending on reference) to define severe prosthetic obstruction or severe PPM; by these standards the immediate post‑implant study in this patient indicated severe functional obstruction.

Why did the Doppler gradient fall from 67 mm Hg immediately to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and remain ≈26 mm Hg at 1 year? The clinical team’s explanation — and the pathophysiology underlying it — is instructive. Immediately after deployment a new leaflet assembly can be mechanically constrained by the pre‑existing frames, producing “leaflet pinning” and a reduced effective orifice area (EOA). Even when an appropriately sized valve is delivered, nested stents and remnants of prior leaflets can limit full leaflet excursion and produce very high instantaneous velocities across a small orifice. The team performed high‑pressure post‑dilatation at the index procedure; that, together with the force of the new device and ongoing hemodynamic cycles, likely produced partial mechanical expansion of the underlying Evolut frame over the following hours to weeks. As the composite stent geometry improved (better radial expansion and commissural alignment), leaflet mobility and central flow improved, producing a larger functional EOA. From fluid dynamics, for a given forward stroke volume an increase in EOA produces a disproportionate fall in transvalvular velocity and therefore a fall in the velocity‑derived gradient (gradient ∝ velocity²). In short, modest increases in EOA can more than offset concomitant increases in stroke volume (the patient’s EF and forward flow also recovered after the relief of obstruction), yielding the net decrease in measured gradient observed between the immediate and follow‑up studies. Other contributors that can transiently elevate early gradients — measurement artifacts, tachycardia, low systemic pressure or leaflet edema/thickening — were considered and were not the dominant explanation here because the improvement persisted and correlated with clinical recovery.

The serial echocardiographic course mirrors this pathophysiology and supports a durable midterm result. At 1 month the mean gradient was 27 mm Hg with AVA 1.1 cm² and no regurgitation; the patient’s symptoms abated and left ventricular volumes remodelled favourably. At 3.5 months he remained asymptomatic and haemodynamics stable, and at 1 year the mean gradient was 26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm² and EF 65% with no recurrence of endocarditis. These findings represent sustained functional benefit despite a triple‑stented anatomy: the residual gradients place this valve functionally in the moderate stenosis / limited EOA range rather than severe obstruction, and the clinical improvement and preserved EF provide the most important corroboration that the haemodynamic result is acceptable at 1 year.

Given the uncertainty of durability in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV construct and the history of prior prosthetic endocarditis, a structured surveillance plan is essential. Reasonable elements of that plan — and the triggers for escalation used in this case — are:

- Early imaging: baseline TTE before discharge or within 48–72 hours, and a formal TTE at ~30 days to document early haemodynamic trajectory and detect complications (thrombosis, residual obstruction, regurgitation).  
- Short‑term follow‑up: repeat TTE at 3 months and 6 months to confirm stability during the period of ongoing leaflet and frame remodelling. The centre obtained and documented imaging at 1 month and 3.5 months with a 1‑year study showing durable results.  
- Long‑term schedule: annual clinical review and TTE thereafter if stable. More frequent imaging is warranted for new/worsening symptoms, a sustained rise in mean gradient (for example an increase >10 mm Hg from prior study), a decline in AVA, or new regurgitation.  
- When to use advanced imaging: gated multislice CT should be obtained if there is unexplained gradient increase, suspected hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT)/thrombosis, concern for underexpansion or for preprocedural planning of any future coronary or transcatheter intervention; TEE and blood cultures should be obtained promptly for any febrile illness or concern for recurrent PVE. The team’s pre‑procedure CT images in this case were indispensable for planning and should be retained for future coronary or reintervention planning.  
- Coronary access planning: because overlapping frames can impede future coronary cannulation, documentation of commissural alignment, fluoroscopic implant landmarks and CT reconstructions should be stored; any future need for PCI should be planned with CT guidance and heart‑team discussion.  
- Reintervention thresholds and decision process: symptomatic severe prosthetic stenosis or severe regurgitation, haemodynamic compromise, or imaging evidence of structural failure should prompt multidisciplinary reassessment. In triple‑stented anatomy the balance of surgical risk, feasibility of another transcatheter approach and patient goals of care must be individualized.

Finally, vigilance for recurrence of prosthetic valve endocarditis is warranted given the prior E. faecalis infection. Any unexplained fever, constitutional symptoms or new murmurs should prompt immediate blood cultures and echocardiography and rapid infectious‑disease and heart‑team input. In this patient the combination of imaging confirmation of infection resolution before the third TAVI, a stable clinical course and durable echocardiographic performance at 1 year provides a reassuring early durability signal — but long‑term uncertainty remains, and follow‑up must be proactive and multidisciplinary.
