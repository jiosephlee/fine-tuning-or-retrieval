Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 1: ## Focused background: TAVI durability, redo-TAVI, and post-endocarditis considerations

As transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has moved from high‑risk patients into lower‑risk and younger populations, clinicians increasingly face the downstream problem of structural valve deterioration (SVD) and the need for repeat interventions. The timeline in this case—initial Lotus implantation in 2014, a TAV‑in‑TAV with an Evolut in 2022, prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) later in 2022, and a third transcatheter implant in 2024—illustrates two common drivers of reintervention: time‑dependent bioprosthetic degeneration and accelerated dysfunction after infection. Understanding the mechanisms of SVD, the physiologic consequences of serial valve layering, and the specific implications of prior endocarditis is essential to judge the indication, plan imaging, and choose a device and implantation strategy.

Mechanisms and timing of transcatheter SVD are multifactorial. Classic degenerative processes—leaflet fibrosis, calcification and mechanical wear—tend to accrue over years and are the dominant cause of mid‑ to late‑term failure. Superimposed processes can accelerate dysfunction: thrombosis can cause early leaflet immobilization, and PVE can produce leaflet destruction, scarring or fusion that produces either stenosis, regurgitation or a mixed lesion. Frame factors also matter: underexpansion of a stent frame at initial implantation or subsequent compression by an inner valve concentrates stress on leaflets and can promote earlier failure. In this patient the first valve functioned for several years (consistent with typical mid‑term durability), while the episode of Enterococcus faecalis PVE after the 2022 TAV‑in‑TAV likely contributed to the more rapid deterioration that prompted the 2024 reintervention.

Prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) is a central concept when considering redo‑TAVI. PPM exists when the effective orifice area (EOA) of a prosthetic valve is too small for the patient’s body size; it is usually quantified as indexed EOA (iEOA = EOA / body surface area). Common thresholds are: iEOA ≤0.85 cm²/m² denotes at least moderate PPM and iEOA ≤0.65 cm²/m² severe PPM (with some adjustment for obesity). The Doppler velocity index (DVI)—the ratio of LVOT to transvalvular velocity—is another practical measure; a DVI ≤0.25 is consistent with severe obstruction. Clinically, PPM is important because it is associated with persistently elevated gradients, less symptomatic improvement, adverse left ventricular remodeling and worse long‑term outcomes. Serial TAVI amplifies the risk of PPM because each added valve and its leaflets occupy lumen, reducing the inner diameter and EOA; the result can be very high transvalvular gradients unless the chosen device or post‑dilatation strategy can restore acceptable orifice area. This case highlights that immediate post‑deployment gradients may be very high (mean gradient 67 mm Hg, DVI 0.24, iEOA 0.4 cm²/m²), and that careful post‑dilatation and time for frame expansion can substantially improve haemodynamics (mean gradient falling to 27 mm Hg at 1 month).

Repeat transcatheter procedures create unique hazards. Coronary obstruction is a leading immediate threat in TAV‑in‑TAV scenarios: implantation of an inner valve can displace or splay the leaflets of the outer valve outward, forming a “neo‑skirt” that can cover coronary ostia. The risk increases when sinuses are small, coronary heights are low, or valve‑to‑coronary distances are reduced by multiple frames. Reduced EOA and severe PPM are physiologic consequences of frame stacking and leaflet confinement. Overlapping stent frames also impede future coronary access because frame struts and commissural misalignment can block catheter passage or make selective cannulation difficult. Some bail‑out or preventive techniques (coronary protection with guidewires/stents, BASILICA leaflet laceration, or bioprosthetic valve fracture) may be limited or technically more hazardous in a triple‑stented anatomy, so preprocedural planning must explicitly evaluate these scenarios.

Guideline‑driven considerations are particularly important after PVE. The 2025 ESC guidance reiterates that active, complicated PVE is usually managed surgically, and that any decision to pursue transcatheter treatment in a patient with prior PVE requires careful individualisation: infection must be controlled, blood cultures should be negative, and imaging (transoesophageal echocardiography and CT) must exclude active vegetation, abscess or paravalvular disruption. A heart‑team discussion weighing surgical risk, anatomical feasibility and the durability and risks of a transcatheter redo is mandatory. In the reported case these prerequisites were met (completed antibiotic therapy, negative cultures and imaging without active infection), and the patient’s prohibitive operative risk supported a CT‑guided transcatheter strategy.

Device design and interaction of frames strongly influence strategy and outcome. Mechanically expanded valves such as the Lotus (implanted in 2014) have high radial strength and a rigid frame that tends to become firmly integrated into the annulus and aortic root; that permanence can limit the ability of subsequent devices to expand the inner lumen. Self‑expanding nitinol frames such as the Evolut are tall, supra‑annular devices with good haemodynamic performance but a high frame that can contribute to reduced valve‑to‑coronary distances when nested. Supra‑annular leaflets (as on Evolut and Acurate neo2) generally provide larger EOAs for a given internal diameter compared with intra‑annular designs and thus are often preferred in small lumens or redo situations to mitigate PPM. The Acurate neo2 combines supra‑annular leaflet position with a relatively lower frame height and a design that facilitates commissural alignment; in this case those features were chosen to maximise effective orifice area, to minimise neo‑skirt encroachment on coronaries and to preserve future coronary access. Post‑dilatation with balloons can sometimes partially expand a previously implanted self‑expanding frame (as was performed here), improving leaflet excursion and reducing gradients even without intentional frame fracture.

In summary, the decision to perform a third transcatheter valve in this patient reflects the intersection of three realities: expanding TAVI use and longer patient survival, multifactorial SVD (with superadded PVE accelerating deterioration), and prohibitive surgical risk. Safe and effective redo‑TAVI in this setting depends on documented eradication of infection, meticulous MSCT and TEE assessment of residual lumen and coronary relationships, device selection that prioritises supra‑annular EOA and manageable frame height, and procedural strategies (high implantation, commissural alignment, and planned post‑dilatation) aimed at minimising PPM and preserving coronary access.
