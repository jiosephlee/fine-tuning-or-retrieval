Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] For Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, what antibiotic regimens and durations are guideline-supported, how do you choose between ampicillin–gentamicin versus ampicillin–ceftriaxone combinations, and what is the role (if any) for suppressive oral therapy once cultures clear and imaging shows no ongoing infection prior to redo TAVR?

Short answer
- For Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE) the accepted, guideline‑supported strategy is 6 weeks of targeted intravenous therapy using an ampicillin‑based combination: either ampicillin + gentamicin or ampicillin + ceftriaxone. Choice is driven by organism characteristics (high‑level aminoglycoside resistance, HLAR) and the patient’s risk of aminoglycoside toxicity (age, renal function). Routine long‑term oral suppressive antibiotics are not recommended once blood cultures are cleared and imaging shows no active infection; suppression is reserved for the unusual situation in which infection cannot be eradicated or prosthetic material cannot be removed.

Why these regimens work (brief pathophysiology)
- Enterococci are relatively tolerant to beta‑lactams alone; bactericidal activity is usually achieved by combining a cell‑wall active agent (ampicillin) with either an aminoglycoside (gentamicin) that gains intracellular access after cell‑wall damage or a second beta‑lactam (ceftriaxone) that provides complementary penicillin‑binding‑protein coverage. The dual‑beta‑lactam (ampicillin+ceftriaxone) regimen achieves synergy without the nephrotoxicity of aminoglycosides.

What guidelines and outcome data support
- Large comparative series and guideline statements endorse two validated approaches for ampicillin‑susceptible E. faecalis IE:
  - Ampicillin (eg, 2 g IV q4h or equivalent) plus gentamicin (traditional dosing ~3 mg/kg/day in divided doses) with gentamicin given for the early part of therapy and ampicillin continued for 6 weeks for PVE.
  - Ampicillin plus ceftriaxone (eg, ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h) for 6 weeks. Multiple studies have shown similar cure/relapse rates for ampicillin+ceftriaxone versus ampicillin+gentamicin with substantially less nephrotoxicity in the dual‑beta‑lactam arm — a frequent reason to prefer it in older or renally impaired patients.

How to choose between ampicillin–gentamicin and ampicillin–ceftriaxone
- Use ampicillin–ceftriaxone when:
  - The isolate demonstrates HLAR to aminoglycosides (no aminoglycoside synergy).
  - The patient is elderly, has baseline renal insufficiency, or is at high risk for aminoglycoside toxicity.
  - You wish to avoid drug‑induced nephrotoxicity (common concern in PVE patients with comorbidities).
- Ampicillin–gentamicin remains an option when:
  - The isolate is ampicillin‑susceptible, aminoglycoside synergy is demonstrable (no HLAR), and the patient has good renal function and low toxicity risk.
  - If used, gentamicin is typically limited to the early phase (many centers give it for the first 2 weeks) with careful level and renal monitoring; ampicillin is continued to complete a full 6‑week course for PVE.
- Practical caveat: in modern practice many centers favour ampicillin+ceftriaxone for elderly/fragile patients because it preserves efficacy and avoids aminoglycoside harm.

Monitoring and adjunctive issues
- Always obtain HLAR testing (high‑level gentamicin resistance) — if present, aminoglycoside synergy is unreliable.
- Monitor renal function closely if gentamicin is used; check drug levels per institutional protocol.
- Rifampin is not routinely used for enterococcal PVE (its benefit is established for staphylococcal prosthetic infections but not for enterococci).
- For penicillin‑allergic patients, ID involvement for desensitization or alternative regimens (eg, vancomycin‑based) is required.

Role of suppressive oral therapy before redo TAVR
- If the patient has completed an appropriate 6‑week course, serial blood cultures are negative, and TEE/MSCT show no residual vegetations or perivalvular complications, continued chronic oral suppressive antibiotics are not indicated and are not standard practice prior to re‑implantation.
- Exceptions in which chronic suppressive therapy might be considered (after multidisciplinary discussion with ID and cardiac surgery) include:
  - Persistent bacteremia or relapse despite appropriate therapy.
  - Retained infected hardware that cannot be removed and where definitive cure is not achievable.
  - Recurrent infection or host factors that preclude clearing infection.
- Peri‑procedural planning: for redo valve procedures after treated PVE, multidisciplinary confirmation of completed therapy, repeated negative blood cultures, and imaging documentation of cure (TEE/MSCT) are the prerequisites for proceeding — as was done in the presented case. Routine long‑term suppressive antibiotics were not given in that case, and the patient remained free of recurrent infection at 1 year.

How this applies to the case report
- The patient had E. faecalis PVE treated with a 6‑week IV ampicillin‑based course followed by oral treatment per ID; at the time of the third TAVR antimicrobial therapy had been completed, repeat blood cultures and imaging (TEE and MSCT) showed resolution, and no long‑term suppressive therapy was continued. This approach reflects contemporary guideline practice: complete a guideline‑recommended course, confirm sterilization and absence of active infection, involve ID and the heart team, and proceed with reintervention without routine chronic oral suppression.
