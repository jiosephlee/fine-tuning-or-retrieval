Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] In a triple-stented aortic valve with an in-valve minimum lumen diameter of approximately 14 mm, what procedural strategies can maximize effective orifice area and reduce prosthesis–patient mismatch, and how do device features (supra-annular leaflet position, frame height, commissural alignment capability) influence your choice of valve?

Bottom line
- In a triple‑stented valve with an in‑valve minimum lumen diameter ≈14 mm, your procedural goal is to preserve or enlarge the flow lumen and avoid creating an additional obstructing “neo‑skirt.” Practically this is achieved by CT‑guided valve selection (favoring supra‑annular leaflets), high implantation that pins prior leaflets outward, aggressive but cautious balloon dilation to expand prior frames, and deliberate commissural alignment to minimise strut/leaflet overlap of the coronaries. These steps were exactly the strategy used in the reported case (MSCT MLD 13.7 mm → Acurate neo2 S, high implant, 22×40 mm predilation, 20×40 mm post‑dilation, commissural alignment) and led to durable improvement in gradients (mean gradient fell from an immediate post‑implant 67 mm Hg to 26–27 mm Hg at 1 month–1 year).

Why the problem is hard
- Each added transcatheter frame reduces the internal lumen and can fold or tether prior leaflets inward, producing a small effective orifice area (EOA) and prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM). In small residual lumens (~14 mm) these effects are magnified: even small changes in how the new valve seats and how much the prior frame expands materially change transvalvular flow.

Practical procedural strategies (what to do and why)
1) Preprocedural CT/3D modelling
   - Measure the minimum in‑valve lumen, valve‑to‑coronary distances, leaflet positions and the geometry of stacked frames. In this case MSCT (MLD 13.7 mm) determined feasibility and guided selection, implant depth and the decision that coronary protection was not required.

2) Choose a valve with supra‑annular leaflet position when EOA is the priority
   - Supra‑annular valves (leaflets open above the stent frame) typically deliver larger EOA in small annuli than intra‑annular designs. That advantage matters when the in‑valve lumen is already constrained. The team selected Acurate neo2 for this reason.

3) Prefer a valve with lower frame height and predictable commissural alignment
   - A relatively low frame reduces additional stacking height and the chance of covering coronary ostia; predictable commissural alignment allows the new commissures to sit between prior posts and coronary ostia, preserving coronary access and reducing neo‑skirt effects. The Acurate neo2 was chosen here for a combination of supra‑annular leaflets, low frame and alignment capability.

4) High implantation to “pin” prior leaflets outward
   - Deploying the new valve at or slightly above the level of the prior valve leaflets fixes those leaflets against the stent, preventing them from prolapsing inward and further reducing the EOA. The case explicitly used this strategy to minimise neo‑skirt formation.

5) Balloon pre‑dilation and high‑pressure post‑dilation (targeted frame expansion)
   - Pre‑dilation assesses compliance and creates room; high‑pressure post‑dilation can partially expand earlier self‑expanding frames (as happened in the report) and improve leaflet motion and central orifice area. The operators used 22×40 mm pre‑dilation and 20×40 mm post‑dilation; post‑dilation likely produced partial mechanical expansion of the Evolut frame and contributed to the improved gradients noted over weeks to months.
   - Caveats: balance benefit against risks (annular injury, coronary obstruction, conduction disturbances). The patient already had a pacemaker, which affects the risk calculus for additional conduction block.

6) Coronary protection / adjuncts when indicated
   - If CT suggests threatened coronaries, plan for coronary wiring, chimney stent strategy, or leaflet laceration (BASILICA/LAMPOON) to prevent obstruction. In this case CT showed adequate clearance and no prophylactic protection was used.

7) Avoid assuming you can “fracture” transcatheter frames
   - Bioprosthetic valve fracture is established for many stented surgical valves but is not a routine or validated technique for transcatheter frames. Rely instead on controlled dilation and valve selection.

How device features influence choice (summary)
- Supra‑annular leaflet position: maximises EOA in small lumens → preferred when residual lumen is small.
- Frame height/profile: lower frames reduce stacked height, limit neo‑skirt formation and preserve coronary access; high/tall frames increase risk of coronary obstruction and difficult re‑access.
- Commissural alignment capability: predictable orientation reduces overlap of struts with coronary ostia and makes future coronary access more likely; important in patients with coronary disease or when lifetime management is a concern.
- Radial force: balloon‑expandable valves have high radial force and can expand previous frames but are intra‑annular (less EOA) and may create larger skirts; choice requires weighing expansion benefit against loss of EOA and coronary risk.

Clinical expectation and counseling
- Even with optimal technique, some degree of residual PPM may be unavoidable with an in‑valve diameter ≈14 mm. Aim to minimise it (supra‑annular valve + high implant + aggressive but safe dilation) and plan long‑term follow‑up. In the reported patient this strategy produced substantial symptomatic and hemodynamic benefit with stable valve function at 1 year.

Teaching pearl
- In layered TAV‑in‑TAV scenarios the two most impactful choices for maximising EOA are (1) a supra‑annular valve that places the functional leaflets outside the tightest ring of prior frames and (2) a deployment strategy (high implant + targeted post‑dilation) that pins existing leaflets outward and expands earlier frames—coupled with commissural alignment to protect coronary access. The Moscow case provides a clear, real‑world example of these principles in action.
