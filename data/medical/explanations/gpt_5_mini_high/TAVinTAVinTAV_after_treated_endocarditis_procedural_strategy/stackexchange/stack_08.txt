Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] What planning principles and implant techniques help preserve future coronary access in sequential TAVR (e.g., commissural alignment, high implantation to minimize the neo-skirt, leaflet-to-ostium relationship), and in what scenarios would you escalate to BASILICA, leaflet modification, or prophylactic coronary protection?

Short answer
Preserve future coronary access in sequential TAVR by rigorous CT-based planning (virtual valve implantation), choosing a valve and an implantation depth/orientation that minimize a “neo‑skirt” at the coronary level, and by actively pursuing commissural alignment. Escalate to leaflet‑laceration (BASILICA), leaflet modification or prophylactic coronary protection when CT modeling (and intra‑procedural tests) predict borderline or high risk of coronary obstruction, sinus sequestration, or when the patient has critical coronary disease that would be catastrophic to lose.

Why (the physiology and the problem)
In valve‑in‑valve and especially repeat TAV‑in‑TAV procedures the main mechanism of coronary compromise is displacement/pinning of the existing bioprosthetic leaflets and creation of a neo‑skirt or sealed neo‑sinus that either directly covers the coronary ostium or prevents coronary flow (sinus sequestration). Multiple concentric stent frames also reduce frame cell size and make later coronary re‑engagement difficult. Every additional valve amplifies these risks, so procedural strategy must anticipate leaflet-to‑ostium geometry and the final position of the neo‑skirt.

Planning and implant techniques that preserve coronary access (practical principles)
1) High‑quality CT with virtual valve implantation is mandatory.
   - Measure coronary ostial heights, sinus of Valsalva (SOV) diameters, STJ height, and valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance; perform 3D modeling to predict where leaflets and skirt will sit relative to the ostia. (The team in the reported case used MSCT and CT modelling to guide valve choice, implantation depth and to decide against routine coronary protection.)
2) Choose the valve to match the lifetime plan.
   - Prefer lower frame height / larger cell design and supra‑annular leaflets when coronary access and haemodynamics are a priority. The operators chose an Acurate neo2 for its supra‑annular leaflets and relatively low frame height to optimise orifice and access in a triple‑stented anatomy.
3) Commissural alignment.
   - Aim to orient the new commissural posts between coronary ostia so ostia fall in large frame cells, not behind posts or a leaflet skirt. Use pre‑CT mapping and delivery system markers/controlled rotation during deployment. The case explicitly used commissural alignment to preserve access.
4) High implantation to minimise the neo‑skirt.
   - Implant the new valve at a level that pins prior leaflets against the frame and keeps the bulk of any neo‑skirt above (or away from) the coronary ostia, reducing the chance of ostial coverage. The case used a high implant aligned with the Evolut leaflets for this reason.
5) Intra‑procedural testing and staged decisions.
   - Balloon pre‑dilatation with simultaneous aortography or selective coronary angiography can confirm coronary patency before permanent implantation (as was done in this patient). If flow remains clear you may proceed without pre‑wiring.
6) Post‑dilatation strategy.
   - Post‑dilatation can improve expansion and gradients (and in this case likely expanded the Evolut frame), but recognize it may also alter leaflet geometry — use only after confirming coronary patency or with protection in place if risk is present.
7) Document orientation and fluoroscopic landmarks for future coronary access.

When to escalate to BASILICA, leaflet modification, or prophylactic coronary protection
Use CT‑guided risk stratification. Common practical thresholds (from contemporary practice):
   - VTC distance < 4 mm, coronary height < ~10–12 mm, SOV diameter small relative to valve size (often < 30–32 mm), or predicted leaflet/neo‑skirt overlap with the ostium → high risk for coronary obstruction/sinus sequestration.
Escalation strategy:
1) BASILICA (intentional leaflet laceration)
   - Indication: CT modelling predicts that intact leaflet tissue (native or bioprosthetic) will obstruct coronary flow (high risk VTC <4 mm, leaflet tip projecting over ostium, sinus sequestration) and the leaflet can be safely crossed/lacerated. BASILICA is most attractive when laceration will create a flow channel and commissural alignment alone is insufficient.
   - Caveat: BASILICA in re‑interventions with multiple stent frames is technically more challenging and sometimes not feasible—this is acknowledged by the authors as uncertain in triple‑stented configurations.
2) Prophylactic coronary protection (pre‑wire ± parked balloon/stent; chimney/stent if needed)
   - Indication: borderline anatomy where BASILICA is not feasible, operator/centre lacks BASILICA experience, or patient has critical coronary disease (left main or proximal LAD lesion) such that temporary loss of that vessel is unacceptable. Pre‑wiring allows immediate stent deployment (chimney) if obstruction occurs.
3) Leaflet/frame modification (bioprosthetic valve fracture or other strategies)
   - Indication: primarily to treat severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch / high residual gradients (to increase effective orifice area), rather than to prevent coronary obstruction; consider when prior valve frame is amenable and fracture is safe. In multiple overlapping transcatheter frames, feasibility and safety are less certain.
4) Surgical or hybrid approach
   - Indicated when CT shows near‑certain sinus sequestration or coronary obstruction risk that cannot be mitigated percutaneously, or when anatomy and comorbidity make percutaneous rescue high risk.

A practical intra‑procedural algorithm (short)
   - Pre‑procedural CT + virtual implantation → classify risk (low / borderline / high).
   - Low risk: aim for commissural alignment + high implant + careful angiographic check (as in the case); proceed without routine pre‑wiring.
   - Borderline risk: plan pre‑wiring of target coronaries; consider readiness to perform chimney stent; consider BASILICA if anatomically feasible.
   - High risk (predictable obstruction / sinus sequestration): consider BASILICA if feasible and team experienced; if not feasible, discuss prophylactic chimney stent or surgical options.

Takeaway from the case
This patient illustrates the core principles: MSCT modelling guided valve choice (Acurate neo2), commissural alignment and a high implant were used deliberately to minimise the neo‑skirt and preserve coronary flow; balloon pre‑dilation + aortography confirmed patency and allowed the team to avoid prophylactic coronary protection. The result was durable at 1 year. However, the report also highlights that in other anatomies — particularly with low coronaries, small sinuses or predicted sinus sequestration — escalation to BASILICA or prophylactic protection remains essential and must be anticipated during lifetime TAV planning.
