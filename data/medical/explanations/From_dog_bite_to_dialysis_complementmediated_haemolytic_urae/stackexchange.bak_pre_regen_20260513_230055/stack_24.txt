Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

### [Management] What clinical scenarios justify initiating eculizumab for suspected CM-HUS before the full diagnostic workup is complete, and what are the risks of delaying therapy?

Teaching answer:

The decision to start eculizumab empirically rests on a time‑sensitive balance: interrupt uncontrolled complement‑mediated endothelial injury early enough to prevent irreversible renal and systemic injury, while avoiding catastrophic suppression of host defence during uncontrolled invasive infection. In practice, empiric complement blockade before the full diagnostic work‑up is justified only in a narrow set of clinical scenarios.

You should strongly consider initiating eculizumab before complete confirmatory testing when a patient has clear evidence of a progressive TMA with the following features: frank microangiopathic hemolytic anemia with schistocytes and rising LDH/haptoglobin loss, persistent severe thrombocytopenia, rapidly worsening or dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury, and clinicopathologic findings strongly supportive of TMA (for example a renal biopsy showing fibrin thrombi/ischaemic glomeruli) — and when other immediately treatable causes have been reasonably excluded or are unlikely. Practically, that means thrombocytopenia/MAHA with an ADAMTS13 activity that is not expected to be severely deficient (or a low pretest probability for TTP by score), negative testing for Shiga‑toxin STEC‑HUS when applicable, and either negative blood cultures or clear clinical control of the inciting infection after appropriate antibiotics. If TTP is a real possibility, urgent plasma exchange must precede or accompany this decision because PEX is lifesaving for TTP and eculizumab does not treat TTP.

In septic patients the additional requirement is clinical control of the infection or convincing microbiologic evidence that bacteremia has been eradicated or is being treated effectively. This is because terminal complement blockade markedly increases risk from encapsulated organisms and can worsen uncontrolled sepsis. In cases like the presented patient — where TMA emerged after initial control of C. canimorsus sepsis, blood cultures turned negative, biopsy confirmed TMA, ADAMTS13 was not severely reduced, and haemolysis/renal failure were progressive — the benefits of prompt eculizumab outweighed the infectious risks and justified starting therapy (after meningococcal vaccination and with antimicrobial prophylaxis).

If you cannot meet those conditions, use plasma exchange as a bridge while expediting ADAMTS13, stool Shiga toxin tests, complement studies and culture results; PEX may transiently stabilise patients and buy time to clarify diagnosis.

Risks of delaying eculizumab are concrete and time‑dependent: ongoing complement‑mediated endothelial injury causes progressive microvascular thrombosis leading to irreversible glomerular and tubular damage, higher likelihood of incomplete renal recovery and progression to end‑stage kidney disease, persistent transfusion dependence, and increased morbidity and mortality. Several series and case reports (including this one) show better renal and hematologic outcomes with earlier complement blockade.

The primary risk of starting too early is infectious harm: life‑threatening meningococcal and other encapsulated bacterial infections (including concern with Capnocytophaga), especially if bacteremia is active and not adequately treated. Thus, when you start eculizumab empirically, vaccinate promptly (and recognize vaccines take time to generate protection), give appropriate antimicrobial prophylaxis, and monitor closely.

In short: initiate eculizumab empirically when the clinical picture clearly indicates progressive CM‑HUS (MAHA + thrombocytopenia + severe/worsening AKI or dialysis dependence with TMA on biopsy) and alternative diagnoses that would require different immediate therapy (notably TTP and STEC‑HUS, or uncontrolled bacteremia) have been reasonably excluded or controlled. Delay risks irreversible renal injury and worse outcome; starting too soon risks life‑threatening infection, so mitigation with antibiotics, vaccination and close monitoring is essential.
