Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

### [Clinical Pearls] In an adult who develops septic shock four days after a dog bite, which zoonotic pathogens must empiric therapy cover, and what initial intravenous antibiotic regimen provides reliable coverage for Capnocytophaga species?

When an adult presents with fulminant infection after a dog bite you must think beyond skin flora: the empiric regimen needs to cover the animal‑mouth organisms that cause rapidly progressive sepsis as well as the usual streptococci/staphylococci and anaerobes. The pathogens you must cover empirically include Capnocytophaga canimorsus and Pasteurella species, aerobic streptococci and staphylococci (add MRSA coverage if risk factors or severe soft‑tissue infection), and anaerobic oral flora from the dog’s mouth.

Clinically pragmatic empiric therapy for severe sepsis from a dog bite is an intravenous beta‑lactam/beta‑lactamase‑inhibitor that reliably covers Capnocytophaga, Pasteurella and anaerobes — for example piperacillin–tazobactam or ampicillin–sulbactam. In patients with shock or concern for resistant Gram‑positives add vancomycin until cultures clarify. Alternatives that cover Capnocytophaga include third‑generation cephalosporins or a carbapenem; doxycycline or a fluoroquinolone have activity but are not preferred as sole agents in septic shock.

Put simply: cover Capnocytophaga + Pasteurella + strep/staph + anaerobes up front — e.g., IV piperacillin–tazobactam (± vancomycin if MRSA concern) provides reliable empiric coverage for Capnocytophaga in the critically ill. Adjust therapy once cultures and susceptibilities return.
