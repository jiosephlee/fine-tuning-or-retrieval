Title: Blog about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

# When DIC Isn’t the Whole Story: Unmasking CM‑HUS After Sepsis

What makes this case striking is how cleanly it demonstrates a trap many clinicians fall into: a dramatic septic presentation with DIC that seems to explain everything — until it doesn’t. A previously well woman develops fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis after a dog bite, with pancytopenia, coagulopathy and dialysis‑dependent AKI. She improves from the infection, then suddenly develops an isolated hemolytic picture: falling hemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH and a peripheral smear full of schistocytes. That late‑appearing microangiopathic hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia and biopsy‑proven TMA point to complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) triggered by infection — a rare but critical diagnosis because timing of complement blockade matters for renal recovery.

Below I’ll walk through the red flags that should make you pivot from a DIC‑centric frame, the lab and biopsy pitfalls to watch for, how to balance plasma exchange vs eculizumab when infection is ongoing, and a practical monitoring plan to catch evolving TMA before irreversible kidney injury sets in.

## Why DIC can mask TMA (and vice versa)
Both DIC and TMA produce thrombocytopenia, schistocytes, organ dysfunction and abnormal coagulation/bleeding. In sepsis, DIC is the natural first explanation. But keep in mind:
- DIC usually presents with prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen and high D‑dimer from widespread clotting and consumption. Platelets fall but often recover as infection is controlled.
- TMA (including CM‑HUS) is characterised by microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), schistocytes, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin and often significant renal dysfunction from glomerular/arteriolar thrombi. Coagulation parameters are often normal or only mildly abnormal.

In this case the patient’s early picture fit DIC during sepsis; later a discrete shift to MAHA — falling hemoglobin (111→68 g/L), undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH and 15–20% schistocytes — appeared only after the inflammatory markers and coagulopathy were improving. That temporal pattern is the key red flag.

## Red flags that should make you suspect TMA/CM‑HUS after sepsis
Pay attention when any of the following occur during or after sepsis:
- New or suddenly worsening hemolysis after initial clinical improvement (falling Hb without bleeding, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH).
- Persistent thrombocytopenia despite resolution of coagulopathy (platelets stuck at 30–40×10^9/L in this case).
- Peripheral smear with schistocytes (even modest percentages matter; this case had 15–20%).
- Ongoing or worsening AKI, oliguria/anuria or dialysis dependence out of proportion to hemodynamics or hypotension history.
- Negative stool PCR for Shiga toxin and ADAMTS13 activity not in the TTP range (here ADAMTS13 was 43.5%, making TTP unlikely).
If several of these line up, start working up TMA aggressively rather than assuming prolonged DIC.

## Lab testing: what to order and how to interpret it (pitfalls)
When TMA is on your differential:
- CBC with smear (repeat smears; schistocytes can be missed), LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin, reticulocyte count.
- Coagulation panel (PT/aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer) to characterise DIC vs TMA.
- Renal function and urine output/proteinuria.
- ADAMTS13 activity (TTP if <10%): do not wait for the result to start empiric therapy if clinical suspicion of TTP is high.
- Shiga toxin PCR (stool) to exclude STEC‑HUS.
- Complement testing (C3/C4, functional assays) and samples for extended complement genetics/autoantibodies — but be cautious: normal C3/C4 does NOT rule out CM‑HUS. Complement assays can be altered by plasma exchange and by eculizumab; timing matters.
Pitfall examples from the case:
- Initial DAT was falsely positive (likely from penicillin or transfusion) but repeat DAT was negative; the platelet and hemolysis pattern pointed away from warm AIHA.
- ADAMTS13 at 43.5% effectively ruled out TTP; that shifted management.
- Extended complement testing (AH50) was low but sampling occurred after PEX and after one eculizumab dose, so results likely reflect treatment effects, not intrinsic deficiency.

Bottom line: send the right tests early, but interpret results in the context of recent plasma exchange, transfusions or complement blockade.

## When to consider kidney biopsy
A renal biopsy is most helpful when:
- Diagnosis is unclear and will change management.
- You need tissue confirmation of TMA vs other glomerulopathies.
In this case, biopsy (performed once platelets allowed) showed ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and crescents consistent with TMA and no immune complex deposition — bolstering the CM‑HUS diagnosis.

Timing considerations:
- Avoid biopsy during active, uncontrolled coagulopathy or while platelets are very low; correct coagulopathy and transfuse as needed.
- If biopsy will change therapy (for example confirming TMA when ADAMTS13 is non‑TTP and STEC is negative), proceed once safe.

## Therapeutic decisions — balancing PEX and complement blockade
Two immediate therapies commonly considered for TMA are plasma exchange (PEX) and eculizumab. The clinical dilemma is acute when infection is recent or ongoing.

Practical approach based on this case and common practice:
- If TTP is suspected clinically (high PLASMIC score or severe ADAMTS13 shortage): start urgent PEX and steroids immediately — do not delay.
- If ADAMTS13 activity comes back >10% and TTP unlikely, and you have MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI consistent with TMA, strongly consider CM‑HUS. Eculizumab is the targeted therapy for CM‑HUS and initiation early improves renal outcomes.
- PEX can be used as a bridge when diagnosis is uncertain or while arranging complement blockade and vaccinations. In this patient, transient diuresis returned after PEX but sustained recovery required eculizumab.
- If recent or ongoing bacteraemia: prefer to wait until blood cultures are negative before starting eculizumab when possible, because terminal complement blockade increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms (notably Neisseria and potentially Capnocytophaga). In this case blood cultures were negative before eculizumab was started.

Vaccination and prophylaxis:
- Vaccinate against meningococcal serogroups (MenACWY and MenB) before or as soon as possible when starting eculizumab, and provide antibiotic prophylaxis if vaccination is recent or delayed. This case received Menveo and Bexsero before induction and prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) because the vaccines had not been in place for 2 weeks.

Duration of complement blockade:
- Genetic testing for complement variants may inform duration but often returns late. Absence of known pathogenic variants does not exclude CM‑HUS caused by an infectious trigger. Decisions about stopping eculizumab should be individualised and made with nephrology and hematology follow‑up.

## Practical monitoring plan to catch evolving TMA early
For any patient with sepsis/DIC and renal dysfunction, adopt a focused monitoring protocol for at least the first 7–14 days (or longer if clinical course is complicated):
- Daily CBC with smear while platelets abnormal (or at least every 48 hours once improving). Look for falling Hb, persistent thrombocytopenia, and schistocytes.
- Daily LDH and haptoglobin when hemolysis suspected. Trending LDH/haptoglobin is more useful than single values.
- Daily creatinine, urine output recording hourly–q8h depending on clinical severity; check urine dipstick for blood/protein.
- Coagulation panel every 24–48 hours while DIC suspected; once coagulation normalises, shift emphasis to hemolysis labs.
- Send ADAMTS13 and stool Shiga PCR early if TMA suspected.
- Send baseline complement studies and samples for extended complement testing before PEX or eculizumab when feasible — but do not delay life‑saving therapy to obtain them.
- If transfusions, antibiotics (eg, beta‑lactams) or PEX have been given, annotate the timing when interpreting tests (DAT, complement assays).
- If platelets recover enough and diagnosis uncertain, arrange renal biopsy promptly — pathology can confirm TMA and guide treatment.

## Key clinical pearls from this case
- A patient with sepsis/DIC who initially improves and then develops new hemolysis should trigger urgent evaluation for superimposed TMA.
- Normal C3/C4 or a normal complement panel does not exclude CM‑HUS; complement assays can be insensitive and are easily affected by PEX or eculizumab.
- ADAMTS13 activity is essential to rule out TTP — levels >10% make TTP unlikely and should redirect towards other TMAs.
- PEX can transiently improve clinical status but may not prevent progression — definitive complement blockade with eculizumab is often required in CM‑HUS for durable response.
- When starting eculizumab in the context of recent infection, try to ensure blood cultures are negative, vaccinate against meningococcus, and give appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis.

Take‑home messages
- Think TMA when hemolysis (falling Hb, high LDH, undetectable haptoglobin) appears or worsens after the initial septic/DIC phase.
- Send ADAMTS13, stool Shiga PCR and complement workup early; interpret results in context of transfusions, PEX and timing.
- Renal biopsy is highly useful when safe to perform and can confirm TMA.
- Use PEX as a bridge when diagnosis is uncertain; start eculizumab promptly for CM‑HUS when infection is controlled and after counselling about meningococcal risk, vaccination and prophylaxis.
- Close, protocolised monitoring (daily CBC/smear, LDH/haptoglobin, creatinine/urine output) is critical to detect evolving TMA before irreversible kidney injury occurs.

This Capnocytophaga canimorsus case is a great reminder: sepsis and DIC may be the obvious diagnosis, but they don’t rule out a second process. Keep your eyes on the labs and your mind open to TMA — protecting kidney function often depends on catching the shift early.
