Title: Blog about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

# Dog and Cat Bites in 2026: Empiric Coverage That Won’t Miss Capnocytophaga

What makes this relevant now? A previously healthy woman developed fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis after a seemingly minor dog bite and later went on to complement-mediated HUS with dialysis dependence before partial recovery. Capnocytophaga is an uncommon but deadly zoonotic pathogen that can be slow to grow in culture, sometimes beta-lactamase–producing, and capable of triggering catastrophic downstream complications. In bite management you don’t just treat skin flora — you cover for fastidious, encapsulated organisms and plan your diagnostics accordingly.

Here’s a practical, evidence-informed approach to empiric IV therapy, outpatient prophylaxis, allergy workarounds, duration, and microbiology logistics that will catch Capnocytophaga (and the usual suspects) without over-cooking therapy.

Clinical overview — who needs coverage?
- All dog and cat bites risk Pasteurella spp., Capnocytophaga (dogs), anaerobes, streptococci, staphylococci and oral anaerobes from the biter. Human bites add Eikenella corrodens.
- Give prophylactic antibiotics for high-risk wounds: deep punctures, hand/foot bites, bites near joints/tendons, crush wounds, devitalised tissue, immunocompromised or asplenic patients, and bites seen >8 hours after injury.
- Always consider tetanus status and rabies risk separately.

Empiric IV choices (severe infection / sepsis / hospitalised)
Aim: reliably cover Pasteurella, Capnocytophaga (including some beta-lactamase producers), anaerobes and Gram-negatives.

First-line (broad, evidence-based)
- Piperacillin–tazobactam IV. Pros: broad Gram-negative + anaerobe + good activity against most Capnocytophaga isolates (including many β-lactamase producers). Ideal for severe sepsis, deep tissue infection or suspected polymicrobial infection.

Alternative IV options
- Ampicillin–sulbactam IV (Unasyn). Good option for many severe bite infections; covers Pasteurella and most Capnocytophaga, but local beta-lactamase patterns may vary.
- Carbapenem (ertapenem or meropenem) when ESBL/ resistant organisms are suspected or in critically ill where maximal Gram-negative coverage is required.

Do not rely on first-generation cephalosporins (cefazolin) alone — they miss Pasteurella/Capnocytophaga. Clindamycin alone misses Gram-negatives.

Step-down and outpatient prophylaxis
- First-line oral step-down/prophylaxis: amoxicillin–clavulanate (Augmentin). Good bioavailability, covers Pasteurella, Capnocytophaga (including many β-lactamase producers) and oral anaerobes. Typical prophylaxis: 3–5 days for simple wounds; longer if infected.
- Established uncomplicated infection: 7–14 days depending on severity and response. Deep infections, osteomyelitis, or septic arthritis require longer IV → oral transition guided by clinical response and imaging.

Allergy workarounds (penicillin allergy)
- If true severe immediate-type penicillin allergy (anaphylaxis): doxycycline plus metronidazole is a practical option
  - Doxycycline covers Capnocytophaga and Pasteurella in most reports.
  - Add metronidazole to ensure anaerobic coverage for deep/bitten wounds.
- Alternatively, for adults: moxifloxacin monotherapy covers Gram-negatives and anaerobes (but variable Pasteurella/Capnocytophaga susceptibility and fluoroquinolone risks). Use with caution and local susceptibility data.
- Cephalosporins: many patients with non–type I PCN allergies can receive second/third-generation cephalosporins, but avoid if high-risk allergy. Discuss with allergy/immunology if needed.

Duration — practical guidance
- Prophylaxis (uninfected but high-risk wound): 3–5 days of oral amoxicillin–clavulanate.
- Superficial infected wound/cellulitis: 5–7 days, extend if not improving.
- Deep infection (tenosynovitis, septic arthritis, osteomyelitis): IV therapy often needed initially; total duration commonly 2–6 weeks depending on site, surgical debridement and clinical course.
- Bacteraemia: individualized—at least 10–14 days after source control and clinical improvement; longer if endovascular/severe complications.

Microbiology — what to send and how to ask the lab
- Blood cultures: get at least two sets before antibiotics if possible for febrile or systemically unwell patients — Capnocytophaga is often cultured from blood in invasive disease.
- Wound swab + tissue samples: for deep infections send tissue aspirates or biopsy material rather than superficial swabs; anaerobic transport recommended.
- Request extended incubation and communication: Capnocytophaga is slow-growing and capnophilic. Ask the lab to hold cultures for at least 7 days (some labs incubate up to 14 days) and to plate on blood and chocolate agar under CO2 conditions.
- Use of molecular diagnostics: if cultures are negative but suspicion is high, request 16S rRNA PCR, MALDI-TOF ID if available, or sequencing (nanopore/next-gen) — these speed identification and can be decisive in slow growers.
- Inform the lab: tell them “dog bite; consider Capnocytophaga — hold cultures, plate on chocolate/blood, anaerobic + aerobic, consider 16S if slow growth.” That simple heads-up matters.

Special considerations
- Beta-lactamase production is increasingly reported in Capnocytophaga: amoxicillin alone may fail; prefer beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations or doxycycline in allergy.
- Immunocompromised and asplenic patients are at higher risk for fulminant disease — lower threshold for hospitalisation and IV therapy.
- Vaccinate/cover: check tetanus status and manage rabies risk depending on animal behavior and local guidelines.
- Complications: be alert for early sepsis, DIC, and rare downstream phenomena like thrombotic microangiopathy/CM‑HUS (as in the case described). Clinical deterioration despite source control should prompt re-evaluation and consultation (ID, nephrology, hematology).

When to involve specialists
- Infectious diseases for complex bite infections, unusual organisms, or resistant isolates.
- Surgery/orthopedics for deep space infections, tendon/joint involvement, or necrotizing infection.
- Nephrology/hematology if haematologic derangements or renal failure develop (early recognition of TMA matters).

Putting it together — an example pathway
- Minor, low-risk bite: local wound care, tetanus review, no antibiotics unless patient high-risk.
- High-risk bite or patient immunocompromise (outpatient): amoxicillin–clavulanate PO x 3–5 days (prophylaxis) or longer if infected.
- Hospitalised with severe infection or systemic features: start piperacillin–tazobactam IV; narrow to amoxicillin–clavulanate PO when improving and cultures permit.
- Penicillin anaphylaxis: doxycycline + metronidazole PO/IV (or moxifloxacin where appropriate) — involve ID as needed.
- Always send blood cultures and deep tissue samples and communicate with the lab to hold cultures for ≥7 days and consider molecular ID if growth is slow or cultures negative.

Key take-home messages
- Don’t underestimate dog bites: Capnocytophaga canimorsus can cause fulminant sepsis and rare complications such as complement-mediated HUS.
- First-line empiric for severe bite-related infection: piperacillin–tazobactam IV or ampicillin–sulbactam; step-down to oral amoxicillin–clavulanate.
- In penicillin anaphylaxis use doxycycline ± metronidazole (or moxifloxacin with caution); avoid cephalosporins only if true type I hypersensitivity.
- Tell the lab “dog bite — consider Capnocytophaga” and request prolonged incubation (≥7 days), aerobic/anaerobic plates in CO2, and molecular methods if cultures are slow/negative.
- High-risk wounds, immunocompromised/asplenic patients and any systemic signs — lower threshold for hospital admission, IV therapy, and early specialist involvement.

Keep your bite protocols current, communicate with your lab early, and remember that a “minor” bite can become life‑threatening — timely broad empiric coverage and targeted diagnostics save lives.



## Biopsy at 77k Platelets: How — and Why — to Pursue Renal Pathology in TMA

What sticks with me from this case isn’t just the dog bite or the rare culprit (Capnocytophaga canimorsus). It’s the moment the team decided to biopsy a severely injured kidney in a thrombocytopenic patient — after platelets rose to 77 × 10^9/L — and how that specimen changed everything. In a patient with sepsis, DIC and dialysis‑dependent AKI, the line between sepsis-associated ATN/DIC and a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is blurred. Histology cut through that fog.

Why this is surprising (and important)
- We’re taught to fear bleeding in thrombocytopenic patients, so invasive diagnostics like percutaneous renal biopsy often get deferred. That delay can cost diagnostic clarity and therapeutic opportunity.
- In TMA, renal histology is not academic — seeing fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli and tubular necrosis can support a diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) and justify high‑cost, high‑risk therapy such as eculizumab. In this case, biopsy findings materially supported a move to complement blockade after other causes were excluded.

When to push for a kidney biopsy in suspected TMA
- When the clinical picture is ambiguous and results will change management: persistent MAHA, schistocytes, thrombocytopenia and AKI where ADAMTS13, stool Shiga testing, and complement studies are equivocal or pending.
- When the patient is dialysis‑dependent or there’s rapidly progressive loss of renal function and you need prognostic information (acute reversible lesion vs established irreversible cortical necrosis).
- When distinguishing TMA from DIC/ATN will alter therapy (plasmapheresis alone vs early complement blockade).

Practical thresholds and risk mitigation
- There’s no absolute universal platelet cutoff but practical guidance:
  - Many centers accept percutaneous biopsy when platelets ≥50 × 10^9/L if INR is normal and counts are stable.
  - For platelets 30–50 × 10^9/L consider platelet transfusion pre‑procedure; for <30 × 10^9/L or coagulopathy favour alternative strategies.
- Timing matters:
  - Delay biopsy until acute, correctable coagulopathy is addressed and infection is controlled enough to justify immunosuppression if needed.
  - In this case the team waited until platelets recovered to ~77 × 10^9/L after PEX and supportive care before performing biopsy.
- Technique choices:
  - Ultrasound‑guided percutaneous biopsy is standard with good safety when counts are adequate.
  - If bleeding risk remains high or coagulopathy persists, consider transjugular renal biopsy — lower bleeding risk and safe in severe thrombocytopenia/coagulopathy.
- Peri‑procedural steps:
  - Correct INR and fibrinogen first; hold anticoagulants.
  - Transfuse platelets immediately before biopsy if counts are borderline.
  - Use experienced operators, real‑time ultrasound guidance, and post‑procedure observation with serial hemoglobin checks and ultrasound if concern for hematoma.

What the biopsy gives you — and how it changes decisions
- Classic TMA histology: fibrin thrombi in glomerular capillaries and arterioles, ischemic wrinkled glomeruli, endothelial swelling, and often acute tubular necrosis.
  - These findings point away from isolated DIC or pure ATN and support a microangiopathic process with endothelial/complement involvement.
- Immunofluorescence/electron microscopy can exclude immune complex GN or other primary glomerulopathies.
- Correlation with clinical/lab data:
  - Normal ADAMTS13 (not TTP), negative Shiga toxin, and biopsy evidence of TMA + ongoing hemolysis tilts to CM‑HUS.
  - That shift supports early use of eculizumab (after reasonable infection control/vaccination/prophylaxis), rather than only prolonged PEX.
- Prognosis and counselling:
  - Presence of chronic changes (cortical scarring, chronic thrombotic lesions) predicts poorer renal recovery and informs discussions about dialysis dependence and need for long‑term eculizumab if genetic predisposition found.

Special considerations in infection-triggered TMA
- Active bacteraemia complicates the decision to start complement blockade because terminal complement inhibition increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms.
- In the case above, clinicians waited until blood cultures were negative, vaccinated against meningococcus, and gave antibiotic prophylaxis before initiating eculizumab — a reasonable compromise when biopsy and clinical picture supported CM‑HUS.
- Biopsy can justify that risk: without histologic confirmation, committing to complement blockade in the setting of recent sepsis is a tougher call.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t let thrombocytopenia alone be an absolute blocker to biopsy. Platelet transfusion, transjugular approach, and timing can make biopsy safely feasible.
- In TMA with ambiguous etiology, renal histology often changes management — particularly when ADAMTS13 is not severely reduced and Shiga toxin testing is negative.
- Normal complement levels (C3/C4) do not exclude CM‑HUS. Histology and clinical trajectory matter.
- If biopsy confirms TMA and other causes are reasonably excluded, early eculizumab improves outcomes; biopsy can be the tipping point that allows justified, prompt therapy.
- Always weigh the bleeding risk against the risk of delayed or missed diagnosis leading to irreversible kidney injury.

Take‑home summary
- Consider renal biopsy in suspected TMA when results will change therapy or prognosis, even with thrombocytopenia — aim for platelets ≥50 × 10^9/L or use platelet transfusion/transjugular approach when needed.
- Biopsy findings of fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli and ATN support TMA/CM‑HUS and can justify escalation to complement blockade after infection control.
- Safety is maximized by correcting coagulopathy, using experienced operators, and choosing the optimal access route.
- In ambiguous, high‑stakes cases, histology is often the most decisive tool you have — it can be both diagnostic and therapeutic because it clears the path to specific treatments.
