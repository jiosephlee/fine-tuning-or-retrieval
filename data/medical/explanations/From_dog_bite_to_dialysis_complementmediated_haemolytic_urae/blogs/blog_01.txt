# When DIC Isn’t the Whole Story: Spotting CM‑HUS After Sepsis

Sepsis does weird things. It provokes coagulopathy, multiorgan failure, and can look like every haematology or nephrology emergency rolled into one. But sometimes the story doesn’t end when the infection starts to get under control. This case — a previously healthy woman who developed fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis with DIC, then later developed isolated hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia and dialysis‑dependent AKI — is a perfect reminder: don’t anchor on DIC. A superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), specifically complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS), can be hiding in plain sight.

Why this is surprising (and dangerous)
- Early sepsis with DIC produces cytopenias, raised LDH, and organ dysfunction — many features overlap with TMA. In a busy ED/ICU, it’s easy to attribute evolving anaemia or low platelets to ongoing sepsis, transfusions, or drug effects.
- In this patient, haemolysis (falling Hb, absent haptoglobin, rising LDH), schistocytes on the peripheral smear, and persistent thrombocytopenia appeared only after systemic inflammation and coagulopathy had started to improve. That temporal pattern is key: infection improves → new/worsening MAHA + thrombocytopenia + worsening AKI = red flag for superimposed TMA.
- Missing CM‑HUS matters. Timely complement blockade (eculizumab) often determines renal recovery; delay risks permanent dialysis dependence. But starting eculizumab during active bloodstream infection is also fraught. You have to balance timing, vaccination, and antibiotic coverage.

How to think through “persistent thrombocytopenia + new hemolysis after sepsis”
1. Reassess the timeline
   - Is the haemolysis new or ongoing from the start? In this case haemolysis appeared after sepsis began to settle — classic for a second hit (infection‑triggered TMA).
2. Look for microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA)
   - Labs to prioritize: Hb trend, reticulocyte count, haptoglobin, LDH, indirect bilirubin, peripheral smear for schistocytes.
   - Schistocytes (≥1% and especially ≥15–20% of fragmented cells reported here) are direct evidence of mechanical RBC destruction and should prompt urgent TMA workup.
3. Don’t rely on C3/C4 to rule CM‑HUS in or out
   - Complement levels are often normal in CM‑HUS. Normal C3/C4 does not exclude disease.
4. Rule out competing TMA diagnoses quickly
   - ADAMTS13 activity to exclude TTP (TTP almost always <10%): use clinical scores (PLASMIC) while awaiting labs but send assay early.
   - Shiga toxin PCR/stool testing to exclude STEC‑HUS.
   - Consider HIT, DIC evolution, severe sepsis‑related consumptive processes — but MAHA with schistocytes points toward a TMA.
5. Tissue helps when safe
   - Kidney biopsy can be diagnostic (fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli, crescents) when noninvasive testing is equivocal and when biopsy risk is acceptable.
6. Treatment pragmatics in infection‑triggered CM‑HUS
   - Plasma exchange (PEX) may temporize and is reasonable while excluding TTP, but PEX is not definitive for CM‑HUS.
   - Eculizumab is the specific therapy for CM‑HUS and can induce haematological remission and renal recovery — earlier is better.
   - If infection is ongoing, delay eculizumab until bacteremia is controlled when possible, because terminal complement blockade increases risk from encapsulated organisms; vaccinate (meningococcal A/C/Y/W and B) and give antibiotic prophylaxis if starting eculizumab without prior immunisation.
   - In this case, blood cultures turned negative, vaccination was given, and eculizumab was started — haematology and kidney function then improved.
7. Complement genetics and functional testing
   - Genetic variants may be present in 50–70% of CM‑HUS cases, but their absence doesn’t rule the diagnosis out; many cases are triggered by infection alone.
   - Extended functional assays can be confounded by recent PEX or eculizumab; timing matters for interpretation.

Practical triggers to reassess DIC → think TMA
- New or worsening anaemia with low haptoglobin and high LDH after apparent improvement of sepsis.
- Persistent thrombocytopenia that does not correct as DIC resolves.
- Schistocytes on peripheral smear.
- Worsening or dialysis‑dependent AKI despite sepsis control.
- Positive DAT that is inconsistent with clinical course (drug interference, transfusion effects) — don’t let a single positive Coombs assay close the case.
- ADAMTS13 >10% (especially >20–30%) — TTP unlikely; think other TMAs including CM‑HUS.
- Negative stool Shiga toxin PCR in the absence of diarrhoeal prodrome — STEC unlikely.

Clinical pearls
- Schistocytes are not subtle: ask the lab for a manual peripheral smear when haemolysis is suspected — automated counters miss fragmentation.
- Normal complement (C3/C4) is common in CM‑HUS; functional assays and genetics are more informative but can be altered by PEX or eculizumab.
- Use PEX as a bridge (especially if TTP not yet excluded), but don’t view it as definitive therapy for CM‑HUS.
- Eculizumab requires careful infection risk mitigation: preferably vaccinate ≥2 weeks before starting, but if urgent therapy is needed give vaccines and cover with appropriate antibiotics until immunity is established.
- Infections with encapsulated organisms (like Capnocytophaga canimorsus) can both trigger complement‑mediated endothelial injury and complicate decisions about complement blockade — timing and multidisciplinary discussion (ID, nephrology, haematology, ICU) are essential.

Take‑home messages
- If sepsis/DIC improves but new MAHA, persistent thrombocytopenia, or worsening AKI appear — STOP and reassess for TMA.
- Key tests: LDH, haptoglobin, peripheral smear (schistocytes), DAT, ADAMTS13, stool Shiga PCR, and send complement studies and genetics early (but interpret in context of PEX/eculizumab).
- Use PEX to bridge while excluding TTP. Start eculizumab promptly for CM‑HUS once active uncontrolled bacteremia is addressed and appropriate vaccination/antibiotic precautions are in place.
- Multidisciplinary care and attention to timing (diagnostics, PEX, vaccination, antibiotic prophylaxis) are critical to maximise recovery and minimise harm.

This Capnocytophaga case teaches a simple but high‑value lesson: in post‑sepsis patients, don’t let the DIC label become diagnostic tunnel vision. When the labs and timeline don’t fit, think TMA — fast recognition changes outcomes.
