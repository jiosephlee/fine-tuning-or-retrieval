Title: Blog about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

# Normal C3, Sick Kidneys: Getting Complement Testing Right in Suspected CM‑HUS

What makes this case interesting: a previously healthy woman develops fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis after a dog bite, then—after apparent stabilization—develops new microangiopathic hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia and dialysis‑dependent kidney injury. Initial routine complement tests (C3/C4) were normal, ADAMTS13 was not consistent with TTP, and kidney biopsy showed thrombotic microangiopathy. The team started plasma exchange (PEX) and then eculizumab with clinical improvement. Extended testing later showed a markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50) — but that was measured after PEX and the first eculizumab dose, so probably a treatment effect. No pathogenic complement gene variants were identified.

This is a great “teach” because it highlights three hard truths we see in the ED/ICU/nephrology interface:
- Normal C3/C4 does NOT rule out complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS).
- PEX and eculizumab distort complement assays (and autoantibody tests).
- Genetic testing is useful but delayed — don’t wait to treat.

Below I’ll walk through which assays to ask for, how to handle specimens and timing, how to interpret results in the context of prior PEX/biologics, and practical decision‑making for infection‑triggered CM‑HUS.

## Quick clinical snapshot (from the case)
A woman in her late 40s: dog bite → severe sepsis with DIC, pancytopenia and AKI → initial improvement on antibiotics and supportive care → new isolated hemolysis (schistocytes, undetectable haptoglobin, ↑LDH), persistent thrombocytopenia, dialysis dependence → ADAMTS13 43.5% (not TTP), stool Shiga PCR negative → kidney biopsy: TMA → PEX then eculizumab with recovery of diuresis and hematologic remission. C3/C4 were normal early; AH50 was low only after PEX and drug exposure. Extended genetics negative.

Use that timeline to anchor testing and interpretation.

## Which complement and related tests to order (ideally up‑front)
When you suspect CM‑HUS, collect both routine and speciality samples before you change the complement milieu (PEX/egress of antibodies) or give complement blockade if at all feasible:

- Routine: C3, C4 (fast, local)
- Functional assays: CH50 (classical pathway) and AH50 (alternative pathway) — serum required
- Complement proteins: Factor H, Factor I, Factor B, C3 (quantitative)
- Anti‑factor H autoantibodies (ELISA)
- Terminal pathway activation marker: sC5b‑9 (plasma/serum; tells you ongoing terminal activation)
- ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor (to exclude TTP)
- Shiga toxin PCR/stool if STEC risk
- Peripheral smear, haptoglobin, LDH, retic count, DAT to phenotype the hemolysis
- Blood for complement genetics panel (CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, MCP/CD46, THBD, and others as per lab) — this is DNA and can be drawn anytime; ask the reference lab what they want (EDTA tube usually)

Important: many of the specialised complement tests are send‑outs; talk to your reference lab early.

## Specimen handling — small stuff that matters
- Functional assays (CH50/AH50) need serum — draw in a plain (red) tube, keep at room temperature, spin and separate promptly, and ship on ice per lab instructions. Avoid multiple freeze‑thaw cycles.
- sC5b‑9 is labile; follow courier instructions (often EDTA plasma or serum on ice).
- Anti‑factor H antibodies: serum (ELISA), best before PEX or immunosuppression.
- Genetic testing: EDTA whole blood; DNA is stable — PEX does not affect genotype.
- Document the exact timing of draws relative to PEX and eculizumab on the request form.

## How PEX and eculizumab ruin (and sometimes help) your tests — the common pitfalls

PEX
- Removes autoantibodies (eg, anti‑factor H), complement proteins and drug (eculizumab if already given) — can create false negatives for autoantibodies and transiently alter quantitative factor levels.
- Replacement fluid (eg, Octaplas/pool plasma) delivers complement proteins and can artificially normalize certain assays immediately after exchange.
- Functional assays performed shortly after PEX may be misleading (either depressed because of removal, or spuriously normal if plasma replacement provided factors).

Eculizumab (anti‑C5)
- Blocks terminal pathway; CH50/AH50 will be markedly depressed after dosing because the assay measures ability to lyse via terminal components.
- sC5b‑9 levels typically drop after complement blockade.
- Anti‑factor H antibody testing is unaffected by drug per se, but prior PEX can remove antibodies.
- Critically: a low CH50/AH50 after eculizumab does not tell you why the patient had TMA — it mostly confirms pharmacodynamic effect.

Timing in the real world
- If you have to start PEX or eculizumab emergently, draw complement and antibody samples first if possible.
- If you must start therapy before sampling, document timing and interpret results in that context — many labs will note “sample collected after PEX/eculizumab; results may be affected.”

In the presented case: basic C3/C4 were normal before PEX; AH50 was measured 8 days after PEX and 4 days after the first eculizumab dose and was markedly reduced (6%) — most consistent with treatment effects, not intrinsic complement depletion.

## Interpreting normal C3/C4
- Normal C3 and C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS. Complement activation in CM‑HUS is often local (endothelial microenvironment) or may not be reflected by plasma C3/C4.
- Some patients with genetic defects have normal baseline C3/C4.
- Use functional pathway assays (AH50/CH50), sC5b‑9, clinical features and kidney biopsy when C3/C4 are non‑revealing.

## Practical workflow for suspected CM‑HUS (reasonable and pragmatic)
1. Rapid bedside evaluation: MAHA (schistocytes, ↑LDH, low haptoglobin), thrombocytopenia, AKI. Exclude obvious DIC bleed source, drug causes.
2. Order immediate tests: CBC, smear, retic, haptoglobin, LDH, coagulation panel, creatinine/urine, C3/C4, CH50/AH50 (if available quickly), ADAMTS13, blood cultures, Shiga PCR.
3. Draw additional specialised samples (anti‑FH, FH/FB/FI) and genetics blood before PEX or eculizumab if you can safely delay therapy to collect them.
4. If TTP suspected strongly (high PLASMIC score), start PEX immediately; draw ADAMTS13 before first exchange if possible.
5. If TTP unlikely and there is strong suspicion for CM‑HUS (biopsy, persistent renal failure, schistocytes, exclusion of STEC/TTP), consider early eculizumab — but balance infection risk. If active bacteremia, obtain culture clearance and vaccinate when possible; give prophylactic antibiotics if starting eculizumab before full vaccination immune protection.
6. Document timing of all interventions relative to sample collection and inform the reference lab — they often annotate results accordingly.

## What about genetics and treatment duration?
- Genetic testing helps prognosticate and guide duration of complement blockade: pathogenic variants (eg, CFH, CFI, CFB, C3) favour longer/indefinite therapy; no variant and a clear, single infectious trigger may allow planned cessation after a period of stability.
- Turnaround for genetic panels is weeks to months. Do not delay eculizumab awaiting genetics.
- In infection‑triggered CM‑HUS without identified pathogenic variants, many centres continue eculizumab for a finite period (months) with careful weaning and close outpatient monitoring for relapse; there are no universally agreed durations. In the case above, the team continued maintenance dosing for 4 months post‑induction and planned reassessment.
- If you stop therapy, educate the patient about relapse signs and have rapid access to reinitiation.

## How to read a post‑treatment complement panel
- Low CH50/AH50 after eculizumab: expected — confirms pharmacologic blockade, not diagnostic of intrinsic deficiency.
- Low AH50 after recent PEX: could be removal of proteins or residual PEX effect; check timing and replacement fluids.
- Anti‑factor H antibodies negative after PEX: might be false negative if antibodies were removed — repeat testing a few days after last PEX before declaring absent.
- Normal factors H/I/B after many PEX sessions with Octaplas: watch for confounding by donor plasma.

In our case the reduced AH50 measured after PEX + eculizumab was most compatible with treatment effects. The meaningful pieces that supported CM‑HUS were the clinical course (new MAHA and persistent thrombocytopenia after early sepsis subsided), kidney biopsy showing TMA, exclusion of STEC and TTP, and sustained response after complement blockade.

## Clinical pearls
- Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS. Think beyond C3/C4.
- Always draw baseline complement and antibody studies before PEX or eculizumab when it’s safe to do so — and get the genetic sample (EDTA) early.
- Tell the lab exactly when PEX and eculizumab were given — interpretation hinges on timing.
- ADAMTS13 <10% = TTP. If higher, pivot your differential.
- Don’t wait for genetics to treat; do reassess genetics later to inform long‑term therapy.
- In infection‑triggered CM‑HUS, try to clear infection and vaccinate before starting complement blockade when possible; if you must start earlier, give antibiotic prophylaxis and close monitoring.

Key take‑home messages
- Normal C3/C4 do NOT rule out CM‑HUS; functional assays (CH50/AH50), factor H/I/B, anti‑factor H antibodies and sC5b‑9 provide complementary information.
- PEX and eculizumab substantially alter complement test results; whenever possible, obtain baseline samples before these therapies.
- Genetic testing is essential for prognosis and long‑term management but is delayed — treat the patient, not the panel.
- Use the clinical trajectory (biopsy, response to eculizumab, exclusion of TTP/STEC) to guide duration of complement blockade in infection‑triggered CM‑HUS without pathogenic variants.

If you’re facing this situation at 0300 with dialysis‑dependent AKI and schistocytes, prioritize: smear + ADAMTS13 + blood cultures + baseline complement/autoantibody draws (if you can), then treat clinically. The labs will help you refine therapy later — but they often won’t change your early, life‑saving decisions.
