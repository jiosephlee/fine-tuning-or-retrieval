Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Chapter 4: ## Therapeutic strategy: sequencing plasma exchange and complement blockade amid infection

This case illustrates the central therapeutic dilemma when thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) appears in the setting of active infection: urgent treatment is required to prevent irreversible organ injury, but terminal complement blockade (the disease‑specific therapy for complement‑mediated HUS) carries a real infectious risk because the terminal complement pathway is essential for defence against encapsulated organisms. Management therefore requires staged decisions that balance immediate safety (treating the most likely, rapidly fatal diagnoses) with prompt disease‑directed therapy once infection is controlled.

Plasma exchange (PEX) was used first in this patient as a reasonable and widely accepted bridge in undifferentiated, severe TMA while definitive laboratory results (most importantly ADAMTS13 activity) were pending. The primary reason for early PEX is to treat thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which is rapidly fatal without PEX and is diagnosed by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). In this case the PLASMIC score and subsequent ADAMTS13 activity (43.5%) argued against TTP, but PEX was appropriately started before that result was available. Clinically, PEX can transiently remove circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators or autoantibodies and may produce short‑lived improvement in kidney perfusion or diuresis, as seen here when spontaneous urine output returned after the final PEX sessions. However, PEX is not disease‑modifying in complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) because it does not correct the underlying dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway; sustained haematological remission and renal recovery in CM‑HUS generally require terminal complement blockade.

Eculizumab (anti‑C5 monoclonal antibody) was therefore introduced as the definitive therapy to halt terminal complement–mediated endothelial injury, microvascular thrombosis and ongoing haemolysis. The mechanistic rationale is that blocking C5 prevents formation of the membrane attack complex and downstream amplification of endothelial damage, leading to resolution of microangiopathic haemolysis and preservation/recovery of renal function. Timing is important: earlier initiation of eculizumab is associated with better renal outcomes, but initiation during uncontrolled bacteremia risks life‑threatening infections because C5 blockade impairs clearance of encapsulated organisms.

To manage that risk, the team waited for clearance of bacteraemia before starting eculizumab. They gave the first doses of meningococcal vaccines (MenACWY and MenB) and then began eculizumab induction (900 mg IV weekly × 4), with maintenance at 1,200 mg IV every 2 weeks as per standard regimens; booster vaccination doses were planned at 8 weeks. Because the vaccines had not been administered ≥2 weeks prior to initiating complement blockade — the interval usually recommended to allow an antibody response — oral antibiotic prophylaxis (phenoxymethylpenicillin/Apocillin) was provided until protective immunity could be assumed. This strategy recognises that while meningococci are the best‑known risk, other encapsulated organisms (and in this specific context, possibly Capnocytophaga) may pose increased risk when terminal complement is inhibited; therefore vaccination, short‑term antimicrobial prophylaxis, and heightened clinical vigilance for fever or sepsis are mandatory when starting eculizumab urgently.

Supportive care remained essential throughout. The patient required continuous renal replacement therapy for anuria and fluid overload during the acute phase, and transfusions and clotting factor or fibrinogen supplementation were given for DIC‑related bleeding. An initial positive direct antiglobulin test prompted corticosteroids for presumed autoimmune haemolysis, but steroids were stopped once a false‑positive DAT and microangiopathic haemolysis (schistocytosis) were demonstrated — an important example of re‑evaluating therapy when additional diagnostic data become available.

Finally, clinicians should be aware that both PEX and eculizumab affect complement assays and may confound interpretation of laboratory testing used to characterise CM‑HUS. In this patient, extended complement testing performed after PEX and a first eculizumab dose showed reduced alternative pathway activity, a result most consistent with treatment effects rather than intrinsic complement depletion. In practice, when possible, baseline complement and autoantibody testing should be obtained before PEX or eculizumab; if treatment cannot be delayed because of clinical urgency, interpret subsequent results in the context of recent PEX or complement blockade.

Key practical lessons from this case are: (1) use PEX as an early bridge in undifferentiated severe TMA while rapidly excluding TTP; (2) once infection is controlled, initiate eculizumab promptly to limit permanent renal injury; (3) vaccinate against meningococci before or immediately upon starting eculizumab and give short‑term antibiotic prophylaxis if vaccination was recent or omitted; and (4) continue aggressive supportive care and reappraise empiric therapies as diagnostic clarity improves.
