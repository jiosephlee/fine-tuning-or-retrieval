Title: Blog about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

# Beyond the Bite: Recognizing Capnocytophaga Sepsis in the ED

What makes this case striking is how a seemingly minor dog bite in a previously healthy woman progressed in days to fulminant sepsis with refractory hypoglycaemia, leukopaenia, DIC, acral ischaemia, multiorgan failure and — later — a complement-mediated TMA that required plasma exchange, eculizumab and dialysis. Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an uncommon but highly morbid pathogen; it’s slow-growing and easy to miss unless you keep it on your radar.

## Why ED clinicians should care
Dog and cat bites are common. Capnocytophaga infections are rare, but when they occur they can kill: published series report mortality approaching 30%. Importantly, severe C. canimorsus disease is not limited to clearly immunocompromised patients — this case was an otherwise healthy woman who developed catastrophic illness within a few days. Early recognition and empiric management can be lifesaving.

## The red flags — clues that this isn’t a garden-variety cellulitis
Think Capnocytophaga (or other fulminant zoonotic sepsis) when you see a bite/animal contact history plus one or more of the following, even if traditional sepsis scores look mild:

- Rapidly progressive systemic illness within days of a bite or close animal contact
- Marked leukopaenia rather than leukocytosis — profound neutropenia is a known ominous sign
- Refractory hypoglycaemia (this patient had persistent hypoglycaemia down to 0.6 mmol/L)
- Early coagulopathy/DIC with bleeding or acral thromboses and skin necrosis (mottling, cyanosis of toes/fingers)
- High lactate, metabolic acidosis or rapid organ dysfunction (AKI, hepatic injury, cardiomyopathy)
- Unexplained severe illness with normal chest/abdo imaging and no other source

These features may precede hypotension or a high qSOFA. Relying on qSOFA alone risks under-triage.

## Practical ED approach
1. Exposure history first. Ask specifically about bites, scratches, licking of broken skin, or close contact with dogs/cats. A “minor” lip bite in this case preceded catastrophe by 4 days.
2. Low threshold for early aggressive management. If any of the red flags above are present, escalate: admit, early ICU involvement, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics that cover Capnocytophaga, and early infectious diseases consult.
3. Empiric antibiotics for severe presentations:
   - In critically ill patients: start piperacillin/tazobactam (used in this case) or a carbapenem. These provide reliable coverage for Capnocytophaga and other polymicrobial bite-associated pathogens.
   - In penicillin-allergic patients, consider alternatives guided by ID.
   - For outpatient prophylaxis after an uncomplicated bite, amoxicillin–clavulanate remains the standard (it covers Capnocytophaga and common oral flora). Note: in the reported case the patient was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin but did not take it.
4. Labs and monitoring beyond routine sepsis workup:
   - CBC with differential (watch for leukopaenia), coagulation profile, fibrinogen, lactate, BMP/renal function, LFTs, blood glucose
   - Frequent reassessment for evolving DIC or TMA (look for hemolysis markers: LDH, haptoglobin, blood smear for schistocytes)
   - Early blood cultures — but be mindful: Capnocytophaga is fastidious and often slow to grow (in this case cultures became positive only after ~7 days).
5. Imaging and surgical input to exclude necrotizing infection when there is skin/soft tissue involvement, but don’t delay antibiotics for imaging.

## Diagnostic pitfalls: culture delays and the growing role of molecular testing
Capnocytophaga can be slow-growing and may need prolonged incubation or specialized methods. Culture confirmation can take several days — in the ED/ward you must act on the clinical picture. Rapid molecular diagnostics (broad-range PCR, targeted sequencing, nanopore approaches) are increasingly available at tertiary centers and can shorten the diagnostic window; involve the microbiology lab early if you suspect an unusual zoonotic pathogen.

## When the clinical course takes a second turn: TMA after sepsis
This case reminds us that sepsis and DIC can mask superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Several clues prompted further workup here: new isolated, abrupt haemoglobin drop, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH and schistocytes on blood film, with persistent thrombocytopaenia. ADAMTS13 was not severely reduced (43.5%), stool Shiga-toxin PCR was negative, and renal biopsy showed TMA — the picture was most consistent with complement-mediated HUS triggered by infection. Management required plasma exchange and eculizumab after blood cultures were negative, plus vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis because complement blockade increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms.

A few pragmatic points:
- Consider TMA when MAHA (schistocytes, low haptoglobin, high LDH), thrombocytopaenia and AKI appear or persist beyond the initial septic phase.
- Don’t rely solely on C3/C4; they can be normal in complement-mediated HUS.
- ADAMTS13 <10% points to TTP; higher values make TTP unlikely and push you toward other TMAs.
- Starting eculizumab during active bacteremia is risky; try to ensure cultures are negative and give meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis per local protocols.

## Triage tips when qSOFA underestimates severity
qSOFA is simple but insensitive for some fulminant infections. If exposure history plus any of the red flags above are present, treat as potentially severe irrespective of qSOFA:

- Err on the side of admission and close monitoring if there’s a recent animal bite and systemic symptoms.
- Use lactate, WBC trends (especially leukopaenia), glucose derangement, coagulation abnormalities and skin findings to guide escalation.
- Early involvement of ICU/ID surgery and prompt empiric antibiotics are crucial.

## Take-home clinical pearls
- Capnocytophaga canimorsus can cause fulminant sepsis and DIC even in immunocompetent hosts; mortality can be high.
- Ask about any dog/cat exposure in septic patients — even minor bites or licks matter.
- Red flags: early leukopaenia, refractory hypoglycaemia, DIC/coagulopathy, acral ischaemia/mottling and rapidly progressive organ dysfunction.
- Empiric therapy for critically ill patients: piperacillin/tazobactam or a carbapenem; for outpatient bite prophylaxis use amoxicillin–clavulanate.
- Blood cultures may be slow to grow C. canimorsus — consider molecular diagnostics and act on clinical suspicion.
- Watch for secondary TMA (CM-HUS) after sepsis: investigate for MAHA, send ADAMTS13 and complement studies, and involve nephrology/hematology early.
- qSOFA can miss dangerous cases — incorporate exposure history, lab abnormalities and skin findings into your triage decisions.

This case is a vivid reminder: a small bite can herald big trouble. Keep the history front and centre, scan for the atypical laboratory clues, and don’t let a “low” bedside sepsis score lull you into false reassurance.



# When DIC Isn’t the Whole Story: Unmasking CM‑HUS After Sepsis

What makes this case striking is how cleanly it demonstrates a trap many clinicians fall into: a dramatic septic presentation with DIC that seems to explain everything — until it doesn’t. A previously well woman develops fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis after a dog bite, with pancytopenia, coagulopathy and dialysis‑dependent AKI. She improves from the infection, then suddenly develops an isolated hemolytic picture: falling hemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH and a peripheral smear full of schistocytes. That late‑appearing microangiopathic hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia and biopsy‑proven TMA point to complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) triggered by infection — a rare but critical diagnosis because timing of complement blockade matters for renal recovery.

Below I’ll walk through the red flags that should make you pivot from a DIC‑centric frame, the lab and biopsy pitfalls to watch for, how to balance plasma exchange vs eculizumab when infection is ongoing, and a practical monitoring plan to catch evolving TMA before irreversible kidney injury sets in.

## Why DIC can mask TMA (and vice versa)
Both DIC and TMA produce thrombocytopenia, schistocytes, organ dysfunction and abnormal coagulation/bleeding. In sepsis, DIC is the natural first explanation. But keep in mind:
- DIC usually presents with prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen and high D‑dimer from widespread clotting and consumption. Platelets fall but often recover as infection is controlled.
- TMA (including CM‑HUS) is characterised by microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), schistocytes, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin and often significant renal dysfunction from glomerular/arteriolar thrombi. Coagulation parameters are often normal or only mildly abnormal.

In this case the patient’s early picture fit DIC during sepsis; later a discrete shift to MAHA — falling hemoglobin (111→68 g/L), undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH and 15–20% schistocytes — appeared only after the inflammatory markers and coagulopathy were improving. That temporal pattern is the key red flag.

## Red flags that should make you suspect TMA/CM‑HUS after sepsis
Pay attention when any of the following occur during or after sepsis:
- New or suddenly worsening hemolysis after initial clinical improvement (falling Hb without bleeding, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH).
- Persistent thrombocytopenia despite resolution of coagulopathy (platelets stuck at 30–40×10^9/L in this case).
- Peripheral smear with schistocytes (even modest percentages matter; this case had 15–20%).
- Ongoing or worsening AKI, oliguria/anuria or dialysis dependence out of proportion to hemodynamics or hypotension history.
- Negative stool PCR for Shiga toxin and ADAMTS13 activity not in the TTP range (here ADAMTS13 was 43.5%, making TTP unlikely).
If several of these line up, start working up TMA aggressively rather than assuming prolonged DIC.

## Lab testing: what to order and how to interpret it (pitfalls)
When TMA is on your differential:
- CBC with smear (repeat smears; schistocytes can be missed), LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin, reticulocyte count.
- Coagulation panel (PT/aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer) to characterise DIC vs TMA.
- Renal function and urine output/proteinuria.
- ADAMTS13 activity (TTP if <10%): do not wait for the result to start empiric therapy if clinical suspicion of TTP is high.
- Shiga toxin PCR (stool) to exclude STEC‑HUS.
- Complement testing (C3/C4, functional assays) and samples for extended complement genetics/autoantibodies — but be cautious: normal C3/C4 does NOT rule out CM‑HUS. Complement assays can be altered by plasma exchange and by eculizumab; timing matters.
Pitfall examples from the case:
- Initial DAT was falsely positive (likely from penicillin or transfusion) but repeat DAT was negative; the platelet and hemolysis pattern pointed away from warm AIHA.
- ADAMTS13 at 43.5% effectively ruled out TTP; that shifted management.
- Extended complement testing (AH50) was low but sampling occurred after PEX and after one eculizumab dose, so results likely reflect treatment effects, not intrinsic deficiency.

Bottom line: send the right tests early, but interpret results in the context of recent plasma exchange, transfusions or complement blockade.

## When to consider kidney biopsy
A renal biopsy is most helpful when:
- Diagnosis is unclear and will change management.
- You need tissue confirmation of TMA vs other glomerulopathies.
In this case, biopsy (performed once platelets allowed) showed ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and crescents consistent with TMA and no immune complex deposition — bolstering the CM‑HUS diagnosis.

Timing considerations:
- Avoid biopsy during active, uncontrolled coagulopathy or while platelets are very low; correct coagulopathy and transfuse as needed.
- If biopsy will change therapy (for example confirming TMA when ADAMTS13 is non‑TTP and STEC is negative), proceed once safe.

## Therapeutic decisions — balancing PEX and complement blockade
Two immediate therapies commonly considered for TMA are plasma exchange (PEX) and eculizumab. The clinical dilemma is acute when infection is recent or ongoing.

Practical approach based on this case and common practice:
- If TTP is suspected clinically (high PLASMIC score or severe ADAMTS13 shortage): start urgent PEX and steroids immediately — do not delay.
- If ADAMTS13 activity comes back >10% and TTP unlikely, and you have MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI consistent with TMA, strongly consider CM‑HUS. Eculizumab is the targeted therapy for CM‑HUS and initiation early improves renal outcomes.
- PEX can be used as a bridge when diagnosis is uncertain or while arranging complement blockade and vaccinations. In this patient, transient diuresis returned after PEX but sustained recovery required eculizumab.
- If recent or ongoing bacteraemia: prefer to wait until blood cultures are negative before starting eculizumab when possible, because terminal complement blockade increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms (notably Neisseria and potentially Capnocytophaga). In this case blood cultures were negative before eculizumab was started.

Vaccination and prophylaxis:
- Vaccinate against meningococcal serogroups (MenACWY and MenB) before or as soon as possible when starting eculizumab, and provide antibiotic prophylaxis if vaccination is recent or delayed. This case received Menveo and Bexsero before induction and prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) because the vaccines had not been in place for 2 weeks.

Duration of complement blockade:
- Genetic testing for complement variants may inform duration but often returns late. Absence of known pathogenic variants does not exclude CM‑HUS caused by an infectious trigger. Decisions about stopping eculizumab should be individualised and made with nephrology and hematology follow‑up.

## Practical monitoring plan to catch evolving TMA early
For any patient with sepsis/DIC and renal dysfunction, adopt a focused monitoring protocol for at least the first 7–14 days (or longer if clinical course is complicated):
- Daily CBC with smear while platelets abnormal (or at least every 48 hours once improving). Look for falling Hb, persistent thrombocytopenia, and schistocytes.
- Daily LDH and haptoglobin when hemolysis suspected. Trending LDH/haptoglobin is more useful than single values.
- Daily creatinine, urine output recording hourly–q8h depending on clinical severity; check urine dipstick for blood/protein.
- Coagulation panel every 24–48 hours while DIC suspected; once coagulation normalises, shift emphasis to hemolysis labs.
- Send ADAMTS13 and stool Shiga PCR early if TMA suspected.
- Send baseline complement studies and samples for extended complement testing before PEX or eculizumab when feasible — but do not delay life‑saving therapy to obtain them.
- If transfusions, antibiotics (eg, beta‑lactams) or PEX have been given, annotate the timing when interpreting tests (DAT, complement assays).
- If platelets recover enough and diagnosis uncertain, arrange renal biopsy promptly — pathology can confirm TMA and guide treatment.

## Key clinical pearls from this case
- A patient with sepsis/DIC who initially improves and then develops new hemolysis should trigger urgent evaluation for superimposed TMA.
- Normal C3/C4 or a normal complement panel does not exclude CM‑HUS; complement assays can be insensitive and are easily affected by PEX or eculizumab.
- ADAMTS13 activity is essential to rule out TTP — levels >10% make TTP unlikely and should redirect towards other TMAs.
- PEX can transiently improve clinical status but may not prevent progression — definitive complement blockade with eculizumab is often required in CM‑HUS for durable response.
- When starting eculizumab in the context of recent infection, try to ensure blood cultures are negative, vaccinate against meningococcus, and give appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis.

Take‑home messages
- Think TMA when hemolysis (falling Hb, high LDH, undetectable haptoglobin) appears or worsens after the initial septic/DIC phase.
- Send ADAMTS13, stool Shiga PCR and complement workup early; interpret results in context of transfusions, PEX and timing.
- Renal biopsy is highly useful when safe to perform and can confirm TMA.
- Use PEX as a bridge when diagnosis is uncertain; start eculizumab promptly for CM‑HUS when infection is controlled and after counselling about meningococcal risk, vaccination and prophylaxis.
- Close, protocolised monitoring (daily CBC/smear, LDH/haptoglobin, creatinine/urine output) is critical to detect evolving TMA before irreversible kidney injury occurs.

This Capnocytophaga canimorsus case is a great reminder: sepsis and DIC may be the obvious diagnosis, but they don’t rule out a second process. Keep your eyes on the labs and your mind open to TMA — protecting kidney function often depends on catching the shift early.



# TMA on the Clock: PLASMIC, ADAMTS13, and the Bridge to the Right Therapy

What makes this case interesting? A previously well woman in her late 40s is bitten by her dog, develops fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis with DIC, then—after initial improvement—develops new, isolated haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia and dialysis-dependent AKI. Peripheral smear shows 15–20% schistocytes, haptoglobin is gone, LDH rises, DAT turns out to be a false positive, and ADAMTS13 comes back at 43.5% (ruling out TTP). Kidney biopsy confirms thrombotic microangiopathy (fibrin thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli, crescents). The diagnosis: infection-triggered complement-mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). She received plasma exchange (PEX) as a bridge and then eculizumab after blood cultures were negative, with partial renal recovery.

This is a classic diagnostic and therapeutic crossroads: when you are faced with MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI, you must move fast—decisions made before formal test results can save lives. Here’s a practical, stepwise approach grounded in the case.

## Stepwise approach when TMA is on the differential

1) Rapid clinical triage: think TTP first because it is the most time-critical.
- Calculate a PLASMIC score while you work up the patient. A high score supports empiric PEX; a low score lowers immediate suspicion but doesn’t rule it out.
- In this case the PLASMIC score was 4/7 (low pretest probability), but clinical concern remained because of ongoing MAHA and severe AKI.

2) Send the right tests immediately (before you change the blood):
- ADAMTS13 activity (urgent): severe deficiency (<10%) confirms TTP and mandates urgent daily PEX ± steroids, and usually rituximab.
- CBC with smear (look for schistocytes), haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, reticulocyte count.
- Coagulation panel (to assess DIC vs TMA—both can coexist).
- Blood cultures (sepsis can mimic or trigger TMA; in this case they grew C. canimorsus).
- Stool PCR for Shiga toxin (rule out STEC‑HUS).
- Basic complement tests (C3/C4) and, if possible, send samples for extended complement testing and genetic testing—ideally before PEX or complement blockade.

3) Start empiric therapy while you wait for ADAMTS13:
- If TTP is possible (high PLASMIC or strong clinical suspicion), start PEX immediately—do not wait for ADAMTS13. PEX can be lifesaving in TTP.
- If infection is suspected or confirmed, treat aggressively with appropriate antibiotics and supportive care (this patient received piperacillin/tazobactam then ampicillin and later oral phenoxymethylpenicillin once cultures allowed).
- Remember that PEX is a bridge, not a definitive therapy for CM‑HUS—its benefit in CM‑HUS is transient.

4) Reassess when ADAMTS13 and microbiology results return:
- ADAMTS13 <10% → TTP: continue PEX and treat for TTP.
- ADAMTS13 >10% (as here, 43.5%) → TTP unlikely, pivot to alternative causes (STEC vs CM‑HUS vs other secondary TMA). If stool PCR is negative for Shiga toxin and there is renal biopsy evidence of TMA with ongoing haemolysis, CM‑HUS becomes more likely.
- In this case, ADAMTS13 effectively ruled out TTP and stool PCR ruled out STEC.

5) When to use eculizumab (complement blockade):
- Eculizumab is the treatment that changes outcomes in CM‑HUS; earlier initiation is associated with better renal recovery.
- But eculizumab blocks terminal complement and increases risk for overwhelming infection with encapsulated organisms (Neisseria spp., and potentially others like Capnocytophaga). So timing matters.
- Practical strategy:
  - Ideally obtain diagnostic blood for ADAMTS13 and complement studies before PEX or eculizumab.
  - If active, uncontrolled bacteraemia is present, treat the infection first and wait for cultures to clear if clinically possible. In this case eculizumab was started after blood cultures became negative.
  - If delaying eculizumab risks irreversible renal damage and infection is controlled with antibiotics, proceed with vaccination (meningococcal ACWY + B) and give prophylactic antibiotics if vaccines cannot reach effective immunity before starting therapy. In this case the patient received Menveo and Bexsero and was given phenoxymethylpenicillin prophylaxis, then eculizumab induction (900 mg IV weekly ×4) and later maintenance (1200 mg IV q2 weeks).
  - If you have already started PEX, remember PEX removes eculizumab—coordinate timing (either give eculizumab after PEX is paused or be aware that repeated PEX will reduce its effect). In this case PEX was used as a bridge; eculizumab was given after PEX and after cultures were negative.

6) Use kidney biopsy when it will change management:
- If diagnosis remains uncertain and platelet counts recover enough to allow safe biopsy, renal histology can confirm TMA (fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli, crescents) and help exclude immune-complex or other primary glomerulopathies. In this patient biopsy after platelet recovery showed TMA and helped justify ongoing complement blockade.
- Timing: biopsy is most useful once bleeding risk is acceptable (platelets typically >50 ×10^9/L depending on local practice).

## Practical pitfalls illustrated by the case

- Don’t be reassured by normal C3/C4—complement levels can be normal in CM‑HUS. Normal C3/C4 does not exclude complement-mediated disease.
- PEX and eculizumab alter complement assays and ADAMTS13—draw diagnostic samples before these therapies, whenever possible. In this case extended complement testing was done after PEX and eculizumab, and the low AH50 likely reflected treatment effects.
- A positive DAT (direct Coombs) can be misleading. Drug-induced or transfusion-related false positives occur (penicillin can cause false-positive DAT). The repeat DAT was negative and the peripheral smear was classic for MAHA (schistocytes).
- Sepsis and DIC can mask or even trigger TMA. New haemolysis after the sepsis starts improving is a red flag for superimposed TMA/CM‑HUS.

## How I think about the immediate algorithm at the bedside
1. Patient with MAHA + thrombocytopenia ± AKI → send ADAMTS13 stat, blood cultures, stool Shiga PCR, CBC + smear, haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, coagulation, complement labs; consider genetic complement testing if CM‑HUS suspected.
2. If PLASMIC suggests TTP or clinical picture is concerning for TTP → start PEX immediately and give steroids.
3. If ADAMTS13 returns >10% and STEC negative → consider CM‑HUS: ensure infection is controlled, vaccinate if planning eculizumab, consider PEX only as a temporary measure, start eculizumab when safe and indicated.
4. If uncertain and platelets recover → consider renal biopsy to confirm TMA and guide prognosis/therapy intensity.

## Case-specific learning points
- Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an unusual but documented trigger of CM‑HUS in immunocompetent adults; the infectious trigger can precede the haemolytic picture by days, sometimes surfacing only after the sepsis starts to settle.
- ADAMTS13=43.5% in this case excluded TTP. Schistocytes 15–20%, undetectable haptoglobin and rising LDH clinched MAHA. Negative Shiga PCR and biopsy-proven TMA plus clinical response to eculizumab supported CM‑HUS.
- PEX helped transiently; sustained haematologic and renal improvement came only after complement blockade. Immunization and antibiotic prophylaxis were used in tandem with eculizumab to mitigate infectious risk.

## Key take-home messages
- Early triage with PLASMIC and urgent ADAMTS13 testing saves lives—start PEX when TTP is possible; stop or de-escalate if ADAMTS13 >10%.
- Send stool PCR for Shiga toxin early to exclude STEC‑HUS.
- Obtain ADAMTS13 and complement samples before PEX or eculizumab if you can; interpret post‑treatment results with caution.
- Eculizumab is the therapy that changes outcomes in CM‑HUS but weigh timing against active infection risk—vaccinate and provide prophylactic antibiotics if immediate blockade is needed.
- Renal biopsy, when feasible after platelet recovery, is a powerful tool to confirm TMA and aid management decisions.

This case is a vivid reminder: when TMA is suspected, act quickly, send the right tests early, bridge safely with PEX when needed, and use targeted therapy (eculizumab) once the diagnosis and infectious risks are addressed.



# Eculizumab in the Hot Zone: Starting Complement Blockade Around Active Infection

What makes this case interesting? A previously healthy woman develops fulminant sepsis after a dog bite from Capnocytophaga canimorsus, evolves into dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury, and then—after an initial clinical improvement—develops a thrombotic microangiopathy consistent with complement‑mediated HUS. Eculizumab ultimately produces haematologic remission and partial renal recovery. The clinical tension here is obvious: eculizumab can be kidney‑saving in CM‑HUS but disables a crucial arm of host defence against encapsulated organisms. When your patient is recovering from—or still being treated for—an invasive bacterial infection, when do you pull the trigger on complement blockade? This post walks through practical, bedside decisions: timing, vaccine logistics, antibiotic prophylaxis options, how PEX affects everything, and how to mitigate infectious risk without unnecessarily delaying organ‑saving therapy.

Why the dilemma matters
- Terminal complement blockade (eculizumab) markedly reduces morbidity and improves renal outcomes in CM‑HUS, particularly when started early.
- But it removes critical defence against encapsulated bacteria—most classically Neisseria meningitidis—and also raises concern for other encapsulated pathogens (the case here highlights Capnocytophaga canimorsus).
- Starting eculizumab during active bacteraemia risks uncontrolled infection; delaying it risks irreversible kidney injury. There is no one‑size‑fits‑all answer, but there are pragmatic, evidence‑informed steps to reduce harm.

Principles to guide timing
- Ideal: Obtain source control, ensure blood cultures are negative (or robustly improving on appropriate antibiotics), vaccinate, then start eculizumab. In practice this ideal is sometimes unattainable because CM‑HUS progression can be rapid.
- If the bloodstream infection is ongoing and culture‑positive for an encapsulated organism, aim to clear bacteremia (documented negative cultures and clinical improvement) before complement blockade whenever feasible.
- If CM‑HUS is rapidly progressive and waiting materially risks irreversible organ loss, you may need to start eculizumab sooner—accept the infectious risk but mitigate it aggressively (see below) and involve Infectious Diseases early.

Vaccine logistics — what, when, and why it matters
- Minimum recommended vaccines before eculizumab: MenACWY (conjugate quadrivalent for serogroups A, C, W, Y) and MenB (serogroup B). These target the primary meningococcal serogroups of concern.
- Timing: aim for vaccination ≥14 days before first eculizumab dose to allow protective antibody generation. This is the ideal.
- If you cannot wait: give both MenACWY and MenB immediately and do not delay eculizumab if clinical urgency dictates. Understand that immunity will not be mature for ~2 weeks.
- Practical caveat: plasma exchange (PEX) removes circulating antibodies. If PEX is ongoing or planned, vaccine antibody titers may be blunted or removed—document timing carefully. If you must vaccinate while PEX is ongoing, plan for revaccination/boosting and prolonged antibiotic prophylaxis; consult ID.

Antibiotic prophylaxis strategies when you can’t wait
- Rationale: bridge the vulnerable window between vaccine administration and development of protective immunity, and mitigate immediate risk when eculizumab is started during or shortly after infection.
- Short‑term bridging (until 2 weeks after vaccine series or until cultures are convincingly sterile): many centres use oral beta‑lactam prophylaxis (phenoxymethylpenicillin/penicillin V or amoxicillin) while on eculizumab if the organism is susceptible and the patient can take oral drugs. In the reported case the team used phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin).
- Typical practical options (adjust to local formularies, susceptibilities and allergies; check local guidance and ID input):
  - Oral penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin) 250–500 mg twice daily, or
  - Amoxicillin 500 mg two–three times daily.
  - If severe penicillin allergy: discuss alternatives with ID—options may include azithromycin (limited meningococcal coverage), or targeted agents guided by local susceptibility; some centres consider ciprofloxacin as a single‑dose post‑exposure option but not ideal for continuous prophylaxis.
- Single‑dose or short‑course options (useful when immediate rapid protection is needed or for non‑compliant patients):
  - Single dose ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO (used for meningococcal post‑exposure prophylaxis in adults), or
  - Single IM ceftriaxone (250–500 mg) — useful if adherence or oral therapy is a concern.
  - Rifampin 600 mg PO twice daily for 2 days is another contact prophylaxis regimen, but rifampin has many drug interactions and is not ideal for ongoing prophylaxis.
- Duration: at minimum until 2 weeks after vaccination if vaccines were given immediately prior to eculizumab. In practice, many centres continue antibiotic prophylaxis for the duration of complement blockade or until ID advises otherwise—there is no universally accepted standard. Shared decision‑making with nephrology/haematology/ID is key.

Special considerations with plasma exchange (PEX) and timing
- PEX removes both eculizumab and vaccine‑induced antibodies. If you are doing PEX:
  - Avoid scheduling vaccination immediately before a PEX session if possible.
  - If PEX cannot be avoided, expect reduced vaccine efficacy; plan for revaccination/boosting after PEX and prolonged antibiotic bridging.
  - Similarly, if you give eculizumab and then perform PEX, eculizumab levels fall and re‑dosing may be required—coordinate dosing with the team performing PEX.
- Practically: if eculizumab must be administered while PEX is ongoing, give the dose after the PEX session and expect to monitor drug levels/clinical response.

Microbiology nuance: Capnocytophaga canimorsus
- C. canimorsus is an encapsulated, slow‑growing gram‑negative rod from dog/cat mouths. It can produce fulminant sepsis and DIC and, as in this case, has been reported rarely to trigger CM‑HUS.
- Because it is encapsulated and complement is important for host defence against encapsulated organisms, complement blockade raises theoretical and practical concerns about worsening infection. In the reported case, the team waited for negative blood cultures and gave vaccines before initiating eculizumab—an approach that balances infection control with need for complement blockade.

Practical bedside tactics and safety nets
- Get blood cultures (and other appropriate cultures) before starting or changing antibiotics. Aim for documented clearance of bacteremia when possible.
- Involve Infectious Diseases early. If you are unsure which prophylactic regimen to use, get ID input.
- If vaccination cannot precede eculizumab by 2 weeks, give MenACWY + MenB immediately and start antibiotic prophylaxis; document why the ideal interval could not be achieved.
- Provide the patient with education and an action plan: any fever or meningitis‑type symptom requires urgent evaluation. Consider giving an emergency supply of antibiotics (local practice variable).
- Document vaccination dates, lot numbers and discussions about infectious risk in the chart; provide the patient with written vaccine and safety information.
- Recognize that even vaccinated patients on eculizumab remain at higher risk for meningococcal disease; vigilance and low threshold for evaluation are warranted.
- Coordinate with nephrology/haematology for PEX timing and with pharmacy for eculizumab logistics and meningococcal vaccine availability.

When to stop prophylactic antibiotics and how long to continue eculizumab
- Duration of eculizumab depends on the underlying diagnosis (genetic complement variants, severity, renal recovery). In CM‑HUS, some patients require prolonged or indefinite therapy; others can discontinue after disease quiescence with close monitoring.
- If eculizumab is continued long term, consider ongoing vaccination (boosters per national schedules) and discuss the pros/cons of continued antibiotic prophylaxis with ID. In the reported case, the treating team continued eculizumab for months with prophylactic penicillin while monitoring renal function.
- If you stop eculizumab, maintain a plan for relapse monitoring and re‑vaccination status.

Bottom line — balancing risks and benefits
- The decision to start eculizumab in the setting of recent or ongoing infection is nuanced. Ideal strategy: clear bacteremia, vaccinate ≥2 weeks, then treat. When urgency precludes that, vaccinate immediately, start antibiotic prophylaxis, coordinate with ID and PEX teams, and proceed with eculizumab to avoid irreversible organ damage.
- Clear documentation, patient counselling, and close follow‑up are essential.

Key take‑home messages
- Eculizumab can be organ‑saving in CM‑HUS, but initiation around active infection requires balancing infection risk against the risk of irreversible kidney injury.
- Aim to start eculizumab after bacteremia is cleared and after MenACWY + MenB vaccination ≥14 days prior when clinically feasible.
- If you cannot wait: give MenACWY + MenB immediately, start antibiotic prophylaxis (commonly oral penicillin V or amoxicillin; alternatives and single‑dose options available), and involve Infectious Diseases early.
- PEX complicates timing: it removes vaccine antibodies and eculizumab—coordinate dosing and consider revaccination or prolonged prophylaxis.
- Provide patients with clear instructions, vaccination documentation, and urgent evaluation plans for fever or meningitic symptoms; consider an emergency antibiotic supply in selected cases.



# The Misleading Coombs: Drug-Related DAT Positivity and Hemolysis Pitfalls

What if a positive Coombs test (direct antiglobulin test, DAT) is the red herring that sends you down the wrong treatment path? That’s exactly what happened in a dramatic ICU case: a previously well woman developed fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis after a dog bite, became anuric and dialysis-dependent, then—after initial improvement—suddenly had a steep fall in hemoglobin with an initially positive DAT. High‑dose steroids were started for presumed autoimmune hemolysis. But the picture didn’t add up: platelets stayed low, haptoglobin fell to undetectable, LDH rose, and the peripheral smear showed 15–20% schistocytes. The DAT later reverted to negative. The true diagnosis was thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) — complement‑mediated HUS — and the patient improved only after plasma exchange and eculizumab.

This case is a great reminder that a positive DAT is not the final word. Let’s walk through why DATs mislead, how beta‑lactams and transfusions cause false positives, how to approach hemolysis in the critically ill, and how to recognize when MAHA/TMA should be prioritized over “autoimmune hemolysis.”

## Why a positive DAT can be misleading

The DAT detects IgG and/or complement (C3) bound to red blood cells. It tells you antibody or complement is attached — not necessarily that clinically important autoimmune hemolysis is happening. In critically ill patients the DAT can be positive for multiple benign or transient reasons:

- Recent transfusion: passively transferred allo- or auto‑antibodies can produce a positive DAT without causing brisk hemolysis.
- Beta‑lactam antibiotics (especially penicillins, cephalosporins, piperacillin): these can produce a positive DAT by several mechanisms (drug‑dependent antibodies, immune‑complex mediated hemolysis, or non‑immunologic protein adsorption). The DAT can be falsely positive even without clinically significant hemolysis.
- Passive adsorption of complement during severe inflammation or sepsis.
- Technical and reagent issues (less common).

In the case above the patient had received piperacillin/tazobactam and later ampicillin/phenoxymethylpenicillin — plausible culprits for a transient DAT positivity. The initial DAT was IgG positive but later became negative, consistent with a drug‑ or transfusion‑related effect rather than ongoing autoimmune hemolysis.

## How to interpret the DAT in context — don’t anchor

A DAT should be one data point in a structured hemolysis assessment. Before committing to steroids or long immunosuppression, reconcile the DAT with the rest of the clinical and laboratory picture.

Important context to collect and interpret:
- Hemolysis labs: LDH (↑), indirect bilirubin (↑), haptoglobin (↓), hemoglobin drop, urine hemoglobin/hemoglobinuria.
- Reticulocyte count: is there an appropriate marrow response?
- Peripheral smear: schistocytes point to microangiopathic (mechanical) RBC destruction; spherocytes suggest warm autoimmune hemolysis.
- Clinical clues: timing relative to drugs/transfusion, sepsis, DIC, renal failure, thrombocytopenia.
- Platelets: persistent thrombocytopenia favors TMA/DIC over isolated autoimmune hemolysis.
- ADAMTS13 activity and Shiga toxin testing when TMA suspected.

In our patient the initial picture during fulminant sepsis showed pancytopenia and normal haptoglobin with non‑compensatory reticulocytes — understandable given marrow suppression from sepsis. After an initial clinical improvement the patient developed abrupt Hb fall, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH and persistent thrombocytopenia — a classic MAHA pattern. The peripheral smear clinched it (high schistocyte burden). The transient DAT was likely drug/transfusion‑related and misdirected early management.

## Reticulocytes can lie — especially in sepsis and marrow stress

Reticulocytosis is the expected marrow response to hemolysis, but in critical illness this can be blunted:

- Sepsis, severe inflammation, bone marrow suppression (eg, leukopenia/pancytopenia), malnutrition or prior transfusion can produce a non‑compensatory reticulocyte count despite active hemolysis.
- Early in immune hemolysis retic rise may lag.
- In this case, leukopenia and pancytopenia during the septic phase meant the retic response was unreliable. Rely more on hemolysis chemistry and smear.

So: a “normal” or low retic count does not exclude significant hemolysis in the critically ill.

## The peripheral smear is your friend — prioritize schistocytes when MAHA is on the table

A high schistocyte percentage (even modest counts >1%–2% can be significant in the right setting) is direct evidence of microangiopathic fragmentation. In our patient the smear showed 15–20% schistocytes — incontrovertible. If you suspect TMA, get an urgent smear and ADAMTS13, and don’t let a positive DAT alone keep you from pursuing TMA workup.

## Practical hemolysis workup in the critically ill (workflow)

1. Rapid screen: Hb, platelets, LDH, bilirubin (total/indirect), haptoglobin, retic count, peripheral smear, DAT.
2. Reconcile the DAT with other data: if DAT+ but no biochemical hemolysis, consider drug/transfusion effect; observe and reassess.
3. If biochemical hemolysis present (↑LDH, ↓haptoglobin) and schistocytes on smear, treat as MAHA/TMA until proven otherwise:
   - Send ADAMTS13 activity (TTP if <10%).
   - Send Shiga toxin testing if diarrheal prodrome.
   - Consult hematology and nephrology early.
4. If TTP suspected, start plasma exchange emergently; if ADAMTS13 ruled out and TMA persists with AKI, consider complement-mediated HUS and discuss plasma exchange + eculizumab, balancing infection risk.
5. Investigate recent drugs (beta‑lactams, cephalosporins), transfusions, and infections that may cause DAT positivity or trigger TMA.

## Don’t reflexively give steroids for every positive DAT

Warm autoimmune hemolysis is steroid‑responsive — but if the hemolysis is actually MAHA/TMA, steroids won’t help and may cause harm. In the case, steroids were started after an initial positive DAT; hemoglobin did not respond and platelets remained low. The smear ultimately redirected care. Key points:

- If DAT+ with spherocytes and a retic rise → consider warm AIHA and treat with steroids.
- If DAT+ but schistocytes, thrombocytopenia, AKI, rising LDH and low haptoglobin → prioritize TMA. Steroids are not the answer.
- Consider stopping offending drugs (eg, beta‑lactams) if a drug‑related DAT is suspected, but only after balancing infection control needs.

## When TMA is the diagnosis — pragmatic lessons from the case

This patient’s course underscores several management lessons:
- TMA can emerge or become apparent as sepsis and DIC resolve — keep reassessing.
- ADAMTS13 testing is essential to exclude TTP; a level of 43.5% here argued against TTP.
- Complement studies (C3/C4 and pathway assays) can be normal even in complement‑mediated HUS — don’t use normal C3/C4 to exclude CM‑HUS.
- Plasma exchange can provide temporary stabilization (bridging) while arranging definitive therapy.
- Eculizumab is highly effective for CM‑HUS but increases risk for encapsulated infections. In this case eculizumab was started after blood cultures cleared; meningococcal vaccination and prophylactic antibiotics were used per standard precautions.

## Bottom line — how to avoid the misled Coombs trap

- A positive DAT is not diagnostic of clinically significant autoimmune hemolysis. Always interpret the DAT in the full clinical and laboratory context.
- Beta‑lactams and recent transfusions commonly cause transient DAT positivity; consider these before committing to steroids.
- In the critically ill, prioritize hemolysis chemistry and the peripheral smear. Schistocytes + MAHA pattern + thrombocytopenia should push TMA high on your differential.
- Reticulocyte counts can be suppressed by sepsis/marrow stress — a normal retic does not exclude active hemolysis.
- If TMA is suspected, get ADAMTS13 and start appropriate hematology/nephrology interventions early (PEX for TTP, consider complement blockade for CM‑HUS), balancing infectious risks.

Key takeaways (short and sharp)
- DAT = evidence of RBC‑bound Ig/complement, not proof of pathogenic autoimmune hemolysis.
- Beta‑lactams and transfusions can give false‑positive DATs; correlate with hemolysis labs.
- Prioritize the peripheral smear: schistocytes = think MAHA/TMA, not warm AIHA.
- Normal reticulocyte count in sepsis ≠ no hemolysis; marrow suppression blunts response.
- Don’t reflexively give steroids for every positive DAT — reassess rapidly and avoid delaying TMA workup/therapy.

If you see a patient with hemolysis in the ICU, don’t let the Coombs test be an anchor — look at the whole picture, especially the smear.



# Normal C3, Sick Kidneys: Getting Complement Testing Right in Suspected CM‑HUS

What makes this case interesting: a previously healthy woman develops fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis after a dog bite, then—after apparent stabilization—develops new microangiopathic hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia and dialysis‑dependent kidney injury. Initial routine complement tests (C3/C4) were normal, ADAMTS13 was not consistent with TTP, and kidney biopsy showed thrombotic microangiopathy. The team started plasma exchange (PEX) and then eculizumab with clinical improvement. Extended testing later showed a markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50) — but that was measured after PEX and the first eculizumab dose, so probably a treatment effect. No pathogenic complement gene variants were identified.

This is a great “teach” because it highlights three hard truths we see in the ED/ICU/nephrology interface:
- Normal C3/C4 does NOT rule out complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS).
- PEX and eculizumab distort complement assays (and autoantibody tests).
- Genetic testing is useful but delayed — don’t wait to treat.

Below I’ll walk through which assays to ask for, how to handle specimens and timing, how to interpret results in the context of prior PEX/biologics, and practical decision‑making for infection‑triggered CM‑HUS.

## Quick clinical snapshot (from the case)
A woman in her late 40s: dog bite → severe sepsis with DIC, pancytopenia and AKI → initial improvement on antibiotics and supportive care → new isolated hemolysis (schistocytes, undetectable haptoglobin, ↑LDH), persistent thrombocytopenia, dialysis dependence → ADAMTS13 43.5% (not TTP), stool Shiga PCR negative → kidney biopsy: TMA → PEX then eculizumab with recovery of diuresis and hematologic remission. C3/C4 were normal early; AH50 was low only after PEX and drug exposure. Extended genetics negative.

Use that timeline to anchor testing and interpretation.

## Which complement and related tests to order (ideally up‑front)
When you suspect CM‑HUS, collect both routine and speciality samples before you change the complement milieu (PEX/egress of antibodies) or give complement blockade if at all feasible:

- Routine: C3, C4 (fast, local)
- Functional assays: CH50 (classical pathway) and AH50 (alternative pathway) — serum required
- Complement proteins: Factor H, Factor I, Factor B, C3 (quantitative)
- Anti‑factor H autoantibodies (ELISA)
- Terminal pathway activation marker: sC5b‑9 (plasma/serum; tells you ongoing terminal activation)
- ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor (to exclude TTP)
- Shiga toxin PCR/stool if STEC risk
- Peripheral smear, haptoglobin, LDH, retic count, DAT to phenotype the hemolysis
- Blood for complement genetics panel (CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, MCP/CD46, THBD, and others as per lab) — this is DNA and can be drawn anytime; ask the reference lab what they want (EDTA tube usually)

Important: many of the specialised complement tests are send‑outs; talk to your reference lab early.

## Specimen handling — small stuff that matters
- Functional assays (CH50/AH50) need serum — draw in a plain (red) tube, keep at room temperature, spin and separate promptly, and ship on ice per lab instructions. Avoid multiple freeze‑thaw cycles.
- sC5b‑9 is labile; follow courier instructions (often EDTA plasma or serum on ice).
- Anti‑factor H antibodies: serum (ELISA), best before PEX or immunosuppression.
- Genetic testing: EDTA whole blood; DNA is stable — PEX does not affect genotype.
- Document the exact timing of draws relative to PEX and eculizumab on the request form.

## How PEX and eculizumab ruin (and sometimes help) your tests — the common pitfalls

PEX
- Removes autoantibodies (eg, anti‑factor H), complement proteins and drug (eculizumab if already given) — can create false negatives for autoantibodies and transiently alter quantitative factor levels.
- Replacement fluid (eg, Octaplas/pool plasma) delivers complement proteins and can artificially normalize certain assays immediately after exchange.
- Functional assays performed shortly after PEX may be misleading (either depressed because of removal, or spuriously normal if plasma replacement provided factors).

Eculizumab (anti‑C5)
- Blocks terminal pathway; CH50/AH50 will be markedly depressed after dosing because the assay measures ability to lyse via terminal components.
- sC5b‑9 levels typically drop after complement blockade.
- Anti‑factor H antibody testing is unaffected by drug per se, but prior PEX can remove antibodies.
- Critically: a low CH50/AH50 after eculizumab does not tell you why the patient had TMA — it mostly confirms pharmacodynamic effect.

Timing in the real world
- If you have to start PEX or eculizumab emergently, draw complement and antibody samples first if possible.
- If you must start therapy before sampling, document timing and interpret results in that context — many labs will note “sample collected after PEX/eculizumab; results may be affected.”

In the presented case: basic C3/C4 were normal before PEX; AH50 was measured 8 days after PEX and 4 days after the first eculizumab dose and was markedly reduced (6%) — most consistent with treatment effects, not intrinsic complement depletion.

## Interpreting normal C3/C4
- Normal C3 and C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS. Complement activation in CM‑HUS is often local (endothelial microenvironment) or may not be reflected by plasma C3/C4.
- Some patients with genetic defects have normal baseline C3/C4.
- Use functional pathway assays (AH50/CH50), sC5b‑9, clinical features and kidney biopsy when C3/C4 are non‑revealing.

## Practical workflow for suspected CM‑HUS (reasonable and pragmatic)
1. Rapid bedside evaluation: MAHA (schistocytes, ↑LDH, low haptoglobin), thrombocytopenia, AKI. Exclude obvious DIC bleed source, drug causes.
2. Order immediate tests: CBC, smear, retic, haptoglobin, LDH, coagulation panel, creatinine/urine, C3/C4, CH50/AH50 (if available quickly), ADAMTS13, blood cultures, Shiga PCR.
3. Draw additional specialised samples (anti‑FH, FH/FB/FI) and genetics blood before PEX or eculizumab if you can safely delay therapy to collect them.
4. If TTP suspected strongly (high PLASMIC score), start PEX immediately; draw ADAMTS13 before first exchange if possible.
5. If TTP unlikely and there is strong suspicion for CM‑HUS (biopsy, persistent renal failure, schistocytes, exclusion of STEC/TTP), consider early eculizumab — but balance infection risk. If active bacteremia, obtain culture clearance and vaccinate when possible; give prophylactic antibiotics if starting eculizumab before full vaccination immune protection.
6. Document timing of all interventions relative to sample collection and inform the reference lab — they often annotate results accordingly.

## What about genetics and treatment duration?
- Genetic testing helps prognosticate and guide duration of complement blockade: pathogenic variants (eg, CFH, CFI, CFB, C3) favour longer/indefinite therapy; no variant and a clear, single infectious trigger may allow planned cessation after a period of stability.
- Turnaround for genetic panels is weeks to months. Do not delay eculizumab awaiting genetics.
- In infection‑triggered CM‑HUS without identified pathogenic variants, many centres continue eculizumab for a finite period (months) with careful weaning and close outpatient monitoring for relapse; there are no universally agreed durations. In the case above, the team continued maintenance dosing for 4 months post‑induction and planned reassessment.
- If you stop therapy, educate the patient about relapse signs and have rapid access to reinitiation.

## How to read a post‑treatment complement panel
- Low CH50/AH50 after eculizumab: expected — confirms pharmacologic blockade, not diagnostic of intrinsic deficiency.
- Low AH50 after recent PEX: could be removal of proteins or residual PEX effect; check timing and replacement fluids.
- Anti‑factor H antibodies negative after PEX: might be false negative if antibodies were removed — repeat testing a few days after last PEX before declaring absent.
- Normal factors H/I/B after many PEX sessions with Octaplas: watch for confounding by donor plasma.

In our case the reduced AH50 measured after PEX + eculizumab was most compatible with treatment effects. The meaningful pieces that supported CM‑HUS were the clinical course (new MAHA and persistent thrombocytopenia after early sepsis subsided), kidney biopsy showing TMA, exclusion of STEC and TTP, and sustained response after complement blockade.

## Clinical pearls
- Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS. Think beyond C3/C4.
- Always draw baseline complement and antibody studies before PEX or eculizumab when it’s safe to do so — and get the genetic sample (EDTA) early.
- Tell the lab exactly when PEX and eculizumab were given — interpretation hinges on timing.
- ADAMTS13 <10% = TTP. If higher, pivot your differential.
- Don’t wait for genetics to treat; do reassess genetics later to inform long‑term therapy.
- In infection‑triggered CM‑HUS, try to clear infection and vaccinate before starting complement blockade when possible; if you must start earlier, give antibiotic prophylaxis and close monitoring.

Key take‑home messages
- Normal C3/C4 do NOT rule out CM‑HUS; functional assays (CH50/AH50), factor H/I/B, anti‑factor H antibodies and sC5b‑9 provide complementary information.
- PEX and eculizumab substantially alter complement test results; whenever possible, obtain baseline samples before these therapies.
- Genetic testing is essential for prognosis and long‑term management but is delayed — treat the patient, not the panel.
- Use the clinical trajectory (biopsy, response to eculizumab, exclusion of TTP/STEC) to guide duration of complement blockade in infection‑triggered CM‑HUS without pathogenic variants.

If you’re facing this situation at 0300 with dialysis‑dependent AKI and schistocytes, prioritize: smear + ADAMTS13 + blood cultures + baseline complement/autoantibody draws (if you can), then treat clinically. The labs will help you refine therapy later — but they often won’t change your early, life‑saving decisions.
