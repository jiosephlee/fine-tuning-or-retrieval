Title: Blog about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

# When DIC Isn’t the Whole Story: Spotting CM‑HUS After Sepsis

Sepsis does weird things. It provokes coagulopathy, multiorgan failure, and can look like every haematology or nephrology emergency rolled into one. But sometimes the story doesn’t end when the infection starts to get under control. This case — a previously healthy woman who developed fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis with DIC, then later developed isolated hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia and dialysis‑dependent AKI — is a perfect reminder: don’t anchor on DIC. A superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), specifically complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS), can be hiding in plain sight.

Why this is surprising (and dangerous)
- Early sepsis with DIC produces cytopenias, raised LDH, and organ dysfunction — many features overlap with TMA. In a busy ED/ICU, it’s easy to attribute evolving anaemia or low platelets to ongoing sepsis, transfusions, or drug effects.
- In this patient, haemolysis (falling Hb, absent haptoglobin, rising LDH), schistocytes on the peripheral smear, and persistent thrombocytopenia appeared only after systemic inflammation and coagulopathy had started to improve. That temporal pattern is key: infection improves → new/worsening MAHA + thrombocytopenia + worsening AKI = red flag for superimposed TMA.
- Missing CM‑HUS matters. Timely complement blockade (eculizumab) often determines renal recovery; delay risks permanent dialysis dependence. But starting eculizumab during active bloodstream infection is also fraught. You have to balance timing, vaccination, and antibiotic coverage.

How to think through “persistent thrombocytopenia + new hemolysis after sepsis”
1. Reassess the timeline
   - Is the haemolysis new or ongoing from the start? In this case haemolysis appeared after sepsis began to settle — classic for a second hit (infection‑triggered TMA).
2. Look for microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA)
   - Labs to prioritize: Hb trend, reticulocyte count, haptoglobin, LDH, indirect bilirubin, peripheral smear for schistocytes.
   - Schistocytes (≥1% and especially ≥15–20% of fragmented cells reported here) are direct evidence of mechanical RBC destruction and should prompt urgent TMA workup.
3. Don’t rely on C3/C4 to rule CM‑HUS in or out
   - Complement levels are often normal in CM‑HUS. Normal C3/C4 does not exclude disease.
4. Rule out competing TMA diagnoses quickly
   - ADAMTS13 activity to exclude TTP (TTP almost always <10%): use clinical scores (PLASMIC) while awaiting labs but send assay early.
   - Shiga toxin PCR/stool testing to exclude STEC‑HUS.
   - Consider HIT, DIC evolution, severe sepsis‑related consumptive processes — but MAHA with schistocytes points toward a TMA.
5. Tissue helps when safe
   - Kidney biopsy can be diagnostic (fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli, crescents) when noninvasive testing is equivocal and when biopsy risk is acceptable.
6. Treatment pragmatics in infection‑triggered CM‑HUS
   - Plasma exchange (PEX) may temporize and is reasonable while excluding TTP, but PEX is not definitive for CM‑HUS.
   - Eculizumab is the specific therapy for CM‑HUS and can induce haematological remission and renal recovery — earlier is better.
   - If infection is ongoing, delay eculizumab until bacteremia is controlled when possible, because terminal complement blockade increases risk from encapsulated organisms; vaccinate (meningococcal A/C/Y/W and B) and give antibiotic prophylaxis if starting eculizumab without prior immunisation.
   - In this case, blood cultures turned negative, vaccination was given, and eculizumab was started — haematology and kidney function then improved.
7. Complement genetics and functional testing
   - Genetic variants may be present in 50–70% of CM‑HUS cases, but their absence doesn’t rule the diagnosis out; many cases are triggered by infection alone.
   - Extended functional assays can be confounded by recent PEX or eculizumab; timing matters for interpretation.

Practical triggers to reassess DIC → think TMA
- New or worsening anaemia with low haptoglobin and high LDH after apparent improvement of sepsis.
- Persistent thrombocytopenia that does not correct as DIC resolves.
- Schistocytes on peripheral smear.
- Worsening or dialysis‑dependent AKI despite sepsis control.
- Positive DAT that is inconsistent with clinical course (drug interference, transfusion effects) — don’t let a single positive Coombs assay close the case.
- ADAMTS13 >10% (especially >20–30%) — TTP unlikely; think other TMAs including CM‑HUS.
- Negative stool Shiga toxin PCR in the absence of diarrhoeal prodrome — STEC unlikely.

Clinical pearls
- Schistocytes are not subtle: ask the lab for a manual peripheral smear when haemolysis is suspected — automated counters miss fragmentation.
- Normal complement (C3/C4) is common in CM‑HUS; functional assays and genetics are more informative but can be altered by PEX or eculizumab.
- Use PEX as a bridge (especially if TTP not yet excluded), but don’t view it as definitive therapy for CM‑HUS.
- Eculizumab requires careful infection risk mitigation: preferably vaccinate ≥2 weeks before starting, but if urgent therapy is needed give vaccines and cover with appropriate antibiotics until immunity is established.
- Infections with encapsulated organisms (like Capnocytophaga canimorsus) can both trigger complement‑mediated endothelial injury and complicate decisions about complement blockade — timing and multidisciplinary discussion (ID, nephrology, haematology, ICU) are essential.

Take‑home messages
- If sepsis/DIC improves but new MAHA, persistent thrombocytopenia, or worsening AKI appear — STOP and reassess for TMA.
- Key tests: LDH, haptoglobin, peripheral smear (schistocytes), DAT, ADAMTS13, stool Shiga PCR, and send complement studies and genetics early (but interpret in context of PEX/eculizumab).
- Use PEX to bridge while excluding TTP. Start eculizumab promptly for CM‑HUS once active uncontrolled bacteremia is addressed and appropriate vaccination/antibiotic precautions are in place.
- Multidisciplinary care and attention to timing (diagnostics, PEX, vaccination, antibiotic prophylaxis) are critical to maximise recovery and minimise harm.

This Capnocytophaga case teaches a simple but high‑value lesson: in post‑sepsis patients, don’t let the DIC label become diagnostic tunnel vision. When the labs and timeline don’t fit, think TMA — fast recognition changes outcomes.



# The Dog-Bite Septic Shock You Can’t Miss: Capnocytophaga canimorsus in Healthy Adults

What makes this case striking is how a mundane dog bite in a previously healthy woman cascaded to fulminant sepsis, DIC, profound leukopenia and—after an initial recovery—unmasked a complement-mediated TMA that left her dialysis dependent. Capnocytophaga canimorsus is usually a “rare” cause of human infection, but when it hits, it can be deceptively rapid and severe, even in immunocompetent hosts.

Why clinicians should care
- Presentation can be atypical and early: severe hypoglycaemia resistant to oral/glucagon therapy, marked leukopenia/neutropenia, rapidly progressive coagulopathy (DIC), mottling and peripheral ischemia. These clues should raise suspicion for C. canimorsus in the right exposure context.
- It’s fastidious and slow-growing — cultures may take days. Delays in diagnosis and therapy are common and costly.
- Severe disease is classically associated with asplenia or alcoholism, but immunocompetent people can get catastrophic infection.
- Rarely, infection can trigger a thrombotic microangiopathy (including complement-mediated HUS), which can be obscured by the initial sepsis/DIC picture.

Clinical pearls from the case
- Timing: severe systemic illness often develops within days of the bite (this case: 4 days).
- Hypoglycaemia can be a striking early feature of fulminant C. canimorsus sepsis. Don’t assume simple fasting — consider sepsis physiology and hepatic dysfunction.
- Marked leukopenia (sometimes profound neutropenia) early in the illness is paradoxical for bacterial sepsis but is well described with C. canimorsus and should heighten suspicion.
- Coagulopathy and DIC are common presentations; evolving microangiopathic haemolysis may only become apparent as sepsis improves.
- Culture limitations: because C. canimorsus is slow-growing and fastidious, blood cultures can be negative early or require prolonged incubation. Consider molecular methods (PCR, sequencing) if clinical suspicion is high.
- Don’t forget other bite pathogens (Pasteurella multocida, anaerobes, staphylococci, streptococci) — initial empiric coverage should be broad enough to cover the usual mix.

Microbiology and diagnostics — practical points
- Organism: gram-negative, capnophilic (prefers CO2), often encapsulated — virulence varies by strain.
- Lab traps: routine blood cultures will eventually grow it but often after several days. If labs report “slow-growing gram-negative rods” in the context of a dog/cat bite, think Capnocytophaga.
- Molecular diagnostics (real-time PCR, 16S rRNA sequencing, nanopore sequencing) can shorten the diagnostic window and are increasingly used in severe or culture-negative cases.
- Don’t rely on normal complement C3/C4 to exclude complement-mediated TMA. ADAMTS13 is critical to exclude TTP (activity <10%).

Antibiotic strategy — empiric and definitive
- Empiric therapy for severe dog/cat bite–associated sepsis should cover Pasteurella, Capnocytophaga and anaerobes: intravenous amoxicillin–clavulanate is common for less severe presentations; for fulminant sepsis consider broader IV regimens.
- Reasonable empiric options in severe sepsis from a dog/cat exposure:
  - Piperacillin–tazobactam IV (used in this case) — broad gram-negative and anaerobic coverage.
  - Ceftriaxone IV plus metronidazole — if anaerobic coverage is needed and local resistance patterns permit.
  - Carbapenem when concern for resistant organisms or polymicrobial severe infection.
- Definitive therapy: most C. canimorsus isolates are susceptible to penicillin and many beta-lactams; once organism identified and susceptibilities known, narrow to an appropriate beta-lactam (e.g., penicillin G or amoxicillin) if susceptibilities confirm.
- Important caveat: always tailor antibiotics based on cultures and local resistance patterns. In severe disease don’t delay broad empiric therapy while awaiting culture.

Supportive and adjunctive issues
- Early source control and ICU-level support if signs of shock/multiorgan failure.
- Anticipate need for transfusions, coagulation support, and renal replacement therapy if AKI/oliguria progresses.
- When TMA is suspected (new MAHA, schistocytes, thrombocytopenia, AKI), initiate prompt workup: peripheral smear, haptoglobin, LDH, DAT, ADAMTS13, complement studies and consider renal biopsy if safe. Early nephrology/haematology collaboration is essential.
- If CM-HUS is diagnosed, eculizumab can be effective but timing is tricky during active bacteraemia. Vaccinate and provide antimicrobial prophylaxis per guidelines before or alongside complement blockade; balance infection control vs treatment of complement-mediated endothelial injury.

Take-home messages
- Consider Capnocytophaga canimorsus in any severe sepsis or unexplained DIC following dog or cat exposure — even in healthy adults.
- Red flags: early refractory hypoglycaemia, marked leukopenia, rapidly progressive coagulopathy and peripheral mottling/ischemia.
- Blood cultures can be slow; use molecular diagnostics when clinical suspicion is high and cultures are negative or delayed.
- Empiric therapy for severe animal-bite sepsis should cover Capnocytophaga (piperacillin–tazobactam or amoxicillin–clavulanate ± IV escalation depending on severity); narrow once susceptibilities known.
- Watch for secondary complications like TMA/CM-HUS — these can present after initial improvement and require a different therapeutic approach (plasma exchange as bridge, and eculizumab for complement-mediated disease).

Bottom line: a seemingly minor dog bite can become a multisystem crisis. Keep Capnocytophaga on your differential for fulminant sepsis after animal exposure, act early with appropriate empiric antibiotics, pursue molecular diagnostics if cultures lag, and be alert for later complications like CM-HUS that need targeted therapy.



# MAHA in Real Time: A Practical Algorithm to Separate TTP, STEC‑HUS, and CM‑HUS

What makes this case stick in your memory? A previously healthy woman bitten by her dog develops fulminant sepsis from Capnocytophaga canimorsus, then—after an initial septic DIC picture—she evolves new microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), persistent thrombocytopaenia and dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury (AKI). ADAMTS13 is not severely deficient, stool Shiga toxin is negative, kidney biopsy shows thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and she recovers haematologically and partially renally after plasma exchange (PEX) and eculizumab. This is a neat, high‑yield illustration of how infectious triggers can unmask complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) and why a pragmatic bedside algorithm is needed when MAHA + thrombocytopenia (± AKI) lands in front of you.

Why this is hard at the bedside
- MAHA + thrombocytopenia is a phenotype, not a diagnosis. Rapidly differentiating TTP, STEC‑HUS and CM‑HUS matters because initial therapies (PEX, antibiotics, complement blockade) differ and delays cost kidneys and lives.
- Early sepsis/DIC can obscure or coexist with TMA. Complement levels (C3/C4) are often normal in CM‑HUS and ADAMTS13 takes time. You need a practical pathway to act before every result arrives.

A pragmatic bedside pathway (what to do, step by step)
1. Recognize the phenotype
   - Any patient with anaemia + schistocytes on smear, elevated LDH, low/undetectable haptoglobin and thrombocytopenia → treat as MAHA/TMA until proven otherwise.
   - Note the context: fever, sepsis/DIC, bloody diarrhoea, pregnancy, meds, recent infection/trauma → these are important triggers but do not exclude primary TMA.

2. Immediate simultaneous actions (the minutes–hours tasks)
   - Send stat labs: CBC with smear, LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin, reticulocyte count, coagulation screen, creatinine, urinalysis, blood cultures if febrile, stool Shiga toxin PCR if diarrhoeal illness, direct antiglobulin test (DAT/Coombs).
   - Send ADAMTS13 activity and antibody (urgent send‑out if necessary).
   - Start PEX immediately if clinical suspicion for TTP is moderate–high OR if patient is unstable and you cannot rapidly exclude TTP (see PLASMIC below). Don’t wait for ADAMTS13 if TTP is plausible.
   - Start broad supportive care: transfuse packed cells for hemodynamic compromise or symptomatic anaemia, platelet transfusion only for life‑threatening bleeding or invasive procedures, manage AKI (early nephrology consult, consider CRRT), treat sepsis aggressively with appropriate antibiotics.

3. Use PLASMIC score to triage for PEX if ADAMTS13 will be delayed
   - PLASMIC variables: Platelet count <30,000; haemolysis (indirect bilirubin/reticulocytes); Absence of active cancer; Absence of solid‑organ/stem cell transplant; MCV <90; INR <1.5; Creatinine <2.0 mg/dL. Score 0–4 = low, 5 = intermediate, 6–7 = high risk of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency.
   - High score → start PEX immediately. Low score → you can usually delay PEX while awaiting ADAMTS13, provided patient is stable and being closely monitored.

4. Interpret ADAMTS13 when it returns
   - ADAMTS13 activity <10% (or <5–10% per local assay): TTP — PEX + immunosuppression (steroids ± rituximab) is indicated. Plasma exchange is lifesaving. ADAMTS13 autoantibody testing helps but don’t wait to treat.
   - ADAMTS13 activity >10–20%: TTP unlikely. Stop PEX once other diagnoses are clarified (unless PEX is helping and decision‑made with haematology).

5. Consider STEC‑HUS early if diarrhoea or exposure suggests it
   - Send stool Shiga toxin PCR/culture. If positive for STEC: supportive care is mainstay; avoid antimotility agents and some antibiotics (controversial), PEX is not routinely effective. Shiga toxin HUS commonly follows bloody diarrhoea and presents in children but occurs in adults.
   - Negative stool PCR and no diarrhoeal prodrome make STEC less likely.

6. Think CM‑HUS when the following pattern emerges
   - MAHA + thrombocytopenia + prominent AKI (oligo/anuria, need for dialysis), schistocytes on smear, kidney biopsy consistent with TMA, negative or non‑diagnostic ADAMTS13, and no STEC.
   - Often there is an identifiable trigger (infection, pregnancy, drugs), and haemolysis may appear after initial infection control—as in the Capnocytophaga case.
   - Complement levels (C3/C4) are frequently normal; normal complements do not exclude CM‑HUS.
   - Genetic testing for complement mutations should be sent but will not guide acute therapy.

7. How to manage suspected CM‑HUS acutely
   - Consider bridging PEX if ADAMTS13 still pending or while organizing complement blockade—PEX may transiently help but rarely cures CM‑HUS.
   - Initiate eculizumab (or newer terminal complement inhibitors) once infection is controlled or bacteremia cleared whenever possible. Vaccinate against Neisseria meningitidis (and consider prophylactic antibiotics) prior to or with early doses according to your local protocol — but do not delay life‑saving therapy unnecessarily when the clinical picture mandates complement blockade.
   - Collaborate with nephrology, haematology and infectious diseases—complement blockade in the setting of recent sepsis requires balancing risks (encapsulated organisms) and benefits (renal salvage).

Applying the pathway to the Capnocytophaga case
- Initial presentation mimicked DIC from fulminant sepsis (pancytopenia, coagulopathy, AKI). Early suspicion for Capnocytophaga led to appropriate empiric antibiotics.
- As infection improved, the patient developed new isolated haemolysis, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH and persistent thrombocytopenia with schistocytes and escalating AKI → this is the classic “TMA appearing after infection” pattern.
- ADAMTS13 was not severely low (43.5%) → TTP unlikely. Stool Shiga toxin PCR was negative → STEC‑HUS unlikely. Kidney biopsy showed fibrin thrombi consistent with TMA.
- PEX was used as a bridge; eculizumab was started after blood cultures were negative and vaccines/prophylactic antibiotics were administered, with marked haematologic recovery and partial renal recovery thereafter.

Clinical pearls
- Schistocytes matter. If you see significant schistocytosis (≥1% in many labs; this case had 15–20%), act fast.
- Normal C3/C4 does NOT exclude CM‑HUS. Functional and genetic testing are useful but not required to start therapy when the clinical picture fits.
- ADAMTS13 is the pivotal test for TTP vs other TMAs, but it’s only useful if you either send it early or use a scoring system (PLASMIC) to decide on immediate PEX.
- In sepsis, DIC and TMA can coexist or evolve sequentially—improvement in sepsis followed by new haemolysis/thrombocytopenia should raise the ceil of suspicion for superimposed TMA.
- Vaccinate and give meningococcal prophylaxis when initiating complement blockade, but balance the timing against the urgency of treating a fulminant CM‑HUS.

Take‑home messages
- Treat the phenotype: MAHA + thrombocytopenia is an emergency—send ADAMTS13, stool Shiga tests, complement studies and smear now.
- Use PLASMIC to decide who needs immediate PEX. When in doubt and unstable, start PEX — TTP kills fast.
- If ADAMTS13 is not severely reduced and STEC is excluded, think CM‑HUS—especially with severe AKI and supportive biopsy findings.
- Complement blockade (eculizumab) can be kidney‑saving in CM‑HUS; balance infection risk by ensuring appropriate antibiotics, cultures and vaccinations where possible.
- Always involve nephrology, haematology and infectious diseases early—these cases are multidisciplinary by nature.

This Capnocytophaga case is a crisp lesson: an unusual zoonotic infection can trigger complement‑mediated TMA, and the bedside workflow of “recognize → send the right tests → score for TTP → bridge appropriately → escalate to complement blockade when indicated” will keep patients out of the dialysis unit and increase chances of renal recovery.



## Normal C3/C4, Sick Kidneys: Interpreting Complement Tests in Suspected CM‑HUS

What makes this case interesting: a previously healthy woman with fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis who develops dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury and later a classic thrombotic microangiopathy (MAHA, schistocytes, thrombocytopenia) on renal biopsy — yet her routine complement tests (C3/C4) are normal. Is this CM‑HUS or not? Short answer: yes, normal C3/C4 do not rule out complement‑mediated HUS. This case is a great teachable moment about the limits of standard complement testing, timing, and how treatments (PEX, eculizumab) and the infection itself can confuse the picture.

Why we get fooled by normal C3/C4
- CM‑HUS is driven by dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway at endothelial surfaces. That activation may be local (microvascular endothelium) and not cause sustained drops in circulating C3/C4.
- C3/C4 are crude snapshots of systemic complement protein abundance; they don’t measure ongoing complement activation at tissue sites, nor do they quantify function of regulatory proteins (CFH, CFI, CD46).
- Many patients with genetically mediated or infection‑triggered CM‑HUS have normal serum C3/C4 at presentation (and even during disease). Relying on them to “rule out” CM‑HUS is unsafe.

How timing and interventions alter complement assays
- When you draw matters:
  - Early in the course, before complement consumption or treatment, C3 might be low in some patients — but often it isn’t.
  - After plasma exchange (PEX), levels of complement proteins (and autoantibodies) are transiently normalized or diluted depending on replacement product (FFP, Octaplas). PEX can therefore mask low C3/C4 or remove autoantibodies.
  - After eculizumab, downstream functional assays (CH50/AH50) will be suppressed because terminal complement is inhibited — but total C3/C4 may remain unchanged.
- In the presented case, basic C3/C4 were normal before PEX. Extended functional AH50 was depressed after PEX and one dose of eculizumab — most likely treatment effect rather than proof of pre‑existing complement depletion.

Functional assays: AH50/CH50 and what they tell you
- CH50 (classical pathway) and AH50 (alternative pathway) measure pathway hemolytic activity:
  - A low AH50 suggests impaired alternative pathway function, but interpretation requires context: was the sample drawn after PEX? after eculizumab? Are there consumptive or genetic causes?
  - Eculizumab blocks C5 → downstream CH50/AH50 are suppressed even if the disease was complement‑driven originally.
  - PEX can transiently raise or lower these assays depending on timing and replacement product.
- Practical rule: draw baseline CH50/AH50 and complement factors (C3/C4, FH/FI/B) before PEX or eculizumab when possible. If you can’t, note the timing of interventions when interpreting results.

Genetics and extended testing — when and how to use it
- Genetic testing for CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, MCP (CD46), THBD and anti‑factor H antibodies helps identify predisposition but:
  - Pathogenic variants are found in ~50% of people with atypical/CM‑HUS; absence of a variant does not exclude CM‑HUS.
  - Results take time; they should not delay treatment decisions in a critically ill patient.
  - Interpret variants with the laboratory’s classification (pathogenic, likely pathogenic, VUS) and involve genetics/nephrology for long‑term planning.
- Use genetic results to guide duration of eculizumab (long‑term therapy if a high‑risk pathogenic variant or recurrent disease) — but acute management is clinical.

Working through the diagnostic dilemmas in sepsis + TMA
- In sepsis you can have:
  - DIC with schistocytes and thrombocytopenia
  - TTP (severe ADAMTS13 deficiency)
  - STEC‑HUS
  - CM‑HUS triggered by infection
- Practical approach:
  - Always get an ADAMTS13 level urgently (or use a clinical score like PLASMIC to triage). If ADAMTS13 <10% → treat for TTP (PEX).
  - Test for Shiga toxin if GI prodrome.
  - If MAHA, persistent thrombocytopenia and AKI persist or worsen despite control of infection — think CM‑HUS even with normal C3/C4.
  - If you have high suspicion, start bridging PEX if waiting for tests, but plan early eculizumab once TTP and uncontrolled infection are excluded/treated.

Counselling the team and family when labs and clinical picture disagree
- Be explicit about test limitations: “Normal C3/C4 don’t exclude CM‑HUS — these tests are not sensitive for local endothelial complement activation.”
- Explain treatment reasoning: eculizumab is the most effective therapy for CM‑HUS and outcomes correlate with early initiation; risks (notably meningococcal infection) must be mitigated with vaccination and antibiotics.
- Clarify why you might treat before genetic results return: genetic testing doesn’t change the acute need for complement blockade when the clinical phenotype fits.
- Document timings: record when samples were drawn relative to PEX/even a single eculizumab dose — it matters for interpreting later functional assays.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t use normal C3/C4 to exclude CM‑HUS. Use the clinical picture (MAHA, schistocytes, low platelets, AKI) and biopsy when indicated.
- Always obtain ADAMTS13 quickly to exclude TTP; treat empirically if high suspicion while awaiting results.
- Aim to draw baseline complement functional assays (AH50/CH50) and factor levels before PEX or complement blockade whenever possible.
- Remember that PEX and eculizumab radically alter downstream assays — and PEX can transiently restore plasma proteins, masking abnormalities.
- Genetic testing informs long‑term management but not acute treatment; absence of pathogenic variants does not negate CM‑HUS.
- In infection‑triggered CM‑HUS, try to clear the infection first; if infection controlled and TMA persists/worsens, eculizumab can be lifesaving — vaccinate and give prophylactic antibiotics if starting complement inhibitors.

Take‑home messages
- Normal C3/C4 do not rule out complement‑mediated HUS.
- Timing is everything: draw baseline complement and functional tests before PEX/eculizumab if you can; otherwise interpret results in context.
- Use ADAMTS13 and stool Shiga tests to exclude other TMAs quickly.
- Start appropriate therapy based on the clinical syndrome — genetic results are for follow‑up, not for acute decision‑making.
- When labs seem discordant with the clinical picture, communicate the limits of testing clearly to the team and family and act on the clinical phenotype.

This patient’s course illustrates the trap: an infection (C. canimorsus) triggered a fulminant TMA; C3/C4 were normal, PEX transiently helped, and sustained recovery required complement blockade. In the ED/ICU, treat the patient, not just the lab numbers.



# Complement Blockade on a Tightrope: Starting Eculizumab After Bacteremia—Timing, Vaccines, and Prophylaxis

What happens when a patient with life‑threatening complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) walks in the door with recent or even ongoing bacteremia? The case that prompted this post is dramatic: previously healthy woman, fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis complicated by DIC and dialysis‑dependent AKI, who then developed delayed microangiopathic hemolysis and biopsy‑proven TMA. Eculizumab was the turning point for durable remission — but only after a careful walk along an infectious risk/benefit tightrope.

This is a practical post for clinicians facing the decision: when do you pull the eculizumab trigger after serious infection, what vaccines do you need and when, what to do if you can’t wait the two weeks, and how do you keep your patient safe while you reverse catastrophic complement activation?

## The clinical dilemma in one sentence
Eculizumab saves kidneys and lives in CM‑HUS, but it disables terminal complement — a key defender against encapsulated organisms. Starting it during or immediately after bacteremia risks uncontrolled infection; delaying it risks irreversible renal injury. How to balance those competing risks?

## Key principles before you act

1. Diagnose aggressively and early
- Treat the immediate sepsis with targeted antibiotics and supportive care. Simultaneously work up the TMA: hemolysis labs, peripheral smear (schistocytes), ADAMTS13, stool Shiga toxin PCR, and get nephrology/hematology involved. Early recognition shortens the window for delayed organ damage.

2. If you suspect CM‑HUS, time matters
- Earlier complement blockade is associated with better renal recovery. But “earlier” must be weighed against infectious safety.

3. Encapsulated organisms matter
- Eculizumab markedly increases risk of invasive Neisseria infections. Other encapsulated pathogens (like Capnocytophaga spp.) can also be problematic — especially when the precipitant organism was encapsulated. If the trigger was an encapsulated bacterium, be extra cautious.

## Vaccination: what and when
Ideal strategy
- Give MenACWY (conjugate quadrivalent) and MenB vaccines at least 2 weeks before starting eculizumab when possible. That two‑week window gives a reasonable chance of protective antibody formation.

When you can’t wait
- If the TMA is severe and you cannot postpone eculizumab, administer both MenACWY and MenB as soon as possible (preferably prior to first dose), but do NOT delay eculizumab for vaccine response.
- Give prophylactic antibiotics (see below) until 2 weeks after completion of the vaccination schedule (or longer per local guidance).

Important nuance: prior plasma exchange (PEX) or fresh frozen plasma/Octaplas can remove recently generated antibodies and blunt vaccine responses. If the patient has had PEX shortly before vaccination, counsel that serologic protection may be reduced and continue prophylaxis.

## Antibiotic prophylaxis if you must start eculizumab early
- Start prophylactic antibiotics immediately and continue at least until 2 weeks after the vaccines have had time to induce protective antibodies.
- Typical choices:
  - Oral penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin) or amoxicillin if not allergic — commonly recommended as daily prophylaxis while on complement blockade.
  - For penicillin allergy, discuss alternatives with infectious disease; options include azithromycin or cipro (local resistance patterns matter).
- If active or recent bacteremia with a non‑Neisseria encapsulated organism (eg, Capnocytophaga), ensure targeted antibiotic treatment has been completed and cultures are negative before relying solely on vaccines/prophylaxis. In practice, many teams wait for blood cultures to clear before starting eculizumab when clinically feasible.

## Practical timing algorithm (clinical common sense)
- Stable, non‑life‑threatening CM‑HUS: vaccinate MenACWY + MenB, wait 14 days, then start eculizumab.
- Rapidly progressive CM‑HUS with high risk of irreversible kidney injury (dialysis dependent, ongoing hemolysis): vaccinate immediately, start prophylactic antibiotics, consider starting eculizumab without waiting 14 days — but only after reasonable control of the infection (ideally sterile blood cultures) and after discussing infectious risk.
- Active bacteremia that is uncontrolled: prioritize source control and antibiotics; delay eculizumab until infection is controlled if at all possible.

In the C. canimorsus case, blood cultures turned negative before eculizumab was started — a pragmatic and prudent approach. Vaccines were given immediately prior to dosing and prophylactic amoxicillin continued because vaccination immunity could not be assumed and PEX had been performed.

## Special considerations

- Plasma exchange effects: PEX can remove complement inhibitors, vaccine‑induced antibodies, and eculizumab itself. If PEX is ongoing, plan vaccination and eculizumab timing around PEX sessions and recognize that vaccine responses may be blunted — maintain antibiotics longer.
- Complement testing: C3/C4 can be normal in CM‑HUS and are not reliable to rule it in/out. ADAMTS13 is priority — TTP must be excluded.
- Vaccination schedule: MenACWY is usually one dose (with booster schedule per local public health), MenB requires its series; check local regimens. You may need to repeat vaccination later when the patient is stable to ensure seroconversion.
- Long‑term prophylaxis: patients on chronic complement blockade generally need ongoing education and often long‑term antibiotic prophylaxis and rapid access to care for febrile illness. Meningococcal vaccination reduces but does not eliminate risk; vigilance remains essential.

## Monitoring and patient education
- Educate patients and caregivers to seek urgent attention for any fever, neck stiffness, severe headache, rash, or sepsis‑like symptoms.
- Consider providing a safety card/alert bracelet indicating complement blockade therapy and need for urgent evaluation.
- Low threshold for empiric IV antibiotics and hospital admission for febrile illnesses.
- Coordinate with infectious disease and public health for meningococcal exposure protocols and post‑exposure prophylaxis for close contacts if indicated.

## Case takeaways (why this matters)
- In our Capnocytophaga case, the team balanced risk by ensuring cultures were negative, vaccinating before dosing, giving prophylactic antibiotics because vaccines can’t protect immediately and PEX had occurred, and initiating eculizumab — resulting in hematologic remission and partial renal recovery.
- This is the practical template: control infection as much as feasible, vaccinate early, use antibiotics as a bridge if you can’t wait, and recognize PEX/vaccine timing interactions.

Key clinical pearls
- Don’t delay infectious workup or antibiotics while evaluating TMA — infection control is essential and can itself be the trigger.
- Vaccinate (MenACWY + MenB) ideally 2 weeks before eculizumab; if urgent therapy is required, vaccinate immediately and start prophylactic antibiotics.
- Plasma exchange can remove vaccine‑derived antibodies and eculizumab; plan dosing and interpret serology accordingly.
- If possible, wait for sterile cultures before starting eculizumab — but weigh this against the irreversible harms of delaying therapy for CM‑HUS.
- Educate patients on the lifelong (or until therapy stops) increased risk of invasive meningococcal disease and maintain low threshold for urgent evaluation and empiric antibiotics for febrile illness.

Bottom line: starting eculizumab after bacteremia is a risk–benefit decision, not a binary rule. Prioritize infection control, vaccinate early, bridge with antibiotics if you can’t wait, and coordinate closely with nephrology, hematology and infectious disease — that multidisciplinary handshake is what turns a tightrope into a survivable crossing.



## The Misleading DAT: Penicillins, Transfusions, and the Hemolysis Red Herring

What makes this case worth your attention? A previously well woman presents with fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis and DIC. After initial improvement, she suddenly develops a brisk hemolytic picture and a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT, Coombs) for IgG. The team starts steroids for presumed warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) — but the patient doesn’t improve. Peripheral smear shows 15–20% schistocytes, platelets remain low, kidney failure persists, haptoglobin is gone, LDH is rising, and ADAMTS13 is 43.5%. The final diagnosis: complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS) triggered by infection. The DAT was a red herring — likely false-positive from penicillin exposure and transfusions — that nearly diverted care away from a life‑threatening thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Why this matters
DAT positivity often steers clinicians straight into “warm AIHA” territory. That’s reasonable — but not always correct. Drug-induced DAT positivity (penicillins and other agents), passive antibodies from transfusion, and technical or timing issues can all produce a positive DAT without true warm AIHA. In a critically ill patient with persistent thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury, and evidence of microangiopathic hemolysis (schistocytes, very low haptoglobin, high LDH), anchoring on a DAT can delay the correct diagnosis and appropriate therapy for TMA (PEX, complement blockade).

How to think when the DAT and the clinical picture disagree
- Context is king. Ask: recent antibiotics? Recent transfusions? Ongoing sepsis? New organ dysfunction (especially kidneys)? Any evidence of bleeding?
- Interpret the DAT in the bigger hematologic picture. Warm AIHA usually presents with:
  - Positive DAT (IgG ± C3)
  - Reticulocytosis if marrow responds
  - Hyperbilirubinemia predominantly indirect
  - No large schistocyte burden
  - Platelets usually preserved
- TMA/MAHA typically shows:
  - Schistocytosis on peripheral smear (even modest %s matter)
  - Severe thrombocytopenia or falling platelets
  - AKI and/or other end-organ ischemia
  - Very low haptoglobin and high LDH
  - DAT often negative — but it can be misleadingly positive if confounders exist

Common causes of false‑positive DAT
- Drug-related antibodies: penicillins and many cephalosporins can form drug-dependent antibodies or lead to nonspecific coating of RBCs that yield a positive DAT without classic AIHA physiology.
- Recent transfusion: passive alloantibodies or immune complexes can transiently coat donor/recipient cells.
- Polyclonal immunoglobulin therapy or IVIG (not in this case, but worth remembering).
- Technical issues and storage or reagent interference.

Practical steps at the bedside when DAT is positive but things don’t fit
- Re-check the peripheral smear. The presence and percentage of schistocytes changes everything. Even 1%–2% is suspicious in the right context; 15–20% as in this case is unequivocal for MAHA.
- Look at the full hemolysis panel: haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin (direct/indirect), reticulocyte count. Noncompensatory reticulocytosis in the face of severe anemia may suggest marrow suppression (eg, from sepsis) or acute process.
- Evaluate platelets and renal function. New or persistent thrombocytopenia + AKI should trigger immediate consideration of TMA.
- Send ADAMTS13 and STEC testing early: ADAMTS13 <10% suggests TTP (treat with urgent PEX); ADAMTS13 >10% makes TTP unlikely and forces consideration of other TMAs (eg, HUS, complement-mediated HUS).
- If TMA suspected and ADAMTS13 pending, consider early plasma exchange as a bridge and consult hematology/nephrology urgently.
- Consider stopping the offending drug (eg, penicillin) if safe and feasible — but recognize that stopping the drug won’t treat a true TMA once triggered.
- Be cautious with empiric steroids for AIHA when the DAT may be false positive; steroid delay in TMA may cost renal recovery time. Use clinical judgment — in unstable patients, simultaneous evaluation and early plasma exchange may be prudent.

Lessons from this case
- A positive DAT is not an automatic diagnosis of warm AIHA. Drug exposure (penicillins), transfusions, and sepsis-related immune phenomena can produce misleading results.
- Persistent schistocytosis, worsening AKI, thrombocytopenia, high LDH and absent haptoglobin should shift your differential toward TMA, even with a positive DAT.
- ADAMTS13 helps separate TTP from other TMAs, but it takes time — treat the most dangerous possibilities promptly (PEX for suspected TTP; consider early nephrology/hematology involvement for possible CM-HUS).
- In infection-triggered CM-HUS, complement blockade (eculizumab) can be kidney‑ and life‑saving, but timing is tricky when active bacteraemia is present. Ideally, culture negativity, vaccination, and antimicrobial prophylaxis should be arranged before complement inhibition.
- Always keep a broad differential. Anchoring on one test without integrating the full clinical and laboratory context can lead to delays in appropriate management.

Take-home messages
- A positive DAT is not definitive for warm AIHA — consider recent drugs (eg, penicillins), transfusions, and sepsis-related artifacts.
- Steroid‑refractory hemolysis with persistent thrombocytopenia, AKI, and schistocytes should shift focus to TMA.
- Send ADAMTS13 and STEC testing early; don’t wait for results to involve hematology/nephrology and consider bridging therapy when indicated.
- In infection-triggered CM-HUS, timely recognition and initiation of complement blockade (after managing infection risk) improve outcomes.

This case is a blunt reminder: labs guide, but the smear and the clinical trajectory decide. Don’t let a solitary DAT draw you away from a life‑threatening TMA.



# Dog and Cat Bites in 2026: Empiric Coverage That Won’t Miss Capnocytophaga

What makes this relevant now? A previously healthy woman developed fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis after a seemingly minor dog bite and later went on to complement-mediated HUS with dialysis dependence before partial recovery. Capnocytophaga is an uncommon but deadly zoonotic pathogen that can be slow to grow in culture, sometimes beta-lactamase–producing, and capable of triggering catastrophic downstream complications. In bite management you don’t just treat skin flora — you cover for fastidious, encapsulated organisms and plan your diagnostics accordingly.

Here’s a practical, evidence-informed approach to empiric IV therapy, outpatient prophylaxis, allergy workarounds, duration, and microbiology logistics that will catch Capnocytophaga (and the usual suspects) without over-cooking therapy.

Clinical overview — who needs coverage?
- All dog and cat bites risk Pasteurella spp., Capnocytophaga (dogs), anaerobes, streptococci, staphylococci and oral anaerobes from the biter. Human bites add Eikenella corrodens.
- Give prophylactic antibiotics for high-risk wounds: deep punctures, hand/foot bites, bites near joints/tendons, crush wounds, devitalised tissue, immunocompromised or asplenic patients, and bites seen >8 hours after injury.
- Always consider tetanus status and rabies risk separately.

Empiric IV choices (severe infection / sepsis / hospitalised)
Aim: reliably cover Pasteurella, Capnocytophaga (including some beta-lactamase producers), anaerobes and Gram-negatives.

First-line (broad, evidence-based)
- Piperacillin–tazobactam IV. Pros: broad Gram-negative + anaerobe + good activity against most Capnocytophaga isolates (including many β-lactamase producers). Ideal for severe sepsis, deep tissue infection or suspected polymicrobial infection.

Alternative IV options
- Ampicillin–sulbactam IV (Unasyn). Good option for many severe bite infections; covers Pasteurella and most Capnocytophaga, but local beta-lactamase patterns may vary.
- Carbapenem (ertapenem or meropenem) when ESBL/ resistant organisms are suspected or in critically ill where maximal Gram-negative coverage is required.

Do not rely on first-generation cephalosporins (cefazolin) alone — they miss Pasteurella/Capnocytophaga. Clindamycin alone misses Gram-negatives.

Step-down and outpatient prophylaxis
- First-line oral step-down/prophylaxis: amoxicillin–clavulanate (Augmentin). Good bioavailability, covers Pasteurella, Capnocytophaga (including many β-lactamase producers) and oral anaerobes. Typical prophylaxis: 3–5 days for simple wounds; longer if infected.
- Established uncomplicated infection: 7–14 days depending on severity and response. Deep infections, osteomyelitis, or septic arthritis require longer IV → oral transition guided by clinical response and imaging.

Allergy workarounds (penicillin allergy)
- If true severe immediate-type penicillin allergy (anaphylaxis): doxycycline plus metronidazole is a practical option
  - Doxycycline covers Capnocytophaga and Pasteurella in most reports.
  - Add metronidazole to ensure anaerobic coverage for deep/bitten wounds.
- Alternatively, for adults: moxifloxacin monotherapy covers Gram-negatives and anaerobes (but variable Pasteurella/Capnocytophaga susceptibility and fluoroquinolone risks). Use with caution and local susceptibility data.
- Cephalosporins: many patients with non–type I PCN allergies can receive second/third-generation cephalosporins, but avoid if high-risk allergy. Discuss with allergy/immunology if needed.

Duration — practical guidance
- Prophylaxis (uninfected but high-risk wound): 3–5 days of oral amoxicillin–clavulanate.
- Superficial infected wound/cellulitis: 5–7 days, extend if not improving.
- Deep infection (tenosynovitis, septic arthritis, osteomyelitis): IV therapy often needed initially; total duration commonly 2–6 weeks depending on site, surgical debridement and clinical course.
- Bacteraemia: individualized—at least 10–14 days after source control and clinical improvement; longer if endovascular/severe complications.

Microbiology — what to send and how to ask the lab
- Blood cultures: get at least two sets before antibiotics if possible for febrile or systemically unwell patients — Capnocytophaga is often cultured from blood in invasive disease.
- Wound swab + tissue samples: for deep infections send tissue aspirates or biopsy material rather than superficial swabs; anaerobic transport recommended.
- Request extended incubation and communication: Capnocytophaga is slow-growing and capnophilic. Ask the lab to hold cultures for at least 7 days (some labs incubate up to 14 days) and to plate on blood and chocolate agar under CO2 conditions.
- Use of molecular diagnostics: if cultures are negative but suspicion is high, request 16S rRNA PCR, MALDI-TOF ID if available, or sequencing (nanopore/next-gen) — these speed identification and can be decisive in slow growers.
- Inform the lab: tell them “dog bite; consider Capnocytophaga — hold cultures, plate on chocolate/blood, anaerobic + aerobic, consider 16S if slow growth.” That simple heads-up matters.

Special considerations
- Beta-lactamase production is increasingly reported in Capnocytophaga: amoxicillin alone may fail; prefer beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations or doxycycline in allergy.
- Immunocompromised and asplenic patients are at higher risk for fulminant disease — lower threshold for hospitalisation and IV therapy.
- Vaccinate/cover: check tetanus status and manage rabies risk depending on animal behavior and local guidelines.
- Complications: be alert for early sepsis, DIC, and rare downstream phenomena like thrombotic microangiopathy/CM‑HUS (as in the case described). Clinical deterioration despite source control should prompt re-evaluation and consultation (ID, nephrology, hematology).

When to involve specialists
- Infectious diseases for complex bite infections, unusual organisms, or resistant isolates.
- Surgery/orthopedics for deep space infections, tendon/joint involvement, or necrotizing infection.
- Nephrology/hematology if haematologic derangements or renal failure develop (early recognition of TMA matters).

Putting it together — an example pathway
- Minor, low-risk bite: local wound care, tetanus review, no antibiotics unless patient high-risk.
- High-risk bite or patient immunocompromise (outpatient): amoxicillin–clavulanate PO x 3–5 days (prophylaxis) or longer if infected.
- Hospitalised with severe infection or systemic features: start piperacillin–tazobactam IV; narrow to amoxicillin–clavulanate PO when improving and cultures permit.
- Penicillin anaphylaxis: doxycycline + metronidazole PO/IV (or moxifloxacin where appropriate) — involve ID as needed.
- Always send blood cultures and deep tissue samples and communicate with the lab to hold cultures for ≥7 days and consider molecular ID if growth is slow or cultures negative.

Key take-home messages
- Don’t underestimate dog bites: Capnocytophaga canimorsus can cause fulminant sepsis and rare complications such as complement-mediated HUS.
- First-line empiric for severe bite-related infection: piperacillin–tazobactam IV or ampicillin–sulbactam; step-down to oral amoxicillin–clavulanate.
- In penicillin anaphylaxis use doxycycline ± metronidazole (or moxifloxacin with caution); avoid cephalosporins only if true type I hypersensitivity.
- Tell the lab “dog bite — consider Capnocytophaga” and request prolonged incubation (≥7 days), aerobic/anaerobic plates in CO2, and molecular methods if cultures are slow/negative.
- High-risk wounds, immunocompromised/asplenic patients and any systemic signs — lower threshold for hospital admission, IV therapy, and early specialist involvement.

Keep your bite protocols current, communicate with your lab early, and remember that a “minor” bite can become life‑threatening — timely broad empiric coverage and targeted diagnostics save lives.



## Biopsy at 77k Platelets: How — and Why — to Pursue Renal Pathology in TMA

What sticks with me from this case isn’t just the dog bite or the rare culprit (Capnocytophaga canimorsus). It’s the moment the team decided to biopsy a severely injured kidney in a thrombocytopenic patient — after platelets rose to 77 × 10^9/L — and how that specimen changed everything. In a patient with sepsis, DIC and dialysis‑dependent AKI, the line between sepsis-associated ATN/DIC and a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is blurred. Histology cut through that fog.

Why this is surprising (and important)
- We’re taught to fear bleeding in thrombocytopenic patients, so invasive diagnostics like percutaneous renal biopsy often get deferred. That delay can cost diagnostic clarity and therapeutic opportunity.
- In TMA, renal histology is not academic — seeing fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli and tubular necrosis can support a diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) and justify high‑cost, high‑risk therapy such as eculizumab. In this case, biopsy findings materially supported a move to complement blockade after other causes were excluded.

When to push for a kidney biopsy in suspected TMA
- When the clinical picture is ambiguous and results will change management: persistent MAHA, schistocytes, thrombocytopenia and AKI where ADAMTS13, stool Shiga testing, and complement studies are equivocal or pending.
- When the patient is dialysis‑dependent or there’s rapidly progressive loss of renal function and you need prognostic information (acute reversible lesion vs established irreversible cortical necrosis).
- When distinguishing TMA from DIC/ATN will alter therapy (plasmapheresis alone vs early complement blockade).

Practical thresholds and risk mitigation
- There’s no absolute universal platelet cutoff but practical guidance:
  - Many centers accept percutaneous biopsy when platelets ≥50 × 10^9/L if INR is normal and counts are stable.
  - For platelets 30–50 × 10^9/L consider platelet transfusion pre‑procedure; for <30 × 10^9/L or coagulopathy favour alternative strategies.
- Timing matters:
  - Delay biopsy until acute, correctable coagulopathy is addressed and infection is controlled enough to justify immunosuppression if needed.
  - In this case the team waited until platelets recovered to ~77 × 10^9/L after PEX and supportive care before performing biopsy.
- Technique choices:
  - Ultrasound‑guided percutaneous biopsy is standard with good safety when counts are adequate.
  - If bleeding risk remains high or coagulopathy persists, consider transjugular renal biopsy — lower bleeding risk and safe in severe thrombocytopenia/coagulopathy.
- Peri‑procedural steps:
  - Correct INR and fibrinogen first; hold anticoagulants.
  - Transfuse platelets immediately before biopsy if counts are borderline.
  - Use experienced operators, real‑time ultrasound guidance, and post‑procedure observation with serial hemoglobin checks and ultrasound if concern for hematoma.

What the biopsy gives you — and how it changes decisions
- Classic TMA histology: fibrin thrombi in glomerular capillaries and arterioles, ischemic wrinkled glomeruli, endothelial swelling, and often acute tubular necrosis.
  - These findings point away from isolated DIC or pure ATN and support a microangiopathic process with endothelial/complement involvement.
- Immunofluorescence/electron microscopy can exclude immune complex GN or other primary glomerulopathies.
- Correlation with clinical/lab data:
  - Normal ADAMTS13 (not TTP), negative Shiga toxin, and biopsy evidence of TMA + ongoing hemolysis tilts to CM‑HUS.
  - That shift supports early use of eculizumab (after reasonable infection control/vaccination/prophylaxis), rather than only prolonged PEX.
- Prognosis and counselling:
  - Presence of chronic changes (cortical scarring, chronic thrombotic lesions) predicts poorer renal recovery and informs discussions about dialysis dependence and need for long‑term eculizumab if genetic predisposition found.

Special considerations in infection-triggered TMA
- Active bacteraemia complicates the decision to start complement blockade because terminal complement inhibition increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms.
- In the case above, clinicians waited until blood cultures were negative, vaccinated against meningococcus, and gave antibiotic prophylaxis before initiating eculizumab — a reasonable compromise when biopsy and clinical picture supported CM‑HUS.
- Biopsy can justify that risk: without histologic confirmation, committing to complement blockade in the setting of recent sepsis is a tougher call.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t let thrombocytopenia alone be an absolute blocker to biopsy. Platelet transfusion, transjugular approach, and timing can make biopsy safely feasible.
- In TMA with ambiguous etiology, renal histology often changes management — particularly when ADAMTS13 is not severely reduced and Shiga toxin testing is negative.
- Normal complement levels (C3/C4) do not exclude CM‑HUS. Histology and clinical trajectory matter.
- If biopsy confirms TMA and other causes are reasonably excluded, early eculizumab improves outcomes; biopsy can be the tipping point that allows justified, prompt therapy.
- Always weigh the bleeding risk against the risk of delayed or missed diagnosis leading to irreversible kidney injury.

Take‑home summary
- Consider renal biopsy in suspected TMA when results will change therapy or prognosis, even with thrombocytopenia — aim for platelets ≥50 × 10^9/L or use platelet transfusion/transjugular approach when needed.
- Biopsy findings of fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli and ATN support TMA/CM‑HUS and can justify escalation to complement blockade after infection control.
- Safety is maximized by correcting coagulopathy, using experienced operators, and choosing the optimal access route.
- In ambiguous, high‑stakes cases, histology is often the most decisive tool you have — it can be both diagnostic and therapeutic because it clears the path to specific treatments.



# Blue Toes, Warm Pulses: Recognizing Microthrombotic Ischemia in DIC and TMA

What stops you cold in a busy ED is not always the dramatic — sometimes it’s the small, odd findings that unlock the diagnosis. A woman in her late 40s presented after a dog bite with fulminant sepsis, mottled lower extremities and later frank cyanosis of toes. Her pulses were intact and sensation preserved. That combination — distal ischemia with warm, well‑perfused limbs — should put microthrombosis high on your differential. In her case the story evolved from DIC in Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis to a superimposed complement‑mediated TMA (CM‑HUS) that required plasma exchange and eculizumab to get her off dialysis.

This post pulls practical lessons from that case: how to recognise microthrombotic ischemia versus embolic or vasculitic causes, what bedside and lab cues to chase, and how that recognition changes your immediate diagnostic and management priorities.

## Why “blue toes with pulses” matters
Peripheral ischemia with palpable pulses is almost always microvascular. Embolic or large‑vessel occlusion gives cold, pulseless, painful limbs with clear vascular territory deficits. Vasculitis can mimic microvascular disease but usually has systemic inflammatory features, livedo reticularis, palpable purpura, neuropathy and often abnormal autoimmune serology. In the septic patient, the rapid development of distal cyanosis and ecchymoses with intact pulses is a classic hint toward disseminated intravascular coagulation and small‑vessel platelet/fibrin microthrombi. But don’t stop there — if haemolysis, schistocytes and worsening AKI appear (or persist) as sepsis improves, think TMA layered on top of sepsis/DIC.

Why this distinction matters: the underlying pathophysiology and treatments differ. DIC is driven by systemic coagulation activation (treat the trigger, support with blood products judiciously). TMA is endothelial and platelet‑microthrombus mediated — think ADAMTS13/TTP, STEC‑HUS, complement‑mediated HUS — and may require plasma exchange or targeted complement blockade (eculizumab).

## Bedside and early diagnostic approach
When you see blue toes with warm pulses, do a quick targeted assessment to separate major possibilities:

- Check pulses, capillary refill, skin temperature and sensory/motor status. Intact pulses and preserved sensation point to microvascular process.
- Review timing: emboli are often sudden and unilateral; vasculitis/subacute processes are slower; DIC/TMA in sepsis can be rapid and bilateral.
- Look for systemic clues:
  - Fever, hypotension, lactic acidosis → sepsis/DIC.
  - Purpura fulminans pattern or extensive bleeding → DIC.
  - New anemia with schistocytes, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin, thrombocytopenia and AKI → think TMA/MAHA.
  - Painful livedo, neuropathy, or positive ANCA/ANA → vasculitis.
- Immediate bedside tests to order:
  - CBC with smear (seek schistocytes), reticulocytes
  - Coagulation panel (PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer)
  - Hemolysis panel (LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin)
  - Renal function and urine dip (proteinuria/hematuria)
  - Blood cultures if infection suspected
  - Consider point‑of‑care glucose, lactate, ABG

Remember: DIC and TMA can coexist or sequentially overlap. Initial labs in sepsis can be misleading — normal haptoglobin or absent schistocytes early on doesn’t exclude evolving MAHA.

## Differentiating DIC vs TMA vs embolic vs vasculitis — the practical distinctions
- Embolic/large vessel
  - Pulses: diminished/absent
  - Temperature: cold
  - Onset: abrupt, often unilateral
  - Vascular imaging will localise occlusion
- Vasculitis
  - Systemic symptoms, rashes (purpura), neuropathy
  - Autoimmune serologies may be positive
  - Biopsy often diagnostic
- DIC (consumptive coagulopathy)
  - Labs: prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, high D‑dimer, thrombocytopenia
  - Bleeding and thrombotic manifestations can co‑exist
  - Trigger: sepsis, trauma, malignancy
- TMA (TTP/HUS/CM‑HUS)
  - Labs: MAHA (schistocytes, ↑LDH, ↓haptoglobin), thrombocytopenia, often AKI (HUS) or neurological features (TTP)
  - ADAMTS13 activity low in TTP (<10%)
  - Complement studies can be normal in CM‑HUS; diagnosis is clinicopathological and by exclusion
  - Kidney biopsy (if safe) can show microthrombi if diagnosis unclear

Key clinical hint from the case: the patient’s coagulopathy initially looked like DIC in severe sepsis (mottling, bleeding, low fibrinogen), but after systemic inflammation improved she developed an isolated haemolytic drop in haemoglobin, new schistocytes and persistent thrombocytopenia with worsening AKI — classic evolution suggesting TMA superimposed on sepsis.

## What this sign should trigger in your management plan
- Early:
  - Treat the obvious trigger. In sepsis with possible Capnocytophaga after dog bite, start broad‑spectrum antibiotics covering dog bite pathogens promptly.
  - Resuscitate as usual (IV fluids, oxygen, vasopressors as needed).
  - Obtain blood cultures before antibiotics when feasible.
- Immediate investigations:
  - CBC with peripheral smear, hemolysis labs, coagulation profile, creatinine, urinalysis.
  - Send ADAMTS13 and Shiga toxin PCR/stool cultures if TMA suspected.
  - Consult hematology and nephrology early if thrombocytopenia + MAHA + AKI.
- While awaiting specialized tests:
  - If TTP is a concern (high pretest probability or unstable patient), start plasma exchange emergently — do not wait for ADAMTS13.
  - If DIC dominates (active bleeding, very low fibrinogen), manage with targeted blood product support (platelets, cryoprecipitate/fibrinogen concentrate) and treat trigger.
- If TMA is confirmed or strongly suspected and infection/bleeding risk is controlled:
  - Consider plasma exchange as a bridging therapy.
  - For CM‑HUS, plan for complement blockade (eculizumab) after weighing infection risks. Vaccinate (meningococcal) and give antibiotic prophylaxis if initiating eculizumab — ideally after blood cultures are negative and infection treated.
- Avoid knee‑jerk anticoagulation for microvascular ischemia unless there’s a clear large‑vessel thrombosis — anticoagulation won’t reverse microthrombi and may worsen bleeding.

## Clinical pearls
- Distal cyanosis with preserved pulses = think microthrombi (DIC/TMA) not embolus.
- DIC and TMA can coexist or evolve sequentially in severe infections; persistence or late onset of MAHA and thrombocytopenia despite resolution of sepsis should prompt reconsideration for TMA.
- Normal C3/C4 or normal complement functional tests do not exclude CM‑HUS.
- ADAMTS13 activity <10% is diagnostic of TTP — anything higher makes TTP unlikely and redirects therapy.
- Start plasma exchange quickly if TTP cannot be excluded; use PEX as a bridge for other TMAs while confirmatory tests and specialist input are obtained.
- Eculizumab is highly effective for CM‑HUS but requires vaccination and consideration of ongoing infection; timing matters.

## Take‑home messages
- Blue toes with warm pulses = small‑vessel microthrombosis until proven otherwise.
- In septic patients with distal ischemia, track trend labs: new or worsening schistocytosis, low haptoglobin, rising LDH and falling platelets point to TMA.
- Early specialist involvement (hematology, nephrology) and targeted diagnostics (ADAMTS13, stool Shiga testing, complement workup, blood cultures) change outcomes.
- Management is nuanced: treat infection, support coagulation as needed, use PEX for TTP/bridging, and consider eculizumab for CM‑HUS once infection control and vaccination/prophylaxis are addressed.

Spot the blue toes, check the smear, call hematology. Small peripheral clues save kidneys — and lives.
