Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

### [Clinical Pearls] In an adult presenting with fulminant sepsis four days after a dog bite, which empiric intravenous antibiotic regimens appropriately cover Capnocytophaga and other dog-bite flora, and what features of the organism and wound microbiology justify this choice?

Direct answer (clinical pearl)
- Empiric IV therapy for a patient with fulminant sepsis after a dog bite should provide reliable coverage of Capnocytophaga canimorsus plus the typical polymicrobial dog‑bite flora (Pasteurella spp., streptococci, staphylococci, and anaerobes). Reasonable empiric options are:
  - IV ampicillin–sulbactam (first‑line for moderate–severe bite infections), or
  - IV piperacillin–tazobactam (preferred for critically ill/ICU patients or when broader gram‑negative/anaerobic coverage is needed), or
  - A carbapenem (e.g., meropenem) for very severe infection or concern for resistant organisms.
- Add empiric anti‑MRSA therapy (vancomycin, daptomycin, or linezolid) only when there is clinical concern for MRSA or deep soft‑tissue infection.
- In patients with severe beta‑lactam allergy, use alternatives that cover oral anaerobes and Pasteurella/Capnocytophaga (for example, doxycycline plus metronidazole or a fluoroquinolone with metronidazole or moxifloxacin), recognizing limited data and tailoring therapy to susceptibility results when available.

Why these choices (microbiology and pathophysiology)
- Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, fastidious, capnophilic gram‑negative rod commonly present in dog and cat oral flora. It grows slowly in culture (diagnostic delay is common), so empiric therapy must be started on clinical grounds.
- Most C. canimorsus isolates are susceptible to beta‑lactams (penicillins, third‑generation cephalosporins, carbapenems), but beta‑lactamase–producing strains have been reported. Using a beta‑lactam combined with a beta‑lactamase inhibitor (ampicillin–sulbactam, amoxicillin–clavulanate orally, or piperacillin–tazobactam IV) protects against such resistance and also covers the common anaerobes and Pasteurella species found in dog‑bite wounds.
- Dog‑bite infections are typically polymicrobial (aerobes and anaerobes). Agents above provide broad gram‑negative, gram‑positive and anaerobic coverage appropriate for severe systemic infection. Carbapenems give the broadest empiric coverage if resistant organisms are suspected.
- Certain agents used for skin/soft‑tissue infections (aminoglycosides, clindamycin alone, or vancomycin alone) do not reliably cover Capnocytophaga or the anaerobic oral flora; hence monotherapy with those drugs is inadequate.

How this applies to the case
- In the reported case the team appropriately started IV piperacillin–tazobactam on presentation for severe sepsis after a dog bite to cover Capnocytophaga and the expected polymicrobial flora; this was later de‑escalated to ampicillin and then to oral phenoxymethylpenicillin after cultures identified C. canimorsus and the patient improved. The organism’s slow growth and susceptibility profile explain both the initial broad empiric choice and the subsequent narrowing of therapy once speciation and sensitivities were known.

Practical points
- Obtain blood cultures before antibiotics when feasible—C. canimorsus is slow‑growing and may require prolonged incubation or molecular methods for identification.
- Reassess antimicrobial regimen once culture and susceptibility data are available and tailor therapy accordingly.
- Consider MRSA coverage only if risk factors or clinical evidence support it.
- For patients with severe beta‑lactam allergy, select alternative regimens that still cover Pasteurella/Capnocytophaga and anaerobes and consult infectious disease if available.

### [Differential Diagnosis] A septic patient initially meeting criteria for disseminated intravascular coagulation later shows an isolated drop in hemoglobin with undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, persistent thrombocytopenia, and normalizing PT/aPTT. Which clinical and laboratory features most strongly suggest superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy rather than ongoing DIC?

The constellation of an isolated, new-onset intravascular haemolysis with ongoing thrombocytopaenia despite normalization of clotting tests — occurring after the septic/DIC phase has settled — is the single strongest clinical signal that a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) has been superimposed rather than that DIC is ongoing.

Why: DIC is a consumptive coagulopathy driven by systemic activation of coagulation; it produces prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen and very high D‑dimer, and bleeding/skin necrosis from fibrin deposition. In contrast, TMA (eg, HUS/TTP) is driven by endothelial injury with platelet‑rich microthrombi in arterioles and capillaries; it causes microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) with schistocytes, severe intravascular haemolysis, organ ischemia (classically kidneys and brain), and typically normal PT/aPTT and fibrinogen because the plasmatic coagulation cascade is not the dominant process.

Features in this case that most strongly point to TMA over persistent DIC
- New, abrupt isolated fall in haemoglobin (111 → 68 g/L over 6 days) without external bleeding or haemodynamic instability — suggests intravascular haemolysis rather than ongoing bleeding from DIC.
- Laboratory evidence of MAHA: undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, and a peripheral smear with 15–20% schistocytes — classic for microangiopathic red‑cell fragmentation.
- Persistent severe thrombocytopenia (30–40 × 10^9/L) despite normalization of coagulation studies (PT/aPTT and fibrinogen) — shows platelet consumption from microthrombi rather than global consumption coagulopathy.
- Timing: haemolysis and thrombocytopenia emerged after clinical improvement of sepsis and resolution of coagulopathy — argues for a new process (TMA) triggered by infection rather than ongoing DIC.
- Exclusion of alternate causes: negative repeat DAT (the initial IgG likely false positive from penicillin/transfusion) excludes warm autoimmune haemolysis; ADAMTS13 activity 43.5% rules out TTP (would be <10%); stool Shiga toxin PCR negative.
- Organ‑specific evidence of microvascular thrombosis: kidney biopsy showing ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and TMA morphology — pathologic confirmation that microvascular platelet thrombi were driving the clinical picture.
- Therapeutic response: transient improvement with plasma exchange and sustained haematologic/renal recovery after complement blockade (eculizumab) supports complement‑mediated TMA rather than DIC.

Practical diagnostic takeaways
- In a septic patient whose coagulopathy is improving, the new appearance of severe intravascular haemolysis (low haptoglobin, high LDH), schistocytes and persistent thrombocytopenia should prompt urgent evaluation for TMA (peripheral smear review, DAT, ADAMTS13, complement studies) and consideration of renal biopsy when safe.
- Use the pattern of coagulation tests to discriminate: abnormal PT/aPTT and low fibrinogen favour ongoing DIC; normalizing PT/aPTT with continued MAHA/thrombocytopenia favors TMA.

In short: isolated MAHA with schistocytosis, undetectable haptoglobin and rising LDH, persistent thrombocytopenia despite normalization of PT/aPTT and fibrinogen, severe AKI with biopsy‑proven microvascular thrombi, and exclusion of TTP point to a superimposed TMA (in this case complement‑mediated HUS) rather than ongoing DIC.

### [Morning Report] In an adult with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury, what laboratory test must be sent urgently to evaluate for thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, and what ADAMTS13 activity threshold essentially rules out TTP?

Send an ADAMTS13 activity assay (functional activity) urgently — ideally with concurrent testing for an ADAMTS13 inhibitor/autoantibody — and draw the sample before plasma exchange or major transfusion if possible. Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10% activity) is the hallmark of immune-mediated TTP; an activity well above that threshold (for practical purposes >10%) essentially rules out TTP and should prompt evaluation for other causes of TMA (eg, HUS/CM‑HUS).

Why: ADAMTS13 is the metalloprotease that cleaves ultra‑large vWF multimers; severe deficiency leads to platelet-rich microthrombi, MAHA and profound thrombocytopenia. The diagnosis is laboratory‑based, so an urgent functional assay is required even when you begin empiric plasma exchange for high clinical suspicion (use PLASMIC score to triage). In the presented case the ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, which effectively excluded TTP and helped redirect the workup toward complement‑mediated HUS.

### [Clinical Pearls] A patient with hemolysis has a direct antiglobulin test (IgG) initially positive while receiving penicillin and after recent transfusions. What are two common causes of a false-positive DAT in this context, and what bedside clues help distinguish autoimmune hemolysis from microangiopathic hemolysis?

Direct answer
- Two common causes of a false‑positive direct antiglobulin (DAT) in this setting are: (1) drug‑induced coating of red cells (penicillin‑type hapten adsorption or formation of drug‑dependent antibodies) and (2) passive transfer of antibodies from recent transfusions or plasma products (including plasma or pooled‑plasma preparations). In this case the patient was receiving penicillin and had recent blood/product exposures, both well‑recognized causes of a spurious DAT.

Clinical reasoning and bedside discrimination between autoimmune hemolysis and microangiopathic hemolysis (MAHA/TMA)
- Platelet count: thrombocytopenia is a cardinal feature of TMA. AIHA usually has preserved platelets unless there is a concurrent process. In the case the platelets remained low (30–46 ×10^9/L), pointing away from isolated AIHA and toward a TMA.
- Peripheral blood smear: schistocytes (fragmented RBCs) indicate mechanical shearing of red cells and support MAHA/TMA; spherocytes favour warm AIHA. In this patient the smear showed 15–20% schistocytes—strong evidence for MAHA.
- DAT and treatment response: a true warm AIHA usually has a reproducible positive IgG DAT and often responds to corticosteroids. A DAT that is transient or converts to negative on repeat testing (especially when predating extensive immune workup) suggests a false positive. Here the initial DAT was positive while on penicillin/after transfusion, corticosteroids produced no durable improvement, and the repeat DAT was negative — consistent with a false‑positive DAT and a non‑autoimmune cause.
- Reticulocyte response and timing: AIHA typically produces a brisk reticulocytosis (unless marrow suppressed); MAHA can occur with variable retic response. In this case the reticulocyte response was blunted earlier (likely sepsis/marrow effects), but the sudden fall in hemoglobin with schistocytes and ongoing thrombocytopenia argued for TMA.
- Organ dysfunction and context: prominent acute kidney injury, new anuria, and other end‑organ ischemic signs (digital ischemia/necrosis) favour TMA. The patient’s severe AKI and biopsy showing fibrin thrombi confirmed TMA.
- Additional labs are supportive but nonspecific: both entities can have low haptoglobin and high LDH and indirect bilirubin; coagulation tests (prolonged PT/APTT, low fibrinogen) suggest DIC rather than isolated AIHA.

Practical teaching points
- Always interpret a positive DAT in clinical context: recent penicillin exposure and transfusions are common causes of a false‑positive test.
- Immediate bedside clues that push you toward TMA rather than AIHA are persistent thrombocytopenia, abundant schistocytes on smear, end‑organ ischemia/AKI, lack of response to steroids, and a DAT that is not reproducibly positive — exactly the pattern seen in this patient that led to the diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway leads to endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy in complement-mediated HUS, including why serum C3/C4 levels and standard pathway assays can remain normal during active disease.

Direct answer
Complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) results from uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway on the endothelial surface. That surface‑restricted amplification generates C3 and C5 convertases, producing anaphylatoxins (especially C5a) and terminal membrane attack complex (C5b‑9). C5a and sublytic or lytic C5b‑9 injure and activate endothelial cells, shifting them to a prothrombotic phenotype (tissue‑factor expression, Weibel–Palade body release of vWF, platelet adhesion), promoting formation of platelet‑rich microthrombi in small renal arterioles and glomerular capillaries. These microthrombi shear red cells (MAHA), consume platelets (thrombocytopenia), and cause renal ischemia and acute kidney injury – the clinicopathologic triad of TMA.

Stepwise pathophysiology (what happens and why)
- The alternative pathway has a baseline “tick‑over” (spontaneous, low‑level C3 hydrolysis) that is normally kept in check by plasma and membrane regulators (factor H, factor I, CD46/MCP, CR1).  
- Genetic defects or autoantibodies that reduce regulation (eg, abnormal factor H, factor I, MCP, factor B variants) permit unchecked surface amplification: C3b deposition → formation of C3bBb (C3 convertase) → an amplification loop → large amounts of C3b and downstream C5 convertase activity.  
- Local generation of C5a recruits and activates neutrophils; C5b‑9 assembles on endothelial membranes. Even sublytic C5b‑9 triggers endothelial cell exocytosis, upregulation of adhesion molecules and tissue factor, loss of antithrombotic surface (eg, thrombomodulin downregulation), and promotes platelet aggregation.  
- The net effect is microvascular thrombosis (fibrin + platelets), mechanical hemolysis across partially occluded capillaries (schistocytes, high LDH, low haptoglobin), and organ injury—most clinically apparent in the kidneys.

Why serum C3/C4 and standard pathway assays can be normal
- Local vs systemic activation: CM‑HUS is driven by complement activation bound to endothelial surfaces. Local surface amplification can cause severe endothelial injury without large‑scale consumption of circulating C3/C4, so serum levels measured in peripheral blood often remain within reference ranges.  
- Pathway specificity: The alternative pathway primarily consumes C3; C4 is part of the classical/lectin pathways and is often unchanged in alternative‑pathway–driven disease, so C4 is expected to be normal.  
- Kinetics and acute phase response: Acute hepatic synthesis of complement components can compensate, and single time‑point “snapshot” assays (C3, C4) are insensitive to ongoing localized activation.  
- Assay limitations and treatment effects: Standard functional assays (CH50, AH50) and C3/C4 may be normal early, and they are also affected by interventions (plasma exchange removes components; eculizumab blocks terminal activation and changes functional readouts). In the presented case, initial routine C3/C4 and pathway tests were normal while the patient was developing TMA; later a markedly reduced AH50 was observed after PEX and eculizumab—most consistent with treatment effects rather than primary systemic depletion.

What tests better reflect complement‑mediated disease
- Split products and terminal activation markers (eg, plasma C3d, C3a, C5a, sC5b‑9) or evidence of reduced functional alternative pathway activity (AH50) can be more informative than static C3/C4 levels. Testing for anti‑factor H autoantibodies, levels of complement regulators (factor H/I/B), and genetic testing for complement regulatory gene variants aid diagnosis. Kidney biopsy showing TMA provides histologic confirmation when clinical picture is unclear.

How this explains the case
- This patient had an infection (C. canimorsus) known to interact with complement and acted as a trigger. Early sepsis/DIC dominated and likely obscured evolving CM‑HUS. The later abrupt MAHA, persistent thrombocytopenia, schistocytes and biopsy‑proven glomerular fibrin thrombi are exactly the downstream consequences of endothelial surface–restricted alternative pathway overactivation. The normal routine C3/C4 and early functional assays did not exclude CM‑HUS because activation was localized and because assays can be normal depending on timing and prior or subsequent therapies (PEX, eculizumab). The later reduced AH50 is explained by the effects of plasma exchange and complement blockade rather than proving systemic complement depletion prior to therapy.

Clinical pearl
In suspected TMA, normal serum C3/C4 does not rule out CM‑HUS. Diagnosis rests on the clinical pattern (MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI), exclusion of TTP (ADAMTS13), exclusion of STEC, targeted complement testing (split products, AH50, anti‑factor H), and histology when needed. Early recognition and terminal complement blockade (eculizumab) can halt endothelial injury and improve outcomes, but timing of sampling and recent therapies must be considered when interpreting complement assays.

### [Management] In suspected complement-mediated HUS complicating recent bacteremia, outline immediate management while awaiting ADAMTS13 and Shiga-toxin results: when to initiate plasma exchange as a bridge, when to start eculizumab, and how to mitigate infectious risk with meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis.

Direct answer
- While awaiting ADAMTS13 and Shiga‑toxin results in a patient with new MAHA, thrombocytopenia and AKI, treat emergently for the most immediately life‑threatening and remediable diagnoses: assume possible TTP and start plasma exchange (PEX) if the clinical picture or a high PLASMIC score supports it; send ADAMTS13 immediately and continue PEX until severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%) is excluded. If TTP is unlikely (low PLASMIC, ADAMTS13 likely >10%) and the clinical picture is instead consistent with complement‑mediated HUS (severe AKI, schistocytes, negative STEC testing), arrange prompt eculizumab — ideally after the infective trigger is controlled or blood cultures are negative and after meningococcal vaccination or while on antibiotic prophylaxis. In all cases obtain baseline complement and genetic testing before PEX or complement blockade when possible, and mitigate meningococcal risk by vaccinating (MenACWY + MenB) and providing immediate antibiotic prophylaxis until vaccine protection is achieved.

Clinical reasoning and practical steps (what to do now and why)
1. Rapid diagnostic priorities
   - Immediately send ADAMTS13 activity (reference lab) and stool PCR for Shiga toxin. These are the decisive tests to distinguish TTP (ADAMTS13 <10%) and STEC‑HUS.
   - Obtain blood cultures before antibiotics if that does not meaningfully delay therapy; start empiric antibiotics promptly for sepsis.

2. When to start plasma exchange (PEX)
   - Indication: start urgent PEX if TTP cannot be excluded and the patient has clinically significant MAHA ± severe thrombocytopenia ± neurologic features — i.e., high or intermediate pretest probability (PLASMIC score high/intermediate) or when ADAMTS13 result is not immediately available.
   - Rationale: PEX is lifesaving in TTP; delaying PEX while waiting for ADAMTS13 risks irreversible organ injury.
   - Use plasma replacement (FFP/Octaplas); reassess daily and stop PEX once ADAMTS13 activity is >10% and an alternative diagnosis is established.
   - In suspected CM‑HUS PEX can be used as a temporizing measure (it may transiently remove complement fragments or autoantibodies) but it is not disease modifying; expect only temporary improvement. This is what occurred in the case: PEX was used as a bridge and produced transient diuresis before definitive therapy.

3. When to start eculizumab (complement blockade)
   - Indication: strong clinical and laboratory evidence of complement‑mediated TMA (MAHA, schistocytes, persistent thrombocytopenia, severe AKI or biopsy‑proven TMA) once TTP (ADAMTS13 <10%) and STEC‑HUS are excluded or unlikely.
   - Timing with infection: because terminal complement is important for defence against encapsulated organisms, delay eculizumab until active, uncontrolled bacteraemia is controlled (blood cultures negative or on effective therapy) if clinically feasible. However, in fulminant CM‑HUS with life‑ or organ‑threatening disease, start eculizumab urgently after multidisciplinary discussion and with maximal antimicrobial coverage — balancing risk of infection against risk of irreversible organ damage.
   - Practical coordination: do not start PEX immediately after giving eculizumab (PEX removes drug). If PEX is required after starting eculizumab, expect to redose eculizumab or adjust dosing strategy.

4. Vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis to mitigate meningococcal (and other encapsulated) risk
   - Vaccinate with both a conjugate quadrivalent meningococcal vaccine (MenACWY) and a serogroup B vaccine (MenB). Best practice is to give both vaccines at least 2 weeks before initiating eculizumab to allow protective immunity.
   - If eculizumab cannot be delayed for 2 weeks after vaccination (common in urgent cases), give vaccines immediately and start antibiotic prophylaxis until 2 weeks after completion of the vaccine series (or until protective period has passed). In practice many centers:
     - Give MenACWY + MenB at diagnosis or immediately before eculizumab.
     - Start oral antibiotic prophylaxis (eg, penicillin V or amoxicillin) until 2 weeks after vaccination; for true penicillin allergy use an alternative per local guidance (eg, ciprofloxacin or doxycycline) and specialist advice.
   - During ongoing eculizumab therapy many centers continue long‑term prophylactic antibiotics and counsel patients to seek urgent care for fever; some also provide a course of standby antibiotics and recommend prompt medical evaluation for any febrile illness.
   - In the reported case the team gave MenACWY (Menveo) and MenB (Bexsero) immediately before starting eculizumab and provided prophylactic amoxicillin because the vaccines had not been given ≥2 weeks beforehand; blood cultures were negative when eculizumab was started — a cautious, guideline‑concordant approach.

5. Testing logistics and interpretation
   - Draw complement tests and genetic testing samples before PEX and before eculizumab whenever feasible; both PEX and eculizumab alter complement assays and can confound interpretation. The case illustrates this: standard C3/C4 were normal and extended testing was done later — some abnormalities were attributed to treatment effects.
   - ADAMTS13 should be drawn before first PEX if possible but do not delay PEX for the result if clinical suspicion of TTP exists.

6. Communication and follow‑up
   - Provide clear counseling and written instructions about infection risk (meningococcal and other encapsulated organisms), the need for urgent evaluation for fever, and the vaccination schedule (boosters as recommended).
   - Coordinate infectious disease, hematology and nephrology input for individual risk–benefit decisions (especially when active bacteremia is present).

Bottom line practical algorithm for the bedside (while awaiting ADAMTS13 and Shiga‑toxin)
   1. Send ADAMTS13, Shiga PCR/stool, blood cultures, baseline complement/genetics.
   2. Empiric antibiotics for sepsis immediately.
   3. If TTP cannot be excluded (high/intermediate PLASMIC or severe clinical disease) → start daily PEX now; continue until ADAMTS13 excludes TTP.
   4. If clinical picture favors CM‑HUS (severe AKI, dialysis dependence, schistocytes, negative STEC) and TTP is unlikely → arrange eculizumab promptly. If active bacteraemia, prefer to wait for culture clearance if feasible; if not feasible because of life‑threatening TMA, start eculizumab with maximal antibiotics and meningococcal prophylaxis.
   5. Give MenACWY + MenB vaccines ideally ≥2 weeks before eculizumab; if eculizumab must start sooner, vaccinate immediately and give antibiotic prophylaxis until vaccine protection is established (and consider continued prophylaxis during therapy).
   6. Reassess daily and tailor therapy (stop PEX if ADAMTS13 >10%; continue or stop eculizumab per response and genetic testing).

The case in point
- This patient exemplifies the approach: ADAMTS13 and complement studies were sent; PEX was started as bridging therapy while TTP was excluded; blood cultures became negative and meningococcal vaccines were given; eculizumab induction followed and produced sustained hematologic and renal improvement. This sequencing minimized infectious risk while treating the complement‑driven TMA in a timely fashion.

### [Pathophysiology] What are the characteristic kidney biopsy findings of thrombotic microangiopathy on light microscopy, and how do immunofluorescence and electron microscopy help distinguish TMA from immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritides?

Direct answer
- On light microscopy (LM) acute thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) characteristically shows platelet‑ and fibrin‑rich thrombi occluding glomerular capillaries and small arterioles, swollen and detached endothelial cells, mesangiolysis, ischemic collapse or wrinkling of glomerular tufts and often acute tubular necrosis from ischemia. Segmental necrosis and crescents may be present. Chronic TMA changes include intimal mucoid/“onion‑skin” thickening of arterioles, recanalization, and double contours (GBM duplication) from new basement membrane formation.
- Immunofluorescence (IF) in TMA is typically negative or shows only nonspecific/trapped IgM or C3 in areas of injury; it does not show the granular or linear immune‑complex staining (IgG, IgA, C3) that defines immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritides.
- Electron microscopy (EM) in TMA demonstrates endothelial cell swelling and detachment, subendothelial widening with electron‑lucent (“fluffy”) fibrinous material or fibrin tactoids and GBM duplication in chronic lesions, but it lacks the electron‑dense immune complex deposits that are the hallmark of immune complex GN (eg, subepithelial “humps” in post‑infectious GN, subendothelial deposits in lupus, mesangial deposits in IgA nephropathy).

Clinical reasoning tied to the case
- The patient’s biopsy in the report showed severe ATN, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and scattered crescents on LM—classic acute TMA findings explaining dialysis‑dependent AKI.  
- IF showed no immune complex deposition and EM had “no evidence of primary glomerulopathy,” which argues against immune complex–mediated GN and supports a diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS/TMA.  
- Practically, presence of occlusive fibrin thrombi on LM plus absent immune complexes on IF/EM shifts the pathology away from immune complex GN and toward a thrombotic (complement‑driven or other) microangiopathic process, consistent with the clinical picture and response to complement blockade in this case.

### [Morning Report] How do plasma exchange and eculizumab therapy alter the interpretation of complement studies (for example, AH50 and C3/C4), and how should the timing of sampling relative to these therapies influence diagnostic conclusions?

Bottom line
- Both plasma exchange (PEX) and eculizumab profoundly and predictably alter complement assays. If you sample after either therapy, functional assays (CH50/AH50) and antigenic factor levels (C3, C4, factor H/I/B) may reflect treatment effects rather than the patient’s intrinsic complement state. For correct diagnostic interpretation, obtain key complement studies before PEX and before starting eculizumab whenever feasible and always document the timing and replacement fluid used.

Why this happens — pathophysiology and assay mechanics
- PEX: removes circulating complement proteins, split products and autoantibodies. The effect on assays depends on the replacement fluid:
  - Plasma replacement (FFP/Octaplas) will tend to replete complement proteins and can transiently normalize antigenic levels and functional activity, and can dilute or replace patient autoantibodies.
  - Albumin replacement will dilute and lower complement proteins and functional activity (false low).
  - Repeated PEX can therefore either mask a deficiency/autoantibody (if plasma replacement) or produce artifactual low complement activity (if albumin).
- Eculizumab: is an anti‑C5 monoclonal antibody that blocks terminal pathway cleavage of C5. It abolishes terminal pathway–dependent haemolytic assays (CH50 and AH50) and renders them markedly reduced or undetectable while the drug is present. Antigenic levels of upstream proteins (C3, C4, factor B, factor H/I) are not directly destroyed by eculizumab, but their measured function may be misleading: ongoing consumption upstream may still be present even if terminal activity is suppressed.
  - Practically: a very low/absent AH50 or CH50 after eculizumab is expected and reflects drug effect, not intrinsic terminal pathway failure.

Practical implications and timing (clinical pearls)
- Best practice: draw baseline diagnostic tests before any PEX and before the first dose of eculizumab:
  - Functional assays: CH50 and AH50 (or functional AP/CP assays)
  - Antigenic complement levels: C3, C4, factor H, factor I, factor B
  - Anti–factor H autoantibodies
  - ADAMTS13 (for TTP exclusion) — also before PEX if possible
  - Genetic testing (complement gene sequencing) — not affected by PEX or eculizumab; can be sent any time
- If PEX has already been given:
  - Record number of sessions, timing of last session and the replacement fluid (plasma vs albumin).
  - Be cautious: a normal C3/C4 or normal AH50 after plasma replacement does not exclude a prior deficiency or autoantibody (may be donor-derived). A low AH50 after albumin PEX suggests depletion from the procedure.
  - Anti‑complement autoantibodies (eg, anti–factor H) may be removed by PEX and produce false‑negative results; ideally sample before PEX or repeat testing several days to weeks after the last exchange to allow reappearance.
- If eculizumab has been given:
  - Expect CH50/AH50 to be suppressed for the duration of C5 blockade (measurable for days–weeks after dosing). Do not use CH50/AH50 to assess underlying alternative pathway dysregulation while on eculizumab.
  - A low AH50 shortly after eculizumab (as in this case — AH50 6% measured 4 days after the first eculizumab dose) is most consistent with drug effect.
  - Antigenic assays and genetic testing remain interpretable, but autoantibody testing can still be affected if prior PEX was performed.
- If you must interpret tests obtained after therapy:
  - Low AH50/CH50 + recent eculizumab → interpret as treatment effect unless no drug was given.
  - Low AH50/CH50 + recent PEX with albumin → likely depletion from exchange.
  - Normal complement levels after PEX with plasma replacement → may be donor-driven; pathology can still exist.
  - Autoantibody assays performed after PEX are unreliable for ruling out antibody‑mediated CM‑HUS.

Applying this to the case
- In the reported patient, basic C3/C4 and pathway tests were obtained before the first PEX and were normal. Extended testing (AH50 markedly reduced to 6%) was performed 8 days after the final PEX and 4 days after the first eculizumab dose. That timing explains the finding: the suppressed AH50 is most consistent with eculizumab’s terminal‑pathway blockade (and to a lesser degree the recent PEX), rather than proving intrinsic alternative‑pathway consumption or deficiency. Genetic testing (unaffected) later showed no pathogenic variants; anti–factor H antibodies had been negative, but interpretation of antibody tests performed after PEX must be cautious because PEX can remove such antibodies.

Summary checklist when you suspect complement‑mediated TMA
- Before therapy (ideal): draw CH50/AH50, C3/C4, factor H/I/B, anti‑factor H antibodies, ADAMTS13, and send genetic testing.
- If therapy already started: document timing of last PEX and eculizumab and the replacement fluid; interpret CH50/AH50 as likely drug/procedure effects; repeat critical antibody assays some time after last PEX if initial sampling was post‑exchange.
- Remember: normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS; functional assays are most informative when obtained before PEX/eculizumab.

### [Management] What clinical factors predict renal recovery and guide duration of complement blockade in complement-mediated HUS, and how do time-to-eculizumab initiation and the presence or absence of pathogenic complement gene variants inform these decisions?

Direct answer
- The strongest clinical determinants of renal recovery in complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) are the severity and duration of the acute kidney injury (degree of oliguria/anuria and need for dialysis), the timing of effective complement blockade (earlier is better), the histologic severity/chronicity on kidney biopsy, and the initial haematologic response to therapy.  
- The presence of a pathogenic complement gene variant predicts a higher risk of relapse and incomplete renal recovery and therefore favors prolonged or indefinite complement inhibition. By contrast, a clearly identified single infectious trigger and absence of pathogenic variants support a time‑limited course of eculizumab with close monitoring and a planned trial of discontinuation after renal and haematologic stabilization.

Clinical reasoning and pathophysiology (concise)
- CM‑HUS is driven by uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway causing endothelial injury, microthrombi, and downstream renal ischaemia. The longer terminal complement-mediated endothelial injury persists, the more likely irreversible parenchymal loss (tubular necrosis, cortical scarring, glomerular sclerosis) will occur. Thus, time from onset of TMA to effective blockade of C5 is a biologically plausible determinant of renal salvage.
- Genetic defects in complement regulators (CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, MCP/CD46, or anti‑FH antibodies) lower the threshold for activation and increase relapse risk; patients with such variants frequently require prolonged therapy because the predisposing abnormality remains.

How this applies to the reported case
- Severity at presentation: the patient developed complete anuria and dialysis dependence — a high‑risk presentation for incomplete renal recovery. Kidney biopsy showed acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and crescents — features that reflect severe acute injury but limited evidence of chronicity, which is compatible with potential partial recovery.
- Timing of eculizumab: eculizumab was started after clinical control of the bacteraemia (negative cultures) and after initial PEX; haematologic remission and sustained renal improvement (partial recovery to creatinine 175 µmol/L and dialysis independence) followed. This course illustrates the principle that once infection is controlled, timely complement blockade can stop further endothelial injury and allow recovery.
- Complement genetics: extended genetic testing in this patient did not identify pathogenic variants. This finding lowers the predicted risk of relapse and supports the treating team’s plan for a finite maintenance period (they continued eculizumab every 2 weeks and planned reassessment for discontinuation once renal function stabilised). It also explains the treating team’s willingness to stop after a period of stability rather than commit to life‑long therapy.

Practical management framework to guide duration of complement blockade
1. Acute management and timing
   - Start eculizumab promptly once CM‑HUS is diagnosed or strongly suspected and active infection has been controlled or when benefits outweigh infection risks. Earlier initiation (days rather than weeks after TMA recognition) is associated with higher likelihood of renal recovery.
   - If active encapsulated infection is present, control with appropriate antibiotics and obtain negative cultures if feasible before starting eculizumab; vaccinate and provide antimicrobial prophylaxis when initiating therapy.

2. Risk stratification to plan duration
   - High‑risk for relapse/poor recovery (consider prolonged therapy): pathogenic/likely pathogenic complement gene variant or anti‑factor H autoantibody; recurrent prior episodes; ongoing complement activation without clear transient trigger; incomplete renal recovery with ongoing proteinuria or hematuria.
   - Lower‑risk for relapse (consider time‑limited therapy and reassessment): clear single trigger (eg, infection) that is controlled, no pathogenic variants, full haematologic remission, dialysis independence with stabilised creatinine, absence of significant proteinuria or chronic histologic changes.

3. Suggested practical timelines (institutional practices vary)
   - For infection‑triggered CM‑HUS without pathogenic variants: continue induction and short maintenance (examples range from 4 weeks to 3–6 months) until haematologic remission and renal function have plateaued; then consider supervised discontinuation with close surveillance.
   - For patients with pathogenic variants or recurrent disease: continue maintenance eculizumab long term, with individualization and specialist nephrology/genetics input.

4. Monitoring after discontinuation
   - Frequent checks initially (eg, weekly to biweekly for first 1–2 months, then spacing out) of urine output, creatinine, urinalysis (protein/hematuria), platelets, hemoglobin, LDH. Re‑initiate eculizumab promptly at first sign of relapse (new MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI).

Important caveats and practical points
- Complement assays and AH50 can be misleading if obtained after plasma exchange or after eculizumab administration; genetic testing should ideally be sent prior to these interventions but treatment should not be delayed for testing results. In the case presented, AH50 was low after PEX and eculizumab, consistent with treatment effects rather than intrinsic complement deficiency.
- Vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis are essential when starting terminal complement inhibitors because of the increased risk for infection with encapsulated organisms; in this case eculizumab was started after first meningococcal vaccine doses and while on prophylactic antibiotics.
- Decisions about duration should be multidisciplinary (nephrology, hematology, infectious diseases, genetics) and individualized; when in doubt, a fixed period of maintenance followed by careful discontinuation and surveillance is a reasonable approach for infection‑triggered CM‑HUS without identified pathogenic variants — exactly the strategy used in this patient.

Bottom line
- Time to effective complement blockade and the degree/duration of acute renal injury are the principal determinants of renal recovery in CM‑HUS. Pathogenic complement gene variants markedly increase relapse risk and usually mandate prolonged therapy. In infection‑triggered CM‑HUS without pathogenic variants (as in this case), a limited course of eculizumab with planned reassessment after haematologic remission and renal stabilisation is an evidence‑informed and pragmatic strategy.

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical syndromes and risk factors are associated with Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection, why is diagnosis often delayed, and which diagnostic methods can improve detection in suspected invasive disease?

Bottom line
- Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a zoonotic, encapsulated Gram‑negative rod carried in dog and cat oral flora that can produce a wide spectrum of disease — from localized wound infection to fulminant sepsis with DIC, purpura fulminans, meningitis, endocarditis, and, rarely, thrombotic microangiopathy (including complement‑mediated HUS). Risk is higher after bites/scratches or close contact, particularly in persons with impaired splenic function, alcoholism or other immunosuppression — but invasive disease can occur in previously healthy patients, as illustrated by this case.
Why the diagnosis is often delayed
- Fastidious and slow growing: C. canimorsus grows poorly on routine plates and often requires enriched media/CO2 conditions and prolonged incubation. Blood culture–based identification can take several days (7 days in this report), delaying organism‑directed therapy and epidemiologic recognition.
- Prior antibiotics and low organism burden: pre‑treatment or low-level bacteraemia lowers culture yield.
- Atypical presentation and low index of suspicion: presentations may mimic common entities (severe sepsis, DIC, meningitis), so clinicians may not immediately link an animal exposure to an unusual pathogen.
- Conventional biochemical methods can misidentify or fail to speciate the organism.
How to improve detection in suspected invasive disease
- Clinical steps
  - Take a careful exposure history (dog/cat bite, scratch or close contact). In any patient with severe sepsis after a canine exposure, alert the microbiology lab that Capnocytophaga is possible.
  - Obtain blood cultures before antibiotics when feasible.
- Laboratory methods that increase yield or speed
  - Prolonged incubation and enriched culture conditions (CO2, chocolate or blood agar, both aerobic and anaerobic bottles) — ask the lab to incubate longer than routine.
  - Modern identification technologies: MALDI‑TOF mass spectrometry rapidly and accurately identifies Capnocytophaga once colonies grow.
  - Molecular diagnostics: targeted 16S rRNA PCR, broad‑range bacterial PCR, and metagenomic sequencing (including rapid nanopore sequencing) can detect the organism faster and when cultures are negative or suppressed by antibiotics. The case report notes increasing use of real‑time nanopore sequencing for this reason.
  - Send isolates or positive cultures to reference labs for confirmatory testing when needed.
Clinical relevance (from this case)
- In the reported patient a dog bite preceded fulminant sepsis; culture confirmation required days (blood cultures confirmed C. canimorsus on day 7). Early empirical coverage for dog‑associated pathogens (piperacillin/tazobactam) was appropriate, and directed therapy was possible once the organism was identified. The organism’s capsule and variable serum susceptibility are biologically relevant: complement interaction both helps clear the pathogen and may, in susceptible hosts, trigger endothelial injury and CM‑HUS — a mechanism illustrated by this case.
Practical pearl
- In severe sepsis after a dog/cat exposure: inform the lab, request extended incubation and enhanced identification (MALDI‑TOF ± 16S/metagenomic testing) early — this shortens time to diagnosis and guides targeted therapy and infection‑related management decisions (including safe timing of complement blockade when TMA is suspected).
