Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

### [Clinical Pearls] In an adult who develops septic shock four days after a dog bite, which zoonotic pathogens must empiric therapy cover, and what initial intravenous antibiotic regimen provides reliable coverage for Capnocytophaga species?

When an adult presents with fulminant infection after a dog bite you must think beyond skin flora: the empiric regimen needs to cover the animal‑mouth organisms that cause rapidly progressive sepsis as well as the usual streptococci/staphylococci and anaerobes. The pathogens you must cover empirically include Capnocytophaga canimorsus and Pasteurella species, aerobic streptococci and staphylococci (add MRSA coverage if risk factors or severe soft‑tissue infection), and anaerobic oral flora from the dog’s mouth.

Clinically pragmatic empiric therapy for severe sepsis from a dog bite is an intravenous beta‑lactam/beta‑lactamase‑inhibitor that reliably covers Capnocytophaga, Pasteurella and anaerobes — for example piperacillin–tazobactam or ampicillin–sulbactam. In patients with shock or concern for resistant Gram‑positives add vancomycin until cultures clarify. Alternatives that cover Capnocytophaga include third‑generation cephalosporins or a carbapenem; doxycycline or a fluoroquinolone have activity but are not preferred as sole agents in septic shock.

Put simply: cover Capnocytophaga + Pasteurella + strep/staph + anaerobes up front — e.g., IV piperacillin–tazobactam (± vancomycin if MRSA concern) provides reliable empiric coverage for Capnocytophaga in the critically ill. Adjust therapy once cultures and susceptibilities return.

### [Morning Report] At the bedside, what clinical or laboratory changes should prompt you to suspect a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in a patient whose sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) seem to be improving?

Teaching answer:

When a patient with sepsis and DIC seems to be improving, remain alert for a new, superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy. The clinical clue is a change in the haematologic or renal picture that diverges from the expected recovery from sepsis/DIC — not subtle shifts in inflammatory markers. Specifically, suspect TMA if you see a sudden, otherwise unexplained fall in haemoglobin without overt bleeding accompanied by persistent or new thrombocytopenia despite normalization of coagulation parameters, and evidence of haemolysis: rising LDH, undetectable or very low haptoglobin, rising indirect bilirubin, and a reticulocytosis. At the bedside the single most important test is a peripheral blood smear showing schistocytes (fragmented red cells). New or worsening anuria/AKI (or an unexplained failure of renal recovery) or new end‑organ ischemic signs (digital ischemia, altered mental status, new hypertension, proteinuria/hematuria) should heighten suspicion.

Why this pattern? In recovering sepsis/DIC the INR, fibrinogen and bleeding tendency usually improve and platelet counts begin to recover. Persistence of thrombocytopenia or a secondary fall in haemoglobin with biochemical haemolysis and schistocytes points to intravascular red‑cell fragmentation from microthrombi (TMA) rather than consumption coagulopathy. Note that routine complement levels (C3/C4) and even early ADAMTS13 can be non‑diagnostic; however, rapid ADAMTS13 (<10%) rules in TTP and must be sought. Thus, when you see the constellation above, immediately obtain a smear and haemolysis panel, draw ADAMTS13 and complement studies, review urine for active sediment/protein, and discuss early hematology/nephrology input and empiric therapy (PEX or complement blockade) as clinically indicated while awaiting results.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Define microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and list the key peripheral smear findings that confirm it.

Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) is a non‑immune hemolytic process caused by mechanical destruction of red blood cells as they traverse small‑vessel platelet‑rich thrombi or severely damaged endothelium (as occurs in thrombotic microangiopathies, DIC, malignant hypertension, cardiac prostheses). Clinically it presents with anemia, schistocytosis on the peripheral smear, reduced haptoglobin, elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin, and often concurrent thrombocytopenia.

The peripheral‑blood smear features that confirm MAHA are the morphologic evidence of mechanical red‑cell fragmentation—most importantly increased schistocytes (fragmented RBCs, including helmet cells and triangular fragments). A schistocyte count ≥1% (or markedly increased fragmented forms on a qualitative read) is considered diagnostic in the appropriate clinical context. Other supportive smear findings include numerous RBC fragments of varying shapes and sizes, occasional microspherocytes from partial fragmentation, polychromasia and nucleated RBCs reflecting brisk marrow response. The smear should be interpreted with the clinical and laboratory hemolysis markers (undetectable haptoglobin, high LDH, reticulocytosis) and with exclusion of immune hemolysis (negative repeat DAT) to distinguish MAHA from other causes—as in this case, where 15–20% schistocytes established the diagnosis of a TMA.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Which test should be obtained urgently to evaluate for thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) in an adult with MAHA and thrombocytopenia, and what activity threshold effectively rules in or out TTP?

Obtain an ADAMTS13 activity assay urgently — drawn before any plasma exchange or plasma infusion — because the diagnosis of TTP is laboratory-defined by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency. An activity level <10% (some labs use ≤10%) effectively confirms TTP and mandates immediate plasma exchange and immunosuppression, whereas activity well above that threshold (generally >10–15%) makes TTP unlikely and should prompt evaluation for alternative TMAs (HUS, complement‑mediated TMA, DIC, secondary causes). Draw a citrated plasma sample, freeze or send per your lab’s protocol, and use clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score to decide empiric PEX while awaiting results. Remember that prior plasma therapy can raise ADAMTS13 activity, so the sample should be obtained before treatment whenever possible.

### [Clinical Pearls] How does the PLASMIC score inform initial management when TTP is suspected, and how should it be used while awaiting ADAMTS13 results?

The PLASMIC score is a practical bedside tool for estimating the pretest probability that an adult with MAHA and thrombocytopenia has severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (TTP). Its value lies not in making the diagnosis but in guiding the urgency and aggressiveness of empiric therapy while you wait for the definitive ADAMTS13 assay.

How it should change initial management
Use the PLASMIC score to stratify risk and decide whether to start immediate therapeutic plasma exchange (PEX) and empiric corticosteroids. A high PLASMIC score (typically 6–7) confers a high probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and should prompt immediate initiation of PEX without waiting for the assay result, because untreated TTP has very high mortality and PEX is lifesaving. A low score (0–4) indicates a low probability of TTP and should prompt careful pursuit of alternative causes of TMA (sepsis-associated DIC, STEC-HUS, complement-mediated HUS, malignant hypertension, drugs, pregnancy-related TMAs) and generally argues against routine empiric PEX unless the clinical picture is otherwise compelling.

How to use it while awaiting ADAMTS13 results
Obtain ADAMTS13 samples immediately (draw before PEX if possible) and send them stat. While awaiting results, apply PLASMIC in the context of the whole clinical picture: a high score plus compatible clinical features → start PEX and steroids promptly; a low or intermediate score → prioritize targeted evaluation for other TMAs, supportive care, and limited or no empiric PEX unless the patient is deteriorating or has features strongly suggestive of TTP (rapid neurologic decline, refractory shock, or otherwise unexplained severe thrombocytopenia/hemolysis). Reassess frequently and be prepared to start or stop PEX based on dynamic clinical course and the ADAMTS13 level when it returns.

Limitations and practical caveats
Remember that PLASMIC is a pretest probability tool, not a rule-out test. It was derived in cohorts where TTP predominated; its performance can be altered by confounders common in sepsis and DIC (abnormal INR, renal dysfunction, transfusions). Clinical judgment must override a low score when the patient is rapidly deteriorating. Also, try to obtain the ADAMTS13 sample before PEX because plasma therapy can alter levels, but do not delay life‑saving PEX to do so.

Connection to the case
In this patient the PLASMIC score was 4/7 (low pretest probability), and the ADAMTS13 activity returned at 43.5%, effectively excluding TTP and redirecting the team toward alternative diagnoses (CM‑HUS triggered by Capnocytophaga). That approach avoided prolonged unnecessary PEX for TTP and allowed focus on complement-directed therapy once the diagnosis of complement‑mediated TMA was supported.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What distinguishing laboratory features help differentiate DIC from primary TMA on presentation (consider PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer, and schistocyte burden)?

Teaching pearl:

Disseminated intravascular coagulation and thrombotic microangiopathies both cause thrombocytopenia and microvascular thrombosis, but they show a characteristic opposite pattern on routine coagulation tests because DIC is a consumptive coagulopathy while primary TMAs are platelet–microthrombus processes that spare the plasmatic clotting cascade.

Clinically the useful distinguishing laboratory pattern is this: in DIC you expect consumptive coagulopathy — prolonged PT/INR and often prolonged aPTT, markedly reduced fibrinogen, and very high D‑dimer/fibrin‑degradation products. Schistocytes may be present but are usually modest in number. By contrast, in primary TMA (TTP, typical or complement‑mediated HUS) the PT/INR and aPTT are typically normal or only minimally prolonged, fibrinogen is normal, D‑dimer is normal or only mildly elevated, and the dominant abnormality is a high schistocyte burden reflecting shear‑stress red‑cell fragmentation (ICSH guidance: schistocytes ≥1% support MAHA; many TMAs show several percent or higher — sometimes double‑digit percentages as in this case).

Put another way: marked coagulopathy with low fibrinogen and very high D‑dimer points toward DIC; preserved coagulation parameters with heavy schistocytosis, very elevated LDH and absent/normal fibrinogen points toward TMA. One must remember overlap can occur during severe sepsis (DIC can precede or coexist with TMA), so use the temporal evolution: early sepsis with abnormal PT/aPTT and low fibrinogen = DIC; later resolution of coagulation tests with persistent hemolysis and high schistocyte burden (as in this patient’s 15–20% schistocytes) argues for a superimposed TMA/CM‑HUS.

### [Pathophysiology] In suspected complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), how reliable are serum C3 and C4 levels for diagnosis, and why might they be normal?

Short answer: Serum C3 and C4 are neither sensitive nor specific for diagnosing complement‑mediated HUS. Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS.

Why: CM‑HUS is driven by dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway at the endothelial surface rather than by sustained, systemic depletion of circulating complement proteins. Several mechanistic and practical reasons explain normal levels. First, complement activation in CM‑HUS is often focal (microvascular endothelium of kidney and other organs) and can produce pathogenic injury without large drops in plasma C3 or C4. Second, the alternative pathway is the dominant driver; C4 is a classical/lectin pathway marker and may remain normal, and C3 can be only modestly consumed or rapidly replenished by hepatic synthesis, keeping measured concentrations in the reference range. Third, timing and interventions matter: sampling after partial recovery, after plasma exchange, or after initiation of eculizumab will alter measurements (PEX can remove fragments and proteins; eculizumab blocks terminal activation and can increase upstream component levels). Fourth, routine C3/C4 assays measure bulk protein concentration, not activation products — they miss evidence of activation such as increased Bb, Ba, or soluble C5b‑9, and do not detect functional dysregulation from pathogenic complement regulators or gain‑of‑function mutations.

Clinical corollary: In a patient with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI and biopsy evidence of TMA, normal C3/C4 should not delay consideration of CM‑HUS or empiric therapy when the clinical probability is high. More informative tests include functional pathway assays and measurements of activation fragments (Bb, Ba, sC5b‑9), and genetic/autoantibody testing for complement regulators — recognizing that these tests can be affected by PEX and complement inhibitors. Diagnosis of CM‑HUS remains clinicopathological and one of exclusion (STEC, TTP), not founded on normal/low C3 or C4 alone.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What is the recommended diagnostic approach to differentiate Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) from CM-HUS in an adult without a diarrheal prodrome?

When an adult presents with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia and AKI the immediate clinical priority is rapid, structured exclusion of the treatable mimics. The two diagnoses that most alter acute management are thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and Shiga toxin–producing E. coli–associated HUS (STEC‑HUS); once these are reasonably excluded, one can proceed to consider complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). In an adult without a diarrheal prodrome you must not assume STEC is absent — it can present atypically — so a methodical, time‑sensitive diagnostic approach is required.

First, obtain ADAMTS13 activity urgently to exclude TTP; values <10% mandate plasma exchange and prompt transfer to appropriate care, values >10% make TTP unlikely and redirect the workup. Second, send stool testing for Shiga toxin (molecular PCR for stx1/stx2 and stool culture for E. coli O157 and non‑O157) immediately — rectal swabs or stool should be sampled before antibiotics if feasible because test sensitivity falls with delay or prior antibiotics. If initial stool PCR is negative but clinical suspicion remains (for example after a prodrome of bloody diarrhea), consider repeat testing or serology for anti‑Stx antibodies later.

Parallel with those tests, perform standard hemolysis and TMA labs (schistocyte count, haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin), baseline complement levels (C3/C4) and rapid infectious workup. Interpret complement levels cautiously: normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS because levels may be normal or fluctuate. If you suspect CM‑HUS, arrange for functional complement assays and genetic testing for complement regulatory gene variants and factor H autoantibodies — recognizing these tests are send‑outs and results will not be immediately available.

Timing matters. Draw complement and ADAMTS13 samples before plasma exchange or eculizumab whenever possible, because PEX and complement blockade alter results (as occurred in this case). If PEX or eculizumab is already given, document timing clearly and interpret reduced alternative‑pathway activity or complement factor levels in that context.

Renal biopsy can be helpful when diagnosis remains unclear: light microscopy showing glomerular and arteriolar thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli and fibrin thrombi supports TMA but does not by itself distinguish STEC‑HUS from CM‑HUS; biopsy results should be integrated with microbiology and complement data.

Put this together in an algorithmic way: rule out TTP first (ADAMTS13), simultaneously test stool for Shiga toxin, and while awaiting results send baseline complement studies and arrange extended complement/genetic testing. If Shiga toxin is detected or there is a convincing diarrheal prodrome, treat as STEC‑HUS (supportive care; avoid eculizumab). If stool testing is negative, TTP excluded, and clinical course (absence of enteric prodrome, persistent thrombocytopenia and MAHA, renal biopsy consistent with TMA, and no other secondary cause) points to CM‑HUS, then initiate complement blockade promptly after ensuring active uncontrolled bacteraemia has been treated and after appropriate vaccinations and antimicrobial prophylaxis as needed.

In short: exclude TTP urgently, test stool for Shiga toxin before antibiotics if possible, obtain baseline complement studies and send extended complement/genetic testing (drawing samples before PEX/eculizumab), use biopsy and clinical context to support the diagnosis, and initiate eculizumab for CM‑HUS once STEC and TTP are reasonably excluded and infectious risks are mitigated.

### [Pathophysiology] List common triggers for complement-mediated HUS and explain why complement blockade can be effective even when no pathogenic complement gene variant is identified.

Common triggers of complement‑mediated HUS are those clinical events that acutely tilt the balance of the alternative complement pathway toward activation — classically infections (especially respiratory and gastrointestinal pathogens, but also unusual agents such as Capnocytophaga canimorsus), pregnancy and the postpartum period, certain drugs (e.g., calcineurin inhibitors, some chemotherapies), organ transplantation, malignancy, autoimmune flares, major surgery, and haematopoietic‑cell transplant or graft‑versus‑host disease. In practice you should think “anything that provides a strong inflammatory or endothelial insult” as a potential precipitant of CM‑HUS.

Why blockade of complement (eculizumab) can be effective even when no pathogenic complement gene variant is found comes down to pathophysiology: most CM‑HUS syndromes converge on uncontrolled activation of the terminal complement pathway and formation of the membrane attack complex with resultant endothelial injury, platelet aggregation and microvascular thrombosis. Genetic predisposition (loss‑of‑function in regulators or gain‑of‑function in activators) is common but not universal; many patients have no identifiable pathogenic variant because testing is incomplete (not all regulatory defects are known), penetrance is variable, somatic or acquired factors (for example anti‑factor H autoantibodies, transient depletion of regulators during sepsis, or overwhelming complement activation by an invading pathogen) produce the same downstream biology, or pathogenic variants lie in regions not assessed. Regardless of the upstream trigger, blocking C5 prevents generation of C5a and the membrane attack complex, halting the final common pathway of endothelial injury and TMA. Thus clinical response to terminal complement inhibition can occur even without a documented inherited complement defect.

Clinically this means: when a patient has MAHA, thrombocytopenia and AKI consistent with TMA and other causes (TTP, STEC‑HUS, immune hemolysis) are excluded or treated, early consideration of complement blockade is warranted if there is ongoing TMA — but balance this against infection risk (vaccinate, provide prophylactic antibiotics, and, when possible, treat and control active bacteraemia first), and reassess duration of therapy once genetic and immunologic testing and clinical course are clarified.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway leads to endothelial injury and the clinicopathologic features of TMA in CM-HUS.

The core pathophysiology of complement‑mediated HUS is uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway on the endothelial surface, with downstream generation of terminal complement that causes endothelial injury and a prothrombotic microvascular state. Put simply: an exaggerated, poorly regulated innate‑immune response turns the endothelium into the nidus for the small‑vessel thrombi that define TMA.

Under normal conditions the alternative pathway is constitutively active at a low level (“tick‑over”) and is tightly regulated on host cells by proteins such as factor H, factor I and membrane cofactor protein (CD46). When regulation fails—because of genetic defects, autoantibodies, or an overwhelming trigger such as infection—C3b deposition is amplified through formation of the C3 convertase (C3bBb). This feed‑forward amplification markedly increases generation of C5 convertase and terminal complement components, culminating in assembly of the membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) on endothelial membranes.

Deposition of C5b‑9 and related anaphylatoxins (C3a, C5a) injures and activates endothelial cells. Activated endothelium shifts from an anticoagulant to a procoagulant phenotype: it releases ultra‑large von Willebrand factor multimers, upregulates tissue factor, exposes subendothelial collagen, and becomes adhesive to platelets and leukocytes. The result is formation of platelet‑rich microthrombi in arterioles and glomerular capillaries. Red cells are sheared as they traverse these partially occluded microvessels, producing schistocytes and intravascular haemolysis; platelets are consumed, producing thrombocytopenia. When the kidney microcirculation is involved, glomerular ischemia, fibrin thrombi, and acute tubular injury produce the characteristic AKI of HUS and the biopsy hallmarks seen in this case (ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, scattered crescents).

Several clinical consequences and diagnostic implications flow directly from this mechanism and explain features of the presented case. First, complement activation may be focal and dynamic so routine serum C3/C4 levels are often normal and cannot exclude CM‑HUS; functional assays or genetic testing add sensitivity but may also be confounded by plasma exchange or complement blockade. Second, an infectious trigger such as Capnocytophaga canimorsus can both activate complement (helpful for host defence) and precipitate uncontrolled amplification in a susceptible host, explaining why TMA often follows or emerges as sepsis is improving. Third, because the injury is driven by terminal complement (C5 cleavage → C5b‑9), blockade at C5 with eculizumab halts ongoing endothelial damage, suppresses further microthrombus formation, and is the pathophysiologically targeted therapy—whereas plasma exchange may only transiently remove mediators or autoantibodies.

Finally, this pathophysiology explains key distinguishing features from other TMAs: in TTP the dominant mechanism is severe ADAMTS13 deficiency with accumulation of ultra‑large vWF multimers, whereas in CM‑HUS the primary driver is endothelial complement attack. In practice, therefore, a normal or modestly reduced ADAMTS13 level and the biopsy showing fibrin thrombi with endothelial injury—together with the clinical course and response to complement blockade—point to complement‑mediated TMA as in this patient.

### [Management] What is the mechanism of action of eculizumab in CM-HUS, and what hematologic and renal responses should clinicians expect in the first weeks after initiation?

Eculizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds complement component C5 and prevents its cleavage into C5a and C5b. By blocking C5 activation it prevents formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) and the downstream inflammatory effects of C5a. In CM‑HUS this stops ongoing complement‑mediated endothelial injury of the microvasculature, halts thrombus formation in glomerular and arteriolar capillaries, and therefore interrupts the pathophysiologic cascade that produces microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopaenia and acute kidney injury.

What to expect clinically and in the laboratory in the first days–weeks after starting eculizumab
- Haematologic response is usually rapid. Platelet counts typically begin to rise within 24–72 hours and often reach a sustained improvement within the first week; transfusion requirements usually fall quickly. Markers of haemolysis (LDH falls, haptoglobin normalizes, bilirubin declines) commonly improve over 48–96 hours, and schistocyte burden decreases over days to weeks. In practice you will see cessation of active haemolysis and stabilization of haemoglobin within the first week in most responders.
- Renal response is slower and more variable. Urine output, if any recoverable kidney function exists, may return within days to a couple of weeks; serum creatinine typically improves more gradually over weeks to months. Some patients who are dialysis‑dependent recover enough function to stop dialysis within days–weeks, but many have only partial recovery and persistent chronic kidney disease despite early haematologic remission. Time to eculizumab initiation and the degree/duration of renal ischemic injury before treatment are the main determinants of renal outcome.

Practical implications
Start eculizumab promptly when CM‑HUS is diagnosed or strongly suspected because haematologic remission is rapid and earlier therapy correlates with better renal recovery. Remember the infectious risk from terminal complement blockade: ensure appropriate meningococcal vaccination and give antimicrobial prophylaxis until vaccine immunity is established, and monitor cultures—ideally start eculizumab after blood cultures are negative if feasible. In this case the clinical course is typical: rapid haematologic remission after eculizumab and only partial renal recovery (creatinine 175 µmol/L, dialysis discontinued) reflecting both the delay to therapy and the severity of initial kidney injury.

### [Management] Why is plasma exchange (PEX) initiated empirically in adults with suspected TMA even when TTP is not yet confirmed, and what is its role once CM-HUS becomes the leading diagnosis?

When an adult presents with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia and organ dysfunction the immediate, treatable diagnosis to exclude is thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). TTP is a hematologic emergency: untreated severe ADAMTS13 deficiency carries a very high early mortality, and plasma exchange (PEX) reduces that mortality dramatically by both removing the pathogenic ADAMTS13 autoantibody and replacing ADAMTS13 with donor plasma. Because ADAMTS13 activity assays take time and clinical scores (eg, PLASMIC) are imperfect, the accepted clinical approach is to start PEX empirically whenever TTP cannot be confidently excluded. That low threshold is a time‑sensitive, life‑saving decision—the risk of a short course of PEX is justified by the potential to prevent fatal microvascular thrombosis.

Once the working diagnosis shifts to complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS), the role of PEX changes. PEX can transiently remove circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators and any pathogenic autoantibodies and can supply regulatory complement proteins from donor plasma, so it may produce short‑lived clinical improvement. However, it does not correct the underlying dysregulation of the alternative pathway in CM‑HUS and is not disease‑specific therapy. The evidence and current practice therefore are to use PEX as a bridge while diagnostic data are pending or when there is diagnostic uncertainty, but to transition to targeted complement blockade (eculizumab) as soon as CM‑HUS is established or strongly suspected. Practical consequences of this principle are important: PEX can confound complement and ADAMTS13 measurements (and can remove administered eculizumab), so timing of diagnostic sampling and of therapeutic exchanges matters. Finally, in the setting of active infection there is an added dilemma—eculizumab increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms—so clinicians may use PEX to temporize until infection is controlled, but definitive therapy for CM‑HUS remains complement inhibition.

The case illustrates these points: PEX was started early because TTP could not be excluded; it produced transient renal improvement, but sustained hematologic and renal recovery required eculizumab once CM‑HUS was recognized and the infection was controlled.

### [Management] Before starting eculizumab, which meningococcal vaccines are recommended, what is the ideal timing, and what antibiotic prophylaxis strategy should be used if complement blockade cannot be delayed?

Before you give terminal complement blockade you must both vaccinate against meningococcus and, if you cannot wait for protective immunity, provide immediate antibiotic cover. The key points are these.

Which vaccines
Use both a quadrivalent capsular conjugate meningococcal vaccine covering serogroups A, C, W and Y (eg, MenACWY — Menveo, Nimenrix) and a serogroup B vaccine (eg, Bexsero or Trumenba). Eculizumab blocks terminal complement and markedly increases risk of invasive disease from encapsulated organisms, particularly Neisseria meningitidis of any serogroup; protection therefore requires both ACWY and B vaccines.

Ideal timing
Ideally give the ACWY and B vaccines at least 2 weeks before the first dose of eculizumab to allow development of protective antibody titres. If you can delay therapy that 14‑day window, do so. If the patient has already received prior appropriate meningococcal immunization according to local adult schedules, proceed based on those records and timing.

If complement blockade cannot be delayed
If eculizumab must be started immediately (active disease, life‑threatening TMA), give the ACWY and B vaccines immediately (do not withhold vaccination) and begin antibacterial prophylaxis. Continue antibiotics until at least 2 weeks after vaccination to cover the window before vaccine‑induced immunity develops. Many centres use oral penicillin (eg, phenoxymethylpenicillin 500 mg twice daily or 250–500 mg three times daily) or amoxicillin as first line; for penicillin allergy use an alternative such as ciprofloxacin (single daily dosing) guided by local susceptibility and infectious‑disease advice. In practice, some experts continue long‑term prophylactic penicillin for the duration of eculizumab therapy because vaccination reduces but does not eliminate risk. Provide written patient education (signs/symptoms of meningococcal sepsis), ensure rapid access to urgent medical review and consider standby parenteral antibiotics for use if fever/meningitis symptoms develop.

Practical summary
Give MenACWY + MenB and wait ≥14 days if clinically safe. If you cannot wait, vaccinate immediately and start antibiotic prophylaxis (eg, oral penicillin/amoxicillin; ciprofloxacin if penicillin allergic) and continue antibiotics until 2 weeks after vaccination — with a low threshold to continue longer or indefinitely while on eculizumab, in consultation with infectious‑disease specialists. Monitor closely and educate the patient about prompt presentation for febrile illness.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which infections are patients at highest risk for during terminal complement blockade, and what monitoring and prevention strategies should be instituted?

Clinical pearl:

Terminal complement blockade (eg, eculizumab) disables formation of the membrane attack complex and markedly impairs host defence against encapsulated, complement‑sensitive organisms — most importantly Neisseria meningitidis. This is why invasive meningococcal disease is the highest‑risk infection and the one that drives mandated vaccination and prophylaxis. More broadly, patients are at increased risk for other encapsulated or complement‑sensitive pathogens (eg, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and less commonly organisms such as Capnocytophaga species that rely on complement killing), so vigilance must extend beyond meningococcus.

Why this matters (clinical reasoning): the terminal complement pathway lyses bacteria directly and enhances opsonophagocytosis; blocking C5 prevents MAC formation and reduces clearance of organisms whose capsule or other virulence factors make them primarily susceptible to complement‑mediated killing. In the case you presented, C. canimorsus is an encapsulated gram‑negative organism whose control can involve complement; thus initiating eculizumab during or soon after bacteremia requires balancing renal/hematologic benefit against infectious risk.

Practical prevention and monitoring strategy (what to do):

- Vaccination: give quadrivalent meningococcal conjugate vaccine (A, C, W, Y) plus serogroup B vaccine ideally ≥2 weeks before first eculizumab dose. Also ensure age‑appropriate pneumococcal (PCV and PPSV) and Haemophilus influenzae type b immunizations are up to date. Document dates and serotypes.

- Antibiotic prophylaxis: if eculizumab must be started urgently (vaccination interval not possible) provide immediate antibiotic prophylaxis (eg, oral penicillin V or amoxicillin where effective) and continue for at least 2 weeks after vaccination, or longer based on local guidance. Some centers consider lifelong prophylaxis or long‑term suppressive penicillin in high‑risk patients or after recurrent infections, but practice varies.

- Patient education and access: give the patient (and family) a written alert card stating they are on complement blockade, the heightened meningococcal risk, and the need for urgent medical review for any fever, new severe headache, neck stiffness, rash, or sepsis signs. Ensure they have rapid access to medical care and a low threshold for empirical parenteral antibiotics if febrile.

- Clinical monitoring: instruct patients to report fever or systemic symptoms immediately; treat any febrile illness as potentially life‑threatening. In healthcare settings, use a low threshold to obtain blood cultures and start empiric broad‑spectrum antibiotics covering Neisseria until directed therapy can be given.

- Laboratory or specialist follow‑up: no routine screening labs prevent infection; instead schedule vaccine boosters per public health guidance and reassess need for ongoing prophylaxis. Consider infectious disease consultation when initiating long‑term complement blockade.

- Special considerations in this case: because the patient had recent C. canimorsus bacteremia, delay initiation of eculizumab until blood cultures are negative when clinically feasible, give vaccines, and use short‑term antibiotic prophylaxis. If eculizumab is begun during or shortly after bacteremia (as in severe CM‑HUS), ensure close clinical surveillance and continued targeted antibiotics until infection is confidently controlled.

Bottom line: eculizumab confers the greatest incremental infectious risk for invasive meningococcal disease but also increases susceptibility to other encapsulated/complement‑sensitive organisms (including Capnocytophaga in specific circumstances). Mitigate risk with appropriate vaccination before therapy when possible, targeted antibiotic prophylaxis if urgent therapy is required, clear patient education and a low threshold for prompt empirical treatment of febrile illnesses.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with new hemolysis and thrombocytopenia after sepsis, how would you interpret a positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test, and what clues suggest TMA rather than autoimmune hemolytic anemia?

A positive direct antiglobulin (DAT, Coombs) in a patient who develops hemolysis after severe sepsis must be interpreted in clinical context — it is neither pathognomonic for autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) nor sufficient to exclude thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). In sepsis, drugs (eg, penicillins), recent transfusions, non‑specific immunoglobulin adsorption to red cells, and circulating immune complexes can give a false‑positive DAT. Even when true IgG or C3 binding is present, that binding can be secondary to endothelial and complement activation rather than primary autoimmunity.

Clues that point to TMA rather than AIHA in this case are the timing, the morphology, the associated organ dysfunction and the lack of expected response to steroids. Specifically, the patient developed a sudden drop in hemoglobin with undetectable haptoglobin and high LDH concurrent with persistent thrombocytopenia and dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury; the blood film showed 15–20% schistocytes (fragmented red cells) rather than spherocytes; coagulation parameters had normalized after the DIC phase yet hemolysis and thrombocytopenia persisted; ADAMTS13 activity was not severely reduced (43.5%, arguing against TTP), stool STEC PCR was negative, kidney biopsy showed microvascular fibrin thrombi consistent with TMA, and the hemolysis did not respond to high‑dose corticosteroids but improved after plasma exchange and complement blockade. In AIHA you would more typically see a strongly positive DAT, spherocytes, reticulocytosis, isolated hemolysis without profound thrombocytopenia or severe AKI, and at least some improvement with steroids.

Practical approach: do not accept a single positive DAT as diagnostic in sepsis. Repeat the DAT (including IgG vs C3d), review recent drugs and transfusions, examine a meticulous peripheral smear for schistocytes, measure LDH/haptoglobin/reticulocyte count and coagulation studies, check ADAMTS13 and STEC testing, and consider tissue (eg, kidney) biopsy when safe. Treat empirically for TTP/TMA while awaiting results when indicated, and reserve steroid‑directed therapy for cases with convincing evidence of AIHA or a steroid‑responsive pattern. Teaching pearl: the DAT is an adjunct — morphology, platelet count, renal involvement, and treatment response are the decisive features that distinguish TMA from AIHA in the septic patient.

### [Pathophysiology] What kidney biopsy findings support the diagnosis of TMA, and how do immunofluorescence and electron microscopy help exclude immune complex–mediated or primary glomerulopathies?

In thrombotic microangiopathy the diagnostic weight rests first on light‑microscopy changes that reflect acute endothelial injury with intravascular thrombosis. In this case the kidney biopsy showed severe acute tubular necrosis and ischaemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi and scattered crescents — a pattern characteristic of TMA. Typical light‑microscopy features that support TMA include glomerular capillary wall thickening with endothelial cell swelling, mesangiolysis, occlusive fibrin‑ or platelet‑rich thrombi in glomerular tufts and arterioles, segmental or global ischaemic collapse of glomeruli, and secondary cortical necrosis or acute tubular injury when injury is severe. New or “double” contours of the glomerular basement membrane may appear later as a consequence of subendothelial expansion.

Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy are complementary because they distinguish pure microvascular/endothelial injury from immune complex– or structural glomerular diseases that require different therapy. A negative or pauci‑immune immunofluorescence (no granular IgG, IgA, IgM or complement deposition) argues against immune‑complex GN (for example IgA nephropathy, post‑infectious GN, or membranous nephropathy), which typically gives granular capillary or mesangial staining. Likewise, electron microscopy in TMA usually lacks electron‑dense immune complexes; instead EM may show endothelial cell swelling, subendothelial widening with loosely arranged (“fluffy”) electron‑lucent material and occasional fibrin tactoids. The absence of organized electron‑dense subendothelial or subepithelial deposits (humps, granular deposits) or of characteristic structural changes (e.g., podocyte effacement of nephrotic‑range membranous disease, dense deposits of C3 glomerulopathy) effectively excludes primary immune‑complex or podocytopathic glomerulopathies as the cause of the lesion.

Putting it together: the combination of light‑microscopy evidence of occlusive fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomeruli with negative immunofluorescence and lack of electron‑dense deposits on EM supports a diagnosis of TMA (here CM‑HUS) rather than an immune complex–mediated or primary glomerular disease, and therefore directs therapy toward addressing microangiopathic/endothelial injury (plasmapheresis/anti‑complement therapy) rather than immunosuppression for immune complex GN.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which host factors are classically associated with severe Capnocytophaga canimorsus infections, and why can severe disease still occur in immunocompetent adults?

Classically, severe Capnocytophaga canimorsus infections occur in patients with impaired splenic function (asplenia or hyposplenism), chronic alcohol use, cirrhosis, diabetes, neutropenia or other forms of immunosuppression (including corticosteroid therapy, malignancy or post‑transplant immunosuppression). These states converge on a few common defects: impaired opsonization and clearance of encapsulated organisms, reduced splenic filtration of bacteria and diminished neutrophil- or complement‑mediated killing. Complement deficiencies or qualitative complement dysfunction also increase risk because C. canimorsus is an encapsulated gram‑negative rod whose clearance relies heavily on opsonophagocytosis and terminal complement activity.

Why then can fulminant disease occur in apparently immunocompetent adults? First, virulence features of the organism — a polysaccharide capsule, variable expression of serum‑resistance factors and enzymes that blunt innate immune recognition — allow some strains to evade normal host defenses. A large inoculum (deep bite) or a delay in effective antibiotic therapy can overwhelm intact defenses. Second, transient perturbations of host defence during severe sepsis (for example, temporary neutropenia, consumption of complement, or haemodynamic gut/translocation effects) can permit unchecked bacteremia. Third, and important for this case, C. canimorsus infection itself can dysregulate complement: in genetically susceptible hosts even without an identifiable pathogenic variant, an infectious “second hit” may trigger uncontrolled alternative‑pathway activation and endothelial injury, producing complement‑mediated TMA. Thus, absence of classic risk factors does not exclude life‑threatening Capnocytophaga infection — clinicians must consider the organism after dog or cat exposure, treat promptly, and be alert for post‑infectious complications such as CM‑HUS.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain how Capnocytophaga canimorsus interacts with the complement system, including the role of capsule expression, and why this creates a therapeutic paradox when considering eculizumab.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus sits at an intersection between host defence and host injury because of how it interacts with the complement system. Innate clearance of gram‑negative bacteria depends on three complementary functions of complement: opsonization (C3b) for phagocytosis, generation of anaphylatoxins (C3a/C5a) to recruit and activate inflammatory cells, and assembly of the terminal membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9) to lyse bacteria or facilitate killing. Most C. canimorsus isolates are serum‑sensitive and are controlled by these mechanisms; however, isolates that express a polysaccharide capsule gain relative serum resistance by reducing deposition of C3b and limiting MAC formation, effectively evading opsonophagocytosis and killing.

Clinically this has two linked implications. First, when complement functions well it helps clear C. canimorsus; second, in some patients an infection that engages the alternative complement pathway can produce excessive complement activation on the vascular endothelium, especially in those with impaired regulation, leading to endothelial injury, microvascular thrombosis and the clinical syndrome of complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). Thus complement activation is both necessary to control the organism and, when dysregulated or excessive, the proximate cause of TMA.

That dual role creates the therapeutic paradox with eculizumab. Eculizumab binds C5 and prevents cleavage to C5a and C5b, blocking generation of the potent pro‑inflammatory fragment C5a and preventing MAC assembly. Blocking C5 rapidly halts the downstream effectors that mediate endothelial damage in CM‑HUS and can produce prompt hematologic and renal improvement. But by inhibiting terminal complement it also removes a major defence against encapsulated and certain gram‑negative organisms (notably Neisseria spp. and organisms like C. canimorsus that rely on capsule‑mediated serum resistance). In short, the drug that treats the TMA undermines one of the host’s best tools for eradicating the triggering infection.

Putting that into practice explains the management choices in the case: start eculizumab only after active bacteraemia is controlled and antibiotics are effective, give appropriate vaccinations against encapsulated organisms when feasible, and provide antibiotic prophylaxis until vaccine responses are present. It also explains why routine complement levels can be unhelpful (C3/C4 may be normal while pathogenic activation of the alternative pathway and terminal components causes TMA) and why plasma exchange may transiently help but not definitively treat CM‑HUS. The decision to use complement blockade therefore rests on balancing the immediate risk of ongoing endothelial injury and irreversible renal damage against the increased risk of overwhelming infection from an organism the complement system helps clear.

### [Pathophysiology] Why might overt hemolysis and thrombocytopenia from CM-HUS emerge only after the initial septic and DIC phase has begun to resolve?

Think of the clinical course as two overlapping processes with different timings: an early, global consumptive coagulopathy driven by overwhelming infection (sepsis–DIC), and a later, focal microvascular endothelial injury driven by dysregulated complement (CM‑HUS). The features of the first process commonly conceal the emergence of the second for several mechanistic and practical reasons.

First, during fulminant sepsis and DIC there is widespread consumption of platelets and coagulation factors, profound inflammation, marrow suppression and often multiorgan hypoperfusion. That global consumptive picture produces thrombocytopenia, elevated D‑dimer, prolonged coagulation times and AKI that mimic or overshadow the laboratory and clinical hallmarks of a thrombotic microangiopathy. In this phase schistocytes may be few, haptoglobin may be normal (because of concurrent liver dysfunction and transfusions), and the bone marrow response can be blunted — all of which make MAHA harder to recognise.

Second, the biology of complement‑mediated TMA often requires a “second hit.” Infection initially activates complement to help clear bacteria; as infection is treated and bacteremia falls, a dysregulated alternative‑pathway amplification loop (especially in a genetically or functionally susceptible host) can become dominant at the endothelial interface. That amplification produces localized microthrombi, red‑cell shearing and platelet consumption within arterioles and glomerular capillaries — the laboratory picture of MAHA and persistent thrombocytopenia that becomes clinically apparent only after the systemic inflammatory storm begins to subside.

Third, iatrogenic and diagnostic factors during the septic phase can mask or delay detection. Transfusions, plasma products, antibiotics (some causing DAT positivity), and early plasma exchange can transiently correct or alter haemolytic markers; clinicians may attribute cytopenias to sepsis, DIC or drug effects and not perform repeat peripheral smears or hemolysis panels until the patient stabilizes. In the reported case, improving coagulation parameters and resolving sepsis unmasked a striking fall in hemoglobin, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin and schistocytosis — features that only became evident once the DIC picture receded.

Finally, complement assays and ADAMTS13 activity are imperfect early markers: C3/C4 may remain normal, and ADAMTS13 is not severely deficient in CM‑HUS. Thus definitive recognition often awaits focused testing and kidney biopsy once the acute septic physiology has settled.

In short, sepsis/DIC creates a noisy, consumptive milieu that both physiologically and diagnostically masks evolving, focal complement‑driven endothelial injury. As the systemic consumptive process resolves, the persistent or newly unleashed endothelial complement activation manifests as overt MAHA and refractory thrombocytopenia — the clinical transition seen in this patient that prompted the diagnosis of CM‑HUS and initiation of targeted therapy.

### [Management] In adults with CM-HUS and no identified pathogenic complement variant, what factors guide the duration of eculizumab therapy and how should clinicians monitor for relapse upon discontinuation?

In adults with complement‑mediated HUS who do not have a demonstrable pathogenic complement gene variant, the decision about how long to continue eculizumab is individualized and balances the risk of recurrent TMA against the real harms of indefinite complement blockade (infectious risk, vaccination/prophylaxis burdens, cost and quality‑of‑life). The key clinical factors that should guide duration are the severity of the index episode, the degree of renal recovery, the presence of extrarenal organ injury, and the presumed role of a reversible trigger.

If the presenting episode was severe (dialysis‑dependent AKI, need for intensive care, histologic TMA, or persistent organ dysfunction) and recovery is incomplete (persistent CKD, ongoing proteinuria or reduced GFR), many experts favour a longer course of maintenance eculizumab (months to indefinite) because relapse could produce further irreversible kidney injury. By contrast, when CM‑HUS clearly follows a single identified and controlled trigger (severe infection, pregnancy, certain drugs), the patient achieves full haematologic remission and substantial renal recovery (return to baseline or near‑baseline creatinine, minimal proteinuria) and there is no family history or identified pathogenic variant, discontinuation after a defined period (commonly 3–6 months of maintenance therapy after induction) is reasonable.

When planning discontinuation, discuss risks and contingency plans with the patient, involve nephrology, hematology and the complement laboratory, and ensure meningococcal and other relevant vaccinations and antibiotic prophylaxis are in place while eculizumab is being stopped or restarted.

Monitoring strategy after stopping eculizumab must be proactive and rapid, because relapses can evolve quickly. Practical monitoring includes close clinical and laboratory surveillance with predefined thresholds for immediate retreatment. In the first 4–8 weeks after cessation, check CBC, serum creatinine, LDH, haptoglobin, total bilirubin, and urinalysis (including urine output and protein) twice weekly to weekly depending on baseline risk; thereafter spacing to every 1–2 weeks for months 2–3, then monthly to every 3 months out to at least 12 months if stable. Always review peripheral smear for schistocytes if haemolysis is suspected. Clinically, instruct the patient to report any new fatigue, jaundice, dark urine, reduced urine output or edema immediately.

Define clear lab/action thresholds for relapse and rapid re‑initiation of therapy: a fall in haemoglobin with rising LDH and falling haptoglobin or new schistocytosis, a ≥20–30% rise in serum creatinine or new/worsening oliguria, or a decline in platelet count without alternate explanation should prompt urgent re‑testing, urgent hematology/nephrology review and re‑dosing of eculizumab (or temporary plasma exchange while arranging eculizumab) without delay. Ensure logistical readiness (rapid access to drug and inpatient beds) before stopping.

Finally, document the rationale for stopping, maintain low threshold for re‑initiation, and reassess the plan if the patient becomes pregnant, develops a new infection, or is exposed to another potential trigger. Shared decision‑making and close multidisciplinary follow‑up are essential because the absence of a detectable variant lowers estimated relapse risk but does not eliminate it.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the preferred microbiologic testing to confirm or exclude STEC-HUS, and how does timing relative to symptom onset affect diagnostic yield?

Preferred testing to confirm or exclude STEC‑HUS is microbiologic detection of Shiga‑toxin–producing E. coli from the patient’s enteric specimen — ideally both stool culture for E. coli with subsequent serotyping (eg, O157) and a direct molecular assay (PCR) for stx1 and stx2 genes performed on stool or a rectal swab. Many modern laboratories perform direct PCR for stx genes (or multiplex gastrointestinal PCR panels) because PCR is more sensitive and faster than culture for detecting toxin‑producing strains.

Timing is critical. Stool/PCR testing has the highest yield very early in illness — typically within the first week of diarrhoeal symptoms (often the prodrome in STEC‑HUS). Sensitivity falls quickly after symptom onset and is substantially reduced by the time HUS develops (often 7–14 days after diarrhoea) or after antibiotics have been given. If stool/PCR is negative but clinical suspicion remains (typical prodrome of bloody diarrhoea followed by MAHA/thrombocytopenia/AKI), serologic testing for antibodies to the infecting LPS (eg, anti‑O157 IgM/IgG) or anti–Shiga toxin antibodies can help establish recent STEC infection when samples are obtained later; these rise after the acute period and remain detectable for weeks. Practical approach: obtain stool or rectal swab for culture and stx PCR as soon as possible (before antibiotics if feasible); if presentation is delayed and stool tests are negative, send convalescent serology or consult public‑health reference labs for toxin or strain typing.

### [Pathophysiology] How do plasma exchange and eculizumab therapy affect complement functional assays (e.g., AH50), and how should these results be interpreted in treated patients?

Key teaching point: functional complement assays (for example AH50, which measures the hemolytic activity of the alternative pathway) are tests of pathway function, not static protein levels, so they are very sensitive to recent interventions that remove complement proteins or block specific steps. Plasma exchange (PEX) and eculizumab each depress functional assays by different mechanisms — therefore a low AH50 in a patient who has just had PEX or received eculizumab usually reflects treatment effect, not proof of an underlying complement deficiency or activation state.

Why this happens, in clinical terms. Plasma exchange replaces the patient’s plasma with donor plasma (or solvent‑detergent plasma such as Octaplas). PEX therefore removes circulating complement proteins, activation fragments and autoantibodies and dilutes any ongoing complement activity. Immediately after PEX you will commonly see reduced functional activity (low AH50/CH50) because you have physically depleted the patient’s complement proteins; depending on the replacement product, some complement function may be restored transiently but the net effect is suppression or fluctuation of functional assays. Eculizumab works by binding C5 and preventing its cleavage to C5a/C5b, thereby blocking formation of the membrane attack complex. Because AH50/CH50 assays require an intact terminal pathway to produce hemolysis, therapeutic C5 blockade renders these assays profoundly low or zero while the drug is active. In other words, eculizumab produces a pharmacologic “false positive” for terminal pathway deficiency on functional testing.

How to interpret results in treated patients and practical guidance. Always record precise timing of blood sampling relative to PEX and to the first and last dose of eculizumab. The highest diagnostic yield is obtained by drawing baseline complement samples (functional assays and factor levels, and any autoantibody testing) before PEX and before starting eculizumab. If that is not possible, a suppressed AH50 obtained after PEX or after eculizumab should be attributed to treatment effect unless there is corroborating prior evidence of low activity. In patients who have already received PEX but not eculizumab, repeat testing after an interval (allowing complement proteins to equilibrate/recover, often days to a week depending on exchanges and replacement) can be informative. In patients who have received eculizumab, functional hemolytic assays will remain suppressed while the drug is present; to assess intrinsic pathway function you must wait for drug clearance (weeks after discontinuation) or measure other complementary data that are not affected by C5 blockade — for example genetic testing for complement regulatory gene variants (which is not affected by PEX or eculizumab) and measurement of C5‑binding/evidence of blockade (drug level or downstream markers such as undetectable free C5 activity or suppressed sC5b‑9 formation depending on assay availability).

Apply this to the case. The patient’s markedly reduced AH50 obtained 8 days after the last PEX and 4 days after the first eculizumab dose is most consistent with treatment effect: prior PEX had depleted/diluted complement components, and eculizumab directly blocks terminal pathway hemolytic activity, so a low AH50 does not establish an intrinsic alternative‑pathway deficiency or ongoing complement consumption. Because genetic testing is unaffected by these therapies, it remains the appropriate test to look for a predisposing complement abnormality; functional assays to clarify baseline complement activity should be done before PEX/eculizumab where possible, or deferred until the effects of therapy have resolved and samples can be interpreted properly.

Bottom line: interpret low functional complement assays in the context of recent PEX or eculizumab as likely treatment effects. Obtain baseline functional testing before these interventions when feasible; if not, rely on genetic testing and repeat functional assays after an appropriate washout interval to distinguish true underlying complement defects from pharmacologic suppression.

### [Management] In an adult with anuric AKI and suspected TMA, what are the indications and safety considerations for kidney biopsy, including a minimum platelet threshold or transfusion strategy?

Teaching pearl:

A kidney biopsy in a patient with anuric AKI and suspected thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is an important diagnostic and sometimes prognostic test, but it must be performed only when the information obtained will change management and when bleeding risk can be made acceptable. In practice the decision rests on three questions: (1) will histology meaningfully alter therapy or prognosis beyond available clinical, laboratory and noninvasive data (for example ADAMTS13, stool Shiga-toxin PCR, peripheral smear, response to plasma exchange and course on dialysis)? (2) can the procedure be performed safely given the patient’s platelet count, coagulation status and hemodynamic stability? and (3) are alternatives (transjugular or open biopsy) preferable?

Why biopsy can be helpful: renal histology documents the presence, chronicity and severity of TMA (endothelial swelling, fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli, crescents) and excludes other diagnoses (immune complex GN, ATN-only, vasculitis) that might change therapy (immunosuppression, continuing PEX versus switching to complement blockade). In the case you presented the biopsy confirmed TMA and supported escalation to eculizumab — an example where biopsy added actionable information.

Safety considerations and practical thresholds: the dominant risk is bleeding. For percutaneous ultrasound‑guided native kidney biopsy the usual institutional safety targets are platelets ≥50 × 10^9/L and INR ≤1.5 (some centers require INR ≤1.3). If platelets are below 50 × 10^9/L, give platelet transfusion immediately before the procedure to raise the count to ≥50 × 10^9/L; there is no evidence that routine transfusion to very high targets (eg, >100 × 10^9/L) improves safety and it may carry harms. If there is ongoing consumptive coagulopathy (eg, DIC) correct fibrinogen (aim >1.5–2.0 g/L) and other clotting abnormalities. Uremic platelet dysfunction can be mitigated with dialysis and/or desmopressin (DDAVP) prior to biopsy.

When thrombocytopenia is refractory or other risks are high (hemodynamic instability, uncontrolled DIC, large hematoma risk), prefer a transjugular renal biopsy — it has a lower bleeding complication rate and is often preferred in patients on anticoagulation, with very low platelets, or with single‑kidney anatomy. Open surgical biopsy is a last resort.

Timing and transfusion strategy in the setting of plasma exchange or recent transfusions: transfuse platelets as close to the procedure as possible (within 30–60 minutes) so they are not removed by subsequent plasma exchange. If planned PEX would follow the biopsy, coordinate timing (perform biopsy after the most recent exchange and delay the next exchange if safe) and inform the team that transfused platelets may be cleared by PEX. Likewise, if eculizumab or complement testing is being performed, be mindful that PEX alters complement assays; plan sampling before PEX when possible.

Contextual nuance for anuric/dialysis patients: dialysis dependence reduces consequences of parenchymal bleeding causing loss of renal function, which may make teams slightly more permissive, but bleeding can still be life‑threatening (retroperitoneal or large perinephric hematoma). Therefore do not lower safety thresholds solely because the patient is on dialysis.

In short: perform biopsy only when it will influence management; ensure platelets ≥50 × 10^9/L and INR ≤1.5 (transfuse platelets immediately before the procedure if needed); correct fibrinogen and consider DDAVP for uremic dysfunction; use transjugular approach when bleeding risk is high; coordinate timing with PEX to avoid removing transfused platelets or altering complement studies.

### [Management] What clinical scenarios justify initiating eculizumab for suspected CM-HUS before the full diagnostic workup is complete, and what are the risks of delaying therapy?

Teaching answer:

The decision to start eculizumab empirically rests on a time‑sensitive balance: interrupt uncontrolled complement‑mediated endothelial injury early enough to prevent irreversible renal and systemic injury, while avoiding catastrophic suppression of host defence during uncontrolled invasive infection. In practice, empiric complement blockade before the full diagnostic work‑up is justified only in a narrow set of clinical scenarios.

You should strongly consider initiating eculizumab before complete confirmatory testing when a patient has clear evidence of a progressive TMA with the following features: frank microangiopathic hemolytic anemia with schistocytes and rising LDH/haptoglobin loss, persistent severe thrombocytopenia, rapidly worsening or dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury, and clinicopathologic findings strongly supportive of TMA (for example a renal biopsy showing fibrin thrombi/ischaemic glomeruli) — and when other immediately treatable causes have been reasonably excluded or are unlikely. Practically, that means thrombocytopenia/MAHA with an ADAMTS13 activity that is not expected to be severely deficient (or a low pretest probability for TTP by score), negative testing for Shiga‑toxin STEC‑HUS when applicable, and either negative blood cultures or clear clinical control of the inciting infection after appropriate antibiotics. If TTP is a real possibility, urgent plasma exchange must precede or accompany this decision because PEX is lifesaving for TTP and eculizumab does not treat TTP.

In septic patients the additional requirement is clinical control of the infection or convincing microbiologic evidence that bacteremia has been eradicated or is being treated effectively. This is because terminal complement blockade markedly increases risk from encapsulated organisms and can worsen uncontrolled sepsis. In cases like the presented patient — where TMA emerged after initial control of C. canimorsus sepsis, blood cultures turned negative, biopsy confirmed TMA, ADAMTS13 was not severely reduced, and haemolysis/renal failure were progressive — the benefits of prompt eculizumab outweighed the infectious risks and justified starting therapy (after meningococcal vaccination and with antimicrobial prophylaxis).

If you cannot meet those conditions, use plasma exchange as a bridge while expediting ADAMTS13, stool Shiga toxin tests, complement studies and culture results; PEX may transiently stabilise patients and buy time to clarify diagnosis.

Risks of delaying eculizumab are concrete and time‑dependent: ongoing complement‑mediated endothelial injury causes progressive microvascular thrombosis leading to irreversible glomerular and tubular damage, higher likelihood of incomplete renal recovery and progression to end‑stage kidney disease, persistent transfusion dependence, and increased morbidity and mortality. Several series and case reports (including this one) show better renal and hematologic outcomes with earlier complement blockade.

The primary risk of starting too early is infectious harm: life‑threatening meningococcal and other encapsulated bacterial infections (including concern with Capnocytophaga), especially if bacteremia is active and not adequately treated. Thus, when you start eculizumab empirically, vaccinate promptly (and recognize vaccines take time to generate protection), give appropriate antimicrobial prophylaxis, and monitor closely.

In short: initiate eculizumab empirically when the clinical picture clearly indicates progressive CM‑HUS (MAHA + thrombocytopenia + severe/worsening AKI or dialysis dependence with TMA on biopsy) and alternative diagnoses that would require different immediate therapy (notably TTP and STEC‑HUS, or uncontrolled bacteremia) have been reasonably excluded or controlled. Delay risks irreversible renal injury and worse outcome; starting too soon risks life‑threatening infection, so mitigation with antibiotics, vaccination and close monitoring is essential.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Beyond CM-HUS, what secondary causes of TMA should be considered in adults (e.g., malignant hypertension, pregnancy-associated TMA, drug-induced TMA, autoimmune disease, antiphospholipid syndrome) and what targeted tests help distinguish them?

When you see MAHA, thrombocytopenia and acute kidney injury in an adult you must keep a broad, structured differential because many disorders produce histologic TMA but have different treatments and urgencies. Start by asking about the clinical context and then order a focused set of tests to rule in or out the common secondary causes.

Malignant hypertension: severe, sudden elevation of blood pressure produces endothelial shear injury with retinal haemorrhages/exudates and rapidly progressive renal failure. Look for markedly elevated BP, funduscopic signs, very high serum creatinine and evidence of left‑ventricular strain. Urgent BP measurement and control are diagnostic and therapeutic priorities; renal biopsy may show onion‑skin arteriolar changes and fibrinoid necrosis rather than classic glomerular platelet‑rich thrombi. Routine labs are non‑specific.

Pregnancy‑associated TMA (preeclampsia/HELLP, postpartum HUS): consider in pregnant/postpartum women. Distinguish from TTP by ADAMTS13 activity (TTP very low) and from CM‑HUS by timing (third trimester/postpartum), hypertension, proteinuria, and transaminase/platelet pattern in HELLP. Check pregnancy test/hCG, urine protein, liver enzymes, platelet trend and ADAMTS13; consider obstetric consultation and fetal assessment.

Drug‑induced TMA: several agents (eg, quinine, some antiplatelet agents, calcineurin inhibitors, certain chemotherapies, anti‑VEGF agents) cause either immune‑mediated (drug‑dependent antibodies) or dose‑dependent endothelial injury. A careful drug/exposure history is key. Targeted tests include drug‑dependent antibody assays when available and temporally stopping the suspected agent; renal biopsy can help but is not diagnostic alone.

Autoimmune disease and antiphospholipid syndrome (APS): SLE and catastrophic APS commonly present with TMA. Look for systemic autoimmune features, serologies (ANA, anti‑dsDNA, complement consumption pattern), and for APS order lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin and anti‑β2‑glycoprotein I (repeat positives 12 weeks apart when feasible). APS often has thrombotic history and prolonged clotting assays (paradoxically with thrombotic tendency); biopsy may show microvascular thrombosis without immune complex deposition.

Sepsis/DIC‑associated TMA and bacterial toxins: severe infection and DIC can produce microthrombi and schistocytes. Distinguish by systemic inflammatory markers, positive blood cultures, and consumptive coagulopathy (prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, high D‑dimer). Management targets the infection and supportive coagulation therapy; if microangiopathic haemolysis emerges late or persists after infection control, consider a superimposed primary TMA (eg, CM‑HUS).

STEC‑HUS: classically after diarrhoeal illness; test stool for Shiga toxin by PCR or culture. Absent enteric prodrome and a negative stool PCR argue against it.

TTP: life‑threatening and must be excluded early. ADAMTS13 activity <10% (and often inhibitor) establishes TTP; use that test urgently (send plasma before PEX) and use PLASMIC score to triage while awaiting results. Clinically TTP has prominent neurologic features and less severe renal failure than HUS.

Malignancy‑associated and transplant‑associated TMAs: look for active malignancy, bone‑marrow involvement, or recent stem‑cell/solid‑organ transplant and exposures (conditioning regimens, calcineurin inhibitors). Workup may include imaging, bone‑marrow biopsy and reviewing transplant immunosuppression.

Complement‑mediated (CM‑HUS) and complement‑triggered secondary TMA: think of CM‑HUS when TTP and STEC are excluded and TMA persists despite control of an initiating insult. Tests useful here are ADAMTS13 (to exclude TTP), Shiga toxin PCR (to exclude STEC), standard complement levels (C3/C4) and functional pathway assays (AH50/AP50), anti‑factor H antibodies, and complement genetic testing when available. Note that normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS and that acute treatments (PEX, eculizumab) and timing of sampling can alter results.

Practical testing approach in the adult with new TMA: immediately send ADAMTS13 activity with inhibitor testing, blood cultures and sepsis workup, stool Shiga toxin PCR if diarrhoea, coagulation panel (PT/aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer) to evaluate for DIC, pregnancy test in women of childbearing potential, ANA and antiphospholipid antibodies if autoimmune features present, and targeted drug exposure history with cessation of suspect agents. If TTP and STEC are excluded and TMA is ongoing or severe, pursue complement studies (C3/C4, functional alternative pathway assays, anti‑factor H antibodies) and consider referral for complement genetic testing and early nephrology/hematology discussion about PEX versus complement blockade.

In this case the sequence—bacteraemia with initial DIC, later isolated MAHA with schistocytes, ADAMTS13 not suggestive of TTP, negative Shiga toxin, normal ANA but evolving alternative pathway assays after treatments—pointed away from TTP, STEC, pregnancy‑related causes and classic autoimmune APS and toward infection‑triggered complement dysregulation. That diagnostic reasoning is what guided biopsy, PEX as a bridge and timely eculizumab once active bacteraemia was controlled.

### [Pathophysiology] Contrast the mechanisms of thrombocytopenia in DIC versus CM-HUS/TTP, and explain how these mechanisms manifest in laboratory patterns and organ involvement.

Think of DIC and complement‑mediated TMA (and TTP) as two different ways the microcirculation and platelets are driven into pathological thrombosis, with distinct upstream drivers, downstream effects on coagulation tests, and patterns of organ injury.

In DIC the initiating event is systemic activation of the coagulation cascade (in this case sepsis), producing widespread generation of thrombin and fibrin. Platelets are consumed as they become enmeshed in fibrin clots throughout the circulation, and the fibrinolytic system is activated, generating large amounts of fibrin degradation products. The laboratory signature is therefore a consumptive coagulopathy: prolonged PT and aPTT, elevated D‑dimer/fibrin degradation products, low fibrinogen, and thrombocytopenia. Schistocytes can appear but are often less prominent than in primary TMAs. Clinically DIC causes both bleeding (because coagulation factors and fibrinogen are depleted) and tissue ischemia from fibrin microthrombi — typical patterns include diffuse oozing, ecchymoses, and ischemic skin changes or distal limb ischemia, often with multisystem hypoperfusion because the coagulation activation is global.

In CM‑HUS/TTP the proximal problem is endothelial injury with platelet‑rich microthrombi formed by platelet aggregation (driven by uncontrolled alternative‑pathway complement activation in CM‑HUS, or by very low ADAMTS13 and accumulation of ultra‑large von Willebrand multimers in TTP). Coagulation proteases are not primarily activated, so PT/aPTT and fibrinogen are usually normal or only mildly abnormal. The laboratory pattern is dominated by microangiopathic hemolytic anemia and platelet consumption: schistocytosis, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin, rising indirect bilirubin, reticulocytosis, and persistent thrombocytopenia without the marked D‑dimer/fibrinogen abnormalities of DIC. Organ involvement follows where platelet‑rich microthrombi form: kidneys are the predominant target in HUS (severe AKI, dialysis dependence), whereas the brain is classically affected in TTP (neurologic deficits, seizures). ADAMTS13 activity <10% points to TTP; normal or only mildly reduced ADAMTS13 with evidence of alternative‑pathway activation and renal‑predominant disease supports CM‑HUS.

Apply that framework to this case: the patient’s early picture — sepsis, prolonged coagulation times, low fibrinogen, high D‑dimer, bleeding and distal ischemic changes — fits DIC with fibrin‑driven platelet consumption. Later, as the coagulation parameters normalised but hemoglobin fell, haptoglobin became undetectable, LDH climbed and schistocytes (15–20%) appeared with persistent thrombocytopenia and biopsy‑proven glomerular fibrin thrombi, the pattern shifted to a platelet‑aggregation, endothelial injury TMA. ADAMTS13 ~43% argued against TTP; complement studies and the clinical response to eculizumab pointed to CM‑HUS triggered by infection. Therapeutically this distinction matters: DIC is managed by treating the trigger and supportive replacement of factors, whereas TTP is treated urgently with plasma exchange and CM‑HUS with complement blockade (eculizumab). Recognising when a sepsis‑associated DIC picture is evolving or being overlaid by a primary TMA is critical because the laboratory patterns (coagulation panel versus hemolysis markers and schistocytes) and the pattern of organ injury (diffuse bleeding/ischemia versus renal‑predominant microvascular thrombosis) guide both diagnosis and definitive therapy.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why can culture confirmation of Capnocytophaga canimorsus be delayed, and what molecular methods can expedite diagnosis in suspected cases?

Clinical pearl: Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a classic “slow and picky” organism — that is precisely why culture confirmation is often delayed, and why molecular methods are increasingly important when you suspect this pathogen after a dog/cat exposure.

Why cultures are slow or falsely negative
Capnocytophaga spp. are fastidious, capnophilic, gram‑negative rods that grow poorly on routine media and often require enriched blood or chocolate agar, a CO2‑enriched atmosphere, and prolonged incubation (often 5–7 days or longer). Prior outpatient or empiric antibiotics (common after animal bites) suppress growth. Automated blood‑culture systems may flag bottles late or not at all, and colonies may be scant, overlooked, or misidentified by conventional biochemical panels. All of these factors delay or prevent microbiologic confirmation and can give a false sense of culture negativity during the critical early management period.

How to shorten the diagnostic window — practical molecular options
If you suspect C. canimorsus, tell the lab to incubate cultures longer, plate on enriched media in CO2, and hold a high index of suspicion for tiny, slow colonies. Beyond optimized culture, the following molecular approaches markedly shorten time to diagnosis:

- MALDI‑TOF mass spectrometry from a positive blood‑culture bottle or a visible colony: rapid species identification within hours once there is growth; much faster and more accurate than biochemical methods.

- Broad‑range (pan‑bacterial) 16S rRNA PCR and sequencing on blood or on a culture-negative specimen: detects bacterial DNA even when cultures are sterile due to prior antibiotics; results can be available in 24–72 hours depending on lab logistics.

- Targeted PCR assays for Capnocytophaga (if available locally) give faster, species‑level results than broad 16S and can be run directly on blood.

- Metagenomic next‑generation sequencing (mNGS) / real‑time nanopore sequencing on blood or culture material: can identify pathogens without prior culture and return results within 24–48 hours in experienced centers; useful when the differential is broad or when prior antibiotics have sterilized cultures.

How to apply this at the bedside
When a patient presents after a dog bite with sepsis or unexplained DIC/TMA, communicate explicitly with microbiology: request prolonged incubation, CO2 conditions, and early MALDI‑TOF identification if a bottle turns positive. If cultures are negative and clinical suspicion remains high (or antibiotics were given), send 16S PCR or mNGS on blood or aspirates. Early molecular identification shortens targeted therapy, informs prognosis (recognition of encapsulated organisms and complement interactions), and can influence timing of immunomodulatory treatments such as eculizumab.

Bottom line: delayed culture is a feature of the organism, not a lab failure — anticipate it, alert the lab, and use molecular diagnostics (MALDI‑TOF, 16S PCR, or mNGS/nanopore) to speed definitive diagnosis when clinically indicated.

### [Pathophysiology] What mechanisms contribute to hypoglycemia in severe sepsis, and how should persistent hypoglycemia be managed in the acute setting?

Teaching pearl:

Hypoglycaemia in severe sepsis is multifactorial — think of supply failure, increased demand, impaired counter‑regulation and drug/organ clearance effects — and management must treat the immediate threat while searching for reversible contributors.

Why hypoglycaemia occurs in sepsis (pathophysiology and clinical reasoning)
Sepsis creates a metabolic tug‑of‑war. Early on there is insulin resistance and hyperglycaemia, but with progressive shock, several processes drive hypoglycaemia. Hepatic failure or shock‑related hepatic hypoperfusion impairs glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis so the primary endogenous glucose supply is exhausted. Renal failure reduces renal gluconeogenesis and decreases insulin clearance, prolonging circulating insulin effect. Systemic inflammation (TNF, IL‑1, IL‑6) increases glucose uptake by activated immune cells and peripheral tissues and can directly blunt hepatic glucose output. Rapidly proliferating bacteria can consume glucose locally/systemically. Adrenal insufficiency or relative corticosteroid deficiency in critical illness removes a key counter‑regulatory mechanism. Finally, decreased oral intake and depleted glycogen stores (especially after several days of illness) and certain drugs (exogenous insulin, sulfonylureas, occasionally antibiotics or quinolones reported) worsen the problem. In short, in a patient with sepsis + liver or renal dysfunction the balance readily tips to hypoglycaemia.

Acute management of persistent hypoglycaemia in sepsis (practical clinical approach)
Immediate goals are to restore glucose, prevent recurrence, and correct reversible causes. If the patient is symptomatic or glucose <3.0 mmol/L, give IV dextrose: adults typically 25–50 mL of D50W (12.5–25 g) as a bolus, reassess in 10–15 minutes, then start a continuous infusion (for example D10W at a rate to maintain euglycaemia) or frequent boluses until stable. If IV access is unavailable, give intramuscular/subcutaneous glucagon (may be ineffective if glycogen stores are depleted or in chronic liver disease). Monitor capillary glucose frequently and taper infusion as endogenous control returns.

While stabilising, search for and treat contributors: measure serum cortisol (and treat empirically with stress‑dose hydrocortisone if concern for adrenal insufficiency), check liver enzymes and lactate, review medications (insulin, oral hypoglycaemics), obtain sulfonylurea/drug screen if ingestion suspected, and check insulin/C‑peptide if endogenous hyperinsulinaemia is a concern. In renal failure anticipate prolonged insulin effect and adjust insulin/opiate/other drug dosing; dialysis will also alter glucose levels and insulin clearance and may require tailoring of dextrose infusion during/after CRRT.

In refractory or recurrent hypoglycaemia consider: stress‑dose steroids for possible adrenal insufficiency, octreotide if sulfonylurea poisoning is identified or suspected, early initiation of enteral or parenteral nutrition once haemodynamically feasible, and urgent treatment of the underlying infection/shock to restore hepatic perfusion and gluconeogenesis. Always reassess for occult bleeding, severe malnutrition, or endocrine causes.

Application to this case
Her profound, refractory hypoglycaemia on presentation is best explained by sepsis with lactic acidaemia plus hepatic dysfunction and later renal failure (anuria) reducing gluconeogenesis and insulin clearance, compounded by prolonged poor oral intake and high metabolic demand from infection. Glucagon given initially can fail when glycogen is depleted or liver function is impaired. Management priorities are IV dextrose to restore levels, a continuous glucose infusion to prevent recurrence, early consideration of stress‑dose steroids if adrenal insufficiency is possible, and aggressive treatment of the sepsis and organ dysfunction that underlie the hypoglycaemia.

### [Management] After blood culture identification of Capnocytophaga canimorsus and clinical stabilization, what is a reasonable step-down antibiotic regimen and duration, and what beta-lactamase considerations influence this choice?

After you have documented clearance of blood cultures and the patient is clinically improving, it is reasonable to “step down” from broad-spectrum IV therapy to a narrower, oral beta‑lactam agent — provided the isolate’s susceptibility testing supports it and there is no deep‑seated focus (endocarditis, abscess) that would mandate prolonged IV therapy.

Practical regimen and duration
- For uncomplicated C. canimorsus bacteraemia that has cleared and in a clinically stable patient, switch to oral amoxicillin–clavulanate to complete a total course of about 10–14 days from the first negative blood culture and clinical improvement. Typical dosing: amoxicillin 875 mg/clavulanate 125 mg twice daily (or 500/125 mg three times daily) depending on local formulary.
- If IV step‑down is preferred (GI intolerance or poor absorption), ceftriaxone 1–2 g IV once daily is an excellent narrower option; complete a similar 10–14 day total course.
- Extend duration (typically 4–6 weeks) if there is evidence of endovascular involvement, deep tissue infection, or prosthetic material infection.

Why these choices (beta‑lactamase considerations)
- Most C. canimorsus isolates are susceptible to penicillins, third‑generation cephalosporins and beta‑lactam/beta‑lactamase inhibitor combinations. However, beta‑lactamase production has been increasingly reported. That means plain penicillin V or ampicillin monotherapy can fail if a beta‑lactamase‑producing strain is present.
- Beta‑lactamase inhibitors (clavulanate, sulbactam, tazobactam) restore activity of penicillins against such isolates, so amoxicillin–clavulanate or ampicillin–sulbactam are preferred oral/IV step‑down choices when beta‑lactamase production is a concern.
- Third‑generation cephalosporins (ceftriaxone) are generally reliable alternatives when susceptibility is confirmed; they are less affected by common TEM-type beta‑lactamases produced by Capnocytophaga.
- If the isolate is resistant or the patient is beta‑lactam allergic, consider doxycycline or a fluoroquinolone guided by susceptibilities, but beware variable activity and limited bacteremia data.

Clinical checklist before step‑down
- Confirm blood‑culture clearance and sustained clinical stability (afebrile, hemodynamically stable).
- Review organism susceptibilities; if beta‑lactamase production is reported or suspected, use a beta‑lactam/inhibitor or ceftriaxone rather than penicillin alone.
- Exclude deep foci (echo if concern for endocarditis).
- Ensure adequate oral absorption and adherence.

In the case you presented — a patient with cleared C. canimorsus bacteraemia and no identified endovascular focus — switching from IV piperacillin–tazobactam to oral amoxicillin–clavulanate to finish a total of ~14 days is a reasonable, evidence‑based step‑down, provided susceptibility data support it and there are no contraindications.
