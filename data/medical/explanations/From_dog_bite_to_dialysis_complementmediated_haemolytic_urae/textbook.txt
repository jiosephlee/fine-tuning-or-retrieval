# From Dog Bite to Dialysis: Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome Triggered by Capnocytophaga Sepsis

## Case as a Lens for Clinical Reasoning
A previously healthy woman in her late 40s presented 4 days after a dog bite with fever, mottled extremities, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, and acute kidney injury (AKI). She rapidly progressed to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), septic cardiomyopathy, and anuric renal failure requiring dialysis. Blood cultures grew Capnocytophaga canimorsus. After initial improvement with antibiotics and supportive care, she developed a new hemolytic anemia with persistent thrombocytopenia and schistocytosis, out of proportion to resolving sepsis. ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5% (excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, TTP). Kidney biopsy showed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). With plasma exchange as a bridge and subsequent terminal complement blockade with eculizumab, she achieved hematologic remission and partial renal recovery. Extended complement testing revealed no pathogenic variants; stool Shiga-toxin PCR was negative.

This case illustrates a unifying sequence: fulminant gram-negative sepsis with DIC, followed by superimposed complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS). It highlights the difficulty of recognizing CM-HUS when masked by early sepsis-related coagulopathy, the central role of ADAMTS13 and Shiga toxin testing to define the TMA subtype, and the importance—and infectious risks—of complement blockade.

## Clinical Approach to Sepsis with Cytopenias and AKI
The initial priorities in a patient with fever, hypothetically septic appearance, cytopenias, and AKI are stabilization and parallel diagnostic sorting of three overlapping emergencies:

1) Sepsis with DIC
2) TMA syndromes (TTP, Shiga-toxin HUS, CM-HUS, and secondary TMAs)
3) Mimics such as acute leukemia, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), severe hepatitis, catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome, and toxic/drug effects

Sepsis and DIC can explain fever, leukopenia, prolonged PT/aPTT, hypofibrinogenemia, elevated D-dimer, and bleeding or acral ischemic changes. However, the triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and organ ischemia (AKI, neurologic dysfunction) demands immediate consideration of TMA. Because TTP is rapidly fatal without urgent therapy, it must be the first TMA excluded.

Key bedside and early laboratory clues help partition the problem:

- DIC pattern: elevated D-dimer, prolonged PT and often aPTT, low fibrinogen, thrombocytopenia; schistocytes may be present but typically fewer; bleeding dominates early.
- TTP pattern: MAHA with many schistocytes, severe thrombocytopenia, normal PT/aPTT and fibrinogen; neurologic symptoms are prominent; renal failure may be mild to moderate; ADAMTS13 activity <10%.
- HUS pattern: MAHA, thrombocytopenia, prominent AKI; prodromal diarrhea suggests STEC-HUS; complement involvement suggests CM-HUS; PT/aPTT generally normal.
- Overlap exists: severe sepsis can cause both DIC and TMA. In this case, early DIC with acral ischemia was followed by a late wave of isolated hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia, raising suspicion for superimposed TMA.

Practical approach in the first hours:
- Send hemolysis panel (LDH, haptoglobin, indirect bilirubin, reticulocyte count), peripheral smear, coagulation panel (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer), basic metabolic panel, urinalysis, and type/screen.
- Obtain ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor testing emergently; calculate a clinical probability score (e.g., PLASMIC).
- Send stool Shiga-toxin PCR/culture if diarrhea or epidemiologic risk exists (send even without diarrhea if HUS suspected).
- Initiate broad-spectrum antibiotics for sepsis; cover Capnocytophaga in the setting of dog bite (β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor or carbapenem).
- If TTP cannot be excluded (e.g., high PLASMIC or pending ADAMTS13), start plasma exchange (PEX) and corticosteroids immediately; consider caplacizumab when suspicion is high.
- Avoid platelet transfusions in suspected TTP unless life-threatening bleeding; in DIC with bleeding, transfusion is appropriate.
- As TTP is excluded (ADAMTS13 ≥10%), pivot swiftly to the appropriate TMA pathway workup and therapy.

In this patient, PLASMIC was low and ADAMTS13 was 43.5%, making TTP unlikely. The new, isolated hemolysis with 15–20% schistocytes and biopsy-proven TMA pointed toward CM-HUS in the context of a recent severe infection.

## Pathophysiology: From Pathogen to Complement-Driven Endothelial Injury
Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a slow-growing, capnophilic, often encapsulated gram-negative rod in canine and feline oropharyngeal flora. Human infections are rare but can be fulminant, particularly in asplenia, alcohol use disorder, liver disease, or complement deficiencies. Sepsis may evolve over days, with leukopenia, DIC, purpura fulminans–like skin ischemia, and multiorgan failure. Despite broad susceptibility in vitro, the clinical course can be rapidly lethal.

Sepsis and DIC. Severe gram-negative sepsis perturbs hemostasis via pathogen-associated molecular patterns that trigger tissue factor expression on monocytes and endothelium, thrombin generation, and fibrin deposition. Natural anticoagulant pathways (protein C/S, antithrombin) are consumed, and fibrinolysis is dysregulated. The net result is simultaneous bleeding (consumption coagulopathy) and microvascular thrombosis (organ ischemia), particularly in acral circulations.

Complement-mediated HUS. CM-HUS (formerly “atypical HUS”) arises from uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway on host microvascular surfaces, particularly the renal microcirculation. The alternative pathway is continuously active at low level (“tickover”), generating C3b that is normally restrained by soluble (factor H, factor I) and membrane-bound (CD46/MCP, CR1, DAF) regulators. Dysregulation—due to genetic variants in regulators or convertase components (CFH, CFI, MCP/CD46, C3, CFB, THBD) or due to acquired anti–factor H antibodies—permits unchecked amplification. Excess C3b favors C5 convertase formation, generating C5a (a potent anaphylatoxin) and C5b-9 (membrane attack complex, MAC). On glomerular endothelium, MAC and C5a drive endothelial swelling, detachment, and prothrombotic transformation, with release of ultralarge von Willebrand factor, platelet activation, and fibrin-rich microthrombi. Red cells fragment traversing these narrowed microvessels (schistocytes), and platelets are consumed, producing the TMA triad.

Triggers and penetrance. Only about half to two-thirds of CM-HUS patients harbor identifiable complement variants or anti–factor H antibodies; penetrance is incomplete, and environmental “second hits”—infections, pregnancy/postpartum, major surgery, malignant hypertension, certain drugs—commonly precipitate disease. Severe sepsis can serve as a trigger by robustly activating complement; critically, systemic C3/C4 levels may remain normal even amid intense local activation on endothelium.

C. canimorsus as a trigger. Capnocytophaga strains vary in serum susceptibility. Capsule expression and surface sialidases can modulate complement deposition and phagocytosis. The host relies on complement for bacterial clearance, yet exuberant or dysregulated activation in susceptible hosts can tip into endothelial injury and TMA. The clinical paradox—complement needed for pathogen control but harmful when uncontrolled—frames the therapeutic dilemma of when to initiate complement blockade in the setting of infection.

Kidney vulnerability. The fenestrated glomerular endothelium and high local flow favor complement deposition and shear-mediated injury, making AKI prominent in CM-HUS. Histology shows glomerular capillary wall swelling, mesangiolysis, double-contour formation of the basement membrane, and fibrin thrombi; arteriolar involvement may be present. Crescents can occur with severe injury. Immunofluorescence typically lacks immune complex deposition; electron microscopy shows endothelial injury without organized deposits.

## Diagnostic Workup: Building and Narrowing the Differential
Once the patient stabilizes, systematic evaluation distinguishes among TMA entities and secondary causes:

Establish MAHA and rule out immune hemolysis
- Peripheral smear for schistocytes (≥1% is abnormal; in this case, 15–20%).
- LDH high, haptoglobin low/undetectable, indirect bilirubin elevated; reticulocytosis may be delayed if marrow suppressed by sepsis.
- Direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test: a positive DAT suggests immune hemolysis; however, false positives occur with recent transfusion (passive antibody) or drug-induced hemolysis (e.g., high-dose penicillin hapten effect). In TMA, the DAT is usually negative. Here, an initial DAT positive for IgG reverted to negative on repeat, consistent with a false positive.

Define coagulation context
- PT/aPTT and fibrinogen: prolonged PT/aPTT and low fibrinogen argue for DIC; normal values favor primary TMA. Early in sepsis, this patient had DIC features, which later normalized as the TMA emerged.

Urgently exclude TTP
- Send ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor. Severe deficiency (<10%) defines TTP and mandates immediate PEX, corticosteroids, and caplacizumab. This patient’s ADAMTS13 was 43.5%, making TTP unlikely; PLASMIC score was low. PEX was used briefly as a bridge while awaiting definitive results and to temporize hemolysis.

Assess for STEC-HUS
- Stool PCR/culture for Shiga toxin, particularly with diarrheal prodrome. A negative test here, plus lack of prodrome, argued against STEC-HUS.

Evaluate for CM-HUS and secondary TMAs
- Complement testing: serum C3/C4 may be normal and do not exclude CM-HUS. Functional assays (CH50, AH50) may be reduced during active disease but are confounded by eculizumab (which drives CH50/AH50 to near zero) and by recent PEX. Anti–factor H antibodies should be checked, particularly in children/young adults. In this case, basic C3/C4 were normal early; later AH50 was low after PEX and eculizumab, consistent with treatment effect.
- Genetic testing for variants in CFH, CFI, MCP/CD46, C3, CFB, THBD, and CFHR rearrangements; absence of a variant does not exclude CM-HUS but influences relapse risk and duration of therapy. No pathogenic variants were found in this patient.
- Secondary TMA screen tailored to context: blood pressure and fundoscopic exam for malignant hypertension; pregnancy test and hemolysis/AST/platelets for HELLP; medication review (quinine; calcineurin inhibitors; gemcitabine/mitomycin; VEGF inhibitors); autoimmune testing (ANA, anti-dsDNA; antiphospholipid antibodies); HIV and other viral testing as indicated; scleroderma features. In this case, autoimmune serologies were negative and the temporal relationship to C. canimorsus sepsis was compelling.
- Kidney biopsy is not required to diagnose TMA but can be decisive when multiple processes overlap (e.g., DIC vs TMA) or when renal recovery is uncertain. Here, biopsy confirmed TMA with fibrin thrombi and severe acute tubular necrosis, without immune deposits.

Timing matters. Sample for ADAMTS13 and baseline complement before PEX whenever possible. Recognize that PEX and complement inhibitors alter interpretation of functional complement assays.

## Management Principles and Rationale
Treat the sepsis you have; treat the TMA you fear. Therapy unfolds in phases, often overlapping.

Sepsis and DIC management
- Early, appropriate, bactericidal antibiotics. For dog-bite sepsis, empiric coverage should include Capnocytophaga, Pasteurella, streptococci, staphylococci, and oral anaerobes. β-Lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor (e.g., piperacillin–tazobactam) or a carbapenem are appropriate; third-generation cephalosporins plus metronidazole are alternatives. De-escalate to a susceptible agent once culture and susceptibility return; note increasing β-lactamase production among Capnocytophaga isolates, making penicillin monotherapy unreliable for empiric therapy.
- Source control of wounds and tetanus prophylaxis as indicated.
- Hemodynamic support; manage hypoglycemia seen in severe sepsis.
- DIC support: transfuse platelets and fibrinogen/cryoprecipitate for bleeding or procedures; avoid unnecessary platelet transfusion in pure TMA without bleeding; correct severe coagulopathy when bleeding risk is high.

Renal support
- Avoid volume overload; initiate renal replacement therapy for refractory hyperkalemia, acidosis, volume overload, or uremic complications. Continuous modalities are often preferred in hemodynamically tenuous patients. Recognize that AKI in this setting may be dual-hit: ischemic ATN and TMA.

Immediate TMA-directed care while ruling out TTP
- When TTP is on the table, begin PEX and corticosteroids without delay. Caplacizumab is added when TTP probability is high.
- If TTP becomes unlikely (ADAMTS13 ≥10%), PEX offers only transient benefit in CM-HUS by removing complement activation fragments or autoantibodies; definitive therapy is complement blockade.

Complement blockade
- Eculizumab (anti-C5 monoclonal antibody) is the standard of care in CM-HUS; earlier initiation correlates with better renal recovery and reduced chronic kidney disease. Induction typically 900 mg IV weekly for 4 weeks, then 1200 mg at week 5, followed by 1200 mg every 2 weeks for maintenance. Ravulizumab, a longer-acting C5 inhibitor, offers comparable efficacy with every-8-week maintenance dosing after loading.
- Infection risk with C5 blockade: high susceptibility to invasive Neisseria infections (including nongroupable strains and serogroups not covered by vaccines) and to some other encapsulated organisms. Vaccinate with MenACWY and MenB at least 2 weeks before first dose when feasible; if urgent treatment cannot wait, provide immediate antibiotic prophylaxis and vaccinate as soon as possible. Many centers continue antibiotic prophylaxis (e.g., oral penicillin V or amoxicillin; use an alternative if allergic) for at least 2 weeks after vaccination and often for the duration of therapy. Educate patients to seek immediate care for fever. Consider Hib and pneumococcal vaccination per local guidance for complement deficiencies.
- Active bacteremia and complement blockade: because complement contributes to bacterial killing, starting eculizumab in the midst of uncontrolled bacteremia is risky. In this case, therapy began after blood cultures cleared, and antibiotics were continued with vaccination and prophylaxis. The decision to start during active infection depends on severity of TMA, control of the infectious source, and multidisciplinary judgment.
- Duration and discontinuation: individualized. Patients with identified high-risk genetic variants or prior relapses often require prolonged or indefinite therapy. In secondary, trigger-associated CM-HUS without detected variants, many clinicians treat for several months beyond complete hematologic and renal remission, then attempt cautious discontinuation with close monitoring (e.g., weekly labs for the first month, then spaced). Relapse mandates prompt reinstitution.

Adjuncts and special situations
- Anti–factor H antibody–mediated disease may benefit from immunosuppression (e.g., rituximab) alongside C5 blockade; PEX can acutely remove antibodies.
- Blood transfusion for symptomatic anemia; avoid overtransfusion in the setting of heart failure risk.
- Platelet transfusions are generally avoided in primary TMA without bleeding but may be necessary in DIC with bleeding or for procedures, as in this case early on.
- Drug-induced TMAs require cessation of the offending agent; complement blockade is considered for refractory cases with evidence of complement activation.

Monitoring response
- Hematologic response: rising platelets, falling LDH, resolving schistocytosis, and stable/increasing hemoglobin without transfusion are expected within days of effective C5 blockade.
- Renal response lags: recovery may take weeks to months and is strongly time-dependent with respect to initiation of therapy. Biopsy evidence of chronic damage or crescents portends less complete recovery.

## Capnocytophaga canimorsus: Microbiology and Clinical Nuances
- Epidemiology: part of normal oral flora of dogs and cats; transmission via bites, scratches, or even close licking/contact. Disease in immunocompetent hosts is rare but documented; asplenia, cirrhosis, alcohol misuse, and complement deficiency increase risk.
- Clinical spectrum: cellulitis, bacteremia, sepsis with DIC, meningitis, endocarditis, ocular infections, and, rarely, TMA.
- Diagnostics: fastidious and slow-growing; cultures may take 5–7 days and benefit from CO2-enriched conditions. Molecular methods (e.g., 16S rRNA sequencing or rapid nanopore-based assays) can accelerate detection. Early empiric coverage is essential when exposure history suggests risk.
- Therapy: susceptible to β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, third-generation cephalosporins, carbapenems; variable β-lactamase production makes penicillin monotherapy unreliable empirically. Duration depends on presentation (often 10–14 days for bacteremia; longer for deep foci).

## Prognosis
Outcomes in CM-HUS hinge on early recognition and rapid initiation of complement blockade. Delays increase the likelihood of dialysis dependence and chronic kidney disease. Factors associated with poorer renal recovery include prolonged anuria, need for dialysis at presentation, severe biopsy changes (arteriolar thrombosis, cortical necrosis, crescents), and delayed therapy. Hematologic remission is the rule with C5 inhibition; renal recovery is variable. Relapse risk is higher with pathogenic complement variants or persistent triggers; in purely secondary cases without variants, sustained remission after finite therapy is common but not guaranteed.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points
- In adults with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and organ injury, treat TTP first in your mind: send ADAMTS13 urgently and start PEX if TTP is plausible. ADAMTS13 activity <10% defines TTP; ≥10% makes TTP unlikely and should pivot management toward HUS/other TMAs.
- DIC and TMA can coexist in severe sepsis. Falling fibrinogen and prolonged PT/aPTT suggest DIC; normal coagulation tests with significant schistocytosis favor TMA. A new wave of hemolysis as sepsis improves should prompt reevaluation for superimposed TMA.
- CM-HUS is a clinicopathologic diagnosis. Normal C3/C4 do not exclude it; local complement activation on endothelium may occur with normal systemic levels. Obtain anti–factor H antibodies and consider genetic testing, recognizing that absence of a variant does not rule out CM-HUS.
- Eculizumab (or ravulizumab) is definitive therapy for CM-HUS. Earlier initiation improves renal outcomes. PEX offers, at best, transient benefit in CM-HUS but is appropriate as a bridge when TTP is not yet excluded or in anti–factor H antibody disease.
- Vaccinate against meningococcus (MenACWY and MenB) and provide antibiotic prophylaxis when initiating C5 blockade; educate patients about fever vigilance. Initiation during active bacteremia is hazardous; weigh risks and benefits carefully and continue appropriate antimicrobials.
- Capnocytophaga sepsis should be suspected after dog or cat exposure. Empiric therapy should include β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor or carbapenem; de-escalate per susceptibilities. Slow growth can delay culture confirmation—do not delay empiric coverage when exposure history and clinical picture align.
- Kidney biopsy is not required to diagnose TMA but can clarify pathology when multiple processes overlap and inform prognosis.
- A positive DAT in a patient with suspected TMA warrants skepticism; drug effects and recent transfusions can produce false positives. Repeat testing and smear review guide interpretation.

This case underscores that a rare zoonotic infection can be the precipitant of a common mechanism—complement-driven endothelial injury—manifesting as TMA. Recognizing the shift from sepsis/DIC to CM-HUS and acting decisively with complement blockade can be the difference between permanent dialysis and renal recovery.
