Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

## Initial Clinical Approach: Sepsis and Cytopenias After a Dog Bite

A patient who presents within days of a dog bite with fever, systemic toxicity and new cytopenias demands rapid assessment for invasive zoonotic infection and early organ support. Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an important although uncommon pathogen to bear in mind: it is a slow‑growing, encapsulated, gram‑negative rod that resides in the oral flora of dogs and cats and can cause fulminant sepsis even in previously healthy adults. The index case illustrates the typical clinical tempo — a short incubation period after the bite followed by high fever, severe systemic inflammation, leukopenia, coagulopathy, hypoglycaemia and early acute kidney injury (AKI). Recognizing the pattern and acting decisively are the keys to favourable outcomes.

Initial triage focuses on identifying “red flags” that mandate ICU‑level monitoring and aggressive management. Hypotension or rising lactate, evidence of tissue hypoperfusion (mottled or cool extremities, evolving fingertip or toe ischemia), refractory hypoglycaemia, leukopenia in the setting of overwhelming sepsis, frank coagulopathy and oliguria/anuria are all warning signs. In such patients, obtain targeted resuscitation (airway, breathing, circulation), place invasive monitoring as indicated, and transfer to a monitored or intensive care setting. Early nephrology involvement is appropriate when urine output falls or creatinine rises rapidly; early renal replacement therapy is often required for severe volume overload, refractory acidosis, hyperkalaemia, or anuria.

Microbiologic diagnosis and prompt empiric antimicrobial therapy are simultaneous priorities. Whenever feasible, obtain blood cultures (both aerobic and anaerobic) before antibiotics, but cultures should not delay appropriate empiric therapy in a sick patient. Empiric regimens for severe dog‑bite–associated sepsis should cover Capnocytophaga as well as common skin and oral flora; beta‑lactam/beta‑lactamase inhibitor combinations (for example piperacillin‑tazobactam or amoxicillin‑clavulanate) or third‑generation cephalosporins are reasonable choices. Capnocytophaga is typically susceptible to penicillins and third‑generation cephalosporins but may be resistant to aminoglycosides; local susceptibility patterns and patient allergies should guide definitive therapy. Because C. canimorsus is fastidious and slow to grow, consider extended culture incubation and, in culture‑negative severe infections, molecular diagnostics (PCR or sequencing) if available.

Concurrent with antimicrobial treatment, address the wound and source control. Thorough irrigation and debridement, avoidance of inappropriate primary closure in deep or heavily contaminated wounds, and tetanus prophylaxis are standard. Rabies prophylaxis is guided by local epidemiology and the animal’s vaccination/behavioral status. For prophylaxis of infection after a bite in the outpatient or emergency setting, amoxicillin‑clavulanate is the first‑line agent; early antibiotics reduce the risk of invasive infection and are particularly indicated for facial bites, puncture wounds, immunocompromised hosts or delayed presentation.

Coagulopathy and cytopenias in sepsis raise a broad differential; the two major, potentially overlapping processes to consider are disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). DIC is common in severe sepsis and usually manifests with prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, markedly elevated D‑dimer/fibrin‑degradation products, consumptive thrombocytopenia and variable bleeding or thrombosis. Management is principally supportive and directed at treating the underlying infection; blood products (platelets, fibrinogen or fresh frozen plasma) are used for active bleeding, very low platelets before invasive procedures, or severe hypofibrinogenaemia.

TMA syndromes — which include Shiga‑toxin HUS, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) — are characterized by microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), schistocytosis on peripheral smear, elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), low or absent haptoglobin, thrombocytopenia and end‑organ ischemia such as severe AKI or neurologic signs. Distinguishing DIC from TMA can be challenging: both can produce schistocytes and thrombocytopenia, and sepsis frequently blurs diagnostic lines. Daily reassessment of hemoglobin, platelets, LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin, reticulocyte count and coagulation studies is essential. A rising LDH with an acute fall in hemoglobin and undetectable haptoglobin in the absence of overt bleeding should prompt a search for schistocytes and consideration of TMA. A pragmatic threshold is a schistocyte proportion above institutional laboratory cutoffs (often >1% or as reported locally) together with biochemical evidence of hemolysis.

Because immediate therapy differs between TTP and other TMAs, rapid discrimination is critical. TTP is defined by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10% activity) and is a hematologic emergency treated with urgent plasma exchange (PEX) and immunosuppression. Use clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score to estimate pretest probability while awaiting ADAMTS13 results, but do not delay PEX in patients with high clinical suspicion. If ADAMTS13 activity is not severely reduced, other causes of TMA should be pursued: stool testing for Shiga toxin to exclude STEC‑HUS, complement studies, and consideration of renal biopsy when safe and when the diagnosis remains uncertain — biopsy may show intraglomerular fibrin thrombi and other features consistent with TMA, as in the index case.

Be aware that CM‑HUS can be triggered by infections, and may present only after the initial septic phase begins to improve — the index patient developed overt MAHA and thrombocytopenia after inflammatory markers and coagulation indices had initially been stabilizing. Complement assays (C3/C4) can be normal in CM‑HUS and do not rule it out; send specialized complement and genetic testing to reference laboratories when available, but do so before plasma exchange or complement blockade if possible because these interventions can alter results. In practice, initiation of bridging therapy (PEX) is often done while investigations are pending; eculizumab (anti‑C5 monoclonal antibody) is highly effective for CM‑HUS but carries important infectious risks and should generally be started after bacteremia is controlled and appropriate vaccination and prophylaxis are in place.

Finally, early multidisciplinary involvement accelerates correct diagnosis and treatment. Infectious diseases input refines empiric antibiotic choices and interpretation of unusual pathogens; hematology helps navigate the DIC/TMA overlap, directs timing of PEX and guides evaluation for TTP; nephrology advises on timing of biopsy and dialysis, and critical care coordinates hemodynamic and metabolic support. The clinician caring for a patient after a dog bite should therefore think beyond local wound care: recognize the potential for rapid systemic deterioration, obtain cultures promptly, start empiric antibiotics that cover Capnocytophaga, monitor daily for evolving MAHA and persistent thrombocytopenia, and engage specialty teams early so that disease‑specific therapies (PEX, eculizumab) can be delivered in a timely and safe fashion should a TMA emerge.

## Pathophysiology of Complement-Mediated HUS and Infectious Triggers

Complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS) is a disease of dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway with downstream consequences that converge on the microvascular endothelium. Understanding the molecular steps of the alternative pathway, how host regulatory mechanisms normally restrain it, and how genetic or acquired defects permit runaway activation explains why patients develop the characteristic triad of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia and organ‑predominant microthrombosis—most often in the kidney.

At baseline the alternative pathway is continuously and spontaneously active at low levels by “tick‑over”: a small proportion of C3 is hydrolysed to C3(H2O), which allows generation of C3b that can covalently attach to nearby surfaces. C3b binds factor B, which is cleaved by factor D to form the C3 convertase (C3bBb). This convertase amplifies C3 cleavage, generating more C3b and creating a positive feedback loop; properdin stabilises the convertase and enhances amplification. Left unchecked, this amplification deposits large amounts of C3b on host cell surfaces and assembles the C5 convertase, leading to generation of C5a (a powerful anaphylatoxin and neutrophil chemoattractant) and C5b, which nucleates the terminal complement complex C5b‑9 (the membrane attack complex, MAC). C5b‑9 formation and the inflammatory milieu it creates are central mediators of the endothelial injury that underpins CM‑HUS.

Normal host tissues are protected from this by complement regulators that act at multiple steps. Soluble regulators—most importantly factor H and factor I—accelerate decay of the convertase and cleave C3b into inactive fragments; membrane regulators such as membrane cofactor protein (MCP/CD46) and decay accelerating factor (DAF/CD55) limit complement assembly on cell surfaces. When these regulators are deficient or dysfunctional, even modest pathway activation can amplify unchecked on endothelial surfaces. The result is endothelial cell activation and injury, with increased expression and release of ultra‑large von Willebrand factor (vWF) multimers, upregulation of procoagulant pathways, platelet recruitment and fibrin deposition in arterioles and glomerular capillaries. Red blood cells are mechanically sheared as they traverse the narrowed, thrombosed microvasculature, producing schistocytes and the laboratory pattern of MAHA.

Genetic susceptibility explains why uncontrolled alternative pathway activity occurs in some people and not in others. Pathogenic variants have been identified in genes encoding circulating regulators (factor H, factor I, factor B) and membrane regulators (MCP/CD46), and autoantibodies (notably anti‑factor H) can phenocopy genetic deficiency. Approximately half to two‑thirds of patients with CM‑HUS carry identifiable genetic variants or autoantibodies, but penetrance is incomplete: many carriers are never symptomatic. This incomplete penetrance reflects the usual requirement for a “second hit” — an environmental insult that tips the balance toward overt disease.

Infections are the commonest and most clinically important of these second hits. Bacterial and viral infections can activate complement systemically, alter endothelial function, or expose subendothelial matrices that promote thrombosis. Some pathogens also interact directly with complement regulators: certain bacteria recruit factor H to their surface to evade complement-mediated killing; others express capsules or surface molecules that variably influence serum susceptibility. Capnocytophaga canimorsus, the organism in this case, is a slow‑growing, encapsulated gram‑negative rod carried in the oropharynx of dogs and cats. Laboratory and clinical observations show that different C. canimorsus strains have variable serum susceptibility: some are complement‑sensitive and readily cleared, others—especially encapsulated strains—show partial resistance. Whether through direct activation of complement, by evading complement in a way that provokes stronger systemic activation, or by triggering inflammatory pathways that amplify complement, C. canimorsus (as with other infections) can serve as the provoking event that precipitates CM‑HUS in a susceptible host. This creates the clinical paradox highlighted by the case: complement activation is essential for control of the infection, but in a patient with impaired regulation it becomes the proximate cause of endothelial damage and thrombosis.

How that pathobiology translates into clinical and pathological findings is important for diagnosis and management. The microthrombi of CM‑HUS are characteristically platelet‑ and fibrin‑rich and affect glomerular capillaries and small arterioles; light microscopy commonly shows endothelial swelling, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, mesangiolysis and sometimes crescents. Laboratory evidence of MAHA—schistocytosis, elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), undetectable haptoglobin—and persistent thrombocytopenia accompany renal dysfunction. Importantly, routine complement measurements (C3 and C4) are often normal in CM‑HUS. Complement activation in CM‑HUS is frequently localised to the microvasculature, or intermittent, and circulating complement proteins can be normal because of synthesis as acute‑phase reactants or because sampling occurs after partial recovery or after plasma therapies. Functional assays (CH50, AH50), anti‑factor H antibody testing, and genetic sequencing increase diagnostic sensitivity, but results may be altered by therapeutic plasma exchange or complement blockade and must be interpreted in context and with attention to timing.

Distinguishing CM‑HUS from other causes of microangiopathy in the critically ill, particularly disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), is crucial because therapies differ. DIC is a syndrome of systemic activation of coagulation with consumption of clotting factors and platelets; laboratory hallmarks include prolonged PT/aPTT, elevated D‑dimer/fibrin degradation products, and low fibrinogen, and clinical bleeding is common. DIC can accompany severe sepsis and produce schistocytes, but the pattern is usually broad coagulation disturbance rather than the kidney‑predominant microvascular thrombosis of CM‑HUS. In practice the two may coexist early in severe infections: the case illustrates this overlap—initial sepsis with DIC and leukopenia was followed by a later, isolated microangiopathic haemolytic process and biopsy‑proven TMA once coagulation parameters were improving. TTP is defined by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%) and often presents with severe thrombocytopenia and neurological manifestations; distinguishing it from CM‑HUS relies on ADAMTS13 assays and clinical context because empiric plasma exchange is required urgently if TTP is suspected.

Therapeutically, the pathophysiology informs treatment selection. Plasma exchange can transiently remove pathogenic autoantibodies and provide functional complement regulators, and it remains the first‑line rescue when the diagnosis is unclear or TTP has not been excluded. However, in CM‑HUS, plasma exchange rarely corrects the underlying regulatory defect. Complement blockade with anti‑C5 monoclonal antibodies (eculizumab and newer agents) prevents generation of C5a and assembly of the MAC, halting endothelial injury and microthrombus formation; early initiation is associated with better hematologic and renal recovery. Because terminal complement is also essential for host defence against encapsulated organisms, using complement inhibitors during or immediately after bacteraemia requires careful timing, antibiotic coverage, vaccinations (meningococcal), and antimicrobial prophylaxis—trade‑offs that were navigated in the present case by initiating eculizumab after blood cultures cleared and by providing vaccination and prophylactic antibiotics.

In summary, CM‑HUS results from unchecked amplification of the alternative complement pathway at endothelial surfaces, leading to MAC‑mediated endothelial activation, platelet aggregation and intravascular microthrombosis. Genetic defects or autoantibodies that impair complement regulation create susceptibility, but an external trigger—commonly an infection such as C. canimorsus—is often necessary to precipitate clinical disease. Recognition that complement assays may be normal, appreciation of the timing of haemolysis relative to sepsis, and awareness of the overlap with DIC and TTP are essential to make the diagnosis and institute pathogen‑directed therapy together with timely complement blockade.

## Differential Diagnosis of TMA in Adults

Thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs) are a group of syndromes united by microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia and microvascular thrombosis causing end-organ ischaemia — most conspicuously in the brain and kidney. The challenge for the clinician is rapid discrimination among the major causes because initial management diverges sharply: thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) requires immediate plasma exchange, Shiga-toxin HUS (STEC‑HUS) is managed supportively, complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) is treated with complement blockade, and many secondary TMAs require treating the underlying disorder. The case above illustrates these diagnostic crossroads: an episode of fulminant sepsis with DIC that later evolved into frank MAHA and renal-limited TMA, mandating a rapid, structured workup.

How to approach a patient with suspected TMA
When MAHA and thrombocytopenia are detected, undertake a simultaneous bundle of actions: (1) send the definitive tests that will change immediate therapy, (2) perform basic laboratory studies that help distinguish DIC and secondary causes, (3) institute empiric, potentially life‑saving therapy when indicated, and (4) search for a triggering condition. Practically, this means sending an ADAMTS13 activity assay urgently (with a rapid-turnaround request), obtaining blood cultures and, if diarrhoea is present, stool testing for Shiga toxin, ordering coagulation studies (PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer), basic haemolysis markers (LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, reticulocyte count), peripheral blood smear (for schistocytes), and complement studies (C3/C4, when available). Consult hematology and nephrology early.

Prioritise exclusion of TTP
The most time‑sensitive diagnosis to exclude is TTP because untreated severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%) carries very high mortality but responds dramatically to plasma exchange (PEX). While awaiting ADAMTS13 results, clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score can estimate pretest probability and help decide whether to start empiric PEX. If clinical probability is high, PEX should be initiated without delay — do not wait for the laboratory result. Empiric corticosteroids and caplacizumab may also be considered in appropriate contexts. In the presented patient, the PLASMIC score was low and ADAMTS13 activity returned at 43.5%, effectively excluding TTP and redirecting management away from continued PEX as sole therapy.

STEC‑HUS: look for the prodrome and test stool
Classic STEC‑HUS typically follows a prodrome of bloody diarrhoea and abdominal pain; stool PCR for Shiga toxin or culture for E. coli O157 are diagnostic. Absence of a diarrhoeal prodrome and a negative stool PCR argue against STEC‑HUS and point toward alternative causes of TMA (as in this case, where stool PCR was negative). Nonetheless, stool testing should be done early in any patient with HUS‑compatible features.

Distinguish primary TMAs from secondary TMAs by context and basic tests
Secondary TMAs are more common than primary forms and are caused by another identifiable process. Key entities to consider:

- Sepsis‑ or DIC‑associated TMA: Severe infections (including meningococcaemia or Capnocytophaga, as in this case) may cause endothelial injury and microthrombi. DIC commonly causes prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen and very elevated D‑dimer; overt coagulopathy and bleeding manifestations favour DIC. TMA can coexist or emerge after the septic phase; careful serial assessment is needed.

- Malignant hypertension: Extremely elevated blood pressure can produce shear stress–mediated endothelial damage and glomerular fibrin thrombi. Control of blood pressure is the essential therapy.

- Pregnancy‑related TMAs: HELLP (haemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets) and pre‑eclampsia spectrum disorders occur during late pregnancy/postpartum and are managed with delivery and supportive care. Distinguish from TTP or CM‑HUS by timing and ancillary features.

- Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) and catastrophic APS: May present with multiorgan thrombosis and MAHA; antiphospholipid antibodies are diagnostic, and management includes anticoagulation and immunosuppression.

- Drug‑induced TMA: Several agents (quinine, calcineurin inhibitors, gemcitabine, VEGF inhibitors) can cause TMA either by immune mechanisms or endothelial toxicity. A careful medication history is essential.

- Transplant‑associated TMA: Solid organ or stem cell transplant recipients may develop TMA related to calcineurin inhibitors, rejection, infection, or conditioning regimens.

- Malignancy‑associated TMA: Advanced cancers (especially mucinous adenocarcinomas) can present with MAHA and thrombocytopenia.

Clinical clues that point toward one diagnosis or another
- Neurologic predominance (seizures, confusion, focal deficits) with relatively milder renal involvement suggests TTP, although overlap exists.  
- Prominent renal failure with oliguria/anuria supports HUS‑spectrum disorders (STEC‑HUS, CM‑HUS), particularly when biopsy demonstrates glomerular/arteriolar thrombi.  
- Overt consumptive coagulopathy (prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen) favours DIC over primary TMA.  
- A clear triggering exposure (diarrhoea, high blood pressure, pregnancy, offending drug, cancer, recent transplantation) often points to a secondary TMA.  
- Time course matters: in sepsis, haemolysis may be obscured early and appear only as the infection or DIC resolves, as in the presented patient.

Important diagnostic pitfalls
- Direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test (DAT): A positive DAT suggests immune haemolysis but does not exclude coexisting MAHA. Penicillins and other drugs can cause DAT positivity, and recent transfusions may yield transient positives. If the clinical and laboratory picture (schistocytes, very low haptoglobin, elevated LDH) fits MAHA, repeat DAT testing and integrate the whole picture rather than rely on one positive result. In our case, an initial positive DAT was later negative and likely represented drug or transfusion interference.  
- Coagulation tests may be normal in primary TMA despite severe microvascular thrombosis; conversely, mild abnormalities can occur in TMA without true DIC. Interpretation must be holistic and serial.  
- Complement testing (C3/C4, functional assays) and genetic testing: Normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS. Functional assays and genotyping are useful, but their results can be distorted by PEX or eculizumab and may return only after acute management decisions have been made.

Role of kidney biopsy
Renal biopsy is not required to make the diagnosis in many cases but becomes valuable when the diagnosis remains unclear or AKI is severe and management would change. Classic light‑microscopy features of TMA include glomerular and arteriolar fibrin thrombi, endothelial swelling, ischemic glomerular collapse, mesangiolysis and sometimes crescents; immunofluorescence is typically negative for immune complex deposition in primary TMAs such as CM‑HUS and TTP. Biopsy should be deferred until platelet count and coagulation are adequate; in the reported patient, biopsy after partial haematologic recovery confirmed TMA and supported the diagnosis of CM‑HUS.

Putting it together: a practical algorithm
- Immediate: identify MAHA (schistocytes, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin) and thrombocytopenia; draw ADAMTS13, blood cultures, stool for Shiga toxin if diarrhoea, coagulation panel, haemolysis labs and peripheral smear; consult hematology/nephrology.  
- If high clinical suspicion of TTP (high PLASMIC score or severe neurologic features), start PEX and steroids emergently while awaiting ADAMTS13.  
- If ADAMTS13 activity >10% and stool Shiga toxin negative, evaluate for CM‑HUS (consider early complement blockade), secondary causes (sepsis/DIC, malignant hypertension, drugs, pregnancy, APS), and plan biopsy if needed. Remember that complement studies and genetics may be diagnostic but can be affected by prior PEX or eculizumab.  
- Treat the trigger: antibiotics for sepsis, blood pressure control for malignant hypertension, cessation of offending drugs, delivery for pregnancy‑related disease, anticoagulation/immunosuppression for CAPS, etc.  
- If CM‑HUS is likely and infection is controlled, consider early eculizumab, bearing in mind the need for meningococcal vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis.

This structured approach — rapid exclusion of TTP, targeted microbiological testing for STEC, assessment for secondary causes by history and basic labs, and selective use of kidney biopsy — allows timely, diagnosis‑directed therapy that maximises the chance of hematological remission and renal recovery, as illustrated by the patient who evolved from septic DIC to CM‑HUS and benefited from complement blockade after other causes were excluded.

## Diagnostic Workup and Testing Strategy

Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is a clinical syndrome, not a single disease, and the initial diagnostic approach must therefore be rapid, systematic and pragmatic. The first objective at the bedside is to determine whether the patient has microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) with thrombocytopenia, to exclude immediately reversible and highly lethal causes (most importantly thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, TTP), and to identify treatable triggers such as infection. In the case presented, the patient arrived critically ill with pancytopenia, coagulopathy and AKI: early recognition that a new episode of hemolysis developed later in her course (dropping hemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH and schistocytes on smear) changed the working diagnosis from pure DIC/sepsis to a superimposed TMA, and prompted the focused TMA workup described below.

Initial, time‑sensitive laboratory evaluation
Obtain a set of “hemolysis” studies and baseline organ tests without delay. A useful panel to draw immediately includes: CBC with platelet count and peripheral smear (request review for schistocytes), LDH, total and indirect bilirubin, haptoglobin, reticulocyte count, basic metabolic profile including creatinine, urinalysis (dip and microscopy) and coagulation studies (PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen and D‑dimer). The peripheral smear is indispensable: schistocytes are the morphologic hallmark of MAHA; while low numbers can be seen in many settings, a rising schistocyte burden (classically >1% and certainly when clearly present as in this case) supports intravascular RBC fragmentation from TMA. LDH and indirect bilirubin corroborate hemolysis; haptoglobin is usually low but can be altered by liver disease or inflammation. Coagulation tests are needed early to distinguish DIC (prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, very high D‑dimer) from TMA, in which coagulation tests are often relatively preserved despite marked microvascular thrombosis.

Immediate actions when MAHA ± thrombocytopenia are present
When MAHA and thrombocytopenia are documented, the highest diagnostic priority is to exclude TTP because delayed plasma exchange (PEX) is associated with very high mortality. Send an urgent ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor (autoantibody) assay and, at the same time, calculate a clinical prediction score (for example PLASMIC) to stratify pretest probability. While awaiting results, initiate PEX promptly if TTP cannot be reasonably excluded on clinical grounds (high PLASMIC score, severe neurologic findings, severe thrombocytopenia with microangiopathy). In our case, ADAMTS13 was 43.5% and TTP was thereby effectively excluded; nevertheless empiric PEX was used as a bridging strategy because the diagnosis and optimal therapy (complement blockade versus other options) were not yet certain.

Microbiological assessment and the infectious trigger
Simultaneously pursue an aggressive infectious workup because infections commonly trigger secondary TMA and because their presence changes management. Obtain blood cultures (ideally before antibiotics) and relevant site cultures; remember that some pathogens grow slowly and require prolonged incubation or molecular diagnostics. Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a classic example: it is fastidious, often slow to grow, and may be missed without prolonged culture or use of molecular methods. If diarrheal prodrome or suspected STEC-associated HUS exists, order stool PCR for Shiga toxin and enteric pathogens early. When cultures are negative but clinical suspicion for an infectious trigger remains high, consider nucleic acid amplification or next‑generation sequencing performed by reference laboratories. Positive blood cultures mandate aggressive antimicrobial therapy and inform timing of immunomodulatory treatment—complement blockade should generally be deferred until bacteraemia is controlled or the risk-benefit balance is carefully considered, as in the present case.

Complement testing and genetic studies: what to send and when
Because complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) is a working diagnosis of exclusion, basic and extended complement studies should be obtained early, ideally before any intervention that alters circulating proteins. Request C3 and C4 as initial screens, but understand their limited sensitivity: normal levels do not exclude complement‑driven disease. Functional assays (CH50 and AH50) assess classical and alternative pathway activity and are more informative; anti‑factor H autoantibodies should be measured when clinically indicated. Send serum for factor H, factor I and factor B levels, and consider soluble membrane cofactor protein (sMCP/CD46) measurements where available. Genetic testing for pathogenic variants in CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, CD46 and related regulators should be arranged early because results influence long‑term management; these tests require whole blood in EDTA and may be performed by a reference laboratory.

Timing of specimen collection relative to therapies is crucial. Plasma exchange will dilute and remove complement components and autoantibodies, potentially normalizing or falsely reducing assays; therefore collect complement serologies and samples for genetic testing before the first PEX where feasible. Likewise, draw ADAMTS13 samples prior to PEX because PEX may remove inhibitory antibodies and change activity measurements. If eculizumab is already in use, functional assays such as CH50/AH50 will be profoundly depressed (expected effect of terminal pathway blockade) and cannot be used to infer pre‑treatment complement activation.

Interpreting tests in the context of confounders
Several common clinical events can confound test interpretation. Transfusions and certain antibiotics (notably penicillins) may cause a positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test, misleading clinicians toward autoimmune hemolysis; in our case the initial DAT was likely a false positive related to penicillin exposure and transfusions. Sepsis itself can produce schistocytes, thrombocytopenia and elevated LDH from severe endothelial activation, and DIC frequently accompanies sepsis; differentiating DIC from primary TMA relies on coagulation parameters, clinical pattern and evolution with treatment of infection. ADAMTS13 activity below 10% is diagnostic for TTP; intermediate levels do not exclude other TMAs and must be interpreted together with clinical data.

When and how to pursue tissue diagnosis
Renal biopsy has an important role when the diagnosis is uncertain or when AKI persists and tissue diagnosis will change management. Typical light microscopy features of TMA include endothelial cell swelling, fibrin‑rich thrombi in glomerular capillaries and arterioles, mesangiolysis, ischemic glomeruli and sometimes crescents. Absence of immune complex deposition on immunofluorescence and the lack of electron‑dense immune deposits on electron microscopy help distinguish primary complement‑mediated TMA from immune complex glomerulonephritis. However, biopsy has risks—especially in the setting of thrombocytopenia and ongoing coagulopathy—so timing is important: defer biopsy until platelets and coagulation are safe (commonly platelets >50×10^9/L and INR acceptable), or after temporary correction with transfusions if necessary, and coordinate closely with nephrology and interventional radiology. In the presented patient, biopsy performed once platelets had recovered demonstrated classic TMA changes and helped confirm the diagnosis.

Operational and logistical considerations
Effective TMA workup requires early and repeated communication with the laboratory and coordination among nephrology, hematology, infectious diseases and transfusion medicine. Specify the required tubes for each assay: ADAMTS13 requires sodium citrate plasma; complement serologies generally require serum (allow to clot, centrifuge and freeze if delayed) and CH50/AH50 samples must be kept chilled and shipped according to laboratory instructions. Genetic testing requires an EDTA whole‑blood sample. If samples cannot be sent to a reference lab the same day, freeze aliquots according to lab protocol so that pre‑treatment material is preserved. Document times of blood draws relative to PEX, transfusions, antibiotic doses and eculizumab administration so that interpreting physicians can adjust their diagnostic conclusions accordingly.

Monitoring and iterative reassessment
Finally, treat the diagnostic process as iterative. Reassess the patient frequently with daily CBCs with smear, LDH, haptoglobin, creatinine and urine output to track response and to inform transitions from empiric to disease‑specific therapies (for example stopping PEX when ADAMTS13 excludes TTP and initiating eculizumab for CM‑HUS). Use trends rather than single values: rising platelets, falling LDH and improving renal function support therapeutic success. Maintain a low threshold for repeating key tests (e.g., DAT, ADAMTS13, complement studies) if clinical status changes or if prior samples were drawn after PEX or complement blockade. The diagnostic pathway in this case—rapid microbiological identification of C. canimorsus, early collection of ADAMTS13 and complement studies, careful consideration of PEX as a bridge, renal biopsy once safe, and prompt initiation of eculizumab when infection was controlled—illustrates how practical laboratory timing, multidisciplinary coordination and repeated clinical reassessment lead to a correct diagnosis and optimized care.

## Management: From Infection Control to Complement Blockade

The care of a patient who develops thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in the context of severe sepsis requires two parallel and sometimes competing streams of therapy: aggressive sepsis management (source control, appropriate antibiotics and organ support) and timely TMA‑directed interventions that may include plasma exchange (PEX) and complement blockade. The clinical priority at presentation is to stabilise the patient and control the infectious source, because untreated sepsis with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is immediately life‑threatening. At the same time, clinicians must remain vigilant for the emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), worsening thrombocytopenia and progressive renal failure that signal a superimposed TMA and prompt a very different set of therapies.

Sepsis and source control come first. Empiric antimicrobial therapy should be started promptly and tailored to the most likely pathogens and the exposure history; in dog‑ or cat‑associated infections this includes Capnocytophaga species. Practical empiric options include piperacillin–tazobactam or a third‑generation cephalosporin such as ceftriaxone; many Capnocytophaga isolates are penicillin‑susceptible and respond to amoxicillin–clavulanate or penicillin, but susceptibility testing and clinical response should guide de‑escalation. Surgical source control or local care of bite wounds is essential when indicated. In the case described, early empiric piperacillin–tazobactam was appropriate, and definitive therapy was narrowed after blood cultures grew Capnocytophaga canimorsus. Timely microbiological diagnosis is critical because it both guides antibiotic selection and informs the safety of later complement‑targeted therapies.

Supportive care for sepsis and DIC is oncologic to outcome and must be individualised. Standard ICU measures include haemodynamic optimisation, ventilatory support when required and correction of metabolic derangements. In DIC, transfusion decisions should be driven by bleeding and procedural needs rather than laboratory numbers alone. Platelet transfusion is indicated for active bleeding, for invasive procedures, or in profound thrombocytopenia with bleeding risk (often <50 × 10^9/L for procedures, lower thresholds for simple transfusion are used), but routine transfusion in TMA is discouraged because it may fuel microvascular thrombosis. Fresh frozen plasma, fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate should be used to correct severe hypofibrinogenemia or ongoing bleeding. In the reported patient, supportive transfusions were used judiciously while awaiting diagnostic clarification.

Renal support often becomes necessary. Indications for renal replacement therapy are the usual ones—refractory volume overload, severe acidosis, hyperkalaemia, uremia and anuria. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is preferred in haemodynamically unstable patients; intermittent dialysis is an option as the patient stabilises. The case patient required CRRT for anuric acute kidney injury and fluid overload, which is a common scenario when TMA overlaps with septic shock and DIC.

When a TMA is suspected, triage for specific therapy must be immediate because untreated TTP is rapidly fatal and eculizumab is time‑sensitive in complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). Two practical rules guide initial management: (1) do not delay life‑saving plasma exchange if severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (TTP) cannot be excluded, and (2) obtain blood for ADAMTS13 testing before starting PEX or plasma infusions when possible. PEX is the emergent therapy of choice for suspected TTP because it removes autoantibodies and replenishes ADAMTS13. In contrast, PEX is only temporising in CM‑HUS: it can remove circulating complement activation products and autoantibodies (for example anti‑factor H) and thus provide transient clinical improvement, but it does not correct the underlying dysregulation in most patients. In the present patient, PEX was appropriately started while ADAMTS13 testing was pending, and it produced only a transient improvement in diuresis until targeted complement blockade was given.

Eculizumab—the monoclonal antibody that blocks terminal complement component C5—is the standard of care for CM‑HUS and substantially improves haematologic and renal outcomes, particularly when administered early. The typical induction regimen used in adults is 900 mg intravenously weekly for four doses followed by a maintenance dose (commonly 1,200 mg) every two weeks; dosing schedules may vary by region and product labelling. In CM‑HUS, earlier initiation is associated with better chances of renal recovery and avoidance of long‑term dialysis. In the case under discussion, the patient received PEX as a bridge and then eculizumab once infection control permitted and appropriate precautions were taken; sustained haematologic remission and partial renal recovery followed.

Complement blockade carries a well‑recognised infectious risk because terminal complement activity is pivotal for defence against encapsulated organisms, most notably Neisseria meningitidis. This risk influences timing and infection‑control strategies. Recommended precautions are:

- Vaccinate against Neisseria meningitidis serogroups ACWY and B ideally at least two weeks before administering the first dose of eculizumab. If vaccination cannot be given two weeks prior because treatment is urgent, give the vaccines as soon as possible and cover the patient with appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis until adequate vaccine‑mediated immunity is expected (commonly at least 2 weeks).
- Provide antimicrobial prophylaxis while vaccination is incomplete or in cases where immediate eculizumab is necessary. Common prophylactic regimens include oral penicillin V or amoxicillin (or an alternative agent in penicillin‑allergic patients, such as ciprofloxacin), but local epidemiology, susceptibilities and allergy history should guide choice.
- Counsel patients thoroughly about early symptoms of meningococcal disease and advise urgent medical evaluation for fever or systemic symptoms.

These principles must be balanced against the immediate risk to the patient. If a patient has active, uncontrolled bacteraemia—especially with an encapsulated organism—full complement blockade may exacerbate infection. Therefore, whenever feasible, clinicians should wait for blood cultures to clear and for the acute infectious process to be controlled before initiating eculizumab. However, if the TMA is fulminant and life‑threatening, it may be necessary to begin eculizumab despite recent bacteremia; in that circumstance, ensure maximal antimicrobial coverage, confirm source control, give vaccines as soon as possible, institute antibiotic prophylaxis, and monitor closely. In the published case, eculizumab was started after blood cultures became negative and vaccination was administered prior to continuing therapy, and prophylactic amoxicillin was used because vaccines had not been given sufficiently in advance.

Adjunctive therapies and transfusion pragmatics deserve specific mention. Red blood cell transfusion should be given for symptomatic anaemia or following standard haemoglobin thresholds adjusted for comorbidity (for example, Hb <70 g/L in otherwise stable patients, higher in those with ischaemic heart disease or severe symptoms). Platelets should generally be avoided in TMA unless there is clinically significant bleeding or a procedure that warrants higher counts; transfusion can exacerbate microvascular thrombosis and worsen outcomes. Corticosteroids have no proven role in CM‑HUS and should not be used routinely for TMA unless there is a separate indication (for example, immune haemolytic anaemia). When autoantibodies to complement regulators are suspected (eg, anti‑factor H), PEX may be particularly useful to remove the pathogenic antibodies prior to initiation of targeted therapy.

Monitoring response and deciding treatment duration are clinical decisions guided by the trajectory of haematologic and renal recovery and by genetic testing. Response to therapy is judged by rising platelet count, falling LDH and bilirubin, rising haptoglobin, clearance of schistocytes on the peripheral smear and recovery of renal function and urine output. Functional complement assays (for example CH50 or AH50) may be used to document complement blockade, although interpretation can be confounded by PEX or timing of sampling. Genetic testing for complement pathway variants and testing for anti‑complement autoantibodies should be obtained because identification of a pathogenic variant or persistent autoantibody supports longer‑term or indefinite complement inhibition; conversely, in patients without demonstrable genetic predisposition and with a clear, removable trigger (such as an infection) who achieve sustained remission, discontinuation of eculizumab may be considered after a period of maintenance therapy with very close follow‑up. A practical approach is to reassess after several months of stability: if haemolysis and thrombocytopenia remain quiescent and renal function has recovered or stabilised, some centres consider stopping therapy with a plan for prompt restart at relapse. The case patient received an induction course followed by maintenance dosing and, in the absence of a pathogenic genetic variant, is being considered for discontinuation if stable renal function persists.

Finally, the management of TMA in the setting of sepsis requires multimodal coordination—infectious disease, nephrology, haematology and critical care teams must collaborate to time interventions safely. The published case illustrates these principles: early broad‑spectrum antibiotics and ICU support controlled the primary infection; PEX was used emergently while TTP was excluded; eculizumab was introduced once blood cultures were negative and after vaccination and prophylactic antibiotics were arranged; and close laboratory and clinical monitoring guided ongoing therapy and the plan for eventual reassessment and possible discontinuation. This staged, pragmatic approach—prioritising infection control without delaying necessary TMA‑directed therapy—offers the best chance of a good outcome in patients who present at the interface of sepsis and complement‑mediated disease.

## Prognosis, Follow-up, and Clinical Pearls

Prognosis in complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS) is governed principally by two interacting factors: the severity of kidney injury at presentation and the timeliness of definitive therapy with complement blockade. Patients who present with severe, dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) have a substantially higher risk of incomplete renal recovery and progression to end‑stage kidney disease (ESKD) than those with milder renal involvement. Observational data and case series show that earlier initiation of eculizumab (or other terminal complement inhibitors) is associated with higher rates of haematological remission and greater recovery of renal function; conversely, delayed treatment increases the probability of irreversible microvascular and tubular injury. In this case the patient’s partial renal recovery after delayed but eventual eculizumab illustrates these principles: haematological remission followed, and some kidney function returned, but she remains at elevated risk for chronic kidney disease because of the initial severity and need for dialysis.

Risk of relapse and the optimal duration of complement blockade are individualized and depend largely on the presence or absence of underlying complement abnormalities and on the clinical context that precipitated the episode. Approximately half of patients with CM‑HUS harbour pathogenic variants or autoantibodies that impair complement regulation; these patients have higher relapse rates and are more likely to require long‑term or indefinite complement inhibition. By contrast, when an episode is clearly triggered by a transient insult (severe infection, certain drugs, pregnancy) and extended genetic testing does not identify pathogenic variants, many experts consider a carefully monitored trial of discontinuation after a sustained period of remission (commonly 6–12 months) reasonable. The decision must weigh the severity of the index episode (need for dialysis, extrarenal organ injury), patient preferences, and logistical considerations for close follow‑up. If eculizumab is stopped, a clear monitoring plan and a rapid response strategy for recurrence are essential.

Follow‑up after an acute episode of CM‑HUS should be structured, frequent initially, and then spaced out as stability is demonstrated. Suggested elements are:

- Clinical review: assess volume status, blood pressure, symptoms of anaemia or bleeding, and any new neurologic or constitutional complaints. Emphasize prompt evaluation of fever or other infection signs in patients receiving complement blockade.
- Laboratory surveillance: during the acute and early recovery phase, check CBC with platelet count, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), haptoglobin, creatinine and electrolytes, and urinalysis (including urine protein). Typical frequency is daily to every 2–3 days while unstable or during induction therapy, then weekly for the first month after discharge, then every 2–4 weeks for the next 2–3 months, and thereafter every 1–3 months depending on stability. Any fall in platelet count, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin or new schistocytes on peripheral smear should trigger urgent reassessment for recurrent TMA.
- Renal monitoring: serial serum creatinine and urine protein quantification. Blood pressure control and ACE inhibitor/ARB therapy (when appropriate) should be optimized for long‑term kidney protection.
- Complement monitoring: routine C3/C4 levels are of limited sensitivity for disease activity, but functional assays (eg, CH50/AH50) may be used in some centres to document effective complement blockade or to guide therapy when available. If genetic testing was performed, the results should be reviewed with the patient and included in long‑term planning.
- ADAMTS13 is not needed routinely after diagnosis unless the clinical picture changes and thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is reconsidered.
- Biopsy and imaging: a kidney biopsy is helpful when the diagnosis is uncertain or when there is a discrepancy between the clinical course and laboratory data; it also provides prognostic information about chronicity and the extent of irreversible injury.

Vaccination, antimicrobial prophylaxis and infection precautions are critically important for any patient receiving terminal complement blockade. Because eculizumab blocks formation of the membrane attack complex, susceptibility to certain encapsulated organisms—classically Neisseria meningitidis but also other pathogens such as Capnocytophaga species—rises. Best practice recommendations include:

- Administer appropriate meningococcal vaccines (both conjugate A/C/Y/W and serogroup B formulations) at least 2 weeks before planned eculizumab therapy when feasible. If urgent therapy is required and vaccines cannot be given 2 weeks beforehand, give vaccines as soon as possible and provide antibiotic prophylaxis until adequate vaccine‑induced immunity is likely (commonly for 2 weeks).
- Consider short‑term antibiotic prophylaxis at eculizumab initiation (eg, oral penicillin V or an appropriate alternative in penicillin allergy) until 2 weeks after vaccination; practices vary and decisions should involve infectious disease specialists.
- Give clear instructions to patients to seek immediate medical attention for any fever or shivering and to inform emergency teams they are receiving complement blockade. Many centres provide a written alert card and advise carrying a short‑course “rescue” antibiotic (eg, ciprofloxacin or amoxicillin‑clavulanate based on local resistance patterns) to begin immediately if fever occurs before definitive medical evaluation.
- Ensure routine immunizations are up to date, and coordinate vaccinations with the timing of complement therapy.

Patient counselling is a central part of follow‑up. Patients should understand the nature of CM‑HUS (a microvascular disease driven by dysregulated complement activity), the rationale for eculizumab, and the real but manageable risks of infection associated with complement inhibition. Specific counselling points include:

- The need for urgent evaluation of fever while on eculizumab and the availability of emergent antibiotics if they cannot get immediate care.
- The signs and symptoms of relapse (new bruising or bleeding, dark urine, fatigue, pallor, reduced urine output, neurologic symptoms) and a low threshold for contacting their care team.
- Lifestyle adjustments while immunized and on prophylaxis (no specific activity restrictions beyond infection vigilance), and the importance of completing vaccine schedules and reporting travel plans that may alter exposure risk.
- For women of childbearing age, discuss pregnancy as a potential trigger for HUS and review contraceptive plans and pre‑conception counselling; management of CM‑HUS in pregnancy requires a multidisciplinary approach.

Frontline prevention and early management of dog bites and related infections are generalizable lessons from this case. Immediate wound care—thorough irrigation and cleansing, avoidance of unsterile manipulation, and assessment for need of closure—reduces bacterial inoculum. Prophylactic antibiotics are recommended for high‑risk bites (deep punctures, hand injuries, immunocompromised or asplenic patients) and amoxicillin‑clavulanate is the usual first‑line agent because it covers Pasteurella and Capnocytophaga species. Tetanus prophylaxis should be updated when indicated, and rabies prophylaxis considered if the animal’s rabies status is uncertain. Importantly, even previously healthy individuals can develop fulminant infections with C. canimorsus; early recognition of systemic symptoms after an animal exposure should prompt urgent assessment.

Clinical pearls that apply to frontline clinicians and help avoid diagnostic and therapeutic pitfalls:

- Normal C3 and C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS. Complement levels are frequently normal in patients with CM‑HUS; reliance on C3/C4 alone can give false reassurance.
- Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and sepsis may mask or precede a superimposed TMA. In patients with recovering sepsis who develop new anaemia, schistocytes, persistent thrombocytopenia or worsening renal function, re‑evaluate actively for TMA.
- A positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test can be misleading. Drug‑related or transfusion‑related antibodies may produce a transient positive DAT; persistent evidence of microangiopathic haemolysis (schistocytes, elevated LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, reticulocytosis when marrow function allows) should prompt evaluation for TMA despite an initial positive DAT.
- Peripheral blood smear is simple and high‑yield. Examination for schistocytes is a key bedside diagnostic step when haemolysis is suspected.
- Coordinate diagnostic sampling before plasma exchange (PEX) or first dose of eculizumab when possible. ADAMTS13 activity, baseline complement functional testing (eg, CH50/AH50) and blood for genetic testing should ideally be drawn prior to plasma infusion or complement blockade to avoid treatment‑related artefacts; if therapy must be started emergently, document timing of samples relative to treatment in the clinical record.
- Renal biopsy is valuable when safe and feasible. In patients with atypical presentations or when the diagnosis is uncertain, histology can distinguish TMA from other causes of AKI and help predict reversibility.
- TTP must be excluded promptly. ADAMTS13 activity <10% identifies TTP and mandates urgent PEX; use clinical tools (eg, PLASMIC score) to triage but confirm with laboratory testing.
- Multidisciplinary care is essential. Early involvement of nephrology, haematology, infectious disease and critical care teams improves diagnostic accuracy, timing of therapies (antimicrobials, PEX, eculizumab), and coordination of vaccination and prophylaxis.

In summary, the prognosis of CM‑HUS depends on the severity of initial kidney injury and the speed of effective complement inhibition. Follow‑up must be proactive, with frequent laboratory and clinical surveillance early after recovery and patient education focused on infection vigilance while on complement inhibitors. Preventive measures for animal bites, high clinical suspicion for TMA in the setting of sepsis, and coordinated, timely sampling before therapeutic interventions are practical steps that improve outcomes for future patients.
