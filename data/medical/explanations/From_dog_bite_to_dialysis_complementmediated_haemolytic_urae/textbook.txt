Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

## Initial presentation: Capnocytophaga sepsis with DIC and multiorgan involvement

This patient’s illness began with a common exposure—an uncomplicated dog bite to the lip—followed by rapid clinical deterioration over four days. The chronology is important: although a bite had occurred and a penicillin prescription was issued, the patient did not start antibiotics. Within days she developed high fever, profound malaise, hypoglycaemia refractory to usual measures and then signs of peripheral ischaemia (numbness and discoloration of the feet). On arrival to hospital the overall picture was that of fulminant sepsis with early multiorgan dysfunction: metabolic acidosis with hyperlactataemia, marked inflammatory response, pancytopenia, liver enzyme abnormalities, coagulopathy and evolving acute kidney injury (AKI). This constellation motivated an immediate working diagnosis of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) related to severe bacteraemia, and dictated prompt empiric management.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus: exposure, virulence and diagnostic implications
Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a gram‑negative, capnophilic rod that colonises the oral mucosa of dogs and cats. Transmission to humans most often follows bites or scratches, but can also occur with close contact. The organism expresses a polysaccharide capsule in some strains and has mechanisms to blunt innate immune responses (including interference with phagocytosis and complement), which can allow rapid systemic spread in susceptible hosts. Clinically the key points are that disease onset can be rapid and severe even in previously healthy adults, and that the organism is fastidious—growing slowly in culture and sometimes requiring prolonged incubation or molecular methods for detection. Because of the risk of delayed microbiological confirmation, clinicians must rely on exposure history and presentational features to guide early empiric therapy.

Sepsis physiology observed in this case
Severe infection produces an inflammatory and metabolic milieu that explains many of the patient’s early abnormalities. Tissue hypoperfusion and mitochondrial dysfunction cause elevated lactate and metabolic acidosis; her lactate of 5.7 mmol/L with pH 7.27 reflected the severity of systemic derangement. Refractory hypoglycaemia in sepsis can result from a combination of impaired hepatic gluconeogenesis (sepsis-associated liver dysfunction), increased peripheral glucose consumption by activated immune cells and bacteria, and adrenal dysfunction or relative cortisol insufficiency in some patients—features compatible with this patient’s course. Early AKI is common in septic patients because of hypotension, renal hypoperfusion, microvascular thrombosis and direct inflammatory injury.

Laboratory pattern: pancytopenia, coagulopathy, AKI and hepatic involvement
On presentation the patient had pancytopenia (leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, anaemia or evolving anaemia), deranged coagulation and elevated liver enzymes alongside AKI. Such a pattern in sepsis can reflect bone marrow suppression or peripheral consumption (as in DIC), hepatic synthetic failure contributing to coagulopathy, and direct or indirect renal injury. Pancytopenia raised the immediate differential of bone‑marrow processes (for example acute leukaemia or haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis), but in the acute septic context marrow suppression from overwhelming infection is also well described and was reflected in the haematology workup that ultimately did not show malignant blasts.

DIC versus severe sepsis with microthrombosis
The patient’s mottled and cool extremities, later progressing to diffuse skin bleeding and distal cyanosis, are characteristic of microvascular thrombotic phenomena seen in DIC. In DIC the systemic activation of coagulation leads to fibrin deposition in the microcirculation (causing ischaemia and mottling), consumption of platelets and coagulation factors (predisposing to bleeding), and fibrinolytic activation. Clinically this may produce a mixed haemorrhagic and ischaemic picture—exactly what occurred here. Preservation of peripheral pulses and sensation argued against large‑vessel occlusion, and targeted clinical and radiologic assessment appropriately ruled out necrotising soft‑tissue infection as the cause of the peripheral changes.

Empiric antimicrobial strategy and its evolution
Given the bite exposure and rapid deterioration, empiric broad‑spectrum coverage was started with piperacillin/tazobactam. This antibiotic provides reliable activity against most dog‑associated pathogens (including Capnocytophaga, Pasteurella, anaerobes and common Gram‑negatives), and is an appropriate initial choice when a severe zoonotic infection is suspected but culture results are pending. Because C. canimorsus is typically susceptible to beta‑lactams, culture confirmation later allowed de‑escalation to narrower agents (ampicillin/penicillin), which reduces collateral effects and the risk of selecting resistant organisms. The delay in starting any antibiotic at the time of the bite (the patient had not collected her outpatient prescription) likely contributed to unchecked bacterial proliferation and systemic dissemination.

ICU course: renal failure, septic cardiomyopathy and initiation of renal replacement therapy
Shortly after ICU transfer she became anuric and developed progressive azotaemia despite aggressive fluid resuscitation and diuretic trials. Septic cardiomyopathy—reversible myocardial depression seen in severe sepsis—contributed to poor cardiac output and fluid overload. With oliguria/anuria, fluid accumulation and escalating metabolic disturbance, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was started. CRRT is often preferred in hemodynamically unstable septic patients because it provides gentle, continuous solute and fluid removal, which is better tolerated than intermittent haemodialysis in this setting.

Early differential diagnoses and targeted investigations
Because pancytopenia and coagulopathy can reflect several primary haematologic or immune disorders, the team considered and investigated acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). Flow cytometry and plans for bone marrow biopsy were reasonable steps to exclude an underlying marrow process; these studies returned without evidence of blasts or a malignant phenotype, making a primary haematologic cause less likely. Necrotising fasciitis was an early surgical concern given the mottled extremities, but was excluded clinically and radiologically. Given the multisystem inflammatory presentation, autoimmune vasculitis was also considered and screened for (ANA and ANCA), which were negative—again supporting an infectious cause for the coagulopathy and organ dysfunction.

Supportive care and haemostatic management
Supportive measures are central in severe sepsis with DIC. The patient received blood products (platelet transfusions and fibrinogen replacement) guided by ongoing bleeding risk and laboratory parameters, and albumin infusions to help with intravascular volume and oncotic support. Stress‑dose corticosteroids were given during the initial septic phase, consistent with practice for patients with refractory shock or suspected relative adrenal insufficiency; importantly, beyond this she did not require prolonged high‑dose vasopressors. Careful hemodynamic monitoring, timely transfusion support and early renal replacement therapy were essential to stabilise physiological derangements while antimicrobials targeted the underlying infection.

Microbiological confirmation and implications for management
Because C. canimorsus is slow‑growing and fastidious, blood cultures may take several days to become positive; in this case aerobic and anaerobic cultures confirmed the organism about seven days after presentation. This delay underscores the need to base early therapy on exposure history and clinical syndrome rather than waiting for culture confirmation. When cultures later identified C. canimorsus and susceptibility was consistent with beta‑lactam therapy, targeted de‑escalation was appropriate and allowed continuation of effective therapy while minimising broad‑spectrum antibiotic exposure.

In summary, the initial phase of this case illustrates how an untreated dog bite can progress to fulminant C. canimorsus sepsis with DIC and multiorgan failure. Rapid recognition of the exposure, empiric broad antimicrobial coverage, aggressive supportive care (including blood products and CRRT) and focused early investigations to exclude alternative diagnoses framed the subsequent diagnostic and therapeutic pathway.

## Turning point: recognition of thrombotic microangiopathy after initial sepsis improvement

The clinical turning point in this case occurred on the nephrology ward when the patient—after apparent recovery from the acute septic/DIC phase—suddenly developed a marked drop in haemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days without any evidence of bleeding, while thrombocytopaenia persisted (platelets 30–40 × 10^9/L). This isolated worsening of anaemia in the absence of hemodynamic instability or an obvious bleeding source prompted reconsideration of the working diagnosis and raised concern for a microangiopathic haemolytic process rather than ongoing pure consumptive coagulopathy.

Laboratory evidence supported frank MAHA. Haptoglobin became undetectable and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) rose—both sensitive markers of intravascular haemolysis. The peripheral blood smear showed 15–20% schistocytes, a very high fraction that is characteristic of severe red cell fragmentation. In practice, even a schistocyte count >1% is considered abnormal and suggestive of MAHA; levels as high as seen here indicate a substantial microangiopathic process. Together with the rising LDH and low haptoglobin, these findings established ongoing intravascular haemolysis rather than anaemia of chronic disease or simple blood loss.

An important early diagnostic pitfall was the initially positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG, which suggested autoimmune haemolytic anaemia and prompted a trial of high‑dose corticosteroids. Several factors argue that this DAT was a false positive in this patient: recent exposure to penicillin (which can cause drug‑dependent DAT positivity), multiple blood transfusions, and lack of any clinical or laboratory response to steroids. The DAT subsequently became negative. This sequence—positive DAT followed by no steroid response and then a negative repeat DAT—is a well‑recognised pattern when penicillin‑related or transfusion‑related antibodies transiently coat erythrocytes, and it should raise suspicion that haemolysis is non‑immune and possibly microangiopathic.

Distinguishing residual or evolving TMA from DIC was central to clinical reasoning. During the sepsis phase the patient had coagulopathy and features compatible with DIC (prolonged clotting times, low fibrinogen, bleeding). By the time the haemoglobin fell, conventional coagulation parameters had normalised and fibrinogen/INR returned toward normal, yet the haemolysis and thrombocytopaenia persisted. In DIC, ongoing coagulopathy with consumption of clotting factors typically accompanies continued bleeding and laboratory evidence of deranged coagulation; persistent intravascular haemolysis and marked schistocytosis despite correction of coagulation studies point away from pure DIC and toward a TMA. Concurrent measurement of ADAMTS13 activity helps separate TTP from other TMAs: this patient’s ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5% (well above the <10% cutoff for TTP), making thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura unlikely and increasing the pretest probability that this was a complement‑mediated or infection‑triggered TMA.

Timing and safety considerations determined when renal biopsy was performed. A native kidney biopsy can provide diagnostic confirmation of TMA, but bleeding risk must be minimised—most centres aim for a platelet count >50 × 10^9/L (and correction of other coagulation abnormalities) before performing an elective biopsy. In this case the team instituted bridging plasma exchange and supportive care until the platelet count recovered to 77 × 10^9/L, at which point biopsy was undertaken. This approach balances the need for histopathology to guide disease‑specific therapy (for example, complement blockade) against procedural haemorrhagic risk.

The renal biopsy findings were diagnostic of a thrombotic microangiopathy: severe acute tubular necrosis from ischaemia, glomeruli with ischaemic collapse containing fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation. Immunofluorescence showed no immune‑complex deposition and electron microscopy revealed no features of a primary immune‑complex glomerulonephritis—findings that favour a TMA process (such as complement‑mediated HUS) rather than an immune‑complex GN. The presence of fibrin thrombi within glomerular capillaries and arterioles is a hallmark of TMA and, together with the clinical and laboratory picture, supported the diagnosis of CM‑HUS in this patient.

Clinical lesson: in a septic patient whose coagulation parameters have normalised but who develops new or worsening intravascular haemolysis (falling haemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH) with schistocytosis and persistent thrombocytopaenia, clinicians should promptly consider superimposed TMA. Early targeted workup—peripheral smear, DAT (interpreted cautiously), ADAMTS13, Shiga‑toxin testing, and timely renal biopsy when safe—can distinguish TMA subtypes and expedite disease‑specific therapy (for example, plasma exchange bridging and complement blockade), which materially affects outcome.

## Pathophysiology of complement-mediated HUS and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS) is driven by dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway on vascular endothelium, most importantly within the renal microcirculation. The alternative pathway is constitutively active at a low level through a process termed “tick‑over,” in which spontaneous hydrolysis of C3 generates C3(H2O) that can bind factor B. Factor D then cleaves factor B to form a fluid‑phase C3 convertase that produces C3b. Surface‑bound C3b associates with factor B to form the membrane‑bound C3 convertase (C3bBb), which is stabilized by properdin and amplifies C3 cleavage in a positive feedback loop. When formed in sufficient quantity this amplification cascade generates C5 convertase activity, leading to C5 cleavage and formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b‑9). Local generation of C3b and MAC on endothelial surfaces promotes opsonisation, inflammation and direct cell injury—setting the stage for the microvascular thrombosis that characterises TMA.

Healthy tissue is protected from this potentially destructive amplification by several regulatory checkpoints. Complement factor H (CFH) is the principal soluble regulator of the alternative pathway: it binds surface‑deposited C3b, competes with factor B for C3b binding, accelerates decay of C3 convertases and serves as a cofactor for factor I (CFI)–mediated proteolytic inactivation of C3b to iC3b. Factor I itself is the serine protease that degrades C3b (and C4b) when appropriate cofactors are present. On cell surfaces membrane‑bound regulators such as membrane cofactor protein (MCP, CD46), decay‑accelerating factor (CD55) and CD59 (which inhibits insertion of MAC) provide local protection from complement‑mediated damage. Loss of function in any of these checkpoints permits unchecked C3 convertase activity, excessive C3b deposition and downstream MAC formation on endothelial cells, which triggers platelet adhesion, coagulation activation and the microvascular platelet‑rich thrombi seen in HUS.

The underlying predisposition to this dysregulation may be genetic or acquired. Pathogenic variants in genes encoding CFH, CFI, CFB, C3 and MCP are identified in a substantial proportion of patients with CM‑HUS (estimates vary by cohort but often cited in the range of 40–60%), and anti‑CFH autoantibodies are an important acquired mechanism particularly in children. Importantly, penetrance of these abnormalities is incomplete: many carriers of complement gene variants never develop disease unless a precipitating event occurs. Because of this, CM‑HUS is best understood as a two‑hit process in which an inherited or acquired impairment of complement regulation lowers the threshold for complement‑mediated endothelial injury, and a second hit triggers overt TMA.

Infections are among the commonest and most important clinical triggers for that second hit. Any process that activates systemic complement, injures endothelium or provokes intense inflammation—severe bacterial sepsis, viral infections, pregnancy, autoimmune flares or certain drugs—can overwhelm compromised regulatory capacity. Infections both increase complement activation (through pathogen‑associated molecular patterns such as lipopolysaccharide) and sensitize the endothelium (via cytokines, ischemia or direct microbial toxins), thereby promoting local complement deposition, endothelial swelling, exposure of procoagulant surfaces and the microthrombi that fragment red cells (producing schistocytes) and consume platelets.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an example of an infectious trigger that illustrates the double‑edged role of complement. As an encapsulated gram‑negative organism present in dog and cat oral flora, C. canimorsus is variably susceptible to complement‑mediated killing. Strains that lack an effective capsule are typically complement sensitive and cleared by circulating complement, whereas capsulated isolates can resist complement deposition and survive in blood. Even for complement‑sensitive strains, systemic infection provokes robust complement activation: pathogen recognition and C3b deposition are central to host defence, but excessive or misdirected activation at the endothelial surface can initiate the cascade toward TMA in a susceptible host. Thus the same complement activity that contributes to bacterial clearance can, under conditions of regulatory failure or intense inflammatory activation, produce injurious local complement deposition, endothelial activation and the thrombotic microangiopathy observed in CM‑HUS.

These mechanistic features explain several practical diagnostic and therapeutic points illustrated by the present case. Standard serum complement measures (C3 and C4) can be normal in CM‑HUS because complement activation may be focal (at the microvascular endothelium) rather than reflected by steady‑state depletion of plasma components; additionally, C3 levels may be replenished or not fall sufficiently to exit reference ranges. Timing and prior interventions also affect results: plasma exchange replaces complement components and eculizumab blocks terminal cleavage, both altering laboratory assays. Functional pathway assays (eg, AH50 for the alternative pathway or terminal pathway activity) can be more informative but are likewise sensitive to recent treatments. Recognition of these limitations is important when interpreting complement tests in a patient with sepsis and suspected TMA.

Finally, understanding this pathophysiology underpins treatment rationale. Targeting the terminal pathway with anti‑C5 therapy (eculizumab) prevents MAC formation and downstream endothelial injury, addressing the proximate mediator of TMA, whereas plasma exchange may transiently remove activating fragments or autoantibodies but does not correct intrinsic regulatory defects. Because terminal complement activity is also important for defence against encapsulated organisms, clinicians must weigh the timing of complement blockade against infection control and employ vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis when initiating therapy—precautions that reflect the same biology linking infection, complement and endothelial injury in CM‑HUS.

## From differential to diagnosis: excluding mimics and confirming CM-HUS

When confronted with a patient who develops microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia and acute kidney injury (AKI) in the setting of recent sepsis, a stepwise, evidence-based approach is required to exclude mimics and to assemble the findings that support complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). The following explains the reasoning used in this case and the general principles that underpin it.

Prioritizing TTP and the role of ADAMTS13
The immediate diagnostic priority in adults with MAHA and thrombocytopenia is to exclude thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), because TTP requires urgent plasma exchange. Clinical triage scores (for example the PLASMIC score) are helpful to estimate pretest probability while awaiting ADAMTS13 testing. A very low ADAMTS13 activity (<10%) is the biochemical hallmark of TTP. In this patient the PLASMIC score was low and the measured ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, effectively excluding TTP as the cause of her TMA and allowing teams to stop treating for TTP specifically. Because ADAMTS13 assays take time, many centres will start plasma exchange empirically when suspicion for TTP is high; in this case plasma exchange was also used as a temporizing intervention while the diagnosis was clarified.

Excluding STEC‑HUS
Shiga toxin–producing E. coli (STEC) is the prototypical infectious trigger of typical HUS and is usually associated with a diarrhoeal prodrome. Absence of an enteric illness and a negative stool PCR for Shiga toxin in this patient made STEC‑HUS unlikely. In practice, stool testing for Shiga toxins or E. coli O157 is standard whenever the history or local epidemiology suggests a possibility of STEC.

Considering other secondary TMAs
A number of secondary conditions can produce a TMA phenotype (malignant hypertension, drugs/toxins, pregnancy/postpartum, autoimmune disorders, haematopoietic or solid‑organ transplantation, certain infections). Decreasing the likelihood of these alternatives relies on history, examination and targeted testing. In this case there was no pregnancy or postpartum state, no exposure to implicated drugs, no transplant history, blood pressures were not consistent with malignant‑hypertensive TMA, and autoimmune screening (ANA, ANCA) was negative—together making these secondary causes improbable contributors to her evolving TMA.

Distinguishing sepsis‑associated DIC from a superimposed TMA
Differentiating disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) from a TMA can be challenging because both may occur in sepsis and both can produce thrombocytopenia and organ dysfunction. Useful discriminators are the coagulation profile and the dominant pathology: DIC typically shows prolonged clotting times, low fibrinogen and very high D‑dimer from consumptive coagulopathy, whereas TMA is characterized by predominant microangiopathic haemolysis (schistocytes, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin), preserved or less severely deranged coagulation parameters, and microvascular platelet‑rich thrombi on tissue biopsy. In this patient the initial presentation was dominated by sepsis‑related DIC (abnormal coagulation parameters and clinical bleeding), but those coagulation abnormalities resolved while haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia and dialysis‑dependent AKI emerged. The kidney biopsy documenting fibrin thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli and features of TMA provided pathological confirmation that a thrombotic microangiopathy was present in the kidney beyond the initial consumptive coagulopathy.

Laboratory evaluation of complement and its limitations
Evaluation for complement dysregulation is central when CM‑HUS is suspected, but available assays have important limitations. Serum C3 and C4 concentrations are often obtained first; low C3 may support alternative pathway activation but normal levels do not exclude CM‑HUS. Functional pathway assays (classical and alternative pathway haemolytic assays such as CH50/AH50) assess overall pathway activity and can indicate functional deficiency or blockade. Tests for specific regulators (factor H, factor I, factor B levels) and anti‑factor H autoantibodies are useful when available. In this patient C3/C4 were within reference ranges—their normality does not argue against CM‑HUS. The AH50 (alternative pathway functional assay) was markedly reduced, but interpretation required caution because timing relative to therapies was confounding (see below).

How treatments alter complement testing: timing confounders
Therapeutic interventions change complement test results. Plasma exchange (PEX) removes complement proteins, regulatory factors and autoantibodies, producing transient alterations in quantitative and functional assays. Eculizumab specifically blocks terminal complement (C5), leading to very low CH50 and often low AH50 results despite ongoing alternative pathway activation upstream. Therefore, assays performed after PEX or after initiation of eculizumab are difficult to interpret as evidence of prior complement activation. In this case extended complement testing was performed after several PEX sessions and after the first eculizumab dose; thus the low AH50 most likely reflects treatment effect rather than proving intrinsic complement depletion or primary activation. This caveat underscores the value of obtaining baseline complement studies and complement samples for specialized testing before PEX or complement blockade whenever patient safety permits.

Genetic and antibody testing: implications of negative results
Genetic testing for known complement regulatory gene variants (CFH, CFI, CFB, CD46/MCP, CFHRs, C3, etc.) and testing for anti‑factor H autoantibodies are part of the extended workup for CM‑HUS. A pathogenic variant or autoantibody supports a predisposition to complement dysregulation and often influences prognosis and the decision to continue long‑term complement blockade. However, only a proportion of patients (roughly 50–70%) harbour identifiable abnormalities, and a negative genetic panel does not exclude CM‑HUS. In this patient no pathogenic variants were identified; that reduces the likelihood of a lifelong genetic predisposition but does not negate the diagnosis of infection‑triggered CM‑HUS. Decisions about duration of eculizumab are individualized and take into account clinical severity, renal recovery, relapse risk and genetic findings.

Clinicopathologic synthesis that supported CM‑HUS
Diagnosis of CM‑HUS is ultimately a clinicopathologic synthesis of exclusion: severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and STEC infection were ruled out; other secondary causes were improbable; DIC improved while MAHA and kidney‑predominant TMA persisted; kidney biopsy showed microvascular thrombosis consistent with TMA; and the haematologic and renal course (transient improvement with PEX followed by sustained recovery after complement blockade) fit complement‑mediated disease. Additional practical points illustrated by the case include recognizing drug‑ or transfusion‑related false‑positive direct antiglobulin tests, and the value of peripheral smear review (schistocytes) in shifting the diagnostic impression toward MAHA/TMA.

Key takeaways for clinical practice
- Use a staged approach: rapidly exclude TTP with ADAMTS13 (and pretest stratification scores), test for STEC when appropriate, and consider common secondary causes by history and targeted testing.  
- Distinguish DIC and sepsis‑related coagulopathy from TMA by following the evolution of coagulation tests, markers of haemolysis, and by using kidney biopsy when the diagnosis remains uncertain.  
- Obtain baseline complement samples before PEX or eculizumab if possible; interpret post‑treatment complement assays cautiously.  
- A negative complement genetic panel does not exclude CM‑HUS; it informs but does not solely determine prognosis or treatment duration.  
- Integrate clinical course, laboratory trends, histopathology and therapeutic response to arrive at the working diagnosis; in this patient, that synthesis supported infection‑triggered CM‑HUS confirmed by pathology and clinical response to complement inhibition.

## Therapeutic strategy in infection-triggered CM-HUS: sequencing PEX, eculizumab, and supportive care

Managing a thrombotic microangiopathy that emerges during or after sepsis requires coordinated decisions about temporizing therapies, definitive complement blockade and organ support, all while minimising infectious risk. The treatment course in this case illustrates common practical principles.

Plasma exchange as a temporising bridge
Plasma exchange (PEX) is rapidly life‑saving in suspected thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) because it removes autoantibodies against ADAMTS13 and replenishes ADAMTS13 activity. In contrast, PEX only transiently benefits complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS): it can remove circulating complement activation products, inflammatory mediators or anti‑factor H antibodies and provide short‑term haemostatic improvement, but it does not correct the underlying dysregulation. Therefore, when a patient presents with MAHA and severe thrombocytopenia before ADAMTS13 results are available, prompt initiation of PEX is appropriate as a bridge. In the present case PEX was started while awaiting ADAMTS13 and complement results; this approach correctly prioritised immediate risk, and PEX partly stabilised the patient (diuresis began to return) although sustained remission required complement blockade. Remember that PEX can confound complement and serologic testing (and remove recently administered monoclonal antibodies), so baseline blood for ADAMTS13, complement functional assays and autoantibodies should be drawn before PEX whenever feasible.

Eculizumab — mechanism, timing and dosing used here
Eculizumab is a monoclonal antibody that blocks terminal complement component C5, preventing formation of the membrane attack complex and interrupting the endothelial injury cascade central to CM‑HUS. Early initiation is associated with better renal recovery. However, because the terminal complement pathway is crucial for defence against encapsulated organisms, timing relative to active infection is critical. In this case the treating team waited until blood cultures were negative and the acute bacteremic phase had abated before initiating eculizumab, reducing the risk of worsening uncontrolled infection.

The induction and maintenance regimen used in this patient was standard for CM‑HUS: induction with eculizumab 900 mg intravenously once weekly for four weeks, followed by maintenance dosing of 1200 mg every two weeks. Individual dosing schedules may vary by indication and local protocols, and treatment duration should be re‑assessed periodically based on clinical course, renal recovery and genetic testing results.

Vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis
Because eculizumab markedly increases susceptibility to meningococcal disease (and may increase risk from other encapsulated bacteria), vaccination against meningococci is recommended ideally at least two weeks before starting complement blockade. Both quadrivalent conjugate vaccines covering A/C/W/Y (e.g., MenACWY) and serogroup B vaccines (e.g., MenB) are recommended. If immediate treatment is necessary and pre‑vaccination is not possible, give the vaccines as soon as feasible and provide antibiotic prophylaxis until two weeks after vaccination. In the reported case the first doses of MenACWY (Menveo) and MenB (Bexsero) were administered immediately prior to starting eculizumab and phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was used as prophylaxis while the vaccine response matured. Counseling should emphasise the need for rapid medical attention for any febrile illness during complement blockade and long‑term vaccination/antibiotic strategies if therapy is continued.

Renal support: indications and monitoring for recovery
Acute kidney injury from CM‑HUS is often severe. The indications for initiating continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) in the critically ill include refractory volume overload, severe acidosis or electrolyte disturbance, and anuria with rising urea/creatinine in the context of haemodynamic instability. In this patient CRRT was started for anuria, fluid overload and septic cardiomyopathy. Close monitoring of urine output and creatinine trends is essential: recovery of diuresis and downward creatinine trajectory are the earliest signs of renal improvement. In CM‑HUS, earlier initiation of eculizumab improves chances for dialysis independence; in this case diuresis returned after PEX and then consolidated after eculizumab, yielding partial renal recovery but ongoing reduced function. Decisions about continuing outpatient dialysis versus expectant recovery depend on serial renal function measurements over weeks to months.

Transfusion strategy in TMA overlapping with DIC
Transfusion decisions must balance bleeding risk from thrombocytopenia/coagulopathy against the prothrombotic milieu of TMA. Red blood cell transfusion is indicated for symptomatic anaemia or haemodynamic instability; in this case transfusions were given for a rapid haemoglobin fall. Platelet transfusions are generally avoided in TMA because they can fuel microthrombus formation, unless there is life‑threatening bleeding or the patient requires an invasive procedure. In DIC with active bleeding or profound coagulopathy, platelets and fibrinogen may be given; this patient received supportive platelet and fibrinogen transfusions early while the diagnosis was uncertain. These interventions should be individualised and frequently re‑evaluated as the diagnostic picture clarifies.

Monitoring response and adjusting therapy
Clinical and laboratory markers guide response assessment. Haematological remission is reflected by rising platelet count, falling lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), rising haptoglobin, stabilisation of haemoglobin, and decreasing schistocyte burden on blood film. Renal response is judged by increasing urine output and falling creatinine/urea. In this case, platelets and LDH were monitored closely and improved after PEX and eculizumab. Steroids that were started empirically for a presumed autoimmune haemolytic anaemia should be tapered once AIHA is excluded (negative repeat direct antiglobulin test and predominance of schistocytes), to avoid unnecessary immunosuppression during infection or while on complement blockade.

Risk–benefit considerations and mitigation when blocking complement during or after infection
Initiating complement blockade in the setting of recent or ongoing bacteraemia — particularly with encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria or Capnocytophaga — presents a clear risk. The benefit is stopping ongoing endothelial injury and preventing irreversible renal damage; the hazard is rendering the patient unable to clear certain pathogens. Mitigation strategies include: ensuring bloodstream infection is controlled (negative cultures) before eculizumab when possible; administering meningococcal (MenACWY and MenB) vaccines at least two weeks prior when feasible; providing prophylactic antibiotics if vaccination cannot be completed two weeks before treatment; close clinical monitoring and low threshold for empirical antibiotics if fever develops; and individualising timing of therapy when the patient’s TMA is life‑threatening. In this case the team balanced these considerations by awaiting negative cultures, vaccinating, administering prophylactic penicillin, and starting eculizumab promptly once infection control permitted.

In summary, management of infection‑triggered CM‑HUS often requires an initial pragmatic use of PEX while excluding TTP, rapid initiation of eculizumab once the infectious risk is mitigated, aggressive organ support (including CRRT when indicated), cautious transfusion practices, and vigilant infection prevention and monitoring during complement blockade. Timing of interventions, collection of baseline diagnostic samples prior to PEX, and clear communication about infection risks are key operational points illustrated by this case.

## Outcomes, prognosis, and clinical lessons

Clinical outcome and trajectory in this patient were encouraging but incomplete. After a turbulent ICU course with DIC and dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury, the patient experienced return of spontaneous diuresis following plasma exchange and, more durably, haematologic remission after initiation of eculizumab. At follow‑up she was dialysis independent with partial renal recovery (serum creatinine ~175 µmol/L), reflecting meaningful but not full restoration of kidney function. This trajectory—haematologic control followed by partial but persistent renal impairment—is typical of severe complement‑mediated HUS when therapy is started after a period of established microvascular injury.

Eculizumab produced durable haematologic remission in this case, and maintenance dosing was continued with a plan to reassess for discontinuation once renal function stabilises. The team’s approach—continuing complement blockade short term because of the severity of presentation, then reconsidering duration in light of genetic results and clinical course—is a practical strategy commonly used in CM‑HUS. Long‑term therapy is most strongly favoured when a pathogenic complement variant or anti‑factor H antibodies are identified, whereas absence of such findings reduces (but does not eliminate) the perceived need for indefinite treatment. In this patient, extended genetic testing did not reveal pathogenic variants; that lowers the presumptive lifetime relapse risk but does not guarantee against recurrence, so surveillance and individualized weighing of relapse risk versus infection risk from continued complement inhibition guided the planned reassessment.

Timing of complement blockade matters. Early and targeted complement inhibition tends to be associated with improved renal outcomes because it halts ongoing endothelial injury and microthrombus formation before irreversible parenchymal loss occurs. In contrast, delays in recognizing CM‑HUS or in initiating eculizumab increase the probability of incomplete renal recovery and chronic kidney disease. This case illustrates that diagnostic uncertainty in the setting of sepsis and DIC can postpone recognition of a superimposed TMA; clinicians should therefore remain alert for a “second hit” when haemolysis or schistocytosis appears as the infectious picture improves.

Interpreting negative genetic testing requires nuance when planning prognosis and follow‑up. A negative extended complement panel lowers the likelihood of an inherited complement dysregulation as the major predisposing factor, but does not preclude a complement‑mediated process triggered by infection or transient autoantibodies. Decisions about the duration of eculizumab should therefore integrate: the severity of the index episode (degree of renal injury and need for dialysis), speed and completeness of recovery after complement blockade, absence or presence of pathogenic variants/autoantibodies, and patient preferences. Even without a genetic cause, a severe episode like the one described justifies a period of maintenance therapy and close outpatient surveillance for relapse after discontinuation is attempted.

There are several practical clinical pearls illustrated by this case. First, consider CM‑HUS when new or isolated haemolysis and thrombocytopenia emerge as sepsis appears to resolve—this temporal pattern (haemolysis arising while inflammatory markers fall) is an important clue that a TMA may be superimposed. Second, routine complement measures (C3, C4) can be normal in CM‑HUS and therefore do not exclude the diagnosis; normal levels should not delay further evaluation when the clinical picture fits. Third, a peripheral blood smear is a high‑yield, inexpensive test: identification of schistocytes is pivotal to recognize MAHA and prompt a TMA workup. Fourth, kidney biopsy can be diagnostic when it is safe to perform: the finding of ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and thrombotic microangiopathic changes supported the diagnosis here and helped exclude immune‑complex glomerulopathy, but biopsy should be balanced against bleeding risk and performed selectively.

Broader implications from this case include awareness that uncommon pathogens such as Capnocytophaga canimorsus may act as triggers for complement‑mediated TMA. Early clinical suspicion of such organisms—prompted by exposure history (dog bite) and by using molecular or targeted culture techniques for fastidious bacteria—can shorten the interval to appropriate antimicrobial therapy and help time decisions about complement inhibition. Finally, initiating complement blockade raises specific infection‑prevention issues: vaccination against encapsulated organisms (meningococcal serogroups and others as indicated), appropriate timing of immunization relative to therapy, and consideration of short‑term antibiotic prophylaxis when vaccines cannot be given ≥2 weeks before eculizumab. These measures were applied in this patient once blood cultures were negative, reflecting the necessary balance between treating a life‑threatening TMA and minimising infectious risk from terminal complement inhibition.

In sum, the case underscores three actionable lessons: maintain a high index of suspicion for CM‑HUS when haemolysis arises in the recovery phase of sepsis; use peripheral smear, ADAMTS13, stool PCR (to exclude STEC) and, when safe, renal biopsy to define the diagnosis rapidly; and, once TTP and STEC‑HUS are excluded or unlikely, consider early complement blockade while implementing vaccine and prophylactic strategies to mitigate infectious risks.
