Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Chapter 2: ## The Case: Dog-Bite Sepsis, DIC, and Early Organ Failure

Four days after a seemingly minor dog bite, this previously well woman presented with an overwhelmingly septic picture: high fever (>40 °C), profound malaise, refractory hypoglycaemia (nadir 0.6 mmol/L), mottled cool extremities and rapidly evolving multiorgan dysfunction. Her initial laboratory panel reflected the severity of the systemic insult — marked inflammatory response, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, metabolic acidosis (pH 7.27) with an elevated lactate (5.7 mmol/L), and rising creatinine with proteinuria and haematuria. This constellation pointed to fulminant sepsis complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and early acute kidney injury (AKI), and rightly prompted urgent, parallel priorities: identify the pathogen, control any surgical nidus, and stabilise organ function.

The first critical step was obtaining blood cultures and beginning broad empiric antimicrobial therapy directed at the typical pathogens of dog bites. Capnocytophaga canimorsus, Pasteurella species, staphylococci/streptococci and anaerobes are all relevant; piperacillin–tazobactam is a reasonable initial choice because it covers gram‑negatives and anaerobes while providing robust empirical coverage for severe bite-related infections. In this case, C. canimorsus was suspected from the outset and later confirmed, but diagnosis was delayed by the organism’s slow, fastidious growth — a practical reminder that negative early cultures do not exclude a clinically significant zoonotic pathogen and that molecular methods (PCR or sequencing) can be useful when cultures remain negative or growth is delayed.

Concurrently, clinicians must rapidly assess for a surgically remediable source. Dog-bite injuries can be complicated by deep soft‑tissue infection or necrotising fasciitis; prompt surgical evaluation and imaging were used here to exclude that possibility. With no surgical target identified, focus turned to medical resuscitation and support.

Management in the intensive care unit emphasised careful haemodynamic and organ support. Initial fluid resuscitation was required to treat distributive shock and maintain perfusion, but was titrated with awareness of the developing cardiorenal compromise: the patient developed septic cardiomyopathy with impaired cardiac function and progressive volume overload. Because she became anuric despite diuretics and fluids, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was started both to replace renal function and to provide precise volume control in the face of cardiac dysfunction. This sequence—aggressive resuscitation followed by early recognition of fluid intolerance and timely CRRT—illustrates the balance needed when fluids are life‑saving but can worsen pulmonary or cardiac failure.

The coagulopathy required focused supportive measures. Her presentation was consistent with DIC — consumptive thrombocytopenia, low fibrinogen and clinical microthrombotic phenomena with ecchymoses and distal ischemic changes. Management of DIC centres on treating the underlying cause (here, sepsis) while replacing critical components when bleeding or invasive procedures demand it. Platelet transfusions and fibrinogen replacement were administered as clinically indicated to control bleeding and to allow invasive monitoring or procedures when necessary. Routine prophylactic transfusion is not indicated in DIC; thresholds should be guided by haemorrhage, planned interventions and laboratory parameters.

The profound pancytopenia prompted a parallel haematologic evaluation to exclude primary marrow processes such as acute leukaemia or haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), both of which may present with fever, cytopenias and coagulopathy. Haematology input was obtained and initial flow cytometry did not reveal blasts or aberrant populations; a planned bone‑marrow biopsy was deferred when testing did not support a primary marrow disorder. Severe infections themselves — notably C. canimorsus — can cause leukopenia and marrow suppression, underscoring the need to consider infection‑mediated marrow dysfunction in the differential.

Once blood cultures eventually grew Capnocytophaga canimorsus, antibiotics were narrowed according to susceptibility: de‑escalation to ampicillin (and later oral phenoxymethylpenicillin in this patient’s course) was appropriate because most C. canimorsus isolates are penicillin‑sensitive. The clinical approach — early broad empiric therapy, timely cultures, surgical assessment, organ‑supportive care in the ICU and targeted transfusion support for DIC — together represent the standard priorities when a patient presents with dog‑bite sepsis complicated by coagulopathy and early organ failure. Awareness of C. canimorsus’ slow growth and the value of molecular diagnostics can shorten the time to definitive identification in future similar cases.
