Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Chapter 3: ## Unmasking Superimposed TMA: Diagnostic Reasoning and Standards

The clinical turning point in this case was not the admission diagnosis of fulminant sepsis but a secondary, abrupt haematologic change: after apparent improvement from Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia and resolving coagulopathy, the patient developed a rapid, isolated fall in haemoglobin together with persistent thrombocytopenia, a rising lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and an undetectable haptoglobin. Those features are the laboratory signature of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), and they should immediately prompt reconsideration of superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Understanding how to interpret and sequence the available tests — and when to proceed to kidney biopsy — is essential because early recognition changes management and prognosis.

MAHA is a laboratory and morphological diagnosis rather than one based on a single serologic result. A falling haemoglobin with high LDH, indirect hyperbilirubinaemia, low or absent haptoglobin and an inadequate reticulocyte response together indicate ongoing intravascular haemolysis. The peripheral smear is pivotal: fragmented erythrocytes (schistocytes) provide direct evidence of mechanical red-cell destruction in the microcirculation. While reporting standards vary, even a small but reproducible increase in schistocytes is important; in this patient a marked 15–20% schistocytosis left little doubt that MAHA was present. Importantly, a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT/Coombs) can mislead. Drugs such as penicillin and recent transfusions commonly produce a transient IgG‑positive DAT without true autoimmune haemolysis. In this case the initial IgG-positive DAT was likely drug- or transfusion-related and converted to negative on repeat testing. Clinicians must avoid anchoring to a single positive DAT when the clinical and morphological picture supports MAHA/TMA.

Once MAHA and thrombocytopenia are documented, rapid exclusion of treatable and time‑sensitive causes is the immediate priority. Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is the critical diagnosis to rule out because it is medical emergency requiring urgent plasma exchange (PEX). Clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score provide useful pretest probability while awaiting ADAMTS13 activity; a low score reduces the likelihood of TTP but cannot definitively exclude it. Definitive laboratory diagnosis rests on ADAMTS13 activity: severe deficiency (<10%) is consistent with TTP, whereas values well above that threshold (for example, 43.5% as in this patient) make TTP unlikely. Because ADAMTS13 assays often take time to return, empirical PEX should be started when TTP cannot be confidently excluded based on clinical assessment and scoring, because delays in PEX in true TTP lead to poor outcomes. It is also important to recognize that PEX is a temporizing measure in complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS) — it may transiently remove mediators or autoantibodies and can stabilise the patient, but it does not target the underlying complement dysregulation.

Shiga-toxin–associated HUS (STEC-HUS) is another consideration when MAHA and AKI coexist. Stool PCR for Shiga toxin and a compatible diarrhoeal prodrome help exclude this diagnosis. In our patient the absence of an enteric prodrome and negative stool PCR argued strongly against STEC-HUS.

Because CM-HUS is a diagnosis of exclusion, complement testing and genetic evaluation are important parts of the workup, but their interpretation requires nuance. Routine complement levels (C3 and C4) are often normal in CM-HUS and therefore cannot be used to exclude the condition. Functional assays of complement activity (for example, AH50) may detect pathway dysfunction but are susceptible to confounding by treatments: both PEX and eculizumab alter circulating complement components and functional assays, producing results that may reflect treatment effects rather than the pre‑morbid state. Accordingly, when feasible, obtain baseline blood samples for complement assays and for ADAMTS13 before initiating PEX or eculizumab; when this is not possible, interpret post‑treatment results cautiously. Genetic testing for complement regulatory gene variants or anti‑factor H autoantibodies is useful for prognostication and to guide long‑term therapy decisions — detection of pathogenic variants increases relapse risk and supports longer duration of complement blockade — but absence of a pathogenic variant does not exclude CM-HUS, nor is genetic confirmation required to make the clinical diagnosis and start therapy.

Kidney biopsy serves a selective but decisive role when diagnostic uncertainty persists, when renal failure is severe and when histology will affect management. In TMA the renal biopsy classically shows glomerular and arteriolar endothelial swelling, capillary wall widening with intra‑luminal fibrin/platelet thrombi, mesangiolysis, and variable ischaemic changes in glomeruli and tubules; immunofluorescence is typically negative for immune complex deposition and electron microscopy does not show electron‑dense immune deposits — findings that help distinguish TMA from immune‑complex glomerulonephritides. In this patient the biopsy demonstrated ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and severe acute tubular necrosis without immune complexes, supporting the diagnosis of TMA. Biopsy timing must balance diagnostic benefit against bleeding risk: thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy should be corrected (or biopsy postponed) until safe thresholds are met, and the procedure should be undertaken only when results are likely to influence therapy.

A frequent and clinically important dilemma is distinguishing sepsis‑associated disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) from superimposed CM-HUS. Both share thrombocytopenia and microthrombi, but they diverge in several respects. DIC is a consumptive coagulopathy with prolonged prothrombin and activated partial thromboplastin times, low fibrinogen, markedly elevated D‑dimer levels and a clinical phenotype dominated by bleeding and diffuse microvascular thrombosis driven by systemic coagulation activation. In contrast, TMA is a microvascular endothelial injury syndrome with predominant MAHA (schistocytes and haemolysis) and thrombocytopenia while routine coagulation tests (PT/aPTT) are often near normal. The temporal pattern is also informative: in this patient, coagulation parameters and inflammatory markers improved as the infection was treated, but haemolysis and thrombocytopenia persisted or recurred — a pattern that favours a secondary, complement‑driven TMA rather than ongoing DIC.

Translating these principles into action: when a patient with recent sepsis develops new anaemia and persistent thrombocytopenia, first confirm MAHA with haemolysis labs and a peripheral smear. Concurrently obtain coagulation studies, blood cultures if infection is active, ADAMTS13 and baseline complement samples before any PEX or complement inhibitor is given. If TTP is possible, initiate PEX without waiting for ADAMTS13 results. If ADAMTS13 excludes TTP and STEC testing is negative, consider CM-HUS and, where appropriate, proceed with targeted therapy such as eculizumab after weighing infectious risks and vaccinating or providing prophylactic antibiotics as indicated. Reserve kidney biopsy for cases where histologic confirmation will change management or where the renal lesion requires characterization, ensuring procedural safety in the thrombocytopenic patient.

In short, unmasking a superimposed TMA requires vigilance for the laboratory hallmarks of MAHA, a structured approach to exclude TTP and STEC‑HUS quickly, an understanding of the limitations of complement testing after therapeutic interventions, and selective use of kidney biopsy to confirm TMA and exclude alternative renal pathologies. Prompt, sequential application of these diagnostic standards underpinned the correct diagnosis and appropriate therapy in this patient, and they should guide clinicians faced with similar diagnostic complexity.
