Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Chapter 5: ## Clinical Pearls, Outcomes, and Integration Into Practice

This case illustrates several practical and conceptual lessons about infection‑triggered complement‑mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS) and highlights how recognition, testing strategy and timely therapy affect outcomes.

Recognize the pattern: In adults with severe sepsis, DIC and multiorgan dysfunction, a secondary TMA can be obscured. A high‑yield clinical clue is the emergence (or persistence) of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) with schistocytes, rising lactate dehydrogenase, undetectable haptoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia accompanied by worsening or new acute kidney injury—especially when these signs appear or worsen after the initial septic picture begins to stabilise. In other words, haemolysis that develops late in the course, or does not correct with resolution of infection/DIC, should prompt an active search for TMA.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus: rare but important trigger and treatment implications: C. canimorsus is an uncommon but well‑documented trigger of fulminant sepsis and, rarely, of complement‑mediated TMA in previously healthy adults. Clinically, bites from dogs (or cats) with rapidly progressive systemic illness should raise suspicion for Capnocytophaga; empiric therapy for severe dog‑bite infections commonly includes agents active against Capnocytophaga (for example, piperacillin–tazobactam in the critically ill). For outpatient bite prophylaxis the recommended first‑line agent is amoxicillin–clavulanate. Early recognition of the infectious agent is important because active infection and bacteremia influence timing of complement blockade.

Don’t rely on C3/C4 alone to diagnose CM‑HUS: Standard serum complement levels (C3 and C4) are frequently normal in CM‑HUS and do not exclude disease. Likewise, functional pathway assays can be altered by plasma exchange or complement blockade and must be interpreted in context of recent treatments. Diagnosis is therefore clinical and exclusionary: demonstrate MAHA with schistocytes, exclude TTP (severely reduced ADAMTS13 activity <10%), exclude Shiga‑toxin HUS by stool testing, consider kidney biopsy when the diagnosis remains uncertain, and assess clinical response to targeted therapy.

Prioritise rapid differentiation from TTP and STEC‑HUS: TTP is time‑sensitive and requires immediate consideration because early plasma exchange (PEX) is lifesaving. Use clinical scores (eg, PLASMIC) to triage, obtain an ADAMTS13 level urgently, and start PEX empirically if TTP cannot be reliably excluded. STEC‑HUS should be considered when there is a compatible diarrhoeal prodrome; stool PCR for Shiga toxin is a rapid, high‑yield test.

Time matters for renal outcomes: Observational data and clinical experience indicate that earlier initiation of eculizumab (anti‑C5) in CM‑HUS is associated with better haematologic control and greater likelihood of renal recovery. PEX can provide transient benefit but does not address terminal pathway activation; use PEX while awaiting TTP exclusion or when an immune cause is suspected, but do not delay complement blockade unnecessarily when CM‑HUS is likely and infection is controlled.

Manage the infection‑therapy dilemma deliberately: Complement blockade increases susceptibility to infections with encapsulated organisms (classically Neisseria meningitidis), and terminal complement is part of defence against some Gram‑negative organisms, including Capnocytophaga. Best practice is to ensure source control and negative blood cultures prior to starting eculizumab when possible, to vaccinate against meningococci at least 2 weeks before blockade if clinically feasible, and to provide immediate antibiotic prophylaxis if vaccination timing is suboptimal. In practice, vaccination is frequently administered immediately before drug initiation (as in this case) with short‑term antibiotic prophylaxis continued until protective immunity is expected.

Multidisciplinary coordination is essential: Management decisions require input from nephrology, haematology, infectious diseases, critical care and pathology. This collaboration is key for timing of PEX, biopsy, complement testing, eculizumab initiation, vaccination strategy and antimicrobial prophylaxis.

Beware common pitfalls
- False‑positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) tests can occur after penicillin exposure or recent transfusion and can distract from an underlying MAHA; repeat testing and assessment of the peripheral smear for schistocytes are helpful.
- Complement assays (functional and component) are influenced by PEX and eculizumab; interpret values relative to treatment timing and obtain baseline samples before PEX when feasible.
- Improvement in diuresis after PEX does not prove the underlying mechanism—PEX may transiently remove circulating mediators but not correct dysregulated complement activation.

A pragmatic adult TMA workflow
1. Stabilise the patient (airway, breathing, circulation), correct life‑threatening abnormalities, and treat active infection.
2. Confirm MAHA on peripheral smear (schistocytes) and document thrombocytopenia and hemolytic markers (LDH, haptoglobin, reticulocytes).
3. Send urgent ADAMTS13 activity and assay for Shiga toxin (stool PCR). Begin PEX immediately if TTP cannot be excluded clinically.
4. Evaluate secondary causes (drugs, autoimmune disease, pregnancy, severe infection). Obtain basic complement studies (C3/C4), but do not delay therapy while awaiting results.
5. If TTP and STEC‑HUS are excluded or unlikely, and clinical suspicion for CM‑HUS is high (MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI with TMA features), discuss early eculizumab initiation. If ongoing bacteremia, strive for microbiological control before blockade; if blockade is needed urgently, ensure vaccination and provide antibiotic prophylaxis.
6. Consider kidney biopsy when the diagnosis is unclear or to define prognosis—histologic TMA supports the diagnosis and helps exclude other glomerulopathies.
7. Arrange genetics and anti‑complement antibody testing to inform prognosis and duration of therapy; collect samples prior to PEX/eculizumab when possible.

Genetic testing, prognosis and duration of therapy: Genetic variants in complement regulatory genes or autoantibodies are present in a substantial minority of CM‑HUS patients and increase relapse risk. A negative genetic panel lowers but does not eliminate risk of recurrence, particularly when an infectious trigger is identified. Genetic and antibody results should be integrated with clinical course when deciding on duration of complement blockade. Many centres tailor maintenance therapy duration to disease severity, renal recovery and genetic risk—continuing treatment for months to years in higher‑risk patients, with plans for careful discontinuation trials under close surveillance in lower‑risk cases.

Monitoring and follow‑up: During acute therapy monitor haemoglobin, platelets, schistocyte count, LDH, haptoglobin, serum creatinine, urine output and blood pressure frequently. After clinical remission, follow renal function, blood pressure and urinalysis for recurrence. Educate patients about infection risk, vaccination needs and prompt evaluation for fever.

Outcome integration from this case: In this patient, eradication of bacteremia and staged use of PEX followed by timely eculizumab led to haematologic remission and partial renal recovery with return of urine output and improvement in creatinine, although dialysis dependence persisted early on. The case underscores that complement blockade can be safely and effectively used after infection control, that kidney recovery can be partial but meaningful, and that decisions about maintenance therapy require individualized consideration of genetics, clinical course and patient preferences.

Takeaway: Maintain a low threshold to suspect TMA when haemolysis, thrombocytopenia and worsening renal function emerge or persist during or after sepsis. Rapid exclusion of TTP and STEC‑HUS, early multidisciplinary coordination and timely consideration of complement blockade—balanced against infection control and appropriate prophylaxis—are the practical steps most likely to improve outcomes in infection‑triggered CM‑HUS.
