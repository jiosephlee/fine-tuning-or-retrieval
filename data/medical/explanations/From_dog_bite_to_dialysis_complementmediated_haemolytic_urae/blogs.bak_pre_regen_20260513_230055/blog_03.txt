Title: Blog about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

# MAHA in Real Time: A Practical Algorithm to Separate TTP, STEC‑HUS, and CM‑HUS

What makes this case stick in your memory? A previously healthy woman bitten by her dog develops fulminant sepsis from Capnocytophaga canimorsus, then—after an initial septic DIC picture—she evolves new microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), persistent thrombocytopaenia and dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury (AKI). ADAMTS13 is not severely deficient, stool Shiga toxin is negative, kidney biopsy shows thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and she recovers haematologically and partially renally after plasma exchange (PEX) and eculizumab. This is a neat, high‑yield illustration of how infectious triggers can unmask complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) and why a pragmatic bedside algorithm is needed when MAHA + thrombocytopenia (± AKI) lands in front of you.

Why this is hard at the bedside
- MAHA + thrombocytopenia is a phenotype, not a diagnosis. Rapidly differentiating TTP, STEC‑HUS and CM‑HUS matters because initial therapies (PEX, antibiotics, complement blockade) differ and delays cost kidneys and lives.
- Early sepsis/DIC can obscure or coexist with TMA. Complement levels (C3/C4) are often normal in CM‑HUS and ADAMTS13 takes time. You need a practical pathway to act before every result arrives.

A pragmatic bedside pathway (what to do, step by step)
1. Recognize the phenotype
   - Any patient with anaemia + schistocytes on smear, elevated LDH, low/undetectable haptoglobin and thrombocytopenia → treat as MAHA/TMA until proven otherwise.
   - Note the context: fever, sepsis/DIC, bloody diarrhoea, pregnancy, meds, recent infection/trauma → these are important triggers but do not exclude primary TMA.

2. Immediate simultaneous actions (the minutes–hours tasks)
   - Send stat labs: CBC with smear, LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin, reticulocyte count, coagulation screen, creatinine, urinalysis, blood cultures if febrile, stool Shiga toxin PCR if diarrhoeal illness, direct antiglobulin test (DAT/Coombs).
   - Send ADAMTS13 activity and antibody (urgent send‑out if necessary).
   - Start PEX immediately if clinical suspicion for TTP is moderate–high OR if patient is unstable and you cannot rapidly exclude TTP (see PLASMIC below). Don’t wait for ADAMTS13 if TTP is plausible.
   - Start broad supportive care: transfuse packed cells for hemodynamic compromise or symptomatic anaemia, platelet transfusion only for life‑threatening bleeding or invasive procedures, manage AKI (early nephrology consult, consider CRRT), treat sepsis aggressively with appropriate antibiotics.

3. Use PLASMIC score to triage for PEX if ADAMTS13 will be delayed
   - PLASMIC variables: Platelet count <30,000; haemolysis (indirect bilirubin/reticulocytes); Absence of active cancer; Absence of solid‑organ/stem cell transplant; MCV <90; INR <1.5; Creatinine <2.0 mg/dL. Score 0–4 = low, 5 = intermediate, 6–7 = high risk of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency.
   - High score → start PEX immediately. Low score → you can usually delay PEX while awaiting ADAMTS13, provided patient is stable and being closely monitored.

4. Interpret ADAMTS13 when it returns
   - ADAMTS13 activity <10% (or <5–10% per local assay): TTP — PEX + immunosuppression (steroids ± rituximab) is indicated. Plasma exchange is lifesaving. ADAMTS13 autoantibody testing helps but don’t wait to treat.
   - ADAMTS13 activity >10–20%: TTP unlikely. Stop PEX once other diagnoses are clarified (unless PEX is helping and decision‑made with haematology).

5. Consider STEC‑HUS early if diarrhoea or exposure suggests it
   - Send stool Shiga toxin PCR/culture. If positive for STEC: supportive care is mainstay; avoid antimotility agents and some antibiotics (controversial), PEX is not routinely effective. Shiga toxin HUS commonly follows bloody diarrhoea and presents in children but occurs in adults.
   - Negative stool PCR and no diarrhoeal prodrome make STEC less likely.

6. Think CM‑HUS when the following pattern emerges
   - MAHA + thrombocytopenia + prominent AKI (oligo/anuria, need for dialysis), schistocytes on smear, kidney biopsy consistent with TMA, negative or non‑diagnostic ADAMTS13, and no STEC.
   - Often there is an identifiable trigger (infection, pregnancy, drugs), and haemolysis may appear after initial infection control—as in the Capnocytophaga case.
   - Complement levels (C3/C4) are frequently normal; normal complements do not exclude CM‑HUS.
   - Genetic testing for complement mutations should be sent but will not guide acute therapy.

7. How to manage suspected CM‑HUS acutely
   - Consider bridging PEX if ADAMTS13 still pending or while organizing complement blockade—PEX may transiently help but rarely cures CM‑HUS.
   - Initiate eculizumab (or newer terminal complement inhibitors) once infection is controlled or bacteremia cleared whenever possible. Vaccinate against Neisseria meningitidis (and consider prophylactic antibiotics) prior to or with early doses according to your local protocol — but do not delay life‑saving therapy unnecessarily when the clinical picture mandates complement blockade.
   - Collaborate with nephrology, haematology and infectious diseases—complement blockade in the setting of recent sepsis requires balancing risks (encapsulated organisms) and benefits (renal salvage).

Applying the pathway to the Capnocytophaga case
- Initial presentation mimicked DIC from fulminant sepsis (pancytopenia, coagulopathy, AKI). Early suspicion for Capnocytophaga led to appropriate empiric antibiotics.
- As infection improved, the patient developed new isolated haemolysis, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH and persistent thrombocytopenia with schistocytes and escalating AKI → this is the classic “TMA appearing after infection” pattern.
- ADAMTS13 was not severely low (43.5%) → TTP unlikely. Stool Shiga toxin PCR was negative → STEC‑HUS unlikely. Kidney biopsy showed fibrin thrombi consistent with TMA.
- PEX was used as a bridge; eculizumab was started after blood cultures were negative and vaccines/prophylactic antibiotics were administered, with marked haematologic recovery and partial renal recovery thereafter.

Clinical pearls
- Schistocytes matter. If you see significant schistocytosis (≥1% in many labs; this case had 15–20%), act fast.
- Normal C3/C4 does NOT exclude CM‑HUS. Functional and genetic testing are useful but not required to start therapy when the clinical picture fits.
- ADAMTS13 is the pivotal test for TTP vs other TMAs, but it’s only useful if you either send it early or use a scoring system (PLASMIC) to decide on immediate PEX.
- In sepsis, DIC and TMA can coexist or evolve sequentially—improvement in sepsis followed by new haemolysis/thrombocytopenia should raise the ceil of suspicion for superimposed TMA.
- Vaccinate and give meningococcal prophylaxis when initiating complement blockade, but balance the timing against the urgency of treating a fulminant CM‑HUS.

Take‑home messages
- Treat the phenotype: MAHA + thrombocytopenia is an emergency—send ADAMTS13, stool Shiga tests, complement studies and smear now.
- Use PLASMIC to decide who needs immediate PEX. When in doubt and unstable, start PEX — TTP kills fast.
- If ADAMTS13 is not severely reduced and STEC is excluded, think CM‑HUS—especially with severe AKI and supportive biopsy findings.
- Complement blockade (eculizumab) can be kidney‑saving in CM‑HUS; balance infection risk by ensuring appropriate antibiotics, cultures and vaccinations where possible.
- Always involve nephrology, haematology and infectious diseases early—these cases are multidisciplinary by nature.

This Capnocytophaga case is a crisp lesson: an unusual zoonotic infection can trigger complement‑mediated TMA, and the bedside workflow of “recognize → send the right tests → score for TTP → bridge appropriately → escalate to complement blockade when indicated” will keep patients out of the dialysis unit and increase chances of renal recovery.



## Normal C3/C4, Sick Kidneys: Interpreting Complement Tests in Suspected CM‑HUS

What makes this case interesting: a previously healthy woman with fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis who develops dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury and later a classic thrombotic microangiopathy (MAHA, schistocytes, thrombocytopenia) on renal biopsy — yet her routine complement tests (C3/C4) are normal. Is this CM‑HUS or not? Short answer: yes, normal C3/C4 do not rule out complement‑mediated HUS. This case is a great teachable moment about the limits of standard complement testing, timing, and how treatments (PEX, eculizumab) and the infection itself can confuse the picture.

Why we get fooled by normal C3/C4
- CM‑HUS is driven by dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway at endothelial surfaces. That activation may be local (microvascular endothelium) and not cause sustained drops in circulating C3/C4.
- C3/C4 are crude snapshots of systemic complement protein abundance; they don’t measure ongoing complement activation at tissue sites, nor do they quantify function of regulatory proteins (CFH, CFI, CD46).
- Many patients with genetically mediated or infection‑triggered CM‑HUS have normal serum C3/C4 at presentation (and even during disease). Relying on them to “rule out” CM‑HUS is unsafe.

How timing and interventions alter complement assays
- When you draw matters:
  - Early in the course, before complement consumption or treatment, C3 might be low in some patients — but often it isn’t.
  - After plasma exchange (PEX), levels of complement proteins (and autoantibodies) are transiently normalized or diluted depending on replacement product (FFP, Octaplas). PEX can therefore mask low C3/C4 or remove autoantibodies.
  - After eculizumab, downstream functional assays (CH50/AH50) will be suppressed because terminal complement is inhibited — but total C3/C4 may remain unchanged.
- In the presented case, basic C3/C4 were normal before PEX. Extended functional AH50 was depressed after PEX and one dose of eculizumab — most likely treatment effect rather than proof of pre‑existing complement depletion.

Functional assays: AH50/CH50 and what they tell you
- CH50 (classical pathway) and AH50 (alternative pathway) measure pathway hemolytic activity:
  - A low AH50 suggests impaired alternative pathway function, but interpretation requires context: was the sample drawn after PEX? after eculizumab? Are there consumptive or genetic causes?
  - Eculizumab blocks C5 → downstream CH50/AH50 are suppressed even if the disease was complement‑driven originally.
  - PEX can transiently raise or lower these assays depending on timing and replacement product.
- Practical rule: draw baseline CH50/AH50 and complement factors (C3/C4, FH/FI/B) before PEX or eculizumab when possible. If you can’t, note the timing of interventions when interpreting results.

Genetics and extended testing — when and how to use it
- Genetic testing for CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, MCP (CD46), THBD and anti‑factor H antibodies helps identify predisposition but:
  - Pathogenic variants are found in ~50% of people with atypical/CM‑HUS; absence of a variant does not exclude CM‑HUS.
  - Results take time; they should not delay treatment decisions in a critically ill patient.
  - Interpret variants with the laboratory’s classification (pathogenic, likely pathogenic, VUS) and involve genetics/nephrology for long‑term planning.
- Use genetic results to guide duration of eculizumab (long‑term therapy if a high‑risk pathogenic variant or recurrent disease) — but acute management is clinical.

Working through the diagnostic dilemmas in sepsis + TMA
- In sepsis you can have:
  - DIC with schistocytes and thrombocytopenia
  - TTP (severe ADAMTS13 deficiency)
  - STEC‑HUS
  - CM‑HUS triggered by infection
- Practical approach:
  - Always get an ADAMTS13 level urgently (or use a clinical score like PLASMIC to triage). If ADAMTS13 <10% → treat for TTP (PEX).
  - Test for Shiga toxin if GI prodrome.
  - If MAHA, persistent thrombocytopenia and AKI persist or worsen despite control of infection — think CM‑HUS even with normal C3/C4.
  - If you have high suspicion, start bridging PEX if waiting for tests, but plan early eculizumab once TTP and uncontrolled infection are excluded/treated.

Counselling the team and family when labs and clinical picture disagree
- Be explicit about test limitations: “Normal C3/C4 don’t exclude CM‑HUS — these tests are not sensitive for local endothelial complement activation.”
- Explain treatment reasoning: eculizumab is the most effective therapy for CM‑HUS and outcomes correlate with early initiation; risks (notably meningococcal infection) must be mitigated with vaccination and antibiotics.
- Clarify why you might treat before genetic results return: genetic testing doesn’t change the acute need for complement blockade when the clinical phenotype fits.
- Document timings: record when samples were drawn relative to PEX/even a single eculizumab dose — it matters for interpreting later functional assays.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t use normal C3/C4 to exclude CM‑HUS. Use the clinical picture (MAHA, schistocytes, low platelets, AKI) and biopsy when indicated.
- Always obtain ADAMTS13 quickly to exclude TTP; treat empirically if high suspicion while awaiting results.
- Aim to draw baseline complement functional assays (AH50/CH50) and factor levels before PEX or complement blockade whenever possible.
- Remember that PEX and eculizumab radically alter downstream assays — and PEX can transiently restore plasma proteins, masking abnormalities.
- Genetic testing informs long‑term management but not acute treatment; absence of pathogenic variants does not negate CM‑HUS.
- In infection‑triggered CM‑HUS, try to clear the infection first; if infection controlled and TMA persists/worsens, eculizumab can be lifesaving — vaccinate and give prophylactic antibiotics if starting complement inhibitors.

Take‑home messages
- Normal C3/C4 do not rule out complement‑mediated HUS.
- Timing is everything: draw baseline complement and functional tests before PEX/eculizumab if you can; otherwise interpret results in context.
- Use ADAMTS13 and stool Shiga tests to exclude other TMAs quickly.
- Start appropriate therapy based on the clinical syndrome — genetic results are for follow‑up, not for acute decision‑making.
- When labs seem discordant with the clinical picture, communicate the limits of testing clearly to the team and family and act on the clinical phenotype.

This patient’s course illustrates the trap: an infection (C. canimorsus) triggered a fulminant TMA; C3/C4 were normal, PEX transiently helped, and sustained recovery required complement blockade. In the ED/ICU, treat the patient, not just the lab numbers.
