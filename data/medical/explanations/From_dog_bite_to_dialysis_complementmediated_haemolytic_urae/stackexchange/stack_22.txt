### [Pathophysiology] How do plasma exchange and eculizumab therapy affect complement functional assays (e.g., AH50), and how should these results be interpreted in treated patients?

Key teaching point: functional complement assays (for example AH50, which measures the hemolytic activity of the alternative pathway) are tests of pathway function, not static protein levels, so they are very sensitive to recent interventions that remove complement proteins or block specific steps. Plasma exchange (PEX) and eculizumab each depress functional assays by different mechanisms — therefore a low AH50 in a patient who has just had PEX or received eculizumab usually reflects treatment effect, not proof of an underlying complement deficiency or activation state.

Why this happens, in clinical terms. Plasma exchange replaces the patient’s plasma with donor plasma (or solvent‑detergent plasma such as Octaplas). PEX therefore removes circulating complement proteins, activation fragments and autoantibodies and dilutes any ongoing complement activity. Immediately after PEX you will commonly see reduced functional activity (low AH50/CH50) because you have physically depleted the patient’s complement proteins; depending on the replacement product, some complement function may be restored transiently but the net effect is suppression or fluctuation of functional assays. Eculizumab works by binding C5 and preventing its cleavage to C5a/C5b, thereby blocking formation of the membrane attack complex. Because AH50/CH50 assays require an intact terminal pathway to produce hemolysis, therapeutic C5 blockade renders these assays profoundly low or zero while the drug is active. In other words, eculizumab produces a pharmacologic “false positive” for terminal pathway deficiency on functional testing.

How to interpret results in treated patients and practical guidance. Always record precise timing of blood sampling relative to PEX and to the first and last dose of eculizumab. The highest diagnostic yield is obtained by drawing baseline complement samples (functional assays and factor levels, and any autoantibody testing) before PEX and before starting eculizumab. If that is not possible, a suppressed AH50 obtained after PEX or after eculizumab should be attributed to treatment effect unless there is corroborating prior evidence of low activity. In patients who have already received PEX but not eculizumab, repeat testing after an interval (allowing complement proteins to equilibrate/recover, often days to a week depending on exchanges and replacement) can be informative. In patients who have received eculizumab, functional hemolytic assays will remain suppressed while the drug is present; to assess intrinsic pathway function you must wait for drug clearance (weeks after discontinuation) or measure other complementary data that are not affected by C5 blockade — for example genetic testing for complement regulatory gene variants (which is not affected by PEX or eculizumab) and measurement of C5‑binding/evidence of blockade (drug level or downstream markers such as undetectable free C5 activity or suppressed sC5b‑9 formation depending on assay availability).

Apply this to the case. The patient’s markedly reduced AH50 obtained 8 days after the last PEX and 4 days after the first eculizumab dose is most consistent with treatment effect: prior PEX had depleted/diluted complement components, and eculizumab directly blocks terminal pathway hemolytic activity, so a low AH50 does not establish an intrinsic alternative‑pathway deficiency or ongoing complement consumption. Because genetic testing is unaffected by these therapies, it remains the appropriate test to look for a predisposing complement abnormality; functional assays to clarify baseline complement activity should be done before PEX/eculizumab where possible, or deferred until the effects of therapy have resolved and samples can be interpreted properly.

Bottom line: interpret low functional complement assays in the context of recent PEX or eculizumab as likely treatment effects. Obtain baseline functional testing before these interventions when feasible; if not, rely on genetic testing and repeat functional assays after an appropriate washout interval to distinguish true underlying complement defects from pharmacologic suppression.
