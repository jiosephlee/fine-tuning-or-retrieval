### [Management] In an adult with anuric AKI and suspected TMA, what are the indications and safety considerations for kidney biopsy, including a minimum platelet threshold or transfusion strategy?

Teaching pearl:

A kidney biopsy in a patient with anuric AKI and suspected thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is an important diagnostic and sometimes prognostic test, but it must be performed only when the information obtained will change management and when bleeding risk can be made acceptable. In practice the decision rests on three questions: (1) will histology meaningfully alter therapy or prognosis beyond available clinical, laboratory and noninvasive data (for example ADAMTS13, stool Shiga-toxin PCR, peripheral smear, response to plasma exchange and course on dialysis)? (2) can the procedure be performed safely given the patient’s platelet count, coagulation status and hemodynamic stability? and (3) are alternatives (transjugular or open biopsy) preferable?

Why biopsy can be helpful: renal histology documents the presence, chronicity and severity of TMA (endothelial swelling, fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli, crescents) and excludes other diagnoses (immune complex GN, ATN-only, vasculitis) that might change therapy (immunosuppression, continuing PEX versus switching to complement blockade). In the case you presented the biopsy confirmed TMA and supported escalation to eculizumab — an example where biopsy added actionable information.

Safety considerations and practical thresholds: the dominant risk is bleeding. For percutaneous ultrasound‑guided native kidney biopsy the usual institutional safety targets are platelets ≥50 × 10^9/L and INR ≤1.5 (some centers require INR ≤1.3). If platelets are below 50 × 10^9/L, give platelet transfusion immediately before the procedure to raise the count to ≥50 × 10^9/L; there is no evidence that routine transfusion to very high targets (eg, >100 × 10^9/L) improves safety and it may carry harms. If there is ongoing consumptive coagulopathy (eg, DIC) correct fibrinogen (aim >1.5–2.0 g/L) and other clotting abnormalities. Uremic platelet dysfunction can be mitigated with dialysis and/or desmopressin (DDAVP) prior to biopsy.

When thrombocytopenia is refractory or other risks are high (hemodynamic instability, uncontrolled DIC, large hematoma risk), prefer a transjugular renal biopsy — it has a lower bleeding complication rate and is often preferred in patients on anticoagulation, with very low platelets, or with single‑kidney anatomy. Open surgical biopsy is a last resort.

Timing and transfusion strategy in the setting of plasma exchange or recent transfusions: transfuse platelets as close to the procedure as possible (within 30–60 minutes) so they are not removed by subsequent plasma exchange. If planned PEX would follow the biopsy, coordinate timing (perform biopsy after the most recent exchange and delay the next exchange if safe) and inform the team that transfused platelets may be cleared by PEX. Likewise, if eculizumab or complement testing is being performed, be mindful that PEX alters complement assays; plan sampling before PEX when possible.

Contextual nuance for anuric/dialysis patients: dialysis dependence reduces consequences of parenchymal bleeding causing loss of renal function, which may make teams slightly more permissive, but bleeding can still be life‑threatening (retroperitoneal or large perinephric hematoma). Therefore do not lower safety thresholds solely because the patient is on dialysis.

In short: perform biopsy only when it will influence management; ensure platelets ≥50 × 10^9/L and INR ≤1.5 (transfuse platelets immediately before the procedure if needed); correct fibrinogen and consider DDAVP for uremic dysfunction; use transjugular approach when bleeding risk is high; coordinate timing with PEX to avoid removing transfused platelets or altering complement studies.
