Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

### [Clinical Pearls] In an adult presenting with fulminant sepsis four days after a dog bite, which empiric intravenous antibiotic regimens appropriately cover Capnocytophaga and other dog-bite flora, and what features of the organism and wound microbiology justify this choice?

Direct answer (clinical pearl)
- Empiric IV therapy for a patient with fulminant sepsis after a dog bite should provide reliable coverage of Capnocytophaga canimorsus plus the typical polymicrobial dog‑bite flora (Pasteurella spp., streptococci, staphylococci, and anaerobes). Reasonable empiric options are:
  - IV ampicillin–sulbactam (first‑line for moderate–severe bite infections), or
  - IV piperacillin–tazobactam (preferred for critically ill/ICU patients or when broader gram‑negative/anaerobic coverage is needed), or
  - A carbapenem (e.g., meropenem) for very severe infection or concern for resistant organisms.
- Add empiric anti‑MRSA therapy (vancomycin, daptomycin, or linezolid) only when there is clinical concern for MRSA or deep soft‑tissue infection.
- In patients with severe beta‑lactam allergy, use alternatives that cover oral anaerobes and Pasteurella/Capnocytophaga (for example, doxycycline plus metronidazole or a fluoroquinolone with metronidazole or moxifloxacin), recognizing limited data and tailoring therapy to susceptibility results when available.

Why these choices (microbiology and pathophysiology)
- Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, fastidious, capnophilic gram‑negative rod commonly present in dog and cat oral flora. It grows slowly in culture (diagnostic delay is common), so empiric therapy must be started on clinical grounds.
- Most C. canimorsus isolates are susceptible to beta‑lactams (penicillins, third‑generation cephalosporins, carbapenems), but beta‑lactamase–producing strains have been reported. Using a beta‑lactam combined with a beta‑lactamase inhibitor (ampicillin–sulbactam, amoxicillin–clavulanate orally, or piperacillin–tazobactam IV) protects against such resistance and also covers the common anaerobes and Pasteurella species found in dog‑bite wounds.
- Dog‑bite infections are typically polymicrobial (aerobes and anaerobes). Agents above provide broad gram‑negative, gram‑positive and anaerobic coverage appropriate for severe systemic infection. Carbapenems give the broadest empiric coverage if resistant organisms are suspected.
- Certain agents used for skin/soft‑tissue infections (aminoglycosides, clindamycin alone, or vancomycin alone) do not reliably cover Capnocytophaga or the anaerobic oral flora; hence monotherapy with those drugs is inadequate.

How this applies to the case
- In the reported case the team appropriately started IV piperacillin–tazobactam on presentation for severe sepsis after a dog bite to cover Capnocytophaga and the expected polymicrobial flora; this was later de‑escalated to ampicillin and then to oral phenoxymethylpenicillin after cultures identified C. canimorsus and the patient improved. The organism’s slow growth and susceptibility profile explain both the initial broad empiric choice and the subsequent narrowing of therapy once speciation and sensitivities were known.

Practical points
- Obtain blood cultures before antibiotics when feasible—C. canimorsus is slow‑growing and may require prolonged incubation or molecular methods for identification.
- Reassess antimicrobial regimen once culture and susceptibility data are available and tailor therapy accordingly.
- Consider MRSA coverage only if risk factors or clinical evidence support it.
- For patients with severe beta‑lactam allergy, select alternative regimens that still cover Pasteurella/Capnocytophaga and anaerobes and consult infectious disease if available.
