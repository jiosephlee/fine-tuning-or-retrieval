### [Differential Diagnosis] What is the recommended diagnostic approach to differentiate Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) from CM-HUS in an adult without a diarrheal prodrome?

When an adult presents with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia and AKI the immediate clinical priority is rapid, structured exclusion of the treatable mimics. The two diagnoses that most alter acute management are thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and Shiga toxin–producing E. coli–associated HUS (STEC‑HUS); once these are reasonably excluded, one can proceed to consider complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). In an adult without a diarrheal prodrome you must not assume STEC is absent — it can present atypically — so a methodical, time‑sensitive diagnostic approach is required.

First, obtain ADAMTS13 activity urgently to exclude TTP; values <10% mandate plasma exchange and prompt transfer to appropriate care, values >10% make TTP unlikely and redirect the workup. Second, send stool testing for Shiga toxin (molecular PCR for stx1/stx2 and stool culture for E. coli O157 and non‑O157) immediately — rectal swabs or stool should be sampled before antibiotics if feasible because test sensitivity falls with delay or prior antibiotics. If initial stool PCR is negative but clinical suspicion remains (for example after a prodrome of bloody diarrhea), consider repeat testing or serology for anti‑Stx antibodies later.

Parallel with those tests, perform standard hemolysis and TMA labs (schistocyte count, haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin), baseline complement levels (C3/C4) and rapid infectious workup. Interpret complement levels cautiously: normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS because levels may be normal or fluctuate. If you suspect CM‑HUS, arrange for functional complement assays and genetic testing for complement regulatory gene variants and factor H autoantibodies — recognizing these tests are send‑outs and results will not be immediately available.

Timing matters. Draw complement and ADAMTS13 samples before plasma exchange or eculizumab whenever possible, because PEX and complement blockade alter results (as occurred in this case). If PEX or eculizumab is already given, document timing clearly and interpret reduced alternative‑pathway activity or complement factor levels in that context.

Renal biopsy can be helpful when diagnosis remains unclear: light microscopy showing glomerular and arteriolar thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli and fibrin thrombi supports TMA but does not by itself distinguish STEC‑HUS from CM‑HUS; biopsy results should be integrated with microbiology and complement data.

Put this together in an algorithmic way: rule out TTP first (ADAMTS13), simultaneously test stool for Shiga toxin, and while awaiting results send baseline complement studies and arrange extended complement/genetic testing. If Shiga toxin is detected or there is a convincing diarrheal prodrome, treat as STEC‑HUS (supportive care; avoid eculizumab). If stool testing is negative, TTP excluded, and clinical course (absence of enteric prodrome, persistent thrombocytopenia and MAHA, renal biopsy consistent with TMA, and no other secondary cause) points to CM‑HUS, then initiate complement blockade promptly after ensuring active uncontrolled bacteraemia has been treated and after appropriate vaccinations and antimicrobial prophylaxis as needed.

In short: exclude TTP urgently, test stool for Shiga toxin before antibiotics if possible, obtain baseline complement studies and send extended complement/genetic testing (drawing samples before PEX/eculizumab), use biopsy and clinical context to support the diagnosis, and initiate eculizumab for CM‑HUS once STEC and TTP are reasonably excluded and infectious risks are mitigated.
