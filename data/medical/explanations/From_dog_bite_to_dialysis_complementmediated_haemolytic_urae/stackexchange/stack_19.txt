### [Pathophysiology] Why might overt hemolysis and thrombocytopenia from CM-HUS emerge only after the initial septic and DIC phase has begun to resolve?

Think of the clinical course as two overlapping processes with different timings: an early, global consumptive coagulopathy driven by overwhelming infection (sepsis–DIC), and a later, focal microvascular endothelial injury driven by dysregulated complement (CM‑HUS). The features of the first process commonly conceal the emergence of the second for several mechanistic and practical reasons.

First, during fulminant sepsis and DIC there is widespread consumption of platelets and coagulation factors, profound inflammation, marrow suppression and often multiorgan hypoperfusion. That global consumptive picture produces thrombocytopenia, elevated D‑dimer, prolonged coagulation times and AKI that mimic or overshadow the laboratory and clinical hallmarks of a thrombotic microangiopathy. In this phase schistocytes may be few, haptoglobin may be normal (because of concurrent liver dysfunction and transfusions), and the bone marrow response can be blunted — all of which make MAHA harder to recognise.

Second, the biology of complement‑mediated TMA often requires a “second hit.” Infection initially activates complement to help clear bacteria; as infection is treated and bacteremia falls, a dysregulated alternative‑pathway amplification loop (especially in a genetically or functionally susceptible host) can become dominant at the endothelial interface. That amplification produces localized microthrombi, red‑cell shearing and platelet consumption within arterioles and glomerular capillaries — the laboratory picture of MAHA and persistent thrombocytopenia that becomes clinically apparent only after the systemic inflammatory storm begins to subside.

Third, iatrogenic and diagnostic factors during the septic phase can mask or delay detection. Transfusions, plasma products, antibiotics (some causing DAT positivity), and early plasma exchange can transiently correct or alter haemolytic markers; clinicians may attribute cytopenias to sepsis, DIC or drug effects and not perform repeat peripheral smears or hemolysis panels until the patient stabilizes. In the reported case, improving coagulation parameters and resolving sepsis unmasked a striking fall in hemoglobin, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin and schistocytosis — features that only became evident once the DIC picture receded.

Finally, complement assays and ADAMTS13 activity are imperfect early markers: C3/C4 may remain normal, and ADAMTS13 is not severely deficient in CM‑HUS. Thus definitive recognition often awaits focused testing and kidney biopsy once the acute septic physiology has settled.

In short, sepsis/DIC creates a noisy, consumptive milieu that both physiologically and diagnostically masks evolving, focal complement‑driven endothelial injury. As the systemic consumptive process resolves, the persistent or newly unleashed endothelial complement activation manifests as overt MAHA and refractory thrombocytopenia — the clinical transition seen in this patient that prompted the diagnosis of CM‑HUS and initiation of targeted therapy.
