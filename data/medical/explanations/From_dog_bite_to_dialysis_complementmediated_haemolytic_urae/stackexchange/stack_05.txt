Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway leads to endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy in complement-mediated HUS, including why serum C3/C4 levels and standard pathway assays can remain normal during active disease.

Direct answer
Complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) results from uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway on the endothelial surface. That surface‑restricted amplification generates C3 and C5 convertases, producing anaphylatoxins (especially C5a) and terminal membrane attack complex (C5b‑9). C5a and sublytic or lytic C5b‑9 injure and activate endothelial cells, shifting them to a prothrombotic phenotype (tissue‑factor expression, Weibel–Palade body release of vWF, platelet adhesion), promoting formation of platelet‑rich microthrombi in small renal arterioles and glomerular capillaries. These microthrombi shear red cells (MAHA), consume platelets (thrombocytopenia), and cause renal ischemia and acute kidney injury – the clinicopathologic triad of TMA.

Stepwise pathophysiology (what happens and why)
- The alternative pathway has a baseline “tick‑over” (spontaneous, low‑level C3 hydrolysis) that is normally kept in check by plasma and membrane regulators (factor H, factor I, CD46/MCP, CR1).  
- Genetic defects or autoantibodies that reduce regulation (eg, abnormal factor H, factor I, MCP, factor B variants) permit unchecked surface amplification: C3b deposition → formation of C3bBb (C3 convertase) → an amplification loop → large amounts of C3b and downstream C5 convertase activity.  
- Local generation of C5a recruits and activates neutrophils; C5b‑9 assembles on endothelial membranes. Even sublytic C5b‑9 triggers endothelial cell exocytosis, upregulation of adhesion molecules and tissue factor, loss of antithrombotic surface (eg, thrombomodulin downregulation), and promotes platelet aggregation.  
- The net effect is microvascular thrombosis (fibrin + platelets), mechanical hemolysis across partially occluded capillaries (schistocytes, high LDH, low haptoglobin), and organ injury—most clinically apparent in the kidneys.

Why serum C3/C4 and standard pathway assays can be normal
- Local vs systemic activation: CM‑HUS is driven by complement activation bound to endothelial surfaces. Local surface amplification can cause severe endothelial injury without large‑scale consumption of circulating C3/C4, so serum levels measured in peripheral blood often remain within reference ranges.  
- Pathway specificity: The alternative pathway primarily consumes C3; C4 is part of the classical/lectin pathways and is often unchanged in alternative‑pathway–driven disease, so C4 is expected to be normal.  
- Kinetics and acute phase response: Acute hepatic synthesis of complement components can compensate, and single time‑point “snapshot” assays (C3, C4) are insensitive to ongoing localized activation.  
- Assay limitations and treatment effects: Standard functional assays (CH50, AH50) and C3/C4 may be normal early, and they are also affected by interventions (plasma exchange removes components; eculizumab blocks terminal activation and changes functional readouts). In the presented case, initial routine C3/C4 and pathway tests were normal while the patient was developing TMA; later a markedly reduced AH50 was observed after PEX and eculizumab—most consistent with treatment effects rather than primary systemic depletion.

What tests better reflect complement‑mediated disease
- Split products and terminal activation markers (eg, plasma C3d, C3a, C5a, sC5b‑9) or evidence of reduced functional alternative pathway activity (AH50) can be more informative than static C3/C4 levels. Testing for anti‑factor H autoantibodies, levels of complement regulators (factor H/I/B), and genetic testing for complement regulatory gene variants aid diagnosis. Kidney biopsy showing TMA provides histologic confirmation when clinical picture is unclear.

How this explains the case
- This patient had an infection (C. canimorsus) known to interact with complement and acted as a trigger. Early sepsis/DIC dominated and likely obscured evolving CM‑HUS. The later abrupt MAHA, persistent thrombocytopenia, schistocytes and biopsy‑proven glomerular fibrin thrombi are exactly the downstream consequences of endothelial surface–restricted alternative pathway overactivation. The normal routine C3/C4 and early functional assays did not exclude CM‑HUS because activation was localized and because assays can be normal depending on timing and prior or subsequent therapies (PEX, eculizumab). The later reduced AH50 is explained by the effects of plasma exchange and complement blockade rather than proving systemic complement depletion prior to therapy.

Clinical pearl
In suspected TMA, normal serum C3/C4 does not rule out CM‑HUS. Diagnosis rests on the clinical pattern (MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI), exclusion of TTP (ADAMTS13), exclusion of STEC, targeted complement testing (split products, AH50, anti‑factor H), and histology when needed. Early recognition and terminal complement blockade (eculizumab) can halt endothelial injury and improve outcomes, but timing of sampling and recent therapies must be considered when interpreting complement assays.
