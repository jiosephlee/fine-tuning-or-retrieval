### [Differential Diagnosis] Beyond CM-HUS, what secondary causes of TMA should be considered in adults (e.g., malignant hypertension, pregnancy-associated TMA, drug-induced TMA, autoimmune disease, antiphospholipid syndrome) and what targeted tests help distinguish them?

When you see MAHA, thrombocytopenia and acute kidney injury in an adult you must keep a broad, structured differential because many disorders produce histologic TMA but have different treatments and urgencies. Start by asking about the clinical context and then order a focused set of tests to rule in or out the common secondary causes.

Malignant hypertension: severe, sudden elevation of blood pressure produces endothelial shear injury with retinal haemorrhages/exudates and rapidly progressive renal failure. Look for markedly elevated BP, funduscopic signs, very high serum creatinine and evidence of left‑ventricular strain. Urgent BP measurement and control are diagnostic and therapeutic priorities; renal biopsy may show onion‑skin arteriolar changes and fibrinoid necrosis rather than classic glomerular platelet‑rich thrombi. Routine labs are non‑specific.

Pregnancy‑associated TMA (preeclampsia/HELLP, postpartum HUS): consider in pregnant/postpartum women. Distinguish from TTP by ADAMTS13 activity (TTP very low) and from CM‑HUS by timing (third trimester/postpartum), hypertension, proteinuria, and transaminase/platelet pattern in HELLP. Check pregnancy test/hCG, urine protein, liver enzymes, platelet trend and ADAMTS13; consider obstetric consultation and fetal assessment.

Drug‑induced TMA: several agents (eg, quinine, some antiplatelet agents, calcineurin inhibitors, certain chemotherapies, anti‑VEGF agents) cause either immune‑mediated (drug‑dependent antibodies) or dose‑dependent endothelial injury. A careful drug/exposure history is key. Targeted tests include drug‑dependent antibody assays when available and temporally stopping the suspected agent; renal biopsy can help but is not diagnostic alone.

Autoimmune disease and antiphospholipid syndrome (APS): SLE and catastrophic APS commonly present with TMA. Look for systemic autoimmune features, serologies (ANA, anti‑dsDNA, complement consumption pattern), and for APS order lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin and anti‑β2‑glycoprotein I (repeat positives 12 weeks apart when feasible). APS often has thrombotic history and prolonged clotting assays (paradoxically with thrombotic tendency); biopsy may show microvascular thrombosis without immune complex deposition.

Sepsis/DIC‑associated TMA and bacterial toxins: severe infection and DIC can produce microthrombi and schistocytes. Distinguish by systemic inflammatory markers, positive blood cultures, and consumptive coagulopathy (prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, high D‑dimer). Management targets the infection and supportive coagulation therapy; if microangiopathic haemolysis emerges late or persists after infection control, consider a superimposed primary TMA (eg, CM‑HUS).

STEC‑HUS: classically after diarrhoeal illness; test stool for Shiga toxin by PCR or culture. Absent enteric prodrome and a negative stool PCR argue against it.

TTP: life‑threatening and must be excluded early. ADAMTS13 activity <10% (and often inhibitor) establishes TTP; use that test urgently (send plasma before PEX) and use PLASMIC score to triage while awaiting results. Clinically TTP has prominent neurologic features and less severe renal failure than HUS.

Malignancy‑associated and transplant‑associated TMAs: look for active malignancy, bone‑marrow involvement, or recent stem‑cell/solid‑organ transplant and exposures (conditioning regimens, calcineurin inhibitors). Workup may include imaging, bone‑marrow biopsy and reviewing transplant immunosuppression.

Complement‑mediated (CM‑HUS) and complement‑triggered secondary TMA: think of CM‑HUS when TTP and STEC are excluded and TMA persists despite control of an initiating insult. Tests useful here are ADAMTS13 (to exclude TTP), Shiga toxin PCR (to exclude STEC), standard complement levels (C3/C4) and functional pathway assays (AH50/AP50), anti‑factor H antibodies, and complement genetic testing when available. Note that normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS and that acute treatments (PEX, eculizumab) and timing of sampling can alter results.

Practical testing approach in the adult with new TMA: immediately send ADAMTS13 activity with inhibitor testing, blood cultures and sepsis workup, stool Shiga toxin PCR if diarrhoea, coagulation panel (PT/aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer) to evaluate for DIC, pregnancy test in women of childbearing potential, ANA and antiphospholipid antibodies if autoimmune features present, and targeted drug exposure history with cessation of suspect agents. If TTP and STEC are excluded and TMA is ongoing or severe, pursue complement studies (C3/C4, functional alternative pathway assays, anti‑factor H antibodies) and consider referral for complement genetic testing and early nephrology/hematology discussion about PEX versus complement blockade.

In this case the sequence—bacteraemia with initial DIC, later isolated MAHA with schistocytes, ADAMTS13 not suggestive of TTP, negative Shiga toxin, normal ANA but evolving alternative pathway assays after treatments—pointed away from TTP, STEC, pregnancy‑related causes and classic autoimmune APS and toward infection‑triggered complement dysregulation. That diagnostic reasoning is what guided biopsy, PEX as a bridge and timely eculizumab once active bacteraemia was controlled.
