Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

### [Pathophysiology] List common triggers for complement-mediated HUS and explain why complement blockade can be effective even when no pathogenic complement gene variant is identified.

Common triggers of complement‑mediated HUS are those clinical events that acutely tilt the balance of the alternative complement pathway toward activation — classically infections (especially respiratory and gastrointestinal pathogens, but also unusual agents such as Capnocytophaga canimorsus), pregnancy and the postpartum period, certain drugs (e.g., calcineurin inhibitors, some chemotherapies), organ transplantation, malignancy, autoimmune flares, major surgery, and haematopoietic‑cell transplant or graft‑versus‑host disease. In practice you should think “anything that provides a strong inflammatory or endothelial insult” as a potential precipitant of CM‑HUS.

Why blockade of complement (eculizumab) can be effective even when no pathogenic complement gene variant is found comes down to pathophysiology: most CM‑HUS syndromes converge on uncontrolled activation of the terminal complement pathway and formation of the membrane attack complex with resultant endothelial injury, platelet aggregation and microvascular thrombosis. Genetic predisposition (loss‑of‑function in regulators or gain‑of‑function in activators) is common but not universal; many patients have no identifiable pathogenic variant because testing is incomplete (not all regulatory defects are known), penetrance is variable, somatic or acquired factors (for example anti‑factor H autoantibodies, transient depletion of regulators during sepsis, or overwhelming complement activation by an invading pathogen) produce the same downstream biology, or pathogenic variants lie in regions not assessed. Regardless of the upstream trigger, blocking C5 prevents generation of C5a and the membrane attack complex, halting the final common pathway of endothelial injury and TMA. Thus clinical response to terminal complement inhibition can occur even without a documented inherited complement defect.

Clinically this means: when a patient has MAHA, thrombocytopenia and AKI consistent with TMA and other causes (TTP, STEC‑HUS, immune hemolysis) are excluded or treated, early consideration of complement blockade is warranted if there is ongoing TMA — but balance this against infection risk (vaccinate, provide prophylactic antibiotics, and, when possible, treat and control active bacteraemia first), and reassess duration of therapy once genetic and immunologic testing and clinical course are clarified.
