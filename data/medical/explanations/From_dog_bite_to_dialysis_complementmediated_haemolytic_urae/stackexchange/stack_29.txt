Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

### [Management] After blood culture identification of Capnocytophaga canimorsus and clinical stabilization, what is a reasonable step-down antibiotic regimen and duration, and what beta-lactamase considerations influence this choice?

After you have documented clearance of blood cultures and the patient is clinically improving, it is reasonable to “step down” from broad-spectrum IV therapy to a narrower, oral beta‑lactam agent — provided the isolate’s susceptibility testing supports it and there is no deep‑seated focus (endocarditis, abscess) that would mandate prolonged IV therapy.

Practical regimen and duration
- For uncomplicated C. canimorsus bacteraemia that has cleared and in a clinically stable patient, switch to oral amoxicillin–clavulanate to complete a total course of about 10–14 days from the first negative blood culture and clinical improvement. Typical dosing: amoxicillin 875 mg/clavulanate 125 mg twice daily (or 500/125 mg three times daily) depending on local formulary.
- If IV step‑down is preferred (GI intolerance or poor absorption), ceftriaxone 1–2 g IV once daily is an excellent narrower option; complete a similar 10–14 day total course.
- Extend duration (typically 4–6 weeks) if there is evidence of endovascular involvement, deep tissue infection, or prosthetic material infection.

Why these choices (beta‑lactamase considerations)
- Most C. canimorsus isolates are susceptible to penicillins, third‑generation cephalosporins and beta‑lactam/beta‑lactamase inhibitor combinations. However, beta‑lactamase production has been increasingly reported. That means plain penicillin V or ampicillin monotherapy can fail if a beta‑lactamase‑producing strain is present.
- Beta‑lactamase inhibitors (clavulanate, sulbactam, tazobactam) restore activity of penicillins against such isolates, so amoxicillin–clavulanate or ampicillin–sulbactam are preferred oral/IV step‑down choices when beta‑lactamase production is a concern.
- Third‑generation cephalosporins (ceftriaxone) are generally reliable alternatives when susceptibility is confirmed; they are less affected by common TEM-type beta‑lactamases produced by Capnocytophaga.
- If the isolate is resistant or the patient is beta‑lactam allergic, consider doxycycline or a fluoroquinolone guided by susceptibilities, but beware variable activity and limited bacteremia data.

Clinical checklist before step‑down
- Confirm blood‑culture clearance and sustained clinical stability (afebrile, hemodynamically stable).
- Review organism susceptibilities; if beta‑lactamase production is reported or suspected, use a beta‑lactam/inhibitor or ceftriaxone rather than penicillin alone.
- Exclude deep foci (echo if concern for endocarditis).
- Ensure adequate oral absorption and adherence.

In the case you presented — a patient with cleared C. canimorsus bacteraemia and no identified endovascular focus — switching from IV piperacillin–tazobactam to oral amoxicillin–clavulanate to finish a total of ~14 days is a reasonable, evidence‑based step‑down, provided susceptibility data support it and there are no contraindications.
