### [Management] In adults with CM-HUS and no identified pathogenic complement variant, what factors guide the duration of eculizumab therapy and how should clinicians monitor for relapse upon discontinuation?

In adults with complement‑mediated HUS who do not have a demonstrable pathogenic complement gene variant, the decision about how long to continue eculizumab is individualized and balances the risk of recurrent TMA against the real harms of indefinite complement blockade (infectious risk, vaccination/prophylaxis burdens, cost and quality‑of‑life). The key clinical factors that should guide duration are the severity of the index episode, the degree of renal recovery, the presence of extrarenal organ injury, and the presumed role of a reversible trigger.

If the presenting episode was severe (dialysis‑dependent AKI, need for intensive care, histologic TMA, or persistent organ dysfunction) and recovery is incomplete (persistent CKD, ongoing proteinuria or reduced GFR), many experts favour a longer course of maintenance eculizumab (months to indefinite) because relapse could produce further irreversible kidney injury. By contrast, when CM‑HUS clearly follows a single identified and controlled trigger (severe infection, pregnancy, certain drugs), the patient achieves full haematologic remission and substantial renal recovery (return to baseline or near‑baseline creatinine, minimal proteinuria) and there is no family history or identified pathogenic variant, discontinuation after a defined period (commonly 3–6 months of maintenance therapy after induction) is reasonable.

When planning discontinuation, discuss risks and contingency plans with the patient, involve nephrology, hematology and the complement laboratory, and ensure meningococcal and other relevant vaccinations and antibiotic prophylaxis are in place while eculizumab is being stopped or restarted.

Monitoring strategy after stopping eculizumab must be proactive and rapid, because relapses can evolve quickly. Practical monitoring includes close clinical and laboratory surveillance with predefined thresholds for immediate retreatment. In the first 4–8 weeks after cessation, check CBC, serum creatinine, LDH, haptoglobin, total bilirubin, and urinalysis (including urine output and protein) twice weekly to weekly depending on baseline risk; thereafter spacing to every 1–2 weeks for months 2–3, then monthly to every 3 months out to at least 12 months if stable. Always review peripheral smear for schistocytes if haemolysis is suspected. Clinically, instruct the patient to report any new fatigue, jaundice, dark urine, reduced urine output or edema immediately.

Define clear lab/action thresholds for relapse and rapid re‑initiation of therapy: a fall in haemoglobin with rising LDH and falling haptoglobin or new schistocytosis, a ≥20–30% rise in serum creatinine or new/worsening oliguria, or a decline in platelet count without alternate explanation should prompt urgent re‑testing, urgent hematology/nephrology review and re‑dosing of eculizumab (or temporary plasma exchange while arranging eculizumab) without delay. Ensure logistical readiness (rapid access to drug and inpatient beds) before stopping.

Finally, document the rationale for stopping, maintain low threshold for re‑initiation, and reassess the plan if the patient becomes pregnant, develops a new infection, or is exposed to another potential trigger. Shared decision‑making and close multidisciplinary follow‑up are essential because the absence of a detectable variant lowers estimated relapse risk but does not eliminate it.
