### [Clinical Pearls] What is the preferred microbiologic testing to confirm or exclude STEC-HUS, and how does timing relative to symptom onset affect diagnostic yield?

Preferred testing to confirm or exclude STEC‑HUS is microbiologic detection of Shiga‑toxin–producing E. coli from the patient’s enteric specimen — ideally both stool culture for E. coli with subsequent serotyping (eg, O157) and a direct molecular assay (PCR) for stx1 and stx2 genes performed on stool or a rectal swab. Many modern laboratories perform direct PCR for stx genes (or multiplex gastrointestinal PCR panels) because PCR is more sensitive and faster than culture for detecting toxin‑producing strains.

Timing is critical. Stool/PCR testing has the highest yield very early in illness — typically within the first week of diarrhoeal symptoms (often the prodrome in STEC‑HUS). Sensitivity falls quickly after symptom onset and is substantially reduced by the time HUS develops (often 7–14 days after diarrhoea) or after antibiotics have been given. If stool/PCR is negative but clinical suspicion remains (typical prodrome of bloody diarrhoea followed by MAHA/thrombocytopenia/AKI), serologic testing for antibodies to the infecting LPS (eg, anti‑O157 IgM/IgG) or anti–Shiga toxin antibodies can help establish recent STEC infection when samples are obtained later; these rise after the acute period and remain detectable for weeks. Practical approach: obtain stool or rectal swab for culture and stx PCR as soon as possible (before antibiotics if feasible); if presentation is delayed and stool tests are negative, send convalescent serology or consult public‑health reference labs for toxin or strain typing.
