Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Chapter 4: ## From differential to diagnosis: excluding mimics and confirming CM-HUS

When confronted with a patient who develops microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia and acute kidney injury (AKI) in the setting of recent sepsis, a stepwise, evidence-based approach is required to exclude mimics and to assemble the findings that support complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). The following explains the reasoning used in this case and the general principles that underpin it.

Prioritizing TTP and the role of ADAMTS13
The immediate diagnostic priority in adults with MAHA and thrombocytopenia is to exclude thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), because TTP requires urgent plasma exchange. Clinical triage scores (for example the PLASMIC score) are helpful to estimate pretest probability while awaiting ADAMTS13 testing. A very low ADAMTS13 activity (<10%) is the biochemical hallmark of TTP. In this patient the PLASMIC score was low and the measured ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, effectively excluding TTP as the cause of her TMA and allowing teams to stop treating for TTP specifically. Because ADAMTS13 assays take time, many centres will start plasma exchange empirically when suspicion for TTP is high; in this case plasma exchange was also used as a temporizing intervention while the diagnosis was clarified.

Excluding STEC‑HUS
Shiga toxin–producing E. coli (STEC) is the prototypical infectious trigger of typical HUS and is usually associated with a diarrhoeal prodrome. Absence of an enteric illness and a negative stool PCR for Shiga toxin in this patient made STEC‑HUS unlikely. In practice, stool testing for Shiga toxins or E. coli O157 is standard whenever the history or local epidemiology suggests a possibility of STEC.

Considering other secondary TMAs
A number of secondary conditions can produce a TMA phenotype (malignant hypertension, drugs/toxins, pregnancy/postpartum, autoimmune disorders, haematopoietic or solid‑organ transplantation, certain infections). Decreasing the likelihood of these alternatives relies on history, examination and targeted testing. In this case there was no pregnancy or postpartum state, no exposure to implicated drugs, no transplant history, blood pressures were not consistent with malignant‑hypertensive TMA, and autoimmune screening (ANA, ANCA) was negative—together making these secondary causes improbable contributors to her evolving TMA.

Distinguishing sepsis‑associated DIC from a superimposed TMA
Differentiating disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) from a TMA can be challenging because both may occur in sepsis and both can produce thrombocytopenia and organ dysfunction. Useful discriminators are the coagulation profile and the dominant pathology: DIC typically shows prolonged clotting times, low fibrinogen and very high D‑dimer from consumptive coagulopathy, whereas TMA is characterized by predominant microangiopathic haemolysis (schistocytes, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin), preserved or less severely deranged coagulation parameters, and microvascular platelet‑rich thrombi on tissue biopsy. In this patient the initial presentation was dominated by sepsis‑related DIC (abnormal coagulation parameters and clinical bleeding), but those coagulation abnormalities resolved while haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia and dialysis‑dependent AKI emerged. The kidney biopsy documenting fibrin thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli and features of TMA provided pathological confirmation that a thrombotic microangiopathy was present in the kidney beyond the initial consumptive coagulopathy.

Laboratory evaluation of complement and its limitations
Evaluation for complement dysregulation is central when CM‑HUS is suspected, but available assays have important limitations. Serum C3 and C4 concentrations are often obtained first; low C3 may support alternative pathway activation but normal levels do not exclude CM‑HUS. Functional pathway assays (classical and alternative pathway haemolytic assays such as CH50/AH50) assess overall pathway activity and can indicate functional deficiency or blockade. Tests for specific regulators (factor H, factor I, factor B levels) and anti‑factor H autoantibodies are useful when available. In this patient C3/C4 were within reference ranges—their normality does not argue against CM‑HUS. The AH50 (alternative pathway functional assay) was markedly reduced, but interpretation required caution because timing relative to therapies was confounding (see below).

How treatments alter complement testing: timing confounders
Therapeutic interventions change complement test results. Plasma exchange (PEX) removes complement proteins, regulatory factors and autoantibodies, producing transient alterations in quantitative and functional assays. Eculizumab specifically blocks terminal complement (C5), leading to very low CH50 and often low AH50 results despite ongoing alternative pathway activation upstream. Therefore, assays performed after PEX or after initiation of eculizumab are difficult to interpret as evidence of prior complement activation. In this case extended complement testing was performed after several PEX sessions and after the first eculizumab dose; thus the low AH50 most likely reflects treatment effect rather than proving intrinsic complement depletion or primary activation. This caveat underscores the value of obtaining baseline complement studies and complement samples for specialized testing before PEX or complement blockade whenever patient safety permits.

Genetic and antibody testing: implications of negative results
Genetic testing for known complement regulatory gene variants (CFH, CFI, CFB, CD46/MCP, CFHRs, C3, etc.) and testing for anti‑factor H autoantibodies are part of the extended workup for CM‑HUS. A pathogenic variant or autoantibody supports a predisposition to complement dysregulation and often influences prognosis and the decision to continue long‑term complement blockade. However, only a proportion of patients (roughly 50–70%) harbour identifiable abnormalities, and a negative genetic panel does not exclude CM‑HUS. In this patient no pathogenic variants were identified; that reduces the likelihood of a lifelong genetic predisposition but does not negate the diagnosis of infection‑triggered CM‑HUS. Decisions about duration of eculizumab are individualized and take into account clinical severity, renal recovery, relapse risk and genetic findings.

Clinicopathologic synthesis that supported CM‑HUS
Diagnosis of CM‑HUS is ultimately a clinicopathologic synthesis of exclusion: severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and STEC infection were ruled out; other secondary causes were improbable; DIC improved while MAHA and kidney‑predominant TMA persisted; kidney biopsy showed microvascular thrombosis consistent with TMA; and the haematologic and renal course (transient improvement with PEX followed by sustained recovery after complement blockade) fit complement‑mediated disease. Additional practical points illustrated by the case include recognizing drug‑ or transfusion‑related false‑positive direct antiglobulin tests, and the value of peripheral smear review (schistocytes) in shifting the diagnostic impression toward MAHA/TMA.

Key takeaways for clinical practice
- Use a staged approach: rapidly exclude TTP with ADAMTS13 (and pretest stratification scores), test for STEC when appropriate, and consider common secondary causes by history and targeted testing.  
- Distinguish DIC and sepsis‑related coagulopathy from TMA by following the evolution of coagulation tests, markers of haemolysis, and by using kidney biopsy when the diagnosis remains uncertain.  
- Obtain baseline complement samples before PEX or eculizumab if possible; interpret post‑treatment complement assays cautiously.  
- A negative complement genetic panel does not exclude CM‑HUS; it informs but does not solely determine prognosis or treatment duration.  
- Integrate clinical course, laboratory trends, histopathology and therapeutic response to arrive at the working diagnosis; in this patient, that synthesis supported infection‑triggered CM‑HUS confirmed by pathology and clinical response to complement inhibition.
