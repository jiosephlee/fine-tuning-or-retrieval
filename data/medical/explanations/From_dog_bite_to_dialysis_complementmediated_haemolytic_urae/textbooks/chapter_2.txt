Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Chapter 2: ## Turning point: recognition of thrombotic microangiopathy after initial sepsis improvement

The clinical turning point in this case occurred on the nephrology ward when the patient—after apparent recovery from the acute septic/DIC phase—suddenly developed a marked drop in haemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days without any evidence of bleeding, while thrombocytopaenia persisted (platelets 30–40 × 10^9/L). This isolated worsening of anaemia in the absence of hemodynamic instability or an obvious bleeding source prompted reconsideration of the working diagnosis and raised concern for a microangiopathic haemolytic process rather than ongoing pure consumptive coagulopathy.

Laboratory evidence supported frank MAHA. Haptoglobin became undetectable and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) rose—both sensitive markers of intravascular haemolysis. The peripheral blood smear showed 15–20% schistocytes, a very high fraction that is characteristic of severe red cell fragmentation. In practice, even a schistocyte count >1% is considered abnormal and suggestive of MAHA; levels as high as seen here indicate a substantial microangiopathic process. Together with the rising LDH and low haptoglobin, these findings established ongoing intravascular haemolysis rather than anaemia of chronic disease or simple blood loss.

An important early diagnostic pitfall was the initially positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG, which suggested autoimmune haemolytic anaemia and prompted a trial of high‑dose corticosteroids. Several factors argue that this DAT was a false positive in this patient: recent exposure to penicillin (which can cause drug‑dependent DAT positivity), multiple blood transfusions, and lack of any clinical or laboratory response to steroids. The DAT subsequently became negative. This sequence—positive DAT followed by no steroid response and then a negative repeat DAT—is a well‑recognised pattern when penicillin‑related or transfusion‑related antibodies transiently coat erythrocytes, and it should raise suspicion that haemolysis is non‑immune and possibly microangiopathic.

Distinguishing residual or evolving TMA from DIC was central to clinical reasoning. During the sepsis phase the patient had coagulopathy and features compatible with DIC (prolonged clotting times, low fibrinogen, bleeding). By the time the haemoglobin fell, conventional coagulation parameters had normalised and fibrinogen/INR returned toward normal, yet the haemolysis and thrombocytopaenia persisted. In DIC, ongoing coagulopathy with consumption of clotting factors typically accompanies continued bleeding and laboratory evidence of deranged coagulation; persistent intravascular haemolysis and marked schistocytosis despite correction of coagulation studies point away from pure DIC and toward a TMA. Concurrent measurement of ADAMTS13 activity helps separate TTP from other TMAs: this patient’s ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5% (well above the <10% cutoff for TTP), making thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura unlikely and increasing the pretest probability that this was a complement‑mediated or infection‑triggered TMA.

Timing and safety considerations determined when renal biopsy was performed. A native kidney biopsy can provide diagnostic confirmation of TMA, but bleeding risk must be minimised—most centres aim for a platelet count >50 × 10^9/L (and correction of other coagulation abnormalities) before performing an elective biopsy. In this case the team instituted bridging plasma exchange and supportive care until the platelet count recovered to 77 × 10^9/L, at which point biopsy was undertaken. This approach balances the need for histopathology to guide disease‑specific therapy (for example, complement blockade) against procedural haemorrhagic risk.

The renal biopsy findings were diagnostic of a thrombotic microangiopathy: severe acute tubular necrosis from ischaemia, glomeruli with ischaemic collapse containing fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation. Immunofluorescence showed no immune‑complex deposition and electron microscopy revealed no features of a primary immune‑complex glomerulonephritis—findings that favour a TMA process (such as complement‑mediated HUS) rather than an immune‑complex GN. The presence of fibrin thrombi within glomerular capillaries and arterioles is a hallmark of TMA and, together with the clinical and laboratory picture, supported the diagnosis of CM‑HUS in this patient.

Clinical lesson: in a septic patient whose coagulation parameters have normalised but who develops new or worsening intravascular haemolysis (falling haemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH) with schistocytosis and persistent thrombocytopaenia, clinicians should promptly consider superimposed TMA. Early targeted workup—peripheral smear, DAT (interpreted cautiously), ADAMTS13, Shiga‑toxin testing, and timely renal biopsy when safe—can distinguish TMA subtypes and expedite disease‑specific therapy (for example, plasma exchange bridging and complement blockade), which materially affects outcome.
