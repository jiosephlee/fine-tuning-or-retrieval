Chapter 6: ## Prognosis, Follow-up, and Clinical Pearls

Prognosis in complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS) is governed principally by two interacting factors: the severity of kidney injury at presentation and the timeliness of definitive therapy with complement blockade. Patients who present with severe, dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) have a substantially higher risk of incomplete renal recovery and progression to end‑stage kidney disease (ESKD) than those with milder renal involvement. Observational data and case series show that earlier initiation of eculizumab (or other terminal complement inhibitors) is associated with higher rates of haematological remission and greater recovery of renal function; conversely, delayed treatment increases the probability of irreversible microvascular and tubular injury. In this case the patient’s partial renal recovery after delayed but eventual eculizumab illustrates these principles: haematological remission followed, and some kidney function returned, but she remains at elevated risk for chronic kidney disease because of the initial severity and need for dialysis.

Risk of relapse and the optimal duration of complement blockade are individualized and depend largely on the presence or absence of underlying complement abnormalities and on the clinical context that precipitated the episode. Approximately half of patients with CM‑HUS harbour pathogenic variants or autoantibodies that impair complement regulation; these patients have higher relapse rates and are more likely to require long‑term or indefinite complement inhibition. By contrast, when an episode is clearly triggered by a transient insult (severe infection, certain drugs, pregnancy) and extended genetic testing does not identify pathogenic variants, many experts consider a carefully monitored trial of discontinuation after a sustained period of remission (commonly 6–12 months) reasonable. The decision must weigh the severity of the index episode (need for dialysis, extrarenal organ injury), patient preferences, and logistical considerations for close follow‑up. If eculizumab is stopped, a clear monitoring plan and a rapid response strategy for recurrence are essential.

Follow‑up after an acute episode of CM‑HUS should be structured, frequent initially, and then spaced out as stability is demonstrated. Suggested elements are:

- Clinical review: assess volume status, blood pressure, symptoms of anaemia or bleeding, and any new neurologic or constitutional complaints. Emphasize prompt evaluation of fever or other infection signs in patients receiving complement blockade.
- Laboratory surveillance: during the acute and early recovery phase, check CBC with platelet count, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), haptoglobin, creatinine and electrolytes, and urinalysis (including urine protein). Typical frequency is daily to every 2–3 days while unstable or during induction therapy, then weekly for the first month after discharge, then every 2–4 weeks for the next 2–3 months, and thereafter every 1–3 months depending on stability. Any fall in platelet count, rising LDH, undetectable haptoglobin or new schistocytes on peripheral smear should trigger urgent reassessment for recurrent TMA.
- Renal monitoring: serial serum creatinine and urine protein quantification. Blood pressure control and ACE inhibitor/ARB therapy (when appropriate) should be optimized for long‑term kidney protection.
- Complement monitoring: routine C3/C4 levels are of limited sensitivity for disease activity, but functional assays (eg, CH50/AH50) may be used in some centres to document effective complement blockade or to guide therapy when available. If genetic testing was performed, the results should be reviewed with the patient and included in long‑term planning.
- ADAMTS13 is not needed routinely after diagnosis unless the clinical picture changes and thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is reconsidered.
- Biopsy and imaging: a kidney biopsy is helpful when the diagnosis is uncertain or when there is a discrepancy between the clinical course and laboratory data; it also provides prognostic information about chronicity and the extent of irreversible injury.

Vaccination, antimicrobial prophylaxis and infection precautions are critically important for any patient receiving terminal complement blockade. Because eculizumab blocks formation of the membrane attack complex, susceptibility to certain encapsulated organisms—classically Neisseria meningitidis but also other pathogens such as Capnocytophaga species—rises. Best practice recommendations include:

- Administer appropriate meningococcal vaccines (both conjugate A/C/Y/W and serogroup B formulations) at least 2 weeks before planned eculizumab therapy when feasible. If urgent therapy is required and vaccines cannot be given 2 weeks beforehand, give vaccines as soon as possible and provide antibiotic prophylaxis until adequate vaccine‑induced immunity is likely (commonly for 2 weeks).
- Consider short‑term antibiotic prophylaxis at eculizumab initiation (eg, oral penicillin V or an appropriate alternative in penicillin allergy) until 2 weeks after vaccination; practices vary and decisions should involve infectious disease specialists.
- Give clear instructions to patients to seek immediate medical attention for any fever or shivering and to inform emergency teams they are receiving complement blockade. Many centres provide a written alert card and advise carrying a short‑course “rescue” antibiotic (eg, ciprofloxacin or amoxicillin‑clavulanate based on local resistance patterns) to begin immediately if fever occurs before definitive medical evaluation.
- Ensure routine immunizations are up to date, and coordinate vaccinations with the timing of complement therapy.

Patient counselling is a central part of follow‑up. Patients should understand the nature of CM‑HUS (a microvascular disease driven by dysregulated complement activity), the rationale for eculizumab, and the real but manageable risks of infection associated with complement inhibition. Specific counselling points include:

- The need for urgent evaluation of fever while on eculizumab and the availability of emergent antibiotics if they cannot get immediate care.
- The signs and symptoms of relapse (new bruising or bleeding, dark urine, fatigue, pallor, reduced urine output, neurologic symptoms) and a low threshold for contacting their care team.
- Lifestyle adjustments while immunized and on prophylaxis (no specific activity restrictions beyond infection vigilance), and the importance of completing vaccine schedules and reporting travel plans that may alter exposure risk.
- For women of childbearing age, discuss pregnancy as a potential trigger for HUS and review contraceptive plans and pre‑conception counselling; management of CM‑HUS in pregnancy requires a multidisciplinary approach.

Frontline prevention and early management of dog bites and related infections are generalizable lessons from this case. Immediate wound care—thorough irrigation and cleansing, avoidance of unsterile manipulation, and assessment for need of closure—reduces bacterial inoculum. Prophylactic antibiotics are recommended for high‑risk bites (deep punctures, hand injuries, immunocompromised or asplenic patients) and amoxicillin‑clavulanate is the usual first‑line agent because it covers Pasteurella and Capnocytophaga species. Tetanus prophylaxis should be updated when indicated, and rabies prophylaxis considered if the animal’s rabies status is uncertain. Importantly, even previously healthy individuals can develop fulminant infections with C. canimorsus; early recognition of systemic symptoms after an animal exposure should prompt urgent assessment.

Clinical pearls that apply to frontline clinicians and help avoid diagnostic and therapeutic pitfalls:

- Normal C3 and C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS. Complement levels are frequently normal in patients with CM‑HUS; reliance on C3/C4 alone can give false reassurance.
- Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and sepsis may mask or precede a superimposed TMA. In patients with recovering sepsis who develop new anaemia, schistocytes, persistent thrombocytopenia or worsening renal function, re‑evaluate actively for TMA.
- A positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test can be misleading. Drug‑related or transfusion‑related antibodies may produce a transient positive DAT; persistent evidence of microangiopathic haemolysis (schistocytes, elevated LDH, undetectable haptoglobin, reticulocytosis when marrow function allows) should prompt evaluation for TMA despite an initial positive DAT.
- Peripheral blood smear is simple and high‑yield. Examination for schistocytes is a key bedside diagnostic step when haemolysis is suspected.
- Coordinate diagnostic sampling before plasma exchange (PEX) or first dose of eculizumab when possible. ADAMTS13 activity, baseline complement functional testing (eg, CH50/AH50) and blood for genetic testing should ideally be drawn prior to plasma infusion or complement blockade to avoid treatment‑related artefacts; if therapy must be started emergently, document timing of samples relative to treatment in the clinical record.
- Renal biopsy is valuable when safe and feasible. In patients with atypical presentations or when the diagnosis is uncertain, histology can distinguish TMA from other causes of AKI and help predict reversibility.
- TTP must be excluded promptly. ADAMTS13 activity <10% identifies TTP and mandates urgent PEX; use clinical tools (eg, PLASMIC score) to triage but confirm with laboratory testing.
- Multidisciplinary care is essential. Early involvement of nephrology, haematology, infectious disease and critical care teams improves diagnostic accuracy, timing of therapies (antimicrobials, PEX, eculizumab), and coordination of vaccination and prophylaxis.

In summary, the prognosis of CM‑HUS depends on the severity of initial kidney injury and the speed of effective complement inhibition. Follow‑up must be proactive, with frequent laboratory and clinical surveillance early after recovery and patient education focused on infection vigilance while on complement inhibitors. Preventive measures for animal bites, high clinical suspicion for TMA in the setting of sepsis, and coordinated, timely sampling before therapeutic interventions are practical steps that improve outcomes for future patients.
