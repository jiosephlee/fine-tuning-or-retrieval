Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Chapter 3: ## Pathophysiology of complement-mediated HUS and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS) is driven by dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway on vascular endothelium, most importantly within the renal microcirculation. The alternative pathway is constitutively active at a low level through a process termed “tick‑over,” in which spontaneous hydrolysis of C3 generates C3(H2O) that can bind factor B. Factor D then cleaves factor B to form a fluid‑phase C3 convertase that produces C3b. Surface‑bound C3b associates with factor B to form the membrane‑bound C3 convertase (C3bBb), which is stabilized by properdin and amplifies C3 cleavage in a positive feedback loop. When formed in sufficient quantity this amplification cascade generates C5 convertase activity, leading to C5 cleavage and formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b‑9). Local generation of C3b and MAC on endothelial surfaces promotes opsonisation, inflammation and direct cell injury—setting the stage for the microvascular thrombosis that characterises TMA.

Healthy tissue is protected from this potentially destructive amplification by several regulatory checkpoints. Complement factor H (CFH) is the principal soluble regulator of the alternative pathway: it binds surface‑deposited C3b, competes with factor B for C3b binding, accelerates decay of C3 convertases and serves as a cofactor for factor I (CFI)–mediated proteolytic inactivation of C3b to iC3b. Factor I itself is the serine protease that degrades C3b (and C4b) when appropriate cofactors are present. On cell surfaces membrane‑bound regulators such as membrane cofactor protein (MCP, CD46), decay‑accelerating factor (CD55) and CD59 (which inhibits insertion of MAC) provide local protection from complement‑mediated damage. Loss of function in any of these checkpoints permits unchecked C3 convertase activity, excessive C3b deposition and downstream MAC formation on endothelial cells, which triggers platelet adhesion, coagulation activation and the microvascular platelet‑rich thrombi seen in HUS.

The underlying predisposition to this dysregulation may be genetic or acquired. Pathogenic variants in genes encoding CFH, CFI, CFB, C3 and MCP are identified in a substantial proportion of patients with CM‑HUS (estimates vary by cohort but often cited in the range of 40–60%), and anti‑CFH autoantibodies are an important acquired mechanism particularly in children. Importantly, penetrance of these abnormalities is incomplete: many carriers of complement gene variants never develop disease unless a precipitating event occurs. Because of this, CM‑HUS is best understood as a two‑hit process in which an inherited or acquired impairment of complement regulation lowers the threshold for complement‑mediated endothelial injury, and a second hit triggers overt TMA.

Infections are among the commonest and most important clinical triggers for that second hit. Any process that activates systemic complement, injures endothelium or provokes intense inflammation—severe bacterial sepsis, viral infections, pregnancy, autoimmune flares or certain drugs—can overwhelm compromised regulatory capacity. Infections both increase complement activation (through pathogen‑associated molecular patterns such as lipopolysaccharide) and sensitize the endothelium (via cytokines, ischemia or direct microbial toxins), thereby promoting local complement deposition, endothelial swelling, exposure of procoagulant surfaces and the microthrombi that fragment red cells (producing schistocytes) and consume platelets.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an example of an infectious trigger that illustrates the double‑edged role of complement. As an encapsulated gram‑negative organism present in dog and cat oral flora, C. canimorsus is variably susceptible to complement‑mediated killing. Strains that lack an effective capsule are typically complement sensitive and cleared by circulating complement, whereas capsulated isolates can resist complement deposition and survive in blood. Even for complement‑sensitive strains, systemic infection provokes robust complement activation: pathogen recognition and C3b deposition are central to host defence, but excessive or misdirected activation at the endothelial surface can initiate the cascade toward TMA in a susceptible host. Thus the same complement activity that contributes to bacterial clearance can, under conditions of regulatory failure or intense inflammatory activation, produce injurious local complement deposition, endothelial activation and the thrombotic microangiopathy observed in CM‑HUS.

These mechanistic features explain several practical diagnostic and therapeutic points illustrated by the present case. Standard serum complement measures (C3 and C4) can be normal in CM‑HUS because complement activation may be focal (at the microvascular endothelium) rather than reflected by steady‑state depletion of plasma components; additionally, C3 levels may be replenished or not fall sufficiently to exit reference ranges. Timing and prior interventions also affect results: plasma exchange replaces complement components and eculizumab blocks terminal cleavage, both altering laboratory assays. Functional pathway assays (eg, AH50 for the alternative pathway or terminal pathway activity) can be more informative but are likewise sensitive to recent treatments. Recognition of these limitations is important when interpreting complement tests in a patient with sepsis and suspected TMA.

Finally, understanding this pathophysiology underpins treatment rationale. Targeting the terminal pathway with anti‑C5 therapy (eculizumab) prevents MAC formation and downstream endothelial injury, addressing the proximate mediator of TMA, whereas plasma exchange may transiently remove activating fragments or autoantibodies but does not correct intrinsic regulatory defects. Because terminal complement activity is also important for defence against encapsulated organisms, clinicians must weigh the timing of complement blockade against infection control and employ vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis when initiating therapy—precautions that reflect the same biology linking infection, complement and endothelial injury in CM‑HUS.
