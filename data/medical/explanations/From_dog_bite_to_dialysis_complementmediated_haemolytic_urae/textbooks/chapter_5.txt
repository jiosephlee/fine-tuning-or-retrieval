Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Chapter 5: ## Management: From Infection Control to Complement Blockade

The care of a patient who develops thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in the context of severe sepsis requires two parallel and sometimes competing streams of therapy: aggressive sepsis management (source control, appropriate antibiotics and organ support) and timely TMA‑directed interventions that may include plasma exchange (PEX) and complement blockade. The clinical priority at presentation is to stabilise the patient and control the infectious source, because untreated sepsis with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is immediately life‑threatening. At the same time, clinicians must remain vigilant for the emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), worsening thrombocytopenia and progressive renal failure that signal a superimposed TMA and prompt a very different set of therapies.

Sepsis and source control come first. Empiric antimicrobial therapy should be started promptly and tailored to the most likely pathogens and the exposure history; in dog‑ or cat‑associated infections this includes Capnocytophaga species. Practical empiric options include piperacillin–tazobactam or a third‑generation cephalosporin such as ceftriaxone; many Capnocytophaga isolates are penicillin‑susceptible and respond to amoxicillin–clavulanate or penicillin, but susceptibility testing and clinical response should guide de‑escalation. Surgical source control or local care of bite wounds is essential when indicated. In the case described, early empiric piperacillin–tazobactam was appropriate, and definitive therapy was narrowed after blood cultures grew Capnocytophaga canimorsus. Timely microbiological diagnosis is critical because it both guides antibiotic selection and informs the safety of later complement‑targeted therapies.

Supportive care for sepsis and DIC is oncologic to outcome and must be individualised. Standard ICU measures include haemodynamic optimisation, ventilatory support when required and correction of metabolic derangements. In DIC, transfusion decisions should be driven by bleeding and procedural needs rather than laboratory numbers alone. Platelet transfusion is indicated for active bleeding, for invasive procedures, or in profound thrombocytopenia with bleeding risk (often <50 × 10^9/L for procedures, lower thresholds for simple transfusion are used), but routine transfusion in TMA is discouraged because it may fuel microvascular thrombosis. Fresh frozen plasma, fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate should be used to correct severe hypofibrinogenemia or ongoing bleeding. In the reported patient, supportive transfusions were used judiciously while awaiting diagnostic clarification.

Renal support often becomes necessary. Indications for renal replacement therapy are the usual ones—refractory volume overload, severe acidosis, hyperkalaemia, uremia and anuria. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is preferred in haemodynamically unstable patients; intermittent dialysis is an option as the patient stabilises. The case patient required CRRT for anuric acute kidney injury and fluid overload, which is a common scenario when TMA overlaps with septic shock and DIC.

When a TMA is suspected, triage for specific therapy must be immediate because untreated TTP is rapidly fatal and eculizumab is time‑sensitive in complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). Two practical rules guide initial management: (1) do not delay life‑saving plasma exchange if severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (TTP) cannot be excluded, and (2) obtain blood for ADAMTS13 testing before starting PEX or plasma infusions when possible. PEX is the emergent therapy of choice for suspected TTP because it removes autoantibodies and replenishes ADAMTS13. In contrast, PEX is only temporising in CM‑HUS: it can remove circulating complement activation products and autoantibodies (for example anti‑factor H) and thus provide transient clinical improvement, but it does not correct the underlying dysregulation in most patients. In the present patient, PEX was appropriately started while ADAMTS13 testing was pending, and it produced only a transient improvement in diuresis until targeted complement blockade was given.

Eculizumab—the monoclonal antibody that blocks terminal complement component C5—is the standard of care for CM‑HUS and substantially improves haematologic and renal outcomes, particularly when administered early. The typical induction regimen used in adults is 900 mg intravenously weekly for four doses followed by a maintenance dose (commonly 1,200 mg) every two weeks; dosing schedules may vary by region and product labelling. In CM‑HUS, earlier initiation is associated with better chances of renal recovery and avoidance of long‑term dialysis. In the case under discussion, the patient received PEX as a bridge and then eculizumab once infection control permitted and appropriate precautions were taken; sustained haematologic remission and partial renal recovery followed.

Complement blockade carries a well‑recognised infectious risk because terminal complement activity is pivotal for defence against encapsulated organisms, most notably Neisseria meningitidis. This risk influences timing and infection‑control strategies. Recommended precautions are:

- Vaccinate against Neisseria meningitidis serogroups ACWY and B ideally at least two weeks before administering the first dose of eculizumab. If vaccination cannot be given two weeks prior because treatment is urgent, give the vaccines as soon as possible and cover the patient with appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis until adequate vaccine‑mediated immunity is expected (commonly at least 2 weeks).
- Provide antimicrobial prophylaxis while vaccination is incomplete or in cases where immediate eculizumab is necessary. Common prophylactic regimens include oral penicillin V or amoxicillin (or an alternative agent in penicillin‑allergic patients, such as ciprofloxacin), but local epidemiology, susceptibilities and allergy history should guide choice.
- Counsel patients thoroughly about early symptoms of meningococcal disease and advise urgent medical evaluation for fever or systemic symptoms.

These principles must be balanced against the immediate risk to the patient. If a patient has active, uncontrolled bacteraemia—especially with an encapsulated organism—full complement blockade may exacerbate infection. Therefore, whenever feasible, clinicians should wait for blood cultures to clear and for the acute infectious process to be controlled before initiating eculizumab. However, if the TMA is fulminant and life‑threatening, it may be necessary to begin eculizumab despite recent bacteremia; in that circumstance, ensure maximal antimicrobial coverage, confirm source control, give vaccines as soon as possible, institute antibiotic prophylaxis, and monitor closely. In the published case, eculizumab was started after blood cultures became negative and vaccination was administered prior to continuing therapy, and prophylactic amoxicillin was used because vaccines had not been given sufficiently in advance.

Adjunctive therapies and transfusion pragmatics deserve specific mention. Red blood cell transfusion should be given for symptomatic anaemia or following standard haemoglobin thresholds adjusted for comorbidity (for example, Hb <70 g/L in otherwise stable patients, higher in those with ischaemic heart disease or severe symptoms). Platelets should generally be avoided in TMA unless there is clinically significant bleeding or a procedure that warrants higher counts; transfusion can exacerbate microvascular thrombosis and worsen outcomes. Corticosteroids have no proven role in CM‑HUS and should not be used routinely for TMA unless there is a separate indication (for example, immune haemolytic anaemia). When autoantibodies to complement regulators are suspected (eg, anti‑factor H), PEX may be particularly useful to remove the pathogenic antibodies prior to initiation of targeted therapy.

Monitoring response and deciding treatment duration are clinical decisions guided by the trajectory of haematologic and renal recovery and by genetic testing. Response to therapy is judged by rising platelet count, falling LDH and bilirubin, rising haptoglobin, clearance of schistocytes on the peripheral smear and recovery of renal function and urine output. Functional complement assays (for example CH50 or AH50) may be used to document complement blockade, although interpretation can be confounded by PEX or timing of sampling. Genetic testing for complement pathway variants and testing for anti‑complement autoantibodies should be obtained because identification of a pathogenic variant or persistent autoantibody supports longer‑term or indefinite complement inhibition; conversely, in patients without demonstrable genetic predisposition and with a clear, removable trigger (such as an infection) who achieve sustained remission, discontinuation of eculizumab may be considered after a period of maintenance therapy with very close follow‑up. A practical approach is to reassess after several months of stability: if haemolysis and thrombocytopenia remain quiescent and renal function has recovered or stabilised, some centres consider stopping therapy with a plan for prompt restart at relapse. The case patient received an induction course followed by maintenance dosing and, in the absence of a pathogenic genetic variant, is being considered for discontinuation if stable renal function persists.

Finally, the management of TMA in the setting of sepsis requires multimodal coordination—infectious disease, nephrology, haematology and critical care teams must collaborate to time interventions safely. The published case illustrates these principles: early broad‑spectrum antibiotics and ICU support controlled the primary infection; PEX was used emergently while TTP was excluded; eculizumab was introduced once blood cultures were negative and after vaccination and prophylactic antibiotics were arranged; and close laboratory and clinical monitoring guided ongoing therapy and the plan for eventual reassessment and possible discontinuation. This staged, pragmatic approach—prioritising infection control without delaying necessary TMA‑directed therapy—offers the best chance of a good outcome in patients who present at the interface of sepsis and complement‑mediated disease.
