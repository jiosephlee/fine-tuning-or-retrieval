Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# The Coronary 'Skyline' Risk: Pre-empting Occlusion in Serial Valve Implantation

There’s a persistent myth in structural heart disease that transcatther aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is the "easy button" for aortic stenosis. Pilot programs in the 2000s may support that notion, but the real data is telling a different story. When patients survive the initial intervention—and fate has them live for another decade beyond their projected life expectancy at diagnosis—a cascade of complications often begins to detonate. We are now treating patients who need valve-in-valve, TAV-in-TAV, and literally, TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.

The clinical challenge isn't just getting a new valve into the tough space; it’s doing so without destroying the plumbing that feeds the heart itself. We are essentially stacking stents inside stents inside stents, and with every layer, coronary artery obstruction becomes a ticking time bomb. A recent case report by Merkulov et al. offers a masterclass in navigating this treacherous skyline, focusing specifically on how to plant a third valve while preserving coronary access for the future.

## The Anatomy of a Trap

In a standard redo-TAVI, we already face the dilemma of overlapping stents reducing the effective orifice area. But add a third iteration, and the geometry changes fundamentally. The "neo-skirt"—the expansion of the new valve’s seal to the calcific or fibrotic invasion layer—can become a cul-de-sac. If you don't plant the valve high enough, that skirt folds back down like a curtain closing on a stage. In this scenario, the membrane of the new valve presses against the ostia of previously diseased coronary arteries that are already encased in old metallicwork.

This risk is amplified when the patient presents with infective endocarditis history. Infection causes leaflet injury and accelerated structural valve deterioration, but it also creates irregular calcium and tissue planes that make calculating plane height difficult. Management is no longer just about getting through the aortic root; it’s about precision window for valve implantation height.

In this case, a 70-something male patient walked quite the tightrope. It started with a 27 mm Lotus valve in 2014 due to severe stenosis. By 2022, he refused the idea of open surgery for a second TAVI in a failing Lotus frame, opting for a 23 mm Evolut prosthesis. The result was a TAV-in-TAV configuration. That, however, was not the end of the road.

In late 2022, he developed prosthetic valve endocarditis (*Enterococcus faecalis*). After treating the infection fully with antibiotics and confirming resolution via TEE and MSCT, his valve failed again mere months later. Severe restenosis and gradients of 49 mm Hg screamed for reintervention. Now, he needed a third valve: an Acurate neo2.

## The CT-Guided "Aerial Survey"

This is where the Heart Team steps out of the comfort zone. You don't guess height anymore. Pre-procedural multislice CT (MSCT) was the absolute linchpin of this operation. In typical TAVI cases, CT is standard for sizing; here, it was the blueprint for survival.

The team examined valve-to-coronary distances and interaction between the frames. Specifically, they paid attention to the relationship between the anticipated neo-skirt and the coronary ostia. The CT confirmed that by implanting the new valve at a specific level, that skirt would clear the coronary ostia.

Crucially, the minimum lumen diameter of the Evolut prosthesis was calculated at 13.7 mm, confirming severe haemodynamic compromise. This number guided the decision to perform the procedure. They didn't use standard fluoroscopic landmarks alone; they used 3D modelling logic to decide the 3D coordinates for the valve. The team elected for a "high" implantation strategy. They aligned the Acurate neo2 leaflets with the Evolut leaflets, ensuring maximum effective orifice area (EOA) and pushing the valve up to avoid compressing the coronary outflow tracts.

Imagine the view from inside the aortic valve: THREE rings of metal. The team’s goal was to ensure the top ring was locked onto the previous leaflets without crushing the middle ring's entrance to any coronary. They avoided coronary protection devices entirely, relying entirely on this pre-procedural CT data to ensure the device's haute temperature zone didn't shadow the arteries.

## The Procedural Execution

The procedure itself was a dance of pacing and precision.

*   **Access:** Left radial and left femoral arterial access.
*   **Safety:** Rapid ventricular pacing was initiated *simultaneously* with balloon pre-dilation (22/40 mm).
*   **Confirmation:** Aortography confirmed coronary flow was unobstructed before committing to the valve.

This simultaneous pacing during pre-dilation is a critical safety step in serial valve cases. It minimizes the pressure wave reflection in the ostia, reducing the risk of dislodging microemboli or momentarily compromising flow while the frame expands.

The implantation was guided by commissural alignment. This is vital in serial TAVI not just for OEA, but to position the valve dynamically. If you miss the alignment, the neo-skirt might catch the underlying frame and rip it off, or weld itself into a rigid structure that forces coronary obstruction. By ensuring the Acurate neo2 was aligned with the Evolut leaflets, they minimized the "landing zone" error that often leads to these "skyline" crashes.

## The Long Game

Post-procedure, the hemodynamics improved. There was an initial gradient of 67 mm Hg (consistent with PPM), which slowed to a manageable 26-27 mm Hg over the year. The EF remained preserved. At the 1-year follow-up, he was asymptomatic.

This is a massive testament to the durability of the strategy. Most TAV-in-TAV reports look for 30-day outcomes. This case, with its unique context of treated endocarditis and triple stenting, provides a rare 1-year window. It proves that even in a root that has been completely carpeted by metal, if you calculate the height correctly and align the commissures, you can restore a functioning valve—and access for the future.

The lesson here is that as we treat more "younger" TAVI patients (due to comorbidity burden rather than strict age cutoffs), we are creating a cohort of structural-valve-degenerated hearts that will require serial reintervention. The corridor is shrinking. The stakes for coronary occlusion are rising.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Height is Life:** In TAV-in-TAV procedures, the "high implantation" strategy is critical to ensure the neo-skirt of the new device does not compress coronary ostia previously obscured by stents.
*   **CT is Non-Negotiable:** Pre-procedural MSCT is the fundamental determinant for procedural strategy, specifically to evaluate valve-to-coronary distance and frame interaction between stented configurations.
*   **Pacing During Dilation:** Using rapid ventricular pacing *during* balloon pre-dilation in serial valve cases can protect coronary perfusion against the pressure waves generated by frame expansion.
*   **PVE Cured is PVE Treated:** Previous infective endocarditis does not automatically contraindicate a third TAVI, provided the infection has fully resolved, and the procedural plan accounts for the altered tissue planes from inflammation.

The "skyline" may be getting lower with every valve we stack, but with planning that goes beyond simple sizing, we can keep those arterial pathways open.
