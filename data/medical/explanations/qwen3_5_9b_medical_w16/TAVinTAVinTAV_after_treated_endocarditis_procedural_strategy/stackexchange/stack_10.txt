Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Clinical Pearls] Compare the immediate post-procedural hemodynamic profile to the 1-year follow-up regarding the mean transvalvular gradient and effective orifice area. What phenomenon may explain the significant initial elevation in gradient that resolved over the subsequent year?

**Clinical Teaching Answer: Hemodynamics and Structural Expansion in Serial TAVI**

When evaluating the serial hemodynamics of TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions, clinicians must recognize that immediate post-procedural gradients often overestimate the final functional valve performance due to acute mechanical constraints in the durational anatomy.

In this specific case report, the patient’s hemodynamic profile at the immediate post-procedural time point (June 7, 2024) was characterized by **severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM)**, with a mean transvalvular gradient of **67 mm Hg** and a small effective orifice area (AVA) of **0.9 cm²**. This occurred despite the successful deployment of the third valve. By contrast, at the 1-year follow-up (June 2025), the mean gradient had stabilized at **26 mm Hg** and the AVA had improved to **1.1 cm²**, accompanied by preserved leaflet motion and absence of regurgitation.

The significant resolution of the gradient and the improvement in AVA between implantation and the follow-up period can be attributed to **partial radial expansion of the underlying transcatheter stent frame** (specifically the prior Evolut prosthesis) facilitated by high-pressure post-dilation and strategic commissural alignment. In triple-stented anatomies, the new valve leaflets can initially occupy a compressed configuration; however, the intentional post-dilation (performed here with a 20/40 mm balloon) not only expanded the neo-valve but also exerts outward force on the underlying locked stenido-synovial frames.

This case illustrates that in complex serial TAVI, the **initial hemodynamic burden is partly mechanical, not perfusion-driven**. The high initial gradient reflects the cumulative stacking of stent frames and restricted flow geometry. The subsequent improvement derives from the **mechanical redistribution and expansion of the confined valve pouch**, allowing for better leaflet coaptation and unobstructed central flow velocity. Therefore, careful planning to utilize high-pressure post-dilation and strict commissural alignment—guided by preprocedural CT modeling—is critical to preventing permanent, severe PPM in high-risk serial reinterventions.
