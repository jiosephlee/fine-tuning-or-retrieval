Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Clinical Presentation and Pathophysiologic Background

The patient in this case is a male in his late 70s who presented with a high burden of complex comorbidities, rendering him a prohibitive candidate for conventional surgical aortic valve replacement. His cardiac history included irreversible third-degree atrioventricular block requiring permanent pacemaker implantation in 2014 and extensive three-vessel coronary artery disease necessitating multiple percutaneous coronary interventions. Concurrent non-cardiac comorbidities included arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding. These factors cumulatively produced a European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE II) of 27.36%, quantifying a high surgical mortality risk. In general cardiovascular practice, a EuroSCORE II above 15% strongly suggests that transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) offers a safety advantage over surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR). Consequently, every subsequent valve intervention was deemed necessary from a risk-stratification perspective, as open heart surgery on this anatomy would likely have required a complex total aortic root or ascending aorta replacement.

The underlying pathophysiologic driver for this case is the natural history of bioprosthetic valve degeneration, known as structural valve deterioration (SVD). Although transcatheter valves are designed for durability, they are not immune to wear and tear. In this patient, the initial 2014 implantation of a 27 mm Lotus bioprosthesis eventually succumbed to SVD resulting from leaflet fibrosis and calcification. By December 2021, the biomechanical integrity of the first prosthesis failed, manifesting as severe bioprosthetic valve stenosis. This condition is characterized by the formation of calcium deposits and fibrous tissue that restricts the opening surface area of the valve leaflets, leading to an obstruction of aortic flow. The progress of this SVD triggered a standard clinical escalation, necessitating a second transcatheter intervention in 2022 using a TAV-in-TAV strategy to overlay a new device (Evolut) within the failed one.

The clinical presentation during the periods of valve failure was dominated by symptoms of left ventricular pressure overload and heart failure. The natural history of severe aortic stenosis begins with asymptomatic progression, eventually evolving into symptomatic heart failure manifesting as exertional dyspnea, chest fatigue, dizziness, and syncope. In this specific case, the patient reported progressive reduction in functional capacity, with walking distances dropping to 200–300 meters prior to hospitalization. Echocardiographic assessment correlated these symptoms with significant hemodynamic compromise, specifically showing a mean aortic valve gradient of 83 mmHg in early 2022. The Left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) initially declined to 42%, a direct consequence of the chronic pressure overload imposed by the stenotic valve. This trajectory underscores the principle that severe gradients lead to compensatory hypertrophy and eventual diastolic dysfunction, eventually causing the systolic dysfunction seen in this patient.

Complicating the existing structural valve disease was an episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) in September 2022. The patient presented with persistent fever and worsening heart failure, leading to the identification of *Enterococcus faecalis* in the blood. The pathophysiology of infective endocarditis involves the seeding of bacteria onto the prosthetic material, vegetations, and subsequent tissue destruction. In the context of a TAV-in-TAV configuration, PVE does not necessarily involve a clean rupture of the avascular stent but can cause leaflet perforation or mechanical restriction through bacterial pitting and inflammation. While the infection was treated with targeted intravenous and oral ampicillin-based therapy, the milieu of inflammation and potential leaflet injury likely accelerated the SVD process. By early 2024, despite the resolution of active infection, the patient exhibited severe restenosis and regurgitation, indicating that the endocarditis had compromised the structural integrity of the already deteriorating bioprostheses.

Ultimately, the decision to proceed with a third transcatheter valve in 2024 was based on the integration of this complete medical profile. The patient’s veterinary risk (EuroSCORE II), his existing compromised anatomy (2014 TAV plus 2022 TAV), and his prior infection history created a "lock" situation where surgical explantation was too hazardous. The clinical team had to navigate the "lifetime management" of structural valve disease, balancing the cumulative risks of multiple overlapping stent frames against the mortality risk of inoperable heart failure. The presence of coronary artery disease and the pacemaker lead required careful planning to avoid coronary obstruction, while COPD and diabetes limited the physiological reserve for tolerance during complex procedures. This case illustrates a critical medical reality: as patients survive TAVI and it evolves into a first-line therapy, the management of the third and fourth valve replacements becomes a complex, long-term stratification of surgical risk, anatomical feasibility, and the durability of prior prosthetic materials.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Interventional Strategy Formulation

The decision to proceed with a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in this patient required a thorough evaluation of his complex cardiac history and current hemodynamic status. The clinical reasoning began with a diagnosis of worsening functional status and echocardiographic evidence of severe prosthetic valve failure following the second TAVI-in-TAVI procedure. By June 2024, the patient exhibited a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg and a significantly reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², confirming severe stenosis. Although the patient had a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) treated with *Enterococcus faecalis* three years prior, the current functional deterioration indicated that the valve degeneration had likely surpassed the threshold for medical management. To determine if intervention was safe, the medical team had to rigorously assess the status of prior infection and the feasibility of placing a valve inside two stented environments without aborting the patient's vascular stability.

The primary prerequisites for re-intervention were establishing complete resolution of the prior infection and ensuring compatibility of the procedural plan with the treated anatomical site. In this case, the criteria for infectious clearance were robust, mirroring standard guidelines for late PVE. Confirmatory evidence included negative follow-up blood cultures transposed from September 2022 to January 2024, completion of a 6-week course of intravenous ampicillin, and absence of vegetation on echocardiography. Crucially, pre-procedural assessment confirmed that the patient was off suppressive antibiotic therapy and showed no clinical or imaging signs of active endocarditis by the time of the June 2024 procedure. This clearance was essential because executing TAVI in the setting of active endocarditis carries high risks of embolization and spread of infection, necessitating strict antimicrobial control prior to vascular manipulation.

Once infection status was confirmed, the Heart Team undertook a risk-benefit analysis comparing serial transcatheter reinterventions against open surgical valve replacement (SAVR). The decision-making process heavily weighted the patient’s surgical risk profile, quantified by a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, alongside extensive comorbidities including arterial hypertension, coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes, and COPD. Furthermore, the anatomical constraints imposed by two previously implanted transcatheter prostheses rendered open surgery prohibitive; explantation of two bioprosthetic valves would likely require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement. In this context, TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV was favored not only for lower immediate procedural mortality compared to open heart surgery in a patient with severe comorbidities but also as a route to avoid cardiovascular trauma that the patient’s stent-framed anatomy might not tolerate surgically. This justified the choice of a catheter-based strategy despite the technical complexity.

Ultimately, the successful execution of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV strategy relied on strategic planning centered on the preservation of coronary ostia and optimization of hemodynamic performance. Imaging planning via multidetector computed tomography (MSCT) identified a minimum lumen diameter of the Evolut prosthesis at 13.7 mm, suggesting enough space for a third, smaller valve. However, with three overlapping frames, the risk of coronary obstruction was amplified, particularly given the patient’s underlying coronary artery disease with prior interventions. The procedural plan prioritized a high implantation depth, aligning the new Acurate neo2 valve with the leaflets of the Evolut valve rather than the stent frame. This deliberate positioning prevented inward leaflet folding, preserved the effective orifice area, and kept the neo-skirt well below the coronary ostia. By leveraging preprocedural CT modeling to visualize valve-to-coronary distances, the team ensured that the third intervention could be safeguarded against the specific risks of coronary occlusion, thereby achieving a sustainable outcome for the patient’s long-term management.

## Preprocedural Imaging and Procedural Planning

In the context of serial transcatheter aortic valve interventions, particularly when addressing a third-generation failure such as TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) serves as the cornerstone of procedural planning. As demonstrated in this case, MDCT is not merely a screening tool but a decision-making engine that dictates valve selection, sizing, and implantation strategy. The complexity of overlapping stent frames from the initial Lotus valve and the subsequent Evolut valve requires a precise evaluation of the annular geometry to mitigate risks of coronary obstruction, left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, and paravalvular leak. Unlike single-intervention TAVI, where the annular plane is relatively distinct, sequential valve implantations create a nested anatomy where the landing zone for the third valve is defined by the exterior landing interface of the second-prosthesis frame. In this patient, the preprocedural multislice CT was critical in confirming the resolution of the recent prosthetic valve endocarditis and quantifying the accessible annular perimeter, ensuring that the risk of vegetation had subsided before subjecting the anatomy to further instrumentation.

A critical component of the planning process involves the quantitative assessment of the landing zone dimensions and the minimum lumen diameter of the underlying stent frame. In this case, MDCT analysis revealed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the second-generation Evolut prosthesis. This measurement directly influences the sizing of the third valve and the severity of necessary predilation. A smaller landing zone, such as that approaching 14 mm in a frame that was originally over-stented to preserve the luminal area of the second valve, increases the risk of fracture during passive inner-fracture or high-pressure balloon post-dilation. The technical team utilized these parameters to calculate the cumulative burden of radial expansion required, ensuring that the balancing force applied during predilation would not compromise the structural integrity of the underlying Lotus and Evolut valve frames. This step is pedagogically essential for understanding that the third valve must physically expand the second valve frame, making the structural strength of the pre-existing scaffolding a variable in the differential pressure gradient calculations during the procedure.

Furthermore, the evaluation of coronary artery anatomy is paramount in triple-valve scenarios due to the increased risk of geometric occlusion. Preprocedural MDCT allows for the reconstruction of valve-to-coronary height distances and the interaction between the anticipated neo-skirt and the coronary ostia. In this specific case, the CT planning focused on the relationship between the neo-skirt of the upcoming third valve and the coronary ostia, analyzing the sinus of Valsalva dimensions. The preservation of coronary perfusion relies heavily on avoiding the sealing of the new neo-skirt or frame over the coronary mouths. While coronary protection devices, such as the PAILS strategy or embolic protection systems, are often utilized in high-risk cases, the multidisciplinary team in this instance determined that the CT-guided geometry allowed for a high-implantation strategy without explicit coronary protection. This decision was predicated on the specific measurements of the aortic root and the clearance provided by the high implantation depth, which prevented the new valve frame from encroaching upon the coronary ostia.

Valve selection is another critical outcome of the CT-guided planning process, driven largely by the need to optimize the effective orifice area (EOA) and preserve future access. The decision to implant an Acurate neo2 self-expanding prosthesis rather than a balloon-expandable option was guided by its frame geometry, which is amenable to high implantation relative to native tissue. High implantation of the third valve, positioned to align with the leaflets of the second Evolut valve, was a deliberate strategy to maximize the EOA. By placing the neo-valve at the leaflet level of the deployed Evolut, the team aimed to coat the underlying leaflet structure to prevent leaflet collapse and restriction. This alignment is crucial for maintaining hemodynamic function, preventing prosthesis-patient mismatch, and ensuring that the third valve does not create a concentric restriction within the already narrowed landing zone. The self-expanding nature of the Acurate device facilitates this deployment by conforming to the irregularities of the stented annulus while allowing for post-dilation under rapid pacing to ensure apposition.

Finally, the planning for commissural alignment serves as the final optimization strategy for hemodynamic efficiency and procedural safety. The preprocedural CT and 3D modeling provided the roadmap for identifying the optimal landing height where the commissures would restrict the third valve frame from bulging into the sino-tubular junction. By aligning the three stacked valves, the team sought to minimize the residual transvalvular gradient and patient-prosthesis mismatch. However, this strategy also demands an acknowledgment of future limitations; the cumulative mesh burden of three valves may complicate potential coronary interventions in the future. Therefore, the CT analysis included not just immediate feasibility but the long-term prognosis of coronary access. The patient’s subsequent follow-up confirmed that the prioritization of commissural alignment and high implantation successfully preserved coronary flow and relieved severe stenosis, validating the MDCT-driven approach for complex, serial aortic valve disease. This case underscores that while surgical risk abnormalities (EuroSCORE II) eliminated open surgical options, comprehensive preprocedural imaging enabled a strategic transcatheter solution that extended the functional lifespan of the valve.

## Procedural Execution: Technical Nuances of Triple Valve Implantation

The technical execution of a third-generation transcatheter valve implantation necessitates a strategy that prioritizes anatomical precision and hemodynamic safety over speed. In this patient, the procedural team utilized a dual arterial access approach, engaging both the left radial and left femoral arteries to manage the procedural load and optimize positioning of complex guiding systems. A Safari guidewire was advanced from the femoral access into the left ventricle, establishing a stable baseline for device advancement. Balloon pre-dilation was performed using a 22/40 mm catheter under rapid ventricular pacing, which was maintained at 180 beats per minute. This high-rate pacing serves a critical role in controlling hemodynamics during the dilation phase; by limiting cardiac filling diastole and increasing stroke volume, it improves aortic flow and significantly reduces the risk of microvascular ischemia or thromboembolic complications that might arise from transient hemodynamic collapse or back-bleeding during the high-pressure dilation of the calcified or struts of prior prostheses.

Central to the procedural success in a triple-stented anatomy is the precise implantation depth and commissural alignment. The team selected a self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (Acurate neo2) but employed a high deployment strategy, positioning the device at the leaflet level of the previously implanted Evolut valve. This supra-annular alignment was deliberate; it prevented the formation of a "neo-skirt" of material extending below the coronary ostia, which could obscure future vascular access or increase the risk of coronary obstruction. By fixing the pre-existing Evolut valve leaflets against the stent frame of the new prosthesis, the team aimed to maximize the effective orifice area and mitigate the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch. This approach minimizes the cumulative burden of overlapping frames, acknowledging that while necessary for relief of severe stenosis, every layer of metal complicates future coronary or re-interventional access.

During the procedure, real-time monitoring was critical to verifying safety before finalizing the deployment. Aortography was performed to track balloon integrity, and simultaneously, coronary flow patency was confirmed immediately following pre-dilation to ensure no stent strut had accidentally impinged on the coronary ostia, a particular concern given the history of endocarditis and valvular calcification. Following successful deployment, the new valve was post-dilated using a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid ventricular pacing to ensure maximal radial expansion of the frame and mobility of the leaflets. Final aortography verified correct positioning, which confirmed that the prosthesis was fully deployed without obstructing the coronary ostia or introducing significant paravalvular leaks. This meticulous verification of coronary flow and valve profile ensured the procedural safety of the complex triple-valve configuration, supporting the feasibility of treating structural degeneration even with multiple prior valve stents.

## 1-Year Clinical Outcomes and Broader Clinical Significance

The clinical trajectory of this patient at the one-year mark demonstrates the potential feasibility of complex serial transcatheter valve interventions even in high-risk anatomies with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis. At the one-year follow-up (June 2025), the patient remained asymptomatic and active, with no signs of heart failure decompensation or recurrent infection. Transthoracic echocardiography confirmed structural stability, reporting a mean transvalvular gradient of 26 mm Hg, an aortic valve area (AVA) of 1.1 cm², and a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. These metrics indicate that while the patient remains within a moderately stenotic range, the valve function is hemodynamically adequate and clinically free of dyspnoea. This stability underscores that, provided infection is eradicated and the procedure is meticulously planned, a third transcatheter intervention can offer durable survival and functional benefit where surgical intervention is prohibitive.

Hemodynamic evaluation of the triple-stented system required careful interpretation of Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) criteria regarding success and device performance. Post-procedural data showed an initial severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM) at 1-month, with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg and an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m². However, subsequent imaging at one month and one year showed marked improvement, with gradients decreasing to 26–27 mm Hg and the AVA increasing to 1.1 cm². This trajectory highlights that high implantation strategies in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures can mitigate mismatch risk over time. The improvement is likely attributed to the high-pressure post-dilation of the Evolut frame and commissural alignment of the Acurate neo2, which prevented inward leaflet folding of the underlying stents. For the resident, this emphasizes that immediate post-procedural hemodynamics may reflect transient resistance, and long-term dilation of the overlapping frames can optimize the functional AOA if structural integrity is maintained.

Given the patient's history of *Enterococcus faecalis* prosthetic valve endocarditis, monitoring for infectious recurrence was a critical component of follow-up management. Clinical clearance was established prior to the final procedure once blood cultures were negative and transoesophageal echocardiography confirmed resolution of vegetations and paravalvular complications. Over the one-year period, there were no clinical signs of fever, new murmurs, or embolic events, and repeat blood cultures remained sterile. This reinforces the principle that patients with treated PVE who lack active infection may be managed for prosthetic dysfunction using standard structural heart disease criteria, though they remain at higher risk for recurrent events. The decision to pursue the third valve required a multidisciplinary heart team discussion to balance the risks of repeated transcatheter interventions against the prohibitive surgical risk of explantation with prior endocarditis and multiple failed bioprostheses.

A defining feature of this case is the management of future coronary access within a triple-stented anatomy. While the procedure utilized preprocedural CT-derived planning to ensure coronary osteal height clearance, the presence of three overlapping valve frames significantly diminishes the elasticity of the aortic runoff and may obstruct future diagnostic coronary angiography or percutaneous coronary intervention. The case illustrates that aggressive commissural alignment was employed to maximize the distance between the neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, preserving at least one coronary artery. Clinicians must counsel patients that while coronary flow is preserved initially, future access for coronary artery disease may remain technically challenging or require retrograde approaches and overwire balloon tactics. This anatomical constraint prioritizes the sustainability of current valve placement and must be explicitly weighed before the first intervention in younger or long-survivor candidates who are likely to require future coronary care.

The broader clinical significance of this case extends to the philosophy of lifetime management for structural heart disease. As the population of transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) patients ages, clinicians face the reality of "valve-in-valve" replantations as a habitual step rather than an exception. This scenario illustrates that initial intervention strategies should account for the likelihood of structural valve deterioration, potentially favoring platforms with lower frame heights or specific commissural alignment capabilities to facilitate future re-interventions. Ultimately, the one-year outcome validates the use of CT-guided, strategy-driven TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV as a viable salvage therapy for high-risk patients with complex degeneration and treated endocarditis. It serves as a practical lesson in balancing procedural complexity with long-term durability, where shared decision-making and centralized imaging assessment are prerequisites for safe outcomes.
