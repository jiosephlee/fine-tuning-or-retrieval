# The Guideline Gap: Treating Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis When Surgery is Contraindicated

Imagine a patient who has survived a valve replacement three times. For context, the average patient gets one heart valve replacement in their lifetime. But this gentleman, now in his late 70s, returned for his third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) just when most clinicians are thinking about cartilage degeneration, not cardiac catheterization. The complexity here isn’t just the mechanical challenge of nesting three valves within the aortic root; it is the management of a messy reality where standard algorithms hit a wall. This case report pulls back the curtain on a "forced" decision that defies the textbook approach to prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) because the operating room was a contraindication on every possible metric.

The tension in this case lies squarely in the Guideline Gap. The 2025 ESC guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis advocate for surgical valve replacement in PVE, particularly when complicated by heart failure or uncontrolled infection. For a modern Heart Team, surgery is often the gold standard for infected prosthetic material. However, in this patient, the EuroSCORE II was a staggering 27.36%, compounded by COPD, diabetes, multiple prior PCIs, and aortic structures compromised by two prior TAVI procedures (Lotus 27mm in 2014 and Evolut 23mm in 2022). In this specific anatomical landscape, open surgery would have necessitated a total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement. To send this patient to the OT was to prioritize the guideline over the patient's survival probability.

The deciding factor in moving away from surgery wasn't just risk; it was the infection itself. Throughout the 2021-2023 period, the patient experienced severe restenosis and incidentally developed endocarditis caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*. In standard practice, active PVE at the time of a structural valve deterioration mandates treating the infection first. In this case, the team managed the *E. faecalis* with a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy, including six weeks of IV ampicillin followed by oral treatment, resulting in clinical signs of resolution. TEE and MSCT confirmed no active vegetations or paravalvular complications before the decision to intervene again. This seriation of events is critical: you cannot deploy a third valve over active infection, but once treated and resolved, procedural decision-making shifted to anatomical feasibility rather than infective control.

Technically, the goal was a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV. The strategy was driven by CT planning to ensure coronary clearance. With multiple overlapping stent frames, the risk of coronary artery obstruction is the primary technical nightmare. The team elected for a high implantation strategy, aligning the new Acurate neo2 prosthesis with the leaflets of the Evolut valve rather than the previous Lotus frame. This avoidance of "neo-skirt" formation below the coronary ostia was essential to preserve coronaries without the use of a protective device, given the prohibitive surgical risk that would otherwise dictate an otherwise life-saving coronary intervention.

The outcome at one year suggests that when surgical risk is truly prohibitive, a "forced" catheter-based strategy can provide durable hemodynamic benefit. The third intervention avoided an ascending aortic replacement, and despite a functional drop in leaflet motion that caused severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM) immediately post-op, the balloon post-dilation optimised effective orifice area over the subsequent month. The takeaway is not that TAVI for PVE is a new standard of care, but that when open surgery is incompatible with survival, these complex anatomical scenarios demand the same rigorous imaging and multidisciplinary planning used for native valve disease. Guidelines provide the anchor, but in high-risk, unstable vessels, individualized Heart Team decision-making provides the only viable compass.

### Key Take-Home Messages
*   When surgical risk is extremely high (EuroSCORE II > 20% or prohibitive anatomic barriers like triple-stented aortic roots) in PVE, TAVI is a viable alternative despite guideline preference for surgery.
*   Active infective endocarditis must be treated and resolved with clinical/radiographic confirmation (TEE/CT) prior to implanting a new valve.
*   In TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenarios, high implantation and commissural alignment are critical to preserve coronary access and minimize prosthesis-patient mismatch.
*   Detailed preprocedural CT planning is non-negotiable for sequential TAVI interventions to assess frame interaction and valve-to-coronary distances.
*   Long-term surveillance is vital, as patients with serial TAVI interventions require lifetime management of structural valve deterioration.



# More Than a Scan: Strategic CT Planning for Triple-Stent Anatomy

TAVI is no longer the "new standard"; for the growing population of survivors, it is part of a lifelong strategy. While we are well-versed in the concept of TAV-in-TAV to treat failed bioprostheses, the inverse—performing an intervention on a patient with a history of endocarditis—is rarer. It became more complex when we examine a case requiring a third valve implantation in a stented system that evolved over a decade.

Consider the case of a 77-year-old man requiring a third TAVI following two prior transcatheter procedures and a managed episode of endocarditis. By 2024, this patient needed a third valve. We are dealing with a Lotus from 2014, an Evolut from 2022, and a post-endocarditis deterioration that necessitated an Acurate neo2. The anatomical challenge isn't just fitting a balloon in a small aortic root; it's navigating a forest of metal struts and managing the increased risk of coronary obstruction. This blog post explores how pre-procedural multislice CT (MSCT) guided the decision-making to make a third intervention feasible in a triple-stent anatomy.

## The Triple-Valve Challenge

In high-risk patients, the "one-and-done" era of structural heart disease is ending. With improved durability in patients requiring repeat procedures, technical challenges are escalating. In this specific scenario, the patient presented with dyspnoea, a history of CAD, and paraortic con disease from the outset. The history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) caused by *Enterococcus faecalis* adds a layer of complexity to the structural valve failure.

Critically, the decision to intervene was not made because the infection failed to resolve, but because the valve structure physically collapsed after the infection was cleared. By January 2024, the calculated aortic valve area was down to $0.5 cm^{2}$ with a $49 mm Hg$ mean gradient. The team faced a choice between surgery or medication. Given the prior TAVIs and the patient's comorbidities (COPD, diabetes, prior GI bleeding), surgery carried a prohibitive EuroSCORE II of 27.36%. The question became: could we safely squeeze another valve in without blocking the coronary arteries?

## Pre-Procedure CT Planning

The pivotal moment in this case was the pre-procedural MSCT. This wasn't just a procedural readiness scan; it was a strategic dashboard. The balancing act in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is one side of the equation: Avoiding coronary obstruction. Because we have three overlapping frames, the risk of the "neo-skirt" of the new valve encroaching on the coronaries is high if we are not meticulous.

The CT analysis revealed a minimum lumen diameter of $13.7 mm$ within the Evolut prosthesis. This specific measurement dictated feasibility, but we went deeper than just size. The MSCT team analyzed the sinus of Valsalva dimensions and the valve-to-coronary distance. Most importantly, they looked at the interaction between the frame of the Lotus, the Evolut, and the landing zone for the Panther Acurate neo2.

A critical decision derived from this planning was the implantation depth. We needed a strategy to maximize the Effective Orifice Area (EOA) while mitigating Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM). Committal alignment was not an option here; implanting based purely on radiopaque markers (like C-arm) wasn't enough when we were looking for non-violating fit against two prior devices. The CT suggested a high implantation, aligning the new struts with the Evolut leaflets rather than the stent frame. This prevented inward leaflet folding by the neo-stent, which is a known mechanism for PPM in serial TAVI. By preserving the leaflets against the frame of the previous valve, we allowed the Evolut to function as a bed for leaflets rather than a complete constriction. This CT-guided strategy allowed the team to deploy without the need for coronary protection devices—a considerable procedural simplification.

## Procedural Strategy and Implantation

Armed with the CT data, the procedure utilized left radial and left femoral access. Using a Safari guidewire (LM and FM visualized well), they pre-dilated the valve with a 22/40 mm balloon under rapid ventricular pacing. This ensures synchronized release of the heart, a standard but critical step in redo TAVI to prevent interference with restrictions in the valve frame. Aortography confirmed every step of coronary flow unobstruction before the neo-valve joined.

The Acurate neo2 (size S, self-expanding) was chosen for its supra-annular leaflet positioning and low frame height, matching the CT findings of limited landing zone available. Deployment had to be perfect to avoid paravalvular leak or coronary occlusion. Post-dilatation with a 20/40 mm balloon was performed off-pump to optimize expansion, specifically to address the mechanical restriction post-infection. The strategy relied heavily on the CT-assessed frame interaction, ensuring that the Acurate neo2's neo-skirt would not violate the coronary ostia height prescribed in the pre-procedural model.

## Outcomes and Follow-up

The immediate result post-procedure showed successful deployment without regurgitation (Grade 0), but the hemodynamics were initially concerning with a mean gradient of $67 mm Hg$ and an indexed AVA of $0.4 cm^{2}/m^{2}$. This is classic for PPM in serial reviews. However, serial echocardiography tracked a significant improvement over time. One month in, the mean gradient dropped to $27 mm Hg$ with an AVA of $1.1 cm^{2}$. Left ventricular remodeling was observed, and the patient remains asymptomatic at 1-year follow-up in 2025, with an EF of 65%.

This timeline highlights the importance of the 1-year mark as the first benchmark of durability. For the Heart Team, the evidence suggests that with careful CT planning and the right valve profile selection, a third transcatheter valve is a viable option even after endocarditis and multiple prior stents.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is becoming more common** as patient survival outpaces initial valve design durability, particularly in high-risk patient profiles.
*   **Endocarditis history does not contraindicate TAVI**, provided the infection is fully treated and no active vegetations remain on TEE or CT.
*   **Multi-slice CT (MSCT) is mandatory**, not optional, for third-generation indications to accurately assess the minimum lumen diameter ($13.7 mm$ in this case) and plan commissural alignment.
*   **High implantation is a strategic choice** in serial procedures to align the new valve leaflets with the prior frame, preserving Effective Orifice Area (EOA) and preventing PPM.
*   **Coronary preservation must be prioritized**, as overlapping stent frames (Lotus, Evolut, Acurate in this case) significantly reduce the safety margin for coronary ostial access.

Now, you remember that in structural heart disease, a scan is never just a picture; it's the blueprint for the reconstruction. Stay sharp on your volumetric analysis, because as we treat younger and longer-lived populations, "Triple-Stent" is a finite anatomy but an increasingly common reality.



# The Coronary 'Skyline' Risk: Pre-empting Occlusion in Serial Valve Implantation

There’s a persistent myth in structural heart disease that transcatther aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is the "easy button" for aortic stenosis. Pilot programs in the 2000s may support that notion, but the real data is telling a different story. When patients survive the initial intervention—and fate has them live for another decade beyond their projected life expectancy at diagnosis—a cascade of complications often begins to detonate. We are now treating patients who need valve-in-valve, TAV-in-TAV, and literally, TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.

The clinical challenge isn't just getting a new valve into the tough space; it’s doing so without destroying the plumbing that feeds the heart itself. We are essentially stacking stents inside stents inside stents, and with every layer, coronary artery obstruction becomes a ticking time bomb. A recent case report by Merkulov et al. offers a masterclass in navigating this treacherous skyline, focusing specifically on how to plant a third valve while preserving coronary access for the future.

## The Anatomy of a Trap

In a standard redo-TAVI, we already face the dilemma of overlapping stents reducing the effective orifice area. But add a third iteration, and the geometry changes fundamentally. The "neo-skirt"—the expansion of the new valve’s seal to the calcific or fibrotic invasion layer—can become a cul-de-sac. If you don't plant the valve high enough, that skirt folds back down like a curtain closing on a stage. In this scenario, the membrane of the new valve presses against the ostia of previously diseased coronary arteries that are already encased in old metallicwork.

This risk is amplified when the patient presents with infective endocarditis history. Infection causes leaflet injury and accelerated structural valve deterioration, but it also creates irregular calcium and tissue planes that make calculating plane height difficult. Management is no longer just about getting through the aortic root; it’s about precision window for valve implantation height.

In this case, a 70-something male patient walked quite the tightrope. It started with a 27 mm Lotus valve in 2014 due to severe stenosis. By 2022, he refused the idea of open surgery for a second TAVI in a failing Lotus frame, opting for a 23 mm Evolut prosthesis. The result was a TAV-in-TAV configuration. That, however, was not the end of the road.

In late 2022, he developed prosthetic valve endocarditis (*Enterococcus faecalis*). After treating the infection fully with antibiotics and confirming resolution via TEE and MSCT, his valve failed again mere months later. Severe restenosis and gradients of 49 mm Hg screamed for reintervention. Now, he needed a third valve: an Acurate neo2.

## The CT-Guided "Aerial Survey"

This is where the Heart Team steps out of the comfort zone. You don't guess height anymore. Pre-procedural multislice CT (MSCT) was the absolute linchpin of this operation. In typical TAVI cases, CT is standard for sizing; here, it was the blueprint for survival.

The team examined valve-to-coronary distances and interaction between the frames. Specifically, they paid attention to the relationship between the anticipated neo-skirt and the coronary ostia. The CT confirmed that by implanting the new valve at a specific level, that skirt would clear the coronary ostia.

Crucially, the minimum lumen diameter of the Evolut prosthesis was calculated at 13.7 mm, confirming severe haemodynamic compromise. This number guided the decision to perform the procedure. They didn't use standard fluoroscopic landmarks alone; they used 3D modelling logic to decide the 3D coordinates for the valve. The team elected for a "high" implantation strategy. They aligned the Acurate neo2 leaflets with the Evolut leaflets, ensuring maximum effective orifice area (EOA) and pushing the valve up to avoid compressing the coronary outflow tracts.

Imagine the view from inside the aortic valve: THREE rings of metal. The team’s goal was to ensure the top ring was locked onto the previous leaflets without crushing the middle ring's entrance to any coronary. They avoided coronary protection devices entirely, relying entirely on this pre-procedural CT data to ensure the device's haute temperature zone didn't shadow the arteries.

## The Procedural Execution

The procedure itself was a dance of pacing and precision.

*   **Access:** Left radial and left femoral arterial access.
*   **Safety:** Rapid ventricular pacing was initiated *simultaneously* with balloon pre-dilation (22/40 mm).
*   **Confirmation:** Aortography confirmed coronary flow was unobstructed before committing to the valve.

This simultaneous pacing during pre-dilation is a critical safety step in serial valve cases. It minimizes the pressure wave reflection in the ostia, reducing the risk of dislodging microemboli or momentarily compromising flow while the frame expands.

The implantation was guided by commissural alignment. This is vital in serial TAVI not just for OEA, but to position the valve dynamically. If you miss the alignment, the neo-skirt might catch the underlying frame and rip it off, or weld itself into a rigid structure that forces coronary obstruction. By ensuring the Acurate neo2 was aligned with the Evolut leaflets, they minimized the "landing zone" error that often leads to these "skyline" crashes.

## The Long Game

Post-procedure, the hemodynamics improved. There was an initial gradient of 67 mm Hg (consistent with PPM), which slowed to a manageable 26-27 mm Hg over the year. The EF remained preserved. At the 1-year follow-up, he was asymptomatic.

This is a massive testament to the durability of the strategy. Most TAV-in-TAV reports look for 30-day outcomes. This case, with its unique context of treated endocarditis and triple stenting, provides a rare 1-year window. It proves that even in a root that has been completely carpeted by metal, if you calculate the height correctly and align the commissures, you can restore a functioning valve—and access for the future.

The lesson here is that as we treat more "younger" TAVI patients (due to comorbidity burden rather than strict age cutoffs), we are creating a cohort of structural-valve-degenerated hearts that will require serial reintervention. The corridor is shrinking. The stakes for coronary occlusion are rising.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Height is Life:** In TAV-in-TAV procedures, the "high implantation" strategy is critical to ensure the neo-skirt of the new device does not compress coronary ostia previously obscured by stents.
*   **CT is Non-Negotiable:** Pre-procedural MSCT is the fundamental determinant for procedural strategy, specifically to evaluate valve-to-coronary distance and frame interaction between stented configurations.
*   **Pacing During Dilation:** Using rapid ventricular pacing *during* balloon pre-dilation in serial valve cases can protect coronary perfusion against the pressure waves generated by frame expansion.
*   **PVE Cured is PVE Treated:** Previous infective endocarditis does not automatically contraindicate a third TAVI, provided the infection has fully resolved, and the procedural plan accounts for the altered tissue planes from inflammation.

The "skyline" may be getting lower with every valve we stack, but with planning that goes beyond simple sizing, we can keep those arterial pathways open.



# The PPM Paradox: Hemodynamic Realities in Cumulative Valve Stacking

When we talk about Prosthesis-Patient Mismatch (PPM) in TAVI, we usually think of it as a static measurement of failure—a valve doesn't open wide enough, the patient suffers, and that's it. Throw in a "gene genealogist" of valve implantations, and things get messy. But when you see the hemodynamic reality in serial increasing, it can be deceptive. Specifically, a patient who presents immediately after a third TAV-I procedure with severe PPM and a gradient of 67 mm Hg seems beaten on pre-dose for good. One year later, you find mean gradient 26 mm Hg and AVA 1.1 cm². The numbers have completely flipped, yet the decision to implant the third valve was technically sound, the patient refused surgery due to prohibitive EuroSCORE, and the infection history is cleanly resolved. It makes me wonder about the dynamics of valve frame settling in a stack. Let's look at this case of a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV to understand why these numbers behave the way they do over time and how we manage these stacked treasures.

## The Clinical Narrative

This patient started out in clinical overdrive, requiring multiple percutaneous coronary interventions starting in 2009 before the aortic valve deteriorated. The team opted for a 27 mm Lotus valve in 2014. By 2022, that first poroma contracted into severe stenosis with a mean gradient of 83 mm Hg. A TAV-in-TAV procedure was performed with a 23 mm Evolut Pro. It worked initially, but complications arose three months later with a pseudoaneurysm of the femoral pseudo. Despite this vascular hurdle, the LV ejection fraction improved from 42 to 54, a clear success.

However, the bioprosthetic TAVI doesn't always hold up for the long haul under nervous biology. The patient developed left ventricular dysfunction, Enterococcus faecalis infective endocarditis, and severe necrosis within nine months of the second TAVI. After aggressive antimicrobial therapy, the infection resolved, but then the stack failed again by 2024. This was the third TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure. The critical juncture here is not just the stacking itself, but the immediate hemodynamics.

The post-procedural echo showed a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg immediately after the third implantation. The AVA was 0.9 cm² and the indexed AVA was 0.4 cm²/m²—this is the definition of severe PPM. A Doppler velocity index of 0.24 confirms the restriction. By all mathematical definitions, this intervention was a failure due to mismatch. Yet, at one month, the same echo showed the gradient had dropped to 27 mm Hg and the AVA had expanded to 1.1 cm². One year out, the patient was asymptomatic with stable LV function. Why did the valve suddenly un-stenose?

## Understanding the Hemodynamic Fluctuation

The case discussion outlines a plausible explanation for this shift, which we must respect when counseling high-risk candidates with stacked valves. The immediate severe PPM dropped 1 year later likely due to the mechanical integration of the new valve with the prior structure, specifically the Evolut frame. The third implant, an Acurate neo2 (a self-expanding valve), was deployed with a strategy of high implantation to preserve coronary ostia and potential coronary access.

But more importantly, the immediate post-dilation might have not been the only force at work. The Discussion report attributes the disappearance of the transvalvular gradient to a combination of optimized commissural alignment, effective post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame. The bacteria-free, post-treatment environment of the annulus likely allowed for improved leaflet kinetics over time.

PPM is traditionally viewed as a post-dilating phenomenon, but in stacked setups, frame expansion can continue to exert pressure over time. The "PPM Paradox" is that we use immediate post-proc data to judge long-term function, but in serial TAVIs, the geometry isn't rigid. We see that high implantation can help fix the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame to prevent further reduction of effective orifice. This is crucial, as a pre-procedural CT scan guided the choice of the Acurate neo2 specifically for its supra-annular leaflet positioning and low frame height to reduce neo-skirt formation.

## Procedural Strategy in the Era of Multi-Stacking

This case highlights a challenging reality: when the surgical risk exceeds the EuroSCORE threshold (this case was a staggering 27.36%), we must settle for transcatheter options that are technically demanding. PVE complicates this, but the guidelines support TAVI if the infection is treated, and there are no vegetations or paravalvular lesions at the moment of intervention.

Here, the team utilized pre-procedural mixed reconstruction to visualize the valve-to-coronary distance and determine the relationship between the anticipated neo-skirt and the ostia. This was the bridge between immediate technical feasibility and long-term viability. The patient refused open surgery, so the TAVI choice was moral and physiological necessity.

Another critical detail was the exclusion of coronary reaccess. While the team attempted commissural alignment to preserve future coronary access, the cumulative mesh burden was significant. The procedure was off-label and approved, but the question remains: can a triple stent reliably accommodate future coronary interventions if another failure occurs? The report suggest that future approaches like BASILICA (Intentional Laceration) might be considered, though this is uncharted territory for a triple-stented anatomy.

## Take-Home Messages

Here is what you should carry in your PACS:

*   TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is more than just "fitting a valve inside a valve"—it requires complex valve-specific planning to manage non-standard frame geometry.
*   Preliminary immediate post-procedure gradients indicate severe PPM in stacked valves, but hemodynamic data can improve significantly over 12 months due to frame expansion and leaflet alignment.
*   Infective endocarditis history does not preclude TAVI if the infection is resolved and anatomical feasibility is confirmed by multislice CT before intervention.
*   estratégically, high implantation depth with self-expanding valves may assist in mitigating future access by preserving coronary clearance and optimizing commissural alignment.
*   Pre-procedural CT remains the gold standard for mitigating unexpected frame interactions and neo-skirt formation risks in multi-stent configurations.



# Beyond the 'One-and-Done': Lifetime Management and the 'Re-Intervention Pipeline'

There was a time when Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation (TAVI) was a palliative bridge for the high-risk patient—a "one-and-done" procedure intended to save them from the OR and allow them to live comfortable lives without surgical re-intervention. But as we push longevity in this population, that "forever" is becoming all too short. We are witnessing a demographic shift where patients stay alive long enough to develop structural valve deterioration, and their second (and sometimes third) TAVI is becoming the reality, not the exception.

Today, we look at a fascinating case report from Russian authors published in *EBM Heart*. It details a 70-year-old man who made it to a **third** TAVI procedure—a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention—after a complicated history of bioprosthetic endocarditis. The question isn't just whether he survived the catheter lab; it's about the strategic planning required to keep him alive for the next decade. This case challenges us to move beyond the initial implantation mindset and start viewing TAVI as the beginning of a polymodal cardiovascular trajectory.

Let's unravel the timeline before we get technical. The patient first underwent a 27 mm Lotus TAVI in 2014. It wasn't his first interaction with the lab, either; he'd already had multiple stents for coronary artery disease. By 2022, symptoms returned. A decision was made to implant an Evolut 23 mm valve *inside* the original Lotus (TAV-in-TAV). This, however, wasn't a smooth ride. The patient developed a pulsatile femoral hematoma requiring open vascular repair. But the twists didn't end there. Three months later in 2022, he presented with fever and explicit signs of Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE) caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*.

He was treated aggressively, clearing the infection with ampicillin. By 2024, he progressed to severe restenosis of the valve. Surgery was off the table due to prohibitive risk (EuroSCORE II of 27.36%) and complex anatomy. The team opted for a third transcatheter intervention using an Acurate neo2 valve. This was the third device stacked onto the first two.

What makes this case particularly instructive is the decision to proceed *post-endocarditis*. While guidelines typically recommend surgery for PVE in high-risk anatomy, the technical risks of redo surgery on top of two stacked catheter frames often preclude this here. The key to this case's success wasn't just the intervention, it was the interrogation of the anatomy *immediately before* the decision was made.

Specifically, the procedural team utilized Multi-Slice CT (MSCT) to guide the strategy of "High Implantation." They didn't just drop the valve in; they modeled the trajectory to align with the Evolut valve leaflets. This was critical for two reasons. First, it prevented the neo-skirt of the new valve from extending below the coronary ostia, mitigating the risk of coronary obstruction in a triple-stented frame. Second, it helped preserve the effective orifice area (EOA). In simple anatomical terms, if you plant a third valve too low, the top two valves get squeezed down, causing prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM). By anchoring high, they kept net flow dynamics acceptable.

You might ask: "If PPM is so common in iterative TAVI, why did he do well at one-year follow-up?" The answer lies in the optimization strategy. They used rapid ventricular pacing to stabilize flow during aortic balloon pre-dilation and post-dilation. The post-procedure mean gradient was initially high (67 mm Hg), indicative of severe PPM, but dropped to 26 mm Hg by one year. This improvement was attributed to a combination of post-dilatation optimization and left ventricular remodeling. The patient remained asymptomatic with preserved EF.

This trajectory highlights a critical gap in our standard workflow. In the excitement of relief for a high-risk patient, we often forget to discuss the "next step." Performing a third TAVI creates a nightmare for future coronary access and significantly complicates the risk profile for even a fourth intervention. The payback cycle is long, and the equipment list gets more complex with every layer. Each stack adds to the radial force required to cross the aortic root for any future intervention.

Furthermore, this case reinforces that the decision matrix for PVE survivors isn't just about mortality risk; it's about anatomical feasibility. The patient had three distinct comorbidities driving the decision: infection control, prohibitive surgical risk, and the mechanical constraints of existing frames. If you look at the current ESC Guidelines, surgery is still the gold standard for PVE. However, when the anatomy screams "surgery risks tangential injury to the ascending aorta" and valve-encompassing surgical risk is astronomical, TAVI remains the viable bridge to the future.

While this result is encouraging—grafting the third valve, stabilizing the heart, and avoiding open surgery—it serves as a stark warning about the "plumbing" of our future work. The valves in this patient are locked into the wall. If the third valve fails, surgical explantation will require total aortic root replacement. If another prosthesis fails, there might be no solution at all. We cannot treat TAVI as a discrete event; we must treat it as a long-term structural engineering project.

Here, we need to be radically collaborative in the Heart Team. The issue isn't just fixing the leak today; it's ensuring we don't make an open surgery of tomorrow for a valve that is 50% failed.

## Key Takehome Messages

*   TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is a feasible strategy for high-risk patients with failed valves, but it requires rigorous preprocedural CT planning to ensure coronary clearance and optimize effective orifice area.
*   In multi-prosthesis settings, "High Implantation" is a critical technical strategy to prevent leaflet folding and preserve coronary ostia from future neo-skirt entrapment.
*   Prior Infective Endocarditis (PVE) does not absolutely rule out repeat TAVI if infection has cleared and surgical re-intervention carries prohibitive risk, but the long-term durability data remains sparse.
*   The "One-and-Done" mindset of TAVI must evolve into a Lifetime Management Curriculum, where future procedural complexity is acknowledged and discussed during the initial intervention.
