# Hiccups and Hyponatremia: A Triad to Watch for CNS Inflammation

There are presentations in the emergency department that often don't spark an alarm: a 60-year-old man walking in with a fever, a headache, and a complaint that seems minor or annoying, like persistent hiccups. Clinicians tend to attribute hiccups to stress, excess alcohol, or a bout of gastritis, and patients often discharge themselves thinking it’s just a minor nuisance. However, when persistent hiccups present alongside severe hyponatremia, that innocent presentation masks a significant neurological and metabolic crisis. This case teaches us that when the sodium crashes, if it is the central nervous system that is burning, the physiological consequences can ripple outward from the hypothalamus to the muscle tissue. We will use a rare case of neurologically active Behçet’s disease complicated by SIADH to illustrate how ignoring the hiccups could have cost a patient significant morbidity, and why safety protocols for hyponatremia are non-negotiable in the academic and general setting alike.

This case is anchored in a man in his sixties who presented with fever, malaise, and a headache lasting three days. The initial differential diagnosis is broad for any patient with fever and altered mental status or neurological symptoms. Clinicians immediately consider viral encephalitis, bacterial meningitis, or even primary malignancy. However, this patient also had oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and eventually developed genital ulcers, raising the specter of a systemic inflammatory disorder. The laboratory picture threw the usual script off the page: the patient had profound hyponatremia of 124 mmol/L and a non-negligible elevation in creatine kinase suggestive of rhabdomyolysis. While standard workups for CNS infection did not find a pathogen, the CSF showed aseptic meningitis.

The diagnosis was acute neurological Behçet’s disease. This is a subset of Behçet’s disease that manifests as inflammation of the central nervous system, usually treated with corticosteroids. The interesting feature here is the concurrent Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion. In this patient, the CNS inflammation, likely driven by cytokines like IL-6, caused the hypothalamus to grip onto water retention, leading to serum sodium values that would cause cramps, confusion, or seizures. The hiccups were not a side effect of a ulcer; in fact, the medical literature notes that persistent hiccups can be a significant clinical clue pointing toward severe hyponatremia caused by CNS inflammation, particularly when the MRI is initially normal. The patient’s MRI showed no specific lesions, highlighting how imaging can lag behind clinical chemistry.

Managing the hyponatremia part of this triad requires surgical rigor. The goal is not just to fix the sodium number but to prevent neurologic injury from rapid shifts or muscle breakdown from severe electrolyte imbalance. In this case, the patient was treated with fluid restriction rather than hypertonic saline, largely due to the response to colchicine monotherapy, which is an atypical course for neuro-Behçet’s where steroids are usually preferred. However, the persistence of severe hyponatremia mandates a conservative approach to fluid and sodium. You must remember that severe hyponatremia itself is a risk factor for rhabdomyolysis. The inflammatory state combined with the metabolic derangement caused the patient myositis, which is why the creatine kinase of 1349 U/L was so elevated. You do not always get the classic "dark urine" complaint, but that myopathy risk is real.

In the intensive care setting, you must resist the urge to aggressively correct chronic hyponatremia with saline boluses unless the patient is symptomatic. Overcorrection leads to Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome. In this case, sodium managed to normalize with fluid restriction and the anti-inflammatory effects of colchicine over two weeks. If the patient had presented with seizures, you might consider hypertonic saline, but even then, you must have a cap in place. Every 24 hours in the ICU requires a reassessment of the serum sodium, the urine output, and the renal function. The resolution of the hiccups, the headache, and the hyponatremia simultaneously suggests that treating the underlying CNS drive is more effective than treating the symptom of fluid excess in isolation.

The broader lesson is that when you see hiccups in a patient with headache and hyponatremia, especially in a demographic where Behçet’s is prevalent or where the clinical picture suggests reactive dermatitis or mucocutaneous ulcers, you should broaden your consciousness for neuro-neoplastic or neuro-inflammatory causes. Do not write off the hiccups as benign. The triad of hiccups, hyponatremia, and CNS inflammation is a dangerous combination if ignored. This case reminds us that the body’s electrolyte maps are a reflection of its central nervous system, and treating with a multi-disciplinary approach, focusing on the inflammatory source rather than just the sodium level, was crucial for the patient’s recovery.

## Take-Home Messages

*   Persistent hiccups in the presence of hyponatremia are a strong red flag for central nervous system inflammation or lesions affecting the brainstem.
*   Hyponatremia caused by SIADH in the setting of CNS inflammation should be managed with conservative fluid restriction unless the patient exhibits life-threatening mental status changes.
*   Severe hyponatremia can precipitate rhabdomyolysis; check creatine kinase in patients with profound serum sodium disturbances alongside other muscle symptoms.
*   A normal brain MRI does not rule out acute neuro-Behçet’s disease or aseptic meningitis, particularly in the early phases of presentation.
*   Colchicine monotherapy may be a practical therapeutic option in specific cases of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, though this is unusual compared to standard steroid regimens.



# When the MRI is Normal: The Diagnostic Trap in Neuro-Behçet's Disease

In emergency medicine, physiology dictates that we trust our vitals and our visible lesions. Sometimes, however, the most dangerous patient to dismiss is the one who looks exactly right to be "everything" and "nothing" all at once. Fever, headache, and hyponatremia are often the first cry for help; when you pair that with a normal Brain MRI, the temptation is to relax your diagnostic vigilance or default to empiric viral coverage.

That can be a costly mistake.

I recently reviewed a case report detailing a 60-year-old man presenting with a constellation of symptoms where the radiological evidence for neuro-Behçet's disease was absent, yet the clinical reality was unmistakable. It serves as a stark reminder that while neuroimaging helps rule out vasculitis or demyelination in many contexts, a normal MRI doesn't rule out acute neurological inflammation. In fact, relying solely on radiology can be the diagnostic trap that delays treatment for serious immune-mediated destruction.

## The Clinical Story: Fever, Hiccups, and Hyponatremia

When this patient presented in the ED, the picture was a classic polymicrobial mimic. A three-day history of fever, headache, malaise, and uncontrolled hiccups was accompanied by arthralgia and oral aphthae. The clinical team initially suspected viral meningoencephalitis or an atypical infection. The hiccups were particularly telling; in this context, they are often associated with severe hyponatremia or irritation of the medula/central brainstem.

The serology told a different story than the initial imaging would allow. The patient had profound hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) coupled with hyperglycemia and elevated CRP. The brain MRI, specifically looking for the classic deep nerve loop vascular lesions often associated with Behçet’s, showed no abnormalities.

However, the CSF provided the missing link. It revealed an aseptic meningitis picture: elevated opening pressure, protein, and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis. Crucially, it was sterile. But it wasn't until genital ulcers appeared a few days later that the "mucocutaneous" puzzle pieced together. This fulfilled the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD) for definitive diagnosis.

## Why the MRI Missed It

The crux of this case for our learning module is the neuroimaging. In typical acute parenchymal Behçet’s disease, we expect to see focal infarctions or meningoencephalitis, often along the venous flow of the brain.

However, acute neuro-Behçet’s disease often presents as meningoencephalitis with **no parenchymal lesions**.

This case illustrates that the "parenchymal-sparing variant" is real. The patient fell into the category of acute neuro-BD presenting with aseptic meningitis. Because the MRI appeared normal, the diagnosis was one of exclusion, reinforced by the progressive SCFI findings and the appearance of the specific mucocutaneous ulcers. If the team had stopped after the normal MRI, the patient would likely have been misdiagnosed as viral or managed with unnecessary antibiotics, missing the window for the initiation of anti-inflammatory therapy.

## The Hidden Complication: SIADH and Tapered Intent

To make the clinical picture even more complex, this patient developed Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH) with hyponatremia, likely secondary to the CNS inflammation.

This is a significant vignette because SIADH is a well-established biological setting for neuro-Behçet's. However, it complicates the diagnosis. You cannot attribute the hyponatremia solely to the Behçet's; rather, you suspect the neuro-inflammatory process is driving ADH release.

Interestingly, this patient developed rhabdomyolysis (elevated creatine kinase) in the setting of the hyponatremia, a complication that is routinely overseen. This emphasizes that severe hyponatremia in the outpatient or acute care setting requires more than just ECG monitoring; it requires checking muscle enzymes and renal function, particularly when associated with systemic inflammation.

## Treatment and Recidivism

Here is where things get actionable. Standard of care for acute neuro-Behçet’s often involves corticosteroids and heavy immunosuppression. In this case, the patient received **colchicine monotherapy**.

Afraid? I'm just recounting the data. While most literature suggests colchicine is insufficient for isolated neurological manifestation, this patient responded well. The fever resolved, the mucocutaneous ulcers closed, and the hyponatremia normalized. He was discharged on colchicine alone and remained seizure-free for years. This suggests that in milder presentations of meningeal neuro-BD that are not hemorrhagic or parenchymal-destroying, anti-migraine or antiphonylil agents might suffice. However, clinicians should be cautious and not view this as a replacement for steroids in severe parenchymal disease, but rather as a pearl in specific, milder inflammatory presentations.

## Take-Home Messages

Here is what I take away from this case for our rotation:

*   **Normal MRI Rules Out Lesions, Not Behçet’s:** A normal MRI does not exclude meningeal or acute neuro-Behçet’s, particularly when the presentation is aseptic meningitis.
*   **Follow the Mucocutaneous Trail:** The diagnosis of Behçet’s relies on the Clinical Criteria. Do not ignore genital ulcers or folliculitis-like rashes in the context of CNS symptoms.
*   **CSF is Your Radiograph:** When imaging is negative but clinical suspicion is high for inflammatory meningoencephalitis, rely on CSF markers (protein, pleocytosis) to drive the differential.
*   **SIADH is a Clue:** Consider SIADH in any patient with Behçet's presenting with unexplained hyponatremia, as the inflammation drives ADH release.
*   **Watch for Rhabdo:** Profound hyponatremia in the setting of neuro-inflammation can lead to myopathy; monitor CK levels, not just sodium.
*   **Therapy Flexibility:** While steroids are standard, consider milder treatments for mucocutaneous + meningeal presentations, but always weigh severity against immunosuppression risk.

## Final Thoughts

The danger in emergency and rheumatology workup is the "scan-first" mentality. Inflammation is a diagnosis of exclusion supported by clinical weight. When a patient screams with fever and headache, but the computer does not scream back, you must get to the physical exam, the vitals, and the clinical history. A normal MRI is not a "no brain injury" test; in the case of immune-mediated diseases like Neuro-Behçet's, it is often just the first part of a much harder diagnostic war.



# Beyond Steroids: The Role of Colchicine Monotherapy in Acute Neuro-Inflammation

Let’s face it: In the Emergency Department, Behçet’s Disease (BD) often flies under the radar. It’s usually the quiet patient with the oral ulcer, not the one screaming. But when the disease wakes up the central nervous system, things get messy. The of trial norm? Treat acute neuro-Behçet’s with aggressive immunosuppression or steroids immediately.

Here’s what makes the case below compelling: We have a man in his 60s presenting with an encephalitic picture, marked hyponatremia, and mucocutaneous lesions. He was diagnosed with concurrent Systemic and Neuro-BD at presentation. The kicker? His neuro symptoms and SIADH resolved on **colchicine monotherapy** without a single dose of systemic corticosteroid. This challenges the dogma that colchicine is insufficient for the neurological spectrum of this disease.

Let’s dissect how we get there, why the diagnosis was so slippery, and what this implies for our management algorithms when standard steroids are contraindicated or failed.

## A Clinical Picture That Ticked Every Box but Ledger
This 60-year-old male walked in with the classic triad of "furious fever, hiccups, and salty body fluids." He had a 3-day history of headache, fever, and malaise. You know your history: A young Asian man? We’d be worried about infection or vasculitis. This patient? Older, Type 2 Diabetic, and presenting with hiccups.

Here is the initial data dump that should have triggered a deep dive:
*   **Hyponatremia:** Serum sodium was 124 mmol/L.
*   **Elevated Inflammatory Makers:** CRP 180 mg/L, ESR 70.
*   **Muscle Enzymes:** Creatine Kinase 1349 U/L.
*   **Mucocutaneous:** Oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes.

The initial workup was a sea of negatives. Normocytic anemia was ruled out, renal function was normal (aside from the sodium), and head CT was clear. The answer wasn't in the standard infectious or oncologic screens. It was looking more like a constellation of findings pointing toward systemic inflammation.

## The SIADH Scare: A Hidden Co-pilot
What did breach the barrier here? Hyponatremia had a three-part haunted house. It was central, fluid-restriction was the mainstay of management, and there was a specific clue we must not miss.

The patient had **persistent hiccups**. In this context, hiccups are a window into the brainstem. They are a classic sign of hypothalamic or brainstem involvement, which can also drive SIADH. If you missed the hiccups, you likely miss the SIADH mechanism in neuro-BD.

Diagnostic Criteria for SIADH:
*   Hypervolemia excluded (volume status euvolemic).
*   Urine Osmolality: 900 mOsm/kg (High).
*   Serum Osmolality: 267 mOsm/kg (Hypo).
*   Sodium: 124 mmol/L.
*   **ADH Level:** Elevated at 22.4 pg/mL.

Brain MRI was surprisingly normal. No parenchatal lesions. No enhancement. But we need to remember that acute Neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without radiographic evidence in the early phase. This case highlights that a negative MRI does **not** exclude Neuro-BD, particularly if you have mucocutaneous findings and aseptic CSF. The CSF LP was the game-changer: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) and elevated protein. Infectious pathogens were ruled out, leaving a clinical diagnosis.

## The Colchicine Monotherapy Surprise
This is where the rubber meets the road. Standard textbooks will slap a prescription for cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate, or high-dose steroids on a neuro-BD patient. Why? Because colchicine’s evidence base for Neuro-BD is weak. Most case series and systematic reviews suggest colchicine is reserved for mucocutaneous control and vascular BD, not central inflammation.

In this case, colchicine was started on **Day 8**. The timeline was elegant:
*   **Day 8:** Colchicine initiated.
*   **Day 16:** Sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L. ADH dropped to 1.8 pg/mL.

The patient didn’t just survive; he recovered functionally without neurologic deficit. The theory presented suggests this might be due to Colchicine's inhibition of neutrophil migration and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, specifically **IL-6**. Given that IL-6 is frequently upregulated in Neuro-BD and can drive ADH secretion, a drug that knocks down IL-6 effectively addresses both the CNS inflammation and the SIADH.

It is important to note the resistance. This case doesn't mean colchicine is *always* first-line for Neuro-BD. It means that **in early, low-risk presentations without major parenchymal damage**, steroid-sparing therapy may be viable. The risk of neurocognitive decline or chronic immunosuppression is real. Colchicine monotherapy is a narrow corner, but it might be a better corner than the empty ones.

## Take-Home Messages
*   **Neuro-BD Diagnosis:** Do not rely solely on MRI. Aseptic meningitis with mucocutaneous lesions in an HLA-B51-positive patient meets criteria even with negative brain parenchyma.
*   **The Hiccups Clue:** Persistent hiccups in a patient with headache and hyponatremia should trigger a search for Central Nervous System inflammation, such as Neuro-BD or meningitis.
*   **SIADH in BD:** Hyponatremia in neuro-BD isn't incidental. It’s likely IL-6 mediated. Management often resolves alongside the mucocutaneous symptoms rather than as a separate calculation.
*   **Therapeutic Caution:** While this case used colchicine monotherapy successfully for 1 year without steroids, remember that this is not yet standard of care. Use it judiciously for patients where steroids pose high risks (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes, severe comorbidities).
*   **Rhabdomyolysis:** Never ignore elevated CK in the setting of severe hyponatraemia. It may be a metabolic consequence of severe SIADH, independent of the underlying cause.

****

## Closing Thoughts
This report serves as a reminder that Behçet’s isn't just oral sores and skin rashes. When the central nervous system joins the party, the diagnostic list expands to include infection, malignancy, and autoimmune disease. In this specific instance, the absence of radiographic lesions shouldn't delay suspicion if the clinical ecosystem points to BD.

As clinicians, we dictate a course of action based on "standard" responses: Infection -> Antibiotics, Steroid-resistance -> Steroids. But sometimes, privacy lies in colchicine. It is not a magic bullet. But in this instance of concurrent SIADH and Neuro-BD, the suppression of inflammatory cytokines may be a quieter, safer route than a flare-up of diabetes or neurological deterioration. Keep the SIADH clues (hiccups, hyponatremia) front of mind. They are saying something even when the MRI is silent.



# The 5-Point Rule: Diagnosing Behçet's Syndrome Without the HLA-B51 Marker

## The Case That Started as Infection and Ended as Inflammation
This 60-year-old man walked into the emergency department with a triad of symptoms that mimicked a viral encephalitis but turned out to be a shocking misdiagnosis of a systemic vasculitis. He presented with fever, headache, persistent hiccups, and severe hyponatremia crashing to 124 mmol/L. When you initially see a patient with fever and neurological decline, your differential is infection and malignancy. You expect a contrast-enhanced MRI of the brain and blood cultures that come back negative or CSF that reveals a pathogen. This patient had the MRI, CSF, and cefepime working against him, yet no bacteria were found. The diagnostic pivot came when the patient unveiled the classics: oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes, followed by the grimacing groin. A genital ulcer appeared on day 5 of his admission. That was the moment the infectious disease board faded into the background and rheumatology lights hit the screen, but by then, the patient's electrolytes were struggling to catch up.

## The International Criteria Checklist: Adding It Up
The beauty of the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) is that it is built on repeatability, yet it was not easy to apply here due to the absence of uveitis and the delayed appearance of genital ulcers. When the clinical picture becomes muddy, the scoring system acts as a mathematical anchor. In this case, the diagnostic team scraped together a score of five points, which constitutionally meets the threshold for definite disease. To break this down for the resident reading the rounds: the score is composed of oral aphthae, which counts as two points, plus genital aphthosis, another two points, and a skin manifestation, the folliculitis-like rash, which added the final point. That sum of 5 points is the magic number. However, this diagnosis required ruling out other systemic mimics first. As the case report details, infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune causes like sarcoidosis and lymphoma were effectively excluded through negative imaging and CSF cytology. Once those were put in a locked box, the points mattered less as a calculation and more as a checklist validation.

## Why HLA-B51 Is the Imposter
It is interesting to note that HLA-B51 testing returned positive in this patient. In Asia, HLA-B51 is strongly associated with Behçet’s disease, and many clinicians instinctively order it as a confirmatory "silver bullet." However, the positive result did not contribute to establishing the diagnosis. The case authors explicitly state that this aligns with international criteria which do not require or score HLA-B51, relying instead on clinical features. A crucial distinction in the emergency department is understanding that genetic association is not equivalent to diagnostic dependency. Relying on HLA-B51 can delay care if a patient does not have it, even though the disease is present, whereas relying on clinical points prevents overreliance on genetics that might not be immediately available. The presence or absence of HLA-B51 should not dictate the threshold for starting clinical immunosuppression or investigation; it is an adjunct, not a requirement. This case highlights that in the acute setting, a specific point total from the International Criteria can be established on clinical grounds alone without waiting for genetic sequencing results.

## When Neuro-BD Goes to the Pit: SIADH and Hiccups
While the ICBD score once clarified the relapse risk, the acute management was complicated by the hyponatremia, which was the most dangerous part of this clinical picture. The patient developed a diagnosis of Acute Neuro-Behçet’s Disease complicated by Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH). This is a classic example of how CNS inflammation dysregulates ADH release, leading to water retention. In the ED, when you see a patient with what looks like aseptic meningitis and a sodium of 124 mmol/L, always squeeze hiccups into your differential. This patient had persistent hiccups, arthralgia, and a normal head MRI, all warning signs of the cyclic neurological inflammation before the ophthalmology referral was ordered. The resolution of the hyponatremia came simply with fluid restriction rather than hypertonic saline or steroid bursts, though it is noted that colchicine monotherapy drove the recovery that normalized the electrolytes over two weeks.

## Take-Home Points
- Neuro-Behçet’s disease can be diagnosed concurrently with systemic Behçet's disease, presenting as aseptic meningitis with a normal MRI in early stages.
- The International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD) relies on clinical point totals (e.g., oral aphthae, genital ulcers, skin rash) rather than HLA-B51 status for a definitive diagnosis.
- Hyponatremia in the setting of acute neurological inflammation should trigger a workup for SIADH, considering hiccups as a potential clinical clue.
- Colchicine monotherapy can be effective for a subset of acute neuro-Behçet's cases, though steroids remain the standard for many, making clinical trials and case reports vital for management strategies.
- A comprehensive rule-out of infectious and neoplastic causes is mandatory before diagnosing Behçet’s disease, even when mucocutaneous lesions are present.



# Fever and Headache: The Infectious vs. Vasculitic Differential

## The Diagnostic Trap of Fever and Meningeal Signs

Fever and headache are the initial sentinels for the clinician. In our usual diagnostic algorithm, a febrile patient with headache goes straight toward meningitis. The path is predictable: satisfy infectious triggers, cover the bases with broad antibiotics, add antivirals for HSV, consider TB, and begin supportive care. But what happens when the infectious workup comes back clean, and the "vasculitic" score takes points from a mucocutaneous ulcer?

Consider a 60-year-old Asian male who walks into the room claiming fever, headache, and general malaise. He has oral aphthae a few days prior to presentation and persistent hiccups. You take his sodium, and it’s profoundly low at 124 mmol/L. You admit him, suspecting sepsis or meningoencephalitis. You start cefepime and acyclovir empirically. This is the standard initial triage for acute neuroinflammation. It is rational, but in the case of acute neurological Behçet’s disease, it is also a race against time while ruling out pathology that isn't "infection."

In this specific instance, the empirical broad coverage didn't do the patient harm, but it highlights a common clinical pitfall: the bias toward infectious etiologies in meningoencephalitis. The clinician in this scenario must recognize that fever and headache in the presence of hyponatremia can signal a systemic inflammatory disorder masquerading as meningitis, especially when MRI findings are not immediately paralyzing.

## Unraveling the Hyponatremia: SIADH in the CNS

The clue that shifted this case away from primary infectious etiology wasn't the viral PCR panel, but the serum sodium. The patient had euvolemic hyponatremia (no edema, no dehydration), low serum osmolality, but high urine osmolality and high urine sodium. The labs fit the diagnosis of Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH).

In emergency medicine, we know SIADH is a direct, albeit poorly defined, link to CNS inflammation. In this patient, the SIADH was compounded by neurological symptoms including headache, vertigo, and persistent hiccups. Hiccups are a specific, often overlooked clinical marker for severe hyponatremia associated with CNS inflammation.

Why does this matter for the differential? Brain MRI in acute neuro-Behçet’s often involves the brainstem or hypothalamus. However, as detailed in this specific case, the MRI was initially unremarkable despite the presence of aseptic meningitis on lumbar puncture. The opening pressure was mildly elevated, and protein was high with a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). The CSF glucose, cultures, and viral PCRs were negative.

When the infectious workup goes quiet—negative cultures, negative HSV/VZV PCR, and no rashes associated with other viral exanthems—the needle starts to turn toward vasculitis or connective tissue disease. At this point, the clinician must pivot from "treating infection" to "ruling out vasculitis."

## The Behçet’s Algorithm: A Case of Exclusion

The diagnosis of Behçet’s Disease relies on the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD). This is not a lab test; it is a clinical scoring system based on symptoms.

For this patient, the initial presentation was confusing. Oral aphthae are oral ulcers, which grant points, but genital ulcers are required to be established to meet the criteria score of 4. This patient had no genital ulcer at the start. On day 5, a painful scrotal ulcer appeared. Only then did the score of 5 (oral aphthae 2 points, genital aphthosis 2 points, rash 1 point) definitively dial up the diagnosis of definite BD.

This temporal drift emphasizes the importance of follow-up in patients with fever of unknown origin. Autoimmune diseases can present indolently or evolve over days. In this case, the neurology (aseptic meningitis + MRI) and the dermatology (rash) matched, but the "genital" sign appeared later.

It is worth noting the exclusionary data. A markedly elevated sIL-2R raised the specter of lymphoma, which is a known complication of vasculitis. However, negative CSF cytology, normal MRI, and lack of other B-symptoms effectively cleared primary CNS lymphoma from the board.

## Treatment Nuances and Clinical Pearls

This case offers a twist that challenges standard guidelines. Acute neuro-BD is typically steroid-heavy, often with immunosuppressants like cyclophosphamide or tumor necrosis factor inhibitors. The discussion in the literature notes that colchicine is generally considered ineffective for neuro-BD as monotherapy and is usually reserved for mucocutaneous lesions.

However, in this specific patient, colchicine monotherapy resolved the mucocutaneous symptoms, the neurological manifestations (headache, vertigo), and the associated SIADH. The improvement in sodium levels (normalizing to 136 mmol/L by day 16) correlated with the inflammation subsiding. This suggests that if acute neuro-BD responds well to colchicine as monotherapy, perhaps the IL-6 mediated cytokine storm driving the SIADH is sufficiently suppressed without needing broad immunosuppression.

Additionally, consider the CK levels. This patient had a Creatine Kinase of 1349 U/L. In the context of acute hyponatremia and hiccups, this represented rhabdomyolysis, a complication of severe electrolyte disturbance that is often found tucked away in the results of a "fever working" rather than investigated proactively during metabolic evaluation.

The resolution of SIADH in this case was without vasopressin receptor antagonists or hypertonic saline. Fluid restriction alone (<800 mL/day) sufficed. This is critical; rapid correction of sodium in this context—driven by inflammation—can be dangerous.

## Take-home Messages

*   **Fever + Headache + Hyponatremia:** Consider CNS inflammation as a primary differential. SIADH is rare but a functional endpoint of neuro-Behçet's.
*   **The MRI Caveat:** Normal MRI in the context of CSF pleocytosis does not rule out neuro-Behçet’s; it may simply be early stage parenchymal sparing.
*   **ICBD Scoring:** Rely on clinical criteria (oral/genital ulcers + rash) but keep the diagnosis of Behçet's in mind even if the genital ulcer presents days after onset.
*   **Colchicine Potential:** While atypical for neuro-BD monotherapy, consider it when prompt response is needed and corticosteroids are contraindicated or unavailable.
*   **Rhabdomyolysis Hyponatremia:** Do not ignore elevated CK in febrile patients, as severe hyponatremia can drive myopathy and rhabdo.
*   **Rule Out the Rare:** When viral and bacterial workups are negative in meningoencephalitis, thoughtfully evaluate rare autoimmune conditions like neurological vasculitis before assuming treatment failure.

This case serves as a reminder that while we treat meningitis patterns well, the "diagnosis of exclusion" remains one of our greatest, yet most frightening, tasks. The absence of organisms is not evidence of a negative scan; it is often just the presence of a different type of fire.
