Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] In patients with CNS inflammation and hyponatremia, which clinical sign served as a critical clue to the severity and etiology of the electrolyte disturbance?

In patients with CNS inflammation and hyponatremia, **persistent hiccups** serve as a critical clinical clue to the severity and etiology of the electrolyte disturbance. 

In this case report of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (Neuro-BD) complicated by SIADH, the patient presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous lesions alongside persistent hiccups and marked hyponatremia (124 mmol/L). The discussion section of the report explicitly notes that persistent hiccups may serve as an important clinical clue to severe hyponatremia caused by SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation, including in conditions like meningitis. 

The pathophysiology in this case involves CNS inflammation stimulating inappropriate ADH release, leading to water retention and neurotoxic hyponatremia. The presence of hiccups specifically alerts the clinician to the central nervous system origin of the SIADH and the severity of the hyponatremia, as hiccuping can be a manifestation of brainstem irritation or dysfunction associated with profound electrolyte imbalance. While the patient also exhibited rhabdomyolysis, evidenced by markedly elevated creatine kinase, the hiccups were the key neurological sign suggesting the specific etiology of a neuroinflammatory driver. Clinicians managing hyponatremia in patients with neurological symptoms should remain wary of this subtle sign as a marker of central pathology.
