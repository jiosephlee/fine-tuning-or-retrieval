Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

The initial clinical picture of this case exemplifies the diagnostic challenge in identifying Behçet’s disease (BD) when it involves multiple organ systems concurrently. The mucocutaneous triad serves as the cornerstone for the diagnosis, consisting of oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and specific skin lesions. At presentation, the patient exhibited recurrent oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on his trunk and face. While oral ulcers are common in BD, the combination of oral aphthae and the subsequent painful genital ulcer on day 5 significantly increases the diagnostic specificity. Under the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, these mucocutaneous features are weighted heavily; in this case, the combination of oral aphthae (two points), genital aphthosis (two points), and a folliculitis-like rash (one point) resulted in a total score of five points, confirming a diagnosis of definite BD. Clinicians must be attentive to the evolution of these lesions, as the appearance of genital ulcers often solidifies the diagnosis when mucocutaneous symptoms are initially viewed in isolation against a background of general malaise.

Superimposed on these dermatological findings, the patient presented with significant systemic alarm symptoms that complicated the clinical reasoning. The presence of fever (38.1°C), persistent headache, and general malaise necessitated a broad differential diagnosis initially prioritizing central nervous system (CNS) infections such as viral or bacterial meningoencephalitis. A critical, though often underappreciated, clinical clue was the patient's persistent hiccups. In the context of CNS inflammation and hyponatraemia, hiccups can indicate irritation of the brainstem or mediastinal involvement. This case highlights the importance of linking non-specific constitutional symptoms like hiccups to severe electrolyte disturbances, as profound hyponatraemia can manifest with neurological irritability preceding obvious focal deficits. Additionally, the patient’s arthralgia and loss of appetite reflected the systemic inflammatory burden associated with the vasculitis.

Baseline laboratory investigations provided immediate evidence of severe systemic inflammation and metabolic derangement. The patient presented with marked hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L) alongside significantly elevated markers of inflammation, including a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 180 mg/L and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 70 mm/hour. In the acute setting, such a combination prompts a urgent search for infection; however, the concurrent elevation of creatine kinase (1349 U/L) raised suspicion for rhabdomyolysis or severe metabolic stress. While myopathy can occur in acute inflammatory states, the profound hyponatraemia itself can lead to muscle breakdown, and clinicians must consider that severe electrolyte imbalances can mimic primary myopathic presentations. These initial laboratory trends provided the bridge to a neurological workup, where the high CRP and ESR supported an inflammatory etiology over a pure infectious process once viral markers were negative.

Physical examination findings were crucial for distinguishing the nature of the hyponatraemia and neurological involvement. The patient was euvolemic with no peripheral oedema or signs of dehydration, a key finding that aligns with the diagnosis of Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH). Identifying a euvolemic state is a prerequisite for SIADH diagnosis because it helps exclude hypovolaemic hyponatraemia caused by volume depletion or congestive heart failure. Neurologically, while the patient experienced vertigo and worsening headache, he had no objective muscle weakness or cranial nerve palsies on examination. This absence of focal deficits explains the initial lack of abnormalities on brain MRI and underscores a critical lesson in clinical reasoning: acute neuro-Behçet's disease can manifest as aseptic meningitis with normal structural imaging, requiring cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis rather than solely relying on radiological evidence.

Finally, understanding the epidemiological context and genetic background aids in prioritizing the diagnosis of Behçet's disease in the differential. The patient was an Asian male in his 60s, a demographic group with a high prevalence of Behçet's disease due to specific genetic susceptibilities, particularly the HLA-B51 allele. Although the patient’s family history was unremarkable, the positive HLA-B51 screening result provided supportive genetic evidence for an autoimmune vasculitis. While HLA-B51 does not confirm the diagnosis, in this case, it helped rule-negative against other autoinflammatory conditions like sarcoidosis or Lymes disease when combined with negative serological workup. Furthermore, the patient had poorly controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus, evidenced by hyperglycaemia and an elevated HbA1c of 8.2%. While unrelated to the acute inflammatory crisis, the presence of poorly controlled diabetes complicates the management of subsequent fluid and insulin therapy, requiring careful monitoring to ensure that metabolic derangements do not mask or exacerbate the resolution of the hyponatraemia.

## Diagnostic Workup: Neurological and Endocrine Evaluation

The diagnostic strategy employed for this patient centered on characterizing the acute neurological inflammation while concurrently evaluating the endocrine dysfunction associated with the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Initial neuroimaging, including non-contrast CT and MRI of the brain, was performed to identify structural abnormalities such as mass lesions, strokes, or granulomatous involvement typical of parenchymal neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD). The imaging revealed no abnormalities involving the hypothalamus or brain parenchyma. This finding is pedagogically significant because it highlights the limitations of neuroimaging in early-stage acute neuro-BD. While parenchymal lesions, particularly in the brainstem or infratemporal fossae, are hallmarks of deep vasculitide forms of neuro-Behçet's, acute meningitis-like presentations may yield normal imaging findings in the absence of restricted diffusion or enhancement. Clinicians must therefore rely heavily on clinical features, such as headache, fever, and the presence of mucocutaneous lesions (categorized under International Criteria for Behçet's Disease), rather than dismissing neurological involvement solely due to a normal MRI scan.

Following the initial imaging, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was crucial to define the nature of the central nervous system (CNS) inflammation. An opening pressure of 17 cmH₂O suggested potential hydrocephalus or inflammation affecting the ventricular system, though not to the degree of acute pseudotumor cerebri. CSF composition showed elevated protein at 0.97 g/L and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL), confirming a mononuclear inflammatory response rather than the neutrophilic predominance seen in bacterial meningitis. To exclude infectious etiologies that are mimics of aseptic meningitis, CSF was cultured for bacterial and mycobacterial pathogens, and specific polymerase chain reaction and antigen tests were negative for *Herpes Simplex Virus* (HSV), *Varicella Zoster Virus* (VZV), and *Cryptococcus*. Furthermore, cytology for malignant cells was negative. This negative infectious screen, in the presence of the clinical picture of fever and CNS symptoms, supported the diagnosis of aseptic meningitis secondary to neuro-Behçet's, emphasizing the necessity of broad pathogen exclusion when infectious causes are possible.

Concurrently, the workup addressed the severe hyponatraemia (serum sodium 124 mmol/L) which necessitated the evaluation of SIADH. Diagnostic standards for SIADH require the combination of hypotonic hyponatraemia (serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg) and inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg), occurring in a clinically euvolemic patient. High urinary sodium (88 mmol/L) further supported this by indicating renal salt wasting was not driven by hypovolaemia. The diagnosis was definitively supported by the presence of elevated antidiuretic hormone (ADH) of 22.4 pg/mL. The endocrine workup also included thyroid function tests and cortisol levels to rule out adrenal insufficiency or hypothyroidism, which can cause secondary hyponatraemia; both were within normal limits. This exclusion process is vital to prevent mismanagement of hyponatraemia with hypertonic saline, which carries risks of cerebral oedema reversal if an endocrine cause is later discovered.

To finalize the differential diagnosis, the team performed metabolic screening to rule out primary systemic disorders and malignancies that could explain the neurological and systemic inflammation. This included serum testing for sarcoidosis, measured via angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) and lysozyme, and haematological malignancy screening, assessed by soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R). While sIL-2R showed a mild elevation (704 U/mL), it was acknowledged as non-specific in the context of systemic inflammation, as was the normal lysozyme and ACE profile, making sarcoidosis or lymphoma unlikely. The absence of positive findings in these specific tumor markers, combined with the negative CSF cytology and lack of focal neurological deficits, allowed the clinicians to conclude that the inflammatory process was driven by the Behçet's disease spectrum rather than an alternative neoplastic or granulomatous mimic.

## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Synthesis

In the context of this patient's multifocal presentation, the initial clinical reasoning prioritized life-threatening etiologies such as infectious meningoencephalitis, malignancy, and other autoimmune conditions before settling on a rare systemic vasculitis. The presence of fever, headache, and lymphocytic pleocytosis in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) initially raised concern for viral or bacterial meningitis. However, the diagnostic process relied heavily on the integration of culture results and polymerase chain reaction testing. Bacterial meningitis was effectively excluded by the absence of neutrophils and negative blood cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was ruled out by the negative results for acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing from the CSF. Furthermore, while viral etiology remained a primary consideration given the clinical syndrome, specific testing for herpes simplex virus (HSV) and varicella zoster virus (VZV) was performed. The negative status of CSF cultures for HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen, alongside the absence of viral antigen and antibodies, substantiated an aseptic etiology rather than a reactive infectious one. Empirical treatment with cefepime and acyclovir was subsequently discontinued once these pathogens were excluded, preventing unnecessary antibiotic exposure and highlighting the importance of serological and microbiological confirmation in neuroacutely ill patients.

Parallel to the infectious workup, the evaluation for primary CNS malignancy required syntheses of structural and cellular data. Although the elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level presented with a malignant differential, solitary elevations of this marker are non-specific and often reflect general inflammatory activity rather than lymphoma. The diagnostic team proceeded with neuroimaging and CSF cytology based on this nuance. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed no structural abnormalities, specifically excluding masses or lesions common in CNS lymphoma or neurosarcoidosis. Crucially, CSF cytology demonstrated no malignant cells. Additionally, screening for haematological malignancy was negative for human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody. While the mild sIL-2R elevation suggested an immune activation, the lack of radiological evidence and cellular malignancy in the fluid supported a diagnosis of aseptic meningitis driven by inflammatory rather than neoplastic causes.

Distinguishing Behçet’s disease from other systemic inflammatory disorders required a targeted exclusion of mimicries, particularly neurosarcoidosis and systemic lupus erythematosus. Neurosarcoidosis was a significant consideration given the headache and CNS involvement, yet diagnostic tests for the disease proved unrevealing. Specifically, serum angiotensin-converting enzyme levels were within normal limits, and lysozyme levels were unremarkable. In the absence of characteristic systemic skin nodules or ocular involvement, and with MRI findings lacking the typical basal meningo-meningeal enhancement, neurosarcoidosis became unlikely. Similarly, screening for other autoimmune connective tissue diseases resulted in negative autoantibody profiles, and the patient lacked the multi-system cutaneous or serositis features typical of systemic lupus or vasculitis syndromes. This exclusionary process reinforced the hypothesis that the systemic and neurological findings were manifestations of a single unifying inflammatory construct rather than overlapping autoimmune entities.

With potential infectious, neoplastic, and alternative autoimmune causes excluded, the diagnostic focus shifted to the formal application of the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD). This scoring system uses oral and genital aphthosis, skin lesions, and ocular symptoms as primary criteria. In this case, the patient scored five points based on the presence of oral aphthae, which contributed two points, genital aphthosis, which is a highly specific marker contributing another two points, and folliculitis-like rashes, adding one point. A cumulative score of four or greater defines definite disease within the ICBD framework. It is important to note that the patient was in a newly diagnosed phase; the clinical features of oral aphthae and the subsequent development of genital ulcers aligned perfectly with the classic mucocutaneous distribution of BD. The patient was HLA-B51 positive, a genetic risk factor that, while not diagnostic in isolation, supports the clinical finding of definite disease.

Finally, the establishment of concurrent systemic and neurological disease relied on linking the mucocutaneous diagnosis with the CNS presentation. The diagnosis of acute neurological Behçet’s disease was assigned based on the criteria linkage, where CNS symptoms occur in patients already classified as BD. In this instance, acute neuro-BD was characterized by aseptic meningitis, evidenced by lymphocytic pleocytosis and increased protein without parenchymal involvement on MRI. Initially, the absence of brain lesions on imaging could have obscured the diagnosis, as neuro-Behçet’s often presents with parenchymal damage involving the brainstem. However, the temporal association of neurological symptoms with the flare of mucocutaneous lesions, combined with the exclusion of other causes of aseptic meningitis, solidified the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Consequently, the patient was diagnosed with definite systemic Behçet’s disease concurrently with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, illustrating the necessity to consider neurological involvement in new cases of BD, even when imaging appears non-specific or normal.

## Pathophysiological Mechanisms: CNS Inflammation and ADH Dysregulation

The clinical presentation of this patient highlights a distinct pathophysiological pathway where acute neuro-inflammation triggered a systemic endocrine complication. In Behçet's disease (BD), the development of syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone (SIADH) is primarily attributed to the inflammatory milieu affecting the central nervous system (CNS). While neuroinflammation is well-documented in Behçet’s neurological manifestations, its direct role in hormonal dysregulation represents a critical diagnostic consideration. In this case, the patient exhibited signs of aseptic meningitis with elevated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein and pleocytosis, despite a normal brain MRI. This underscores that functional inflammation within the CNS parenchyma can occur without radiographic evidence of lesions. Specifically, the hypothalamus, which regulates water balance and antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion, is susceptible to dysregulation when subject to intense meningeal or parenchymal inflammation. The absence of focal neuroimaging abnormalities in early-stage neuro-BD, as seen in this patient, does not exclude central involvement that impacts hypothalamic osmoregulatory centers.

The biological link between CNS inflammation and the subsequent hyponatremia involves inflammatory cytokine dysregulation. Central nervous system inflammation induces the release of proinflammatory mediators, most notably interleukin-6 (IL-6). Elevated IL-6 levels have been shown to directly stimulate the release of ADH from the posterior pituitary and override normal inhibitory feedback from plasma osmolality. In the context of this case, while direct IL-6 serum or CSF levels were not measured, the markedly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) concentration (180 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate served as markers of a robust systemic and CNS inflammatory response, consistent with a cytokine-driven mechanism. The hypothalamus perceives a change in osmolality as secondary hyper-tonicity when ADH is inappropriately secreted, leading to impaired water excretion. Therefore, the hyponatremia observed (124 mmol/L) is not merely a side effect but a manifestation of cytokine-mediated interference in endocrine signaling pathways.

Understanding the complications of severe hyponatremia requires a recognition of the systemic metabolic sequelae, such as rhabdomyolysis. In this patient, the profound hyponatremia was accompanied by elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels of 1349 U/L. While muscle injury can arise from Behçet's vasculitis, in this context, the elevated myogenic enzymes were a recognized complication of severe sodium imbalance. Cellular swelling due to water influx in a hypotonic environment can lead to muscle membrane disruption and necrosis, particularly in the setting of acute neurological stress. These findings illustrate that patients with acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH should be monitored for secondary manifestations of electrolyte derangement that may not be immediately apparent without specific evaluation, such as monitoring myogenic markers.

Distinguishing idiopathic mechanisms from pharmacological triggers is essential, as SIADH in Behçet’s disease can occur secondary to medications like cyclophosphamide. However, the drug profile in this case did not include cyclophosphamide or other immunosuppressants known to precipitate SIADH. This effectively rules out drug-induced dysfunction, suggesting that the pathophysiology was intrinsic to the active autoimmune process rather than iatrogenic. This distinction reinforces that in newly diagnosed cases where SIADH presents concurrently with specific neurological and mucocutaneous features of Behçet's, the primary disease activity—and associated inflammatory cytokines—should be viewed as the primary etiology. The spontaneous nature of the thyroid and adrenal axes (normal cortisol and thyroid function) further helped exclude other endocrine pathologies, narrowing the differential diagnosis to inflammatory CNS dysregulation.

Finally, the resolution of symptoms provides insight into the specific inflammatory targets within this pathway. The patient's improvement on colchicine monotherapy, without the use of corticosteroids, suggests a mechanism sensitive to pyrin-associated blockade of cytokine production. Colchicine inhibits neutrophil migration and suppresses proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-6. This supports the hypothesis that IL-6-mediated inflammation, driven by aberrant monocyte activation characteristic of Behçet's disease, is the mechanistic driver of the hyponatremia. Since the monocytes in Behçet's produce spontaneous IL-6, the suppression of this specific cytokine arm by colchicine likely normalized ADH secretion. This highlights that endocrine complications of neuro-BD may respond to anti-inflammatory agents that target cytokine pathways, offering a targeted approach in patients where corticosteroids may be contraindicated or ineffective.

## Therapeutic Strategy and Clinical Outcomes

The therapeutic approach in this case highlights the nuance required in managing neurological manifestations of Behçet’s disease, particularly when the standard of care diverges from established protocols. While high-dose systemic corticosteroids remain a mainstay for purulent neuro-Behçet’s disease due to their rapid anti-inflammatory action, this patient was successfully managed with colchicine monotherapy. This decision was based on the patient’s concurrent diagnosis of systemic BD, where colchicine is already established for mucocutaneous control, and the absence of more aggressive meningoencephalitis (such as brainstem parenchymal damage) that typically warrants stronger immunosuppression. Colchicine exerts its therapeutic effect by inhibiting microtubule polymerization, thereby preventing neutrophil migration and reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and IL-6. In this specific instance, the choice to avoid corticosteroids minimized the metabolic and infectious risks associated with high-dose steroids, demonstrating that in selected cases of acute neuro-BD, milder immunomodulation can effectively control both mucocutaneous and neurological disease activity.

Concurrently, the management of Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH) required careful application of fluid and electrolyte principles in the context of CNS inflammation. SIADH was confirmed by the triad of euvolemic hyponatremia, inappropriately concentrated urine, and high urine sodium in the setting of low serum osmolality. Standard protocols often involve hypertonic saline for severe neurologic symptoms to prevent seizures, but the patient was managed conservatively with fluid restriction and strict sodium monitoring. This approach was necessitated by the likely reversible nature of the SIADH responding to the resolution of the underlying neuroinflammation. Rapid correction of hyponatremia was avoided to prevent Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome. The successful management of hyponatraemia without hypertonic saline underscores the importance of treating the primary inflammatory driver rather than aggressively correcting electrolytes before the CNS inflammation subsides.

The clinical response to therapy provides a clear timeline for the correlation between systemic inflammation, neurological stability, and electrolyte homeostasis. Symptoms such as fever, headache, vertigo, and general malaise began to resolve immediately upon colchicine initiation, coinciding with the control of mucocutaneous lesions. However, the normalization of serum sodium levels and the resolution of pleocytosis took longer, occurring over a two-week period. This delayed response in hyponatremia resolution reflects the time required for the hypothalamic-pituitary axis to re-equilibrate following the reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokine drive. The absence of focal neurological deficits and the lack of need for rehabilitation for motor function despite the initial decline indicate that the neurological involvement was primarily meningoencephalitic rather than parenchymal.

Long-term follow-up data from this patient offers critical insights into the prognosis of concurrent systemic and neurological disease activity. After hospital discharge, the patient remained on colchicine monotherapy for approximately four years without recurrence of Behçet’s disease or neurological symptoms. This stability suggests that the acute episode, while severe, did not progress to a fatal or permanent disabling course, likely due to the effective suppression of the inflammatory cascade. Clinicians should use this case to recognize that not all acute neuro-BD cases require aggressive escalation to cyclophosphamide or intravenous steroids, provided the clinical phenotype is not dominated by rapidly disabling brainstem or capillary syndromes.

Pedagogically, this case reinforces several essential diagnostic and safety principles regarding hyponatremia in systemic inflammatory disorders. Students must recognize that persistent hiccups can be a clinical surrogate for severe, untreated hyponatremia, especially in patients with undiagnosed CNS inflammation. Furthermore, the case illustrates that inadequate investigation into underlying endocrine disorders (SIADH) in a feverish patient with headache can lead to missed diagnoses of neuro-Behçet’s. By understanding the link between elevated inflammatory markers (like CRP) and ADH dysregulation, clinicians can prioritize CNS evaluations in patients presenting with unexplained hyponatremia and mucocutaneous lesions, ensuring that the root cause of the electrolyte disturbance is addressed to prevent complications such as rhabdomyolysis and neurological decline.
