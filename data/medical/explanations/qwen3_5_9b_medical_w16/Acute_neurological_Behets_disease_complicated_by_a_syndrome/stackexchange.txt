Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Morning Report] Typically, neurological involvement in Behçet's disease occurs years after initial diagnosis; what does the concurrent presentation of systemic and acute neurological disease imply for the workup of this patient?

When Behçet’s disease presents acutely with concurrent neurological and systemic involvement, the diagnostic workup shifts from a chronic management model to an acute exclusionary protocol aimed at distinguishing vasculitis from infection, malignancy, or other inflammatory CNS disorders. As demonstrated in this case, while neuro-Behçet’s typically manifests several years after initial diagnosis, the concurrent onset requires a high index of suspicion for primary CNS inflammation but mandates rigorous exclusion of infectious or neoplastic mimics.

First, the presence of fever and acute neurological signs necessitates immediate evaluation for CNS infection, such as HSV, VZV, or bacterial meningitis, and malignancy, including lymphoma. In this case, the team appropriately prioritized CSF analysis to distinguish aseptic meningitis from viral or bacterial etiologies, confirmed by negative viral panels and cultures, and ruled out primary CNS malignancy via normal MRI and cytology. However, clinicians must recognize that acute neuro-Behçet’s disease can present with aseptic meningitis or focal deficits despite a normal initial brain MRI, particularly when the inflammation is limited to meningeal involvement; therefore, a normal neuroimaging study should not preclude the diagnosis.

Second, the workup must address systemic complications that may obscure clinical symptoms, such as the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH). This patient’s hyponatraemia and persistent hiccups required specific endocrine evaluation to rule out thyroid, adrenal, or renal causes, confirming the SIADH diagnosis which, in isolation, could suggest a malignancy or lung pathology. The resolution of hyponatraemia alongside neurological symptoms suggests the neuro-inflammatory process disrupted ADH regulation, underscoring the need to evaluate volume status and electrolytes in patients with CNS Behçet’s.

Finally, the diagnosis relies on the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease combined with exclusion of alternative causes. In this concurrent presentation, the appearance of the characteristic genital ulcer progressed from an initial equivocal state to a definitive sign, validating the systemic nature of the disease. Therefore, for patients with acute fever and neurological deficits, the initial mucocutaneous evaluation (oral and genital lesions) is as critical as the neuroimaging to anchor the diagnosis, ensuring that the inflammatory etiology is not misattributed to infection or malignancy.

### [Clinical Pearls] In patients with CNS inflammation and hyponatremia, which clinical sign served as a critical clue to the severity and etiology of the electrolyte disturbance?

In patients with CNS inflammation and hyponatremia, **persistent hiccups** serve as a critical clinical clue to the severity and etiology of the electrolyte disturbance. 

In this case report of acute neurological Behçet’s disease (Neuro-BD) complicated by SIADH, the patient presented with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous lesions alongside persistent hiccups and marked hyponatremia (124 mmol/L). The discussion section of the report explicitly notes that persistent hiccups may serve as an important clinical clue to severe hyponatremia caused by SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation, including in conditions like meningitis. 

The pathophysiology in this case involves CNS inflammation stimulating inappropriate ADH release, leading to water retention and neurotoxic hyponatremia. The presence of hiccups specifically alerts the clinician to the central nervous system origin of the SIADH and the severity of the hyponatremia, as hiccuping can be a manifestation of brainstem irritation or dysfunction associated with profound electrolyte imbalance. While the patient also exhibited rhabdomyolysis, evidenced by markedly elevated creatine kinase, the hiccups were the key neurological sign suggesting the specific etiology of a neuroinflammatory driver. Clinicians managing hyponatremia in patients with neurological symptoms should remain wary of this subtle sign as a marker of central pathology.

### [Pathophysiology] How does the proinflammatory cytokine interleukin-6 (IL-6) mediate the release of antidiuretic hormone in the setting of acute neuro-Behçet's disease?

In this case of concurrent Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), the release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) leading to the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is mediated by a cytokine storm originating from CNS inflammation, specifically involving interleukin-6 (IL-6).

As detailed in the discussion, the pathophysiology relies on the concept that aberrant monocyte activation within the central nervous system during acute neuro-BD leads to spontaneous IL-6 production. While serum or CSF levels of IL-6 were not directly measured in this specific patient, the markedly elevated C-reactive protein serves as a surrogate marker for this underlying inflammatory response. Literature cited in the case indicates that IL-6, a proinflammatory cytokine frequently elevated in neuroinflammatory disorders, is associated with increased ADH release. This cytokine-driven dysregulation disrupts the normal osmoregulatory control, resulting in the inappropriate ADH secretion observed in this patient.

The therapeutic response supports this mechanistic pathway. The patient’s hyponatremia and neurological symptoms resolved with colchicine monotherapy. This is consistent with colchicine’s mechanism of inhibiting neutrophil migration and suppressing cytokine production, including IL-1β and IL-6. The simultaneous normalization of serum sodium and ADH levels suggests that suppressing this proinflammatory IL-6 pathway effectively reduced the central drive for inappropriate ADH secretion. Clinicians should recognize that in patients with neuro-BD presenting with hyponatremia, even in the absence of visible brain lesions on MRI, cytokine-mediated SIADH should be suspected and managed judiciously.

### [Diagnostic] Before confirming a diagnosis of SIADH as the cause of hyponatremia, which endocrine conditions must be excluded to rule out pseudo-hyponatremia?

Thank you for this important question. First, it is vital to correct a common physiological misconception: **endocrine conditions do not cause pseudo-hyponatremia.**

Pseudo-hyponatremia is an analytical artifact caused by severe hyperlipidemia or hyperproteinemia, which dilutes the plasma water fraction and artificially lowers the measured sodium concentration while maintaining normal serum osmolality. To rule out pseudo-hyponatremia, one must rely on direct measurement of serum osmolality (Kakehi et al. found a low serum osmolality of 267 mOsm/kg, confirming true hypotonic hyponatremia) rather than endocrine testing.

However, to confidently diagnose SIADH, one must exclude endocrine causes of **true** hypotonic hyponatremia, specifically **primary hypothyroidism and secondary adrenal insufficiency**. Both conditions significantly impair free water excretion and can mimic SIADH physiologically. As demonstrated in the Kakehi et al. case, the diagnostic workflow confirmed SIADH by first establishing low serum osmolality and then systematically excluding these endocrine mimics. In this patient, thyroid-stimulating hormone (1.11 mIU/L) and serum cortisol (42 µg/dL) were both within normal limits, effectively ruling out hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency.

**Key Clinical Teaching Point:** Before confirming SIADH, you must (1) verify low serum osmolality to exclude pseudo-hyponatremia via lipids/proteins, and (2) exclude hypothyroidism (via TSH and Free T4) and adrenal insufficiency (via morning cortisol or ACTH stimulation) to ensure the hyponatremia is isolated to dysregulated ADH release. In the setting of CNS inflammation or Behçet’s disease, SIADH often stems from central dysregulation rather than peripheral endocrine failure, as evidenced by the normal endocrine panel in this case.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In the context of acute neuro-Behçet's disease, how should a normal brain MRI without hypothalamic involvement be weighed against clinical findings to support the diagnosis?

A normal brain MRI, particularly without hypothalamic involvement, should not preclude the diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD). While parenchymal lesions, especially involving the brainstem or hypothalamus, are characteristic, acute neuro-BD frequently presents as aseptic meningitis without visible inflammatory lesions on structural imaging, particularly in the early phase. As demonstrated in this case, the patient presented with headache, fever, and hyponatraemia, yet the brain MRI and CT scan revealed no abnormalities involving the hypothalamus or other regions.

In this context, the diagnosis relies on a triad of clinical findings: persistent mucocutaneous manifestations, cerebrospinal fluid evidence of aseptic meningitis, and the exclusion of mimics. This patient met the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (definite status via a score of 5, driven by oral aphthae and genital ulcers) and demonstrated cerebrospinal fluid findings consistent with CNS inflammation, specifically mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein. The resolution of symptoms with colchicine monotherapy further validated the inflammatory etiology.

Furthermore, the presence of the syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone (SIADH) in the absence of a radiological hypothalamic lesion underscores that functional CNS inflammation regarding hypothalamic regulation can precede or exist independently of structural imaging abnormalities. Clinicians must balance the low yield of negative imaging against high-suspicion clinical vignettes—specifically the combination of mucocutaneous findings with CNS symptoms and hyponatraemia—to avoid delaying diagnosis of neuro-BD solely based on normal MRI findings.

### [Management] Why is the interpretation of mildly elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) levels insufficient to definitively distinguish between CNS malignancy and neuro-inflammatory conditions?

Soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) is a biomarker of T-cell activation, proliferation, or death, and it is frequently employed to screen for primary CNS lymphomas in patients presenting with nonspecific neurological syndromes. However, relying on this marker in isolation is clinically precarious because mild elevations occur in both neoplastic and inflammatory states. 

In this case of concurrent acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) and systemic disease, the patient presented with fever, headache, and aseptic meningitis. Correspondingly, his serum sIL-2R level was mildly elevated at 704 U/mL, exceeding the reference upper limit of 496 U/mL. Although sIL-2R is informative for T-cell lymphoma, as explicitly noted in the discussion of this case report, "mild elevations are non-specific and may reflect inflammatory activity." The distinction arises because both malignant and autoimmune processes involve T-cell activation; for instance, the robust immune response driving Behçet’s disease pathology can release sIL-2R due to cytokine-mediated activation of monocytes and T-cells, independent of malignancy. 

In this specific patient, the diagnostic utility of the elevated sIL-2R was nullified by the definitive exclusion of malignancy through brain MRI and negative CSF cytology, and the confirmation of inflammatory etiology via normal immunological susceptibility patterns and the classic presence of oral aphthae and genital ulcers. Therefore, a mildly elevated sIL-2R must be interpreted as a sign of immune activity rather than neoplasia when contextualized by a full neurological, radiological, and pathological workup. Clinicians should avoid using sIL-2R as a standalone discriminator, as failure to do so may lead to false-positive attributions of malignancy in patients with active neuroinflammatory disorders.

### [Management] Despite standard guidelines recommending corticosteroids for acute neuro-Behçet's disease, what clinical reasoning supports the use of colchicine monotherapy in this specific case?

Good morning. While high-dose corticosteroids remain the standard first-line therapy for acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD), the rationale for colchicine monotherapy in this specific case rests on the pathophysiology of the SIADH complication and the limited radiological burden of the neurological involvement.

The primary clinical reasoning addresses the mechanism of the complications. In this patient, SIADH-induced hyponatraemia accompanied neurological symptoms, a combination attributed to CNS inflammation driving antidiuretic hormone release via IL-6-mediated pathways. Colchicine exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting neutrophil migration and suppressing cytokine production, specifically IL-1 and IL-6. In this instance, targeting this cytokine cascade successfully resolved both the systemic mucocutaneous inflammation and the hyponatraemia, addressing the root inflammatory driver without the broad immunosuppression required by corticosteroids.

Secondarily, the neurological presentation was atypical for severe neuro-BD. The patient presented with aseptic meningitis and, critically, had no abnormalities on initial brain MRI involving the hypothalamus or other typical lesion sites. The authors note that severe vascular involvement typically requiring aggressive treatment often manifests later in the disease course, whereas early-onset meningoencephalitis can be a milder, self-limiting course.

Therefore, the decision to use colchicine was supported by the patient's specific clinical trajectory: a lack of severe parenchymal lesions, the presence of a treatable cytokine-mediated complication (SIADH), and a rapid resolution of symptoms that justified trial of a non-immunosuppressive agent over standard steroid therapy in this early phase of diagnosis.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the direct pathophysiological link between severe hyponatremia and the development of rhabdomyolysis observed in this patient's laboratory profile?

Good morning. Regarding the link between the severe hyponatremia and rhabdomyolysis observed in this patient, the case report highlights that **severe hyponatremia itself directly precipitates muscle injury.**

In this specific instance, the patient’s syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone (SIADH) caused serum sodium to drop to 124 mmol/L, coinciding with an elevated creatine kinase of 1349 U/L. While the patient had significant systemic inflammation associated with Behçet’s disease, the authors explicitly identify the rhabdomyolysis as a "recognised but often overlooked complication of severe hyponatraemia" that is frequently not routinely investigated. The normalization of myogenic enzymes following the resolution of hyponatremia (and without the use of systemic corticosteroids) supports the conclusion that the profound electrolyte disturbance caused direct metabolic or mechanical muscle damage distinct from the primary inflammatory drive.

**Clinical Pearl:** When managing patients with acute hyponatremia, particularly severe cases associated with SIADH, you must include myogenic enzyme analysis in your workup. Do not attribute muscle weakness or enzyme elevation solely to the underlying inflammatory or infectious etiology; profound sodium imbalance is itself a direct pathophysiological driver of rhabdomyolysis.

### [Morning Report] What steps were taken in the initial differential diagnosis to rule out infectious encephalitis and neurosarcoidosis prior to the final diagnosis?

**Teaching Point: Dianosing Acute Neuro-Behçet’s Disease**

The diagnostic challenge in this case centered on distinguishing acute Behçet’s disease from infectious encephalitis and neurosarcoidosis, given the patient’s fever, headache, and aseptic meningitis. Our approach relied on a hierarchy of exclusion based on clinical, radiological, and laboratory data.

First, we prioritized ruling out infectious etiologies common in meningoencephalitis. Bacterial meningitis was excluded by the absence of neutrophilic predominance in the CSF and negative bacterial and acid-fast cultures. Viral meningoencephalitis, the most likely mimic, was further ruled out via negative CSF analysis for HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen.

Second, we evaluated neurosarcoidosis, which can present with normal MRI findings in early stages. To exclude this diagnosis, we assessed serum biomarkers typically associated with granulomatous inflammation. Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) and lysozyme levels were within normal limits. Additionally, while raised soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) can suggest malignancy, it is non-specific in inflammation and did not support lymphoproliferative disease in the absence of cytological evidence.

Once these mimics were excluded, we focused on the patient’s mucocutaneous etiology. While the initial presentation lacked genital ulcers, this finding emerged on day 5 of admission. Combined with oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and fever, the patient met the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease. This confirmed acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, explaining the concurrent syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone (SIADH). This case underscores that while infections are always the priority in a febrile patient with head-of-hospital, a high index of suspicion for autoinflammatory disorders may emerge once pathogen workup is thorough but negative.
