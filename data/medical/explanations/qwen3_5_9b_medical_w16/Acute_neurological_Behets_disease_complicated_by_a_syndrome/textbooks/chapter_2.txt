Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Workup: Neurological and Endocrine Evaluation

The diagnostic strategy employed for this patient centered on characterizing the acute neurological inflammation while concurrently evaluating the endocrine dysfunction associated with the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Initial neuroimaging, including non-contrast CT and MRI of the brain, was performed to identify structural abnormalities such as mass lesions, strokes, or granulomatous involvement typical of parenchymal neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD). The imaging revealed no abnormalities involving the hypothalamus or brain parenchyma. This finding is pedagogically significant because it highlights the limitations of neuroimaging in early-stage acute neuro-BD. While parenchymal lesions, particularly in the brainstem or infratemporal fossae, are hallmarks of deep vasculitide forms of neuro-Behçet's, acute meningitis-like presentations may yield normal imaging findings in the absence of restricted diffusion or enhancement. Clinicians must therefore rely heavily on clinical features, such as headache, fever, and the presence of mucocutaneous lesions (categorized under International Criteria for Behçet's Disease), rather than dismissing neurological involvement solely due to a normal MRI scan.

Following the initial imaging, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was crucial to define the nature of the central nervous system (CNS) inflammation. An opening pressure of 17 cmH₂O suggested potential hydrocephalus or inflammation affecting the ventricular system, though not to the degree of acute pseudotumor cerebri. CSF composition showed elevated protein at 0.97 g/L and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL), confirming a mononuclear inflammatory response rather than the neutrophilic predominance seen in bacterial meningitis. To exclude infectious etiologies that are mimics of aseptic meningitis, CSF was cultured for bacterial and mycobacterial pathogens, and specific polymerase chain reaction and antigen tests were negative for *Herpes Simplex Virus* (HSV), *Varicella Zoster Virus* (VZV), and *Cryptococcus*. Furthermore, cytology for malignant cells was negative. This negative infectious screen, in the presence of the clinical picture of fever and CNS symptoms, supported the diagnosis of aseptic meningitis secondary to neuro-Behçet's, emphasizing the necessity of broad pathogen exclusion when infectious causes are possible.

Concurrently, the workup addressed the severe hyponatraemia (serum sodium 124 mmol/L) which necessitated the evaluation of SIADH. Diagnostic standards for SIADH require the combination of hypotonic hyponatraemia (serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg) and inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg), occurring in a clinically euvolemic patient. High urinary sodium (88 mmol/L) further supported this by indicating renal salt wasting was not driven by hypovolaemia. The diagnosis was definitively supported by the presence of elevated antidiuretic hormone (ADH) of 22.4 pg/mL. The endocrine workup also included thyroid function tests and cortisol levels to rule out adrenal insufficiency or hypothyroidism, which can cause secondary hyponatraemia; both were within normal limits. This exclusion process is vital to prevent mismanagement of hyponatraemia with hypertonic saline, which carries risks of cerebral oedema reversal if an endocrine cause is later discovered.

To finalize the differential diagnosis, the team performed metabolic screening to rule out primary systemic disorders and malignancies that could explain the neurological and systemic inflammation. This included serum testing for sarcoidosis, measured via angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) and lysozyme, and haematological malignancy screening, assessed by soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R). While sIL-2R showed a mild elevation (704 U/mL), it was acknowledged as non-specific in the context of systemic inflammation, as was the normal lysozyme and ACE profile, making sarcoidosis or lymphoma unlikely. The absence of positive findings in these specific tumor markers, combined with the negative CSF cytology and lack of focal neurological deficits, allowed the clinicians to conclude that the inflammatory process was driven by the Behçet's disease spectrum rather than an alternative neoplastic or granulomatous mimic.
