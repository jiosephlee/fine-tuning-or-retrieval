Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 3: ## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Synthesis

In the context of this patient's multifocal presentation, the initial clinical reasoning prioritized life-threatening etiologies such as infectious meningoencephalitis, malignancy, and other autoimmune conditions before settling on a rare systemic vasculitis. The presence of fever, headache, and lymphocytic pleocytosis in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) initially raised concern for viral or bacterial meningitis. However, the diagnostic process relied heavily on the integration of culture results and polymerase chain reaction testing. Bacterial meningitis was effectively excluded by the absence of neutrophils and negative blood cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was ruled out by the negative results for acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing from the CSF. Furthermore, while viral etiology remained a primary consideration given the clinical syndrome, specific testing for herpes simplex virus (HSV) and varicella zoster virus (VZV) was performed. The negative status of CSF cultures for HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen, alongside the absence of viral antigen and antibodies, substantiated an aseptic etiology rather than a reactive infectious one. Empirical treatment with cefepime and acyclovir was subsequently discontinued once these pathogens were excluded, preventing unnecessary antibiotic exposure and highlighting the importance of serological and microbiological confirmation in neuroacutely ill patients.

Parallel to the infectious workup, the evaluation for primary CNS malignancy required syntheses of structural and cellular data. Although the elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level presented with a malignant differential, solitary elevations of this marker are non-specific and often reflect general inflammatory activity rather than lymphoma. The diagnostic team proceeded with neuroimaging and CSF cytology based on this nuance. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed no structural abnormalities, specifically excluding masses or lesions common in CNS lymphoma or neurosarcoidosis. Crucially, CSF cytology demonstrated no malignant cells. Additionally, screening for haematological malignancy was negative for human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody. While the mild sIL-2R elevation suggested an immune activation, the lack of radiological evidence and cellular malignancy in the fluid supported a diagnosis of aseptic meningitis driven by inflammatory rather than neoplastic causes.

Distinguishing Behçet’s disease from other systemic inflammatory disorders required a targeted exclusion of mimicries, particularly neurosarcoidosis and systemic lupus erythematosus. Neurosarcoidosis was a significant consideration given the headache and CNS involvement, yet diagnostic tests for the disease proved unrevealing. Specifically, serum angiotensin-converting enzyme levels were within normal limits, and lysozyme levels were unremarkable. In the absence of characteristic systemic skin nodules or ocular involvement, and with MRI findings lacking the typical basal meningo-meningeal enhancement, neurosarcoidosis became unlikely. Similarly, screening for other autoimmune connective tissue diseases resulted in negative autoantibody profiles, and the patient lacked the multi-system cutaneous or serositis features typical of systemic lupus or vasculitis syndromes. This exclusionary process reinforced the hypothesis that the systemic and neurological findings were manifestations of a single unifying inflammatory construct rather than overlapping autoimmune entities.

With potential infectious, neoplastic, and alternative autoimmune causes excluded, the diagnostic focus shifted to the formal application of the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD). This scoring system uses oral and genital aphthosis, skin lesions, and ocular symptoms as primary criteria. In this case, the patient scored five points based on the presence of oral aphthae, which contributed two points, genital aphthosis, which is a highly specific marker contributing another two points, and folliculitis-like rashes, adding one point. A cumulative score of four or greater defines definite disease within the ICBD framework. It is important to note that the patient was in a newly diagnosed phase; the clinical features of oral aphthae and the subsequent development of genital ulcers aligned perfectly with the classic mucocutaneous distribution of BD. The patient was HLA-B51 positive, a genetic risk factor that, while not diagnostic in isolation, supports the clinical finding of definite disease.

Finally, the establishment of concurrent systemic and neurological disease relied on linking the mucocutaneous diagnosis with the CNS presentation. The diagnosis of acute neurological Behçet’s disease was assigned based on the criteria linkage, where CNS symptoms occur in patients already classified as BD. In this instance, acute neuro-BD was characterized by aseptic meningitis, evidenced by lymphocytic pleocytosis and increased protein without parenchymal involvement on MRI. Initially, the absence of brain lesions on imaging could have obscured the diagnosis, as neuro-Behçet’s often presents with parenchymal damage involving the brainstem. However, the temporal association of neurological symptoms with the flare of mucocutaneous lesions, combined with the exclusion of other causes of aseptic meningitis, solidified the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. Consequently, the patient was diagnosed with definite systemic Behçet’s disease concurrently with acute neuro-Behçet’s disease, illustrating the necessity to consider neurological involvement in new cases of BD, even when imaging appears non-specific or normal.
