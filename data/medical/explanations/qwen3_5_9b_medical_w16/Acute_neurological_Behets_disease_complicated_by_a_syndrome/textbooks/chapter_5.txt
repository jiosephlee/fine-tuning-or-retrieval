Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 5: ## Therapeutic Strategy and Clinical Outcomes

The therapeutic approach in this case highlights the nuance required in managing neurological manifestations of Behçet’s disease, particularly when the standard of care diverges from established protocols. While high-dose systemic corticosteroids remain a mainstay for purulent neuro-Behçet’s disease due to their rapid anti-inflammatory action, this patient was successfully managed with colchicine monotherapy. This decision was based on the patient’s concurrent diagnosis of systemic BD, where colchicine is already established for mucocutaneous control, and the absence of more aggressive meningoencephalitis (such as brainstem parenchymal damage) that typically warrants stronger immunosuppression. Colchicine exerts its therapeutic effect by inhibiting microtubule polymerization, thereby preventing neutrophil migration and reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and IL-6. In this specific instance, the choice to avoid corticosteroids minimized the metabolic and infectious risks associated with high-dose steroids, demonstrating that in selected cases of acute neuro-BD, milder immunomodulation can effectively control both mucocutaneous and neurological disease activity.

Concurrently, the management of Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH) required careful application of fluid and electrolyte principles in the context of CNS inflammation. SIADH was confirmed by the triad of euvolemic hyponatremia, inappropriately concentrated urine, and high urine sodium in the setting of low serum osmolality. Standard protocols often involve hypertonic saline for severe neurologic symptoms to prevent seizures, but the patient was managed conservatively with fluid restriction and strict sodium monitoring. This approach was necessitated by the likely reversible nature of the SIADH responding to the resolution of the underlying neuroinflammation. Rapid correction of hyponatremia was avoided to prevent Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome. The successful management of hyponatraemia without hypertonic saline underscores the importance of treating the primary inflammatory driver rather than aggressively correcting electrolytes before the CNS inflammation subsides.

The clinical response to therapy provides a clear timeline for the correlation between systemic inflammation, neurological stability, and electrolyte homeostasis. Symptoms such as fever, headache, vertigo, and general malaise began to resolve immediately upon colchicine initiation, coinciding with the control of mucocutaneous lesions. However, the normalization of serum sodium levels and the resolution of pleocytosis took longer, occurring over a two-week period. This delayed response in hyponatremia resolution reflects the time required for the hypothalamic-pituitary axis to re-equilibrate following the reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokine drive. The absence of focal neurological deficits and the lack of need for rehabilitation for motor function despite the initial decline indicate that the neurological involvement was primarily meningoencephalitic rather than parenchymal.

Long-term follow-up data from this patient offers critical insights into the prognosis of concurrent systemic and neurological disease activity. After hospital discharge, the patient remained on colchicine monotherapy for approximately four years without recurrence of Behçet’s disease or neurological symptoms. This stability suggests that the acute episode, while severe, did not progress to a fatal or permanent disabling course, likely due to the effective suppression of the inflammatory cascade. Clinicians should use this case to recognize that not all acute neuro-BD cases require aggressive escalation to cyclophosphamide or intravenous steroids, provided the clinical phenotype is not dominated by rapidly disabling brainstem or capillary syndromes.

Pedagogically, this case reinforces several essential diagnostic and safety principles regarding hyponatremia in systemic inflammatory disorders. Students must recognize that persistent hiccups can be a clinical surrogate for severe, untreated hyponatremia, especially in patients with undiagnosed CNS inflammation. Furthermore, the case illustrates that inadequate investigation into underlying endocrine disorders (SIADH) in a feverish patient with headache can lead to missed diagnoses of neuro-Behçet’s. By understanding the link between elevated inflammatory markers (like CRP) and ADH dysregulation, clinicians can prioritize CNS evaluations in patients presenting with unexplained hyponatremia and mucocutaneous lesions, ensuring that the root cause of the electrolyte disturbance is addressed to prevent complications such as rhabdomyolysis and neurological decline.
