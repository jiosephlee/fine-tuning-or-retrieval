Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 4: ## Pathophysiological Mechanisms: CNS Inflammation and ADH Dysregulation

The clinical presentation of this patient highlights a distinct pathophysiological pathway where acute neuro-inflammation triggered a systemic endocrine complication. In Behçet's disease (BD), the development of syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone (SIADH) is primarily attributed to the inflammatory milieu affecting the central nervous system (CNS). While neuroinflammation is well-documented in Behçet’s neurological manifestations, its direct role in hormonal dysregulation represents a critical diagnostic consideration. In this case, the patient exhibited signs of aseptic meningitis with elevated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein and pleocytosis, despite a normal brain MRI. This underscores that functional inflammation within the CNS parenchyma can occur without radiographic evidence of lesions. Specifically, the hypothalamus, which regulates water balance and antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion, is susceptible to dysregulation when subject to intense meningeal or parenchymal inflammation. The absence of focal neuroimaging abnormalities in early-stage neuro-BD, as seen in this patient, does not exclude central involvement that impacts hypothalamic osmoregulatory centers.

The biological link between CNS inflammation and the subsequent hyponatremia involves inflammatory cytokine dysregulation. Central nervous system inflammation induces the release of proinflammatory mediators, most notably interleukin-6 (IL-6). Elevated IL-6 levels have been shown to directly stimulate the release of ADH from the posterior pituitary and override normal inhibitory feedback from plasma osmolality. In the context of this case, while direct IL-6 serum or CSF levels were not measured, the markedly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) concentration (180 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate served as markers of a robust systemic and CNS inflammatory response, consistent with a cytokine-driven mechanism. The hypothalamus perceives a change in osmolality as secondary hyper-tonicity when ADH is inappropriately secreted, leading to impaired water excretion. Therefore, the hyponatremia observed (124 mmol/L) is not merely a side effect but a manifestation of cytokine-mediated interference in endocrine signaling pathways.

Understanding the complications of severe hyponatremia requires a recognition of the systemic metabolic sequelae, such as rhabdomyolysis. In this patient, the profound hyponatremia was accompanied by elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels of 1349 U/L. While muscle injury can arise from Behçet's vasculitis, in this context, the elevated myogenic enzymes were a recognized complication of severe sodium imbalance. Cellular swelling due to water influx in a hypotonic environment can lead to muscle membrane disruption and necrosis, particularly in the setting of acute neurological stress. These findings illustrate that patients with acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH should be monitored for secondary manifestations of electrolyte derangement that may not be immediately apparent without specific evaluation, such as monitoring myogenic markers.

Distinguishing idiopathic mechanisms from pharmacological triggers is essential, as SIADH in Behçet’s disease can occur secondary to medications like cyclophosphamide. However, the drug profile in this case did not include cyclophosphamide or other immunosuppressants known to precipitate SIADH. This effectively rules out drug-induced dysfunction, suggesting that the pathophysiology was intrinsic to the active autoimmune process rather than iatrogenic. This distinction reinforces that in newly diagnosed cases where SIADH presents concurrently with specific neurological and mucocutaneous features of Behçet's, the primary disease activity—and associated inflammatory cytokines—should be viewed as the primary etiology. The spontaneous nature of the thyroid and adrenal axes (normal cortisol and thyroid function) further helped exclude other endocrine pathologies, narrowing the differential diagnosis to inflammatory CNS dysregulation.

Finally, the resolution of symptoms provides insight into the specific inflammatory targets within this pathway. The patient's improvement on colchicine monotherapy, without the use of corticosteroids, suggests a mechanism sensitive to pyrin-associated blockade of cytokine production. Colchicine inhibits neutrophil migration and suppresses proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-6. This supports the hypothesis that IL-6-mediated inflammation, driven by aberrant monocyte activation characteristic of Behçet's disease, is the mechanistic driver of the hyponatremia. Since the monocytes in Behçet's produce spontaneous IL-6, the suppression of this specific cytokine arm by colchicine likely normalized ADH secretion. This highlights that endocrine complications of neuro-BD may respond to anti-inflammatory agents that target cytokine pathways, offering a targeted approach in patients where corticosteroids may be contraindicated or ineffective.
