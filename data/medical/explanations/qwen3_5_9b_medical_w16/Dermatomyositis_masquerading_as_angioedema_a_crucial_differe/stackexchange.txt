Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Pathophysiology] What is the underlying pathophysiological mechanism of the subcutaneous edema observed in this case of dermatomyositis, and how does it differ from the histaminergic or bradykinin pathways underlying typical angioedema?

The subcutaneous edema observed in this case is best characterized as **pseudoangioedema** driven by active **myositis** rather than a specific mediator release pathway. Ultimately, this swelling is a local manifestation of the systemic autoimmune process affecting the skin and muscles.

To understand the pathophysiology, one must distinguish the inflammatory mechanisms involved. In this patient with anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis, the neck swelling results from direct **inflammatory infiltration and tissue reaction** associated with the myositis. As the discussion notes, "the subcutaneous oedema in our patient's case arises from muscle inflammation in the setting of myositis." Inflammatory cytokines and infiltrating cells cause increased vascular permeability and tissue expansion in the neck strap muscles and deep fascia, mimicking the bulk of angioedema without utilizing the specific vascular mediators of true angioedema.

This contrasts sharply with the pathways underlying typical angioedema:
1.  **Histaminergic Angioedema:** Driven by mast cell degranulation and histamine release, typically IgE-mediated. It usually responds to antihistamines and often involves urticaria (whealing), which was absent here.
2.  **Bradykinin-mediated Angioedema:** Occurs via Complement C1 inhibitor deficiency (hereditary) or ACE inhibitor therapy, leading to unopposed bradykinin release.
3.  This Case (Dermatomyositis Pseudoangioedema):** It is **non-histaminergic and non-bradykinin** in the classic sense. Crucially, the workup showed **normal C3 and C4 complement levels**, effectively ruling out hypocomplementemic vasculitis or complement-mediated bradykinin surges often seen in angioedema variants.

Therefore, the key teaching point is that neck swelling in dermatomyositis is a **secondary inflammatory process** due to direct myositis extension, not a primary vascular permeability disorder mediated by kinins or histamines. The presence of elevated CK, characteristic cutaneous signs (periungual erythema, nailfold hemorrhages), and normal complement levels should prompt a workup for dermatomyositis rather than immediate empiric treatment for idiopathic angioedema.

### [Clinical Pearls] During the physical examination, which specific nailfold finding and extra-muscular muscle sign should alert the clinician to a possible autoimmune inflammatory myopathy in a patient presenting with unexplained neck swelling?

During the physical examination of a patient presenting with unexplained neck swelling, the combination of **nailfold capillaroscopy findings showing proximal microhaemorrhages and avascular areas** and **proximal muscle weakness (specifically hip flexor weakness)** should prompt a high-index suspicion for autoimmune inflammatory myopathy, specifically dermatomyositis.

In the case described, a man in his 40s was initially misdiagnosed with idiopathic angioedema or deep neck space infection. The crucial pivot in diagnosis occurred after the clinician recognized subtle cutaneous and musculoskeletal clues inconsistent with typical angioedema. While the patient had diffuse neck edema, a thorough nailfold capillaroscopy revealed **microhaemorrhages and avascular areas**, a hallmark vascular abnormality associated with dermatomyositis. Furthermore, concurrent **proximal muscle weakness**—manifesting as reduced power in his hip flexors—and subsequent **dysphagia** (indicating pharyngeal muscle involvement) were identified on examination.

The clinical reasoning here relies on the recognition that the neck swelling was not mediated by histamine or bradykinin, but rather represented inflammatory myositis involving the neck muscles and deep cervical fascia. The presence of nailfold capillary dropout and hemorrhage in the context of proximal weakness, even in the absence of classic cutaneous signs like Gottron’s papules (which were absent initially), strongly favors an inflammatory myopathy. This presentation underscores that dermatomyositis can mimic angioedema, and the "extra-muscular" manifestation of the disease in this case included both the swallowing difficulty and the inflammatory swelling of the neck muscles. Elevated creatine kinase levels should be investigated when these physical signs are present alongside unexplained neck swelling.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When a patient presents with progressive neck swelling, what are the primary differential diagnoses that should be ruled out before considering an autoimmune etiology like dermatomyositis in the absence of classic cutaneous rashes?

When evaluating a patient with progressive neck swelling, the clinician must categorically rule out life-threatening infectious, structural, and vascular etiologies before attributing the presentation to an immune-mediated process like dermatomyositis. In the case we are reviewing, the patient presented with a 3-week history of swelling initially mimicking Ludwig’s angina. Standard workup for this included a CT of the neck, a flexible nasendoscopy by ENT, and a search for bacterial or deep neck space infection. Because infectious causes were excluded, the differential shifted to primary angioedema.

Before concluding an autoimmune etiology, you must exclude hereditary or acquired angioedema (hence C1 esterase inhibitor function and C3/C4 levels) and bradykinin-mediated causes, such as ACE inhibitor use or beta-blocker hypersensitivity. In the reported case, the patient’s C1 esterase and complement levels were normal, and he denied angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor use, making these less likely.

While less common than infection or angioedema, one must also rule out superior vena cava syndrome and orofacial granulomatosis, particularly if swelling is asymmetric or associated with lymphadenopathy and dysphagia. Myasthenia gravis and other neuromuscular disorders should also be considered if weakness is prominent, though they rarely cause massive tissue edema.

The diagnostic pivot point in this specific presentation, and your critical teaching point for the team, is recognizing **pseudoangioedema** secondary to inflammatory myopathy. As demonstrated in this case, subcutaneous tissue oedema can arise from active myositis (muscle inflammation) rather than true angioedema. To secure this diagnosis, a high index of suspicion is required when infectious and angioedemic workups are unrevealing. Specifically, you should inquire about proximal muscle weakness and, crucially, obtain a creatine kinase level. In this patient, a level of 16,184 U/L was obtained during the immune workup phase, triggering the correct referral to immunology.

Therefore, if infection, bradykinin-mediated angioedema, and compressive syndromes are excluded, and the patient has elevated inflammatory markers or muscle enzymes, you must look for the "subtle cutaneous signs" that often accompany the swelling, such as nailfold capillary changes. Dermatomyositis typically involves the pharyngeal muscles, causing dysphagia, which was a key early sign in this patient despite the absence of classic heliotrope rash or Gottron’s papules at presentation. This case underscores that autoimmune inflammatory myopathies should be in your differential for unexplained cervical swelling once the common "rule-outs" have been thoroughly excluded.

### [Clinical Pearls] How does the pathophysiologic mechanism of dysphagia in dermatomyositis manifest clinically, and why is the barium swallow study critical in confirming the diagnosis and guiding nutritional management?

**Answer**

In dermatomyositis, dysphagia typically manifests as a direct consequence of inflammatory myositis affecting the skeletal muscles involved in swallowing, specifically the pharyngeal and laryngeal musculature. In this case, despite the initial focus on skin changes and neck swelling, the underlying pathophysiology was an autoimmune inflammatory process involving the head and neck muscles, evidenced by the elevated creatine kinase and inflammatory muscle changes on PET and biopsy. This muscle inflammation compromises the coordinated closure of the glottis and propulsion of the bolus, leading to aspiration. Clinically, this patient demonstrated not only proximal muscle weakness but also indirect signs of bulbar involvement, such as increased secretions and coughing during nasendoscopy, which were precursors to the aspiration pneumonitis and subsequent severe pharyngeal dysphagia.

The video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow study is critical in this context for two principal reasons: confirming the functional diagnosis of the swallowing disorder and guiding nutritional management. While the diagnosis of dermatomyositis itself was established via myositis serology (anti-SAE1 antibodies) and biopsy, the barium swallow provided the objective evidence required to confirm **oropharyngeal dysphagia**. In this patient, the study revealed severe pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration of fluid into the vocal cords, confirming that the hydro-dynamics of the swallow were compromised rather than the structural airway alone being obstructed by the neck swelling.

This objective visualization was decisive for nutritional management. Recognizing the aspiration risk identified during the study, the team appropriately initiated enteral feeding via a nasogastric tube at the time of discharge rather than tolerating oral intake, thereby preventing further aspiration injury while the immunosuppressive therapy (corticosteroids, IVIG, and rituximab took effect) allowed for muscle recovery. Ultimately, the barium swallow study validated the need for temporary enteral access, which was safely discontinued once muscle strength and swallowing coordination recovered during the follow-up period.

**Clinical Pearl:** In patients presenting with unexplained neck swelling and dysphagia, always consider inflammatory myopathy, particularly when standard infectious or allergic etiologies are excluded. While serology and biopsy confirm the systemic etiology, the barium swallow is the gold standard for characterizing the swallow mechanism and mitigating aspiration risk through appropriate feeding tube placement.

### [Morning Report] In the setting of diagnostic uncertainty between deep neck space infection and angioedema, what single laboratory finding provided the most immediate evidence to reevaluate the clinical diagnosis and initiate immunosuppression?

In this case, the pivotal laboratory finding that reoriented the clinical diagnosis from deep neck space infection or idiopathic angioedema to dermatomyositis was a markedly elevated serum creatine kinase (CK) level. Specifically, the level had risen to 16,184 U/L, providing immediate evidence of significant muscle breakdown.

While angioedema and deep neck space infections are inflammatory processes, they do not typically cause the massive release of muscle enzymes seen in severe myopathy. In contrast, dermatomyositis is an inflammatory myopathy characterized by direct inflammation and necrosis of skeletal muscle fibers. This specific enzyme profile distinguished the subcutaneous "angioedema" from the underlying pathology of myositis causing the tissue swelling. This laboratory data justified the shift to a provisional diagnosis of dermatomyositis, prompting the initiation of aggressive immunosuppression, including intravenous methylprednisolone, to prevent airway compromise and further muscle damage.

In clinical practice, when a patient presents with unexplained neck swelling and atypical physical signs, such as proximal weakness or cutaneous changes, a creatine kinase level should be prioritized. A significantly elevated CK, alongside subtle nailfold changes and rash, is a critical "red flag" that should trigger rheumatologic evaluation for inflammatory myopathy over medical management for primary otolaryngologic pathology.

### [Management] Why was methotrexate contraindicated or avoided in the treatment regimen of this patient, specifically considering the patient's baseline pulmonary status as confirmed by imaging and lung function tests?

Methotrexate was avoided in this patient primarily due to the well-documented association with interstitial lung disease (ILD) and pulmonary fibrosis, given the patient's underlying pulmonary compromise. Although the initial chest CT did not show frank pulmonary fibrosis, the patient demonstrated abnormal pulmonary function tests with a restrictive pattern and radiographic evidence of pulmonary nodules and consolidation.

Furthermore, this patient’s specific serology (anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis) is associated with a high prevalence of ILD, reported at approximately 43% in cohort studies. In patients with inflammatory myopathies who already exhibit baseline pulmonary abnormality, the addition of methotrexate poses a significant risk of inducing methotrexate-induced pneumonitis or exacerbating existing lung pathology. Therefore, the treatment regimen prioritized lung-sparing immunosuppressants, specifically mycophenolate mofetil, which allows for effective disease control while minimizing the risk of further respiratory injury. This decision underscores the necessity of evaluating baseline pulmonary function and imaging in inflammatory myopathies before selecting steroid-sparing agents with potential pulmonary toxicity.

### [Management] This patient received a multi-modal immunosuppressive regimen including corticosteroids, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and rituximab. Under what clinical circumstances would such an aggressive combination be justified over standard monotherapy?

This aggressive, multi-modal immunosuppressive regimen is justified in cases of dermatomyositis where there is **organ-threatening involvement, rapid functional decline, or a high-risk serological phenotype that suggests a difficult-to-treat course.**

In the context of this case report, the decision to deploy corticosteroids, mycophenolate mofetil, IVIG, and rituximab was driven by three specific clinical factors:

1.  **Airway and Dysphagia Risk:** The patient presented with cervical myositis mimicking angioedema, carrying a direct threat to the airway. Crucially, he had severe dysphagia and aspiration pneumonia. In severe myositis affecting bulbar function or the airway muscles, standard monotherapy (e.g., steroids alone) may not suppress inflammation rapidly enough or sufficiently to prevent long-term damage. The prompt course of IV methylprednisolone addresses the acute crisis, while IVIG and rituximab provide rapid and broad immunomodulation for the autoimmune storm.
2.  **Antibody Phenotype:** The patient tested positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies. Literature reviews of anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis consistently associate this profile with severe functional impairment, including a high burden of dysphagia (reported in 46% in the review) and interstitial lung disease (43%). This phenotype often requires more than standard therapy to achieve durable remission.
3.  **Safety and Steroid-Sparing Necessity:** The clinicians avoided methotrexate due to the patient's existing ILD risk (restrictive pattern on PFTs) and the known association of methotrexate with pulmonary fibrosis. Conversely, IVIG was favored as it is effective for cutaneous and myopathic disease subtypes with lymphoproliferative tendencies and has a high therapeutic index in the acute setting.

**The Clinical Pearl:**
Do not dismiss dermatomyositis in patients presenting with unexplained neck swelling or dysphagia. When the presentation mimics angioedema but is accompanied by elevated creatine kinase and distinct cutaneous signs (e.g., nailfold hemorrhages), escalate treatment immediately from corticosteroids alone to a multi-modal regimen involving B-cell depletion (rituximab) or cytokine modulation (IVIG), particularly if high-risk myositis antibodies (anti-SAE1, anti-MDA5) or interstitial lung disease are present. This "salami" approach avoids single-agent toxicity (like fibrosis from methotrexate) while maximizing speed of control in life-threatening scenarios.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the function of the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme (SAE1) targeted by the autoantibody in this case, and what clinical phenotype is typically associated with anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis?

Anti-SAE1 antibodies target the small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1, a protein structurally similar to ubiquitin that is responsible for the post-translational modification of target proteins via sumoylation. While this patient notably presented with the unusual, pseudo-angioedematous swelling of the neck, the clinical phenotype associated with SAE1-positive dermatomyositis is classically characterized by specific cutaneous findings, including heliotrope rash and Gottron’s papules. This antibody profile is further defined by a significant burden of extra-muscular disease and malignancy risk; the literature cited in the case indicates that 46% of SAE1-positive patients develop dysphagia, 43% develop interstitial lung disease, and 18% are diagnosed with malignancy. Recognizing this association in a patient with myopathy or unexplained facial/neck oedema is critical, as it necessitates aggressive immunosuppressive therapy to prevent aspiration and confirms the high need for comprehensive cancer surveillance.

### [Pathophysiology] What specific histopathological and immunohistochemical features on muscle biopsy differentiate dermatomyositis from polymyositis, as seen in this patient's diagnosis?

In this patient, the muscle biopsy distinguished dermatomyositis from polymyositis through two specific pathological features: **perifascicular necrosis** and **membrane attack complex (C5b-9) deposition**.

The histopathology demonstrated foci of necrotic, vacuolated, and regenerating myofibres located specifically in a **perifascicular distribution**. While polymyositis typically presents with endomysial inflammation and T-cell invasion of non-necrotic fibers, the perifascicular pattern is the hallmark of dermatomyositis. The immunohistochemistry further confirmed this diagnosis by revealing **complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposition in the perifascicular endomysial capillaries**. This finding reflects the microangiopathic pathophysiology of dermatomyositis—complement-mediated vascular injury—whereas polymyositis lacks this specific vascular deposition pattern.

These features, combined with the patient's clinical findings of skin changes (Holster sign, periungual erythema) and elevated creatine kinase, confirmed the diagnosis of dermatomyositis in this case.

### [Morning Report] According to current International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines, why was this patient classified as high-risk for malignancy despite the absence of clinically evident tumors at presentation?

This patient was classified as high-risk for malignancy according to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) criteria because the guidelines stratify risk based on specific serological and clinical features, not solely on the absence of radiological findings. 

While no malignancy was identified on his initial CT, PET scan, or tumor markers, his case met two of the IMACS-defined high-risk factors: the confirmed diagnosis of dermatomyositis and the presence of clinically significant dysphagia. He also presented with two intermediate risk factors: male sex and anti-SAE1 antibody positivity. 

In the IMACS framework, the accumulation of these risk factors dictates the screening protocol rather than the initial imaging appearance. Therefore, the patient was deemed high-risk, necessitating comprehensive baseline surveillance (including upper and lower GI endoscopy) to exclude occult paraneoplastic disease. This highlights a critical learning point for residents: in dermatomyositis, the risk of associated malignancy is driven by the immunophenotype (antibody profile) and specific clinical phenotypes (like dysphagia) requiring rigorous screening even when constitutional symptoms and imaging are initially reassuring.
