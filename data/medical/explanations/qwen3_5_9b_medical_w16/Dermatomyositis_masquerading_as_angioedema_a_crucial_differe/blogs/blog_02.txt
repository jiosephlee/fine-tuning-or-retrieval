Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# The Nailfold Window: Capillaroscopy as a High-Yield Triage Tool in Unexplained Swelling

**## A Diagnosis of Dissonance**

A 44-year-old man walks into the ED with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling. Your initial differential? Ludwig’s angina, deep neck space infection, or perhaps he’s on an ACE inhibitor and suffering idiopathic angioedema. You run the CT, the tube, and maybe the C1-esterase levels, but the diagnosis remains elusive. The patient is afebrile, has no fever response, and despite the alarming swelling, there is no airway compromise. This is the classic dissonance that separates rule-out from rule-in medicine. In this scenario, the patient is not facing an infection or mediator-driven edema; he is battling a systemic autoimmune process.

The case below highlights exactly how easy it is to miss the underlying pathology when faced with "self-limiting" but progressive swelling. The patient presented with diffuse neck oedema involving strap muscles, initially reviewed by ENT. While structural and infective causes were ruled out, he was eventually referred for suspected idiopathic angioedema. Crucially, the system was triggered not by a classic rash or breathing difficulty, but by the persistence of swelling and subtle muscle signs (hip flexor weakness). The true culprit was a creatine kinase level of 16,184 U/L and, even earlier, a bedside tool that could be easily overlooked: nailfold capillaroscopy.

**## Capillaroscopy: The Window to the Pathogenesis**

Nailfold capillaroscopy is often relegated to dermatology specialty clinics or PLAID (Lupus) rounds, but its utility as a triage tool in internal medicine and emergency settings is underutilized. It is a low-cost, rapid adjunct that can reveal microvascular changes indicative of inflammatory myopathies before serum markers are even drawn. In the case described, the exam revealed proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas.

These findings are the tip of the iceberg. In dermatomyositis, capillaroscopy often demonstrates dilated capillary loops, gap formation (dilations between capillaries), and even erythema. These are distinct from the telangiectasias often seen in CREST syndrome. When you see these microvascular laxities amidst vague or "angioedema-like" swelling, you are bridging the gap between a soft tissue complaint and a systemic process. It transforms the clinical question from "Why is this neck swollen?" to "Is this an inflammatory myopathy?"

**## Clinical Reasoning and the CK Test**

This patient was not discharged on a simple course of steroids because a standard workup for angioedema failed to yield an answer (normal C1 esterase, normal C3/C4). However, the physical exam eventually revealed erythema over the nasolabial folds and a rash on the lateral thigh. Why were these signs initially missed? Because the neck swelling was the dominating complaint.

The traction of the case was picking up a "red flag" that shouldn't just be ignored: persistent, multifocal swelling that doesn't respond to time and inflammatory markers. A key learning point here is the utility of the creatine kinase (CK) level. Requested as "atypical," the CK blew the lid off with a rise to 16,184 U/L, confirming the muscle inflammation. While you shouldn't blame the CK, you should be ready to order it earlier when clinical pictures don't fit the anatomic presentation. Furthermore, this patient had a positive anti-SS1 (SAE1) autoantibody, which carries specific implications regarding prognosis and malignancy risk.

**## Differential Diagnosis: When Is It Not Angioedema?**

Not all neck swelling is allergic. In this specific case, the differential included deep neck space infections, orofacial granulomatosis, and superior vena cava syndrome. However, the presence of a "pseudoangioedema" phenomenon in dermatomyositis represents a crucial distinction. Pseudoangioedema is uncommon—only 8 out of 121 patients in one cohort—but it is where we stand in the case provided.

When you see unexplained angioedema, especially with constitutional symptoms like proximal weakness or coughing (indicating aspiration due to dysphagia), move your list of suspects. Interstitial lung disease was present here too, mandated by the restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests and the specific pulmonary nodules on CT. The takeaway is that if a patient presents with "angioedema" symptoms but has no response to standard therapies or has systemic signs, don't stick to the script of histamine/bradykinin. Look for the subcutaneous tissue oedema arising from muscle inflammation in the setting of myositis.

### Take-Home Points

*   **Don't discard inflammatory myopathy for "angioedema**: Unexplained neck swelling with proximal weakness or aspiration risk should trigger a re-evaluation for dermatomyositis.
*   **Capillaroscopy is a triage tool**: Simple nailfold changes (microhaemorrhages, avascular areas, gap formation) can point to Dermatomyositis before serum CK and antibody panels return.
*   **Get the labs early**: If the presentation is atypical for angioedema (e.g., persistent, no trigger, unresponsive), order a Creatine Kinase level to rule out myositis rapidly.
*   **Monitor for Malignancy**: Anti-SAE1 associated Dermatomyositis carries a malignancy risk (approx. 18% in literature) and warrants comprehensive screening including CT, tumor markers, and endoscopy per IMACS guidelines.
*   **Treat the swelling from within**: Reducing the myositis resolves the pseudoangioedema; treatment involves steroids and immunosuppression (e.g., IVIG, Rituximab) rather than antihistamines.
