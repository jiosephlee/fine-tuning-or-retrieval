Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# When "Idiopathic Angioedema" Ignores the Physical Exam: A Dermatomyositis Differential

Here’s the thing about neck swelling in the emergency department: the differential list usually scrolls down from Ludwig’s angina to drug history, then to idiopathic angioedema. In almost every case of subacute neck swelling, we are looking for infection, structural anomalies, or bradykinin/histamine dynamics. We rule out deep neck abscesses with a CT scan, order C1 esterase inhibitor levels, and if everything looks normal, we watch and wait. It’s the safe path. But sometimes, the safe path leads right into a minefield of missed systemic illness. 

This is a story of a patient who looked like they had a neck full of water but turned out to be a classic autoimmune presentation of dermatomyositis masquerading as a primary ENT or allergy diagnosis. A 40-year-old male presented with three weeks of progressive neck swelling and hip flexor weakness. He was afebrile, not hypoxic, and his CT scan showed diffuse oedema of the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space without abscess formation. He was transferred to a quaternary center because the initial workup for angioedema and deep neck infection came back negative. By the time the clinicians asked, "Do you have muscle weakness," the answer was yes. The bottom line for every emergency physician seeing this presentation is to pause and ask: Is this fluid, or is it inflammation?

## The Diagnostic Pivot Point

The subtlety in this case lies in the physical examination details that often get swallowed up by the headline of a swollen neck. The patient wasn't just swollen; he was photodistributed and had specific texture changes. The on-call immunologist noted generalised facial erythema over the nasolabial folds, along with subtle periungual erythema and nailfold microhaemorrhages seen on capillaroscopy. These are the Reginald Swyer signs of dermatomyositis: the rash and the nailfold changes. 

What really clinched it for the clinicians was a singular pivot: a Creatine Kinase test. In a patient with a "normal" white blood cell count and localized swelling, we often think we need to rule out angioedema causes first. Because the C1 esterase inhibitors and complement levels were normal, the doctors caught the discrepancy between a "normal" angioedema workup and a swollen neck. They didn't just accept "idiopathic"; they ordered a myositis screen. A CK level of 16,184 U/L blew the doors off. The dysphagia that became apparent during the nasendoscopy was not just functional; it was obstructive, requiring a barium swallow to document residue and penetration. This wasn't just a rash with myopathy; this was significant subcutaneous tissue oedema and muscle inflammation driving the neck presentation.

## Anti-SAE1 Dermatomyositis and Airway Risk

The genetic mutation testing confirmed the suspicion. This patient tested positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies. While anti-Mi-2 or anti-TIF1-gamma are often talked about regarding malignancy, SAE1-positive dermatomyositis comes with its own distinct risk profile, particularly regarding interstitial lung disease and malignancy screening. In this patient, the clinical impact was immediate: the airway was the primary concern because the neck swelling involved muscle inflammation and oedema rather than histaminergic vascular permeability. 

The treatment was aggressive because the risk of progression was high. He received high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone, followed by maintenance prednisone, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and mycophenolate mofetil. We have to recognize the intensity required. In dermatomyositis, especially when associated with significant dysphagia or subcutaneous oedema, unaddressed inflammation can lead to aspiration pneumonia and rapid motor decline. This patient aspirated frequently and required a nasogastric tube for feeding, but the CK normalised within three weeks and the swelling resolved. However, this case also flagged the need for cancer screening. With a dysphagia history and the antibody profile, he was put on a high-risk malignancy surveillance protocol, highlighting that dermatomyositis is not just a skin and muscle disease; it is a systemic marker.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Messages

*   **Do not default to angioedema:** If a patient has "idiopathic" angioedema or neck swelling that doesn't respond to standard therapy, think inflammatory myopathy immediately.
*   **Look for the Holster sign:** Erythema over the face and nasolabial folds alongside nailfold capillaroscopy changes (microhaemorrhages) are critical screening tools for DM when neck swelling is present.
*   **CK is your pivot point:** In unexplained neck swelling, a Creatine Kinase test should be as high on the list as C1 esterase assays; a massive elevation with normal muscle strength initially can precede the full picture of myositis.
*   **Dysphagia drives risk:** The presence of dysphagia in this context signals not only airway risk but a systemic severity index that mandates malignancy screening per IMACS guidelines.
*   **Subacute swelling equals more than infection:** A 3-week timeline for neck swelling is too long for simple angioedema and too short for typical deep neck infections, making a rheumatological differential essential before discharging home.
