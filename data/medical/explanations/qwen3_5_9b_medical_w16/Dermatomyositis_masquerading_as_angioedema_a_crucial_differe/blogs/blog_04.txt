Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Beyond the Swollen Neck: The Mandatory Malignancy Screen in Autoimmune Myopathy

Nothing is quite as frustrating in the ED as the patient with the neck that looks like an injury, acts like an infection, or mimics an allergy. You crack the story, order the CT, wait for the ESR and CRP to dance, and then you realize the emergency wasn't anaphylaxis or a deep neck space abscess—it was an autoimmune over-reaction with a systemic thief on the loose.

Recently, we evaluated a 40-year-old male who presented with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. The initial radiological finding showed diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles and inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space. It screamed Ludwig’s angina to anyone with a standard differential. He was afebrile, however, and eventually worked up for idiopathic angioedema. That was the easy part. The heavy lift began when a clinician noticed proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages and turned a treat-ment-able side note into a life-saving diagnosis request via a Creatine Kinase (CK). The CK was a staggering 16,184 U/L. This wasn't an allergic reaction; it was dermatomyositis.

But here lies the hidden complexity that every clinician must grapple with when this picture is presented to us. While we celebrate the diagnosis of dermatomyositis, we must immediately pivot our thinking to oncology. In adults, particularly those over 40, inflammatory myopathies are classified as paraneoplastic syndromes in a significant subset of patients. This case highlights that the "swollen neck" might have been the window of patience we needed before the screen we held at the diagnostic hurdle.

## The Oncologic Elephant in the Economic Room

It is tempting to get lost in the dermatology—the helmet eruption, the Gottron’s papules, or the nailfold changes that we found on this patient. However, dermatomyositis (DM) in a 40-year-old male carries a significant weight. We have to treat the tumor risk as aggressively as the myositis, especially because the presentation is notoriously difficult to distinguish from idiopathic skin conditions without a malignancy screen.

According to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines referenced in this case, dermatomyositis patients require rigorous stratification. Our patient wasn't just "at risk." He was high risk by definition. He had diabetes of the dermato-muscular form (the malignancy itself). He had progressive functional impairment (dysphagia). He had a positive anti-SAE1 antibody which adds to the risk profile. If we view this strictly as "inflammation," we risk missing the driver.

In this specific case, the risk was elevated because the diagnosis of DM is a high-risk factor, the male sex is an intermediate-risk factor, and dysphagia is a known high-risk hook. The combination creates a landscape where you cannot simply apply the standard "rule-out infection" protocols. It is not just about neck swelling; it is about the subcutaneous tissue where the inflammation resides due to the underlying muscle pathology, not histamine or bradykinin, as the text suggests.

The patient in the report had a PET scan to rule out active metabolic disease elsewhere and a Chest CT to clear pulmonary pathology. He also had plans for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to exclude occult GI malignancy, which is particularly crucial in the context of dysphagia and intestinal myositis susceptibility. We cannot leave the room until these risks are mitigated or a clear timeline for surveillance is established, as malignancy surveillance should continue well beyond the initial hospitalization.

## The Critical Takeaways

When you walk into a room with adult dermatomyositis, the skin tells the story, but the cancer risk dictates the choreography. Don't let the angioedema mimic steal this opportunity for early detection. We must ask ourselves:
Which features of myositis and clinical presentation warrant a prompt malignancy screen?
What is the timeline and scope for that screen?
How do specific antibodies alter the baseline risk?

*   **The "Angioedema" Trap:** In adult dermatomyositis, subcutaneous edema and dysphagia can mimic angioedema or deep neck space infections. A high CK level often serves as the differentiator in this scenario.
*   **Risk Stratification is Non-Negotiable:** Active surveillance via IMACS guidelines is warranted for Dermatomyositis, specifically for Dysphagia and Anti-SAE1 positive cases. High-risk features mentioned include male sex, anti-SAE1 antibodies, and dysphagia.
*   **Screening Must Be Aggressive:** The workup should extend beyond the chest. As implied in the case, gastrointestinal screening for occult mucosal malignancy is critical, often necessitating upper and lower endoscopy, especially when dysphagia is present.
*   **Follow the PET/CT:** In unclear cases, a PET scan can help rule out metastatic disease despite localized findings, as referenced by the FDG accumulation in muscle groups without active disease elsewhere in this specific patient.
*   **Treat the Whole Patient:** While we treat the inflammation with steroids and immunosuppression, we cannot deselect the paraneoplastic potential. The mortality risk decreases significantly when associated malignancy is identified and treated concurrently.
*   **Screen Again:** Interval surveillance is not a one-time checkbox. Even without a successful initial screen, patients must be re-evaluated regularly based on disease course and initial risk stratification.

If you see a neck that won't resolve and a CK that climbs to the ceiling, look deeper than the lymph nodes. Look for the cancer, because treating the skin is mandatory, but finding the tumor is what extends the horizon for these patients.
