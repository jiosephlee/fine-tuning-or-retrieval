Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Escalating Immunosuppression: A Protocol for Severe Dermatomyositis with Respiratory Involvement

## The Angioedema Mimic
We often get bogged down in the alleyways of the airway algorithm when a patient presents with neck swelling. Our standard mental model screams deep space infection or histaminergic angioedema, and while those are the most common adversaries, there are exceptions that require a different set of keys entirely. This case highlights a patient presenting with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling, initially labeled as probable Ludwig's angina, then reviewed by ENT as a deep neck infection, and finally labeled as idiopathic angioedema. None of those diagnoses fit the picture once you really look at the labs. The patient was afebrile, had a high inflammatory marker (CRP 55.6), and crucially, had a creatine kinase sky-high at 16,184 U/L. That number is the bell noise you can't ignore in the face of paresthesias or unexplained swelling. It is a classic example of subcutaneous tissue edema arising from myositis rather than vasodilation or complement activation, a presentation that essentially forces you to step outside the standard dermatologist-ear nose throat boundaries.

## Recognizing the Subtle Signals
The diagnostic journey here began with a subtle physical sign that was almost under the radar of the initial assessment. Reviewing the case, one notes that this patient lacked the classic textbook features for quite some time. There were no Gottron papules, no established heliotrope eruption, and no visible rash initially. Instead, the exam revealed periungual microhaemorrhages and subtle nailfold changes on capillaroscopy. These are often the "aesthetic" dermatomyositis signs that shock clinicians once they have been fishing for the classic myositis presentation. Even more telling was the presence of the Holster sign, noted on the operative MRI and later confirmed by biopsy, which represents edema of the skin overlying the extensor muscles—a specific marker of the disease process that occurred in the head and neck region.

The diagnostic pivot came when the immunology team identified a positive anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme 1 (SAE1) antibody. This is a specific marker for a clinical phenotype where dysphagia is particularly prominent (seen in 46% of cases), and malignancy risk is elevated compared to other types of myositis. The patient presented with severe aspiration pneumonitis on CT, confirming the airway involvement, which drove the urgency. The biopsy confirmed perifascicular atrophy and C5b-9 deposition, the definitive histological sign of dermatomyositis, but the clinical management had to account for the subcutaneous swelling which was inflamed muscle tissue, not histologicalangioedema. This distinction is vital because it means antihistamines and potentially epinephrine or methylprednisolone won't treat the swelling as quickly as needed given the inflammatory nature of the myositis.

## When Standard Care Isn't Enough: A Multidisciplinary Approach
When you land a patient in this trap with significant functional compromise—like this patient's severe dysphagia requiring a nasogastric tube—you have to consider an aggressive immunosuppression protocol before waiting too long for a taper. In this case, the team recognized that corticosteroids alone were insufficient to resolve the airway and swallowing risks in the short term. They started with bolus intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for three days. This is a high-dose regimen often reserved for SIAD/urethral obstruction or severe autoimmune conditions where humoral output is high. It was an upfront decision to treat the inflammatory edema in the retrogenginal space immediately rather than waiting to see if a prednisone taper would work.

Once the patient was stabilized, the intensity ramped up to a combination regimen. They utilized Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG) at 2g/kg split over five days, followed by maintenance cycles four weeks apart. For this particular antibody profile (SAE1) and the presence of pulmonary involvement, Rituximab was also added at 1000mg boluses. Crucially, despite the standard consideration of methotrexate for inflammatory myopathies, they explicitly avoided it due to the patient's existing pulmonary nodules and restrictive pattern, suggesting interstitial lung disease (ILD) risk. This is a critical clinical pearl: in patients with clear respiratory compromise or nodular disease, DM therapy must be tailored to spare the lungs from compounds that could exacerbate ILD. They switched prophylaxis to nebulized pentamidine after the patient developed hemolysis on TMP-SMX, demonstrating the rigidity often needed in managing immunocompromised inpatients.

The approach here supports the growing consensus that SAE1-positive patients require more robust immunosuppression than typical DM phenotypes, often sparing methotrexate for steroid carry-over while using mycophenolate mofetil for long-term control. The patient required a taper from 60mg Prednisone but faced long-term airway management requiring a monitoring plan for malignancy per IMACS guidelines. The multi-month follow-up shows close surveillance of CT chest and GI endoscopy is not optional but required given the dysphagia and skin involvement.

## Take Home Messages
*   **Unexplained neck swelling in a patient >40 with proximal weakness is dermatomyositis until proven otherwise.**
*   **Look for the "micro" signs:** Periungual changes, nailfold hemorrhages, and the Holster sign often precede classic heliotrope or Gottron signs.
*   **AB-cr:** Always order a CK level when you see unexplained angioedema or subcutaneous edema without histamine/bradykinin history.
*   **SAE1 phenotype:** Associated with higher risk of dysphagia and malignancy; consider aggressive therapy (IVIG/Rituximab) early.
*   **Lung protection:** Avoid methotrexate if there is evidence of ILD or pulmonary nodules; MMF is a preferred alternative.
*   **Clinical Surveillance:** High-risk DM patients require strict malignancy screening (CT chest, GI endoscopy) per IMACS guidelines.
