Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Airway Safety First: Managing Dysphagia in Acute Inflammatory Myopathy

Sometimes the most dangerous part of a medical mystery isn't the diagnosis itself, but the treatment delay it causes. In a recent case, a man in his 40s presented with severe neck swelling. The front-line teams immediately chased an infectious etiology—Ludwig’s angina. When that failed, they shifted gears to angioedema. Both diagnoses are correct in their own right for the *symptom*, but disastrous for long-term outcomes if they miss the underlying muscle pathology.

What we have here is dermatomyositis presenting with "pseudo-angioedema." But the airway implications go far deeper than a swelling neck. The underlying inflammatory process was actively chewing up the myofibres of the swallow musculature and shoulder girdle. The lesson here is critical for the Emergency Department and the Ward team alike: When you see unexplained neck swelling with high inflammatory markers, before you name an angioedema medication, think about the CK level and the dysphagia trial because malnutrition and aspiration risk can be the coma-equivalent complications of this disease.

## The Radiographic Deception and the Silent Danger

The initial CT of this patient’s neck showed diffuse oedema involving the strap muscles and inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. This image screams deep neck infection to the unwary radiologist. But he was afebrile, afebrile room air saturating, and denied fever on history. The ENT team looked for compression and congestion on nasendoscopy. They found increased secretions and coughing, but no structural blockage.

The key to unlocking this clinical picture lies in understanding that muscle hypertrophy and inflammation can mimic soft tissue oedema. In inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis, the neck swelling can be a subacute manifestation of muscle inflammation rather than a histaminergic or bradykininergic reaction. In this case, the C4, C1 esterase function, and C4 levels were all normal, effectively ruling out hereditary angioedema. However, the path to definitive treatment was blocked by the assumption that this was purely a cutaneous or primary airway issue.

Crucially, the dysphagia in this patient was not just a minor annoyance; it was a major airway threat. During the flexible nasendoscopy, the patient had increased secretions and coughing during the exam. The culmination of this finding was a barium swallow study that revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with significant pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration. This is not a patient who can safely eat dinner. It is a patient who is increasingly inhaling their own secretions.

The consequence of this aspiration was seen on admission CT chest: patchy areas of peribronchial consolidation likely representing aspiration pneumonitis. The patient was essentially suffering from the consequences of their muscle wasting and inflammation before they were even aggressive enough to be reclassified.

## Ensuring Nutritional Security and Preventing Complications

In a patient with this degree of proximal muscle involvement and swallowing dysphagia, nutritional status is an independent predictor of morbidity and mortality. Oral intake was impossible to maintain safely given the risk of aspiration pony-mania.

The management strategy involved securing the airway and nutrition early. By Day 1 of admission, it was clear that a nasogastric tube was required. The barium swallow confirmed the severity, and he was admitted with a plan to feed him per-aloogastric route because simply pushing him back to a diet of ice chips wasn't going to fix the aspiration.

This isn't a one-week fix. In the follow-up, he remained on a nasogastric tube for a further two months. This highlights a key teaching point for the emergency clinician: treat the dysphagia aerodynamically. A diet of fluids without protection from aspiration can lead to silent aspiration, pneumonia, and sepsis, which complicates the inflammatory picture further. If the patient isn't nutritional and hydrated, the immune system is stressed.

Furthermore, respiratory muscle involvement. The case report notes an extrathoracic restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests and fractional acidosis on a PET scan of multiple muscle groups including the chest wall. This leaves us in a position where we have to balance the need to suppress inflammation with the risk of respiratory muscle fatigue.

## Balancing Immunosuppression with Infection Risk

Once the diagnosis of Anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis was confirmed, the pharmacological path is aggressive. He got methylprednisolone 1000mg, IVIG, Mycophenolate, and Rituximab. That is a heavy hammer.

The mortality risk in inflammatory myopathy comes not only from the disease but from the prophylaxis. This patient has a high-risk profile. He needed PJP prophylaxis because of high-dose steroids and lung involvement. He was started on sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim but developed hemolytic anaemia, which was a challenge to manage as no G6PD deficiency was found. They switched him to nebulised pentamidine.

This detail is important. Infection management in these patients is precarious. If you suppress the inflammation and give them immunosuppression, you increase their susceptibility to *Pneumocystis*. If you try to get the infection cleared, you might delay the window to prevent muscle necrosis. You have to decide based on the clinical picture.

The resolution of the case is quite remarkable. The patient recovered. His CK normalized. His dysphagia resolved enough to pull the feeding tube. But the transition period was where the risk lay.

## Take-Home Clinical Pearls

*   **The CK is your best friend in neck swelling:** 
    In any patient presenting with unexplained neck swelling, lymphadenopathy, or restrictive symptoms, consider Dermatomyositis if the C-reactive protein is frankly elevated and the CT shows diffuse muscle involvement rather than abscess or pus.
*   **Dysphagia is a medical emergency:**
    Identify subtle signs (coughing on nasendoscopy, thick secretions) early. A Barium swallow can distinguish swallowing dysfunction from structural obstruction.
*   **Watch the airway:** 
    Inflammatory myopathy often involves the muscles of respiration. If your patient is on high-dose steroids or IVIG, they are at risk for PJP pneumonia with the immune system. Monitor for infiltrates that might mimic infectious pneumonia or PJP.
*   **Support via the nose, protect against aspiration:**
    *   **Nasogastric feeding:** Indicated for significant dysphagia to prevent progression to severe deconditioning.
    *   **Anesthesia:** Review the side effects of immunosuppression drugs like Methotrexate with interstitial lung disease.
    *   **Infection monitoring:** Ensure prophylaxis is appropriate.

There is a common adage: "Treat the patient, not the lab result." In this case, it was the neck swelling that brought the patient in, but the muscle enzyme kinetics and the swallow study that saved the patient from malnutrition and treatable further pneumonia. When you see a patient with "angioedema" refilling the hospital floor, remember that it is not always histamine. It could be muscle. And saving the patient's life might just depend on a simple swallow screen.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Red Flag:** Unexplained neck swelling in a patient with no cold lull symptoms matches Dermatomyositis.
*   **Action:** 
*   **Screen:** High-resolution CT and CK levels are required to distinguish pseudo-angioedema from infection.
*   **Therapy:** Aggressive suppression with IVIG and steroids is necessary but requires careful infection prophylaxis balancing.
*   **Monitor:** Periodic CT scans are essential for cancer screening in patients with SAE1 positive myositis.
