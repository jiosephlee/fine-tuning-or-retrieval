Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Anti-SAE1 Dermatomyositis: Unpacking the High-Risk Serological Phenotype

## A Neck Swelling That Wasn't Angioedema

In the ALS and ENT rotation, or any ED where you’ve seen a patient walk in with bilateral neck swelling, Ludwig’s angina, or idiopathic angioedema is always high on the differential. It’s the dangerous stuff—the airway, the stridor, the hypotension. But here’s a case that hits you at a different angle: A clinical paradox where subcutaneous edema is the leading symptom, but the pathology is autoimmune myopathy.

Think about the clinical vignette. A male in his 40s walks in with 3 weeks of progressive neck swelling and proximal hip weakness. The CT neck shows diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space. It looks like deep neck infection or angioedema. It feels like a surgical or allergology emergency. But the C1 esterase function is normal, C3 and C4 are normal, and there are no polyps or anatomical lumps found on the upper endoscopy.

The pivot point in the diagnosis is the forensic look—nailfold capillaroscopy. Subtle but here of the nailfold changes (periungual erythema, microhaemorrhages) and a skin patch on the thigh. Then, the cliffhanger: a creatinine kinase (CK) level of 16,184 U/L. In the world of undiagnosed myalgias with facial swelling, a CK that *cries out* for attention acts as the magic bullet that shifts the diagnostic category from "ENT infection/Angioedema" to "Inflammatory Polymyopathy." This case is a strong reminder that "angioedema unresponsive to standard etiology" is a敲门砖 (knockbrick) to consider cutaneous and muscular autoimmune diseases, particularly the myositis-subtypes we often think of as purely limb-restricting.

## The SAE1 Serological Signature

Let's zoom in on the specific pathophysiology driving this case. The patient was found to have a massive positive titration for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1). For the immunologists and rheumatologists among you, how SAE1 profiles compare to other myositis antibodies is crucial for prognostication.

While classic myositis antibodies like anti-Mi-2 (for DM) or anti-Jo-1 (for polymyositis) are well-characterized, SAE1-positive cases, as described in the literature review within the case, carave a distinct ill health risk profile. The case discussion explicitly highlights that SAE1-positive dermatomyositis typically carries higher burdens of organ involvement compared to negative serology or other antibody subgroups. Specifically:
*   **Dysphagia:** ~46% incidence, often severe enough to require tube feeding in acute presentations.
*   **Interstial Lung Disease (ILD):** ~43% incidence, which dictates screening intensity and medication choice.
*   **Malignancy Risk:** ~18% incidence, marking this group as high-risk by IMACS guidelines.

It is not enough to just find the antibody; the SAE1 status necessitates a paradigm shift from routine screening to proactive surveillance. The presence of microbiologically SAE1 antibodies suggests an autoantibody pattern that is often associated with more aggressive cutaneous and visceral involvement, particularly the subcutaneous edema seen here that mimics angioedema but arises from muscle inflammation and associated tissue infiltration rather than bradykinin or histamine release.

## High-Risk Management & Screening

This patient’s management strategy reflects the "aggressive" nature required for the SAE1 phenotype. Because of the dysphagia, rapid progression of myositis, and lung involvement, the treatment was not a standard steroid trial-and-error. It was heavy artillery: High-dose IV methylprednisolone followed by high-dose oral prednisone, plus combination immunomodulation. The regimen included Mycophenolate Mofetil (2g daily total), Rituximab, and intravenous Immunoglobulins (IVIG) in large dosing (165g over 5 days, plus maintenance).

Why the intensity?
1.  **Airway Protection:** The initial dysphagia and aspiration pneumonia risk (noted on chest CT) necessitated IVIG for rapid modulation and steroids to prevent necrosis.
2.  **Lung Safety:** With a restrictive pattern and nodules, Methotrexate was avoided despite being a common DM drug, as it carries risks with ILD. Mycophenolate was preferred for tolerability and efficacy profile in this context.
3.  **Cancer Screening:** Per IMACS guidelines, the patient had high-risk factors (dermatomyositis + dysphagia) and intermediate ones (SAE1+ + male sex). This triggered the full protocol: CT chest, abdominal workup, tumour markers, and plans for gastroscopy/colonoscopy to rule out occult malignancy.

The outcome speaks to the importance of timing. Despite the aggressive swelling and pulmonary nodules, CK normalized, muscle strength recovered, and the lung nodules and ground-glass opacities resolved after clearance of the underlying inflammation and aspiration. However, the long-term trajectory remains one of immune monitoring—IVIG maintenance and steroids are not "cure-als" yet; they are management tools to prevent the high-risk SAE1 profile from bouncing back or flaring into organ-threatening levels.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **The Neck Swelling Red Flag:** While subcutaneous edema is rare in DM (yet it is not uncommon in autoimmunity), neck swelling in an adult should trigger a CK check if C4/C1-inhibitor screens are negative and the patient has skin changes.
*   **SAE1 = Aggressive Disease:** The SAE1 serological phenotype is strongly associated with dysphagia, ILD, and a measurable malignancy risk, warranting earlier oncological screening than non-specific myositis.
*   **Avoid Toxic Myelosuppression:** In ILD-positive DM cases, favor Mycophenolate or Azathioprine over Methotrexate.

## Key Take-Home Messages

*   Distinguish myositis from angioedema when neck swelling is present if C4 and C1q levels are normal.
*   Check creatine kinase in unexplained neck swelling with concomitant skin/nail changes (heliotrope rash, nailfold telangiectasia, erythema).
*   SAE1 antibody positivity is associated with high-risk features including interstitial lung disease (43%), dysphagia (46%), and malignancy (18%).
*   SAE1-positive patients should undergo comprehensive cancer screening per IMACS guidelines (CT, tumor markers, upper/lower GI endoscopy).
*   Treatment in SAE1-DM often requires multi-agents (Steroids + IVIG + Rituximab/Myco) due to tissue inflammatory infiltration and risk of ILD/malignancy.
*   Avoid Methotrexate in patients with evidence of ILD or pneumonitis (use Mycophenolate instead).
