Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Differential Considerations

In emergency settings, patients presenting with acute neck swelling are aggressively evaluated for life-threatening infections, particularly Ludwig's angina, given the high morbidity associated with deep neck space infections. This case illustrates the diagnostic pitfall of anchoring on infectious or allergic etiologies when non-specific findings dominate. The patient, a male in his 40s, presented with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling accompanied by proximal muscle weakness. Crucially, unlike the toxic presentations typical of severe bacterial neck infections, the patient was afebrile and hemodynamically stable. This lack of systemic inflammatory markers initially puzzled the clinical team, yet the physical appearance of the neck—diffuse swelling involving the strap muscles and extending to the retropharyngeal space on CT imaging—strongly favored a structural or infectious mimic. The radiological finding of diffuse oedema without distinct abscess formation meant that standard imaging failed to definitively distinguish between a deep space infection, cellulitis, or inflammatory myopathy-related edema.

As the infectious etiology was ruled out due to the absence of fever and appropriate surgical clearance, the differential diagnosis naturally shifted toward spontaneous angioedema and other benign causes. Deep neck space infections were excluded by ENT assessment which found no anatomical abnormalities in the larynx or vallecula, yet the clinical picture still lacked the classic urticaria associated with histaminergic angioedema. This led to workups for hereditary angioedema (including C1 esterase and complement levels) and drug-induced causes, such as ACE inhibitor use. However, the patient had no trigger medications and normal complement levels, rendering idiopathic allergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema unlikely. Recognizing that the swelling was non-infectious but refractory to conservative management required clinicians to broaden their differential to include inflammatory myopathies, specifically reconsidering that the eye and throat inflammation was muscular in origin rather than allergic.

While the swelling was the primary complaint, a thorough physical examination revealed the critical clues that diverted the diagnosis away from angioedema. Examination of the nailfold capillaries identified microhaemorrhages and avascular areas, a finding highly specific for connective tissue disease rather than allergic reaction. Additionally, a subtle erythematous rash was observed on the lateral thigh and nasolabial folds, which would have been missed if the focus remained solely on the neck. The patient also demonstrated proximal muscle weakness, specifically reduced hip flexor power, revealing that the neck swelling was likely linked to myositis rather than localized edema. These cutaneous and muscular signs constituted the "patient-specific" features that challenge the "angioedema" red herring, as they suggest a systemic autoimmune process where muscle inflammation causes periorbital and cervical subcutaneous swelling, distinct from histamine or bradykinin pathways.

The diagnostic journey in this case underscores the importance of recognizing dermatomyositis as a potential mimic for unexplained "angioedema." When neck swelling is isolated without a clear allergic or infectious trigger, clinicians should investigate for musculoskeletal involvement, particularly in the context of proximal weakness or subtle rashes. Initial imaging limitations cannot always differentiate viral resolution, myositis, or infection without serological confirmation. Consequently, the decision to request a creatine kinase (CK) level—even with low suspicion—proved vital, revealing the massive muscle enzyme elevation that confirmed the autoimmune etiology. This presentation highlights that in inflammatory myopathies, socalled angioedema may actually be a manifestation of profound muscle inflammation, necessitating immunosuppression rather than antihistamines or androgen-targeted therapies.

Ultimately, the case serves as a teaching module on maintaining a broad differential for head and neck swelling in the absence of fever. It demonstrates how an initial focus on Ludwig's angina or idiopathic angioedema can obscure signs of dermatomyositis if detailed physical examinations, including nailfold and skin assessment, are not prioritized. The identification of subtle skin findings and weakness was the turning point that triggered the correct serological and pathological workup. Medical students and residents should be reminded that subclinical or atypical presentations of dermatomyositis, such as pseudoangioedema, require vigilance for cutaneous markers like nailfold changes or heliotrope-like erythema to prevent treatment delays and avoid unnecessary airway interventions.

## Dermatomyositis Pathophysiology and the SAE1 Phenotype

The distinction between polymyositis and dermatomyositis is a fundamental step in diagnosing idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM), as it dictates differential diagnosis and management. While polymyositis is primarily defined by direct immune-mediated muscle injury without cutaneous involvement, the case of this 40-year-old male highlights the diagnostic challenge where dermatomyositis masqueraded as a simpler condition. The clinical presentation of neck swelling initially led to a consideration of Ludwig’s angina or idiopathic angioedema, illustrating how IR-IIM can mimic non-musculoskeletal disorders. In this case, the distinction was solidified by recognizing that the swelling was driven by an underlying autoimmune process rather than infectious or allergic etiology. Recognizing that dermatomyositis represents a hybrid of skin and muscle pathology, rather than a pure myopathy, is essential for clinicians when encountering unexplained edema and myositis markers in patients who lack the obvious proximal muscle weakness in the initial phase.

The pathophysiological confirmation in this patient was provided by the muscle biopsy, which demonstrated the hallmark complement-mediated microangiopathy characteristic of dermatomyositis. Unlike polymyositis, which typically shows endomysial inflammation with T-cell infiltration, the patient’s thigh biopsy revealed follicular atrophy and necrotic myofibres arranged in a perifascicular distribution. Immunohistochemistry of this perifascicular damage showed specific deposition of membrane attack complex components (C5b-9) in the perifascicular endomysial capillaries, indicating a microvascular injury mechanism that is driven by immune complex deposition and complement activation. This specific vascular pathology explains the severe elevation in creatine kinase to 16,184 U/L observed in the patient, differentiating the clinical severity from inflammatory patterns that do not manifest with such extensive muscle necrosis. The presence of C5b-9 on capillaries is a key diagnostic differentiator that validates the cutaneous-muscle link and the ischemic nature of the muscle damage in dermatomyositis.

Cutaneous features serve as the critical surface markers for this systemic disease, although their presentation can be variable and subtle. In this case, typical cutaneous markers such as Gottron’s papules or the classic heliotrope eruption were initially absent, emphasizing the importance of examining less obvious dermatological signs. The diagnosis was supported by the presence of proximal nailfold changes, specifically microhaemorrhages and avascular areas identified during capillaroscopy, along with erythema of the face and a positive Holster sign. These findings provided tangible evidence of the perivascular inflammation and associated cutaneous immune responses that accompany the deep tissue injury. The development of specific cutaneous manifestations, including the nailfold changes identified in the patient, serves as a vital diagnostic clue when the more prominent rashes like the shawl sign are not yet evident.

Serological analysis further defined the specific trajectory of this patient’s disease through the identification of anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies. The patient tested strongly positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies with a signal intensity of 173, which is well above the normal range. This specific antibody profile correlates with distinct clinical phenotypes that were evident in this patient, including a high tendency for dysphagia. Literature connecting this phenotype suggests that patients with anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis are more likely to experience extra-muscular complications such as dysphagia and interstitial lung disease compared to other myositis antibody subsets, explaining the patient's severe swallowing issues requiring nasogastric tube feeding. Identifying this antibody allows for a more robust prognosis and a heightened index of suspicion for complications like aspiration, which was supported by the patient's need for fluoroscopic swallow study and subsequent aspiration pneumonitis.

Finally, the anti-SAE1 status and the broader diagnosis of dermatomyositis necessitated a structured approach to malignancy screening, as anti-SAE1 positivity is considered a risk factor associated with cancer. This patient required malignancy assessment in accordance with the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines, which identified him as high risk given his sex, presence of dysphagia, and positive antibody status. Although a comprehensive PET scan and tumor marker panel did not detect active malignancy, the clinical decision to screen aggressively reflected the diagnostic standard for anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis, which links the phenotype with an increased risk of associated malignancies. The absence of disease on follow-up imaging supports the high-risk profile acknowledged in the patient's management, reinforcing that dermatomyositis, particularly the SAE1-positive type, mandates vigilant ongoing surveillance for occult malignancy to guide long-term safety and therapeutic planning.

## Diagnostic Workup: From Immunology to Histopathology

The transition from an initial working diagnosis of idiopathic angioedema to definitive dermatomyositis relied on a systematic diagnostic workup that prioritized the identification of inflammatory myopathy markers while rigorously excluding structural, infectious, and malignant etiologies. The biochemical evidence acting as the primary pivot point in this case was the creatine kinase (CK) level of 16 184 U/L. Such a marked elevation indicated significant muscle breakdown, which is inconsistent with uncomplicated angioedema or Ludwig’s angina. Concurrently elevated transaminases (ALT 143 U/L, AST 396 U/L) and inflammatory markers (CRP 55.6 mg/L) suggested a systemic inflammatory process. Because statins were excluded as a cause and the patient was not undergoing necessary occupational exertion, the probability of an autoimmune myopathy increased significantly. This biochemical signal prompted the submission of an advanced myositis antibody panel rather than pursuing further otolaryngological investigations.

Serological interpretation provided specificity to the diagnosis. While a positive antinuclear antibody (ANA) with a speckled pattern offered non-specific evidence of autoimmunity, it was insufficient for classification. The critical finding was the presence of anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (anti-SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 compared to a normal range of <35. In the context of inflammatory myopathies, anti-SAE1 antibodies are strongly associated with dermatomyositis, particularly with cutaneous and mucosal involvement. Specificity was enhanced by the absence of other myositis-specific autoantibodies like anti-Jo-1 or anti-TIF1-γ, which are associated with different clinical phenotypes. Furthermore, the negative ANCA screen and normal C1 esterase protein levels helped rule out granulomatosis with polyangiitis and hereditary or acquired bradykinin-mediated angioedema, respectively. This antibody profile supported a diagnosis of a distinct subset of dermatomyositis characterized by significant dysphagia and cutaneous manifestations, rather than a purely idiopathic swelling disorder.

Advanced imaging played a dual role in locating the active inflammation and ruling out structural airway obstruction. While the initial CT of the neck suggested deep neck space infection or edema, subsequent MRI of the thighs localized the myositis to specific muscle groups, including the bilateral obturator internus and externus and iliopsoas muscles. This was confirmed by fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography (PET), which demonstrated diffuse abnormal accumulation throughout multiple muscle groups, including the head and neck. These functional imaging findings were superior to the initial nasendoscopy, which revealed normal anatomy but functional secretions and coughing, highlighting the signs of bulbar involvement despite the lack of anatomical blockage. Additionally, chest imaging was essential for assessing pulmonary risk factors given the known association of dermatomyositis with interstitial lung disease. The patient’s chest CT showed pulmonary nodules and consolidation likely from micro-aspiration, and pulmonary function tests revealed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, confirming potential organ involvement that would impact long-term prognosis.

The muscle biopsy remains the histopathological gold standard for establishing the diagnosis. Biopsy of the thigh demonstrated moderate numbers of necrotic and vacuolated degenerate myofibres in a perifascicular distribution, flanked by regenerative myofibres and scattered perimysial inflammation. The identification of perifascicular atrophy is a hallmark histological feature of dermatomyositis. Complementing this light microscopy finding was the immunohistochemical demonstration of complement component 5b-9 (C5b-9) deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries. This indicates complement-mediated endothelial injury and microvascular inflammation, which distinguishes dermatomyositis from other immune-mediated necrotizing myopathies and confirms the diagnosis.

Finally, the diagnostic process required comprehensive exclusion of malignancy, as anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis carries an elevated risk of underlying neoplasm according to International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines. The patient was deemed high-risk due to the combination of dermatomyositis and dysphagia. Serial imaging, including CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, along with cytology results, failed to identify lymphadenopathy or metastatic disease. Acute infectious differentials, such as tuberculosis (QuantiFERON-TB Gold and acid-fast bacilli) and viral hepatitis or HIV, were also systematically ruled out. By synthesizing the biochemical, serological, imaging, and histopathological data while definitively excluding structural causes for the neck swelling and malignancy, the clinical team could provide a targeted immunosuppressive regimen for dermatomyositis rather than continuing empiric treatment for angioedema or infection.

## Therapeutic Management: Induction, Maintenance, and Safety

The management of anti-SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis in this case required an aggressive, multi-modal immunosuppressive regimen to address severe proximal weakness, significant cervical edema, and dysphagia. Acute induction therapy prioritized airway protection and rapid inflammation reduction using high-dose corticosteroids. The patient received three days of intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily, followed by a tapering prednisone regimen starting at 60 mg daily weaning by 5 mg every two to three weeks. This pulse steroid therapy is standard for severe dermatomyositis to rapidly lower pathological muscle enzymes like creatine kinase, which in this case were markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L, and to reduce the risk of respiratory compromise associated with the inflammatory neck swelling. Steroid tapering was performed cautiously to prevent the rebound of myositis symptoms which can occur if the underlying autoimmune activity is not adequately controlled before maintenance therapy is established.

Given the patient's specific comorbidities, the selection of steroid-sparing agents was driven by concurrent pulmonary findings rather than standard dermatomyositis protocols. While methotrexate is a conventional steroid-sparing agent, it was explicitly avoided in this case due to the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests showing a restrictive pattern and CT evidence of pulmonary nodules and consolidation. Methotrexate is known to be associated with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis; therefore, mycophenolate mofetil was selected as the alternative to mitigate these risks while effectively suppressing the autoimmune response. To further manage the resistant nature of the disease, especially with significant airway involvement, the patient was treated with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and rituximab. Total IVIG dosing was calculated at 2 g/kg split over a loading dose and subsequent months, while rituximab 1000 mg was administered twice two weeks apart to deplete B-cells. This combination reflects an intensive strategy often reserved for severe multiorgan involvement or refractory disease where standard agents like methotrexate are contraindicated or insufficient.

Concurrent supportive care was essential to manage functional decline and the safety risks associated with the aggressive immunosuppression. The patient suffered from severe dysphagia with documented aspiration risk, necessitating the placement of a nasogastric tube for nutrition and medication administration throughout the hospital stay and for two months after discharge. Regarding infection prophylaxis, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP) prophylaxis was initiated given the high-dose immunosuppression, but the starting medication, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, was discontinued following the development of hemolytic anemia, even in the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. This adverse event required a timely switch to nebulized pentamidine. Finally, the management plan included rigorous long-term monitoring for disease flares and malignancy screening, as anti-SAE1 antibody status places the patient in a high-risk category for cancer, requiring serial CT chest imaging and endoscopy despite the reassuring initial workup. The long-term stability achieved demonstrates the importance of individualizing treatment plans that balance aggressive disease suppression with organ preservation and drug toxicity mitigation.

## Prognostic Factors, Follow-up Care, and Clinical Pearls

The patient’s recovery trajectory highlights the potential for substantial rejuvenation in anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis (DM) when treated aggressively, although resolution is multi-phasic. Within three weeks of discharge and following a tapered course of corticosteroids, the patient demonstrated full recovery of proximal muscle strength and normalization of creatine kinase levels from 16,184 U/L down to 37 U/L. While cervical soft tissue swelling resolved completely by three months post-diagnosis, functional recovery for the oropharyngeal muscles lagged behind limb strength; dysphagia persisted for six months beyond discharge before the nasogastric tube could be removed. This timeline serves as a critical teaching point for the clinical management of DM with dysphagia: while limb weakness may respond rapidly to immunosuppression, bulbar involvement often requires sustained therapy to prevent aspiration pneumonia and malnutrition, even as other symptoms improve. Consequently, discharge planning must prioritize secure airway nutrition and educate patients on medication adherence to mitigate relapse risk, particularly given the patient's initial inability to tolerate oral intake without a feeding tube.

In the context of long-term management, malignancy surveillance remains the most significant prognostic consideration for newly diagnosed dermatomyositis, particularly in older male adults with dysphagia and specific autoantibody profiles. This case adhered to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines, which categorize the patient as high risk due to the underlying disease (dermatomyositis) and specific complications (dysphagia), coupled with intermediate risk factors including male sex and the anti-SAE1 antibody positivity. The protocols established by IMACS necessitate comprehensive baseline screening, including thoracic imaging and planned gastrointestinal endoscopy, regardless of negative initial tumor markers. This example demonstrates that relying on initial tumor markers alone is insufficient; persistent surveillance via serial imaging and endoscopy is required to exclude occult neoplasia, as the development of malignancy occurs in approximately 18% of anti-SAE1 cases. Although this patient showed no radiological evidence of malignancy at 12 months, continued monitoring is standard of care. While the patient exhibited an uncomplicated course with no immediate progression of interstitial lung disease or other organ manifestations, clinicians must remain vigilant for late-emerging complications such as pulmonary fibrosis.

Predictably, early misdirection in the diagnostic process represents a significant cognitive hazard in dermatology and emergency medicine. The initial presentation of neck swelling mimicked a surgical emergency like Ludwig’s angina or a primary immunodeficiency condition like idiopathic angioedema, leading to a delay in actionable neuroimmunology interventions. The critical pivot occurred when the clinical team questioned the efficacy of standard angioedema workups in the presence of atypical physical signs, specifically nailfold capillaroscopy and the presence of skin changes. For residents and emergency physicians, the key clinical lesson is to maintain a low threshold for requesting a creatine kinase (CK) level whenever neck swelling is unexplained and not responding to standard histaminergic or bradykininetic blockade therapies. Ignoring the interaction between skin findings and neck edema risks the assumption that the condition is purely local, whereas dermatomyositis can cause myositis-mediated edema even in the absence of typical heliotrope rash or Gottron’s papules. The IMACS guidelines offer a framework for standardizing this surveillance, but the application of clinical judgment to identify DM in atypical presentations is equally vital. Ensuring that cancer screening protocols are rigorously followed per established guidelines is the final pillar in optimizing outcomes for patients with this rare and potentially masquerading autoimmune disease.
